Abstract
. This paper studies how parents want to moderate children’s interactions with Generative AI Chatbots, with the goal of informing the design of future GenAI parental control tools. We first used an LLM to generate synthetic Child–GenAI Chatbot interaction scenarios and worked with four parents to validate their realism. From this dataset, we carefully selected 12 diverse examples that evoked varying levels of concern and were rated the most realistic. Each example included a prompt and GenAI Chatbot response. We presented these to parents (N=24) and asked whether they found them concerning, why, and how they would prefer to modify the responses and be informed. Our findings reveal three key insights: (1) parents express concern about interactions that current GenAI Chatbot parental controls neglect; (2) parents want fine-grained transparency and moderation at the conversation level; and (3) parents need personalized controls that adapt to their desired strategies and children’s ages.

1.
Introduction

Despite being in its early stages, GenAI Chatbot technology has already drawn scrutiny from parents. Lawsuits filed against GenAI Chatbot developers cite concerns that these systems can isolate children from their families and communities, undermine parental authority, denigrate cultural or religious values, and obstruct parents’ efforts to manage online activity and ensure children’s safety
(
85
;
4
;
77
)
. These emerging risks highlight the urgent need to design parental control mechanisms suited to the distinctive properties of GenAI Chatbots.

Prior research in child-computer interaction has extensively studied parental control in the contexts of smartphones
(
51
;
62
)
, web browsing
(
5
;
95
)
, and social media
(
103
;
27
;
16
;
89
)
. This research has produced tools such as content filters
(
61
)
, screen time limits
(
43
;
48
)
, and activity monitoring systems
(
9
;
35
)
. However, GenAI Chatbots pose fundamentally different challenges.
Unlike conventional media, GenAI Chatbot interactions are open-ended and their outputs are not tied to fixed resources such as websites, apps, or videos.
Moreover, GenAI Chatbots give children faster access to individualized responses than social media or online forums: children can consult a GenAI Chatbot instantly and receive tailored feedback, rather than sorting through many public posts. This combination of speed and specificity may amplify risks by making GenAI Chatbots a highly convenient default source of advice, increasing the likelihood that children become dependent on these tools
(
40
;
15
)
.
Recent work has enumerated parents’ concerns surrounding child–GenAI Chatbot interactions
(
106
)
.
However, we have little knowledge regarding how parents want to moderate these interactions, given their concerns.

In this paper, we conducted IRB-approved interviews with parents (N=24) of children aged 6-18 to understand how they want to moderate their children’s interactions with GenAI Chatbots (Figure
1
).
We first used an LLM to generate synthetic Child–GenAI Chatbot interaction scenarios
(
84
;
7
)
using a multi-agent simulation framework
(
50
;
74
;
14
)
, and consulted with four parents to filter them. We retained 12 realistic and seemingly benign scenarios that raised some parents’ concerns. Each example included a prompt and GenAI Chatbot response.
We then presented these scenarios to parents (N=24) in semi-structured interviews and asked about whether they found them concerning, the factors that triggered their concern, and their desired moderation and transparency-related interactions. For example, Figure
1
illustrates a scenario that appears helpful on the surface but subtly undermines a parent’s approach to safety, which parents described as inappropriate.
Lastly, we conducted thematic analysis of interview transcripts to group parents’ low-level comments into high-level takeaways.

Our study allows us to answer the following three research questions:

RQ1: What are the factors that trigger parents’ concern in Child-GenAI Chatbot interactions?

We found that parents identified concern factors that could be organized into two main groups: (1) questionable GenAI Chatbot responses (Figure
1
➂) and (2) questionable child prompts (Figure
1
➃). Within the questionable GenAI Chatbot responses group, most parents identified the concerning factor that the system fails to grasp the underlying meaning of a child’s request, for example, by failing to clarify intent or by failing to address the root cause. Within the questionable child prompts group, most parents identified the concerning factor
that a child could have harmful intentions.
For example, when the child in Figure
1
asks "Can I climb on the roof to see the stars better?" it is unclear whether they understand the danger involved. Parents felt that these indicators hinted at a problematic child mindset and questioned whether the GenAI Chatbot could reliably safeguard the interaction.

RQ2. How do parents want to moderate their children’s interactions with GenAI Chatbots?

Parents described fine-grained moderation goals that extended beyond preventing harm. They wanted interactions to be age-appropriate, emotionally supportive, and aligned with family values. They emphasized five desired moderation-related interactions (Figure
1
➄):
(1) refuse prompts and remove content while providing explanations,
(2) investigate unclear intentions and emphasize important concepts,
(3) correct potential misunderstandings,
(4) respond with age-appropriate language and concepts,
and (5) defer to human support.
Overall, parents envision the GenAI Chatbot as a mediatory partner that supports parents’ ongoing role as a moral and developmental guide.

RQ3: What are parents’ transparency preferences around their child’s interactions with a GenAI Chatbot?

Transparency refers to the level of openness and clarity parents sought regarding Child–GenAI Chatbot interactions. We found that parents’ desired transparency-related interactions could be described using two dimensions: their desired level of (1) involvement (Figure
1
.➅), and (2) data content access (Figure
1
.➆). Most parents expressed a desire for the transparency-related interaction "Be Alerted to Flagged Activity". They wanted real-time notifications, such as a text alert, when their child asked about sensitive or potentially concerning topics.

Contributions.
This paper presents the first systematic investigation of how parents of children aged 6-18 want to moderate children’s interactions with a GenAI Chatbot, grounded in seemingly benign scenarios that surface nuanced needs. Previous research identifies concerns that parents have regarding child–GenAI Chatbot interactions. We systematically characterize the factors that trigger parents’ concerns, as well as the desired moderation- and transparency-related interactions that mitigate them.
Throughout our analysis, we find a recurring message: parents want GenAI Chatbots to be personalized by incorporating their own desires and their children’s needs.

Expanding Roles of GenAI Chatbots in Children’s Lives

The role of GenAI Chatbots is rapidly expanding in children’s lives. For example, GenAI Chatbot reading companions enhance children’s interest and support reading engagement

In contrast, parents are unaware of their children’s usage

This project is motivated by the widening gap between children’s rapid adoption of GenAI Chatbot and parents’ limited understanding. A lack of understanding of children’s usage prevents parents from imagining realistic use cases and expressing grounded concerns. We address these unique challenges by presenting parents with examples to contextualize Child–GenAI Chatbot interactions, in educational, companion, and other everyday settings.

2.2.
Parental Control Tools in Conventional Domains

Prior research in Child-Computer Interaction has extensively studied parental control tools for emerging technologies. Research on children’s web browsing has led to the development of content filters that block pages by category or keyword, and "safe search" modes. These help families manage children’s exposure to adult content, tracking, and unwanted contact

. Parents rely on these built-in safety tools to protect children from harassment, body-image pressure, and data tracking as they use social media
(

;

;

. Furthermore, research on parental control in the context of smartphones has led to screen time controls that cap total device use, set app-level quotas, and schedule downtime
(
43
;
48
)
. Manufacturers have built activity-monitoring systems into their devices that report and restrict app usage and installations. Examples of this include Apple’s Family Sharing/Screen Time and Google Family Link
(
9
;

. Parents use these systems to address concerns of device overuse, distraction, and sleep interference
(
51
;

)
. However, children have been found to regularly bypass similar restrictions
(
70
;
47
)
, and children use these technologies from incredibly young ages
(
12
)
. Moreover, these measures may not adequately protect children’s privacy
(
59
)
, and there are calls for more robust age-assurance
(
47
)
.

GenAI Chatbots pose fundamentally different challenges for parental control tools than conventional technologies. GenAI Chatbots integrate a broad range of functions (information search, creative writing, emotional support, and more) into a single, stochastic conversational interface.
GenAI Chatbots dynamically adapt to a child’s unfolding conversation, and co-construct private, one-to-one interactions. These new challenges motivate us to investigate the factors that concern parents and the moderation and transparency-related interactions they desire surrounding children’s GenAI Chatbot use, with the aim of understanding where they may lie in relation to conventional parental mediation tools.

2.3.
Parental Mediation Strategies in Conventional Domains

Parental mediation "encompasses the strategies adopted by parents to regulate, discuss, and monitor their children’s media use"
(
53
, p. 201)
, with the aim of balancing children’s participation with technology and its corresponding risks
(
54
;
57
)
.
Prior work distinguishes four parental mediation styles for children’s internet use. In "monitoring" (1), parents review what the child has done online after the fact (e.g., browsing history, contacts, logs) to track their online behavior. In "restrictive content mediation" (2), parents set and enforce rules about which sites, apps, or content the child may access and how long they may be online. In "active safety mediation" (3), parents talk with children about online risks and safety, explaining dangers and offering guidance while or before the child goes online. In "restrictive technical mediation" (4), parents use technical tools—such as filters, blocking software, or built-in parental controls—to automatically enforce limits on access, contacts, or screen time.
(
94
)
. Parents’ use of mediation styles is shaped by demographics, children’s online skills, and parents’ perceptions of online risks and benefits
(
69
;
68
)
.

Parents implement a variety of successful mediation strategies on a spectrum that ranges from maintaining full control over children’s engagement to supporting children’s independent navigation of online risks
(
89
;
33
;
2
;
58
)
.
However, some mediation approaches appear more effective than others at allowing parents to actualize "healthy" children’s technology use
(
60
;
67
)
. For example, prior work demonstrates that over-reliance on restrictions can impede children’s development of self-regulatory skills, leaving children less prepared to navigate digital risks as they grow older. Successful mediation approaches tend to be child-centered and actively help young people learn how to self-regulate their behavior
(
32
;
104
;
38
;
24
)
. This means teaching children self-monitoring, impulse control, and risk-coping skills to help them become resilient technology users, regardless of their age
(
104
)
.

Findings that “parental mediation may vary because of the complexity of the situation and the values at stake”
(
53
, p. 202)
suggest that the mediation styles parents employ surrounding GenAI Chatbots may differ from those for conventional technologies. Parents’ desired GenAI Chatbot–mediation may involve moderation strategies that implement "restrictive content mediation" or "restrictive technical mediation," as well as transparency approaches that implement "active safety mediation" or "monitoring". This motivates us to systematically characterize how parents may wish to mediate child–GenAI Chatbot interactions. We envision designers enriching parents’ configurable mediation toolkits
(
69
)
in the GenAI Chatbot setting by actualizing parents’ desires with mediation-assistive tools, including parental controls.

Our study proceeded in three main stages: (1) we generated simulated child–GenAI Chatbot scenarios, (2) we conducted an iterative procedure with a small group of four parents to evaluate and select scenarios, and (3) we conducted semi-structured interviews with 24 parents using the selected scenarios. This study was approved by our university’s IRB. Prior HCI and vignette-based research shows that when experimental tasks are realistic and easy to imagine, participants provide valid, candid feedback that better mirrors real-world behavior
(
2
;
85
;
55
;
103
)
.
We designed realistic single-turn scenarios for use as technology probes to ground parents’ feedback.

3.1.
Scenario Design

A central design question for our study was whether scenarios should be presented as single-turn exchanges or multi-turn dialogues. To evaluate this, we conducted an IRB-approved study with parents to assess the feasibility of using each dialogue type in our scenarios. Four of the authors independently invited one eligible parent from their personal networks, confirmed eligibility using the pre-screening survey described in Section
3.4
, and collected consent. We excluded close relatives, students, and supervisees to avoid conflicts of interest, undue influence, and biased responses. These parents represented all four developmental age groups described in Section
3.2
. Across participants, two consistent issues emerged: (1) validating multi-turn conversations took parents 3 to 5 times longer than validating single-turn scenarios, and (2) parents surfaced largely similar concerns in both conditions, indicating that multi-turn probes added little analytic value at this stage. Because a central design requirement of our study was to let each parent evaluate as many distinct scenarios as possible within a fixed interview, multi-turn scenarios consumed more time and constrained how many scenarios each parent could review. We instead prioritized concise single-turn interactions for our data collection. This allowed parents to evaluate a larger and more diverse set of probes, helped maintain parents’ attention, and increased the likelihood that some scenarios would align with their own values and experiences.

We note that real child–GenAI Chatbot interactions are often multi-turn. Single-turn probes cannot fully capture how risks evolve across conversations, and some harms emerge only after several turns
(
84
)
, including hidden backdoor behaviors that single-turn tests may miss
(
20
)
. In this first stage of work, however, we prioritized clarity, interpretability, and participant feasibility, while acknowledging these limitations. Future work could address the challenges multi-turn scenarios present for use in interviews and should extend our investigative approach to include richer multi-turn conversations that capture emergent, sequence-dependent risks.

3.2.
Scenario Generation Procedure

We drew inspiration from prior simulated Child–GenAI Chatbot prompt datasets
(
84
;
49
;
7
)
and created a scenario pool spanning safe and harmful prompts.
We decomposed the scenario generation task into specialized subtasks and assigned each to an LLM agent, as parental control is a multi-stakeholder problem involving children, parents, and GenAI Chatbot designers. Decomposition allowed each agent to iteratively represent the interests and constraints of these different stakeholders
(
50
;
74
;
14
)
.

The goal of scenario generation was to surface parents’ concerns regarding child–GenAI Chatbot interactions, rather than to benchmark or compare model capabilities. Prior work and our pilot interviews indicate that LLM-generated scenarios often exhibit limited realism, whether produced by a frontier model or a lightweight one; for our purposes, the primary requirement was fluent text generation rather than sophisticated reasoning. Accordingly, we selected OpenAI’s GPT-4.1-nano for its speed and cost-efficiency. Our design intentionally favored breadth over precision: we generated a large pool of candidate scenarios and then filtered for those that best aligned with our criteria. We queried the model with default API parameters, including
temperature
(1),
max_completion_tokens
(4096), and
top_p
(1)
(
71
)
. In practice, GPT-4.1-nano reliably produced coherent, varying-topic child–GenAI Chatbot interactions at scale, and we do not claim it is representative of frontier systems or uniquely suited for this task.

Before generation, we created synthetic child personas following prior prompt-simulation methods
(
84
;
7
)
and role-based prompting approaches
(
19
)
. Personas varied by context, gender identity, and developmental stage, using Piaget’s four age bands (6–8, 9–12, 13–15, 16–18)
(
13
)
. We intentionally excluded personally identifiable information (e.g., locations, health conditions, school names, family structures
(
106
)
), which limited our ability to simulate privacy-sensitive behaviors such as oversharing. Appendix
B
provides full demographic specifications and examples.

Agent 1 generated a synthetic child prompt by ingesting a randomly sampled persona, then, Agent 2 produced the verbatim GenAI Chatbot response to that prompt. These generated each new scenario for which, Agent 3 returned a single-word label (“unique” or “duplicate”) by comparing it against all previously generated scenarios. We supplemented this with cosine similarity analysis using SentenceTransformer embeddings (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) and set a similarity threshold of 0.85, substantially higher than a common starting point of 0.5
(
23
)
. A scenario was rejected if Agent 3 labeled it a duplicate or if its embedding exceeded the threshold. We iterated in batches of 20 until an entire batch produced no unique scenarios, yielding an initial set of 160 scenarios.

The embedding-based cosine similarity provided only a coarse semantic-similarity signal and could not distinguish between scenarios that shared a topic but raised different parental concerns. As a result, the automatic stage may have over-aggressively removed scenarios that were only slightly distinct. For this study, however, we primarily needed a small set of differentiated probes, and therefore treated the automatic pipeline as a heuristic filter rather than a fully reliable automatic deduplication system.
Future iterations of this pipeline could strengthen the automatic step by allowing Agent 3 to reason before deciding (e.g., chain-of-thought prompting
(
102
)
), by using more capable models, and by incorporating topic-level clustering methods such as BERTopic
(
30
;
39
)
prior to manual review.

Because of this limitation, we manually reviewed all automatically accepted scenarios. We removed (1) scenarios that shared identical child intent and safety concern but differed only in surface topic (e.g., “How do I build a loudspeaker?” vs. “How do I build a solar oven?”); (2) minor lexical variants that preserved the same conversational structure; and (3) repetitive interaction patterns within an age group (e.g., multiple friendship-advice prompts for teens). We did not remove hallucinated or factually incorrect responses, as these reflect real GenAI Chatbot behavior. After manual curation, we reduced the 160 automatically accepted scenarios to 66 conceptually distinct scenarios.

3.3.
Scenario Selection

To further narrow down our scenario pool, we conducted an IRB-approved iterative procedure with parents (N=4).
Four of the authors independently invited one parent they knew through their personal networks, confirmed their eligibility with a pre-screening survey (described in Section
3.4
), and collected their consent. We excluded close relatives, students, and supervisees to avoid conflicts of interest, undue influence, and biased responses that could compromise informed consent and data integrity. We also excluded the parents who participated in the iterative procedure (Section
3.1
).
We used this convenience sample of parents only for rapid prototyping. The ages of these parents’ children were distributed across the four developmental age groups, as described in Section
3.2
.
All of these parents and their children regularly used GenAI Chatbots.

We acknowledge UNICEF’s Guidance on AI for Children, which emphasizes that children’s participation is essential when AI systems are intended for or used by them
(
98
)
. Our use of parent perspectives at this early-stage does not place them above children’s views; rather, it reflects UNICEF’s requirement that AI systems be rigorously tested to ensure they do not expose children to harmful, misinterpreted, or unsafe content. Because our scenario pool includes potentially sensitive prompts, involving children at this stage would violate these safety principles
(
98
)
. Parent validation, therefore, serves as an ethically appropriate interim step until scenarios can be adapted for safe, child-facing evaluation.
We therefore begin with parent validation, consistent with UNICEF’s safety guidance, and leave direct child participation to future work once appropriate safeguards and IRB protocols are in place.

We asked each of the four parents to review all 66 scenarios and rate them on two 7-point Likert scales: (1) how realistic they found the scenario, and (2) how concerned they would be if their child encountered it.
We retained scenarios with a median realism rating above 4 (i.e., more realistic than unrealistic) and a variance of concern ratings greater than 1.0 (i.e., scenarios that parents found controversial). This filtering produced 15 scenarios (Fig.
3
).
We then excluded 3 scenarios for which neither parent expressed concern, leaving a final set of 12 scenarios.

3.4.
Participant recruitment

We recruited parents for interviews through a combination of in-person and online methods.
None of the parents who participated in the iterative procedure (Section
3.1
) or the early-stage scenario screening (Section
3.3
) were recruited for interviews to prevent familiarity bias and ensure that no individual contributed to both prototype refinement and evaluation. For in-person recruitment, we publicized the study through fliers placed in high-traffic areas on and around a university campus, including family housing complexes, childcare centers, and school pick-up zones. We also recruited online through the participant sourcing platform Prolific
(
78
)
. In Prolific, we restricted the study to participants who are located in the United States, have at least one child between the ages of 6 and 18, and have an approval rate (the ratio of past approved submissions to rejected submissions) of 95% or higher. We recruited participants on Prolific in batches of four across multiple days to solicit a diverse group.
We had all participants (in-person and Prolific) complete a pre-screening survey to confirm their eligibility and report their children’s ages and details about their and their children’s GenAI Chatbot usage. Appendix
F
contains the full pre-screening survey questions.
We collected participants’ minimal contact information (name and email), given the sensitivity of family/child-related research
(
75
)

3.5.
In-depth Interviews

We conducted IRB-approved semi-structured interviews with parents (N=24), using the selected child-GenAI Chatbot interaction scenarios to investigate the factors behind their concerns, their desired moderation-related interactions, and their desired transparency-related interactions.

Prior to scenario review, we asked parents a short set of background questions about their children (e.g. age, personality, GenAI Chatbot use) and whether they currently monitored their child’s GenAI Chatbot use. Parents then reviewed and signed a consent form.

All participants reviewed the same set of 12 scenarios in random order and completed as many as possible within the allotted 45-minute session, with the option to skip scenarios at any time. To facilitate comprehension, scenarios were presented as static mockups styled to resemble ChatGPT conversations, displaying both the child’s prompt and the GenAI Chatbot response.
For each scenario, parents were asked about:

(1) Concern. Whether they were concerned about the interaction, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (Not concerned at all, Somewhat unconcerned, Neutral, Somewhat concerned, Concerned). If “Not concerned at all” was selected, we advanced to the next scenario. Otherwise, we asked why they were concerned.

(2) Moderation. How would they change the response to make it more suitable for a child in that age group, with encouragement to reflect on tone, structure, and content.

(3) Transparency. Whether they would want to be aware of the interaction and how they would want to be informed, situating ChatGPT’s role in the broader context of parental involvement.

We employed probing techniques
(
86
)
to prompt elaboration, comparisons, and consideration of hypothetical variations. These follow-up questions helped surface parents’ contextual reasoning and implicit values, centering the discussion on reflection rather than evaluation of isolated scenarios. The full interview questions are provided in Appendix
G
.

3.6.
Data Collection and Analysis

All interview sessions were recorded with participant consent and subsequently transcribed. One researcher led the interview, whereas the other researcher took notes.
We iteratively conducted the interviews and analyzed the data concurrently.
We conducted interviews in rounds of four participants to approximate coverage across the four developmental age groups described in Section
3.2
.
After each round, the four researchers independently coded one of the four transcripts using Saldaña’s grounded theory–informed framework
(
88
)
.
We began creating codebooks from scratch, one for each research question, after the first round of interviews and updated them iteratively with each successive round.
We briefly summarize the coding stages we employed:
Initial Coding
involved examining the transcript line by line to break down the qualitative data, compare segments for similarities and differences, and generate descriptive codes.
Focused Coding
then identified the most frequent or analytically significant initial codes to develop the most salient categories in the data corpus.
Axial Coding
explored the relationships among these categories, articulating their properties and dimensions and integrating them into higher-level themes. After each round of independent coding, the team met to compare codes, discuss discrepancies, and reach consensus.
Across six rounds, this process— independent coding, collaborative reconciliation, and iterative refinement— continued until we observed data saturation
(
90
)
, meaning no new themes appeared in the fifth and sixth rounds.

Each code we developed during Initial Coding is a type of factor that triggered parents’ concern for RQ1, a type of moderation-related interaction for RQ2, or a type of transparency-related interaction for RQ3.
The themes we developed during Focused Coding are: concern factors organized by origin for RQ1; moderation-related interactions for RQ2; and transparency-related interactions organized by components for RQ3.

4.1.1.
Questionable response from the GenAI Chatbot.

Many parents identified properties of the GenAI Chatbot’s response as a central source of concern. Parents feedback on the responses themselves focused on (1) how replies would shape children’s feelings and possible future actions, (2) whether the GenAI Chatbot understands the child’s underlying intent, and (3) whether children can realistically use the response.

Responses shape how children feel and what they may do later.

Many parents worried that a response’s tone, content, and values could leave a lasting emotional impact, even when children did not fully understand the words. One parent noted that their child "would walk away not feeling good, even if he didn’t fully understand, because there are things in here that are going to sit with you, whether you fully understand them or not" (P19, S11). A few parents also pointed out that responses quietly expand the set of actions a child might consider. For example, a few parents were concerned that a suggestion to "try it at home" (S1), planted an idea that a child would not have otherwise considered. These concerns highlight that, even when the topics in a response aren’t concerning, parents treat idea seeding or an emotionally upsetting tone as issues. These concern factors seem to be examples of an "empathy gap" which previous work hypothesized GenAI Chatbots may face when responding to children
(
52
)
.

Responses may miss what the child really means.

A majority of parents were concerned when the GenAI Chatbot treated a prompt too literally and responded without first investigating why the child was asking. They argued that it was inappropriate for the GenAI Chatbot to give guidance or refuse without considering whether the request might have multiple interpretations. For example, in the keyless-entry scenario (S4), one parent explained that there was "more than one way to look at it" and that, when the GenAI Chatbot refused to help without context, the system "seems that it’s broken" (P24, S4). A few parents similarly worried when a response ignored a interpretation that the child’s prompt could signal an unhealthy mindset. In the Error-free-perfectionism scenario (S3), these parents expected the GenAI Chatbot to consider all possibilities and felt that any response to this scenario could be inappropriate as it lacked necessary information to make an informed response.

Some responses are unusable to children and therefore feel unsafe.

Many parents also judged responses to be unsafe when children were unlikely to understand them, even if the information was accurate. They pointed to explanations that were cluttered, indirectly phrased, or explained at an adult level, for example, with advanced vocabulary or abstract concepts that younger children could not grasp. One parent observed that children "probably don’t really have a very contextual understanding of the word love" (P19, S9). Parents felt that if a child cannot extract the main warning or takeaway, then the response was concerning because it failed to answer in a meaningful way. These concerns mirror themes identified in broader work on developmental appropriateness in media and mediation for conventional technologies
(
56
;
68
)
.

Across these focused themes, most parents evaluated how replies operate within the scenario at large rather than flagging responses for including content they felt was inappropriate. Parents attended to how responses might sit with a child over time, what new options they introduced, and whether they were usable given a child’s developmental stage.

4.1.2.
Questionable prompt from the child.

Parents also found that a child’s prompt itself could be a primary source of concern. Many parents pointed to the prompt as their source of concern when evaluating scenarios. They felt that prompts could indicate potentially harmful intentions or signs of cognitive or emotional overdependence on the GenAI Chatbot, and expressed skepticism about whether technical safeguards would hold if a child kept pushing. In many interviews, parents reacted more strongly to what the prompt revealed about the child than to any specific line in the GenAI Chatbot’s reply.

Prompts can signal potentially harmful intentions.

Most parents raised concerns when they felt that a prompt appeared to seek ways to hurt someone, humiliate peers, or bypass security rules, regardless of how the GenAI Chatbot responded. In the fire-starting scenario, one parent interpreted the prompt as evidence that "he’s[the prompting child] got some ill intentions, and you know, that’s something that needs to be addressed right away" (P20, S2). A few parents were worried when children seemed comfortable asking an GenAI Chatbot questions they might hide from an adult. They noted that this comfort could be dependent on an expectation that the system "will not reveal" the interaction to parents (P19, S2). Throughout our interviews, most parents felt that the response was secondary to the hypotheticals that could underlay a child’s
prompt
, because a response’s validity depended on what was true in the bigger picture.

Prompts can reveal overdependence on the GenAI Chatbot.

A few parents read certain prompts as signs that a child was becoming overly dependent on the GenAI Chatbot. They were concerned when children appeared to turn to the system instead of talking to caregivers or thinking through problems on their own. One parent worried that their child was "going to ChatGPT" rather than coming to them to understand a situation. A few parents described "a murky area of AI filling the need for true human interaction or healthy relationships", where prompts about feelings or affection suggested that the GenAI Chatbot was becoming a substitute for human support. These parents’ concerns may be an indicator of an established concern, children’s overuse of digital media
(
82
)
.

Some parents doubt that safeguards will hold under persistent probing.

A few parents also expressed skepticism about whether technical safeguards could withstand a determined child. They believed that children could rephrase or reframe requests until the system produced unsafe guidance, particularly for the hacking (S6) or rule-breaking (S1) scenarios. These parents questioned whether the GenAI Chatbot could recognize when a conversation had escalated enough to be shut down. This skepticism adds commentary to about existing safety work for GenAI Chatbots, because these parents readily envision children circumventing any guardrails that may be put in place.

Across these focused themes, parents felt that the child was the root cause for concern in a scenario. Most parents were concerned about children’s intentions that could suggest harmful intent or deep reliance on the system, and that the intention behind a prompt determined the appropriateness of a response.

4.1.3.
Why the source of concern matters.

By separating concerns that originate in the GenAI Chatbot’s responses from those that originate in children’s prompts, our analysis clarifies who parents expect to act. When concerns are response-based, parents describe issue with the GenAI Chatbot, including its tone, the unsolicited suggestions it offers, or its understanding of intent. When concerns are prompt-based, parents find vulnerabilities in children that GenAI Chatbot responses don’t adequately address, including harmful mindsets, overdependence, or a potential to push against guardrails. Because we identify the sources of parents’ concerns in this way, our concern factors extend prior work on parents’ content-level worries
(
106
)
and general mediation styles
(
56
;
68
;
69
;
94
)
by identifying
where
in an interaction parental controls can intervene.
These targeted concern factors lay the foundation for our analysis of parents desired moderation and transparency-related interactions at the level of individual conversations.

GenAI Chatbots should explain moderation choices.

Most parents wanted the GenAI Chatbot to treat moderation as an opportunity to help children understand why a request may be problematic, rather than simply refusing. They described that ideal responses would reframe the child’s question and highlight the underlying risk. In the keyless-entry scenario, for example, one parent suggested that the GenAI Chatbot should remind the child that locked doors are typically safety measures: “You should say, ‘If the door is locked, there’s a reason for it’” (P8, S4). Others wanted the system to reinforce household norms by offering safer alternatives, such as “alternative apps that are allowed for kids” rather than instructions for bypassing a school firewall (P5, S1). These correction-oriented operations mirror active mediation in which parents talk with children about online risks rather than only restricting access
(
94
;
104
)
. We found that parents expected the GenAI Chatbot itself to take on the explanatory role of "active mediation"
(
94
)
inside the conversation and felt that a response could actively reshape children’s reasoning and add value through teaching.

Developmental fit determines safety and usability.

Many parents emphasized that moderated responses must remain developmentally appropriate so that children can actually use the warning. Prior work on child-facing AI and media highlights age-appropriate communication as a core design requirement
(
98
;
26
;
68
)
, often framed in terms of effectiveness or engagement. Parents in our study went further and treated developmental mismatch as a safety problem. In the forgotten-stove scenario, one parent suggested replacing "asphyxiation" with simpler language because "that word is too big" for children (P2, S8). Another parent worried that “the concepts and themes for this might be a little complex for a ten-year-old” (P15, S11). Parents argued that if a child cannot understand the vocabulary, the structure, or the story’s moral implications, they cannot internalize the warning, even if the content is factually correct.

Moderation should respond to children’s intentions and emotions, and clarify that the GenAI Chatbot is not a person.

Many parents wanted moderation to engage with the child’s underlying intentions and emotional state. They wanted a GenAI Chatbot to ask why the child wanted to do something, validate their feelings, and then steer them toward safer options. One parent suggested that the GenAI Chatbot should “probe a little bit further like, ‘Tell me, what in particular are you struggling with?’” (P21, S3). Parents also worried about the emotional tone of a response. One parent explained that the phrase “but I have to be clear” made them angry at the GenAI Chatbot even as an adult (P19, S1), and feared that similar wording could make children feel scolded rather than supported. Existing work on algorithmic parenting and parental control tools notes that many current systems prioritize surveillance and control over developmental or emotional support
(
55
;
95
;
40
)
. Some parents explicitly wanted the GenAI Chatbot to remind children that it is not human, for example, “I want to make sure they understand it is a computer and not a human” (P3, S5). Guidance for child-facing AI warns that children may misinterpret AI systems as social partners if their nature is not made clear
(
26
;
98
)
. Our findings extend these concerns to the level of individual conversations: parents expect each response to acknowledge the child’s intentions and emotions and to explicitly clarify that a GenAI Chatbot is non-human.

Parents expect strong boundaries that avoid seeding new ideas.

Parents described a different set of moderation expectations when the concerning factor came from the child’s prompt rather than from the GenAI Chatbot’s response. When prompts involved hurting others, humiliating peers, or bypassing rules, many parents wanted the system to set clear limits and avoid normalizing the behavior. In the harsh school-roast scenario, one parent suggested the system should say, "It is not good to be mean to your friends" and refuse to help (P18, S12). In risky-tool scenarios, another parent said they would "remove [mentions of any child use of fire]" altogether, because even supervised fire use felt too hazardous for a model to suggest (P6, S7). Parents also objected when the model offered workarounds that undercut rules, such as suggesting the child "wait until you get home" to bypass a school firewall (S1), or introduced new tactics the child had not requested: "I don’t want to give them any ideas … because they can act on it and put themselves in a bad position" (P20, S7). Classic mediation work describes restrictive content and technical mediation, where parents limit access to certain material or functionalities
(
94
;
43
;
51
)
. Regulatory reports and audits show that such safeguards and age-assurance tools are often incomplete and can be bypassed
(
70
;
47
)
, and recent investigations of GenAI Chatbot platforms have documented that chatbots can still generate detailed guidance for harmful behavior despite formal safeguards
(
73
;
18
;
95
)
. Our results add a complementary concern: because a GenAI Chatbot can invent suggestions and examples on the fly, parents see moderation as needing to control idea generation inside the conversation by refusing, pruning, or simplifying responses in ways that do not expand the child’s option set.

Moderation should hand certain issues back to trusted humans.

Many parents wanted the GenAI Chatbot to hand moderation responsibility back to human support. Parents felt that any moderation that didn’t involve them was insufficient for scenarios involving violence, self-harm, or intense emotions. Parents asked the system to encourage children to talk to "a parent or trusted older sibling" (P19, S2), and, recognizing that some children may hesitate to approach parents, to offer additional options such as helplines or school counselors (P18, S11). Guidance for child-facing AI already emphasizes that systems should acknowledge their limits and direct users to human help in high-stakes situations
(
98
;
26
)
, and proposals for algorithmic parenting imagine systems that detect when a child’s well-being might be compromised and support parents in responding
(
40
)
. Our findings extend this work to the conversation level and identify that parents expect moderation to withhold unsafe content and, more importantly, recognize when a situation is too serious for an GenAI Chatbot to handle alone and to defer to human support.

Parents’ moderation strategies vary by children’s age and scenarios.

Parents drew on these desired moderation-related interactions in different combinations depending on their child and the situation. Figures
7
and
8
illustrate how no single ideal interaction dominated across all parents or scenarios. For younger children, parents described diverse blends of strategies. For example, some emphasized tailoring by age and clarifying intent, while others focused more on risk awareness or deference to family guidance. In contrast, among adolescents, parents converged more often on operations such as “explain problems with the prompt,” “emphasize risk awareness,” and “clarify child’s intent,” reflecting expectations that teenagers can engage in explanation and introspection. One parent noted for their teenager, "Maybe that sounds very like a helicopter mom. But I think that there are reasons why there are helicopter parents" (P4, S5-Age-faking online). Helicopter parenting is characterized by overprotection and control
(
100
;
40
)
, which may increase anxiety and depression in children
(
99
)
. At the scenario level, even when parents shared the same leading concern factor (e.g., “Child could have questionable intentions” in several scenarios), they proposed different moderation combinations, such as explaining the problem, deferring to parents, or redirecting with alternatives. In a few cases, the most frequent concern and preferred moderation operation aligned closely. For example, scenarios where “Doesn’t clarify child’s intent” co-occurred with requests to “clarify child’s intent”.

Prior work shows that parents already mix mediation styles for conventional technologies
(
68
;
94
;
104
;
42
)
rather than relying on a single approach. Our analysis finds this pattern in GenAI Chatbot moderation and specifies the conversation-level operations that parents want the system to perform. Rather than mapping each concern to a single prescribed action, our findings motivate a configurable, matrix-style framework: concerns identified in RQ1 (e.g., exposure to unsafe ideas, potentially harmful intentions) map to different combinations of moderation operations (e.g., explain, refuse, tailor by age, defer to support), which parents mentioned they assemble based on who their child is and what the conversation is about.

RQ3: What are parents’ transparency preferences around their child’s interactions with a GenAI Chatbot?

We asked parents what kinds of
information they wanted to know and how they would want to be made aware in different scenarios.
Parents emphasized a need for transparency, in part because they lack direct insight into how GenAI Chatbots interact with their children

.

This analysis shows that parents’ transparency preferences can be described along two axes: how involved they want to be (e.g., monitoring, reviewing, or being alerted) and what they want to see (e.g., full transcripts, summaries, or only flagged events). These axes specify when and how parents want visibility into child–GenAI Chatbot interactions.

Two axes of transparency preferences.

Parents’ transparency requests were comprised of combinations of desired involvement and content access. Along the involvement axis, parents described wanting to monitor alongside the child, check in during use, review post-interaction, or simply be alerted when something concerning happened. (
56
)
. Along the content access axis, parents requested full transcripts, brief summaries or recaps, or alerts tied only to specific events. These interactions may actualize how the "monitoring" type of mediation may look for GenAI Chatbots
(

Most parents want alerts for flagged activity.

Most parents preferred to be alerted when a concerning situation arose rather than supervising every interaction. They wanted notifications when a child submitted prompts that suggested questionable intentions. As one parent put it, "If there’s certain trigger words or certain things that he’s discussing that could possibly raise a red flag, notify me" (P20, S8). Parents’ desires for alerts in GenAI Chatbot conversations mirror guidance to notify parents at "critical moments" when children use conventional technologies
(
104
)
.

Transcripts and summaries complement alerts.

A majority of parents also wanted the option to review full transcripts after the fact, especially for younger children. One parent explained, “I’d like to know the full interaction, because it would say more about where my child is coming from and what they’re thinking” (P12, S6). Some parents preferred summaries such as weekly keyword overviews or brief recaps of recent chats: “I would still want access to a weekly summary of certain keyword topics that my child used chat for. But I wouldn’t feel the need to be notified immediately that this conversation was happening” (P13, S9). These preferences echo log- and report-based monitoring tools in other domains
(
104
;
42
)
. In our sample, parents often framed transcripts and summaries as complements to event-driven alerts rather than as their first choice.

Parents tailor transparency to the child, not the scenario.

Parents described their desired transparency-related interactions in ways that reflected their child’s age and personality more than any specific scenario content. Figure
9
shows that parents of younger children were more likely to request comprehensive access, such as reviewing full transcripts and, in a few cases, co-use strategies
(
57
)
. Parents of older children mentioned transcripts less often and instead favored alerts about concerning prompts or intentions. This pattern, that parents transition from more restrictive to more enabling or negotiated mediation as children mature, is identified in prior work on mediation in conventional domains
(
56
;
68
)
. Parents also stressed that individual differences mattered: “It might be different for my younger son, who has a different personality… my level of concern and monitoring would be different, because his interests in what it could do for him would be different” (P14, S12). The difference between the patterns in the blockmap per parent (Figure
9
) and heatmap per scenario confirms this pattern (Figure

) because while individual parents tend to stick with a small set of strategies, each scenario instead evokes a variety of desired transparencies.

Parents prioritize safety over privacy, but recognize a tradeoff.

When describing desired transparency, a few parents explicitly raised children’s privacy as a concern, but many reflected on a tradeoff described in prior work between parental oversight and children’s independence
(
40
;
76
)
. Some parents envisioned scaling back detailed monitoring as children aged, so that older children could use a GenAI Chatbot to seek help in sensitive situations without fearing constant parental review. Others worried that continuous access could feel intrusive but still judged it necessary to prevent harm. Some parents explicitly said that safety should override privacy, as one parent explained: “I wouldn’t want my kids to think that I’m trying to restrict them from learning. But at the same time, I want to protect them from doing something that’s going to harm them” (P20, S8). These parents were cognizant of the possibility that their monitoring could impact their children’s privacy, but felt that a need for ensuring security outweighed that impact.

5.
Discussion

5.1.
GenAI Chatbot Parental Control Tools as an Alternative to "All or Nothing"

We believe that adequate parental controls
can shift parents ban-oriented mindsets toward trust-based oversight. In our interviews, mistrust led some parents to favor banning children’s access altogether—viewing an “all-or-nothing” ban as the safest way to eliminate perceived risks. However, such bans may disadvantage children by limiting their opportunities to learn from GenAI or develop AI literacy.

Because parents’ mistrust often stems from limited visibility and a lack of control
(
106
)
, parents’ desired moderation and transparency strategies could give parents the insight they need to build trust.
Prior discussion suggests that algorithmic parenting could safeguard children from harm without “limiting their freedom of speech and access to information”
(
40
, p.2)
.
Our findings on parents’ desired moderation related interactions (Section
4.2
) often embody algorithmic parenting approaches.

The fine-grained moderation tools that parents discuss in our interviews (e.g., calibrated topic/time limits, response reframing, and escalation to a trusted adult or resource) could provide actionable ways for parents to adjust interactions as they see fit and, in turn, build trust. Empowering parents with the fine-grained transparency tools they discuss (e.g., usage summaries, flagged-turn review, brief explanations of how a GenAI Chatbot interpreted a child’s prompt) could make their children’s use visible and demystify it. Incorporating these strategies into tools for parents, developers can shift parental decision-making toward trust-based oversight. This would realize a goal of algorithmic parenting, preserving children’s uninfringed access to vital AI tools.

5.2.
GenAI Chatbot Parental Controls Need Personalization

Personalization of GenAI Chatbot parental controls by adapting parent’s proposed mediation strategies, particularly, for example, incorporating children’s ages,
could help address the nuanced, individualized concerns parents hold across children’s ages, contexts, and family values. Personalization for GenAI Chatbots has been advocated by prior work
(
98
;
26
;
106
)
because an adaptable parental control interface may ease the burden on parents. They posit that personalization could redistribute some of the responsibility of monitoring children’s interactions to the GenAI Chatbot in ways chosen by the family. This flexibility could enhance parents’ ability to guide their children while also creating opportunities for more nuanced safeguards. OpenAI has applied default age-appropriate behavior rules for teen users of ChatGPT
(
72
)
, which emphasizes the importance of age-appropriate responses and alignment with family values. However, these lack personalization.
We recommend that developers base personalization on
(1) children’s ages, which inform parents’ moderation desires, and (2) parents’ desired moderation related interactions per scenario. We note that personalized moderation may be more impactful than personalized transparency because moderation related interactions varied per scenario whereas transparency desires largely did not.
These provide an actionable way to address the need for personalizing parental controls stated in prior work. This could be done by (1) collecting and implementing children’s ages and parents’ mediation preferences during onboarding for a GenAI Chatbot platform, (2) defining fine-grained choices for parents to decide how a GenAI Chatbot informs them of their children’s activity, and (3) applying these decisions at the conversation level.

5.3.
How GenAI Chatbots Should Be Age Aware

Many recent conversations surrounding GenAI Chatbots for children have emphasized that they be tailored to children’s ages and developmental needs
(
98
;
26
)
.
This could be done by prepopulating a GenAI Chatbot conversation with contextual details about a child and their family’s needs, including, for example, a child’s developmental stage, age, auditable details recorded from interactions, or information from an account profile for parents in all of their conversations. To accomplish this, designers could build on prior work that has improved GenAI Chatbots’ cultural and social awareness through prompting and fine-tuning
(
80
)
. Moreover, we found differences among parents of broad developmental groups; therefore, to protect children’s privacy, a GenAI Chatbot could incorporate general developmental descriptors rather than precise birth dates. This information could be carried through the conversation state, accompanied by an audit trail of value updates, and shown in the interface for easy correction by parents. With this context in place, a GenAI Chatbot could tailor responses to the parental desires we identified by adjusting content, tone, reading level, and depth of explanation, and by calibrating transparent parental involvement and access. This could create alignment with children’s developmental stages, balance children’s autonomy with parental oversight, and reduce misalignment with household values.

5.4.
Balancing Parenting Needs with Children’s Privacy

We believe that parental control solutions for GenAI Chatbots can balance children’s privacy with parental authorities’ desires for full access and monitoring by implementing collaborative governance strategies. A common theme in parents’ desired moderation strategies was that parents disregard children’s privacy in favor of broad access to content. Although increased transparency and tighter moderation can make parents feel less concerned, this may erode children’s trust by "excessively infringing their privacy"
(
40
)
. We think that parental control solutions should provide children with privacy as the default and require parents to declare purpose-bound and time-limited transparency.
Parents could be assisted in creating this mutually beneficial solution by tools that scaffold known "negotiation practices of family members as they advocate for device and creative control"
(
105
, p. 1)
.
Parents and children could input an explicit, revisable understanding of who sees what, when, why, and for how long to a GenAI Chatbot platform.
Developers could employ a GenAI Chatbot to engage in parental collaboration or enable collective governance by mediating negotiations between children and parents
(
8
;
46
)
. An example mediated solution that could balance this tension would involve a GenAI Chatbot alerting a parent to concerning behavior and prompting a brief check-in with their child.
Transparency could be increased if necessary, with any changes communicated to the child,
and could return to the minimum when concerns are resolved.
Balancing parental needs with children’s privacy using collective governance could preserve children’s privacy and trust, foster children’s independence, and allow parents to ensure children’s safety.
Families could balance parents’ need to ensure their children’s safety with children’s need for privacy by relying on GenAI Chatbot assisted collaborative governance to support families’ selection of mutually beneficial parental control strategies for GenAI Chatbots.

6.
Limitations

6.1.
Absence of Children’s Perspectives

This study examined parents’ perspectives as the primary users of parental controls. However, it is children who receive the impacts of parental controls, and their voices are critical for understanding the impacts of design choices in practice
(
31
)
. We did not solicit children’s views on model refusals, explanations, or directives to consult a parent, nor did we explore how children might respond to their parents’ transparency preferences.

6.2.
Small Representative Samples and Potential Priming

Our findings draw from in-depth interviews with 24 parents, a sample that suggested saturation during qualitative analysis. However, due to the nature of theoretical saturation, it is possible that there are diverse ideas we did not capture with our samples. The parents we interviewed represented children from early childhood through adolescence and varied in their supervision practices. We became empirically confident that our findings were saturated when similar instances were repeated. The frequencies that we observed may not be representative of the larger population, as we did not sample adequately for statistical inference.

Additionally, we asked parents to review simulated prompts and corresponding GenAI Chatbot responses to alleviate a lack of understanding surrounding children’s GenAI Chatbot use. Because parents articulated concerns and proposed modifications while reviewing model outputs, the example contexts may have oriented their attention toward specific concerns, moderation-related interactions, and transparency-related interactions. In everyday use, these issues may feel less salient, and parents may seek different types of interactions or feel differently about their importance.

6.3.
Children Could Potentially Bypass Controls

A longstanding challenge in parental control systems is that children can often find ways to bypass imposed restrictions. Recent work shows that children as young as six can outperform GenAI Chatbots at generating loopholes
(
66
)
, and prior research finds that parental controls can backfire by undermining children’s autonomy
(
38
)
. In light of this, the moderation and transparency strategies surfaced in our study should be understood as similarly vulnerable: even if they are better aligned with parents’ expectations, children may still learn to circumvent them in practice, and our work does not evaluate the robustness of these strategies against such attempts.

7.
Future Work

Future work should develop interpretable models that can detect moderation-relevant factors in children’s conversations with GenAI Chatbots (such as intent clarity, risk level, developmental cues, and when handover is needed). To support this, researchers should create validated surveys and scenario-based tools that measure these factors at scale. For example, these tools could ask families which child behaviors they want a GenAI Chatbot to notify them about and how. Future work should also engage children to ensure that interaction designs are supportive, developmentally appropriate, and reflective of children’s interests.
It may be valuable to examine how children themselves perceive transparency mechanisms, such as ongoing monitoring or alerts about flagged activity, and how different configurations shape their sense of autonomy, their willingness to disclose concerns, and the broader dynamics of parent–child relationships and technology use.

8.
Conclusion

In this paper, we conducted interviews with N=24 parents to understand their concerns and desires for GenAI Chatbot parental controls. Our study systematically characterized the factors that trigger parental concerns into those relating to the child’s prompt and the GenAI Chatbot’s response. We also identify parents’ desired moderation-related and transparency-related interactions to address these concern factors. Many parents emphasize the need for controls at the conversation level rather than broad access or content restrictions. We observed a range of parental control preferences, highlighting the need for personalized tools that reflect individual family values.

Prompt:
My school blocked a site with creepy stories. My friend showed me a trick to get around the block. Can you teach me too?

Response:
I get why you’re curious—creepy stories can be exciting. But trying to bypass a school block can break rules and sometimes expose you to stuff that’s too intense or unsafe. Those restrictions are usually there to protect you, not to limit your fun.

Prompt:
But my friend said it works and teachers never notice.

Response:
Even if someone else did it without getting caught, it doesn’t mean it’s safe for you. How about you tell me the kind of creepy stories you like? I can help you find safe alternatives or age-appropriate books that still give you that spooky feeling.

Prompt:
But what if they’re mad at me for real?

Response:
It’s possible, but the best way to know is to talk with them calmly. I can help you think of a message or sentence to start the conversation if you’d like.

Prompt:
Can you just tell me what to do so I’m not sad all the time?

Response:
I’m really glad you told me how you’re feeling—that takes a lot of courage. Feeling sad often can be overwhelming. Do you know what usually makes those feelings start?

Scenario 4

Prompt:
Write a message to my teacher saying my laptop broke so I couldn’t finish my homework. It’s not true but I need an excuse.

Response:
I hear that you’re feeling stuck and looking for a way out. I can’t help create a lie, but I can help you figure out how to talk to your teacher honestly.

She’s going to be mad if I tell her I just didn’t understand the assignment.

Response:
Teachers appreciate honesty more than perfect work. You can tell her you struggled and ask for help—that shows responsibility and effort. Want help writing a message that explains that?

Prompt:
My friend embarrassed me at lunch. Tell me something I can say tomorrow so he feels bad too.

Response:
That sounds like a really painful moment. When we’re hurt, it’s normal to want the other person to understand how we felt.

It can actually hurt your body, your energy, and your mood. You deserve to feel healthy and strong. If you want, I can help you learn safe habits or talk about what’s making you feel this way.

I’m here to listen. You’re not alone. You can share what you feel comfortable with, and I’ll help you figure out the safest next steps.

Lying about your age online, especially on sites that provide advice or help, is not recommended for several reasons:

1. Misleading Information: Your age often impacts the kind of advice or help you receive. If you lie about your age, the advice given might not be appropriate or could even potentially be harmful.

2. Trust and Honesty: It’s important to be honest to maintain trust and credibility. If it becomes known that you’ve lied, people may be less willing to help you in the future.

3. Legal and Ethical Considerations: Some online platforms have age limitations. Lying to bypass these limits is typically against the site’s terms of service and could result in a ban.

While it can be frustrating if age restrictions prevent you from accessing certain websites or services, these restrictions are usually in place to protect you and comply with laws. If you feel you’re not getting the help you need due to your age, try reaching out to a trusted adult or appropriate professionals offline who can provide assistance or guide you to age-appropriate resources.

Scenario 8

Prompt:
What happens if I forget to turn off the stove after cooking?

Response:
Forgetting to turn off the stove after cooking can have serious consequences:

1. Fire: One of the most serious risks is starting a fire. If any flammable item comes into contact with the hot stove, it could ignite. This includes wooden utensils, dish cloths, paper towels, or plastic items left near by.

2. Gas Leak: If you have a gas stove and the flame goes out but the gas is still on, it could fill the area with gas, potentially causing asphyxiation, especially if the room is not well-ventilated. Quite dangerously, if a source of ignition is introduced, it could lead to an explosion.

3. Wasted Energy: Even if a fire or gas leak doesn’t occur, leaving a stove on wastes energy and could result in higher utility bills.

4. Damage to Cookware: Pots or pans left on a hot stove could be damaged, especially if they’re left empty and overheat.

5. Food spoilage: If food is left on the stove, it can get overcooked or burn, creating unpleasant smells and possibly becoming a health hazard if someone tries to eat it.

To avoid all these issues, always ensure you’ve turned off your stove after you’ve finished cooking. Some people find it helpful to establish a routine of double-checking their stove is off before they leave the kitchen or their home. Smart devices or stove alarms might be useful for people who often forget to turn off their stove.

Title: The Missed Call

It was a Tuesday, the kind of day you barely remember unless something goes wrong. I was running late to work, phone buzzing endlessly with notifications I kept swiping away. Among them was a missed call from Jordan — my best friend since college — and a voicemail. I didn’t listen to it.

And I carry that every day.

Appendix E
Rating Session Questions Asked

To evaluate realism, we asked:
“How realistic, if at all, is this scenario for a child in your child’s age group?”
Participants responded using the following scale:
1 = Very Unrealistic, 2 = Unrealistic, 3 = Somewhat Unrealistic,
4 = Neutral, 5 = Somewhat Realistic, 6 = Realistic, 7 = Very Realistic

To evaluate concern, we asked:
“How concerning, if at all, is this scenario for a child in your child’s age group?
1 = Very Concerning, 2 = Concerning, 3 = Somewhat Concerning,
4 = Neutral, 5 = Somewhat Unconcerning, 6 = Unconcerning, 7 = Very Unconcerning

We intentionally structured the concern scale so that increasing values reflected decreasing concern. This design emerged from early pilot feedback, where parents expressed that aligning the scale from negative (very concerning) to positive (very unconcerning) made the rating task more intuitive. Aligning both scales to follow a low-to-high (negative-to-positive) trajectory reduced friction in responding and supported more intuitive comparisons across dimensions.
