Thick points under Gaussian free field dynamics
Felipe Espinosa Vergara †, Avelio Sepúlveda ∗

Abstract
. We investigate the evolution of thick points under two natural dynamics for the Gaussian free field (GFF) in dimension 2. The first dynamic we analyze is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck GFF. We prove that, simultaneously for all points, the evolution of their thickness is continuous. Additionally, we characterize all deterministic functions f : ℝ → ℝ f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} such that there are points whose thickness function is f f . The second dynamic we study is the stationary solution of the additive stochastic heat equation. In this case, the thickness of points is not continuous. Moreover, this rougher dynamic generates super-thick points, namely points with thickness greater than 2 2 . As a function of γ > 2 \gamma>2 , we identify infinitely many phase transitions corresponding to the existence of exceptional times where at least N N points are γ \gamma -thick. These phase transitions, occurring at γ 2 = 8 , 6 , 16 / 3 , … \gamma^{2}=8,6,16/3,\dots , converge to 4 4 as N → ∞ N\to\infty . Mapping to the critical FK-model parameter via q = 4 ​ cos 2 ⁡ ( 4 ​ π / γ 2 ) q=4\cos^{2}(4\pi/\gamma^{2}) , these critical values correspond to the Beraha numbers, which are precisely the points at which the CFT for critical FK percolation should be minimal.

Introduction

In the past two decades, there has been significant interest in the study of the geometry of the two-dimensional Gaussian free field (GFF). This interest stems from the fact that the GFF is a powerful tool for understanding important conformally invariant objects and constructing their scaling limits. The first class of such objects includes those where the GFF itself is the limiting object, as seen in the fluctuations of the height function in domino tilings

The overarching goal of this article is to lay the groundwork for understanding the geometry of the two-dimensional GFF under natural time evolutions. We approach this through two distinct dynamics. We first study the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck GFF (OU-GFF), an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process in the Sobolev space

Despite the SHEF acting as a basic model in the study of SPDEs, the temporal evolution of its geometric structures has remained unaddressed.

In the static case, despite the fact that the two-dimensional GFF itself is not an

1.1.2.
Additive stochastic heat equation field

Let us now discuss our second model: the SHEF. This field is the solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\Phi=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\Phi+\sqrt{2\pi}W,&\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{+}\times D\\
\Phi(0,\cdot)=\Phi_{0}(\cdot),\end{cases}

where
\Phi_{0}
is a GFF independent of
W
, a space-time white noise. It can be shown that the GFF is the stationary measure of this reversible dynamics and thus the solution can also be extended to negative times. This can be seen from the fact that we can express the solution as

\displaystyle\Psi(\cdot,t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{k}(t)e_{k}(\cdot),

where
(\beta_{k}(t))_{k}
are independent Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes in
\mathbb{R}
with different speeds. That is to say, they are the invariant solutions of

\displaystyle d\beta_{k}(t)=-\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\beta_{k}(t)dt+\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}dB^{(k)}(t),

where
(B^{(k)})_{k}
are independent Brownian motions.

In this case, the thickness function of a typical point is discontinuous on every interval (see Remark
5.16
). However, this irregularity increases the entropy governing the potential spatial locations of thick points. As a consequence the SHEF has super-thick points
2
2

2

2
-thick

Let
\Psi
be a SHEF,

-thick points as

\displaystyle T_{\gamma}:=\left\{(x,t):\gamma^{+}_{x}(t):=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)}{-\log(\varepsilon)}=\gamma\right\}.

Then,

•

If
\gamma>\sqrt{8}

\gamma

(3-\gamma^{2}/4)\wedge(4-\gamma^{2}/2)

1.1.3.
Critical values and Beraha numbers

Let us make a short comment on the values of
\gamma^{c}_{N}
, as they suggest a connection to the algebraic structures of the
q
-state Potts model.

By mapping the thickness parameter
\gamma
to the SLE parameter via
\kappa=\gamma^{2}
, and subsequently to the
Fortuin-Kasteleyn (FK) cluster weight through the relation

q=4\cos^{2}(4\pi/\kappa)
(Theorem 1.2 of
[
34
]
), one recovers the Beraha numbers,

B_{n}=4\cos^{2}(\pi/n)
.

When the parameter
q
of a Potts model is equal to a Beraha number interesting things happen. In the ferromagnetic regime, they
identify the specific central charges for which the corresponding Conformal
Field Theories (CFT) are minimal
[
33
]
; notably, the first few values
correspond to canonical objects in probability:
q=0
(Uniform Spanning Trees),

q=1
(Critical Percolation) and
q=2
(the FK-Ising model).
For the antiferromagnetic case,
the Beraha numbers identify the specific points where the infinite continuous
spectrum of the underlying GFF is truncated, causing the model
to undergo a second-order phase transition rather than a first-order one

[
49
,
29
]
.

The fact that these algebraic degeneracies in
lattice models align exactly with the existence thresholds for
N
-point
exceptional times in the SHEF suggests an underlying Coulomb Gas
structure that remains to be fully elucidated. Even though these possible connections seem profound, in this paper, we make no attempt to rigorously (or non-rigorously) find them.

1.2.
Ideas of the proofs and organization of the paper

To prove continuity of the OU-GFF (Proposition
1.1
), we follow the approach of
[
28
]
. We begin by estimating the modulus of continuity of
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
with respect to
\varepsilon
,
x
and
t
. We then provide a tail estimate on the probability of observing large gradients. This method is subsequently refined to prove the second part of Theorem
1.2
.

The existence part of Theorem
1.2
is more abstract. We observe that for any continuous function
\lambda(t)
, the field
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda(t)\Phi(t,\cdot)dt
has the law of a GFF multiplied by an explicit constant. We then construct the Liouville measure associated with this GFF, which is possible only when this constant is strictly smaller than 2. We show that a point sampled according to this measure follows a path given by an explicit transformation of
\lambda
.

In the case of SHEF, to obtain an upper bound on the dimension of
E_{N,\gamma}
we need to first compute the dimension on the lifted domain
D^{N}\times[0,T]
of the set
(x_{1},\dots,x_{N},t)
, where
(x_{i},t)
are different super thick points. We then project this set in time to get the upper bound.

The lower bound is less direct: we create a measure such that a typical point chosen according to it is a time with
N
super thick points. To achieve this, we introduce the
N
-Liouville measure, defined on the lifted domain
D^{N}\times[0,T]
, as the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
of

\displaystyle e^{\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{k},t)-N\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\log(1/\varepsilon)}dt\prod dx_{k}.

We then prove that
(x_{1},...,x_{N},t)
, a typical point of this measure, satisfies that
(x_{i},t)
is a
\gamma
-thick point. The
N
-Liouville measure is then used to obtain the correct lower bound on the dimension of the exceptional times
E_{N,\gamma}
.

The paper is organised as follows. We start with preliminaries on the GFF and GMC in Section
2
. Then, in Section
3
we show that the thickness function of the OU-GFF is continuous and in Section
4
we characterise all possible thickness functions that can be found on an OU-GFF. We finish by studying the properties of the thick points of the SHEF in Section
5
.

Preliminaries

Before presenting the preliminaries needed related to the GFF, we introduce some technical results that will be used later in this work. The first tool in this small tool-kit is the following lemma that will be useful to prove one of the main propositions of this work.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\hskip 2.84544pt(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be two real sequences. We have that,

\displaystyle|\limsup a_{n}-\limsup b_{n}|\leq\limsup|a_{n}-b_{n}|.

Proof.

The result follows directly from,

\displaystyle\limsup|a_{n}-b_{n}|\geq\limsup a_{n}+\liminf-b_{n}=\limsup a_{n}-\limsup b_{n}.

∎

The second lemma we will need is the following one that gives us a characterization of
H^{1}([-T,T])
. This characterization may already have appeared somewhere else in the literature, but as we were unable to find a reference, we give an overview of the proof.

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider
f\in C([-T,T])
. For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, consider
\Delta_{N}=T2^{-N}
and

\displaystyle I_{N}=\{t_{k}=k\Delta_{N},\hskip 2.84544ptk\in\{-2^{N},...,2^{N}\}\}.

Then

if and only if

(2.1)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k=-2^{N}}^{2^{N}-1}\Delta_{N}\left(\frac{f(t_{k+1})-f(t_{k})}{\Delta_{N}}\right)^{2}<\infty.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k=-2^{N}}^{2^{N}-1}\Delta_{N}\left(\frac{f(t_{k+1})-f(t_{k})}{\Delta_{N}}\right)^{2}=\int_{-T}^{T}|f^{\prime}|^{2}dt,

where

is the weak derivative of
f
.

It is important to note that the limit of (
2.1
) always exists as the sequence is increasing. This is because, at level

we are computing the minimal energy of any continuous function that takes values

at all
x\in I_{N}
. Additionally, note that
t_{k}=t_{k}^{(N)}
strongly depends on
n
. We are not making this dependent explicit, unless strictly necessary.

Proof.

The direct implication follows due to the fact that for every
g

H_{0}^{1}
with
g(x)=f(x)
for all

we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=-2^{N}}^{2^{N}-1}\Delta_{N}\left(\frac{f(t_{k+1})-f(t_{k})}{\Delta_{N}}\right)^{2}\leq\int_{-T}^{T}(g^{\prime}(x))^{2}dx.

We conclude by taking
g=f
.

To prove the converse, we start by taking

f_{N}(s)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=-2^{N}}^{2^{N}-1}\frac{f(t_{k+1})-f(t_{k})}{\Delta_{N}}\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}(s),

and showing that this is a Cauchy sequence in

This implies that the sequence

is a Cauchy sequence in

For each

\displaystyle\int_{-T}^{x}g(s)ds=f(x)-f(-T).

∎

Notice that assuming that

3.
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck GFF and continuity of its thickness function

As stated in the introduction, our goal is to study (natural) dynamics where the GFF is the stationary measure. In this section, we introduce the first dynamic we study: the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck GFF (OU-GFF). This is the generalization of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process to take “GFF values”. To be more precise, let us properly introduce it.

Definition 3.1

(Ornstein-Uhlenbeck GFF)
.

Let
(\alpha_{k}(t))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an i.i.d. sequence of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes with rate
1/2
and initial condition a standard Gaussian distribution. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck GFF (from now on OU-GFF) is defined as

(3.1)

\varPhi(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{k}(t)e_{k},

where
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis for
H_{0}^{1}(D)
.

Recall here that an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with rate
1/2
, is the strong solution of the following SDE

(3.2)

\displaystyle dX_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}X_{t}dt+dB_{t}.

In Definition
3.1
we are fixing a specific basis of
H_{0}^{1}(D)
, however the law of an OU-GFF is independent of the chosen basis. This can be proven by simply doing a change of basis and using that the sequence
(\alpha_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an i.i.d. sequence of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck. The other way is what we present here, by characterizing the field
\varPhi
using its correlations.

Lemma 3.2
.

Take
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
an orthonormal basis of
H_{0}^{1}(D)
with the inner product of (
2.3
) to define the OU-GFF
\varphi
. Then, the collection
(\langle\varphi(t),f\rangle_{\nabla})_{t\in\mathbb{R},f\in H_{0}^{1}(D)}
is a centered Gaussian process with covariance given by

(3.3)

\mathbb{E}[\left\langle\varPhi(t),f\right\rangle_{\nabla}\left\langle\varPhi(s),g\right\rangle_{\nabla}]=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|s-t|}\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\nabla},\ \ \ \text{ for any $s,t\in R$ and $f,g\in H_{0}^{1}(D)$.}

As a consequence, for
f,g\in H_{0}^{-1}(D)
and
s,t\in\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{E}[\left\langle\varPhi(t),f\right\rangle\left\langle\varPhi(s),g\right\rangle]=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|t-s|}\left\langle f,(-\Delta)^{-1}g\right\rangle,

thus the law of
\varPhi
does not depend on the choice of the basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

We start by noting that

\displaystyle\langle\varphi(t),f\rangle_{\nabla}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha_{k}(t)\langle e_{k},f\rangle_{\nabla}.

This immediately implies that
(\langle\varphi(t),f\rangle_{\nabla})_{t\in\mathbb{R},f\in H_{0}^{1}(D)}
is a centered Gaussian process.

To check (
3.3
), we start from (
3.1
). Since for
s,t
fixed we know that that series converges in
L^{2}
, we used the independence
(\alpha_{k}(\cdot))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
to see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left\langle\varPhi(t),f\right\rangle_{\nabla}\left\langle\varPhi(s),g\right\rangle_{\nabla}]=\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{k}(s)\alpha_{k}(t)]\left\langle f,e_{k}\right\rangle_{\nabla}\left\langle g,e_{k}\right\rangle_{\nabla}=\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|s-t|}\left\langle f,e_{k}\right\rangle_{\nabla}\left\langle g,e_{k}\right\rangle_{\nabla}.

To conclude the uniqueness in law, we just note that the law
(\langle\varphi(t),f\rangle)_{t\in\mathbb{R},f\in H_{0}^{1}(D)}
determines the law of
\varPhi
.
∎

Lemma

implies that one can decompose the OU-GFF at different times in the same way as the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.

Lemma 3.3

4.1.

The objective of this section is to construct an appropriate Hilbert space

Let us check that the above bilinear function is in fact an inner product.

Lemma 4.2

5.
Stochastic heat equation

From this section onward, we study the behavior of the thick points of the additive stochastic heat equation field (SHEF). This is the solution of the following stochastic equation

(5.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\Psi=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\Psi+\sqrt{2\pi}\xi,\\
\Psi(0)=\Psi_{0}.\end{cases}

Here
\xi
is space-time white noise in
D\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\Psi_{0}
is a GFF independent of
\xi
.

Let us note that the solution is, in fact, a dynamic whose invariant law is that of the GFF.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
(e_{k},\lambda_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
-\Delta
the Dirichlet renormalised Laplacian so that
(e_{k})_{k}
is an orthonormal basis of

when equiped with the inner product (
2.3
). Then,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\Psi(\cdot,t)=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}(t)e_{k}(\cdot),

where
\beta_{k}(t)
are independent Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes with speed
\lambda_{k}

(5.3)

\displaystyle d\beta_{k}(t)=-\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\beta_{k}(t)dt+\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}dB^{(k)}_{t},

and initial condition
\beta^{0}_{k}
having normal distribution with variance
1
.

Proof.

Recall the notion of solution of a linear SPDE given by Definition 6.1 from
[
26
]
. Since the operator driving equation (
5.1
) is proportional to minus the Laplacian it is enough to test our equation against
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and solve the corresponding system of equations. Note that

\displaystyle\left\langle\Psi(t),e_{k}\right\rangle-\left\langle\Psi_{0},e_{k}\right\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\Psi(r),(-\Delta)e_{k}\right\rangle dr+\sqrt{2\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\xi(dr),e_{k}\right\rangle

where the integral against the white noise can be interpreted as evaluated with respect to
\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}(s)e_{k}(x)
. Since
(e_{k})_{k}
are orthogonal in

with square norm equal to

, we can see that

\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\xi(dr),e_{k}\right\rangle\overset{law}{=}\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}B^{(k)}(t),

for
(B^{(k)})_{k}
an iid sequence of Brownian motions.

To conclude, we note that we can write
\beta_{k}:=(\lambda_{k}/2\pi)\left\langle\Psi,e_{k}\right\rangle
so that (
5.2
) holds. Using that
\left\langle\Psi,(-\Delta)e_{k}\right\rangle=\lambda_{k}\left\langle\Psi,e_{k}\right\rangle
, we obtain that

solves (
5.3
) with i.i.d initial conditions distributed as

. The initial conditions follows from the fact that
\left\langle\Psi_{0},e_{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle\Psi_{0},(-(2\pi)^{-1}\Delta)^{-1}e_{k}\right\rangle_{\nabla}
and that
\Psi_{0}
has the law of a GFF.
∎

5.7.

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D^{N}}\int_{D^{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}_{G^{N}_{\rho,\varepsilon_{k},\varepsilon^{l}_{0}}(\vec{x},t)G^{N}_{\rho,\varepsilon^{l}_{0}}(\vec{y},s)}\mu_{\varepsilon_{k}}^{\gamma,N}(d\vec{x}dt)\mu_{\varepsilon_{k}}^{\gamma,N}(d\vec{y}ds)}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{D^{N}}\int_{D^{N}}\frac{1}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{(|x_{i}-y_{i}|\lor\sqrt{|t-s|}\lor\varepsilon)^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}+\delta}}.

By arguing as in Proposition
5.12
we can reduce the problem to check
for which values of
\alpha
the following integral is finite

I_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{[0,1]^{N}}\frac{1}{s^{\alpha}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{r_{i}}{(r_{i}\lor\sqrt{s})^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}+\delta}}dr_{i}ds.

Using (
5.8
), we can see that

(5.43)

I_{N}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{s^{\alpha}}\left(c_{1}+c_{2}\frac{1}{s^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}-1}}\right)^{N}ds.

From here we deduce the following condition on
\alpha

\alpha+N(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}-1)<1,

or, equivalently

(5.44)

\alpha<N+1-\frac{N\gamma^{2}}{4}.

Therefore, we can conclude the proposition.
∎

From here, we can see that, the sequence defined by

(5.45)

\gamma_{N}=\sqrt{\frac{4}{N}+4},

gives us a sequence of phase transitions of this model. Since we have that
\dim_{H}(\mathrm{ET}^{\gamma_{N}}_{N})=0

Appendix A
GMC measure for pseudo-log correlated fields

We now check that the work done in
[
14
]
can be extended in a more general setting that we call “pseudo-log-correlated fields”. This can be understood as knowing under which conditions, the following sequence in
\varepsilon
converges almost surely weakly in the space of probability measures,

(A.1)

\widehat{\mu}_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}(dx)=\exp\left(\gamma h_{\varepsilon}(x)-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(h_{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}\right]\right)\sigma(dx).

Sufficient conditions are given in the present theorem.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
h
be a Gaussian, such that the following hypotheses are fulfiled

(H1)

The correlations of
(h_{\varepsilon}(x))_{\varepsilon\in(0,1),x\in D}
fulfil

(A.2)

\mathrm{cov}(h_{\varepsilon}(x),h_{\delta}(y))=-\log\left(d(x,y)\lor\varepsilon\lor\delta\right)+O(1),

uniformly for
x,y\in D
and
\varepsilon,\delta\in[0,1]
.

(H2)

There is a
\rho>0
such that

\int\int_{\overline{D\times D}}\frac{\sigma(dx)\sigma(dy)}{d(x,y)^{\rho-\varepsilon}}<\infty,

for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Then for every
\gamma^{2}<2\rho
, the sequence
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}
defined in (
A.1
) converges in probability to a non-trivial measure
\mu^{\gamma}
.

We prove this result in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
endowed with a metric
d
that generates the usual topology. We consider a Kernel
K:D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
such that for every
x\not=y\in D

(A.3)

K(x,y)=-\log(d(x,y))+g(x,y),

where
g\in C(\overline{D\times D})
. On the other hand we also consider subset of
\mathcal{M}(D)
(the set of positive measures) such that,

\mathcal{M}^{+}_{K}(D):=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(D):\int\int_{D\times D}|K(x,y)|\mu(dx)\mu(dy)<\infty\right\}.

From here we take
\mathcal{M}_{K}(D)
as the set of signed measures
\rho=\rho_{+}-\rho_{-}
with
\rho_{\pm}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{K}(D)
. Fix
\theta
a nonnegative Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
whose support is contained in the unit ball
\overline{B(0,1)}\subset\mathbb{R}^{l}
for
1\leq l\leq k
such that its total mass is equal to
1
, and assume that

(A.4)

\int|\log(d(x,y)|\theta(dy)\leq C<\infty,

where
C
is uniform over
B(0,5)
(under certain cases one can prove the above, some examples are
|\cdot|
the usual norm, or norms of the form
|\cdot|^{\alpha}+|\cdot|^{\beta}
for
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1]
).
We denote by
\theta_{\varepsilon,x}
the measure
\theta_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)=\theta(\cdot/\varepsilon)
shifted by
x

\theta_{\varepsilon,x}(\cdot)=\theta_{\varepsilon}(\cdot-x)
and assume
\theta\in\mathcal{M}_{K}(D)
. Now consider
(h)_{\sigma\in\mathcal{M}_{K}(D)}
a centered Gaussian process with covariance Kernel given by
K
.

Take a Radon measure
\sigma
on
\partial D
. We now want to construct the measure whose Radon-Nykodin derivative with respect to
\sigma
is
\exp(h)
. To do this, we consider
h_{\varepsilon}(x)=h(\theta_{\varepsilon,x})
, and we will assume that
(H1)
and
(H2)
hold.

Notice that in particular that
(H1)
implies that for each
\varepsilon>0
,
(h_{\varepsilon}(x))_{x\in D}
is well defined for each given
x\in D
. On the other hand we observe that (H1) is not always a direct consequence of (
A.3
) and one might need to prove it case by case. As an example, the GFF with the uniform measure in
B(x,\varepsilon)
fulfils our hypothesis when we consider
\sigma=\lambda
the Lebesgue measure in
D
.

In
[
14
]
, one of the ideas that help prove the convergence of the above sequence is by taking in consideration the set of so-called
good points
. A point
x\in D
is called a good point if, for a given fix
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, the following holds for every positive
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
,

(A.5)

G_{\alpha,\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}}(x):=\left\{h_{r}(x)\leq\alpha\log(1/r),\forall r\in(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}]\right\}.

From now on we fix
\varepsilon_{0}
and consider
G_{\alpha,\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}}(x)=G_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(x)
. The above set has an important influence on the limit behavior of
\mu^{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
, since the limit is concentrated (with high probability) in this set when
\alpha
is close to
\gamma
, this is formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma A.2
.

For
\alpha>\gamma
, there is
p:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
such that

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{G_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(z)}e^{\gamma h_{\varepsilon}(x)-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[h_{\varepsilon}^{2}(x)]}]\geq 1-p(\varepsilon_{0}),

and
p
is such that tends to
0
as
\varepsilon_{0}\to 0
. This implies that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{G^{c}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(z)}e^{\gamma h_{\varepsilon}(z)-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}[h_{\varepsilon}^{2}(z)]}\right]\underset{\varepsilon_{0}\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

The proof of the lemma is the same computation performed in Section 3 of
[
14
]
.
∎

Now that we see that the contribution of points that are not good points is small (in expectation), we give the first proposition that will help to prove the convergence.

Proposition A.3
.

Let
h
be the Gaussian random field with covariance
K
as in (
A.3
). For a given
f\in C\left(\overline{D}\right)
, define

I_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}}=\int_{D}f(x)\mathbf{1}_{G_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(x)}\exp\left(\gamma h_{\varepsilon}(x)-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[h^{2}_{\varepsilon}(x)\right]\right)dx.

If
I_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}
and
\mathrm{cov}(h_{\varepsilon}(x),h_{\delta}(y))
converges punctually to a function
G(x,y)
when
\varepsilon
and
\delta
go to
0
for
x\not=y
, then,
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}
given by (
A.1
) converges in probability to a non-trivial random measure
\mu^{\gamma}
.

Proof.

We argue as in Section 4 of
[
14
]
. We first check that the random variable
(I_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}
is Cauchy in
L^{2}
. To achieve this, we first notice that for
\varepsilon,\delta
given, we have that, after applying Fubini’s theorem,
\mathbb{E}\left[I_{\varepsilon}I_{\delta}\right]
is equal to

(A.6)

\int_{D}\int_{D}f(x)f(y)e^{\gamma^{2}\mathrm{cov}(h_{\varepsilon}(x),h_{\delta}(y)}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(G_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(x),G_{\delta,\alpha}(y)\right)dxdy,

where
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
is the measure whose Radon-Nikodyn derivative with respect to
\mathbb{P}
is

(A.7)

\frac{d\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}{d\mathbb{P}}(h)=\frac{e^{\gamma(h_{\varepsilon}(x)+h_{\delta}(y)}}{\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\gamma(h_{\varepsilon}(x)+h_{\delta}(y)}\right]}.

Given the above expression, if we separate
D\times D
in
\Delta_{\eta}
the points at distance at most
\eta
of the diagonal, and its complement, since
I_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded we can argue that as
\varepsilon,\delta
goes to
0
the punctual limits of
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(G_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(x),G_{\delta,\alpha}(y)\right)
and
\mathrm{cov}(h_{\varepsilon}(x),h_{\delta}(y))
exists (the last one is by hypothesis). On the other hand, by the uniform bound of
I_{\varepsilon}
the integration over
\Delta_{\eta}
goes to
0
as
\eta
goes to 0 uniformly. Given the above, when one do as follows

\mathbb{E}[|I_{\varepsilon}-I_{\delta}|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[I_{\varepsilon}^{2}-I_{\varepsilon}I_{\delta}]+\mathbb{E}[I_{\delta}^{2}-I_{\varepsilon}I_{\delta}],

can conclude as before by arguing via Fatou’s lemma. From here we can argue for the existence of the limit in probability as in Section 6 of the same cited work.
∎

We now prove that the conditions from Proposition

It is direct from

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(G_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(x),G_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(y)\right)\leq C(d(x,y)\lor\varepsilon)^{\frac{(2\gamma-\alpha)^{2}}{2}}.
