 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

For fiscal year (FY) 2024, the Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL) focused on two experimental
tasks related to Hanford Double Shell Tank (DST) chemistry and integrity. The first task focused on
understanding risk of corrosion due to formation of either continuous layers or discrete patches of solids on
the tanks’ inner sidewalls and bottoms. The objective of this task was to determine the effect of solid
deposits on the corrosion risk to the tank bottom, and whether a combination of scale, saltcake and loose
solids lead to underdeposit corrosion. Electrochemical testing in a single cell arrangement was employed
to determine a threshold inhibitor level for the interstitial liquid in the solids, above which carbon steel was
not susceptible to corrosion beneath the solids. The inhibitor levels tested were based on the previously
determined probability of failure for carbon steel in a simulated waste (i.e., Pitting Factor).

In 2022, a borescope inspection of the annulus between the encasement and the primary for one of the
pre-1990 transfer lines revealed standing liquid in contact with the primary pipe. Additionally, a significant
build-up of corrosion products on both carbon steel surfaces was observed. One of the compensatory
actions performed by the Hanford tank farm facility was to inject dry air into the annulus between the
primary and the encasement to remove moisture. Prevention of water accumulation would mitigate further
initiation and propagation of corrosion. The Hanford facility requested guidance for maintaining a dry
environment in the transfer line annulus. Testing conducted in FY23 was undertaken to define the threshold
dewpoint temperature that could be utilized to monitor the probes that are stationed in the field. In FY24,
this work was continued to refine the dewpoint temperature required to minimize the risk of continued
corrosion of the transfer lines.

Another option for corrosion mitigation that the Hanford tank farm facility is considering is the deployment
of a nitrogen blanket in the annular region between the encasement and core piping. Testing to determine
a threshold value of nitrogen above which corrosion is mitigated was undertaken by SRNL in FY24. An
action level for the quantity of nitrogen present in the pipeline annulus was provided.

A summary of the results and conclusions for each activity is discussed below.

Tank Bottom Solids Contact Corrosion Testing

 • A quantifiable difference amongst the corrosion potential, corrosion rate, and passive
 current density between the interstitial liquid simulants and their compressed solid counter
 parts was measured. More specifically, the interstitial liquid simulants 6 and 7 (PF = 0.8)
 demonstrated no corrosion activity in the CPP curves or on the physical coupon. However,
 the compressed solid counterparts exhibited increased corrosion activity in comparison to
 the IL simulants.
 • No differences were noted among corrosion behavior when the interstitial liquid of the
 compressed solids were spiked with aggressive species. The spiked compressed solid that
 had added chlorides, simulant 7, had increased corrosion activity in comparison to the
 spiked compressed solid that had no added chlorides, simulant 6.
 • In FY25, higher values of PF, between 0.8 and 1.2, will be tested to determine a threshold
 PF above which attack beneath the solids is mitigated. Additionally, SRNL will investigate
 the use of a multiple crevice assembly to simulate the compressed solid environment.

 vi

Transfer Line Drying

 • The dew point was used as a criterion for the effectiveness of corrosion mitigation. In
 general, if the dew point was less than 20 °C minimal general and pitting corrosion would be
 expected. The tests from FY24 confirmed the results observed in FY23.
 • Significant corrosion was not observed for tests at 35 °C and at relative humidity between 60
 and 100%. Although the cause is not completely known, it appears that the condition of the
 metal surface precluded condensation on the surface.
 • Pre-corroded samples were tested in FY24. However, after exposure there was no apparent
 visual change in the appearance of the coupon nor change in the mass of the coupon. Thus,
 conclusions as to whether drying mitigates propagation of existing corrosion pits could not
 be inferred.
 • For FY25, other means for pre-corroding the sample such that propagation of existing pits
 may be investigated.

Transfer Line Nitrogen Blanket

 • Corrosion of carbon steel was effectively mitigated in the liquid and vapor space when the
 nitrogen reduced the oxygen concentration to 0%.
 • Corrosion of carbon steel was effectively mitigated in the liquid with oxygen
 concentrations of 5% and 10% oxygen.
 • Corrosion of carbon steel increased significantly in the vapor space with 5% and 10%
 oxygen presence. Both general and pitting corrosion was observed. Based on these results,
 the oxygen concentration should be maintained below 5% to mitigate general and localized
 corrosion.
 • Horizontally oriented carbon steel coupons experienced a greater degree of attack in the
 vapor space than vertically oriented coupons.
 • Pre-corroded coupons were tested to investigate whether corrosion would re-initiate and
 propagate. No difference between samples which had been exposed to the nitrogen blanket
 environment and those, which had not been exposed could be detected. No conclusion
 regarding the effectiveness of the nitrogen blanket could be made.
 • For FY25, other means for pre-corroding the sample such that propagation of existing pits
 should be investigated.

1.0 Introduction
The Hanford Site in Washington State has millions of gallons of legacy radioactive waste that is being
retrieved from single-shell tanks (SSTs) and transferred to newer, double-shell tanks (DSTs) for eventual
closure of the SSTs. The waste will be maintained in DSTs until eventual immobilization can be performed
at the Waste Treatment and Immobilization Plant, currently under construction. A detailed integrity
program plan, implemented by the Hanaford Tank Waste Operations and Closure (H2C) Tank and Pipeline
Integrity (TAPI) organization, has been developed for the DSTs, which has many elements including waste
chemistry control for corrosion mitigation, visual inspections and non-destructive examination of DSTs [1].

Corrosion testing for DSTs has been guided by the Tank Integrity Expert Panel-Corrosion Sub-Group
(TIEP-CSG) to provide the technical guidelines for waste chemistry control. Corrosion testing has been
performed at three independent laboratories: Det Norske Veritas (DNV) Columbus, Savannah River
National Laboratory (SRNL), and the 222-S facility at Hanford operated by Hanford Laboratory
Management and Integration (HLMI). SRNL has focused its corrosion studies on vapor space corrosion,
development of waste chemistry control limits to mitigate pitting corrosion, corrosion protection for the
DST secondary liner and corrosion of the tank bottom due to contact with waste solids.

Recently, SRNL has been instrumental in developing a new approach to waste chemistry control (i.e.,
Pitting Factor approach) [2] and investigation of remediation of transfer line corrosion [3]. For Fiscal Year
2024 (FY24), the experimental activity to investigate corrosion beneath the tank solids was continued from
FY23. Additionally, further investigation of mitigation strategies for transfer line corrosion by drying or
nitrogen de-aeration were conducted [4].

2.0 Background

The Task Technical and Quality Assurance Plan for DST corrosion testing in FY24 included 4 activities
[5]. Of the 4 activities, two were experimental studies: 1) Tank bottom solids contact corrosion testing and
2) Transfer line corrosion mitigation. This report will focus on the results of these two activities. A
background is provided below for these two tasks.

2.1 Tank Solids Contact Testing

Hanford DST 241-AY-102 leaked waste into the secondary liner due to failure of the primary tank bottom
[6]. Large, pit-like features were identified during visual inspection of the interior bottom of the
tank. These pits grew beneath a solids layer. The ultrasonic in-service inspection program for the DST was
unable to detect the growth of these pits due to inability to access the bottom of the primary tank. The same
solid layer (although local interstitial liquid chemistry and solids chemistry may have differed) was also
present on the interior side wall of the tank. However, ultrasonic inspection did not indicate the presence
of significant degradation.

Formation of saltcake and scale-like deposits on the wall and the bottom of several Hanford DSTs have
been observed; this is based on the loose, small particulate solids in core samples taken from the tanks and
post retrieval inspection of the inner surface of the primary tank of AY-102 [6]. These solids may promote
underdeposit corrosion, which occurs when the interstitial liquid in the solids layer seeps in between the
solids and the metal surface and creates an aggressive local environment. The objective of this task was to
determine the effect of solid deposits on the corrosion risk to the tank bottom, and whether a combination
of scale, saltcake and loose solids lead to underdeposit corrosion. The potential for deposition of an
adherent solids layer in this environment, and the risk of corrosion beneath the layer, was tested. The

 1

historical conditions that have existed in DST AZ-101 were selected as a representative tank for this
activity. Although these conditions were utilized for initial inputs, the tests went outside these conditions
in order to investigate the susceptibility, and hence the risk, for underdeposit corrosion of a tank bottom.
Therefore, the results of these tests do not necessarily reflect the condition of DST AZ-101.

SRNL initiated testing to investigate corrosion beneath solids in contact with the DST bottoms during the
second half of FY20 and continued testing through FY24 [5]. The investigation has not been tied
specifically to AY-102, but more generically to situations that could occur in the other Hanford DSTs. The
objective of the testing has been to discern whether differences in the environment at the tank bottom/solids
interface (e.g., crevice environment) vs. the sidewall or supernate liquid could explain preferential pitting
of the tank bottom [6]. These differences include localized temperature increases, pressure due to weight
of the solids and the degree of solids adherence to the metal surface. The latter effect may be due to
differences in the percentage of liquid remaining in the solids due to compression and heating. To date,
underdeposit corrosion in the form of micropits and crevice attack beneath both compressed and adherent
solids were observed in laboratory tests [7]. These features were absent from areas of the metal surface not
covered by the loosely adherent deposits or if the solids were not compressed.

2.2 Transfer Line Testing

Figure 2-1 shows the pipe-in-pipe design of the transfer line. The pimary inner pipe transports the waste,
while the outer pipe acts as an encasement. Corrosion on the interior of the encasement and exterior of the
primary is an issue. Although currently no ultrasonic test (UT) measurements have been made, the degree
of corrosion observed visually has resulted in restricted use of several of the transfer lines [8].

 Figure 2-1 Pipe-in-Pipe Transfer Line Design.

In 2022, a borescope inspection of a pre-1990 transfer line revealed standing liquid inside the annulus space
between the encasement and the primary pipe, in contact with the primary. Additionally, a significant build-
up of corrosion products on both the encasement and primary piping surfaces was observed [8]. To assess
the extent of condition, borescope inspections were performed in other facility transfer lines to provide a
baseline. The inspection revealed that 29 lines had either moisture present or evidence that moisture had

been present in the past (e.g., waterline corrosion where the pipe had been exposed to standing water) [8].
An image of the carrier pipe with several pits is presented in Figure 2-2 (a) and the profiled image of the
transfer line section with pit depths shown is presented Figure 2-2 (b). As seen in the figure, the deepest
pit was 73 mils on the pipe section. These lines were designated as “do-not-use” until further compensatory
actions are implemented to return the lines to service.

One of the compensatory actions performed by the Hanford tank farm facility was to inject warm, dry air
into the annulus between to remove moisture. Prevention of water accumulation would mitigate further
initiation and propagation of corrosion. To ensure that the relative humidity remains below a critical level,
probes will be installed to monitor the annulus region in four lines to evaluate the effectiveness of the drying
strategy. The Hanford tank farm facility requested guidance on action levels for these probes, i.e., a
definition of the relative humidity level that would require the facility to operate the system that injects the
warm, dry air into the annulus, if needed.

Another option for corrosion mitigation that the Hanford tank farm facility is considering is the deployment
of a nitrogen blanket in the annular region to exclude oxygen from the system. Oxygen provides for the
cathodic reduction reaction for the corrosion process and is typically a key driver in the rate of corrosion in
aqueous systems. This approach has been considered at other DOE facilities such as the waste tank annulus
system at West Valley [9]. During testing to support the use of a nitrogen blanket, a 100% nitrogen
environment was demonstrated to mitigate the corrosion of carbon steel to negligible rates [10]. However,
implementation in the field was complicated by air leaking into the annulus, which resupplied oxygen into
the system [11]. Use of the nitrogen blanket was abandoned in this instance because the presence of a non-
quantified amount of oxygen in the annulus allowed corrosion to continue [10, 11]. Testing to determine a
threshold oxygen value below which corrosion is mitigated was undertaken by SRNL in FY24. An action
level for the oxygen present in the pipeline annulus would be provided.

3.3 Task 3: Tank Bottom Solids Contact Testing

In FY24, short term electrochemical tests continued to be used to investigate underdeposit corrosion in a
single cell set-up [5]. However, the interstitial liquid simulants used in FY24 were more aggressive than
that used in FY23. The electrochemical testing utilized included open circuit potential (OCP)
measurements and cyclic potentiodynamic polarization (CPP) testing [12].

The metal coupons for these tests were fabricated from AAR TC-128 rail car steel. AAR-TC-128 steel was
selected for testing since it approximates the chemistry and microstructure of ASTM A515, Grade 60
carbon steel [13], the steel from which the DSTs in AY-Farm and AZ-Farm were fabricated. The solids for
the tests, as they were in FY21-23, were prepared from a recipe for the AZ-101 sludge [14].

The interstitial liquid simulant that has been used in the past was based on an interstitial liquid sample
obtained from Tank AZ-101 [15]. Initially, the corrosivity of the simulant, as measured by the Pitting
Factor (PF), was approximately 2. During tests with this simulant, micro-pitting of the carbon steel surface
was observed in both short-term electrochemical tests in a solids environment and long-term exposure tests
(4 months). However, in both instances, the pits did not appear to be sustainable and re-passivation
commenced before significant growth occurred. In FY23, the aggressiveness of the simulant was increased
by the addition of sodium chloride to obtain a PF of 1.2 [16]. However, this simulant also failed to yield
sufficient localized attack during short term testing to properly assess the impact of the solids on
susceptibility to localized corrosion. Therefore, the aggressiveness of the solution was further increased in
FY24 to allow for the possibility of the initiation of corrosion to assess the role that the presence of solids
plays in corrosion of the tank bottom.

More aggressive simulants with chemistries that may be more sensitive to the presence of solids, and the
crevices that are created, were investigated. An iterative approach, shown schematically in Figure 3-1, was
utilized to identify the aggressive simulants. The first step in the protocol identified a moderately corrosive
environment. The probability of failure of a CPP test was utilized for this purpose [2]. A range of solution
chemistries that produces a failure probability (P(1)) of approximately 0.5 in a free liquid (i.e., no solids)
was investigated initially. That is, an equal likelihood of a pass or failure exists in the CPP test. An example
of a simulant that meets this criterion is shown in Table 3-1. Although all simulants will have a similar
probability of failure, the concentrations of inhibitor and aggressive species may vary considerably.
Solutions may be corrosive due to either high nitrate, high halide, or a mixture of both. The effects of each
will be explored as a simulant is selected.

The second step was to conduct a CPP test in the recommended simulant with no solids at a temperature of
70 °C. The test was performed with a 3” diameter disk electrode made of the rail car steel. If the test
indicated that the simulant produces a failure condition (i.e., pitting), another moderately corrosive solution
will be tested. However, if the simulant produces a pass condition, the simulant will then progress to step
3, testing in a solid’s environment.

Step 3 uses the experimental configuration shown in Figure 4-9 to determine if conditions containing the
more aggressive simulant with the solids produce a failure condition. A 3” diameter coupon fabricated
from the rail car steel was placed in a vessel with wet sludge that had been “spiked” with the moderately
aggressive simulant. The plunger was utilized to compress or hold the solids in place during the test. As

 4

in previous years, the CPP test was performed at a temperature of 70 °C [7]. The reasons for utilizing this
temperature is that it represents the highest temperature for a DST that contains solids (i.e., AZ-101) [3].
If the test indicated that the conditions produce a failure condition, the simulant was used for the full suite
of short-term electrochemical tests. On the other hand, if the simulant produced a pass condition, the
iterative process was returned to step 1 to identify another moderately corrosive condition. The new
simulant may have a similar or higher probability of failure than the previous test.

3.4 Task 4: Transfer Line Drying Testing

An experimental program was undertaken to define the threshold relative humidity, below which corrosion
is mitigated, in FY23 [3]. Initially, the material, configuration and environmental conditions that exist
within the pipe were reviewed. From a materials consideration, both the encasement and the primary pipes
were fabricated from ASTM A106 carbon steel, a seamless pipe with circumferential butt-welds. The
carrier pipe is supported at intervals with stainless steel “spiders”. A review of Hanford pipeline
temperatures indicated a range of temperatures between 15 and 30 °C and a range of humidities between
40% and 80% depending upon the time of day and the season of the year. The previous research data
indicated that higher rates of corrosion would occur in the humidity conditions of 40% and 80%;
considering this, 35 °C was selected as the temperature condition [3]. The ability of the drying conditions
to mitigate pitting corrosion was investigated in this work.

The objectives for the tests were: 1) to measure the corrosion rates of carbon steel and stainless steel with
respect to humidity levels and temperatures, and 2) to determine the temperature and humidity levels that
mitigate pit initiation and propagation. These relationships of corrosion behavior and humidity parameters
will be used to determine thresholds that will need to be maintained in the transfer line annuli to ensure
corrosion, both localized and uniform, is minimized.

In FY23 testing, temperature and relative humidity were critical variables for the encasement environment
[3]. The temperature and relative humidity combinations that were tested are shown in Table 3-2. Tests at
~100% relative humidity were performed to simulate the condition where standing liquid is present in the
transfer line annulus.

For tests conducted at 30 °C, a significant decrease in corrosion rate was observed between 80% and 60%

observed at 100% relative humidity was more localized (depths up to 3.2 mils) [3].

For tests conducted at 15 °C, corrosion rates for all relative humidity levels evaluated were significantly
lower than was observed at high relative humidity (80% and 100% relative humidity) at 30 °C, on the order
of 0.0 to 0.03 mpy. In addition, no significant decrease in corrosion rate with decreased relative humidity
was observed at 15 °C. The relationship between corrosion rate and relative humidity is displayed in Figure

Figure 3-2 Relationship between relative humidity and surface average corrosion rate for 30 °C (full

As observed in Figure 3-2, the relationship between relative humidity and corrosion differs greatly between
the two temperatures tested. As such, a different metric for quantifying the amount of water vapor that is
present and incorporates a temperature component, dew point [3, 17], was used to assess the effect of the
amount of water vapor present on corrosion rate. For coupons exposed at 30 °C, it was observed that
corrosion rates were essentially negligible at a relative humidity of 30%, corresponding to a dew point of
12.1 °C. This dew point is like the dew point at a temperature of 15 °C and a relative humidity of 80%
(10.1 °C). This condition also produced minimal corrosion on the carbon steel coupon. As such, there
appeared to be a relationship between the amount of water present in the vapor phase, which is temperature
dependent, and corrosion rate that was not captured by using relative humidity as a metric. Figure 3-3
displays the relationship between corrosion rate and dew point [3, 17].

4.1.4 Set-up for Short-Term Electrochemical Tests
Several unique setups were used for short-term electrochemical testing. Various combinations of interstitial
liquid simulant and/or loose sludge were used to simulate different tank bottom environments. AAR TC128
Rail Car Steel disks measuring 3 inches in diameter and 0.125 inches thick were used for testing. Insulated
lead wires were affixed to the back surface of each coupon using conductive silver epoxy to ensure a proper
electrical connection to the electrode. The coupons were then mounted in a two-part clear epoxy solution
(EpoKwick® from Buehler) so that the electrical connection was protected from the environment, and only
the top surface of the carbon steel coupon was exposed to the test environment. These mounted samples
were then placed in the glass vessels shown in Figure 4-8 with the lead wire exiting the vessel through one
of the ports.

In the case of tests conducted with interstitial liquid simulant only, approximately 300 mL of AZ-101
interstitial liquid simulant (described in Section 4.1.3) was added to the test vessel. A salt bridge was
inserted into a vessel containing 0.1 M sodium nitrate solution, which provided an ionic pathway for
measuring potential of the test coupon using a saturated calomel reference electrode (SCE). Two different
types of counter electrodes were used throughout these tests. Initially, two graphite rods served as counter
electrodes for the system. Due to issues with available cathodic area, a stainless-steel mesh plunger
(described below) was used in subsequent experiments. In the case of the stainless-steel plunger, the salt
bridge for the reference electrode protruded through the wire mesh to eliminate any interference of the wire
mesh with potential measurements. All ports were then sealed to mitigate loss of water through evaporation.
Test vessels were heated to 70 ± 2 °C using a hot plate. The electrodes were connected to the leads of a
potentiostat with the carbon steel coupon connected to the lead for the working electrode, the graphite rods
(connected with a jumper wire) or stainless-steel plunger connected to the lead for the counter electrode,
and the saturated calomel electrode attached to the lead for the reference electrode. Once the test
temperature in the vessel was reached, the sequence of electrochemical tests described in Section 4.1.5 was
conducted.

In the case of tests involving loose sludge, AZ-101 Insoluble Solids (Sludge) (described in Section 4.1.3)
was added prior to the Interstitial Liquid Simulant. Approximately 300 grams of sludge was added for each
test, followed by approximately 100 mL of interstitial liquid simulant. The cell set-up involved compressed
sludge, in which the stainless-steel mesh plunger confines the sludge to a fixed volume and does not allow
it to freely mix with the liquid above. Images of the plunger and compressed sludge configuration are shown
in Figure 4-8. A cross-sectional diagram of the cell configuration is also shown in Figure 4-9.

4.2.3 Pre-Corrosion of Coupons
Since the key objective of the testing was to understand mitigation of the pitting corrosion under the drying
conditions, several coupons were pre-corroded using dilute Ground Water (GW) solution at 45 °C; the
chemical composition of the dilute GW solution is listed in Table 4-9 The undiluted GW solution is
primarily sodium bicarbonate with small amounts of nitrate, sulfate, and chloride; the total anion

 21

concentration is less than 0.005 M [3]. The undiluted GW solution was diluted by mixing GW with
deionized water such that total chloride concentration in the resultant was approximately 1 ppm, which is
akin to hydrotest water and could have been left in the annular space between the carrier and the casing
pipes.

The objective of the pre-corrosion step was to corrode the coupons to such as extent so that a measurable
amount of pitting corrosion occurs on the coupons. The temperature value of 45 °C was selected so that
the coupons experience measurable corrosion in a six-week duration. The glass vessels, which were used
for the long-term experiments, were also used for the pre-corrosion step. The container was designed to
minimize the influence of any temperature variations acting upon the container and the contents. Each
glass vessel was designed for each test such that the volume to solution surface area ratio was less than 25
cm as per ASTM E104 [21]. Additionally the overall container headspace volume was no larger than
necessary to confine the coupons. The pre-corrosion was conducted using dilute GW at 100% RH. Several
coupons were placed inside the vessels during the pre-corrosion step at the following positions: (a) hung in
the vapor space, (b) immersed in diluted GW, (c) positioned such that half of each coupon was immersed
in liquid and remaining half in vapor space. The position (c) coupons are labeled as liquid-air-interface
(LAI) hereafter. A set of coupons were analyzed for their mass losses and corrosion feature immediately
after the pre-corrosion, and the remaining coupons were placed inside the 35 °C exposure conditions.
Representative images of the pre-corroded coupons are presented in Figure 4-13.

In addition, standard coupons, i.e., coupons which were pre-corroded but not exposed, were prepared for
these tests. The purpose for the standard coupons was to determine if a change in the the amount of
corrosion could be detected as a result of the exposure to the environment (e.g., an increase in mass loss).
A total of 18 standard coupons were prepared; six were immersed, six were exposed to the vapor space,
and six were exposed to the liquid-air interface. To determine if the change in the amount of corrosion was
significant, the mass loss of the standard coupon was compared with the mass loss of a pre-corroded sample
that was exposed to the test environment. A t-test was performed to determine if there was a significant
difference between the two values. The t-test function in EXCEL™ was utilized for this calculation. Visual
pictures of the samples and the mass loss data are presented in Appendix B.

4.3.5 Nitrogen Test Monitoring

The volume percent of oxygen inside the vessels was monitored using Alpha Omega Series 2000 Oxygen
Percent Analyzers, which has a tolerance of ±1% in a range from 1-100 vol%. Sensor readings were
recorded into a data logger in 5-minute intervals. Figure 4-19 shows the oxygen concentration in Vessels
1, 2 and 3 with respect to time, with each data marker representing an average over six hours. Tracking
began at 07/03/2024 and was concluded at 11/11/2024. During the first month, only one oxygen meter was
present for the first two weeks. A mass flow controller was also not in place, which caused nitrogen flow
rate to drift when a second analog meter was deployed, and although the correct theoretical flowrate was
used, the sensor readings undershot the expected O2% measurements. Flowrates were adjusted by ±1-5
sccm to see if the vol% reading could be improved, but it was not until the two meters were recalibrated on
7/30/2024 that the problem was alleviated. From 08/2024 to 11/2024, the vol% of oxygen in Vessel 3
stabilized around 9.9%, and the vol% in Vessel 2 stabilized around 4.6-4.7%, which was within tolerance.
Because only two meters were present, Vessel 1 was only periodically checked for leaks by disconnecting
one of the two meters and attaching it temporarily to Vessel 1. The Alpha Omega analyzer determines
oxygen concentration via an electrochemical voltage that forms by electrode reactions, which will not occur
in the absence of oxygen. As a result, the sensor is extremely accurate at determining conditions without
oxygen. Over the course of the experimental timeframe, no oxygen was detected in the intermittent
recordings, indicating no leaks.

5.1 Tank Solids Contact

5.1.1 Short –term Electrochemical Tests

Two distinct conditions were evaluated in FY24. The first condition was carbon steel coupons exposed to
an interstitial liquid simulant only. The interstitial liquid simulant was used as a control experiment to
evaluate the behavior of the carbon steel in the absence of any deposited solids. The second condition was
compressed solids. This condition incorporated the solids but allowed the compaction and confinement of
insoluble solids that prevent free mixing to create a condition where solids are more likely to adhere to the
metal surface.

Using the listed conditions above, OCP was measured with respect to time, and CPP scans were used to
generate polarization curves. These scans provide insight on the corrosion potential, passive current density,
and the presence of localized corrosion.

Figure 5-6 shows the CCP curve for the compressed solids with the spiked interstitial liquid using
simulant 6. The polarization curve demonstrates clear failure in accordance with the CPP guidelines due
to positive hysteresis. A broadening of the curve is also present, demonstrating characteristics of crevice
attack. Retrieval of the coupon revealed that, while the majority of the sludge could be removed by
simply rinsing the coupon with water, adhered deposits continued to stick to the coupon surface.
Removing the deposits revealed areas of general attack, in addition to distinct areas of more localized,
deeper attack that resembled crevice corrosion. No pitting was apparent on the surface through visual
examination of the sample. Other areas of the sample that were not covered by loosely adhered solids
appeared pristine.

Figure 5-7 shows the CPP curve for the compressed solids with the spiked interstitial liquid using
simulant 7. Similar to Figure 5-6, positive hystersis is seen (fail condition) and a broadening of the curve
characteristic of crevice attack, although more pronounced than the CCP curve for compressed solids with
the spiked interstitial liquid with simulant 6. Upon examination of the coupon after testing, sludge was
adhered to the full surface of the coupon and required physical wear to be removed. Severe attack was
apparent on the entire surface beneath the adhered solids.

A quantifiable difference between the corrosion potential, corrosion rate, and passive current density
between the interstitial liquid simulants and their compressed solid counter parts is apparent. More
specifically, the interstitial liquid simulants 6 and 7 demonstrated no corrosion activity based on the CPP
curves and by visual observation of the coupons. However, the compressed solid counterparts exhibited
increased corrosion activity in comparison to the interstitial simulants, with the compressed solids with
the spiked interstitial liquid that had chlorides, simulant 7, having increased corrosion activity in
comparison to the compressed solids with the spiked interstitial liquid that had no added chlorides,
simulant 6.

The pre-corroded coupon mass loss and standard deviation are listed in Table 5-3, in addition to standard
coupon data, i.e., coupons which were pre-corroded but not exposed. Student’s t-test was used to determine
the significance of difference between the exposed and standard coupon mass loss data. Student’s t-test is
based on the hypothesis that there is no statistically significant difference between the mass losses for the
two sets of the coupons used in the t-test—that is, that they are essentially identical to each other in terms
of the coupon mass losses. The statistical result calculated by the test, P-value, is the probability that the
hypothesis is true. If the P-value is equal or less than 0.05, it indicates a low probability that the two sets
of the coupons have similar mass losses—that is, there is a statistically significant difference in the mass
loss between the two coupon sets. The P-values for various comparison sets of the coupons in Table 5-3
are greater than 0.05, indicating that the mass loss data of the pre-corroded coupons and the mass loss data
of the pre-corroded plus exposed are statistically similar, i.e., even if the additional corrosion occurred
during the exposure condition, it was not significant enough to cause statistically significant increase in the
coupons’ mass losses.

5.3.4 General Corrosion Rates and Pitting Depths in Nitrogen Environments

Equation 1 was utilized to calculate the corrosion rate based on the mass loss data from the fresh coupons.
After the coupons were cleaned, the post-test mass was subtracted from the initial coupon mass to determine
the mass loss. Figure 5-25 shows the calculated corrosion rate as a function of nitrogen concentration,
coupon orientation and phase. For each coupon orientation in the vapor space it is observed that the
corrosion rate decreases with nitrogen concentration. For 100% nitrogen, the corrosion rate was essentially
nonexistent. A significant decrease in corrosion rate in the vapor space with % nitrogen was seen for
horizontal and vertical coupons, 3 and 2 mpy, respectively. Finally, the vapor space corrosion rate at 90%
nitrogen continued to increase for the horizontal coupons (4.5 mpy), while for the vertical coupon the
corrosion rate remained unchanged. As has been discussed, the results indicate that the horizontal coupons
are corroding at a faster rate than the vertical coupons. This is likely due to the fact that the horizontal
orientation allows for accumulation of condensed water, whereas condensed water would run off the surface
of the vertical coupon.

The immersed coupons were oriented horizontally as well. However, the dissolved oxygen in the water is
limited by solubility. At 100% nitrogen concentration, the rate was again essentially nonexistent. While
there is a slight increase in the corrosion rate in the immersed phase (< 1 mpy), the lack of oxygen precludes
significant corrosion from occurring. Thus, for the transfer lines, general corrosion in the nitrogen
atmosphere would be greatest in the horizontally oriented, vapor space surfaces, and if oxygen leaks into
the system.

These results correlated well with visual observations of the coupons.

 Figure 5-25 Corrosion rate on fresh coupons as a function of N2%, orientation and location.

Cleaned, fresh coupons were scanned with the microscope to determine pit depths as a function of oxygen
concentration, orientation, and phase (see Figure 5-26). No pitting was observed in the immersed
samples, likely a result of the lack of oxygen in the liquid phase. As with the general corrosion rate, depth
of the pits increased with oxygen concentration. The greatest depth of 265 µm (~10 mils) was observed
on a horizontal coupon at 90% nitrogen. However, there does not appear to be a significant difference in
the pit at 95% nitrogen vs, that at 90% nitrogen. Similar to general corrosion, the horizontal vapor space
surfaces were more vulnerable to pitting

6.0 Conclusions

For FY24 two experimental studies were conducted: 1) tank bottom solids contact corrosion testing and 2)
transfer line corrosion mitigation. For the tank bottom solids contact testing, aggressive simulants with
chemistries that may be more sensitive to the presence of solids, and the crevices that are created, were
investigated. Electrochemcial tests with free liquid and beneath compressed solids were performed. Two
mitigation strategies for transfer line corrosion were investigated; 1) Dehumidification or drying, and 2)
Nitrogen blanketing of the annulus region between the core pipe and the encasement.

A summary of the results and conclusions for each activity is discussed below.

6.1 Tank Bottom Solids Contact Corrosion Testing

 • A quantifiable difference amongst the corrosion potential, corrosion rate, and passive current
 density between the interstitial liquid simulants and their compressed solid counter parts was
 measured. More specifically, the interstitial liquid simulants 6 and 7 (PF = 0.8) demonstrated

 56

 no corrosion activity in the CPP curves or on the physical coupon. However, the compressed
 solid counterparts exhibited increased corrosion activity in comparison to the IL simulants.
 • No differences were noted among corrosion behavior when the interstitial liquid of the
 compressed solids were spiked with aggressive species. The spiked compressed solid that
 had added chlorides, simulant 7, had increased corrosion activity in comparison to the spiked
 compressed solid that had no added chlorides, simulant 6.
 • In FY25, higher values of PF, between 0.8 and 1.2, will be tested to determine a threshold
 PF above which attack beneath the solids is mitigated. Additionally, SRNL will investigate
 the use of a multiple crevice assembly to simulate the compressed solid environment

6.2 Transfer Line Drying

 • The dew point was used as a criterion for the effectiveness of corrosion mitigation. In
 general, if the dew point was less than 20 °C minimal general and pitting corrosion would be
 expected. The tests from FY24 confirmed the results observed in FY23.
 • Significant corrosion was not observed for tests at 35 °C and at relative humidity between 60
 and 100%. Although the cause is not completely known, it appears that the condition of the
 metal surface precluded condensation on the surface.
 • Pre-corroded samples were tested in FY24. However, after exposure there was no apparent
 visual change in the appearance of the coupon nor change in the mass of the coupon. Thus,
 conclusions as to whether drying mitigates propagation of existing corrosion pits could not
 be inferred.
 • For FY25, other means for pre-corroding the sample such that propagation of existing pits
 may be investigated.

6.3 Transfer Line Nitrogen Blanket

 • Corrosion of carbon steel was effectively mitigated in the liquid and vapor space when the
 nitrogen reduced the oxygen concentration to 0%.
 • Corrosion of carbon steel was effectively mitigated in the liquid with oxygen
 concentrations of 5% and 10% oxygen.
 • Corrosion of carbon steel increased significantly in the vapor space with 5% and 10%
 oxygen presence compared to the 0% oxygen level. Both general and pitting corrosion was
 observed. Based on these results, the oxygen concentration should be maintained below
 5% to mitigate general and localized corrosion.
 • Horizontally oriented carbon steel coupons experienced a greater degree of attack in the
 vapor space than vertically oriented coupons.
 • Pre-corroded coupons were tested to investigate whether corrosion would re-initiate and
 propagate. No difference between samples which had been exposed to the nitrogen blanket
 environment and those, which had not been exposed could be detected. No conclusion
 regarding the effectiveness of the nitrogen blanket could be made.
 • For FY25, other means for pre-corroding the sample such that propagation of existing pits
 should be investigated.
