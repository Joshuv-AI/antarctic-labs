Postmortem brain single-nucleus and bulk gene expression analyses identify shared and distinct abnormalities in bipolar disorder and major depressive disorder
Authors: Rongwei Gao, Ikuo Otsuka, Toshiyuki Shirai, Masao Miyachi, Kiriko Minami, Shohei Okada, Ryo Tsukamoto, Takaki Tanifuji, Satoshi Okazaki, Akitoyo Hishimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bipolar disorder (BD) and major depressive disorder (MDD) are severe and chronic mood disorders which impairs the psychosocial functioning of patients. Besides manic or hypomanic episodes, many patients with BD also experience depressive episodes similar to those seen in MDD, suggesting the similarities and differences between the two diseases. However, the shared and distinct abnormalities of gene expression and cellular function in the brain underlying the disorders remain to be elucidated. Here we analyzed publicly available single-nucleus and bulk RNA expression datasets of postmortem brain samples from BD patients, MDD patients, and healthy donors. For single-nucleus gene expression profiling of postmortem orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), differential abundance testing, differential gene expression analysis and gene set enrichment analysis performed on each cell type showed abnormal gene expression profiles in both neuronal and non-neuronal cells. Cell-cell communication inference revealed the dysfunction of parvalbumin interneurons, as well as enhanced interactions among certain subtypes of neurons, in both diseases. Gene co-expression network analysis identified the overall alterations in both diseases, as well as the differences in co-expression modules between BD and MDD. Compared to MDD, most neurons in BD exhibited particularly downregulated expression of modules related to translation and mitochondrial ATP production. Transcription factor network analysis revealed the regulatory relationships among the modules. Further analyses of two additional datasets supported the main findings of the postmortem OFC analyses. Overall, our findings clarified the molecular mechanisms underlying BD and MDD, which highlight distinct molecular pathways that may inform therapeutic strategies for mood disorders.

Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) and major depressive disorder (MDD) are psychiatric disorders characterized by emotion dysregulation. Both mood disorders have become serious health issues, since their impairment of work performance and social functioning leads to a decline in quality of life [1]. BD and MDD affect approximately 40 million and 280 million people worldwide, respectively [2, 3]. The most notable difference between the two diseases is the presence or absence of manic (or hypomanic) episodes. On the other hand, while patients with BD may experience prolonged depressive states, it is difficult to distinguish between BD and MDD during their depressive episodes [4]. Although the symptoms during depressive episodes are similar, there are also subtle differences. For example, compared to unipolar depression, bipolar II depression exhibits earlier age of onset, stronger paranoia, and higher suicidal tendency [5], suggesting different pathophysiological mechanisms between the two diseases.

Regarding current pharmacotherapy for mood disorders, BD and MDD require different treatment approaches. Generally, antipsychotics and mood stabilizers are commonly used for BD [6], while antidepressants such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are frequently used as the main therapeutic drug for MDD [7]. Because the presence or absence of manic episodes is the key diagnostic criterion distinguishing BD from MDD, antidepressants are often prescribed for patients experiencing depressive episodes without manic (or hypomanic) episode history. However, administering antidepressants to BD patients frequently triggers conversion to a manic (or hypomanic) state, which leads to increased mood instability [8]. Therefore, there is a strong need for the development of safer antidepressant therapies applicable to both BD patients and MDD patients, based on the mechanisms common in mood disorders.

Even though much remains unknown, advances in neuroimaging and genetics have provided new insights into the pathophysiology of mood disorders. Studies based on functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have suggested that abnormalities in the frontal lobe, particularly in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), are characteristic features distinguishing patients with mood disorders, including BD and MDD, from the general population [9]. Moreover, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have suggested that excitatory and inhibitory neurons in multiple brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex (PFC), ACC, amygdala, and hippocampus, are most relevant to the molecular biology of BD and MDD [10–12].

While genetic variants represent important risk factors, transcriptomic changes are also thought to play a significant role in psychiatric disorders. Many prior studies of postmortem brains from psychiatric patients using bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) have revealed gene expression changes that contribute to diseases including schizophrenia (SCZ), BD, and MDD [13–15]. These studies have highlighted the abnormalities in brain immune responses, synaptic function, and neurodevelopment in psychiatric disorders. However, it was unclear which cell types contribute most to the diseases. Single-nucleus gene profiling, which emphasize the differences between cells, have enabled the examination of gene expression characteristics at the individual nucleus level [16–18]. Here, we reanalyzed postmortem OFC single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset GSE254569, which comprises samples from individuals with psychiatric disorders and healthy donors and is publicly available on Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) [16]. While the contributors of GSE254569 revealed global changes associated with aging or psychiatric disorders [16, 17], disease-specific changes were only examined for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across cell types. Furthermore, we confirmed the main findings using two additional postmortem gene expression datasets: a snRNA-seq dataset of dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) [19, 20], and a bulk RNA-seq dataset of multiple brain regions [21]. In this study, we aim to elucidate not only overall and disease-specific gene expression profiles in BD and MDD but also alterations in cell-cell interactions and gene co-expression networks.

Materials and methods

Data of postmortem brain single-nucleus gene profiling

We used postmortem OFC snRNA-seq dataset GSE254569 (available at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=gse254569) from GEO database [16]. In this study, we only included samples derived from BD patients, MDD patients, and healthy individuals. To match the sample quality between case and control group [22, 23], we only used samples with 6.3 < pH < 7 and RNA integrity number (RIN) > 6. Sample information is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

In addition, to verify our main findings regarding BD, we used postmortem dlPFC snRNA-seq dataset published in the CELLxGENE (RRID:SCR_021059) portal (available at https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/84ce6837-548d-4a1f-919f-0bc0d9a3952f) [19, 20]. In this study, we only included samples derived from BD patients and healthy individuals in the Human Brain Collection Core (HBCC) cohort, aged 18 to 65 years. Cases with comorbidities were excluded. Information for postmortem interval (PMI) and brain pH was obtained from https://github.com/DiseaseNeuroGenomics/psychAD_SciData [19]. Sample information is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Data of postmortem brain bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

We used postmortem brain bulk RNA-seq dataset GSE80655 (available at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE80655) from GEO database [21]. In this study, we only included postmortem brain ACC and dlPFC samples derived from BD patients and MDD patients. Sample information is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data processing

The count matrix of GSE254569 was preprocessed by the contributors, but in this study, we applied stricter filtering to improve the accuracy of downstream network analyses. Details for preprocessing and cell type annotation are described in the Supplementary Methods [24, 25]. For plotting cells of each cell type, we performed batch correction using the R package Harmony version 1.2.3 [26], followed by re-clustering. The number of dimensions and resolution used for each cell type are described in the Supplementary Materials.

In addition, a subset of samples from the HBCC cohort was used for confirmation. Details for data processing of the samples from the HBCC cohort are described in the Supplementary Methods [25, 27].

Differential abundance (DA) analysis

To identify cell sub-clusters showing differences between the case and control group, we applied the R package miloR version 2.5.1 [28], a single-cell differential abundance (DA) testing framework using k-nearest neighbor (KNN) graphs. Details are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Single-nucleus differential gene expression analysis

We identified DEGs in each cell type using the pseudobulk method. We used the R package edgeR version 4.7.3 [29] for differential gene expression analysis. Because the number of cells per donor varies widely, we performed the analyses at cell-type levels rather than at cell-subtype levels. We also excluded cell types with low numbers, to avoid bias caused by the differences in cell numbers per donor. Details are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Enrichment analysis

We used the R package clusterProfiler version 4.17.0 [30] (as well as fgsea version 1.35.6 [31]) for gene ontology (GO) analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Details are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Cell-cell communication (CCC) inference

To infer the differences in cell-cell interaction patterns within the brains of BD patients, MDD patients, and healthy individuals, we performed cell-cell communication (CCC) inference using the R package CellChat version 2.2.0 [32]. Details are described in the Supplementary Methods.

High-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA)

We performed cell-type-specific high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) [33] based on excitatory neurons as well as transcription factor (TF) regulatory network analysis using the R package hdWGCNA version 0.4.07 [34–37]. Details are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Bulk RNA-seq WGCNA

We performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis on bulk gene expression using the R package WGCNA version 1.73 [33]. Details are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Single-nucleus transcriptomic profiling and cell population of case and controls in the OFC

To dissect the cell-type-specific gene expression changes in bipolar disorder (BD) and major depressive disorder (MDD), we analyzed the postmortem brain orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset of 5 donors with BD, 5 donors with MDD and 26 healthy donors, which is the subset of GSE254569 (Fig. 1A) [16]. We identified the following 7 cell types and 17 subtypes: glutamatergic excitatory neurons (ExN, including 6 subtypes: ExN.L2-3IT, ExN.L3-5IT, ExN.L6IT, ExN.L5-6NP, ExN.L6CT and ExN.L5ET), GABAergic inhibitory neurons (InN, including 6 subtypes: InN.Lamp5, InN.Sncg, InN.Vip, InN.Sst, InN.Pvalb-Ba and InN.Pvalb-Ch), astrocytes (Ast), microglia (Mic), oligodendrocytes (Oli), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPC) and endothelial cells (End), based on the expression profiles of cell type- or subtype-specific marker genes (Fig. 1B, D and Supplementary Fig. S1A–F). We also calculated the cell proportion for each classification (Fig. 1C). Differential abundance (DA) analysis [28] revealed subclusters in Ast and Mic that lacked cells from the case group. In Ast, cluster 2 contained a large number of cells from healthy individuals, but few cells from the patients (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, we confirmed that most of cells in cluster 4 originated from a single CTRL individual (Supplementary Fig. S2E), so we do not consider this to be a subcluster that is abundant in either group. In Mic, there were fewer cells from the case group in clusters 2 and 4 (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S2F). Notably, these subclusters of Ast and Mic exhibited distinct marker gene expression patterns and expressed more genes than other subclusters (Fig. 1G, H). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [30, 31] revealed that positive regulation of epithelial cell proliferation was activated in the cluster 2 of Ast, and biological processes (BPs) such as DNA replication and DNA repair were activated in the cluster 2 and 4 of Mic. Moreover, GSEA showed that these subclusters had few BP related to synapses, suggesting that their higher gene expression levels were not due to the contamination of neuronal debris (Supplementary Fig. S2G, H). These findings suggest the possibility of astrocyte and microglia dysfunction in mood disorders.

Cell-type-specific gene expression changes associated with BD and MDD

To clarify gene expression changes associated with mood disorders as well as those specific to BD and MDD, we performed single-nucleus differential gene expression analyses [29] in (1) case (BD + MDD) versus CTRL, (2) BD versus CTRL, (3) MDD versus CTRL, and (4) BD versus MDD.

For case versus CTRL, FAN107A was identified as a significantly (FDR < 0.05) upregulated gene in OPC, while there were no differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with FDR < 0.05 in other cell types (Supplementary Fig. S3A–E). In ExN, it was found that BPs related to cellular catabolic process was significantly activated, while BPs related to cytoplasmic translation and synaptic transport were significantly suppressed (Fig. 2A). Dysfunction of parvalbumin interneurons and hyperactivity of excitatory neurons in BD and MDD

To elucidate changes in cellular functions and signaling pathways contributing to BD and MDD, we performed cell-cell communication (CCC) inference [32] in (1) case versus CTRL, (2) BD versus CTRL, (3) MDD versus CTRL, and (4) BD versus MDD. Interestingly, compared to CTRL, the entire case group showed a decrease in the number of interactions sent from InN.Pvalb-Ba to all neurons (Fig. 3A). This was also observed when comparing BD or MDD to CTRL, respectively (Fig. 3B, C). Furthermore, case group exhibited increased interaction strength among all neurons, except ExN.L5-6NP and InN.Pvalb-Ba (Fig. 3A). In BD, increased interaction strength from IT neurons to ExN was observed. ExN.L5-6NP exhibited a different pattern of alteration compared to other neurons, particularly showing decreased interaction strength sent to ExN.L5-6NP and InN. Enhanced bi-directional communication between neurons and Mic, as well as increased interaction sent from End to neurons, was also detected in BD (Fig. 3B). In MDD, in contrast to the decrease in the number of interactions sent from InN.Pvalb-Ba, interactions sent from InN.Pvalb-Ch to all neurons were clearly increased. The number and strength of interaction sent from Ast, OPC, and End to most of the neurons were also increased (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, we identified the information flow as well as the outgoing and incoming signaling patterns that showed differences in each comparison (showed the results of case versus CTRL and BD versus MDD in Supplementary Fig. S5A–F). When we examined the dysfunction of InN.Pvalb-Ba in detail, we observed downregulation of GABA-A and GABA-B outgoing signaling pathways, as well as upregulation of glutamate incoming signaling pathway, in BD and MDD (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. S6A, B). Since parvalbumin interneurons regulate the spikes of their neighboring excitatory neurons [38, 39], we further examined the altered signaling pathways sent from InN.Pvalb-Ba to all neurons. It was found that communications mediated by GABA-A signaling pathway were substantially suppressed in case group, particularly BD (Supplementary Fig. S6C–E). In addition, JAM signaling pathway showed an increase in information flow in the entire case group (Supplementary Fig. S5A–C) and was particularly associated with the bi-directional interactions involving layer 5-6 excitatory neurons, InN.Pvalb-Ba and Mic (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S5B, C). Although SPP1 signaling pathway showed only slight alterations in the comparison between case and CTRL (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S5A–C), it was found that signaling pathways sent from Mic were clearly enhanced in BD compared to MDD (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. S5D–F). These findings suggest alterations in GABAergic transmission in parvalbumin interneurons and dysfunctional communications involving neurons and glial cells in mood disorders, particularly BD.

We next evaluated the preservation of modules constructed in case group within CTRL group, based on the correlation structure of co-expression modules [40]. All modules passed the quality check, and modules including M4, M7, M8 and M11 were suggested to have relatively lower preservation in CTRL (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S7F). We also performed differential module eigengene (DME) analysis and module-trait correlation analysis based on the expression of module eigengenes (MEs, i.e., the first principal component of gene expression matrix in each module [34]). DME analysis between BD and MDD revealed that the expression of M1 was significantly reduced in ExN in BD (Fig. 4C). Results of DME testing by subtype indicated that the expression of M1 was significantly decreased in many subtypes of neurons in BD (though it showed a significant increase in ExN.L5-6NP), while M2 expression was significantly increased in most of the ExN subtypes (Fig. 4D). In addition, module-trait correlation analysis revealed that M1, M2, M5, M7, M8, M10 and M11 showed significant associations with the “Classification” trait (i.e., BD or MDD) across many subtypes of neurons (Supplementary Fig. S7G). Thus, we consider M4 as the most important module that regulates differences between case and CTRL, and M1 as the module that clearly distinguishing BD from MDD. Enrichment analysis of genes within each module (for all, see Supplementary Fig. S7H) revealed that M1 contains many genes related to translation, cellular respiration, and ubiquitin modification (Fig. 4E, F), while M4 contains many genes related to RNA and protein processing (Fig. 4G). In summary, consistent with the results of enrichment analysis based on gene expression changes, network analysis also indicated that abnormalities in translation were a common feature between BD and MDD, and specifically, biological processes related to translation and mitochondrial ATP production showed dysfunction in BD.

Transcription factor network analysis reveals key regulatory relationships

To estimate the regulatory patterns across modules and key transcription factors (TFs) in gene expression regulation, we performed TF regulatory network analysis, in which TF-gene regulatory links were identified by an ensemble machine learning approach [36]. Overall, M1 and M4 received substantial negative regulation from TFs in modules including M5, M7, and M8 (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S8A, B). We examined the regulatory networks of TFs identified as hub genes and specifically expressed in ExN (i.e., CUX2, RORB, POU6F2, FOXP2, PKNOX2, and BCL11A) (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S8C–G). We found that PKNOX2, a hub gene of M7 (Fig. 4A), distinctly exhibited negative regulation over the genes in M1 and M4 (Fig. 5C). Enrichment analysis showed that negative regulons of PKNOX2 were associated with mitochondrial function (Fig. 5E). Differential regulon analysis between BD and MDD identified 5 TFs showing significantly differential expression: BHLHE40, ETS2, CREM, ZNF675, and PPARG (Fig. 5B). Notably, BHLHE40 not only exhibited a substantial change of positive and negative regulon score but also showed a strong correlation with the general trend of variation (i.e., close to the regression line). Furthermore, genes positively regulated by BHLHE40 were abundant in M1 (Fig. 5D). Enrichment analysis showed that positive regulons of BHLHE40 were associated with responses to increased oxygen levels (Fig. 5F). For each TF (PKNOX2 and BHLHE40), the presence of numerous predicted targets was confirmed, particularly PKNOX2 (Supplementary Fig. S8H–J). Consistency with results from snRNA-seq of the dlPFC

We further analyzed a large-scale dataset of postmortem dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) snRNA-seq [19, 20], to verify the findings regarding BD versus CTRL. Glutamatergic excitatory neurons (EN, including EN_L2_3_IT, EN_L3_5_IT_1, EN_L3_5_IT_2, EN_L3_5_IT_3, EN_L6_IT, EN_L5_6_NP, EN_L6B and EN_L6_CT), GABAergic inhibitory neurons (IN, including IN_LAMP5_LHX6, IN_LAMP5_RELN, IN_ADARB2, IN_VIP, IN_SST, IN_PVALB and IN_PVALB_CHC), astrocytes (Astro), oligodendrocytes (Oligo), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPC), immune cells (Immune), mural and vascular cells (Mural), and endothelial cells (Endo), were identified in the dlPFC (Supplementary Fig. S9E). Cell composition of the dlPFC dataset was similar to the OFC dataset (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S9E), as both originate from cortical regions.

For each cell type in the dlPFC, differential gene expression analysis [29] for BD versus CTRL revealed a large number of DEGs with FDR < 0.05 (particularly those upregulated in astrocytes) (Supplementary Fig. S10A–F). GSEA demonstrated that BPs related to cellular respiration and cytoplasmic translation were significantly suppressed in both EN and IN, which was largely consistent with the findings in the OFC (although ExN exhibited no significant alterations in cellular respiration in the OFC) (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B and Supplementary Fig. S11A, B). In addition, enriched BPs in Astro, Immune, Oilgo and OPC were also observed in GSEA (Supplementary Fig. S11C–F).

Furthermore, we performed CCC inference [32] in the dlPFC and compared BD to CTRL. In BD, it was found that the number of interactions sent from most neurons (particularly several subtypes of IN) was reduced, while the strength of most neuron-to-neuron interactions was increased (Supplementary Fig. S12A). Notably, in IN_PVALB, downregulation of GABA-A and GABA-B outgoing signaling pathways as well as upregulation of glutamate incoming signaling pathway were demonstrated in BD, which was consistent with the findings in the OFC (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Fig. S6A and Supplementary Fig. S12B). Additionally, the information flow of JAM signaling pathway was slightly increased in BD, which was similarly observed in the OFC when comparing the overall case group to CTRL (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Fig. S5A and Supplementary Fig. S12C). Together, these results suggest that dysfunction of cellular respiration and cytoplasmic translation in neurons, as well as the dysregulated GABAergic signaling pathways involving inhibitory neurons, are common signatures in cortical regions including the OFC and the dlPFC in BD patients.

Consistency with results from bulk RNA-seq of cortical regions

To confirm the universality of the co-expression network associated with BD and MDD constructed with the snRNA-seq dataset, we performed gene co-expression network analysis [33] on a bulk RNA-seq dataset of other cortical regions [21]. 13 modules were detected in the integrated dataset of the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and dlPFC (Supplementary Fig. S13A). Comparing the genes of each module to the top 20 marker genes of each cell type in snRNA profiling (Supplementary Materials), we found that most neuronal marker genes belonged to ME3, ME6, ME7, or ME10, while most non-neuronal marker genes belonged to ME1, ME2, ME4, ME5, or ME11 (Supplementary Fig. S13B). We then performed module-trait correlation analysis and compared BD to MDD. It was found that the expression of ME1 and ME4 was most upregulated, while the expression of ME3 was most downregulated (Supplementary Fig. S13C). Moreover, GO analysis revealed that genes of ME1 are significantly enriched in catabolic process, genes of ME3 in mitochondrial ATP synthesis, and genes of ME4 in cilium movement (Supplementary Fig. S13D–F). These results are considered to demonstrate the universality of the disease-associated gene co-expression network and to support the main conclusion from the single-nucleus gene expression analyses that mitochondrial function of neurons is particularly impaired in BD compared to MDD.

Discussion

Here we report findings from postmortem brain snRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq analyses of mood disorders. This is the first study to investigate shared and distinct molecular pathophysiology in BD and MDD by focusing on cell-type-specific gene expression variations, as well as alterations in cell-cell interactions and gene co-expression networks. We not only identified gene expression changes in neurons and glial cells in BD and MDD as well as downregulation of biological processes related to translation and cellular respiration in neurons but also discovered the dysfunction of GABAergic signaling pathways sent from inhibitory neurons. In this study, downregulation of translation in neurons was observed in both BD and MDD. Abnormalities in translation within the brain have been reported to be involved in various neuropsychiatric disorders [41]. Since neurons are vulnerable to defects in proteostasis [42], dysfunction in translation may have a major impact on brain function.

Furthermore, we identified mitochondrial dysfunction in neurons within the mood disorders, particularly BD. This is consistent with findings from numerous observational and postmortem studies [43, 44]. Mitochondrial dysfunction in BD has been hypothesized as the etiology of BD since 2000 [45], though it remains unclear whether it is a cause or a consequence. In addition, previous studies have also demonstrated that mitochondria are responsible for the differences between BD and MDD [46–48]. Our results suggested that the module related to cellular respiration (i.e., M1), which is downregulated in BD, is regulated by many TFs from other modules within the TF regulatory network. Therefore, we hypothesize that mitochondrial dysfunction in neurons in mood disorders, particularly BD, is more likely to be a consequence of external stimuli. Protecting the mitochondria of neurons from harmful stimuli may offer a potential therapeutic approach for mood disorders.

Changes in excitatory and inhibitory activities in the brain, and consequently alterations in the excitation-inhibition balance (E/I balance), have been reported as a critical factor contributing to the pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders in animal stress models, human postmortem studies as well as magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) studies [49, 50]. Consistent with the increased excitation-inhibition ratio (E/I ratio) in BD and MDD demonstrated in previous studies [50–53], our study highlighted the dysfunction of parvalbumin interneurons and hyperexcitability of excitatory neurons in BD and MDD, suggesting a disturbed E/I balance. Parvalbumin interneurons, particularly parvalbumin-positive basket cells, are known for their crucial role in regulating the E/I balance [39]. Since parvalbumin interneurons are fast-spiking inhibitory neurons with high energy consumption [54], mitochondrial dysfunction may cause reduced activity in these neurons. Furthermore, prior studies based on induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) also revealed that iPSC-derived neurons from BD or MDD patients exhibit hyperexcitability [55–58]. Therefore, there may be a potential to treat mood disorders by suppressing excitatory neural activity or enhancing inhibitory neural activity.

We identified two important TFs, PKNOX2 and BHLHE40, that contribute to mood disorders. Interestingly, GWAS has indicated that PKNOX2 is associated with alcohol dependence and SCZ [59–61]. BHLHE40 (also known as DEC1 or BHLHB2) is a circadian rhythm gene [62] and has been reported to be associated with BD [63]. Furthermore, Bhlhe40 KO mice exhibited hyperexcitability of hippocampal neurons and decreased hippocampal synaptic plasticity [64], suggesting its important role in regulating neural function. Thus, novel therapeutic approaches targeting these TFs may be promising.

We analyzed two additional datasets to confirm the main findings from our analyses of the OFC dataset. While the universality of disease-related changes across cortical regions has been demonstrated, attention should also be paid to the differences among distinct brain regions. For example, gene expression patterns may vary greatly across different cortical regions, and each region may also be involved in different diseases in different ways [65, 66].

This study also has limitations. First, while snRNA-seq has enabled us to examine cell-type-specific gene expression characteristics, since only genes expressed in the isolated nuclei were detected, specific gene expression patterns in axons, dendrites, synapses, or neuron-glial junctions may be missed [67, 68]. Moreover, although there are many cells, the number of donors is relatively small, particularly when comparing BD with MDD. Furthermore, we applied strict filtering during data preprocessing to minimize false positive results in cell-cell communication and co-expression network analyses, but this led to a limited number of nuclei available for analysis, resulting in a large variation in the number of cells among donors for some cell types. Finally, for postmortem brain gene expression studies, while the potential effects of drug use should be considered [69], such information is not available.

In conclusion, we identified shared and disease-specific abnormalities through single-nucleus and bulk gene expression analyses of BD and MDD, highlighting abnormalities in neuronal and non-neuronal cells involved in the pathobiology of both mood disorders. Multiple lines of evidence converge on the involvement of mitochondrial dysfunction, protein translation impairment, and disruption of the excitation-inhibition balance, presenting promising avenues for developing therapies based on the underlying molecular mechanisms of the diseases.
