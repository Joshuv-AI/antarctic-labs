Parietal and prefrontal transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) for mild cognitive impairment (MCI): A pilot randomized clinical trial
Authors: Joy L. Taylor, Beatriz Hernandez, Laura C. Lazzeroni, M.Windy McNerney, Alesha Heath, J.Wesson Ashford, Jauhtai (Joseph) Cheng, Steven Chao, Priyanka Bhatt, Michael Iv, Fatemeh S. Mojabi, Jerome A. Yesavage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Even though studies of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) have demonstrated improvements in cognitive function, we know little about what site of stimulation would be ideal for improving memory.Methods:We investigated two sites: (1) the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC); and (2) the lateral parietal cortex (LPC), a more novel site. Forty amnestic MCI participants were randomized to one of three rTMS groups: (1) active DLPFC; (2) active LPC; and (3) sham. After completing 20 sessions of MRI-neuronavigated, bilateral rTMS, the participants were followed for 6 months. The primary outcome/endpoint was the California Verbal Learning Test-II (CVLT-II) list learning score assessed at 1-week post intervention.Results:Thirty-seven of 40 randomized participants provided primary endpoint data. Compared with the sham group, the active LPC group performed significantly higher on CVLT-II list learning (p = .048; standardized mean difference (SMD) = 0.86). No significant differences in CVLT-II list learning were detected between the active DLPFC and sham groups (p = .629; SMD = 0.20). Exploratory analyses revealed that the active LPC group performed better than sham on CVLT-II long-delay recall at the 1-week-post primary endpoint (p = .004; SMD = 1.30), and higher on CVLT-II semantic clustering at the 3-month follow-up (p = .015, SMD = 1.16).Conclusion:The parietal cortex is a promising TMS target for improving memory function in MCI. More definitive trials are needed to compare the efficacy of parietal and DLPFC TMS for treatment of memory and cognitive impairment.

Introduction

In this study we tested the short-term efficacy of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) as a treatment for amnestic Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI). MCI is an important public health concern due to its prevalence, risk of progression to dementia, and lack of well-accepted treatments [1-3]. Even when a safe, effective pharmacotherapy is widely available, it is likely that some MCI patients will not meet the prescription guidelines. Others may wish to avoid adverse treatment effects such as amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA). In this context, effective alternative therapies and adjunctive treatments for MCI are greatly needed. rTMS is a non-invasive method for stimulating the brain’s neurocircuitry [4]. rTMS has shown promise for improving verbal episodic memory and global cognitive function in older adults who have MCI [5-10]. Meta-analyses of the effects of rTMS have reported significant effects for improvement of memory [11,12] and cognitive function in patients with MCI [11-13], and in patients with AD dementia [14]. rTMS is delivered by generating a pulsating magnetic field near the scalp. The small electrical currents that are induced can pass through the skull and stimulate cortical areas involved in memory, mood, attention, and language, as well as lead to downstream effects over time in connected regions [15]. In this way, rTMS can modify the functional connectivity of large-scale brain networks [16] such as the default network [17,18], the dorsal attention network [19], and the salience network [20].

Presently, it is not clear what cortical site and brain network target would be ideal for improving memory and other cognitive abilities of older adults with MCI or AD dementia [21]. rTMS that targets a cortical node of the default network has a compelling rationale for treatment of early-stage AD [9] and clinically defined amnestic MCI [22]. In individuals with very early AD, accumulation of β-amyloid and loss of functional connectivity have been found to preferentially impact the default network [23,24]. A meta-analysis of 21 resting-state fMRI studies found that regions within the default network–including the posterior cingulate, precuneus, and right angular gyrus–were consistently lower in activity in clinically defined amnestic MCI compared to unimpaired samples [22].

Two rTMS AD trials used state-of-the-art personalized fMRI-navigated methods to stimulate a left parietal region of the default network. Both studies observed increased functional connectivity within the default network following rTMS treatment [25,26]. By contrast, the majority of rTMS trials for AD dementia and MCI targeted the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) [11,14,27-29]. These DLPFC trials did not use personalized navigation methods to target a particular network, although two recent studies noted that the DLPFC is a core region of the lateral frontoparietal cognitive control network [28,30]. Critically, there is evidence that the DLPFC and surrounding lateral prefrontal cortex is functionally heterogenous. Multiple networks–including the default, dorsal attention, and salience networks–show evidence of activity within the prefrontal cortex [31-33]. The DLPFC is the recommended TMS site used in the treatment of depression [34]. It is not a given that the DLPFC is the ideal stimulation site for treatment of MCI and AD because of differences in abnormalities between major depressive disorder and the neuropathology of AD.

In terms of cognitive efficacy, both prefrontal and parietal rTMS have shown encouraging results for improving cognitive function in the short term [11,13,14,29], and for slowing progression of cognitive decline over longer time periods [28,35]. There is not yet enough evidence to recommend targeting a particular TMS site (or set of sites) for treatment of MCI-AD. In addition to a paucity of evidence, past differences in treatment duration, targeting methods, and outcomes make it challenging to compare the efficacy of prefrontal and parietal rTMS as well as therapeutic mechanisms.

In this pilot sham-controlled, double-blind, randomized clinical trial, we investigated two sites of active 10 Hz rTMS treatment: the DLPFC; and the lateral parietal cortex (LPC) near the angular gyrus. All participants received 20 sessions of intervention–in line with preliminary recommendations to administer at least 20 sessions [11,14] or a total of 39,000 total pulses [36]–for treatment of cognitive impairment in AD and MCI. For both sites, we employed the same methodology for coil navigation. Specifically, each participant’s T1-weighted MRI was used to navigate the TMS coil to a pre-selected Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) coordinate. The MNI coordinates of the two sites were selected using functional connectivity maps computed from large-sample resting-state fMRI data, e.g. Weigand et al. [37]. The DLPFC coordinate has been previously identified as a site for efficacious TMS treatment of depression [38]. Our motivation for using a depression-centric approach stems from the fact that most TMS trials for MCI and mild AD employed coil-placement methods that originally developed for TMS treatment of depression [11,29]. Predictive models of TMS indicate that this particular DLPFC MNI coordinate is likely to target the salience/ventral attention network and secondarily the lateral frontoparietal network [39,40]. The MNI coordinate we chose for the LPC site was intended to stimulate the same cortical area that has been shown in previous rTMS research to improve memory and increase functional connectivity within the posterior default network, particularly between the hippocampus and precuneus [25,41,42]. Active and sham interventions were delivered to the left and right hemispheres. The decision to use bilateral stimulation was based on the only published meta-analysis of rTMS-AD trials at the time [43], which reported that rTMS was significantly effective in improving cognitive function when stimulation was bilateral or to the right hemisphere.

As described in the published protocol [44], our primary aim was to test the hypothesis that MCI participants receiving active DLPFC or active LPC rTMS would show more short-term improvement in memory than the sham-control group. The primary outcome and endpoint was the California Verbal Learning Test, Second edition (CVLT-II) [45] List Learning score, assessed 1 week after intervention. We selected the CVLT-II because of its high retest reliability, sensitivity for detecting mild memory impairment [46], and potential utility for detecting differential effects of rTMS on memory processes in relation to the site of stimulation (described in Section 2.4 “Outcome measures”). The study was designed to meet two secondary aims: a) examine effects of rTMS on secondary behavioral outcomes and biomarkers of response in relation to stimulation site; and b) assess the durability of rTMS effects on the behavioral outcomes over a 6-month follow-up period, which is longer than assessed in most of the previous rTMS studies for MCI or AD [29]. This is the first study to our knowledge that investigated two popular TMS sites within the same study–while holding constant the intervention procedures, participant selection criteria, and outcomes.

Materials and methods

Design

As described in the published protocol [44], the design of this pilot clinical study was a phase II randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled, three parallel-arm design. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of three arms: 1) active DLPFC rTMS; 2) active LPC rTMS; or 3) sham. Within the sham group, participants were randomly assigned to receive either sham stimulation applied to the DLPFC or sham stimulation applied to the LPC. Thus, each participant had a 66% probability of receiving active TMS. (Note: We anticipated that potential study participants would be more willing to enroll in a placebo-controlled trial when the chance of being assigned to active treatment was greater than 50%. Our motivation for the current design was to pursue the trial’s aims more economically, compared to the time and costs of enrolling and testing twice the number of sham participants.) The study was reviewed and approved by the Stanford University IRB and preregistered at www.ClinicalTrials.gov under identifier NCT03331796. A CONSORT checklist is included in Table S1 of the Supplementary material.

Participants

The target population was defined to be patients with single- or multi-domain amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI), ages 55–90 years inclusive [44]. The key medical criteria for study eligibility were: safe to undergo MRI, no history of epilepsy or repetitive seizures, no history of a medical condition or current use of medications/substances that would increase the risk of a seizure, and good general health with no diseases expected to interfere with the study. Detailed eligibility criteria and accepted medications appear in the published protocol [44].

For characterization of aMCI, we used the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI-3) classification criteria [47], which include: subjective memory concern, below normal memory function documented by a score below the education-adjusted cutoffs on the Logical Memory II subscale (Delayed Paragraph Recall, Story A) from the Wechsler Memory Scale–Revised [48], a Mini-Mental State Exam score between 24 and 30 inclusive [49]; global Clinical Dementia Rating of 0.5 [50], Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) total score <6/15 [51], Modified Hachinski Ischemic score l≤4 [52]; an available study partner, and no diagnosis of dementia made by the study physician during the screening process.

Participants were enrolled at the Stanford/VA Aging Clinical Research Center over a 4-year period, which encompassed a hiatus of 13 months during the COVID-19 pandemic. >170 prospective participants were pre-screened by telephone; 69 participants provided written consent and were further assessed for eligibility. A total of 40 participants were randomized to intervention. The final participant visit occurred on March 28, 2023. Enrollment and randomization are depicted in the CONSORT diagram of Fig. 1.

Trial procedures and intervention

After written informed consent was obtained, participants under-went screening to determine eligibility. Eligible participants completed a baseline visit that included T1-weighted MRI and resting-state fMRI. The database manager randomized the participant to one of the three intervention arms. Efficacy and safety data were collected at baseline, 1-week post-intervention, and at 3- and 6-month follow-up visits. Plasma levels of brain-derived neurotropic factor (BDNF) were measured at the beginning and end of intervention as a biomarker of response to rTMS. Bias in data collection was minimized through permuted block randomization with concealed allocation and blinding of participants, TMS operators and outcome assessors, and the use of standardized intervention and assessment protocols. Primary and secondary outcomes and outcome derivation rules were prespecified in the study protocol. Data management procedures included range and consistency checks, duplicate detection, and double data entry for primary and secondary outcome measures with discrepancy resolution against source documentation. Further details are described in the published protocol [44].

During each of 20 sessions, 10 Hz rTMS was delivered bilaterally (2000 pulses to the right side, followed by 2000 pulses to the left) [44]. The pulses were delivered at 10 Hz using a pattern of a 4 s train duration (40 pulses), followed by 11 s of no stimulation, repeated over a period of 25 min. The target intensity was 120% of resting motor threshold (RMT) and specific to each hemisphere. We used each participant’s T1-weighted MRI in conjunction with a Localite TMS navigation system to position the coil over the target area. Participants in the active DLPFC arm received stimulation targeted to MNI coordinate −38, 44, 26 ([38], p. 598) and the corresponding MNI +38, 44, 26 on the right. Participants in the active LPC arm received stimulation targeted to MNI −47, −68, 36 ([42], Supplementary Material p.3) and to the corresponding right MNI coordinate +47, −68, 36. Participants in the sham arm were assigned to either sham DLPFC or sham LPC rTMS. The basis for selecting the left MNI coordinates is presented above in the Introduction. rTMS was carried out using a MagVenture X100 magnetic biphasic stimulator and an unmarked active-placebo 70-mm diameter figure-of-eight coil (Cool-B65 A/P) [44]. Throughout the rTMS session, the position and angle of the coil were constantly monitored using the navigation system coupled with an infrared camera. During the first two sessions, if a participant experienced difficulty tolerating the target intensity of 120% RMT, the operator was permitted to ramp up the intensity from 80% to 120% RMT to provide the participant time to adapt. The intervention was administered on weekdays during a period of 2 to 4 weeks, depending on scheduling constraints. Participants were permitted to undergo a morning session and an afternoon session (with a minimum one-hour interval between sessions) to reduce the number of trips to the research center. Each participant received a total of 80,000 pulses. Participants were not engaged in any other cognitive-improvement interventions during the study.

Details of blinding the TMS operators, participants, study coordinator/outcomes assessor, and other clinical staff are described in the published protocol [44]. Briefly, the database manager assigned the participant a unique 6-digit number. When the TMS operator entered the assigned number at the control panel of the TMS system, the software enabled the unmarked coil and the stimulator to deliver the assigned treatment (active or sham stimulation) to that participant. The adequacy of the blinding procedure was assessed using a questionnaire completed by the participant and TMS operator. We note that although the participants and TMS operators were aware of the coil’s position, the study coordinator/outcomes assessor and other clinical staff were not aware of participants’ assigned TMS sites.

Outcome measures

Efficacy measures.

The primary outcome measure was the California Verbal Learning Test-Second Edition (CVLT-II) [45] total List Learning score, which measures the total number of words correctly recalled during the five learning trials of List A (range = 0 to 80 words; higher values represent a better outcome). Two secondary CVLT-II measures were selected: semantic clustering during the learning phase [53] and delayed free recall. These secondary measures are of interest because of the distinct roles that DLPFC and LPC-connected regions appear to have in successful memory encoding and delayed free recall [54,55]. Other secondary assessments were: the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; [56]); category fluency (boys’ and girls’ first names) [57]; a 42-item version of the Boston Naming Test (1986)[58]; Trail Making parts A and B [59], the Brief Visuospatial Memory Test–Revised (BVMT-R) [60]; the Rey-Osterrieth Complex and Modified Taylor Complex Figures (copy task only) [61,62]; the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) [51]; and the Everyday Cognition Scale (ECog) [63] . The participants’ study partners completed the Informant version of the ECog and the Functional Assessment Questionnaire [64]. With the exception of the Trail Making test, all cognitive measures had two or more alternate stimulus forms in order to reduce effects of practice [44].

Biomarkers of response.

Plasma levels of BDNF were measured twice: (1) on the first day of intervention and (2) on the last day of intervention. Details of the BDNF ELISA assay protocol are available online: Taylor et al. [44] supplementary “Additional file 4″. Of the 37 participants, 6 participants are missing BDNF data because blood draws were not performed during the COVID pandemic era. The group sizes for the analyses were: active LPC (n = 10); active DLPFC (n = 12); and sham (n = 9). Resting-state fMRI data were collected at baseline and 1-week after completing intervention for the planned aim of examining changes in functional connectivity within and between the default and salience networks in relation to the two TMS sites. The acquisition of fMRI data was halted by the COVID pandemic and a subsequent funding cut. The obtained fMRI dataset was comprised of 32 baseline scans and 19 post-intervention scans. Details of the acquisition and preprocessing methods are described in Taylor et al. [40].

Safety monitoring.

The TMS operator assessed adverse events (AEs) at each intervention session by administering a safety questionnaire and eliciting participants’ verbal reports. The study coordinator assessed AEs at other study visits. An external Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) monitored the safety data of all participants. In this paper, we report AEs for the 37 participants who completed the rTMS intervention. The other 3 randomized participants who discontinued the intervention did not report any AEs during their brief exposure to rTMS.

Statistical analysis

The target enrollment for this trial was 99 participants. This sample size was estimated to have 80% power to detect a moderately large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.80) of either rTMS treatment on the primary outcome at the primary endpoint of 1-week post-intervention [44]. Moderately large effect sizes of 1.0 and 0.80 were reported in an early meta-analysis of rTMS studies in AD [43] and an rTMS trial in MCI (which employed left DLPFC rTMS) [7]. Enrollment and in-person study visits were negatively affected by the COVID pandemic and a subsequent funding cut. Consequently, statistical power was lower than originally planned. Also, the post-intervention resting-state fMRI data were not analyzed because of the small dataset (N = 19) and lack of personnel.

Screening and baseline characteristics of the three treatment groups were compared using general linear modeling (SAS 9.4 Proc GLM) for continuous variables. For categorical variables, the Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, was used. Any statistically significant differences among the groups, despite randomization to treatment, are reported as F(2,34) or X2(2). Primary analysis of the short-term efficacy of rTMS: To compare the treatment groups at the primary 1-week post endpoint, we ran a regression model for CVLT-II list learning, adjusting for the baseline score of the measure [65]. Preplanned contrasts were made between active LPC vs sham and active DLPFC vs sham [44]. The sham group was comprised of all participants assigned to sham stimulation, regardless of coil site. We report the baseline-adjusted parameter estimate β, SE, and p values of the differences between: active LPC vs sham and active DLPFC vs sham. Multiple comparison correction was not applied as the two contrasts were pre-specified. Exploratory analyses: We evaluated the contrasts between active DLPFC vs active LPC; we also explored short-term effects of rTMS on the secondary behavioral measures and longer-term behavioral effects of rTMS [44]. To do this, we compared the three treatment groups using regression models that adjusted for the baseline score of the measure [65]. Separate regression models for each secondary measure and endpoint. Uncorrected p values are reported for these exploratory results. Primary and exploratory analyses were conducted as single, pre-specified runs per endpoint on the finalized dataset. All analyses were conducted using reproducible, pre-specified scripts in SAS 9.4.

Results

Sample characteristics, attrition, and adherence to treatment

Table 1 shows demographic, clinical, and medication characteristics of each treatment group at the screening visit. The mean GDS score of the sham group was significantly higher than that of the active LPC group (p = .031). There were no other significant differences between sham and active groups in screening characteristics (p’s ≥ 0.18). However, the participants who would be randomized to active LPC TMS scored significantly higher on Logical Memory-II story recall than those randomized to active DLPFC (p = .024). The sham group’s mean CVLT-II List Learning score at baseline was numerically lower than the active LPC group (p = .11) and numerically higher than the active DLPFC group (p = .40; see Table 2). The active LPC group’s mean CVLT-II List Learning score at baseline was significantly higher than that of the active DLPFC group (p = .013) despite randomization to treatment. There were no other statistically significant baseline differences in the secondary CVLT-II measures (i.e. semantic clustering and delayed free recall, p’s > 0.27) or other secondary cognitive measures (p’s > 0.10).

Of the 40 randomized participants, 37 completed the intervention and 3 did not. One active LPC participant and one sham participant were administratively discontinued because of pandemic-mandated cessation of face-to-face study visits. The third discontinuation was a sham participant who said she “felt nothing” and withdrew consent after the first session. All 37 participants who completed the intervention phase provided data for the primary endpoint assessed at 1-week post intervention. A total of 34 participants provided secondary endpoint data, of whom 31 have CVLT-II List Learning scores at the final 6-month follow-up visit. There were four reasons for not completing the follow-up phase of the trial: illness (one active DLPFC and one sham participant); lost to follow-up (one sham participant); termination of funding (one active LPC); and withdrawal of consent related to burden of testing (one active DLPFC). Participation at study endpoints is depicted in Fig. 1 (CONSORT diagram). The treatment groups did not significantly differ in terms of attrition rate, Fisher’s exact test p = 0.61.

All participants who completed the intervention phase met the protocol’s definition of adherence, i.e. completion of at least 18 of 20 rTMS sessions. The mean resting motor threshold (RMT) was similar across groups: 57.0 ± 9.3 in the active LPC group, 52.1 ± 12.5 in the active DLPFC group, and 55.4 ± 10.4 in the sham group (F(2,34) < 1, p = .514). The overall average intensity of stimulation that active LPC and active DLPFC participants received during intervention was similar: active LPC group: 117.6% ± 5.2% RMT; active DLPFC group: 114.0% ± 11.7% RMT; t = 1.15, p = .26). The procedures for blinding participants to active vs. sham rTMS treatment were sufficient, based on exit questionnaires: Participants who received sham rTMS were as likely to guess that the treatment received was “active (real) rTMS” as they were to guess “inactive (placebo) rTMS.” A minority of participants who received active rTMS correctly guessed they had received “active rTMS” (4 of 12 active LPC participants, 5 of 13 active DLPFC participants). The response choices were not significantly different across the three groups, Fisher’s exact test, p = .86, n = 36.

Primary aim: test the short-term efficacy of rTMS for improving memory

At 1-week post intervention, the active LPC group’s performance on the primary outcome–CVLT-II List Learning–was significantly better compared to sham: baseline-adjusted estimate of the difference between active LPC and sham groups = 6.9 words; SE = 3.3; p = .048; standardized mean difference (SMD) = 0.86. The list-learning scores of the active DLPFC participants were not significantly better than sham (baseline-adjusted estimate = 1.5 words; SE = 3.1; p = .629; SMD = 0.20). The exploratory contrast between active LPC and active DLPFC was not significant (baseline-adjusted estimate = 5.3 words; SE = 3.3; p = .117; SMD = 0.63). (Note: All of the above statistical comparisons between groups were adjusted for the CVLT-II List Learning score at baseline.) The results are shown in Fig. 2(A), and the upper rows of Table 2 and Table 3.

Exploratory results (Secondary aims: other effects of rTMS)

Short-term effects of rTMS

The effects of rTMS on CVLT-II delayed recall and semantic clustering were of particular interest because the LPC and DLPFC play distinct roles in memory encoding and delayed free recall [44,54,55]. At 1-week post-intervention, the active LPC group’s delayed recall scores were higher than the sham group (baseline-adjusted estimate of the difference between active LPC and sham groups = 3.2 words; SE = 1.0; p = .004; SMD = 1.30). The active DLPFC group’s delayed recalled scores were not significantly different than sham (baseline-adjusted estimate = 1.0 word; SE = 1.0; p = .33; SMD = 0.40). The exploratory contrast between active LPC and active DLPFC revealed a possible superiority of LPC TMS (baseline-adjusted estimate of the difference between the two active groups = 2.2 words; SE = 1.0; p = .033; SMD = 0.88). There were no significant differences between groups in the tendency to use a semantic clustering strategy during learning, p’s > 0.28. All statistical comparisons between groups were adjusted for baseline scores on the dependent variable of interest. The results are shown in Fig. 2(B) and Tables 2 and 3.

No significant group differences were observed in the mood, ECog everyday function, executive function, or other cognitive measures at 1-week post-treatment (all p’s > 0.05). The results of general linear regression models for each outcome, adjusted for baseline scores on the dependent variable of interest, are listed in Table 3. The group means (SDs) for the cognitive, mood, and functional measures are shown in Table 2. No significant changes in plasma levels of BDNF were detected (Treatment Group × Time interaction, p = 0.77).

Durability of rTMS effects

At the 3-month follow-up, the active LPC group’s mean CVLT-II List Learning score was numerically higher compared to sham (p = .205; SMD = 0.58), and higher compared to active DLPFC (p = .041; SMD = 0.89), adjusted for the CVLT-II list learning score at baseline. No significant differences in list learning were detected between active DLPFC and sham, p = .45. At the 6-month post-treatment, no significant group differences in list learning performance were observed, p’s ≥ 0.24.

Regarding CVLT-II delayed recall, no significant group differences were observed at 3-months or 6-months post-treatment, p’s ≥ 0.19. List learning and delayed recall performance for the 34 followed participants, from baseline to final follow-up, can be seen in Supplementary Figure S1 A-B. Interestingly, participants in the active LPC group showed more semantic clustering on the CVLT-II while learning List A, compared to the sham group at the 3-month follow-up visit (p = .015, SMD = 1.16). For other secondary measures, no significant group differences were observed at either follow-up, p’s > 0.05. The results of regression models for these outcomes, adjusted for baseline, can be found in Supplementary Table S2. Group means (SDs) are provided in Tables S3 and S4.

Adverse events

During the intervention phase, 15 adverse effects (AEs) were reported by 13 of the 37 participants (35%). The rate of AEs was numerically higher among active TMS participants (LPC: 5 of 12 or 42%; DLPFC: 5 of 14 or 36%) than sham participants (3 of 11 or 27%). The most frequently occurring AE was application site discomfort, which was reported by 5 participants (active LPC: 2 of 12 or 16.7%; active DLPFC: 3 of 14 or 21.4%; sham: 0%). Headaches were the next most frequently occurring AE, reported by 2 active LPC participants (2 of 12 or 16.7%). Two-thirds of the AEs were judged to be definitely related or possibly related to the TMS device. Most AEs were well tolerated; 80% were of mild severity; the remainder were moderate in severity. None of the events led to a discontinuation of rTMS. These AEs are summarized in Table S5.

Discussion

In this clinical trial we investigated two distinct cortical sites for stimulation: (1) the DLPFC, which has been well studied in major depressive disorder [34]; and (2) the lateral parietal cortex (LPC), a more novel site with functional connectivity to AD-vulnerable limbic regions such as the hippocampus [17,18,25,26]. The group sizes were modest; yet compared to the sham group, the active LPC group showed a beneficial effect of rTMS on CVLT-II list learning and on delayed free recall at the 1-week-post primary endpoint. These results should be interpreted with caution, in view of the borderline p value for the primary outcome.

As expected, we did not observe a faciliatory effect of rTMS on CVLT-II semantic clustering in the active LPC group at 1-week post-treatment. Semantic clustering is a memory encoding and retrieval strategy [66] that is largely supported by the frontal lobes, especially the DLPFC [54, 55]. Delayed recall on the other hand is a hallmark of hippocampal-dependent memory [54]. The hippocampal-cortical network includes regions of the LPC [17,25,18]. Importantly, rTMS targeted to the LPC contributed to superior delayed recall performance compared to sham at 1-week post-treatment. We also note that the average increase in the delayed recall scores of the active LPC group was numerically small relative to baseline, and that the sham group’s scores tended to decrease (Fig. 2B). A similar pattern was observed in our center’s previous study of DLPFC rTMS, which also employed the CVLT-II as the primary outcome [5]. In contrast, rTMS studies that involved participants with MCI or prodromal AD but used the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) reported significantly improved scores in the active rTMS groups, and no changes in sham groups [6,9]. One possible explanation is the use of multiple CVLT-II forms across study visits, in contrast to repetition of a single RAVLT word list. Or, it may be due to variability in the results of studies with modest sample sizes. In the active DLPFC group, we did not detect a statistically significant rTMS effect on CVLT-II word-list learning, delayed free recall, or semantic clustering compared to the sham group. Two meta-analyses of the effects of rTMS in MCI reported significant effect sizes for improvement of memory in the range of 0.75 [11,12]. Subsequently, Cheng et al. [5] found no significant effects of rTMS on word-list recall when assessed immediately after the last rTMS session yet a significant effect at 4-months post-intervention. Taken together, inadequate sample size is likely the chief reason we did not detect a statistically significant effect of DLPFC rTMS on word-list recall.

A meta-analysis of sham-controlled rTMS trials in MCI found that rTMS can improve global cognitive function (SMD = 0.83) [12]. More recently, a rigorous RCT in MCI of high-definition transcranial direct current stimulation (HD-tDCS) targeting the left DLPFC did not detect a significant effect of HD-tDCS on global cognition, despite being powered to detect a small effect size of at least 0.22 [67]. Chu et al. [67] discussed five reasons for the negative result including: an intervention of only 10 sessions; and the heterogeneity of MCI which may in turn lead to substantial variability in response to treatment. In the present study involving participants with aMCI, we did not detect rTMS-related differences in global cognitive function. Statistical power was low in our study for secondary outcomes such as the MoCA which include visual stimuli. The COVID-19 pandemic imposed constraints on administering these types of assessments by telephone, as well as a hiatus in participant enrollment. No significant group differences were observed at 1-week post-treatment in the assessments of executive function, visual memory, or language. A meta-analysis of the specific cognitive effects of multi-session rTMS in MCI found little evidence for improvement in executive function (SMD < 0.20, based on 3 studies) [11]. Other meta-analyses, focused on AD, did not find substantial evidence for effects on executive function [14] or language skills [29]. As more data for individual cognitive domains accrues, these effect-size estimates could change.

There were no measurable effects of rTMS on mood ratings. This was anticipated because inclusion criteria restricted the range of GDS scores to 5 or less. In the current literature on rTMS for MCI or AD, there is no compelling evidence for mood improvement [11,14,29,68]. Even so, because DLPFC rTMS is FDA-cleared for treatment of major depressive disorder, cognitively impaired older adults with comorbid depression may also benefit from rTMS. A handful of AD clinical trials of DLPFC rTMS have included participants with symptoms of comorbid depression, apathy, or other behavioral disturbances. All of these trials reported encouraging results [68-72]. More studies should be conducted to learn how much benefit cognitively impaired older adults with psychiatric symptoms gain when rTMS is an add-on treatment. Finally, there were no measurable effects of rTMS on plasma levels of BDNF. Other MCI clinical trials failed to detect an effect of treatment on BDNF plasma levels [5,73], which perhaps may be a reflection of declining circulating BDNF with age, particularly in the context of hippocampal atrophy and mild memory loss [74].

Durability of TMS treatment

Evidence concerning the durability of TMS effects on cognition comes primarily from trials that targeted the DLPFC and the AD dementia population. The meta-analyses that analyzed these types of trials reported that TMS is likely to improve global cognitive function for at least 6 weeks [14,29]. Little is known about the durability of parietal TMS. To date, three randomized controlled parietal trials in AD have been published in which the follow-up period ranged from 4 to 12 weeks. Jung et al. [25] were able to demonstrate significant improvements in global cognitive function and memory 4 weeks after completing 20 sessions of “personalized hippocampal network–targeted” parietal rTMS. The two trials with longer follow-up reported nonsignificant trends for improvement in the active group compared to sham [26,75].

In the present study, the active LPC group’s CVLT-II list-learning performance was no longer statistically better than sham 3 months after the end of treatment. Interestingly, the active LPC group evidenced more semantic clustering during the CVLT’s five learning trials, compared to sham. While the CVLT-II test has strengths for clinical use in terms of standardization and psychometric qualities, it only has two equivalent forms. We administered the Standard form at baseline and again at the 3-month follow-up (and the Alternative form at the 1-week post-intervention and 6-month visits). We speculate that the CVLT-II semantic clustering result may be attributed to a “savings” effect in which there is familiarity with the semantic categories of the words to be learned. Because savings is a “very pure measure of memory” [76], future researchers could use savings as an indicator of memory retention to measure in a more refined manner how long TMS effects last.

Toward optimization of rTMS for treatment of MCI and AD

There is not yet enough evidence to recommend targeting a particular cortical site (or set of sites) for TMS treatment of MCI and AD. Two meta-analyses that explored optimization of TMS for improving global cognitive function in patients with AD dementia [14,36] understandably did not compare the efficacy at different cortical targets because at that time there were only a few studies of parietal-targeted TMS. Directly comparable evidence is needed, including equivalent treatment durations, outcomes and follow-up intervals, in order to evaluate effects of TMS at different targets. Moreover, it is worth noting that–following multiple sessions of rTMS–outwardly distinct DLPFC and parietal targets may have similar effects downstream in the brain. Parietal TMS targets such as the precuneus and angular gyrus are well-recognized nodes of the default brain network. Yet, the default network has been mapped to areas within or near the DLPFC, along with three other large-scale networks [33,77]. Opitz et al. [39] mapped functional connectivity data to finite-element TMS models of the human head, and discovered that both the typical 5 cm scalp site and the EEG-based F3 site were located primarily within the default network. Several left DLPFC TMS trials have used the 5 cm, 5.5 cm, and F3 methods for positioning the TMS coil in studies to improve memory in patients with AD or MCI [6,7,30,71]. Changes in connectivity within the default network have been reported when the DLPFC was targeted by these means [6] as well as when the parietal lobe was targeted [25,26,78]. In contrast, the DLPFC brain MNI coordinate we selected is likely to target the salience/ventral attention network and secondarily the lateral frontoparietal network [39,40]. Two DLPFC TMS studies involving patients with AD dementia targeted the same MNI coordinate [28,79]. The sample sizes of the Wu et al. studies were nearly twice that of ours. When Wu et al. [79] assessed participants one day after receiving 14 days of accelerated intermittent theta-burst TMS (iTBS), memory and cognitive scores of the active TMS group were significantly improved compared to the sham TMS group. In contrast, Wu et al. [28] reported that when participants were assessed a week after completing 14 days of iTBS, their scores of were not significantly better than those of the control group. When the 14-day iTBS intervention was repeated every 3 months during the 49-week study, iTBS-treated patients showed stable MOCA scores and less hippocampal atrophy [28]. Wu and colleagues speculated that repeated DLFPC iTBS may improve the lateral frontoparietal network’s connectivity with other brain areas to slow hippocampal atrophy. Our study is limited by the lack of pre- and post-treatment fMRI data to examine connectivity changes in the hypothesized networks, small group sizes, and the presence of pre-treatment differences between the active LPC and DLPFC groups in Logical Memory and CVLT-II list learning scores despite randomization to treatment. More definitive TMS trials need to be conducted in which different, well-defined cortical targets are compared, and the time-course of cognitive change post-treatment is evaluated using mixed-effects models.

Likewise, there is not enough evidence to recommend bilateral vs. single-site TMS for treatment of cognitive impairment. Our decision to deliver TMS bilaterally was based on an early meta-analysis [43]. These authors also reported that the effect size of left DLPFC stimulation (SMD = 0.52) for cognitive improvement was not statistically significant and half that of bilateral DLPFC stimulation. Liao and colleagues acknowledged that the small 7-study dataset limited the robustness of their findings. A subsequent meta-analysis of AD TMS studies reported that “stimulation of left DLPFC (SMD = 0.84, P = .03) was more effective than multi-point stimulation (SMD = 0.30, P = .05)”, defined as one to three bilateral sites [29]. Li et al. [14], on the other hand, reported that single and multiple target stimulation were equally efficacious for patients with AD (SMDs = 0.43, Ps ≤ 0.02). Two MCI meta-analyses reported on the efficacy of TMS for overall cognitive improvement, sub-grouped as bilateral DLPFC/PFC vs. left DLPFC stimulation [11]; and bilateral DLPFC vs. stimulation of any single target [12]. The effect sizes of all sub-groups were statistically significant, with the SMDs of bilateral DLPFC TMS being numerically larger than single-site TMS in both meta-analyses. In contrast to bilateral DLPFC TMS, bilateral parietal TMS, or single-site DLPFC or parietal TMS, Zhang et al. [80] described a novel “dual-targeted rTMS” protocol to target left DLPFC and medial parietal cortex. In an initial study involving 15 MCI participants, EEG-based methods were used to target the left DLPFC at F3 and the left precuneus daily for 20 sessions of 10 Hz rTMS [80]. Scores on the MoCA, Chinese AVLT, and several other cognitive tests improved on average from pre- to post-rTMS. In addition, the magnitude of MoCA change correlated with increased functional connectivity between the right posterior cingulate of the DMN and the dorsal caudate. This dual-targeted protocol could be an effective, behaviorally comprehensive rTMS treatment if confirmed in a randomized, sham-controlled trial.

Limitations and conclusion

This is the first randomized sham-controlled, three-arm trial to our knowledge that investigated two popular TMS sites–while holding the stimulation protocol, subject selection criteria, and timing of cognitive assessments constant. The delivery of the intervention was rigorous: At each session, placement of the TMS coil was guided by neuronavigation using the participant’s individual MRI and monitored during treatment sessions. A single “active-placebo” coil and other masking procedures were used to double-blind the participant and research staff. The main limitation of this study is the relatively small number of participants in the three treatment arms. AD diagnostic biomarkers would have been useful in characterizing the study participants. Functional MRI or EEG-derived measures of network connectivity as well as amyloid-β and tau biomarkers would have been valuable toward elucidating therapeutic mechanisms of TMS.

In conclusion, this novel three-arm trial provides evidence that rTMS targeted to the LPC may improve memory function of patients with MCI. The results of this study provide additional momentum for clinical development of TMS as a non-pharmacological intervention and adjunctive therapy for treatment of memory impairment.
