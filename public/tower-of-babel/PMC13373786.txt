Sex-related differences in histopathology and hormonal associations in ascending aortic aneurysms
Authors: Tamara C Borsboom, Maximiliaan L Notenboom, Lambertus J Wisse, Kevin M Veen, Jonathan R G Etnel, Jolien W Roos-Hesselink, Johanna J M Takkenberg, Marie José T H Goumans, Marco C DeRuiter, Magnus Bäck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsSex-related differences in ascending aorta aneurysm (AscAA) pathology and the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In this study, sex-specific patterns of aortic media degeneration (MD) in idiopathic AscAA were investigated.Methods and ResultsAortic tissue and blood samples were obtained from 32 patients undergoing elective AscAA resection (mean age 65.6 years, 25% women). Two donor aortas served as healthy controls. Tissues were evaluated using a modified consensus scoring system assessing elastin integrity, mucoid extracellular matrix accumulation (MEMA), smooth muscle cell organization, and collagen remodelling. Immunohistochemistry was used to quantify extracellular matrix and remodelling markers. Outcome parameters included aortic diameter, immunohistochemical profiling, MD scores, and sex hormone profiling. Absolute ascending aortic diameters did not differ between sexes. Women exhibited significantly more advanced and diffuse MD across all sampled locations, with higher total MD scores before and after adjustment for aortic size. This was attributed to increased elastin degradation, MEMA, and elevated local MMP2 expression, consistent with a degradative remodelling profile. In contrast, men displayed a more focal and inflammatory phenotype, characterized by increased MMP9, cKIT, and CD68 expression, and relatively thicker media. Circulating hormone levels were reduced in both sexes but were not directly associated with MD severity. However, female-specific decreases in DHEA and male-specific decreases in testosterone, AMH, and androstenedione were observed.ConclusionThis study reveals pronounced sex-specific differences in AscAA wall degeneration. Women demonstrate a structurally more vulnerable aortic wall at comparable dilatation, fuelling discussion for revision of female treatment plans.Keywords: Ascending aortic aneurysm, Medial wall degeneration, Male–female differences

Introduction

An ascending aortic aneurysm (AscAA) is an abnormal enlargement of the ascending aorta, defined by a diameter of ≥40 mm or ≥1.5 times the expected artery size, which is known to be influenced by age, sex, and body size.1–3 Mechanical, genetic, and cardiovascular factors contribute to the formation and progression of AscAA.4 Interestingly, many studies have shown pronounced sex-related differences in aortic size, AscAA growth rates, and outcomes.1–3,5–10 However, to date, those differences are not included in disease guideline recommendations. Typically, women with AscAA are older at diagnosis and have a higher risk of acute aortic dissection, whereas AscAAs are more common in men.6,8,9,11–15 In addition, a recent study showed that pregnant women have an increased risk of aortic dissection.16 This supports the hypothesis of sex hormones influencing vascular wall integrity and aortopathy.17

For decades, the pathophysiology of women with idiopathic AscAA has been linked to post-menopausal declining oestrogen levels.18 However, the results of oestrogen therapy on these women remain inconclusive, suggesting that supplying oestrogens does not have the same effect as endogenously produced oestrogens or that other hormones or a hormone imbalance may be involved in AscAA pathophysiology. Recently, a study demonstrated that elevated follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels play a role in osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease (CVD).19,20 Although the effects on aneurysm formation remained unexplained, this supports the need for broader investigation. In men, low testosterone and high luteinizing hormone (LH) levels are linked to increased abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) susceptibility.21 Although AAAs and AscAAs have distinct aetiologies, sex hormones are considered to influence both diseases.18 To date, studies examining sex differences, a comprehensive hormone panel, and tissue analysis to compare AscAA aetiology in both sexes are lacking.22–26

Media degeneration (MD) is a complex vascular degenerative process characterized by mucoid extracellular matrix accumulation (MEMA), elastin fibre degradation, loss of smooth muscle cells, disruption of the laminar unit, and collagen accumulation.22–26 These changes reduce aortic elasticity and contribute to vascular ageing. However, the severity of MD varies among individuals and is hypothesized to be more profound in post-menopausal women. Sex-related differences in MD patterns and associations between MD and sex-hormone levels, in men and women, remain poorly characterized.

Here, we analysed ascending aortic tissues and blood samples from patients undergoing elective resection of AscAAs. Parameters of interest included AscAA diameter, MD, vascular protein expression profiles, and circulating sex hormones, as well as their interrelations between men and women. We aimed to elucidate sex-related differences in pathophysiology.

Methods

Inclusion and hospitalization

Circular aortic tissue samples were obtained from patients at the time of elective aortic repair for an aortic aneurysm of the aortic root and/or the ascending aorta between February 2022 and December 2022. All patients undergoing replacement of the tubular ascending aorta were eligible for inclusion. In all patients, aortic dilation was assessed using computed tomography, and the maximum aortic diameter (MAD) was recorded. Patients with known genetic diseases such as Turner or Marfan Syndrome were excluded. Two patients had been taking spironolactone for at least 30 days before surgery.

Circulating hormones

All participants donated additional venous blood samples (BD Vacutainer® Barricor, Dickinson and Company, Plymouth, UK) during the standard-of-care preoperative blood tests. Samples were stored between 4 and 25°C until further use. An overview of the analysed circulating hormones is provided in Supplement S1. Age- and sex-matched normalization was performed using the standard range provided by the local determination guide for laboratory diagnostics at Erasmus University Medical Center.27

Aortic specimen collection

The aortic segment from the AscAA (1–2 cm) that was most significantly dilated or affected between the sino-tubular junction (STJ) and the brachiocephalic trunk, according to the surgeons operating, was excised. The tissue was marked at the proximal anterior side (preferably in the adventitia) with a monofilament suture (3-0 Monocryl, Ethicon LAB-0012859, Johnson & Johnson). After excision from the patient, the sample was kept in NaCl (4°C) and directly circumferentially cut and tailored to a ∼3 mm wide rim of tissue (see Supplement S2) by a single researcher (M.L.N.). Within 30 min after excision, the sample was hand-stored in formalin for at least 24 h before embedding in paraffin for histological analysis. The attending surgeon confirmed the specimen's exact location during surgery on an assessment form (see Supplement S2).

Immunostaining and histochemistry

The Paraffin-embedded circular aortic tissue samples were sectioned at 7.0 μm on a microtome and mounted consecutively onto coated glass slides (Klinipath KLINKP-SIL-3057). Histochemical and immunohistochemical stains were performed on all tissue sections for semi-quantitative assessment of medial degeneration. Before staining, samples were deparaffinized in xylene, followed by a series of graded ethanol washes to rehydrate them, then washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Standard protocols were followed for Mayer’s Haematoxylin-Eosin (HE), Weigert’s Resorcin-Fuchsin (RF), MOVATs pentachrome, and Sirius Red. For immunostaining, slides were subjected to temperature-controlled microwave (Tissuewave2, Thermo Scientific) antigen retrieval in citric acid buffer (10 mM citric acid, pH 6.0) for 12 min at 97°C. Sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies (see Supplement S3), diluted in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 and 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; A8022; Sigma–Aldrich). Between subsequent incubation steps, all slides were rinsed twice in PBS, followed by a single rinse in PBS-Tween 20. Primary antibodies were visualized by incubation with biotin labelled secondary antibodies (Biotin-rabbit, goat anti-rabbit (Vector Laboratories, BA-1000), Biotin-goat, horse anti-goat (Vector Laboratories, BA-9500), Biotin-mouse, horse anti-mouse (Vector Laboratories, BA-2000)), followed by a third incubation with the Vectastain HRP ABCkit (Vector Laboratories, PK-6100) and visualized with 400 mg/L 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, Sigma-Aldrich Chemie, D5637) in tris/maleate pH7.6 + 4 drops H2O2 and a nuclear counter stain for Mayer’s Haematoxylin. All sections were mounted in Entellan (Merck 107961) and air-dried for at least 24 h.

Microscopic analyses

All stained slides were scanned with the Panoramic 250 Flash III slide scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary), and images at an identical scale and exposure were exported using Histech Case Viewer (3DHISTECH Ltd.).

Histopathological analysis

Medial degeneration (MD) was evaluated based on the consensus statement on surgical aortic pathology from the Society for Cardiovascular Pathology and the Association for European Cardiovascular Pathology,24 with study-specific adaptations (see Supplement S4). Two researchers (T.C.B., L.J.W.) scored four equal areas of the histological slides to capture regional heterogeneity and were blinded to all patient data until the end of the analysis (see Supplement S5). The assessment of MD severity and the other components of the consensus statement were conducted independently. Consensus was reached after all slides had been evaluated through discussion.

Definition of medial degeneration

The definition of MD was adopted from Halushka and colleagues.24 It was defined as the presence of overall degenerative alterations/damage to the aortic media resulting from the sum of individual histopathologic degenerative lesions affecting the lamellar unit. The MD severity score was calculated as the sum of the severity of all individual components and ranged from 0 to 46.

Individual components

Individual histopathologic degenerative components analysed were mucoid extracellular matrix accumulation (MEMA), elastic fibre fragmentation/loss, elastic fibre thinning, elastic fibre disorganization, smooth muscle cell nuclei loss, smooth muscle cell disorganization, laminar medial collapse, and collagen alterations.24 When applicable, these were subclassified as interlamellar when pathological degeneration did not significantly extend beyond the lamellar unit, or translamellar when pathological degeneration altered the arrangement of lamellar units to varying degrees. Precise definitions, along with explanatory figures, are provided in Supplement S4.

The result for each component was then converted into a quantitative metric, i.e. a score reflecting its severity. In addition to the abovementioned components, we assessed media thickness (average of t researchers) and lamellar thickness (average of 10 measurements, 5 per researcher) using an eyepiece micrometre.

Quantification of DAB expression

Quantification of antibody staining was performed using QuPath (see Supplement S6).28 For each patient (each aorta), the same areas as for scoring MD were annotated and analysed in QuPath. For each antibody and staining session, a threshold was set to determine the percentage of the DAB-positive regions within the media. All data were exported to Excel and combined with other patient data.

Statistical analyses

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on the outcome, either an unpaired t-test or a Mann–Whitney U test was applied. When appropriate, multiple-comparison corrections were applied using a false discovery rate (FDR) of 5%. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies were ≤5. Patients were stratified by sex (male, female) and aortic valve phenotype (bicuspid, tricuspid).

Circulating sex hormone levels were normalized for each patient. Standard deviations (SDs) from the general Dutch population, matched for age and sex, were calculated as the difference between the upper and lower limits of the 95% confidence interval (CI). The lower limit of normal was identified as −2.0SD from the population mean, and the upper limit of normal was identified as +2.0SD from the population mean, assuming a normal (Gaussian) distribution. Hormone levels are reported as both absolute and normalized values, summarized as means ± SD and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Multiple-testing adjustments were not applied.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel Office 2021 (v16.93.1, Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA), the R Statistical Software (RStudio v2023.06.0 and R Software v4.2.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and GraphPad Prism (v10.1.2, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Specific tests used are described in the corresponding figure legends.

Study approval

This multicentre study was approved by the local medical ethics committee (MEC-2020-0085) of the Erasmus University Medical Center in Rotterdam, The Netherlands. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects before participation. Study protocols were present in every operating room and available to all nurses, surgeons, and researchers. A material transfer agreement (MTA) was established between Erasmus University Medical Center and Leiden University Medical Center, Leiden, The Netherlands, to facilitate the transfer of aortic tissue specimens between the hospitals.

Data storage

Clinical data were entered directly from the electronic patient files into Castor Electronic Data Capture (Castor, Claymont, DE, USA).29 All aortic samples were coded with the laboratory working numbers used by the Department of Anatomy and Embryology at the LUMC, thereby blinding researchers. Throughout the analysis, M.L.N. retained patient data at Erasmus MC. LUMC codes are linked with antibody usage, staining sessions, and scanned slides in Microsoft Office 2021 (v16.93.1; Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Local backup drives hold the scanned files from the 3D Histech scanner, which is linked to the QuPath analysis. QuPath is a cross-platform, open-source software for digital pathology image analysis and was used for immunohistochemistry quantification (QuPath v0.4.0).28

Results

Patient characteristics

Patient characteristics are presented in Table 1. Thirty-two patients (25% women) were included. No significant differences in age or body mass index (BMI) were observed between men and women. However, men had a larger body surface area (BSA). The absolute maximal aortic diameter (MAD) at surgery was comparable between sexes, while after indexing for height or BSA, women showed significantly greater dilatation. In most patients, the MAD was in the ascending aorta, whereas men more frequently had a root-dominant enlargement. Women had larger ascending aortic diameters, while men had larger aortic root diameters. Aortic stenosis (AS) was present in nearly half of the cohort and was more common in men. Furthermore, one-third of patients had a bicuspid aortic valve (BAV). Additional surgical details are provided in Supplement S7.

Patient characteristics for men and women

Severe media degeneration in the female aorta

The vascular wall of each patient was systematically evaluated (Figure 1A) using a modified histopathological scoring system (explained in Supplement S4). A recurrent pathological sequence of changes in the aortic wall was observed: Starting with interlamellar MEMA, followed by thinning and fragmentation of elastic lamellae, translamellar MEMA, and increased interlamellar collagen, accompanied by loss of smooth muscle cell (SMC) nuclei, SMC disorganization, and translamellar collagen deposition, indicative of advanced degeneration. The mean numeric outcome of the scoring system (MD score) was 23.2/46.0 (SD: 8.9), and women had significantly higher scores than men (men: 20.9, SD: 8.3, women: 30.0, SD: 7.5; P = 0.01), a significance which remained after correction for dilatation (P = 0.03); Figure 1B). MD severity was classified as mild in 29.6% (n = 38), moderate in 45.3% (n = 58), or severe in 25.0% (n = 32) of the samples (Table 2). Women showed comparable severity of degeneration across all analysed sections, whereas in men, one location often yielded a higher score. Severe degeneration was more frequent in women than in men (men: 17%, women: 50%; P = 0.0001, Table 2), driven by higher scores for individual components MEMA, elastin fibre (EF) fragmentation and thinning, and SMC disorganization (all P < 0.05) (Figure 1C). These changes are illustrated in Figure 1D, showing representative tissues with a score of 27.

Results of individual components of medial degeneration (MD)

Media degeneration of the female aorta is structurally different

To further assess sex-based differences in medial degeneration, individual histopathological features were analysed (Table 2, explanatory figures are included in Supplement S4). Women showed more advanced aortic wall remodelling, with a higher frequency of translamellar MEMA (men: 30%, women: 72%; P < 0.0001), more severe and frequently more extensive elastin fragmentation and/or loss (men: 21%, women: 69%; P < 0.0001, men: 22%, women: 81%; P < 0.0001), and greater elastin fibre thinning (P < 0.01). This was accompanied by reduced elastic lamellae thickness (men: 3.3 μm, women: 2.8 μm, P = 0.04, Figure 2A and B, Supplement S8). Furthermore, elastin fibre disorganization was more absent in males (26%) than in females (3%; P = 0.02). VSMC disorganization was more severe in women (P = 0.0006), although no sex differences were observed in VSMC nuclei loss. Laminar medial collapse was equally common between sexes (P = 0.62), but differed in pattern: more focal in women and more multifocal in men (P = 0.048). Translamellar collagen accumulation was more prevalent in women (men: 28%, women: 56%; P = 0.004), while intralamellar and translamellar collagen grade and distribution showed no differences.

Female aortas are more dilated, more degenerated, and have a thinner media

Considering differences in dilatation and media degeneration, we investigated whether media thickness varied by measuring absolute media thickness across all samples (Figure 2C). Overall, women exhibited a smaller absolute medial thickness than men. However, this difference was no longer significant after adjusting for height or BSA (Figure 2D). Adjustment for aortic dilation (Figure 2E) revealed thinner aortas in women, suggesting reduced wall thickness at comparable levels of dilatation. Thus, while media thickness does not differ between sexes when corrected for body size, women’s aortas remain thinner at similar degrees of aortic dilatation. A similar pattern was observed for the total MD score, with women exhibiting thinner aortas at the same degeneration score (Figure 2E).

Upregulated local protein expression of oestrogen receptors and ECM markers in aneurysm tissue

The differential expression of hormone receptors, enzymes, and markers of extracellular matrix turnover and homeostasis in aneurysmal lesions of the ascending aorta was examined with immunohistochemistry (Table 3). To determine whether local changes in specific markers were associated with MD, AscAA patient tissue was compared with age- and sex-matched healthy aortic tissue from heart transplantation donors (n = 1 per sex). Although the number of controls was limited, consistent differences were observed. ESR1 and ESR2 were upregulated in aneurysmatic patients of both sexes. CYP19, the enzyme responsible for local oestrogen synthesis, showed a 23-fold increase in men and a 2-fold increase in women with AscAA. The vascular regeneration marker cKIT was also elevated, particularly in men. In addition, pSMAD3 and CD68 expression levels were elevated in AscAA tissues, whereas SMA and collagen III expression were reduced.

Immunohistochemical comparison reveals divergent ECM remodelling pathways

Immunohistochemical data were compared across sexes to identify sex-specific differences in aortic pathomorphology (Table 3). Women showed higher absolute expression of MMP2 in the aortic media, whereas men showed higher absolute expression of cKIT and MMP9; no differences in expression localization were observed (Figure 3). Since women presented with more advanced medial degeneration at the time of surgery (Figure 1B), sex differences in marker expression could reflect disease stage rather than intrinsic differences. To address this, expression levels were normalized to MD scores, serving as a marker of disease progression (Table 3). After normalization, men showed higher expression of SMA, ESR2, MMP9, Collagen III, cKIT, and CD68, whereas MMP2 remained elevated in women. These findings indicate that, beyond differences in disease severity, distinct sex-specific expression profiles are associated with AscAA.

Circulating sex hormone profiles

To investigate whether histological differences were associated with hormone patterns, a panel of circulating sex hormones was measured in each patient (see Supplement S9). Blood samples from five patients were unavailable. Hormones were compared to the reference population (see Supplement S9–S10) and tested vs. 0 with a Wilcoxon-rank test. Female patients showed significantly lower FSH (P = 0.016), LH (P = 0.031), oestradiol (P = 0.016), and DHEA (P = 0.031). Male patients showed significantly lower FSH (P = 0.012), LH (P < 0.0001), AMH (P < 0.0001), oestradiol (P < 0.0001), testosterone (P < 0.0001), and androstenedione (P = 0.002, Figure 4A). To compare hormone levels across sexes, hormone levels were adjusted using these age- and sex-specific reference values. Interestingly, females with low DHEA exhibited significantly higher MD scores than males with low DHEA (women: 35.5; men: 17.5; P = 0.008) after stratification by DHEA levels (Figure 4C).

Figure 5B shows correlations between patient characteristics and media degeneration. Several associations were observed between MD markers and body size-related parameters. MAD correlated positively with MD score (r = 0.42, P = 0.02), with a stronger correlation after BSA correction (r = 0.62, P < 0.0001, Figure 6C). In contrast, no correlation was observed with root dilatation, whereas ascending dilatation correlated with the overall MD score (r = −0.49, P = 0.004, Figure 6D).

Figure 5C shows correlations between hormones and structural parameters of the aortic wall. Lamellar thickness correlated positively with oestradiol (r = 0.41, P = 0.03, Figure 6E) and LH (r = 0.39, P = 0.04, Figure 6F). In addition, lamellar thickness shows negative correlations with almost all individual MD markers and a strong negative correlation with overall MD score (r = −0.64, P < 0.0001, Figure 6G). Testosterone was negatively correlated with media thickness (r = −0.46, P = 0.01, Figure 6H), a relationship predominantly observed in males.

Figure 5D shows correlations between patient characteristics and immunohistochemical markers. Ascending dilatation was positively correlated with SMA (r = 0.40, P = 0.02) and MMP2 (r = 0.38, P = 0.04), and negatively correlated with Collagen 3 (r = −0.38, P = 0.03) and pSMAD3 (r = −0.46, P = 0.009). In women, MADasc correlated negatively with ESR2 (r = −0.79, P = 0.02).

Figure 5E shows correlations between hormones and immunohistochemical markers. FSH and LH correlated positively with MMP9 (r = 0.45, P = 0.02, and r = 0.42, P = 0.03, respectively) and pSMAD3 (r = 0.68, P < 0.0001, and r = 0.53, P = 0.005, respectively), correlations predominantly observed in males and markers elevated in the male population. In female patients, MMP2 correlated strongly with DHEA (r = 0.80, P = 0.03), AMH correlated with CD68 (r = −0.78, P = 0.04), and LH correlated negatively with ESR1 (r = −0.86, P = 0.01). In males, AMH correlated with MMP9 and CD68 (r = −0.57, P = 0.008, and r = −0.52, P = 0.026, respectively).

Figure 5F shows correlations between media degeneration markers and immunohistochemical markers. MMP2, a marker elevated in women with more advanced aortic degeneration, revealed positive correlations with MEMA (r = 0.47, P = 0.009), SMC disorganization (r = 0.51, P = 0.004), and MD score (r = 0.41, P = 0.03), implicating it in female-predominant degenerative aneurysm formation.

Collectively, no association was found between media degeneration (MD) and circulating hormones in the overall cohort or in sex-stratified analyses. Aortic dilatation, however, does correlate with multiple hormones, and these relationships are often sex-specific. Structural degeneration of the aortic wall is strongly linked to aortic size, especially after BSA correction. In women, increased aortic dilatation was associated with more advanced degeneration and thinner lamellae. The positive association between ascending aortic dilatation and FSH in women contrasted with the negative association observed in the overall cohort. Lamellar thickness correlated positively with oestradiol and LH, suggesting a potential protective role of female sex hormones, and showed a strong inverse relationship with MD score. Female participants also showed more elastin degradation, SMC disorganization, and matrix remodelling, with MMP2 emerging as a key marker of advanced degeneration and correlated with multiple MD markers, including a female-specific association with DHEA. In men, distinct hormone–dilatation relationships were observed, with FSH correlating negatively with ascending aortic and positively with aortic root dilatation. Additionally, testosterone correlated negatively with aortic root dilatation and media thickness, and low testosterone was linked to reduced collagen remodelling, suggesting sex-specific remodelling patterns. Hormone-IHC associations regarding inflammation are more pronounced in men, supporting a male-specific inflammatory signature.

Finally, a possible effect of the presence of a BAV in the male patients (female group was to small) on the aneurysm formation was excluded. No differences in patient characteristics, aortic diameter, corrected aortic diameter, hormone levels, or media analysis between BAV and TAV males were observed (see Supplement S11). Male patients with AS, however, showed a smaller MAD and height- or BSA-corrected MAD. Male patients with AS showed less deviant testosterone decrease (no AS = −2.05 ± 0.59, AS = −1.16 ± 0.93; P-value = 0.029). In addition, those patients had smaller media thickness (no AS = 1770 ± 195.0, AS = 1520 ± 227.7; P-value = 0.010). This emphasizes the effect of testosterone on media thickness in male patients (see Supplement S12).

Discussion

This study demonstrates sex-specific differences in medial degeneration (MD), extracellular matrix remodelling, and circulating sex hormone profiles in patients with idiopathic AscAA. Our key findings were:

Women consistently showed a more advanced degenerative phenotype at the time of surgery, despite comparable age, BMI, and absolute maximal aortic diameter.

Sex-specific ECM remodelling was observed in AscAA, with an upregulation of remodelling and inflammatory markers in males after normalization for disease severity, while females were characterized by elevated MMP2 expression, thinner media, and thinner elastin lamellae.

Sex-specific ECM remodelling was observed in AscAA, with an upregulation of remodelling and inflammatory markers in males after normalization for disease severity, while females were characterized by elevated MMP2 expression, thinner media, and thinner elastin lamellae.

Compared to the reference population, both sexes showed reduced pre-operative levels of sex hormones (FSH, LH, oestradiol), as well as reduced DHEA in females and reduced testosterone, AMH, and androstenedione in males. Within the cohort, DHEA levels were significantly lower in females, while testosterone levels were significantly lower in males.

Compared to the reference population, both sexes showed reduced pre-operative levels of sex hormones (FSH, LH, oestradiol), as well as reduced DHEA in females and reduced testosterone, AMH, and androstenedione in males. Within the cohort, DHEA levels were significantly lower in females, while testosterone levels were significantly lower in males.

No differences in medial degeneration or hormonal profiles were observed between BAV and TAV males. Similarly, no differences in medial degeneration were found between males with and without AS. However, males without AS had lower testosterone levels.

Aneurysmal aortas typically present elastin degradation, MEMA, and VSMC loss, often accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration.4,25,30 In our cohort, women demonstrated a predominantly degenerative phenotype while male patients displayed a more inflammatory phenotype at the time of surgery. These divergent pathways may explain the observed differences in tissue architecture, wall thickness, and the severity of degeneration.

Women undergoing AscAA surgery showed significantly more severe medial degeneration than men, even after adjusting for aortic size. They showed more extensive elastin fragmentation, translamellar MEMA, elastin thinning, and SMC disorganization, accompanied by increased MMP2 expression. This was reported to be involved in aneurysm expansion and aortic tissue repair.31 These changes were widespread across the aortic wall, in contrast to more localized degeneration observed in men. In addition, women also had thinner elastic lamellae and reduced media thickness. Other studies have shown that reduced lamellar thickness increases wall stress and predisposes to dissection,32 although none of our patients experienced dissection. Our findings support the hypothesis that women may reach a critical threshold of structural vulnerability earlier than men, even when absolute diameters appear similar.9 BSA correction resulted in further dilated aortas in female patients, a finding previously reported by Panfilov et al. (2023). They also demonstrated that differences in indexed dilatation did not affect overall survival.32 In our study, an increased ascending aortic diameter was associated with higher MD scores.

In contrast to the diffuse degradative phenotype in women, men showed a structurally more ‘functional’ aortic wall, reflected by thicker media and higher levels of SMA and Collagen III, despite ongoing localized inflammation. Elevated expression of MMP9, CD68, and SMA in male aortas may indicate tissue repair or enhanced ECM stabilization, particularly when combined with elevated cKIT, a vascular regeneration marker associated with reduced atherosclerotic burden, phenotype switching, and foam cell infiltration.33,34 Furthermore, MMP9 was shown to play a critical role in the progression of aneurysm rupture during rapid dilatation with heightened inflammatory activity.31 MMP9-regulating drugs in male rats with abdominal aortic aneurysms resulted in further growth prevention, suggesting it as a possible clinical therapy.35 These findings support the hypothesis that increased MMP2 levels represent a female-associated degenerative phenotype, whereas elevated MMP9 levels reflect a male-associated inflammatory phenotype. The observed increase of MMP2 in females aligns with the general pattern of increased aortic dilatation, given MMP2’s role in ECM degradation.36

Both sexes exhibited reduced circulating sex hormone levels, although the patterns differed. Women predominantly showed reduced gonadotropins, oestrogen, and DHEA, whereas men exhibited reduced gonadotropins, oestrogen, AMH, androstenedione, and testosterone. Notably, women with low DHEA had substantially higher medial degeneration scores than men with similarly low DHEA, suggesting that DHEA decrease may be a marker, or a contributor, to accelerated degeneration in women. However, besides the strong correlation with MMP2 expression in women, other correlations supporting that hypothesis are lacking. In addition, the correlations found between lamellar thickness and LH, oestradiol, and MAD/BSA could suggest a role for these hormones in female vascular pathology. Oestrogen, which declines after menopause, has previously been linked to vascular remodelling.37 Moreover, ESRβ expression has been associated with coronary calcification and atherosclerosis, independent of age,38 processes that are more visually apparent in the male aneurysm phenotype. The decrease in AMH in the male population was associated with elevated MMP9 and CD68, predisposing to inflammatory effects in the vascular wall in male patients.

Given the conflicting literature on AscAA formation in BAV patients, we investigated whether BAV was a confounding factor. Although 30% of our cohort had a BAV, only two women were included. Therefore, male patients with BAV and TAV were analysed, and no differences in medial wall degeneration were found (see Supplement S8). Coherent with observations of Mennander et al (2022), who applied a similar MD scoring approach.39 Additionally, this finding is relevant to the ongoing debate on whether BAV constitutes an independent risk factor for aortic dissection. Additionally, sensitivity analysis revealed that excluding BAV or AS did not alter the observed sex differences in MAD, media degeneration, or circulating hormone levels. Differences presented in this study are thus not driven by underlying valve pathology but reflect sex-specific biological variation.

Study limitations

The relatively small cohort size, particularly among women, and partial data loss may have limited our ability to detect statistically significant differences, particularly in subgroup analyses. While we identified clear associations between hormones and tissue changes, our cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference. Larger, long-term studies that track hormonal changes, molecular alterations, and vascular mechanics are needed to elucidate how hormones influence vascular ageing. Furthermore, whether these histopathological differences and an increase in MD will contribute to susceptibility to acute aortic dissection remains to be elucidated. Theoretically, these pathologic processes would induce aortic stiffening and thinning,40 which may affect the aorta more severely in women than in men, according to recent research.40,41 By including patients with concomitant aortic valve disease, the clinical practice was correctly reflected. However, this resulted in the inclusion of some relatively small aortas (±45 mm). Although the scope of this research was limited to the ascending aorta, our results demonstrate an association between testosterone and aortic root dilatation in a subgroup of male participants. Future studies should also focus on aortic sinus tissue, given the demonstrated male-female differences.6 In this study, we investigated tissue from two healthy controls (heart transplant donors), both in their fifties and of both sexes. Although these samples are scarce and these analyses are valuable, the sample sizes are too small for statistical analysis.

Conclusions

Our findings highlight marked sex differences in both circulating sex hormones and aortic wall remodelling in patients with AscAA. Women exhibited a more advanced, structurally destructive form of aortic degradation, particularly in elastin architecture and smooth muscle integrity, resulting in higher MD scores that correlated with low DHEA levels and were associated with elevated MMP2 levels in the tissue. In contrast, men exhibited less structurally severe degeneration. However, they showed adaptive wall thickening, an increased inflammatory phenotype (as indicated by MMP9 and CD68), and elevated vascular regeneration, alongside decreased circulating sex hormones. Collectively, this suggests potential sexual dimorphism in aortic degeneration, possibly resulting in different surgical outcomes and follow-up. Validation of this statement requires further exploration of the effects of medial degeneration on surgical outcomes, ultimately providing a solid evidence base for male- and female-tailored diagnosis and treatment strategies in AscAA.
