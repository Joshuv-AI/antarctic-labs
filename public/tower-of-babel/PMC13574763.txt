Effects of chin supported maxillary protraction on symphsis morphology
Authors: Fırat Oğuz, Sevi Kaderoğlu Türk, Mehmet Ali Yavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis controlled retrospective study aimed to evaluate the effects of maxillary protraction therapy on mandibular symphysis morphology. In this regard, 35 growing individuals with skeletal class III malocclusion due to maxillary retrognathia were assigned to two groups. The experimental group (n = 20) was treated with a Petit-type face mask (FM), while the control group (n = 15) had similar characteristics and were untreated. Linear and angular measurements related to the mandibular symphysis were evaluated on lateral cephalometric radiographs obtained before treatment (T0) and after treatment (T1). Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, paired T-tests, and independent-samples T-tests were used for statistical analyses. In the FM group, there were significant increases in the lingual height (p = 0.021), B–Pogonion (p = 0.016), and Infradentale–Menton (Id–Me; p = 0.003) parameters following treatment, whereas a significant decrease was found in the incisor mandibular plane angle (IMPA) angle (p = 0.001). In the control group, there were no significant changes in either linear or angular measurements (p > 0.05). Intergroup comparison of treatment and observation-related changes revealed significant differences in the IMPA (p = 0.023) and Id–Me measurements (p = 0.048). The absence of significant differences in parameters other than the Id–Me-which could be related to the retrusive effect of the face mask on the lower incisors-suggests that face mask treatment does not have a significant short-term effect on mandibular symphysis morphology.Keywords: Class III malocclusion, Face mask therapy, Mandibular symphysis, Growth modificationSubject terms: Anatomy, Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Early maxillary protraction is an effective and widely used orthopedic treatment option for patients with maxillary retrognathia1–3. For this purpose, the most preferred option nowadays is the face mask designed by Henri Petit, which consists of a single vertical midline bar and two pads positioned on the forehead and chin4–6. With the Petit-type face mask, force is transmitted from the intraoral anchorage unit via elastics to the transverse bar and subsequently to the forehead and chin pads. Use of this face mask is recommended for an average of 14–24 h per day7. Owing to its design, the applied forces from the face mask tend to concentrate stress on the skin areas beneath the forehead and particularly the chin pads8. Previous studies have shown that the clinical effects of maxillary protraction include downward and backward rotation of the mandible, an increase in facial height, and lingual inclination of the lower incisors, while side effects, such as attachment loss in the lower incisor region and skin irritation at the site of the chin cup, are also reported4,9,10.

The mandibular symphysis is an anatomical region with a complex phenotype that is shaped by genetic and adaptive factors11,12. Morphological characteristics of mandibular symphysis -including height, width, thickness, inclination, convexity, and concavity-vary depending on several factors, such as sex, genetic background, ethnicity, sagittal and vertical growth patterns, and lower incisor inclination11–22. Mandibular symphysis morphology plays a crucial role in orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning and serves as a fundamental reference point for lower facial aesthetics15,23. A symphysis with sufficient bone volume and appropriate angulation provides a stable foundation for incisor positioning, whereas inadequate symphyseal support can predispose patients to post-treatment relapse, tooth mobility, and periodontal complications, such as dehiscence and fenestration15. It has also been reported that the functional environment-particularly adaptive responses to biomechanical loading during mastication-can influence the shape and dimensions of the symphysis15.

Individuals with class III malocclusion exhibit reduced anterior concavity of the mandibular symphysis and greater inclination of its alveolar portion toward the mandibular plane11,24 and considerably increased symphyseal area, height, and convexity25. The close association of mandibular symphysis morphology with adaptive processes and growth patterns raises the possibility that the chin component of the face mask might also induce short-term morphological changes in this region. However, a review of the literature reveals a lack of studies specifically investigating changes in mandibular symphysis morphology following face mask therapy. Accordingly, the null hypothesis of this study was that face mask therapy has no significant effect on mandibular symphysis morphology or dimensions.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study included data from the archives of the Faculty of Dentistry, Adıyaman University. The study protocol was approved by the Adıyaman University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (decision no: 2025/8–15, date: 14/10/2025). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the same ethics committee. Sample size calculation was performed with G*Power 3.1 software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on 80% statistical power, a significance level of 0.05, and an effect size of 1.1, the analysis indicated that at least 13 individuals were required for each group26. To increase statistical power, a total of 30 individuals were included in the study. The inclusion criteria for all participants were as follows: being in the active growth period, presenting skeletal Class III malocclusion due to maxillary retrognathia, and having an anterior crossbite. Skeletal maturation was assessed using the cervical vertebral maturation (CVM) method on lateral cephalometric radiographs, and only individuals in CVM stages 1–3, representing the active growth period, were included in the study.

For the face mask (FM) group (n = 20), in addition to the general inclusion criteria, the following conditions were required: treatment with bonded rapid maxillary expansion combined with Petit-type face mask therapy, application of protraction forces of 450–500 g on each side throughout treatment and directed 20–30° downward and forward relative to the occlusal plane,

with elastics attached to hooks located in the canine–premolar region of the expansion appliance, daily use of the appliance for 16–18 h, continuation of protraction until a 4 mm overjet was achieved, availability of high-quality pre-treatment and post-treatment lateral cephalometric radiographs, and complete clinical records.

To distinguish the effects of face mask therapy on the mandibular symphysis caused by growth-related changes, lateral cephalometric radiographs obtained before and after the observation period from 15 untreated individuals who met the inclusion criteria were also included as a control group.

Individuals with craniofacial anomalies or systemic diseases affecting growth or bone development, those with a history of previous orthodontic or orthopedic treatment, patients with poor compliance or irregular face mask use during treatment, and those with incomplete clinical records were excluded from the study.

Linear and angular measurements defining mandibular symphysis morphology on pre-treatment and post-treatment or observation lateral cephalograms were evaluated using the VistaDent® analysis software (v.4.2.44, GAC International, Inc., Bohemia, NY, USA) by the same investigator (SKT; Table 1; Fig. 1). To assess method error and intra-examiner reliability, all measurements were repeated by the same investigator after a 2-week interval.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Intragroup comparisons were made using paired T-tests and intergroup comparisons with independent-samples T-tests. Measurement results were reported as mean ± standard deviation. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows version 22.0. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) index was used to assess the reliability of the measurements, showing high reliability for all (ICC > 0.90). The mean treatment duration in the FM group was 9.25 ± 0.91 months, whereas the mean observation period in the control group was 6.10 ± 0.15 months. There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of pretreatment demographic characteristics (p > 0.05). Likewise, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups regarding pretreatment mandibular symphysis measurements (p > 0.05; Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of applying chin-supported maxillary protraction on mandibular symphysis morphology in growing individuals. The null hypothesis of the study assumed that the face mask treatment would not result in significant changes in mandibular symphyseal morphological measurements; however, the findings reject this hypothesis for one parameter.

The effect of growth on mandibular symphysis morphology remains controversial. While Buschang et al.27 reported spontaneous vertical growth on the upper anterior surface of the mandibular symphysis during adolescence, Ruiz et al.28 found no significant morphological changes during the same period. Therefore, we included an untreated control group in this study to distinguish treatment effects from growth-related changes.

At the end of the treatment period, the FM group showed significant increases in LH, B–Pogonion distance, and Id–Me height. In contrast, a marked decrease in the IMPA angle was observed after treatment, which was also highly significant. These findings suggest that face mask therapy might partially influence mandibular symphysis morphology. Conversely, no significant changes were seen in either linear or angular parameters in the control group during the observation period (p > 0.05). Most measurements remained stable, with only minimal changes attributable to growth, which is consistent with Ruiz et al.28 who found no significant spontaneous changes in mandibular symphysis morphology during adolescent growth. Accordingly, the morphological changes observed in the FM group are likely to be primarily related to the face mask treatment rather than growth effects.

In our study, there was a significant retroclination of the lower incisors following face mask therapy, in agreement with the existing literature. The significant increases observed in LH, B–Pogonion, and Id–Me values after face mask treatment suggest that this morphological response is closely associated with lower incisor tipping. The increase in LH (p = 0.021) can be explained by a combined effect of the lower incisor root apex moving closer to the labial cortical plate rather than lingually, together with a clinically minimal vertical displacement of Menton in growing patients. Similarly, the increase in the B–Pogonion distance (p = 0.016) indicates an anterior relocation of point B associated with the lower incisor root during treatment, which represents an expected consequence of lower incisor retroclination29,30. In addition, the increase in Id–Me height (p = 0.003) can be attributed to the upward displacement of the Infradentale point due to lower incisor tipping. Indeed, previous studies have reported that changes in incisor inclination across different anteroposterior relationships can induce adaptive remodeling on the symphyseal surface31. This retroclination, which is frequently observed in patients with skeletal class III malocclusion, has been shown to promote remodeling of the dentoalveolar surface of the symphysis, leading to posterior inclination of its alveolar portion that contributes to a reduction in anterior concavity24,31.

Previous studies have reported that in individuals with skeletal class III malocclusion, the bone thickness posterior to the lower incisor apex (LP) is greater than in the anterior region (LA), resulting in closer proximity of the root apex to the labial cortical plate15. Therefore, careful evaluation of the initial inclination of the incisors and their relationship with the surrounding alveolar bone is essential when planning incisor movements, as failure to do so can lead to root resorption or periodontal dehiscence32. In contrast, the finding that LA was greater than LP in our study suggests that, despite the skeletal class III pattern, our cohort did not exhibit pronounced excessive retroclination and the initial inclination of the lower incisors was relatively more physiological compared with typical class III cases reported in the literature. This may indicate that the included individuals predominantly presented with mild-to-moderate skeletal Class III discrepancies, in which the lower incisors had not yet developed severe natural lingual compensation. This observation is consistent with the presence of greater bone support on the anterior surface of the dentoalveolar symphysis. Moreover, following face mask therapy, a tendency toward an increase in LP and a decrease in LA in association with the reduction in IMPA was observed. Although these changes did not reach statistical significance, they suggest a mild effect of the treatment toward enhancing posterior bone support in the mandibular symphysis region.

When the changes in mandibular symphysis morphology parameters following face mask therapy were compared with growth-related changes observed in the control group, significant differences were found only in the Id–Me and IMPA measurements. This finding suggests that face mask therapy could cause an increase in the vertical dimension of the symphysis, primarily attributable to lower incisor tipping. The absence of significant changes in the remaining parameters suggest that the orthopedic forces applied by the face mask to the mandibular symphysis are largely compensated by downward and backward displacement at the condylar level, and that face mask therapy does not exert a substantial effect on symphysis morphology beyond lower incisor retroclination.

The main limitations of this study include its retrospective design, which might increase the risk of selection bias and the reliance on short-term follow-up data. Considering that mandibular symphysis morphology can change with time in growing individuals, the lack of long-term evaluation limits the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, the treatment duration in the face mask group was longer than the observation period in the control group, which may have allowed additional normal craniofacial growth and slightly influenced certain linear measurements. In addition, the two-dimensional nature of lateral cephalometric imaging restricts the accurate assessment of three-dimensional morphological changes in the symphysis region. Future studies incorporating longer follow-up periods, three-dimensional imaging techniques, and larger samples with different growth patterns would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of face mask therapy on mandibular symphysis morphology.

Conclusions

This study showed that although face mask therapy applied in growing individuals with skeletal class III malocclusion resulted in significant changes in certain mandibular symphysis-related parameters, no significant differences were observed compared with the control group except for one parameter attributable to lower incisor retroclination.
