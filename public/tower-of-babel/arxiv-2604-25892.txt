Dynamics, Random Products, and Ultrametric Geometry in Kiselman’s Semigroup
Luka Andrenšek

Abstract
We study certain dynamical and metric aspects of Kiselman’s semigroup K n K_{n} . The level function ℒ \mathcal{L} is introduced and shown to admit a simple description in terms of right multiplication by generators. We show that every sequence of partial products in K n K_{n} is eventually constant. Using ℒ \mathcal{L} , we further study sequences of random partial products in K n K_{n} and show that, in the independent and identically distributed setting where every generator is chosen with positive probability, the hitting time of the eventual constant value is distributed as a sum of n n independent geometric random variables. Finally, we define a natural ultrametric on K n K_{n} arising from the level function and obtain some basic results on the associated metric balls and spheres.



keywords
Kiselman’s semigroup, dynamics, infinite products, partial products, random products, ultrametric, Markov chain, probability

1
Introduction

Let
n
be a natural number.
We define
Kiselman’s semigroup
by the following presentation:

K_{n}=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\mid a_{i}^{2}=a_{i},a_{i}a_{j}a_{i}=a_{j}a_{i}a_{j}=a_{i}a_{j},1\leq j<i\leq n\rangle,

and denote by
e
the unit element of
K_{n}
.
Throughout the paper, we assume
n>1
.

This family of semigroups arose as a generalisation of a semigroup of operators in convex analysis, which was studied by Kiselman in
5
.
Many fundamental results on
K_{n}
were proved by Kudryavtseva and Mazorchuk in
6
.
Kiselman’s semigroup arises in graph dynamics, as discussed by Collina and D’Andrea in
3
, and admits interesting combinatorial properties in various settings.

D’Andrea and Stella showed in
4
that the cardinality of Kiselman’s semigroups grows double-exponentially.
Moreover, we described the endomorphism monoid of
K_{n}
in
1
, and studied certain equations in
K_{n}
in
2
.

2
Main Results

The central object of this paper is the
level function

\mathcal{L}:K_{n}\to\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, defined in Section
5
.
The function
\mathcal{L}
will later be shown to coincide with the distance from the zero element of
K_{n}
with respect to a natural ultrametric, and its most important property is the following formula:

\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})=\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}(x)-1,&i=\mathcal{L}(x),\\
\mathcal{L}(x),&i\neq\mathcal{L}(x).\end{cases}

The function
\mathcal{L}
is the main tool used to study sequences of random partial products, and it also gives rise to the ultrametric structure on
K_{n}
.

We investigate sequences of partial products in
K_{n}
, that is, sequences of the form

x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{j},

where
(x_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is a sequence with values in
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
We prove that every such sequence is eventually constant, and we determine its eventual value under certain assumptions.

Using
\mathcal{L}
, we study sequences of random partial products in
K_{n}
, that is, sequences of random variables of the form

X_{1}X_{2}\dots X_{j},

where
(X_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables with values in
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
We show that the random variables defined by

\mathcal{L}(X_{1}X_{2}\dots X_{j})

form a Markov chain.
We further study the hitting time at which a sequence of random partial products attains its eventual constant value under the additional assumption that
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=a_{i})>0
for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
We show that this hitting time is distributed as a sum of
n
independent geometric random variables with success probabilities
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=a_{i})
for
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
In particular, if
(X_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are uniformly distributed on
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
, then the expected time until the sequence of random partial products becomes constant is
n^{2}
.

We also define a natural ultrametric on
K_{n}
, arising from the definition of the level function
\mathcal{L}
, and prove some results about the metric balls and spheres.

The paper is organised as follows.
In Section
3
, we recall some background and necessary results on
K_{n}
.
In Section
4
, we define deletion endomorphisms and prove some of their properties.
These endomorphisms are used in the definition of
\mathcal{L}
, which we introduce in Section
5
and study further there.
In Section
6
, we study sequences of partial products and their random counterparts.
In Section
7
, we define an ultrametric on
K_{n}
and describe some of its basic properties.

Preliminaries

For a set
X
, denote by
W(X)
the set of all finite words over
X
and denote the empty word by
\mathbf{e}
.
By equipping
W(X)
with the operation of

,

becomes a monoid with unit element
\mathbf{e}
.
Let

Denote by

\varphi(\mathbf{a}_{i_{1}}\mathbf{a}_{i_{2}}\dots\mathbf{a}_{i_{k}})=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\dots a_{i_{k}},

and

, and
\varphi(\mathbf{e})=e
.
In what follows, elements of
W(A)
are written in boldface, for instance
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{a}_{i}
, while elements of
K_{n}
are written in standard mathematical italic, for example
x,y,a_{i}
.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
[k]=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
and also set
[0]=\emptyset
.

In
(
6
, Lemma 1)
, the following lemma was proved.

Lemma 3.1
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

Let
i\in[n]
and
\mathbf{w}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{i-1}\})
.
Then we have
a_{i}\varphi(\mathbf{w})a_{i}=a_{i}\varphi(\mathbf{w})
.

(ii)

Let
i\in[n]
and

.

3.1
Idempotents in
K_{n}

Let
X\subseteq[n]
.
If
X=\emptyset
, we define

, the unit element of

, and
\mathbf{e}_{X}=\mathbf{e}
, the empty word in

.
Otherwise, write

, where
i_{1}>i_{2}>\dots>i_{k}

\varphi(\mathbf{e}_{X})=e_{X}
for every

.
In
(

, it was observed that

is the zero element of

and we denote it by

.
Moreover, in
(

Define the

\mathbf{a}_{i}
occurs in

In
(
6
, Lemma 10)
, it was shown that

is well-defined and that it is a semigroup epimorphism when

is equipped with the operation
\cup
.

In
(
6
, Lemma 12)
, the following lemma was proved.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
. Then
x^{k}=e_{c(x)}
for all

.

3.2
Morphisms of
K_{n}

In
(
6
, Proposition 20)
, the following was proved.

Proposition 3.3
.

The only automorphism of
K_{n}
is the identity. The map
a_{i}\mapsto a_{n-i+1}
extends uniquely to an
antiautomorphism of
K_{n}
. This is the only antiautomorphism of
K_{n}
.

We denote the unique antiautomorphism of
K_{n}
by
\tau
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}\times k_{2}}
be real matrices with
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 2
.
The matrix
A
is said to be a
2\times 2
submatrix
of
M
if there exist rows

x,y\in[k_{1}]
and columns
i,j\in[k_{2}]
with
x<y
and
i<j
such that

A=\begin{pmatrix}M_{x,i}&M_{x,j}\\
M_{y,i}&M_{y,j}\end{pmatrix},

where
M_{x,i}
denotes the entry of
M
in row
x
and column
i
.
Let
P
be the following
2\times 2
matrix:

P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(1)

In
1
, we introduced the following set for
n\geq 2
:

D_{n}=\{M\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\mid P\text{ is not a }2\times 2\text{ submatrix of }M\}.

The binary operation
\cdot
on
D_{n}
is defined as follows.
Let
A,B\in D_{n}
.
Then
C=A\cdot B
is given by

\displaystyle C_{i,j}=\bigvee_{k=1}^{n}(A_{i,k}\land B_{k,j}).

Then
D_{n}
becomes a monoid with unit element being the identity matrix
I
.
Here,
\lor
denotes the Boolean join and
\land
the Boolean meet on
\{0,1\}
.

In
(
1
, Theorem 1)
, we proved that the endomorphism monoid
\mathrm{End}(K_{n})
is isomorphic to
D_{n}
.
Here, we denote this isomorphism by
\Theta:\mathrm{End}(K_{n})\to D_{n}
.
We showed that for any endomorphism
\psi\in\mathrm{End}(K_{n})
, there exist sets
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\subseteq[n]
such that

\psi(a_{i})=e_{X_{i}},

for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
Then
\Theta(\psi)
is a Boolean matrix where
\Theta(\psi)_{x,i}=1
if and only if
x\in X_{i}
for
x,i\in[n]
.

In
(
6
, Section 7)
, the authors defined the
height

h(x)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
for
x\in K_{n}
and proved the following lemma
(
6
, Lemma 23)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
x,y\in K_{n}
be such that

.
Then

.

4
Deletion Endomorphisms

In this section, we define deletion endomorphisms and prove several properties that play an important role in Section
5
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
X\subseteq[n]
.
We define the
deletion map
associated with
X
to be the map
\partial_{X}:W(A)\to W(A)
given as follows.
If
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{a}_{i_{1}}\mathbf{a}_{i_{2}}\dots\mathbf{a}_{i_{k}}
, then

\partial_{X}(\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{e}_{\{i_{1}\}\setminus X}\mathbf{e}_{\{i_{2}\}\setminus X}\dots\mathbf{e}_{\{i_{k}\}\setminus X},

and
\partial_{X}(\mathbf{e})=\mathbf{e}
.
In other words,
\partial_{X}
deletes from a word all letters
\mathbf{a}_{i}
with
i\in X
.

It is easy to verify that
\partial_{X}
is an endomorphism of
W(A)
.

Let
i\in[n]
and denote by
I^{(i)}
the
n\times n
Boolean matrix with entry
1
in row
i
and column
i
, and
0
elsewhere.
For
X\subseteq[n]
, we define

I_{X}=\sum_{i\in X}I^{(i)}.

Since
I_{X}
is a diagonal Boolean matrix, it lies in
D_{n}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
X\subseteq[n]
.
We define the
deletion endomorphism
associated with
X
to be the endomorphism
\overline{\partial}_{X}:K_{n}\to K_{n}
, defined by

\overline{\partial}_{X}=\Theta^{-1}(I_{X^{c}}).

Let
X\subseteq[n]
.
Since
\overline{\partial}_{X}
is an endomorphism of
K_{n}
, there exist sets
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\subseteq[n]
such that

\overline{\partial}_{X}(a_{i})=e_{X_{i}},

for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
By the definition of
\Theta
, we have
\Theta(\overline{\partial}_{X})_{x,i}=1
if and only if
x\in X_{i}
.
Since
\Theta(\overline{\partial}_{X})=I_{X^{c}}
, we have
x\in X_{i}
if and only if
(I_{X^{c}})_{x,i}=1
, which is further equivalent to
x=i
and
x\notin X
.
Hence
X_{i}=\{i\}\setminus X
.
Therefore, for all
i
, we have

\overline{\partial}_{X}(a_{i})=e_{\{i\}\setminus X}=\begin{cases}a_{i},&i\notin X,\\
e,&i\in X.\end{cases}

(3)

Hence, the maps
\partial_{X}
and
\overline{\partial}_{X}
are closely related.
We now establish several properties of the deletion endomorphisms analogous to those holding for the maps
\partial_{X}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
X\subseteq[n]
and
x\in K_{n}
.
If
x=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
, where
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
, then
\overline{\partial}_{X}(x)=\varphi(\partial_{X}(\mathbf{w}))
.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{e}
, then
x=e
, and the conclusion follows.
Otherwise, write
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{a}_{i_{1}}\mathbf{a}_{i_{2}}\dots\mathbf{a}_{i_{k}}
and hence

x=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\dots a_{i_{k}}
.
Then we have

\varphi(\partial_{X}(\mathbf{w}))=\varphi(\mathbf{e}_{\{i_{1}\}\setminus X}\mathbf{e}_{\{i_{2}\}\setminus X}\dots\mathbf{e}_{\{i_{k}\}\setminus X})=e_{\{i_{1}\}\setminus X}e_{\{i_{2}\}\setminus X}\dots e_{\{i_{k}\}\setminus X}.

On the other hand, by (
3
) and the fact that
\overline{\partial}_{X}
is an endomorphism, we have

\overline{\partial}_{X}(x)=\overline{\partial}_{X}(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\dots a_{i_{k}})=e_{\{i_{1}\}\setminus X}e_{\{i_{2}\}\setminus X}\dots e_{\{i_{k}\}\setminus X}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X,Y\subseteq[n]
.
Then
\overline{\partial}_{X}(e_{Y})=e_{Y\setminus X}
.

Proof.

The claim follows by applying Lemma
4.3
with
x=e_{Y}
and
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{e}_{Y}
, and using the fact that
\partial_{X}(\mathbf{e}_{Y})=\mathbf{e}_{Y\setminus X}
.
∎

For
X,Y\subseteq[n]
, we write
\overline{\partial}_{X}\overline{\partial}_{Y}
and
\partial_{X}\partial_{Y}
instead of
\overline{\partial}_{X}\circ\overline{\partial}_{Y}
and
\partial_{X}\circ\partial_{Y}
, respectively.
For
i\in[n]
we also write
\overline{\partial}_{i}
and
\partial_{i}
instead of
\overline{\partial}_{\{i\}}
and
\partial_{\{i\}}
, respectively.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
X,Y\subseteq[n]
.
Then
\overline{\partial}_{X\cup Y}=\overline{\partial}_{X}\overline{\partial}_{Y}
.

Proof.

Since
\Theta
is an isomorphism, it suffices to prove that
I_{(X\cup Y)^{c}}=I_{X^{c}}\cdot I_{Y^{c}}
.
Since
(X\cup Y)^{c}=X^{c}\cap Y^{c}
, it suffices to show that for any sets

A,B\subseteq[n]
, we have
I_{A\cap B}=I_{A}\cdot I_{B}
. The latter equality is immediate.
∎

In particular, Proposition
4.5
implies that the deletion endomorphisms commute.

We now prove some technical results on the endomorphisms
\overline{\partial}_{[m]}
for
m\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.
These results will be crucial in proving properties of the level function
\mathcal{L}
in Section
5
.
For
m\in[n]
, by Proposition
4.5
, we have
\overline{\partial}_{[m]}=\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}\overline{\partial}_{m}=\overline{\partial}_{m}\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}
.
Also note that
\overline{\partial}_{[0]}(x)=x
for any
x\in K_{n}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
,
m\in\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}
, and
r\in\{0,1,\dots,n-m\}
.
If
\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[m]}
, then

\overline{\partial}_{[m+r]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[m+r]}.

Proof.

We proceed with induction on
r
.
The base case
r=0
holds by the assumption of the lemma.
Assume that
r\in\{1,2,\dots,n-m\}
and
\overline{\partial}_{[m+r-1]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[m+r-1]}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}_{[m+r]}(x)

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{m+r}(\overline{\partial}_{[m+r-1]}(x))

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{m+r}(e_{[n]\setminus[m+r-1]})

\displaystyle=e_{([n]\setminus[m+r-1])\setminus\{m+r\}}

\displaystyle=e_{[n]\setminus[m+r]},

where we used Lemma
4.4
on the second to last step.
This proves the induction step and the proof is finished.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
and
m\in[n]
.
Then we have

\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}(x)a_{m}=\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x)a_{m}.

Proof.

We write
x=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
for some
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
.
By Lemma
4.3
, we have

\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}(x)=\varphi(\partial_{[m-1]}(\mathbf{w})).

Set
\mathbf{v}=\partial_{[m-1]}(\mathbf{w})
and observe that
\mathbf{v}\in W(\{\mathbf{a}_{m},\mathbf{a}_{m+1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}\})
.
We obtain

\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}(x)a_{m}=\varphi(\mathbf{v})a_{m}.

If
\mathbf{a}_{m}
does not occur in
\mathbf{v}
, we have
\mathbf{v}=\partial_{m}(\mathbf{v})
.
If
\mathbf{a}_{m}
occurs in
\mathbf{v}
, then
\mathbf{a}_{m}
is the letter of minimal index occurring in
\mathbf{v}
.
Lemma
3.1
(
ii
) implies that we can delete all occurrences of
a_{m}
in
\varphi(\mathbf{v})a_{m}
, except for the rightmost
a_{m}
.
Hence we get

\varphi(\mathbf{v})a_{m}=\overline{\partial}_{m}(\varphi(\mathbf{v}))a_{m}=\varphi(\partial_{m}(\mathbf{v}))a_{m}.

Thus, in both cases, we have
\varphi(\mathbf{v})a_{m}=\varphi(\partial_{m}(\mathbf{v}))a_{m}
, and hence

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}(x)a_{m}

\displaystyle=\varphi(\mathbf{v})a_{m}

\displaystyle=\varphi(\partial_{m}(\mathbf{v}))a_{m}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{m}(\varphi(\mathbf{v}))a_{m}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{m}(\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}(x))a_{m}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x)a_{m}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
and
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.
Then

xe_{[i]}=\overline{\partial}_{[j]}(x)e_{[i]},

for all
j\in\{0,1,\dots,i\}
.

Proof.

We proceed with a proof by induction on
j
.
The base case
j=0
is obvious since
\overline{\partial}_{[0]}(x)=x
.
Now assume that
j\in\{1,2,\dots,i\}
and

xe_{[i]}=\overline{\partial}_{[j-1]}(x)e_{[i]}.

Since

e_{[i]}=e_{[i]\setminus[j]}a_{j}e_{[j-1]},

we have

\displaystyle xe_{[i]}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[j-1]}(x)e_{[i]}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[j-1]}(x)e_{[i]\setminus[j]}a_{j}e_{[j-1]}.

Since
\overline{\partial}_{[j-1]}(e_{[i]\setminus[j]})=e_{[i]\setminus[j]}
, we further have

\displaystyle xe_{[i]}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[j-1]}(x)e_{[i]\setminus[j]}a_{j}e_{[j-1]}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[j-1]}(x)\overline{\partial}_{[j-1]}(e_{[i]\setminus[j]})a_{j}e_{[j-1]}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[j-1]}(xe_{[i]\setminus[j]})a_{j}e_{[j-1]}.

Since
j\in\{1,2,\dots,i\}\subseteq[n]
, Lemma
4.7
implies

\displaystyle xe_{[i]}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[j-1]}(xe_{[i]\setminus[j]})a_{j}e_{[j-1]}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[j]}(xe_{[i]\setminus[j]})a_{j}e_{[j-1]}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[j]}(x)\overline{\partial}_{[j]}(e_{[i]\setminus[j]})a_{j}e_{[j-1]}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[j]}(x)e_{[i]\setminus[j]}a_{j}e_{[j-1]}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[j]}(x)e_{[i]},

which proves the induction step and completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
and
m\in[n]
.
If
\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[m]}
, then

\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}(xa_{m})=e_{[n]\setminus[m-1]}.

Proof.

By (
3
), we have
\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}(a_{m})=a_{m}
, and hence

\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}(xa_{m})=\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}(x)a_{m}.

Lemma
4.7
further implies

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}_{[m-1]}(x)a_{m}

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x)a_{m}

\displaystyle=e_{[n]\setminus[m]}a_{m}

\displaystyle=e_{[n]\setminus[m-1]}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
,
m\in\{0,1,\dots,n-2\}
,
k\in\{2,3,\dots,n-m\}
, and
r\in\{1,2,\dots,n-m-1\}
.
If

\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(xa_{m+k})=e_{[n]\setminus[m]}\quad\text{or}\quad\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(a_{m+r}x)=e_{[n]\setminus[m]},

then

\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[m]}.

Proof.

Since
\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(a_{m+k})=a_{m+k}
and
\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(a_{m+r})=a_{m+r}
by (
3
), we get

\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x)a_{m+k}=e_{[n]\setminus[m]}\quad\text{or}\quad a_{m+r}\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[m]}.

(4)

Denote by
S=\langle a_{m+1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle
the subsemigroup of
K_{n}
generated by
\{a_{m+1},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
Then
S
is isomorphic to
K_{n-m}
.
Let
\phi:S\to K_{n-m}
be the semigroup isomorphism such that
\phi(a_{i})=a_{i-m}
for
i=m+1,\dots,n
.

Write
x=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
for some
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
.
By Lemma
4.3
, we have
\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x)=\varphi(\partial_{[m]}(\mathbf{w}))\in S
, and we also have
a_{m+k},a_{m+r},e_{[n]\setminus[m]}\in S
.
Applying
\phi
to (
4
), and using the fact that
\phi
is an isomorphism and
\phi(e_{[n]\setminus[m]})=e_{[n-m]}
, we obtain

\phi(\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x))a_{k}=e_{[n-m]}\quad\text{or}\quad a_{r}\phi(\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x))=e_{[n-m]}.

Since
k\in\{2,3,\dots,n-m\}
and
r\in\{1,2,\dots,n-m-1\}
, we may apply Proposition
3.5
in
K_{n-m}
to conclude that

\phi(\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x))=e_{[n-m]}.

Since
\phi
is injective and
\phi(e_{[n]\setminus[m]})=e_{[n-m]}
, we get

\overline{\partial}_{[m]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[m]}.

This completes the proof.
∎

5
The Level Function

In this section, we define the level function and study its properties.

Definition 5.1
.

The
level function

\mathcal{L}:K_{n}\to\{0,1,\dots,n\}
is defined by

\mathcal{L}(x)=\min\{i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}\mid\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[i]}\}.

Let
x\in K_{n}
and
x=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
with
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
.
Since

\overline{\partial}_{[n]}(x)=\varphi(\partial_{[n]}(\mathbf{w}))=\varphi(\mathbf{e})=e=e_{\emptyset}=e_{[n]\setminus[n]},

the set

\{i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}\mid\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[i]}\}

contains
n
, and is therefore nonempty.
Therefore,
\mathcal{L}(x)
is well-defined.

Intuitively,
\mathcal{L}
measures how close an element is to the zero element
f
.
In Section
7
, we define an ultrametric
d
on
K_{n}
in (
14
), and show that, for
x\in K_{n}
, we have

\mathcal{L}(x)=d(x,f).

We focus on
d(x,f)
rather than
d(x,y)
for general
y\in K_{n}
, since
\mathcal{L}(x)
admits properties, such as Theorem
5.10
, that do not hold for the map
x\mapsto d(x,y)
for arbitrary
y\in K_{n}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
. Then
\mathcal{L}(x)=0
if and only if
x=f
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{L}(x)=0
, then

x=\overline{\partial}_{[0]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[0]}=f.

Conversely, we have

\overline{\partial}_{[0]}(f)=f=e_{[n]\setminus[0]},

and hence
\mathcal{L}(f)=0
.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
and let
k\in\{\mathcal{L}(x),\mathcal{L}(x)+1,\dots,n\}
. Then

\overline{\partial}_{[k]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[k]}.

Proof.

Set
l=\mathcal{L}(x)
.
Then
\overline{\partial}_{[l]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[l]}
.
Lemma
4.6
implies that for any
r\in\{0,1,\dots,n-l\}
, we have

\overline{\partial}_{[l+r]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[l+r]}.

Hence, for any
k\in\{l,l+1,\dots,n\}
, we obtain

\overline{\partial}_{[k]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[k]}.

∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
. Then
\mathcal{L}(x)=n
if and only if
c(x)\subseteq[n-1]
.

Proof.

Write
x=\varphi(\mathbf{w})
for some
\mathbf{w}\in W(A)
.
If
\mathcal{L}(x)=n
, then by the definition of
\mathcal{L}
,
\overline{\partial}_{[n-1]}(x)\neq e_{[n]\setminus[n-1]}=a_{n}
, and hence

\varphi(\partial_{[n-1]}(\mathbf{w}))\neq a_{n}.

If
\mathbf{a}_{n}
occurs in
\mathbf{w}
, it follows that
\varphi(\partial_{[n-1]}(\mathbf{w}))=a_{n}
, which is a contradiction, therefore
\mathbf{a}_{n}
does not occur in
\mathbf{w}
.
Hence
c(x)\subseteq[n-1]
.
Conversely, if
c(x)\subseteq[n-1]
, then

\overline{\partial}_{[n-1]}(x)=\varphi(\partial_{[n-1]}(\mathbf{w}))=\varphi(\mathbf{e})=e\neq a_{n}=e_{[n]\setminus[n-1]}.

Corollary
5.3
then implies
\mathcal{L}(x)>n-1
, and hence
\mathcal{L}(x)=n
.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
x,y\in K_{n}
. Then

\mathcal{L}(xy)\leq\min\{\mathcal{L}(x),\mathcal{L}(y)\}.

Proof.

Denote
l_{1}=\mathcal{L}(x)
and
l_{2}=\mathcal{L}(y)
.
Since
c(\overline{\partial}_{[l_{1}]}(y))\subseteq[n]\setminus[l_{1}]
, Lemma
3.1
(
i
) implies

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}_{[l_{1}]}(xy)

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[l_{1}]}(x)\overline{\partial}_{[l_{1}]}(y)

\displaystyle=e_{[n]\setminus[l_{1}]}\overline{\partial}_{[l_{1}]}(y)

\displaystyle=e_{[n]\setminus[l_{1}]},

which proves
\mathcal{L}(xy)\leq l_{1}
.
Since
c(\overline{\partial}_{[l_{2}]}(x))\subseteq[n]\setminus[l_{2}]
, Lemma
3.1
(
ii
) implies

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}_{[l_{2}]}(xy)

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[l_{2}]}(x)\overline{\partial}_{[l_{2}]}(y)

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[l_{2}]}(x)e_{[n]\setminus[l_{2}]}

\displaystyle=e_{[n]\setminus[l_{2}]},

which proves
\mathcal{L}(xy)\leq l_{2}
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
. If
\mathcal{L}(x)>0
, then

\mathcal{L}(xa_{\mathcal{L}(x)})=\mathcal{L}(x)-1.

Proof.

We denote
l=\mathcal{L}(x)
.
Since
\overline{\partial}_{[l]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[l]}
and
l\in[n]
, Lemma
4.9
implies that

\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(xa_{l})=e_{[n]\setminus[l-1]},

and therefore
\mathcal{L}(xa_{l})\leq l-1
.

If
l=1
, then
\mathcal{L}(xa_{l})\leq 0
, and hence
\mathcal{L}(xa_{l})=0=\mathcal{L}(x)-1
.

Now assume that
l\geq 2
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{L}(xa_{l})\leq l-2
.
By Corollary
5.3
, we obtain

\overline{\partial}_{[l-2]}(xa_{l})=e_{[n]\setminus[l-2]}.

Since
l-2\in\{0,1,\dots,n-2\}
, Lemma
4.10
for
m=l-2
and
k=2
implies that

\overline{\partial}_{[l-2]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[l-2]}.

Hence

\mathcal{L}(x)\leq l-2=\mathcal{L}(x)-2,

which is a contradiction.
Hence we must have
\mathcal{L}(xa_{l})\geq l-1
.
Therefore, we indeed have

\mathcal{L}(xa_{\mathcal{L}(x)})=\mathcal{L}(x)-1.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
with
\mathcal{L}(x)>0
and let
i\in\{1,2,\dots,\mathcal{L}(x)-1\}
.
Then

\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})=\mathcal{L}(x).

Proof.

We denote
l=\mathcal{L}(x)
.
If
l=1
, then the set
\{1,2,\dots,l-1\}
is empty and the claim vacuously holds.
Now assume
l\geq 2
.
By Proposition
5.5
, we have
\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})\leq l
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})\leq l-1
.
By Corollary
5.3
, we have

\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(xa_{i})=e_{[n]\setminus[l-1]}.

Since
i\in\{1,2,\dots,l-1\}
, we have
\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(a_{i})=e
, by (
3
).
It follows that

\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(x)=\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(x)\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(a_{i})=\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(xa_{i})=e_{[n]\setminus[l-1]}.

This implies

\mathcal{L}(x)\leq l-1=\mathcal{L}(x)-1,

which is a contradiction.
Hence we must have
\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})\geq l
, and therefore

\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})=\mathcal{L}(x).

∎

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
with
\mathcal{L}(x)>0
and let
i\in\{\mathcal{L}(x)+1,\mathcal{L}(x)+2,\dots,n\}
.
Then

\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})=\mathcal{L}(x).

Proof.

We denote
l=\mathcal{L}(x)
.
If
l=n
, there does not exist any
i\in\{l+1,l+2,\dots,n\}
, and the statement is vacuously true.
Now assume that
l\leq n-1
.
By Proposition
5.5
, we have
\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})\leq l
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})\leq l-1
.
By Corollary
5.3
, we have

\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(xa_{i})=e_{[n]\setminus[l-1]}.

Since
l-1\in\{0,1,\dots,n-2\}
and
i-l+1\in\{2,3,\dots,n-l+1\}
, we use Lemma
4.10
for
m=l-1
and
k=i-l+1
to obtain

\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[l-1]}.

Hence

\mathcal{L}(x)\leq l-1=\mathcal{L}(x)-1,

which is a contradiction, and hence we must have
\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})\geq l
.
Thus we have

\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})=\mathcal{L}(x).

∎

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
.
If
\mathcal{L}(x)=0
, then for any
y\in K_{n}
, we have

\mathcal{L}(xy)=\mathcal{L}(yx)=\mathcal{L}(x).

Proof.

By Lemma
5.2
, we have
x=f
.
Since
x
is the zero element, we have
xy=yx=x
for any
y\in K_{n}
, and the result follows.
∎

The following formula tells us how
\mathcal{L}
behaves under right multiplication by the generators
a_{i}
.
It is its most important property.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
and
i\in[n]
.
Then

\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})=\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}(x)-1,&i=\mathcal{L}(x),\\
\mathcal{L}(x),&i\neq\mathcal{L}(x).\end{cases}

(5)

Proof.

If
\mathcal{L}(x)>0
, Propositions
5.7
and
5.8
imply that

\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})=\mathcal{L}(x),

for all
i\in[n]\setminus\{\mathcal{L}(x)\}
.
By Proposition
5.6
, we have

\mathcal{L}(xa_{\mathcal{L}(x)})=\mathcal{L}(x)-1,

which proves the validity of (
5
) in this case.

If
\mathcal{L}(x)=0
, then
i\neq\mathcal{L}(x)
for all
i\in[n]
.
We use Proposition
5.9
for
y=a_{i}
to conclude that (
5
) also holds in this case.
∎

Theorem
5.10
describes the behaviour of
\mathcal{L}
under right multiplication by generators.
The following proposition gives the corresponding rule for left multiplication by generators
a_{i}
with
i<n
.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
and
i\in[n-1]
. Then

\mathcal{L}(a_{i}x)=\mathcal{L}(x).

Proof.

Set
l=\mathcal{L}(x)
.
If
l=0
, the conclusion follows by Proposition
5.9
with
y=a_{i}
.
If
l=n
, then by Lemma
5.4
, we have
c(x)\subseteq[n-1]
.
Hence we have
c(a_{i}x)\subseteq[n-1]
, and again by Lemma
5.4
, we conclude

\mathcal{L}(a_{i}x)=n=\mathcal{L}(x).

Now we assume that
1\leq l\leq n-1
.
Proposition
5.5
implies
\mathcal{L}(a_{i}x)\leq l
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}(a_{i}x)\leq l-1
.
Then by Corollary
5.3
, we have

\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(a_{i}x)=e_{[n]\setminus[l-1]}.

(6)

If
i\leq l-1
, then by (
3
),
\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(a_{i})=e
, and hence from (
6
) we obtain

\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[l-1]},

which implies

\mathcal{L}(x)\leq l-1=\mathcal{L}(x)-1,

which is a contradiction.

Now assume that
i\geq l
.
Since
l-1\in\{0,1,\dots,n-2\}
and
i-l+1\in\{1,2,\dots,n-l\}
, we use Lemma
4.10
for
m=l-1
and
r=i-l+1
to conclude from (
6
) that

\overline{\partial}_{[l-1]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[l-1]},

which again leads to

\mathcal{L}(x)\leq l-1=\mathcal{L}(x)-1,

a contradiction.

Hence, we reached a contradiction in both cases, and therefore we must have
\mathcal{L}(a_{i}x)\geq l
, and therefore

\mathcal{L}(a_{i}x)=\mathcal{L}(x).

∎

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
x,y\in K_{n}
with
c(y)\subseteq[n-1]
. Then

\mathcal{L}(yx)=\mathcal{L}(x).

Proof.

If
y=e
, the conclusion follows.
Otherwise, write
y=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\dots a_{i_{k}}
where

i_{j}\in[n-1]
for all
j\in[k]
.
By Proposition
5.11
, we have

\mathcal{L}(x)=\mathcal{L}(a_{i_{k}}x)=\mathcal{L}(a_{i_{k-1}}a_{i_{k}}x)=\cdots=\mathcal{L}(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\dots a_{i_{k}}x)=\mathcal{L}(yx).

∎

Lemma
5.4
implies that for every
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
, we have

\mathcal{L}(e_{[n-1]\setminus[j]})=n.

Since

\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(e_{[n]\setminus[j]})=e_{([n]\setminus[j])\setminus[i]}=e_{[n]\setminus[\max\{i,j\}]},

for
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, we obtain

\mathcal{L}(a_{n}e_{[n-1]\setminus[j]})=\mathcal{L}(e_{[n]\setminus[j]})=\min\{i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}\mid e_{[n]\setminus[\max\{i,j\}]}=e_{[n]\setminus[i]}\}=j.

Hence, Proposition
5.11
does not hold for
i=n
.
In contrast to the case
i<n
, left multiplication by
a_{n}
can strictly decrease the value of
\mathcal{L}
.

Definition 5.13
.

We define the function
g:\{0,1,\dots,n\}\times[n]\to\{0,1,\dots,n\}
by

g(i,j)=\begin{cases}i-1,&i=j,\\
i,&i\neq j.\end{cases}

We can rewrite Theorem
5.10
in terms of
g
.
Let
x\in K_{n}
and
i\in[n]
.
Then Theorem
5.10
states that

\mathcal{L}(xa_{i})=g(\mathcal{L}(x),i).

(7)

Let
x\in K_{n}
.
If
x=e
, then
\mathcal{L}(x)=n
by Lemma
5.4
.
Otherwise, write
x=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\dots a_{i_{k}}
.
Set
l_{0}=\mathcal{L}(e)=n
.
By (
7
), we have

\mathcal{L}(a_{i_{1}})=\mathcal{L}(ea_{i_{1}})=g(\mathcal{L}(e),i_{1})=g(l_{0},i_{1}).

We denote
l_{1}=\mathcal{L}(a_{i_{1}})
and then we further have

\mathcal{L}(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}})=g(\mathcal{L}(a_{i_{1}}),i_{2})=g(l_{1},i_{2}).

Now let
l_{2}=\mathcal{L}(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}})
and we have

\mathcal{L}(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}a_{i_{3}})=g(\mathcal{L}(a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}),i_{3})=g(l_{2},i_{3}),

and so on.

We have constructed a finite sequence
l_{0},l_{1},\dots,l_{k}
defined recursively by

l_{j}=\begin{cases}n,&j=0,\\
g(l_{j-1},i_{j}),&j=1,2,\dots,k.\end{cases}

Formula (
7
) implies that

\mathcal{L}(x)=l_{k}.

Hence this provides an algorithm to compute
\mathcal{L}(x)
.

For
x\in K_{n}
, we denote

\mathcal{A}_{x}=\{i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}\mid\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[i]}\},\quad\mathcal{B}_{x}=\{i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}\mid xe_{[i]}=f\}.

(8)

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
x\in K_{n}
. Then
\mathcal{A}_{x}=\mathcal{B}_{x}
.

Proof.

If
xe_{[i]}=f
, then by applying
\overline{\partial}_{[i]}
and using
\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(e_{[i]})=e
, we obtain

\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(x)=\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(x)\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(e_{[i]})=\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(xe_{[i]})=\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(f)=e_{[n]\setminus[i]},

and hence
\mathcal{B}_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{x}
.

If
\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[i]}
, then Lemma
4.8
with
j=i
implies

xe_{[i]}=\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(x)e_{[i]}=e_{[n]\setminus[i]}e_{[i]}=e_{[n]}=f,

which proves
\mathcal{A}_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{x}
.
Hence
\mathcal{A}_{x}=\mathcal{B}_{x}
.
∎

Recall the function
m:K_{n}\to\{0,1,\dots,n\}
defined in (
2
).

Theorem 5.15
.

We have that
\mathcal{L}=m
.

Proof.

Let
x\in K_{n}
.
By the definition of
\mathcal{L}(x)
, we have
\mathcal{L}(x)=\min\mathcal{A}_{x}
.
By the definition of
m(x)
, we have
m(x)=\min\mathcal{B}_{x}
.
By Theorem
5.14
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{x}=\mathcal{B}_{x}
, and hence
\mathcal{L}(x)=m(x)
.
∎

6
Sequences of Products

6.1
Sequences of Partial Products

In this subsection, we study sequences of partial products in
K_{n}
.
We prove that every such sequence is eventually constant, and in certain cases we determine its eventual value.

Let
(x_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence with values in
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
We define the sequence of partial products
(s_{j})_{j\geq 0}
by

s_{j}=x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{j}

for
j\geq 1
and set
s_{0}=e
.
Then
s_{1}=x_{1}
and
s_{j}=s_{j-1}x_{j}
for
j\geq 1
.

Proposition 6.1
.

For every sequence
(x_{j})_{j\geq 1}
with values in
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
, there exists
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

s_{m}=s_{m_{0}},

for all
m\geq m_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose
s_{j}\neq s_{j-1}
for some
j\geq 1
.
Since
s_{j}=s_{j-1}x_{j}
, we have

s_{j-1}x_{j}\neq s_{j-1}.

Applying the antiautomorphism
\tau
, we obtain

\tau(x_{j})\tau(s_{j-1})\neq\tau(s_{j-1}).

Applying Lemma
3.4
with
x=\tau(x_{j})
and
y=\tau(s_{j-1})
, we get

h(\tau(s_{j}))=h(\tau(x_{j})\tau(s_{j-1}))<h(\tau(s_{j-1})).

Since
h
takes values in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there can only be finitely many indices
j\geq 1
such that
s_{j}\neq s_{j-1}
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

For a sequence
(x_{j})_{j\geq 1}
, we define

M_{(x_{j})}=\{i\in[n]\mid x_{k}=a_{i}\text{ for some }k\geq 1\},

and

N_{(x_{j})}=\{i\in[n]\mid x_{k}=a_{i}\text{ for infinitely many }k\geq 1\}.

Clearly,
N_{(x_{j})}\subseteq M_{(x_{j})}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
(x_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence with values in
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
, and assume that
N_{(x_{j})}=M_{(x_{j})}
.
Then there exists
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

s_{m}=e_{M_{(x_{j})}},

for all
m\geq m_{0}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.1
, there exists
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
s_{m}=s_{m_{0}}
for all
m\geq m_{0}
.
Fix
k\in M_{(x_{j})}
.
Since
M_{(x_{j})}=N_{(x_{j})}
, there exists
i>m_{0}
such that
x_{i}=a_{k}
.
Since
i-1\geq m_{0}
, we have

s_{m_{0}}=s_{i}=s_{i-1}x_{i}=s_{m_{0}}a_{k}.

Since
k\in M_{(x_{j})}
is arbitrary, we have

s_{m_{0}}a_{k}=s_{m_{0}},

for all
k\in M_{(x_{j})}
.
Hence, for all
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{l}\in M_{(x_{j})}
, we have

\displaystyle s_{m_{0}}

\displaystyle=s_{m_{0}}a_{i_{l}}

\displaystyle=s_{m_{0}}a_{i_{l-1}}a_{i_{l}}

\displaystyle=\cdots

\displaystyle=s_{m_{0}}a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\dots a_{i_{l}}.

(9)

Using the content map yields

c(s_{m_{0}})=c(s_{m_{0}})\cup\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{l}\},

and hence
M_{(x_{j})}\subseteq c(s_{m_{0}})
.
By the definition of
M_{(x_{j})}
, we have
c(s_{m_{0}})\subseteq M_{(x_{j})}
, and hence
c(s_{m_{0}})=M_{(x_{j})}
.
Hence, using (
9
), we obtain

s_{m_{0}}=s_{m_{0}}^{k^{\prime}},

for all
k^{\prime}\geq 1
.
Since
c(s_{m_{0}})=M_{(x_{j})}
and
M_{(x_{j})}\neq\emptyset
, by Lemma
3.2
, we further have

s_{m_{0}}=s_{m_{0}}^{|c(s_{m_{0}})|}=e_{M_{(x_{j})}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
(x_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence with values in
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
and assume that
N_{(x_{j})}=[n]
.
Then there exists
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

s_{m}=f,

for all
m\geq m_{0}
.

Proof.

If
N_{(x_{j})}=[n]
, then
N_{(x_{j})}=M_{(x_{j})}=[n]
.
The result then follows from Theorem
6.2
.
∎

6.2
Sequences of Random Partial Products

In Subsection
6.1
, we showed that every sequence of partial products in
K_{n}
is eventually constant.
In this subsection, we study such sequences in a probabilistic setting
and investigate the rate at which they attain their final value.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, and let
(X_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be
a sequence of random variables taking values in
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
For each
j\geq 1
, let
I_{j}
be the random variable with values in
[n]
such that

X_{j}=a_{I_{j}}.

Let
(P_{j})_{j\geq 0}
be random variables taking values in
K_{n}
, defined by
P_{0}=e
and

P_{j}=X_{1}X_{2}\dots X_{j}=P_{j-1}X_{j},

for
j\geq 1
, where the product is taken in
K_{n}
.
We define the hitting time of state
f
of the random process
(P_{j})_{j\geq 0}
as

T=\inf\{j\geq 0\mid P_{j}=f\}.

The goal of this subsection is to understand the random variable
T
.
Also observe that if
P_{j_{0}}=f
for some
j_{0}\geq 1
, then
P_{j}=f
for all
j\geq j_{0}
.
In other words, if the process
(P_{j})_{j\geq 0}
reaches state
f
, then it remains there indefinitely.

We define random variables
(L_{j})_{j\geq 0}
with values in
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
by

L_{j}=\mathcal{L}(P_{j}),

for all
j\geq 0
.
Then (
7
) implies that, for each
j\geq 1
,

L_{j}=\mathcal{L}(P_{j})=\mathcal{L}(P_{j-1}X_{j})=\mathcal{L}(P_{j-1}a_{I_{j}})=g(\mathcal{L}(P_{j-1}),I_{j})=g(L_{j-1},I_{j}).

(10)

For each
i\in[n]
, set

p_{i}=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=a_{i})\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{i}=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\neq a_{i})=1-p_{i}.

Theorem 6.4
.

Assume that
(X_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are independent and identically distributed.
Then
(L_{j})_{j\geq 0}
is a Markov chain with state space
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.
The initial distribution vector
\pi
is

\pi=(0,0,\dots,0,1),

and the transition probability matrix
\mathcal{P}
is given by

\mathcal{P}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\\
p_{1}&q_{1}&0&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\\
0&p_{2}&q_{2}&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&p_{n-1}&q_{n-1}&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&p_{n}&q_{n}\end{pmatrix},

(11)

where the rows and columns are indexed by
0,1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

Since
(X_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are independent and identically distributed, so are
(I_{j})_{j\geq 1}
.
Since for each
j\geq 1
,
I_{j}
is independent of
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{j-1}
and
L_{0},L_{1},\dots,L_{j-1}
are functions of
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{j-1}
, it follows that
I_{j}
is independent of
L_{0},L_{1},\dots,L_{j-1}
.
Hence, (
10
) implies that
(L_{j})_{j\geq 0}
is a Markov chain.

Since
L_{0}=\mathcal{L}(P_{0})=\mathcal{L}(e)=n
is constant, we have
\mathbb{P}(L_{0}=n)=1
, and hence the initial distribution vector
\pi
is

\pi=(0,0,\dots,0,1).

From (
10
) and the fact that
(I_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are identically distributed, it also follows that the transition probability from state
i
to state
j
is given by

\mathcal{P}_{i,j}=\mathbb{P}(g(i,I_{1})=j).

(12)

If
i=0
, then
g(i,I_{1})=0
, since
I_{1}
takes values in
[n]
, and hence
\mathcal{P}_{0,0}=1
.
Now assume that
i>0
.
By the definition of
g
, we can write

g(i,I_{1})=i-\mathbbm{1}_{\{I_{1}=i\}}.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(g(i,I_{1})=j)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(i-\mathbbm{1}_{\{I_{1}=i\}}=j)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{I_{1}=i\}}=i-j).

Since
\{I_{1}=i\}=\{X_{1}=a_{i}\}
, we get

\mathcal{P}_{i,j}=\mathbb{P}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{I_{1}=i\}}=i-j)=\mathbb{P}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{1}=a_{i}\}}=i-j).

By noting that
\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{1}=a_{i}\}}
takes values in
\{0,1\}
with probability
1
, we further have

\mathcal{P}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}0,&i-j\notin\{0,1\},\\
\mathbb{P}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{1}=a_{i}\}}=1),&i-j=1,\\
\mathbb{P}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{1}=a_{i}\}}=0),&i-j=0,\end{cases}=\begin{cases}0,&i-j\notin\{0,1\},\\
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=a_{i}),&i-j=1,\\
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\neq a_{i}),&i-j=0.\end{cases}

Therefore,

\mathcal{P}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}0,&j>i\text{ or }j<i-1,\\
p_{i},&j=i-1,\\
q_{i},&j=i.\end{cases}

This proves (
11
) and concludes the proof.
∎

For
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, define

T_{i}=\inf\{j\geq 0\mid L_{j}=i\},

and also set

V_{i}=T_{i-1}-T_{i},

for
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
In the following proof, we show that all
V_{i}
are well-defined under the additional assumption

p_{j}>0
for all
j=1,2,\dots,n
.

Proposition 6.5
.

Assume that
(X_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are independent and identically distributed and assume
p_{i}>0
for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
Then
V_{n},V_{n-1},\dots,V_{1}
are independent and
V_{i}
is a geometric random variable with success probability
p_{i}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.4
,
(L_{j})_{j\geq 0}
is a Markov chain with transition probability matrix
\mathcal{P}
.
By (
10
), we have

L_{j}\leq L_{j-1}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{j-1}-L_{j}\in\{0,1\},

for all
j\geq 1
.
Hence at each step, the chain either remains in the previous state or decreases its state by exactly one.
Since
p_{i}=\mathcal{P}_{i,i-1}>0
, we have
\mathcal{P}_{i,i}=q_{i}<1
, and thus
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{i,i}^{k}=0
.
Hence the probability that the chain stays at state
i\in[n]
indefinitely is zero.
Hence, every
T_{i}
is almost surely finite.
For
i\in[n]
and
k\geq 1
, we further have

\{V_{i}=k\}=\{T_{i-1}-T_{i}=k\}

is the event that once
(L_{j})_{j\geq 0}
reaches state
i
, it remains there for exactly
k-1
steps, and then jumps to state
i-1
.
Therefore, by the strong Markov property, we have

\mathbb{P}(V_{i}=k)=(\mathcal{P}_{i,i})^{k-1}\mathcal{P}_{i,i-1}=q_{i}^{k-1}p_{i}.

(13)

Hence
V_{i}
is a geometric random variable with success probability
p_{i}
.

Now we show independence.
Let
k_{n},k_{n-1},\dots,k_{1}\geq 1
.
Then the event

\{V_{n}=k_{n},V_{n-1}=k_{n-1},\dots,V_{1}=k_{1}\}

is the event that
(L_{j})_{j\geq 0}
starts in state
n
, remains there for
k_{n}-1
steps, and then jumps down to state
n-1
.
Then it stays at state
n-1
for
k_{n-1}-1
steps, and then it jumps down to state
n-2
, and so on.
When it reaches state
1
, it remains there for
k_{1}-1
steps and then jumps to
0
.
Hence,
\mathbb{P}(V_{n}=k_{n},V_{n-1}=k_{n-1},\dots,V_{1}=k_{1})
is equal to

(\mathcal{P}_{n,n})^{k_{n}-1}\mathcal{P}_{n,n-1}(\mathcal{P}_{n-1,n-1})^{k_{n-1}-1}\mathcal{P}_{n-1,n-2}\dots(\mathcal{P}_{1,1})^{k_{1}-1}\mathcal{P}_{1,0},

which is by (
13
) equal to

\mathbb{P}(V_{n}=k_{n})\mathbb{P}(V_{n-1}=k_{n-1})\dots\mathbb{P}(V_{1}=k_{1}).

Hence,
V_{n},V_{n-1},\dots,V_{1}
are independent as well.
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

Assume that
(X_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are independent and identically distributed and assume
p_{i}>0
for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
Then
T
is distributed as a sum of
n
independent geometric random variables with success probabilities
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n}
.

Proof.

Since
L_{j}=\mathcal{L}(P_{j})
for
j\geq 0
, Lemma
5.2
implies

P_{j}=f\iff L_{j}=0,

which implies

T=\inf\{j\geq 0\mid P_{j}=f\}=\inf\{j\geq 0\mid L_{j}=0\}=T_{0}.

Since
L_{0}=n
, we have
T_{n}=0
, and hence

T=T_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(T_{i-1}-T_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}.

By Proposition
6.5
, the random variables
V_{n},V_{n-1},\dots,V_{1}
are independent and
V_{i}
is a geometric random variable with success probability
p_{i}
.
This finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 6.7
.

Assume that
(X_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are independent and identically distributed and assume
p_{i}>0
for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
Then

\mathbb{E}[T]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{-1}.

In particular, if
(X_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are uniformly distributed on
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
, then

\mathbb{E}[T]=n^{2}.

Proof.

This result follows from Theorem
6.6
and the well-known fact that if
X
is a geometric
random variable with success probability
p
, then
\mathbb{E}[X]=p^{-1}
.
∎

7
K_{n}
as a Metric Space

In Section
5
, we defined the level function
\mathcal{L}
.
We can rewrite its definition as

\mathcal{L}(x)=\min\{i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}\mid\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(x)=\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(f)\}.

This gives rise to the definition of the map
d:K_{n}\times K_{n}\to\{0,1,\dots,n\}
:

d(x,y)=\min\{i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}\mid\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(x)=\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(y)\}.

(14)

This map is well-defined, since
\overline{\partial}_{[n]}(x)=\overline{\partial}_{[n]}(y)
for any
x,y\in K_{n}
.
We also have
\mathcal{L}(x)=d(x,f)
.

Theorem 7.1
.

The pair
(K_{n},d)
is an ultrametric space.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in K_{n}
.
We of course have
d(x,y)\geq 0
.
We have
d(x,y)=0
if and only if
\overline{\partial}_{[0]}(x)=\overline{\partial}_{[0]}(y)
.
Since
x=\overline{\partial}_{[0]}(x)
and
y=\overline{\partial}_{[0]}(y)
, the condition
d(x,y)=0
is equivalent to
x=y
.

By the definition of
d
, we have
d(x,y)=d(y,x)
.

Let
z\in K_{n}
.
We set
i=d(x,z)
,
j=d(z,y)
, and
k=\max\{i,j\}
.
Since
[i],[j]\subseteq[k]
, we have

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}_{[k]}(x)

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[k]}(\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(x))

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[k]}(\overline{\partial}_{[i]}(z))

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[k]}(z)

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[k]}(\overline{\partial}_{[j]}(z))

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[k]}(\overline{\partial}_{[j]}(y))

\displaystyle=\overline{\partial}_{[k]}(y).

Hence we obtain

d(x,y)\leq k=\max\{d(x,z),d(z,y)\},

which concludes the proof.
∎

For
a\in K_{n}
and
r\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, we denote by

\displaystyle B(a,r)

\displaystyle=\{x\in K_{n}\mid d(a,x)\leq r\},

\displaystyle S(a,r)

\displaystyle=\{x\in K_{n}\mid d(a,x)=r\},

the metric ball and metric sphere with centre
a
and radius
r
, respectively.

Lemma 7.2
.

We have that
x\in B(f,r)
if and only if

xe_{[r]}=f
.

Proof.

If
x\in B(f,r)
, then
\mathcal{L}(x)\leq r
and by Corollary
5.3
, we have

\overline{\partial}_{[r]}(x)=e_{[n]\setminus[r]}.

By Theorem
5.14
, we then have
xe_{[r]}=f
.

If
xe_{[r]}=f
, then
m(x)\leq r
, and hence by Theorem
5.15
, we have
\mathcal{L}(x)\leq r
, and hence
x\in B(f,r)
.
∎

Corollary 7.3
.

We have that
|B(f,1)|=1+|K_{n-1}|
and consequently
|S(f,1)|=|K_{n-1}|
.

Proof.

By Lemma
7.2
, we have

B(f,1)=\{x\in K_{n}\mid xa_{1}=f\}.

By Proposition
3.6
, it follows that
|B(f,1)|=1+|K_{n-1}|
.
Since
S(f,1)=B(f,1)\setminus\{f\}
, we have
|S(f,1)|=|K_{n-1}|
.
∎

Proposition 7.4
.

We have that
S(f,n)=\{x\in K_{n}\mid c(x)\subseteq[n-1]\}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemma
5.4
.
∎

Corollary 7.5
.

We have
|S(f,n)|=|K_{n-1}|
.

Proof.

The proof is immediate from Proposition
7.4
and the
fact that

\{x\in K_{n}\mid c(x)\subseteq[n-1]\}
is in bijection with
K_{n-1}
.
∎
