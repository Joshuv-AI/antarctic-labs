Machine Learning‐Driven Prediction of Coronary Artery Disease Risk Based on UK Biobank Plasma Proteomics
Authors: Yuezhong Huang, Xiaoli Chen, Hao Zhang, Zhanpei Bai, Yifan Shen, Daishan Zheng, Longyu Fang, Hongzi Song, Hebei Gao, Fan Lu, Xiu‐Feng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery disease (CAD) is a leading global cause of mortality, yet the predictive accuracy of conventional risk models is limited. Here, we integrate conventional risk factors, polygenic risk scores, and large‐scale proteomics to develop a unified model for enhanced CAD risk prediction.MethodsUsing data from UK Biobank, participants with plasma proteomics and genetic risk data were included after excluding prevalent CAD. Participants from England were split into training (n=32 330) and internal validation (n=13 857) sets, and Scotland/Wales participants formed an external validation set (n=5775). Incident CAD was ascertained from linked health records. A 202‐protein proteomic risk score was derived by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator Cox regression, and CatBoost models were trained using conventional risk factors alone and with incremental addition of polygenic risk scores and protein proteomic risk scores; Shapley Additive Explanations‐guided forward selection identified a compact protein panel.ResultsAcross cohorts, the median age was 58 years and ∼45% were men. Protein proteomic risk score was dose‐dependently associated with CAD risk. Compared with conventional risk factors alone, integrating polygenic risk scores and protein proteomic risk scores improved discrimination, with the area under the curve increasing from 0.750 (95% CI, 0.732–0.767) to 0.789 (95% CI, 0.772–0.805) in internal validation and from 0.717 (95% CI, 0.683–0.750) to 0.762 (95% CI, 0.732–0.791) in external validation. A 9‐protein panel (GDF15 [growth differentiation factor 15], MMP12 [matrix metalloproteinase 12], NPPB [natriuretic peptide B], PGF [placental growth factor], REN [renin], ADGRG2 [adhesion G‐protein coupled receptor], ACE2 [angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2], CDCP1 [CUB domain‐containing protein 1], CXCL17 [C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 17)]) captured most proteomic predictive information.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that integrating conventional risk factors, polygenic risk scores, and proteomic data improves CAD risk prediction. This study highlights the utility of proteomics in precision cardiovascular medicine and simplified risk stratification tools.

Coronary artery disease (CAD), the predominant subtype of cardiovascular disease, is the leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide. 1 , 2 According to the Global Burden of Disease 2019 study, global CAD cases nearly doubled from 271 to 523 million between 1990 and 2019, with related deaths increasing from 12.1 to 18.6 million. 1 In light of the limitations of conventional risk factor‐based models in predicting CAD risk, the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines recommend incorporation of novel biomarkers and the use of multiomics technologies to enable more precise prevention and control strategies. 3

Although >300 cardiovascular disease risk prediction models based on traditional risk factors have been developed and validated across various populations, most suffer from limitations such as inconsistent prediction time frames, inadequate reporting of calibration and discrimination, and insufficient external validation. 4 Several studies show that polygenic risk scores (PRS) and lifestyle factors independently and additively contribute to CAD risk. 5 , 6 , 7 These findings suggest the need to jointly consider genetic and environmental factors in CAD risk assessment. However, despite its potential to capture dynamic physiological states, proteomic data remain underused in risk prediction due to limited data availability. 8

Recent advances in large‐scale proteomics have enhanced our understanding of CAD pathogenesis. A genome‐wide protein quantitative trait loci study in 54 219 UK Biobank participants identified >14 000 gene–protein associations, revealing key proteins involved in cardiovascular metabolism and inflammation. 9 These findings offer a valuable resource for investigating the risk factors of CAD. It is also noted that observational associations based on clinical factors and genetic variants are insufficient to inform precision medicine strategies. 10 Thus, integrating proteomics data is essential for early risk identification and mechanism exploration of CAD.

Although previous studies have assessed CAD risk using traditional risk factors, genetic risk scores, or proteomic data individually, relatively few have systematically integrated multiomics information within a unified predictive framework. Moreover, only a limited number of studies have explicitly contrasted alternative proteomic representations, validation strategies, and clinical positioning. To clarify the methodological and conceptual context of the present work, we summarize and compare our study with several recent proteomics‐ and PRS‐based CAD risk prediction studies (Table S1). 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Building on this context, our study uses large‐scale plasma proteomics from the UKB‐PPP (UK Biobank Pharmaco‐Proteomics Project) together with conventional risk factors (CRFs) and polygenic risk to develop an integrated framework for early CAD risk stratification. In addition to establishing prediction models, we aim to characterize what the proteomic signals represent biologically by conducting functional enrichment and protein interaction analyses and to explore potential translational relevance through systematic drug target mapping of CAD‐associated proteins. By integrating risk prediction with biological interpretation and translational prioritization, this study provides a more interpretable and clinically oriented evidence base for multiomics risk stratification.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data used in this study were obtained from the UKB under a licensed agreement and are therefore not publicly available. Access to the data set requires a formal application through the UKB's official process, which can be initiated at https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/use‐our‐data/apply‐for‐access/. This study was conducted using data accessed under UKB application number 97441.

Study Population

This study is based on the UKB, a large prospective cohort covering England, Wales, and Scotland, designed to explore the epidemiological characteristics of common diseases in the middle‐aged and older population. 15 , 16 A total of 502 493 participants underwent baseline assessment at 22 standardized centers across the United Kingdom. For model development and validation, participants with available plasma proteomic data were stratified into 3 cohorts. Specifically, individuals from England were randomly split into a training set (70%) and an internal validation set (30%), and participants from Scotland and Wales were assigned to an external validation set to ensure geographic independence of the testing population (Figure S1).

All study procedures followed the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Northwest Multi‐Center Research Ethics Committee (reference: 11/NW/0382). Participants provided written informed consent before baseline assessment, and biospecimen collection and analysis followed strict standard operating procedures. By integrating multidimensional health information—spanning social and behavioral characteristics, clinical biomarkers, genomics, and metabolomics—the UKB offers a unique platform for dissecting the biosocial interaction mechanisms underlying complex diseases.

Proteomic Profiling

Proteomic data were derived from the UKB‐PPP, a consortium of 13 global biopharmaceutical companies. High‐throughput proteomic profiling was conducted on baseline plasma samples from 54 306 UKB participants, collected between 2006 and 2010, using the Olink Explore 3072 platform based on Proximity Extension Assay technology. All blood samples were collected following standardized procedures. 9 , 17 To ensure data quality, the research team implemented multilevel quality control: First, assays with technical warnings or quality control failures were excluded (<1% of the original data set). Subsequently, outlier samples were identified and removed through standardized quality assurance pipeline. 9 , 18 Additionally, proteins with >20% missing values (n=13) were excluded based on strict data completeness criteria, resulting in 2910 proteins retained for downstream analyses (Table S2).

Ascertainment of CAD Outcomes

CAD events were ascertained by integrating multi‐source health data, including hospital admission records, death registration systems, primary care electronic medical records, and self‐reported information from the UKB. Case identification was conducted using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes I21 (acute myocardial infarction), I22 (subsequent myocardial infarction), I23 (complications of myocardial infarction), I24 (other acute ischemic heart diseases), and I25.2 (old myocardial infarction). Participants were followed from the date of baseline assessment until the earliest of the following events: CAD diagnosis, death, loss to follow‐up, or the region‐specific data censoring date—October 31, 2022 for England, August 31, 2022 for Scotland, and May 31, 2022 for Wales. To ensure accurate identification of incident cases, individuals with a known CAD diagnosis before the baseline visit (n=815) were excluded. Incident CAD is defined as a medical record that first occurs during the follow‐up period and meets the ICD‐10 diagnostic criteria.

Assessment of Covariates

Covariates included demographic, metabolic, and behavioral factors, defined as follows. Age and sex (male/female) were obtained from baseline registration data. Body mass index (kg/m2) and the Townsend Deprivation Index were included as continuous variables. Smoking status was classified into 4 groups, including current smoker, former smoker, never smoker, and unknown smoking status. Alcohol consumption frequency was grouped as high (≥3 times/week), moderate (1–2 times/week), and low/nondrinking (≤3 times/month or never). Regular physical activity was defined as engaging in moderate‐intensity exercise >4 days per week or vigorous‐intensity exercise >3 days per week. Family history of CAD was determined by the presence of clinically diagnosed coronary heart disease (ICD‐10: I20–I25) in either parent. Racial background was categorized based on baseline UK Biobank records and included as a dichotomous covariate (White versus Other). Baseline diagnoses of diabetes (ICD‐10: E10‐E14) and hypertension (ICD‐10: I10‐I15) were confirmed through electronic medical records.

Calculation of CAD Protein Risk Score

In the training set, we used a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression model to identify proteins significantly associated with CAD, adjusting for covariates as defined in the Assessment of Covariates section. The process involved the following steps. First, we performed imputation for missing values across the 2910 proteins (after excluding 13 proteins with >20% missingness). Next, normalized protein expression values of the proteins, together with the aforementioned covariates, were entered into the model. The model was fitted using the glmnet package in R, with the LASSO penalty applied only to protein variables, whereas coefficients for the covariates were left unpenalized to ensure they were retained in the model. 19 Tenfold cross‐validation was used to identify the optimal penalty parameter (λ) that minimized the partial likelihood deviance. A total of 202 proteins with nonzero coefficients were selected. The CAD protein risk score (ProRS) was then calculated using the following formula: ProRS=∑i=1kCoefProteini×Proteini where CoefProteini is the regression coefficient of the ith protein from the LASSO model, and Proteini is its corresponding normalized protein expression value. Although covariates were adjusted for during model training, the final ProRS calculation includes only protein terms to independently quantify the proteomic contribution to CAD risk. To evaluate the dose–response relationship between ProRS and CAD events, restricted cubic spline curves were plotted in both the internal and external validation cohorts, adjusted for covariates.

Polygenic Risk Scores

The CAD PRS was derived from the standardized PRS set released by the UKB (data field ID: 26227), based on summary statistics from previous genome‐wide association studies of CAD (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/refer.cgi?id=5202). The score accounts for linkage disequilibrium using genotype data from the 1000 Genomes Project and adjusts for population stratification via principal component analysis. 20 The PRS incorporates weighted effect sizes of millions of single‐nucleotide variants, with weights derived from external genome‐wide association studies identifying variants significantly associated with CAD. 20 The score computation was performed independently by Genomics PLC (Oxford, United Kingdom) within the framework of the UKB project to ensure reproducibility and transparency. The resulting PRS was included as a standardized continuous variable in downstream analyses to assess the independent contribution of genetic risk to CAD events.

Prediction Model Construction and Evaluation

In the training set, 202 candidate proteins identified by LASSO Cox regression were first entered into a CatBoost classifier to estimate their relative importance. 21 The proteins were then ranked according to their Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values. Following this ranking, a forward sequential selection procedure was performed by adding proteins to the model one at a time, starting from the highest‐ranked protein. After each addition, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of the updated model was compared with that of the previous model using the DeLong test. 22 The selection procedure was terminated when the AUC improvement was not statistically significant (P≥0.05) for 4 consecutive steps. The protein subset retained at that point was defined as the final ProtProfs. SHAP values were further used to visualize each selected protein's positive or negative contribution to CAD risk within the CatBoost classifier, illustrating its risk‐enhancing or protective effect. 23 To assess the stability of protein selection, we conducted a bootstrap resampling analysis within the training set using 100 iterations. In each resample, the same SHAP‐guided forward selection procedure was applied, and the selection frequency of each protein was calculated.

Based on CRFs, genetic information, and proteomic indicators, 6 CatBoost classification models were constructed and compared: Model 1 included only CRFs (age, sex, ethnicity, body mass index, Townsend Deprivation Index, smoking status, alcohol consumption frequency, physical activity, family history of CAD, and metabolic disease status); Model 2 added the PRS to Model 1; Model 3 added the ProRS to Model 1; Model 4 added the forward‐selected ProtProfs to Model 1; Model 5 incorporated both PRS and ProRS into Model 1; and Model 6 incorporated both PRS and ProtProfs into Model 1. All models were tuned using 10‐fold cross‐validation in the training set and evaluated in both the internal and external validation cohorts for their discrimination and calibration performance over the full follow‐up period for CAD events. Evaluation metrics included AUC at the Youden index, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1‐score. Receiver operating characteristic and decision curve analyses were also plotted. To assess heterogeneity and model generalizability across key demographic groups, we additionally evaluated model performance stratified by sex and by age group (<60 versus ≥60 years) in both the internal and external validation cohorts, reporting the same discrimination and classification metrics with 95% CIs. In addition, participants were stratified into quintiles based on predicted risk scores, and cumulative incidence curves, calibration plots, and survival curves were generated for each quintile to further evaluate the long‐term utility and robustness of models with different feature combinations.

Statistical Analysis

Participants were first stratified into groups with and without CAD based on the occurrence of events during follow‐up, and their baseline characteristics were compared. Continuous variables with nonnormal distributions were described using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using the chi‐square test. For proteomic data, protein targets with a missingness rate >20% (n=13) were excluded, and the normalized protein expression values of the remaining proteins were imputed using the missForest package in R. For functional annotation, we used the clusterProfiler package to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on 202 CAD‐risk proteins screened through LASSO Cox regression in 3 ontologies: biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components, respectively. Subsequently, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using STRING, visualized in Cytoscape, and node degree centrality was calculated with the CytoNCA plugin to identify key hub proteins. 24 To assess potential therapeutic relevance, DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/) was queried to identify whether any of the associated proteins have approved or investigational cardiovascular drug‐target annotations. For matched entries, UniProt identifiers, drug status, cardiovascular indications, and pharmacologic mechanisms were recorded. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.4.1) and Python (v3.12). CIs for performance metrics of the CatBoost prediction models were estimated using 2000 bootstrap iterations. Differences in AUC between models were assessed using the DeLong test, and Kaplan–Meier curves were compared using the log‐rank test. All statistical tests were 2 tailed, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics of Study Participants

This study included a training set (N=32 330), an internal validation set (N=13 857), and an external validation set (N=5775), with a median follow‐up duration of 13.70 years. As shown in Table1, the 3 cohorts were broadly similar in key baseline characteristics, including median age (training set: 58.00 years [IQR, 50.00–64.00]; internal validation set: 58.00 years [IQR, 50.00–64.00]; external validation set: 57.00 years [IQR, 50.00–63.00]), sex distribution (male: 45.5%, 45.9%, and 45.0%, respectively), and body mass index (26.72 [IQR, 24.16–29.82], 26.72 [IQR, 24.12–29.83], and 26.91 [IQR, 24.35–30.18], respectively). The distributions of smoking status, alcohol consumption, regular physical activity, and metabolic diseases (diabetes and hypertension) were also broadly comparable between the training and internal validation sets. Participants in the external validation set were slightly younger on average and had a marginally higher prevalence of family history of CAD (20.2% versus 17.4%/18.4%).

Dose–Response Relationship Between the CAD Protein Risk Score and Event Risk

Using LASSO Cox regression, 202 proteins significantly associated with CAD risk were identified, comprising 91 risk‐enhancing and 111 protective proteins (Figure S2; Table S3), and the ProRS was constructed accordingly. In the internal validation set (Figure 1A), after adjusting for conventional covariates, ProRS was significantly positively associated with CAD event risk throughout the full follow‐up period (P‐overall <0.001), and there was a nonlinear effect (P‐nonlinear=0.042). Specifically, CAD risk increased gradually as ProRS shifted from low to moderate levels, then rose exponentially once ProRS exceeded zero. In the external validation cohort (Figure 1B), a similarly significant positive association between ProRS and CAD risk was observed (P‐overall <0.001), but the nonlinear test was not significant (P‐nonlinear=0.168), indicating that the ProRS‐risk relationship was approximately linear in the independent cohort.

Predictive Performance of Integrated Genetic and Proteomic Features

Based on the 202 CAD‐associated proteins identified, we employed the CatBoost classifier to construct a predictive model and applied forward sequential selection to identify the 9 most predictive proteins: (GDF15 [growth differentiation factor 15], MMP12 [matrix metalloproteinase 12], NPPB [natriuretic peptide B], PGF [placental growth factor], REN [renin], ADGRG2 [adhesion G‐protein coupled receptor], ACE2 [angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2], CDCP1 [CUB domain‐containing protein 1], CXCL17 [C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 17)]) (Figure S3). SHAP value analysis revealed that, except for ADGRG2, which was negatively associated with the CAD risk, the remaining 8 proteins were all positively associated with the CAD risk, with GDF15 and MMP12 contributing the most (Figure 2). In the bootstrap analysis, these proteins were repeatedly selected across resamples, with particularly high selection frequencies for GDF15 and MMP12, supporting the robustness of the 9‐protein signature (Tables S4 and S5).

We then compared the discrimination and decision performance of s6ix CatBoost classification models in both internal and external validation sets. As illustrated by the receiver operating characteristic curves (Figure 3A and 3C) and decision curve analyses (Figure 3B and 3D), incorporating proteomic and genetic features significantly improved both model discrimination and net clinical benefit. In the internal validation cohort, the AUC of the conventional risk factor model (Model 1) was 0.750 (95% CI, 0.732–0.767). The inclusion of PRS (Model 2), ProRS (Model 3), ProtProfs (Model 4), PRS+ProRS (Model 5), and PRS+ProtProfs (Model 6) led to AUCs of 0.768 (95% CI, 0.751–0.785), 0.780 (95% CI, 0.763–0.796), 0.777 (95% CI 0.759–0.794), 0.789 (95% CI, 0.772–0.805), and 0.785 (95% CI, 0.767–0.802), respectively, with Model 5 achieving the best performance.

In the external validation cohort, the AUCs for Models 1 to 6 were 0.717 (95% CI, 0.683–0.750), 0.731 (95% CI, 0.700–0.763), 0.750 (95% CI, 0.720–0.780), 0.742 (95% CI, 0.710–0.773), 0.762 (95% CI, 0.732–0.791), and 0.752 (95% CI, 0.720–0.783), again with Model 5 achieving the highest AUC. Notably, across both internal and external validations, the comparisons between Model 3 versus Model 4 and Model 5 versus Model 6 showed nearly equivalent AUC improvements, suggesting that the selected ProtProfs may have captured most of the proteomic information conveyed by the full ProRS. Moreover, the combination of PRS with proteomic features further enhanced CAD risk prediction performance, with additional performance metrics summarized (Table S6). In addition, stratified analyses showed consistently higher discrimination in women than in men across models. Model performance was generally higher in participants aged <60 years than in those aged ≥60 years (Tables S7 and S8). Across both sex and age strata, the integrated models incorporating genetic /or proteomic features maintained superior overall discrimination.

CAD Risk Stratification

To visually assess the risk stratification capabilities of each model, we plotted cumulative incidence curves, risk calibration plots, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves for both the internal and external validation sets. In the internal validation set, the cumulative incidence of CAD in the lowest and highest predicted risk quintiles using the CRFs model was 0.64% and 12.44%, respectively. With the sequential addition of PRS, ProRS, ProtProfs, PRS+ProRS, and PRS+ProtProfs, this range expanded to 0.59% to 13.06%, 0.51% to 13.52%, 0.46% to 13.48%, 0.47% to 14.38%, and 0.42% to 14.06%, respectively (Figure 4A). The calibration plot (Figure 4B) showed that, except for a slight deviation in the CRF model, the predicted risks in the remaining models are highly consistent with the observed risks. The survival curve (Figure 4C) also indicated that the models integrating both genetic and proteomic characteristics provided the most significant risk stratification.

GO Enrichment and PPI Network of CAD‐Associated Risk Proteins

We conducted GO enrichment and PPI network analysis on the 202 CAD‐associated risk proteins to elucidate their biological functions and interaction patterns. The GO enrichment results (Figure 5A) showed significant involvement in several biological processes, including protein processing, phagocytosis, regulation of lipid localization, and lipid transport. In the cellular components ontology, these proteins were mainly localized to the collagen‐containing extracellular matrix, protein‐lipid complexes, plasma lipoprotein particles, and general lipoprotein particles. At the ontological level of molecular functions, they are involved in endopeptidase activity, sulfur compound binding, cytokine activity, and proteoglycan binding (Table S9).

The PPI network constructed based on STRING (Figure 5B) comprised 144 nodes and >1000 high‐confidence edges. Among them, CD8A, IL2 (interleukin‐2), and FCGR3B (Fc gamma receptor IIIb) are located at the core of the network with the highest degree values, and ITGAM (integrin alpha M), IL15, PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog), PLAU (plasminogen activator, urokinase), IL7R, and CCL20 (chemokine C‐C motif ligand 20), are also closely connected. The network formed core modules centered on immune regulation and cytokine signaling, along with peripheral subnetworks related to lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and vascular homeostasis. Taken together, these results highlight the complex interplay among CAD‐associated proteins involved in diverse pathological processes.

Cardiovascular Therapeutic Relevance of Selected Proteins

DrugBank mapping showed that only a small subset of CAD‐associated proteins could be linked to cardiovascular drug‐target or drug‐related annotations (Table S10). REN was annotated as a direct target of renin inhibitors (eg, aliskiren, remikiren), and PCSK9 (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9) was linked to approved inhibitors including monoclonal antibodies (alirocumab, evolocumab) and nucleic acid or protein‐based modalities (inclisiran, lerodalcibep). Additional lipid‐related entries involved LPA (lipoprotein(a); muvalaplin, aminocaproic acid) and LPL (lipoprotein lipase; alipogene tiparvovec, clofibrate, gemfibrozil, omega‐3‐carboxylic acids, semaglutide). ACE2 and PLA2G7 (platelet‐activating factor acetylhydrolase) were associated with experimental or investigational compounds, and NPPB appeared primarily as a biomarker‐associated entry.

DISCUSSION

Based on the large‐scale prospective cohort of the UKB, this study systematically integrated Olink Proximity Extension Assay‐based proteomics with CRFs and PRS to construct a comprehensive CAD risk prediction model. Our results demonstrated that the AUC of the baseline model relying only on CRFs in the internal validation set was 0.750. Upon sequentially adding PRS and proteomics characteristics, the AUC significantly improved to 0.789. In the independent external validation set, the AUC similarly increased from 0.717 to 0.762, clearly demonstrating that the integrating genetic and proteomic data substantially enhances the discriminatory performance of CAD risk prediction models. In addition, stratified analyses suggested that model discrimination tended to be higher among women and participants younger than 60 years, which may reflect that inherited susceptibility and proteomic signatures capture earlier and more specific subclinical processes, whereas increasing multimorbidity and pathway heterogeneity at older ages can attenuate risk separation. 25 , 26 At the same time, the overall performance patterns were broadly consistent across sex and age strata, further supporting the robustness and generalizability of the integrated models across key demographic subgroups.

Further analysis revealed that the ProRS exhibited a significant nonlinear dose–response relationship with CAD risk in the internal validation set (P‐overall <0.001; P‐nonlinear=0.042), while maintaining a linear association in the external cohort (P‐overall <0.001; P‐nonlinear=0.168). This indicates that the ProRS has a robust predictive power for long‐term CAD risk. Among the nine key proteins identified via CatBoost with forward sequence selection, GDF15 and MMP12 had the highest SHAP contribution. Notably, previous studies have shown that GDF15 has superior predictive performance to traditional biomarkers for all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with chronic heart failure, and serves as a biomarker for risk stratification in acute coronary syndrome. 27 , 28 MMP12, which is highly expressed in atherosclerotic plaques, contributes to plaque instability by degrading the extracellular matrix and is considered a key mediator of plaque rupture. 29 Furthermore, ADGRG2 levels were negatively associated with CAD risk in the SHAP analysis, suggesting a potential protective role via adhesion G protein‐coupled receptor‐mediated signaling and cell adhesion pathways. 30 , 31 The observed selection stability across resamples further supports that these proteins capture reproducible proteomic signals relevant to CAD pathophysiology rather than idiosyncratic features of a single split.

Beyond demographic predictors such as age and sex, genomic and proteomic analyses provide complementary biological information that informs long‐term CAD risk. PRS reflects inherited susceptibility that is not reducible to demographic profiles, whereas proteomic markers may capture intermediate biological processes and subclinical disease states (eg, inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, cardiac stress, and neurohormonal activation) that mediate risk but are not fully represented by CRFs. 32 , 33 Consistent with this biological rationale, integrating genetic and proteomic features improved both identification and stratification precision of long‐term CAD risk. In the internal validation set, the difference in cumulative incidence between the lowest and highest risk quintiles of the basic CRFs model was 11.80 percentage points (0.64% versus 12.44%). Following sequential addition of PRS and proteomic characteristics, this difference increased to a maximum of 13.91 percentage points (0.47% versus 14.38%). In the external validation cohort, although calibration consistency was slightly reduced, models integrating omics features still achieved the broadest risk stratification range (0.85% versus 11.22%) and demonstrated the most pronounced risk separation in Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. These findings are consistent with prior studies showing that molecular risk layers can enhance stratification beyond traditional predictors, including the incremental stratification observed by Wurtz et al. 34 with metabolomics and the identification of high‐risk individuals through PRS reported by Khera et al. 32 Importantly, our study provides a more functional biomarker combination through proteomics, providing new tools for hierarchical intervention of precise prevention strategies. Future research should focus on verifying the expression patterns of core proteins in these high‐risk quantiles and exploring the feasibility and cost‐effectiveness of simplified panels based on ProtProfs in clinical screening.

In terms of functional enrichment analysis, results from GO analysis revealed the multilevel synergistic roles of CAD risk proteins in disease pathogenesis. At the biological process level, the enrichment in protein processing and phagocytosis suggests that dysregulation of macrophage‐mediated inflammatory clearance is a core driver of atherosclerosis progression. 35 , 36 Enrichment of lipid localization and transport pathways emphasizes the critical role of plasma lipoprotein homeostasis imbalance in plaque formation and instability. 37 At the cellular component level, enrichment of collagen matrix and lipoprotein particles is highly consistent with plaque matrix remodeling and lipid deposition. For instance, MMP‐12 secreted by plaque‐resident macrophages increases rupture risk by degrading the weak fibrous cap of collagen, 29 and low‐density lipoprotein crystals activate the NLRP3 (NOD‐, LRR‐ and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3) inflammasome, triggering the release of proinflammatory factors and intensifying local inflammation and lipid accumulation. 38 Meanwhile, reduced high‐density lipoprotein levels impair both cholesterol efflux and anti‐inflammatory functions, further promoting the lesion progression. 39

PPI network analysis identified key hub proteins such as CD8A, IL2, and FCGR3B, which were enriched in the immunomodulatory and cytokine signaling modules, highlighting the core role of T cells and Th1 cytokines in maintain plaque stability. 40 Tedgui and Mallat further pointed out that key cytokines such as IL‐2 exacerbate chronic inflammation and promote plaque progression by regulating the activation and apoptosis balance of monocytes/macrophages. 41 The lipid metabolism factors, such as LPL and APOC1 (apoliporprotein C1), enriched on the periphery of the network, as well as the REN/ACE2 axis, confirm the protective role of the ACE2/angiotensin‐(1–7) pathway in regulating vascular tension, inhibiting inflammation, and maintaining endothelial homeostasis. 41 Taken together, our CAD risk protein interaction network not only reproduces the core nodes of the immune‐inflammatory pathway but also reveals the complex synergistic relationship between lipid metabolism and vascular homeostasis regulation.

Building on these pathway‐level patterns from the PPI network, DrugBank target annotations were further cross‐referenced for the selected associated proteins to relate the identified nodes to existing cardiovascular pharmacology. Across the broader set of associated proteins, only a small subset aligned with approved or investigational cardiovascular drug‐target categories. Notably, PCSK9 corresponded to approved low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol lowering therapies, REN mapped to direct renin inhibition within the renin–angiotensin system, and PLA2G7 was linked to investigational inhibition of lipoprotein‐associated phospholipase A2. In contrast, ACE2‐related entries were primarily annotated in experimental or research contexts, and NPPB was more consistent with a biomarker‐associated profile rather than a direct pharmacologic target. Together, these annotations contextualize the network signals within current cardiovascular pharmacology and point to a subset of nodes with plausible therapeutic tractability.

This study has several significant advantages. First, the multicenter design covers 22 assessment centers in England, Wales, and Scotland. All samples were collected and processed according to a unified process, effectively reducing system bias. Second, it is the first to integrate CRFs, PRS, and proteomic risk metrics (ProRS/ProtProfs) to construct a comprehensive CAD risk prediction model. Third, the developed CatBoost model has demonstrated excellent discriminative ability and calibration consistency in both internal and external independent validation sets. Fourth, the equivalence of ProtProfs and ProRS in discriminative performance indicates that only a few core proteins are needed to represent the overall risk information, providing a feasible solution for simplifying the development of protein biomarker panels. However, this study still has several limitations. The included participants were aged 37 to 73 years and were recruited from the UKB, a cohort predominantly composed of White participants, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to younger, older, and more ethnically diverse populations. Future studies in independent cohorts with broader ethnic representation are needed to further evaluate the transportability and fairness of the proposed models across populations. Proteomics indicators were measured only once at the baseline and could not reflect their dynamic changes. There are still blind spots in the detection of low‐abundance or structurally specific proteins by Olink Proximity Extension Assay technology, and the comparability among different platforms remains to be verified. Furthermore, this study has not delved deeply into the interaction mechanism between protein levels and specific genotypes, and the impact on the risk of CAD awaits further research.

CONCLUSIONS

In our study integrating large‐scale plasma proteomics and genetic risk, we developed and geographically validated machine learning models for incident CAD. Adding proteomic risk measures, including either a multiprotein score or a parsimonious protein panel, together with genetic risk improved discrimination and risk stratification beyond conventional clinical risk factors, with overall good calibration in both internal and external validation. Functional enrichment, protein interaction analyses, and drug‐target mapping provided biological and translational context for the identified proteins, supporting the potential of proteomics‐informed tools for more precise CAD risk assessment and prevention.
