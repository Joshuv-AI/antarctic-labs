Explicit Ensemble Mean Synchronization for Time Scale Generation with Mixed Atomic Clock Ensembles*
Priyanka Dey, Takahiro Kawaguchi, Yuichiro Yano, Yuko Hanado, Takayuki Ishizaki

Abstract
In this paper, we consider a mixed ensemble containing a mixture of cesium-type and hydrogen maser-type atomic clocks. For the mixed ensemble, the conventional Kalman filtering algorithm has certain limitations due to divergence of the error covariance matrix. To overcome these limitations, we obtain a Kalman filtering algorithm based on observable canonical decomposition that does not have any diverging terms. We use the estimates from the transformed Kalman filter to propose a time scale generation algorithm called explicit ensemble mean synchronization algorithm for the mixed ensemble. In this algorithm, we synchronize the time deviation of each clock from the ideal clock behavior to the unobservable ensemble mean of the phases where the weighting can be decided by the user. By regulating the free-running dynamics associated with the unobservable state, through choosing an appropriate weight vector, the frequency stability of the generated time scale or the synchronized time shared by the clocks is optimized over shorter (resp. longer) intervals, as measured by Hadamard variance. An illustrative example is given to demonstrate the efficiency of our algorithm.



I
Introduction

An atomic clock ensemble is a group of highly precise atomic clocks of different types, such as cesium and hydrogen maser clocks, that operate collectively to provide a reliable and accurate timing infrastructure
[
1
]
.
Atomic clocks are devices that measure time based on the vibrations of atoms with constant resonance frequencies.
The national metrology institute of each country uses the clock difference measurements from several atomic clocks within an ensemble to reduce these vibrations of atoms to create a reliable and robust timekeeping system
[
2
]
.
This development of accurate time scales provides significant advantages across several critical sectors, including satellite
navigation, financial networks with high-frequency trading and time-sensitive transactions, smart grids, etc
[
3
]
.
The fluctuations in the tick rates of atomic clocks are referred to as time deviations from ideal clock behavior, which can be modeled as a stochastic process.
Based on this stochastic nature of time deviation, the cesium-type (Cs-type) clock has a second-order model
[
4
]
while the hydrogen maser-type (Hm-type) clock has a third-order model with an additional frequency drift state
[
5
]
.

In the task of time scale generation, the time deviation of each clock in the ensemble is estimated to correct the reading of the clock either by software offset or by physically steering the clock.
Conventional Kalman filter (CKF) is extensively used to accomplish this estimation
[
6
,
7
]
.
However, the system model of atomic clock ensemble is undetectable because of its inherent characteristic that only clock reading differences can be measured.
As a result, the error covariance matrix grows boundlessly, which may lead to numerical instability.
However, the Kalman gain converges to a constant value.
This indicates that the CKF has some indeterminate operations in the algorithm.
Some works proposed methods to add a modification step to the error covariance matrix in the CKF algorithm
[
8
,
6
,
9
]
.
The time scale is obtained by
implicitly
taking the weighted average of the corrected clock readings termed as implicit ensemble mean (IEM)
[
8
,
10
]
.

IEM has better accuracy and precision than individual clock in the ensemble.
In time and frequency community, frequency stability quantifies accuracy and precision of the time scale as it describes how well the frequency is maintained over a certain period of time
[
11
]
.
Hadamard variance (HVAR) is used to measure the frequency stability of clocks and time scales
[
12
,
13
]
.
A smaller value of HVAR over an interval indicates that the time scale has reduced divergence rate from the ideal time over that interval.
Cs-type clock has excellent frequency stability for long intervals, while exhibiting a lower frequency stability for short intervals while Hm-type clock has excellent short-term frequency stability but lower long-term frequency stability
[
1
,
12
]
.

Our group has proposed a time scale generation algorithm from an ensemble containing only Cs-type clocks
[
14
]
.
The algorithm includes decomposing the ensemble into an observable component, representing the synchronization error, and an unobservable component which symbolizes the weighted average clock states termed as the generated time scale.
However, due to existence of frequency drift in Hm-type clocks, the technique involved in this algorithm
[
14
]
needs careful modifications and cannot be applied as it is for a mixed ensemble.
In
[
15
]
, we developed a new steering technique for arbitrary-order atomic clocks based on output stabilization for tracking of a virtual reference time such as GPS composite clock
[
6
]
by a single follower atomic clock. Recently, we proposed an algorithm to generate a time scale with excellent long-term frequency stability by using only the ensemble of Hm-type clocks
[
16
]
.
A separate ensemble of Cs-type clocks is used solely to optimize the long-term frequency stability
[
16
]
.
We developed a time synchronization algorithm for mixed ensemble in
[
17
]
.
However, neither we have not paid attention on how this work can be exploited to introduce a technique for generating a time scale nor we concentrated on the frequency stability aspect, which is extremely important for time scale generation.

In this paper, we introduce a novel time scale generation algorithm, which we call explicit ensemble mean synchronize algorithm for a mixed ensemble containing Cs-type and Hm-type clocks.
Firstly, we obtain a Kalman filtering algorithm based on the observable canonical decomposition of the mixed ensemble which does not contain any diverging terms.
Secondly, using the observable state estimates from the transformed Kalman filter, we regulate the frequency of the clocks to synchronize the time deviations of clocks to the
unobservable
weighted average phase state, where the weighting can be
explicitly
specified by the user.
In order to optimize the short-term/long-term frequency stability of the synchronized time or the generated time scale (GTS), we consider the free-running dynamics associated with the unobservable state.
We evaluate the HVAR of the free-running dynamics to find appropriate weight vector to improve the short-term (resp. long-term) frequency stability of the generated time scale.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
II
describes the model of the mixed ensemble and presents the problems considered in the paper.
In Section
III
, we obtain the transformed Kalman filter and its steady-state version, which is free from any diverging terms, by employing observable canonical decomposition on the mixed ensemble.
We present our explicit ensemble mean synchronization algorithm for generating a time scale in Section
IV
.
We illustrate the efficacy of our proposed algorithm through a numerical example in Section
V
.

The discrete-time clock deviation model of a Hm-type atomic clock

(3)

p_{j}[k]

where

,

Using Kronecker product, we can obtain a compact representation of the ensemble as

\left\{\;\begin{aligned} \left[\;\begin{matrix}x[k+1]\\
z[k+1]\end{matrix}\>\right]&=\left[\;\begin{matrix}A\otimes I_{N}&&\beta\otimes J\\

z[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]+\\
&\left[\;\begin{matrix}B\otimes I_{N}\\

v^{z}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right],\\
y[k]&=\left[\;\begin{matrix}C\otimes V&&0\end{matrix}\>\right]\left[\;\begin{matrix}x[k]\\

(5)

where
x[k]:=\left[\;\begin{matrix}p^{\top}[k]&f^{\top}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}

\displaystyle:=\left[\;\begin{matrix}p_{1}[k],\ldots,p_{N}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]^{\top},\;f[k]=\left[\;\begin{matrix}f_{1}[k],\ldots,f_{N}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]^{\top},

\displaystyle z[k]

\displaystyle:=\left[\;\begin{matrix}z_{N-M+1}[k],\ldots,z_{N}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

\displaystyle u[k]

\displaystyle:=\left[\;\begin{matrix}u_{1}[k],u_{2}[k],\ldots,u_{N}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle:=\left[\;\begin{matrix}0_{(N-M)\times M}\\
I_{M}\end{matrix}\>\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M},\;v^{x}[k]:=\left[\;\begin{matrix}v^{p}[k]\\

\displaystyle v^{p}[k]

\displaystyle:=\left[\;\begin{matrix}v_{11}[k],v_{12}[k],\ldots,v_{1N}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]^{\top},

\displaystyle v^{f}[k]

\displaystyle:=\left[\;\begin{matrix}v_{21}[k],v_{22}[k],\ldots,v_{2N}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]^{\top},

\displaystyle v^{z}[k]

\displaystyle:=\left[\;\begin{matrix}v_{3N-M+1}[k],\ldots,v_{3N}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]^{\top}.

The measurements
y[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
are the difference of time deviation (or said differently clock reading) measured independently among the clocks.
The matrix

\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\Sigma_{2}+\frac{\tau^{4}}{8}\Sigma_{3}&\tau\Sigma_{2}+\frac{\tau^{3}}{3}\Sigma_{3}&\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\Sigma_{4}\\
\frac{\tau^{3}}{6}\Sigma_{4}^{\top}&\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\Sigma_{4}^{\top}&\tau\Sigma_{5}\end{matrix}\>\right],

(6)

where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(\sigma^{2}_{11},\sigma^{2}_{12},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{1N}),

(7)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(\sigma^{2}_{21},\sigma^{2}_{22},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{2N}),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{3}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(\underbrace{0,0,\ldots,0}_{N-M},\sigma^{2}_{3N-M+1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{3N}),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{4}

\displaystyle=\left[\;\begin{matrix}0\\

\displaystyle\Sigma_{5}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(\sigma^{2}_{3N-M+1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{3N}).

As mentioned in the Introduction, CKF continues to be widely applied in practical applications for generating time scales from clock ensembles.
The CKF algorithm for the mixed ensemble is

\displaystyle\left[\;\begin{matrix}\hat{x}^{-}[k]\\
\hat{z}^{-}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\left[\;\begin{matrix}\hat{x}[k-1]\\
\hat{z}[k-1]\end{matrix}\>\right]+\mathcal{B}u[k-1],

(8)

\displaystyle P^{-}[k]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}P[k-1]\mathcal{A}^{\top}+Q,

\displaystyle L[k]

\displaystyle=P^{-}[k]\mathcal{C}^{\top}\Big(\mathcal{C}P^{-}[k]\mathcal{C}^{\top}+rI_{N-1}\Big)^{-1},

\displaystyle P[k]

\displaystyle=\Big(I_{2N+M}-L[k]\mathcal{C}\Big)P^{-}[k],

\displaystyle\left[\;\begin{matrix}\hat{x}[k]\\
\hat{z}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\left[\;\begin{matrix}\hat{x}^{-}[k]\\
\hat{z}^{-}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]+L[k]\Big(y[k]-\mathcal{C}\left[\;\begin{matrix}\hat{x}^{-}[k]\\
\hat{z}^{-}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]\Big),

\mathcal{A}=\left[\;\begin{matrix}A\otimes I_{N}&&\beta\otimes J\\

\mathcal{B}=\left[\;\begin{matrix}B\otimes I_{N}\\

\mathcal{C}=\left[\;\begin{matrix}C\otimes V&&0\end{matrix}\>\right]

L[k]
is the Kalman gain, and
P^{-}[k]
and

are prior and posterior error covariance matrices, respectively.

In
[
17
]
, we observed that the mixed ensemble model (

\image\left[\;\begin{matrix}I_{2}\otimes\one_{N}\\

.
In fact, the mixed ensemble model is undetectable.
Due to undetectability, the error covariance matrix may diverge while running the algorithm.
This may lead to numerical instability and inaccurate estimation.
As a consequence, we address the following problem for the mixed ensemble model (
5
).

Problem 1
: Find a Kalman filtering algorithm for the mixed ensemble (
5
) which is free from any diverging terms?

II-A

Hadamard Variance (HVAR)

HVAR is a three-sample variance and can be defined as the variance of the third-order
difference between successive averages of phase deviation taken over sampling period specified; see
[
5
,
12
]
for more details.
For a free-running clock
i
, the third-order difference is given by

\Delta_{m}^{3}p_{i}[k]=p_{i}[k+3m]-3p_{i}[k+2m]+3p_{i}[k+m]-p_{i}[k],

where
m\geq 1
is a step.
Then, the HVAR is defined as

\sigma_{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\tau):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{(\Delta_{1}^{3}p_{i}[k])^{2}}{6\tau^{2}}\Big].

(9)

In fact, by using (
9
), the HVAR of a clock
i
can be obtained as
[
12
]

\sigma_{\mathsf{H}}^{2}(\tau)=\frac{1}{\tau}\sigma_{1i}^{2}+\frac{\tau}{6}\sigma_{2i}^{2}+\frac{11\tau^{3}}{120}\sigma_{3i}^{2},

(10)

where it is solely the function of the sampling interval
\tau
. Note that
\sigma_{3i}=0
for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
.
For a controlled clock
i
, the HVAR is estimated from a clock reading sequence
\{p_{i}[0],p_{i}[1],\ldots,p_{i}[T]\}
measured over the sampling interval
\tau
as

\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{H}}(m\tau;\{p_{i}[k]\}):=\frac{1}{T-3m}\sum_{k=0}^{T-3m-1}\frac{(\Delta_{m}^{3}p_{i}[k])^{2}}{6(m\tau)^{2}}.

HVAR is represented as a function of the time interval
m\tau
using bilogarithmic plots. In this paper, HVAR is employed to assess the generated time scale in the numerical example.

In addition to Problem 1, we consider the problem of generating a time scale from the mixed ensemble model.

\circ

Problem 2(a)
: Generate a time scale by synchronizing the time deviations of all the clocks in the ensemble in the sense

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[p[k]-\mathds{1}_{N}p^{*}[k]\big]=0,

where
p^{*}[k]\in\mathbb{R}
is some synchronization state.
The synchronized time shared by all the clocks is referred as the generated time scale (GTS).
To improve the accuracy and precision of the GTS, the following two requirements are considered separately.

\circ

Problem 2(b)
: Ensure that the GTS has the least short-term HVAR, i.e., we address the following optimization problem

\min_{u_{i}[k]}\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{H}}(\tau;\{p_{i}[k]\})\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{N}\cup\mathcal{M}.

\circ

Problem 2(c)
: We consider the following optimization problem to ensure least long-term HVAR of the GTS

\min_{u_{i}[k]}\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{H}}(T;\{p_{i}[k]\})\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{N}\cup\mathcal{M},

where
T\gg\tau
denote the longer time interval.

III
Solution of Problem 1

We begin with briefly discussing the observable canonical decomposition employed in
[
17
]
, to decompose the system into observable and unobservable states.
We consider the following transformation

\left[\;\begin{matrix}x[k]\\
z[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]=\underbrace{\left[\;\begin{matrix}I_{2}\otimes V^{+}&0_{2N\times M}&I_{2}\otimes\one_{N}\\
0_{M\times 2(N-1)}&I_{M}&0_{M\times 2}\end{matrix}\>\right]}_{\scriptsize\mathbf{T}^{-1}}\left[\;\begin{matrix}\eta_{o}[k]\\
\eta_{\bar{o}}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right],

(11)

where
\eta_{o}[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{2(N-1)+M}
is the observable state,
\eta_{\bar{o}}[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the unobservable state,
\mathbf{T}
is the transformation matrix such that
\mathbf{T}\left[\;\begin{matrix}x[k]\\
z[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]=\left[\;\begin{matrix}\eta_{o}[k]\\
\eta_{\bar{o}}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]
.
In the transformation
\mathbf{T}^{-1}
, the matrix
V^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(N-1)}
is the generalized inverse of
V
associated with a weight vector
q\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
q^{\top}\one_{N}=1
.
Specifically, we consider a full column rank matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(N-1)}
such that
q^{\top}W=0
, and we represent the generalized inverse as
V^{+}=W(VW)^{-1}
. It is easy to see that
VV^{+}=I_{N-1}
.
Note that

\mathbf{T}=\left[\;\begin{matrix}I_{2}\otimes V&0_{2(N-1)\times M}\\
0_{M\times 2N}&I_{M}\\
I_{2}\otimes q^{\top}&0_{2\times M}\end{matrix}\>\right].

(12)

Note that
(I_{2}\otimes q^{\top})x[k]=\eta_{\bar{o}}[k]
which is the weighted average of clock phase and frequency states.
Under transformation
\mathbf{T}
, the new state-space realization is

\displaystyle\left[\;\begin{matrix}\eta_{o}[k+1]\\
\eta_{\bar{o}}[k+1]\end{matrix}\>\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\;\begin{matrix}\mathbf{A}_{oo}&0\\
\mathbf{A}_{\bar{o}o}&A\end{matrix}\>\right]\left[\;\begin{matrix}\eta_{o}[k]\\
\eta_{\bar{o}}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]+\left[\;\begin{matrix}\mathbf{B}_{o}\\
\mathbf{B}_{\bar{o}}\end{matrix}\>\right]u[k]

(13)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{T}\left[\;\begin{matrix}v^{x}[k]\\
v^{z}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right],

\displaystyle y[k]

\displaystyle=\left[\;\begin{matrix}\mathbf{C}_{o}&0_{(N-1)\times 2}\end{matrix}\>\right]\left[\;\begin{matrix}\eta_{o}[k]\\
\eta_{\bar{o}}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]+w[k],

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{oo}

\displaystyle=\left[\;\begin{matrix}A\otimes I_{N-1}&\beta\otimes VJ\\
0&I_{M}\end{matrix}\>\right],

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{\bar{o}o}

\displaystyle=\left[\;\begin{matrix}0_{2\times 2(N-1)}&\beta\otimes q^{\top}J\end{matrix}\>\right],\;\mathbf{B}_{o}=\left[\;\begin{matrix}B\otimes V\\
0\end{matrix}\>\right],

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\bar{o}}

\displaystyle=B\otimes q^{\top},\mathbf{C}_{o}=\left[\;\begin{matrix}C\otimes I_{N-1}&0_{(N-1)\times M}\end{matrix}\>\right].

III-A

Transformed Kalman Filter (TKF)

\displaystyle+rI_{N-1}\Big)^{-1},

\displaystyle\breve{P}[k]

\displaystyle=\Big(I_{2N+M}-\left[\;\begin{matrix}L_{o}[k]\\

\displaystyle\left[\;\begin{matrix}\hat{\eta}_{o}[k]\\

\Psi(q)
is obtained as

q_{\mathsf{H}}^{\infty}:=\lim_{\tau\to\infty}q_{\mathsf{H}}(\tau)=\left[\;\begin{matrix}\frac{\Sigma_{2,N-M}^{-1}\one_{N-M}}{\one^{\top}_{N-M}\Sigma_{2,N-M}^{-1}\one_{N-M}}\\
0_{M\times 1}\end{matrix}\>\right],

(44)

where
\Sigma_{2,N-M}=\text{diag}(\sigma^{2}_{21},\sigma^{2}_{22},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{2N-M})
is a submatrix of
\Sigma_{2}
whose diagonal elements contain the standard deviations of the random walk frequency noise of the Cs-type clocks.
Intuitively, it is reasonable to associate zero weight to each Hm-type clock since they have lower frequency stability in longer intervals as compared to Cs-type clocks.

It is interesting to note that if
q
is set equal to
q_{\mathsf{H}}^{\infty}
then
(q_{\mathsf{H}}^{\infty})^{\top}J=0
.
As a consequence, the dynamics of the unobservable state (
36
) becomes

\eta_{\bar{o}}[k+1]=A\eta_{\bar{o}}[k]+\big(I_{2}\otimes(q_{\mathsf{H}}^{\infty})^{\top}\big)v^{x}[k],

which is equivalent to the dynamics of
\Psi(q_{\mathsf{H}}^{\infty})
.
Thus, by Theorem
2
and setting
q=q_{\mathsf{H}}^{\infty}
, we can generate a time scale while maximizing long-term frequency stability. This solves Problem 2(c).
