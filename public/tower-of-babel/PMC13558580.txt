The effect of 6-day fasting on physical performance and neuromuscular control in healthy men
Authors: Rima Solianik, Nerijus Eimantas, Goda Daugė, Marius Brazaitis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding physical functioning during fasting is critical for both survival and everyday performance. Thus, the main aim of the present study was to determine the effects of a 6-day fasting period on fine motor skills, drop jump (DJ) performance, and neuromuscular properties of the ankle plantar flexors in healthy, physically inactive men. Non-obese men (n = 14) completed a 6-day fast followed by 7 days of habitual diet. Changes in mood state, fine motor skills, reflexes (V-wave, H-wave and M-wave), involuntary muscle torque, maximal voluntary contraction (MVC), central activation ratio (CAR) during MVC, and DJ performance were measured. Fasting had no effect on physical performance (i.e., fine motor skills and DJ) and MVC; however, it increased subjective perceived fatigue, and reduced the Hmax/Mmax and Vsup/Mmax amplitude ratios after both 4 and 6 days of fasting (P < 0.05). In contrast, a decrease in CAR and an increase in H-reflex latency were observed only after 6 days of fasting (P < 0.05). Following the return to a habitual diet, CAR and reflexes returned to prefasting values (P > 0.05), while improvements in mood, including decreased perceived fatigue and depressive symptoms, and increased vigor were accompanied by enhanced fine‑motor performance (P < 0.05). To conclude, despite adaptive modifications at both supraspinal and spinal levels and increased fatigue, MVC, jump performance, and fine‑motor skills were preserved after prolonged fasting. After resuming a habitual diet, changes at supraspinal and spinal level returned to baseline, and mood improvements coincided with enhanced fine-motor performance.Trial registration Retrospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05545943).

Introduction

Fasting is becoming an increasingly popular lifestyle choice, integrated into the daily routines of many individuals for disease prevention and treatment1–3. Combat sports athletes also incorporate rapid weight loss strategies, including fasting, to qualify for their desired weight category4. However, fasting is not always a voluntary or health-driven choice, in some parts of the world, it is a harsh reality imposed by famine, poverty, and war; the conditions that remain among the most prevalent causes of starvation having detrimental effects on health5. Starvation may also result from prolonged entrapment during mining or cave exploration due to unforeseen incidents6,7. Furthermore, astronauts may encounter food shortages if a manned spacecraft lands in an unplanned and hostile environment, such as a desert or ocean, rather than its intended landing location6,8. In all these cases, physical functioning during starvation/fasting is not well understood, but is a critical component not only for survival in extreme environments, but also for athletic achievements and for daily life, as it can significantly impact work efficiency and safety.

In a recent study, participants reported their work ability in relation to self-perceived psychological, physiological, and disease-related factors during a 10-day fasting period. A marked decline in work ability was observed after six days of fasting, followed by a return to baseline levels thereafter7. This can be linked to increased perceived fatigue and reduced perceived vigor, aligning with a subjective decline in work ability that peaks after 6 days of fasting and gradually returns to baseline thereafter7. It is noteworthy that the highest score for self-rated anxiety was indicated in the middle of a long-term fasting period7, which can also have an impact on physical work abilities9. Likewise, it is also proposed that mentally demanding complex tasks are more strongly impaired by hypoglycemia10. Over a 10-day fasting period, blood glucose levels gradually decline during the initial days, reaching their lowest point on day 6, and begin to normalize by day 97. This suggests that day 6 may represent a critical phase during which changes in physical performance and motor control could be most pronounced, yet its impact remains largely unexplored.

Despite the crucial role of fine motor skills in both everyday activities and specialized professional tasks9,11, their sensitivity to fasting remains incompletely understood. Prior investigation has primarily assessed fine motor performance using pursuit tracking tasks during a short-term two-day fasting protocol, and have reported no significant impairments in a women’s group12,13. By contrast, in a task requiring both accuracy and speed for a grooved pegboard task (GPT), it was shown that fasting increased completion time in the female group, but not in the male group14. However, to the best of our knowledge, the effects of prolonged fasting on fine motor function have not yet been evaluated, and it remains unclear whether men exhibit comparable resilience in tasks requiring speed and accuracy during extended fasting periods. Furthermore, the effects of 6-day fasting on muscle strength in a sedentary cohort remains unclear, if any subtle modulations at the supraspinal, spinal or peripheral level occurred remains unknown. A previous study has established that knee maximal voluntary isometric and isokinetic strength is quite well preserved during a 6-day fasting in physically active subjects, despite loss in lean body mass15. However, greater aerobic endurance is likely to facilitate the capacity for dealing with fasting12 and decreasing its potential detrimental effect on motor behavior. Furthermore, the authors indicated that participants were unrestricted from engaging in exercise and physical work15, thus, it remains unclear whether these uncontrolled conditions masked the effects of fasting. Notably, the ankle plantar flexor strength was not evaluated. The strength of ankle plantar flexors plays a critical role in maintaining balance16, which is a modifiable risk factor for both falls17 and sports-related injuries18. Furthermore, plantar flexors contribute to explosive tasks such as maximal sprinting19,20 and jumping20,21. There was already evidence that adaptive modulation at spinal (decreased Hmax/Mmax amplitudes ratio) and supraspinal level (decreased central activation ratio (CAR) during maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) and V-wave latency) can exist despite preserved ankle muscle strength after 2-day fasting in a female group, but not in a male group14. Thus, it is essential to understand the full impact of prolonged fasting on neuromuscular function beyond basic strength measures, particularly within a male cohort.

The main aim of this study was to determine the effects of a 6-day fasting period on fine motor skills requiring both speed and accuracy, drop jump (DJ) performance, and neuromuscular properties of the ankle plantar flexors in healthy, physically inactive men. Additionally, the study aimed to determine whether any observed changes persisted following a 7-day return to a habitual diet. It was hypothesized that the most pronounced temporary deterioration in motor behavior and changes in neuromuscular control would occur following six days of fasting, compared to shorter durations of two or four days.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The experimental protocols, approved by the Lithuanian Sports University’s Committee of Bioethics (No. MNL-SFZ(M)-2021-339), were conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants during the initial familiarization phase. The trial was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (No. NCT05545943; 19/09/2022).

Participants

Participants were recruited by advertising the study through public seminars and social networks. Eligible participants were males with a body mass index (BMI) between 19.5 and 29.9 kg/m2 and with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Exclusion criteria were (i) individuals meeting World Health Organization physical activity guidelines, defined as engaging in more than 150 min of moderate-intensity activity, more than 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination per week22, (ii) history of skeletal or neuromuscular disorders, or other conditions that could impair participation in the study or influence experimental outcomes; and (iii) intolerance to electrical stimulation. Fourteen healthy young nonobese participants (age: 32.1 ± 6.6 yrs, range: 22–41; weight: 88.3 ± 13.6 kg, range 65.7–117.2; and body mass index: 25.7 ± 2.4 kg/m2, range 22.3–29.5) agreed to participate in this study. Limb dominance was assessed using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory23 and the Waterloo Footedness Questionnaire-Revised24, all participants were classified as right-hand and right-leg dominant.

Sample size determination

An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Düsseldorf, Germany) based on pilot data from three participants who completed the study. Assuming a significance criterion of α = 0.05 and statistical power of 0.80, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 11 participants were required to detect changes for central motor control indicators CAR, ratios of Vsup/Mmax and Hmax/Mmax amplitudes and subjective fatigue, which were hypothesized to change following fasting and evoke behavioral changes. Furthermore, three participants were added after considering the attrition rate and missing data.

Experimental protocol

A non-randomized, non-blinded interventional study design took place at the Institute of Sports Science and Innovations, Lithuanian Sports University. At least four days prior to the experimental session, participants completed a familiarization visit during which they were introduced to all experimental procedures to ensure a stable level of performance. Each participant underwent a fine motor skills assessment and was trained to perform a DJ and to achieve and maintain maximal-effort ankle plantar flexion for 3–4 s with a 250-ms stimulation train test at 100 Hz (PTT100) superimposed on a MVC. In addition, the subjective tolerance to electrical stimulation was evaluated. If a participant reported that the required intensity was intolerable, the procedure was discontinued. Participants were then instructed to refrain from consuming alcohol or caffeine for at least 72 h prior to the assessments. Participants were also asked to sleep for > 8 h the night before each assessment and were instructed to refrain from moderate to vigorous physical activities for at least 72 h before the baseline assessment and throughout both the fasting and regular‑diet periods.

Baseline assessments were initiated between 08:00 and 09:00. Upon arrival, each participant had completed an overnight fast of 8–13 h and voided their bladders to estimate the level of hydration based on the specific gravity (SG) of urine (PocketChem UA PU-4010, Arkray Factory Inc., Kyoto, Japan). All males were found to be hydrated (SG, 1.015–1.030 g/ml)25, and hydration status remained stable throughout all measurements. The participant was then instructed to rest in a semi‑recumbent position for 20 min in a quiet room maintained at 24 °C and 60% relative humidity. Afterward, the participant was seated at a table, completed the Brunel Mood Scale (BRUMS), and fine motor skills requiring both speed and accuracy were assessed. After the fine motor skills assessment, the participant sat in the dynamometer chair, stimulating electrodes were positioned over the tibial nerve and surface electromyography (EMG) electrodes were placed over the soleus and anterior tibialis muscles of the right leg, and the reflexes were assessed. After a 5-min rest, the force-generating capacity of the posterior calf muscle was assessed using 1-s trains of electrical stimuli at 20, PTT-100, and 100 Hz. A period of approximately 3 s was needed to adjust the stimulation frequency. After a 1-min rest, the participant performed two attempts of a 3–4-s MVC interspaced with a 1-min rest interval with a PTT100 superimposed on the MVC. Then, DJ performance was assessed, allowing participants three to five attempts. During physical performance and neuromuscular control testing, no more than two researchers were present in the laboratory to minimize potential crowd‑related influences on movement outcomes26. After the baseline assessment, each participant rested for 24 h before beginning a 6‑day fast. During the fasting period, participants adhered to a prescribed zero‑calorie diet, meaning that they consumed no food and were permitted to drink water ad libitum. All previously described assessments were repeated on days 2, 4, and 6 (the end of the fasting trial). Participants then resumed their regular diet for 7 days, and all measurements were repeated on day 7 of the refeeding period. Physical activity was monitored using a Garmin Vivosport wrist-worn activity tracker (Garmin Ltd., Olathe, KS, USA), which recorded step counts for 1 day prior to each fasting measurement and again for 3 days prior to the recovery assessment. At baseline, males averaged 6424.8 ± 2722.6 steps per day; on the days preceding fasting days 2, 4, and 6, daily step counts were 6318.1 ± 4701.2, 5674.8 ± 4278.6, and 5758.9 ± 3079.3, respectively; and during recovery, participants averaged 6552.8 ± 2826.8 steps per day. Overall, physical activity levels remained stable throughout all measurement periods. No participants were lost to follow‑up, and complete data were available for all 14 participants.

Assessment of mood state

Current mood state (“how do you feel right now?”) was assessed using the validated BRUMS27, which comprises 24 items grouped into six subscales: anger, confusion, depression, fatigue, tension, and vigor. Each subscale consists of four items. Participants rate each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“extremely”). For each subscale, raw scores were calculated by summing the four corresponding items, yielding a score in the range of 0 to 16.

Assessment of fine motor skills

Fine motor skills were assessed using the GPT. The Grooved Pegboard (Lafayette Instrument Company, Inc.; Lafayette, IN, USA) consists of a 25-hole board arranged in a 5 × 5 grid, with each hole featuring a randomly oriented keyhole. Participants were instructed to insert the cylindrical metal pegs, each containing a lateral key, by aligning the key with the corresponding keyhole orientation and placing the pegs one at a time using the right hand (dominant for all participants). Pegs were inserted sequentially, filling the board line by line from left to right and from top to bottom, as quickly as possible. During the GPT, each participant was asked to keep the left hand on the table. Furthermore, participants were not allowed to pick up pegs that fell and were requested to continue the GPT by picking up another peg. The timing was not paused when a peg was dropped. The total time to complete each trial of the GPT (i.e., from when the test administrator gave the cue for the participant to begin to when the last peg was inserted in the grid) was recorded using a stopwatch. The test was performed twice with a 1-min rest between trials. The shorter completion time of the two trials was recorded.

Assessment of torque-generating capacity

The involuntary and voluntary torques of ankle plantar flexion muscles were assessed using an isokinetic dynamometer (System 4; Biodex Medical Systems, Shirley, NY, USA) as described in our previous studies14. Participants were positioned in the dynamometer chair with the backrest set at a 45° incline relative to the vertical. The hip joint was positioned at 90° of flexion, the knee joint at 80° of flexion, and the ankle joint was maintained in a neutral (0°) position.

During involuntary torques of ankle plantar flexion muscles assessment, muscle stimulation was applied using flexible surface electrodes (MARP Electronic, Krakow, Poland), covered with a thin layer of electrode gel (ECG Gel; Ceracarta, Forli, Italy). A cathode was positioned in the popliteal fossa, and the anode was placed distal to the patella over the posterior tibial nerve, with an interelectrode distance of approximately 4 cm. A high-voltage stimulator (Digitimer DS7A, Digitimer, Hertfordshire, UK) delivered 0.5 ms square-wave pulses at a constant current of 100 mA and a constant voltage of 200 V. Peak torques induced by electrical stimulation at 20 Hz (P20; representing the steep section of the force–frequency relationship curve), and at 100 Hz (P100; which is close to maximal force) were measured with a 3-s rest interval between stimulations. Then, the peak amplitude, contraction time (CT) and half-relaxation time (HRT) were measured in resting TT-100 Hz (PTT100) contractions.

After a 1‑minute rest, MVC was assessed as participants were instructed to achieve and maintain maximal‑effort ankle plantar flexion for 3–4 s. During two 3–4-s MVC attempts, separated by a 1-min rest interval, PTT100 stimuli were superimposed at approximately the third second of the contraction. The highest torque of the two MVC attempts was recorded. To ensure maximal effort, standardized verbal encouragement was provided by the same experienced male researcher. The PTT100 contractions were used to assess the CAR of the posterior surae muscle. The CAR was calculated as described elsewhere14,28 using the following equation: CAR = MVC torque/total torque (i.e., MVC torque + superimposed PTT100 stimulus evoked torque) × 100. A CAR of 100 reflects complete activation, whereas values below 100 indicate central activation failure or inhibition.

Assessment of muscle activity-generating capacity

Assessment of muscle activity-generating capacity was performed as described in our previous studies14,28. After preparation of the skin (shaving, abrading, and cleaning with alcohol wipes) bipolar Ag–AgCl surface bar electrodes (1 cm diameter, 2 cm center-to-center distance) (DataLog type No. P3X8 USB; Biometrics, Gwent, UK) were used to record EMG activity. For the soleus muscle, the electrode was positioned at 2/3 of the line between the medial femoral condyle to the medial malleolus. For the tibialis anterior muscle, the electrode was positioned at 1/3 on the line between the tip of the fibula and the tip of the medial malleolus. The electrode placement was marked with a waterproof marker to ensure that the same recording site was used throughout all assessments. The ground electrode was positioned on the tarsus of the same limb. EMG files were stored simultaneously on a Biometrics memory card and PC hardware. Root mean square (RMS) values, representing EMG amplitude, and EMG mean frequency (MnF) values for a 1000-ms epoch coinciding with 1-s force interval just before PTT100 were superimposed on MVCs, were obtained directly from the dedicated analysis software (DataLog; Biometrics). In addition, RMS values were normalized to the isometric MVC value.

Assessment of reflexes

Assessment of muscle reflexes was performed as described in our previous studies14. Maximal soleus H‑reflexes (Hmax) and M‑waves (Mmax) were elicited using 0.5‑ms square‑wave pulses delivered via a cathode positioned in the popliteal fossa and an anode placed distal to the patella over the posterior tibial nerve, with an interelectrode distance of approximately 4 cm. Resting Hmax, reflecting motoneuron excitability and the efficiency of Ia afferent–motoneuron transmission29, and Mmax, indicating sarcolemmal excitability30, were obtained by increasing the stimulation intensity in 1‑mA increments every 10 s across a 10–100 mA range at a constant voltage of 200 V. As stimulation intensity increased, the H-reflex amplitude initially increased progressively, subsequently declined, and ultimately disappeared, whereas the M-wave reached its maximal amplitude and remained stable thereafter. Participants were then instructed to perform two brief MVCs of the plantar flexor muscles, with a minimum of 1 min of rest between contractions. A superimposed stimulus (at Mmax intensity) was evoked to elicit the V-wave (Vsup), which reflects the magnitude of the central descending neural drive to spinal motoneurons, although spinal factors such as motoneuron excitability and pre- or postsynaptic inhibition may also contribute29. M-wave amplitude was also used to normalize the amplitude of the recorded H-reflex and Vsup responses, ensuring that any changes in the evoked amplitudes were not influenced by changes at the muscle fiber membrane or neuromuscular junction31. Furthermore, latencies were determined by measuring the interval between the stimulation artefact and the peak of each evoked waveform.

Assessment of jump performance

For the assessment of DJ performance, participants were instructed to step forward with their right leg from a 50‑cm box, ensuring that both feet left the box and contacted the force plate (AccuPower ACP, AMTI, Watertown, MA, USA) simultaneously. Upon landing, participants were verbally encouraged to jump as high as possible immediately after ground contact. Knee flexion was monitored visually by an experienced researcher to maintain a consistent 90° knee angle during the landing phase. If participants deviated from this target angle, they were immediately instructed to adjust their technique before continuing. Throughout all DJ trials, participants kept their hands placed on their waist to minimize upper‑body contribution. Jump height and reactive strength index (RSI), during the DJs were obtained directly from the dedicated manufacturer’s analysis software (AccuPower, version 2.0.3, AMTI, Waterdown, MA, USA). After each trial, participants received standardized verbal feedback regarding their performance and were encouraged to maximize jump height on subsequent attempts. The highest jump of three to five attempts was used for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the results was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric tests were used for data that follow a normal distribution, such as vigor, GPT completion time, MVC, involuntary torques, CT, HRT, Vsup latency and amplitude, Hmax amplitude and latency, EMG signal frequencies of soleus and tibialis anterior muscles, RMS and MnF ratios of agonist/antagonist, and jump height and RSI. Fasting-evoked changes were assessed using a general linear model (GLM) repeated measures analysis, followed by the LSD post hoc test. The effect sizes of the evoked significant changes were determined by calculating partial eta squared (ηp2). The effects of refeeding (recovery) was assessed using paired samples t test. In this case, effect size (ES) was computed using Cohen’s d.

Nonparametric tests were used for data that did not follow a normal distribution, such as anger, confusion, depression, fatigue, tension, CAR, Mmax amplitude and latency, ratios of Hmax/Mmax and Vsup/Mmax amplitudes, EMG amplitudes of soleus and tibialis anterior muscles, and ratio of RMS/MVC of soleus muscle. Fasting-evoked changes were assessed using a Wilcoxon signed‑rank test. In this case, to examine the ES of the evoked significant changes, these were determined by the Z statistic divided by the square root of the sample size.

The level of significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 29.0.2.0 (20); Armonk, NY, USA). Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Graphs were created using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.0 (621); GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA USA).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of prolonged fasting on fine motor performance requiring both speed and accuracy, jump performance, and maximal ankle strength, and to determine whether performance responses may be explained by alterations at supraspinal, spinal, or peripheral levels. Unique findings of our study are that fine motor skills, DJ performance and MVC were preserved in males throughout the 6-day fasting period, despite fasting-induced temporary subjective fatigue, and pronounced modulation at the supraspinal (decreased Vsup/Mmax) and spinal (decreased Hmax/Mmax) levels after both 4 and 6 day fasting, with greater modulations after 6 day fasting (increased Hmax latency and decreased CAR). A noteworthy observation was that mood improvements, reflected by reduced fatigue, increased vigor, and diminished depressive symptoms, were accompanied by enhanced fine‑motor performance once participants returned to their habitual diet.

Fine motor skills

It was established that fasting induces stress in males, especially during the first 4 days, as indicated by increased epinephrine and heart rate response32–34. The activation of the sympathetic adrenomedullary (SAM) system can support optimal performance during critical incidents,however, under more extreme stress, the SAM response may become dominant or maladaptive, leading to a decline in skilled performance35,36. However, in our previous studies, we established that despite increased SAM activation after 2 days of fasting32,34, fine motor skills were not affected in males14. The present findings extend this observation by showing that fine motor performance is maintained even after 4 and 6 days of fasting.

As highlighted in previous reviews37,38, mental fatigue can impair psychomotor performance, motor skills performance, and decision-making. The BRUMS fatigue subscale (“worn out, exhausted, sleepy, tired”)27 can primarily be understood as reflecting physical fatigue, although it may also include a mental component. One of the core characteristics of mental fatigue is a subjective feeling of tiredness39. Earlier studies have reported reduced subjective work ability, increased perceived fatigue (consistent with our findings), and decreased vigor during prolonged fasting7. In our study, GPT performance, which assesses fine motor control, psychomotor speed, and visuomotor coordination40, remained stable. Although fatigue increased during fasting, it may not have reached the threshold required to impair fine motor control, and maintained vigor could have contributed to preserved performance. It is also important to note that mental fatigue is typically induced by prolonged, demanding cognitive activity41, which may not be directly comparable to the conditions of the present study, and the fatigue experienced during fasting was likely related to decreased glucose concentrations7.

Mentally demanding and complex tasks are also known to be particularly vulnerable to hypoglycemia10. Although glucose levels progressively decline during prolonged fasting, reaching their lowest point on day 6, this reduction is counterbalanced by the increased availability of ketone bodies7,33,42, which serve as alternative energy substrates and may help preserve GPT performance, a task that also reflects aspects of executive functioning43. Our findings align with findings by Yang et al.7, who observed that basic cognitive skills were maintained over a 10-day fast even as mood state and glucose concentrations changed.

Interestingly, following refeeding, participants exhibited increased vigor alongside reductions in depressive symptoms and fatigue. In an acute prolonged fasting study in men, β-endorphin concentrations remained elevated even after five days of refeeding compared with baseline values44. Such sustained elevations in endogenous opioids may contribute to enhanced well-being, β-endorphins are also implicated in reward-related processes and behaviors that support the restoration of physiological homeostasis, including the reestablishment of energy balance by stimulating food intake45,46. Consequently, their prolonged elevation after refeeding may reflect a neurobiological rebound mechanism through which the opioid system supports both the recovery of physiological equilibrium and the improvements in mood. Refeeding of food-restricted animals also elicits increases in dopamine concentrations47. Given dopamine’s central role in both mood regulation and motor control48, enhanced dopaminergic activity may also help explain the observed pattern of improved fine motor performance accompanying enhanced well-being. As discussed earlier, fasting-induced fatigue had no measurable effect on GPT performance. Consequently, the subsequent reduction in fatigue after one week was unlikely to produce detectable improvements, whereas the increase in vigor during recovery may have supported the observed enhancement in performance. Additionally, a reduction in depressive symptoms may further contribute to enhanced fine motor performance, as fine motor impairments are well documented in individuals with depression49.

Muscle strength and neuromuscular control

In a recent study, it was established that ankle muscle involuntary torque was not affected in males after 2-day fasting14, despite loss of skeletal muscle proteins50. The present findings extend this observation by showing that involuntary torque is maintained even after 4 and 6 days of fasting. Although skeletal muscle protein degradation is expected during prolonged fasting, phosphorylation of 5′ AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a regulator of muscle protein degradation, remains unchanged in humans under these conditions15, suggesting that AMPK-dependent proteolytic pathways may not be further activated51. In addition, it is well established that ketosis rises substantially during a 6‑day fast7,33,42, and that protein loss diminishes as ketogenesis increases50.

Although ankle plantar-flexor contractile force and soleus muscle activity (RMS and MnF) were preserved, prolonged fasting elicited clear neurophysiological alterations, reflected by reduced Hmax/Mmax ratios and decreased Vsup/Mmax ratios after both 4 and 6 days of fasting, with more pronounced effects after 6 days, including a drop in CAR and prolonged H-reflex latency. Although the H-reflex and V-wave are indirect spinal and supraspinal markers, and the H-reflex can also be influenced by peripheral axonal excitability52,53, fasting-related mechanisms described in previous studies indicate that the most plausible explanation for these reductions still lies at the spinal and supraspinal levels. During periods of reduced blood glucose, such as during fasting, the brain increasingly relies on ketone bodies as an alternative substrate. This metabolic shift is essential for maintaining energy homeostasis, but is accompanied by slower energy availability due to increased reliance on anaerobic pathways54. Under such conditions, it is plausible that the central nervous system adjusts its activation strategy to conserve energy while still preserving muscle function and activity14. Moreover, chronic ketosis enhances the brain’s energy reserves by stabilizing synaptic function and reducing neuronal excitability55. In epileptic animal models, a ketogenic diet has been shown to elevate β‑hydroxybutyrate acid concentration, which in turn enhances brain γ‑aminobutyric acid (GABA) concentrations, the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter, and helps preserve the GABA/glutamate balance56. Ketone bodies have also been demonstrated to stabilize brain networks, irrespective of whether ketosis is induced through dietary manipulation or exogenous ketone esters. This metabolic transition from glucose to ketone utilization, which shifts neural activity to a more stable network state, likely reflects an adaptive mechanism for conserving energy under constrained metabolic resources57, and may account for the optimized neuromuscular control observed in men, whereby central and spinal input decrease while muscular performance and jump efficiency are maintained. Additional evidence shows that ketogenic diets increase GABA concentrations in the cerebrospinal fluid58,59, suggesting that ketone-induced modulation may extend to the spinal level. At the same time, the reduction in H-wave amplitude may not necessarily indicate altered movement control, but rather a central nervous system–driven adjustment of sensorimotor pathways53.

It is well established that the degree of ketosis increases progressively over a 6‑day fasting period in men33, which may account for the more pronounced supraspinal and spinal alterations observed after 6 days of fasting compared with 4 days. Interestingly, women exhibited supraspinal and spinal or modulations earlier in the fasting period, with measurable changes already apparent after 2 days of fasting14. Such delayed supraspinal and spinal modulations in males may be linked to the magnitude of the metabolic shift from glucose to ketone utilization, females exhibit a stronger ketone response after 2 days of fasting34, whereas in males the increase in ketone production required to evoke these neurophysiological changes appears to occur later.

Limitations

The present study has some limitations. Firstly, one factor that may account for the absence of observable changes in GPT performance is the brief task duration (~ 50 s), which may be insufficient to capture subtle performance decrements. For instance, shorter versions of the Psychomotor Vigilance Test (for example 90 s or 3 min) have been shown to exhibit reduced sensitivity compared with the standard 10-min test60,61. In this context, tasks requiring more prolonged fine-motor performance should be evaluated in future research in order to rule out the second hypothesis of brief-task insensitivity. Secondly, in a recent study we demonstrated that women exhibit distinct motor responses to short-term fasting14, and motor function may also be influenced by the menstrual cycle62,63. Taken together, these findings indicate that investigation of prolonged fasting on motor behavior and control in women is warranted. However, the hormonal fluctuations that occur within each menstrual cycle64 introduce methodological challenges, thereby limiting the feasibility of such an investigation. Thirdly, the EMG measures used in this study (RMS and MnF) provide only low-level information about muscle activation and do not allow inference of higher-order neural features such as motoneuron discharge properties, coherence, persistent inward currents, or motoneuron input–output gain65. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should incorporate more advanced EMG approaches to examine these neural responses in greater depth. Lastly, the small sample size and the power analysis based on limited pilot data (n = 3) may increase the likelihood of chance findings. This is especially relevant for the isolated significant effects on fine motor skills observed during the recovery period, which should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, the sample size used in this study is comparable to previous research examining the effects of fasting on motor behavior (n = 8–14)12,14,15,66, suggesting that our sample is consistent with established methodological practice in this field.

Conclusions

The present findings demonstrate that prolonged fasting induces adaptive neurophysiological modifications at both supraspinal and spinal levels, with more pronounced alterations after 6 days compared with 4 days. Despite these central adjustments and increased subjective fatigue, MVC, jump performance, and fine‑motor skills were preserved, underscoring the robustness of the nervous system in maintaining physical performance under metabolic stress. Following the return to a habitual diet, supraspinal and spinal alterations return to prefasting values, while improvements in mood are accompanied by enhanced fine‑motor performance. Taken together, these findings illustrate that the neuromuscular system maintains functional capacity during prolonged metabolic stress and rapidly returns to baseline once dietary intake is restored.
