Title: Time-, space-, and molecule-resolved multiple plasma reactive species in complex settings
using an innovative approach of cavity ringdown spectroscopy

 What are the research goals of the project?

The primary research goal of this 2-year project is to demonstrate the feasibility of an innovative
approach to measure reactive plasma species in the most challenging settings: right on the surface
of water and under water. We propose to use the Brewster angle-cavity ringdown spextroscopy
(BA-CRDS). Research objectives include:
 1) Measurements of OH and NO in the plasma gas-phase zone (Z1) using different types of
 LTP sources including continuous and pulsed plasma jets, sheets, torches, etc.
 2) Measurements of OH and NO in the multiphase reaction zones, right on the surface of
 water (Z2) and under water (Z3).
 3) RS formation and loss mechanisms and reaction dynamics in the three zones.
 4) Generalization of the proposed system to be configurable with other selected innovative
 techniques for other LTP parameters; invitation of selected PIs to form a strong
 collaborative team to pursue the follow-up effort (Category-2).

C. Significant results

1. In-house Brewster angle cell

 • An in-house cuvette using two 0.5-mm quartz plates is developed for testing.
 • The absorption coefficients of the quartz plates are measured to be 0.5671±0.00104 using
 the BA-CRDS.

According to the Beer-lambert law, absorbance is proportional to the length of the light path. Our
initial test finds that UV light at a wavelength of 256 nm experiences a large absorbance when
passing through a standard spectrometer cuvette set at the Brewster angle, mainly due to a long
light path (typically 2.5 mm). Therefore, a Brewster angle (BA) cell/cuvette with a shorter length
of light path is developed in our lab for testing. Fig. 1 shows that the in-house cuvette that is
mounted on high precision stages allowing three axes of translation and two axes of rotation for
the experimental setup and measurements at various positions. The specifications of the cuvette
are listed in Table 1. The absorbances of fused silica plates of difference thickness between 0.15-
2 mm are measured using cavity ringdown spectroscopy (CRDS), as shown in Fig. 2. The quartz
plates are mounted on a high precision rotation mount and positioned at the Brewster angle with a
minimum reflection loss. Therefore, the absorbance can be interpreted to be solely due to light
absorption. The thickness is corrected to the corresponding light pathlength. The plot shows a good
linearity as theoretically calculated, and its slope produces an absorption coefficient of the material
of 0.5671±0.00104. Gluing thinner plates (<0.5 mm) to the 3D printed frames leads to deformation
of the plates, which increases absorbance (light loss) due to the greater occurrence of scattering.
This may result from a change in the tension between the glue and frame/plate, or a change in
temperature during the super glue hardening. Thus, the 0.5-mm plates are chosen to construct the

 3

cuvette because of their robustness when glued onto the frames and their relatively small
absorbance.

2. The new Brewster angle-cavity ringdown spectroscopy (BA-CRDS) system

 • A BA-CRDS system is successfully set up in our lab, producing the ringdown time of the
 water-filled BA cell to be 167 ns with an uncertainty of 0.78%.
 • The system is integrated with an OES system and an imaging system for simultaneous
 measurements of plasma interacting with a liquid.

Fig. 3 illustrates the schematic of the Brewster angle-cavity ringdown spectroscopy (BA-CRDS)
system, and Fig. 4 (a) and (b) show pictures of the side view and top view of our BA-CRDS system.
The ringdown (RD) cavity is constructed using a pair of high-reflectivity concave mirrors (MLD
technology, reflectivity 99.80% at 256 nm) with a diameter of 25.4 mm and a radius of curvature
of -1 m, placed at 71 cm apart. The cuvette at the Brewster angle is placed at the center of the
cavity. The BA cell is rotated along the z axis until no/minimum reflection is observed in a dark
room. The angle is measured to be 56.4±0.5o. The rear RD mirror needs to be slightly translated
along the x- and z- axes after placing the BA cell inside the RD cavity. The 256-nm ringdown
 5

(RD) beam is generated from frequency doubling of a dye laser (Radiant dyes laser Narrowscan
G-R) with a linewidth of 0.07 cm-1. The dye laser that currently uses Coumarin 500 is pumped by
a 355-nm beam from an Nd:YAG laser (Continuum Powerlite 8020) with a pulse width of 8-12 ns
and a repetition rate of 20 Hz. The pulsed energy of the RD beam is ~ 10 μJ. The horizontal
polarization of the RD beam is achieved by using a 1/2 waveplate (Thorlab WPHSM05-266) and
a polarizing beamsplitter cube (Lambda research optics HPB-12.7U-R257, extinction rate 200:1).
The light transmitted through the RD cavity is monitored by a photomultiplier tube (PMT,
Hamamatsu) that is screened by a 254±10 bandpass filter (Andover Corporation 254FS10-25). The
RD signal from the PMT is digitized by an oscilloscope (Tektronix TDS46A) and transferred to a
homemade software tool for data processing via a GPIB interface connection. The characteristic
RD time is determined by fitting the natural logarithm of the RD signal to a straight line using a
weighted least-squares fitting algorithm. Each data point, a single RD time, is typically generated
by averaging 100 RD waveforms followed by the fitting routine. The RD time is 1200 ns with an
uncertainty of 0.49% for an empty cavity, 222 ns with an uncertainty of 1.07% for the RD cavity
with an empty BA cell, and 167 ns with an uncertainty of 0.78% when the BA cell is filled with
DI water. The homemade low temperature plasma device is mounted directly above the BA cell,
and its height is controlled by a z-axis translation stage. The plasma plume structure is monitored
by a CCD camera (Point Grey), and the emission spectra of the plasma is detected by a
spectrometer (Avantes) with a confocal apparatus constructed using two lenses with focal lengths
of 70 mm and 35 mm.

3. BA-CRDS measurements of HgBr2 solutions in different concentrations

 • The new BA-CRDS system is characterized and validated via measurements of HgBr2
 solutions in various concentrations.
 • The measured absorption cross section of HgBr2 solution is close to the published data
 with a correction of the Chako-Linder ratio.
 • HgBr2 molecules under UV radiation or electron impact dissociation generate HgBr
 radical that is an important atmpsheric radical. HgBr2 is used for initial testing of the BA-
 CRDS system.

4. Plasma interactions with water and HgBr2 solutions

 • Our low temperatue plasma (LTP) jet device allows a low helium flow rate without
 splashing the liquid.
 • OES fails to detect characteristic plasma spectra at the air-liquid interface and below.
 • BA-CRDS detects RD time changes from the LTP interactions with water and HgBr2
 solution, arguably indicating a direct and indirect detection of RSs.

Fig. 7(a) illustrates the diagram of the low temperature plasma (LTP) jet system which is used for
interacting with solutions in this project. This plasma device is constructed using a dielectric
cylinder with inner and outer diameters of 19.0 mm and 28.0 mm, respectively, with electrodes
attached at one end, and an 1/4-inch gas inlet on the other end. Two circular copper electrodes are
attached on the opposite sides of two 4-mm thick dielectric discs that are centrally punctured with
1-mm openings to generate a thin plasma jet. The electrodes are connected via a 0.6-m low loss
coaxial cable to a high voltage pulse generator (DEI PVX-4110) that is powered by a high voltage
power supply (Spellman SL 1200) and modulated by a function/waveform generator (Kuman).

 10

The device is capable of producing a stable plasma jet at a low helium flow rate of 315 sccm
without inducing any visible turbulence at the air-liquid surface. Fig. 7(b) shows the image of the
plasma plume interacting with the liquid surface captured by the integrated camera system (Point
Grey). The RD beam is passing through the BA cell 1-2 mm under the bottom of the liquid
meniscus. Fig. 8 shows the emission spectra detected by the OES system in the three special
settings: in the plasma gas, right above the solution surface, and the air-water interface. The
characteristic OH band, the N2 (C-B) system, and the N2+ (B-X) system are indistinguishable from
the noise in the emission spectra captured at the air-water interface and below, while they are
distinctive at the location of just above water and ~5 mm above water (in air).

We measure the RD time changes when the plasma jet interacts with water and HgBr2 solutions
of different concentrations. Fig. 9 shows the changes in the RD times in water and in 5.6 μg/mL
HgBr2 solutions interacting with the LTP. The baselines are collected with a continuous helium
flow, and the high voltage is gradually increased to 5.2 kV when the plasma is stably generated.
Coinciding with the high voltage pulsed generator being enabled, although the high voltage power
supply has not yet reached the maximum, the RD time jumps up, which suggests the interference
of the high-frequency pulse power supply with the RD data acquisition system (e.g. PMT,
oscilloscope etc.). The RD time in water decreases by nearly 6%, likely due to the light absorption
of the accumulating reactive species (RSs) from the plasma, such as OH, HOO, H2O2 etc. [5][6].
The lower limit of the total concentration of the RSs is estimated to be 3×1014 molecule cm-3,
which suffices to be detected by our current BA-CRDS system. The RD time in 5.6 μg/mL HgBr2
solution increases by ~7%, which suggests a consumption of HgBr2 due to certain chemical
reactions between HgBr2 and the RSs, such as 𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻2 + 𝑂𝑂𝑂𝑂 → 𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻𝐻 + 𝐵𝐵𝐵𝐵 [7]. If the
change of the RD time is accounted for the HgBr2 consumption from such reactions, the
concentration change of HgBr2 is around 3×1014 molecule cm-3, which means that the reaction is

 12

limited by the concentrations of the RSs. It’s unclear the cause of the continued increase of the RD
time in HgBr2 solution after the plasma is turned off. The literatures on possible reactions of HgBr2
reaction in aqueous phase is very scarce. To confirm the existence of the RSs, especially OH
radicals, we will switch a different solution, e.g. Counmarin solution, for such detection, such as
coumarin. Coumarin is commonly used for indirect OH measurements by means of known
chemical reactions and a subsequent fluorescence probe method [8][9]. The BA-CRDS method
not only has the advantages of higher sensitivity than the fluorescence probe method and does not
require a calibration, but also can directly measure concentrations of the reactive species at their
distinctive wavelengths, such as OH at 308 nm, NO at 226 nm, etc.

5. The custom-designed Brewster angle cell

 • Several issues/challenges in using the in-house BA cell.

 13

 • A custom-designed BA cell will provide a soliution. The detection limit can be improved
 to 3×10-5 by using the custom-designed BA cell.

There are several issues and challenges with our homemade cuvette. First, making a decent
homemade cuvette has a low success rate, which largely depends on the angle between the two
parallel plates. The main impacting factors are the flatness of the 3D printed frame, the amount of
glue used, the position of the plate placed on the frame, etc. The angle between the two parallel
plates in the cuvette currently used in our system is estimated to be less than 11 arcmin. Second,
the in-house cuvette is hard to clean because the super glue used is soluble to normally used organic
solutions/cleansers, such as acetone and alcohol, so the cuvette cannot be rinsed or immersed in
the cleansers to completely remove the residues, which could also contribute to a bias to the CRDS
measurements. Third, the transmission of our homemade cell material is 78% for GE124 fused
silica, but it can be increase up to 92% if it is replaced by other glasses with grades of Spectrosil
2000 and Suprasil 300. Hence, a custom-manufactured quartz cuvette is needed for better
performance, and we have ordered it from FireflySci Inc (see Table 2 for specifications and Fig.10
for design details). The detection limit of the BA-CRDS system with the custom-designed cuvette
is predicted to be 3×10-5, which is capable of detecting OH radical in water with concentration as
low as 2 parts per billion.

D. Key outcomes
A BA-CRDS system has been constructed with the detection limit of 1×10-5, and used to:

 (1) measure the absorption coefficient of the GE124 grade quartz without surface reflection
 loss;
 (2) measure the RD time of the empty in-house BA cell and the BA cell filled with water and
 HgBr2 solutions of different concentrations;
 (3) measure the absorption cross section of HgBr2 in aqueous phase at room temperature;
 (4) detect the RD time changes in water and HgBr2 solutions when interacting with the LTP
 jet, and the changes are speculated due to a change in RSs, resulting from the LTP
 interactions.
The system can be further improved by using a custom-designed BA cell for liquid detection. The
RSs, especially OH radicals, can be detected indirectly using CRDS measurements of their known
reactants or products, and directly using CRDS measurements at their distinctive wavelengths.
