Innovative pathways for sustainable health financing for migrants and refugees in Iran: a stakeholder and social network analysis approach
Authors: Ahad Bakhtiari, Alireza Olyaeemanesh, Masoud Behzadifar, Sanaz Bordbar, Efat Mohamadi, Hakimeh Mostafavi, Sahar Kargar, Fateme Yaftian, Amirhossein Takian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveFinancing health services for migrants and refugees remains challenging in settings with constrained fiscal space, fragmented governance and protracted displacement. This study aimed to identify existing, underused and potential mechanisms for financing migrant and refugee health services in Iran and to map the stakeholders involved in revenue raising, pooling, purchasing and policy-making.DesignA multi-source qualitative policy analysis combining a scoping review, desk review of national documents, semistructured interviews, stakeholder analysis and social network analysis (SNA).SettingNational and subnational actors involved in financing, regulating, purchasing, delivering or supporting health services for migrants and refugees in Iran, with particular relevance to Afghan migrants and refugees.Participants44 purposively selected participants took part in the interviews, including representatives of governmental bodies, insurance organisations, international agencies, non-governmental organisations, healthcare providers and Afghan migrants with different legal and insurance statuses. Of these, 15 professional and institutional participants completed the SNA questionnaire.MethodsThe scoping review covered literature published from 2010 to 2026 and searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar and relevant grey-literature sources. National document findings from the broader project were re-examined from a health-financing perspective. Interview data were analysed using qualitative content analysis. Stakeholders were assessed according to their interest, capacity to mobilise or generate financial resources and authority to influence financing policy.ResultsThe updated review identified 43 eligible studies. Current financing arrangements rely on a fragmented combination of public insurance for eligible groups, donor assistance, non-governmental organisations and charitable support, disease-specific programmes and out-of-pocket payment. Additional mechanisms included employer-based contributions, earmarked public revenues, religious and philanthropic funds, corporate social responsibility, community-based pooled support, regional cooperation, concessional financing and digital financing tools. These mechanisms differed in current use, feasibility and required policy action. SNA highlighted the central roles of Ministry of Health and Medical Education, Iran Health Insurance Organization, Social Security Organization, Parliament, United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, WHO and International Organization for Migration.ConclusionSustainable migrant and refugee health financing in Iran requires a coordinated multi-source strategy, stronger governance, improved pooling and purchasing arrangements and feasibility assessment of underused financing options.

Introduction

Terms such as ‘asylum seekers’, ‘immigrants’, ‘migrants’ and ‘refugees’ classify individuals affected by migration, which impacts public health in host communities.1 Although these terms are sometimes used interchangeably in health policy discussions, they are different legal and administrative designations that can affect eligibility for insurance, access to publicly funded services and out-of-pocket payments. The term ‘migrants and refugees’ is used in this study as an inclusive term, while noting that the Iranian context is mainly shaped by the long-standing presence of Afghan migrants and refugees. Host countries use different strategies to provide healthcare to migrants and refugees, influenced by factors such as the integration of migrants with host communities, the number of migrants and the level of health-system development. Four types of healthcare approaches are used to respond to the health of migrants: mainstream, specialised-focus, gateway and limited.2 These approaches also have direct financing implications: mainstream models require stable public financing and effective pooling mechanisms; specialised-focus and gateway models may improve access for specific groups but can create parallel financing structures and limited models may reduce short-term public expenditure while increasing unmet need, delayed care and reliance on emergency or charitable services.

Almost 1 in 8 of the world’s population, or over 1 billion individuals, are active migrants. International migrants make up 281 million of this total population.3 By mid-2023, 110 million people had been forcibly relocated; 43.3 million were children and 1.9 million were born into refugee families between 2018 and 2022. Displacement is expected to increase due to poverty, insecurity, conflict, natural disasters and limited access to essential services.4 69% of refugees reside in neighbouring countries. Afghanistan is the second-largest country of origin for refugees with 6.1 million fleeing. Iran is the world’s top host country with 3.4 million refugees.4 The transition from global displacement to Iran is particularly important as Iran is not only a transit or temporary country for Afghans but a long-term host environment for Afghan populations. Therefore, migrant and refugee health in Iran should be viewed as a long-term health system and health financing issue and not just a short-term humanitarian response.5

The 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol are key international instruments for protecting refugees. But legal protection alone does not guarantee effective financial protection unless entitlements are translated into funded benefit packages, clear rules of eligibility and purchasing arrangements in the health system.6 The health needs of migrants vary by country of origin, age, gender, cause of migration, socioeconomic status, residency status, host country macroeconomic setting and service provision model.7 There are certain obstacles to access healthcare faced by migrants and refugees as identified by Levesque’s framework, including barriers related to approachability, acceptability, availability, affordability and appropriateness, but the framework also suggests that innovative solutions can overcome them.7 For the purpose of this study, the affordability and ability-to-pay dimensions are especially relevant because migrant households may experience limited income, informal employment, weak insurance coverage and administrative barriers to enrolment.

Sustainable, effective health finance systems are necessary to achieve universal health coverage (UHC) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) that are home to a large number of immigrants and refugees with low socioeconomic status.8–10 Other host countries have demonstrated that financing arrangements for migrants and refugees may include tax-funded coverage, social health insurance (SHI), humanitarian assistance, employer-based contributions, community-based insurance or combined models. Each mechanism has its strengths and limitations: donor-supported schemes can play a role in emergencies but are often unpredictable; contributory insurance can be difficult to implement for informal workers; tax-funded approaches require sufficient fiscal capacity and political commitment. Various mechanisms can enhance the health financing of refugees, including donors, bonds, non-governmental organisations (NGOs) and faith-based organisations.9 In this article, ‘innovative financing’ refers to financing approaches beyond traditional donor aid and individual premium payments, which can help mobilise, pool or allocate additional resources for migrant and refugee health. These mechanisms may be novel to the Iranian migrant-health context, underused in existing policy arrangements or not yet systematically embedded in the health-financing system.

In this study, ‘innovative financing’ refers to financing approaches that extend beyond conventional dependence on short-term donor assistance and individual premium payments and that may be underused, not yet systematically institutionalised or potentially adaptable to the Iranian migrant-health context. The term does not necessarily indicate that each mechanism is novel internationally. ‘Sustainable financing’ is used as a policy-oriented objective, referring to financing arrangements that are more predictable, diversified, coordinated and less dependent on short-term external assistance. The study identifies potential pathways towards this objective but does not establish their long-term financial or operational sustainability.

Since Iran is a major host country for migrants and refugees, this study investigates financing sources and key players in funding, pooling and allocating resources for migrant health. This is particularly relevant given the Iranian context which features a large informal labour market, limited fiscal space, inconsistent external assistance and fragmented responsibilities of stakeholders, which may affect the sustainability of financing mechanisms for Afghan migrants and refugees.

This study aimed to determine innovative and sustainable revenue streams, suitable stakeholders and characteristics related to financing the healthcare of migrants and refugees in Iran. Specifically, the study sought to identify potential revenue-raising mechanisms, map the actors involved in migrant and refugee health financing and explore how stakeholder power, interest and authority could inform a more coordinated and sustainable financing model.

Methods

This qualitative study is based on a scoping review of the literature, a desk review of national documents and semistructured interviews with purposively selected experts and individuals. This study was a multi-source qualitative policy analysis to identify existing and potential financing mechanisms for migrant and refugee health services in Iran and to delineate the stakeholders involved in revenue raising, pooling, purchasing and policy making. The qualitative aspects were reported according to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research, and the scoping review aspect was developed according to the main steps proposed for scoping reviews.

Step 3: selection of included studies

We considered studies addressing financing mechanisms, financial protection and insurance. We excluded non-English-language studies and studies focused exclusively on internal migration. Studies were eligible if they included migrants, refugees, asylum seekers or undocumented migrants and reported at least one issue related to health financing, insurance coverage, financial protection, service entitlement, payment mechanism, revenue raising or policy arrangements for financing health services. We excluded studies not focused on health services or financing; studies focused only on internal migration; studies not relevant to migrant or refugee populations; studies not in English; conference abstracts with insufficient methodological or empirical detail and documents that could not be accessed in full text.

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers (AB and MB). Full texts of potentially eligible records were then reviewed against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer (AT). Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded and summarised in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram.

Steps 4 and 5: charting data; organising, summarising and reporting results

To ensure data-extraction accuracy, a third researcher (AT) reviewed and verified the data extracted by AB and MB, including publication year, study design, first author, target population, type of health services, service-delivery method, financing sources or laws and insurance laws.

Data were charted with a structured extraction form developed for this study. Extracted items consisted of country or setting, year of publication, study design or document type, target population, legal status of population where reported, type of health services covered, service-delivery model, financing source, insurance or payment arrangement, relevant laws or policies and reported barriers or facilitators to financial access. For this purpose, the collected data were summarised narratively and the financing mechanisms used, proposed in the literature, or potentially adaptable to the Iranian context were identified.

Desk review of national documents

The desk review of national documents was part of the broader research project from which the earlier published article and the present manuscript were developed. In this phase, Iranian laws, regulations and policy documents and health-related programmes on foreign nationals, refugees, immigrants, asylum seekers, health, treatment, employment and insurance were reviewed using national legal and governmental sources such as the National Database of Parliamentary Laws and Regulations, governmental websites and the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MoHME).

The previous article focused on Iran’s health-system response and access to healthcare for Afghan migrants and refugees. In the present manuscript, the same documentary evidence was used from a health financing perspective. Specifically, document-based findings were extracted and interpreted in relation to insurance eligibility, social security coverage, out-of-pocket payment, financial protection, revenue raising, pooling, purchasing and benefit-package design for migrants and refugees. These findings were triangulated with scoping review and interviews to identify existing mechanisms, potential financing options and policy or legal changes required for sustainable migrant health financing in Iran. Further details of the document review process are reported in the related published article.7

Semistructured interviews

Next, we conducted semistructured interviews with 44 purposively selected stakeholders identified through snowball sampling. The interviews involved managers and experts from the MoHME, the State Welfare Organization (SWO) of Iran, the UNHCR, NGOs, the Iran Health Insurance Organization (IHIO), other relevant insurance companies and healthcare service providers (online supplemental table S1). The sample also included Afghan migrants with mixed legal and insurance statuses, therefore enabling the study to tap into perspectives at both the policy and the user levels. Participants were chosen for their direct experience in migrant health policy, health financing, service delivery, insurance, humanitarian support or lived experience of accessing health services in Iran.

Purposive and snowball sampling were used to select participants. The initial participants were requested to give information about other persons or organisations with relevant expertise. Recruitment was continued until interviews produced no substantially new financing mechanisms, stakeholder categories or process challenges. Interviews were done in person or online. Interviews ranged from 23 to 75 min in length. Interviews were carried out in Persian. Participants were informed about the study objectives and confidentiality procedures before each interview. Verbal informed consent was obtained. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim with participants’ permission. Transcripts and quotations were de-identified before analysis. Although the interview guide was structured around these core questions, probes were used to discuss the feasibility, sustainability, legal basis, administrative barriers and equity implications of each financing mechanism. The interview guide is provided in online supplemental appendix section C.

A purposively selected subgroup of 15 professional and institutional participants subsequently completed the social network analysis (SNA) questionnaire. We aimed to identify multiple sources of revenue-raising through content analysis of the articles included in the scoping review, as well as content analysis of the online and in-person interviews.

Qualitative content analysis was performed on the interview data. Codes were initially developed based on study questions and financing functions of revenue raising, pooling, purchasing, benefit-package design and service delivery. Further codes were derived inductively from the interview data. AB and MB coded the data. Emerging categories were discussed by the research team to enhance consistency and credibility. Findings from the interviews, document review and the scoping review were triangulated to identify mechanisms that were repeatedly reported, contextually relevant or potentially feasible for Iran.

The research team also enhanced trustworthiness through triangulation of data sources, iterative discussion of emerging codes and categories, review by senior researchers with expertise in health policy and financing and the maintenance of an audit trail of data extracted, coding decisions and classification of stakeholders.

Stakeholders’ identification and analysis for increasing revenues

Stakeholders involved in financing health services for migrants and refugees were identified through the scoping review, national document review and semistructured interviews. The interview phase included 44 purposively selected participants representing governmental organisations, insurance organisations, international agencies, non-governmental organisations, healthcare providers, hospital charities and Afghan migrants with different legal and insurance statuses.

A purposively selected subgroup of 15 professional and institutional participants subsequently completed the stakeholder and SNA questionnaire. These respondents had direct professional knowledge of migrant and refugee health financing, policy-making, insurance, resource mobilisation, interorganisational coordination or healthcare delivery. The SNA questionnaire respondents included four participants from the MoHME; two from NGOs; two healthcare providers; and one participant each from the SWO of Iran, a rehabilitation centre, UNHCR, the Bureau for Aliens and Foreign Immigrants Affairs, the IHIO, Asia or Alborz Insurance and the Social Security Organization (SSO). Afghan migrant participants and representatives of hospital charities were included in the qualitative interviews but did not complete the SNA questionnaire. The characteristics and organisational distribution of both the interview participants and SNA questionnaire respondents are presented in online supplemental table S1.

Stakeholders were defined as organisations, institutions, groups or actors with an actual or potential role in financing, regulating, purchasing, delivering or supporting health services for migrants and refugees in Iran. The preliminary stakeholder list was generated from the scoping review, national document review and interviews and was subsequently refined through expert review to clarify stakeholder definitions, remove duplicate entries and include relevant current and potential actors.

The questionnaire assessed three actor-level attributes for each stakeholder: interest in migrant and refugee health financing, capacity to mobilise or generate financial resources and authority to formulate or regulate relevant financing policies. Interest was defined as the stakeholder’s perceived level of engagement in migrant and refugee health-financing policies. Resource-generation power referred to the stakeholder’s perceived capacity to mobilise, allocate or generate financial resources. Policy authority referred to the stakeholder’s formal or perceived capacity to develop, approve, regulate or expand relevant financing policies.

Respondents assigned each stakeholder a numerical score ranging from 0 to 100 for each of the three attributes. Scores were subsequently classified into five categories: very low (0–20), low (21–40), moderate (41–60), high (61–80) and very high (81–100).

The questionnaire also assessed relationships between stakeholder pairs in relation to migrant and refugee health financing, including relationships concerning resource mobilisation, policy-making, coordination and implementation. In the network analysis, each node represented a stakeholder organisation, institution, group or actor, whereas each edge represented an expert-rated relationship between two stakeholders. Edge weights reflected the aggregated perceived strength of the corresponding relationship.

Ratings provided by the 15 SNA respondents were averaged for each stakeholder attribute. Pairwise relationship ratings were similarly aggregated across respondents to determine the weight of each network edge. No formal inter-rater reliability or uncertainty analysis was conducted; therefore, the resulting scores and network relationships should be interpreted as aggregated expert perceptions rather than objective measurements of institutional authority or actual financial flows.

Social network analysis

A network characterised by appropriately coordinated stakeholder interactions can facilitate the development and implementation of effective health-related policies.11 Analysing such a network can help clarify the structure of the policy-making process and the relationships and interactions among relevant stakeholders.12 SNA enables the calculation of network metrics, including degree, closeness, betweenness and eigenvector centrality. Degree centrality represents the number of direct relationships connecting a stakeholder to other actors in the network. Closeness centrality is based on the inverse of the total shortest-path distance between a stakeholder and all other actors and reflects the stakeholder’s relative accessibility within the network. Betweenness centrality reflects the extent to which a stakeholder lies on the shortest paths between other actors and therefore may indicate a bridging or brokerage role. Eigenvector centrality reflects a stakeholder’s connection to other highly connected or influential actors.13

Of the 44 participants who took part in the semistructured interviews, a purposively selected subgroup of 15 professional and institutional experts completed the SNA questionnaire. These respondents were selected based on their direct experience with migrant and refugee health financing, policy-making, insurance, resource mobilisation, interorganisational coordination or healthcare delivery. Their organisational affiliations and distribution are reported in online supplemental table S1.

The questionnaire collected two types of information. First, respondents assessed each stakeholder according to three actor-level attributes: interest in migrant and refugee health financing, power to mobilise or generate financial resources and authority to formulate or regulate relevant financing policies. Second, respondents assessed the relationships and interactions of each organisation or institution with other stakeholders in the financing network. These relationship ratings were used to construct the stakeholder network.

Each stakeholder was represented as a node, and each reported or expert-rated relationship between two stakeholders was represented as an edge. Edge weights reflected the perceived strength of the corresponding relationship or interaction. Ratings provided by the 15 experts were averaged for each stakeholder attribute and each stakeholder-to-stakeholder relationship. The resulting aggregated relationship matrix was imported into Gephi V.0.10.1 for network analysis and visualisation.

Degree centrality was interpreted as the level of direct connectedness of each stakeholder. Closeness centrality was interpreted as the stakeholder’s relative access to other actors in the network. Betweenness centrality was interpreted as an indication of a potential bridging or brokerage role. Eigenvector centrality and PageRank were interpreted as measures of connection to other influential stakeholders, while hub and authority scores were used as complementary indicators of network position and influence.

Interest, resource-generation power and policy authority were treated as stakeholder attributes rather than network centrality measures. Three network visualisations were generated using the same underlying stakeholder relationship structure to illustrate the relative positions of actors in terms of interest, resource-generation power and policy authority. In these visualisations, node size represented the aggregated expert rating for the corresponding stakeholder attribute, while edge thickness represented the aggregated strength of the relationship between stakeholder pairs.

The complete network comprised 82 nodes, including 75 stakeholder actor nodes and seven nodes representing components of the health-financing system, and contained 360 edges. The network was analysed as an undirected, weighted network. Network visualisations were generated using the ForceAtlas2 layout algorithm.

Results

We found a total of 2362 articles in the initial search; 351 were duplicates and removed. We reviewed the titles and abstracts of 2011 articles and assessed the full text of remaining 276 articles. 43 studies were ultimately selected based on the study criteria.9 14–55 Further details are provided in online supplemental figure S1 and online supplemental tables S2 and S3.

The evidence included was used to map the financing mechanisms rather than to estimate the effect of the interventions. The results are presented in three complementary dimensions. The first dimension relates to the existing and potential revenue-raising mechanisms. The second dimension relates to the source and feasibility of each mechanism in the Iranian context. The third dimension relates to the network of stakeholders through which these mechanisms could be mobilised, pooled or regulated.

Key and innovative resources for raising revenue

Our scoping review, national document review and interviews identified a range of existing, underused and potential mechanisms for financing health services for migrants and refugees in Iran. These mechanisms included donor assistance and international humanitarian funding, NGO and philanthropic contributions, corporate social responsibility (CSR), religious and faith-based funds, tax-based financing, employer-based contributions, public health insurance and social security mechanisms, disease-specific funds, regional cooperation, development financing, community-based pooled support, out-of-pocket payment and volunteer-based or community-based service-delivery support. These mechanisms correspond to different health financing functions: revenue raising, pooling, purchasing, subsidisation of premiums or services, financial protection and cost containment.

The mechanisms identified ranged significantly in their current use and feasibility. Iran already has some mechanisms in place, including donor assistance, public insurance for eligible groups, disease-specific programmes, NGO or charity support and out-of-pocket payment, but they are fragmented and not evenly accessible. Other mechanisms were also identified as potential or underused options such as earmarked taxation, employer-based contributions, religious funds, regional cooperation, concessional financing and digital financing tools, which would need varying degrees of legal, administrative, financial and political change before implementation. Therefore, the mechanisms were categorised based on the type of evidence, their current status in Iran, their main function of funding, their feasibility and the policy action required. To distinguish between mechanisms that are already used, potentially feasible or primarily conceptual, the identified financing options were classified according to their current status in Iran, main financing function, feasibility and required policy action (table 1).

Stakeholder findings suggest that the health sector cannot achieve sustainable financing for migrant and refugee health on its own. Actors with high authority such as MoHME, Parliament, IHIO, SSO and relevant national councils are important for defining eligibility, benefit package and purchasing rules. Actors with higher revenue generating potential such as UNHCR, international donor partners, Global Concessional Financing Facility, regional financial institutions, employers and selected domestic financial or tax authorities may contribute to resource mobilisation. Actors with less formal authority but high interest, such as NGOs, community organisations, volunteer providers and migrant-related organisations, can support outreach, navigation, service delivery and implementation.

These results also indicate that several actors with potential financing relevance currently engage only to a limited extent. This gap is especially significant for actors related to tax, employment, regional cooperation and banking systems. Their low or medium level of interest suggests that the mobilisation of these resources would need deliberate policy engagement, intersectoral negotiation and a clear governance mechanism, rather than spontaneous participation.

Social network analysis

We calculated social network metrics, including degree centrality, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, eigenvector centrality, hub and PageRank. The complete network comprised 82 nodes and 360 edges, with a density of 0.108 and a diameter of 5. The MoHME, IHIO, SSO, Ministry of Cooperatives, Labour, & Social Welfare (MCLSW), Iranian National Tax Administration (INTA), Parliament, WHO, WHO-EMRO, IOM and Ministry of Foreign Affairs showed the highest authority over sustainable healthcare financing for Afghan refugees’ decisions and policy-making processes. Closeness centrality is high for MoHME, IHIO, SSO, WHO and IOM, indicating that these actors have greater access to others within the network and are better positioned to mobilise support for financing the health of migrants and refugees (for more details see online supplemental tables S4–S6).

The SNA findings need to be read in the context of the role played by each actor in financing. MoHME was central because of its role as steward and coordinator in health policy. IHIO and SSO were central because they were directly involved in insurance coverage, pooling and purchasing. Parliament and national policy councils mattered because they had a say in legal and budgetary decisions. UNHCR, WHO, WHO-EMRO and IOM were identified as important bridging actors between domestic institutions and international or humanitarian financing channels.

In particular, betweenness centrality was useful in identifying actors who might bridge disconnected parts of the network. Actors with high betweenness can facilitate coordination among health, migration, insurance, labour and international organisations. Therefore, while they are not the biggest direct revenue generators, these key actors may be important in reducing fragmentation in migrant health financing.

The comparison of power, interest and authority networks showed that the same actors do not necessarily possess all three attributes. Some actors have high authority but low current interest, whereas others have high interest but low formal authority or financial capacity. This mismatch explains why potentially useful financing mechanisms can be underused in practice. For example, actors involved in taxation, labour and regional cooperation may have potential roles in revenue generation, but for their effective contribution, there needs to be stronger engagement by MoHME, IHIO, SSO and national policy-making bodies.

Overall, the analysis of table 3 and figures 1–3 illustrates the complex dynamics of stakeholders’ interactions, power, authority and interests in financing healthcare for migrants and refugees in Iran. The results highlight the need for strategic collaboration among influential actors to ensure sustainable healthcare funding. By leveraging the power and authority of key stakeholders, and fostering partnerships with interested parties, the proposed healthcare financing model can effectively address the unique healthcare challenges faced by migrant populations in Iran.

In conclusion, the findings suggest that sustainable financing for migrant and refugee health in Iran requires a mix of existing mechanisms with carefully selected additional sources. Public insurance, social security, humanitarian assistance, NGO and charitable support and disease-specific programmes are already pertinent but are still fragmented. Additional funding could come from tax revenues, employer contributions, pooled philanthropy or religion, regional cooperation and concessional finance, but the feasibility of these options varies greatly. The SNA findings suggest that these mechanisms would require coordinated stewardship by the MoHME and close collaboration with IHIO, SSO, Parliament, UNHCR, WHO, IOM and selected labour, tax and financial institutions.

Discussion

The healthcare models for refugee and migrant populations include mainstream services, specialised-focus services, gateway services and limited services.2 In Iran, the mainstream model offers UNHCR-registered refugees access to the same healthcare services as citizens, while others can access primary and emergency care. The UNHCR covers premiums for the most vulnerable, while others are self-paying.2 7 32 Despite this inclusive strategy, challenges arise due to the high proportion of refugees working in the informal economy. These service delivery models have important financial implications. Generalist models can promote equity by including eligible migrants and refugees within national health systems, but these models rely on stable public financing, transparent eligibility rules and effective pooling and purchasing mechanisms. Specialised focus and gateway models may improve navigation and early access for legally or administratively vulnerable groups, but can become fragmented if they are not connected to national insurance arrangements. Limited models may cut short-term public expenditure but could shift costs to households, emergency services, charities and humanitarian actors.

Globally, over 60% of the employed population is engaged in informal work,56 which lacks tax revenue to fund public services. In LMICs, including Iran, the informal economy accounts for 50%–75% of non-agricultural employment, resulting in funding shortfalls for healthcare.56 The COVID-19 pandemic has further expanded the informal sector, exacerbating health-financing difficulties. Health coverage initiatives in LMICs often prioritise formal sector workers, leaving informal workers underserved.57 Thus, in the Iranian context, the main challenge is not only the availability of health services but whether access is supported by predictable financing and affordable coverage. For Afghan migrants and refugees, legal status, formality of employment, eligibility for insurance and ability to pay are closely interlinked and make purely contributory insurance mechanisms insufficient for groups in informal or unstable employment.

Access to insurance is particularly problematic for refugees in the informal economy. Standard financial models, such as SHI and community insurance, are often unavailable to informal sector workers (ISWs), increasing the risk of adverse selection. Some countries offer public subsidies with less comprehensive benefit packages for ISWs, while others shift towards tax-financed systems. Broad reforms are necessary to increase public funding and improve coverage for ISWs. Identifying employment status is crucial to facilitate transitions from informal to formal employment. Türkiye offers a notable example of supporting refugees in this transition.58

Migrants often face administrative hurdles in joining insurance schemes, including complex tax and work permit requirements, inflexible premium structures and narrow enrolment periods.7 59 The Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration emphasises the need for access to basic services, including healthcare, education and housing, highlighting the importance of a holistic approach for migrant well-being.59

In Iran, although several stakeholders such as the SSO, the Parliament and the Supreme Council of Health Insurance influence migrant health policies, their engagement remains limited. Strengthening stakeholder participation is crucial for sustainable revenue generation. Aid and insurance premiums alone are insufficient; thus, better coordination among NGOs and donors is recommended to consolidate resources and enhance financial sustainability.

Additionally, the INTA can play a key role in revenue generation by implementing supportive policies that facilitate the transition from informal to formal employment. Regional cooperation, through initiatives such as the Economic Cooperation Organization (ECO) or the Shanghai Cooperation Organization (SCO), can also enhance financial resources by promoting joint infrastructure projects and harmonising policies.

The INTA, the MCLSW and the Ministry of Economic Affairs and Finance could play significant roles in expanding the domestic financing base, particularly through policies that facilitate the transition of migrant workers from informal to formal employment.58 However, these options should be seen as medium- to long-term reforms rather than sources of financing immediately available, since they require legal clarity, administrative capacity, employer compliance and intersectoral coordination. Similarly, regional cooperation through mechanisms such as the ECO and SCO can facilitate avenues of shared responsibility and cross-border financing, but such mechanisms would require formal agreements and defined financial commitments before they can become operational.

Regional organisations can be supranational (as in the EU) or intergovernmental (as in the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, the Southern Common Market (Mercosur) or the Southern African Development Community), where member states retain their sovereignty while engaging in collective efforts. Governance issues and/or geopolitical and economic interests are examples of shared difficulties in cooperation revenue generation.60 By engaging in relevant forums at the level of the heads of states, member countries of regional cooperation improve their ability to address migrant-related issues along the pathway towards sustainable health financing.

In the context of financing, different models are in use across various countries, including mainstream services, which are the main approach for supporting migrants in Iran. Given Iran’s context (big informal economy, sanctions, weak tax system, low income of migrants, lack of willingness to pay premiums), it is advisable to transition to mixed models of care. Implementing a comprehensive or limited benefits package that includes all migrants, regardless of their legal status, while informing them of their rights, can help meet their health needs more effectively. Australia has adopted a mainstream approach to healthcare for refugees and migrants. Migrants with a reciprocal healthcare agreement can use Medicare services.38 61 Canada uses a phased care paradigm (gateway/mainstream); initially, refugees are registered in the Interim Federal Health Programme, which covers doctors’ visits, inpatient and outpatient hospital services and limited vision and urgent dental care.16 23 Germany’s phased gateway/mainstream model provides different degrees of healthcare access based on the asylum claim stage and length of stay, starting with basic healthcare at reception centres.27 35 37

Turkey employs specialised focus and gateway care strategies. The country had 183 migrant health centres spread across 29 provinces to provide legal Syrians with temporary protection status and equal access to healthcare as nationals. However, irregular migrants in Turkiye face access disparities, do not have equal access to healthcare facilities and are categorised as stateless when seeking medical care. A small number of Syrian refugees live in temporary housing, such as government-run camps and refugee centres, where the government provides basic primary healthcare through disaster and emergency management authority, with referrals to public hospitals as needed.31 39 40 59 These country experiences indicate that the transferability of financing models depends on the fiscal capacity of the host country, the labour market structure, the legal treatment of migrants and the administrative ability to enrol migrants into insurance or public coverage schemes. Thus, international models are not directly applicable to Iran but can be used as a guide for designing a mixed financing approach based on the following: public insurance, targeted subsidies, disease-specific protection, employer-related contributions and humanitarian or charitable support.

The study identified several existing, underused and potential sources of financing for migrant healthcare, including value added tax (VAT), CSR contributions, international aid, religious funds such as zakat and khums, employer-based insurance schemes, disease-specific resources, regional cooperation mechanisms, banking or concessional financing, digital financing tools and community-based insurance models. However, these mechanisms should not be seen as equally feasible or equally established in Iran. Others, such as public insurance, donor aid, NGOs or charitable aid and disease-specific programmes, already exist, either in a partial or fragmented manner. Others, such as earmarked taxation, employer-based contributions, religious funds, regional cooperation, concessional financing and digital financing tools, would need varying degrees of legal, administrative, financial and political changes to be implemented. SNA revealed that MoHME, IHIO and SSO are the most influential actors in resource generation and policy formulation. However, despite the availability of diverse financial tools, these resources remain underused due to fragmentation and lack of prioritisation. The SNA results indicate that the viability of these mechanisms hinges on not only the existence of potential sources of funding but also on whether actors with authority, capacity to finance and operational engagement can be coordinated through a clear governance mechanism.

The following implications should be interpreted as policy options that require feasibility assessment rather than as immediately implementable recommendations.

Methodological limitations

This study has some methodological limitations. The scoping review was undertaken first to identify and map financing mechanisms rather than to assess the effectiveness or cost-effectiveness of specific financing models. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as a policy-oriented evidence map and not a comparative effectiveness review. Second, although the literature guided the identification of mechanisms, some recent country experiences or policy documents may have escaped attention due to the rapid changes in migration policies and financing arrangements. Third, the review of national documents was conducted as part of the broader research project and interpreted in this manuscript specifically from a health financing perspective; therefore, specific legal and administrative details may require further dedicated legal analysis.

Fourth, interview results are the opinion of selected stakeholders and may be influenced by institutional position, recall or social desirability. Although the study encompasses governmental, insurance, international, NGO, provider and migrant perspectives, the voices of undocumented migrants and highly marginalised groups may still be underrepresented. Fifth, the SNA was derived from expert ratings and aggregated scores. Though small differences in expert ratings did not materially alter the overall classification of the main stakeholders, formal uncertainty analysis or inter-rater agreement testing was not conducted. Centrality measures should therefore be understood as structured representations of perceived stakeholder roles rather than direct measures of actual financial flows or legal authority. Finally, this study did not perform costing, budget-impact analysis or implementation evaluation of the proposed mechanisms. Future research should assess the fiscal feasibility, administrative requirements, acceptability and equity impacts of selected financing options.

Conclusions

This study offers an evidence-informed framework for enhancing health financing for migrants and refugees in Iran. The results suggest that a coordinated, multi-source approach is preferable to reliance on a single financing mechanism. Such a strategy should seek to improve the utilisation of existing mechanisms and to critically appraise the feasibility of underused or untapped sources, including earmarked public revenues, employer contributions, pooled charitable and religious funds, disease-specific resources, regional cooperation and concessional financing.

Aligning financial instruments with broader policy reforms—such as tax earmarking, formalisation of migrant employment and pooling disease-specific funds—is essential for creating a resilient, predictable funding ecosystem. Incorporating regional and faith-based solidarity mechanisms further aligns the financing strategy with Iran’s cultural and geopolitical context.

The identified mechanisms should therefore be interpreted as potential pathways towards innovative and sustainable health financing rather than as validated or implementation-ready financing solutions. Their practicality requires further fiscal, legal, administrative and implementation assessment.

Ultimately, by shifting from fragmented, ad-hoc approaches towards coordinated, multi-source strategies, Iran could advance health equity for migrants and refugees. The findings offer valuable lessons for other countries facing similar demographic and financial challenges in migrant health governance.
