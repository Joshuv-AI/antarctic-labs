Mechanically adaptive crack-resistant hydrogels based on strain-induced macroscopic phase separation and hierarchical energy dissipation
Authors: Xiangren Kong, Zhongwei Wang, Bo Gong, Li Xin Hou, Zhi Jian Wang, Rui Xiao, Zi Liang Wu, Jin Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It remains a significant challenge to simultaneously achieve high stiffness, large extensibility, and strong resistance to crack propagation in hydrogels. Here, we report a hydrogel that undergoes a fully reversible strain-induced whitening transition with a high modulus ( ~ 30 MPa), high tensile strength (~15 MPa), large stretchability ( > 1000%). Importantly, the hydrogel achieves an exceptional fracture toughness around 100 kJ m−2 and exhibits the ability to autonomously suppress crack growth under load. The hydrogel is constructed from a strong polycation and a weak polyanion at an asymmetric molar ratio, producing a transparent and initially isotropic network containing ionic-bond-rich and hydrogen-bond-rich nanodomains. In situ X-ray scattering measurements, combined with molecular dynamics simulations, reveal a multiscale structural transition under tensile deformation. Specifically, yielding of the hydrogen-bond-rich soft domains enhances interdomain contrast, inducing whitening, together with orientation and adaptive dissociation of ionic-bond-rich domains. This process forms an extended anisotropic dissipative zone, thus effectively blunting and arresting cracks. This work demonstrates how compositional asymmetry enables a macroscopically uniform and transparent hydrogel to autonomously evolve anisotropic energy-dissipation pathways under strain, providing an efficient strategy for creating damage-tolerant soft materials for extreme mechanical environments.

Introduction

Hydrogels have emerged as a vital class of soft materials for human-machine interfaces1, soft robotics2,3, tissue engineering4–6, and flexible electronics7,8, owing to their exceptional structural programmability and biocompatibility. For such applications, mechanical robustness, manifested as high modulus, strength, and stretchability, is crucial for maintaining performance under complex mechanical loads. However, these mechanical attributes are often mutually constrained9. For example, conventional single-network hydrogels typically exhibit brittle or weak behavior, reflecting the long-standing trade-off between stiffness and toughness, or equivalently, between crack resistance and deformability10. Achieving a simultaneous enhancement of these key properties remains a central challenge in hydrogel science. To achieve tough hydrogels, numerous toughening strategies have been proposed in the past decades, including the incorporation of sacrificial bonds11, nanoparticle fillers12, and crystalline or physically confined structures13,14. These architectures can effectively dissipate energy under large deformation and enhance fracture energy, albeit at the cost of irreversible damage and permanent loss of function. Hydrogels utilizing reversible interactions such as hydrogen bonding15, ionic association16, or host-guest complexation17, can recover their structure and performance to some extent. However, these materials typically remain vulnerable to stress concentration at crack tips, leading to catastrophic fracture under large stress.

Crack insensitivity is essential for reliable operation under complex loading conditions, as hydrogels invariably encounter sharp defects and stress concentrators during service. A variety of mechanisms have been explored to resist crack propagation, including macroscopic chain orientation18, force-induced crystallization19, directional freezing20, fiber reinforcement21, secondary-network polymerization22,23, chain entanglement24, sequential crosslinking22,25, cyclic heating/cooling26,27, solvent exchange28, or nanoscale confinement polymerization29. Although these strategies significantly improve fatigue resistance, they typically rely on anisotropic microstructures and multi-step fabrication, and severe stress localization under large strain still triggers rapid crack growth. Photocoupling-based crosslinking strategies have enabled faster fabrication and improved interphase stress transfer30, yet the rigid and poorly deformable hard domains in these systems limit their ability to resist crack propagation. Hence, developing an intrinsically isotropic and strain-adaptive mechanism that can effectively suppress crack propagation persists as a critical challenge11,31–33.

Inspired by the hierarchical architectures and adaptive energy-dissipation mechanisms of biological tissues34–36, we introduce a class of hydrogels constructed by complexing a strong cationic polyelectrolyte with a weak anionic component at a markedly asymmetric feed ratio. This asymmetric molecular design breaks the conventional requirement for strict charge stoichiometry in polyion-complex (PIC) hydrogels, yielding a transparent gel with nanoscale ionic-bond-rich and hydrogen-bond-rich domains. During uniaxial stretching, the hydrogel undergoes a continuous transition from yielding to strain hardening, accompanied by pronounced macroscopic whitening. In situ ultra-small-angle X-ray scattering (USAXS) and molecular dynamics simulations reveal that the whitening originates from the rapid deformation of hydrogen-bond-rich domains and the delayed deformation of ion-rich domains, which increase the density contrast and drive strain-induced macroscopic phase separation of the pre-existing heterogeneous network. At high strains, the naturally interpenetrated architecture enables efficient stress transfer between the two phases. The ionic-bond-rich domains continue to dissipate energy through ionic bond dissociation and chain alignment, forming crack arrestors oriented along the stretching direction. Benefiting from this cooperative energy-dissipation mechanism, the hydrogel achieves a high modulus (~30 MPa), high tensile strength (~15 MPa), and ultrahigh fracture toughness (~100 kJ m−2). This design provides a simple yet general strategy for achieving synergistic enhancement of stiffness, strength, toughness, and crack resistance under simplified fabrication conditions, offering insights into the rational design of mechanically robust soft materials.

Hydrogel preparation, strain-induced whitening, and crack resistance

When stretched, the hydrogel exhibits a distinctive mechanical response characterized by a high initial modulus, a broad yielding plateau, and a pronounced strain-hardening regime (Supplementary Movie 1). The whitening threshold, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon }_{w}$$\end{document}εw, was defined as the abrupt change of the transmittance-strain curve, i.e., the intersection between the initial high transmittance regime and the subsequent steep-decline regime (Supplementary Fig. 4). For the 1:3 formulation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon }_{{w}}$$\end{document}εw is approximately 400% and closely coincides with the onset of strain hardening after the yield-like plateau, linking the whitening transition to stress-activated structural reorganization within the network (Fig. 1C). SEM examination of samples fixed in the whitened state revealed no obvious pores in the whitened region (Supplementary Fig. 5). Further scattering comparison shows that, unlike cavitation-related patterns, our hydrogel exhibits an opposite 2D anisotropy and no cavitation-like low-q jump upon whitening (Supplementary Fig. 6), supporting a density-contrast origin rather than pore- or cavitation-driven whitening. Despite this drastic optical change, the material sustains exceptionally large deformations (>1000%) and achieves high tensile strength. Upon stretching, the densely distributed physical bonds and extensive chain entanglements provide a high modulus10,24. As deformation proceeds, weak hydrogen bonds and other labile interactions rupture first, leading to a yielding plateau in which the deformation is relatively uniform and the densities of the two phases remain comparable (Fig. 1D), thus maintaining transparency. When the stress starts to activate the strong ionic bonds or chain entanglements, strain hardening occurs. At this stage, the density of the hydrogen-bond-rich phase decreases rapidly, whereas the density of the ionic-bond-rich phase changes slightly, resulting in an increased density contrast and macroscopic whitening (Fig. 1E). Critically, the intimate interconnection between the two phases facilitates efficient stress transfer, thereby enhancing the stretchability and load-bearing capability of the gel37. As the ionic-bond-rich regions experience partial bond dissociation under the large transmitted load, their hard phase domains enlarge and become oriented along the tensile direction, forming crack arrestors that block crack propagation. Meanwhile, the strong interphase coupling promotes collective hardening of multiple PAAc chains31, leading to increase in the observed fracture strength.

The whitening marks a transformation in the gel’s damage tolerance. In a notched sample, whitening nucleates only near the crack tip at a global strain of ~200%, while the surrounding material remains transparent (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Movie 3), indicating that local strain amplification enables the crack-tip region to exceed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon }_{w}$$\end{document}εw, thereby blunting the crack and suppressing catastrophic propagation. This self-organized protective zone arises without any predefined anisotropy, highlighting the intrinsically isotropic yet strain-adaptive nature of the network. As a result of this adaptive dissipation mechanism, the hydrogel achieves an exceptional combination of high modulus (~30 MPa), high tensile strength (~15 MPa), and ultrahigh fracture toughness approaching 100 kJ m−2 (Fig. 1G), exceeding the representative tough hydrogels9,10,38–42.

Feed-ratio-governed mechanical response and whitening transition

The tensile whitening is highly sensitive to the monomer feed ratio of the two polyelectrolytes (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Increasing the cation fraction mainly reduces the strength and modulus of the gel, whereas the effect on fracture strain is non-monotonic (Supplementary Fig. 7B). In particular, at PMATAC:PAAc\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,=$$\end{document}=1:2.5, the gel shows a slightly higher fracture strain than the whitening formulations, but the tensile strength decreases by ~80% and the modulus by ~90%, and no whitening occurs during stretching (Fig. 2A). To probe the origin, we compared the rheology of whitening and non-whitening gels. In comparison, the non-whitening gel (1:2.5) exhibits a single tan \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document}δ peak over 0–80 °C (Fig. 2C), consistent with a uniform network at the observation scale. We attribute these differences to the excess cations in the 1:2.5 formulation: once the weak polyanion is saturated, additional cationic charges introduce electrostatic repulsion that lowers the effective binding energy of the network, thereby diminishing load-bearing capacity and suppressing whitening. Furthermore, the final water content of different proportions of hydrogels also varies (Supplementary Fig. 8), consistent with the trend of mechanical properties. We further performed stress-relaxation tests to understand network dynamics at different deformation stages (Fig. 2D). Two strains were selected: 200% (below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon }_{w}$$\end{document}εw, transparent yielding plateau) and 500% (above \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon }_{w}$$\end{document}εw, whitened hardening regime). In both cases, the stress decays to around 50% within 10 s, reflecting rapid breakage/reformation of a large number of dynamic physical crosslinks-the primary source of yielding and toughness. Over 500 s, the transparent gel at 200% loses ~80% of its stress, while the whitened gel at 500% loses \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ > $$\end{document}>92%, approaching negligible load-bearing. This indicates that whitening creates additional dissipation pathways: the entangled backbone and ionic-bond-rich domains are forced to stretch and align, forming a load-bearing skeleton during hardening; under fixed strain, these highly tensioned segments subsequently relax via ionic bond dissociation, disentanglement, and sliding, causing the pronounced stress decay.

Maintaining 500% strain for 6 h leads to a gradual and ultimately complete recovery of transparency (Fig. 2E). This slow optical recovery indicates that the whitening state does not relax through simple viscoelastic relaxation alone, but instead involves slow structural/density re-equilibration between two physically crosslinked domains with distinct bond strengths. Over sufficiently long times, the dynamic bonds (particularly reversible ionic associations) reorganize and rebalance, allowing the oriented network to relax back to a macroscopically uniform transparent state.

The tensile-whitening hydrogel exhibits pronounced reversibility. During unloading from a strain close to fracture, the whitened region progressively faded and nearly vanished (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Movie 1), supporting reversible concentration fluctuations between the two phases. Below the whitening threshold, the stress–strain response recovered within 1 h, implying rapid and reversible internal rearrangement (Fig. 2G). When stretched into the whitening regime, recovery was slower; nevertheless, the gel ultimately regained its dimensions, transparency and tensile response despite apparent yielding and strain hardening (Fig. 2H). This complete recovery indicates that the physical network preserves structural memory, without irreversible large-scale rearrangement of ionic associations or chain entanglements.

Microstructural evolution during stretch-induced whitening

The mesoscale structural evolution of the hydrogel during tensile whitening was elucidated by in-situ ultra-small-angle X-ray scattering (USAXS). Figure 3A shows the 2D scattering patterns at different deformation stages. The scattering images clearly reveal a transition from isotropic to anisotropic features as the strain increases (refer to the diagram at the bottom right corner). At low strain, or before the whitening threshold is reached, the SAXS pattern remains nearly isotropic and featureless, mainly because the scattering contrast between the coexisting soft and hard domains is still too low for the underlying heterogeneity to be clearly resolved43. This can be attributed to the smaller deformation of ionic-bond-rich domains compared with the hydrogen-bond-rich matrix, which compresses the lateral space and drives neighboring ionic-bond-rich regions to cluster, generating a laterally aligned morphology. This produces laterally periodic spacing between adjacent domains. Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) was employed to probe finer structural features at the tens-of-nanometer scale. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11, the 2D SAXS patterns of the tensile-whitening hydrogel exhibit elliptical contours oriented perpendicular to the stretching axis, even at small strains, indicating a rapid alignment of the polymer network at this length scale. The degree of orientation intensifies progressively with increasing strain, reflecting the continuous reorganization of the nanoscale network. In contrast, the non-whitening hydrogel maintains isotropic, diffuse ring patterns throughout deformation (Supplementary Fig. 12), demonstrating the absence of any pronounced orientation or phase separation. This contrast highlights the multiscale and hierarchical energy dissipation in the tensile-whitening hydrogel, whereas the non-whitening counterpart exhibits a single, uniform dissipation behavior.

In addition, in-situ wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) was performed to capture the structural evolution at the molecular scale (~1 nm) during stretching. As strain increases, the initially diffuse, isotropic scattering halo transforms into a broad circular ring, indicating that the intermolecular spacing evolves from a random distribution toward a more uniform distance (Fig. 3G). The integrated 1D profiles show a wide, shallow peak in the undeformed state, which gradually shifts to higher q and narrows upon stretching, signifying a decrease in interchain spacing and enhanced chain alignment. Notably, the in-situ WAXS results of the non-whitening hydrogel are nearly identical to those of the whitening gel (Supplementary Fig. 13), both exhibiting reduced interchain spacing upon strain and enhanced chain alignment. This indicates that reduced interchain spacing and chain entanglement, and alignment alone, are insufficient to account for the dramatic difference in crack resistance.

Fracture toughness under different conditions was quantified using the pure-shear tests51,52. Specifically, the stretch at crack initiation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda }_{C}$$\end{document}λC) in the precut specimen was recorded, and the strain energy density was obtained by integrating the stress-strain curve of the corresponding uncut specimen to the same stretch, from which \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${G}_{c}$$\end{document}Gc was calculated (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 16)53,54. The results show that, for the non-whitening gel (Supplementary Fig. 17A), once a crack initiated, it propagated rapidly to failure-the inset reveals the crack swiftly traversing the specimen, indicating brittle fracture. By contrast, in the tensile-whitening gel (Supplementary Fig. 17B), a pronounced whitening band developed at the crack tip with increasing strain, which effectively suppressed further crack advance. Across compositions, the whitening gels exhibited fracture toughness exceeding that of the non-whitening gels by over an order of magnitude, rising from 2 to 3 kJ m-2 to ≈ 35–70 kJ m−2 (Fig. 5C). This confirms that strain-induced macroscopic phase separation and microstructural reorganization are the key to enhanced toughness.

The dynamic physical crosslinks make fracture toughness highly tunable, enabling the network to adapt its dissipation capacity to the loading environment. Figure 5D shows that toughness strongly depends on strain rate: at low rates (leftmost points), the gel remains transparent with toughness <10 kJ m-2; at rates above ≈10 mm min−1, tensile whitening emerges and toughness rapidly rises, reaching ≈100 kJ m−2 (Supplementary Fig. 18). This indicates that the formation of dissipative structures at the crack tip is rate-controlled, with energy dissipation primarily originating from viscoelastic losses in the phase-separated domains and interfacial dissipation. Figure 5E shows that the fracture toughness decreases monotonically with increasing temperature and drops sharply to ≈4 kJ m−2 above 50 °C, where whitening disappears (Supplementary Fig. 19). This suggests that the whitening-assisted toughening mechanism has a thermal stability limit: at elevated temperature, faster relaxation of dynamic ionic and hydrogen-bonded interactions reduces the density contrast and structural persistence of the phase-separated domains, so that the crack-tip dissipative structure can no longer be maintained. Notably, the fracture toughness of the transparent gel remains competitive among hydrogels11,31,32.

In high-stress engineering scenarios, hydrogels inevitably come into contact with rigid materials. Sharp edges can introduce surface cuts during stretching, often leading to catastrophic failure. Here, we demonstrate the exceptional crack-growth resistance of the tensile-whitening hydrogel under such extreme conditions. During the continuous stretching (at a speed of 100 mm/min), a sharp blade was used to introduce a notch corresponding to ~40% of the sample width. As shown in Fig. 5F, the tensile non-whitening gel fractured immediately after the cut, whereas the whitening gel exhibited a distinct whitening zone nucleating around the notch at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}λ = 5 (Fig. 5G). With further stretching to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}λ = 7, the whitening zone gradually shrank to a triangular region with its apex at the crack tip. This dynamic evolution visualizes the stress-release pathway of a soft, physically crosslinked material under coupled large deformation and fracture, offering a direct and intuitive experimental route to probe stress redistribution in soft networks under complex loading. The strain field evolution at the crack tip was captured in situ using a digital image correlation (DIC) system to visualize deformation during uniaxial stretching of notched samples (Supplementary Fig. 20). For the tensile-whitening hydrogel, when the local strain at the crack tip reaches \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}λ = 5, the strain-growth rate near the tip gradually decreases, while regions farther away from the crack tip begin to deform rapidly. Eventually, most of the gel reaches a nearly uniform strain distribution.

Based on the combined experimental and simulation results, we propose a mechanistic model for the crack-propagation resistance of tensile-whitening hydrogels (Fig. 5H). During stretching, the region immediately ahead the crack tip undergoes the highest strain rate, leading to rapid local deformation. Once the local hardening strain is reached, macroscopic phase separation and alignment of ionic-bond-rich domains first emerge at the crack tip, serving as dissipative barriers to hinder crack advancement. Owing to the interpenetrated molecular connectivity between the two phases (with no discrete interface) and the dynamically dissociable and deformable ionic-bond-rich hard phase, the hydrogel network exhibits an exceptionally efficient stress-transfer capability30,36. Consequently, the high stress at the crack tip is rapidly redistributed throughout the network, allowing strain in distant regions to increase gradually; as a result, the fracture process zone (FPZ) expands rapidly. Ultimately, the entire hydrogel reaches a uniform strain state and undergoes global rupture, rather than catastrophic crack propagation.

Discussion

The work designs a mechanically adaptive tough hydrogel that reconciles high stiffness, large extensibility, and exceptional crack resistance within a structurally simple, physically crosslinked network. In contrast to many state-of-the-art tough and crack-resistant hydrogels11,18–23,31–33,55, our system starts from an isotropic, optically transparent polyelectrolyte complex and develops anisotropic dissipation pathways only when and where large deformation is imposed. The asymmetric pairing of a strong cationic and a weak anionic polyelectrolyte breaks the conventional requirement for strict charge stoichiometry in PIC hydrogels, driving the formation of ionic-bond-rich and hydrogen-bond-rich domains that are initially difficult to distinguish at the mesoscale. Under load, however, this latent compositional asymmetry is converted into a pronounced mechanical asymmetry. Hydrogen bond-rich regions deform and relax rapidly, while ionic bond-rich domains resist deformation and eventually align along the tensile direction, producing a strain-induced macroscopic phase separation that both governs energy dissipation. This mechanochemical transduction mechanism complements and extends existing design frameworks for tough hydrogels, in which energy dissipation is commonly built into the initial microstructure rather than that dynamically generated by the mechanical field11,31–33.

Our multiscale characterization also clarifies how the present whitening mechanism differs from several established toughening routes in hydrogels. First, it differs from lower-hydration, densification-dominated systems, where reinforcement is commonly associated with reduced water content, chain packing, and entanglement9,10,24. In our system, however, the in-situ WAXS response is similar in the whitening and non-whitening gels, with both showing reduced interchain spacing and enhanced chain alignment under strain, indicating that molecular-scale densification alone is not sufficient to explain the pronounced difference in crack resistance. Instead, the mesoscale amplification of density contrast and the anisotropic reorganization of ionic-bond-rich domains are directly correlated with macroscopic whitening and crack-tip protection. Second, it differs from crystallization-based PVA systems. Extensive and elegant studies by Zhao et al. have demonstrated strong fatigue resistance in low-hydration PVA hydrogels, where reinforcement is typically associated with crystallite/nanofibril formation or pre-engineered anisotropic hierarchical architectures13,14. By contrast, the present hydrogel starts from a macroscopically transparent and isotropic network and develops crack-tip protection adaptively only under load. Third, it also differs from previously reported bicontinuous, microphase-separated, and double-network hydrogels. In those systems, the key dissipative pathways are largely predefined during fabrication, or fracture energy is mainly supplied by irreversible scission of a brittle network22,23,56. For example, bicontinuous self-healing hydrogels enhance fatigue resistance by distributing energy across percolated soft and hard pathways16,38,39, but their microstructure is largely set during fabrication and only evolves modestly under load. In the present system, the ion-rich domains instead serve as reconfigurable hard phases, which stiffen at high strain, act as crack arrestors, and still recover via reversible ionic dissociation and reassociation. This dynamic compositional asymmetry and hierarchical structural reorganization cooperate to transform localized stress concentration into spatially distributed deformation and, ultimately, global rupture without catastrophic crack propagation16,29,33,43,44.

We have developed a general strategy for creating adaptive, damage-tolerant hydrogels with a strain-activated macroscopic phase separation between two dynamically bonded phases. The asymmetric polyelectrolyte complexation platform offers a broad design space, such as charge ratio and ionization degree of the weak polyelectrolyte. In the meanwhile, the temperature or loading rate jointly control the mechanical performance. Accordingly, the whitening-assisted toughening effect is most pronounced below 50 °C and at strain rates above 1.6 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×10−2 s−1. The high modulus and ultrahigh fracture toughness achieved in this work, together with the visual readout of damage through reversible whitening, make these hydrogels promising candidates for load-bearing biointerfaces, damage-tolerant soft robotic components operating under extreme or intermittent mechanical loading, and protective coatings where early warning and suppression of crack growth are critical. In addition, integrating this mechanically triggered whitening/phase-amplification mechanism with other noncovalent motifs or with spatial patterning may enable soft materials that not only withstand complex mechanical histories but also encode, visualize, and actively regulate them, further narrowing the gap between synthetic hydrogels and the adaptively tough architectures that exist in biological tissues34,36.

Methods

Materials preparation

Acrylic acid (AAc), Methacrylatoethyl trimethyl ammonium chloride (MATAC), 2-Ketoglutaric acid (photoinitiator) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Millipore deionized water (resistivity: 18.2 MΩ/cm) was used in all experiments.

The cationic monomer was photopolymerized in water at a concentration of 2 M using α-ketoglutaric acid as the initiator. Then, the product was dried to remove moisture and was prepared for use. A specific amount of solid PMATAC was weighed. A predetermined proportion of AAc and a photoinitiator (0.5% of the total monomer molar quantity) were added, followed by the addition of deionized water to achieve a total monomer molar concentration of 3 M. In this work, reported compositions such as 1:3 and 1:3.5 refer to the monomer feed molar ratio of MATAC to AAc in the precursor formulation. Unless otherwise specified, all formulations discussed in this manuscript are denoted by this feed-ratio notation. The mixture was stirred thoroughly until all solid components were dissolved. The resulting homogeneous solution was purged with argon gas for 10 min to remove dissolved oxygen and subsequently transferred into a glass mold. Finally, the solution in the mold was exposed to double-sided ultraviolet irradiation at a wavelength of 365 nm and polymerized at room temperature for 8 h to initiate free radical polymerization. The as-prepared PAAc/PMATAC hydrogel was immersed in a large amount of deionized water, and the water was changed every 12 h. The gels reached an equilibrium state after 5 days of immersion.

Mechanical tests

The hydrogel sheets were cut into dumbbell-shaped samples with an initial gauge length of 12 mm and a width of 2 mm. Uniaxial tensile tests were performed on the samples at a specific stretching rate at a specific temperature. The nominal stress and engineering strain were recorded, and Young’s modulus was extracted from the initial slope of the tensile stress–strain curve with an engineering strain below 20%. Characterizations

The interactions between ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds during the swelling process of this hydrogel were characterized by attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Nicolet iS10 ATR-FTIR; Thermo Scientific). The spectra were obtained with 32 scans and 4 cm−1 resolution in the range 4000–400 cm−1. The transmittance of the gel was recorded by a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, UV-2550) at a wavelength of 500 nm.

Atomic force microscopy in combination with infrared spectroscopy (AFM–IR) was conducted by a multimode AFM microscope (Nano-IR2-FS, Anasys Instruments) with the contact mode at a fixed wavenumber of 1710 cm−1, highlighting the distribution of carbonyl groups.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The initial atomic model of the asymmetric polyelectrolyte hydrogel was constructed to replicate the experimental system, comprising 30 chains of polycation PMATAC (degree of polymerization, DP = 40) and 60 chains of polyanion PAAc (DP = 60) at a deliberate 1:3 molar ratio within a cubic simulation box with initial dimensions of approximately 250 × 250 × 250 Å3. The initial amorphous configuration was generated using PACKMOL, which was subsequently solvated with 12,000 water molecules using the SPC/E model. Chloride counterions were added to ensure charge neutrality of the overall system. All interatomic interactions were described by the OPLS-AA force field. Long-range electrostatic interactions were resolved using the Particle-Particle Particle-Mesh (PPPM) solver with a precision of 10−4, while short-range van der Waals interactions were truncated at a cutoff distance of 12 Å. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three directions, and the SHAKE algorithm was applied to constrain high-frequency bond vibrations involving hydrogen atoms. All molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) package, and visualization of the simulation trajectories was performed using the Open Visualization Tool (OVITO).

The simulation workflow initiated with energy minimization using the conjugate gradient algorithm to relieve any local steric clashes. The equations of motion were integrated using the Velocity-Verlet algorithm with a time step of 1.0 fs. The system was then equilibrated under isothermal-isobaric (NPT) conditions at 1 atm through a two-stage protocol. Namely, it was first heated from 100 K to the target temperature of 300 K over 0.5 ns, followed by an extensive 20 ns relaxation period at 300 K, maintained using the Nosé-Hoover thermostat and barostat. For mechanical characterization, uniaxial tensile deformation was applied along the Z-axis at a constant engineering strain rate of 5 × 10⁸ s⁻¹. During deformation, the transverse dimensions (X and Y) were independently coupled to the barostat to maintain zero lateral stress (1 atm), allowing for natural Poisson contraction. The atomic stress tensor was computed at regular intervals using the virial theorem.

To quantitatively correlate with scattering data, the chain orientation degree of both polyelectrolytes during stretching was characterized by calculating Herman’s orientation factor for the polymer backbones relative to the tensile direction. Furthermore, to decipher the mechanism behind the macroscopic whitening, the simulation box was partitioned into 27 sub-regions. The sub-region with the highest concentration of cationic (PMATAC) chains was defined as the ionic-bond-rich domain, while the one with the highest concentration of anionic (PAAc) chains was defined as the hydrogen-bond-rich domain. The temporal evolution of the respective polyion densities in these two representative domains was tracked throughout the tensile deformation to monitor the strain-induced density divergence process.
