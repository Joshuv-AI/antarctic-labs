Expressive Power of Property Graph Constraint Languages
Stefania Dumbrava, Nadime Francis, Victor Marsault, Steven Sailly

Abstract
We present the first principled and systematic study of the expressive power of property graph constraint languages, focused on the recent PG-Keys language, set to inform the upcoming revision of the GQL standard. To this end, we position PG-Keys within the broader landscape of existing formalisms. In particular, we compare PG-Keys with two core property graph constraint languages: Graph Functional Dependencies (GFD) and Graph Generating Dependencies (GGD). One hurdle is that these formalisms allow different kinds of graph pattern languages and data predicates. To make a fair comparison, based on their structural differences only, we first present a unifying framework. Within this framework, we consider conjunctive regular path queries (CRPQ) as graph patterns with equality and inequality predicates. We then identify well-behaved fragments, establish expressiveness inclusion, and prove separation results, yielding a complete and strict hierarchy of expressive power. The results identify precisely when PG-Keys provide strictly greater expressive power, clarifying their place among state-of-the-art property graph constraint formalisms.

Introduction

Integrity constraints are central to ensuring data quality and supporting reliable
data integration and exchange. Constraint languages have been
extensively investigated
[
1
,
11
]
for relational databases,
leading to a deep understanding of their expressive power, complexity, and impact on
query processing. Conversely, the comparative expressiveness of constraint languages for
graph databases

remains underexplored, as the interplay between graph topology and data values introduces new modeling and reasoning challenges.

The most generic form of integrity constraints is given by general dependencies
[
8
]
, defined as being of the form
\forall\overline{x}\forall\overline{y}\big(\varphi(\overline{x},\overline{y})\Rightarrow\exists\overline{z}\psi(\overline{y},\overline{z})\big)
. The body of the formula,
\varphi
, is a non-empty conjunction of relational atoms and its head,
\psi
, is either a single equality atom, for equality generating dependencies (
EGD
), or a non-empty conjunction of relational atoms, for tuple generating dependencies (
TGD
). When considering graph dependencies, relations are restricted to arity two. Building on this foundation, several constraint formalisms draw inspiration from and extend TGD and EGD in graph-specific ways:
Graph Generating Dependencies
(
GGD
)
[
29
]
extends
TGD
with generic similarity functions in head atoms, while
Graph Functional Dependencies
(
GFD
)
[
14
]
and
Graph Entity Dependencies
(
GED
)
[
13
]
adapt
EGD
, the latter allowing equalities between vertex identifiers.

Property graphs
[
6
]
are widely used as data models for graph databases, representing data as multi-labeled multigraphs with key-value properties on both nodes and edges. As their adoption for highly interconnected and semantically rich data grows, several dedicated constraint formalisms have been proposed.

Recently, the
PG-Keys
language
[
5
]
was introduced to reference and identify property graph objects.
PG-Keys
leverages three
assertion keywords
:

{\mathrel{\text{\lstinline{{\lst@@@set@language\lst@@@set@numbers\lst@@@set@frame\lst@@@set@rulecolor\lst@@@set@language\footnotesize{\@listingGroup{ltx_lst_keyword}{\color[rgb]{0.4961,0,0.332}{EXCLUSIVE}}}}}}}}

2.2
Query Languages

Our results are stated for
conjunctive regular path queries
, as defined next. Our results also apply to the case of
conjunctive queries
, which are here defined as a subcase of
CRPQ
.

Definition 2

(Conjunctive Regular Path Query)
.

A
conjunctive regular path query

(\text{\rm\sc CRPQ})
is a tuple
Q(\overline{x})=(V_{Q},P_{Q},\eta_{Q},\lambda_{Q})
where
V_{Q},P_{Q}\subset\var
are pairwise disjoint finite sets of

and
path

\eta_{Q}:P_{Q}\to V_{Q}\times V_{Q}

and

\lambda_{Q}=\lambda_{Q,1}\cup\lambda_{Q,2}

\lambda_{Q,1}:V_{Q}\to{\lab}
and
\lambda_{Q,2}:P_{Q}\to\reg(\lab)

\overline{x}
is a tuple of variables from
V_{Q}
and

, called

.
We write
\var_{Q}=V_{Q}\cup P_{Q}
the set of variables that appear in

A

is interpreted as a graph pattern, whose edges represent paths of the graph that conform to the given regular expression, as defined below.

Definition 3

(Match)

Let

2.

for all

,

such that
\eta(h(e))=h(\eta_{Q}(e))
and
\lambda(h(e))\cap L(\lambda_{Q}(e))\neq\emptyset

The
answer

over

Q(G)=~{\mskip-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu(h(x_{1}),\dots,h(x_{k}))|h\text{ is a match for }Q\text{ over }G\mskip-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}

(1)

Remark 4
.

We only consider

Given two

In the property graph model, both vertices and edges may bear properties, but paths are not allowed to.
Hence, data predicates will later be allowed only for path variables that always bind to a single edge; that is, for edge variables, which are defined next.

Definition 5

(Edge variables, Conjunctive Queries)
.

Let
Q(\overline{x})\mathbin{=}(V_{Q},P_{Q},\eta_{Q},\lambda_{Q})

1.

A path variable

2.

In the special case of

In addition to the topological conditions expressed by

More precisely,

are expressions of the form

We also use the following derived predicates:
\bot

{\mskip-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mux.a=1,x.a=2\mskip-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}

Similarly, we will use

{\mskip-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu\nu=\nu|\nu\in\overline{\nu}\mskip-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu}

.

Definition 6

(Conditional

)
.

A

(simply called

(Q(\overline{x}),C(\overline{x}))

{\text{\rm\sc CRPQ}[=,\neq]}

2.3
Revisiting Known Graph Constraint Languages

We revisit the main graph constraint languages that form the basis of our study, namely
GFD

[
14
]
,
GGD

[
29
]
, and
PG-Keys

[
5
]
, and recast them in a common, parametric framework. Note that, for this purpose, for
GGD
, we only consider (in)equality predicates.

Definition 7

(
GFD
and
GGD
)
.

A
Graph Generating Dependency

(\text{{\rm\sc{}GGD}})

is a tuple
\varphi=(Q_{s}(\overline{x},\overline{y}),C_{s}(\overline{x},\overline{y})\Rightarrow Q_{t}(\overline{x},\overline{z}),C_{t}(\overline{x},\overline{z}))

where
\overline{x},\overline{y},\overline{z}
are disjoint tuples of variables, and

(Q_{s},C_{s})
and
(Q_{t},C_{t})

are queries.
A
Graph Functional Dependency

(\text{{\rm\sc{}GFD}})

is a
GGD
where
\overline{z}
is empty and
Q_{t}(\overline{x},\overline{z})
is trivial. A
GFD
will be written as
(Q_{s}(\overline{x},\overline{y}),C_{s}(\overline{x},\overline{y})\Rightarrow C_{t}(\overline{x}))
for short.

Moreover, we say that a graph
G
satisfies
\varphi
, denoted as
G\models\varphi
, if for all matches
h_{s}
of
(Q_{s},C_{s})
in
G
there exists a match
h_{t}
for
(Q_{t},C_{t})
over
G
that is consistent with
h_{s}
.
Otherwise, we say that
G

violates

\varphi
, denoted as
G\not\models\varphi
.

For instance, the
GFD

(\texttt{(x)}\xleftarrow{\texttt{:hasMember}}\texttt{(z)}\xrightarrow{\texttt{:hasMember}}\texttt{(y)},\emptyset\Rightarrow{\mskip-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu\texttt{x.speaks}=\texttt{y.speaks}\mskip-4.0mu plus -2.0mu minus -4.0mu})
enforces that two members of a same forum speak the same language.
Similarly, the
GGD

(\texttt{(z)}\xrightarrow{\texttt{:containerOf}}\texttt{(y)}\xrightarrow{\texttt{:hasCreator}}\texttt{(x)},\emptyset\Rightarrow\texttt{(z)}\xrightarrow{\texttt{:hasMember}}\texttt{(x)},\emptyset)
enforces that the creator of a message is a member of the forum where the message is posted.

Graph Entity Dependencies (
GED
, see
[
13
]
) are not explicitly defined in this document, as they are subsumed by our parametric definition of
GFD
.
Indeed, the difference between
GFD
and
GED
lies in the fact that
GED
can compare vertex and edge identities, while
GFD
can only test data values.
In our work, this difference is captured by the predicates that are allowed between square brackets.

Indeed, the original definition of
GFD
corresponds to the case where the comparison between object identifiers is forbidden.

PG-Keys

[
5
]
have been introduced to identify unique vertices, edges, and properties in a property graph.
It allows for the selection of the set of objects to which it applies within a
scope
, to specify how to obtain a
key value
through a

6
Related Work

For query languages over RDF graphs, extensive research has examined their expressive power by comparing SPARQL dialects with conjunctive queries, Datalog, and navigational languages
[
26
,
7
]
. These studies highlight the trade-offs that arise when adding operators such as
UNION
,
OPTIONAL
,
FILTER
, and property paths. A complementary line of work has focused on schema languages. In RDF, the two dominant formalisms, i.e., SHACL (Shapes Constraint Language)
[
17
]
and ShEx (Shape Expressions)
[
28
]
, have been compared in terms of expressiveness and computational complexity. Recently, Ahmetaj et al.
[
2
]
proposed a uniform framework that also incorporates PG-Schema
[
4
]
, clarifying commonalities and distinctions across different RDF and property graph schema formalisms. The work in
[
33
]
introduces the
PG-Schema-PC
extension of PG-Schema for property-level modeling with structural, cardinality, and range constraints and positions it against SHACL and ShEx.

In contrast, aside from
[
13
]
, property graph constraint languages have largely been studied in isolation.
GFD

[
14
]
generalizes relational functional dependencies to account for attributes and graph patterns. Extended Uniqueness Constraints
[
32
,
30
]
are
EGD
that comprise
GFD
and are strictly less expressive than the
PG-Keys
and
GGD
fragments we analyze.
GED

[
13
]
extends
GFD
with global identifiers to support entity resolution;
GDD

[
20
]
extend
GED
with distance and similarity functions for approximate entity resolution.

Several proposals have targeted keys in graphs. GKeys
[
12
]
capture the subset of
GED
that only enforces equality between identifiers. Pokorný et al.
[
27
]
extend Cypher with uniqueness constraints on multiple attributes, while Neo4j Keys
[
21
]
adapt relational candidate keys to property graphs. Skavantzos and Link
[
31
]
propose g
FD
, a restriction of
GFD
aimed at normalization. The most expressive among these,
PG-Keys

[
5
]
were introduced as lightweight mechanisms to identify and reference property graph objects.

Beyond keys, richer constraints have been proposed. While
TGD
and their fragments (e.g., guarded, frontier-guarded, sticky) have been extensively studied in relational and RDF contexts
[
9
,
16
]
, a comparable systematic investigation for property graphs remains lacking. Manouvrier and Belhajjame
[
22
,
23
]
provide a first answer. They present a comprehensive review of property graph constraint languages and identify mapping rules to translate several formalisms into
PG-Keys
, complemented by a prototype implementation. In particular, they study translations of FD-like constraints and restricted forms of
GGD
into what we call unconstrained
PG-Keys
(see
\cref
r:npgk-is-ggd-cq-eq),
an
overapproximation
of
PG-Keys
that relaxes the main restriction of having only one shared variable. Under this relaxation, it is indeed expected that
PG-Keys
subsume all other formalisms. Their focus is on implementable mappings rather than on formal expressiveness bounds.

Our work is complementary: we study the original
PG-Keys
language and establish a complete hierarchy of strict inclusions and separations. The most natural formalisms for comparison are
GFD
/
GED
and
GGD
, as
PG-Keys
target key-like and identification constraints over graph patterns. Note that
GED
does not appear in
\cref
fig:inclusion-cqeq-cqneq, as our parametric
GFD
definition subsumes both.
GDD

[
20
]
introduce orthogonal features (e.g., similarity predicates) outside our scope, and the other formalisms are subsumed by
GFD
/
GED
.

7
Conclusion and perspectives

We present the first systematic comparison of
PG-Keys
with the prominent constraint languages
GFD
,
GED
, and
GGD
, establishing strict expressiveness hierarchies. Our analysis reveals that the number of shared variables is the crucial parameter governing expressive power. In particular, for
{\text{\rm\sc CRPQ}[=,\neq]}
,
PG-Keys
= 1
GGD
, meaning that the assertion keywords of
PG-Keys
are syntactic sugar: every
PG-Key
can be compiled into a 1
GGD
. This result can directly inform future language design, separating user convenience from underlying expressiveness. Our results open several future directions, that we detail below.

Connectedness

Query connectedness is motivated by practical considerations. For example, Neo4j issues a warning when a query
MATCH
contains disconnected patterns, as the resulting Cartesian product may produce large amounts of data and degrade performance
[
25
]
. Consider the query
Q(x,y)=(x)(y)
returning all pairs of vertices of the graph. This allows disconnected constraints such as
\varphi=\big((x)(y),\emptyset\Rightarrow(x)\rightarrow(y),\emptyset\big)
enforcing that the graph is a clique.

In general, imposing connectedness seems to strictly reduce the expressive power of
GGD
,
GFD
and
PG-Keys
, but not necessarily to the same extent.
For instance, we used disconnected constraints to simulate the
{\mathrel{\text{\lstinline{{\lst@@@set@language\lst@@@set@numbers\lst@@@set@frame\lst@@@set@rulecolor\lst@@@set@language\footnotesize{\@listingGroup{ltx_lst_keyword}{\color[rgb]{0.4961,0,0.332}{EXCLUSIVE}}}}}}}}
keyword in
GGD
(
\cref
p:pgk-sub-ggd-cq-eq,p:mpgkey=pgkey). Under connectedness, we expect the inclusion to not hold, and
PG-Keys
to be incomparable with
GGD
in expressive power.
Assertion keywords can thus be interpreted as a weak form of disconnectedness: no connected
GGD
can simulate the
PG-Key

Underlying query language

Practical property graph query languages, such as Cypher
[
15
]
and GQL
[
10
,
18
]
, are more expressive than the fragment we consider (
CRPQ
with (in)equality). For instance, they allow navigation of edges in both directions (that is, of (
C
)2
RPQ
). From an expressiveness perspective, 2
RPQ
strictly subsume
RPQ
, which might collapse some of the constraint fragments.

Moreover, our
RPQ
and
CRPQ
are evaluated under the so-called
all-walk semantics
, in reference to the
{{\color[rgb]{0.4961,0,0.332}\mathrel{\text{\lstinline{{\lst@@@set@language\lst@@@set@numbers\lst@@@set@frame\lst@@@set@rulecolor\lst@@@set@language\footnotesize{\@listingGroup{ltx_lst_identifier}{{ALL}}}}}}}}}

{{\color[rgb]{0.4961,0,0.332}\mathrel{\text{\lstinline{{\lst@@@set@language\lst@@@set@numbers\lst@@@set@frame\lst@@@set@rulecolor\lst@@@set@language\footnotesize{\@listingGroup{ltx_lst_identifier}{{WALK}}}}}}}}}
keywords in GQL
[
18
]
.
Other
RPQ
semantics (e.g., trail or shortest semantics) are used in Cypher and GQL.
In those cases, we expect the landscape to differ significantly from the results in
\cref
fig:inclusion-cqeq-cqneq.
Indeed,
CRPQ
under trail semantics are not stable under homomorphism,
and
CRPQ
are not monotonic under shortest semantics.

Cypher and GQL allow to
decompose
path variables.
For instance, the GQL query
Q_{s}={}
MATCH p = (x)-[e]->(y)-[f]->(z)
decomposes the path variable
p
into the variables
x,e,y,f,z
: the variable
y
(resp.
f
) is necessarily bound to the second vertex (resp. edge) of the path associated with
p
. This would effectively remove the limit on shared variables.
Indeed, consider the constraint where
Q_{s}
is the source query and the target query is
Q_{t}={}
MATCH p = (x’)-[e’]->(y’)-[f’]->(z’)
.
Although those queries share only one (path) variable
p
, the equalities
x
{}={}
x’
,
e
{}={}
e’
, and so on, are implicitly enforced.
For any connected source query, a path can cover all variables and
transmit them to the target with a single path variable.

Restrictions on the number of shared variables

Our work shows that the amount of shared variables between the left- and right-hand sides of constraints plays an important role in analyzing and comparing the expressiveness of constraint formalisms. In addition to expressiveness, this also impacts the complexity of related tasks.

For instance, the validation problem consists in deciding whether a graph
G
satisfies a set of constraints
\Sigma
. In data complexity,
GFD
validation is
coNP
-complete
[
14
]
and
GGD
validation is
\mathbf{\Pi_{2}^{P}}
-complete
[
29
]
.
To our knowledge, no work parametrizes this complexity by the number of shared variables.
In fact, a careful analysis shows that, when
n
is fixed,
the naive validation algorithm for
n
GGD

[
29
]
is in
\mathbf{\Delta_{2}^{P}}
, a class believed to be strictly included in
\mathbf{\Pi_{2}^{P}}
.
The number of shared variables, which governs the polynomial degree in the aforementioned algorithm, may also be relevant for fine-grained complexity analysis, a topic actively studied for
CQ
evaluation
[
24
]
but unexplored for constraint validation.
