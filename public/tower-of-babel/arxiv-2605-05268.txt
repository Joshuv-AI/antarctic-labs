Quantum Proper Scoring Rules: Minimax Estimation and Resource-Theoretic Advantages Thanks: Email: mwalmasri2003@gmail.com
M.W. AlMasri

Abstract
We generalize proper scoring rules to the quantum domain, replacing probability distributions with density operators. We define Quantum Value Functionals via operator convex generators and establish a complete duality theory yielding proper quantum scoring rules. We derive minimax optimal bounds for quantum state tomography under McCarthy-type incentives, proving a Quantum Cramér-Rao-McCarthy Bound that explicitly links minimax risk to the curvature of the generating function and the Quantum Fisher Information. We quantify the economic value of quantum resources (coherence, entanglement, adaptivity) in forecasting tasks, establishing scaling separations between classical and quantum estimation strategies. Our results guide the design of quantum sensors, incentive-compatible quantum data markets, and robust quantum machine learning protocols.



Keywords:
Quantum Information, Proper Scoring Rules, Minimax Tomography, Quantum Fisher Information, Resource Theories, Coherence Economics.

Introduction

The theory of proper scoring rules, pioneered by McCarthy
[
1
]
and developed by Good
[
2
]
, Savage
[
3
]
, and Gneiting & Raftery
[
4
]
, provides a rigorous economic foundation for probabilistic forecasting. A scoring rule assigns a numerical reward
S(x,q)
to a forecaster who reports a probability distribution
q
when the outcome
x
is realized. The rule is
proper
if the expected score is maximized by reporting the true belief
p
, and
strictly proper
if this maximum is unique. McCarthy’s seminal insight established that proper scoring rules are in one-to-one correspondence with convex value functionals
\mathcal{V}(p)
via the subdifferential relation
S(\cdot,q)\in\partial\mathcal{V}(q)
, a duality that has found widespread application in statistics, machine learning, economics, and decision theory.

However, classical scoring rules rest on commutativity, measurement non-invasiveness, and a classical information structure—all of which fail in quantum mechanical settings. Non-commuting observables and density operators dictate measurement order; backaction collapses states to preclude repeated sampling; and forecasting involves incompatible measurements, quantum channels, or entangled systems with no classical analogue. These fundamental discrepancies motivate the development of
quantum proper scoring rules
: incentive mechanisms that elicit truthful reports of quantum states, channels, or measurement outcomes while respecting quantum constraints. Our approach draws on recent advances in quantum information theory
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, quantum estimation
[
9
,
8
]
, and quantum resource theories
[
10
]
.

In this paper, we establish a comprehensive framework for quantum proper scoring rules that
extends and unifies
several strands of prior work. While Frongillo
[
11
]
introduced quantum scoring rules via convex analysis and spectral scores, our contribution is threefold:
(1) We prove that operator convex generators yield scoring rules coinciding with Petz’s quantum
f
-divergences under explicit symmetry conditions (Theorem
2.2
), thereby connecting incentive design to the well-studied monotonicity properties of quantum divergences
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
;
(2) We derive a
curvature-dependent
Quantum Cramér-Rao-McCarthy Bound (Theorem
3.2
) that explicitly links minimax risk to the second derivative of the generating function
f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)
and the Quantum Fisher Information, refining the asymptotic bounds of Quadeer et al.
[
16
]
and Ferrie & Blume-Kohout
[
17
]
which treated only relative entropy or Hilbert-Schmidt risk;
(3) We establish
scaling separations
(Theorem
4.1
, Proposition
4.2
) that quantify the economic value of coherence and entanglement in forecasting tasks, providing an operational interpretation of resource theories
[
10
]
in decision-theoretic contexts.
Collectively, these results bridge McCarthy’s classical duality theory
[

[

Notation.

Throughout,
\mathcal{H}
denotes a
d
-dimensional complex Hilbert space,
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
the set of density operators on
\mathcal{H}
, and
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
the space of linear operators. We write
\operatorname{Tr}(\cdot)
for the trace,
\|\cdot\|
for the operator norm, and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
for the trace norm. The Hilbert-Schmidt inner product is
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\operatorname{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)

2
Quantum Proper Scoring Rules

Definition 2.1

(Quantum Value Functional)
.

A functional
\mathcal{V}_{Q}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{R}
is a Quantum Value Functional if it is convex, unitarily invariant, and lower-semicontinuous. By the spectral theorem,
\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)=\sum_{i}f(\lambda_{i})
for some convex function
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\{\lambda_{i}\}
are the eigenvalues of
\rho
.

Theorem 2.2

(Quantum Duality and Properness)
.

If
\mathcal{V}_{Q}
is closed, convex, and unitarily invariant, the quantum scoring rule
\mathbf{S}(\sigma)=\nabla\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\sigma)
satisfies:

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{S}(\rho))\geq\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{S}(\sigma)),\quad\forall\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}).

(1)

If
f
is operator convex, then
\mathbf{S}(\sigma)=f^{\prime}(\sigma)
(via functional calculus) and the quantum Bregman divergence
D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}
coincides with Petz’s quantum
f
-divergence under the symmetry condition that
\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}
commutes with
\sigma

[
12
]
:

D_{f}(\rho\|\sigma)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sigma^{1/2}f\!\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}\right)\sigma^{1/2}\right].

(2)

This connection imports the extensive literature on monotonicity and data processing for quantum
f
-divergences
[
12
,
14
,
15
]
into the scoring rule framework.

Proof.

We prove properness and the operator convex case separately.

Part 1: Properness via Convex Analysis.

Since
\mathcal{V}_{Q}
is convex on the convex set
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, by the definition of the subdifferential in the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product space, for any
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
:

\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)\geq\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\sigma)+\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\sigma),\rho-\sigma\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}},

(3)

where
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\operatorname{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)
. Since
\nabla\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\sigma)=\mathbf{S}(\sigma)
is Hermitian (as the gradient of a real-valued functional on Hermitian matrices), we have:

\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)\geq\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\sigma)+\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{S}(\sigma)(\rho-\sigma)).

(4)

Rearranging terms:

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{S}(\rho))-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{S}(\sigma))\geq\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)-\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\sigma)-\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{S}(\sigma)(\rho-\sigma))=D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}(\rho\|\sigma)\geq 0,

(5)

where the last equality is the definition of the quantum Bregman divergence, and non-negativity follows from convexity. This establishes properness.

Part 2: Explicit Gradient for Spectral Functionals.

For unitarily invariant
\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(f(\rho))
, we compute the Fréchet derivative using the Daleckii-Krein theorem
[
18
,
19
]
. Let
\sigma=U\Lambda U^{\dagger}
with
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
. For a perturbation
H
with
\operatorname{Tr}(H)=0
, the directional derivative is:

D\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\sigma)[H]=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(f(\sigma+\epsilon H))-\operatorname{Tr}(f(\sigma))}{\epsilon}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(U\left(f^{[1]}(\Lambda)\circ(U^{\dagger}HU)\right)U^{\dagger}\right),

(6)

where
f^{[1]}
is the first divided difference matrix:

f^{[1]}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{f(\lambda_{i})-f(\lambda_{j})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}&\text{if }\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j},\\
f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})&\text{if }\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}.\end{cases}

(7)

The gradient in the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product satisfies
\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\sigma),H\right\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=D\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\sigma)[H]
, so:

\mathbf{S}(\sigma)=\nabla\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\sigma)=U\left(f^{[1]}(\Lambda)\circ I\right)U^{\dagger}=U\operatorname{diag}(f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}))U^{\dagger}=f^{\prime}(\sigma),

(8)

where the last equality holds because the Hadamard product with the identity extracts the diagonal, and functional calculus gives
f^{\prime}(\sigma)=U\operatorname{diag}(f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}))U^{\dagger}
.

Part 3: Operator Convexity and Petz Divergence.

If
f
is operator convex, the operator Jensen inequality
[
12
]
implies:

f(\rho)\succeq f(\sigma)+f^{\prime}(\sigma)(\rho-\sigma)

(9)

in the Löwner order. Taking the trace with
\rho\succeq 0
preserves the inequality:

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho f(\rho))\geq\operatorname{Tr}(\rho f(\sigma))+\operatorname{Tr}(\rho f^{\prime}(\sigma)(\rho-\sigma)).

(10)

Rearranging:

\operatorname{Tr}(\rho f^{\prime}(\rho))-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho f^{\prime}(\sigma))\geq\operatorname{Tr}(f(\rho))-\operatorname{Tr}(f(\sigma))-\operatorname{Tr}(f^{\prime}(\sigma)(\rho-\sigma))=D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}(\rho\|\sigma).

(11)

The right-hand side is precisely Petz’s quantum
f
-divergence
[
12
]
:

D_{f}(\rho\|\sigma)=\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma^{1/2}f(\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2})\sigma^{1/2}),

(12)

which coincides with the Bregman divergence when
f
is operator convex, differentiable, and the symmetry condition that
\sigma^{-1/2}\rho\sigma^{-1/2}
commutes with
\sigma
holds
[
12
]
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.3

(Relation to prior characterizations)
.

The duality structure of Theorem
2.2
parallels the convex-analytic characterizations of Frongillo
[
11
]
, but our focus on operator convex generators yields two key distinctions: (i) the explicit identification of the gradient
\mathbf{S}(\sigma)=f^{\prime}(\sigma)
via functional calculus, which simplifies implementation in quantum algorithms; and (ii) the coincidence with Petz’s
f
-divergence under symmetry, which imports the extensive literature on monotonicity and data processing
[
12
,
14
,
15
]
into the scoring rule framework. In particular, recent work on optimized quantum
f
-divergences
[
14
,
15
]
suggests that scoring rules derived from certain generators may exhibit enhanced robustness under noisy quantum channels. This connection was not established in prior work on quantum information elicitation.

Minimax Optimal Quantum Estimation

Definition 3.1

(Quantum Minimax Risk)
.

For a scoring rule
\mathbf{S}
derived from
\mathcal{V}_{Q}
, the minimax risk over
n
copies of the state is:

R_{n}^{*}(\mathcal{V}_{Q})=\inf_{\hat{\rho}_{n}}\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})}\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\otimes n}}\left[\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{S}(\rho))-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{S}(\hat{\rho}_{n}))\right],

(13)

where the infimum is over all POVMs and estimators
\hat{\rho}_{n}
based on
n
i.i.d. copies.

Theorem 3.2

(Quantum Cramér-Rao-McCarthy Bound)
.

For twice differentiable
f
with
f^{\prime\prime}>0
:

R_{n}^{*}(\mathcal{V}_{Q})\geq\frac{1}{2n}\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})}\operatorname{Tr}\left(f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\cdot\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}(\rho)^{-1}\right)+o(n^{-1}),

(14)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}
is the Quantum Fisher Information matrix associated with the Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative. Equality holds asymptotically for the von Neumann entropy with the Maximum Likelihood Estimator when
\rho
is nearly pure or the parametrization is locally orthogonal with respect to the QFI metric.

Proof.

The proof combines Local Asymptotic Normality (LAN) for quantum states with second-order expansion of the quantum Bregman divergence.

Step 1: Local Parametrization and LAN.

Let
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a local parametrization of the state space near
\rho_{0}
, with
\rho_{\theta}
smooth in
\theta
. The Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative (SLD) operators
\{L_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
satisfy:

\partial_{i}\rho_{\theta}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\theta}L_{i}+L_{i}\rho_{\theta}),\quad\text{where }\partial_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}.

(15)

The Quantum Fisher Information (QFI) matrix has elements
\mathcal{I}_{ij}=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{\theta}L_{i}L_{j})
.

By the LAN theorem for quantum states, for large
n
, the statistical experiment of estimating
\theta
from
\rho_{\theta}^{\otimes n}
converges to a Gaussian shift model:

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}(\theta)^{-1}),

(16)

where
\hat{\theta}_{n}
is an efficient estimator (e.g., MLE).

Step 2: Second-Order Expansion of Risk.

The risk is the expected quantum Bregman divergence:

R_{n}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\theta}^{\otimes n}}\left[D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}(\rho_{\theta}\|\hat{\rho}_{n})\right].

(17)

Expanding
D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}
to second order around
\theta
:

D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}(\rho_{\theta}\|\hat{\rho}_{n})\approx\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)^{T}\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\theta)(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta),

(18)

where
\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{V}_{Q}
is the Hessian of
\mathcal{V}_{Q}
with respect to
\theta
.

For the spectral functional
\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(f(\rho))
, the second variation in the direction of a tangent vector
H
is given by the Daleckii-Krein formula
[
18
]
:

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)[H,H]=\sum_{i,j}f^{[2]}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})\,|\left\langle u_{i},Hu_{j}\right\rangle|^{2},

(19)

where
f^{[2]}
is the second divided difference and
\{|u_{i}\rangle\}
are the eigenvectors of
\rho
. When
\rho
is nearly pure or
f
is smooth with slowly varying derivatives, the off-diagonal terms are negligible, yielding the approximation
\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{V}_{Q}\approx f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)
in the eigenbasis of
\rho
with error
O(\|\rho-\rho_{0}\|)
for smooth
f

[
19
]
.

Step 3: Applying the Holevo Bound.

The covariance matrix
V
of any unbiased estimator satisfies the Holevo bound
[
9
]
:

V\geq\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}^{-1}.

(20)

Thus, the expected risk satisfies:

\displaystyle R_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)^{T}\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\hat{\theta}_{n}-\theta)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{V}_{Q}\cdot V\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\cdot\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}^{-1}\right).

(21)

Taking the supremum over
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
yields the minimax lower bound.

Step 4: Achievability for Von Neumann Entropy.

For
f(t)=t\log t
(von Neumann entropy),
f^{\prime\prime}(t)=1/t
, so
f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)=\rho^{-1}
on the support of
\rho
. For qubit states with full-rank
\rho
, the trace term
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}^{-1})
is bounded and scales as
O(d^{2})
for the full parameter space. The Maximum Likelihood Estimator achieves the bound asymptotically
[
6
]
when
\rho
is nearly pure or the parametrization is locally orthogonal with respect to the QFI metric.

The
o(n^{-1})
term accounts for higher-order corrections in the LAN approximation and boundary effects near pure states. This completes the proof.
∎

4
Quantum Advantage and Resource Tradeoffs

Theorem 4.1

(Quantum Advantage for Entropic Scoring)
.

For non-commuting output channels, the forecasting gap between classical (fixed-basis) and quantum (joint measurement) strategies scales as:

\Gamma_{n}=\Omega\left(\frac{d\log d}{n}\right)-O(n^{-3/2}),

(22)

where the implicit constant depends on
f
and the channel geometry. Quantum strategies reduce sample complexity by a factor of
O(d)
compared to classical strategies in the worst case of basis misalignment.

Proof.

We compare the minimax risks under classical and quantum measurement constraints.

Classical Risk Analysis.

A classical forecaster restricted to fixed-basis measurements (e.g., computational basis) effectively applies the dephasing channel
\Delta(\rho)=\sum_{k}|k\rangle\langle k|\rho|k\rangle\langle k|
before estimation. The classical Fisher information
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{CL}}
obtained from this measurement satisfies:

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{CL}}(\theta)\leq\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}(\theta)

(23)

with equality only if the measurement basis aligns with the eigenbasis of the SLD operators.

For a
d
-dimensional system, there exist directions in parameter space where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{CL}}
is suppressed by a factor of
d
relative to
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}
when the basis is maximally misaligned with the state’s eigenbasis
[
6
]
. Averaging over worst-case state orientations, the classical risk scales as:

R_{n}^{\text{classical}}\sim\frac{d}{n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{CL}}^{-1}\right).

(24)

Quantum Risk Analysis.

A quantum forecaster using joint entangled measurements across
n
copies can achieve the full QFI. By Theorem
3.2
:

R_{n}^{\text{quantum}}\sim\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}^{-1}\right).

(25)

Gap Calculation.

The forecasting gap is:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=R_{n}^{\text{classical}}-R_{n}^{\text{quantum}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\left(d\cdot\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{CL}}^{-1}-\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}^{-1}\right)\right)+O(n^{-3/2}).

(26)

For non-commuting observables in the worst-case misalignment scenario,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{CL}}\approx\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}
, so
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{CL}}^{-1}\approx d\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}^{-1}
. Thus:

\Gamma_{n}\geq\frac{d^{2}-1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(f^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}^{-1}\right)+O(n^{-3/2}).

(27)

The trace term scales as
\Omega(\log d)
for entropic scoring rules due to the entropy of the
d
-outcome distribution
[
6
]
. Thus the forecasting gap scales as:

\Gamma_{n}=\Omega\left(\frac{d\log d}{n}\right)-O(n^{-3/2}),

(28)

where the implicit constant depends on
f
and the channel geometry.

Sample Complexity Reduction.

To achieve a target risk
\epsilon
, the classical strategy requires
n_{\mathrm{CL}}=O(d\log d/\epsilon)
samples, while the quantum strategy requires
n_{Q}=O(\log d/\epsilon)
. Thus, quantum strategies reduce sample complexity by a factor of
O(d)
in the worst-case misalignment scenario.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.2

(Coherence-Risk Tradeoff)
.

Let
C(\rho)
denote the relative entropy of coherence. Then for the quantum relative entropy scoring rule (
f(t)=t\log t
):

R_{n}^{\text{classical}}\geq R_{n}^{\text{quantum}}+\frac{C(\rho)}{n}+O(n^{-3/2}),

(29)

provided that
\Delta(\rho)
is the information projection of
\rho
onto the diagonal submanifold with respect to the Bregman divergence
D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}
in the dually flat quantum information geometry induced by the relative entropy
[
20
]
. Thus, coherence directly and linearly reduces the required number of samples to achieve a target accuracy, up to
O(1/n)
corrections from the dephasing-induced QFI reduction.

Proof.

We use the data processing inequality for quantum Bregman divergences and the definition of coherence.

Step 1: Classical Forecaster as Dephasing.

A classical forecaster restricted to measurements in basis
\{|k\rangle\}
effectively applies the dephasing channel
\Delta(\rho)=\sum_{k}|k\rangle\langle k|\rho|k\rangle\langle k|
before estimation. The risk incurred is:

R_{n}^{\text{classical}}=\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}(\rho\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{CL}})\right],

(30)

where
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{CL}}
is estimated from dephased data.

Step 2: Pythagorean-like Relation.

For the quantum relative entropy (
f(t)=t\log t
), which induces a dually flat quantum information geometry
[
20
]
, the following Pythagorean relation holds when
\Delta(\rho)
is the information projection of
\rho
onto the diagonal submanifold:

D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}(\rho\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{CL}})\geq D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}(\rho\|\Delta(\rho))+D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}(\Delta(\rho)\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{CL}}).

(31)

The first term is exactly the relative entropy of coherence
[
22
]
:

C(\rho)=D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}(\rho\|\Delta(\rho))=S(\Delta(\rho))-S(\rho),

(32)

where
S
is the von Neumann entropy.

Remark 4.3

(Geometric conditions)
.

The Pythagorean relation in Step 2 holds for the quantum relative entropy due to the dually flat structure of the associated quantum information geometry
[
20
]
. For general operator convex
f
, additional conditions on the manifold structure may be required; see
[
21
]
for recent developments.

Step 3: Risk Decomposition.

Taking expectations:

\displaystyle R_{n}^{\text{classical}}

\displaystyle\geq C(\rho)+\mathbb{E}\left[D_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}}(\Delta(\rho)\|\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{CL}})\right]

\displaystyle=C(\rho)+R_{n}^{\text{quantum}}(\Delta(\rho)),

(33)

where
R_{n}^{\text{quantum}}(\Delta(\rho))
is the risk of estimating the dephased state
\Delta(\rho)
using quantum methods.

Step 4: Asymptotic Expansion.

By Theorem
3.2
, the risk for estimating
\Delta(\rho)
scales as:

R_{n}^{\text{quantum}}(\Delta(\rho))=\frac{1}{2n}\operatorname{Tr}\left(f^{\prime\prime}(\Delta(\rho))\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}(\Delta(\rho))^{-1}\right)+o(n^{-1}).

(34)

Since
\Delta(\rho)
is diagonal in the measurement basis, its QFI is generally smaller than that of
\rho
, but the leading term remains
O(1/n)
, up to
O(1/n)
corrections from the dephasing-induced QFI reduction. Thus:

R_{n}^{\text{classical}}\geq R_{n}^{\text{quantum}}+\frac{C(\rho)}{n}+O(n^{-3/2}),

(35)

where the
O(n^{-3/2})
term accounts for higher-order corrections in the LAN approximation and the difference between
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{SLD}}(\Delta(\rho))
.

This shows that coherence
C(\rho)
provides a direct linear reduction in the required sample size to achieve a target accuracy, completing the proof.
∎

Example 4.5

(Qubit Forecasting: Coherence as a Forecasting Resource)
.

Consider the pure qubit state
\rho=|+\rangle\langle+|
where
|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
. In the computational basis, this state has density matrix

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}+\sigma_{x}),

(36)

corresponding to the Bloch vector
\mathbf{r}=(1,0,0)
. We analyze forecasting performance under two measurement strategies.

Classical Forecaster (
Z
-basis restriction).

A classical forecaster restricted to measurements in the computational basis
\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}
effectively observes the dephased state

\Delta_{Z}(\rho)=\sum_{k=0}^{1}|k\rangle\langle k|\rho|k\rangle\langle k|=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\mathbb{I}}{2}.

(37)

The outcome distribution is uniform:
p(0)=p(1)=1/2
, independent of the true
x
-component
r_{x}=1
. Consequently, any estimator
\hat{r}_{x}
based solely on
Z
-basis data satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\hat{r}_{x}]=0
, incurring a squared bias of

\text{Bias}^{2}=(\mathbb{E}[\hat{r}_{x}]-r_{x})^{2}=(0-1)^{2}=1.

(38)

The classical Fisher information for estimating a parameter
\theta
that rotates
\rho
about the
y
-axis (i.e.,
\rho_{\theta}=e^{-i\theta\sigma_{y}/2}\rho e^{i\theta\sigma_{y}/2}
) is

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{CL}}(\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{1}\frac{1}{p(k|\theta)}\left(\frac{\partial p(k|\theta)}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2}=0,

(39)

since
