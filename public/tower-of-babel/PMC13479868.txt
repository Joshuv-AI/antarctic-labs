The pyruvate transporter hermes regulates autophagy and health by modulating ROS production
Authors: Panagiotis D Velentzas, Fei Chai, Eric H Baehrecke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYAutophagy is a catabolic process that degrades cytoplasmic materials and is controlled by nutrient availability and signaling. The plasma membrane-associated pyruvate-solute carrier hermes (hrm) is required for regulation of the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling and the activation of autophagy during development. Here, we screen for pyruvate-influencing genes that suppress the hrm mutant phenotype. We show that the inhibitory effect of hrm loss on autophagy depends on pyruvate transport into mitochondria and the Krebs cycle. Loss of hrm results in an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS), and attenuation of the increase in ROS is sufficient to suppress the effects of hrm loss on autophagy and mTOR signaling. Importantly, we show that in adult animals, loss of hrm results in decreased lifespan, with defects in autophagy in intestine tissues. These results link a plasma membrane pyruvate carrier to mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism, ROS, autophagy, and organismal health.

INTRODUCTION

Solute carriers (SLCs) represent the second largest family of membrane proteins, with over 450 members.1 They are localized to the plasma membrane and organelle membranes, transport many types of metabolites and ions, and play an important role in maintaining cellular homeostasis.2 SLC proteins have been shown to play an important role in many processes like nutrient sensing3 and drug transport4 and have also been implicated in many human diseases, such as diabetes,5 Niemann-Pick disease,6 and nephropathic conditions.7

Macroautophagy, hereafter referred to as autophagy, is a mechanism used by cells to deliver cytoplasmic materials and organelles to the lysosome for degradation,8 and it has been associated with many human diseases.9 Autophagy is an important catabolic process that is regulated by the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)10,11 and can contribute to both cell survival and cell death.12,13 Several model organisms, from the single-cell yeast to mice, have been used to study the different roles of autophagy during development.14 Autophagy-associated cell death has been extensively studied in Drosophila salivary glands, which undergo a developmentally programmed clearance.15–17

The pyruvate transporter hermes (hrm) is essential for autophagy during the developmental clearance of Drosophila larval salivary glands.17 Pyruvate is a crucial metabolite for the homeostasis of cells and is mainly produced by glycolysis and enters mitochondria to fuel the Krebs cycle and the oxidative phosphorylation to produce ATP.18 Alternatively, pyruvate can be transported across the plasma membrane by monocarboxylate transporters.19 Interestingly, hrm is a plasma membrane-associated pyruvate transporter, and despite the presence of pyruvate in all cell culture media, little is known about the relationship between plasma membrane and mitochondrial pyruvate transport to maintain cell health. Importantly, pyruvate and its related metabolism have been implicated in multiple disorders,20–22 but its role in the regulation of autophagy is not clear.

Here, we demonstrate that the pyruvate transporter hrm controls the clearance of Drosophila salivary glands by regulating mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism. Loss of hrm results in an increase in flux of pyruvate into mitochondria and the Krebs cycle. This increase elevates the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which results in the activation of mTORC1 signaling and the inhibition of autophagy. Loss of hrm causes a reduction in autophagy in adult intestines, and this has a negative effect on the lifespan of animals. These results establish a connection between a plasma membrane pyruvate transporter, mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism, ROS production, and autophagy to maintain organismal health.

RESULTS

Loss of hrm inhibits salivary gland clearance by enhancing mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism

The larval salivary glands of Drosophila undergo programmed cell clearance that requires autophagy and caspases during development.15,23 We previously identified hrm, a plasma membrane pyruvate transporter, that is required for autophagy during the Drosophila larval salivary gland clearance.17 To better understand how disruption of pyruvate transport can affect autophagy in salivary glands, we altered the functions of genes that are involved in pyruvate metabolism, together with downregulation of hrm in a salivary gland-specific manner. We fixed animals 24 h after puparium formation (h APF) (8 h after this tissue is completely cleared in wild-type animals), embedded them in paraffin, sectioned, and stained them for analyses of the persistence of salivary gland material.

Pyruvate is the final product of glycolysis, and its main function is to fuel Krebs cycle through its import into mitochondria.24,25 To promote the flux of pyruvate in mitochondria, we ectopically expressed the mitochondrial pyruvate carriers (mpc1 and mpc2), which are required for pyruvate import to the mitochondria,26 in salivary glands of animals that also had hrm downregulation by expression of RNAi (hrmIR). These animals possessed a salivary gland clearance defect, similar to animals with only hrm downregulation (Figures S1A and S1B). Pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (Pdk) phosphorylates the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex to inhibit the entry of pyruvate to the Krebs cycle.27 Similarly, lactate dehydrogenase (Ldh) converts pyruvate to lactate.28 Animals with salivary gland downregulation of hrm and downregulation of either pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (Pdk) or lactate dehydrogenase (Ldh) had persistent salivary gland material that was similar to animals with only hrm knockdown in salivary glands (Figures S1C–S1F). Collectively, these data indicate that enhancing mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism did not modify the salivary gland clearance phenotype caused by hrm knockdown.

We next sought to reduce the flux of pyruvate into mitochondria when hrm function is reduced in salivary glands. Therefore, we simultaneously knocked down hrm and mpc1 in a salivary gland-specific manner. Interestingly, significantly fewer of these animals had a salivary gland clearance defect (35%) compared to animals with hrm knockdown alone in the salivary glands (75%) (Figures 1A and 1B). Similarly, salivary gland-specific knockdown of hrm, together with downregulation of either pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) E1 alpha subunit 1 (Pdha1) (Figures 1C and 1D) or pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 beta subunit (Pdhb) (Figures 1E and 1F) or overexpression of Ldh (Figures 1G and 1H), significantly reduced the percentage of animals with a salivary gland clearance defect compared to that of animals with hrm downregulation alone.

To better understand how the loss of hrm affects intracellular pyruvate levels, we used the fluorescent pyruvate reporter PyronicSF.29 Salivary gland-specific knockdown of hrm resulted in a decrease in cytoplasmic pyruvate levels (Figures 2A and 2B) and an increase in the mitochondrial pyruvate levels (Figures 2C and 2D). Furthermore, salivary glands from hrm mutant animals 13.5 h APF had reduced levels of phosphorylated Pdha1 compared to glands from control animals (Figures 2E and 2F). Interestingly, salivary gland-specific knockdown of Pdk resulted in the inhibition of autophagy at 13.5 h APF (Figures S1G and S1H). These data indicate that the inhibition of salivary gland clearance by loss of hrm depends on mitochondrial pyruvate import and oxidative pyruvate metabolism that involves PDH complex-dependent entry into the Krebs cycle.

hrm regulates autophagy and mTORC1 signaling by controlling mitochondria ROS production

To better understand the role of hrm in the regulation of autophagy during the developmentally programmed clearance of salivary glands, we performed metabolomics analysis. We isolated metabolites from salivary glands of control animals and animals with salivary gland-specific hrm knockdown 13.5 h APF, performed liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and detected 107 metabolites (Table S1). We analyzed differences in the levels of these metabolites and found that the loss of hrm has a significant effect on the glutathione (GSH) metabolism pathway, with the levels of GSH being reduced by 4.5-fold in salivary glands with hrm downregulation compared to control, while the level of the oxidized form of glutathione (GSSG) was reduced by 12-fold, and the level of glutamate, which is required for the de novo synthesis of GSH, was reduced by 2.5-fold (Figures 2G and S2).

GSH plays a crucial role in antioxidant defense against mitochondria-induced ROS, and its decrease can lead to increased ROS levels.30 The reduction in GSH in salivary glands after hrm knockdown, together with the influence of hrm on mitochondrial pyruvate levels, prompted us to test whether loss of hrm influences ROS levels. We utilized an established GFP-based reporter, named ro-GFP2-Orp1,31 to detect changes in the levels of intracellular ROS. Interestingly, salivary glands from animals with hrm downregulation had significantly higher levels of ROS than salivary glands from control animals at 13.5 h APF (Figures 2H and 2I). Furthermore, downregulation of either mpc1 or Pdhb in salivary glands with hrm knockdown was able to reduce the ROS levels compared to glands with hrm knockdown alone 13.5 h APF (Figures 2J and 2K). These data indicate that the loss of hrm in salivary glands has a negative effect on the antioxidant capacity of cells and that reducing the function of genes that control the flux of pyruvate into mitochondria and the Krebs cycle can alleviate oxidative stress.

We next tested whether the increase in ROS levels in salivary glands that lack hrm is what inhibits the salivary gland clearance and the attenuation of autophagy, or if this elevated ROS level is a secondary effect of the disruption of pyruvate transport. Catalase (Cat) is an enzyme that reduces free radicals by breaking down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen.32 Interestingly, salivary glands from animals with hrm downregulation and Cat overexpression had significantly lower levels of ROS than salivary glands from animals with hrm downregulation alone at 13.5 h APF (Figures 3A and 3B). Furthermore, no animals with salivary gland downregulation of hrm and overexpression of Cat had a salivary gland clearance defect compared to 93% of the animals with hrm downregulation alone at 24 h APF (Figures 3C and 3D). Interestingly, significantly more animals with salivary gland-specific knockdown of Cat had a gland clearance defect compared with control animals, at 24 h APF (Figures S3A and S3B). These data indicate that the increase in ROS in salivary glands with hrm downregulation is required for the clearance defect.

We have shown that animals lacking hrm have a defect in autophagy and increased mTORC1 signaling in salivary glands at 13.5 h APF.17 Consistent with our previous data, salivary glands from animals with hrm downregulation are devoid of autophagosomes, as is evident by the lack of the autophagosome marker mCherry-Atg8a at 13.5 h APF (Figures 3E and 3F). Moreover, salivary glands from animals with downregulation of hrm and overexpression of Cat had normal Atg8a puncta formation at 13.5 h APF (Figures 3E and 3F). In addition, salivary glands from animals with downregulation of hrm and overexpression of Cat had significantly lower levels of p-S6K, an mTORC1 target, than the glands from animals with only hrm downregulation and similar levels of p-S6K to the salivary glands from control animals at 13.5 h APF (Figures 3G and 3H).

Reverse electron transfer (RET) at mitochondrial complex I induces ROS production and perturbs autophagy in aging flies.33 To test whether ROS accumulation in hrm-deficient salivary glands is because of mitochondrial RET, we downregulated the complex I subunit ND-30/NDUFS3 in the salivary glands of animals with hrm knockdown. Interestingly, only 15% of animals with salivary gland downregulation of both hrm and ND-30 had a salivary gland clearance defect, relative to 95% of the animals with hrm downregulation alone at 24 h APF (Figures 3I and 3J). Furthermore, salivary glands from animals with downregulation of both hrm and ND-30 had increased Atg8a puncta formation and reduced levels of p-S6K at 13.5 h APF relative to salivary glands with hrm downregulation alone (Figure 3K–3N). Interestingly, downregulation of the mitochondrial complex I subunit ND-51/NDUFV1 that initiates forward electron transfer in mitochondria resulted in an enhancement of the salivary gland clearance defect in animals with hrm downregulation, as evident by the presence of intact salivary glands at 24 h APF (Figures S3C and S3D). These data indicate that the increase in ROS levels, potentially through RET, in salivary glands with reduced hrm function is responsible for the increase in mTORC1 signaling and the defect in autophagy in salivary glands.

Loss of hrm results in reduced lifespan and autophagy inhibition in adult animal tissues

Previously, we generated Drosophila with deletion of hrm and reported that these animals were viable, with no obvious morphological phenotypic defects.17 Therefore, we tested if the loss of hrm results in any longevity defects in adult animals. Interestingly, adult animals that lacked hrm had significantly decreased lifespan relative to control animals (Figure 4A). These data indicate that hrm has a role in overall health of animals.

We next tested if hrm is expressed in tissues that have been implicated in lifespan. We used a hrm-GAL4 line (CRIMIC)34 in combination with UAS-NLS-mCherry to investigate hrm expression in different tissues. We detected mCherry expression in the brain of adult animals, and the signal co-localized with both the neuronal marker elav (Figure S4A) and the glial marker repo (Figure S4B). Downregulation of hrm in either neurons or glial cells using tissue-specific GAL4 drivers (elav-GAL4 for neurons and repo-GAL4 for glia) did not negatively affect lifespan (Figures S4C and S4D).

We then examined if hrm is expressed in the adult intestine and detected nuclear mCherry signal in both the enterocytes and the intestinal stem cells (ISCs) of this tissue (Figure 4B). Intestines from hrm mutant animals had fewer ISCs (esg-GFP-positive cells) per gut area than the intestines from control animals (Figures 4C and 4D). Furthermore, hrm mutant guts had an increased number of autophagy adaptor Ref(2)p protein puncta compared with control intestines (Figures 4E and 4F). Next, we tested if the downregulation of hrm in different cell types in the adult intestine influences animals’ lifespan. Interestingly, downregulation of hrm in the enterocytes of the gut, using NP1-GAL4, resulted in a decrease in lifespan relative to control animals (Figure 4G). Similarly, downregulation of hrm in the ISCs, using esg-GAL4, led to a modest but statistically significant reduction in the lifespan of the animals compared with control animals (Figure 4H). Importantly, downregulation of hrm specifically in either the enterocytes or the ISCs resulted in an increase in the number of puncta of the autophagy adaptor Ref(2)p in the gut tissue compared with control guts (Figures 4I–4L). It should be noted that the increase in Ref(2)p in the gut tissue of animals with hrm downregulation in the ISCs was modest and localized mostly in the enterocytes.

We found that the loss of hrm inhibits autophagy in the salivary glands and that the overexpression of Cat can reverse that inhibition. Therefore, we next tested whether the expression of Cat in the different cell types of the adult intestine can also rescue lifespan and the autophagy defect. Interestingly, misexpression of Cat and downregulation of hrm in the enterocytes resulted in both a rescue of the lifespan phenotype and a statistically significant decrease in Ref(2)p puncta in the gut compared with hrm downregulation alone in the enterocytes (Figures 4G, 4I, and 4J). Similarly, downregulation of hrm together with misexpression of Cat in the ISCs resulted in a rescue of the lifespan phenotype and a decrease in Ref(2)p puncta in the adult gut, albeit not statistically significant, compared with the number of Ref(2)p puncta in this tissue with only hrm downregulation in the ISCs (Figures 4H, 4K, and 4L). These data indicate that hrm is required for the homeostasis of the adult intestine and autophagy.

DISCUSSION

Regulation and alteration of metabolism have been implicated in many disease-associated processes such as aging,35 cell death,36 and immunity.37. Autophagy is one of the important catabolic processes that contributes to metabolism,38,39 but the relationships between autophagy, metabolism, cell function, and animal health in physiological settings remain incompletely understood. Although it is widely known that extracellular nutrients influence metabolism,2 little is known about how plasma membrane-localized metabolite solute carriers influence mitochondria and autophagy in the context of animal physiology and health. Here, we report that the Drosophila plasma membrane pyruvate transporter hrm regulates developmentally programmed autophagy by altering mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism and ROS production. Importantly, hrm plays a role in the lifespan of adult animals by regulating autophagy in the intestine.

The role of pyruvate transport in the regulation of autophagy is not well understood. The mammalian lactate transporters MCT1 and MCT4 have been shown to either induce or inhibit autophagy in cell lines under different conditions,40–43 but the role of the pyruvate plasma membrane transporters in autophagy regulation has not been extensively studied. Here, we show that the Drosophila plasma membrane pyruvate transporter hrm promotes autophagy-dependent cell clearance by regulating mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism. Our data are consistent with a model in which the loss of hrm alters mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism, leading to increased ROS and inhibition of autophagy. Recent studies in mammalian systems have also implicated pyruvate import into mitochondria with autophagy regulation and Parkinson disease,44,45 thus highlighting the importance of understanding the role of pyruvate transport in cellular homeostasis.

Studies in mammalian cell culture systems have linked increased ROS production to autophagy activation.46–48 Interestingly, a recent study in Drosophila showed that the overexpression of Cat promotes autophagy by switching the cell to an oxidizing redox state and regulating the autophagy protein Atg4 despite reducing overall ROS production.49 By contrast, our studies of Drosophila salivary gland development revealed that the loss of hrm increases ROS and negatively impacts autophagy, while reducing the total ROS by expressing Cat reversed this effect. Interestingly, our metabolomics data suggest that the loss of hrm alters the cellular redox balance despite increased global ROS (Table S1). This apparent paradox may reflect complex compartmentalized redox dynamics.

Mutations of isocitrate dehydrogenase 3b, that encodes a mitochondrial enzyme of the Krebs cycle, in Drosophila also inhibit autophagy in the salivary glands at pupariation.50 Conversely, mutations of the Drosophila malate dehydrogenase 2, that encodes another mitochondrial enzyme of the Krebs cycle, inhibits caspase-induced cell death but not autophagy in the salivary glands during development.51 These findings together with those of the present study highlight the complexity of mitochondrial function in the regulation of this process.

Activation of mTORC1 complex is known to negatively regulate autophagy.10,11 Interestingly, increased ROS levels have been shown to induce the activation of mTORC1 and increase p70-S6K phosphorylation in cultured cells under certain conditions.52–54 Consistent with these observations, our data show that the loss of hrm in salivary glands results in increased ROS production and activation of mTORC1 signaling, accompanied by reduced autophagy.

Mitochondrial pyruvate has been shown to play a critical role in the control of ISCs. Blocking pyruvate’s entry into the mitochondria of Drosophila ISCs by knocking down mpc1 resulted in over-proliferation, while the overexpression of mpc1 and mpc2 resulted in fewer stem cells.26 In the same study, suppression of Ldh, which is known to increase pyruvate levels, in the ISCs55 also led to fewer stem cells. Our data are consistent with a model in which the loss of hrm perturbs pyruvate metabolism and redox homeostasis in the adult intestine, thereby impairing autophagy and reducing organismal health. Interestingly, autophagy inhibition has also been linked to ISC proliferation inhibition.56

The human homolog of hrm, SLC16A11/MCT11, has been implicated in type 2 diabetes5 and recently shown to control the effector function of CD8+ T cells.57 Here, we show that hrm regulates mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism to alter the oxidative state of cells and control autophagy and mTORC1 signaling. Therefore, our observations may help inform future studies on MCT11 function in human physiology and disease.

Limitations of the study

Further investigations are needed to understand how hrm and alterations in pyruvate influence the regulation of autophagy in other cell contexts. Even though we show that the loss of hrm results in an increase in mitochondrial pyruvate levels and related metabolism, we do not fully understand how a plasma membrane pyruvate transporter facilitates this change. Importantly, the use of rigorous genetics and cellular markers is an advantage of this study of Drosophila development, but this in vivo model limits the analysis of metabolite flux using biochemical approaches. It is important for future studies to resolve the relationships between hrm, pyruvate, autophagy, and metabolism in humans because of their potential roles in type 2 diabetes and other diseases.

METHOD DETAILS

Histology

The histology process has been described in detail.59 Slides with stained sections were imaged on a Zeiss AxioImager Z1 microscope with an EC Plan NEOFLUAR 5×/0.16 objective lens and a Zeiss HRc camera. Zeiss AxioVision was used for image acquisition.

Protein extracts and western blotting

For salivary glands experiments, glands were dissected from animals at 13.5 h after puparium formation, homogenized in SDS Lysis Buffer (1% SDS, 10mM Tris, pH 8.0 and 1mM EDTA pH8.0) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Scientific, #78445) and centrifuged at 16000 g for 10min. Supernatants were mixed with 4× Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610747) and incubated for 5 min at 100°C. Proteins were separated on 4%–20% Tris-Glycine protein gels (Thermo Scientific, #XP04202BOX) and transferred to 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad, #1620112) according to standard procedures. We used rabbit anti-phospho-S6K (1:500, Cell Signaling Technologies, #9209) and mouse anti-actin (1:1000, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, #JLA20) primary antibodies.

Immunolabeling

Intestine tissue from 20-days-old female adult animals or salivary glands from 13.5 h APF pupae were dissected in PBS and fixed in 4% formaldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 1 h. They were permeabilized in PBS with 0.1% Triton X-(PBS-T) for 30min at room temperature and blocked in PBS-T with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Tissues were incubated with a primary antibody (Ref(2)p, 1:2000, Abcam, #ab178440 or p-PDHA, 1:200, Abcam, #ab92696) overnight at 4°C. Next, the tissues were washed in PBS-T four times for 15min each at room temperature, incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature, and washed for 1 h in PBS-T four times for 15min each at room temperature. Tissues were mounted in Vectashield (Vector Laboratories, # H-1900). For reporters with fluorescent proteins, tissues were dissected in PBS, fixed in 4% formaldehyde in PBS for 1 h and mounted in Vectashield (Vector Laboratories). Imaging was performed on either a Zeiss LSM700, a Zeiss LSM900 Airyscan 2 or a Nikon A1R HD25 confocal microscope using either Zeiss Zen or Nikon NIS-Elements acquisition software. For puncta quantification the analyze particles function of Fiji60 was used.

Metabolite extraction for metabolomics

Twenty salivary glands were dissected at 13.5 h APF in cold PBS for each sample and placed in a tube on ice. The cold PBS was removed from the tube and 100μL of −20°C cold methanol with 1μM standards (Cambridge Isotope Labs, #MSK-A2–1.2) was added and tissue was homogenized with a pestle. 10μL was kept for the measurement of protein concentration using a BCA kit (Thermo Scientific, #23227). Next, 45μL of LC/MS grade water and 66.6μL of chloroform were added to the tube, mixed and centrifuged at 16000 g for 10min at 4°C. The two layers were carefully collected, dried in a vacuum concentrator and shipped on dry ice for analysis by the Metabolite Profiling Core Facility at the Whitehead Institute (https://wi.mit.edu/core-facilities/metabolomics). The data were analyzed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca).61 For statistical significance the Statistical Analysis (one factor) module was used, and a volcano plot was generated with a raw p-value threshold of 0.05 and a fold change threshold of 1.5. The Pathway Analysis module was used for pathway enrichment using the Drosophila melanogaster KEGG pathway library, the Global test enrichment method and Topology measure Relative-betweenness Centrality.

Lifespan assay

Newly eclosed adult animals (15 males and 15 females) were transferred to a vial with standard fly food. Flies were transferred to new vials containing fresh food three times a week and checked daily for deceased flies. The deceased flies on each day were plotted on a graph using GraphPad Prism, and statistical significance was assessed using the log rank (Mantel–Cox) test.

Pyruvate sensor imaging

For ex vivo pyruvate measurements, the fluorescent reporters, PyronicSF and mito-PyronicSF, were used.29 Salivary glands were dissected from 13.5 h APF animals in Schneider’s media (Thermo Scientific, #21720024). The salivary glands were mounted on a glass bottom 35mm Petri dish and imaged with a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope equipped with an LD C-Apochromat 40×/1.1 water immersion objective lens and Zeiss Zen acquisition software. The intensity was measured using Fiji.60 The data from different genotypes were analyzed for statistical significance with a two-tailed unpaired t test.

ROS sensor imaging

For ex vivo ROS measurements, a ratio-metric fluorescent reporter, tub-cyto-roGFP2-Orp1, was used.31 Salivary glands were dissected from 13.5 h APF animals in Schneider’s media (Thermo Scientific, #21720024). The salivary glands were mounted on a glass bottom 35mm Petri dish and imaged with a Zeiss LSM 700 confocal microscope equipped with an LD C-Apochromat 40×/1.1 water immersion objective lens and Zeiss Zen acquisition software. The reporter was excited sequentially with a 405 nm and a 488 nm laser (line by line) and detected at 505–600 nm. Images were opened with Fiji60 and a 32-bit (float) ratio image was created by dividing the 405 nm image by the 488 nm image pixel by pixel with the Image Calculator process. The intensity was measured and the data from different genotypes were analyzed for statistical significance with a two-tailed unpaired t test or a One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSES

For histology analyses, n denotes the number of animals studied and the p value was calculated using a chi-square test. For immunolabeling and fluorescent reporter experiments, n denotes number of animals, and the p value was calculated using a two-tailed unpaired t test or a One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. For western blot and metabolomics experiments n denotes the number of repeats and statistical significance was calculated with a two-tailed unpaired t test or a One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. For lifespan experiments n denotes the number of animals and statistical significance was assessed using the log rank (Mantel–Cox) test.
