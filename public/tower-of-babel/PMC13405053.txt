Using geographic information systems to identify Stop the Bleed training locations
Authors: Anna E Garcia Whitlock, Daniela Schmulevich, Elinore J Kaufman, Justin S Hatchimonji, Karina M Hiroshige, Mark J Seamon, Daniel N Holena, John J Gallagher, Patrick M Reilly, Jeremy W Cannon, Shariq S Raza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe Stop the Bleed (STB) Campaign seeks to minimize preventable deaths from hemorrhage by educating community members on how to respond to life-threatening bleeding. A persistent challenge is connecting the training to individuals most at risk of witnessing a bleeding event. We hypothesize that geographic information system (GIS) software can identify STB training locations in at-risk communities and that trained individuals will be willing to respond to a bleeding event.MethodsPublicly available shooting locations in a large urban city with a high violent crime rate were assessed for spatial clustering using ArcGIS to map “hot spots.” Community training locations were colocalized on this map to identify sites in areas more likely to experience a violent event. Of eight high-risk sites identified, two were receptive to hosting a STB course, and one was chosen. Skills, knowledge, and attitudes were assessed precourse and postcourse. Knowledge, attitudes, and real-world tourniquet use were also evaluated at 1-month and 12-months.Results15 community members were trained at one community center within an area with a high violent crime rate. As expected, skills improved significantly from precourse to postcourse as did knowledge. Knowledge remained improved at 1-month and 12-months compared with pre-course. Attitudes toward responding to life-threatening bleeding were more positive after the training and remained more positive at follow-up. None of the participants applied a tourniquet within 12-months of the STB course.ConclusionsGIS software can identify STB training sites within high-risk communities with a receptive audience as evidenced by durable knowledge retention and favorable attitudes toward controlling hemorrhage. Future efforts should seek to identify barriers to community training site participation in STB and to further leverage GIS software to identify and train individuals most likely to be present at a life-threatening bleeding event.Level of evidencePrognostic and Epidemiological, Level III.

Background

Trauma is the leading cause of death and disability worldwide, and a large proportion of those deaths are due to uncontrolled hemorrhage–and potentially preventable.1 2 This alongside the increased incidence of gun violence and mass casualty events in the USA inspired clinicians and policymakers alike to develop a reproducible, scalable means of teaching laypersons how they can help in the event of a bleeding emergency.3 The Stop the Bleed (STB) Campaign is a public health initiative aimed at increasing the number of civilians able to act as first-responders in the event of life-threatening bleeding.4 The initiative is hallmarked by the STB Course, a didactic and hands-on curriculum that teaches participants how to recognize and respond to bleeding events including how to effectively hold pressure, pack a bleeding wound, and place a tourniquet.5 As of 2025, nearly 5 million people globally have been trained in the STB Program with documented improvements in the knowledge, skill, and willingness required to respond to a bleeding emergency.6–9

Despite its successes, the STB program continues to struggle with finding ways to effectively disseminate knowledge to the groups that need it most.10 For example, many trainees are those affiliated with the healthcare fields, not true laypersons. Further, training sites are often at hospitals or other healthcare-associated sites which are not necessarily in the neighborhoods or communities that experience a disproportionate amount of the violence.11 12 Surveys of trauma patients support the idea that individuals in high-risk neighborhoods would be willing to act as first responders, validating the need to concentrate training in these communities.13 Conducting trainings at high schools has been proposed as a solution, but improved strategies are needed to better connect receptive audiences within high-risk communities with this life-saving training.14

A potential tool in optimizing the delivery of STB programming is geospatial analysis or the use of geographic information systems (GIS) software to identify areas in highest need of intervention.12 Geospatial mapping in public health is not new—famous early examples include mapping the employment locations of Mary Mallon to identify the origins of the typhoid epidemic and using death reports to map the source of the London Cholera Outbreak to the Broad Street Pump.15 16 What is new, however, is the advancement of the field through technology that can more efficiently identify clustering of events frequently known as “hot spots”.17 18 A recent single institution retrospective study from Draper et al used this approach to compare the locations of their STB training sites relative to over 1,000 origin locations for patients with penetrating trauma treated at their center.12 This work found that of the 45 STB locations in their catchment area, only seven were in close proximity to areas with high frequency of violent events—suggesting clustering their training efforts to these locations might be the most efficient means of connecting STB training with the people who need it most.

Given the potential benefits of geospatial targeting, we hypothesized that GIS software could be used to prospectively align STB trainings in areas with highest shooting incidence with the hopes of targeting the training to communities at highest risk of experiencing a bleeding event. We posited that individuals trained in these areas would maintain the knowledge and skills acquired during the training at 1 year follow-up and would be more likely to have used the training as a first responder in a bleeding event.

Methods

IRB approval and consent process

The project was conducted according to the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies guidelines (Checklist available as online supplemental digital content). A participation incentive of US$25 reloadable gift card at initial survey and US$50 at the time of the follow-up survey was provided. A verbal script was used to obtain consent to survey participants’ knowledge of tourniquet usage. This script included a description of what the survey would measure, timing of survey including day of training, one month later, and one year later. Of note, this script reassured the individual that participation in the study was not required to take the course and that they are welcome to excuse themselves at any point.

We also reassured participants regarding the data privacy plan of this project, namely that no individually identifiable data will be collected other than contact information regarding scheduling the follow-up survey and gift card distribution. Participants were identified with a code after the initial survey that allowed us to track surveys longitudinally. This information was secured and then destroyed on completion of the study.

Identifying hot spots for penetrating injury

Locations of shootings in a large, urban city with a high violent crime rate, were mapped for spatial clustering using GIS software. This technology allows users to map and analyze geographic data into practically useful data points, often described as clustering or “hot spots.” In this study, street address data for gunshot incidents from 2017 to 2018 was obtained from our city’s open data repository of city shooting events maintained by a local geospatial software firm and civic technology volunteers (Open Data Philly Shooter Database).19 This data was submitted to the Hot Spot Analysis tool from ArcGIS Pro to calculate the Getis-Ord statistic or GI* (ESRI, Redlands, California, USA).20 This resultant z-score and p value speak to an area having high or low spatial clustering, thus enabling identification of an area with high crime, or in this case, gunshot incidence activity. The larger, more positive the z-score, the more intense the clustering of hot spots in that area. We used this value to define hot spots for penetrating injury.

Identifying a training site within hot spots

Our team collected a comprehensive list of potential training centers in the city, hypothesizing that police stations, firehouses, community centers, recreation centers, churches, and Young Men's Christian Associations (YMCAs) might be open to hosting a STB training. These candidates were mapped by publicly available street address and this map was overlaid with the map of hot spots in the same communities to identify potential training sites in proximity to the previously identified hot spots.

Once potential training site locations were identified, study team members attempted to contact each location via email and/or telephone call to gauge interest in hosting a STB training at no cost to them. Benefits of participation were detailed for the prospective sites including: (1) Each trained participant would receive a free bleeding control kit; (2) Participants who were healthcare providers would become certified instructors and given teaching supplies to train others in the community; (3) The community center would receive a bleeding control station, which includes eight individual hemorrhage control kits, a canvas storage bag, a wall-mounted polycarbonate cabinet for intended placement in a prominent public location, and an instructional poster to be displayed near the cabinet. Our goal was to identify one to two centers that would be most receptive to hosting a training including a receptive audience based on their community leader’s assessment of training interest among its members.

Course details and data collection

The STB Course was conducted in accordance with guidelines set forth by the American College of Surgeons including using approved curriculum and course materials. Certified instructors executing the training included attending surgeons, surgery residents, and nurses from our institution’s outreach team. At the conclusion of the training all participants were provided with a bleeding control kit, and the site was provided with a bleeding control station as described.

Data collection tools for assessing (1) hands-on skills, (2) knowledge, and (3) comfort level with the use of tourniquets were developed by experts in civilian trauma (JSH and SSR) with formal training in STB and incorporated into the training day. Participants were identified in collaboration with the community partner. Skills were assessed before and after the course, and the knowledge and attitude survey was administered pretest and post-test as well as 1-month and then 1-year later. For 1-month and 12-month follow-up, participants were contacted by a member of the study team at each respective time point and were asked to again complete follow-up surveys that assessed their knowledge and attitudes. Of note, these later time points included two questions inquiring about real-world experiences encountering bleeding victims and tourniquet use since the last time point surveyed in the study. Participants were given a reloadable gift card with US$25 for participating in the initial in-person assessment, and an additional US$25 for each assessment at the 1-month and 12-month time points.

Assessment details

The hands-on precourse and postcourse skills assessment tool was used by study faculty at the in-person training to score eight tasks. Each task was assigned a score of zero if improperly executed and a score of one if properly executed, with a possible total skills score ranging from zero to a maximum of eight. Examples of tasks included applying direct pressure with two hands and placing tourniquets away from joints (online supplemental figure 1).

A knowledge and attitude survey assessment was completed by participants precourse and postcourse, and at 1-month and 12-month follow-ups. Knowledge was assessed using six questions. Four were “check-all-that-apply” style questions pertaining to bleeding control without the use of a tourniquet where each correct answer was given a score of one point, with four points being the maximum total discrete knowledge score. Participants also answered two further questions rating their perceptions associated with tourniquet use on a sliding scale from zero to 100 points, with a total combined sliding scale score of 200 points. Discrete and sliding scale knowledge questions were analyzed independently. Four questions assessed attitudes, including willingness to help a bleeding person, and confidence in ability to apply a tourniquet correctly. These were true-or-false styled questions with positive responses given a score of one for a maximum overall attitude score of four.

All data was collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure, web-based software tool designed to support data capture for research studies hosted at Vanderbilt University.21 Surveys completed manually were entered into REDCap by a study team member.

Data analysis

Our primary outcome was a significant increase in hands-on skills, knowledge, and comfort level among participants with the use of tourniquets immediately after the course. Our secondary outcome was the incidence of actual utilization of skills imparted for the care of an injured patient after our training. Repeated measures were compared using two-tailed paired Student’s t-tests for normally distributed data and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for non-normally distributed data. Statistical significance for all tests was set a priori at p<0.05. All analyses were performed using Stata V.15.1 (College Station, Texas, USA) and R V.4.5.1 (Comprehensive R Archive Network).

We identified 39 community centers within the hot spots and contacted 8 of those that were centrally located within the hot spots (figure 1). Notably during the follow-up period, none of the participants reported being in a situation where someone was experiencing life-threatening bleeding outside of a hospital, nor having applied a tourniquet on a bleeding person in the year after the training. The community center chose to mount the bleeding control station cabinet in the church basement after the training, citing concerns for theft. There have been no reported incidents of using the provided hemorrhage control kit.

Discussion

To our knowledge this is the first study to prospectively select STB training locations by using GIS software to identify potential training sites within areas of high incidence of penetrating injury. We found that this method effectively identified locations within areas of violence “hot spots” in our community though problems with receptivity to hosting a training persisted. We ultimately identified a location within a hot spot that was willing to host a training where we found a committed target audience who demonstrated significantly improved skills and knowledge after the course. Interestingly, in the year after the training, no participants in our small cohort were present during a situation involving a life-threatening bleed where they could have used their newfound skills, however, participants continued to exhibit a positive attitude toward bleeding control intervention should such a situation arise. Together this study supports that GIS software is a useful adjunct to identify communities most likely to benefit from STB training, including those with individuals who are receptive to participating.

The STB Campaign has demonstrated that bleeding control interventions such as the application of pressure and tourniquets are potentially life-saving skills that can be effectively taught to laypersons through standardized hands-on training; however, disseminating that training to the communities that need it most continues to pose a challenge.10 Commonly, these trainings are conducted at medical or academic centers where the trainees are often already healthcare professionals and of varying likelihood of encountering a bleeding event outside of the hospital.12 Efforts have been made to impart STB training to predictably high-risk populations, for example individuals whose hands-on job descriptions put them at high risk for workplace injury, but similar efforts are needed to target the neighborhoods that experience a disproportionate amount of the penetrating trauma.22 Targeting inner-city high school students has shown promise, but the participants may not be the most at-risk individuals.14 Given the recidivism of violent events, surveys of hospitalized trauma victims suggest that violence survivors are receptive to these trainings and intervening, however, the strategy of how best to identify effective training locations to reach these people and execute the trainings themselves remains elusive.13

This study demonstrates that GIS software is an effective means of identifying training locations within violent injury “hot spots”. Strengths of our study include using city-wide data rather than institutional data as the source of violent events as well as including a wide variety of community centers as potential training locations, at least two of which were receptive to hosting a training. We achieved a high proportion of follow-up responses at 1-month (100%) and 12-months (80%) and were encouraged that our approach selected a population capable of employing bleeding control skills given the positive and persistent effects of the training on participant knowledge, skills and willingness to help.

Interestingly though, none of the study participants applied their training or used a tourniquet in the year after the course despite the large volume of penetrating trauma victims transported to our Level I trauma center from the vicinity of the training location—in fact the volume increased during the study period.23 Although it is possible that our training failed to recruit our target demographic, an alternative and likely explanation is that the COVID-19 pandemic took hold of our country in the months immediately after the training as did social distancing mandates. Thus whereas the violent crime rate increased, so did social interaction, likely limiting participant availability to help in life-threatening situations during follow-up.

Although we hypothesize the lack of utilization of skills by our trainees has more to do with a competing public health crisis than a failure to select a community in need, it does allude to the persistent challenge of identifying not just individuals and locations at risk, but individuals and locations willing to help. For example, of the eight locations we contacted, only two were open to hosting a training. Further, despite engagement and outreach to community members of all ages, our study participants were largely comprised of engaged but older individuals who may have been less likely to find themselves at the scene of a shooting. Additionally, we did not anticipate the installation of the bleeding control station in a locked basement, severely hampering its ready access and potential for impact in the event of a nearby shooting.

Traditional means of promoting community engagement include town halls and targeting community leaders for buy-in, but a novel potential approach to addressing this limitation might lie again in GIS software.24 For example, traditionally if our goal was to target young people, we could focus on locations that offer sporting activities such as recreation centers, however a more objective means might be to gather information on the mean age of a given neighborhood or even community center complement thus creating another layer for our hot spot map to identify training locations based on participant age. Other potential considerations besides age to consider include ways to use the degree of involvement by community leaders, violence intervention teams, or trainings from other organizations such as the American Red Cross as surrogates for potential interest in being a potential training location. Future work should analyze if participants identified through these microtargeted approaches are more likely to not only witness a life-threatening trauma event but to perform a life-saving intervention.

A notable facet of our study is that of the willing 15 trainees, 20% were healthcare professionals and two had taken the course before. This potential selection bias may have overestimated the observed increase in participants’ bleeding control knowledge and skills postcourse as they started from a higher baseline familiarity. Nevertheless, all participants, regardless of their prior STB or healthcare exposure, demonstrated post-course improvement in their bleeding control skills, knowledge, and attitudes. Thus, we think the STB course achieved our goal of increasing overall bleeding control skills and knowledge in a community impacted by gun violence even in the setting of prior healthcare exposure.

Further, our recruitment efforts were hampered by unforeseen circumstances. After the completion of this study, the COVID-19 pandemic and social distancing mandates prevented our team from hosting additional STB courses in the immediate years that followed this project. This limited our efforts to recruit more sites and participants not only due to public health concerns, but also personnel and budgeting constraints that impacted many of the community service-minded institutions in our area. However, we are happy to report that we have recently reinvigorated this project using GIS mapping to select STB training locations in partnership with community organizations. Even with new study team members, we have found GIS mapping to be a sustainable method to target our STB training efforts in violence hot spots and that community centers are increasingly receptive to hosting an event.

A final limitation of our approach is cost. Like many STB training programs, our trainers are volunteers, yet funding constraints still end up limiting the number of participants and locations due to the ability to provide participants with supplies thus making this a single-center study. In addition, the cost of GIS software is another potential specific limitation for mass implementation of our targeted approach to choosing STB locations. For example, the software we used, ArcGIS, ranges in cost from US$700–4,200 a year, posing a potential financial barrier to employing this software to identify violent hot spots. Draper et al foresaw this as a potential limitation to the equitable use of GIS technology to identify efficient training locations.12 They used geospatial software to retrospectively analyze whether existing STB training locations were in violent hot spots, however, the real triumph of their study is that they did so using Rosymap, a homegrown statistical coding package for the free, open-source programming language and software environment R V.4.3.2. They have subsequently offered the raw code freely to the public, providing an open-source GIS software solution for institutions interested in using a targeted approach to select training locations on a limited budget and hopefully promoting the utility of this potentially paradigm-changing approach to choosing STB training locations.

Conclusion

This pilot study suggests that GIS software can be used not only to prospectively identify high-yield sites for STB training, but also individuals who are receptive and willing to deliver potentially life-saving bleeding control interventions. Future work will focus on dissemination of this technique in improved iterations, including identifying not just locations at high risk but those with high levels of buy-in and with younger audiences.
