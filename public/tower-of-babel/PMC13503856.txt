FedAttn-Credit: attention-augmented federated learning with adaptive differential privacy for rural inclusive finance credit assessment
Authors: Zhishan Xie, Liangyu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rural inclusive finance faces persistent challenges in credit assessment due to fragmented data ecosystems, heterogeneous borrower profiles, and stringent privacy constraints. This paper proposes FedAttn-Credit, a multi-party collaborative credit assessment framework that integrates horizontal federated learning with an attention-augmented scoring model and an adaptive differential privacy mechanism. The framework connects five categories of rural data holders—commercial banks, rural credit cooperatives, government platforms, agricultural e-commerce providers, and village-level microfinance institutions—through a central aggregation server that never accesses raw data. A multi-head self-attention module with group-wise importance gating enables the model to dynamically weight heterogeneous feature groups according to their contextual relevance for each borrower. An adaptive differential privacy strategy calibrates noise magnitude based on each participant’s data volume and training-round gradient dynamics, providing stronger protection for small-sample participants without disproportionately degrading model utility. Experimental results on public microfinance records and a synthetic rural credit dataset show that FedAttn-Credit achieves an AUC of 0.8734, narrowing the gap to the centralized upper bound to 1.8 percentage points while outperforming all federated baselines. At equivalent accuracy, the adaptive privacy mechanism cuts cumulative privacy cost by roughly 47% relative to fixed-budget alternatives, a figure that we derive in “Privacy protection effectiveness and model performance tradeoff analysis” section from the Privacy Efficiency Ratio reported there (the two quantities express the same comparison from opposite directions). Ablation and robustness analyses confirm that the attention module, adaptive noise scheduling, and Shapley-based contribution evaluation provide compounding benefits under both standard and adversarial conditions.

Introduction

Rural inclusive finance has emerged as a strategic priority across both developing and developed economies, driven by the recognition that equitable access to credit serves as a cornerstone for poverty alleviation and agricultural modernization1. Government policies in China, India, and several Southeast Asian nations have explicitly targeted financial inclusion in rural areas, yet the gap between policy ambition and operational reality remains wide2. A central obstacle lies in credit assessment: conventional scoring models, originally designed for urban borrowers with rich transactional histories, perform poorly when applied to rural populations whose financial footprints are sparse, irregular, and scattered across heterogeneous institutional databases3.

The difficulty is compounded by the fragmented nature of rural data ecosystems. Agricultural cooperatives, microfinance institutions, village-level banks, and government subsidy platforms each hold partial views of a borrower’s creditworthiness, but these views seldom converge into a unified profile4. This fragmentation—often described as the data silo problem—means that no single institution commands enough information to produce reliable credit scores for smallholder farmers or rural entrepreneurs5. Attempts to centralize such data raise immediate privacy concerns, particularly given tightening regulatory frameworks like the Personal Information Protection Law in China and the General Data Protection Regulation in Europe6. Rural borrowers, many of whom are first-time participants in formal financial systems, are especially vulnerable to the consequences of data breaches, and their willingness to engage with digital financial services hinges on trust that their information will not be misused7.

Against this backdrop, federated learning (FL) has attracted growing interest as a paradigm that enables collaborative model training without requiring raw data to leave its source8. Originally proposed by McMahan and colleagues for mobile keyboard prediction, FL allows multiple participants to jointly optimise a shared model by exchanging only gradient updates or model parameters rather than the underlying datasets themselves9. Several research groups have since adapted FL to high-stakes multi-institutional settings: Yang and colleagues catalogued the taxonomy of horizontal, vertical, and transfer federated learning paradigms together with their cross-organisation applications5; Sheller and colleagues, working in clinical imaging, showed that federated training across ten institutions can reach roughly 99% of the model quality attainable under centralised aggregation, with no raw data ever leaving its source10; and closer to our own domain, Li and colleagues applied horizontal FL to consumer credit scoring among urban commercial banks11. Taken together, these studies confirm that FL can approach—and at times match—the predictive accuracy of centralised training while preserving data locality.

However, direct transplantation of existing FL-based credit models into rural financial settings encounters at least three unresolved difficulties. First, rural data sources are markedly more heterogeneous than their urban counterparts: crop yield records, land tenure documents, weather indices, and social network indicators differ in format, granularity, and update frequency, posing alignment challenges that standard horizontal FL architectures do not address12. Second, the credit scoring models embedded within most FL frameworks—typically logistic regression or shallow neural networks—lack the capacity to capture the complex, nonlinear interactions among agricultural, behavioral, and demographic features that characterize rural borrower risk13. Third, although FL avoids direct data sharing, recent work has shown that gradient leakage attacks can reconstruct sensitive input data from exchanged model updates, meaning that FL alone does not guarantee privacy14. Differential privacy (DP) offers a mathematically rigorous remedy by injecting calibrated noise into model updates, yet a fixed noise budget often degrades model utility unacceptably when participant datasets are small—a condition typical of rural institutions with limited customer bases15.

A handful of recent studies have begun to address subsets of these challenges. Wang and colleagues proposed a vertical FL framework for agricultural lending that integrates satellite imagery with banking records, although their design assumed only two participating institutions and did not incorporate formal privacy guarantees16. Wei and colleagues introduced a DP-enhanced FL pipeline for microfinance scoring; their uniform noise-injection strategy, however, induced substantial accuracy loss whenever it was applied to participants with fewer than one thousand samples17. Neither line of work considered the attention-based feature weighting mechanisms that have proven effective in other heterogeneous data-fusion tasks, nor did they propose adaptive noise-calibration strategies responsive to local data characteristics.

This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a multi-party collaborative credit assessment framework that couples horizontal federated learning with an attention-augmented scoring model and an adaptive differential privacy mechanism, all tailored to the structural realities of rural inclusive finance. Because participants share a common indicator template but rarely populate every slot in it, our framework accommodates the multi-source rural data landscape through a client-side feature-schema reconciliation layer that aligns and imputes the partially overlapping indicator subsets each participant can actually report; this is not a vertical mapping between distinct feature spaces, but a normalisation of variable coverage within a shared schema. Within each local participant, we construct a credit scoring model that integrates an attention module to dynamically weight heterogeneous feature groups—agricultural production indicators, financial transaction summaries, and social-demographic attributes—according to their contextual relevance to default prediction. At the privacy protection level, we design a self-adaptive noise-injection strategy that adjusts the DP noise scale based on each participant’s local data volume and gradient sensitivity, thereby balancing privacy guarantees against model convergence and predictive accuracy more effectively than fixed-budget alternatives18.

The principal contributions of this work can be stated as follows. We design a horizontal federated learning architecture explicitly oriented toward the multi-source data environment of rural finance, incorporating a client-side feature-schema reconciliation layer that aligns and imputes the partially overlapping indicator subsets each participant can actually populate within a shared template—what varies across agricultural cooperatives, village banks, and government platforms is variable coverage, not the underlying feature space itself, which is fixed by the global model. We construct a credit scoring model that embeds a multi-head attention mechanism for adaptive feature-group weighting, enabling the model to learn context-dependent importance scores rather than treating all input dimensions uniformly. We propose an adaptive differential privacy noise injection strategy in which the noise magnitude is dynamically calibrated to each participant’s data scale and gradient norm distribution, mitigating the accuracy penalty that uniform DP imposes on small-sample participants. Experimental evaluation on both synthetic rural credit datasets and real-world microfinance records demonstrates that the proposed framework achieves competitive predictive performance relative to centralised baselines while satisfying formal privacy constraints, offering a viable path toward privacy-preserving, multi-institutional credit assessment in rural inclusive finance.

Theoretical foundation and technical framework

Rural inclusive finance credit assessment system

Rural inclusive finance, as a policy-driven paradigm, refers to the provision of affordable and accessible financial services—credit, insurance, savings, and payment—to underserved rural populations who are excluded from conventional banking channels19. The concept has evolved from early microcredit experiments in the 1970s to a broader institutional commitment codified in national development strategies; China’s 2016–2020 Plan for Advancing Inclusive Finance, for instance, set explicit targets for rural credit coverage and digital financial infrastructure20.

What distinguishes rural credit subjects from their urban counterparts is not merely lower income but a qualitatively different risk profile. Farmer households derive earnings from agricultural activities subject to weather shocks, pest cycles, and volatile commodity prices—income streams that resist the steady periodicity assumed by standard scoring algorithms21. Much of their wealth is tied to non-standardized assets: arable land under collective ownership, livestock, stored grain, and informal mutual aid obligations that lack market-legible valuations22. Compounding these issues, a large fraction of rural borrowers have no formal credit history at all, having operated entirely within cash-based or relationship-mediated economies23.

We construct a five-dimensional indicator system for rural credit assessment, drawing on both the existing microfinance evaluation literature and field-level observations from pilot programs in western China. Table 1 summarizes the proposed framework. Each dimension captures a distinct facet of borrower risk: basic demographic information provides baseline identification; operational status reflects income-generating capacity and its stability; the asset-liability dimension quantifies net worth and indebtedness; social credit captures community-level reputation and cooperative membership records; and behavioral characteristics encode repayment patterns and digital engagement traces where available24. Quantification approaches differ across dimensions—categorical encoding for demographic attributes, ratio-based normalization for financial metrics, and ordinal scoring derived from village committee assessments for social credit indicators. Federated learning fundamentals and algorithms

Federated learning partitions the training process across K distributed participants, each holding a private dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${D}_{k}$$\end{document}, such that model optimization proceeds without any raw data leaving its origin25. Three taxonomic variants exist. Horizontal FL applies when participants share the same feature space but differ in sample populations—the scenario most relevant to rural credit institutions that collect similar indicator types for distinct borrower groups. Vertical FL assumes overlapping samples but disjoint feature spaces, suited to cases where, say, a bank and a tax bureau hold different attributes for the same individuals26. Federated transfer learning relaxes both assumptions, bridging participants that differ in both sample and feature domains, though at the cost of additional alignment complexity27.

Differential privacy and secure aggregation mechanisms

Even though federated learning keeps raw data local, the exchanged gradient updates remain vulnerable to inference attacks, as demonstrated by Zhu et al. through deep gradient leakage reconstruction14. Differential privacy (DP) provides a formal remedy. The parameter ε governs the privacy-utility tradeoff: smaller values impose tighter indistinguishability but demand more noise. Mironov proposed Renyi differential privacy (RDP) as a relaxation that enables tighter composition accounting31. Secure aggregation complements DP by ensuring the server observes only the sum of participant updates, never individual contributions. When combined with DP, each participant first clips and perturbs its gradient locally, then feeds the noised update into the secure aggregation protocol. A multi-head self-attention block then operates over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{i}$$\end{document} to model inter-group dependencies. The motivation here is concrete: when agricultural yield features are missing or noisy for a particular borrower, the attention weights should redistribute representational emphasis toward behavioral or social credit indicators—a form of adaptive imputation learned from data rather than prescribed by hand rules38.

Beyond inter-group attention, we introduce a feature-group importance gate that produces a scalar relevance weight per group. The gated representations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{z}}_{i}^{(g)}$$\end{document} are concatenated and fed into a two-layer feed-forward network with ReLU activation, followed by a sigmoid output neuron that produces the predicted default probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{y}}_{i}\in [\mathrm{0,1}]$$\end{document}.

The regularization coefficient λ is set proportionally higher for lightweight participants (those with fewer than 1000 samples) to counteract their elevated variance, a heuristic we found necessary during preliminary experiments on the pilot dataset from rural Gansu province40. Adaptive differential privacy protection mechanism

A fixed privacy budget applied uniformly across all rounds and all participants is, we argue, poorly matched to the dynamics of federated training in rural credit scenarios. Early rounds produce large, information-rich gradients where individual records exert relatively diluted influence; later rounds yield smaller gradients that are more susceptible to reconstruction attacks41. Participant heterogeneity compounds this mismatch—a village microfinance institution with 300 records needs far more aggressive protection than a commercial bank contributing 15,000 samples, yet uniform ε treats both identically.

Our mechanism operates primarily at the gradient level, where each participant clips and perturbs its local update before transmission. A secondary, low-magnitude Gaussian term is also added at the server before redistribution; rather than describing this as a second layer of differential privacy, we now treat it explicitly as an aggregation regulariser whose role is to suppress one-off outlier updates, and we exclude it from the formal privacy accounting reported in Eq. (22). The participant-side mechanism alone supplies the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\epsilon ,\delta )$$\end{document}-DP guarantee we report. At the gradient level, participant k first clips its local gradient to a dynamic bound \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{k}^{t}$$\end{document} determined by the running median of observed gradient norms over the previous ω rounds (adaptive clipping strategy adapted from42):18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{k}^{t}=\gamma \cdot \mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{n}\left(\{|\nabla {F}_{k}({w}^{r}){|}_{2}{\}}_{r=t-\omega }^{t-1}\right)$$\end{document}where γ >1 is a slack factor (we set γ =1.2 throughout our experiments). This median-based calibration avoids the pitfall of static clipping thresholds, which either truncate informative gradients excessively in early training or fail to constrain them adequately near convergence.

The per-round privacy budget for participant k follows a decay schedule that allocates more budget to early exploratory rounds and tightens protection as the model stabilizes (proposed formulation):19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\epsilon}_{k}^{t}={\epsilon}_{0}\cdot {\left(\frac{{n}_{k}}{N}\right)}^{\rho }\cdot {e}^{-\mu t/T}$$\end{document}

Here \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\epsilon}_{0}$$\end{document} is the base budget, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \in (\mathrm{0,1})$$\end{document} controls the data-size scaling, and μ governs the temporal decay rate. The term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({n}_{k}/N{)}^{\rho }$$\end{document} ensures that participants with larger datasets receive proportionally more budget—they can afford it, since each individual record contributes a smaller fraction of the gradient. After clipping, Gaussian noise is injected with standard deviation (derived from Eq. (8) with the adaptive parameters):20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{k}^{t}=\frac{{C}_{k}^{t}\sqrt[]{2\mathrm{l}\mathrm{n}(1.25/\delta )}}{{\epsilon}_{k}^{t}}$$\end{document}

The perturbed gradient transmitted by participant k is thus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{g}}_{k}^{t}=\mathrm{c}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{p}(\nabla {F}_{k}({w}^{t}),{C}_{k}^{t})+\mathcal{N}(0,({\sigma}_{k}^{t}{)}^{2}I)$$\end{document}.

At the server level, a second layer of protection adds low-magnitude Gaussian noise to the aggregated global model before redistribution, with scale \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{s}^{t}$$\end{document} calibrated to a fixed server-side budget \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\epsilon}_{s}$$\end{document} (standard Gaussian mechanism):21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{s}^{t}=\frac{{\Delta}_{s}\sqrt[]{2\mathrm{l}\mathrm{n}(1.25/{\delta}_{s})}}{{\epsilon}_{s}}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta}_{s}$$\end{document} is bounded by the maximum aggregated update norm across rounds. Because the server-side noise scale is calibrated for aggregation stability rather than for indistinguishability against an external adversary, and because its proper RDP composition with the participant-side mechanism would require a substantially more involved analysis that goes beyond the scope of this paper, we deliberately do not include it in the privacy budget. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\epsilon ,\delta )$$\end{document}-DP guarantee summarised in Eq. (22) is therefore the participant-level guarantee alone; the server step contributes robustness, not additional indistinguishability.

Cumulative privacy loss across T rounds is tracked via RDP composition. For participant k, the total RDP cost at order α is (following the composition theorem from31):22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\epsilon}_{k}^{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}}(\alpha )=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\alpha }{2({\sigma}_{k}^{t}{)}^{2}}$$\end{document}

Converting to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\epsilon ,\delta )$$\end{document}-DP via the standard bound \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon ={\epsilon}_{k}^{\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}}(\alpha )+\frac{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{n}(1/\delta )}{\alpha -1}$$\end{document} and optimizing over α yields the tightest achievable guarantee. Because the adaptive schedule front-loads larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\epsilon}_{k}^{t}$$\end{document} values (and hence smaller \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{k}^{t}$$\end{document}) when gradients are naturally large and back-loads tighter budgets when gradients shrink, the cumulative cost grows sub-linearly relative to a uniform allocation consuming the same total budget—a property we verify empirically in “Experimental analysis and results discussion” section. The net effect, grounded in the exponential decay of Eq. (19), is that small-sample participants receive stronger per-round protection without disproportionately degrading their contribution to the global model, addressing the core tension identified by Wei et al. in heterogeneous federated settings17.

Experimental analysis and results discussion

Experimental setup and data description

Two datasets underpin our evaluation. The first is a publicly available consumer credit dataset from a regional microfinance platform, containing 28,450 loan records with 43 features spanning demographic, financial, and behavioral categories43. The second is a synthetic rural credit dataset we constructed to mirror the structural properties of agricultural lending in western China, drawing on published statistical profiles from county-level rural credit cooperatives44. We generated 35,200 borrower records across five simulated institutions with deliberately unequal sample sizes (ranging from 480 to 12,600) and heterogeneous feature availability—some institutions lack digital footprint indicators entirely, while others carry no guarantee network data. Default labels were assigned using a logistic model calibrated to real-world rural default rates of approximately 4.8%. Ablation experiments and robustness analysis

We systematically removed individual components from FedAttn-Credit to isolate their contributions. Three ablated variants were tested: (A) replacing the multi-head attention block with simple feature concatenation while retaining everything else, (B) substituting the adaptive DP mechanism with a fixed uniform budget at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\epsilon}_{0}=8.0$$\end{document}, and (C) disabling the Shapley-based contribution evaluation and reverting to pure sample-size-weighted aggregation as in standard FedAvg. Real-world rural federations will encounter operational disruptions that controlled experiments cannot fully anticipate, but we can simulate their broad contours. We designed three stress scenarios. In the first, we degraded data quality at two of five participants by injecting 30% random label noise after round 40, simulating corrupted records entering a village bank’s system. The full FedAttn-Credit model’s AUC dropped by 1.9 percentage points under this corruption, while the variant without contribution evaluation (C) fell by 3.7 points—the Shapley scores detected the sudden quality shift within five rounds and down-weighted the corrupted participants accordingly. In the second scenario, one participant (Cooperative B) dropped out permanently at round 50, mimicking a connectivity failure that our asynchronous protocol could not recover. The remaining four participants completed training with an AUC of 0.8612, a tolerable degradation given that 24% of total training data vanished mid-process. The staleness-bounded aggregation from Eq. (11) smoothed this transition by gradually discounting Cooperative B’s last contribution rather than abruptly removing it. A third scenario introduced intermittent communication—two participants alternated between available and offline every ten rounds—and the model converged to 0.8581 AUC with 15 additional rounds, demonstrating that the asynchronous protocol absorbs sporadic dropout without catastrophic failure.

Byzantine resilience received dedicated testing. We simulated a malicious participant that submitted gradient updates scaled by a factor of five (a simple model poisoning attack) and, separately, one that inverted its gradient signs to maximize the global loss50. Under naive FedAvg aggregation, the scaled-gradient attack reduced AUC to 0.7812. We augmented our framework with coordinate-wise median aggregation, a lightweight Byzantine-tolerant strategy that replaces the weighted mean with the median across participants for each parameter dimension51. With this defense active, AUC under the same attack recovered to 0.8523. The sign-flip attack proved less damaging even without defenses (AUC 0.8189), likely because the adaptive clipping bound from Eq. (18) already truncated the inverted gradients’ anomalous norms. Combining median aggregation with our adaptive DP mechanism yielded the most robust configuration: AUC remained above 0.84 across all attack scenarios, at the cost of roughly three additional convergence rounds compared to the non-adversarial setting. These results suggest that the layered design—attention-based feature weighting, adaptive privacy, contribution evaluation, and Byzantine-tolerant aggregation—provides compounding defensive benefits that no single mechanism achieves in isolation.

Discussion

The performance advantage of FedAttn-Credit over flat federated architectures stems, at its core, from a representational insight rather than a purely algorithmic one. Standard federated models treat all input features as occupying a single homogeneous space, which works adequately when participants share clean, complete, and similarly distributed records. Rural credit data violates every one of these assumptions. The attention mechanism addresses this by learning, for each borrower, which feature groups carry genuine predictive signal and which are dominated by noise or missingness. What surprised us during development was how consistently the model learned to suppress the digital footprint group for borrowers from village microfinance institutions—where such data is sparse and unreliable—while amplifying the same group for e-commerce platform borrowers. This context-dependent reweighting is something no fixed feature engineering pipeline could replicate without extensive manual tuning per institution, and it explains why the ablation of the attention module produced the largest single performance drop.

Practical deployment in rural settings introduces constraints that our experimental simulation only partially captures. Communication costs, while manageable in our five-participant setup, will scale with both federation size and model dimensionality. Each round of FedAttn-Credit transmits approximately 1.2 MB of gradient data per participant—feasible over 4G mobile networks prevalent in Chinese rural counties, but potentially prohibitive for institutions relying on intermittent satellite connectivity in remote mountainous regions. Model compression techniques such as gradient sparsification or quantization could alleviate this burden, though their interaction with differential privacy noise injection remains an open question we have not yet explored. Computational requirements at the participant side are modest—local training completes in under 90 s per round on a consumer-grade GPU—but village-level institutions may lack even this hardware, necessitating cloud-assisted local training that introduces its own trust and latency complications.

The tension between privacy and utility, while measurably reduced by our adaptive mechanism, cannot be eliminated. Regulatory environments differ: China’s Personal Information Protection Law imposes consent requirements that may demand participant-level ε reporting, while the GDPR’s privacy-by-design principle is less prescriptive about specific noise budgets. Our framework accommodates both postures through the tunable parameters ρ and μ in Eq. (19), but choosing appropriate values requires institution-specific risk assessment that falls outside the scope of a purely technical contribution. We note, too, that our privacy guarantees assume honest-but-curious adversaries at the server level; a fully malicious server could potentially correlate temporal patterns in model updates across rounds, a threat vector that secure multi-party computation—rather than DP alone—would need to address.

Several experimental findings warrant candid acknowledgment of their limits. The synthetic rural dataset, while calibrated to published statistics, cannot reproduce the full complexity of real agricultural lending—seasonal default clustering, intergenerational land inheritance disputes, and informal credit relationships all remain absent from our simulation. The relatively modest gap between FedAttn-Credit and the centralized baseline (1.8 AUC points) may widen on genuinely messy field data. Our Byzantine resilience testing covered only two attack types; more sophisticated data poisoning strategies that mimic legitimate gradient distributions could evade both median aggregation and clipping-based defenses. Finally, the Shapley-based contribution evaluation, while effective, adds computational overhead that scales combinatorially—our Monte Carlo approximation keeps this tractable for five participants but may require further algorithmic refinement for federations exceeding twenty institutions.

Conclusion

This paper presented FedAttn-Credit, a multi-party collaborative credit assessment framework built on horizontal federated learning and tailored to the structural realities of rural inclusive finance. Three interconnected contributions define the work. First, we designed a federated architecture that accommodates the multi-source, heterogeneous data landscape of rural credit ecosystems—spanning commercial banks, rural credit cooperatives, government platforms, e-commerce providers, and village-level microfinance institutions—through a feature alignment layer, asynchronous communication with staleness-bounded aggregation, and Shapley-based contribution evaluation. Second, we constructed a credit scoring model embedding multi-head self-attention and group-wise importance gating, enabling the network to learn borrower-specific weightings across five indicator dimensions rather than treating all features uniformly. Third, we proposed an adaptive differential privacy mechanism that calibrates noise magnitude jointly to each participant’s data volume and the training round’s gradient dynamics, achieving formal privacy guarantees while preserving model utility more effectively than fixed-budget alternatives.

Experimental evaluation on both public microfinance records and a synthetic rural credit dataset demonstrated that FedAttn-Credit reaches 0.8734 AUC—within 1.8 points of the centralized upper bound—while outperforming all federated baselines across AUC, F1-Score, accuracy, and KS statistic. The adaptive privacy mechanism proved especially valuable for small-sample participants: Village MFI E accumulated only half the privacy cost of the largest participant while maintaining competitive local prediction quality. Ablation experiments confirmed that each component—attention weighting, adaptive noise scheduling, and contribution evaluation—contributes measurably and that their benefits compound under adversarial and operationally disrupted conditions.

For rural financial inclusion, these results carry both theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, the work demonstrates that the data silo problem in agricultural lending is addressable without centralizing sensitive borrower information, provided that the federated architecture accounts for the pronounced heterogeneity of rural data sources. Practically, the framework offers a compliance-compatible pathway for multi-institutional credit collaboration under tightening data protection regulations.

We are candid about what remains unresolved. Our experiments relied on simulated rural data and a moderately sized public dataset; validation against large-scale field records from operational rural credit cooperatives is essential before deployment claims can be made with confidence. The five-participant federation, while representative of typical county-level configurations, does not test scalability to provincial or national networks. Seasonal dynamics in agricultural default patterns—harvest cycles, drought events, policy subsidy shifts—were absent from our simulation and could alter model behavior in practice.

Future work should pursue several directions. Federated transfer learning could bridge the gap between participants whose feature spaces diverge beyond what horizontal FL accommodates—for instance, integrating satellite-derived crop health indices with banking transaction logs across institutions that share no common borrowers. Blockchain-based audit trails would add verifiable transparency to the aggregation process, addressing trust concerns that pure cryptographic protocols leave unresolved. Multimodal data fusion, incorporating unstructured inputs such as field photographs and voice-based loan applications increasingly common in rural fintech, represents a natural extension of the attention-based architecture we have introduced here. We view this paper as a foundational step—one that establishes the technical viability of privacy-preserving federated credit assessment in rural settings, while leaving ample room for the refinements that real-world deployment will inevitably demand.
