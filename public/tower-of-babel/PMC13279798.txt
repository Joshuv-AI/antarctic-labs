Exploration on the influence of menstrual cycle phase on biceps brachii tendon mechanics
Authors: Cori A. Calkins, Parisa Alaei, John Winkler, Rowan R. Smart, Jennifer M. Jakobi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tendons are essential for force transmission, yet the influence of menstrual cycle phase on upper-limb positional tendon mechanics is unknown. This exploratory study investigated biceps brachii tendon mechanics across the menstrual cycle. Eight females (23.9 ± 4.3 years) underwent ultrasonographic assessment during the menses, follicular, and luteal phases. Between 0 and 20% of maximal voluntary contraction (MVC), tendon elongation was greater (Menses 5.4 ± 2.6 mm; Follicular 4.8 ± 2.7 mm; Luteal 3.3 ± 2.0 mm; p < 0.001), and stiffness lower (Menses 38.4 ± 31.7 N mm−1; Follicular 43.09 ± 35.1 N mm−1; Luteal 55.0 ± 32.4 N mm−1; p = 0.009) during menses compared to the luteal phase. Young’s modulus was lower (p = 0.02) and strain was higher (p = 0.005) during menses compared with the luteal phase. Tendon stress across force levels was greater (~ 15%) during menses compared to both the follicular (p = 0.002) and luteal (p = 0.002) phases. There was no significant difference for tendon stiffness from 20 to 100% MVC or hysteresis observed between phases (p > 0.1). The greater tendon elongation during menses compared to the luteal phase arises from the changes in the toe-initial region, which governs the initial mechanical response of the tendon. This emerging research highlights the importance of the distinct regions of elongation, and significance of the menstrual cycle on tendon mechanics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56244-8.

Introduction

Tendons transmit and modulate force between muscle and bone, making them fundamental to both movement efficiency and physical performance in everyday activities1–3. Tendons can be broadly categorized into two types: energy-storing and positional tendons. Energy-storing tendons, as the name suggests, release and return stored energy via elastic recoil and they facilitate the optimization of locomotion4. In contrast, positional tendons, transmit energy from muscle to bone to facilitate the joint angle change and/or optimize the position of a limb in space for holding a load. These functional distinctions are reflected in mechanical properties, most notably hysteresis where energy-storing tendons exhibit lower hysteresis and greater elasticity compared to their positional counterparts5. The composition of the tendon matrix can contribute to these differences in tendon mechanics between tendon types. For example, Birch (2007) suggests that the difference in matrix composition between energy-storing and positional tendons might influence the amount of strain that the tendon will undergo during a muscle contraction6. As such, tendon type must be carefully considered when evaluating tendon mechanics, particularly in the context of movements that demand either high energy return or positional control.

Upper limb function is fundamental to daily life, occupational and athletic performance. Hence, characterizing upper-limb tendon mechanics in relation to female hormones is crucial for understanding female-specific patterns of natural age-related decline towards improving functional outcomes and reducing injury risk in females. Despite increasing interest in sex-related differences in muscle and tendon physiology, few studies have examined positional tendons, particularly in relation to female physiology. Most human research to-date has primarily involved male participants7,8. Studies that have considered sex-based comparisons report no significant sex-related difference for Achilles tendon stiffness9 yet others have found greater stiffness and stress in males compared with females10,11. Likewise, greater tendon stiffness and stress was recently observed in the distal biceps brachii (BB) tendon, a positional tendon in the upper limb in males compared to females12. However, existing literature largely overlooks the potential influence of menstrual cycle-related hormonal fluctuations on tendon mechanical properties and this consideration would enhance understanding of functional ability in females.

Typically, estrogen concentration is greatest during the follicular phase with a secondary peak occurring in the luteal phase, while progesterone reaches its peak concentration during the luteal phase; both hormones are at their lowest concentration during menses. Estrogen and progesterone receptors are present in human tendon tissue13, and the cyclical variation in these hormones throughout the menstrual cycle may affect tendon mechanics. Assessing the phases (i.e. follicular and luteal) with higher hormone concentration compared to a hormonally reduced and self-reported clear hormonal phase (menses) enables comparisons to indirectly assess the impact of hormonal influence on tendon mechanics. The influence of menstrual phase on upper-limb positional tendons has not yet been examined. Studies of lower-limb energy-storing tendons, typically show no variation in tendon stiffness, maximum elongation, or hysteresis across the menstrual cycle14,15. Likewise, Saito el al. (2024) reported no differences in patellar or Achilles tendon properties between early follicular and luteal phases16; however, Eiling et al. (2007) used a hoping protocol and during the late follicular phase reported lower musculotendinous stiffness17. Thus, within comparable experimental protocols, tendon mechanical behaviour appears stable throughout the menstrual cycle. This stability may relate to the functional demands and elastic recoil properties of energy-storing tendons, which may buffer mechanical changes despite hormonal fluctuations.

Tendon crimp is an important factor when examining tendon mechanical properties. In the toe region of the force-elongation curve, the crimp must be taken-up before the tendon can stretch further. This occurs at the start of the muscle contraction. Once this crimp is fully taken up, the tendon enters the linear region, where it responds more predictably to force. Because the linear region makes up a larger portion of the force-elongation curve compared to the portion of the curve during the start of a contraction which contains the toe region, both regions should be considered independently to understand their potentially unique contributions to tendon mechanics. There is a clear gap in the literature on the mechanical behaviour of positional tendons across the menstrual cycle. To address this gap, the aim of this exploratory study was to investigate the mechanical properties of the distal biceps brachii (BB), a positional tendon in the upper limb, between phases of the menstrual cycle, specifically between high (follicular and luteal) and low (menses) hormone concentration.

Methods

Participants

Eight right hand dominant females (23.9 ± 4.4 years; 166.3 ± 4.3 cm; 62.4 ± 10.5 kg) volunteered and gave written informed consent to participate in the study approved by the University of British Columbia Okanagan Behavioral Research Ethics Board (H16-00948-A010). All methods were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Exclusion criteria included hormonal contraception use, right forearm/arm injuries or surgeries within the past 6-months, reported neurological disorders, and/or participation in intensive upper body strength training.

Experimental setup

Participants were seated upright in a custom-built chair, which was adjusted to achieve a 90° angle of the hips and knees. The right elbow was positioned to 110° flexion, with the forearm and wrist in a supinated position and the hand grasping the manipulandum (Model: MLP150, Transducer Techniques, Temecula CA., USA). Participants were secured to the chair with an adjustable strap to maintain position throughout the protocol. The force signal was displayed in real time on a 52 cm monitor placed 100 cm in front of the participant, and adjusted to the participant’s eye level. The chair position and monitor height were consistent across sessions for each participant. Force signals were sampled at 1000 Hz using a 1401 plus analogue to digital converter (Cambridge Electronic Design (CED), Cambridge, UK), and then stored for offline analysis using Spike2 software (version 10; CED, Cambridge, UK). The force transducer recorded force at the manipulandum, which was used to calculate muscle moment and distal biceps tendon force.

Experimental Protocol

Participants completed four experimental sessions. The first was a familiarization session. This consisted of three isometric elbow-flexion maximal voluntary contractions (MVCs), each separated by two minutes of rest. During each MVC, participants increased their force with visual ramp feedback and once MVC was achieved a ~ 3-second hold was verbally encouraged. Prior to MVCs, single square wave pulses (200µs pulse width) were delivered to the biceps brachii through carbon rubber electrodes (4 × 4.5 cm) placed over the muscle belly to elicit a twitch. Stimulation settings were determined by increasing intensity until a twitch plateau was reached, and subsequently increased 10% to reach supramaximal. Voluntary activation was assessed via the interpolated twitch technique18. Three twitches were delivered before, during and after the MVC.

Participants self-reported on the familiarization session the first day of menstruation for determination of their menstrual cycle phase. Participants also self-reported their typical cycle length to confirm cycle regularity. The subsequent three sessions were randomized for menses (day 1–5), follicular (day 7–14), and luteal (day 16–30). Because ovulation was not tested, the follicular and luteal phases are estimated based on self-reported cycle timing, and thus for statistical comparison the follicular and luteal phases were compared back to menses to best understand phase-related greater hormone levels relative to lower hormonal levels19. Saliva samples were collected under researcher supervision at the same time of day for each testing session to measure sex hormone levels during each phase. Participants were instructed to avoid food and drinks other than water 60 min prior to saliva sampling as advised by the ELISA kit instructions. Participants were instructed to passively drool, approximately one-two millilitre of saliva into a vial with a collection aid (Salimetrics, PA, USA). The saliva samples were stored in a commercial freezer immediately after collection and stored at -20 degrees Celsius.

Resting tendon ultrasound and surface anatomical measures were captured as previously described3,20. Briefly, resting BB tendon length was measured with a panoramic scan between the radial tuberosity and the distal BB muscle tendon junction, and cross-sectional area (CSA) was acquired with a single image (LOGIQ E9, GE, USA). Surface anatomical measurements of lever arm length and BB moment arm at rest were undertaken. Surface anatomical measurements of lever arm length and BB moment arm at rest were undertaken. The lever arm was defined as the distance from the elbow joint axis to the point of force application, in line with standard biomechanical definitions3,21. Specifically lever arm was measured from the lateral epicondyle of the humerus to the force transducer used to record elbow flexion force. The moment arm was defined as the perpendicular distance from the tendon’s line of action to the joint axis of rotation21,22. The distal biceps tendon line of action was reconstructed using ultrasound imaging to identify the distal muscle–tendon junction and the tendon insertion on the radial tuberosity3. The moment arm was then calculated as the perpendicular distance from this tendon line of action to the elbow joint axis, approximated by the lateral epicondyle23. Following the MVCs, on the three experimental sessions participants also completed two submaximal isometric pyramidal elbow flexion contractions to practice trace matching. Subsequently, three maximal isometric pyramidal elbow flexion contractions were performed by tracing a custom image set at 30 N/s (Spike2 v10; CED, Cambridge, UK) with approximately two minutes of rest in between trials (Fig. 1a). During the pyramidal contraction the distal BB muscle-tendon junction (MTJ) was captured with a GE ML6-15 ultrasound probe (5-15 MHz, GE LOGIQ E9, General Electric, Fairfield, CT, USA) in the longitudinal orientation using continuous real-time B-mode video ultrasonography at a frame rate of 74 Hz (Hz), gain of 44 and a depth of 3 cm. Repeatability of resting and isometric tendon mechanical measures of the biceps brachii tendon has previously been validated20.

Data analysis

MVCs from each trial were measured as the peak force achieved. All ultrasound analysis was performed using the inherent measurement tools of the LOGIQ E9 (GE, USA). Resting BB tendon length (i.e. distal MTJ of the BB to the insertion of the tendon onto the radial tuberosity) was measured using the open spline measurement, which allows for quantification along a non-linear path. Resting tendon CSA were measured using an open trace function along the anatomical borders at the thickest point of the structure. From the second, third and fourth lab visit, seven trials (7/72,9.7%: Menses 1, Follicular 4, Luteal 2) were removed due to low ultrasound video quality where the muscle tendon junction of the biceps brachii could not be fully tracked throughout the entire video and/or probe shift occurred during the trial. A total of 65 trials were used for data analysis.

For quantification of hysteresis, tendon displacement was captured at 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100% of MVC on both the ascending and descending pyramidal contraction. On the descending curve when the force returned to 0% MVC the ultrasound recording ended and the resting tendon length was recorded at this time point. To measure tendon displacement at each force level, the corresponding time from the force trace was used to identify the specific ultrasound video frame required for analysis. At maximal force the video was inspected to ensure that maximal tendon elongation was achieved. Tendon displacement was defined as the difference between the BB tendon length taken from the edge of the screen to the distal MTJ at rest for each force level. Measurements using the hyperechoic marker were also completed to ensure probe stability and account for any shift during contraction (Fig. 1.B).

Tendon stiffness was quantified from 0 to 20%24–26 (to capture the toe and initial region where force production occurs) and 20% to 100% (linear region) MVC as the slope of tendon force by tendon displacement relation. Tendon strain was quantified as the percentage of the tendon displacement by resting tendon length. Tendon stress was calculated from the quotient of tendon force by resting tendon CSA. Young’s modulus was calculated as the ratio of tendon stress to tendon strain (stress/strain). Elbow flexion force was the primary input for subsequent calculations. Muscle moment was calculated by multiplying this measured force by the lever arm measured at rest21. Distal biceps tendon force was subsequently estimated by dividing the calculated muscle moment by the distal biceps tendon moment arm27. Tendon force, strain and stress were quantified for each force level from 0 to 100% MVC in increments of 20% MVC.

To quantify hysteresis a total box area (ABox) was calculated by multiplying the greatest tendon elongation (TE) value by 100% MVC. The area over (AOver) the ascending and descending curves were estimated from midpoint Riemann sums. The AOver the ascending or descending curves were each subtracted from the ABox to give the area under each curve (AUnder). This process accounted for the curves that had lower elongation values at a higher force level than the preceding force levels on the ascending curve or succeeding force levels on the descending curve. In preparation for hormone analysis, saliva samples were thawed, vortex, and centrifuged at 1500xg for 15 min to separate mucin. All samples were visually inspected for discolouration and food particulate. Estradiol and progesterone levels were measured in undiluted saliva using the 17-beta-estradiol and progesterone enzyme immunoassay kits (Salimertics, CA, USA) following manufacture protocols28,29. Absorbance was measured with the iMark microplate reader at 450 nm (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and a 4-parameter logistic curve of the five standards was used to interpolate the unknown concentrations. The concentration limits for the estradiol and progesterone immunoassays are 1pg/ml-32pg/ml and 10pg/ml-2430pg/ml, respectively.

Statistics

To characterize our participants and understand tendon mechanics across the menstrual phases, linear mixed effects (LME) models that included menstrual cycle phase as a fixed effect and participant as a random intercept, were conducted using R (R version 4.3.2, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Prior to analysis, the data were verified to have met all assumptions required for the use of LME models.

Ovulation was not directly confirmed in the present study, and menstrual cycle phases were therefore estimated based on self-reported menstruation and cycle timing. As a result, follicular and luteal classifications reflect expected hormonal profiles rather than biochemically verified phase boundaries. Given that menses represents a visibly evident and consistently low-hormone state, statistical comparisons were structured relative to this phase to contrast a low-hormone reference condition with luteal and follicular phases expected to exhibit elevated concentrations of estrogen and/or progesterone. Therefore, menses was specified as the reference category in all linear mixed-effects models to ensure comparisons were anchored to a consistent lower-hormone baseline, enabling direct estimation of differences in follicular and luteal phases relative to this physiological and self-reported menstruation reference state. For the tendon mechanics of tendon stress and strain and Young’s modulus LME models included phase, force and the interaction of phase and force as fixed effects and participant as a random intercept. Force level was coded as a numeric variable. Progesterone concentrations were also analyzed using an LME model with menstrual cycle phase (menses, follicular, luteal) as a fixed effect and participant as a random intercept, with menses as the reference category. LME models with Satterthwaite’s method were fit with packages lme430 and lmerTest31. Trials were removed if values were greater than 3x the interquartile range32. In this study, tendon stiffness measures obtained between 0 and 20% MVC, and 20–100% MVC had 2 trials (2/65, 3.1%) and 5 trials (5/65, 7.7%) removed, respectively as they were 3x greater than the interquartile range.

To quantify credible intervals and conclude confidence in the changes observed throughout the menstrual cycle Bayesian linear mixed models were conducted using rstanarm33. Bayesian linear models were coded using the same fixed effects and random intercepts as the LME models. See supplemental Table 1 for output.

Results

The MVCs did not differ between phases (Menses 132.1 ± 26.3 N; Follicular 134.5 ± 26.3 N; Luteal 133.4 ± 25.3 N) and the maximal force from the pyramidal contractions to determine hysteresis and mechanics also did not differ between phases (Menses 126.5 ± 25.2 N; Follicular 124.7 ± 24.1 N; Luteal 121.9 ± 27.0 N) (p > 0.2). Voluntary activation was 96.0 ± 2.3%, and arising from a computer backup error on the familiarization day voluntary activation is unavailable for four participants.

Stress showed a significant Phase × Force interaction (p = 0.002). At low force levels stress did not differ across phases (Force 20–60%, p > 0.05; Cohen’s d ranging from 0.1 to 0.8), but at higher force levels stress was lower in follicular and luteal compare to menses (Force 80 and 100%, p < 0.05; Cohen’s d ranging from 1.0 to 1.4). There was a main effect of Force; stress increased with force level (p < 0.001; Cohen’s d ranging from 1.9 to 7.5), while the Phase main effect was non-significant (p > 0.5) (Fig. 3a).

There was no Phase × Force interaction for Young’s modulus (p > 0.5). There was a main effect for Force, as expected Young’s modulus increased as force increased (p < 0.001; Cohen’s d ranging from 0.2 to 1.0). There was a main effect for Phase. Young’s modulus was significantly greater in luteal compared with menses (p = 0.02; Cohen’s d = 0.3), but there was no difference observed between follicular and menses (p = 0.9; Cohen’s d = − 0.1) (Fig. 3c).

Tendon hysteresis during the follicular phase (22.6 ± 39.7%; p = 0.2; Cohen’s d = 0.3) and luteal phase (31.3 ± 39.1%; p = 0.8; Cohen’s d = 0.1) did not differ from menses (33.6 ± 23.6%) (Fig. 3d). Stiffness from 0 to 20% during the luteal phase (55.0 ± 32.4 N mm−1; p = 0.009; Cohen’s d = − 0.1) was higher than menses (38.4 ± 31.7 N mm−1) and did not differ from the follicular phase (43.09 ± 35.1 N mm−1; p = 0.4; Cohen’s d = − 0.5) (Fig. 3d). Stiffness from 20 to 100% MVC did not differ between the follicular phase (121.7 ± 69.5 N mm−1; p = 0.1; Cohen’s d = − 0.4) or the luteal phase (110.6 ± 59.0 N mm−1; p = 0.6; Cohen’s d = − 0.2) (Fig. 3e) and menses (100.3 ± 27.6 N mm−1). Progesterone concentration was greater during the luteal phase compared to the follicular phase (p = 0.05) and menses (p = 0.03) (Fig. 4a). Estrogen was not analyzed statistically due to 25% (6/24) of the samples falling below the acceptable standard curve value (Fig. 4b).

Discussion

This exploratory study evaluated the influence of the menstrual cycle on tendon mechanics of the BB, a positional tendon, in young females not using hormonal contraceptives. Tendon hysteresis was not affected by the menstrual cycle, as elongation from 20 to 100% did not differ between phases. However, during the luteal phase compared to menses, tendon elongation from 0 to 20% was less which contributed to stiffness being greater in the luteal phase. Additionally, tendon stress across forces was lower during the follicular and luteal phase relative to menses. These findings indicate that the mechanical properties of female positional tendons are influenced by menstrual hormones. Specifically, during menses, when menstrual hormones are lowest, tendons exhibit greater elongation at low forces, resulting in lower stiffness. In contrast, during the luteal phase when progesterone levels are elevated the tendon is stiffer. These findings provide novel insights into upper-limb positional tendon mechanics across the menstrual cycle, a topic that has been largely understudied. This study demonstrates phase-dependent changes in the toe-initial region of the distal biceps brachii, highlighting that positional tendon may respond differently to hormonal fluctuations than energy-storing tendons. These results position our study as a complementary extension to existing literature, providing a more nuanced understanding of how menstrual hormones can modulate tendon mechanics in females.

A key strength of this initial approach to studying an upper limb positional tendon is the independent assessment of tendon elongation at low (0–20%) and higher forces (20–100%), as well as the comprehensive assessment of elongation (0-100%). At low forces, within the toe region, tendon crimp must be taken-up as force increases influencing total elongation of the tendon over time. In contrast, crimp has lesser of an influence in the linear region (20–100%) where according to Hooke’s law, elongation is proportional to the force34. Since MVC did not differ between the phases, and there was similar elongation in the linear region and lower elongation in the toe-initial region during the luteal phase the data suggests that the overall reduced elongation (0–100%) arises from the toe-initial region. This is in-part because the linear region is the largest portion of the force-elongation curve, and the area between the ascending and descending curves, defined as hysteresis, would proportionally equate to the linear region. Importantly, even though the linear region dominates the force-elongation curve, and is visibly wider in luteal relative to menses and follicular, changes in the toe region are functionally meaningful. The toe region reflects the initial uncrimping of collagen fibers, which governs tendon compliance during the onset of force production. Modulations in this region can influence how quickly force is transmitted from muscle to bone. Thus, tendon hysteresis did not differ significantly across menstrual phases. Similarly, Burgess et al. reported no differences in hysteresis of the medial gastrocnemius tendon, an energy-storing tendon, across the menstrual cycle14. This distinction underscores the contribution of crimp as well as considering the contributions of both regions of the force-elongation curve.

Previous studies, mostly in energy-storing tendons of the lower limbs report no menstrual cycle effects on tendon stiffness15,16. This study uniquely quantifies mechanics in an upper-limb positional tendon; the distal BB. We showed lower stiffness in the toe-initial region during menses compared to luteal phase, suggesting greater “slack” or laxity in the system in menses. Since resting tendon length did not vary across phases, changes in strain reflect true difference in tendon elongation. Supporting this, Lavagnino et al. showed in rat tail tendons that greater laxity resulted in shortened tendon crimp to maintain homeostatic tension35. Accordingly, the BB tendon crimp during menses was likely shorter, allowing for faster uncrimping and greater elongation during the toe-initial region. Shojaee further emphasizes tendon type differences in their review of equine tendons, reporting larger crimp angle and shorter crimp length in energy-storing compared to positional tendons36. These findings highlight the need for future research into how sex hormones influence tendon crimp.

Crimp is a collagenous-based tendon property, making collagen synthesis a potentially important factor in tendon mechanics. Although the acute effects of collagen synthesis on tendon structure are not fully understood, increased mechanical loading increases tendon stiffness alongside elevated collagen synthesis37. Hansen et al. reported that collagen turnover increased with serum estradiol levels in individuals undertaking estradiol replacement therapy38. Evidence from animal models suggests that estrogen deficiency modulates collagen-related extracellular matrix remodeling39. In contrast, Miller et al. reported no change in patellar tendon collagen synthesis across the menstrual cycle40. Similarly, Iwańska et al. observed that markers of collagen synthesis and breakdown did not differ significantly across menstrual cycle phases in physically active women41. Determining the hormonal regulation of collagen synthesis in humans is essential to advance understanding of the role of collagenous structures, and crimp in menstrual phase related differences in tendon stiffness.

Tendon stress across force levels was lower during the luteal phase and follicular phases compared with menses. Although neither resting tendon CSA and MVC differed significantly across phases, resting tendon CSA was slightly lower during menses. This large difference potentially contributed to greater stress across forces as the load was concentrated over a reduced area. Another method to quantify tendon stress, as previously described by our lab, uses active tendon CSA rather than resting CSA which provides a more precise estimate of tendon stress12. However, due to the limited surface area of the arm, it was not feasible to position ultrasound probes longitudinally and horizontally simultaneously to capture tendon elongation and active CSA. Future studies should aim to quantify active tendon CSA across the menstrual cycle to improve the precision of tendon stress measures. Young’s modulus was greater during the luteal phase compared with menses, indicating reduced strain relative to stress during the luteal phase. Because Young’s modulus was calculated as stress divided by strain, this finding is consistent with the lower strain observed during the luteal phase. Together, these results suggest small differences in tendon stress–strain behavior across menstrual cycle phases.

Additional to evaluating tendon mechanics across the three phases, hormone measures were also attempted. The saliva ELISA test for progesterone levels followed the expected pattern, where progesterone was highest in the luteal phase. In contrast, estrogen yielded an unreliable result, with 25% of the samples falling outside the standard curve. Since estrogen concentration levels were not determined with an ovulation test, the hormone peak may have been missed. Even without direct hormone levels, exploring menstrual phase offers value in everyday life of females as they operate on a daily basis across phase not specific hormone levels. Hence understanding phase-related trends are still meaningful.

Although estradiol concentrations did not show the expected phase-dependent increase relative to menses this pattern was primarily driven by two participants. Exploring the data, without these participants revealed the anticipated increase in estradiol across the follicular and luteal phases, suggesting that the majority of participants were likely tested in the pre-planned cycle phase. The atypical hormonal profiles observed in these cases may reflect methodological and participant-related factors, including the sensitivity and variability of the assay kits used or differences in compliance with pre-testing instructions (e.g., dietary or fluid intake prior to data collection). These considerations highlight the inherent challenges in accurately quantifying sex hormone fluctuations. Additionally, it is important to note that the follicular phase encompassed the mid- to late-follicular period, during which estradiol concentrations can increase rapidly and exhibit substantial inter-individual variability. This temporal heterogeneity may have contributed to the absence of a clear group-level increase in estradiol across the follicular phase. Notably, one of the two participants exhibiting atypical estradiol concentrations also demonstrated a decrease in progesterone from the follicular to luteal phase.

Though not significant the ~ 11% difference in hysteresis between menses and the follicular phase warrants further investigation. In ovariectomized female rats, estrogen and progesterone upregulated relaxin receptor expression on the patellar tendon whereas testosterone had the opposite effect42. Upregulation of relaxin receptor expression should result in greater laxity in the system which is contradictory to our results where during the toe-initial region menses had the lowest stiffness. Relaxin might have distinct influence based upon the region of tendon elasticity, as difference in hysteresis curve expression was visibly evident in the toe region as well as on the ascending (80–100%) and descending (100%-80%) areas, suggesting the potential for area-specific influence. The serum concentration of hormones such as relaxin43 and testosterone44 also fluctuate throughout the menstrual cycle. However, in humans, lower serum relaxin levels were associated with greater patellar tendon stiffness, though this was not observed in the medial gastrocnemius tendon45. A recent review indicates that receptor location, rather than serum levels may be the key determinant of relaxin’s activity43. To-date females remain understudied in the sports science and sports medicine research, and most notably few studies consider menstrual status in study designs46. These early findings of hormonal influence are novel, as this study uniquely examines an upper-limb positional tendon and underscores the nuanced role of female sex hormones, highlighting the need for further research and improved hormone measurement techniques.

High interindividual variability in hormone levels across the menstrual cycle may partially explain the variability observed in tendon hysteresis. A strength of this study was the statistical methods that account for this variability. Most previous studies have relied on repeated measures ANOVAs to assess tendon mechanics across menstrual cycle phases, but these methods often overlook the complex interpersonal and intrapersonal fluctuations in hormone levels. In contrast, LME models, which were used in this study, are better suited as they incorporate random intercepts or slopes to account for participant-specific variability19. Moreover, recognizing the limitation of a smaller sample for an exploratory study, we employed Bayesian linear mixed models alongside LME models to account for sample size constraints and confirm that observed changes were meaningful (see Supplemental Table 1). Future tendon mechanics studies exploring hormonal influence should consider relaxin receptor expression as well as alternative hormone measurement methods, such as urine or blood assays, to improve reliability and facilitate relational exploration of specific hormone concentrations with tendon mechanics. To better capture the various nuances of hormonal fluctuations and inter- and intra-individual variability as well as enhance generalizability, future studies should also consider multi-site collaborations to increase participant numbers. Additionally, assessment of the entire muscle-tendon unit, including both proximal and distal regions, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how hormonal fluctuations influence tendon mechanics across the full length of the biceps brachii.

The findings of this study provide novel evidence that the menstrual cycle phase significantly influences the mechanical properties of a positional tendon in the upper limb. This study demonstrated that during menses, when hormone levels are lowest, tendon elongation in the toe-initial region is greater, and stiffness is reduced, compared to the luteal phase. These alterations may have functional implications for upper limb performance, particularly during the onset of force production. From an applied perspective, menstrual cycle–related fluctuations in upper-limb tendon compliance could influence training, rehabilitation, and injury prevention strategies in females. For instance, the increased stiffness observed during the luteal phase may support higher-load training. Additionally, recognizing these phase-dependent changes allows for more personalized conditioning programs and rehabilitation protocols, with the potential to optimize performance while supporting tendon health.

Conclusion

Overall, this initial study on hormones and an upper limb tendon suggests that menstrual cycle phase may influences tendon mechanics in females, particularly in positional tendons. The luteal phase, characterized by elevated estrogen and progesterone, was associated with reduced elongation in the toe-initial region, corresponded with greater tendon stiffness compared to the menses phase. This variation across phases suggests that hormonal fluctuations may alter early-phase tendon mechanics, and might contribute to functional implications for upper limb performance, especially during the onset of force production. These early findings emphasize the need for menstrual cycle phase to be systematically considered in future research involving females and tendon mechanics. This exploratory study also highlights the importance of evaluating the toe-initial region as well as the liner region of the force-elongation curve independently and in combination to gain a more complete understanding of tendon hysteresis.
