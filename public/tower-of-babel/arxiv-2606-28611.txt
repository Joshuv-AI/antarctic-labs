Structure of Cayley Codes
Vishnuram Arumugam, Cheryl E. Praeger, Daniel Rademacher

Abstract
. Cayley codes, introduced by Kaufman and Wigderson, are linear codes constructed from a Cayley graph and a smaller linear code. We explore general properties of the class of Cayley codes for finite groups. In particular we give a reduction to Cayley codes for connected Cayley graphs that maintains code properties such as rate, minimum distance and symmetry. Also, for a given Cayley code, we identify a family of symmetric Cayley codes, each associated with a normal edge-transitive Cayley graph, such that the given Cayley code embeds into the direct sum of the symmetric Cayley codes. We analyse several families of examples, in particular studying the behaviour of the Cayley code construction under forming direct products and cartesian products of Cayley graphs, and we pose a number of open questions. Key-words: Cayley graph, Cayley code, linear code, graph symmetry 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 05B05, 20B25, 05B25 Corresponding author: Cheryl E Praeger



1.
Introduction

In 2010, Kaufman and Wigderson introduced a new construction of linear codes, which they called Cayley codes, taking as input a Cayley graph and a smaller linear code (see
[
5
]
, or
[
6
, Definition 19]
for the journal version). They proved
[
6
, Theorem 7]
that, for suitable input graphs and codes, the Cayley code construction preserves certain bounds on the rate or relative distance, and desirable code properties such as being symmetric. They presented explicit families of Cayley codes based on Cayley graphs of elementary abelian
2
-groups
[
6
, Section 4]
, obtaining codes of length
n
which were ‘almost good’ in the sense that the relative distance was
O((\log\log n)^{-2})
, rather than being strictly bounded away from zero, see
[
6
, Theorem 11]
. Kaufman and Lubotzky
[
3
]
commented in 2012 that ‘
it seems that all the known explicit constructions of symmetric codes give codes which are not good
’, and provided, via the Cayley code construction, the first explicit infinite family of good binary symmetric codes based on the edge-transitive bipartite Ramanujan graphs – particular Cayley graphs for the projective linear groups
{\rm PGL}_{2}(q)
,
[
7
,
8
]
(see Notation
1.1
for definitions of technical terms such as ‘good’). Becker
[
1
]
explored the impact of the expansion and symmetry properties of Ramanujan graphs on the properties of these Cayley codes; while Kaufman and Oppenheim
[
4
]
developed a higher dimensional version.

The aim of this paper is a little different. We explore general properties of the class of Cayley codes for finite groups. We show how to reduce to Cayley codes for connected Cayley graphs while maintaining the same rate and minimum distance and symmetry propertties (Theorem
4.1
). We identify a family of symmetric Cayley codes related to an arbitrary given Cayley code (Theorem
1.3
). Normal edge-transitive Cayley graphs introduced in
[
9
]
play a critical role in the second reduction. Finally we give several families of examples, in particular exploring the behaviour of the Cayley code construction under the Cartesian product construction for graphs (Theorem
1.4
).

We give broad statements of the major results in Subsection
1.1
, and to facilitate these statements we introduce the relevant concepts in Notation
1.1
. The final Subsection
7.3
briefly discusses some open questions about the structure of finite Cayley codes.

Notation 1.1
.

A
linear
[n,k,d]
-code
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
is a
k
-dimensional subspace
C
of
\mathbb{F}^{X}
, where
|X|=n
, such that the minimum (Hamming) weight of the non-zero
codewords
(elements of
C
) is
d
. A family
\mathcal{F}
of such codes with length
n\to\infty
is called
good
if there exists
\epsilon>0
such that the
rate

r(C):=\frac{k}{n}
and the
relative distance

\delta(C):=\frac{d}{n}
are both at least
\epsilon
. The
automorphism group

{\rm Aut}(C)
of a linear code
C<\mathbb{F}^{X}
is the subgroup of the symmetric group
{\rm Sym}(X)
that leaves
C
invariant in its induced action on
\mathbb{F}^{X}
, and
C
is called
symmetric
if
{\rm Aut}(C)
is transitive on
X
. We regard
\mathbb{F}^{X}
as the
\mathbb{F}
-vector-space of functions
f:X\to\mathbb{F}
, so that in particular, for a subset
Y\subseteq X
, the restriction map
f\to f|_{Y}
defines a natural projection
\mathbb{F}^{X}\to\mathbb{F}^{Y}
.

For a group
G
and an inverse-closed subset
S\subseteq G\setminus\{1\}
, the
Cayley graph

\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)=(G,E)

is the graph with vertex-set
V\Gamma=G
, and edge-set
E\Gamma=E:=\{\{g,sg\}\mid g\in G,s\in S\}
. For
g\in G
, the edge-subset

(1)

E_{g}:=\{\{g,sg\}\mid s\in S\}\quad\text{is the set of edges incident with $g$,}

the map

(2)

\phi_{g}:S\to E_{g}\quad\text{given by}\quad\phi_{g}:s\to\{g,sg\}\quad\text{for $s\in S$}

is a bijection, and we obtain a linear projection
\phi:\mathbb{F}^{E}\to\mathbb{F}^{S}
, where
\phi:f\to\phi_{g}\circ f
.
The
Cayley code

\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
corresponding to a given Cayley graph
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
and linear code
B\leq\mathbb{F}^{S}
, is

(3)

\mathbf{C}(G,S,B):=\{f\in\mathbb{F}^{E}\mid\forall\ g\in G,\ \phi_{g}\circ f\in B\}.

That
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
is a linear code in
\mathbb{F}^{E}
follows from the fact that
B
is a linear code in
\mathbb{F}^{S}
. The automorphism group
{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{C}(G,S,B))
contains the group
G\rtimes A(G,S,B)
, where

(4)

A(G,S,B)=\{\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(G)\mid S^{\sigma}=S,\sigma^{S}\in{\rm Aut}(B)\}

and in particular, if
G\rtimes A(G,S,B)
is transitive on the edge-set
E
then
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is normal edge-transitive and also
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
is symmetric (Proposition
3.1
- this strengthens a result
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
, see Remark
3.2
).

1.1.
The main results

A Cayley graph
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is disconnected if and only if the subgroup
H:=\langle S\rangle
is a proper subgroup of
G
and the connected component of
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
containing the identity is
{\rm Cay}(H,S)
(Lemma
2.2
). Thus we can form both Cayley codes
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
and
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
, for appropriate
B
, and our first result shows how they are related.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
S
a non-empty inverse-closed subset of
G\setminus\{1\}
such that
H:=\langle S\rangle\neq G
. Then for each linear code
B\leq\mathbb{F}^{S}
,

(a)

the Cayley code
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
decomposes as a direct sum of
|G:H|
linear codes, each isomorphic to
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
.

(b)

Moreover,
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
and
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
have the same rate and minimum distance, and
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
is symmetric if and only if
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
is symmetric.

This result allows us to restrict to connected Cayley graphs if we wish. It follows immediately from a more technical version Theorem
4.1
which is proved in Section
4
.

We next show that each Cayley code determines a family of symmetric Cayley codes. For
G,S,B,A(G,S,B)
as in Notation
1.1
,
the group
A(G,S,B)
leaves invariant a unique finest partition
S=\cup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}S_{i}
such that each
S_{i}
is inverse-closed and
A(G,S,B)
-invariant, and we have
B\leq\mathbb{F}^{S}=\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbb{F}^{S_{i}}
. For each
i
, let
B_{i}
be the restriction of
B
to
\mathbb{F}^{S_{i}}
. We show that each of the corresponding Cayley codes
\mathbf{C}(G,S_{i},B_{i})
is symmetric.

Theorem 1.3
.

With the notation above, for each
i\in\mathbf{I}
, the Cayley graph
{\rm Cay}(G,S_{i})
is normal edge-transitive and the Cayley code
\mathbf{C}(G,S_{i},B_{i})
is symmetric. Further, if
B=\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i}
, then

\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)=\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{C}(G,S_{i},B_{i})
.

This result is proved in Section
5
. Finally we look at various examples of Cayley codes. In Section
6
we consider Cayley codes for several standard choices of the input code
B
, and for the family of Cayley graphs which are cycles. In the final Section
7
we show how the Cayley code construction behaves with respect to forming direct products and Cartesian products of Cayley graphs (see Definition
7.1
). The Cartesian product of Cayley graphs
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
and
{\rm Cay}(H,T)
is
{\rm Cay}(G\times H,S\dot{\cup}T)
(where we identify
S,T
with the subsets
S\times 1
,
1\times T
of
G\times H
), and the Cayley code for the Cartesian product is described in Theorem
1.4
in terms of the Cayley codes for the Cartesian factors. The length, rate and distance of this Cayley code are described in terms of the parameters for the Cartesian factors in Remark
7.3
. The direct product of
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
and
{\rm Cay}(H,T)
is
{\rm Cay}(G\times H,S\times T)
, and our result for Cayley codes for the corresponding Cayley code is not so precise: we show (Theorem
1.4
) that a subcode projects onto a tensor product of the Cayley codes for the factors. Theorem
1.4
follows from Propositions
7.2
and
7.5
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G,H
be finite groups with inverse-closed generating sets
S,T
, respectively, let
\mathbb{F}
be a finite field, and let
A\leq\mathbb{F}^{S},B\leq\mathbb{F}^{T}
be linear codes. Then

\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\dot{\cup}T,A\oplus B)=(\mathbf{C}(G,S,A)\otimes\mathbb{F}^{|H|})\oplus(\mathbb{F}^{|G|}\otimes\mathbf{C}(H,T,B))
,

and a certain sub-code of
\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\times T,A\otimes B)
projects onto

\mathbf{C}(G,S,A)\otimes\mathbf{C}(H,T,B).

The paper is structured as follows.
Section

In Section

2.
Spaces, graphs and codes

In this section we introduce the notation and basic properties of the codes and graphs we will work with, especially Cayley graphs.

2.1.
Vector spaces

As mentioned in the introduction, a linear code over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
is a subspace of a finite vector space
\mathbb{F}^{X}
of dimension
n=|X|
. To avoid confusion later, it is helpful to view
\mathbb{F}^{X}
as the set of all functions
f:X\to\mathbb{F}
with pointwise addition and multiplication, that is,

af+a^{\prime}f^{\prime}:x\to a(xf)+a^{\prime}(xf^{\prime})\quad\text{for $f,f^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{X},a,a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}$.}

For a subset
Y\subseteq X
and
f\in\mathbb{F}^{X}
, we denote by
f|_{Y}
the restriction of
f
to
Y
, so
f|_{Y}\in\mathbb{F}^{Y}
. Each expression of
X
as a disjoint union
X=\bigcup_{k\in K}X_{k}
, for some index set
K
, corresponds to a direct sum decomposition
\mathbb{F}^{X}=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathbb{F}^{X_{k}}
such that
f\in\mathbb{F}^{X}
corresponds to the tuple
f=(f_{k})_{k\in K}
with
f_{k}=f|_{X_{k}}
for
k\in K
. If
\rho\in{\rm Sym}(X)
preserves the decomposition
X=\bigcup_{k\in K}X_{k}
, that is to say, if
\rho
permutes the
X_{k}
among themselves, then
\rho
induces a permutation of
K
and we may write
(X_{k})^{\rho}=X_{k\rho}
for each
k\in K
. Then for
f=(f_{k})_{k\in K}
, the restriction
(\rho\circ f)|_{X_{k}}
first maps
X_{k}\to X_{k\rho}
under
\rho
and then
f
induces the restriction
f|_{X_{k\rho}}
on
X_{k\rho}
. Thus

(5)

(\rho\circ f)_{k}=(\rho\circ f)|_{X_{k}}=\rho\circ(f|_{X_{k\rho}})=\rho\circ f_{k\rho},\quad\mbox{so $\rho\circ f=(\rho\circ f_{k\rho})_{k\in K}$.}

We summarise this discussion in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

For a field
\mathbb{F}
, a set
X
, and a disjoint union
X=\bigcup_{k\in K}X_{k}
, for some index set
K
, the following hold.

(a)

\mathbb{F}^{X}=\bigoplus_{k\in K}\mathbb{F}^{X_{k}}
such that each
f=(f_{k})_{k\in K}\in\mathbb{F}^{X}
with
f_{k}=f|_{X_{k}}
, the restriction to
X_{k}
, for
k\in K
;

(b)

if
\rho\in{\rm Sym}(X)
preserves the decomposition
X=\bigcup_{k\in K}X_{k}
, then for
f=(f_{k})_{k\in K}\in\mathbb{F}^{X}
we have
\rho\circ f=(\rho\circ f_{k\rho})_{k\in K}
.

Cayley graphs

Recall from Notation
1.1
the notion of a Cayley graph
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)
for

It follows from the definition of

that the connected component of
\Gamma
containing the identity element

has vertex set
H=\langle S\rangle
and edge set

(6)

E^{\circ}:=\{\{h,sh\}\mid h\in H,s\in S\}=\cup_{h\in H}E_{1}h,\ \mbox{with $E_{1}$ as in \eqref{e:eg}}

and hence is equal to

For an arbitrary element

, the connected component of
\Gamma
containing the

has vertex set equal to the coset

, and edge set

(7)

\{\{ht,sht\}\mid h\in H,s\in S\}=E^{\circ}t

and so is the graph
(Ht,E^{\circ}t)
. The action of

by right multiplication on

and on pairs(

) induces an isomorphism from

to
(Ht,E^{\circ}t)
. We summarise this discussion in the following statement.

Lemma 2.2
.

, and let

. Then

(a)

the Cayley graph

has exactly

(Ht_{i},E^{\circ}t_{i})
(the connected component containing

) for

.

(b)

Moreover
(H,E^{\circ})={\rm Cay}(H,S)
, and for each

, and

(c)

2.4.
Automorphisms of Cayley graphs

Each Cayley graph
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)
admits as a subgroup of automorphisms the group

(8)

\mbox{$A(\Gamma):=G\rtimes A(G,S)$, where $A(G,S)=\{\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(G)\mid S^{\sigma}=S\}$},

where for all
g,x\in G
and
\sigma\in A(G,S)
,

x:g\to gx\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma:g\to g^{\sigma}
.
It is straightforward to check that each of these maps preserves
E\Gamma
, and hence defines a graph automorphism, so
A(\Gamma)\leq{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
. In particular, the group
G
acts by right multiplication as a
regular permutation group
on
V\Gamma
(transitive with trivial vertex-stabilisers), so
\Gamma
is always vertex-transitive (with apologies for the dual use of the term ‘regular’ from graph theory and permutation group theory).

The subgroup
A(\Gamma)
is the normaliser of
G
in
{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
; it is the largest subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
which preserves the structure of
\Gamma
as a Cayley graph of
G
. Although
A(\Gamma)
is vertex-transitive, it is not always edge-transitive on
\Gamma
. In fact
A(\Gamma)
is transitive on
E\Gamma
if and only if either
A(G,S)
is transitive on
S
, or
S
is the disjoint union
S_{0}\,\dot{\cup}\,S_{0}^{-1}
such that
S_{0}
(and hence also
S_{0}^{-1}
) is an
A(G,S)
-orbit (see
[
9
, Proposition 1(b)]
). Cayley graphs with this property were called
normal edge-transitive
in
[
9
]
. Thus
\Gamma
is normal edge-transitive if and only if
A(\Gamma)
is edge-transitive. Note that, if
S
generates
G
, then
A(G,S)
acts faithfully on
S
, and so can be identified with a subgroup of
{\rm Sym}(S)
.

In our application to Cayley codes we will need to work with some subgroup of
A(G,S)
and we now set up the general context for this. We use the following notation: for each
g\in G
and subgroup
L\leq A(G,S)
we denote by
g^{L}=\{g^{\sigma}\mid\sigma\in L\}
the
L
-orbit in
G
containing
g
.

Notation 2.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
S
an inverse-closed subset of
G\setminus\{1\}
, and let
1\leq L\leq A(G,S)
with
A(G,S)
as in (
8
). Let
S=\cup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}S_{i}
be the partition of
S
such that, for each
i
, and
s\in S_{i}
, we have
S_{i}=s^{L}\cup(s^{-1})^{L}
. That is to say, the partition
\cup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}S_{i}
is the (unique) finest partition of
S
such that each part
S_{i}
is both
L
-invariant and inverse-closed.

For example, if the group
L=1
in Notation
2.3
, then each
S_{i}
has the form
\{s,s^{-1}\}
with size
1
or
2
, and each connected component of the Cayley subgraph
{\rm Cay}(G,\{s,s^{-1}\})
is a cycle of length

. Similarly, as noted above, if

is such that

is edge-transitive on
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
, then the partition consists of a single part and in particular
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is normal edge-transitive.

The partition of
S
in Notation
2.3
corresponds to an edge-disjoint decomposition of the Cayley graph

into normal edge-transitive Cayley graphs for

.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G,S,L
and the partition
S=\cup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}S_{i}
be as in Notation
2.3
, and let
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)=(G,E)
. Then the following hold.

(a)

\Gamma
is an edge-disjoint union of Cayley subgraphs
\Gamma_{i}={\rm Cay}(G,S_{i})
, for
i\in\mathbf{I}
, and the group
G\rtimes L\leq\cap_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\,{\rm Aut}(\Gamma_{i})
;

(b)

for each
i\in\mathbf{I}
, the edge-set

and is a
(G\rtimes L)
-orbit, so
\Gamma_{i}
is a normal edge-transitive Cayley graph.

Proof.

(a) The first assertion follows from the definition of a Cayley graph, and the fact that, for each
i\in\mathbf{I}
,

G\rtimes L\leq{\rm Aut}(\Gamma_{i})
follows from (
8
) and the discussion above.

(b) Again the form of
E\Gamma_{i}
comes from the definition of
{\rm Cay}(G,S_{i})
, and the discussion above shows that
E\Gamma_{i}
is a
(G\rtimes L)
-orbit, so
\Gamma_{i}
is normal edge-transitive.

3.
Basic properties of Cayley codes

Recall from Notation
1.1
that the ingredients for constructing a Cayley code are a Cayley graph
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)=(G,E)
and a linear code
B\leq\mathbb{F}^{S}
, where
S
is a non-empty inverse-closed subset of
G\setminus\{1\}
. The corresponding Cayley code
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
, given by (
3
), is contained in
\mathbb{F}^{E}
. Recall that
{\rm Aut}(B)\leq{\rm Sym}(S)
(in its induced action on
\mathbb{F}^{S}
), and for
\sigma\in A(G,S)
(as in (
8
)) we denote by
\sigma^{S}
the permutation induced by
\sigma
on
S
. Recall also the subgroup
A(G,S,B)
of
A(G,S)
from (
4
).

We verify several simple properties of Cayley codes, some of which can be found in
[
3
]
or
[
6
]
, while one strengthens a result in these papers (see Remark
3.2
).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G,S,B,E,\mathbb{F},\Gamma
be as above. Then

(a)

\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
is a linear code in
\mathbb{F}^{E}
of length
|E|=|G|\cdot|S|/2
and rate at least
2r(B)-1
, where
r(B)
is the rate of
B
.

(b)

If
0\leq B_{1}\leq B_{2}\leq\mathbb{F}^{S}
, then
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B_{1})\leq\mathbf{C}(G,S,B_{2})
, and in particular,
\mathbf{C}(G,S,0)=0
, and

4.
Disconnected Cayley codes

Recall the description in Lemma
2.2
of the connected components of a disconnected Cayley graph
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)=(G,E)
. The edge set
E
is a disjoint union of the edges sets of the connected components, and this gives a
G
-invariant partition of
E
, and hence of
\mathbb{F}^{E}
(Lemma
2.1
). We now show in Theorem
4.1
that these decompositions lead to corresponding decompositions of Cayley codes. Theorem
1.2
follows immediately from this result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
S
an inverse-closed subset of
G\setminus\{1\}
such that
H:=\langle S\rangle\neq G
, and let
\mathcal{T}
be a set of coset representatives for
H
in
G
such that
1\in\mathcal{T}
. Let
B\leq\mathbb{F}^{S}
be a linear code. Then, with
E^{\circ}
as in (
6
) and
\phi_{g}
as in (
2
) and, for each
t\in\mathcal{T}
, setting

(9)

\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B):=\{f\in\mathbb{F}^{E^{\circ}t}\mid\,\forall\ g\in Ht,\ \phi_{g}\circ f\in B\},\quad

(a)

\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
is the Cayley code corresponding to
{\rm Cay}(H,S)
and
B
;

(b)

for each
t\in\mathcal{T}
,
\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)
is a linear code and, for the right multiplication action
t^{E^{\circ}}:E^{\circ}\to E^{\circ}t
, the map
f\to t^{E^{\circ}}\circ f
defines an isomorphism
\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)\to\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
;

(c)

the Cayley code
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)=\bigoplus_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)
, a direct sum of
|\mathcal{T}|
copies of
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
;

(d)

\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
has the same rate and mminimum distance as
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
, while its relative distance is
|\mathcal{T}|
times greater than that of
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
. Also
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
is symmetric if and only if
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
is symmetric.

Proof.

(a) First we note that part (a) follows from the definition of a Cayley code in (
3
).

(b) We define a vector space isomorphism
\mathbb{F}^{E^{\circ}t}\to\mathbb{F}^{E^{\circ}}
by
f\to t^{E^{\circ}}\circ f
, for each
f\in\mathbb{F}^{E^{\circ}t}
. To see that this map restricts to an isomorphism

\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)\to\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
we consider
f\in\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{E^{\circ}t}
. By (
2
) and (
9
), this is equivalent to the condition that, for all
ht\in Ht
,
B
contains

\phi_{ht}\circ f=(\phi_{h}\circ t^{E^{\circ}})\circ f=\phi_{h}\circ(t^{E^{\circ}}\circ f)

and hence
t^{E^{\circ}}\circ f\in\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
by (
9
). Then since
t^{E^{\circ}}\circ f
runs over all elements of
\mathbb{F}^{E^{\circ}}
as
f
runs over all elements of
\mathbb{F}^{E^{\circ}t}
it follows that the map
f\to t^{E}\circ f
takes
\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)
into
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
. A similar argument shows that
t^{E\circ}\circ f\in\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
implies that
f\in\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)
, so
\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)
is isomorphic to the Cayley code
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
, proving part (b).

(c) By Lemma
2.2
, the
u:=|\mathcal{T}|
connected components of
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)
are the graphs
(Ht,E^{\circ}t)
, for
t\in\mathcal{T}
, with
E^{\circ},E^{\circ}t
as in (
6
) and (
7
), and each
(Ht,E^{\circ}t)
is isomorphic to the connected component
{\rm Cay}(H,S)=(H,E^{\circ})
containing the identity
1\in\mathcal{T}
, via the maps
h\to ht,
and
\{h,sh\}\to\{ht,sht\}
on vertices and edges.
The edge set
E
is therefore
the disjoint union
E=\cup_{t\in\mathcal{T}}E^{\circ}t
, and this decomposition is in particular
G
-invariant. Thus, by Lemma
2.1
, the vector space
\mathbb{F}^{E}
decomposes as
\mathbb{F}^{E}=\bigoplus_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{F}^{E^{\circ}t}
and, for
f=(f_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{F}^{X}
and
g\in G
, the
t^{th}
-component of
g^{E}\circ f
is
f_{t^{\prime}}
where
Ht^{\prime}=Htg
. More precisely, for a fixed
t\in\mathcal{T}
and any
h\in H
, the map
\phi_{ht}=\phi_{h}\circ t^{E}
maps
s\to\{ht,sht\}
(an edge of
E^{\circ}t
), for
s\in S
. Moreover, for
f\in\mathbb{F}^{E}
, we have
\phi_{ht}\circ f=\phi_{ht}\circ(f|_{E^{\circ}t})
, so if
f\in\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
then
\phi_{ht}\circ f\in B
for all
h\in H,t\in\mathcal{T}
, and hence
f|_{E^{\circ}t}\in\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)
for each
t\in\mathcal{T}
. It follows that
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)\leq\bigoplus_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)
.

To prove that equality holds, fix
t\in\mathcal{T}
and let
f\in\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)
. Define
\widehat{f}\in\mathbb{F}^{E}
by

(e)\widehat{f}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(e)f&\text{if}\ e\in E^{\circ}t\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then
\widehat{f}\in\mathbb{F}^{E}
. For
ht\in Ht
we have shown above that
\phi_{ht}\circ\widehat{f}=\phi_{ht}\circ(\widehat{f}|_{E^{\circ}t})=\phi_{ht}\circ f
which, by (
9
), lies in
B
. For all other elements
ht^{\prime}\in Ht^{\prime}
with
t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\{t\}
,
\phi_{ht^{\prime}}\circ\widehat{f}=\phi_{ht^{\prime}}\circ(\widehat{f}|_{E^{\circ}t^{\prime}})=0\in B
. Thus
\widehat{f}\in\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
and the
t^{th}
component of
\widehat{f}
is
f
. We conclude that
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)=\bigoplus_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{C}(Ht,S,B)\cong\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)^{u}
, and part (c) is proved.

(d) It follows from Proposition
3.1
(a) and part (c) that the rates
r_{G}
,
r_{H}
of
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
and
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
satisfy

r_{G}=\frac{2\cdot\dim(\mathbf{C}(G,S,B))}{|G|\cdot|S|}=\frac{2\cdot u\cdot\dim(\mathbf{C}(H,S,B))}{u\cdot|H|\cdot|S|}=r_{H}

so
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
and
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
have the same rate. Further it follows from part (c) that a non-zero codeword in
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
of minimum weight must lie in one of the direct summands and by part (b),
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
must contain such a codeword, say of weight
d
. Hence
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
and
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
have the same minimum distance
d
, while the relative distances of
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
and
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
are, by Proposition
3.1
(a),
\frac{2d}{|H|\cdot|S|}
and
\frac{2d}{|G|\cdot|S|}
respectively. Finally, it follows from Proposition
3.1
(c) that
G
acts transitively on the direct summands of
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
in part (c), and hence
\mathbf{C}(H,S,B)
is symmetric if and only if
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
is symmetric.
∎

As a consequence of Theorem
4.1
, when studying Cayley codes we usually assume that the Cayley graph is connected.

5.
Different decompositions of Cayley codes

In this section we discuss a process to identify a family of symmetric Cayley codes related to a given Cayley code, which allows us to embed the given Cayley code into a direct sum of symmetric Cayley codes (using Proposition
3.1
(b)). However the given Cayley code itself only admits a decomposition if the input code
B
admits a corresponding decomposition. The process discussed in this section does not necessarily preserve Cayley graph connectivity, so one should perform this decomposition to symmetric Cayley codes first, if available, and then reduce to the connected case using Section
4
.

We will eventually use the natural decomposition of the Cayley graph
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
given in Notation
2.3
and Lemma
2.4
corresponding to the
(G\rtimes L)
-orbits on edges of the associated Cayley graph, but first we consider a more general decomposition.

Hypotheses 5.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group, let
S
a non-empty inverse-closed subset of
G\setminus\{1\}
, let
\mathbb{F}
be a finite field, and let
B\leq\mathbb{F}^{S}
, a linear code.

(a)

Suppose that
S
is the disjoint union
S=\cup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}S_{i}
, where each
S_{i}=S_{i}^{-1}
is inverse-closed.
Then
\mathbb{F}^{S}=\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbb{F}^{S_{i}}
, and for each
i\in\mathbf{I}
, if
B_{i}
is the restriction of
B
to
\mathbb{F}^{S_{i}}
, then
B
is subdirect in
\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i}\leq\mathbb{F}^{S}
(projects onto each direct summand).

(b)

Let
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)=(G,E)
and let
\Gamma_{i}={\rm Cay}(G,S_{i})
, for
i\in\mathbf{I}
, so
E\Gamma=E
is the disjoint union
\cup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}E\Gamma_{i}
and
\mathbb{F}^{E}=\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbb{F}^{E\Gamma_{i}}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Suppose that Hypothesis
5.1
holds.
Then the Cayley code
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)\leq\mathbf{C}(G,S,\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i})
and
\mathbf{C}(G,S,\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i})=\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{C}(G,S_{i},B_{i})
holds.

Remark 5.3
.

Certainly
G,S,B
and the disjoint union
S=\cup_{i\in\mathbf{I}}S_{i}
determine the
B_{i}
and hence the direct decomposition
\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{C}(G,S_{i},B_{i})
. However it is not clear to us whether the inclusion
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)\leq\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{C}(G,S_{i},B_{i})
is necessarily subdirect in general.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
(b),
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)\leq\mathbf{C}(G,S,\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i})
. Next we verify the direct decomposition. Let
f\in\mathbf{C}(G,S,\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i})
. Then
f\in\mathbb{F}^{E}=\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbb{F}^{E\Gamma_{i}}
, so
f=\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}f_{i}
where
f_{i}=f|_{E\Gamma_{i}}
. The defining condition for membership of
\mathbf{C}(G,S,\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i})
is that, for all
g\in G
,
\phi_{g}\circ f\in\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i}
with
\phi_{g}
as in (
2
). For each
i
, the restriction
\phi_{i,g}:=\phi_{g}|_{S_{i}}
has image

(S_{i})\phi_{i,g}=(S_{i})\phi_{g}=\{\{g,sg\}\mid s\in S_{i}\}\subseteq E\Gamma_{i}
, and hence

f_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{E\Gamma_{i}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\phi_{i,g}\circ f_{i}=(\phi_{g}\circ f)|_{S_{i}}\in\mathbb{F}^{S_{i}}.

Moreover
\phi_{g}\circ f\in\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i}
, and hence
\phi_{i,g}\circ f_{i}=(\phi_{g}\circ f)|_{S_{i}}\in B_{i}
. Since this holds for all
g\in G
, we have
f_{i}\in\mathbf{C}(G,S_{i},B_{i})
, and since this holds for all

, it follows that
\mathbf{C}(G,S,\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i})\leq\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{C}(G,S_{i},B_{i})
. On the other hand, for each
i
and each

\widehat{f_{i}}:E\to\mathbb{F}
be the function such that the restriction of
\widehat{f_{i}}
to

is the constant zero function if

if

we have

\phi_{g}\circ\widehat{f_{i}}\in B_{i}\leq\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i}

\widehat{f_{i}}\in\mathbf{C}(G,S,\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i})

Thus we have proved that
\mathbf{C}(G,S,\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}B_{i})=\oplus_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{C}(G,S_{i},B_{i})
holds.
∎

We now deduce Theorem
1.3
from this general result.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.
Let
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)
with

a non-empty inverse closed subgroup of

, let

, and
let

determine the partition

6.
Some examples of Cayley codes

In this section we present several families of Cayley codes, where we specify certain possibilities for the local input code
B
(Subsection
6.1
) or the Cayley graph
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
(Subsection
6.2
).

6.1.
B
the repetition code or augmentation code

For a finite set
S
, the
repetition code

R(S)<\mathbb{F}^{S}
consists of all constant functions
c\in\mathbb{F}
, for
c\in\mathbb{F}
, where
f_{c}:x\to c
for
x\in S
. Also the
augmentation code

A(S)<\mathbb{F}^{S}
is the co-dimension
1
subspace
A(S):=\{f\in\mathbb{F}^{S}\mid\sum_{x\in S}(x)f=0\}
. Both
R(S)
and
A(S)
are invariant under the full symmetric group
{\rm Sym}(S)
, and hence may be chosen as the local input code
B
for any Cayley graph.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
{\rm Cay}(G,S)=(G,E)
,
\mathbb{F},B
be as in Notation
1.1
, and let
R(S),A(S)
be as above. Then

(a)

If
B=R(S)<\mathbb{F}^{S}
and
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is connected, then
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
is the repetition code
R(E)<\mathbb{F}^{E}
.

(b)

If
B=A(S)
and
\mathbb{F}
has odd characteristic, then
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)\leq A(E)
, the augmentation code in
\mathbb{F}^{E}
.

Proof.

(a) Suppose that
B=R(S)
. Then it follows from (
3
) that
R(E)\leq\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
. Conversely let

and

. Then
e_{0}=\{1,s\}\in E
, and we let
c=(e_{0})f
, and
e\in E
be an arbitrary edge with
e\neq e_{0}
. We will show that
(e)f=c
, and from this it follows that
f=f_{c}\in R(E)
, proving that
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)=R(E)
. Since
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is connected there is a path (an edge-sequence)
e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{t}
in
{\rm Cay}(G,S)
such that
e_{t}=e
and consecutive edges are incident. Choose a path with
t
minimal, so
t\geq 1
since
e\neq e_{0}
. Also, for each
i<t
, the edges
e_{i}
and
e_{i+1}
are distinct by the minimality of
t
and we let
\{g_{i}\}=e_{i}\cap e_{i+1}
. We will prove by induction on
i\leq t
that
(e_{i})f=(e_{i-1})f=\dots=(e_{0})f=c
. If
i=1
then both
e_{0}
and
e_{1}
are incident with
g_{0}
and by (
3
),
\phi_{g_{0}}\circ f\in B=R(S)
, so
f
is constant on the edges incident with
g_{0}
. Thus
(e_{1})f=(e_{0})f=c
, and we are finished if
t=1
. Now assume that
t>1
, that
1<i\leq t
, and assume inductively that the claim holds for
i-1
, that is to say,
(e_{i-1})f=\dots=(e_{0})f=c
. Now
\{g_{i-1}\}=e_{i-1}\cap e_{i}
and, again by (
3
),
\phi_{g_{i-1}}\circ f\in B=R(S)
, so
f
is constant on the edges incident with
g_{i-1}
. Thus
(e_{i})f=(e_{i-1})f=c
, and by induction the claim holds for all
i\leq t
. Thus
(e_{t})f=c
, and as discussed above, part (a) follows.

(b) Let
f\in\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
. Then, for all
g\in G
,
\phi_{g}\circ f\in A(S)
. This implies that
\sum_{e\in E_{g}}(e)f=0
, where
E_{g}
is as in (
1
). Now as
g
ranges over
G
, the sets
E_{g}
cover each edge exactly twice, since an edge
\{x,y\}
lies precisely in the edge sets
E_{x}
and
E_{y}
. Thus

0=\sum_{g\in G}\left(\sum_{e\in E_{g}}(e)f\right)=2\sum_{g\in G}(e)f

and since
\mathbb{F}
has odd characteristic, this implies that
\sum_{g\in G}(e)f=0
, so
f\in A(E)
.
∎

The Cayley code in part (b) above can be significantly smaller than the augmentation code. We give a family of examples in Subsection
6.2
for which this is the case with the Cayley graph being a cycle of arbitrary length.

6.2.
Cayley codes for cycles

For
n\geq 3
, the cycle
\Gamma=\mathbf{C}_{n}
of length
n
has vertex set
V\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{n}=\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
, which we often view as the additive group of integers modulo
n
, and edge-set
E=E\Gamma=\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1}\}
, where
e_{i}=\{i-1,i\}
for each
i
.
It is a Cayley graph for the additive group
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
relative to the inverse-closed generating set
S=\{-1,1\}
. Thus
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)
.
Note that, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, the map
\phi_{s}
from (
2
) is
\phi_{i}:s\mapsto\{i,i+s\}
where
s\in\{-1,1\}
.

To construct a
Cayley code for
\Gamma
, we require a linear code
B\leq\mathbb{F}^{S}\cong\mathbb{F}^{2}
. By Proposition
3.1
(b),
\mathbf{C}(G,S,B)
is the zero code if
B=0
and is the complete code
\mathbb{F}^{E}
if
B=\mathbb{F}^{S}
, so our interest is in the remaining cases where
B
is a one-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{S}
. For
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
there are exactly
q+1
such subspaces, and we label them as

B=B_{a}=\langle b_{a}\rangle\ \textbf{for $a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\cup\{\infty\}$, where}\ b_{\infty}:-1\to 0\ \textbf{and }\ 1\to 1,

and for
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
b_{a}:-1\to 1
and
1\to a
. The corresponding Cayley codes are

(10)

\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}:=\mathbf{C}(G,S,B_{a})=\{f\in\mathbb{F}^{E}\,|\,\phi_{g}\circ f\in B_{a}\textrm{ for all }g\in G\}.

We have the following possibilities for these Cayley codes.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\Gamma,n,G,B_{a},\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}
be as above. Then

(a)

if either
a=\infty
or
a^{n}\neq 1
, then
\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}=0
;

(b)

if
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
a^{n}=1
, then
\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}=\langle f_{a}\rangle
where
(e_{i})f_{a}=a^{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

Proof.

By the definition of a Cayley code in (
3
), an element
f\in\mathbb{F}^{E}
lies in
\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}
if and only if
\phi_{i}\circ f\in B_{a}
for all
i\in G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
where
\phi_{i}:s\to\{i,i+s\}
(for
s\in S=\{-1,1\}
) as in (
2
).

Suppose first that
a\not\in\{\infty,0\}
. Then the only element
b\in B_{a}
such that the image
(S)b
contains
0
is the zero element
0\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
. Suppose first that
\phi_{0}\circ f
is the zero element
0\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
, that is to say,
(e_{0})f=(e_{1})f=0
. Then
(S)(\phi_{1}\circ f)
contains
(e_{1})f=0
and hence
\phi_{1}\circ f
lies in
B_{a}
if and only if
\phi_{1}\circ f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
. An inductive argument using the same reasoning yields that
f\in\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}
if and only if
\phi_{i}\circ f=0
for all
i
, and hence
f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{E}
. Now suppose that
\phi_{0}\circ f
is a non-zero element of
\mathbb{F}^{S}
. A necessary condition for
f
to lie in
\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}
is that
\phi_{0}\circ f\in B_{a}
, and this holds if any only if
\phi_{0}\circ f
is a scalar multiple of the element
b_{a}
defined above, and without loss of generality we assume that
\phi_{0}\circ f=b_{a}
. Note that
\phi_{0}\circ f=b_{a}
is equivalent to
(e_{0})f=1,(e_{1})f=a
. In particular
(-1)(\phi_{1}\circ f)=(e_{1})f=a
, and hence
\phi_{1}\circ f\in B_{a}
if and only if
\phi_{1}\circ f=a\,b_{a}
(equivalently
(1)(\phi_{1}\circ f)=(e_{2})f=a^{2}
). This time the same inductive argument shows that, for all
i\in G
,
\phi_{i}\circ f\in B_{a}
if and only if
\phi_{i}\circ f=a^{i}b_{a}
(equivalently
(e_{i})f=a^{i},(e_{i+1})f=a^{i+1}
). In order for this condition to hold for
i=n-1
we require
(e_{0})f=(e_{(n-1)+1})f=a^{(n-1)+1}=a^{n}
, but we already have
(e_{0})f=1
. Thus, if
a^{n}\neq 1
then the code
\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}=0
, while if
a^{n}=1
then
\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}=\langle f_{a}\rangle
with
f_{a}
as in part (b).

It remains to consider the cases
a\in\{\infty,0\}
. The arguments for these cases are similar. Suppose first that
f\in\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}
and that
\phi_{0}\circ f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
, that is,
(e_{0})f=(e_{1})f=0
. If
a=1
then the only element
b\in B_{a}
with
(-1)b=0
is the zero element of
\mathbb{F}^{S}
, and the argument in the previous paragraph shows that
\phi_{i}\circ f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
for all
i
so that
f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{E}
. Similarly if
a=\infty
then the only element
b\in B_{a}
with
(1)b=0
is
0\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
, and again we find that
\phi_{i}\circ f=0
for all
i
and
f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{E}
. Now assume that
f\in\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}
and that
\phi_{0}\circ f
is a non-zero element of
B_{a}
(a necessary condition for
f\in\mathbf{Cyc}_{a}
), so
\phi_{0}\circ f
is a scalar multiple of
b_{a}
, and without loss of generality we assume that
\phi_{0}\circ f=b_{a}
. Note that
\phi_{0}\circ f=b_{a}
is equivalent to
(e_{0})f=1,(e_{1})f=0
if
a=0
, and to
(e_{0})f=0,(e_{1})f=1
if
a=\infty
. If
a=0
then
(-1)(\phi_{1}\circ f)=(e_{1})f=0
, and as the only element
b\in B_{a}
with
(-1)b=0
is the zero element, we have by (
10
) that
\phi_{1}\circ f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
and, in turn, the usual inductive argument shows that
f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{E}
, which is a contradiction. Thus
\mathbf{Cyc}_{0}=0
. Finally suppose that
a=\infty
. Then the only element
b\in B_{\infty}
with
(1)b=0
is the zero element, and since
(1)\phi_{n-1}\circ f=(e_{0})f=0
, it follows from (
10
) that
\phi_{n-1}\circ f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
. The usual inductive reasoning shows that
\phi_{n-i}\circ f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
for all
i
, and hence that
f=0\in\mathbb{F}^{E}
, whence
\mathbf{Cyc}_{\infty}=0
, completing the proof.
∎

7.
Products of graphs and Cayley codes

Several product constructions for graphs take the vertex set to be the Cartesian product of the vertex sets of two input graphs. We consider two such constructions defined as follows, and then discuss implications for Cayley codes under these graph constructions.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\Gamma=(V\Gamma,E\Gamma)
and
\Sigma=(V\Sigma,E\Sigma)
be graphs. For each of the product constructions below the vertex set is
V\Gamma\times V\Sigma
.

(a)

The
(direct) product

\Gamma\times\Sigma
has edge set (see
[
2
, Section 6.3]
):

E(\Gamma\times\Sigma)=\{\{(\alpha,\sigma),(\alpha^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\}\mid\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}\in E\Gamma\ \mbox{and}\ \{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\}\in E\Sigma\}.

(b)

The
Cartesian product

\Gamma\,\square\,\Sigma
has edge set

[
2
, Section 7.14]
:

E(\Gamma\,\square\,\Sigma)=\{\{(\alpha,\sigma),(\alpha^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\}\mid\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}\ \mbox{with}\ \{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\}\in E\Sigma,\\
\mbox{or}\ \{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}\in E\Gamma\ \mbox{with}\ \sigma=\sigma^{\prime}\end{array}\}.

Let
\Delta
be
\Gamma\,\times\,\Sigma
or
\Gamma\,\square\,\Sigma
as in Definition
7.1
. Then
{\rm Aut}(\Delta)
contains
{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)\times{\rm Aut}(\Sigma)
in its natural product action, that is,

(g,h):(\alpha,\sigma)\to(\alpha^{g},\sigma^{h})
for
(g,h)\in{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)\times{\rm Aut}(\Sigma)
and
(\alpha,\sigma)\in V\Delta
. In particular, if
G\leq{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
and
H\leq{\rm Aut}(\Sigma)
with
G,H
regular on
V\Gamma,V\Sigma
, respectively, then
G\times H
is regular on
V\Delta
, and consequently, for Cayley graphs
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)
and
\Sigma={\rm Cay}(H,T)
, the product graph
\Delta
is a Cayley graph for
G\times H
, see
[
2
, Lemmas 3.7.1 and 3.7.2]
: the ‘joining sets’ are
S\times T
for the direct product, and
S\dot{\cup}T
for the Cartesian product (identifying
S,T
with subsets of
G\times H
). More generally, if
\Gamma
and
\Sigma
are both vertex-transitive, then also
\Delta
is vertex-transitive.
Also edge-transitivity of
\Gamma,\Sigma
guarantees edge-transitivity of
\Gamma\times\Sigma
, but in general does not guarantee this for the Cartesian product
\Gamma\,\square\,\Sigma
. Thus Cayley codes for direct products of Cayley graphs may have more desirable properties than those for Cartesian products. We now introduce the Cayley codes for these products where
\Gamma
and
\Sigma
are both Cayley graphs.

7.1.
Cayley codes for Cartesian products

We consider Cayley graphs
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)
and
\Sigma={\rm Cay}(H,T)
for finite groups
G,H
with inverse-closed generating sets
S,T
of
G,H
, respectively. For notational simplicity we identify the subset
S
of
G
with the subset
S\times 1=\{(s,1_{H})\mid s\in S\}
of
G\times H
, and similarly we identify
T
with
1\times T=\{(1_{G},t)\mid t\in T\}
, and note that each of these is an inverse-closed subset of
G\times H
. Consider the
\Delta={\rm Cay}(G\times H,S\dot{\cup}T)
. Then, as discussed above,
\Delta
is the Cartesian product
\Gamma\,\square\,\Sigma
(as in Definition
7.1
(b)).
The generating set
S\dot{\cup}T
for this Cayley graph is a disjoint union as in Hypothesis
5.1
(a), and so, for a finite field
\mathbb{F}
and linear codes

and

5.2
that the Cayley code
\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\dot{\cup}T,A\oplus B)
has the following decomposition:

(11)

\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\dot{\cup}T,A\oplus B)=\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S,A)\oplus\mathbf{C}(G\times H,T,B).

We make a further analysis of the structure of the two direct summands and prove the following.

Proposition 7.2
.

With the notation as above.

(a)

\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S,A)\cong\mathbf{C}(G,S,A)\otimes\mathbb{F}^{|H|}
;

(b)

\mathbf{C}(G\times H,T,B)=\mathbb{F}^{|G|}\otimes\mathbf{C}(H,T,B)
;

and the assertion of Theorem
1.4
is valid.

For the proof we use a natural map linking the edge-set of
\Delta
with those for
\Gamma
and
\Sigma
. For
A\Delta,A\Gamma,A\Sigma
the sets of arcs of
\Delta,\Gamma,\Sigma
, define a natural bijection
\psi\colon A\Delta\to(A\Gamma\times H)\cup(G\times A\Sigma)
by

\psi:(\,(g,h),\ (sg,h)\,)\to(\,(g,sg),\ h\,);\quad(\,(g,h),\ (g,th)\,)\to(\,g,\ (h,th)\,).

This induces a bijection

\widehat{\psi}:E\Delta\to(E\Gamma\times H)\dot{\cup}(G\times E\Sigma)
, namely

(12)

\widehat{\psi}:\{(g,h),\,(sg,h)\}\to(\,\{g,sg\},\,h\,);\quad\{(g,h),\,(g,th)\}\to(\,g,\,\{h,th\}\,).

Proof.

We give the proof details for part (a), since the proof of part (b) is entirely similar. The group
G_{0}:=G\times\{1_{H}\}
has index
u:=|H|
in
G\times H
, and
\mathcal{T}:=\{(1_{G},h)\mid h\in H\}
is a set of right coset representatives for
G_{0}
in
G\times H
. By Lemma
2.2
(a),

E_{0}=\{\{(g,1_{H}),(sg,1_{H})\}|(g,1_{H})\in G_{0},s\in S\}

is the edge-set, as in (
6
), for the connected component of
{\rm Cay}(G\times H,S)
containing the identity, and there are exactly
u
connected components. It follows from Theorem
4.1
(c) that

(13)

\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S,A)=\oplus_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\,\mathbf{C}(G_{0}t,S,A)

is isomorphic to a direct sum of
u
copies of
\mathbf{C}(G_{0},S,A)
and this, in turn, is isomorphic to
\mathbf{C}(G,S,A)\otimes\mathbb{F}^{|H|}
. The last assertion uses the natural bijection
\widehat{\psi}
given in (
12
). Thus part (a) is proved, and part (b) follows by an identical argument. Theorem
1.4
follows from parts (a) and (b) and (
11
).
∎

Finally we deduce information about the parameters (see Table

7.2.
Cayley codes for direct products

As in Subsection
7.2
we consider Cayley graphs
\Gamma={\rm Cay}(G,S)
and
\Sigma={\rm Cay}(H,T)
for finite groups
G,H
with inverse-closed generating sets
S,T
of
G,H
, respectively, and we identify
the
S
and
T
with the subsets
S\times 1
and
1\times T
of
G\times H
. Also, as discussed above, the graph
\Delta={\rm Cay}(G\times H,S\times T)
is the direct product
\Gamma\times\Sigma
defined in Definition
7.1
(a). The number of edges of
\Delta
is
\frac{|G\times H|\cdot|S\times T|}{2}=2\cdot|E\Gamma|\cdot|E\Sigma|
, so the length of a Cayley code for
\Delta
is twice the product of the lengths of Cayley codes for
\Delta
and
\Sigma
. We show that the Cayley codes for
\Delta
are linked with tensor products of Cayley codes for
\Delta
and
\Sigma
.
First we define a natural surjection
\psi
from
E\Delta
to
E\Gamma\times E\Sigma
, noting that each edge of
\Delta
is of the form

(14)

e_{g,h,s,t}=\{(g,h),(sg,th)\}
, for
g\in G,h\in H,s\in S,t\in T
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Define
\psi:E\Delta\to E\Gamma\times E\Sigma
, by
(e_{g,h,s,t})\psi=(\,\{g,sg\},\,\{h,th\}\,)
, with

e_{g,h,s,t}
as in (
14
). Then,

(a)

for
g\in G,h\in H,s\in S,t\in T
, the following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

e_{g,h,s,t}=e_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}
;

(ii)

either
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(g,h,s,t)
or
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(sg,th,s^{-1},t^{-1})
;

(b)

\psi
is a well-defined surjection.

(c)

If
(e_{g,h,s,t})\psi=(e_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime}})\psi
, then either
e_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}=e_{g,h,s,t}
, or

e_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}=e_{sg,h,s^{-1},t}=e_{g,th,s,t^{-1}}
. Thus
\psi
is a
2
-to-
1
map.

Proof.

(a) Suppose that (i) holds. Then
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\{(g,h),(sg,th)\}
. If
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})=(g,h)
, then also
(s^{\prime}g^{\prime},t^{\prime}h^{\prime})=(sg,th)
by part (i), and we have
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(g,h,s,t)
. On the other hand, if
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})=(sg,th)
, then also
(s^{\prime}g^{\prime},t^{\prime}h^{\prime})=(g,h)
by part (i), and it follows that
s^{\prime}=s^{-1}
and
t^{\prime}=t^{-1}
, so
(g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(sg,th,s^{-1},t^{-1})
.

Conversely, suppose that (ii) holds. For the first alternative part (i) requires no proof, while for the second alternative we have
s^{\prime}g^{\prime}=s^{-1}(sg)=g
and
t^{\prime}h^{\prime}=t^{-1}(th)=h
, and part (i) holds in this case also.

(b) To see that
\psi
is well defined, suppose that (a)(i) holds. Then also (a)(ii) holds, and either alternative implies that
\{g,sg\}=\{g^{\prime},s^{\prime}g^{\prime}\}
and
\{h,th\}=\{h^{\prime},t^{\prime}h^{\prime}\}
, so
(e_{g,h,s,t})\psi=(e_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime}})\psi
. Thus
\psi
is well defined. Clearly
\psi
is surjective.

(c) Suppose that
(e_{g,h,s,t})\psi=(e_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime}})\psi
. Then
\{g,sg\}=\{g^{\prime},s^{\prime}g^{\prime}\}
and
\{h,th\}=\{h^{\prime},t^{\prime}h^{\prime}\}
holds by the definition of
\psi
, so
(g^{\prime},s^{\prime}g^{\prime})=(g,sg)
or
(sg,s)
, and
(h^{\prime},t^{\prime}h^{\prime})=(h,th)
or
(th,h)
. Suppose also that
e_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}\neq e_{g,h,s,t}
. Then it follows from part (a)(ii) that
e_{g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}=e_{g,th,s,t^{-1}}
or
e_{sg,h,s^{-1},t}
, and by part (a) these are the same edge, but distinct from
e_{g,h,s,t}
. Thus part (c) is proved.
∎

Now we discuss the Cayley codes. Our conclusions are not as strong as for Cartesian products.
As input for a Cayley code construction for
\Delta
in (
3
), we need a linear code in
\mathbb{F}^{S\times T}
, and it is most convenient to consider this space as the tensor product space
\mathbb{F}^{S}\otimes\mathbb{F}^{T}
. The space containing a Cayley code for
\Delta
is
\mathbb{F}^{E\Delta}
and the map
\psi
induces a natural surjective linear homomorphism from
\mathbb{F}^{E\Delta}
onto
\mathbb{F}^{E\Gamma}\otimes\mathbb{F}^{E\Sigma}
(acting on bases for these spaces).
So suppose that we are given linear codes
A\leq\mathbb{F}^{S}
and
B\leq\mathbb{F}^{T}
, and that we have constructed
\mathbf{C}(G,S,A)\leq\mathbb{F}^{E\Gamma}
and
\mathbf{C}(H,T,B)\leq\mathbb{F}^{E\Sigma}
. Then
A\otimes B
is a linear code in
\mathbb{F}^{S}\otimes\mathbb{F}^{T}
, and in Proposition
7.5
we identify a certain sub-code of the Cayley code
\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\times T,A\otimes B)\leq\mathbb{F}^{E\Delta}
.
Note that, by (
3
), for
\mathbf{C}:=\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\times T,A\otimes B)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}(G,S,A)

\displaystyle=\{f^{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{F}^{E\Gamma}\mid\phi_{g}^{\Gamma}\circ f^{\Gamma}\in A\ \text{for all $g\in G$}\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}(H,T,B)

\displaystyle=\{f^{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{F}^{E\Sigma}\mid\phi_{h}^{\Sigma}\circ f^{\Sigma}\in B\ \text{for all $h\in H$}\},\ \text{and}

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\{f^{\Delta}\in\mathbb{F}^{E\Delta}\mid\phi_{(g,h)}^{\Delta}\circ f^{\Delta}\in A\otimes B\ \text{for all $(g,h)\in G\times H$}\}.

Proposition 7.5
.

With the notation as above, and
\psi
as in Lemma
7.4
,

(a)

Let
(g,h)\in G\times H
and let
\phi_{g}^{\Gamma},\phi_{h}^{\Sigma},\phi_{(g,h)}^{\Delta}
be as in (
2
) for the appropriate graphs. Then
\phi_{(g,h)}^{\Delta}\circ\psi=\phi_{g}^{\Gamma}\otimes\phi_{h}^{\Sigma}
.

(b)

Let
f^{\Gamma}\in\mathbf{C}(G,S,A)
and
f^{\Sigma}\in\mathbf{C}(H,T,B)
. Then
\psi\circ(f^{\Gamma}\otimes f^{\Sigma})\in\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\times T,A\otimes B)
. Thus
\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\times T,A\otimes B)
contains the preimage under
\psi
of
\mathbf{C}(G,S,A)\otimes\mathbf{C}(H,T,B)
.

Moreover, the second assertion of Theorem
1.4
holds.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\times T,A\otimes B)
, as above.

(a) For
(s,t)\in S\times T
, the image
(s,t)(\phi_{(g,h)}^{\Delta}\circ\psi)
is equal to

\{(g,h),(sg,th)\}\psi=\left(\{g,sg\},\ \{h,th\}\right)=(s\phi_{g}^{\Gamma},t\phi_{h}^{\Sigma})=(s,t)(\phi_{g}^{\Gamma}\circ\phi_{h}^{\Sigma}),

proving part (a).

(b) Let
f=\psi\circ(f^{\Gamma}\otimes f^{\Sigma})
. Then
f\in\mathbb{F}^{E\Delta}
. To show that
f\in\mathbf{C}
we need to show that
\phi_{(g,h)}^{\Delta}\circ f\in A\otimes B
for all
(g,h)\in G\times H
. By part (a),

\phi_{(g,h)}^{\Delta}\circ f=(\phi_{g}^{\Gamma}\otimes\phi_{h}^{\Sigma})\circ(f^{\Gamma}\otimes f^{\Sigma})=(\phi_{g}^{\Gamma}\circ f^{\Gamma})\otimes(\phi_{h}^{\Sigma}\circ f^{\Sigma})
, which lies in
A\otimes B
since
f^{\Gamma}\in\mathbf{C}(G,S,A)
and
f^{\Sigma}\in\mathbf{C}(H,T,B)
. Since this holds for all
(g,h)
, we conclude that
f\in\mathbf{C}
. Thus part (b) is proved. Finally we note that part (b) implies the second assertion of Theorem
1.4
.
∎

7.3.
Future directions

The decompositions of Cayley codes given in Theorem
4.1
and Proposition
5.2
allow us, if desired, to focus on symmetric Cayley codes for connected Cayley graphs. On the other hand, we note that the most useful Cayley codes so far discovered have been those in
[
6
]
for a family of edge-transitive bipartite Ramanujan graphs for almost simple groups
{\rm PGL}_{2}(q)
. To understand the role that simple groups might play in the theory we ask:

Question 1
.

Is there a useful notion of a ‘normal quotient’ of a (finite, symmetric) Cayley code which allows one to restrict to Cayley codes in the case where the group
A(\Gamma)
in (
8
) for the Cayley graph
\Gamma
is quasiprimitive or bi-quasiprimitive on vertices?

The finite Cayley graphs
\Gamma
for which
A(\Gamma)
is quasiprimitive and bi-quasiprimitive on vertices are Cayley graphs for characteristically simple groups, that is, groups of the form
T^{k}
for a simple group
T
and integer
k\geq 1
(
[
9
, Section 3, especially Theorem 4]
). Recall that a Cayley graph
\Gamma
is normal edge-transitive if
A(\Gamma)
is edge-transitive. The open question
[
9
, Question 3]
asks for a useful description of normal edge-transitive Cayley graphs for finite characteristically simple groups. One might extend this question and ask:

Question 2
.

Is there a useful description of symmetric Cayley codes corresponding to normal edge-transitive Cayley graphs for finite characteristically simple groups?

In Subsections
7.1
and
7.2
we analysed Cayley codes for Cartesian products and direct products of Cayley graphs. There are other common graph products, such as the strong product and the lexicographic product
[
2
, Section 7.15]
.

Question 3
.

How does the Cayley code construction behave under the strong product and the lexicographic product of Cayley graphs?

In Proposition
7.5
(b) we identified a subcode of the ‘direct product Cayley code’

\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\times T,A\otimes B)
that projected to a tensor product of the Cayley codes for the factors.

Question 4
.

Find families of examples of Cayley codes where
\mathbf{C}(G\times H,S\times T,A\otimes B)
is equal to the subcode in Proposition
7.5
(b), and also families of examples where it is strictly larger.

We hope that such examples could give more insight into the structure of Cayley codes under the direct product construction.
