Determinants and district-level variation in childhood undernutrition in Sindh, Pakistan: evidence from MICS 2018
Authors: Benazir Mahar, Tazeen Shah, Noor Nasir Rajpoot, Noman Sadiq, Muhammad Ali Ansari, Shazia Ali, Dalpat Rai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood undernutrition remains a persistent public health challenge in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in South Asia. In Pakistan, both chronic (stunting) and acute (wasting) forms of undernutrition continue to affect a substantial proportion of children. Sindh province faces pronounced socioeconomic, environmental, and health-related disparities that may influence child nutritional outcomes. This study aimed to examine the determinants of stunting and wasting among children aged 0–23 months and to assess district-level variations using Sindh Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) 2018 data.MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized secondary data from MICS 2018, including 5,985 children with valid anthropometric measurements. Stunting and wasting were defined as height-for-age z-score (HAZ) and weight-for-height z-score (WHZ) below − 2 standard deviations, respectively, based on WHO standards. Analyses were conducted using the complex samples module in SPSS to account for sampling weights, clustering, and stratification. Weighted descriptive statistics were computed, and bivariate associations were assessed using the Rao–Scott chi-square test. Multivariable logistic regression models were applied to identify determinants, with adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. District-level prevalence estimates were also generated.ResultsThe prevalence of stunting and wasting was 38.4% and 17.4%, respectively. In bivariate analysis, both outcomes increased with age; however, after adjustment, younger children aged 0–5 months exhibited significantly lower odds of wasting compared with children aged 12–23 months, indicating comparatively higher odds among older children. Household socioeconomic status was significantly associated with stunting, with children from poorer households having significantly higher odds. Access to improved drinking water was associated with reduced odds of stunting (AOR = 0.70; 95% CI: 0.49–0.98). Male children showed relatively higher odds of wasting, although the association was not statistically significant after adjustment. while other factors, including sanitation, feeding practices, and recent illness, were not statistically significant after adjustment. The apparent association between vaccination and wasting observed in bivariate analysis was attenuated in multivariable analysis, suggesting confounding, particularly by child age. Substantial district-level disparities were observed, with higher burdens in interior districts compared to urban areas.ConclusionChildhood undernutrition remains a significant concern in Sindh, with distinct determinants for stunting and wasting. Socioeconomic inequalities and environmental conditions remain important factors associated with childhood under nutrition. While geographic disparities highlight the need for targeted, context-specific interventions. Strengthening nutrition programs with a focus on vulnerable populations and high-burden districts is essential to reduce undernutrition in the province.Keywords: Stunting, Wasting, Children under two years, Pakistan, Sindh, Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS), Determinants, Socioeconomic factors, Geographic variation, Public health nutrition

Introduction

Child malnutrition remains one of the most serious public health challenges worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Among the various forms of undernutrition, stunting and wasting represent two critical indicators of child nutritional status. Stunting, defined as impaired linear growth resulting from chronic nutritional deprivation, reflects long-term cumulative effects of inadequate nutrition and repeated infections [2]. In contrast, wasting, defined as low weight-for-height, indicates acute undernutrition and reflects recent and severe weight loss often associated with insufficient dietary intake or illness [3].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), both conditions are major contributors to child morbidity and mortality, particularly in resource-limited settings [4]. Globally, an estimated 149 million children under five years of age are stunted, while approximately 45 million children are wasted, with the majority of cases concentrated in South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa [5]. These forms of undernutrition have serious consequences for child survival, growth, and development. Stunting not only affects physical growth but is also associated with impaired cognitive development, reduced educational attainment, lower productivity in adulthood, and increased risk of chronic diseases later in life [6]. Wasting, on the other hand, is strongly linked to increased mortality risk and reflects acute nutritional vulnerability during early childhood [7].

South Asia carries one of the highest burdens of childhood undernutrition globally. Despite considerable economic growth in the region, malnutrition persists due to complex socioeconomic, environmental, and health-related factors [8]. In Pakistan, child malnutrition has remained a persistent public health concern over the past decades [9]. National surveys consistently show alarmingly high levels of stunting and wasting among children. The Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey (PDHS 2017–18) reported that approximately 38% of children under five years are stunted, while a considerable proportion also suffer from wasting, indicating both chronic and acute forms of undernutrition in the country [10]. Similar findings have been reported in other national surveys, such as the National Nutrition Survey, which highlights persistent inequalities in child nutrition across provinces and socioeconomic groups.

Childhood undernutrition is influenced by a wide range of determinants operating at different levels [11]. According to the UNICEF conceptual framework of malnutrition, the causes of undernutrition can be categorized into immediate, underlying, and basic determinants. Immediate causes include inadequate dietary intake and recurrent childhood infections, while underlying causes involve household food insecurity, poor maternal and childcare practices, limited access to health services, and unhealthy environments characterized by poor water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) conditions. At the broader level, structural factors such as poverty, maternal education, social inequality, and geographic disparities further influence child nutritional outcomes.

Previous studies conducted in Pakistan have identified several important predictors of childhood undernutrition, including child age, maternal education, household wealth status, sanitation conditions, and place of residence. Children from poorer households and rural communities are often more vulnerable to malnutrition due to limited access to healthcare services, inadequate infrastructure, and lower maternal literacy levels [12]. Environmental conditions, particularly access to improved water and sanitation facilities, have also been shown to play an important role in child growth outcomes by reducing exposure to infections such as diarrheal diseases that impair nutrient absorption [13].

Within Pakistan, substantial regional disparities in child malnutrition have been reported. Provinces such as Sindh and Baluchistan frequently exhibit higher levels of undernutrition compared with other regions of the country [14]. Sindh, the second most populous province of Pakistan, faces multiple socioeconomic and environmental challenges, including rural poverty, food insecurity, limited healthcare access, and recurrent climatic shocks such as droughts and floods. These conditions contribute to the persistence of poor child nutritional outcomes in the province [15]. Moreover, variations in infrastructure, economic development, and health service availability across districts of Sindh may further contribute to geographic inequalities in childhood undernutrition [16].

Although several studies have examined determinants of child malnutrition in Pakistan [9], limited research has specifically explored both stunting and wasting simultaneously, particularly with a focus on district-level variation and determinants of undernutrition among children under two years of age in Sindh. Understanding these patterns is particularly important because the first two years of life represent a critical window for growth and development, during which nutritional deficiencies can have irreversible consequences [17].

Therefore, this study aims to examine the determinants of childhood stunting and wasting among children under two years of age in Sindh using Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) data. Specifically, the study investigates the influence of child-level, maternal and household-level, and community-level factors, including water and sanitation conditions. In addition, the study explores district-level variations in stunting and wasting prevalence across Sindh, providing important insights into geographic disparities that may inform targeted public health interventions and nutrition policies. Understanding these determinants is essential for designing effective strategies to reduce childhood undernutrition and improve child health outcomes in Sindh and Pakistan as a whole.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This study used a cross-sectional analytical design based on secondary data obtained from the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) 2018, conducted in Sindh, Pakistan. MICS is an internationally standardized household survey program developed by UNICEF to collect national and sub-nationally representative data on health, nutrition, education, and living conditions. The survey uses structured questionnaires to gather information on household characteristics, maternal and child health, nutrition, water and sanitation, and other social indicators.

The Sindh MICS survey employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design to ensure representativeness at the provincial and district levels. In the first stage, enumeration areas (clusters) were selected using probability proportional to size. In the second stage, households were randomly selected within each cluster. All eligible women aged 15–49 years and children under five years residing in the selected households were included in the survey.

For the present study, the analysis was restricted to children aged 0–23 months, as this age group represents the critical window for growth and development during the first 1,000 days of life.

Study population and sample size

The study population consisted of children aged 0–23 months included in the Sindh Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) 2018. Children with missing or flagged anthropometric measurements were excluded based on standard data quality criteria.

A total of 5,985 children with valid height-for-age (HAZ) measurements were included in the analysis. Analyses for stunting and wasting were conducted separately using all available valid observations for each outcome variable.

Outcome variable

Two outcome variables were assessed:

Stunting (chronic undernutrition): Defined as height-for-age z-score (HAZ) < − 2 SD

Stunting (chronic undernutrition): Defined as height-for-age z-score (HAZ) < − 2 SD

Wasting (acute undernutrition): Defined as weight-for-height z-score (WHZ) < − 2 SD

Wasting (acute undernutrition): Defined as weight-for-height z-score (WHZ) < − 2 SD

Explanatory variables

The selection of independent variables was guided by existing literature on the determinants of child undernutrition, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. The variables were conceptually grouped into three categories:

(i)Child-level factors

Child-level factors

Child-level variables included age (0–5, 6–11, and 12–23 months), sex, recent illness (diarrhea, fever, or cough in the preceding two weeks), and vaccination status. These factors are recognized as key biological and health-related determinants of child nutritional status [20]. Age reflects critical stages of growth and feeding transitions, while illness and immunization status influence nutritional outcomes through their effects on infection and immunity [21].

(ii)Maternal and household-level factors

Maternal and household-level factors

Maternal and household characteristics included mother’s education level, household wealth index, breastfeeding status, and complementary feeding practices (meal frequency categorized as low, medium, and high). These variables capture the socio-economic and caregiving environment of the child. Maternal education is widely associated with improved childcare practices and health-seeking behavior [22], while household wealth reflects access to food and healthcare resources. Feeding practices, including breastfeeding and meal frequency, are directly linked to dietary adequacy and child growth [23].

(iii)Environmental factors

Environmental factors

Environmental variables included area of residence (urban/rural), drinking water source (improved/unimproved), and sanitation facilities (improved/unimproved). These factors represent the broader living conditions that influence exposure to infections and overall child health [24]. Poor water and sanitation conditions are well-established contributors to undernutrition through increased risk of enteric infections [25]. The variable selection was based on the relevant literature on child undernutrition. Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26. Considering the complex multistage sampling design of the MICS survey, all analyses were conducted using the Complex Samples module to account for sampling weights, clustering, and stratification. Weighted descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population, and weighted percentages were reported for categorical variables to obtain population-representative estimates. Bivariate associations between explanatory variables and the outcomes (stunting and wasting) were assessed using the Rao–Scott adjusted chi-square test.

To identify factors associated with stunting and wasting, separate multivariable logistic regression models were fitted for each outcome using the Complex Samples Logistic Regression procedure. The outcome variables were binary, coded as stunted/not stunted and wasted/not wasted according to the WHO child growth standards. Explanatory variables included child-level factors (age, sex, recent illness, vaccination status), maternal and household-level factors (maternal education, household wealth index, breastfeeding status, and meal frequency), and environmental factors (place of residence, drinking water source, and sanitation facility). Variables were selected based on prior literature, biological plausibility, and the UNICEF conceptual framework for child under nutrition.

In addition, district-wise prevalence estimates for stunting and wasting were calculated to assess geographic variation across Sindh Province.

Ethical considerations

The study used secondary anonymized data from the MICS survey. Ethical approval for data collection was obtained by the original survey implementing agencies. Since the dataset used in this study did not contain any personal identifiers, additional ethical approval was not required.

Results

A total of 5,985 children aged 0–23 months were included in the analysis (Table 3). The prevalence of stunting was 38.4% (n = 2,299), indicating that more than one-third of children were affected by chronic undernutrition. In contrast, 61.6% of children were not stunted. The prevalence of wasting was 17.4% (n = 1,065), reflecting a substantial burden of acute malnutrition, while the majority of children(82.6%) were not wasted.

Bivariate analysis

Stunting

Bivariate analysis (Table 4) demonstrated that age, maternal education, household wealth, area of residence, and sanitation facility were significantly associated with stunting among children aged 0–23 months. A strong age-related increase in stunting was observed. The prevalence of stunting increased from 24.5% among children aged 0–5 months to 32.3% among those aged 6–11 months and reached the highest level among those aged 12–23 months (50%) (p < 0.001). Maternal education showed a significant inverse relationship with stunting. Children with no formal maternal education had the highest prevalence (43.2%), which progressively decreased with higher maternal education, reaching 25.3% among children of highly educated mothers (p < 0.001). A pronounced socioeconomic gradient was evident across wealth quintiles. The prevalence of stunting was highest among children from the poorest households (47.7%), followed by the second (44.9%) and middle (37.4%) quintiles, and lowest among the richest households (22.3%) (p < 0.001). Stunting was also significantly higher among children residing in rural areas (43.6%) compared to urban areas (31.5%) (p < 0.001). Similarly, children living in households with unimproved sanitation facilities had a markedly higher prevalence of stunting (48.4%) compared to those with improved sanitation (32.4%) (p < 0.001). In contrast, sex and household size were not significantly associated with stunting, while drinking water source showed a borderline association (p = 0.056), with slightly higher prevalence observed among children using improved water sources.

Wasting

Bivariate analysis showed that age, sex, maternal education, household wealth, area of residence, sanitation facility, meal frequency, and vaccination status were significantly associated with wasting among children aged 0–23 months (Table 5). A clear age-related increase in wasting was observed. The prevalence of wasting was lowest among children aged 0–5 months (10.1%), increasing to 16.4% in 6–11 months, and reaching the highest level among children aged 12–23 months (22.5%) (p < 0.001). Wasting was more prevalent among male children (19.3%) compared to females (15.6%) (p < 0.001). Maternal education showed a significant association with higher prevalence among children whose mothers had no formal education (19.0%), compared to those with higher education levels (13.7%) (p = 0.002). A strong socioeconomic gradient was evident across wealth quintiles. The prevalence of wasting was highest among children from the poorest households (22.8%), gradually decreasing among wealthier groups (p < 0.001). Children residing in rural areas (18.5%) had a higher prevalence of wasting compared to urban children (16.1%) (p = 0.024).

Multivariable logistic regression

Stunting

Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that age and household wealth were significantly associated with stunting (Table 6). Compared to children aged 12–23 months, children aged 0–5 months (AOR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.23–0.40) and 6–11 months (AOR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.39–0.64) had significantly lower odds of stunting, indicating a strong age-related pattern. A clear socioeconomic gradient was observed across wealth quintiles. Compared to children from the richest households, those from the middle (AOR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.04–2.33), second (AOR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.15–2.85), and poorest households (AOR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.56–4.20) had significantly higher odds of stunting, with the highest risk observed among the poorest group (p = 0.004). Children living in households with improved drinking water sources had significantly lower odds of stunting (AOR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.49–0.98; p = 0.040) compared to those using unimproved sources. In contrast, sex, area of residence, sanitation facility, and maternal education were not significantly associated with stunting after adjusting for other variables. Although the odds ratios for some categories suggested potential differences, their confidence intervals included unity, indicating a lack of statistical significance.

Wasting

Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that age was significantly associated with wasting among children aged 0–23 months (Table 7). Compared to children aged 12–23 months, children aged 0–5 months had significantly lower odds of wasting (AOR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.32–0.87). A similar but non-significant pattern was observed among children aged 6–11 months (AOR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.41–1.06), indicating comparatively higher odds of wasting among older children. Male children showed higher odds of wasting compared to females (AOR = 1.33, 95% CI: 0.93–1.92), although the association was not statistically significant after adjustment. Children from poorer households also showed relatively higher odds of wasting compared to those from the richest households; however, these associations did not reach statistical significance. Similarly, maternal education, area of residence, sanitation facility, meal frequency, recent diarrhea, breastfeeding status, and vaccination status were not significantly associated with wasting after adjustment, as their confidence intervals included unity. Although children who were not vaccinated had slightly higher odds of wasting compared to vaccinated children (AOR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.73–1.61), the association was not statistically significant.

Discussion

This study examined the determinants of childhood stunting and wasting among children under two years of age in Sindh using Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) 2018 data. The findings revealed that 38.4% of children were stunted, indicating a substantial burden of chronic undernutrition, while 17.4% were wasted, reflecting a considerable level of acute malnutrition in the province. These findings highlight the coexistence of both chronic and acute forms of undernutrition among children in Sindh. The prevalence of stunting observed in this study is comparable with national estimates reported in Pakistan, where stunting remains persistently high. According to the Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey (PDHS 2017–2018), approximately 38.6% of children under five years of age are stunted, highlighting the severity of the problem in the country [27]. Similar patterns have been reported in previous studies analyzing child malnutrition in Pakistan [12, 28].

Child-level determinants

Child age emerged as a strong and consistent determinant of both stunting and wasting. The prevalence of undernutrition increased markedly with age in the bivariate analysis, particularly among children aged 12–23 months. This pattern aligns with existing literature showing that growth faltering typically begins after six months of age, when children transition from exclusive breastfeeding to complementary feeding, which is often nutritionally inadequate in low-resource settings [29, 30].

In the adjusted analysis, younger children aged 0–5 months exhibited significantly lower odds of wasting compared with children aged 12–23 months, indicating a comparatively higher burden of wasting among older children. A similar pattern, although not statistically significant, was also observed among children aged 6–11 months. This age-related pattern may reflect increased nutritional vulnerability during the complementary feeding period, when children are more exposed to inadequate dietary intake, recurrent infections, and environmental contaminants. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, where post-weaning nutritional deficits and increased exposure to infections may partly explain the higher prevalence of undernutrition among older infants and young children [18, 31]. Sex differences were not significant for stunting but were more pronounced for wasting, with male children showing relatively higher odds. Although this association did not remain statistically significant after adjustment, similar patterns have been reported in previous studies, suggesting greater biological susceptibility among male children to acute nutritional stress and infections [32, 33].

Maternal and household factors

Maternal education demonstrated a clear protective gradient in the descriptive analysis, with lower prevalence of stunting observed among children of more educated mothers. This is well supported in the literature, as maternal education improves caregiving practices, health literacy, and utilization of healthcare services [34, 35]. Similar associations have been reported in studies from Pakistan and other South Asian countries [36, 37]. However, this association did not remain statistically significant after adjustment, suggesting that its effect may be mediated through other socio-economic factors.

Household wealth was one of the strongest factors associated with stunting. Children from poorer households had significantly higher odds of stunting compared to those from the richest households, confirming the well-established socioeconomic gradient in chronic undernutrition [38–40]. In contrast, although children from poorer households showed relatively higher odds of wasting, the association did not remain statistically significant after adjustment. This may suggest that acute malnutrition is influenced by a combination of immediate and short-term factors, including illness, dietary inadequacy, and environmental exposure.

Environmental and WASH factors

Environmental conditions were associated with child nutritional outcomes. Access to improved drinking water was associated with lower odds of stunting, highlighting the role of safe water in preventing infections and improving nutrient absorption [41, 42]. The difference between crude and adjusted findings may reflect confounding by socioeconomic and environmental factors.

Similarly, unimproved sanitation was associated with higher prevalence of both stunting and wasting in the bivariate analysis, although its effect was attenuated after adjustment [43].

These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that poor WASH conditions have been associated with greater exposure to enteric infections and poorer growth outcomes [44]. However, the lack of significance in the adjusted model suggests that sanitation alone may not fully capture environmental risk, which is often influenced by broader contextual factors.

The study also identified significant rural–urban differences, with a higher prevalence of stunting observed in rural areas. These findings align with previous research showing that rural populations face greater challenges related to healthcare access, infrastructure, and food security [45].

Feeding practices and child health

Feeding practices were particularly important determinants of wasting [46]. Children who were not currently breastfed and those receiving suboptimal meal frequency showed higher prevalence of wasting, although these associations did not remain statistically significant after adjustment. Feeding practices may be linked with other factors such as age, illness, and household conditions [47].

Recent diarrheal episodes showed a higher prevalence of wasting in the descriptive analysis, reinforcing the well-established link between infection and acute malnutrition [48]. However, the adjusted model did not show a statistically significant association, likely due to confounding and overlapping effects with other variables. A notable finding of this study was the apparent discrepancy between bivariate and multivariable results for vaccination. In the bivariate analysis, wasting prevalence was higher among vaccinated children (21.5%) compared to non-vaccinated children (16.4%), suggesting a misleading positive association. However, this association disappeared after adjustment, with vaccination showing no statistically significant association (AOR: 1.08; 95% CI: 0.73–1.61).

This pattern reflects confounding, particularly by child age. Vaccination status is inherently cumulative, meaning older children are more likely to be fully vaccinated. At the same time, older children in this study also exhibited a higher prevalence of wasting, likely due to increased exposure to infections and challenges associated with complementary feeding. As a result, the crude association observed in the bivariate analysis was confounded by age, producing a spurious relationship.

After adjusting for age and other covariates, the association between vaccination and wasting was attenuated, indicating that the crude association was likely confounded by age and related factors. This highlights the importance of accounting for key confounders when interpreting crude associations in cross-sectional data. Similar patterns have been reported in previous studies where age-related confounding influenced the observed relationship between preventive health interventions and nutritional outcomes [25].

District-level variation

The district-level analysis further revealed substantial spatial variation in both stunting and wasting across Sindh. Higher prevalence of stunting was observed in districts such as Dadu and Kashmore, whereas Karachi districts showed comparatively lower levels. Similarly, wasting was highest in districts such as Shahdad Kot, Tharparkar, and Tando Allahyar.

These geographic disparities may reflect differences in socioeconomic conditions, healthcare access, maternal education, and environmental factors across districts. Interior districts of Sindh are characterized by higher poverty rates, limited healthcare infrastructure, and frequent environmental shocks such as droughts and floods [49]. In contrast, urban districts benefit from better infrastructure and healthcare services [50]. Mapping district-wise prevalence, therefore, provides valuable insights into priority areas for targeted interventions.

An important consideration is that the data used in this study are from 2018, and the nutritional landscape in Sindh may have changed substantially since then. In recent years, the province has experienced major environmental shocks, including severe floods, droughts, and climate-related disruptions [51]. These events have significantly affected food security, healthcare access, and living conditions, particularly in rural and vulnerable districts. Evidence suggests that such shocks are strongly associated with increased risk of acute malnutrition, particularly wasting [52]. Therefore, the findings of this study are likely to represent a baseline situation, and the current burden of undernutrition may be even higher in the post-disaster context. Future analyses using updated survey data will be essential to capture these evolving trends.

Policy implications

The findings underscore the multifactorial nature of childhood undernutrition. While stunting reflects long-term structural inequalities, wasting is more sensitive to immediate factors such as infection and feeding practices. Addressing undernutrition requires integrated, multisectoral strategies focusing on poverty reduction, maternal education, improved feeding practices, WASH interventions, and access to healthcare. Given the pronounced geographic disparities observed, targeted interventions in high-burden districts are essential. Strengthening resilience to environmental shocks should also be prioritized in nutrition programs.

Conclusion

The present study examined the determinants and district-level variation of stunting and wasting among children aged 0–23 months in Sindh using MICS 2018 data. The findings indicate that both chronic and acute forms of undernutrition remain prevalent in the province. Child age, socioeconomic status, environmental conditions, feeding practices, and recent illnesses were associated with undernutrition. The study also revealed considerable geographic disparities across districts of Sindh, suggesting that child malnutrition appears to be associated with both household-level and contextual factors. Addressing undernutrition, therefore, requires integrated, multisectoral approaches focusing on poverty reduction, maternal education, improved feeding practices, enhanced sanitation, and equitable access to healthcare services.

Strengths of the study

This study has several important strengths. First, it utilizes data from the Sindh Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey 2018 (MICS 2018), which is a large, provincially representative dataset with standardized data collection procedures. Second, the study provides a comprehensive analysis of determinants of both stunting and wasting among children under two years of age, capturing both chronic and acute dimensions of undernutrition during the critical first 1,000 days of life. Third, the study explores district-level variations in both stunting and wasting, offering valuable insights into geographic disparities in child nutrition across Sindh. The inclusion of both outcomes allows for a more holistic understanding of undernutrition patterns, distinguishing between long-term growth faltering and short-term nutritional deficits. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to provide a detailed and combined analysis of stunting and wasting using Sindh MICS 2018 data, thereby contributing important evidence for policymakers and public health practitioners in the region.

Limitations of the study

Despite its strengths, the study has some limitations. First, the analysis is based on secondary cross-sectional data, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the identified determinants and nutritional outcomes (stunting and wasting). Second, some potentially important factors influencing child nutritional status, such as dietary diversity, detailed feeding practices, maternal nutritional status, and household food security, were not available in the dataset and therefore could not be included in the analysis. Third, the wealth index used in the survey is a proxy measure of household socioeconomic status and may not fully capture income inequality or economic vulnerability. Finally, although district-level prevalence of both stunting and wasting was examined, more advanced spatial or multilevel analyses could further explore contextual and geographic influences on childhood undernutrition.
