 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
LandCast Mosaic (LCM) provides a global, high-resolution gridded population dataset span-
ning 1975–2025, representing annual, scenario-consistent estimates of daytime, nighttime, and
ambient population distributions. LCM builds on the 2025 LandScan Mosaic (LSM) population
data by backcasting to earlier years using historical changes in built-surface area derived from the
Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL) and authoritative population counts from international
datasets. The workflow scales 2025 building-informed gridded population estimates according to
observed changes in built surface, applies linear interpolation for intermediate years, and normal-
izes estimates to match administrative- and country-level totals. The resulting dataset offers con-
sistent, globally gridded population estimates over fifty years, suitable for temporal analyses of
population dynamics, disaster risk modeling, and urban planning applications.

 2. INTRODUCTION
Accurate, high-resolution population data are critical for understanding population dynamics,
assessing disaster risk, planning urban infrastructure, and modeling human-environment inter-
actions [4]. Previous LandScan products, although widely used, were typically not suitable for
change detection or temporal analysis, because their methodology evolved over time, preventing
consistent year-to-year comparisons [3].
The LandCast Mosaic (LCM) dataset addresses this limitation by providing annual global popu-
lation estimates at 3 arc-second resolution for the period 1975–2025, leveraging the building-level
LandScan Mosaic (LSM) population product [1]. By combining LSM with historical built-surface
information and authoritative population counts, LCM enables consistent time-series analysis of
population change, which has not been possible using previously released LandScan datasets.
Because LSM’s gridded population estimates are derived from a building-level model rather than
using traditional dasymetric or random-forest approaches, it is well situated to adjust popula-
tion estimates based on observed changes in built-surface area. By using GHSL built-surface area
data as a proxy for building presence and size, LCM can scale 2025 populations backward in time
while maintaining spatial realism.
To produce these estimates, LCM employs a backcasting workflow that integrates GHSL data to
capture changes in the spatial distribution of infrastructure over time. Changes in built-surface
area at the pixel level are applied to the 2025 building-level population data, and estimates are
normalized to administrative- and country-level population totals. This produces temporally con-
sistent population grids that reflect both historical trends and local spatial dynamics. Figure 1
illustrates the population dynamics in Jakarta between 1975 and 2025, showing how the urban
population expanded and shifted over time.
The resulting dataset supports a wide range of applications, including temporal analyses of pop-
ulation change [5], disaster and risk modeling [7], urban growth studies [10], and policy planning
[9]. This workflow ensures reproducibility and transparency, with all processing steps detailed in
this technical report. These estimates represent scenario-based reconstructions anchored to a con-
sistent present-day baseline, rather than causal simulations of historical population processes.

3.1 LANDSCAN MOSAIC 2025
The LandScan Mosaic (LSM) 2025 dataset is the foundational population input for the LandCast
Mosaic (LCM) backcasting workflow. LSM provides a building-level gridded population dataset
covering the entire globe, with both daytime and nighttime population distributions at 3 arc-
second spatial resolution [1]. Unlike previous LandScan products, which used evolving method-
ologies and were therefore unsuitable for consistent temporal analysis [3], LSM represents a single,
standardized baseline year (2025) that enables reliable time-series modeling. The dataset esti-
mates the number of people per pixel and is derived by combining high-resolution building foot-
prints with ancillary demographic and land-use data, in contrast to traditional dasymetric ap-
proaches that redistribute aggregate population counts using statistical methods [4].
LSM 2025 is particularly well-suited for the LCM backcasting workflow because its building-level
modeling allows population to be adjusted based on changes in built-surface area. By applying
changes in built-surface area from GHSL to 2025 populations and normalizing to administrative-
and country-level totals, LCM produces temporally consistent population estimates that retain
spatial realism. The dataset also supports analysis of diurnal population dynamics, capturing
both daytime, nighttime and ambient population distributions [1]. Ambient population represents
the average population distribution over a 24-hour period, combining daytime and nighttime dy-
namics. Overall, LSM 2025 provides the spatial fidelity and methodological consistency necessary
for producing reliable global population time series.

3.2 GHSL BUILT-SURFACE
The Global Human Settlement Layer – Built-Up Surface (GHSL-BUILT-S) dataset provides a
global measure of built-surface area over time and serves as the primary source for temporal scal-
ing in the LandCast Mosaic (LCM) backcasting workflow [6]. The version used in this workflow
is the 2023 release, covering the period 1975–2030 at 5-year intervals. GHSL-BUILT-S quantifies
the total square meters of built-up surface per 3 arc-second cell, derived from consistent remote
sensing and satellite imagery sources. The dataset is globally consistent and standardized, making
it suitable for detecting historical trends in urbanization and built-surface area change.
For LCM, GHSL-BUILT-S provides a quantitative measure of built infrastructure that can be
used to scale the 2025 building-level population from LandScan Mosaic (LSM) backward in time.

 3

Changes in built-surface area serve as a proxy for population redistribution, allowing LCM to
produce temporally consistent population estimates while maintaining spatial fidelity. Although
GHSL-BUILT-S extends to 2030, only observations through 2025 are used to align with the LSM
reference year.
The strengths of GHSL-BUILT-S for backcasting include its temporal consistency, high spatial
resolution, and global coverage, which make it uniquely suited to inform population changes over
multiple decades [6]. When combined with LSM, GHSL-BUILT-S enables the creation of a con-
tinuous, globally consistent population time series suitable for urban planning, disaster risk as-
sessment, and demographic analysis [5].

3.3 POPULATION DATASETS
In addition to the high-resolution LandScan Mosaic (LSM) 2025 dataset, the LandCast Mosaic
(LCM) backcasting workflow relies on historical population reference data to normalize cell-level
estimates across time [3]. The LSM 2025 baseline is itself normalized to CIA World Factbook
data [2], ensuring consistency with authoritative population estimates for 2025.
For backcasting to earlier years (1975–2024), LCM uses annual country-level population counts
from the US Census Bureau International Database [8]. These national-level totals provide the
target populations for each year, ensuring that cell-level distributions remain consistent with ob-
served demographic trends. To inform the spatial distribution of population within countries, his-
torical LandScan Global (RF, dasymetric) data are used to calculate relative zonal distributions
at the administrative level. These relative distributions are applied to scale LSM 2025 popula-
tions proportionally, and the resulting estimates are normalized to match the annual US Census
Bureau IDB totals at the first sub-national level (ADM1) [8].
This approach allows LCM to produce annual, globally consistent population grids that reflect
both temporal dynamics and spatial patterns of population distribution, while maintaining align-
ment with authoritative historical population counts [3, 8]. The population datasets are used
solely for normalization purposes; no additional demographic modeling or interpolation is applied
beyond proportional scaling.

 4. METHODOLOGY
The pre-processing stage establishes the foundation of the LandCast Mosaic workflow by har-
monizing, structuring and aggregating input datasets. All gridded spatial data are aligned to a
common 3-arc second resolution with an origin at −180◦ longitude and 90◦ latitude. The Land-
Scan Mosaic (LSM) 2025 dataset serves as the baseline for the LandCast Mosaic workflow. Each
3 arc-second pixel is assigned a globally consistent row and column index, corresponding to 3 arc-
second grid mentioned above. Raster layers representing daytime, nighttime and ambient popula-
tions are converted to a tabular format to simplify processing and enable efficient aggregation and
analysis. GHSL-BUILT-S data are also resampled to the same consistent grid. An illustration of
the workflow is provided in Figure 2 and a detailed walkthrough of the methodology is provided

4.3 SCALING
Following interpolation and aggregation, the assembled dataset contains, for each LandScan Mo-
saic (LSM) pixel (i, j), an annual time series of GHSL-derived built-surface values Gi, j,t for t ∈
[1975, 2025], along with the baseline LSM population estimate for the reference year 2025. To
backcast population distributions, we compute a per-pixel scaling factor that represents the rel-
ative change in built-surface area at year t with respect to 2025.
For each pixel (i, j) and year t, the scaling factor Ci, j,t is defined as the ratio of built-surface area
in year t to that in the 2025 reference year. This relationship is illustrated in Figure 2, Panel (c),
where an example pixel has 1000 m2 of built surface in 2025 and 500 m2 in 2002, yielding a scal-
ing factor of 0.5.

4.4 ADJUSTMENT
For each year t ∈ [1975, 2025], population counts are backcast by applying the GHSL-derived scal-
ing factor to the LandScan Mosaic (LSM) population baseline for the 2025 reference year. This
adjustment is performed independently for each pixel (i, j) and for each population type.
The adjustment procedure is illustrated in Figure 2, Panel (d). In the example shown, a pixel
with an LSM population of 42 in 2025 is associated with a scaling factor of 0.5 for the year 2002
(Panel (c)), resulting in an adjusted population estimate of 21 for that year.

Pixels with zero population in 2025 remain zero for all years, regardless of changes in built-surface

This operation preserves the spatial distribution of the 2025 LSM population while modulating
population magnitudes through time according to the local history of built-surface change. The
output of this step is a set of un-normalized annual population estimates for each population type
spanning 1975–2025.

4.5 NORMALIZATION
Sub-national population totals are used to normalize pixel-level population estimates such that
they match authoritative demographic statistics. In the LandScan Mosaic (LSM) product, national-
level population totals are sourced from the CIA World Factbook (WFB) (who themselves, source
from the U.S. Census Bureau International Database), while sub-national population distributions
are derived from LandScan Global (LSG). For the 2025 LSM baseline, population values within
each administrative unit sum to the corresponding LSG total, and all administrative units within
a country together sum to the CIA WFB national total.
The U.S. Census Bureau IDB is used for historical consistency and data availability back to 1975.
The Sub-national population distributions are derived from historical versions of LandScan Global
spanning 2000–2024. For years prior to 2000, sub-national population distributions are linearly

4.6 FINAL OUTPUTS
The workflow generates over 50 years of gridded population data at 3-arc second resolution, avail-
able as individual or stacked rasters. Each cell contains annual p50 population values for daytime,
nighttime, and ambient populations, maintaining strict alignment with the LandScan Mosaic 2025
grid and the corresponding row and column indices. This ensures consistent pixel-level referencing
across the entire temporal series.

4.7 QUALITY CHECKS
All output datasets undergo manual inspection by reviewers to verify consistency, completeness,
and plausibility. This includes visual inspection of raster layers, checking cell-level population
ranges, computing zonal statistics, and summary statistics aggregated by administrative units.
Manual review is complemented by automated summary statistics and consistency checks to en-
sure reproducibility across processing runs.

 5. APPLICATIONS
The LandScan Mosaic (LCM) methodology and dataset represents a new capability for the Land-
Scan program, providing high-resolution (3 arc-seconds), temporally consistent representations of
global population dynamics over more than 50 years. By producing annual daytime, nighttime,
and ambient population surfaces that are fully aligned with the LandScan Mosaic 2025 grid, LCM
enables a wide range of applications in research, planning, and operational contexts.
Key potential applications of LCM include:
 • Temporal Population Analysis: LCM allows researchers to examine historical trends in
 population distribution, urban growth, and rural-urban migration over the period 1975–2025.
 An example for Jakarta, Indonesia is available in Figure 3.
 • Disaster Risk Assessment: High-resolution population grids can be used to model expo-
 sure to natural hazards, estimate potential impacts, and support emergency response plan-
 ning.
 • Urban and Infrastructure Planning: Planners can assess population changes at fine
 spatial scales to inform infrastructure development, service allocation, and sustainable urban
 expansion.
 • Public Health and Epidemiology: LCM supports analyses that require population de-
 nominators at high spatial and temporal resolution, such as disease modeling and vaccina-
 tion coverage planning.
 • Integration with Environmental and Socioeconomic Data: The pixel-level alignment
 with other LandCast Mosaic components allows integration with datasets on land use, mo-
 bility, economic activity, and environmental exposure.

 6. ASSUMPTIONS AND LIMITATIONS
The LandCast Mosaic (LCM) workflow integrates multiple datasets and modeling steps, each of
which introduces assumptions and potential sources of uncertainty. While the approach is de-
signed to produce spatially and temporally consistent population estimates, users should consider
the following limitations when interpreting results.
Model structural assumptions.
LCM inherits several structural assumptions from the LandScan Mosaic (LSM) 2025 baseline,
which are held constant across all backcast years. Population is assumed to scale proportionally
with changes in built-surface extent, implying constant average occupancy rates, stable building
use composition, and unchanged structural density through time. The workflow does not account
for temporal variation in household size, vacancy rates, building function transitions, or vertical
densification processes, as GHSL-BUILT-S captures horizontal built-surface change only.
As a result, population change in LCM is driven exclusively by observed changes in built-surface
area and externally supplied population totals. These assumptions are unlikely to hold uniformly
across regions and historical periods, particularly in rapidly urbanizing or economically transition-
ing areas.
Proxy-based scaling and interpolation.
Changes in GHSL built-surface area are used as a proxy for population redistribution, implicitly
assuming a stable relationship between built infrastructure and population over time. Linear in-
terpolation between 5-year GHSL reference epochs assumes gradual change and does not capture
abrupt population shifts due to conflict, disasters, or rapid development. Pixels with zero built
surface in the 2025 reference year are assigned constant scaling factors, which may underestimate
historical population in locations where built infrastructure existed prior to 2025 but is absent in
the baseline year.
Input data reliability.
All input datasets—including LandScan Mosaic, GHSL-BUILT-S, and census-based population
statistics—are assumed to be internally consistent and free of systematic bias. Errors or uncer-
tainties in any source data may propagate through the workflow and affect final population esti-
mates.
Spatial aggregation.
Aggregation of GHSL built-surface data to the LandScan Mosaic grid is required due to differ-
ences in raster alignment. While this procedure preserves broad spatial patterns, it may smooth
fine-scale heterogeneity in fragmented or highly localized settlement structures.
Normalization.
Pixel-level population estimates are normalized to authoritative national and sub-national pop-
ulation totals. This ensures consistency with external demographic statistics but may not fully
correct localized spatial inaccuracies, particularly where administrative boundaries do not align
with settlement patterns or where census estimates are themselves uncertain.
Accordingly, LCM should be interpreted as a set of internally consistent, scenario-based recon-
structions anchored to the 2025 LSM baseline, rather than as a causal model of historical popula-
tion processes. Each year represents a retrospective application of present-day building–population
relationships, constrained by observed built-surface change and authoritative population totals.

 7. CONCLUSION
LCM represents a significant advancement in global population modeling by combining a building-
level baseline with historical built-environment data and authoritative demographic counts to pro-
duce consistent, long-term, gridded population estimates. The workflow preserves spatial fidelity
at the pixel level while ensuring that aggregate population totals align with official sub-national
and national statistics.
While the dataset is subject to assumptions and limitations inherent in input data and modeling
choices, it provides a reproducible, transparent, and globally consistent time series suitable for a
wide range of scientific, policy, and operational applications. The resulting time series should be
interpreted as a scenario-consistent reconstruction of historical population distributions rather
than a fully causal demographic model. By making over five decades of annual, high-resolution
population surfaces available, LCM fills a critical gap in spatiotemporal demographic information
and supports improved understanding of population dynamics worldwide.
