Right-sided chyle leak encountered during supraclavicular brachial plexus exposure: illustrative case
Authors: Jacob Bethel, Asma Eslami, Amgad S. Hanna

Chyle leak is a rare but recognized complication in neck surgeries, particularly those involving lymphatic structures and the thoracic duct (TD). Chyle leaks occur in about 1%–2.5% of radical neck dissections.1 When they do happen, they are much more likely to be on the left side, accounting for 75%–92% of cases.1

From an anatomical perspective, the lymphatic system primarily drains through two major vessels: the TD and the right lymphatic duct. The TD, the largest lymphatic vessel, is responsible for transporting up to 75% of the body’s lymph, including drainage from the entire left side and the right side below the diaphragm. In contrast, the right lymphatic duct primarily collects lymph from the right upper quadrant of the body, including the right thorax, upper limb, and head and neck regions.2

While commonly reported in neck dissections, chyle leaks are less frequently encountered in neurosurgery. Nerve transfers, such as those used for brachial plexus reconstruction, typically involve careful dissection of neural structures while avoiding vascular and lymphatic injury. However, unexpected chyle leak during such a procedure presents a unique surgical challenge. This case report discusses an incidental chyle leak encountered during a right-sided supraclavicular nerve transfer, emphasizing its possible anatomical variations, intraoperative recognition, and management strategies to prevent complications.

Illustrative Case

A male in his late 50s presented with a 6-month history of dyspnea, right hand paresthesias, and biceps atrophy. Examination revealed diminished sensation over the right thumb and little finger and a positive scratch-collapse test over the right paraspinal muscles. Diaphragmatic fluoroscopy demonstrated an immobile right hemidiaphragm with paradoxical elevation during deep inspiration. MRI and CT of the cervical spine revealed severe right C3–4 and moderate C4–5 foraminal stenosis (Fig. 1). No abnormal lymphatic anatomy was noted on preoperative MRI. The patient underwent a right-sided C3–4 and C4–5 posterior foraminotomy without complication. Postoperative imaging continued to show an elevated right hemidiaphragm. Electromyography confirmed right phrenic neuropathy with partial recovery of C5 function. Treatment options, including nerve transfer, diaphragmatic plication, and pacer implantation, were discussed. The patient elected to proceed with a dorsal scapular-to-phrenic nerve transfer.

Surgical Technique

Patient Positioning and Preparation

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position with continuous intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) of the right upper extremity. The head and shoulder were positioned to optimize exposure of the supraclavicular brachial plexus. The surgical field was prepared and draped in the usual sterile fashion.

Exposure and Initial Dissection

A curvilinear incision was made over the right supraclavicular region, approximately two fingerbreadths above the clavicle, within the posterior triangle of the neck. The skin and subcutaneous tissues were elevated superiorly and inferiorly to expose the platysma, which was divided along its fibers to reveal the underlying deep cervical fascia. A subplatysmal fat pad was dissected and retracted laterally. During this step, an enlarged lymph node was identified, circumferentially dissected, and excised for permanent pathological evaluation.

The omohyoid muscle was visualized coursing mediolaterally and was transected to enhance exposure. The transverse cervical vessels were identified, ligated, and cut.

Identification of the Phrenic Nerve

The upper trunk of the brachial plexus was exposed and protected with a vessel loop. The phrenic nerve was then identified as it coursed over the anterior scalene muscle. Electrical stimulation with a bipolar nerve stimulator failed to elicit diaphragmatic contraction or changes in end-tidal CO2 tracing, consistent with preoperative phrenic nerve dysfunction. The phrenic nerve was dissected caudally and posteriorly in a stepwise fashion and mobilized under gentle medial traction with a vessel loop.

Donor Nerve Harvest and Transfer

Attention was turned to the donor nerve. The dorsal scapular nerve was identified between the scalenus medius and posterior muscles. After careful dissection and mobilization, intraoperative stimulation confirmed contraction of the rhomboid muscles, verifying the donor nerve identity. The dorsal scapular nerve was harvested as a size-appropriate match for the recipient phrenic nerve and divided distally. The phrenic nerve was similarly transected proximally to ensure clean, coaptable ends.

Microsurgical coaptation of the dorsal scapular nerve branches to the phrenic nerve was performed in a tension-free fashion using interrupted 9-0 Vicryl sutures under an operating microscope. The coaptation was reinforced with a nerve tube, and fibrin sealant was applied circumferentially to stabilize the repair (Fig. 2). Hemostasis and Closure

The omohyoid muscle was reapproximated. During wound inspection, a slow leak of milky fluid was noted in the region of the lymph node dissection, suggestive of a chyle leak (Fig. 3). This persisted despite multiple rounds of irrigation. The suspected site was identified carefully at the site of lymph node removal and subsequently ligated with 3-0 Vicryl suture and reinforced by fibrin glue, resulting in cessation of leakage. Meticulous hemostasis was obtained. Layered closure was then performed. FIG. 3.Intraoperative photograph demonstrating a right-sided chyle leak during dorsal scapular-to-phrenic nerve transfer. Postoperative Findings

Final pathological analysis revealed a benign lymph node. The patient tolerated the procedure well with stable intraoperative neuromonitoring.

Discussion

Observations

Chyle leak in cervical procedures most commonly occurs due to inadvertent injury to the TD or its tributaries. The TD typically terminates at the left venous angle, making left-sided surgeries more prone to chyle leaks. However, anatomical variations can lead to the presence of an aberrant right-sided lymphatic drainage system, which may account for chyle leaks occurring during right-sided neck surgeries.2

Chyle leaks are significantly more common on the left side, with right-sided occurrences accounting for less than one-quarter of cases.3,4 As a result, many surgeons may underestimate or overlook the risk of chyle leak as a potential complication of right-sided neck surgery.

In this case, an unexpected chyle leak was identified during a right-sided nerve transfer. This suggests an anatomical variation, possibly involving a right-sided lymphatic duct or an accessory branch of the TD. Early recognition and management are crucial, as persistent chyle leaks can lead to nutritional deficiencies, electrolyte imbalances, and an increased risk of infection.5 If combined with a pleural breach (pneumothorax), this can result in chylothorax.

Lessons

Intraoperative identification and immediate ligation of the leaking duct remain the most effective strategies. Figure 3 is unique in showing an intraoperative chyle leak to help with intraoperative recognition. The duct itself may not be visible, so suturing is based on the location of chyle egress. This should always be reinforced with fibrin glue since suturing may not be watertight. A structured approach to postoperative chyle leak based on severity and response to initial treatment is crucial. Conservative treatment is the first-line option, involving dietary modifications (low-fat, medium-chain, triglyceride-rich diets), total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogs (such as octreotide), and measures to reduce lymph flow, including low-fat diets, bowel rest, and parenteral nutrition.6–8

Lymphatic vessels are subtle and not easily identifiable. Typically, there is no obvious hole to close. The leak is identified when a milky fluid is seen in the operative field and careful suction may reveal the source. A stitch can be placed around it, preferably a figure-of-eight stitch. This has to be supplemented with some sort of glue (we prefer fibrin glue) to ensure watertight closure. This should then be observed for a few minutes before more superficial layers are finally closed, to make sure there is no ongoing low-flow leak. Adjunct techniques noted below become necessary when initial measures fail.

Surgical intervention becomes necessary in high-output leaks (> 600 mL/day) or cases unresponsive to conservative management. Additional techniques, including muscle flaps, fibrin glue application, and TD embolization, may be required for refractory cases.9–11

Plutecki et al. conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis to better understand the anatomy and variation of the TD and cisterna chyli (CC), which are important structures in the lymphatic system.12 They reviewed 40 studies identified from several major medical databases, following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) and CATAM (Critical Appraisal Tool for Anatomical Meta-analysis) guidelines. Their results revealed that the TD most commonly terminates at the left venous angle (45.29%) and usually does so as a single vessel (78.41%), although it can also split into two or more branches in about 22% of cases. The CC was present in approximately 55.5% of the population and most often located between the L1 and L2 vertebrae, with a mean length of 18.25 mm. These anatomical variations are clinically significant, as they increase the risk of complications like chyle leaks during thoracic and neck surgeries.12

Novegno et al. reported on a 42-year-old man who underwent a standard right anterior cervical discectomy and fusion using the Cloward approach for a C6–7 disc herniation.13 On postoperative day 5, he developed a delayed prevertebral chyle collection, presenting with mild breathing and swallowing difficulties. The condition was successfully managed conservatively with bed rest, a low-fat diet, and drainage pouch placement, leading to complete resolution of the fluid collection.13

Hammad et al. presented the case of a 51-year-old man who was diagnosed with papillary thyroid carcinoma with bilateral cervical lymph node metastasis and left vocal cord palsy.14 He underwent total thyroidectomy, bilateral neck dissection, and injection laryngoplasty. Intraoperatively, a right-sided chyle leak was identified in the lower neck. This was successfully controlled with nonabsorbable sutures and bilateral drains. However, on postoperative day 2, milky fluid was noted bilaterally from the drains, confirming persistent chyle leak. The patient was managed conservatively with bed rest, a fat-free diet, and strict monitoring of fluid balance. The drains’ output gradually decreased, and the leak was resolved by postoperative day 7. He was discharged without further complications.14

In comparing our case to the above-reported cases, no diet changes, activity restrictions, or drain placement was necessary. This highlights the variation in management despite similar positive outcomes.

Anatomical studies indicate that variations in lymphatic duct anatomy are common and clinically significant, increasing the risk of complications during neck surgery.12,15 They can be encountered by the spine surgeon or the brachial plexus surgeon and need to be promptly recognized and managed.
