Knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward bowel preparation for sedation-assisted colonoscopy among patients with digestive disorders: a structural equation modeling analysis
Authors: Junjun Xu, Ziyan Zhang, Liuyang Yu, Yi Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding bowel preparation prior to sedation-assisted colonoscopy among patients with digestive disorders.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted at Peking University People’s Hospital between June and August 2025. Data were collected through structured questionnaires.ResultsA total of 459 participants had undergone sedation-assisted colonoscopy. The majority of these patients were female (58.8%), most respondents were undergoing the procedure for the first time (66.9%), primarily due to gastrointestinal symptoms (47.3%). Their knowledge, attitudes, and practices scores were 9.61 ± 6.60 (possible range: 0–18), 30.72 ± 4.29 (possible range: 8–40), and 35.09 ± 5.99 (possible range: 8–40), respectively. SEM analysis showed that knowledge was significantly associated with attitude (β = 0.424, P = 0.006) and practice (β = 0.555, P = 0.025).ConclusionPatients with digestive disorders demonstrated limited knowledge and suboptimal attitudes despite generally favorable bowel preparation practices, with knowledge being most strongly associated with both attitudinal and behavioral outcomes. To enhance overall bowel preparation effectiveness, future interventions should prioritize structured, evidence-based educational strategies that target knowledge deficits and cognitive barriers, particularly among first-time or lower-literacy patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05007-w.

Background

Gastrointestinal endoscopy remains the gold standard for diagnosing and screening digestive disorders due to its superior diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic capability [1]. The global burden of gastrointestinal malignancies is substantial, with colorectal cancer ranking third in incidence worldwide with 1.93 million new cases in 2022, while gastric cancer accounted for 968,350 new cases that same year [2]. China exemplifies this burden, with 478,500 confirmed gastric cancer cases in 2020 accounting for 43.9% of global cases [3] and a projected 592,000 colorectal cancer cases by 2022 [4]. Despite the availability of effective diagnostic modalities, screening adherence remains critically low, with rates of only 12.9% and 17.4% for colorectal and gastric cancer screening respectively among high-risk populations in China, contributing to delayed diagnosis and reduced survival outcomes [5]. In gastrointestinal endoscopy, sedation practices and bowel preparation quality significantly influence both participation rates and diagnostic outcomes [6, 7]. While adequate sedation improves patient tolerance and is utilized in over 90% of gastrointestinal endoscopy procedures in Europe, it is employed in only 48.3% of procedures in China [8].

Bowel preparation presents an even greater challenge, with inadequate preparation occurring in approximately 18–35% of colonoscopies, leading to increased procedural times, prolonged examination duration, and the need for repeat procedures [9]. When inadequate preparation rates exceed 13%, colonoscopy becomes less cost-effective than alternative screening methods [10]. Patients frequently report low adherence due to poor palatability of cleansing agents, excessive fluid requirements, and complex protocols, while inadequate health literacy compounds these problems by reducing comprehension of preparation instructions [11]. Current research has primarily focused on optimizing preparation regimens and pharmaceutical approaches, while limited attention has been given to comprehensively assessing behavioral and cognitive determinants of adherence, including patient-specific risk factors such as health literacy, socioeconomic status, and comorbidities [12, 13]. This gap is particularly evident in sedation-assisted colonoscopy, where systematic evaluation of patients’ preparation-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices remains largely lacking despite evidence that propofol-mediated sedation improves both patient satisfaction and colonoscopy quality indicators [14, 15].

The Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) framework provides a validated model for evaluating health-related behaviors, in which knowledge represents the cognitive foundation, attitudes serve as intrinsic motivators, and practices reflect observable behaviors [16, 17]. KAP studies have been widely applied in chronic disease management and cancer screening [18, 19]. However, current investigations have primarily focused on patients’ general knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward gastrointestinal endoscopy procedures themselves, with studies examining overall patient perspectives, procedural anxiety, and satisfaction measures [20]. Few investigations have systematically explored KAP specifically related to bowel preparation quality and adherence, particularly among patients undergoing sedation-assisted colonoscopy in China. Addressing this gap has important clinical implications.

Therefore, this study was designed as a descriptive KAP investigation to explore the knowledge, attitudes, and practices concerning bowel preparation among patients undergoing sedation-assisted colonoscopy (referred to herein as ‘painless colonoscopy’, consistent with established Chinese clinical terminology for propofol-based sedated colonoscopy) in China. The primary aim was to characterize behavioral and cognitive patterns within this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Peking University People’s Hospital between June and August 2025. Participants were recruited consecutively during the study period using a convenience sampling approach. Eligible participants met the following criteria: (1) age of 18 years or older; (2) scheduled to undergo colonoscopy under sedation. In this study, the term ‘sedation-assisted colonoscopy’ refers specifically to propofol-based sedated colonoscopy (locally termed ‘painless colonoscopy’), consistent with its standard usage in Chinese clinical practice. All participants received bowel preparation according to the standardized institutional protocol of Peking University People’s Hospital, including uniform instructions regarding diet restriction, laxative type, dosage, and timing. Individuals were excluded if they had any of the following conditions: (1) contraindications to bowel preparation, such as severe cardiopulmonary disease or intestinal obstruction; (2) cognitive impairment or significant communication difficulties that might interfere with questionnaire completion; or (3) a history of colorectal surgery or an existing diagnosis of inflammatory bowel disease. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University People’s Hospital, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The questionnaire was completed anonymously. Although optional contact information could be provided for follow-up clarification, no identifiable personal information was used in the analysis. All data were de-identified prior to statistical processing, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board with appropriate data protection measures.

Questionnaire development

The questionnaire was developed based on Expert Consensus Opinion on Bowel Preparation for Colonoscopy (2023 version). Its content and structure were subsequently refined through expert consultation involving five clinicians, including three endoscopists (one associate chief physician and two attending physicians) and two anaesthesiologists (one associate chief physician and one attending physician). This iterative process ensured content validity and clinical relevance across all domains. A pilot test was conducted prior to the main survey. A total of 41 questionnaires were returned in the pre-experiment phase; one case failed the attention-check item and was excluded, resulting in 40 valid responses. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the questionnaire was 0.8943. The Cronbach’s α values for the knowledge, attitude, and practice domains were 0.9154, 0.7115, and 0.8673, respectively, indicating acceptable to excellent internal consistency.

The finalized instrument, administered in Chinese, comprised four core sections: demographic information, knowledge assessment, attitude evaluation, and practice reporting. Of these, three sections were quantitatively scored. The knowledge domain consisted of 10 items, including one intentionally misleading item (Item 4) designed as a quality control measure. Item 4 was not included in the calculation of the total knowledge score. Questionnaires with incorrect responses to this attention-check item were excluded from the final analysis. The remaining nine items were scored on a three-point ordinal scale: 0 (“unclear”), 1 (“heard of”), and 2 (“very familiar”), yielding a total possible score ranging from 0 to 18. The attitude domain included eight items evaluated using a five-point Likert scale, where responses ranged from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), resulting in a cumulative score between 8 and 40. Similarly, the practice domain comprised eight items assessed on an identical five-point scale, with response options from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), also producing a total score of 8 to 40. The primary observation indicators of this study were the total scores of the knowledge, attitude, and practice domains, which were predefined to evaluate patients’ cognitive, affective, and behavioral status regarding bowel preparation. The Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS) score was included as a secondary outcome to reflect objective bowel preparation quality. The questionnaire was electronically distributed through the Questionnaire Star platform and administered on the day of the scheduled colonoscopy. Participants completed the form in the waiting area under the guidance of a trained research physician assistant who provided standardized instructions to ensure consistency. Respondents achieving scores exceeding 80% of the maximum in each domain were classified as demonstrating adequate procedural knowledge, positive attitudes toward bowel preparation, and proactive health practices [21].

For context, the BBPS is a validated instrument used to quantify the quality of bowel cleansing during colonoscopy. It scores three segments of the colon-right, transverse, and left-on a scale from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating better mucosal visualization. The total BBPS score ranges from 0 to 9 and serves as a reliable endpoint for evaluating the effectiveness of bowel preparation protocols in both research and clinical settings. In this study, the total BBPS score was used as a continuous indicator to preserve statistical information and facilitate modeling, rather than dichotomizing adequacy based on segmental thresholds. The BBPS is freely available for academic and clinical research use, and its application in this study complied with its original publication and usage guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and KAP scores. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables, including individual item responses, were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The distribution of continuous variables was examined using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For non-normally distributed variables, nonparametric methods were applied. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two independent groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis H test was employed for comparisons involving three or more groups. These tests were used to assess variations in KAP scores across different demographic strata. Associations among the KAP components were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. To further identify factors independently associated with bowel preparation behaviors, logistic regression analyses were conducted. Univariate logistic regression was first performed to screen potential predictors of high practice scores. Variables with a p-value < 0.1 were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to adjust for confounding effects. Practice scores were dichotomized based on an 80% threshold of the maximum possible score, a commonly used cut-off in KAP studies to indicate adequate practice levels. Continuous modeling (e.g., linear or quantile regression) was considered but not pursued for two reasons: (1) the dichotomized approach yields directly interpretable odds ratios aligned with the study’s aim of identifying factors associated with adequate versus inadequate preparation behavior; and (2) the 80%-of-maximum threshold corresponds to a clinically meaningful distinction that facilitates comparison with published KAP benchmarks [22]. Sample size consideration: No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because this was a cross-sectional convenience sample of consecutive eligible patients during the study period. For the multivariable logistic regression, we evaluated adequacy using the events-per-variable (EPV) rule of thumb (approximately 5–10 events per parameter). The smaller outcome group in the dichotomized practice analysis included 94 participants, and the final multivariable model included 16 parameters, yielding an EPV of 5.9, which meets the minimum recommended threshold. Additionally, with the observed sample size of 459, the primary Spearman correlation estimates (r = 0.388–0.645) are associated with 95% confidence intervals of approximately ± 0.09, indicating sufficient precision for the study’s descriptive and exploratory aims. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to examine the hypothesized pathways linking demographic variables, KAP dimensions, and bowel preparation quality. The BBPS score was included in the model as an outcome measure to evaluate its association with knowledge, attitude, and practice components. In this study, logistic regression was conducted to identify predictors of proactive preparation behavior (practice domain), whereas BBPS was modeled separately in SEM as an objective indicator of bowel preparation quality rather than as a behavioral outcome. Model fit was assessed using the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), and incremental fit index (IFI). The structural model in this study is a just-identified (saturated) path model, in which the number of estimated parameters equals the number of observed variances and covariances. Consequently, the model produces a mathematically perfect fit by construction (e.g., RMSEA = 0, CFI = 1), and conventional goodness-of-fit indices do not provide meaningful diagnostic information in this context. The path directionality was specified a priori based on the established KAP theoretical framework rather than derived empirically, and alternative model structures were not formally tested. Therefore, the SEM findings should be interpreted as exploratory path estimates consistent with the theoretical model rather than as confirmatory structural evidence. Parameter estimation was performed using the maximum likelihood method. To enhance robustness of indirect effect estimates, bias-corrected bootstrap procedures with 5,000 resamples were applied to obtain 95% confidence intervals. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance across all analyses. For variables involving more than two subgroups in Table 1, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni-adjusted P values to control for multiple testing.

Results

Initially, this study collected a total of 510 questionnaires. After quality control, exclusions comprised: 2 cases with age anomalies; 9 cases failing trap questions; 19 cases showing response bias (10 all-B and 9 all-D responses in practice section); 17 cases with Q14-15 inconsistencies; and 4 unidentifiable cases. Ultimately, 459 valid questionnaires remained, with an effective rate of 90.00%. Participants were recruited consecutively during the study period, and the questionnaire was administered on-site prior to the procedure.

Demographic characteristics

This study included 459 patients undergoing sedation-assisted colonoscopy, predominantly female (58.8%), urban residents (89.1%), with bachelor’s degrees or higher (44.9%), and monthly household incomes ≥¥10,000 (49.5%). Most were undergoing their first procedure (66.9%) for gastrointestinal symptoms (47.3%). Their knowledge, attitudes, and practices scores were 9.61 ± 6.60 (possible range: 0–18), 30.72 ± 4.29 (possible range: 8–40), and 35.09 ± 5.99 (possible range: 8–40), respectively. Detailed information was shown in Table 1. And the corrected P values are presented in Table S1.

Knowledge, attitudes, and practices dimensions

The distribution of knowledge dimensions showed that the three questions with the highest number of participants choosing the ‘Uncertain’ option were ‘Knowledge that bowel preparation typically requires specific laxatives.’ (K4) with 40.96%, ‘Understanding the correct administration method of laxatives.’ (K5) with 35.95%, and ‘Understanding that laxative dosage requires individualized adjustment based on medical conditions, bowel habits and professional advice.’ (K8) with 32.68% (Table S2).

Responses to the attitude dimension showed that 26.14% strongly agreed and 23.75% agreed that the laxative taste or administration method of the laxative made it difficult for them to persevere with the bowel preparation (A5), 15.03% strongly agreed and 32.24% agreed that the bowel preparation for sedation-assisted colonoscopy is overly complex and difficult to complete (A2), 9.80% were very concerned and 16.56% were concerned that laxatives might cause other physical discomfort, such as bloating or excessive diarrhoea (A6) (Table S3).

Responses to the practice dimension showed that 17.43% rarely and 3.49% never educate themselves about bowel preparation procedures and precautions before undergoing sedation-assisted colonoscopy (P1), 14.60% rarely and 2.83% never adhere to medical staff’s instructions regarding laxative administration timing during preparation (P2), 12.85% rarely and 2.83% never modify their diet as advised (P3) (Table S4).

Correlations between KAP

Correlation analysis indicates that knowledge was positively correlated with attitude (r = 0.425, P < 0.001), practice (r = 0.645, P < 0.001), and BBPS (r = 0.129, P = 0.006). Attitude was positively correlated with practice (r = 0.338, P < 0.001) and BBPS (r = 0.133, P = 0.004). Practice was also positively correlated with BBPS (r = 0.151, P = 0.001) (Table 2).

Structural equation modeling analysis

The structural equation model with standardized path estimates is presented in Fig. 1. As the model is just-identified, all fit indices reach their theoretical maximum (CFI = 1.000, TLI = 1.000, RMSEA = 0.000, IFI = 1.000) by construction, and should not be interpreted as evidence of superior model fit. Knowledge was significantly associated with attitude (β = 0.424, P = 0.006) and practice (β = 0.555, P = 0.025), whereas the association between attitude and practice did not reach statistical significance (β = 0.063, P = 0.081) (Table 4).

Discussion

Patients with digestive disorders demonstrated inadequate knowledge and suboptimal attitudes, despite generally proactive practices regarding bowel preparation, highlighting a disconnect between cognitive understanding and behavioral adherence. Targeted, multi-modal patient education strategies should be integrated into routine pre-endoscopy workflows to improve knowledge retention, strengthen attitudes, and ultimately enhance the quality of bowel preparation outcomes. Notably, 66.9% of participants were undergoing colonoscopy for the first time, which may partly explain the relatively low knowledge scores observed, as prior procedural exposure is likely to enhance familiarity with preparation requirements.

The analysis revealed a pronounced association between procedural knowledge and behavioral adherence in bowel preparation, a relationship that remained consistent even after accounting for attitudinal variables. This finding challenges the conventional KAP paradigm, in which affective disposition is typically assumed to mediate the association between knowledge and action. Evidence from randomized trials suggests that improving cognitive clarity through targeted procedural education-whether via structured physician counseling or audiovisual re-education-substantially enhances preparation quality, independent of attitudinal orientation [23, 24]. In this case, the pathway from understanding to behavior appears to operate with relative autonomy, suggesting that tasks involving detailed procedural steps may depend more on cognitive clarity than on motivational alignment.

Attitudinal measures, while moderately correlated with behavior at the bivariate level, did not retain predictive significance in the multivariate framework. This attenuated role implies that positive orientation toward the procedure-such as trust in physicians or belief in the necessity of colonoscopy-does not suffice to ensure compliance when the practical demands of preparation are involved. Prior research has demonstrated that educational interventions targeting understanding of the procedure itself, rather than simply cultivating positive attitudes, result in significantly improved adherence outcomes, including up to 98.7% adequate preparation rates when procedural instructions were emphasized [23].

It should be noted that the structural model employed was just-identified, and the reported path estimates represent exploratory associations consistent with the KAP framework rather than confirmatory causal evidence. Alternative model structures were not evaluated. The SEM model indicated that knowledge showed the strongest association with both attitude and practice among the measured variables. Attitude demonstrated only a weak indirect pathway, suggesting that cognitive understanding was more strongly associated with patient behavior in bowel preparation than affective orientation in this cross-sectional model. Notably, the association between attitude and practice did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.081), and no significant indirect effect was observed, further supporting cautious interpretation of this pathway. This finding suggests that patient knowledge level may be an important correlate of compliance and preparation quality, though longitudinal evidence is needed to confirm any directional relationship. This finding is consistent with previous studies highlighting the critical role of patient knowledge in improving adherence to bowel preparation protocols [12, 23].

Item-level analysis of the knowledge dimension highlighted systematic gaps. Questions involving laxative timing, fluid intake protocols, and stool consistency were frequently answered incorrectly or left blank. These are not marginal details but foundational components of effective preparation. The consistency of such errors across participants points to structural deficiencies in how preparatory information is conveyed. Research comparing different educational modalities has confirmed that graphical or audiovisual instruction formats significantly outperform text-only materials in conveying detailed procedural information [25].

By contrast, attitudinal responses demonstrated an uneven distribution. General confidence in colonoscopy and willingness to cooperate with clinicians were common, yet a sizeable proportion expressed concern about the unpleasantness of laxatives or doubts about completing the preparation correctly. Such ambivalence mirrors findings from studies where patients exhibited high approval of the procedure but lacked confidence in execution due to unclear instruction or prior negative experiences [26]. The gap between endorsement and enactment becomes especially salient in preparation tasks that lack immediate feedback.

In the behavioral domain, self-reported adherence was high for actions initiated or monitored by clinical staff, such as attending pre-procedure consultations or following explicit dosing instructions. However, activities requiring independent initiative-such as checking stool clarity or reviewing preparation guidelines without prompting-were less consistently reported. Similar patterns were observed in trials using computer-based patient education (CBE), where adherence to unmonitored steps declined compared to nurse-guided education, even when BBPS outcomes were non-inferior overall [27].

Demographic differentials revealed further gradients of vulnerability. Participants with higher education, greater income, or urban residency scored more favorably across knowledge and practice dimensions. These disparities may reflect differential access to medical instruction, prior exposure to clinical environments, and baseline health literacy. Observational studies have found that Medicaid recipients, patients with psychiatric illness, or those with prior inadequate preparation episodes are at substantially higher risk of poor bowel prep outcomes [28]. That first-time colonoscopy participants performed consistently worse than their experienced counterparts across all domains underscores the value of procedural familiarity as a scaffold for behavior.

The finding that BBPS scores were also higher among experienced patients suggests a reinforcing loop: successful past performance may enhance future compliance by anchoring expectations and reducing ambiguity. In this study, associations between KAP dimensions and BBPS were primarily evaluated within the structural model rather than through direct pairwise subgroup comparisons, and therefore should be interpreted as model-based associations. This interpretation is supported by evidence showing that patients with prior exposure to colonoscopy preparation were more confident, more adherent, and achieved better cleansing scores-especially when prior feedback was linked to educational reinforcement [29].

From an implementation standpoint, these findings point to several practical adjustments. Educational materials should be restructured to prioritize procedural clarity. This means moving beyond abstract descriptions or passive instructions toward materials that explicitly depict timing, sequencing, and observable outcomes. Studies have shown that smartphone-based audiovisual re-education can significantly improve patient satisfaction and bowel cleanliness scores, with mean BBPS improvements exceeding 1 point compared to controls [26].

For first-time participants, relying on static handouts or brief consultations is unlikely to suffice. Structured education sessions-whether in-person, telehealth-based, or delivered through mobile platforms-should allow space for patient questions, rehearsal of key steps, and clarification of ambiguous instructions. Interventions using intensive education programs have consistently shown improved knowledge acquisition and preparation quality, even in low-literacy populations [23]. Providing patients with explicit feedback on their preparation adequacy, using clear descriptive terms rather than numerical scales, may similarly enhance understanding and reinforce adherence behaviors in future procedures.

Given the observed variation by socioeconomic status, uniform strategies are unlikely to yield equitable outcomes. Patients with limited formal education or from rural settings may benefit from guided preparation delivered via community health workers or through primary care integration. Evidence suggests that such tailored interventions improve adherence and cleanliness scores among vulnerable populations, particularly when simplified educational tools are employed [24].

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center cross-sectional investigation using convenience sampling at a tertiary hospital in an urban setting, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other regions or healthcare environments, particularly rural or primary care settings with differing patient demographics, healthcare access, and health literacy levels. Because of the cross-sectional design, the directionality of the SEM paths was specified based on the KAP theoretical framework, and potential reverse or bidirectional relationships cannot be excluded. As this study was designed as a KAP survey without control or comparison groups, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive associations within the surveyed population rather than as comparative effects specific to sedation-assisted procedures. Second, although efforts were made to ensure questionnaire reliability through expert validation and inclusion of a quality control item, self-reported data remain subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, which may affect the accuracy of reported knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Third, while the sample size was adequate for the statistical analyses performed, subgroup sizes for certain variables (e.g., prior bowel preparation experience or therapeutic indications) were relatively small, potentially limiting the statistical power to detect nuanced associations. Additionally, subgroup comparisons in Table 1 were exploratory in nature; Bonferroni-corrected P values for post hoc pairwise comparisons are presented in Table S1, and these findings should still be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional and convenience-sampled design. Dichotomizing practice scores may have reduced statistical information compared with treating the variable as continuous; however, this approach was adopted to facilitate interpretability and consistency with commonly used KAP thresholds. Future analyses using continuous or ordinal regression approaches are encouraged to validate and extend these findings. Comorbidities were summarized descriptively; however, they were not included in the multivariable models because several categories had small cell counts, which could lead to unstable estimates. Finally, no formal a priori power calculation was performed, and the sample size was determined by the recruitment period; therefore, some subgroup analyses may have been underpowered. In addition, although bootstrap confidence intervals were calculated for indirect effects in the SEM analysis, some intervals were relatively wide and crossed zero, suggesting potential instability in certain pathways that warrants cautious interpretation. Similarly, certain logistic regression estimates, particularly those for small subgroups such as BMI < 18.5 (OR = 2.058, 95% CI: 0.333–12.725), showed wide confidence intervals, likely attributable to small cell sizes, and should therefore be interpreted with caution as potentially unstable estimates. Bowel preparation adequacy was evaluated using the total BBPS score rather than the commonly used adequacy rate (each segment ≥ 2), which may limit direct comparability with some clinical benchmarks. Future longitudinal or prospective studies are warranted to clarify temporal relationships and to further explore potential causal pathways among knowledge, attitudes, and bowel preparation behaviors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, patients with digestive disorders demonstrated limited knowledge and suboptimal attitudes, yet maintained relatively proactive practices concerning bowel preparation prior to sedation-assisted colonoscopy. To enhance bowel preparation quality and patient outcomes, targeted educational interventions-particularly those tailored to first-time examinees and low-literacy populations-should be integrated into routine pre-procedural care, leveraging digital platforms and physician-led counseling to reinforce cognitive understanding and improve behavioral adherence. The structural model further suggests that knowledge is strongly associated with both attitudes and behavioral compliance; however, given the cross-sectional design, these relationships should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects, particularly with regard to the attitude–practice pathway. Overall, the findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of causal pathways.
