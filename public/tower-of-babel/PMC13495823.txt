Predictive Biomarkers in Metastatic Colorectal Cancer Patients Treated With Bevacizumab: A Turkish Oncology Group (TOG) Study
Authors: Büsra Akay Hacan, Fatma Gizem Sonugür, Müge Öçal, Cansu Babahan, Samira Abgarmi, Ülkü Yalçıntaş Arslan, Mehmet Ali Şendur, Ahmet Demirkazık, Güngör Utkan, Hakan Akbulut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEfforts to improve outcomes for patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) treated with bevacizumab are limited by the lack of validated predictive biomarkers. To overcome this challenge, our study prospectively assessed the combined prognostic and predictive significance of circulating angiogenic biomarkers and T‐cell subsets. Eighty‐eight patients with unresectable mCRC were prospectively enrolled. Pre‐treatment serum levels of VEGF, bFGF, PDGF‐B, and endothelin‐1 (ET‐1) were measured by ELISA, nitric oxide by colorimetric assay, and T‐cell subsets by flow cytometry. Bevacizumab trough levels and anti‐bevacizumab antibodies were assessed by ELISA. Higher baseline levels of proangiogenic factors, including VEGF, PDGF‐B, and ET‐1, were associated with improved survival. Based on these findings, we developed a novel Angiogenesis Index (AI) using the cut‐off values for VEGF, PDGF‐B, and ET‐1. This AI was a significant predictor of efficacy for bevacizumab‐based therapy for both PFS and OS. Furthermore, bevacizumab trough levels exceeding 25 μg/mL on day 14 were associated with improved OS. Likewise, patients with lower baseline circulating FoxP3+ regulatory T cells (Treg) tended to have improved survival. The AI, bevacizumab trough levels, and baseline Treg levels are promising biomarkers for predicting efficacy and refining patient selection for bevacizumab therapy in mCRC.Keywords: bevacizumab, colorectal cancer, endothelin‐1, PDGF‐B, VEGF

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is still one of the leading causes of cancer‐related deaths worldwide. Though metastatic disease (mCRC) is found in 20%–25% of patients at diagnosis, a significant proportion of patients with localized tumors eventually develop metastases. While systemic chemotherapy is the mainstay of mCRC treatment, the introduction of molecularly targeted agents over the past two decades has transformed clinical management [1].

Angiogenesis plays a pivotal role in the progression of CRC. The vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway serves as a primary driver. Elevated VEGF levels are linked to advanced disease stages and poor prognosis, making VEGF an essential therapeutic target [2, 3]. This insight resulted in the development of bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits VEGF [4]. A landmark study by Hurwitz et al. showed that combining bevacizumab with standard chemotherapy significantly improved survival, establishing its importance in first‐line mCRC treatment [5]. In addition to its promoting effects on angiogenesis, VEGF contributes to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by limiting the recruitment of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and increasing Tregs [6].

Bevacizumab is a well‐established treatment for mCRC, but tumors can bypass VEGF blockade by activating other pro‐angiogenic pathways, such as basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF), and endothelin‐1 (ET‐1) [7, 8, 9]. This biological complexity makes it difficult to identify a reliable predictive biomarker, a major challenge in anti‐VEGF therapy. Unlike anti‐EGFR treatments, where RAS and BRAF mutation status help guide patient selection, bevacizumab is mostly used based on experience. Because of this, many patients may suffer side effects such as hypertension, bleeding, and thromboembolism without clear clinical benefit.

Instead of focusing on a single biomarker, a more comprehensive approach would be valuable to address this critical unmet need. In this prospective study, we aimed to evaluate the prognostic and predictive value of circulating biomarkers and immune cell subsets in patients with mCRC treated with standard chemotherapy plus bevacizumab. We measured VEGF levels and bevacizumab dip concentrations, as well as key molecules from alternative angiogenic pathways (PDGF‐β, ET‐1, bFGF, nitric oxide (NO)), all of which have been associated with survival [3, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Simultaneously, we assessed circulating T cell subsets (CD4+, CD8+, and Tregs) to evaluate the immunomodulatory role of VEGF. The primary goal of the study was to develop a multiparameter marker to predict treatment outcomes. A second important objective was to create a new angiogenesis index (AI) using this data, thereby identifying patients who would benefit most from bevacizumab therapy.

Methods

Patients

The consecutive patients who meet all of the following criteria were included in the study: histologically confirmed metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC); age between 18 and 70 years; life expectancy greater than 3 months; ECOG performance status of 2 or less; absence of known renal disease; no contraindications to bevacizumab in combination with chemotherapy; and adequate bone marrow, liver, and kidney function as defined below: WBC > 3000/μL; platelet count ≥ 100,000/μL; hemoglobin ≥ 10 g/dL; ALT/AST ≤ 5 times the upper limit of normal; ALP ≤ 2.5 times the upper limit of normal (≤ 10 times if bone metastasis is present); serum bilirubin ≤ 1.5 times the upper limit of normal; serum calcium ≤ 12 mg/dL; and serum creatinine ≤ 2 mg/dL. Previous chemotherapy was permitted, with or without EGFR monoclonal antibodies, where applicable. The exclusion criteria were as follows: brain metastases; potentially resectable disease; major surgery or trauma within the last 2 months; prior treatment with bevacizumab or other antiangiogenic agents.

All patients underwent comprehensive baseline assessments, including physical examination, complete blood count, liver and kidney function tests, chest X‐ray, and abdominal CT scan. During the study, physical examination and routine blood tests were performed before each chemotherapy cycle. A CT scan was performed every two cycles to assess treatment response. Following informed consent, venous blood samples were collected at baseline (1 day before the first treatment cycle) and at Week 8 (after two cycles). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated within 3 h of collection, and serum and plasma samples were stored at −80°C until analysis. To assess the lowest concentrations, bevacizumab blood levels were measured on Day 15 (immediately before the second dose).

Chemotherapy regimens, either FOLFOX, FOLFIRI, or FOLFIRINOX, were selected at the discretion of the attending physician. Bevacizumab was administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg every 2 weeks in conjunction with chemotherapy. Treatment continued until disease progression or intolerable toxicity occurred. Routine prophylactic use of granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor was not allowed. Treatment responses were assessed every four cycles using the RECIST criteria.

Angiogenic Factor Assays

We determined the serum concentrations of VEGF‐A, PDGF‐B, ET‐1, and bFGF using a commercial immunoassay kit (FineTest Biotech Inc., CO). Serum samples, previously stored at −80°C, were brought to room temperature before analysis. Each sample (40 μL) was pipetted into designated wells, and then 10 μL of the appropriate detection antibody was added. To create a standard curve, serial dilutions of standard proteins were prepared and added in 50 μL portions to the designated wells. Then, 50 μL of streptavidin‐HRP conjugate was added to all wells, and the plate was incubated at 37°C to ensure optimal antibody–antigen interaction. After incubation, unbound substances were removed by washing. The colorimetric reaction was initiated by the addition of TMB substrate and stopped by the addition of hydrochloric acid. Absorption was recorded at 450 nm, with 650 nm used as the reference wavelength. Analyte concentrations were determined by extrapolating absorption values obtained from standard curves constructed using reference standards. Detection limits for the analyses were as follows: VEGF‐A and PDGF‐B, 31.25–2000 pg/mL; bFGF, 12.5–800 pg/mL; and ET‐1, 1.25–80 pg/mL.

NO Assay

Plasma NO levels were measured quantitatively using a commercial colorimetric NO assay kit that uses nitrate reductase and the Griess reagent method (BioVision, CA). Briefly, a two‐step colorimetric method to indirectly measure NO levels by quantifying its stable metabolites, nitrate and nitrite follows: In the first step, the Nitrate Reductase enzyme converts all nitrate in the sample to nitrite, combining the two metabolites into a single measurable form. Next, Griess Reagents are added, reacting with the total nitrite content to form a dark purple azo compound. The intensity of this color is then measured at 450 nm to accurately determine the amount of NO in the plasma sample. The detection limit for the assay is approximately 1 nmol nitrite/well.

Bevacizumab Quantification and Anti‐Bevacizumab Antibody (ABA) Assays

Bevacizumab concentrations were determined using a commercial SHIKARI Q‐BEVA kit (Matriks Biotek, TR). In summary, samples and standards were initially incubated in wells pre‐coated with VEGF, followed by the addition of biotin‐labeled VEGF. After the washing step, a TMB substrate was added to initiate enzymatic color development, and the wells were then incubated with an HRP‐conjugated probe. The reaction was terminated by adding an acid solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

ABA in patient sera were detected using a commercial ELISA kit (SHIKARI S‐ATB, Matriks Biotek, TR). According to the manufacturer's protocol, 25 μL of serum, negative controls, and positive controls were added to a pre‐coated 96‐well plate and incubated for 60 min. After washing, the wells were incubated for an additional 60 min at room temperature with 100 μL of peroxidase‐labeled antibody conjugate. A second wash was performed before adding the TMB substrate to initiate the colorimetric reaction. The reaction was then stopped with HCl. Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm. Samples were considered positive for ABA if the ratio of the OD values of the samples to the OD value of the negative control was greater than three.

Flow Cytometry– T Cell Subset Quantification

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from whole blood samples within 3 h of collection using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. Two separate staining panels were used for T cell immunophenotyping. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) were counted by staining samples for surface CD4, surface CD25, and intracellular FOXP3 using a commercial kit (MACS Human Treg Detection Kit, CD4/CD25/FOXP3) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In a separate panel, CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations were identified using fluorochrome‐conjugated monoclonal antibodies (Beckman Coulter, CA). Data collection was performed using a Navios EX flow cytometer, and analysis was performed using hardware‐compensated Kaluza software (Beckman Coulter, CA).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical comparisons of continuous variables were performed using non‐parametric tests. The Mann–Whitney U test for independent groups and the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for paired data were used. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted for the CD4+/CD8+ T cell ratio, Treg levels, and angiogenic factors to determine optimal prognostic thresholds. The cut‐off value was selected based on the maximum Youden index. For survival analysis, probability curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The log‐rank test was used to assess the statistical differences between these curves. To determine hazard ratios (HRs) and identify independent prognostic factors, a Cox proportional hazards regression was used. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Treatment Efficacy

Eighty‐eight patients with mCRC were enrolled over a two‐year period. Following the ethics committee's decision, patient recruitment began in October 2014 and continued through April 2017. Patients were followed until death or until the data cut‐off date. The last data update date for surviving patients was May 2025. Four patients were excluded after switching from bevacizumab to anti‐EGFR agents following the first cycle. One additional patient developed venous thrombosis after the first dose of bevacizumab and was withdrawn from the study for safety reasons. As a result, the final analysis included 83 patients who met all inclusion criteria. None of the patients had previously received bevacizumab or any other anti‐angiogenic agent. The median age was 61 years, with males comprising the majority (66%). Most patients were diagnosed with colon cancer (73%). Liver metastases were the most common site (67%), and 27% of patients had isolated unresectable liver metastases. Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

One third of the patients received bevacizumab plus chemotherapy as first‐line treatment, while the remaining patients received it as second‐line therapy. The objective response rate (ORR), including both complete and partial responses, was 31.9%. The median duration of response was 25 weeks (95% CI, 10–44 weeks). Disease stabilization was achieved in 61 patients (73.4%). The median PFS was 6.0 months, and the median OS was 15.0 months (Table S1).

Increased Angiogenic Factors and Lower Treg Cells Predict Better Prognosis

In addition to measuring serum concentrations of VEGF‐A, PDGF‐B, ET‐1, bFGF, and NO, immunological parameters—specifically the CD4/CD8 ratio and the proportion of Treg cells—were evaluated as potential biomarkers of treatment efficacy. Serum angiogenic factors were quantified using ELISA, and T cell subsets were analyzed by flow cytometry. Median values and cut‐off points based on the Youden Index for these biomarkers are summarized in Table S2. For survival analysis, Youden Index–derived thresholds were used to identify the optimal cut‐off for each angiogenic factor distinguishing survival outcomes. Similarly, cutoffs for the CD4/CD8 ratio and Treg cell percentage among PBMCs were established. These thresholds were closely consistent across all biomarkers (Table S2).

Patients with elevated levels of angiogenic factors generally had longer survival. Patients with VEGF concentrations above 140 pg/mL (VEGF_hi) showed significantly improved PFS and OS compared to those with lower VEGF levels (VEGF_lo) (Table S3, Figures 1 and 2). The VEGF_hi group had nearly double the PFS duration of the VEGF_lo group. Similarly, higher PDGF‐B and ET‐1 levels were associated with significantly better survival outcomes (Table S3, Figure 2). Although not statistically significant, patients with increased bFGF and NO levels also tended to have longer survival (Table S3). Likewise, patients with higher CD4/CD8 ratios and lower circulating Treg cells demonstrated a trend toward increased PFS and OS, though these differences were not significant (Table S3, Figure S1).

Increased Blood Bevacizumab Levels Correlated With Prolonged Survival

Serum bevacizumab levels were measured by ELISA in blood samples collected 14 days after the first dose (Bev2w, immediately before the second dose) and prior to the fifth administration (Bev10w). Fourteen days after the initial dose, bevacizumab concentrations ranged from 0 to 108 μg/mL. Patients with bevacizumab levels above 25 μg/mL after the first dose tend to have better PFS and experience significantly longer OS (Table S3, Figure S1).

ABA levels were assessed by ELISA in blood samples collected after the first and eighth bevacizumab doses. No antibodies were detected after the initial administration. By the third month of treatment, however, 10% of patients had developed anti‐bevacizumab antibodies. The presence of these antibodies was not associated with significant changes in PFS or OS (Table S3).

AI is a Significant Predictor of Survival

In our univariate analysis, we demonstrated that higher circulating angiogenic factor levels were associated with improved survival. Based on this analysis, we developed a composite AI. AI was calculated from serum levels of VEGF, PDGF‐β, and ET‐1, and patients received a score for each factor that exceeded its corresponding threshold. This resulted in a total score ranging from 0 to 3. Patients were then divided into two groups, namely, AI‐high (AI_hi, 2 or 3 points) and AI‐low (AI_lo, 0 or 1 points). Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that the AI_hi group experienced significantly longer PFS and OS than the AI_lo group (Table S3; Figure 3). Multivariate analysis further confirmed that AI was an independent and significant predictor of both PFS and OS (Table 2).

Discussion

Although the addition of bevacizumab to systemic chemotherapy remains a cornerstone in the treatment of mCRC, its variable effectiveness continues to present a significant clinical challenge. In our cohort the median OS for the entire group was 15 months. This outcome, slightly lower than some first‐line reports exceeding 24 months, is likely attributable to the fact that most participants received bevacizumab as a second‐line treatment and the exclusion of potentially resectable cases [1]. Efforts to optimize therapy and minimize toxicity depend on identifying reliable biomarkers that can predict which patients will benefit from adding bevacizumab to standard chemotherapy—a goal that remains largely unmet [12, 13]. Consequently, research has focused on molecules in the angiogenesis pathway, particularly VEGF, the direct target of bevacizumab. Elevated serum VEGF levels are generally considered a negative prognostic indicator in CRC patients treated with chemotherapy alone [3, 14]. However, its prognostic significance in patients treated with bevacizumab remains debated. While some studies associate high baseline VEGF levels with worse prognosis, others have observed a positive association between high VEGF and better PFS in patients treated with bevacizumab [15, 16]. This study presents a novel Index of Angiogenesis (AI) based on baseline VEGF, PDGF‐β, and ET‐1 levels to predict progression‐free and overall survival in patients with mCRC treated with bevacizumab. By integrating these angiogenic markers with bevacizumab trough levels and Treg cell subgroups, the investigation offers a multiparameter approach with the potential to improve patient selection and integrate potential immunotherapy applications in mCRC into treatment. Our findings regarding the prognostic value of the AI are further supported by the recent work of Moisuc et al. who demonstrated that integrating circulating angiogenic biomarkers with clinical parameters through personalized mathematical models can significantly enhance survival prediction in bevacizumab‐treated MCRC patients [17].

Tumor angiogenesis is regulated by multiple, overlapping pathways involving factors such as VEGF, PDGF, ET‐1 and Tie2. In a recent trial (TRAVASTIN study), plasma Tie2 was found to be a significant vascular response biomarker, with reduced levels correlating with improved progression‐free survival and serving as a potential guide for bevacizumab reuse in mCRC patients [18]. Interestingly, our data also suggests that while VEGF is a strong prognostic factor, its predictive value for bevacizumab efficacy might be enhanced when combined with other markers such as PDGF‐β and ET‐1. A key novelty of our study is the integration of multiple angiogenic factors into a composite AI to provide a more comprehensive and reliable assessment of tumor angiogenic activity for prognostic and predictive purposes. This approach was based on our previous work, in which a similarly constructed index was negatively correlated with prognosis in patients on chemotherapy alone [3]. However, the current study reveals that addition of bevacizumab to standard chemotherapy regimens has beneficial effect on patients with high proangiogenic factor levels. AI_high group benefitted more than AI_low group (Figure 3).

Beyond the primary biomarkers included in the AI, we observed that patients with higher baseline bFGF and NO levels, which take part in the angiogenesis process, showed a trend toward improved survival. However, this was not statistically significant (Table S3). The biological role of NO in colorectal cancer remains complex and highly concentration‐dependent: while low levels typically support tumor formation and angiogenesis, higher concentrations can exert cytotoxic effects [19]. Previously, we have shown that NO levels can predict chemotherapy response in mCRC patients [3]. Contrary to earlier reports reporting greater bevacizumab efficacy in patients with low serum NO, our findings indicated a non‐significant trend toward longer OS in those with higher NO levels.

Angiogenesis is known to modulate not only the vascular structure but also the tumor's immune microenvironment. VEGF plays a bidirectional pro‐tumorigenic role in the tumor microenvironment. In addition to its well‐known role in promoting angiogenesis, VEGF creates a highly immunosuppressive environment by inhibiting dendritic cell maturation and increasing Treg accumulation. Bevacizumab has been shown to increase the recruitment of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, previously suppressed by VEGF signaling via vascular endothelial cells [20, 21]. Consequently, the therapeutic benefits of bevacizumab extend beyond its anti‐angiogenic properties. Bevacizumab alleviates immunosuppression, possibly by neutralizing VEGF, and reduces intratumoral Treg populations and improves the effector function of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells [20, 22]. We found no significant association between the CD4+/CD8+ T cell ratio and survival outcomes [23]. In the present study, bevacizumab plus chemotherapy resulted in a significant increase in CD4+ T cells (p = 0.002) and a decrease in CD8+ T cells (p = 0.002). Furthermore, the proportion of circulating Treg decreased significantly (p = 0.006). Although not statistically significant, a trend was observed that patients with low pre‐treatment Treg levels (≤ 0.40%) had longer progression‐free and OS than those with high levels (> 0.40%). Reducing immunosuppressive Tregs may enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy and help overcome resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors. These results support the idea that combining VEGF antagonism with immune checkpoint blockade is a logical tactic to enhance antitumor activity.

Bevacizumab is a monoclonal antibody with complex pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties [24]. Therefore, there is considerable variability in the clinical efficacy of bevacizumab. Factors such as tumor burden and target affinity contribute to substantial inter‐patient differences, leading to variations in drug exposure and clinical response [25]. Recent evidence indicates that drug exposure—specifically the lowest concentration—is a key determinant of efficacy. For example, a previous study found that the lowest levels of bevacizumab were significantly associated with OS in patients with colorectal cancer [11]. We found that maintaining bevacizumab trough levels above 25 μg/mL was associated with superior survival outcomes. This finding may suggest a potential role for therapeutic drug monitoring in optimizing bevacizumab dosing. A recently initiated phase III trial (PHARBEVACOL trial) investigating bevacizumab‐based chemotherapy‐adapted dosing of bevacizumab in mCRC would help elucidate the role of optimizing bevacizumab dosing [26].

We also assessed the development of anti‐drug antibodies to bevacizumab in patients from the first through the eighth administration. During this initial three‐month period, about 10% of patients developed ABAs. While we observed no significant association between ABA formation and survival outcomes, these antibodies may contribute to treatment resistance by neutralizing bevacizumab or modifying its pharmacokinetics [27]. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the clinical implications of ABA development throughout the course of therapy.

From a modern precision oncology perspective, integrating our angiogenic profile with dynamic ctDNA tracking and inflammatory indices provides a critical prognostic framework for predicting bevacizumab efficacy in mCRC patients [28, 29]. In our data, we found that high baseline VEGF levels were associated with improved survival. Survival improved significantly in the AI_lo group as defined by our criteria. These results gain further significance when integrated with next‐generation liquid biopsy studies in the literature, as it has been reported that chromosomal instability (CIN) levels determined via cfDNA can classify patients with 92.3% accuracy, and that patients with high CIN levels benefit more from bevacizumab [30]. Furthermore, the observed decreases in methylation scores during treatment, reflecting vascular normalization, can be considered an indicator supporting the biological equivalent of our “AI_lo” profile. In addition to these genomic alterations, inflammatory markers such as the systemic immuno‐inflammation index and aspartate aminotransferase‐lymphocyte ratio index are independent indicators of poor prognosis that complement our analyses of Treg and bevacizumab trough levels in our study [27]. Specifically, the association of drug levels above 25 μg/mL and low baseline Treg levels with survival advantage confirms the complex interaction between the host immune response and the angiogenic pathway. In conclusion, these holistic data highlight the need for a multi‐omics strategy—a multifaceted approach ranging from genetic polymorphisms to functional imaging—to optimize patient selection and improve clinical outcomes in anti‐angiogenic therapy.

RAS/BRAF and MSI status are established factors for guiding chemotherapy and immunotherapy in mCRC [1]. However, they do not fully account for the dynamic tumor microenvironment or the pharmacological response to anti‐angiogenic agents. Bevacizumab improves survival in both the RAS/BRAF wild type and mutant mCRC patients; however, not all patients benefit equally. The new AI integrating VEGF, PDGF‐β, and ET‐1 might provide a functional output of the angiogenic driver that mutation status alone cannot capture. Immunotherapy is currently limited to patients with mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) or high microsatellite instability (MSI‐H) [31]. However, many mCRC patients fall into “Cluster 1” (CIN‐low), which is often characterized by the MSI‐H phenotype but may still exhibit lower basal methylation and different angiogenic needs [29]. Tregs are highly immunosuppressive and contribute to resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors [31]. Our findings demonstrate that lower pretreatment Treg levels are associated with a trend toward better survival and potentially define a “prepared” immune environment. High VEGF levels not only drive angiogenesis but also increase Treg numbers and disrupt dendritic cell maturation [6]. Identifying patients with high angiogenic potential using the AI can better predict whether patients may need VEGF antagonism to “reprogram” the microenvironment and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy. Effective bevacizumab treatment can lead to “vascular normalization”. Normalization of vascular structure facilitates better penetration of cytotoxic T cells, potentially transforming “cold” tumors, which might be disregarded based on RAS/BRAF status, into “hot” tumors responsive to immunotherapy [32].

Our study has some limitations. Firstly, the small number of patients reduces the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Therefore, this reduces the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the inclusion of both first‐line and second line patients introduces some heterogeneity. Although the timing of bevacizumab administration in mCRC patients is not particularly important for clinical outcomes, the fact that some of our patients received it as first‐line and others as second‐line treatment is considered a limitation. Given the prospective observational nature of our study, although it shows a strong clinical association between biomarkers and survival outcomes, this association needs to be validated in prospective studies, especially for the clinical use of the new AI we created, to provide further insights.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that Tregs and bevacizumab trough levels are valuable parameters that warrant further evaluation in larger, multi‐center trials, especially within the context of the immunotherapy era. The proposed AI represents a promising tool for predicting bevacizumab efficacy in mCRC. However, further prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and establish evidence‐based guidelines for clinical practice.

Figure S1: Kaplan–Meier survival analysis in metastatic colorectal cancer patients treated with bevacizumab‐based regimens. Progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) curves are stratified according to the optimal cut‐off thresholds determined by the Youden index. (A) PFS based on low versus high FoxP3+ Treg ratios, showing a trend toward improved survival in the low‐ratio cohort (8.0 ± 0.7 vs. 5.0 ± 2.0 months; p = 0.085). (B) OS based on FoxP3+ Treg ratios (17.0 ± 3.3 vs. 15.0 ± 0.6 months; p = 0.180). (C) PFS stratified by plasma bevacizumab concentrations measured at the end of the first treatment cycle (Bev_2w_lev) (7.0 ± 1.1 vs. 8.0 ± 1.3 months; p = 0.188). (D) OS stratified by plasma bevacizumab concentrations, demonstrating a statistically significant survival advantage in patients with higher concentrations (24.0 ± 2.8 vs. 12.0 ± 1.9 months; p = 0.009). Statistical significance was evaluated using the log‐rank (Mantel‐Cox) test.
