Hui o Hau‘ula (HoH) is a community organization dedicated to the well-being of the population of
the Ko‘olauloa district on the Hawaiian island of Oahu. In response to growing concerns about
challenges related to extreme weather events or natural disasters, HoH formulated the concept of
the Ko‘olauloa Community Resilience Hub, or KCRH. The KCRH facility would serve as a focal
point for the community during normal conditions, while also providing essential services and acting
as a safe space during emergencies, natural or man-made. The concept of the KCRH was initially
developed in partnership with the Hawaii Natural Energy Institute, +Lab Architects, and the City
and County of Honolulu.

 Figure 1. Rendering of the KCRH

In the fall of 2023, the U.S. Department of Energy, under the Energy Technology Innovation
Partnership Project Program (ETIPP), provided support to the KCRH project, in the form of
technical assistance (TA) to be provided by its National Laboratory complex. In this case, the core
TA was provided by Sandia National Laboratories, and it was directed to providing options for
designing an energy system based at the KCRH that could support critical loads in the event of a 30-
day grid outage. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) provided communications
and logistics support in the project.
The project was organized in four phases, shown in Figure 2: Scope definition involved
understanding what resilience means to the Hau‘ula community, and the collection of data on the
energy use of critical loads. In the second phase of the project, the team worked with the community
to determine what energy systems would be geographically and culturally appropriate, and to provide
to the community a preliminary assessment of the expected size and cost of these energy systems. In
the third phase, advanced analysis tools were used to determine possible options of energy system
that meet previously developed criteria, along with information of the cost, resilience and other
measures of performance. The fourth and final phase is the communication of the study results to
HoH and its partners, and to the broader community, so that they can be used to promote progress
for the KCRH.

Close consultation with the community resulted in the identification of several critical electrical loads
that must be always supported. These are: medical apparatus, potable water pumps, small
refrigerators, communications, plug loads in first-responder areas, emergency lighting and fire

suppression water pumps operating for 4-hour intervals. Loads of lower criticality include HVAC
and general lighting, refrigerated room, plugs for entire building and EV charging.
Distributed resources considered as possible components of the KCRH power system are solar
photovoltaic, building-integrated wind, conventional generation (propane or diesel, with associated
fuel storage), battery storage and hydrogen storage. These components are not mutually exclusive:
for example, solar PV can work together with a propane generator and a battery, so that when solar
power is not available and the battery is fully discharged, the generator is activated. It is important to
note that a mix of resources generally provides better energy availability and resilience, as each
operates with different resources and constraints. The sun is not always shining, fuel delivery is not
always guaranteed, and battery capacity is limited by footprint and cost.
Generation resources, storage and loads in the KCRH would not operate in isolation but rather
would be organized within an electrical system known as a microgrid. A microgrid is defined by the
DOE as a group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources (DERs) that are within a defined electrical
boundary and can operate as a single, controllable entity. It can connect to and disconnect from the larger electrical grid,
allowing it to function in either a grid-connected or an "island" mode.
For the majority of the time, the KCRH microgrid would operate in grid connected mode,
importing or exporting energy from the Hawaiian Electric Company (HECO) grid and supporting it
as needed. During grid outages, the KCRH would island and provide energy to loads within its
boundary, prioritizing loads with higher criticality.
Sandia technical staff used multiple analysis tools to provide the community with several options for
a microgrid that would meet its resilience needs. One of the analysis tools, HOMER, was used to
model cash flows over the expected lifetime of the microgrid, including capital expenditures, utility
bills, energy sales to the utility, maintenance costs, and produce list of microgrid options ranked by
net present cost. The other tool, the Microgrid Design Toolkit, was used to assess the resilience of
the microgrid against other metrics such as cost and environmental performance, and to produce a
portfolio of optimized microgrid designs that would emphasize individual performance metrics.
A typical MDT output looks like the plot in Figure 3. The three axes represent resilience, cost and
environmental performance, while individual dots represent a specific technology portfolio. As in
most multi-objective optimization problems, there are tradeoffs between the optimization
parameters: for example, other things being equal, higher resilience corresponds to higher costs.
Ultimately, users must select the portfolio that best meets their needs. MDT’s role is to guide
stakeholders and to provide the best compromises.

met the constraint that were set. These are an all-renewable portfolio (as requested by HoH), a high-
renewable portfolio with moderate backup generation, and a moderate-renewable with high fuel

range of costs is a notable feature: a solution that provides 100% renewable power to the KCRH
during grid outages would cost $4.7M, compared to a solution that only provides 71% renewable
power, that costs $1.0M. High fractions of renewable energy during grid outages require high energy
storage capacity, hence the choice of large hydrogen storage for the all-RE and high-RE solutions.
On the other hand, the moderate-RE solution requires a relatively large 1,700 gallon propane storage
tank. While propane storage does not pose a risk to groundwater resulting from leaks, care must be
taken to ensure ventilation to the tank / genset area to reduce the risk of explosion should any leaks
occur. Propane fuel was chosen over diesel storage due to lower risk of environmental damage from
leaks, and because propane fuel does not degrade over time.

When operating in grid-connected mode, this latter system produces more energy that is consumed
by the KCRH, so overall the environmental performance is high. Resilience is however limited by
fuel supply, but this could be overcome by allowing for larger tanks. Added resilience could be
provided by using redundant conventional generation, at a lower cost than storage.
To conclude, choices need to be made by microgrid stakeholders and by the wider community. The
lowest cost solution is a combination of solar PV and conventional backup generation, with the
understanding that backup generation would only be used during grid outages when solar PV is not
sufficient to meet the load. On the other hand, energy storage would enable more resilient operation
during outages with smaller reliance on fuel storage or fuel supply, but at a higher cost. These costs
could be reduced by appropriately valuing grid services that could be provided by a microgrid with
energy storage, via appropriately designed electricity rates and markets. While this is beyond the
scope of this study, assigning value to microgrid services could provide more effective grid
operation at scale.
