Recommendations for ventriculostomy and cisternostomy in TBI management: Results of the WFNS-Neurotraumatology committee “Cusco consensus”
Authors: Andrés M Rubiano, Wendy D Gonzalez-Tobar, Laura M Loaiza-Cardona, Andrés F Salazar, Santiago Cardona-Collazos, Denis Hermosa, Shugar Hinojosa, Amaya Alvarez, Eve Tsai, Adrián Zapata, Mario Canitrot, Robson Amorin, Piedad Acurio, Luigi Berra, Ahmed El Fiki, Leonardo Zapata, Marcos Wagner, Mario Ganau, Wellingson Paiva, Andrew Reisner, Oscar Alves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntracranial hypertension is a life-threatening complication of traumatic brain injury (TBI) and a major target in neurocritical care. While ventriculostomy for cerebrospinal fluid diversion and the emerging technique of cisternostomy are used on a regular basis in different areas of the world, their application is heterogeneous on indications, timing, and technical aspects in the surgical procedures. This variability highlights a critical lack of standardized protocols, particularly in resource-constrained environments where optimized recommendations are most needed.Research questionWhat are the specific, expert-consensus recommendations to standardize the use of ventriculostomy and cisternostomy within a protocol for TBI management?Material and methodsThe Neurotrauma Committee of the World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies develop an international consensus, including a methodological group and a panel of 16 expert neurosurgeons following a Delphi process, involving systematic literature review, statement formulation, and iterative blinded voting using a 5-point Likert scale. Consensus was predefined as ≥75% agreement.ResultsThe panel ratified 69 consensus statements. Core recommendations advocate for ventriculostomy as an early therapeutic intervention for TBI requiring fluid drainage and define cisternostomy as an adjunctive procedure for selected patients with TBI and significant traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage. Protocols with algorithms were proposed for both procedures.Discussion and conclusionThis consensus provides a practical framework to guide surgical management of TBI using ventriculostomy and or cisternostomy as techniques. It promotes a strategic shift towards prior heterogeneous concepts on ventriculostomy and defines a specific, consensus-guided role for cisternostomy. The statements aim to harmonize global practice and direct future research.Keywords: Ventriculostomy, Subarachnoid space, Intracranial pressure, Traumatic brain injury, Neurosurgery

Introduction

Intracranial hypertension (ICH) is a critical driver of secondary brain injury following severe traumatic brain injury (TBI). The well-established association between elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) and poorer neurological outcomes underscores that the timely detection and management of ICH is a cornerstone of modern neurotrauma care (Nattino et al., 2023; Canac et al., 2020). The evolution in TBI management has revealed a broad spectrum of patient phenotypes, moving away from a one-size-fits-all approach and toward the need for tailored strategies (Pugh et al., 2021; Cruz Navarro et al., 2022). Within the therapeutic armamentarium, surgical interventions have traditionally been considered high-morbidity options, often reserved for later stages of management (Fong et al., 2017). However, minimally invasive techniques are emerging as a way to deliver the potency of surgical intervention for controlling secondary injury mechanisms with a more favorable risk profile (Pitton Rissardo et al., 2025).

Among these, external ventricular drainage (EVD) and cisternostomy represent two pivotal tools to target secondary injury mechanisms. Ventriculostomy serves as a fundamental intervention for ICP control via cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diversion and as a monitoring modality (Hawryluk et al., 2019; Chau et al., 2019). More recently, cisternostomy has emerged as a complementary surgical technique, proposed to not only relieve mass effect but also to potentially restore physiological CSF dynamics by opening the basal cisterns, thereby reducing ICP and improving perfusion (Cherian et al., 2016). For selected patient phenotypes, these procedures offer the potency of a surgical intervention for addressing secondary injury mechanisms with a more favorable risk profile compared to more extensive cranial procedures.

Despite their potential, the evidence guiding their use, particularly in selecting the right patient phenotype for each procedure, remains scarce, and standardization in real-world clinical practice is lacking (Cherian et al., 2019). This lack of standardization is particularly problematic in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where resource constraints requires highly precise and pragmatic surgical algorithms (Cnossen et al., 2017).

International consensus protocols, such as those from the Seattle International Severe Traumatic Brain Injury Consensus Conference (SIBICC), provide a comprehensive framework for tiered therapy escalation but offer limited specific guidance on the precise role, timing, and selection between these minimally invasive surgical options (Hawryluk et al., 2019). This gap is critical, as it perpetuates practice variation and may delay effective, targeted intervention (Cnossen et al., 2017).

To address this critical gap in guidance and the lack of standardization, an international panel of neurotrauma experts developed this consensus document. Its objective is to provide updated, consensus-based guidance on the use of ventriculostomy and cisternostomy, establishing a clear, practical framework for surgical decision-making to improve the management of ICH and patient outcomes globally.

Methods

The methodology for this consensus, including a systematic literature review, expert deliberation, iterative consideration development, and final approval, was designed and endorsed by the Neurotraumatology Committee of the World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) with methodological support of Fundación para la Investigación y Educación en Emergencias Médicas (MEDITECH). A panel composed exclusively of neurosurgeons that use the one or both of procedures on regular basis was selected to fulfill the required expertise in surgical decision-making processes related to ventriculostomy and cisternostomy in TBI. The final panel included 16 neurosurgeons from different regions of the world, exposed to high volumes of TBI cases in their practice, and commonly using one or the two techniques, representing a balanced distribution of expertise across high-income and low/middle-income settings. The general selection criteria include:

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed and Google Scholar databases, complemented by a grey literature search, covering the period from 2014 to 2024. The search terms included: "Cisternostomy", "External Ventricular Drainage", "Ventriculostomy", "Ventricular drain", "Traumatic Brain Injury", "TBI", "Neurotrauma", and "Trauma". The complete search strings, filters, and PRISMA flow diagrams are provided in Supplementary Material S1, and the full list of included studies with their characteristics is provided in Supplementary Material S2.

For the cisternostomy review, the initial search yielded 348 records. After removal of 6 duplicates, 342 records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 320 were excluded (307 for irrelevance, 4 due to language restrictions, 8 for ineligible study design, and 1 letter to the editor). Twenty-one full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and all were included in the qualitative synthesis (Supplementary Material 2).

For the ventriculostomy review, 281 records were identified. Following removal of 7 duplicates, 274 records were screened. A total of 255 records were excluded (242 for irrelevance, 4 due to language, and 9 for ineligible study design). Nineteen full-text reports were assessed, and all met the criteria for inclusion in qualitative synthesis (Supplementary Material 2).

The articles identified were organized and distributed to the respective expert working groups (Cisternostomy or Ventriculostomy) three months prior to the in-person consensus meeting (Lele et al., 2017; Taylor et al., 2024; Stuart et al., 2021; Hepburn-Smith et al., 2016; Piccirilli et al., 2023; Giglio et al., 2025; Fried et al., 2016; Sheppard et al., 2018; Herbert; Tunkel et al., 2017; Bertuccio et al., 2023; Dorresteijn et al., 2019; Parthiban et al., 2021; Ciobanu-Caraus et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2022; Kumarasamy et al., 2024; Lino-Filho et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Chandra et al., 2022; First degree specialist in Neurosurgery et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025; Giammattei et al., 2020; Kumari et al., 2023; Vemula et al., 2022; Cherian and Burhan, 2019; Han et al., 2025; Satyarsa et al., 2023; Ramirez et al., 2023; Youssef et al., 2020; Cherian et al., 2013; Thapa et al., 2019; Baishya and Kamat; Goyal and Kumar, 2021). This allowed panelists to review and familiarize themselves with the current evidence on both topics. Experts were also encouraged to suggest additional literature, and preliminary discussion questions were shared in advance to allow for modifications. The consensus meeting was held in Cusco (Peru), in November 2025, with participation from the selected WFNS-Neurotraumatology Committee members. During this meeting, panelists were divided initially into two dedicated working groups (Cisternostomy and Ventriculostomy) according to their expertise. Selection for inclusion in those working groups was based on the very precise skill set required to offer holistic neurosurgical care to severe TBI patients. For instance, neurosurgeons participating in the Cisternostomy working group had previous roles as trainers in national and international live surgery or hands on cadaveric courses on Cisternostomy (e.g. M.G. served as Faculty for Exoscope-Assisted Basal Cisternostomy Courses), or completion of Vascular/Skull Base Fellowship (e.g. S.J.H.M. completed post-graduate fellowship under the guidance of Prof. I.Cherian, expert in Cisternostomy). Detailed biographical information for each panel member, including years of independent experience with the techniques is provided in Supplementary Material S3.

The process was structured across three phases. In Phase 1 (pre-meeting preparation), all panelists received the systematic literature and a set of pre-formulated clinical questions via a shared folder approximately three months before the meeting, and were asked to arrive with preliminary responses to each question. In Phase 2 (in-person meeting), the process was organized in three sequential steps. In Step A, panelists were divided into two dedicated working groups according to their primary expertise, one for ventriculostomy and one for cisternostomy.

Each group discussed the pre-specified clinical questions, formulated draft statements, and reached intragroup agreement through structured discussion. In Step B, both groups convened in a joint plenary session in which a representative of each group presented the draft statements to all panel members; all panelists were invited to suggest modifications, challenge wording, and discuss areas of uncertainty. In Step C, each statement was submitted to a final vote by all 16 panel members using a 5-point Likert scale (1: strong disagreement to 5: strong agreement); statements that did not achieve the required agreement threshold at the first vote were returned for further discussion and re-voted until consensus was reached. In Phase 3 (post-meeting consolidation), the methodological team structured the approved statements into a unified document, which was circulated to all panel members for individual review and final sign-off before submission.

According to the categorization after literature appraisal (Supplementary Material S2), the statements were considered evidence-based recommendations if supported by high-quality literature, or consensus-based considerations if the available evidence was limited. The level of evidence for each statement was graded individually using a hierarchical framework consistent with that used by the Brain Trauma Foundation and the WFNS Neurotrauma Committee, ranging from Level I (high-quality RCTs or systematic reviews) to Level IV/E (expert consensus without comparative data), as detailed in Table 1.

To facilitate the anchoring of the recommendations to well defined surgical technique for Cisternostomy and Ventriculostomy, technical papers were circulated among participants and to agree on which technique was representative of the procedures being discussed. For instance, the Cisternostomy technique considered as the most representative of current practice was the one recently described by Sharafudeen et al. (2025) including basic surgical images.

Results

The consensus panel formulated 69 statements through the structured Delphi process: 30 addressing ventriculostomy and 39 addressing cisternostomy, of which 18 were categorized as evidence-based recommendations and 51 as consensus-based considerations based on individual evidence grading. As detailed in Table 1, all statements achieved the required consensus threshold and were ratified with strong agreement at the final vote. The voting records confirm that agreement was strong but not trivially uniform: for ventriculostomy (n = 16, 30 statements), the proportion of strong agreement votes per statement ranged from 43.8% to 100%, with one statement (preoperative antibiotic timing) receiving a single neutral vote. For cisternostomy (n = 16, 39 statements), strong agreement ranged from 43.8% to 100% per statement. No statements received sufficient votes to be categorized as disagreement or to be rejected.

The panel agreed that EVD placement in TBI is primarily indicated when CSF diversion is expected to support the management of intracranial hypertension. This includes post-traumatic hydrocephalus, intraventricular hemorrhage, and neurological decline attributable to elevated ICP. When indicated, EVD placement should occur early, provided it does not delay urgent life-saving procedures such as hematoma evacuation or decompressive craniectomy. Relative contraindications such as uncorrected coagulopathy, slit ventricles, or scalp infection must be assessed within each institutional context.

Kocher's point on the non-dominant side remains the preferred entry site, unless anatomical distortion necessitates alternatives (Table 2). The catheter trajectory should be perpendicular to the calvarium and directed toward the foramen of Monro, with depth guided by the return of CSF rather than a fixed measurement. Long subcutaneous tunneling and secure fixation were emphasized to reduce displacement and infection. Image-guided techniques, including neuronavigation or ultrasound, may be beneficial in cases of distorted anatomy or small ventricles.

Because EVDs are less accurate for continuous ICP monitoring than integrated with/or intraparenchymal sensors, the panel highlighted the value of combined monitoring strategies in patients who require both drainage and reliable ICP measurement. In patients with prohibitive surgical risks, contralateral placement relative to the midline shift should be considered.

CSF drainage should begin gradually and be guided by ICP trends rather than fixed volumes. Continuous and intermittent drainage strategies are acceptable once ICP stabilizes and should be individualized. Absence of waveform pulsatility or inadequate drainage requires prompt evaluation of catheter patency prior to imaging.

Infection prevention remains critical. Preoperative antibiotics are recommended, but routine postoperative prophylaxis or scheduled catheter exchange is not. EVD removal should occur as soon as clinically feasible. CSF sampling is optional and should be reserved for cases with clinical suspicion of infection. Diagnosis of ventriculitis requires integration of clinical, microbiological, and CSF findings (Table 3). Treatment involves prompt removal of infected catheters, appropriate antimicrobial therapy, and consideration of intraventricular antibiotics for refractory cases (Table 4).

Regarding cisternostomy, the panel agreed that it may be considered an adjunctive surgical option in patients with TBI and documented intracranial hypertension, as well as in cases of refractory brain edema. Clinical tools such as the GCS and pupil index, along with imaging findings including traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage and obliterated basal cisterns, were identified as supportive indicators. The procedure was not recommended in isolated diffuse axonal injury without intracranial hypertension or in patients with anterior or middle fossa skull base fractures due to the risk of CSF leakage. The panel highlighted the need for further studies to better define optimal timing.

Microsurgical training and the use of magnification—preferably an operating microscope—were emphasized. A frontal approach was considered the safest and fastest, especially in markedly swollen brains. Recommended technical steps included appropriate patient positioning, prioritizing the intradural stage to relieve mass effect, and opening the Liliequist membrane. Anterior clinoidectomy was generally deemed unnecessary.

All patients undergoing cisternostomy should be connected to a closed sterile drainage system, ideally using an EVD. Gravity-based drainage was preferred, with the zero-reference point at the tragus and the drainage level set 5–7 cm above it. Catheter duration should not exceed 7 days, and the exit site should be positioned 5–10 cm from the incision.

Infection prevention strategies included perioperative antibiotics and limiting catheter duration. Diagnosis of infection should follow the same clinical and CSF criteria used for EVD-related infections, with CSF samples obtained before empirical therapy, and contrast-enhanced imaging considered when suspicion persists. Management requires prompt initiation of broad-spectrum antibiotics, adjustment based on cultures, and catheter removal when the system is involved.

Discussion

This international consensus provides a structured set of considerations and detailed technical considerations for the use of ventriculostomy and cisternostomy in the management of TBI. These guidelines were developed to address the persistent gap between current evidence, heterogeneous practices across institutions, and the growing interest in incorporating cisternal techniques into acute neurotrauma care. Importantly, the panel emphasized that these recommendations and considerations should not be interpreted as substitutes for established evidence-based guidelines, but rather as complementary tools intended to support clinical decision-making in scenarios where variability in training, resources, or institutional protocols may influence practice.

With respect to ventriculostomy, the panel reaffirmed its role as a common intervention in the monitoring and management of intracranial hypertension (Taylor et al., 2024; Stuart et al., 2021). The recommendations and considerations highlight key aspects including early placement when indicated, careful assessment of contraindications, meticulous technique, and strict infection-prevention strategies (Lele et al., 2017; Piccirilli et al., 2023; Dorresteijn et al., 2019). Several aspects of EVD management, including indications for placement, infection prevention strategies, and diagnosis and treatment of ventriculitis, are supported by Level I–II or Level II evidence and are therefore categorized as evidence-based recommendations in the revised Table 1. The expert consensus underscores that EVDs remain less reliable than intraparenchymal sensors for continuous ICP monitoring, and therefore combined monitoring strategies should be considered when feasible. The importance of context-specific decision-making, particularly in settings where surgical risk, anatomical distortion, or resource limitations exist.

Regarding cisternostomy, the panel acknowledged the growing interest in this technique as an adjunctive option for selected patients with refractory intracranial hypertension and refractory brain edema (Lino-Filho et al., 2024; Chandra et al., 2022; Giammattei et al., 2020; Kumari et al., 2023; Youssef et al., 2020). The experts highlighted promising physiological rationale and emerging clinical experience, while simultaneously recognizing the lack of high-quality evidence to establish optimal timing, indications, and expected outcomes. As a result, the panel emphasized that cisternostomy should not be considered a replacement for standard decompressive or CSF-diversion strategies, but rather a potential complement in highly selected cases and under appropriate expertise (Ciobanu-Caraus et al., 2024; Kumarasamy et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Kumari et al., 2023). Reflecting the current state of evidence, all cisternostomy statements related to indications, surgical technique, and drainage management are categorized as consensus-based considerations, with the exception of infection-related statements, which are graded Level I–II based on extrapolation from the established EVD infection literature. The technical considerations provided, including the frontal approach, use of magnification, and specific drainage parameters, aim to optimize safety and reproducibility, yet the need for structured training programs remains evident (Parthiban et al., 2021; Kumar et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024; Chandra et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2025; Giammattei et al., 2020; Youssef et al., 2020; Thapa et al., 2019).

Across both procedures, infection prevention emerged as a shared priority. The panel emphasized perioperative antibiotic strategies, minimizing catheter duration, and applying consistent diagnostic criteria aligned with existing EVD-related infection literature (Piccirilli et al., 2023; Herbert; Ciobanu-Caraus et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2022; Lino-Filho et al., 2024; Ramirez et al., 2023; Bell et al., 2022). The reliance on clinical context, neuroimaging, and institutional resources was highlighted as a critical component in early recognition and management of complications.

Finally, the experts noted several knowledge gaps. These include the need for multicenter observational studies evaluating cisternostomy outcomes, comparative studies assessing drainage strategies in ventriculostomy, and standardized metrics for infection surveillance. The predominance of consensus-based considerations over evidence-based recommendations, particularly for cisternostomy, underscores the urgent need for multicenter prospective studies and adequately powered randomized trials to generate the higher-quality evidence required to upgrade these considerations to formal recommendations in future iterations of this consensus. The consensus should therefore be viewed as a dynamic document intended to guide clinical reasoning, inform training, and support the design of future research protocols.

Conclusions

The recommendations and considerations formulated in this consensus aim to provide clinicians with practical, expert-derived guidance on the use of ventriculostomy and cisternostomy in the management of TBI. By integrating technical expertise, clinical experience, and current evidence, this work seeks to harmonize practice patterns and support decision-making in diverse healthcare environments. The experts contributing to this document collectively possess extensive experience in neurotrauma care and in the application of the procedures described. As such, these considerations may be adapted to different institutional contexts according to local resources, levels of expertise, and patient needs.

Given the variability in the available evidence, particularly regarding cisternostomy, the panel stresses that further research is essential. Future multicenter studies and randomized trials will be crucial to validate the safety, efficacy, and optimal use of these interventions. This consensus may also serve as a foundation for training initiatives and as a template for research protocols aimed at advancing the field of surgical neurotrauma.
