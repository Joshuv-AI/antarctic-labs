Deep homography-based image stitching with enhanced small object detection
Authors: Jicheng Cong, Xiaotian Ran, Chunbin Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a novel image stitching methodology leveraging the backbone of YOLOv8 as a feature extractor, designed specifically to overcome limitations prevalent in traditional stitching techniques concerning small object detection and complex backgrounds. The proposed approach integrates a low-resolution feature map enhancement, a multi-scale feature extraction mechanism, and a deep homography estimation module. The low-resolution enhancement module utilizes an adaptive Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) mechanism to significantly enhance the detection precision of small objects. Concurrently, the multi-scale feature extraction employs Spatial Pyramid Pooling to efficiently capture and fuse features across various resolutions, improving robustness and feature alignment in images characterized by complex textures and disparities. A core innovation of this research is the introduction of a deep learning-based homography estimation module, utilizing regression networks and Direct Linear Transformation (DLT) algorithms to compute accurate geometric transformations. Moreover, a specially designed weighted loss function emphasizes the alignment accuracy of small object regions during model training, thus reducing stitching artifacts and improving visual continuity. Experimental validation was conducted using the UDIS and MVS-Synth datasets. Results show significant improvements over baseline and comparative methods, achieving higher PSNR (26.36 and 26.42, respectively) and SSIM values (0.8414 and 0.8494, respectively), with substantial reductions in model size and computational complexity, as demonstrated by the model size (188 MB) and GFLOPs (14.5) in our experiments. Ablation studies further confirm the effectiveness of each module in enhancing stitching accuracy and computational efficiency. Future work may explore extending the model’s capability for real-time application in dynamic and variable environments.

Introduction

Deep learning-based methods for image stitching, especially in terms of small object detection and stitching accuracy in complex background scenarios, have become a research hotspot in the field of computer vision. With the development of drone technology and computer vision, traditional geometry-based image stitching methods face significant challenges, particularly when dealing with complex scenes and regions of large disparity1–5, where their performance notably declines. In recent years, deep learning methods, which adaptively learn image features and semantic information6–12, have overcome these challenges, demonstrating superior results in various applications. For example, Aboah et al.13 developed a real-time helmet detection model using the YOLOv8 architecture, significantly improving detection accuracy and processing speed. Moreover, this model improved the ability to automatically identify regions of interest, effectively eliminating irrelevant object interference14. However, existing deep learning-based image stitching methods still have limitations in terms of small object alignment, stitching quality, and computational efficiency.

The motivation behind our work is threefold. First, traditional geometry-based stitching methods and existing deep learning approaches suffer from poor alignment of small objects (e.g., distant pedestrians, small signs, or fine structures) because these objects occupy few pixels and their features are easily suppressed in low-resolution feature maps. Second, most deep homography networks regress transformations from raw features without explicitly enhancing small-object information, leading to misalignments in regions with small objects. Third, while some recent stitching methods achieve good overall quality, they often require heavy reconstruction networks (e.g., UDIS1 with 2105 MB) or complex multi-stage pipelines, making them impractical for resource-constrained applications. Therefore, we aim to design a lightweight yet accurate stitching method that specifically improves small-object alignment while maintaining high computational efficiency.

This paper aims to propose an improved image stitching method that integrates low-resolution feature map enhancement, multi-scale feature extraction, and a deep homography module, specifically targeting stitching tasks for small objects and complex backgrounds. By introducing an adaptive weighting mechanism and deep homography estimation, the method can effectively improve the accuracy of small object detection and significantly improve stitching quality. Compared to existing image stitching techniques, the proposed method not only addresses the large disparity issue but also maintains high stitching accuracy and low computational complexity across various scenarios. The main contributions of this paper are as follows. Related work

Traditional image stitching

Adaptive warping techniques, such as Dual-Homography Warping (DHW)15, aim to address disparity issues by independently aligning different scene planes. Although these methods perform well in simple scenarios, their effectiveness is often significantly impacted in complex environments, especially when diverse depth information is present. To overcome this limitation, Zaragoza16 proposed techniques such as Smoothly Varying Affine (SVA) and As-Projective-As-Possible (APAP), which apply homographies to image grids to enhance local alignment accuracy. However, the APAP method faces considerable challenges, particularly near object boundaries and regions with significant depth variation16–19.

Seam-driven methods primarily focus on minimizing the creation of stitching artifacts20. Liao et al.21 proposed a multihomography deformation method guided by segmentation to handle large parallax image stitching and to use image segmentation information to guide multiple homography deformations. Zhang and Liu22 introduced a seam-based alignment method, optimizing with global homographies, which is applicable to stereo stitching problems, as discussed in23. Meanwhile, Lin et al.24 employed iterative deformation and seam estimation to identify the optimal stitching regions, effectively preserving key structural elements such as curves and straight lines. Although these methods show promising results in practical applications, their performance often depends on the accuracy of feature detection, which can be significantly affected in scenarios with sparse features or low image resolution.

Deep image stitching

In the development of deep learning-based image stitching, DeTone et al. initially employed the Visual Geometry Group (VGG) network to predict homographies by determining the offsets of the four corners of the image25,26. Building upon this, an unsupervised method with a similar architecture was introduced in27, incorporating a novel unsupervised loss function. In contrast, deep stitching approaches do not rely on the continuous design of geometric features but instead automatically extract high-level semantic features from large datasets. These methods operate in supervised24,28–31, weakly supervised32, or unsupervised23 manners, enabling them to adapt to various complex scenarios. However, these methods face challenges when dealing with large disparities, as homography-based alignment models may result in unnecessary blurring in the disparity regions during the reconstruction process. In33, an adaptive selection algorithm was proposed that sequentially performs rapid feature extraction and matching, introducing local deformation methods to smoothly transition the overlapping regions, thereby achieving high-precision image alignment.

The first unsupervised image stitching framework was proposed in9, aiming to eliminate artifacts from features to pixels through an unsupervised image reconstruction network. To improve the stitching of underwater terrain images and address the slow speed of traditional image stitching methods, an improved algorithm based on spatial gradient feature blocks for underwater terrain image stitching was presented in4.

Moreover, in recent years, with the advancement of drone-mounted platforms, significant progress has been made in small object detection research34–36. An improved YOLOv5 model was proposed in37, focusing on enhancing small object detection performance38,39. By optimizing the network structure and improving feature extraction capabilities, this model significantly improved the accuracy of small object detection. Simultaneously, a lightweight small object detection algorithm for drone platforms reduced model complexity and computational requirements, providing efficient real-time detection capabilities to effectively address small object image stitching issues encountered during drone imaging. By integrating these emerging technologies, deep learning-based image stitching algorithms have not only made breakthroughs in handling large disparity problems but also better addressed small object alignment challenges in complex environments.

Methodology

Network model

This section presents an image stitching method based on the YOLOv8 framework. As shown in Fig. 1, we have made several improvements to the original YOLOv8 architecture. First, we removed the neck part of the YOLOv8 framework to reduce computational complexity and obtain a lightweight model, thus enabling more efficient handling of image stitching tasks. Next, we incorporated a low-resolution feature map enhancement module and a multi-scale feature extraction module into the backbone to better capture important information in the image while enhancing the detection capability for small objects. Additionally, we introduced a deep homography module, which allows for more accurate estimation of the geometric transformations between images during the stitching process, further improving the image stitching results. With these optimizations, the modified model is better equipped to handle complex image stitching tasks, significantly enhancing both stitching quality and efficiency.

We adopt the backbone of YOLOv8 (without its detection heads and neck) as our feature extraction network. The neck of YOLOv8 is omitted to reduce computational complexity. In the original YOLOv8, the neck aggregates multi-scale features for object detection; however, in our stitching framework, the combination of the SPPF module (“Multi-scale feature extraction method”) and the attention mechanism (“Introducing the attention mechanism”) already captures multi-scale information directly from the backbone, making the neck redundant. This design follows the philosophy of lightweight adaptation for aerial vision tasks40. Our multi-scale feature extraction and deep homography modules are appended to the backbone, and the final output is a stitched image produced by warping and fusion based on the estimated homography matrix.

Novelty of the integration

While individual components such as SE, SPPF, and DLT-based homography estimation are not new in isolation, the novelty of our work lies in their purposeful integration and adaptation for small-object image stitching. Specifically: (a) we embed the SE module into the low-resolution feature path of YOLOv8’s backbone – a placement not used in prior stitching works; (b) we combine SPPF with a dual-attention mechanism (channel and spatial) to preserve small-object details across scales; (c) we feed the enhanced features into a deep homography regressor, whereas prior deep homography methods (e.g., DeTone et al.) operate on raw image pairs. This integrated design, together with our small-object weighted loss, constitutes a novel system that achieves state-of-the-art results for small-object alignment.Fig. 1Overview of the model architecture.

To address the issue of insufficient small object information in low-resolution feature maps, this paper designs a low-resolution feature map enhancement technique. By enhancing the low-resolution feature maps within the YOLOv8 framework, the precision of small object detection and alignment is improved.

Multi-scale feature extraction method

Objects in images typically exhibit varying sizes and characteristics. Consequently, capturing and integrating multi-scale information is critical for enhancing stitching accuracy. This is particularly vital when processing images with significant parallax, complex backgrounds, or small objects, where multi-scale feature extraction can substantially improve stitching quality and alignment precision. To address the need for capturing information across different scales, this chapter proposes a Multi-Scale Feature Extraction Module. In traditional approaches, feature extraction often relies on fixed-scale keypoint detection, which tends to fail in scenarios with large parallax or complex textures. In contrast, deep learning methods, utilizing convolutional neural networks, can generate multi-level feature maps. However, directly employing features from a single level may overlook critical information at other scales. The multi-scale feature extraction method presented in this chapter integrates low-, medium-, and high-resolution features, thereby enhancing the perception of small objects and global structures while improving the robustness of feature matching. The Multi-Scale Feature Extraction Module first employs a convolutional neural network to extract features from the input image and construct multi-scale feature maps. Incorporating a Spatial Pyramid Pooling (SPPF) module into the multi-scale feature extraction process further strengthens the expressive capacity of image features, particularly for handling small objects at varying scales. SPPF is used because it captures multi-scale context with few parameters, preventing over-smoothing of small objects. The pyramid pooling module, through multi-level pooling operations, captures contextual information across different scales, thereby preserving more detailed information and enhancing focus on small objects in multi-scale feature extraction. To further enhance the expressive power of the multi-scale feature map, this chapter introduces both spatial attention mechanisms and channel attention mechanisms within the multi-scale feature extraction module. The spatial attention mechanism focuses on the important regions of the image, enhancing the representation capability of key areas within the feature map. The channel attention mechanism, on the other hand, adaptively adjusts the importance of each channel, allowing the network to dynamically allocate computational resources across multiple channels. Through adaptive weighting, the fused multi-scale feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{multi}$$\end{document} is weighted to obtain the final enhanced feature map representation, which is expressed by the following formula:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {F_{enhanced}} = \alpha \cdot {F_{multi}} + (1 - \alpha ) \cdot {F_{att}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F_{att}}$$\end{document} represents the feature map processed through the attention mechanism, while α is a weighting hyperparameter used to balance the multi-scale feature map and the attention-weighted feature map. Note that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{multi}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{att}$$\end{document} have the same height, width, and number of channels after the attention operation. Through the pooling pyramid module and multi-scale feature fusion, the final feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{final}$$\end{document} will contain rich information captured from different scales and exhibit a stronger representation capability, particularly in handling small objects and fine-grained details. The formula for this can be expressed as:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {F_{final}} = \mathscr {T}({F_{enhanced}}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {T}( \cdot )$$\end{document} represents the further spatial alignment or optimization operation, which denotes a convolutional block consisting of two 3× 3 convolutional layers, batch normalization, and ReLU activation, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{final}$$\end{document} denotes the final output feature map.

Deep homography estimation and image registration

To address issues such as feature matching errors, geometric deformations caused by disparities, and lighting differences between images, this chapter designs and implements a deep learning-based homography estimation module (Homography Estimator) within the YOLOv8 framework. The deep homography module is capable of accurately estimating the geometric transformations between images, thereby effectively solving the image registration problem under different viewpoints. The core task of this module is to predict the ground-truth homography matrix H between images and perform appropriate transformations on the input images. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the deep homography estimation module consists of feature extraction, displacement regression, and DLT-based homography computation. The DLT solver is a standard component, but we use it only after our feature enhancement, which is not done in prior work.Fig. 3Deep homography transformation process.

Deep homography transformation process.

The deep homography estimation module consists of several key stages to accomplish the complete process from feature extraction to homography transformation estimation. The network first extracts features from the images using a convolutional neural network (CNN) and passes these features to fully connected layers for further processing. When estimating the homography matrix, the deep learning network outputs the parameters of the homography matrix through a regression approach. Specifically, for two images \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${I_1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${I_2}$$\end{document}, the feature extraction module outputs features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F_1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F_2}$$\end{document}, respectively. The network uses a regression module to predict the transformation matrix H between the images. Based on the constructed multi-scale network module, the vertex displacement is estimated from the fused feature maps. For each scale, the network outputs an 8-dimensional vector representing the displacement of the four vertices in the x and y directions. These displacements reflect the geometric differences between the two images and are crucial for calculating the homography matrix. Subsequently, using a Direct Linear Transformation (DLT) solver, the homography matrix is computed based on the accumulated vertex displacements. For an input pixel coordinate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({x_s},{y_s})$$\end{document}, the transformed coordinate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({x_t},{y_t})$$\end{document} can be expressed by the following formula:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} x_t' \\ y_t' \\ z_t' \end{bmatrix} = H \begin{bmatrix} x_s \\ y_s \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad (x_t, y_t) = \left( \frac{x_t'}{z_t'}, \frac{y_t'}{z_t'} \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x_s, y_s)$$\end{document} are coordinates in the source image, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x_t, y_t)$$\end{document} are coordinates in the target image after transformation, and H is the 3× 3 homography matrix. To optimize the accuracy of homography estimation, the network is trained by minimizing the reprojection error. The reprojection error is defined by the following formula:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} E = \sum _{i} \left\| p_2^{i} - \text {normalize}(H \cdot p_1^{i}) \right\| ^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_1^{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_2^{i}$$\end{document} are homogeneous coordinates of matched points, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {normalize}(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes division by the third coordinate to obtain inhomogeneous coordinates. The product \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H \cdot p_1^{i}$$\end{document} is computed as a standard matrix-vector multiplication, yielding a 3-vector. This vector is then normalized by its third component to obtain the predicted inhomogeneous coordinates before computing the Euclidean distance to the target point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_2^{i}$$\end{document}. By optimizing the reprojection error defined above, the network can accurately estimate the homography matrix, thus achieving high-precision image registration. Unlike traditional methods, the deep homography estimation approach automatically learns features and performs geometric transformation estimation through the neural network, significantly improving registration accuracy.

While the DLT solver itself is a standard component, the key contribution of our homography module is the use of SE-enhanced and multi-scale pooled features (derived from Sections 2.2.1–2.2.3) as input to the regression network. This design improves the estimation accuracy for scenes with small objects.

Within the deep learning framework, image registration relies not only on the estimation of the homography matrix but also involves multiple stages, including feature extraction, feature matching, and transformation optimization. Initially, the network extracts multi-level features from the images using a convolutional neural network (CNN) and matches these features to establish correspondences between the images. Next, the deep homography estimation module computes the transformation matrix between the images. Finally, after transformation optimization, the network outputs the final alignment result.

In the image registration process, in addition to the homography matrix, deep matching and transformation optimization modules are introduced. The deep homography matching module leverages deep features to enhance the matching accuracy between images, particularly in regions with complex backgrounds and small objects. Weighted loss function design

Traditional loss function designs typically assign equal weights to all pixel regions, which can lead to insufficient training and affect the image stitching quality, especially when dealing with small objects or complex details. To overcome this issue, this chapter introduces a weighted loss function that emphasizes the small object regions, thereby increasing their contribution to the overall loss during training and improving their alignment accuracy in the stitching process.

To better optimize the small object regions, this chapter incorporates a weighting mechanism into the loss function for the image stitching task. Specifically, the importance of each pixel region is dynamically adjusted, altering its weight in the loss function. The weighted design of the loss function is expressed by the following formula:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {L_{weighted}} = \sum \limits _{i = 1}^n {{\lambda _i}} \cdot {L_i}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L_{weighted}}$$\end{document} represents the weighted loss function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L_i}$$\end{document} denotes the standard loss for the i-th pixel, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda _i}$$\end{document} is the weighting coefficient for the i-th pixel, which controls the importance of that pixel region during training. The weighting coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda _i}$$\end{document} can be dynamically adjusted based on the region information of small objects, ensuring that small object areas receive higher weight when calculating the loss.

To dynamically adjust the weighting coefficient for each pixel region, this paper introduces a weighting calculation method based on image region features. The weighting coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda _i}$$\end{document} is primarily adjusted according to the size of the pixel’s region and the significance of small objects. The weighting coefficient for small object regions can be calculated using the following formula:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _i = \frac{1 / A_i}{\sum _{j=1}^{n} (1 / A_j)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where n is the total number of pixel regions. This ensures that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum _i \lambda _i = 1$$\end{document} and that regions with smaller area have proportionally larger influence on the loss. This inverse-area weighting compensates for the natural under-representation of small objects in the loss calculation, forcing the network to pay more attention to accurately aligning small object regions during training.

The area-based weighting is motivated by the fact that small objects occupy fewer pixels; without such weighting, their contribution to the total loss is inherently suppressed. This strategy is analogous to class-balancing techniques in object detection. Other strategies such as gradient-based or saliency-based weighting are promising but left for future exploration.

Compared to distance-based or saliency-based weighting schemes, the area-based method is simpler and computationally efficient, which is desirable for real-time stitching tasks.

In the design of the loss function, this chapter also introduces a multi-weighting mechanism, combining different loss terms to ensure that each loss term contributes to the optimization of the small object regions. The weighted loss function is divided into several components, including position loss, content loss, and stitching error loss. The weighting formula for each loss term is as follows:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {L_{total}} = \alpha \cdot {L_{position}} + \beta \cdot {L_{content}} + \gamma \cdot {L_{stitch}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Position loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\text {position}}$$\end{document}: Measures the alignment accuracy of keypoints or feature correspondences after warping.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{\text {position}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{k=1}^{N} \Vert \text {normalize}(\hat{H} \cdot q_k) - r_k \Vert ^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_k$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_k$$\end{document} are homogeneous coordinates of matched keypoints in the two input images (extracted by SIFT or learned features), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{H}$$\end{document} is the estimated homography, and N is the number of matches.

Content loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\text {content}}$$\end{document}: Preserves perceptual and structural information, especially important for small object details. It is defined as a perceptual loss using pre-trained VGG-19 features:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{\text {content}} =\sum _{l} \frac{1}{C_l H_l W_l} \Vert \phi _l(I_{\text {stitched}}) - \phi _l(I_{\text {target}}) \Vert _2^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_l, H_l, W_l$$\end{document} denote the number of channels, height, and width of the feature map at layer l, respectively; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _l(\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the feature map at layer l of VGG-19, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{\text {stitched}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{\text {target}}$$\end{document} are the stitched image and the target image (the warped version of the second image), respectively.

Stitch loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\text {stitch}}$$\end{document}: Minimizes seam artifacts along the stitching boundary. It is computed as the mean squared intensity difference between overlapping pixels after warping:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{\text {stitch}} = \frac{1}{|O|} \sum _{(u,v) \in O} \Vert I_1(u,v) - \text {warp}(I_2)(u,v) \Vert ^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where O is the set of overlapping pixel coordinates between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_1$$\end{document} and the warped \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_2$$\end{document}, and |O| is the number of overlapping pixels. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {warp}(I_2)(u,v)$$\end{document} denotes the pixel value of the warped image (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_2$$\end{document} transformed by the estimated homography) at coordinate (u,v).

The coefficients α, β, and γ are the respective weighting factors for each loss term. By applying weights to different loss components, this approach allows for a more focused alignment of small objects during training, while ensuring the consistency of the global stitching result.

Experimental results and analysis

This section introduces the dataset, experimental platform, evaluation indicators and comparative experimental results used in the experiment. It includes the performance comparison between the baseline model and our model, as well as ablation experiments. The experimental results verify the effectiveness of the proposed model in the image stitching task.

Dataset introduction

In this study, we pre-train our model using the Warped MS-COCO dataset25 and subsequently fine-tune and train it on the UDIS9 and MVS-Synth41 datasets. The deep homography module is first pre-trained on the Warped COCO dataset using synthetic homographies. For the main experiments on UDIS and MVS-Synth, the entire model is then fine-tuned without any additional pre-trained weights from object detection models. These datasets exhibit an average overlap rate of over 90% and encompass a variety of dynamic scenes, including indoor, outdoor, nighttime, and dimly lit environments. The average overlap rate is 92.3% for UDIS and 91.7% for MVS-Synth, calculated as the ratio of overlapping area to total image area over all image pairs. Figure 5 presents some of the scenes included in these datasets, along with the overlap rates of a subset of scene images. The training set of the UDIS dataset consists of 10,500 samples, while the test set includes 1,100 samples. The training set of the MVS-Synth dataset comprises 6,200 samples, with 150 samples in the test set. Due to the presence of numerous small objects or targets in the sample images, the samples are resized or normalized to 640×640 to ensure the accuracy of the detection process.

Our training is unsupervised for the stitching task (no ground-truth stitched images). The networks are trained to minimize the weighted loss defined in Eq. (18). Although the datasets contain small objects, we do not use bounding box annotations; instead, we evaluate stitching quality in small-object regions via PSNR/SSIM and visual inspection.Fig. 5Overlap rates.

Overlap rates.

Each stage is optimized relative to YOLOv8, and the Adam optimizer42 is used to train the distortion network and fusion network. The homography estimation network is trained for 100 epochs, and the fusion network for 50 epochs (total 150 epochs), employing an exponentially decaying learning rate strategy. The learning rate is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-4}$$\end{document}, with a batch size of 192 for the first stage and 128 for the second stage. These batch sizes are feasible on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 with 24 GB VRAM; gradient accumulation was not needed. The entire training process is unsupervised, requiring only the target images as input without the need for labels. During the testing phase, stitching two input images with a resolution of 640×640 takes approximately 0.3 seconds. To ensure fairness and comparability, no pre-trained weights were used during the model training for all ablation and comparison experiments.

Experimental platform and details

In terms of hardware and software, we utilized an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8255C processor with 12 cores and 24 threads, 24 GB of system memory, and a NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 graphics processing unit (GPU) with 24 GB of video memory. The deep learning model framework used was PyTorch 1.11.0, along with the baseline version of YOLOv8 and the Ultralytics library. The operating system employed was Ubuntu 18.04.

For evaluation, we used metrics such as Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), and GFLOPs as operation count. A higher PSNR value indicates better image quality with less distortion or noise. The SSIM value ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating greater similarity between the image and the original. In our experiments, The Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) is computed between the stitched output image and the ground-truth stitched image (or, when ground truth is unavailable, between the overlapping regions of the two input images after alignment). Let T and F denote the reference (ground-truth) image and the stitched image generated by our method, respectively. Both images have the same dimensions: height m and width n. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) is defined as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} MSE = \frac{1}{{m \cdot n}}\mathop \sum \limits _{i = 0}^{m - 1} \mathop \sum \limits _{j = 0}^{n - 1} {\left[ {T(i,j) - F(i,j)} \right] ^2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where T(i,j) and F(i,j) are the pixel values at position (i,j) in the reference and stitched images, respectively.

The PSNR is then computed as:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} PSNR({I^A},{I^B}) = 10 \cdot {\log _{10}}(\frac{{MAX_I^2}}{{MSE}}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {MAX}_I$$\end{document} is the maximum possible pixel value (e.g., 255 for 8-bit images). The MSE formula calculates the square of the difference between each corresponding pixel, and then substitutes the result into the PSNR formula. The calculation formula of SSIM is used to evaluate performance. The specific calculation formula is as follows:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} SSIM({I^A},{I^B}) = l{({I^A},{I^B})^\alpha } \cdot c{({I^A},{I^B})^\beta } \cdot s{({I^A},{I^B})^\gamma }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l{({I^A},{I^B})^\alpha }$$\end{document} is used to compare the brightness of two images, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c{({I^A},{I^B})^\beta }$$\end{document} is used to compare the contrast of two images, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s{({I^A},{I^B})^\gamma }$$\end{document} is used to compare the structural information of two images. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l(I^A, I^B) = \frac{2\mu _A\mu _B + C_1}{\mu _A^2 + \mu _B^2 + C_1}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c(I^A, I^B) = \frac{2\sigma _A\sigma _B + C_2}{\sigma _A^2 + \sigma _B^2 + C_2}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s(I^A, I^B) = \frac{\sigma _{AB} + C_3}{\sigma _A\sigma _B + C_3}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu , \sigma , \sigma _{AB}$$\end{document} denote mean, standard deviation, and cross-covariance, respectively. α, β, and γ are used to adjust the weight of the comparison function. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PSNR({I^A},{I^B})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SSIM({I^A},{I^B})$$\end{document} are used to calculate the PSNR and SSIM values between two images respectively.

The hyperparameter α in Eqs. (4) and (9) controls the balance between original and enhanced feature maps. Based on a grid search on a validation subset, we set α = 0.3 for low-resolution enhancement (Equation 4) and α = 0.5 for multi-scale fusion (Eq. 9). These values achieved the best PSNR on the validation set. For general use, α = 0.3-0.5 is recommended for similar stitching tasks.

Conclusion

This study presents an image stitching method based on an enhanced YOLOv8 framework, aimed at addressing the limitations of traditional image stitching techniques in small object detection and complex background scenarios. By introducing low-resolution feature map enhancement, multi-scale feature extraction modules, and a deep homography module, the accuracy of image stitching is significantly improved, particularly when handling images with large disparities and small objects. Through innovative modifications to the YOLOv8 framework, this method leverages an adaptive weighting mechanism for low-resolution feature maps, effectively enhancing the detection of small objects in complex scenes. Additionally, the multi-scale feature extraction module strengthens the model’s ability to capture features at various scales, while the deep homography module reduces errors caused by viewpoint differences through more precise geometric transformation estimation. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves higher accuracy compared to traditional geometric methods and other deep learning-based stitching techniques, especially when addressing small object detection and large disparity issues. Future research could further focus on improving the model’s adaptability to dynamic scenes and exploring its potential in real-time applications, thereby expanding the practical applications and value of image stitching technology. A limitation of this study is the moderate size of the public datasets and the absence of real drone-captured evaluations. Future work will collect a larger drone image stitching dataset and evaluate the proposed method on real UAV platforms under various unseen environments.Fig. 6Visualization result comparison.
