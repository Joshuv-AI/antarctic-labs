Multi-Target Bactericidal Mechanisms of Cinnamon Essential Oil Against Xanthomonas euvesicatoria: Transcriptomic Insights into Severe Metabolic Suppression and Divisome Arrest
Authors: Yu Na Kim, Hyo-Song Nam, Jang Hoon Lee, Seon Hwa Kim, Young Cheol Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While cinnamon essential oil (CEO) is increasingly recognized as a potent natural antimicrobial, its precise multi-target mechanisms against the destructive agricultural pathogen Xanthomonas euvesicatoria remain unclear. This study comprehensively elucidated the physiological, biochemical, and transcriptomic responses of X. euvesicatoria 173 to CEO-induced stress. We investigated its antibacterial efficacy and specific modes of action using microdilution assays, scanning electron microscopy, biochemical viability tracking, and RNA-seq. CEO exhibited robust dose-dependent antibacterial activity, with a minimum inhibitory concentration of 400 mg/L and a half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) of 164 mg/L. Analyses revealed that CEO severely compromised cell membrane integrity, leading to a rapid 100-fold increased hypersensitivity to osmotic stress and significant suppression of biofilm formation. Furthermore, sub-lethal CEO exposure induced a severe energy crisis by disrupting membrane-dependent ATP synthesis—measured as a significant reduction in F1F0-ATPase activity—and caused abnormal cellular elongation, indicating cell division arrest. These distinct phenotypes were supported by comparative transcriptomics at the sub-lethal EC50 dose, which revealed 209 highly significant differentially expressed genes (|log2FC| > 3.321). CEO massively downregulated genes associated with the electron transport chain and core energy metabolism (nuo, ndh, cyd, and cyo operons), the ftsZ divisome network, and biofilm-associated type IV pilus and chemotaxis. Conversely, oxidative stress defense genes (kat and ahpD) were upregulated. In conclusion, CEO exerts its bactericidal activity through a synergistic, multi-target action—simultaneously dismantling membrane energetics, inducing severe metabolic suppression, and repressing vital developmental architectures. These findings highlight the significant potential of CEO as a sustainable, resistance-breaking biopesticide for crop protection.

Bacterial leaf spot, caused by Xanthomonas euvesicatoria (X. euvesicatoria) is one of the most destructive diseases of pepper and tomato in warm, humid climates worldwide, leading to significant economic losses (Potnis et al., 2015; Shi and Panthee, 2020). Strain X. euvesicatoria 173 is a pathogenic strain isolated from diseased pepper in Korea (Kyeon et al., 2016). Current management strategies for the bacterial leaf spot include the use of resistant cultivars (Potnis et al., 2019; Subedi et al., 2024), disease-free seeds and seedlings, crop rotation (Osdaghi et al., 2021), and biological control (Hernández-Huerta et al., 2023). Chemical control by copper-based compounds, streptomycin, and mancozeb, suffers from the emergence of strains resistant to these compounds (Martin et al., 2004; Minsavage et al., 1990; Ritchie, 1991). Copper-resistant Xanthomonas strains were reported as early as the 1980s, and streptomycin resistance is a long-standing issue (Adaskaveg and Hine, 1985; Marco, 1983). These limitations not only complicate disease control but also raise environmental and human health concerns, underscoring the urgent need for sustainable alternative control methods.

Plant-derived essential oils (EOs) have emerged as a promising source of novel antimicrobial agents. For example, recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of oregano and thyme essential oils in suppressing bacterial leaf spot in tomato and pepper (Lucas et al., 2012; Mancini et al., 2015). Many EOs are classified as ‘Generally Recognised as Safe’ (GRAS) by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, making them appropriate for application in food and agriculture (Nabavi et al., 2015). Furthermore, because EOs are complex mixtures of compounds, the development of resistance in the pathogen is less likely compared to bactericides with single target sites (Ju et al., 2023). These characteristics make EOs an attractive and eco-friendly alternative to supplement or replace conventional chemical pesticides (Ju et al., 2023).

Among various EOs, cinnamon essential oil (CEO) with cinnamaldehyde as the primary component, has broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity inhibiting growth of human and plant pathogens, including Staphylococcus, Bacillus, and Enterobacter isolates. Cinnamaldehyde disrupts the bacterial cell membrane structure (Jaramillo Jimenez et al., 2024), inhibits cell division (Chai et al., 2022), and suppresses the activity of enzymes like ATPase (Jaramillo Jimenez et al., 2024). These effects impair multiple vital cellular processes simultaneously, enabling effective bactericidal action (Ju et al., 2023).

While the effects of CEO on foodborne pathogens are relatively well-documented, the mechanisms against agriculturally important phytopathogens like X. euvesicatoria are lesser studied. Nam et al. (2025) reported altered levels of proteins associated with membrane function in treatments of X. euvesicatoria strain 173 by CEO. A TonB-dependent receptor and Clp proteases increased in accumulation, whereas ATP-binding cassette transporter proteins were decreased with CEO presence.

Although these findings suggest that CEO’s primary mode of action involves disrupting cellular homeostasis, the underlying regulatory changes at the gene expression level remain poorly understood. To address this knowledge gap, we (1) investigated the physiological, biochemical, and morphological impacts of CEO on X. euvesicatoria 173 through microscopic and biochemical analyses, and (2) profiled the global transcriptomic changes after sub-lethal CEO treatment using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to uncover the molecular mechanisms driving these phenotypes. Our integrated approach reveals that CEO exerts a potent, multi-target bactericidal effect by simultaneously inducing severe membrane damage, catastrophic energy depletion via respiratory chain suppression, and the transcriptional silencing of critical developmental networks, specifically the ftsZ divisome and biofilm-associated processes. By dismantling these fundamental cellular systems simultaneously, this study elucidates the primary cellular targets of CEO and highlights its exceptional potential as a sustainable, resistance-breaking bio-bactericide for the effective management of pepper bacterial spot.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

The X. euvesicatoria strain 173 causes bacterial spot on pepper (Kyeon et al., 2016; Nam et al., 2025). Cultures were grown on nutrient broth (NB, Difco™; BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) at 28°C with shaking at 150 rpm. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using a UV/VIS spectrophotometer (MicroDigital Co., Ltd., Seongnam, Korea), and the colony-forming units (cfu) obtained from colony numbers cultured on nutrient broth agar (NA).

Determination of effective concentrations

Cinnamon essential oil (CEO, ≥98% purity) was purchased from Green Focus Co., Ltd. (Asan, Korea). A stock solution of CEO stock solution (20,000 mg/L) was prepared by dissolving in 70% ethanol. The half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of CEO against X. euvesicatoria 173 were determined from cultures grown in NB in 96-well plates. Each well contained a final volume of 100 μL.

The inoculum was prepared from a late exponential phase culture of X. euvesicatoria 173 grown in nutrient broth (NB) at 28°C. The CEO stock was serially diluted in NB medium to achieve targeted test concentrations of 2,000, 1,000, 500, 250, 125, and 62.5 mg/L in a 96-well microtiter plate. Following the initial screening, a narrower concentration range (e.g., 300, 350, 400, and 450 mg/L) was tested to pinpoint the exact MIC. Each well was then inoculated with the bacterial suspension to achieve an initial bacterial density of 1 × 108 cfu/mL. The final volume in each well was 100 μL, consisting entirely of the NB medium, the specific CEO concentration, and the bacterial inoculum. The microtiter plates were incubated at 28°C with shaking at 150 rpm for 48 h. The extent of bacterial growth was quantitatively assessed by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600). The effective concentrations required for 50% (EC50) and 70% (EC70) growth inhibition were calculated using linear regression analysis. Specific to the initial cell density and growth phase of these test conditions, the MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of CEO that completely suppressed bacterial multiplication, corresponding to no net increase in OD600 relative to the initial baseline inoculum.

RNA-seq analysis

Cells of X. euvesicatoria 173 were cultured in 100 mL of NB in flasks to the late exponential phase. A suspension of these cells, adjusted to an initial density of 1 × 108 cfu/mL in NB, was amended with the CEO stock solution to achieve a final sub-lethal EC50 concentration (164 mg/L). This specific sub-lethal dose and a short exposure time of 2 h were deliberately selected; unlike the 48-h growth inhibition assays used to determine final biomass, the 2-h time point captures the pathogen’s primary and highly active transcriptional reprogramming in response to stress, completely avoiding the secondary degradation of RNA and terminal cell lysis that obscure transcriptomic data at lethal doses (MIC) or prolonged exposures. The cultures were incubated for 2 h with shaking at 150 rpm, following the procedures of Nam et al. (2025). As a negative control, an equal volume of 70% ethanol (the solvent used for the CEO stock) was added to the cell suspension instead of the CEO stock. This entire treatment process was independently performed in three biological replicates. Following the 2-h treatment, the cells were immediately harvested by centrifugation, and total RNA was extracted from the pelleted cells using a RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was removed from 1 μg aliquots of the total RNA extracted from three independent biological replicates for both the CEO-treated and control groups using the RiboCop rRNA Depletion for Bacteria Probe Mix G+/− Kit (Lexogen, Inc., Vienna, Austria). The cDNA libraries were constructed from the rRNA-depleted RNA using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA-seq Kit (New England BioLabs, Inc., Ipswich, MA, USA). The quality and average fragment size of the libraries were assessed with a TapeStation HS D1000 Screen Tape (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and quantification was performed using a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA). The 100 bp paired-end sequencing was conducted on a Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

The raw sequencing reads from each of the six samples (three treated and three controls) were quality-controlled before mapping to the reference genome from strain X. euvesicatoria 173 (RefSeq assembly GCF_000756295.1) using Bowtie2 (v2.3.5) software. Gene expression levels were quantified as read counts using Bedtools. Normalization across the biological replicates was performed using the EdgeR package (v3.34.1) in R, which accurately estimates biological dispersion based on the triplicate data. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis comparing the CEO-treated group to the control group was conducted using ExDEGA v5.1.1.4 software (e-Biogen, Republic of Korea), defining genes with a fold change > 10 (|log2 fold change| > 3.321) and a P-value < 0.05 as significant DEGs. Functional enrichment analysis of the selected DEG list was performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) v2023q4 (Huang et al., 2007).

Loss of salt tolerance

To measure changes in the salinity tolerance of cells following CEO treatment, X. euvesicatoria 173 cells were examined for their survival on nutrient agar (NA) containing 5% NaCl (Carson et al., 2002). Cells of X. euvesicatoria 173, grown in NB to the late exponential phase (1 × 108 cfu/mL), were treated with CEO at the EC50 concentration (164 mg/L) at 28°C with shaking at 150 rpm for 2 h. Following the 2-hour CEO treatment, aliquots were collected at 30, 60, and 120 minutes after the initial treatment to assess the time-dependent reduction in salt tolerance. As a negative control, the bacterial suspension was treated with an equal volume of 70% ethanol (the solvent used for the CEO stock) under the same conditions. Following incubation, ten-fold serial dilutions of both the CEO-treated and control cultures were spread onto NA plates supplemented with 5% NaCl. The viable colony-forming units (cfu) were counted after a 48-h incubation at 28°C.

Measurement of ATPase activity

To determine if CEO disrupts the membrane-bound F1F0-ATP synthase complex—a critical energy-generating component linked to the electron transport chain (ETC)—the intracellular ATPase activity was measured. X. euvesicatoria 173 was cultured in NB to the late exponential phase to maintain consistency across all physiological assays. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to an initial density of 1 × 108 cfu/mL in NB. These cultures were then treated with CEO at the EC50 (164 mg/L) and MIC (400 mg/L) concentrations. An untreated NB culture served as the control. The samples were incubated at 28°C with shaking at 150 rpm. After 2 h of treatment, aliquots of the treated and control growth cultures were directly harvested by centrifugation. To thoroughly remove the culture medium and residual CEO, the pelleted cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). The PBS-washed bacterial cells from each treatment were then resuspended in PBS and strictly adjusted to a uniform density of 1 × 108 cfu/mL. Intracellular ATPase activity was quantified using a commercial ATPase assay kit (ATPase Assay Kit; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This assay measures the release of inorganic phosphate (Pi) resulting from the enzymatic hydrolysis of ATP, serving as an in vitro proxy for the functional capacity of the F1F0-ATPase complex. The results were expressed as a relative percentage (%) of ATPase activity in the CEO-exposed cells compared to the untreated control (which was set to 100%). The entire assay was independently performed using three biological replicates, with three technical replicates included per biological sample.

Biofilm inhibition assay

Suspensions of the X. euvesicatoria 173 strain, grown to the late exponential phase, were adjusted to 1 × 108 cfu/mL in NB and transferred to 96-well microtiter plates. The bacterial cells were then treated with CEO at the EC50 and MIC concentrations. To allow for successful surface attachment and microcolony formation, the samples were incubated at 28°C under static conditions for 48 h. After incubation, the planktonic cell suspension was carefully discarded, and the wells were washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4) to remove non-adherent cells. The plates were air-dried and stained with a 0.2% (w/v) crystal violet solution for 15 min at room temperature. The excess staining solution was discarded, and the plates were gently washed with distilled water and dried completely. The retained biofilm cells were then solubilized in 100% ethanol to release the bound crystal violet. The absorbance of the released crystal violet in the extracts was measured at 590 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Relative biofilm formation was calculated by normalizing the absorbance values of the CEO-treated groups against the values obtained from the untreated control (Kamimura et al., 2022; O’Toole, 2011; Stepanovic et al., 2000). This assay was independently performed in four biological replicates.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and quantitative morphological analysis

To evaluate the effect of CEO on the morphology and cell division of X. euvesicatoria 173, SEM was performed. Bacterial cultures grown to the late exponential phase (1 × 108 cfu/mL) were treated with CEO at the EC50, EC70, and MIC levels for 2 h at 28°C. An untreated culture incubated under the same conditions served as the control. Following the 2-h treatment, the cells were harvested by centrifugation (8,000 × g, 5 min) and washed twice with PBS (pH 7.4). The cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS, the samples were sequentially dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% v/v) for 15 min at each step. The dehydrated cells were dried using a critical point dryer, mounted on aluminum stubs, and sputter-coated with platinum (or gold). Observations were made using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Gemini 500, Zeiss, Germany) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectroscopy system (EDS; Oxford X-MaxN 80, Cambridge, UK).

Quantitative analysis of bacterial cell length was conducted to statistically evaluate the extent of filamentation and cell division arrest. For each treatment group (Control, EC50, EC70, and MIC), the lengths of 100 randomly selected intact bacterial cells were manually measured from the obtained high-resolution SEM images using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). To ensure statistical robustness, these measurements were taken from samples representing three independent biological replicates (n = 100 per treatment). The quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between the CEO-treated groups and the untreated control was analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using three independent biological replicates (with the exception of the biofilm assay, which utilized four replicates) to ensure reproducibility. Within each independent biological replicate, three technical replicates were performed for each treatment condition. Data are presented as the mean ± SD derived from the biological replicates. The statistical significance between two groups was analyzed using a Student’s t-test (α = 0.05). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.05). Homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test (α = 0.05). For comparisons involving three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA, α = 0.05) was performed. When statistically significant differences were detected, Dunnett’s post hoc test was used to compare the specific treatment groups directly against the untreated control. Where applicable across multiple inter-group comparisons, Tukey’s method and Scheffé’s method were utilized. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Antibacterial activity of cinnamon essential oil against X. euvesicatoria 173

As summarized in Table 1, CEO exhibited a potent, dose-dependent inhibitory effect on the growth of X. euvesicatoria 173 cells, which were treated at the late exponential phase with an initial inoculum density of 1 × 108 cfu/mL. The EC50 was 164 mg/L (95% confidence interval [CI]: 147–181 mg/L), and the EC70 value was 314 mg/L (95% CI: 287–357 mg/L). Complete suppression of bacterial multiplication—defined as the MIC where no net increase in optical density (OD600) was observed relative to the initial inoculum—was achieved at a CEO concentration of 400 mg/L. The subsequent physiological and transcriptomic assays employed these specific concentrations (EC50, EC70, and MIC) depending on the experimental objectives.

CEO induces severe morphological damage and cellular elongation

To investigate the physical impact of CEO on the developmental morphology and envelope integrity of X. euvesicatoria 173, high-resolution SEM and quantitative cell length analyses were performed (Fig. 1). Untreated control cells exhibited a standard, uniform rod-shaped morphology with smooth surfaces (Fig. 1A). Following a 2-h exposure to sub-lethal concentrations of CEO (EC50 and EC70), the cells displayed distinct morphological aberrations, characterized by pronounced surface roughening and cellular elongation. Quantitative measurement corroborated these visual observations (Fig. 1B); the mean cell length significantly increased from approximately 1,600 nm in the control to nearly 1,900 nm (****P < 0.0001) in the sub-lethal groups, indicating robust filamentation due to cell division arrest. Conversely, treatment at the MIC induced severe structural damage, including deep surface wrinkling and catastrophic cell rupture (Fig. 1A, red arrow). Interestingly, the MIC-treated cells did not exhibit significant elongation compared to the control (ns), reflecting an immediate arrest of all growth processes and the rapid induction of physical lysis before the slower process of filamentation could occur.

CEO increases bacterial sensitivity to osmotic stress

However, the sensitivity of cells to 5% NaCl also increased after exposure to CEO at EC50 (Fig. 2A). Within 30 minutes of exposure, the number of culturable cells dropped by nearly 100-fold compared to the control (**P < 0.01). This significantly suppressed survival rate was consistently maintained at 60 minutes (*P < 0.05) and 120 minutes (**P < 0.01) post-treatment. These results indicated that the structural integrity of the cell envelope was impaired by CEO treatment.

CEO significantly suppresses biofilm formation

To evaluate the anti-biofilm potential of CEO against X. euvesicatoria 173, a crystal violet staining assay was performed (Fig. 2B). At the EC50 dose biofilm formation was reduced approximately 50% (***P < 0.001) and this increased to about 70% (***P < 0.001) with treatments with the MIC dose compared with the density of biofilm cells formed by nontreated cells. These findings suggest that CEO would limit surface-attached communities, a process likely to be important in pathogenesis for this pathogen.

CEO disrupts membrane-dependent ATP synthesis

Plasma membrane integrity is strictly required for the production of ATP by the membrane-embedded F1F0-ATP synthase complex, which functions utilizing the proton motive force generated across the intact lipid bilayer by the electron transport chain. To determine if the structural envelope damage caused by CEO disrupts this critical energy-generating machinery, the functional activity of this complex (measured in vitro as ATPase activity) was quantified (Fig. 2C). The assay revealed a significant, dose-dependent decrease in ATPase activity following CEO treatment. Exposure to the sub-lethal EC50 dose resulted in a significant impairment of this enzyme function (**P < 0.01). Furthermore, treatment at the MIC reduced the measurable activity by approximately 25% compared to the untreated control (***P < 0.001). These biochemical changes are highly consistent with our morphological findings, confirming that CEO profoundly alters the membrane structure, thereby dissipating the transmembrane proton gradient and severely compromising the pathogen’s capacity to synthesize ATP.

Transcriptomic profiling of X. euvesicatoria 173 in response to CEO stress

Treatment of X. euvesicatoria 173 with CEO at the sub-lethal EC50 concentration (164 mg/L induced massive transcriptional reprogramming. Initial screening using a standard significance threshold (|log2 fold change| > 1, p < 0.05) identified 1,548 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs), of which 684 were upregulated and 864 were downregulated (data not shown). To focus on the most profound transcriptomic shifts and eliminate background noise, we applied a highly stringent cutoff of |log2 fold change| ≥ 3.321 (corresponding to a 10-fold change). As illustrated in the volcano plot (Fig. 3), this strict criterion refined the data to 209 core DEGs, comprising 134 significantly upregulated and 75 downregulated genes. The complete list of these 209 core DEGs, along with their comprehensive Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway functional annotations, is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Specific subsets of genes, each with related functions, are presented in Table 2 and Fig. 4.

Notably, the most significantly upregulated genes were associated with oxidative stress management, including superoxide dismutase (sodB), catalases (katE, katG, katB), and an alkyl hydroperoxide reductase (ahpD, Log2 FC = 4.242). These changes are consistent with CEO inducing severe accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Simultaneously, a profound downregulation of genes encoding factors associated with fundamental cellular metabolism and energy production was observed. The transcriptomic data revealed a severe repression of the respiratory ETC. Specifically, genes in the nuo and ndh operons, which encode structural subunits of the NADH-quinone oxidoreductase complex, alongside the terminal cytochrome oxidases (cyd and cyo operons), were significantly downregulated (Table 2). Crucially, this impairment of the ETC was accompanied by the reduced expression of genes encoding the ETC-associated F1F0-ATP synthase subunits (e.g., atpF, atpH), indicating a comprehensive severe suppression of energy metabolism (Table 2). Furthermore, as a consequence of this energetic crisis, downstream essential processes were halted; genes associated with DNA replication (dnaN, dnaE, dnaQ) and transcription (rpoA, rpoB, rpoC, rpoZ) exhibited heavily reduced accumulation (Table 2 and Fig. 4).

Consistent with our phenotypic observations of inhibited cell division and biofilm formation, structural and developmental gene networks were highly repressed. Key components of the divisome complex, particularly the master regulator ftsZ (Log2 FC = −1.077) and its associated spatial regulators (minD, minE), were significantly downregulated, providing a molecular basis for the observed septation failure. Additionally, genes encoding the structural and assembly proteins of the Type IV pilus (e.g., pilT, pilQ, filM) and chemotaxis signal transduction (e.g., cheB, cheR) were heavily suppressed (Log2 FC < −2.0) (Table 2 and Fig. 4).

Discussion

The quantitative assessment of CEO’s antibacterial activity reveals a highly potent inhibitory effect against X. euvesicatoria 173, highlighted by an MIC of 400 mg/L. Gram-negative bacteria, such as Xanthomonas species, are intrinsically more resistant to many hydrophobic essential oils due to the presence of an outer membrane rich in hydrophilic lipopolysaccharides (LPS), which restricts the penetration of lipophilic compounds (Burt, 2004). Despite this structural barrier, the low MIC observed with CEO is significantly lower than the effective concentrations (often >1,000 mg/L) typically required for the complete inhibition of Gram-negative phytopathogens (Fan et al., 2024). The robust antimicrobial efficacy of CEO at 400 mg/L is attributed to its primary constituent, cinnamaldehyde, which effectively penetrates the outer membrane, disrupts the lipid bilayers, and ultimately leads to severe metabolic suppression (Jaramillo Jimenez et al., 2024; Ju et al., 2023).

Exposing bacterial cells to lethal concentrations (MIC or higher) often causes rapid death, which can obscure the specific molecular targets of the antimicrobial agent. However, treatment at the sub-lethal EC50 (164 mg/L) concentration provided a critical window to study the specific stress responses of the pathogen. At this dose, the bacterial membrane experienced severe stress; although surface roughening was evident, the cells maintained sufficient physical integrity to prevent outright lysis and massive protein leakage. This distinct physiological state perfectly paralleled our transcriptomic findings, which demonstrated highly active but desperate transcriptional regulation. The treated cells mounted a robust active defense response, highlighted by the significant upregulation of genes encoding ROS-mitigating enzymes and multidrug efflux pumps (e.g., bepF, oqxB, and oprM), indicating an active attempt to manage oxidative stress and expel the lipophilic toxins (Supplementary Table 1).

However, this defense mechanism is ultimately undermined by a severe suppression in energy metabolism. Our transcriptomic data revealed a severe downregulation of the terminal functional components of the respiratory electron transport chain and ATP synthesis machinery, specifically the cytochrome oxidases (cyd and cyo operons) and ATP synthase subunits (atp genes) (Table 2). The simultaneous transcriptional silencing of these vital cyd/cyo and atp complexes perfectly explains the drastic reduction in intracellular ATPase activity observed in our biochemical assays. Consequently, while the cell attempts to upregulate energetically expensive defense mechanisms like efflux pumps, CEO simultaneously starves the pathogen of the ATP required to sustain these defenses, ensuring bacterial death.

Transcriptome analyses found that other sets of critical genes were downregulated in cells treated with the EC50 dose. Reduced expression of genes involved in the divisome correlated with the observed increased cell length, which is consistent with impaired cell division. When septation is specifically inhibited, the bacteria form elongated, filamentous structures (Justice et al., 2008). While the transcriptional downregulation of the ftsZ divisome network clearly contributes to the observed cellular elongation, this filamentation is likely not solely a direct morphological consequence of specific gene suppression. It represents a broader, classic SOS response triggered by CEO-induced severe environmental stress. By arresting cell division while allowing continued cell growth, the bacteria attempt to survive and initiate DNA repair mechanisms under lethal stress conditions. Bacterial cell division is primarily driven by the tubulin homolog FtsZ. FtsZ polymerizes to form a dynamic Z-ring at the midcell, which recruits other division proteins and constricts the envelope to achieve septation. Spatial regulators for the correct positioning of this septum are encoded by minD and minE. Each of these essential divisome genes was significantly downregulated in our transcription analyses. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that cinnamaldehyde directly binds to and inhibits the GTPase activity essential for FtsZ function (Domadia et al., 2007). This biochemical inhibition perfectly aligns with our transcriptomic and morphological observations of robust filamentous growth in X. euvesicatoria 173 exposed to sub-lethal CEO.

The macroscopic phenotypes of CEO-induced stress—namely, cellular filamentation and impaired biofilm formation—are intrinsically linked to the profound transcriptional silencing of specific structural and developmental gene networks. The observed cellular elongation is fundamentally explained by the downregulation of the FtsZ divisome complex, including its essential spatial regulators (minD and minE). When ftsZ expression is repressed, the bacteria fail to form the central septal Z-ring, resulting in a complete arrest of cell division and subsequent septation failure. Concurrently, CEO effectively neutralizes the pathogen’s ability to colonize surfaces by heavily suppressing chemotactic signal transduction (cheB, cheR) and Type IV pilus assembly (pilT, pilQ, filM). Because chemotactic motility and pilus-mediated attachment are strictly required for the initiation of microcolony formation and mature biofilm architecture (Li and Wang, 2011), this genetic repression provides a clear molecular mechanism for our in vitro anti-biofilm results. More importantly, as these structural appendages are critical virulence factors for host colonization in Xanthomonas species (Felipe et al., 2018), the simultaneous disruption of cell division and biofilm-associated networks suggests that CEO not only halts bacterial proliferation but also severely cripples the pathogen’s overall infectivity in planta.

Similarly, the reduced biofilm formation observed with the EC50 doses directly correlated with the severe downregulation of genes required for type IV pilus formation (pilT, pilQ, filM) and chemotaxis (cheB, cheR). A loss in mobility and biofilm formation has also been reported in studies with other bacterial species when treated with cinnamaldehyde (Firmino et al., 2018; Topa et al., 2018). The initiation of biofilm formation heavily relies on these precise functions for initial surface attachment and subsequent microcolony formation (Topa et al., 2018). Crucially, biofilm architecture and pilus-mediated motility are essential virulence factors for Xanthomonas pathogenicity, playing a critical role in the early stages of bacterial spot disease development and host colonization (Felipe et al., 2018). Consequently, the profound inhibition of biofilm formation and motility networks by CEO treatments could significantly reduce initial infection rates and overall disease pressure on pepper crops.

Damage to the membrane structure from CEO exposure is evident from the increased sensitivity to osmotic stress and the pronounced loss of ATPase activity. The accumulation of CEO components in the lipid bilayer causes physical destabilization, increased membrane fluidity, and the generalized disruption of membrane-bound transporters and osmoregulatory complexes, rather than targeting specific ion channels (Di Pasqua et al., 2007; Oussalah et al., 2006). The weakened survival of CEO-treated cells under osmotic pressure from 5% NaCl correlates with the concept of “sublethal injury,” where bacterial cells become hypersensitive to secondary environmental stresses; this response is a well-documented hallmark of membrane-active essential oils (Di Pasqua et al., 2007). This phenomenon suggests that field treatments with CEO could severely weaken the pathogen’s resilience to natural environmental fluctuations, a process that could significantly limit disease intensity and progression in planta.

The altered surface roughness of cells exposed to sub-lethal CEO doses likely reflects the disrupted assembly of the extracellular polymeric substance matrix and outer LPS leaflet. Because synthesizing these complex surface structures is highly energy-dependent, their impairment strongly indicates a severe cellular energy crisis. Indeed, our biochemical finding of significantly reduced F1F0-ATPase activity perfectly aligns with previous reports that cinnamaldehyde directly disrupts membrane architectures and transmembrane ATPase complexes in X. euvesicatoria (Shen et al., 2015). Crucially, this functional loss is entirely corroborated by our transcriptomic data, which revealed a comprehensive transcriptional silencing of the entire ETC and energy-converting machinery. Specifically, the nuo and ndh operons (initiating electron flow), the cyd and cyo operons (terminal cytochrome oxidases), and the atp genes (F1F0-ATP synthase subunits) were drastically downregulated. Furthermore, the simultaneous physical and transcriptional dismantling of these ETC components inevitably causes aberrant electron leakage. This leakage directly triggers an intracellular burst of ROS, comprehensively explaining the pathogen’s compensatory upregulation of essential antioxidant defense genes (sodB, katG, and ahpD) to survive the CEO-induced oxidative stress.

In conclusion, this study finds that the antimicrobial effect of CEO involves multi-target impacts rather than acting through a single effect that fuels rapid acquisition of resistance in the microbe. Our work illustrates that at sublethal levels, X. euvesicatoria 173 cells respond by changing gene expression to mitigate oxidative stress whilst there is downregulation of genes for growth and cell division. Disturbances in both the outer surface of the cell, as well as the function of the electron chain in the inner membrane contribute to depleted cell performance. Processes such as biofilm formation important in pathogenesis of X. euvesicatoria 173 are retarded by CEO. Severe suppression of energy metabolism likely related to the disruption of membrane structure by cinnamaldehyde in CEO reduced cell viability. The MIC value at 400 mg/L indicates CEO is an attractive alternative to other bacteriocides, including formulations of essential oils. Collectively the findings suggest that CEO applications would be beneficial in reducing the disease caused by X. euvesicatoria 173 in crop production.
