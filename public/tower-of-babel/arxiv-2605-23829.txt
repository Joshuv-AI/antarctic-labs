Outer automorphism groups of hyperbolic groups, bounded extensions, and hierarchical hyperbolicity
Ervin Hadžiosmanović, Giorgio Mangioni

Abstract
. We prove that the outer automorphism group of a one-ended hyperbolic group is virtually a hierarchically hyperbolic group (HHG), under mild orientability conditions on the associated JSJ decomposition. This is done by proving that a finite-index subgroup is a central extension of a product of orbifold mapping class groups, and the extension has bounded Euler class. Our theorem is sharp: we exhibit a surface amalgam whose fundamental group has full outer automorphism group which is not a HHG. To prove this, the main technical tool is the fact that a top-dimensional Abelian subgroup of a HHG is a standard flat.

Introduction

Outer automorphism groups of hyperbolic groups are of utmost importance in combinatorial and geometric group theory. One of the many reasons is that this class includes both
\operatorname{Out}\left(\mathbb{F}_{n}\right)
and mapping class groups of closed surfaces, which are ubiquitous in the study of low-dimensional topology. Furthermore, a key step in the solution of the isomorphism problem for hyperbolic groups (see, among others,
[
51
,
53
,
19
]
) was a description of the outer automorphism group of a one-ended hyperbolic group
G
in terms of certain virtually Abelian extensions of mapping class groups. We improve this description by showing that
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
is virtually a
bounded
central extension:

Theorem A

(See
2.5
)
.

Let
G
be a one-ended hyperbolic group. Then
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
is virtually a direct product
\mathbb{Z}^{q}\times M
, where
q\geq 0
and
M
fits inside a bounded central extension

1\to Z_{s}\to M\to\prod\operatorname{MCG}\left(G_{v}\right)\to 1.

Here
Z_{s}
is finitely generated Abelian, the product has finitely many factors, and each
\operatorname{MCG}\left(G_{v}\right)
is a finite extension of a finite-index subgroup of the mapping class group of a hyperbolic orbifold.

Let us explain the above terminology. We say that a group
G
is a
finite extension
of a group
H
if
G
surjects onto
H
with finite kernel. Furthermore, a central extension
1\to K\to G\to H\to 1
is
bounded
if the associated Euler class
[G]\in H^{2}(H,K)
is represented by a cocycle
\omega\colon H\times H\to K
taking bounded values. Finally, we refer to
Section

1.2
below for more details on orbifolds. For experts, the product is over all
quadratically hanging subgroups

G_{v}
appearing in the
JSJ decomposition
over virtually
\mathbb{Z}
subgroups: see
Section

1.4
and the outline below.

If we further assume that
G
is torsion-free, then all orbifolds are genuine surfaces, and all finite extensions are trivial. Hence in this case
A
has a simpler form:

Corollary B
.

Let
G
be a one-ended, torsion-free hyperbolic group. Then
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
is virtually a bounded central extension of a direct product of finitely many mapping class groups of hyperbolic surfaces.

While the existence of the central extension from
A
follows from a careful inspection of several, often implicit, results in the literature (see, among others,
[
53
,
14
,
37
,
19
]
), the fact that the extension is bounded is new. To the eye of a geometric group theorist, one of the main consequences of boundedness is that the extension is
quasi-isometrically trivial
, meaning that it is quasi-isometric to the direct product of the kernel and the quotient
[
27
]
. In the case where all orbifolds from
A
are
orientable
, we can dramatically improve our understanding of the coarse geometry of
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
:

Theorem C

(See
2.10
)
.

Let
G
be a one-ended hyperbolic group. Suppose that all quadratically hanging subgroups appearing in the JSJ decomposition of
G
are orientable. Then
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
is virtually a hierarchically hyperbolic group.

Hierarchically hyperbolic groups
(HHGs), as first introduced in
[
8
]
, provide a common framework for mapping class group of surfaces, most cubulated groups, most 3-manifold groups, and more. As such, techniques from low-dimensional topology and the world of CAT(0) cube complexes can be exploited to prove a plethora of properties of (virtual) HHGs; a very non-exhaustive list includes results on their asymptotic dimension
[
7
]
, quasiflats
[
9
]
, and on being quasi-isometric to CAT(0) cube complexes
[
50
]
.

We emphasise that the JSJ decomposition from
A
can be trivial: this happens precisely when
G
is itself a finite extension of a
cocompact Fuchsian
group (that is, the fundamental group of a closed, hyperbolic
2
-orbifold). In this case, under the same orientability assumption,
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
is genuinely hierarchically hyperbolic, without need to pass to a finite-index subgroup:

Theorem D

(See
2.9
)
.

Let
G
be a finite extension of an orientable, cocompact Fuchsian group. Then
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
is a hierarchically hyperbolic group.

As the full
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
is quasi-isometric to its finite-index HHG subgroup, it has the structure of a hierarchically hyperbolic
space
, the non-equivariant analogue of a hierarchically hyperbolic group. Unfortunately, being a hierarchically hyperbolic
group
might not pass to finite-index overgroups: in other words, there might not be any equivariant structure (see
[
49
, Corollary 4.5]
for an explicit counterexample). This is indeed the case in our setting, even under the assumption that
G
has no torsion. For a concrete counterexample, let
\Sigma=S_{1}^{1}
be a torus with one open disk removed, and let
K
be the
2
-complex obtained by gluing three copies
\Sigma_{1}
,
\Sigma_{2}
,
\Sigma_{3}
along the boundary circle
C
, as in
Figure

1
.

Theorem E

(See
3.20
)
.

\pi_{1}(K)
is torsion-free and satisfies the assumptions of
C
, but
\operatorname{Out}\left(\pi_{1}(K)\right)
is not a HHG.

Figure 1.
Depiction of the complex
K
, obtained by gluing three tori with one boundary to a common circle (here, the dashed line) along the boundaries. The cyclic permutation of the three surfaces descends to an order-three element
\sigma
of
\operatorname{Out}\left(\pi_{1}(K)\right)
.

Outline of sections and arguments

JSJ decompositions

The main tool for the study of outer automorphism groups of hyperbolic groups, which also played a central role in the solution of the isomorphism problem for hyperbolic groups, is Bowditch’s
JSJ decomposition
over virtually cyclic subgroups
[
14
]
. This is a graph-of-groups decomposition of a one-ended hyperbolic group with virtually cyclic edge groups, and whose vertex groups are either:

•

maximal virtually cyclic subgroups;

•

“rigid” quasiconvex subgroups, which cannot be split further relative to the edge groups;

•

or
quadratically hanging subgroups
, which are (finite extensions of) fundamental groups of hyperbolic orbifolds.

The decomposition is invariant under automorphisms of
G
, in a suitable sense, and this makes it possible to study automorphisms of
G
by their action on the vertex groups.

In order to uniformise the notation among the numerous references on the subject, we did an extensive literature review which we then condensed in
Section

1
. There the reader will find all the relevant background material on (bounded) central extensions, orbifolds, their mapping class groups, and JSJ decompositions of hyperbolic groups. While we do not claim any completeness of our exposition, as we omitted several facts that we do not need in this paper, we still hope it might serve as a gentle, self-contained entry point for the reader interested in any of the above topics.

(Virtual) bounded central extensions

Section

2
contains the virtual description of the outer automorphism group as a bounded central extension of a product of mapping class groups, which proves
A
. Though a sizeable part of what we show is implicit in work of Levitt
[
37
]
, the proof of boundedness is new, and relies on a careful analysis of the analogue of the capping extension for orbifold mapping class groups.

We now illustrate the general idea with a simple example. Take the complex
K
depicted in
Figure

1
and let
G
be its fundamental group. By an analogue of the Dehn-Nielsen-Baer Theorem
[
36
, Theorem 1.2]
, automorphisms of
G
are realised by self-homeomorphisms of
K
; hence restricting a homeomorphism to (the interior of) each
\Sigma_{i}
gives an epimorphism
\rho\colon\widetilde{\operatorname{Out}}\left(G\right)\to\prod_{i=1}^{3}\operatorname{MCG}\left(S_{1,1}\right)
, where
\widetilde{\operatorname{Out}}\left(G\right)
is the finite-index subgroup of
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
mapping each torus to itself in an orientation-preserving way, and
S_{1,1}
is a torus with one puncture. The kernel of
\rho
is generated by the
Dehn twists

T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}
around the boundaries of the
\Sigma_{i}
’s; these elements span a subgroup isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
as the product
T_{1}T_{2}T_{3}
induces the conjugation by the core curve of
C
on
G
. Since homeomorphisms of
K
fix
C
up to homotopy, Dehn twists are central in
\widetilde{\operatorname{Out}}\left(G\right)
, so we get a central extension

1\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\widetilde{\operatorname{Out}}\left(G\right)\to\prod_{i=1}^{3}\operatorname{MCG}\left(S_{1,1}\right)\to 1.

The boundedness of the above extension reduces to the boundedness of the classical
capping extension

1\to\langle T\rangle\to\operatorname{MCG}\left(S_{1}^{1}\right)\to\operatorname{MCG}\left(S_{1,1}\right)\to 1,

where again the map on the right is the restriction to the interior, which in turn follows from the existence of a certain quasimorphism
\operatorname{MCG}\left(S_{1}^{1}\right)\to\langle T\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}
obtained from the action on the
annular curve graph
of the boundary.

We stress that the capping extension can be shown to be bounded by other means: for instance, one could combine the boundedness of the classical Euler class
[
25
, Sections 10.2, 10.3]
with the results in
[
23
, Section 5.5]
. Such an argument could likely be extended to general orbifolds, but we preferred to use quasimorphism, as our techniques are more elementary and extend to the general case with little effort.

Hierarchical hyperbolicity

Very roughly, a
hierarchically hyperbolic group
is a finitely generated group admitting suitable “coordinate projections” to a collection of Gromov-hyperbolic spaces. The motivating example is the mapping class group of a finite-type, orientable surface, which projects to the curve graphs of its subsurfaces via subsurface projection. Combining this with the fact that bounded central extensions of HHGs are themselves HHGs
[
24
, Theorem 1.3]
, in
Section

2.2
we deduce
C
from
A
.
D
, which considers the cocompact Fuchsian case, is proven along the same lines; the only difference is that, by carefully describing
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
as a finite extension of a finite-index subgroup of a mapping class group, we do not need to pass to a finite-index subgroup to get hierarchical hyperbolicity. We strived to make
Section

2.2
as clear as possible for the reader who is not an expert on HHG: the only prerequisites we need are given as self-contained blackboxes, while a comprehensive background on HHGs is postponed to
Section

3.1
.

Standard periodic quasiflats in hierarchically hyperbolic groups

Section

3
starts with an overview on HHGs. The main technical novelty of
Section

3.1
is the following, which we believe to be of independent interest to anyone working with hierarchical hyperbolicity:

Theorem F

(see
Section

2.2
)
.

Let
G
be a HHG of rank
\nu
, and let
F\leq G
be a subgroup isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{\nu}
. Then
F
is a standard flat, and in particular it is hierarchically quasiconvex.

Very roughly, the
rank
of
G
is the maximal
\nu
for which
G
contains quasi-isometrically embedded copies of
\mathbb{R}^{\nu}
, which we call
maximal quasiflats
for short. Moreover, a
standard flat
is a maximal quasiflat obtained by considering a maximal collection of unbounded coordinate spaces which are
orthogonal
(roughly meaning that their coordinates can be chosen independently) and taking the product of one quasiline in each coordinate space. Standard flats are canonical examples of
hierarchically quasiconvex
subspaces, the natural notion of quasiconvexity in the hierarchical setting.

To prove
F
, we first notice that, since Abelian subgroups of HHGs are undistorted
[
30
, Corollary E]
, the Quasiflat Theorem from
[
9
]
covers
F
by a finite union of
standard orthants
(the same as standard flats, but given by selecting rays instead of lines). Then we exploit the
F
-action by translation to ensure that the orthants can be patched together to get a standard flat.

Remark G
.

In forthcoming work, Azuelos and Hagen
[
5
]
independently prove a “flat torus theorem” for virtually Abelian subgroups of HHGs, showing they preserve quasiflats satisfying a weak version of hierarchical quasiconvexity. As a consequence, they recover part of
F
, namely that an Abelian subgroup of maximal rank is genuinely hierarchically quasiconvex. We stress that our methods and theirs are completely different: our proof relies on the Quasiflat Theorem and is specific to top-rank Abelian subgroups, but has the advantage to describe them as standard flats; on the other hand, their work pushes further techniques of
cubical approximations
of convex hulls in HHSs, and applies to Abelian subgroups of any rank.

The counterexample

Finally, in
Section

3.2
we construct the counterexample. Very roughly, assuming that
\operatorname{Out}\left(\pi_{1}(K)\right)
is a HHG, we use
F
to deduce that the
(3,3,3)
triangle group
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes S_{3}\leq\operatorname{Out}\left(\pi_{1}(K)\right)
, generated by Dehn twists around the boundaries of the
\Sigma_{i}
’s and the homeomorphisms permuting the three tori, is hierarchically quasiconvex. In analogy with quasiconvex subgroups in other non-positively curved groups, hierarchically quasiconvex subgroups inherit a “HHG-like” structure from the ambient group; we use this to deduce a contradiction, mimicking an argument of Petyt-Spriano, who proved that the
(3,3,3)
triangle group carries no HHG structure (see
[
49
, Corollary 4.5]
).

Questions

As explained in
2.11
, we could remove the orientability assumption in
C
, and therefore prove virtual hierarchical hyperbolicity for outer automorphism of all one-ended hyperbolic groups, if the following question had a positive answer:

Question H
.

Is the mapping class group of a non-orientable hyperbolic orbifold a hierarchically hyperbolic group?

As pointed out in
1.23
, the mapping class group of an orbifold has finite index in the mapping class group of the surface obtained by removing the singular locus; this surface might have some boundary components which are either segments or circles. Since being a hierarchically hyperbolic group passes to finite-index subgroups, it would be enough to answer the following:

Question I
.

Is the mapping class group of a non-orientable surface (possibly with punctures and boundaries) a hierarchically hyperbolic group?

To the best of our knowledge, this is unknown even for closed non-orientable surfaces.

A positive answer to
I
would follow from a more general result about centralisers in HHGs. Indeed, let
S
be a non-orientable surface, let
\widehat{S}
be its orientable double cover, and let
i\colon\widehat{S}\to\widehat{S}
be the order-two deck transformation. By results of
[
12
,
57
,
38
]
, except in a few sporadic cases,
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(S\right)
embeds in
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}(\widehat{S})
as the subgroup of orientation-preserving mapping classes that commute with
i
. So we ask:

Question J
.

Let
G
be a HHG, and let
F\leq G
be a finite subgroup. Is the centraliser of
F
a HHG?

The above is motivated by the principle that centralisers in non-positively curved groups should inherit the geometric properties of the ambient group: for example, centralisers in hyperbolic groups are quasiconvex, hence hyperbolic (see e.g.
[
15
, Proposition III.
\Gamma
.3.9]
), and centralisers in CAT(0) groups are themselves CAT(0)
[
52
]
. The analogue of
J
for centralisers of virtually Abelian subgroup will be considered in forthcoming work of Azuelos and Hagen
[
5
]
.

Background

1.1.
Bounded central extensions

Here we gather generalities on (bounded) central extensions, and recall a characterisation of boundedness in terms of quasihomomorphisms: see
1.6
.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a central extension
1\to K\to E\to G\to 1
with finitely generated kernel, let
\sigma\colon G\to E
be a set-theoretic section. The map
\omega(g,h)=\sigma(g)\sigma(h)\sigma(gh)^{-1}\in K
is a
2
-cocycle
, i.e. for all
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\in G

\omega(g_{2},g_{3})-\omega(g_{1}g_{2},g_{3})+\omega(g_{1},g_{2}g_{3})-\omega(g_{1},g_{2})=0.

Hence
\omega
defines a class in
H^{2}(G;K)
, called the
Euler class
of the extension, which is independent of the choice of
\sigma
and we denote by
[E]
. We say the extension is
bounded
if
[E]
is bounded, meaning that there exists a
2
-cocycle
\alpha\colon G^{2}\to K
such that
[\alpha]=[E]
and
\alpha
takes finitely many values.

Bounded extensions are quasi-isometrically trivial, in the following sense:

Theorem 1.2

(
[
27
]
)
.

Let
1\to K\to E\to G\to 1
be a bounded central extension. Then
E
is quasi-isometric to the direct product
K\times G
.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
(K,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed Abelian group, and let
E
be another group. A map
\chi\colon E\to K
is a
quasihomomorphism
if there exists
D(\chi)\geq 0
, called the
defect
of
\chi
, such that, for every
e_{1},e_{2}\in E
,

\|\chi(e_{1})+\chi(e_{2})-\chi(e_{1}e_{2})\|\leq D(\chi).

When
K=\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{R}
with the Euclidean norm, we say
quasimorphism
instead of quasihomomorphism. A quasihomomorphism is
homogeneous
if it restricts to a homomorphism on every cyclic subgroup.

Remark 1.4
.

Given any quasihomomorphism
\phi\colon E\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the map
\psi(e)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\phi(e^{k})}{k}
, called the
homogeneisation
of
\phi
, is a homogeneous quasihomomorphism within finite distance from
\phi
(see e.g.
[
16
, Lemma 2.21]
). Notice that a homogeneous quasimorphism
\psi
coincides with its homogeneisation, and is therefore invariant under conjugation: given
g,h\in E
,

\psi(ghg^{-1})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\psi(gh^{k}g^{-1})}{k}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\psi(g)+\psi(h^{k})+\psi(g^{-1})}{k}=\psi(h),

where we used that
\psi(gh^{k}g^{-1})
differs from the sum of the
\psi
-images by at most
2D(\psi)
.

Natural examples of quasimorphisms arise from actions on quasilines:

Example 1.5

(Busemann quasimorphism, see e.g.
[
43
, Section 4.1]
)
.

Let
E
be a group acting on a
quasiline

X
, i.e. a space quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}
. Suppose the action does not swap the ideal endpoints. Given a sequence
\{x_{n}\}\in X
converging to an ideal endpoint
p
, let
\phi_{\{x\}}\colon E\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as

\phi_{\{x\}}(g)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{d}(gx_{0},x_{n})-\mathrm{d}(x_{0},x_{n}),

and let
\phi(g)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\phi_{\{x\}}(g^{k})}{k}
. One can show that the limit exists, does not depend on the choice of the sequence, and gives a homogeneous quasimorphism
\phi\colon E\to\mathbb{R}
which is non-trivial on
g
if and only if
g
acts loxodromically on
X
.

Instead of the definition with bounded cocycles, we shall always use the following equivalent characterisation of boundedness for central extensions: see
[
24
, Proposition 2.9]
and, independently,
[
58
, Lemma 5.6]
.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
1\to K\to E\xrightarrow{\pi}G\to 1
be a central extension with finitely generated kernel. The following are equivalent:

(1)

[E]
is bounded.

(2)

There exists a quasihomomorphism
\chi\colon E\to K
such that
\chi|_{K}
is the identity.

We give two easy applications of the above criterion, which are surely known to experts via other means. The first regards products of central extensions:

Corollary 1.7
.

For
i=1,2
let
1\to K_{i}\to E_{i}\to G_{i}\to 1
be central extensions with finitely generated kernels. Then the direct product
1\to K_{1}\times K_{2}\to E_{1}\times E_{2}\to G_{1}\times G_{2}\to 1
is a bounded central extension.

Proof.

It is enough to notice that, if for
i=1,2

\psi_{i}\colon E_{i}\to K_{i}
is a quasihomomorphism which is the identity on
K_{i}
, then
\psi_{1}\times\psi_{2}\colon E_{1}\times E_{2}\to K_{1}\times K_{2}
is a quasihomomorphism which is the identity on
K_{1}\times K_{2}
.
∎

The second consequence was already pointed out in
[
24
, Corollary 2.10]
:

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
1\to K\to E\to G\to 1
be a bounded central extension with finitely generated kernel. If
L\leq K
, then the extension
1\to K/L\to E/L\to G\to 1
is also bounded.

1.2.
Orbifold mapping class groups

We recall here definition and properties of the
mapping class group
of a hyperbolic 2-orbifold, gathering notation and results from across the literature for improved clarity. We refer to
[
40
,
14
,
26
,
19
]
for the original treatments, and to
[
44
]
for a more recent, comprehensive introduction.

In what follows, let
\mathbb{H}^{2}
be the hyperbolic plane, and let
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
its boundary at infinity, which is homeomorphic to a circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Remark 1.9

(Isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
)
.

An isometry
g\in\operatorname{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
is of one of
5

types
. If
g
is orientation-preserving, then it is either:

•

elliptic
if it fixes a point in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
;

•

parabolic
if it fixes exactly one point of the boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
;

•

loxodromic
if it fixes exactly two ideal points of
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
and acts as a translation along the unique geodesic that connects them, called the axis of
g
.

If instead
g
reverses orientation, then it is either:

•

a
reflection
along a geodesic;

•

a
glide-reflection
along a geodesic, that is, the composition of a reflection along a geodesic and a loxodromic along the same geodesic.

The subgroup of
\operatorname{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
fixing a point is isomorphic to the orthogonal group
O(2)
via the action on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
, seen as a circle with the standard round metric.

Definition 1.10

(Bounded Fuchsian group)
.

A
bounded Fuchsian group
is a non-virtually-cyclic, finitely generated, discrete subgroup
G
of
\operatorname{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
without parabolics. Such a group acts properly and cocompactly on the
convex hull

D_{G}
of the limit points
\Lambda(G)\subseteq\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
for the action. If moreover
D_{G}=\mathbb{H}^{2}
then
G
is called a
cocompact
Fuchsian group. A bounded Fuchsian group is
orientable
if it lies in the index-two subgroup
\operatorname{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
of orientation-preserving isometries.

Remark 1.11
.

The term “bounded Fuchsian group” has appeared in the literature with different meanings. For example, in
[
19
]
, Dahmani and Guirardel do not require the action on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
to be faithful, but only to have finite kernel; their bounded Fuchsian groups are therefore
finite extensions
of groups satisfying
1.10
. Their notion is taken from Bowditch’s paper
[
14
]
, which further makes a distinction between bounded Fuchsian groups and cocompact ones. Classically (see e.g.
[
33
]
) Fuchsian groups are commonly understood as subgroups of
\operatorname{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, so we prefer to exclude non-faithful actions. As a consequence, with our notation Fuchsian groups have no non-trivial finite normal subgroups: see
1.12
. On the other hand, we do include orientation-reversing isometries: our cocompact Fuchsian groups are also known as
non-Euclidean crystallographic groups

[
39
]
. Finally, in contrast with Bowditch’s notation, we consider cocompact Fuchsian group as special examples of bounded ones, to simplify the notation later.

The following is well-known, but we provide a proof for completeness.

Lemma 1.12
.

Let
G
be a bounded Fuchsian group. Then
G
is Gromov-hyperbolic. Moreover, it has no non-trivial finite normal subgroups, and in particular it has trivial centre.

Proof.

Gromov-hyperbolicity follows from the fact that
G
acts properly and cocompactly on
D_{G}
, which is a convex subspace of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and is therefore hyperbolic. Therefore, since
G
is not virtually cyclic, it admits a maximal finite normal subgroup
K
, which is the kernel of the action of
G
on
\Lambda(G)=\partial D_{G}
(see e.g.
[
6
, Corollary 1.2]
, which is far more general). Now
D_{G}
has more than two points at infinity, as otherwise
G
would be virtually cyclic; hence every element
k\in K
is a finite-order isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
fixing at least three points at infinity, and is therefore trivial (this is because any finite-order isometry fixes a point in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and an element of
O(2)
fixing three points on the circle is trivial). Hence
G
has no non-trivial finite normal subgroups; in particular,
G
has trivial centre, as the latter is a finite normal subgroup (see e.g. the far more general
[
18
, Theorem 6.14.(a)]
).
∎

We now specialise the more general definition of an
orbifold
to our setting.

Definition 1.13

(Orbifold)
.

Let
G
be a bounded Fuchsian group. The associated
compact orbifold
is the quotient
O=D_{G}/G
, while we denote the full quotient
O^{\prime}=\mathbb{H}^{2}/G
as the
punctured orbifold
associated to
G
. The group
G
is called the
orbifold fundamental group
of both
O
and
O^{\prime}
, denoted
\pi_{1}^{\text{orb}}\left(O\right)=\pi_{1}^{\text{orb}}\left({O^{\prime}}\right)
. We say that
O
and
O^{\prime}
are
orientable
if
G
is orientable.

Remark 1.14
.

One could define orbifolds (and their fundamental groups) in greater generality as topological spaces locally modelled after quotients of the Euclidean space by finite subgroups of isometries, without passing through the universal cover: see, e.g.
[
44
, Section 3.6]
.

Definition 1.15

(Singular locus)
.

The
singular locus
is the image in
O
(resp.
O^{\prime}
) of the points of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with non-trivial
G
-stabiliser. Given a point
x
in the singular locus, and a preimage
p\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
of
x
, we say that:

•

x
is a
cone point
if
\operatorname{Stab}_{G}\left(p\right)\cong\mathbb{Z}/m
, generated by a rotation;
m
is called the
weight
of
x
, and by construction the total angle around
x
is
2\pi/m
.

•

x
is a
mirror point
if
\operatorname{Stab}_{G}\left(p\right)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2
, generated by a reflection.

•

x
is a
corner reflector
if
\operatorname{Stab}_{G}\left(p\right)
is a dihedral group, generated by two reflections along lines intersecting at
p
.

The quotient map
D_{G}\to O
(resp.
\mathbb{H}^{2}\to O^{\prime}
) is a branched covering, branched over the singular locus. See
Figure

for an example of an orbifold with cone points, and
Figure

3
for an example of an orbifold with mirrors and corner reflectors.

Definition 1.16

(Orbifold boundary)
.

The
orbifold boundary
of the compact orbifold
O
, denoted
\partial O
, is the image of the boundary of
D_{G}
. Given a boundary line
\ell\subset\partial D_{G}
, the stabiliser of
\ell
in
G
is isomorphic to either
\mathbb{Z}
, generated by a loxodromic along
\ell
, or the infinite Dihedral group
D_{\infty}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2*\mathbb{Z}/2
, generated by two reflections along lines orthogonal to

. Hence a boundary component
C
of

is either a circle or a segment, accordingly. A
peripheral subgroup
associated to

is the
\mathbb{Z}
or
D_{\infty}
subgroup of
\pi_{1}^{\text{orb}}\left(O\right)
stabilising a line in the preimage of

; any two such subgroups are conjugate, so with a little abuse of notation we speak of “the” peripheral subgroup associated to
C
.

Remark 1.17

(Topological boundary versus orbifold boundary)
.

Notice that the orbifold boundary might be a proper subset of the topological boundary of
O
. Indeed, the latter might also contain
mirrors
, which do not come from the boundary of
D_{G}
: see
Figure

3
above for an example. We therefore stress that
\partial O
will always denote the
orbifold
boundary.

Remark 1.18
.

The punctured orbifold
O^{\prime}
is obtained from
O
by attaching to each orbifold boundary component
C\subseteq\partial O
a copy of
C\times[0,\infty)
, which is the image of a half-space of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
cut out by a boundary line of
D_{G}
. If
C
is a circle, this is the same as capping the circle with a once-punctured disk, justifying the terminology “punctured orbifold”. We stress that, while
O
and
O^{\prime}
are homotopy equivalent and have the same (orbifold) fundamental group, they are not homeomorphic; this distinction will later be reflected also in their mapping class groups.

Remark 1.19

(Punctures, cone points, and marked points)
.

In the literature, cone points and punctures are often conflated and simultaneously treated as
marked points
; in the interest of clarity, we prefer to make the distinction that cone points belong to
O^{\prime}
, while punctures do not.

Definition 1.20

(Type preserving automorphisms)
.

Let
G
be a bounded Fuchsian group. An automorphism
\phi\in\operatorname{Aut}\left(G\right)
is
type-preserving
if it preserves types of elements, as defined in
1.9
, also distinguishing when a loxodromic has an axis in the interior or on the boundary of
D_{G}
. Equivalently, an automorphism is type-preserving if it preserves the conjugacy classes of peripheral subgroups. We denote by
\operatorname{Out}^{\star}\left(G\right)
the quotient of type-preserving automorphisms of
G
by inner automorphisms (which are clearly type-preserving, in view of the equivalent formulation).

Definition 1.21

(Orbifold mapping class group)
.

Let
G
be a bounded Fuchsian group, and let
P
be either the compact or the punctured orbifold associated to
G
. Let
\operatorname{Homeo}(P)
be the group of self-homeomorphisms of
P
which fix the orbifold boundary
\partial P
pointwise (mirrors need not be fixed), and which map each cone point to a cone point of the same weight. The
extended orbifold mapping class group
of
P
, denoted by
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(P\right)
, is the quotient of
\operatorname{Homeo}(P)
by isotopies relative to
\partial P
and to the cone points. If
P
is orientable, we define the
mapping class group
of
P
, denoted by
\operatorname{MCG}\left(P\right)
, in the same way, but restricting to orientation-preserving homeomorphism. If
P
has punctures, the
pure
(extended) mapping class group, denoted
\operatorname{PMCG}\left(P\right)
(resp.
\operatorname{PMCG}^{\pm}\left(P\right)
), is the finite index subgroup of
\operatorname{MCG}\left(P\right)
(resp
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(P\right)
) of mapping classes which fix each puncture.

Remark 1.22
.

Notice that
\operatorname{MCG}\left(O\right)
and
\operatorname{MCG}\left(O^{\prime}\right)
are not isomorphic: the former must fix each boundary component pointwise, while the latter can permute punctures.

Let
P
be an orbifold associated to a bounded Fuchsian group, either compact or punctured. Denote by
S
the surface obtained from
P
by removing the singular locus: this has the effect of removing mirrors and corner reflectors, and replacing cone points by punctures. Notice that the topological boundary of
S
is the same as the orbifold boundary of
P
. We now relate the mapping class groups of
S
and
P
:

Lemma 1.23
.

\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(P\right)
is a finite-index subgroup of
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(S\right)
.

Proof.

Any element of
\operatorname{Homeo}(P)
, as defined in
1.21
, restricts to a homeomorphism of
S
fixing the (topological) boundary
\partial S
pointwise. Moreover, if an element of
\operatorname{Homeo}(P)
is isotopic to the identity (relative to
\partial S
) when restricted to
S
, it is also isotopic to the identity on
P
(relative to
\partial P
and the cone points). This is proven in
[
40
, Lemma 2]
for the orientable case; as argued in
[
26
, Section 3]
, the same argument holds in the presence of mirrors, as it builds on
[
22
]
which has no orientability assumption. Hence
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(P\right)
is naturally identified with a subgroup of
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(S\right)
. Furthermore, an element of
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(S\right)
comes from an element of
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(P\right)
precisely when it permutes the punctures coming from cone points of the same weight, so
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(P\right)
has finite-index in
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(S\right)
.
∎

The mapping class groups of the two orbifolds associated to a bounded Fuchsian group fit into the following central extension:

Lemma 1.24

(Capping extension)
.

Let
G
be a bounded Fuchsian group, and let
O^{\prime}=\mathbb{H}^{2}/G
and
O=D_{G}/G
be the associated orbifolds. Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k}
be the circular boundary components of
O
, and denote by
T_{i}
the
Dehn twist
along
\gamma_{i}
(see e.g.
[
23
, Section 3.1.1]
). The map
\operatorname{MCG}\left(O\right)\to\operatorname{PMCG}\left(O^{\prime}\right)
, given by extending a mapping class on
O
to the identity on
O^{\prime}-O
, fits into a short exact sequence

1\to\prod_{i=1}^{k}\langle T_{i}\rangle\to\operatorname{MCG}\left(O\right)\to\operatorname{PMCG}\left(O^{\prime}\right)\to 1.

Proof.

This is essentially the short exact sequence given in
[
23
, Proposition 3.19]
. Indeed, if
O
is orientable and
S
is the surface obtained from
O
by removing the singular locus, then the above extension is a finite-index subgroup of the corresponding extension of
S
. In the non orientable case, one also has to check that, if a homeomorphism of
O
becomes isotopic to the identity after gluing an infinite band
[0,1]\times[0,\infty)
to a segment in the orbifold boundary, then it was already isotopic to the identity on
O
. This is true since the isotopy can be assumed to fix the band at all times, and therefore restrict to an isotopy on the orbifold minus the band. Furthermore, the extension is central, because every mapping class on
O
can be homotoped to be the identity on a neighbourhood of the boundary and therefore commutes with boundary Dehn twists.
∎

The central extension from
1.24
is almost never trivial (i.e., a product). Take for example
S
to be an orientable compact surface with
k
boundary components and genus
g
, and let
S^{\prime}
be the capped surface. If the extension above were trivial, the first homology group
H_{1}(\operatorname{MCG}\left(S\right),\mathbb{Z})
would contain a
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
factor by the Künneth formula (see e.g.
[
31
, Theorem 3B.5]
), contradicting the fact that
H_{1}(\operatorname{MCG}\left(S\right),\mathbb{Z})
is finite as long as
g\geq 2

[
35
, Theorem 5.1]
. However:

Lemma 1.25
.

The extension from
1.24
is bounded.

Proof.

Let
S
be the surface obtained from
O
by removing the singular locus. For every curve
\gamma_{i}
corresponding to a circular boundary component, let
\mathcal{C}(\gamma_{i})
be the associated
annular curve graph
, as in
[
45
, Section 2]
, whose definition we now recall. Let
H_{i}\leq\pi_{1}^{\text{orb}}\left(O\right)
be a peripheral subgroup associated to
\gamma_{i}
, let
\overline{S}_{i}
be the compactification of
\mathbb{H}^{2}/H_{i}
to an annulus, and let
\mathcal{C}(\gamma_{i})
be the simplicial graph whose vertices are arcs in
\overline{S}_{i}
connecting the two boundary components, up to isotopy relative to the boundary, and whose edges correspond to pairs of arcs with disjoint interiors. The arguments from
[
45
, Section 2]
run verbatim to show that
\mathcal{C}(\gamma_{i})
is a quasiline, on which
\operatorname{MCG}\left(S\right)
acts without inverting the ideal endpoints and where the Dehn twist
T_{i}
acts loxodromically. Notice that
\operatorname{MCG}\left(O\right)
, which is a subgroup of
\operatorname{MCG}\left(S\right)
, acts on
\mathcal{C}(\gamma_{i})
as well.

Now let
\phi_{i}\colon\operatorname{MCG}\left(O\right)\to\mathbb{R}
be the Busemann quasimorphism associated to the action, as in
1.5
, which we can rescale to map
T_{i}
to
1
. The integer part of
\phi_{i}
is therefore a quasimorphism
\psi_{i}\colon\operatorname{MCG}\left(O\right)\to\mathbb{Z}
which identifies
\langle T_{i}\rangle
with
\mathbb{Z}
. Taking the product over all boundary components yields a quasihomomorphism
\psi=(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n})\colon\operatorname{MCG}\left(O\right)\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
which is the identity on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cong\langle T_{i}\rangle_{i=1,\ldots,n}
, so the extension is bounded by
1.6
.
∎

We finally relate the type-preserving automorphisms of a bounded Fuchsian group
G
and the mapping class group of the corresponding punctured orbifold
O^{\prime}=\mathbb{H}^{2}/G
. Let
\phi\in\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(O^{\prime}\right)
be a mapping class and choose a representative

. We can assume, up to isotopy, that

fixes a basepoint
x_{0}
in the interior of

. Consider now a point
\widetilde{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
which projects to
x_{0}
. We can identify
G=\pi_{1}^{\text{orb}}\left(O^{\prime}\right)
with the group of deck transformations
\operatorname{Aut}\left((\mathbb{H}^{2},\widetilde{x}_{0})\to(O^{\prime},x_{0})\right)
of the pointed branched covering. Let
\widetilde{f}\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}
be the lift of
f
which fixes the basepoint
\widetilde{x}_{0}
. For every element
\gamma\in G
, let
\widetilde{f}_{*}(\gamma)\in G
be the unique deck transformation such that the following diagram commutes:

. Changing

in its isotopy class, or changing the basepoint, gives elements of

which differ by an inner automorphism of

\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(O^{\prime}\right)\to\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)

Remark 1.26
.

In the case where

is torsion-free, so that

is a surface with punctures, the above recovers the usual map
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(O^{\prime}\right)\to\operatorname{Out}^{\star}\left(G\right)
induced by the action on the fundamental group: every mapping class sends closed curves to closed curves, and therefore acts on the conjugacy classes of elements of
\pi_{1}(O^{\prime})
(see e.g.
[
23
, Section 8.1]
).

We now state the Dehn-Nielsen-Baer theorem in the language of our setup.

Theorem 1.27

(Dehn-Nielsen-Baer)
.

Let
G
be a bounded Fuchsian group, and let
O^{\prime}=\mathbb{H}^{2}/G
be the associated punctured orbifold. The map
\operatorname{MCG}^{\pm}\left(O^{\prime}\right)\to\operatorname{Out}^{\star}\left(G\right)
defined above is an isomorphism.

The classical version of the Dehn-Nielsen-Baer theorem concerns
closed
orientable surfaces, and its proof can be found in e.g.
[
23
, Chapter 8]
. For orientable orbifolds, we refer to
[
40
]
and
[
61
]
, who also treat the punctured case, which is the case of our interest. These results were then generalised in
[
26
, Section 3]
to the non-orientable setting. Though we won’t need it, we also mention that Elysia Wang recently generalised

, proving an isomorphism between the mapping class group of an orientable surface with boundary and its fundamental groupoid, with one basepoint on each boundary component
[

]
.

1.3.
Bass-Serre theory

In this Subsection we recall the definition of a graph-of-groups decomposition and the associated Bass-Serre tree, referring to
[
54
]
or
[
13
, Section 16]
for further details.

Definition 1.28

(Graph-of-groups)
.

A
bidirected graph

\Gamma
consists of two sets
V,E
together with maps

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\to V\times V;

\displaystyle\quad E

\displaystyle\to E;

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle\to(i(e),t(e))

\displaystyle\quad e

\displaystyle\to\overline{e}

satisfying
\overline{\overline{e}}=e
,
e\neq\overline{e}
, and
i(\overline{e})=t(e)
. To parse this definition, the reader should think of
V
as the set of vertices;
E
as the set of oriented edges, which come in pairs;
\overline{e}
as the opposite edge of
e
, and
i(e),t(e)
as the initial and terminal vertices of
e
.

A
graph-of-groups
is the data of:

•

a finite bidirected graph
\Gamma
, with vertices
V
and directed edges
E
;

•

a collection of
vertex groups

\{G_{v}\}_{v\in V}
;

•

for every edge
e\in E
, an
edge group

G_{e}
, satisfying
G_{e}=G_{\overline{e}}
, together with an injective homomorphism
\tau_{e}\colon G_{e}\to G_{t(e)}
.

For every
v\in V
let
E_{v}
be the collection of edges whose initial endpoint is
v
. With a little abuse of notation, we often implicitly identify
G_{e}
with
\tau_{\overline{e}}(G_{e})
and regard it as a subgroup of
G_{v}
.

Let
Y
be a spanning tree inside
\Gamma
. The
fundamental group
of
\mathcal{G}
based at
Y
is the group
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},Y)
obtained from the free product

\left(\bigast_{v\in V}G_{v}\right)*\left(\bigast_{e\in E}\langle x_{e}\rangle\right)

by adding the following relations:

•

x_{e}=x_{\overline{e}}^{-1}
;

•

x_{e}=1
if
e
is an edge of
Y
;

•

For all
g\in G_{e}
,
x_{e}\tau_{e}(g)x_{e}^{-1}=\tau_{\overline{e}}(g)
.

By
[
13
, Corollary 16.7]
, up to isomorphism the fundamental group does not depend on the choice of the spanning tree, so we just denote it by
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G})
.

A group
G

splits
over a family of subgroups
\mathcal{A}
if
G
is isomorphic to the fundamental group of a graph of groups
\mathcal{G}
whose edge groups are in
\mathcal{A}
. In this case we say that
\mathcal{G}
is a
splitting
of
G
over
\mathcal{A}
. A splitting is
relative
to a family of subgroups
\mathcal{P}
if every subgroup in
\mathcal{P}
is conjugated into a vertex group.

Definition 1.29

(Bass-Serre tree)
.

Given a graph of groups decomposition
\mathcal{G}
of a group
G
, the associated
Bass-Serre tree
is the bidirected tree whose vertices are the
G
-cosets of vertex groups, and for every edge
e=(v,w)\in E
and
g\in G
there is an oriented edge
gG_{e}=(gG_{v},gx_{e}G_{w})
. We often think of
T
as the underlying undirected graph, forgetting edge orientations. There is a
G
-action on
T
by simplicial isometries and without edge inversions, defined by
h\cdot gG_{v}=hgG_{v}
for every
v\in V
and
g,h\in G
. Notice that the stabiliser of
gG_{v}
is
gG_{v}g^{-1}
for every
v\in V
, and similarly
\text{Stab}({gG_{e}})=g\tau_{\overline{e}}(G_{e})g^{-1}=gx_{e}\tau_{e}(G_{e})x_{e}^{-1}g^{-1}
for every
e\in E
.

Remark 1.30

(Canonical splitting)
.

If vertex groups coincide with their normalisers in
G
, we can equivalently see the Bass-Serre tree as a graph whose vertices are
conjugates
of the vertex groups, and where
G
acts by
conjugations
. In particular, the action factors through the central quotient
G/Z(G)
, identified with the group of inner automorphisms
\operatorname{Inn}\left(G\right)\leq\operatorname{Aut}\left(G\right)
. We further say that the splitting is
canonical
if the
\operatorname{Inn}\left(G\right)
-action on
T
extends to an
\operatorname{Aut}\left(G\right)
-action by simplicial isometries; if this happens, every automorphism permutes the conjugacy classes of vertex groups.

1.4.
JSJ decompositions of hyperbolic groups

We conclude the background section with some properties of the
JSJ decomposition
of a one-ended hyperbolic group
G
, and the structure that this splitting induces on
\operatorname{Out}\left(G\right)
.

Theorem 1.31
.

Let
G
be a one-ended hyperbolic group. There exists a canonical splitting
\mathcal{G}
of
G
over virtually
\mathbb{Z}
subgroups, called the
JSJ decomposition
of
G
, whose vertex groups
\{G_{v}\}_{v\in E(\Gamma)}
come in three pairwise exclusive types:

(1)

Elementary vertex subgroups
:
G_{v}
is a maximal virtually
\mathbb{Z}
subgroup of
G
.

(2)

Quadratically hanging subgroups
:
G_{v}
fits in an extension

1\to K_{v}\to G_{v}\xrightarrow{\pi_{v}}Q_{v}\to 1,

where
K_{v}
is finite and
Q_{v}
is a bounded Fuchsian group.
The edge groups incident to
G_{v}
coincide with the preimages in
G_{v}
of the boundary subgroups of
Q_{v}
.

(3)

Rigid vertex subgroups
:
G_{v}
is a maximal quasiconvex subgroup (not of type (
2
)), and admits no splitting over virtually-cyclic subgroups relative to the edge groups.

Let
V_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
be the disjoint subsets of
V=V(\Gamma)
corresponding to the three types of vertices. Then, the following facts hold:

(i)

Every edge of
\Gamma
connects a vertex in
V_{1}
to a vertex in
V_{2}\cup V_{3}
.

(ii)

If
v\in V_{2}\cup V_{3}
and
e\in E_{v}
,
G_{e}
is maximal among virtually cyclic subgroups of
G_{v}
.

(iii)

If
v\in V_{2}\cup V_{3}
and
e\in E_{v}
, the centraliser
Z_{G_{v}}(G_{e})
coincides with the centre
Z(G_{e})
.

We say that a quadratically hanging subgroup
G_{v}
is
orientable
if the quotient Fuchsian group
Q_{v}
is orientable.

Proof.

If
G
is a finite extension of a cocompact Fuchsian group, it is enough to consider the trivial splitting, whose unique vertex group (of type (
2
)) is
G
and without edge groups. For all other one-ended hyperbolic groups, the existence of a canonical splitting with the required vertex and edge groups is a result of Bowditch
[
14
, Theorem 0.1]
, who built on work of Rips and Sela
[
51
,
53
]
; in view of a result of Swarup
[
56
, Theorem 1]
, the construction applies to all one-ended hyperbolic groups (besides finite extension of cocompact Fuchsian groups).

For the “furthermore” part,
Item

(i)
is proven in
[
14
, Theorem 5.28]
. Next,
Item

(ii)
was already pointed out by Levitt (see
[
37
, page 13]
), but we prove it for completeness. Suppose that
e=(v,w)
, where we can assume that
w\in V_{1}
by
Item

(i)
, and recall that we are identifying
G_{e}
with the subgroup
\tau_{\overline{e}}(G_{e})=x_{e}\tau_{e}(G_{e})x_{e}^{-1}\leq G_{v}
. Then

3.
…but not a HHG

In this Section we present an example of a torsion-free hyperbolic group whose full outer automorphism group fails to be a hierarchically hyperbolic group, proving

3.1.
Background on hierarchical hyperbolicity

Definition 3.1

(Hierarchically hyperbolic space)
.

Let
\delta>0
and
X
be a
(\delta,\delta)
–quasigeodesic space. A
hierarchically hyperbolic space (HHS) structure with constant
\delta
for
X
is the data of an index set
\mathfrak{S}
and a set
\{\mathcal{C}(W)\colon W\in\mathfrak{S}\}
of
\delta
-hyperbolic spaces
(\mathcal{C}(W),\mathrm{d}_{W})
such that the following axioms are satisfied.

(1)

(Projections.)
For each
W\in\mathfrak{S}
, there exists a
projection

\pi_{W}\colon X\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{C}(W)}
that is a
(\delta,\delta)
–coarsely Lipschitz,
\delta
–coarsely onto,
\delta
–coarse map.

(2)

(Nesting.)
If
\mathfrak{S}\neq\emptyset
, then
\mathfrak{S}
is equipped with a partial order
\sqsubseteq
and contains a unique
\sqsubseteq
–maximal element, denoted by
S
. When
V\sqsubseteq W
, we say
V
is
nested
in
W
. For each
W\in\mathfrak{S}
, we denote by
\mathfrak{S}_{W}
the set of all
V\in\mathfrak{S}
with
V\sqsubseteq W
. Moreover, for all
V,W\in\mathfrak{S}
with
V\sqsubsetneq W
there is a specified non-empty subset
\rho^{V}_{W}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(W)
with
\mathrm{diam}(\rho^{V}_{W})\leq\delta
.

(3)

(Finite complexity.)
Any
\sqsubseteq
–chain has length at most
\delta
.

(4)

(Orthogonality.)
The set
\mathfrak{S}
has a symmetric relation called
orthogonality
. If
V
and
W
are orthogonal, we write
V\perp W
and require that
V
and
W
are not
\sqsubseteq
–comparable. Further, whenever
V\sqsubseteq W
and
W\perp U
, we require that
V\perp U
. We denote by
\mathfrak{S}_{W}^{\perp}
the set of all
V\in\mathfrak{S}
with
V\perp W
.

(5)

(Containers.)
For each
W\in\mathfrak{S}
and
U\in\mathfrak{S}_{W}
with
\mathfrak{S}_{W}\cap\mathfrak{S}_{U}^{\perp}\neq\emptyset
, there exists
Q\in\mathfrak{S}_{W}-\{W\}
such that
V\sqsubseteq Q
whenever
V\in\mathfrak{S}_{W}\cap\mathfrak{S}_{U}^{\perp}
. We call
Q
the
container of
U
in
W
.

(6)

(Transversality.)
If
V,W\in\mathfrak{S}
are not orthogonal and neither is nested in the other, then we say
V
and
W
are
transverse
, denoted
V\pitchfork W
. Moreover, for all
V,W\in\mathfrak{S}
with
V\pitchfork W
, there are non-empty sets
\rho^{V}_{W}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(W)
and
\rho^{W}_{V}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(V)
, each of diameter at most
\delta
.

(7)

(Consistency.)
For all
x\in X
and
U,V,W\in\mathfrak{S}
:

•

if
V\pitchfork W
, then
\min\left\{\mathrm{d}_{W}(\pi_{W}(x),\rho^{V}_{W}),\mathrm{d}_{V}(\pi_{V}(x),\rho^{W}_{V})\right\}\leq\delta
,

•

if
U\sqsubseteq V
and either
V\sqsubsetneq W
, or
V\pitchfork W
and
W\not\perp U
, then
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\rho^{U}_{W},\rho^{V}_{W})\leq\delta
.

(8)

(Bounded geodesic image (BGI).)
For all
V,W\in\mathfrak{S}
and for all
x,y\in X
, if
V\sqsubsetneq W
and
\mathrm{d}_{V}(\pi_{V}(x),\pi_{V}(y))\geq\delta
, then every
\mathcal{C}(W)
–geodesic from
\pi_{W}(x)
to
\pi_{W}(y)
must intersect
N_{\delta}(\rho_{W}^{V})
.

(9)

(Large links.)
For all
W\in\mathfrak{S}
and
x,y\in X
, there exists a collection
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{m}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{W}-\{W\}
such that
m\leq\delta\mathrm{d}_{W}(\pi_{W}(x),\pi_{W}(y))+\delta
, and for all
U\in\mathfrak{S}_{W}-\{W\}
, either
U\sqsubseteq V_{i}
for some
i
, or
\mathrm{d}_{U}(\pi_{U}(x),\pi_{U}(y))\leq\delta
.

(10)

(Partial realization.)
If
\{V_{i}\}
is a finite collection of pairwise orthogonal elements of
\mathfrak{S}
and
p_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(V_{i})
for each
i
, then there exists
x\in X

realising
the tuple
(p_{i})
, meaning that, for every
i
and every
W\in\mathfrak{S}
:

•

\mathrm{d}_{V_{i}}(\pi_{V_{i}}(x),p_{i})\leq\delta
;

•

if
V_{i}\sqsubsetneq W
or
W\pitchfork V_{i}
then
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\pi_{W}(x),\rho^{V_{i}}_{W})\leq\delta
.

(11)

(Uniqueness.)
There exists a function
\theta\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
so that for all
r\geq 0
, if
x,y\in X
and
\mathrm{d}_{X}(x,y)\geq\theta(r)
, then there exists
W\in\mathfrak{S}
such that
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\pi_{W}(x),\pi_{W}(y))\geq r
.

We use
\mathfrak{S}
to denote the HHS structure. We call an element
U\in\mathfrak{S}
a
domain
, the associated space
\mathcal{C}(U)
its
coordinate space
, and call the maps
\rho_{W}^{V}
the
relative projections
from
V
to
W
. The quantity
\delta
is called a
hierarchy constant
for
\mathfrak{S}
. We often suppress reference to the projection maps, so for every
x,y\in X
and
U\in\mathfrak{S}
we write
\mathrm{d}_{U}(x,y)
to mean
\mathrm{d}_{U}(\pi_{U}(x),\pi_{U}(y))
.

Definition 3.2

(Hierarchically hyperbolic group)
.

A finitely generated group
G
is a
hierarchically hyperbolic group
(HHG) if the following hold.

(i)

G
acts metrically properly and coboundedly on a space
X
admitting a HHS structure
\mathfrak{S}
.

(ii)

There is a
\sqsubseteq
–,
\perp
–, and
\pitchfork
–preserving action of
G
on
\mathfrak{S}
by bijections such that
\mathfrak{S}
contains finitely many
G
–orbits.

(iii)

For each
W\in\mathfrak{S}
and
g\in G
, there exists an isometry
g_{W}\colon\mathcal{C}(W)\to\mathcal{C}(gW)
satisfying the following for all
V,W\in\mathfrak{S}
and
g,h\in G
.

•

The maps
(gh)_{W}\colon\mathcal{C}(W)\to\mathcal{C}(ghW)
and
g_{hW}\circ h_{W}\colon\mathcal{C}(W)\to\mathcal{C}(hW)
coincide.

•

For each
x\in X
,
g_{W}(\pi_{W}(x))=\pi_{gW}(g\cdot x)
in
\mathcal{C}(gW)
.

•

If
V\pitchfork W
or
V\sqsubsetneq W
, then
g_{W}(\rho^{V}_{W})=\rho^{gV}_{gW}
in
\mathcal{C}(gW)
.

We often drop the indices and denote each
g_{W}
simply by

. When the underlying HHS is not relevant, we denote a HHG by
(G,\mathfrak{S})
.

Remark 3.3

(Moral compass)
.

When reading the definitions above, one should keep in mind the motivating example of a HHG, which is the mapping class group of an orientable surface

]

Remark 3.4

It follows from

Remark 3.5

(HHS versus HHG)

If

is a quasi-isometry and

is a HHS, then composing the coordinate projections with

3.1.2.
Maximal (standard) flats and hierarchical quasiconvexity

The following is the natural notion of quasiconvexity for subspaces of a HHS.

Definition 3.9
.

A subset
Y\subseteq X
of a HHS
(X,\mathfrak{S})
is
hierarchically quasiconvex
(hqc) if there exists a function
\kappa\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that:

(1)

For every
W\in\mathfrak{S}
,
\pi_{W}(Y)
is
\kappa(0)
-quasiconvex;

(2)

For every
r\geq 0
and
x\in X
, if
\sup_{W\in\mathfrak{S}}\mathrm{d}_{W}(x,Y)\leq r
, then there exists

such that
\mathrm{d}_{X}(x,y)\leq\kappa(r)
.

We say that a subgroup
H\leq G
of a HHG is hierarchically quasiconvex if so is

for some (equivalently, any) basepoint
x_{0}
.

The next lemma follows by combining

Lemma 3.10
.

The intersection of two hierarchically quasiconvex subgroups of a HHG is hierarchically quasiconvex.

While geodesics in hierarchically hyperbolic spaces (and already in Cayley graphs of mapping class groups) are quite mysterious, any two points can be connected by a special quasigeodesic which remains quasigeodesic in the coordinate spaces:

Definition 3.11
.

Given
D\geq 0
, a
D
-hierarchy path
is a
(D,D)
-quasigeodesic in
\overline{X}
whose projection to every domain is an unparameterized
(D,D)
-quasigeodesic. A hierarchy line (resp. hierarchy ray) is a hierarchy path quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{R}
(resp.
\mathbb{R}^{+}
).

Remark 3.12
.

Notice that, if a hierarchy path
\gamma
has unbounded projection to some domain
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, then
\pi_{V}(\gamma)
is bounded for every
V\sqsubsetneq U
. Indeed,
\pi_{U}(\gamma)
can be reparameterised as an infinite quasi-geodesic: this means that, for every
R\geq 0
, there exists a bounded subsegment
\gamma_{R}\subsetneq\gamma
such that, if
x\not\in\gamma_{R}
, then
\mathrm{d}_{U}(x,\rho^{V}_{U})\geq R
. Thus the bounded geodesic image axiom (
3.1
.
(8)
) implies that, for a suitable choice of
R
, the projection of
\gamma-\gamma_{R}
to
\mathcal{C}(V)
is bounded (here we are using that quasi-geodesics in hyperbolic spaces are within finite Hausdorff distance from geodesics with the same endpoints, see e.g.
[
15
, III.H.1.7]
). In turn, since
\gamma_{R}
is a bounded subsegment, we get that
\mathrm{diam}\pi_{V}(\gamma)<\infty
.

Definition 3.13
.

The
rank
of
(X,\mathfrak{S})
is the maximum integer

for which there exists
\nu
pairwise orthogonal, unbounded domains. By
[

is a quasi-isometry invariant of

, as it coincides with the maximal

satisfying the following: there exists

such that, for every

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
(K,K)
-quasi-isometrically embedded inside
X
.

Definition 3.14
.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{\nu}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}
be pairwise orthogonal, unbounded domains, and let
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{U}}=\bigcup_{U_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathfrak{S}_{U_{i}}
. The
product region
associated to
\mathcal{U}
is the subspace

\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{U}}=\left\{x\in X\mid\mathrm{d}_{W}\left(x,\bigcup_{U_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\rho^{U_{i}}_{W}\right)\leq\delta\mbox{ for all }W\not\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathcal{U}}\right\}.

As argued in
[
10

is quasi-isometric to a product
\prod_{U_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbf{F}_{U_{i}}
of
factors
, where every parallel copy of
\mathbf{F}_{U_{i}}
has uniformly bounded projection to
\mathcal{C}(W)
whenever

. As argued in e.g.
[
21

, any two parallel copies of
\mathbf{F}_{U_{i}}
are uniformly quasi-isometric, so we can regard
\mathbf{F}_{U_{i}}
as a coarsely well-defined subspace of

.

For every
i=1,\ldots,\nu
let
\gamma_{i}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{U_{i}}
be a hierarchy line (resp. a hierarchy ray) such that
\pi_{U_{i}}(\gamma_{i})
is unbounded. The product
\gamma_{1}\times\ldots\gamma_{\nu}\subseteq\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{U}}
is called a
standard flat
(resp.

) supported on
\mathcal{U}
. Notice that a standard flat (resp. orthant) supported on
\mathcal{U}
has bounded projection to every domain

, as a consequence of
3.12
. Furthermore, standard flats (resp. standard orthants) are hierarchically quasiconvex by
[
9
, Lemma 4.3]
.

The main result of
[
9
]
states that top-dimensional quasiflats are unions of standard orthants:

Theorem 3.15

(Quasiflat Theorem
[
9
, Theorem 4.13]
)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{\nu}\to X
be a quasi-isometric embedding. Then there exist standard orthants
\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{N}
so that

\mathrm{d}_{Haus}\left(f(\mathbb{R}^{\nu}),\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{O}_{i}\right)<\infty.

In the next proposition, which is a technical tool of independent interest, we show that top-dimensional
periodic
quasiflats are actually standard:

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
be a HHG structure for
G
of rank
\nu
, and let
x_{0}\in X
be a basepoint. Let
F\leq G
be a subgroup isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{\nu}
. Then
F\cdot x_{0}
coarsely coincides with a standard flat, and in particular
F
is hierarchically quasiconvex.

Proof.

Firstly,
F
is undistorted in
G
by
[
30
, Corollary H]
, so
F\cdot x_{0}
is a quasiflat of rank
\nu
. By the Quasiflat Theorem, there exist standard orthants
\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{N}
so that
\mathrm{d}_{Haus}\left(F\cdot x_{0},\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{O}_{i}\right)<\infty
. We can assume that this collection of standard orthants is minimal, meaning that no
N-1
of them coarsely covers
F\cdot x_{0}
. For every
i
let
\mathcal{U}_{i}
be the support of
\mathbf{O}_{i}
. Since standard orthants have bounded projections outside their support, and since
F\cdot x_{0}
lies within finite Hausdorff distance from the union of orthants, we have that
\pi_{W}(F\cdot x_{0})
is unbounded if and only if
W\in\mathcal{U}\coloneq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{U}_{i}
. Hence
F
acts on
\mathcal{U}
, because for every
f\in F

\mathrm{diam}_{fU}(F\cdot x_{0})=\mathrm{diam}_{fU}(fF\cdot x_{0})=\mathrm{diam}_{U}(F\cdot x_{0}).

Thus let
F^{\prime}\leq F
be the finite-index subgroup fixing all domains in
\mathcal{U}
. We now show that all orthants have the same support:

Claim 3.17
.

\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}_{i}
for all
i
.

Proof of
3.17
.

Towards a contradiction, suppose without loss of generality that there exists a domain
U\in\mathcal{U}_{1}-\mathcal{U}_{2}
. The idea of this proof is that we can find an arbitrarily large ball inside
F^{\prime}\cdot x_{0}
which is coarsely contained inside
\mathbf{O}_{1}
, and therefore has arbitrarily large projection to
\mathcal{C}(U)
; on the other hand, we can also find an
F^{\prime}
-translate of this ball which is coarsely contained inside
\mathbf{O_{2}}
, and therefore projects to a uniformly bounded set in
\mathcal{C}(fU)
. This will contradict the fact that
F^{\prime}
fixes
U
.

To make the above idea precise, we first fix some constants. Let
T=\mathrm{diam}\pi_{U}(O_{2})
; let
D=\mathrm{d}_{Haus}\left(F^{\prime}\cdot x_{0},\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{O}_{i}\right)
; let
\delta
be a HHS constant for
(X,\mathfrak{S})
; and set
C\coloneq T+4\delta(D+1)+1
. Since
O_{1}
has unbounded projection to
U
, there exist
x_{1}\in\mathbf{O}_{1}
and
R\geq 0
that

\mathrm{diam}\pi_{U}(B(x_{1},R)\cap\mathbf{O}_{1})\geq C.

Let
f_{1}\in F^{\prime}
be such that
\mathrm{d}(f_{1}\cdot x_{0},x_{1})\leq D
. Every point in
B(x_{1},R)\cap\mathbf{O}_{1}
is within distance
D
from a point in
F^{\prime}\cdot x_{0}
, which must therefore be
(R+2D)
-close to
f\cdot x_{0}
: see
Figure

4
to understand the situation. Hence, using that coordinate projections are
(\delta,\delta)
-coarsely Lipschitz, we get

\mathrm{diam}\pi_{U}(B(f_{1}\cdot x_{0},R+2D)\cap F^{\prime}\cdot x_{0})\geq C-2\delta(D+1).

Now let
f_{2}\in F^{\prime}
be such that
B(f_{2}\cdot x_{0},R+2D)\cap\bigcup_{i\neq 2}N_{D}(\mathbf{O}_{i})=\emptyset
, which exists as otherwise the sub-collection
\{\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{3},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{N}\}
would coarsely cover
F^{\prime}\cdot x_{0}
, contradicting minimality. Since
F^{\prime}
fixes
U
, we have

\mathrm{diam}\pi_{U}(B(f_{2}\cdot x_{0},R+2D)\cap F^{\prime}\cdot x_{0})\geq C-2\delta(D+1).

Every point in
B(f_{2}\cdot x_{0},R+2D)\cap F^{\prime}\cdot x_{0}
is
D
-close to a point in the union of orthants, which must actually lie in
\mathbf{O_{2}}
by our choice of

. Hence, again using that projections are
(\delta,\delta)
-coarsely Lipschitz, we find a point
x_{2}\in\mathbf{O_{2}}
such that

\mathrm{diam}\pi_{U}(B(x_{2},R+4D)\cap\mathbf{O}_{2})\geq C-4\delta(D+1)=T+1.

However we defined

to satisfy
\mathrm{diam}\pi_{U}(\mathbf{O}_{2})\leq T
, a contradiction.
∎

For every

Lemma 3.19

With the above notation,

A finite subgroup of

For the “furthermore” part, the only non-trivial fact to check is that

With the above notation,

Proof.

\widetilde{\operatorname{Out}}\left(G\right)
