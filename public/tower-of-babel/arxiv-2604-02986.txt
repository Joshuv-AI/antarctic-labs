Mitigating Reward Hacking in RLHF via Advantage Sign Robustness
Shinnosuke Ono, Johannes Ackermann, Soichiro Nishimori, Takashi Ishida, Masashi Sugiyama

Abstract
Reward models (RMs) used in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) are vulnerable to reward hacking: as the policy maximizes a learned proxy reward, true quality plateaus or degrades. We make the assumption that reward hacking is often caused by flipped advantage signs: instead of reducing the likelihood of a bad response, a flipped sign causes the update to increase it. By considering an adversarial perturbation in the RM parameter space, we can derive a certified sign-preservation radius , which is the smallest perturbation that can flip the advantage sign during policy optimization. Based on this formulation, we propose Sign-Certified Policy Optimization (SignCert-PO) , down-weighting non-robust completions in the policy gradient update. Unlike prior approaches that require multiple RMs or access to the RM training data, SignCert-PO is lightweight and operates purely at the policy optimization stage using only the RM parameters and on-policy completions. On TL;DR summarization and AlpacaFarm benchmarks, SignCert-PO consistently achieves a better win rate than baselines and reduces reward hacking.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has become a standard paradigm for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human intent
(
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Stiennon et al. 2020
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
. In the typical RLHF pipeline, a reward model (RM) is trained on pairwise human preference data and then used as a proxy for the true, unobservable preference signal during policy optimization.
Because the RM is inherently an imperfect proxy, optimizing the policy against it often causes the policy to exploit local inaccuracies in the reward landscape. This phenomenon is known as reward hacking
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
. As optimization progresses, proxy reward continues to rise while true quality stagnates or degrades. This is especially acute when the proxy RM is trained with limited preference data
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
or when the policy drifts from the RM’s training distribution during policy optimization by reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Ackermann et al. 2025
)
.

Prior approaches to mitigating reward hacking can be organized along an axis from
ante-hoc
to
post-hoc

(
Retzlaff et al. 2024
)
. Ante-hoc methods incorporate uncertainty quantification into the reward modeling pipeline. For instance, ensemble-based methods
(
Coste et al. 2024
;
Eisenstein et al. 2024
)
train multiple RMs and optimize conservatively with respect to their disagreement, while distributional or uncertainty-aware RMs
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
modify the RM architecture or training objective. These approaches can directly quantify RM uncertainty, but require either multiple RM training and inference runs or changes to the RM pipeline, limiting their practical applicability. In contrast, in the post-hoc approach, the RM is left unchanged and robustness is introduced only at the policy optimization stage. AdvPO
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, the closest prior work to ours, is only partially post-hoc: it derives single-RM uncertainty estimates from last-layer embeddings without retraining, but still requires access to the entire RM training dataset to calibrate its uncertainty model. Our approach is
entirely post-hoc
: it operates solely with the current RM parameters and on-policy completions, and thus we do not need to train new RMs nor retain the dataset used for RM training. We discuss additional related work in Appendix
A.2
.

We argue that what matters during policy optimization is whether the RM correctly predicts the
sign
of each completion’s advantage, since that sign determines whether the policy increases or decreases the completion’s probability. Crucially, the reliability of this prediction varies per completion, and a naive robustness penalty fails to capture this variability.
We therefore ask: for a given completion, how much would the RM need to change before that advantage sign flips? Based on randomized smoothing
(
Cohen et al. 2019
)
, we formalize this quantity as a
certified sign-preservation radius
and down-weight completions whose advantage signs are easy to flip (Figure
1
).
We first show that directly considering perturbations of all RM parameters is computationally challenging, as it requires per-completion gradients.
This quickly causes us to run out of memory, even for relatively small RMs.
Instead, we propose to approximate this solution by only considering perturbations of the RM head, yielding a lightweight re-weighting of the standard policy gradient update. The resulting method,
Sign-Certified Policy Optimization (SignCert-PO)
, prevents policy updates from being dominated by directions whose update signs are locally fragile under RM perturbations.

We evaluate SignCert-PO on TL;DR summarization
(
Stiennon et al. 2020
)
and AlpacaFarm
(
Dubois et al. 2023
)
with Pythia
(
Biderman et al. 2023
)
and Qwen2.5
(
Qwen et al. 2025
)
models. Using the gold-model evaluation setup
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
, we compare against Dr.GRPO
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
, uncertainty-weighted optimization (UWO) with RM ensembles
(
Coste et al. 2024
)
, behavior-supported policy optimization (BSPO)
(
Dai et al. 2025
)
, and AdvPO
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. SignCert-PO achieves the highest gold-RM win rate in most settings, with improved RM accuracy during policy optimization.

2
Background

We denote the set of prompts by
\mathcal{X}
and the set of responses by
\mathcal{Y}.
We assume a prompt distribution
P
over
\mathcal{X}
, where
P(x)
denotes the probability mass of a prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}.
An autoregressive language model
\pi_{\phi}
with parameters
\phi
is then conditional probability
\pi_{\phi}(y|x)=\Pi_{t}\pi_{\phi}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t}),
where
x\in\mathcal{X}
is a prompt,
y\in\mathcal{Y}
is the response,
y_{t}
is the
t
-th response token and
y_{<t}
are the response tokens before
y_{t}
. Following the RL literature
(
Sutton & Barto 2018
)
, we sometimes use the word
policy,
referring to the language model
\pi_{\phi}
. We denote the
\ell_{2}
norm by
\|\cdot\|_{2}
.

2.1
Reinforcement learning from human feedback

RLHF typically consists of three key steps: supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reward modeling, and reinforcement learning
(
Stiennon et al. 2020
)
. We denote the SFT model as
\pi_{\text{SFT}}.

Reward modeling.

Suppose the true reward function
r^{*}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that mirrors human preference exists. Since
r^{*}
is generally unknown, an RM is trained as a proxy with the following procedure: given a prompt
x\in\mathcal{X},
two responses
y_{0},y_{1}\in\mathcal{Y}
are sampled from
\pi_{\text{SFT}}(y|x)
. A human annotator then selects their preferred response, yielding a dataset of triples
(x,y_{\mathrm{w}},y_{\mathrm{l}})
where
y_{\mathrm{w}}
(winning) is preferred over
y_{\mathrm{l}}
(losing). A scalar RM
r_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is trained under the Bradley-Terry (BT) model
(
Bradley & Terry 1952
)
by minimizing the cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BT}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{\mathrm{w}},y_{\mathrm{l}})}\left[\log\sigma\bigl(r_{\theta}(x,y_{\mathrm{w}})-r_{\theta}(x,y_{\mathrm{l}})\bigr)\right],

(1)

where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the sigmoid function:
\sigma(z):=1/(1+\exp(-z)).

Reinforcement learning.

The policy
\pi_{\phi}
is optimized to maximize the Kullback-Leibler (KL)-regularized expected reward
(
Ziegler et al. 2020
)
:

J(\phi,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P(x),y\sim\pi_{\phi}}\bigl[r_{\theta}(x,y)-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\phi}(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x))\bigr],

(2)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)
is the KL divergence of probability densities
p
from
q,
and
\beta\geq 0
controls the strength of KL regularization against a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
. This regularization discourages the policy from diverging too far from the RM’s training distribution
(
Gao et al. 2023
)
. We denote this regularized reward by
r^{\prime}_{\theta}(x,y):=r_{\theta}(x,y)-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\phi}(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)).
Policy optimization proceeds via the policy gradient (PG)
(
Sutton et al. 1999
)
:

\nabla_{\phi}J(\phi,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P,y\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot|x)}[A^{\pi_{\phi}}_{\theta}(x,y)\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(y|x)],

(3)

where
A_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(x,y):=r^{\prime}_{\theta}(x,y)-V_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(x)
is the advantage function with value function
V_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\phi}}[r^{\prime}_{\theta}(x,y)].
Different PG-based algorithms for RLHF differ mainly in their update rules and advantage estimation. In this work, we adopt Dr.GRPO
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
, a widely-used variant of GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
. Given a prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we sample
K
completions
y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(K)}\in\mathcal{Y}
from the current policy
\pi_{\phi}(\cdot|x).
Each receives a reward
r_{\theta}(x,y^{(j)})
for
j\in[K]:=\{1,\ldots,K\}.
Dr.GRPO then provides the following advantage estimation:

A_{j}(\theta):=r_{\theta}(x,y^{(j)})-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{\theta}(x,y^{(k)}).

(4)

Since the RM
r_{\theta}
is only a proxy for true human preferences, the policy can exploit regions where it is inaccurate. As optimization progresses, proxy reward continues to rise while true quality degrades, especially when the proxy RM is trained with limited preference data. This phenomenon is known as
reward hacking

(
Gao et al. 2023
)
.

2.2
Randomized smoothing

Reward modeling reduces to a binary classification problem on reward margins
(
Tang et al. 2024
;
Nishimori et al. 2025
)
. Randomized smoothing (RS)
(
Cohen et al. 2019
)
is a framework for certifying the robustness of a classifier’s prediction against perturbations. Consider a classifier
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[C]
over
C
classes. Given a noise scale
\sigma>0
for the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}
, RS creates the
smoothed classifier

g(x):=\argmax_{c\in[C]}\Pr\big(f(x+\xi)=c\big),\quad\text{where}\ \ \xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d}),

which returns the class most likely to be predicted under random perturbations of the input.
Cohen et al. 2019
showed that if one predicted class is sufficiently more likely than the others under these perturbations, then the prediction of the smoothed classifier is provably consistent throughout an
\ell_{2}
ball around
x.
The radius of this ball is called the
certified radius.
That is, the certified radius is the maximum
\ell_{2}
perturbation to the input under which the smoothed classifier’s prediction is guaranteed to remain unchanged.

3
Robust policy gradient method

We argue that when the RM
r_{\theta}
assigns incorrect advantage signs, the policy is not properly updated and thus reward hacking can occur. Guided by the notion of the certified radius
(
Cohen et al. 2019
)
, we formalize this by introducing the
certified sign-preservation radius
, the smallest perturbation of the RM parameters that flips a completion’s advantage sign (§
3.1
). This leads to our proposed method of re-weighting the policy gradient updates, using the radius as a reliability measure (§
3.2
). Finally, we compare our method of using per-completion perturbations to the standard global perturbation setup, which we show suffers uniform pessimism unlike our method. We focus on the advantage estimate used in Dr.GRPO (
4
) throughout the rest of the paper, but we note that other PG methods can be used instead, as shown in Appendix
E
.

3.1
Certified sign-preservation radius

The PG (
3
) is a weighted sum of per-completion directions:

\nabla_{\phi}J(\phi,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P,\{y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(K)}\}\sim\pi_{\phi}}\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(\theta)\,\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(y^{(j)}|x)\right].

(5)

Each term pushes the policy to increase (
A_{j}(\theta)>0
) or decrease (
A_{j}(\theta)<0
) the probability of the
j
-th completion. Consider a single gradient step
\phi^{\prime}=\phi+\eta\nabla_{\phi}J(\phi,\theta)
with step size
\eta>0
. Then, a first-order expansion gives

\log\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}(y^{(j)}|x)-\log\pi_{\phi}(y^{(j)}|x)\approx\eta A_{j}(\theta)\|\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(y^{(j)}|x)\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\eta>0
and
\|\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
, the sign of
A_{j}(\theta)
alone determines the update direction, when considering the
j
-th term in isolation. Crucially, the reliability of this sign prediction varies per completion: since the RM is only a proxy, some predictions in a group may not agree with the true reward, while others do agree (Figure
1
a).

To quantify the sensitivity for a given completion, we follow the idea of RS, which certifies robustness against input perturbations.
As the input space of the RM is discrete, we cannot directly utilize input perturbations.
Instead, we model epistemic uncertainty about the RM as perturbations in the parameter space:
\mathcal{U}_{\tau}^{\theta}:=\{\theta^{\prime}:\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|_{2}\leq\tau\}

(
Ben-Tal et al. 2009
)
.

Definition 3.1

(Certified sign-preservation radius)
.

Under an uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{\tau}^{\theta}
, the
certified sign-preservation radius
for the
j
-th completion is

\Delta_{j}:=\sup\bigl\{\tau\geq 0:\mathrm{sign}(A_{j}(\theta^{\prime}))=\mathrm{sign}(A_{j}(\theta))\ \forall\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\tau}^{\theta}\bigr\}.

(6)

This definition follows the certified-radius framework of
Cohen et al. 2019
, where the advantage sign is viewed as a binary classification decision in the RM parameter space (see Appendix
C
for the formal connection). As such, (
6
) has a natural interpretation as a sensitivity measure. A completion with large
\Delta_{j}
has a
robust advantage sign
: the RM would need to change substantially before the sign flips, so the corresponding gradient term in (
5
) can be trusted. On the other hand, for a completion with small
\Delta_{j}
, a small perturbation to the RM could reverse whether the policy reinforces or suppresses it. The certified sign-preservation radius thus provides a per-sample confidence measure: for each completion individually, it tells us how much the update direction depends on the exact choice of the proxy RM parameters
\theta
.

For a general differentiable RM
r_{\theta}
with full-parameter uncertainty
\mathcal{U}_{\tau}^{\theta}
, a first-order Taylor expansion of the advantage around
\theta
gives
A_{j}(\theta^{\prime})\approx A_{j}(\theta)+\nabla_{\theta}A_{j}(\theta)^{\mathsf{T}}(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)
. The certified sign-preservation radius is then

\Delta_{j}(\theta)\gtrsim\frac{|A_{j}(\theta)|}{\|\nabla_{\theta}A_{j}(\theta)\|_{2}}.

Unfortunately, computing
\|\nabla_{\theta}A_{j}(\theta)\|_{2}
requires the per-completion gradient norm of the RM, which our preliminary experiments found to be computationally infeasible for larger RMs.
We thus propose to use a simpler perturbation model, for which we can efficiently calculate the certified sign-preservation radius, as we will show below.

3.2
Sign-Certified Policy Optimization (SignCert-PO)

We consider the RM architecture
r_{\theta}(x,y)=w^{\mathsf{T}}h_{\psi}(x,y)+b
, consisting of a feature extractor
h_{\psi}(x,y)
with parameters
\psi
and a linear head with parameters
(w,b)
, as commonly used in practice
(
Stiennon et al. 2020
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
.
Instead of perturbing all parameters
(\psi,w,b)
, we only perturb the linear head, and thus use the uncertainty set
1
1

1

We discuss other possible choices of perturbation models in Appendix
C.2
.

\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}:=\{w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|w^{\prime}-w\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\}.

(7)

In what follows,
A_{j}(w)
denotes
A_{j}(\theta),
stressing perturbations for the head parameters
w.

Under the linear head model, the certified sign-preservation radius (
6
) admits a closed form that is easy to compute during policy optimization without additional information:

Theorem 3.1

(Certified radius)
.

Under the linear head model and the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}
(
7
), the certified sign-preservation radius is

\Delta_{j}=\frac{|A_{j}(w)|}{\|h_{\psi}(x,y^{(j)})-\bar{h}\|_{2}},\quad\text{where}\ \ \bar{h}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}h_{\psi}(x,y^{(k)}).

(8)

The proof follows from the linearity of the reward head and is given in Appendix
B
.

Based on the certified radius above, we propose
SignCert-PO
, a conservative variant of the standard PG (
5
). For each completion, let the adversary choose
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}
to maximally corrupt that completion’s gradient contribution. Concretely, the adversary minimizes the advantage for completions the policy with the nominal RM would reinforce (
A_{j}(w)>0
) and maximizes it for completions the policy would suppress (
A_{j}(w)<0
):

\tilde{A}_{j}:=\begin{cases}\inf_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}A_{j}(w^{\prime})&\text{if }A_{j}(w)>0,\\[4.0pt]
\sup_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}A_{j}(w^{\prime})&\text{if }A_{j}(w)<0.\end{cases}

(9)

This formulation asks: Given that the RM could be wrong within
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}
, what is the most conservative advantage we can still attribute
to each completion
?

Theorem 3.2

(Worst-case advantage under per-completion adversary)
.

Under the linear head model and the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}
(
7
), the worst-case advantage (
9
) for the
j
-th completion is

\tilde{A}_{j}=\rho_{j}^{*}\cdot A_{j}(w),\quad\text{where}\quad\rho_{j}^{*}:=1-\frac{\epsilon}{\Delta_{j}}=1-\frac{\epsilon\,\|h_{\psi}(x,y^{(j)})-\bar{h}\|_{2}}{|A_{j}(w)|}.

(10)

The proof is given in Appendix
B
. SignCert-PO thus replaces each advantage with its worst-case value:

\widehat{\nabla}_{\phi}J(\phi,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}\rho_{j}^{*}\cdot A_{j}(w)\cdot\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(y^{(j)}|x)\right].

(11)

This is the standard GRPO gradient with each term re-weighted using its certified sign-preservation radius. Setting
\epsilon=0
recovers standard GRPO. (
11
) can also be derived as the PG of a certain robust optimization problem but the discussion is deferred to Appendix
D
.

Practical implementation.

The coefficient
\rho_{j}^{*}
(
10
) depends only on the advantage
A_{j}(w)
and the feature deviation
\|h_{\psi}(x,y^{(j)})-\bar{h}\|_{2}
. Rather than fixing
\epsilon
globally, we set it adaptively using the empirical distribution of the ratio
\|h_{\psi}(x,y^{(j)})-\bar{h}\|_{2}/|A_{j}(w)|
within each batch. Specifically, we set
\epsilon
to the
q_{t}
-th quantile of
\{\|h_{\psi}(x,y^{(j)})-\bar{h}\|_{2}/|A_{j}(w)|\}_{j=1}^{B\cdot K}
over all
B\cdot K
completions in the current batch, where
B
is the number of prompts and
K
is the group size. As shown in Appendix
G.3
, the parameter
q_{t}\in[0,1]
provides a natural interpretation of controlling the proxy RM accuracy during policy optimization. The full pseudocode is provided in Algorithm
1
(Appendix
F
).

3.3
Comparison with the global robust objective

Finally, we compare our per-completion adversary (
9
) with the standard approach of taking a single worst-case reward for all completions over an uncertainty set
(
Ben-Tal et al. 2009
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, which we show that cannot distinguish reliable from unreliable completions. Consider the
global
robust objective, with the shared perturbation
w^{\prime}
:

J_{\mathrm{global}}(\phi,\theta)=J_{\mathrm{global}}(\phi,\{\psi,w,b\}):=\inf_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}J(\phi,\{\psi,w^{\prime},b\}).

(12)

The following lemma shows that this approach falls short (the proof is given in Appendix
B
):

Lemma 3.3

(Policy gradient of the global robust objective)
.

Under the linear reward head, the PG of the worst-case objective (
12
) is

\nabla_{\phi}J_{\mathrm{global}}(\phi,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(w)\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(y^{(j)}|x)\right]-\epsilon\nabla_{\phi}\bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[h_{\psi}(x,y)]\bigr\|_{2}.

(13)

Here, the correction term
\nabla_{\phi}\|\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[h_{\psi}(x,y)]\|_{2}
is a single direction shared across all completions
y\sim\pi_{\phi}.
It does not depend on
which
completions receive reliable reward signal and which do not: a completion whose ranking is highly certain receives the same penalty as one whose ranking could be flipped by a small perturbation of the RM parameters.

This uniform pessimism is also observed beyond the linear-head case.
Zhang et al. 2024
empirically observed that their uncertainty set over the whole RM parameters
\theta
led to overly pessimistic reward corrections.
Eisenstein et al. 2024
similarly found that ensemble-based penalties fail when all members agree on the same spurious features, suggesting that completion-level discrimination is necessary.

In contrast, our per-completion formulation (
9
) and re-weighting (
11
) provide this discrimination: completions with robust advantage signs receive the gradient essentially unchanged, while completions with unreliable signs are effectively removed. SignCert-PO therefore selectively suppresses the unreliable gradient directions while preserving the reliable ones.

4
Experiments

To evaluate our proposed method in LLM alignment tasks, we adopt the gold-model setup of
Gao et al. 2023
, which allows us to investigate reward hacking by simulating the true reward
r^{*}
with a larger “gold” RM.
The pipeline proceeds in four stages:

1.

SFT.
A base LLM is fine-tuned on reference responses to obtain the SFT policy
\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}
.

2.

Preference data generation.
For each prompt
x
, two candidate responses are sampled from
\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}
and labeled by the gold RM to form pairwise preference data.

3.

Proxy RM training.
A proxy reward model is trained on the generated preference data using the Bradley–Terry objective (
1
). We add a randomly initialized scalar head on top of the same base LLM backbone as the SFT model
\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}
. During RM training, although we derived (
6
) under the frozen-backbone assumption, we train the full RM parameters to test broader applicability of our proposed method.

4.

Policy optimization and evaluation.
The SFT policy is optimized against the proxy RM using one of the methods described below. The resulting policy is evaluated by the gold RM that was not used during policy optimization.

We conduct experiments on the foundational summarization from human feedback task (TL;DR)
(
Stiennon et al. 2020
)
and on the AlpacaFarm benchmark
(
Dubois et al. 2023
)
. We use Pythia
(
Biderman et al. 2023
)
and Qwen2.5
(
Qwen et al. 2025
)
series as base models. For Pythia 1B and Qwen2.5 1.5B policies, the gold RM is Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B-v0.2
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
, one of the top-performing discriminative RMs on RewardBench
(
Malik et al. 2025
)
. For larger policy models (Pythia 2.8B and Qwen2.5 3B), we use OpenAI’s GPT-4.1 Nano as the gold RM. More experimental details are given in Appendix
F
.

5
Conclusion

We presented SignCert-PO, a lightweight method for RLHF that mitigates reward hacking by down-weighting policy gradient contributions of completions whose advantage signs are fragile under RM parameter perturbations. Our approach derives a certified sign-preservation radius that quantifies, for each completion, the smallest perturbation to the RM that can flip the advantage sign. This per-completion formulation yields a simple re-weighting mechanism of the standard PG update, requiring only the current RM head parameters and on-policy completions. Experiments on TL;DR summarization and AlpacaFarm across Pythia and Qwen2.5 model families show that SignCert-PO achieves the highest gold-RM win rate in most settings while adding negligible computational overhead.

Our method makes several simplifying assumptions (§
3.2
). Further, our per-sample adversary formulation is more conservative than a shared-adversary variant. Analyzing the tighter coupled formulation is a future direction. Finally, extending the certified sign-preservation radius to general RMs without incurring the cost of full-parameter gradient norm computation remains an open problem.

Appendix A
Additional background and related work

We present a more comprehensive discussion of related work here.

A.1
Policy gradient algorithms

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

PPO
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
is a canonical PG-based method for RLHF that uses a clipped surrogate objective and jointly trains a value network for advantage estimation. Given responses sampled from an old policy
\pi_{\phi_{\mathrm{old}}}
, PPO maximizes the clipped surrogate objective:

L^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P,y\sim\pi_{\phi_{\mathrm{old}}}}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\Big(\nu_{t}(\phi)\hat{A}_{t},\mathrm{clip}\big(\nu_{t}(\phi),1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}},1+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\big)\hat{A}_{t}\Big)\right],

(14)

where
T=|y|
is the response length,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}>0
is the clipping hyperparameter, and

\nu_{t}(\phi):=\frac{\pi_{\phi}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\phi_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}

is the density ratio. The token-level advantage
\hat{A}_{t}
is estimated using a learned value network
V_{\omega}(x,y_{<t})
with parameters
\omega
, trained jointly with the policy:

\hat{A}_{t}=r_{t}+\gamma V_{\omega}(x,y_{\leq t})-V_{\omega}(x,y_{<t}),

(15)

where
\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor and
r_{t}
is the per-token reward. In RLHF,
r_{t}=-\beta\log\bigl(\pi_{\phi}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})\bigr)
for all
t
, with the RM score
r_{\theta}(x,y)
added at the final token
t=T

(
Ziegler et al. 2020
)
.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
replaces the learned value network in PPO with a group-based Monte Carlo baseline. For each prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
, a group of
K
completions
y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(K)}\in\mathcal{Y}
is sampled from the current policy
\pi_{\phi}(\cdot|x)
, and each receives a reward
r_{j}:=r_{\theta}(x,y^{(j)})
. In the original GRPO, the advantage is the normalized reward:
\widehat{A}_{j}:=(r_{j}-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r}))/\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r}),
where
\mathbf{r}
is the vectorized reward
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{K})^{\mathsf{T}},
and
\mathrm{mean}(\cdot)
and
\mathrm{std}(\cdot)
denote the mean and standard deviation of the elements of a vector, respectively.

GRPO can be viewed as replacing PPO’s learned value baseline with a within-group relative baseline computed from sampled completions. This removes the need to train a separate value network, allowing for preserving VRAM and scaling up the RLHF pipeline.

Dr.GRPO
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
is a widely-used variant that removes the standard-deviation normalization to avoid biased gradient estimation (see §
2.1
in the main text).

A.2
Additional related work

Reward hacking arises when the policy distribution drifts outside the region where the proxy RM is reliable. Prior work addresses this through ensemble-based uncertainty, modified RM training, or adversarial robustness.

RM ensembles.

Ensemble-based methods mitigate reward hacking by optimizing a policy conservatively with respect to multiple RMs.
Coste et al. 2024
train several proxy RMs and study conservative objectives such as worst-case and uncertainty-weighted optimization (UWO), showing that ensembles reduce reward hacking in the gold-RM setup of
Gao et al. 2023
.
Eisenstein et al. 2024
observe that ensembles still exploit spurious features when all members agree on them.
Zhai et al. 2026
propose LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
-based RM ensembles to reduce the cost of training multiple full RMs. These approaches require multiple RM training and inference runs.

Distribution shift and modified RM training.

Reward hacking can also be viewed as a distribution shift problem: as the policy moves away from the SFT distribution, the RM’s training data becomes unrepresentative.
Ackermann et al. 2025
propose off-policy corrected reward modeling, which uses importance weighting to make the RM consistent under the evolving policy distribution.
Dai et al. 2025
propose BSPO, which regularizes the RM training objective to improve robustness.
Xu et al. 2024
train distributional RMs with explicit uncertainty outputs. These methods modify the RM training pipeline, whereas SignCert-PO operates purely at policy optimization time using any pre-trained RM.

Adversarial policy optimization.

The closest work to ours is AdvPO
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, which derives single-RM uncertainty estimates from last-layer embeddings and formulates a distributionally robust policy optimization problem. Two differences distinguish our approach. First, AdvPO requires constructing a matrix
M_{D}
from the RM training dataset
D
at policy optimization time; in many practical pipelines, this data is unavailable when using third-party RMs
(
Malik et al. 2025
;
Chijiwa et al. 2025
)
. SignCert-PO requires only the current head parameters and on-policy completions. Second, AdvPO constructs a confidence region for the reward function using that matrix and then optimizes the policy against a pessimistic reward within that region. In contrast, SignCert-PO models uncertainty as perturbations of the RM parameters and asks whether those perturbations can flip the sign of the group-relative advantage for each completion. This aligns our adversarial formulation directly with GRPO’s update geometry, as discussed in §
3.3
.

Appendix B
Proofs

In what follows,
h_{\psi}(x,y)
is referred to as
h(x,y)
for brevity, and
h_{j}
denotes
h(x,y^{(j)}).
We also write
A_{j}
instead of
A_{j}(w)

Theorem B.1

(Certified radius (Theorem
3.1
, restated))
.

The certified sign-preservation radius for sample
j
under head parameter uncertainty is

\Delta_{j}^{\mathrm{param}}=\frac{|A_{j}|}{\|h_{j}-\bar{h}\|_{2}}.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We first establish how the advantage changes under head parameter perturbation. By linearity of the reward head,

\displaystyle A_{j}(w^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(w^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}h_{j}+b-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big((w^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}h_{k}+b\big)

\displaystyle=(w+\delta)^{\mathsf{T}}h_{j}-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(w+\delta)^{\mathsf{T}}h_{k}

\displaystyle=A_{j}(w)+\delta^{\mathsf{T}}(h_{j}-\bar{h}),

where
\bar{h}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}h_{k}
. The worst-case perturbation for flipping the sign of
A_{j}>0
is

\delta^{*}=-\epsilon\cdot(h_{j}-\bar{h})/\|h_{j}-\bar{h}\|_{2}
,
yielding
A_{j}(w+\delta^{*})=A_{j}-\epsilon\|h_{j}-\bar{h}\|_{2}
. The sign flips when this becomes non-positive, i.e., when
\epsilon\geq|A_{j}|/\|h_{j}-\bar{h}\|_{2}
. The case
A_{j}<0
is symmetric.
∎

Theorem B.2

(Worst-case advantage under per-completion adversary (Theorem
3.2
, restated))
.

Under the linear head model and the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}
(
7
), the worst-case advantage (
9
) for the
j
-th completion is

\tilde{A}_{j}=\rho_{j}^{*}\cdot A_{j},\quad\text{where}\quad\rho_{j}^{*}:=1-\frac{\epsilon}{\Delta_{j}}=1-\frac{\epsilon\,\|h_{j}-\bar{h}\|_{2}}{|A_{j}|}.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

For
A_{j}>0
, the worst case under
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}
is
\inf_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}A_{j}(w^{\prime})=A_{j}-\epsilon\|h_{j}-\bar{h}\|_{2}=A_{j}(1-\epsilon/\Delta_{j})
, where the first equality follows from the perturbation analysis in the proof of Theorem
3.1
. For
A_{j}<0
, the analogous worst case is
\sup_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}A_{j}(w^{\prime})=A_{j}+\epsilon\|h_{j}-\bar{h}\|_{2}=A_{j}(1-\epsilon/\Delta_{j})
, where we used
|A_{j}|=-A_{j}
. In both cases, the worst-case advantage equals
\rho_{j}^{*}\cdot A_{j}
with
\rho_{j}^{*}
given by (
10
).
∎

Lemma B.3

(Policy gradient of the global robust objective (Lemma
3.3
, restated))
.

Under the GRPO sampling with
K
on-policy completions per prompt, the PG of the global robust objective is

\nabla_{\phi}J_{\mathrm{global}}(\phi,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(y^{(j)}|x)\right]-\epsilon\nabla_{\phi}\bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\phi}}[h(x,y)]\bigr\|_{2}.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

We first derive the worst-case objective. Since the KL term is independent of
w^{\prime}
, the infimum reduces to
\inf_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\phi}}[{w^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{T}}h(x,y)+b]=w^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h]+b-\sup_{\|\delta\|_{2}\leq\epsilon}\delta^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r_{w}(x,y)]-\epsilon\|\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h]\|_{2}
,
where the supremum is attained at
\delta^{*}=\epsilon\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h]/\|\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h]\|_{2}
by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Thus,

J_{\mathrm{global}}(\phi,w)=J(\phi,w)-\epsilon\bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\phi}}[h(x,y)]\bigr\|_{2}.

The first term of the PG is the standard GRPO gradient of
J(\phi,w)
.
The second term follows from differentiating the penalty
-\epsilon\|\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h]\|_{2}
through the policy, which depends on
\phi
via
\pi_{\phi}
but not on any per-completion quantity.
∎

Appendix C
Connection with randomized smoothing

In §
3.1
, we defined the
certified sign-preservation radius

\Delta_{j}
(
6
) as the largest perturbation under which the sign of the
j
-th completion’s advantage remains constant. This section shows that
\Delta_{j}
admits a formal interpretation through RS in the binary classification case.

C.1
Randomized smoothing interpretation of the certified sign-preservation radius

Consider the binary decision given by the sign of the advantage, and inject Gaussian noise
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)
into the space where uncertainty is modeled (e.g., the RM head parameter space). In RS, the certified radius for a binary decision is

R=\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p),

where
p
is the probability that the decision is preserved under the Gaussian perturbation
(
Cohen et al. 2019
)
.
For linear perturbations, the sign-preservation probability has the form
p=\Phi(|A_{j}|/(\sigma\cdot c))
for some constant
c
. Substituting into the RS formula recovers exactly the certified radii in (
8
) and (
16
).

The above results imply that responses with low coefficients
\rho_{j}^{*}
, or equivalently low radii
\Delta_{j}^{\textrm{param}}
, contribute less to the policy update with the robust policy optimization objective. The radius
\Delta_{j}^{\mathrm{param}}=|A_{j}|/\|h_{j}-\bar{h}\|_{2}
is small when:

•

The advantage
|A_{j}|
is small. In this case, the preference signal is weak and easily flipped.

•

The feature deviation
\|h_{j}-\bar{h}\|_{2}
is large. This means the response is an outlier in feature space, where the RM may be exploring.

The first category corresponds to the delta learning hypothesis
(
Geng et al. 2025
)
. Our theoretical results suggest that reward hacking also exploits responses in the second category. Template-like or verbose outputs that achieve high rewards through spurious correlations often lie in sharp, high-norm feature directions, yielding small certified radii despite large advantages.

C.2
Alternative uncertainty models

The same derivation applies to the other uncertainty models, yielding analogous certified radii.

Feature uncertainty.

Under perturbation
h_{j}^{\prime}=h_{j}+\xi_{j}
with
\xi_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{h}^{2}I)
, the perturbed advantage is

\tilde{A}_{j}=A_{j}+w^{\top}(\xi_{j}-\mathrm{mean}(\boldsymbol{\xi})).

Since
\xi_{j}-\mathrm{mean}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{h}^{2}(1-1/K)I)
, the perturbed advantage is Gaussian:

\tilde{A}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(A_{j},\sigma_{h}^{2}(1-1/K)\|w\|_{2}^{2}\right).

The probability of sign preservation and certified radius are:

p_{j}^{\mathrm{feat}}=\Phi\left(\frac{|A_{j}|}{\sigma_{h}\|w\|_{2}\sqrt{1-1/K}}\right),\qquad\Delta_{j}^{\mathrm{feat}}=\frac{|A_{j}|}{\|w\|_{2}}.

(16)

Note that
\Delta_{j}^{\mathrm{feat}}
is a monotone function of
|A_{j}|
, unlike
\Delta_{j}^{\mathrm{param}}
which also depends on feature geometry.

Full parameter uncertainty.

Under first-order approximation with
\theta^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma_{\theta}^{2}I)
:

A_{j}(\theta^{\prime})\approx A_{j}(\theta)+\nabla_{\theta}A_{j}(\theta)^{\top}(\theta^{\prime}-\theta).

This yields:

p_{j}^{\mathrm{full}}=\Phi\left(\frac{|A_{j}|}{\sigma_{\theta}\|\nabla_{\theta}A_{j}\|_{2}}\right),\qquad\Delta_{j}^{\mathrm{full}}=\frac{|A_{j}|}{\|\nabla_{\theta}A_{j}\|_{2}}.

We expect spurious patterns to have large gradient norms
\|\nabla_{\theta}A_{j}\|_{2}
, as the RM assigns high rewards through sharp, non-robust computations. Such patterns receive small radii and are appropriately down-weighted.

Appendix D
Robust optimization perspective and its limitations

The re-weighted gradient (
11
) can be viewed as the PG of a per-completion robust optimization problem. For each completion, the adversary chooses
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}
to maximally corrupt that completion’s gradient contribution:

J_{\mathrm{robust}}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}\inf_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}A_{j}(w^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{1}[A_{j}(w)>0]+\sup_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}A_{j}(w^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{1}[A_{j}(w)<0]\right].

(17)

This formulation takes the
\inf
and
\sup
inside the sum over completions, allowing the adversary to choose different perturbations for each completion.

We now argue that this per-sample decomposition is a necessity: any formulation that robustifies an aggregate advantage is vacuous under GRPO’s mean-subtracted structure.

Zero-sum obstruction.

A more standard robust optimization approach
(
Ben-Tal et al. 2009
)
would robustify the aggregate advantage directly, e.g.,
\inf_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(w^{\prime})
. However, under the linear head model, for any
w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(w^{\prime})=(w^{\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}(h_{j}-\bar{h})=0,

since
\sum_{j=1}^{K}(h_{j}-\bar{h})=0
by definition of
\bar{h}
. Group-relative advantages sum to zero regardless of the RM parameters, so any robust objective of the form
\inf_{w^{\prime}}\sum_{j}A_{j}(w^{\prime})
evaluates to zero trivially and provides no useful signal for policy optimization.

This complements Lemma
3.3
, which showed that the global robust reward objective yields only a completion-agnostic penalty (
13
).

Conservatism of the per-sample formulation.

The per-sample objective (
17
) circumvents the zero-sum obstruction by partitioning the sum according to the sign of
A_{j}(w)
before applying the
\inf
and
\sup
. This is a stronger (i.e., more conservative) setting than the standard robust optimization formulation, where a single worst-case parameter
w^{\prime}
would be shared across all terms:

J_{\mathrm{robust}}^{\mathrm{shared}}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\left[\inf_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(w^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{1}[A_{j}(w)>0]+\sup_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(w^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{1}[A_{j}(w)<0]\right].

(18)

The per-sample formulation provides a tractable upper bound on the conservatism: because each term is optimized independently, the resulting coefficients
\rho_{j}^{*}
decompose into per-sample quantities, yielding the closed-form solution in Theorem
3.1
. The shared-adversary variant (
18
), while tighter, couples all completions and requires solving a joint optimization problem over the group. The further analysis of different robust optimizations is left for future work.

Appendix E
Extension to other policy gradient methods

The sign-certification principle of §
3
applies beyond Dr.GRPO to any PG-based RLHF method whose update involves a scalar coefficient depending on the RM. Since the exact form of the certified radius depends on the update geometry, we only discuss REINFORCE
(
Williams 1992
)
, PPO
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
and vanilla GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
.

E.1
REINFORCE

The simplest case is REINFORCE, or more generally any response-level PG method with advantage

A^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y;w)=r_{w}(x,y)-V(x),

where
r_{w}(x,y)=w^{\mathsf{T}}h(x,y)+b
is the linear reward head and
V(x)
is a baseline independent of the RM head
w
(e.g., a learned value function, a Monte Carlo return, or simply
V(x)\equiv 0
for vanilla REINFORCE). The PG then takes the form
\nabla_{\phi}J(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[A^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y;w)\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(y|x)]
, matching the form in (
3
).

Under the same linear-head uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}
(
7
) as in §
3
, the certified sign-preservation radius admits an exact closed form.

Proposition E.1

(Certified sign-preservation radius for response-level PG)
.

Under the linear reward head and the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}^{w}
(
7
), with a baseline
V(x)
independent of the RM head
w
, the certified sign-preservation radius for response
y
is

\Delta^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y)=\frac{|A^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y;w)|}{\|h(x,y)\|_{2}}.

(19)

Proof.

Under perturbation
w^{\prime}=w+\delta
with
\|\delta\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
:

A^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y;w^{\prime})=A^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y;w)+\delta^{\top}h(x,y),

since
V(x)
does not depend on
w
. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
|\delta^{\top}h(x,y)|\leq\epsilon\|h(x,y)\|_{2}
, with equality when
\delta=\pm\epsilon\,h(x,y)/\|h(x,y)\|_{2}
. The sign flips when
\epsilon\geq|A^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y;w)|/\|h(x,y)\|_{2}
.
∎

3.2
:

Corollary E.2

(Worst-case advantage under per-completion adversary)
.

Under the same setting as Proposition

, the worst-case advantage under the per-completion adversary (
9
) is

\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y)=\rho^{\mathrm{R}}\cdot A^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y;w),\quad\text{where}\quad\rho^{\mathrm{R}}:=1-\frac{\epsilon}{\Delta^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y)}=1-\frac{\epsilon\,\|h(x,y)\|_{2}}{|A^{\mathrm{R}}(x,y;w)|}.

E.2
Approximate extensions

Standard PPO and vanilla GRPO do not generally admit a single response-level coefficient whose sign alone determines the update for the whole completion. Sign certification for these methods therefore requires a first-order approximation.

E.2.1
PPO

Standard PPO in RLHF uses a token-level clipped surrogate objective (
14
) with token-level advantages
\hat{A}_{t}
(
15
) estimated via a learned value network (§
A.1
). Unlike Dr.GRPO or the response-level PG of §
E.1
, PPO does not generally admit a single scalar response-level coefficient. Its per-token structure means that the sign of the effective coefficient can differ across tokens within a single response.

Sign certification for PPO should therefore be formulated for the token-level coefficient appearing in each surrogate term. Under the linear reward head, the token-level advantage
\hat{A}_{t}
depends on the RM head
w
only through the terminal reward
r_{w}(x,y)
added at
t=T
. A first-order approximation gives

\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{PPO}}\approx\frac{|\hat{A}_{t}|}{\|\nabla_{w}\hat{A}_{t}\|_{2}},

which is consistent with the general first-order extension in §
3.1
.

E.2.2
Vanilla GRPO

In vanilla GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
, the advantage is the normalized reward
\hat{A}_{j}=(r_{j}-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r}))/\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})
, where
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{K})^{\mathsf{T}}
. Because
\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r}(w^{\prime}))
depends non-linearly on
\delta=w^{\prime}-w
through all
K
rewards, the perturbed advantage
\hat{A}_{j}(w^{\prime})
is no longer affine in
\delta
. This breaks the exact analysis that yields the Dr.GRPO closed form (
8
). A first-order expansion yields the approximate radius

\Delta_{j}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}\approx\frac{|\hat{A}_{j}|}{\|\nabla_{w}\hat{A}_{j}\|_{2}},

which requires the gradient of the normalized advantage with respect to
w
. This is more expensive than the Dr.GRPO closed form but avoids the full-parameter gradients needed for the general RM extension (§
3.1
).

Appendix F
Training and implementation details

F.1
Conceptual experiment (Figure
1
)

Before presenting the full-scale LLM experiments, we design a controlled conceptual experiment to demonstrate advantage sign flipping causes reward hacking and to verify that SignCert-PO prevents it.

F.1.1
Setup

We consider a stateless
M
-armed bandit (
M=8
). Each action (completion)
m
is associated with a fixed feature vector
h_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=6
), analogous to the last-layer hidden states
h(x,y^{(j)})
of an RM backbone in §
3.2
. Both the proxy and true RMs use a linear head on top of these shared features:

r_{k}=w^{\mathsf{T}}h_{k},\qquad r^{*}_{k}=w^{*\mathsf{T}}h_{k},

matching the linear-head architecture assumed in §
3.2
. The feature space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is split into two subspaces:
shared
dimensions (0–2), where both heads assign weight
1.0
;
divergent
dimensions (3–5), where the proxy assigns weight
0.4
but the true RM assigns
-0.1
. Actions 1–3 (
reliable positive
) use only shared dimensions and have positive proxy and true rewards. Actions 4–6 (
reliable negative
) also use only shared dimensions but have negative rewards. Actions 7–8 (
hacking
) use only divergent dimensions. As a result, the proxy RM assigns them the highest advantages in the group, while their true advantages are negative.

The policy is a categorical stochastic policy
\pi(a_{m}|\phi)=\mathrm{softmax}(\phi)_{m}
initialized to uniform (
\phi=\mathbf{0}
). Because
M
is small and there is a single state, we use the exact expected policy gradient.

F.1.2
Policy optimization methods

We compare three methods, all sharing the softmax policy gradient structure but differing in how advantages are weighted:

•

Dr.GRPO
:
\nabla_{\phi_{i}}J=\pi_{i}(A_{i}-V_{\pi})
, where
V_{\pi}=\sum_{k}\pi_{k}A_{k}
.

•

Global robust objective
(Lemma
3.3
): adds a penalty
-\epsilon\pi_{i}(h_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h])^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h]/\|\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h]\|)
that corrects all actions through a single direction. We sweep
\epsilon
over 20 log-spaced values in
[10^{-2},10]
and report the best.

•

SignCert-PO
(Theorem
3.1
):
\nabla_{\phi_{i}}J=\pi_{i}(\rho_{i}A_{i}-V_{\rho})
, where
\rho_{k}=1-\epsilon/\Delta_{k}
and
\epsilon
is set to the
q_{t}
-th quantile of
\{\Delta_{k}\}
.

F.1.3
Results

Figure
1
(a) shows the proxy advantages per action. Red bars indicate completions whose advantage sign disagrees with the true RM. The hacking actions (7–8) have the largest proxy advantages. Figure
1
(c) shows the per-action certified sign-preservation radius
\Delta_{j}
. The completions 7 and 8 cluster at low
\Delta
values because they are outliers in the feature-space (i.e., large
\|h_{j}-\bar{h}\|
), confirming that
\Delta_{j}
identifies unreliable completions.

Figure

The global robust penalty partially mitigates reward hacking, but the single penalty direction cannot distinguish per-action reliability and is thus too conservative. SignCert-PO achieves the highest true reward, by suppressing the hacking actions’ gradient contributions via their small
\rho_{k}
weights.

Datasets

Models

Policy and proxy RM.

We use Pythia

(

Gold RM.

For the TL;DR experiments with Pythia 1B and Qwen2.5 1.5B policies, the gold reward model is Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B-v0.2

Malik et al. 2025

(
OpenAI et al. 2024
)

F.3.3
Baselines

We compare SignCert-PO (Algorithm
1
) against the following baselines:

•

SFT
: the SFT policy
\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}
without any RL optimization.

•

Dr.GRPO

(
Liu et al. 2025
)
, as introduced in §
2.1
. The KL coefficient is swept over
\{0,0.001,0.005,0.01,0.1,0.2\}
for Pythia-1B and Qwen2.5-1.5B, and the best configuration is selected per dataset. Due to computational constraints, we do not sweep the KL coefficient for larger models and instead use 0.005 for Pythia 2.8B and 0.01 for Qwen2.5 3B.

•

UWO

(
Coste et al. 2024
)
: an RM ensemble method that trains multiple proxy RMs with different random seeds and uses the mean reward minus a penalty proportional to the ensemble standard deviation. We use an ensemble of three RM members. Following the setup of
Zhang et al. 2024
, to control total model capacity, we use smaller individual RMs (Pythia 410M for 1B experiments and Qwen2.5 0.5B for 1.5B experiments). For larger policy models (Pythia 2.8B, Qwen2.5 3B), we use Pythia 1B and Qwen2.5 1.5B as proxy RMs in the ensemble, respectively.

•

BSPO

(
Dai et al. 2025
)
: a method that constrains policy updates to remain within the support of the behavior policy to prevent out-of-distribution exploitation.

•

AdvPO

(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
: an adversarial policy optimization method that augments the reward with a learned uncertainty penalty. We sweep the perturbation budget
B\in\{1,5,10,15\}
, following the range used in the original paper.

For both benchmarks, we set the learning rate to
10^{-6}
, with micro batch size 32 on 8 GPUs and
K=8
generations per prompt.
Pythia policies are trained for 2,000 steps; Qwen2.5 for 500 steps since proxy reward converges faster.
For SignCert-PO, we sweep the quantile threshold
q_{t}\in\{0.1,0.2,0.25,0.3,0.4,0.5\}
.
For TL;DR experiments with Pythia 1B and Qwen2.5 1.5B, we report mean
\pm
standard deviation over 3 random seeds to account for variance from limited preference data.

F.3.4
Evaluation metrics

We report the win rate of policy outputs against reference responses, judged by the gold RM. We also measure proxy RM accuracy and KL divergence during policy optimization, measured at every 50 global optimization steps. Proxy RM accuracy is calculated as agreement between the proxy RM and the gold RM on pairwise ranking comparisons of on-policy completions.

Certified radius validation (Figure
