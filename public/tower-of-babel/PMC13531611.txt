Sport-Specific Athletic Adaptation
Authors: Prashant Rao, Connor Riegal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

“Form follows function” is a foundational architectural principle asserting that structure should reflect purpose. The athlete's heart provides a compelling biological example, as cardiac structure and function adapt over time to reflect the physiological demands imposed by regular exercise training. Since its seminal description by Eugene Darling and Salomon Henschen,1 we now know that the athlete’s heart is governed by multiple determinants, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, genetics, and sport type. Distinguishing physiologic exercise-induced cardiac remodeling from inherited and acquired cardiomyopathies remains one of the most important and consequential tasks in sports cardiology. Misclassification risks both unwarranted disqualification and missed disease. Accurate interpretation of imaging data from competitive athletes, therefore, requires a comprehensive understanding of exercise-induced cardiac remodeling and an ability to distinguish the characteristics of this process from findings suggestive of pathology. Specific to U.S. Soccer, this is even more important as echocardiography plays an integral role in the screening of these professional athletes. However, distinguishing between pathology and physiology is not possible without a clear definition of what constitutes normal cardiac remodeling for a professional soccer player.

In this issue of JACC: Advances, Bavishi et al report echocardiographic findings from 1,368 male Major League Soccer players, which represents the largest echocardiographic characterization of adult male professional soccer players in North America.2 The cohort (mean age: 26 years) was racially diverse with a mix of White (46%), Black (24%), and Hispanic (29%) athletes. The key findings may be summarized as follows. First, left ventricular (LV) cavity dimensions, wall thickness, and mass frequently exceeded general population reference limits. LV internal diameter in diastole (LVIDd) exceeded 58 mm in 8.5% of athletes; however, frank dilation by indexed criteria (LVIDd >3.1 cm/m2) was present in only 2%. Posterior and septal wall thickness exceeded 10 mm in 43% and 31%, respectively, whereas LV hypertrophy was identified in 14% by mass index (>115 g/m2). Importantly, no athlete had a wall thickness ≥13 mm, a threshold that enters the diagnostic “gray zone” where there might be overlap with early hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Second, most athletes demonstrated normal geometry (67.7%), whereas a minority had concentric remodeling (18.2%), eccentric hypertrophy (9.6%), and concentric hypertrophy (4.5%). Third, right ventricular enlargement was common (basal diameter >41 mm in 30%) and correlated with larger LV cavity size, whereas resting systolic function was preserved in nearly all athletes. Finally, <1% of athletes demonstrated aortic root enlargement (aortic root diameter >40 mm).

In keeping with the principle that “form follows function”, professional soccer players typically exhibit a pattern of mild LV cavity enlargement accompanied by mild wall thickening, reflecting the physiological demands of a predominantly endurance- and skill-based sport. However, this pattern of cardiac remodeling is best interpreted in the context of other professional athletic populations. For example, in the National Football League, the frequent cyclical elevations in blood pressure from isometric loads, strength training, and/or high-intensity repetitive short duration sprints on the background of possible subclinical hypertension results in 27% of these athletes demonstrating a maximal wall thickness ≥13 mm.3,4 Of course, body size is also a key determinant of cardiac structure, which explains why 36% of professional athletes in the National Basketball Association demonstrate LVIDd >59 mm and 12% exhibit a maximal wall thickness ≥13 mm that scales with body size.5 With regards to the impact of race/ethnicity on cardiac remodeling, these data in U.S. soccer players again demonstrate that self-reported Black athletes display greater wall thickness than White athletes. Importantly, the present study has a large number of Hispanic athletes, a U.S. sporting population that remains undercharacterized in the literature and the inclusion of Hispanic athletes is an important step toward understanding how cardiac adaptations vary by race/ethnicity. These data also add to our understanding of how age (which is inextricably linked to body size and training exposure) is a further modifier of the athlete’s heart. An echocardiographic study of adolescent soccer players in the United Kingdom (mean age: 16 years) demonstrated only mild cavity dilation with ∼5% of adolescent soccer players having an LVIDd >58 mm.6 We should therefore be cognizant of the limitations of adult-derived reference ranges when extrapolating across the developmental spectrum.

The present study also raises a few pertinent questions. First, it challenges, to a degree, the classical Morganroth dichotomy, that endurance and resistance training cause divergent patterns of remodeling, with the former being a pure “volume” load and the latter exerting a “pressure” hemodynamic stress, similar to hypertension or aortic stenosis.7 In the present study, most athletes exhibited normal LV geometry, whereas concentric remodeling patterns were observed nearly twice as frequently as eccentric hypertrophy, despite the predominantly endurance-based nature of professional soccer. Consistent with prior observations, these findings further support the notion that the geometric response to training is probably more heterogeneous than the Morganroth model predicts.8 Moreover, it’s worth noting that the four-group geometric classification (normal, concentric remodeling, concentric hypertrophy, and eccentric hypertrophy) was derived from hypertensive populations, limiting its applicability to athletes.9 It is possible that applying this framework to healthy cardiac adaptation risks conflating pathology with expected physiology. Without more detailed blood pressure, hemodynamic phenotyping, and longitudinal follow-up, it remains unclear whether soccer players with concentric geometry represent early pathological remodeling or simply reflect the limitations of applying disease-derived discrete geometric classifications to heterogenous physiological stimuli. Next, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes causal inference regarding whether the observed cardiac phenotype reflects the cumulative effects of soccer training or characteristics that predate athletic participation. In addition, because all participants were male, these findings cannot be assumed to apply to female athletes. As professional women's soccer continues to grow globally, dedicated investigations are needed to define the unique structural and functional characteristics of the female athlete's heart and establish sex-specific reference standards for soccer. Finally, the discordance in right ventricular dimensions between this cohort and a prior study of 116 male U.S. national team soccer players may offer an important methodological lesson.10 Sport-specific reference ranges derived from relatively small cohorts can be vulnerable to sampling variability. Reliable reference values require sufficiently large and diverse samples to generate reproducible estimates across cohorts. In this regard, the authors should be commended for assembling one of the largest echocardiographic datasets in professional soccer, providing a valuable foundation for distinguishing physiological remodeling from pathology. Future efforts should build on this model through national and international collaborations capable of generating similarly robust reference standards across sporting disciplines, sex, and racial/ethnic groups.

As we look to the future, we must also recognize the limitations of sport-specific reference ranges. Most sports encompass varying combinations of endurance, resistance, and skill-based training, with substantial heterogeneity not only between sports but also among athletes competing within the same sport. Ultimately, what clinicians need is not simply a sport-specific benchmark, but a detailed characterization of the individual’s training exposure, including the cumulative dose and composition of endurance and resistance exercise over time that produced the cardiac adaptation in question. Wearable technologies may now aid in making such quantification feasible. Coupled with advances in artificial intelligence, it may be possible to integrate rich physiologic wearable data with demographic and anthropometric characteristics such as age, sex, ethnicity, body size, and sport to generate an individualized reference range, against which the observed phenotype can be interpreted. Such an approach may ultimately prove more informative than asking whether a given value is “normal” for a sport, but rather determine whether it is expected for a particular athlete.

Although the future may lie in individualized, data-driven models of exercise-induced cardiac adaptation, clinicians must make decisions using the tools available today. In that regard, the present study represents a major contribution. By defining the echocardiographic phenotype of professional soccer players, Bavishi et al provide a valuable reference for the world's most popular sport. As our understanding of the athlete's heart continues to evolve, studies of this scale remain critical for improving the care we provide for competitive athletes.
