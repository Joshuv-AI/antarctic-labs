Efficacy of Disitamab Vedotin in Treating HER2 2+/FISH‐Bladder Cancer: A Case Report
Authors: Zhi‐ruo Jin, Cheng‐yong Dong, Yu‐jun Li, Yang Zou, Xi‐mei Wang, Hui‐ying Li, Gang Wang, Xiao‐nan Cui, Ying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDisitamab Vedotin achieved durable partial response (> 8 months PFS) in advanced HER2 2+/FISH‐ bladder cancer after first‐line chemo‐immunotherapy progression. This case provides real‐world evidence that disitamab vedotin may benefit HER2 2+/FISH‐ patients, supporting broader HER2 testing and treatment consideration in this subset.Keywords: antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs), bladder cancer, Disitamab Vedotin (RC48), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the ninth most frequently diagnosed cancer worldwide. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), there were 613,791 new cases and 220,349 related deaths globally in 2022 alone, accounting for 3.1% and 2.3% of all studied sites, respectively [1]. The burden and rates are considerably higher in men than in women. The majority of bladder cancers originate from the urothelium, resulting in urothelial carcinoma (UC) [2], which is the most common pathological type of bladder cancer [3], also known as transitional cell carcinoma. Clinically, it often presents initially with painless hematuria [4], and tobacco use is a major risk factor. The incidence of bladder cancer has been steadily rising worldwide [5]. Approximately 80% of bladder cancers are nonmuscle‐invasive, with a 5‐year survival rate of up to 90%. In contrast, muscle‐invasive bladder cancers account for about 20%, but the prognosis for advanced metastatic cancer is poor, with a 5‐year survival rate of only 6% [6]. The sites of distant metastasis vary depending on the molecular subtype of the cancer, with common sites including the bones, lungs, and liver, all of which can be severely affected [7]. Metastatic UC of the bladder lacks optimal treatment strategies based on current research data; chemotherapy is the main treatment, and multimode therapy is recommended [8]. The GP regimen (gemcitabine combined with cisplatin) is the most commonly used chemotherapy protocol. In addition, based on pathway relevance and emerging therapeutic approaches, four targeted therapy strategies have been selected for the treatment of muscle‐invasive urothelial carcinoma of the bladder: signaling pathway inhibitors, cell cycle regulation, heat shock protein inhibitors, and immunotherapy [9].

Here, we report a muscle‐invasive bladder cancer patient with pathologically confirmed human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) (2+) by immunohistochemistry (IHC) in biopsy tissues, and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) test showed no amplification. After the patient showed progression following first‐line standard treatment, we opted for disitamab vedotin as the second‐line therapy. The patient's tumor was effectively controlled; the patients benefited significantly after the switching. To date, the patient remains in a progression‐free state.

Case History/Examination

The patient, a 56‐year‐old male, was admitted to the hospital in 2019, with progressive difficulty urinating for nearly 1 year. He had a history of type 2 diabetes for almost 13 years, and he denied having a family history of cancer. His Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) was 1 at the time of initial diagnosis. Cystoscopy highly suggests the presence of a malignant bladder tumor. For pathological diagnosis, transurethral resection of a bladder tumor (TURBT) was performed on October 14th, 2019 at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. The pathology report indicated invasive UC of the bladder, with infiltration into the lamina propria. Postoperatively, he received regular intravesical instillations of Gemcitabine for chemotherapy, continuing until April 2020. In April 2020, a follow‐up cystoscopy revealed diffuse redness and swelling of the bladder wall. He subsequently underwent another TURBT. The postoperative pathology report confirmed invasive urothelial carcinoma with infiltration into the lamina propria. Following the surgery, he received 4 cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy with Gemcitabine and Nedaplatin, continuing until July 2020. Subsequent routine cystoscopy examinations showed no significant abnormalities. In April 2021, the patient presented to West China Hospital of Sichuan University with gross hematuria. Under general anesthesia, he underwent robot‐assisted radical cystectomy, ileal neobladder reconstruction, bilateral internal iliac artery ligation, adhesiolysis, and appendectomy. During the surgery, we observed that the sigmoid colon in the abdominal cavity was slightly adherent to the abdominal wall. No hematomas or fluid collections were noted. The omentum and intestinal segments showed no enlarged lymph nodes or masses. The abdominal fat was abundant, and the bladder was relatively full. Upon dissecting the bladder, we noted that the walls were slightly rough, but no significant nodular lesions were observed.

The postoperative pathology report revealed urothelial carcinoma with invasion extending to the perivesical fat/soft tissue, along with cancer emboli in the blood vessels. The surgical margins of the left and right ureters and urethra were negative. Lymph node involvement was identified in 2 out of 14 left pelvic lymph nodes and 2 out of 2 perivesical lymph nodes. No cancer metastasis was detected in the 12 right pelvic lymph nodes. The immunohistochemical stainings showed GATA3(+), CK7(+), CK20(+), P53(Partially +), P63(+), CK5/6(−), CD44(−). In addition, HER2 immunohistochemistry (IHC) was 2+, and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) showed no HER2 gene amplification. Mismatch repair protein testing demonstrated MSH6(+), MSH2(+), PMS2(+), and MLH1(±), with PD‐L1 (22C3) CPS < 1, collectively indicating a microsatellite stable (MSS) phenotype. The original pathology reports are provided as Supporting Information. A postoperative PET‐CT scan showed no significant abnormal FDG metabolism throughout the body. The family chose to proceed with follow‐up observation (July 8th, 2021, The First affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University, Figure 1).

Follow‐up PET‐CT in April 2023 (Figure 2A,C) showed newly identified FDG‐avid lymph nodes adjacent to the descending colon, left iliac vessels, and left iliac fossa; multiple FDG‐avid lymph nodes in both inguinal regions, either newly detected or enlarged compared to previous scans. Based on the above pathology and imaging results, the following diagnosis was made according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Staging Manual, 8th edition: Bladder invasive urothelial carcinoma, pT3bN3M0 (IIIB stage), microsatellite stable (MSS) type, with multiple pelvic lymph node metastases.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The First Line of Treatment

Based on the diagnosis and the results of the genetic test, a treatment plan was formulated after consultation with the Multiple Disciplinary Team (MDT).

The patient initially underwent 6 rounds of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy with the specific drugs Gemcitabine, Cisplatin, and Atezolizumab (IVGTT 1200 mg Q3wks) from May to September 2023. After 2 cycles of treatment, the therapeutic response was assessed as partial remission (PR) by PET‐CT evaluation (June 27th, 2023, The First affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University, Figure 2B,D). After 6 cycles of treatment, the response was evaluated as sustained PR. Followed by maintenance therapy with atezolizumab for six cycles, continuing until February 2024. The Progression‐Free‐Survival (PFS) of the first‐line treatment is about 10 months.

The Second Line of Treatment

February 2024, Follow‐up PET‐CT (Figure 3A,C) indicated that lymph nodes and nodules with increased FDG uptake were observed near the descending colon, alongside the iliac vessels, in the left iliac fossa, within the pelvic mesentery, adjacent to the right side of the rectum, and within the right lower anterior abdominal wall and medial lower abdominal wall, as well as in the pelvic floor. Multiple new bone lesions with increased FDG uptake were also noted throughout the body, all suggestive of metastatic tumors. Considering the patient's medical history, these findings are indicative of disease progression.

Pretreatment PET‐CT scans shown in A and C; posttreatment scans in B and D following disitamab vedotin, Denosumab, and TCM, demonstrating partial remission. Notable reduction in FDG uptake in the left iliac fossa.

Supplementary postoperative pathology immunohistochemistry showing that the HER2 expression level was 2+, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) test showed no amplification. Therefore, Disitamab Vedotin (IVGTT 120 mg Q2wks) was administered on March 2024 as the second‐line targeted therapy. Along with the antineoplastic therapy, Denosumab (IH 120 mg Q4wks) for bone metastases, and traditional Chinese medicine were provided. PET‐CT in June 2024 (Figure 3B,D) showed a significant reduction in FDG metabolic activity in the patient's lesion, which was consistently assessed as a PR. No further follow‐up was available after October 2024, as the patient was lost to follow‐up despite repeated attempts.

Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

In this T3bN3M0 urothelial carcinoma patient, Gemcitabine and Cisplatin combined with Atezolizumab as the first‐line therapy achieved a PFS of 10 months. Monotherapy of disitamab vedotin achieved partial response over 8 months, and no significant adverse events were observed during the second‐line therapy. Conclusion

The current case report provides objective clinical evidence for endorsing disitamab vedotin as a promising therapeutic drug for patients with advanced UC or UC metastasis and especially for patients with HER2‐overexpression. In addition, the guiding significance of HER2 detection in UC is becoming increasingly important with the development of precision therapy.

Discussion

Disitamab Vedotin is the first novel antibody drug conjugate (ADC) independently developed in China. ADC is a complex formed by covalently conjugating monoclonal antibodies with small molecule drugs through linkers, which include three structural modules: antibody, linker, and small molecule cytotoxic drug [10]. Using monoclonal antibody as a carrier, small molecule cytotoxic drugs can be efficiently transported to the target tumor cells to play an antitumor role [11]. ADC has both targeting and drug toxicity. Currently, a number of ADC drugs have been approved and marketed for antitumor therapy, among which Enfortumab vedotin (EV), Sacituzumab govitecan‐hziy (SG), and disitamab vedotin are used for uroepithelial carcinoma.

Disitamab Vedotin contains the antihuman epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) extracellular domain antibody conjugated to cytotoxin monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) via a cleavable linker [12]. HER2 is an EGFR family member encoded by the ERBB2 gene and is situated in the chromosomal region 17q11.2‐q12 [13]. It is a transmembrane protein with tyrosine kinase activity, which mediates signal transduction activation and downstream signaling pathways through heterodimer and tyrosine kinase autophosphorylation [14]. Thereby regulating the metabolism and proliferation of normal cells. In tumor tissues, such as gastric cancer, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, nonsmall cell lung cancer, and bladder cancer, the amplification and overexpression of HER2 often exist, which can lead to persistent hyperactive cellular network signaling, resulting in abnormal cell proliferation [15]. HER2‐positive tumors are prone to metastasis, and most have a poor prognosis.

In the present study, we reported on the clinical efficacy of disitamab vedotin in treating a HER2‐positive metastatic bladder cancer patient whose disease progressed after surgery, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy. Disitamab Vedotin has been approved for use in patients with locally advanced or metastatic uroepithelial carcinoma who have received platinum‐containing chemotherapy in the past and have overexpression of HER2 (HER2 immunohistochemical results are 2+ or 3+) in China [16]. In a phase II trial (NCT03809013; C009) [17] of disitamab vedotin in patients with HER2‐overexpression (IHC2+, 3+) who had failed platinum therapy, gemcitabine and taxane therapy, the duration of response (DOR) was 8.3 months, the median Progress Free Survival (PFS) was 4.3 months and the median Overall Survival (mOS) was 14.8 months. Disitamab Vedotin also showed good efficacy in a similarly designed phase II trial (NCT03507166; C005) [18] that enrolled patients with locally advanced or metastatic urothelial carcinoma (HER2 IHC2+, 3+). The DOR was 6.9 months, the median Progress Free Survival (mPFS) was 6.9 months, the mOS was 13.9 months [19]. Based on these experimental results, the 2021 version of the “CSCO Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Urothelial Carcinoma” included disitamab vedotin as a level III recommendation for second‐line and subsequent treatments in advanced urothelial carcinoma (mUC). Furthermore, the 2022 version of the guidelines made additional updates, elevating the recommendation level for disitamab vedotin as a monotherapy in second‐line and later treatments from III to II. It also introduced the combination of disitamab vedotin and immunotherapy as a new level III recommendation for first‐line and third‐line treatments. The recommendation for first‐line treatment is based on the outstanding results of the updated RC‐48‐C014 study [20] presented at the 2022 ASCO GU and ASCO annual meetings. This study demonstrated that the combination of disitamab vedotin and Toripalimab achieved an overall objective response rate (ORR) exceeding 70% in patients with mUC, with a mPFS of 9.2 months. In terms of safety, the incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse events (AEs) was 36%, lower than reported rates for other ADCs internationally. With close monitoring of AEs, this combination therapy has shown significant benefits for mUC patients. As for side effects, the main adverse events of disitamab vedotin vary in accordance with patients' conditions, including: decreased white blood cell count, alopecia, decreased neutrophil count, increased aspartate aminotransferase, fatigue, increased alanine aminotransferase and hypoesthesia [21]. The 2022 edition of the “CSCO Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of UC” also updated the section on “Pathological Diagnosis.” It recommends that all patients with locally advanced or metastatic urothelial carcinoma, as well as those with postoperative pathological diagnoses of muscle‐invasive urothelial carcinoma (≥ pT2), undergo routine HER2 testing. Disitamab Vedotin specifically targets HER2 and has received comprehensive treatment recommendations in the CSCO guidelines. However, it hasn't yet been included in the NCCN guideline recommendations till now.

Recent clinical advances have further solidified the role of disitamab vedotin specifically in bladder cancer. In the phase II RC48G001 study (NCT04879329), disitamab vedotin monotherapy achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 54.9% (complete response 16.9%) in HER2‐positive (IHC 3+ or IHC 2+/ISH+) locally advanced or metastatic urothelial carcinoma, with a median progression‐free survival (PFS) of 5.7 months and median overall survival (OS) of 20.0 months [22]. Notably, in patients with HER2‐low expression (IHC 2+/ISH‐ or IHC 1+), a similar ORR of 52.6% (CR 18.4%) was observed, with median PFS of 5.7 months and median OS of 17.0 months [22]. A multicenter real‐world study further confirmed that disitamab vedotin demonstrated favorable efficacy in HER2‐low and null expression advanced UC, with an ORR of 30.8%, disease control rate of 76.9%, and median PFS of 7.4 months, with manageable safety [23]. More recently, a proof‐of‐concept study demonstrated that disitamab vedotin combined with toripalimab and radiotherapy achieved a complete response in 83.3% of patients with HER2‐positive muscle‐invasive bladder cancer [24], establishing the feasibility of a novel bladder‐preserving regimen. In addition, in BCG‐unresponsive or ineligible high‐risk nonmuscle‐invasive bladder cancer, disitamab vedotin‐based systemic therapy (alone or combined with PD‐1 inhibitors) achieved a complete response rate of 71.9%, with a median event‐free survival of 22.1 months [25]. Preliminary data from a phase II study also showed promising early disease control of disitamab vedotin combined with intravesical mitomycin‐C in HER2‐expressing high‐risk NMIBC, with no grade ≥ 3 toxicities observed [26].

In this case, the patient also received traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) as an adjunctive therapy alongside second‐line disitamab vedotin. TCM was primarily used for symptom management, including relief of fatigue and improvement of general well‐being, rather than as a direct antitumor intervention. The role of TCM in this regimen was likely supportive, and there was no evidence of interaction that compromised the efficacy or safety of disitamab vedotin. Further studies are needed to clarify the potential synergistic or attenuating effects of TCM when combined with antibody‐drug conjugates.

Together with our case, these accumulating data indicate that disitamab vedotin is an effective treatment option not only for HER2‐overexpressing UC but also for patients with HER2‐low expression, offering a potential strategy for those progressing after first‐line chemo‐immunotherapy.
