To the Editor:

Regulation of epithelial innate inflammatory gene expression and metabolic responses to viral infections has emerged as a key molecular mechanism underlying clinical heterogeneity in paediatric asthma as the airway epithelium serves to integrate multiple regulatory pathways in asthma [1]. Epigenetic mechanisms, such as DNA methylation, are altered by acute and chronic environmental exposures, such as viral infections and air pollution, as an important regulator of gene expression within airway epithelia contributing to disease presentations [2–6]. As prior viral infections are hypothesised to contribute to chronic dysfunction in airway epithelia, identifying how acute stimuli can alter the epigenetic landscape is important for understanding how epigenetic patterns in disease relevant tissues and cells are shaped by prior exposures. Work by our group and others indicate that interferon expression is finely balanced, as lower baseline interferon expression prior to viral infection is associated with increased viral load upon infection [7]. The increased viral load subsequently leads to an exaggerated interferon response that correlates with exacerbation risk [7–9]. Fine tuning of gene expression in bronchial epithelial cells (BECs) may be regulated by epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation. Prior studies have identified epigenetic changes within the first 24–48 h during the acute peak of response to rhinovirus (RV) infection in submerged cultures of basal nasal epithelial cells but did not investigate persistent changes or in organotypic BEC cultures [4, 10, 11]. Ex vivo organotypic BEC cultures are powerful models to interrogate the molecular and gene expression responses to stimuli such as viral infections and cytokines [7]. Furthermore, our work has demonstrated that ex vivo epithelial transcriptional responses to respiratory viral infection (e.g. respiratory syncytial virus, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 and RV-16) differ by disease state (asthma versus healthy) and correlate with clinical features in asthma donors (lung function and severe exacerbation history) indicating that ex vivo primary cells likely retain epigenetic signatures in culture [7, 8]. Here, we used organotypic BEC cultures from children with and without asthma to interrogate how DNA methylation at key asthma loci is altered in response to interleukin (IL)-13 exposure or RV infection and correlates with gene expression.

Organotypic BEC cultures were generated from 10 children with asthma and six healthy children from bronchial brushings obtained while donors were undergoing elective surgery, as previously described, under study #1596 approved by the Seattle Children's Hospital Institutional Review Board [12]. Age ranged from 6.4 to 17.6 years in donors with asthma and from 7.9 to 18.1 years in nonatopic, healthy donors; 40% of the donors with asthma and 50% of healthy donors were female. Fraction of exhaled nitric oxide was similar between the two groups (mean±sd 10.9±4.3 versus 10.5±3.6 ppb). Forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) was similar (97.4±11.3% versus 101.3±21.5% pred) while FEV1/forced vital capacity ratio was lower in asthma donors (0.81±0.05 versus 0.88±0.03). Among the donors with asthma, 30% (n=3) were positive for three or more aeroallergens on allergen-specific IgE testing, 70% (n=7) were atopic by history or allergen testing and 40% (n=4) had a history of severe exacerbation defined as needing systemic corticosteroids for ≥3 days and/or a hospitalisation/emergency room visit for asthma requiring systemic corticosteroids [13]. After differentiation to an organotypic, pseudostratified state, BEC cultures were infected with RV-16 at a multiplicity of infection of 0.5 or stimulated with IL-13 at 10 ng·mL−1 added to the medium every 48 h. RNA and DNA were harvested 10 days after RV-16 infection and 7 days after IL-13 treatment [12]. Collected RNA was analysed via RNA sequencing for gene expression with quality control as previously described [7, 12], and DNA methylation was quantified in DNA using the Asthma and Allergy array [14]. After quality control, 44 900 CpG sites were included for analysis. Methylated and unmethylated channel intensities were quantile-normalised separately and implemented in the ENMix R package, and methylation (M) was calculated as: M=log2(methylated+a)unmethylated+awith an offset value (α) of 100 [15]. Differential methylation was estimated using linear mixed effects contrasting RV-16 infection or IL-13 stimulation to the uninfected/unstimulated control (condition), with covariates accounting for age, sex and DNA concentration as well nested random effects for donor and array using kimma. Differential gene expression was estimated using analogous linear mixed effects and covariates. Multiple hypothesis testing correction was performed within each analysis to adjust p-values using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate. We calculated the Pearson correlation of expression×methylation. Full data tables and methods are available from to DataDryad at https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.3ffbg79zc.

Analysis of DNA methylation revealed that IL-13 stimulation modifies DNA methylation at 146 CpG sites annotated to 115 unique genes, with a greater number of loci having decreased methylation with IL-13 stimulation (figure 1a). Genes with differential methylation after IL-13 that correlated with altered expression were SERPINB2, EFNB2, CLEC16A and CISH (figure 1b). RV-16 infection altered DNA methylation at 202 CpG sites related to 159 unique genes (figure 1c). Genes identified with differential methylation following RV-16 infection that correlated with expression included RHOB, AK2, ITPRIP and SORL1 (figure 1d).

Here, we present how two stimuli linked to asthma pathogenesis (acute infection with RV-16 and chronic stimulation by IL-13) alter DNA methylation in BECs. We found that DNA methylation exhibited significant individual donor variation, indicating that the epigenetic landscape is maintained in air–liquid interface (ALI) culture, as has been previously shown for submersion cultures [5]. As expression of genes within the epithelium correlate with altered responses to stimuli, such as RV, our study provides initial insights into stimuli that can alter the epigenetic landscape and lead to interindividual differences in expression of genes regulating innate immune and inflammatory responses [9]. The changes we observe reflect chronic epigenetic reprogramming as we assessed methylation changes after 7 days of IL-13 stimulation and 10 days after RV-16 infection. Future studies are necessary to assess the kinetics of these changes and how acutely methylation changes can be observed, as a prior study found an IL-13 induced response within 24 h of stimulation [5]. Our study used organotypic BECs differentiated at an ALI, which is a powerful tool for mechanistic studies, to pair with prior studies correlating methylation in cultured BECs with parallel in vivo measurements of DNA methylation [5]. SERPINB2, CISH and CLEC16A are biomarkers for Th2-high, allergic asthma and have been implicated in modulating airway epithelial responses to allergen exposures. Our finding that IL-13 alters DNA methylation near these genes indicates a possible mechanism for how allergic stimulation over time may reprogramme airway epithelial function. Similarly, RHOB, ITPRIP and SORL1 have been linked to intracellular signalling and trafficking, which may impact both intracellular viral trafficking and viral responses. Altered methylation in the airway epithelia may contribute to ongoing remodelling and responses that persist past the acute phase of infection. Given that the CpG sites on the custom array used were selected in regions associated with asthma and allergic diseases, these specific findings provide intriguing hypotheses for how the epigenetic changes associated with asthma may arise – either through allergic stimulation via IL-13 or as a result of prior infections with viruses such as RV-16, in addition to genetic contributions [2]. We found more hypomethylated CpGs after IL-13 stimulation, consistent with results of an earlier study [5]. In contrast, RV-16 infection induced a similar number of changes that were hyper- and hypomethylated. These differences may reflect more widespread activation with IL-13 stimulation, whereas RV-16 infection results in both upregulation of antiviral genes and downregulation of cellular metabolic pathways [7].

Our study has potential limitations: while we did include BECs from children with and without asthma, our sample size was too small to correlate methylation changes with asthma diagnosis, comorbid atopy, lung function or history of severe exacerbation. Including atopic status or diagnosis of asthma as a covariate in the model did not change the findings presented here. Given the interindividual variability across donors, identification of differential effects of RV-16 or IL-13 in those with or without asthma would require a larger cohort to have sufficient power to associate group-level clinical characteristics with methylation changes and further elucidate which changes may be more specific in the development of asthma. Finally, our measurements used a custom DNA methylation array that is enriched for functional CpG sites in regions of the genome associated with asthma and allergic diseases but does not comprehensively cover the genome.
