Synergistic nanosenolytic therapy reverses age-related dry eye disease by targeting cellular senescence and oxidative stress
Authors: Qianyi Ren, Xingyu Zhu, Yujiao Zhai, Guangyu Rong, Xujiao Zhou, Jia Lv, Jiaxu Hong, Yiyun Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dry eye disease (DED) is a prevalent, age-related ocular disorder that severely impairs vision and quality of life. Age-related cellular senescence drives disease progression through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which establishes a self-perpetuating cycle of oxidative stress and chronic inflammation. However, current therapeutic options are insufficient to directly disrupt this pathogenic feedback loop. To address this, we developed a dynamically responsive polypeptide-based nanosenolytic eye drop co-encapsulating quercetin and superoxide dismutase (SOD). Quercetin serves a dual function as both an anti-aging agent and a hydrophobic motif driving nanocarrier self-assembly, enabling efficient SOD loading and pH-responsive endosomal escape. This nanoplatform demonstrated superior corneal permeability compared to free SOD, leading to suppressed oxidative stress and apoptosis, restored lacrimal gland function, and reversed key DED manifestations. Furthermore, by effectively attenuating SASP activity in aged DED models, the treatment showed profound disease-modifying efficacy. Collectively, this work establishes local nanosenolysis as a potent therapeutic strategy for multifactorial DED by simultaneously targeting oxidative damage and cellular senescence.

Introduction

DED is a prevalent ocular surface disorder, characterized by a loss of tear film homeostasis and accompanied by a range of ocular symptoms [1]. Its pathogenesis involves multiple factors, including tear film instability and hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation and damage, and neurosensory abnormalities [2]. As the global population ages, DED has become a significant public health concern. This concern is amplified by growing evidence underscoring a strong link between DED and the aging process, where age-related physiological and pathological changes in the cornea and lacrimal gland are pivotal to the onset and progression of the disease [3,4].

Cellular senescence, a hallmark of aging, is characterized by irreversible growth arrest, resistance to apoptosis, and the development of a distinctive secretory profile known as the SASP [5,6]. The SASP encompasses a broad array of secreted factors, including cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases, that disrupt tissue microenvironments and promote pathological processes through paracrine signaling [7,8]. Aging is also accompanied by a chronic, low-grade systemic inflammatory state, driven largely by dysregulated immune responses and the accumulation of senescent cells [9]. Within this context, oxidative stress serves as an upstream trigger, both inducing cellular senescence and directly activating inflammatory pathways, thereby fueling SASP development. Through these mechanisms, the SASP amplifies local inflammation and propagates senescence to neighboring cells, expanding the burden of senescent tissue and perpetuating a cycle of chronic dysfunction [[10], [11], [12]]. This vicious cycle is particularly relevant to the ocular surface, where accumulating senescent cells and sustained SASP signaling exacerbate local inflammation, disrupt tear film stability, impair lacrimal gland function, and contribute centrally to the pathology of DED [[13], [14], [15]]. The resulting tear film instability further promotes evaporation and hyperosmolarity, aggravating ocular surface damage and closing the feedback loop that drives DED progression [16]. Therefore, we hypothesize that a therapeutic strategy capable of simultaneously clearing senescent cells and quenching oxidative stress could effectively break this cycle and modify the disease course of age-related DED.

Endogenous antioxidants such as SOD, which catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide radicals, a key upstream event in the induction of cellular senescence and activation of inflammatory pathways, show promise as potential treatments for DED [17,18]. However, their clinical translation is hindered by poor membrane permeability and rapid clearance from the ocular surface. Enhancing the corneal penetration and intracellular delivery of such enzymes is therefore critical to achieving therapeutic efficacy [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. To address these critical ocular physiological barriers, various nanotechnology-based strategies have been developed to prolong precorneal residence time and promote transcorneal transport [23,24]. Polymeric nanocarriers have emerged as promising platforms for ocular and intraocular protein delivery, leveraging tunable architectures, efficient membrane penetration, and endosomal escape capabilities [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. In addition to overcoming ocular surface barriers, maintaining the inherent structural stability and biological activity of proteins during nanocarrier loading presents another distinct technical challenge. To improve the stability of polymer/protein complexes against ionic competition under physiological conditions while maintaining the bioactivity of cargo proteins, functional groups are often introduced onto the surfaces of cationic polymers to facilitate multiple non-covalent interactions with proteins, thereby forming stable complexes [[29], [30], [31]]. Additionally, amphiphilic polymers have been developed to form stable delivery complexes via supramolecular assembly [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Natural polyphenols, which can interact with proteins through hydrogen bonding and cation–π interactions, represent a class of promising functional groups for enhancing protein binding [36,37]. Moreover, natural polyphenols possess notable antioxidant and anti-senescence activities, showing broad application potential in oxidative stress-, aging-, and inflammation-related diseases [38,39]. Nonetheless, their clinical application is limited by extremely poor aqueous solubility and low bioavailability, primarily due to nonspecific interactions with various proteins [40,41].

In this study, we designed a dynamically responsive, amphiphilic polypeptide by covalently conjugating the natural polyphenol quercetin (QR) to a polylysine backbone via pH- and ROS-cleavable bonds, effectively addressing its solubility limitations while introducing multi-responsive release capabilities. Crucially, QR not only functions as a therapeutic agent with intrinsic antioxidant and anti-senescence properties but also serves as a hydrophobic moiety that promotes polymer self-assembly, enhances protein loading capacity via synergistic interactions, and facilitates endosomal escape through protonation in acidic compartments. The resulting amphiphilic polymer enabled stable encapsulation of SOD via synergistic ionic and hydrophobic interactions, which facilitated efficient corneal penetration and intracellular delivery. The dual ROS- and pH-responsive characteristics of the polymer ensured controlled intracellular release of both SOD and QR, maximizing their synergistic bioactivity. We systematically evaluated the eye drops formed by our QR-modified polymer and SOD in terms of corneal penetration, antioxidant and anti-senescence activity, and therapeutic efficacy in both benzalkonium chloride-induced and age-related mouse models of DED. Our results demonstrate that this synergistic nanoformulation effectively disrupts the senescence-oxidative stress cycle, representing a potent and novel therapeutic approach for DED (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the synergistic nanosenolytic strategy for age-related DED by targeting cellular senescence and oxidative stress.

Synthesis and characterization of polymers and conjugates

4-nitrophenyl 4-(4,4,5,5-tetramethyl-1,3,2-dioxaborolan-2-yl)benzyl carbonate (NBC) was synthesized according to a previously reported method [43]. 1H NMR analysis (Bruker, Switzerland) in DMSO-d6 confirmed the successful synthesis and structure of NBC. NBC-PLL (denoted as NP) was synthesized according to a previously reported method [43]. Briefly, PLL (50 mg), NBC (0.20 equiv. relative to PLL lysine residues), and triethylamine (TEA, 3 equiv. relative to NBC) were dissolved in 3 mL anhydrous DMF. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 h. The product was then dialyzed sequentially against DMF and ultrapure water, during which the pinacol boronate ester was deprotected and removed. The resulting solution was freeze-dried to obtain the final polymer. The grafting density of phenylboronic acid (PBA) groups was quantified using 1H NMR.

For fluorescence tracking, NP-RBITC was synthesized by reacting equimolar amounts of NP and RBITC for 24 h, followed by dialysis and lyophilization. Fluorescently labeled proteins were similarly prepared by incubating proteins with FITC (3:1 molar ratio) or Cy5.5 (1:1 molar ratio) in PBS for 24 h. The resulting conjugates were purified by sequential dialysis against 10% DMSO/PBS and pure PBS to remove unconjugated dyes.

NP-polyphenol conjugates were prepared using a thin-film hydration method. Briefly, polyphenols (QR, hispidin, CAPE, mangiferin, purpurin, baicalin, or baicalein) were dissolved in ethanol and dried under an argon stream to form a uniform thin film. These films were then rehydrated with an aqueous NP solution. To optimize the formulation for QR, various QR-to-PBA molar ratios (0.25:1, 0.5:1, 0.75:1, and 1:1) were evaluated. The conjugate prepared at a 1:1 QR-to-PBA molar ratio was termed NPQ. For all other natural polyphenols, a fixed 1:1 molar ratio relative to PBA groups was employed. The successful complexation and chemical structures of NP, QR, and NPQ were confirmed using 1H NMR and UV-Vis spectroscopy (Agilent Technologies, USA). Furthermore, the conjugation ratio of QR to the polymer NP was quantitatively evaluated based on the polymer characteristics and 1H NMR analysis. The degree of polymerization of the PLL backbone was determined to be 22, and the grafting ratio of PBA groups in NP was 15%. Accordingly, the average number of PBA functional groups per NP polymer chain was calculated to be 3.3 (22 × 15%). According to the 1H NMR spectrum of the NPQ conjugate, the integration ratio of the isolated aromatic proton peak of QR (peak e, ∼6.7 ppm) to the aromatic protons from the PBA group on the polymer backbone (peaks a and a', ∼7.5 ppm) indicated a nearly quantitative complexation between the QR and PBA moieties. Consequently, the average number of QR molecules successfully conjugated to each NP polymer chain was determined to be 3.3.

Preparation and characterization of polymer/protein complexes

To prepare the polymer/protein complexes, the obtained NP and NPQ were mixed with BSA in deionized water with 30-min incubation. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the resulting complexes were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern, UK), while their morphology was visualized via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi, Japan). Fluorescence properties were acquired using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan).

To determine the protein loading efficiency, NP/BSA and NPQ/BSA complexes were incubated in H2O and PBS, respectively, and then centrifuged at 15000 rpm for 30 min. The precipitates were freeze-dried, resuspended in 1× protein loading buffer (Epizyme, China), and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. Protein loading was quantified via SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoresis, gels were stained with Coomassie Blue Fast Staining Solution and imaged using a BG-gdsAUTO gel documentation system (Baygene, China).

To analyze the stimulus-responsive release profile, the NPQ/BSA-FITC complex was incubated in either 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 5 mM H2O2 or 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 5.0) without H2O2. The release behavior was monitored using agarose gel electrophoresis at a constant voltage for 30 min, followed by imaging with the BG-gdsAUTO gel imaging system.

In vitro cellular assays and functional evaluations

For cell culture, human osteosarcoma cells (143B), HeLa cells stably expressing galectin-8 and yellow fluorescent protein fusion protein (HeLa-Gal8-YFP), human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) and mouse embryo fibroblast (NIH 3T3) cells were cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) PS.

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of the materials, various cells were seeded in 96-well plates and grown overnight to approximately 60% confluence. Cells were then treated with polymer or polymer/protein complexes in DMEM for 6 h at concentrations consistent with the delivery assays. The medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh DMEM containing 10% FBS, and the cells were incubated for another 20 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

To assess intracellular delivery, cells (∼90% confluence) were treated for 6 h with polymer/protein complexes, which were pre-assembled by incubating the polymers and cargo proteins in MEM for 30 min at room temperature. The final working concentrations used in the delivery experiments were as follows: NP (8 μg/mL), QR (2.344 μg/mL), BSA-FITC (32 μg/mL), EGFP (20 μg/mL), α-chymotrypsin-FITC (α-Chyt-FITC, 8 μg/mL), catalase-FITC (CAT-FITC, 32 μg/mL), horseradish peroxidase-FITC (HRP-FITC, 20 μg/mL), lysozyme-FITC (20 μg/mL), and ovalbumin-FITC (OVA-FITC, 20 μg/mL). To analyze the delivery of fluorescent proteins (e.g., BSA-FITC), extracellular fluorescence was quenched with 0.02% trypan blue in PBS. The cells were then imaged via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Leica SP5, Germany) and quantitative analysis via flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa, USA).

To investigate the endosomal escape capability, HeLa-Gal8-YFP cells were treated with NP/BSA or NPQ/BSA for 2, 4, and 6 h, followed by nuclear staining with Hoechst 33342. Cells were imaged by CLSM, and the number and area of Gal8-YFP-positive puncta (yellow clusters indicating endosomal rupture) were quantified using ImageJ software across three independent replicates.

To evaluate the functional activity of delivered SOD, treated cells were washed and then exposed to 300 μg/mL ROSUP (a standardized positive control reagent provided in the commercial ROS assay kit, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 1 h to rapidly elevate intracellular ROS levels. Cells were then stained with 10 μM DCFH-DA for 10 min and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry.

To assess in vitro anti-senescence activity, cellular senescence was first induced in HCECs using 20 mg/mL D-Gal [44] for 48 h. The senescent cells were then treated with either DMEM (control) or NPQ/SOD (2.586 μg/mL NPQ, 5 μg/mL SOD) for 4 h. Afterwards, the treatment medium was replaced with complete growth medium for a further 20 h. Cellular senescence was evaluated using an In Situ β-Galactosidase Staining Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions, with senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) positive cells (blue) imaged via optical microscopy (Nikon, Japan). Finally, to quantify senescence-related gene expression, total RNA was extracted, evaluated for quality (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and reverse-transcribed (1000 ng) into cDNA. Reverse Transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using a SYBR Green qPCR Kit on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA), utilizing the primers listed in Table S1.

To evaluate the pro-proliferative effect of the NPQ/SOD nanoformulation in vitro, immunofluorescence staining for Ki67 was performed. Briefly, HCECs were seeded in confocal dishes and allowed to attach overnight. To construct the in vitro cellular senescence model, HCECs were incubated with D-Gal (20 mg/mL) for 48 h. Following senescence induction, the cells were treated with the NPQ/SOD nanoformulation (2.586 μg/mL NPQ, 5 μg/mL SOD) for another 24 h. Simultaneously, the Control and D-Gal groups were incubated with fresh standard medium for 24 h.

After the respective treatments, the cells were gently washed with cold PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with a primary antibody against Ki67 at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS three times, the cells were incubated with a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (red emission) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 for 10 min. Finally, the cells were imaged using a CLSM. The average fluorescence intensity of Ki67 per cell was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Free radical scavenging assays

To evaluate the DPPH radical scavenging capacity, a 0.1 mM DPPH working solution was prepared in ethanol. Subsequently, 200 μL of this solution was mixed with 2 μL of the test compounds: QR (1.94 mM), NP (0.39 mM), or NPQ (0.39 mM). The absorbance at 517 nm was measured in real time with a microplate reader for 30 min, and the scavenging activity was calculated accordingly. The resulting reaction mixture was documented by photography.

To evaluate the ABTS+ radical scavenging capacity, the ABTS+ cation was first generated by reacting ABTS (54.04 mg) with potassium persulfate (9.93 mg) in 15 mL of deionized water for 12 h in the dark. The resulting stock solution was diluted 30 times with deionized water. Similarly, 200 μL of the ABTS+ working solution was mixed with 2 μL of each compound at the concentrations stated above. The absorbance at 734 nm was measured in real time with a microplate reader for 30 min, and the scavenging activity was calculated accordingly. The resulting reaction mixture was documented by photography.

Animals and DED modeling

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 6-8 weeks and male BALB/c mice (6-8 weeks old and 1 year old) were obtained from Sibeifu Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Mice were housed under standard SPF conditions at 25 °C and 60% humidity, with a 12/12-h light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum.

To establish the DED model, 5 μL of 0.2% benzalkonium chloride (BAC) was administered topically to the eyes of the C57BL/6J mice twice daily for 14 days. The BAC-induced mice and old BALB/c mice were allocated into groups (n = 6 each). Each group received 5 μL of one of the following treatments topically three times a day (11:00 a.m., 4:00 p.m., and 9:00 p.m.) for 14 days: PBS, HALO-DUAL, SOD(5 mg/mL), NPQ ( 8 mg/mL NP equivalent), or NPQ/SOD (a combination of NPQ and SOD).

Evaluation of the ocular surface

To assess corneal epithelial integrity, corneal fluorescein sodium staining was performed. Briefly, 1 μL of 0.5% fluorescein sodium solution was applied to the ocular surface. The cornea was then examined under blue light from a slit-lamp microscope. The corneal surface was divided into four quadrants, and each quadrant was graded on a 0–4 scale. A grade of 0 indicated no detectable fluorescein staining. Grade 1 reflected minimal punctate staining involving fewer than 30 spots. Grade 2 corresponded to moderate punctate staining with more than 30 discrete spots but without diffuse involvement. Grade 3 denoted marked staining in the absence of confluent or coalescent areas. Grade 4 represented the presence of patchy or coalescent staining. The quadrant scores were then summed to generate a total corneal fluorescein staining score ranging from 0 to 16.

To quantify tear production, the phenol red thread test was utilized. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% Avertin. Tear secretion was measured using cotton threads (Jingming New Technology Development Co., China) following the manufacturer's instructions. A piece of phenol red cotton thread was inserted into the conjunctival sac at the outer one-third of the mouse eye. After a 30 s placement, the thread was withdrawn, and the extent of the red-dyed segment was quantified as a measure of tear production.

Immunofluorescence staining

After anesthetizing and euthanizing the mice, the eyeballs were enucleated and fixed in 4% PFA solution for 12 h. The eyes were then dehydrated in a 20% sucrose solutions for 16 h each. Following dehydration, the eyes were embedded in OCT compound for cryosectioning. Corneal sections from the apex were collected and washed three times with PBS. The sections were then immersed in 4% PFA for 20 min for fixation, followed by permeabilization in 0.1% Triton X-100 prepared in PBS at 37 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, sections were blocked with 3% BSA at room temperature for 1 h.

For the TUNEL assay, sections was conducted at 37 °C for 1 h. Next, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: CK7 (1:50) and CK3 (1:100). On the following day, after three washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor™488-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000) for 1 h. For Ki67 staining, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti‑Ki67 (1:200), followed by Alexa Fluor™488‑conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000) for 1 h. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI-containing immunofluorescence mounting medium. Sections were washed three times with PBS between each step. Images were captured using an SP8 confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) at 40x magnification.

Evaluation of ocular oxidative stress

To comprehensively evaluate ocular oxidative stress, both biochemical quantification and in situ fluorescence imaging were conducted. Mice were anesthetized and euthanized, after which the eyeballs were enucleated. Corneal tissues were carefully excised along the corneoscleral margin for subsequent malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione reductase (GR) assays. The GR activity was measured using a standard kit (Glutathione Reductase Assay Kit with DTNB) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The absorbance of the final solution was detected at 412 nm using a microplate reader. The MDA level was determined with a standard kit (Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit) following the manufacturer's protocol. The total MDA content was calculated by measuring the absorbance at 532 nm.

Furthermore, to detect in situ ROS production, frozen corneal sections were analyzed utilizing a ROS Fluorescence Detection Kit. Sections were incubated with DHE at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min, followed by imaging under a fluorescence microscope for analysis.

Evaluation of anti-senescence activity

To evaluate the in vivo anti-senescence efficacy of the treatments, both senescence-associated enzyme activity and gene expression profiles were analyzed. To assess SA-β-Gal activity, corneas were carefully excised, shaped into a four-leaf clover configuration, and processed using a commercial Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. The tissues were fixed in β-galactosidase fixative solution at room temperature for 15 min, followed by three washes in PBS (5 min each) to remove residual fixative. The staining solution was then applied, and the corneas were incubated at 37 °C overnight in the dark. Bright-field images of the stained tissues were captured using a standard optical microscope for subsequent quantitative analysis.

Furthermore, to quantify the expression of senescence-related genes, total RNA was isolated from the corneal samples using a commercial extraction kit, with its concentration and purity verified via a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Subsequently, 1000 ng of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. RT-qPCR was then executed using SYBR Green chemistry on a Bio-Rad real-time PCR platform, utilizing the primer sequences detailed in Table S2.

Histological evaluation

To evaluate ocular morphology and specific histological changes, enucleated eyes were fixed in 4% PFA, dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, and embedded in paraffin. The tissue sections were subsequently deparaffinized and rehydrated prior to staining. To evaluate corneal structure, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following standard protocols. To evaluate goblet cell distribution, Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was performed using a commercial PAS Staining Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions; briefly, the sections were oxidized, stained with Schiff's reagent, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Following their respective staining procedures, all slides were dehydrated, mounted, and examined under a light microscope to capture representative images for analysis.

Transcriptomic analysis

Corneal transcriptomes from three experimental groups were analyzed. The OLD group consisted of two biological replicates (originally three, with one sample excluded prior to downstream analysis due to a highly anomalous outlier profile), whereas the NPQ and NPQ/SOD groups each included three replicates, with each replicate derived from pooled total RNA of six mouse corneas. Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol™ reagent. Polyadenylated mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, purified, and fragmented. Sequencing libraries were constructed from high-quality RNA, amplified by PCR, purified, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system. Gene expression levels were estimated in FPKM.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (v3.2.0) based on raw read counts. Log2 fold changes and normalized expression values (baseMean) were computed using default parameters. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change > 1 and padj < 0.05 were classified as DEGs.

Functional enrichment of DEGs was carried out via GO analysis. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)-based Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was additionally performed to determine significantly enriched pathways without applying a fixed significance threshold.

Data analysis

Group differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test (GraphPad Prism 10.0), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Significance is indicated as: ∗ (p < 0.05), ∗∗ (p < 0.01), ∗∗∗ (p < 0.001); ns (not significant).

Results and discussion

Design of a dual-responsive polypeptide for efficient cytosolic protein delivery

PLL was reacted with NBC [43] to yield a boronic acid-functionalized polymer (NP) with a grafting ratio of 15% (Fig. S1 and S2). The resulting NP was subsequently conjugated with QR at varying molar ratios (25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%) via boronate-catechol ester bond formation, yielding a series of amphiphilic polymers. We initially evaluated the intracellular protein delivery efficiency of these polymers in 143B cells using FITC-labeled BSA (BSA-FITC) as a model protein cargo. As shown in Fig. 2a and b, delivery efficiency increased with higher QR grafting ratios, with the 100% QR formulation exhibiting the highest performance. This optimal polymer, designated NPQ, was therefore selected for all further studies. The successful conjugation of QR to the polymer backbone was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy, which showed a 1:1 molar ratio between QR and PBA based on the integral of their respective phenyl ring protons (Fig. S3). Mechanism of NPQ for cytosolic protein delivery

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the superior protein delivery efficiency of NPQ over NP, a comparative analysis was conducted across various stages of the intracellular protein delivery process. DLS and TEM results showed that both NP and NPQ could bind to BSA, forming spherical nanoparticles with positive surface charges (Fig. 2c and Fig. S5). Specifically, NP formed complexes with BSA approximately 476 nm in size. Incorporating QR into NPQ resulted in more uniform complexes and reduced the nanoparticle size (337 nm). This size reduction is primarily attributed to the additional non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions, introduced by the conjugated QR. These additional interactions promote tighter binding and assembly both among the NPQ polymer chains themselves and between the NPQ polymer and the cargo proteins, thereby yielding more compact nanoparticles with a reduced size. Fluorescence spectroscopy further corroborated this observation, revealing significantly stronger quenching of BSA-FITC fluorescence in the NPQ formulation compared to NP (Fig. S6), which provides additional evidence for the formation of more compact polymer-protein complexes. To investigate the protein encapsulation efficiency of the polymers, we separated the complexes from free proteins using ultracentrifugation and subsequently analyzed the protein content in the complexes via SDS-PAGE. As shown in Fig. S7, NP and NPQ encapsulated approximately 59% and 57% of the cargo protein in aqueous solutions, respectively. However, in PBS buffer, one-third of the protein cargo dissociated from NP. In contrast, NPQ retained all encapsulated cargo proteins in PBS, indicating that the incorporation of QR into NP effectively enhanced the nanoparticle stability.

We further investigated the cellular uptake efficiencies of the polymers. NP was labeled with Rhodamine B isothiocyanate (RBITC) for visualization and subsequently conjugated with QR to obtain NPQ-RBITC. The resulting polymers were then mixed with BSA-FITC to formulate the delivery complexes. The internalization efficiencies of the nanoparticles were compared in 143B cells at various time points. As shown in Fig. S8a–S8c, the cellular fluorescence intensity of NP-RBITC was notably stronger than that of NPQ-RBITC across 2 to 6 h. However, the fluorescence intensity of the cargo proteins delivered by NP-RBITC was considerably lower than that of NPQ-RBITC. Considering that FITC fluorescence is quenched in acidic environments such as endosomes, we sought to determine whether the lower fluorescence intensity of NP-RBITC-delivered cargo proteins was due to low uptake or ineffective endosomal escape. To this end, we collected the culture medium from cells treated with the complexes. Heparin sodium was used as a competitor to displace the bound protein from the polymer, allowing us to quantify the non-internalized proteins. In vitro experiments demonstrated that 2 mg/mL of heparin sodium could effectively displace the protein from both NP and NPQ, restoring protein fluorescence intensity to the same level (Fig. S6). As shown in Fig. 2d, direct quantification of cellular uptake revealed that NPQ-RBITC/BSA-FITC nanoparticles were internalized more efficiently than NP-RBITC/BSA-FITC nanoparticles. To further investigate this, we conducted a comparative analysis of the endosomal disruption capability of NPQ/BSA and NP/BSA nanoparticles using HeLa cells stably expressing Galectin 8 (Gal8) fused with yellow fluorescent protein (YFP). Gal8, which is normally distributed in the cytosol, rapidly localizes to damaged endosomes due to its affinity for galactosides on the inner leaflet of the endosomal membrane. This localization results in the formation of distinct fluorescent spots, indicating endosomal disruption. As shown in Fig. 2e. f, and S9, cells treated with NPQ/BSA nanoparticles displayed a higher number and larger area of yellow fluorescent aggregates compared to those with NP/BSA. This observation confirms that NPQ/BSA-treated cells experienced more extensive endosomal membrane disruption. The peak number of yellow fluorescent spots in NPQ/BSA-treated cells was observed at 2 h, suggesting rapid endosomal escape of the nanoparticles. Previous studies have established that the 7-OH and 4′-OH groups of QR exhibit pKa values below 7.4, enabling protonation under endosomal pH conditions. The protonation enhances the hydrophobicity of polyphenols, thereby increasing their endosomal membrane-disrupting capacity and promoting more efficient vesicle escape[[45], [46], [47]]. To investigate the acidification-dependence of the complexes' endosomal escape, we employed bafilomycin A1, a specific V-ATPase inhibitor. Bafilomycin A1 pretreatment significantly decreased Gal8-YFP puncta formation in NPQ/BSA-treated cells, confirming that V-ATPase-driven H+ influx is essential for the endosomal escape of NPQ complexes (Fig. 2g).

We next investigated the intracellular protein release behavior of the NPQ carrier. Confocal microscopy imaging revealed minimal colocalization between the NPQ-RBITC and BSA-FITC in treated cells (Fig. 2h and S8a), suggesting efficient cytosolic release of the proteins. This responsive release is attributed to the dual-stimuli-sensitive design of NPQ. QR is conjugated to the polymer via a boronic ester bond. The acid-cleavable nature of this bond was confirmed by UV spectroscopy, which showed the pH-dependent dissociation of QR from NPQ at pH 5.0 (Fig. S4). Furthermore, the PBA moiety is attached using an ROS-cleavable linker, facilitating release in the elevated ROS environment of the cytosol. To quantitatively assess this release profile, we incubated NPQ/BSA complexes under different conditions, including physiological pH (7.4), acidic pH (5.0), or in the presence of H2O2, and analyzed the samples by agarose gel electrophoresis. As shown in Fig. 2i and j, NPQ retained approximately 80% of the cargo protein at pH 7.4, whereas near-complete release was triggered upon acidification or H2O2 addition. This result directly demonstrates the pH- and ROS-dual-responsive protein release capability of NPQ, which is crucial for enabling the subsequent biological activity of the delivered protein.

Polyphenol modification as a robust strategy for enhancing protein delivery

To investigate whether the enhancement of protein delivery efficiency by boronic acid-functionalized polymers is specific to QR or broadly applicable to hydrophobic polyphenols with catechol structures, we conjugated various natural polyphenols, including hispidin, caffeic acid phenethyl ester, mangiferin, purpurin, baicalin, and baicalein, to the NP polymer at a 1:1 molar ratio of polyphenol to PBA. The resulting polymers were designated as NPH, NPC, NPM, NPP, NPBL, and NPBE, respectively (Fig. 3a). As shown in Fig. 3b and c, all polyphenol modifications significantly improved the intracellular delivery efficiency of NP. Furthermore, similar to QR, these polyphenols enhanced the endosomal membrane disruption capability of NP (Fig. 3d), leading to uniform cytoplasmic distribution of the cargo protein. In addition to BSA, NPQ efficiently delivered a range of proteins with diverse molecular weights and isoelectric points, including EGFP, α-Chyt, CAT, HRP, lysozyme, and OVA, into the cytosol (Fig. S10). Formulation of an NPQ/SOD eye drop with anti-senescence and antioxidant activities

Given the critical roles of oxidative stress and cellular senescence in the pathogenesis of DED, we developed a nanosenolytic eye drop by complexing NPQ with SOD, a protein with broad antioxidant and anti-senescence activity, and evaluated its capacity for intracellular SOD delivery along with anti-senescence and antioxidant efficacy. SOD was first labeled with Cy5.5 to yield SOD-Cy5.5, which was incubated with NPQ to form the eye drop formulation. The assembled NPQ/SOD nanoparticles exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 323 nm and a remarkably low polydispersity index (PDI = 0.055) as determined by DLS, confirming a highly monodisperse size distribution. TEM further corroborated their well-dispersed, spherical morphologies (Fig. S11). We then assessed its corneal SOD delivery capability. As shown in Fig. 4a, minimal fluorescence was detected in corneal sections from mice treated with SOD-Cy5.5 alone, whereas strong red fluorescence throughout the corneal layers was observed after treatment with NPQ/SOD-Cy5.5 complexes, demonstrating significantly enhanced penetration and accumulation of the cargo proteins. Pretreatment of mice with benzalkonium chloride (BAC), a common dry eye inducer, further increased corneal retention of the complexes. We further evaluated the anti-senescence and ROS-suppressing effects of the NPQ/SOD eye drop in vitro. A cellular senescence model was established in HCECs using D-galactose (D-Gal) pretreatment. SA-β-Gal staining confirmed successful senescence induction (Fig. 4e), and qPCR analysis revealed upregulated expression of the classic cellular senescence marker p21, as well as multiple downstream SASP factors including IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-18 (Fig. 4f). Treatment with NPQ/SOD significantly reduced both SA-β-Gal activity and SASP expression in senescent HCECs, demonstrating its potent anti-senescence activity. To further validate these anti-senescence effects at the functional level, we evaluated cellular proliferative capacity using Ki67 immunofluorescence staining. Consistent with senescence-associated proliferative arrest, the senescent HCECs exhibited severely suppressed nuclear Ki67 expression. Notably, NPQ/SOD treatment markedly restored Ki67 positivity, indicating a robust rescue of cellular mitotic vitality (Fig. S12). It is well-established that aging and oxidative stress reinforce each other in a vicious cycle: oxidative stress promotes cellular senescence by inducing DNA damage, telomere shortening, and activation of p53/p21 and p16INK4a/Rb pathways, while senescent cells sustain ROS production, which in turn amplifies the SASP via NF-κB and related pathways [48]. Therefore, simultaneously targeting both senescence and ROS may more effectively disrupt the progression of age-related diseases such as DED. We further assessed the ability of NPQ/SOD to scavenge intracellular ROS. As shown in Fig. 4g and h, NPQ/SOD effectively attenuated ROSUP-induced oxidative stress, significantly outperforming SOD or NPQ. SOD alone showed negligible ROS clearance, which may be attributable to its rapid degradation and poor cellular uptake, while NPQ itself exhibited considerable ROS scavenging capacity. The radical scavenging capacity was further evaluated using DPPH (Fig. 4i) and ABTS+(Fig. 4j) assays. NP itself showed minimal activity, but conjugation with QR endowed NPQ with pronounced radical scavenging abilities comparable to those of free QR. This intrinsic antioxidant property of the NPQ polymer, combined with its delivery function, is highly beneficial for its utility in DED treatment.

The NPQ/SOD eye drop alleviated DED induced by benzalkonium chloride

We further evaluated the therapeutic potential of the eye drops for treating DED in vivo. First, the ocular biosafety of the formulation was assessed. Mice were treated topically with NPQ/SOD eye drops three times daily for 14 days. Histological examination of corneal and conjunctival tissues, along with TUNEL staining, revealed no structural damage or significant apoptotic cell death (Fig. 5a and b). Consistent with these observations, CCK-8 assays confirmed negligible cytotoxicity at the administered dosage (Fig. S13). We next examined the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of NPQ/SOD eye drops using a BAC-induced murine DED model (Fig. 5c). After 14 days of twice-daily BAC administration to establish DED, mice were treated with SOD, NPQ, or NPQ/SOD eye drops, with PBS serving as the control. Corneal epithelial integrity was evaluated after 14 days of treatment by fluorescein sodium staining, where higher fluorescence intensity corresponds to more severe epithelial injury. As shown in Fig. 5d and e, the NPQ/SOD treatment group exhibited significantly reduced corneal epithelial damage compared to the control group. NPQ alone also mitigated damage, though to a lesser extent. In contrast, free SOD showed no therapeutic benefit. Tear secretion did not significantly improve in any treatment group (Fig. 5d and f). To further evaluate antioxidant effects, we measured oxidative stress markers in ocular tissues, including MDA content, GR activity, and ROS levels. As shown in Fig. 5g and h, DED mice exhibited significantly reduced GR activity and elevated MDA levels compared to healthy controls. While NPQ effectively lowered MDA concentrations, it did not significantly restore GR activity. In contrast, NPQ/SOD not only markedly reduced MDA accumulation but also restored GR activity to near-normal levels, indicating comprehensive antioxidant protection. Cryosectioning and ROS-specific staining further revealed that both NPQ and NPQ/SOD treatments reduced the ROS-positive area on the corneal surface, with NPQ/SOD achieving a significantly greater reduction than NPQ alone (Fig. 5i). Given that DED promotes apoptosis in corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells, a process that contributes to barrier dysfunction and reduced mucin secretion, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress, we quantified apoptosis using the TUNEL assay. DED mice showed significantly increased apoptosis in both corneal and conjunctival epithelia relative to healthy controls. Both NPQ and NPQ/SOD treatments attenuated this apoptotic response, with NPQ/SOD showing superior cytoprotective efficacy (Fig. 5j and k). Together, these results demonstrate that NPQ/SOD eye drops effectively alleviate oxidative stress and suppress apoptosis in corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells in a murine DED model.

NPQ/SOD ameliorates age-related DED

Based on the significant anti-senescence activity of NPQ/SOD eye drops observed at the cellular level, together with the well-established vicious cycle linking cellular senescence and DED, we investigated their therapeutic potential for age-related DED by leveraging the spontaneous DED development in aged (12-month-old) BALB/c mice as a natural disease model (Fig. 6a). The aged mice exhibited hallmark features of naturally occurring age-related DED, showing significantly elevated corneal fluorescein staining and markedly reduced tear production compared to the young (6-8 weeks) controls (Fig. 6b-d), confirming the presence of spontaneous disease pathology. Capitalizing on this age-associated phenotype, we directly evaluated NPQ/SOD treatment in the aged mice without additional disease induction. The treatment demonstrated significant therapeutic effects, reducing corneal epithelial damage by 53% and restoring tear secretion to levels comparable to those in young controls. These findings validate the efficacy of NPQ/SOD in ameliorating key pathological features, including corneal epithelial damage and lacrimal hypofunction, in age-related DED. To evaluate the anti-senescence effects of NPQ/SOD eye drops on mice corneal cells after therapy, we quantified SA-β-Gal activity, a well-established biomarker of cellular senescence. Corneal flat-mount analysis demonstrated that NPQ/SOD treatment led to a significant reduction in SA-β-Gal activity compared with PBS-treated controls (Fig. 6e and f), indicating an attenuated senescent cell burden. Furthermore, qPCR analysis revealed significantly downregulated expression of key senescence markers in treated corneas, including p21 (Fig. 6g), p53 (Fig. 6h), and p16 (Fig. 6i). Additionally, Ki67 immunofluorescence staining of the corneal tissues revealed that the NPQ/SOD treatment significantly restored Ki67 expression levels (Fig. S14). Age-related DED is characterized by impaired tear secretion, which depends on mucin production from conjunctival goblet cells and aqueous secretion by lacrimal glands. Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed significant atrophy of lacrimal gland acinar structures in aged mice, which was effectively reversed by NPQ/SOD treatment, restoring the integrity of lacrimal gland acini (Fig. 6j and k). No significant differences in goblet cell numbers were observed between aged and young mice, and NPQ/SOD treatment did not alter this parameter (Fig. 6j and l). Currently, there are no commercially available therapeutic eye drops that share the exact same mechanism as our formulation. To enable a head-to-head comparison with a clinically used product, we selected HYLO-DUAL (0.05% sodium hyaluronate + 2% ectoine) as a comparator, as it represents one of the mainstream clinical treatments with a mechanism most closely aligned with ours. Ectoine is a natural compatible solute known for its cytoprotective and antioxidant properties, which parallels the antioxidant and cellular protective effects of our NPQ/SOD system. Therefore, we comparatively evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of the NPQ/SOD nanoformulation and the clinical drug HYLO-DUAL in a 12-month-old aging DED mouse model. After 14 days of consecutive treatment, both NPQ/SOD and HYLO-DUAL demonstrated comparable efficacy in improving clinical phenotypes, such as tear secretion and corneal fluorescein staining (Figs. S15 and S16). Heatmap analysis of the top 50 DEGs (ranked by adjusted p-value; Fig. 7b) revealed pronounced shifts in inflammation- and senescence-associated transcripts. NPQ-treated corneas displayed upregulation of genes involved in anti-inflammatory or stress-response pathways, including Lgals3bp, Dusp1, Txnip, and Tnfaip3. Consistently, volcano plot analysis (Fig. S18a) showed significant downregulation of canonical SASP mediators such as Cxcl13, Cxcl9, Ccl7, Il17c, Tnf, and Ccl28, indicating suppression of proinflammatory signaling after NPQ treatment. In NPQ/SOD-treated corneas, the heatmap revealed a predominant transcriptional repression pattern, with marked downregulation of Tnfaip2, Grem1, Cd36, and Myh3 (Fig. 7c). In agreement, volcano plot analysis highlighted reductions in SASP-associated inflammatory mediators, including Ccl28, Cxcl1, and Mmp9. Notably, Sprr family genes (Sprr1a, Sprr1b), which are implicated in epithelial repair, were upregulated in both NPQ and NPQ/SOD groups (Fig. S18b), consistent with the observed restoration of epithelial integrity in aged corneas. Together, these data suggest that NPQ primarily activates anti-inflammatory and stress-responsive pathways, whereas co-administration of SOD more effectively suppresses SASP-related inflammatory transcription.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further emphasized the central role of immune-inflammatory and secretory phenotypes. Genes elevated in aged corneas (relative to treated groups) were enriched for immune-related categories consistent with an inflammaging signature. Both NPQ and NPQ/SOD reversed these enrichments. In particular, DEGs in NPQ vs OLD were enriched for pathways such as “protein kinase A signaling” and “apoptotic signaling pathway”, suggesting attenuation of inflammatory signaling and apoptosis-associated programs after NPQ treatment (Fig. 7d). DEGs in NPQ/SOD vs OLD showed broader enrichment across immune-inflammatory pathways centered on “inflammatory response”, indicative of SASP modulation (Fig. 7e).

To specifically evaluate senescence-associated transcriptional programs, we performed GSEA using KEGG pathway gene sets relevant to SASP and cellular senescence. NPQ-treated corneas exhibited significant negative enrichment of classical SASP-associated gene sets, including the “NF-κB signaling pathway” (NES = −1.79, P < 0.001, FDR < 0.05), indicating suppression of SASP transcriptional activity (Fig. S19). This negative enrichment was more pronounced in NPQ/SOD-treated corneas (Fig. 7f), which also showed positive enrichment of gene sets associated with proliferative and replicative pathways, such as the cell cycle (NES = 1.53, P < 0.001, FDR = 0.133) and DNA replication (NES = 2.61, P < 0.001, FDR < 0.001) pathways (Fig. 7g). To provide multi-level validation of these transcriptomic insights, we performed RT-qPCR targeting the core genes within these key enriched pathways. In agreement with the RNA-seq data, NPQ/SOD treatment significantly downregulated the expression of hallmark pro-inflammatory and SASP-associated factors, including Tnf, Il6, and Cxcl1. Conversely, core functional genes driving cell cycle progression (Mki67), DNA replication (Pcna), and mismatch repair (Msh2) were significantly upregulated, reflecting the restoration of DNA damage repair capabilities and epithelial regenerative capacity (Fig. S20). Collectively, these data indicate that NPQ markedly suppresses SASP-related inflammatory transcriptional activity, while co-administration of SOD further amplifies this suppression and coincides with transcriptional signatures indicative of partial reversal of cellular senescence.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed NPQ, a biologically active and multi-functional amphiphilic polypeptide platform with dual-responsive properties. The strategic incorporation of QR serves a triple function: it confers self-assembly capability for efficient protein encapsulation, promotes endosomal escape, and imparts intrinsic senolytic and antioxidant activities. In DED models, the NPQ/SOD formulation acts synergistically to disrupt the critical senescence-oxidative stress cycle. It effectively reduces oxidative damage, clears senescent cells, suppresses the SASP, restores lacrimal gland function, and ultimately reverses core DED pathology. This work establishes local nanosenolytic delivery as a novel and potent therapeutic paradigm for age-related DED, with potential applicability to other senescence-associated disorders.
