Co-rotating Vortices on Surfaces of Variable Negative Curvature: Hamiltonian Structure and Drift Dynamics
Gaurang Mangesh Joshi, Rickmoy Samanta

Abstract
Vortices in fluids and superfluids underpin phenomena ranging from Bose–Einstein condensates and superfluid films to neutron stars and hydrodynamic micro-rotors, where geometry can strongly influence their motion. Curvature can induce vortex motion with no planar analogue. We study Hamiltonian vortex motion on a catenoid, a minimal surface of variable negative curvature, and derive explicit equations of motion, conserved quantities, and reductions for co-rotating vortex pairs. For two identical vortices we find an exact antipodal solution in which the pair rotates rigidly at fixed latitude, with angular velocity Ω = ( Γ / 16 ​ π ) ​ K ′ ​ ( V ) / − K ⁡ ( V ) \Omega=(\Gamma/16\pi)\,K^{\prime}(V)/\sqrt{-K(V)} , where K ⁡ ( V ) K(V) is the Gaussian curvature. Thus the motion is governed by the curvature gradient rather than the curvature itself. The symmetric state is linearly unstable, with growth rate λ = 3 ​ | Ω | \lambda=\sqrt{3}|\Omega| , in agreement with numerical simulations. For generic equal-strength pairs, conservation of the Hamiltonian and rotational momentum reduces the nonlinear dynamics to a single quadrature, yielding bounded relative oscillations together with a secular azimuthal drift. Simulations of the full equations confirm the reduced theory and reveal the same curvature-induced transport mechanism in a localized many-vortex cluster, motivating a broader theory of collective vortex drift on curved surfaces.



I
Introduction

Vortices are fundamental carriers of vorticity in a wide range of physical
systems, from Bose–Einstein condensates and superfluid films to
astrophysical fluids such as neutron star interiors, as well as engineered
hydrodynamic rotors and active flows. A minimal theoretical framework for
their motion is provided by point-vortex dynamics, which offers one of the
simplest Hamiltonian descriptions of two-dimensional rotating incompressible flow.
In the plane, the motion is governed by the Kirchhoff–Routh function and
inherits the Euclidean symmetries of the underlying domain, leading to
conserved linear and angular momenta and to logarithmic pair interactions

;

;

On curved surfaces, geometry fundamentally alters vortex motion.
Both the hydrodynamic Green function and the self-interaction (also known as the
Robin function) acquire explicit geometric dependence. As a result, curvature
acts as an effective field: it breaks translational invariance, modifies the
phase-space structure, and generates drift and confinement mechanisms with no
direct planar analogue.

The study of point vortices on curved manifolds has developed along several
directions. Vortex dynamics on spheres and other constant-curvature surfaces
has revealed how global topology and curvature alter relative equilibria,
streamline topology, and integrability properties

not merely a geometric background, but an active ingredient in the organization

Curved-surface vortex dynamics is also relevant for quantum fluids. In thin
superfluid films and quasi-two-dimensional Bose–Einstein condensates, point
vortices provide an effective description of quantized vortex motion, and

In this paper we focus on co-rotating point vortices on a catenoid. The
catenoid is a minimal surface with tunable throat radius
a
and nonuniform
negative Gaussian curvature. Its axial symmetry yields a conserved momentum,
while its conformal metric allows the Green function, Hamiltonian, symplectic
form, and equations of motion to be written explicitly. This makes the catenoid
a useful example for studying how curvature gradients influence vortex motion
beyond constant-curvature geometries.

We first derive the global Hamiltonian formulation for
N
vortices on the catenoid, revisiting and extending the framework of Ref.
banthia2026
, where dipolar vortex motion was studied. In contrast, the present work focuses on co-rotating vortex pairs. The Hamiltonian consists of the pairwise Green-function interaction
together with a curvature-induced self-interaction term. The corresponding
symplectic form is weighted by the catenoid area element, and the rotational
symmetry gives a momentum map. These two conserved quantities, the Hamiltonian

H
and the momentum
J
, provide the main organizing structure for the
two-vortex dynamics.

Our main analysis concerns two equal-strength co-rotating vortices. We show
that the system is Liouville integrable: fixing
J
eliminates the collective
meridional coordinate, while fixing
H
eliminates the relative azimuthal
angle, leaving a single quadrature for the relative meridional separation. This
reduction gives a complete analytic description of the nonlinear two-vortex
motion and allows direct reconstruction of the mean azimuthal drift.

We then identify an exact symmetric solution in which two identical vortices
remain antipodal at a fixed latitude and rotate rigidly around the catenoid.
The angular velocity is controlled by the curvature gradient rather than by the
curvature itself. In particular, if
K(V)
is the Gaussian curvature along the
meridional coordinate, the angular velocity can be written as a local geometric
combination involving
K^{\prime}(V)/\sqrt{-K(V)}
. Consequently, the rotation vanishes
at the throat, where the curvature is extremal but its gradient is zero, and is
maximal away from the throat. We also show that this symmetric co-rotating
state is linearly unstable, with growth rate proportional to the rigid-rotation
frequency. Direct numerical simulations of the full two-vortex equations
confirm both the instability rate and the reduced quadrature dynamics.

Finally, we include a brief outlook on many-vortex dynamics. A representative
simulation of a localized same-sign vortex cluster shows coherent azimuthal
drift while the cluster remains compact. This indicates that the
curvature-induced transport mechanism found for vortex pairs persists in the
many-vortex problem, although with additional shape-dependent collective
corrections. A detailed theory of such cluster drift will be developed in future work
samanta2026
.

II
Model Setup

We begin by setting up the geometric and Hamiltonian ingredients needed for
the rest of the paper. The key point is that, on a curved surface, vortex
motion is affected not only by the interaction between different vortices but
also by the way the surface geometry changes from point to point. For the
catenoid these effects can be written explicitly, which allows us to derive a
closed dynamical system for vortices in the global coordinates
(v,u)
. A detailed derivation of the Hamiltonian structure,
including the conserved quantities on surfaces of variable negative curvature,
is given in Ref.
banthia2026
.

We consider a catenoid of throat radius
a>0
, parametrized by

X(v,u)=\big(a\cosh(v/a)\cos u,\;a\cosh(v/a)\sin u,\;v\big),\qquad u\in[0,2\pi),\;v\in\mathbb{R}.

Here
u
is the azimuthal angle around the symmetry axis, while
v
measures
position along the meridional direction. The induced metric is

g=\cosh^{2}\!\big(\tfrac{v}{a}\big)\,dv^{2}+a^{2}\cosh^{2}\!\big(\tfrac{v}{a}\big)\,du^{2}=\cosh^{2}\!\big(\tfrac{v}{a}\big)\,\big(dv^{2}+a^{2}du^{2}\big),

with corresponding area element

dA=a\cosh^{2}\!\big(\tfrac{v}{a}\big)\,dv\,du.

The azimuthal periodicity in
u
allows the hydrodynamic Green’s function to be written as

G(v_{i},u_{i};v_{j},u_{j})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\log\!\Big(\cosh\!\big(\tfrac{v_{i}-v_{j}}{a}\big)-\cos(u_{i}-u_{j})\Big).

For notational convenience, we introduce

F_{ij}:=\cosh\!\big(\tfrac{v_{i}-v_{j}}{a}\big)-\cos(u_{i}-u_{j}),\qquad h(v):=\cosh\!\big(\tfrac{v}{a}\big).

For a system of
N
point vortices with circulations
\Gamma_{i}
located at

(v_{i},u_{i})
, the interaction Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\Gamma_{i}\Gamma_{j}\,G(v_{i},u_{i};v_{j},u_{j})-\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{i}^{2}\log h(v_{i}).

(1)

The corresponding Hamilton equations are
banthia2026

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}ah^{2}(v_{i})\,\dot{v}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial u_{i}},\qquad\Gamma_{i}ah^{2}(v_{i})\,\dot{u}_{i}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial v_{i}}.

(2)

Differentiating the Green’s function gives

\frac{\partial G}{\partial u_{i}}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\frac{\sin(u_{i}-u_{j})}{F_{ij}},\qquad\frac{\partial G}{\partial v_{i}}=\frac{1}{4\pi a}\,\frac{\sinh\!\big(\tfrac{v_{i}-v_{j}}{a}\big)}{F_{ij}},

while the curvature-induced self-interaction contributes

\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}\Big(-\frac{1}{4\pi}\Gamma_{i}^{2}\log h(v_{i})\Big)=-\frac{1}{4\pi a}\Gamma_{i}^{2}\,\tanh\!\big(\tfrac{v_{i}}{a}\big).

Substituting these expressions into Hamilton’s equations leads to the explicit dynamical system

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi a\,h^{2}(v_{i})}\sum_{j\neq i}\Gamma_{j}\,\frac{\sin(u_{i}-u_{j})}{F_{ij}},

(3)

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi a^{2}\,h^{2}(v_{i})}\sum_{j\neq i}\Gamma_{j}\,\frac{\sinh\!\big(\tfrac{v_{i}-v_{j}}{a}\big)}{F_{ij}}+\frac{1}{4\pi a^{2}\,h^{2}(v_{i})}\Gamma_{i}\,\tanh\!\big(\tfrac{v_{i}}{a}\big).

These equations form the basis for all subsequent analysis. We now identify a second conserved quantity arising from symmetry; see Ref.
banthia2026
. The catenoid is invariant under rotations
u\mapsto u+\theta
, corresponding to a
U(1)
action. The associated conserved quantity is obtained via the momentum map. Contracting the symplectic form with the generator
\partial/\partial u
gives

\iota_{\partial/\partial u}\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{i}\,a\,h^{2}(v_{i})\,dv_{i}.

This must be an exact differential, implying

\iota_{\partial/\partial u}\omega=d\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{i}S(v_{i})\right),\qquad S^{\prime}(v)=a\,h^{2}(v).

Integrating,

S(v)=\frac{a}{2}v+\frac{a^{2}}{4}\sinh\!\big(\tfrac{2v}{a}\big).

Hence the conserved momentum is

\displaystyle J=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{i}\left(\frac{a}{2}v_{i}+\frac{a^{2}}{4}\sinh\!\big(\tfrac{2v_{i}}{a}\big)\right),\qquad\frac{dJ}{dt}=0.

(4)

Together with the conservation of the Hamiltonian
H
, these invariants provide important constraints on the dynamics and serve as useful diagnostics in the analysis that follows.

III
Collective Variables and Quadrature Reduction for Two Vortices

To explore the geometric structure of the two–vortex dynamics on the catenoid, it is convenient to introduce collective and relative variables

V=\frac{v_{1}+v_{2}}{2},\qquad\Delta v=v_{1}-v_{2},\qquad U=\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2},\qquad\Delta u=u_{1}-u_{2},

(5)

so that

v_{1}=V+\frac{\Delta v}{2},\qquad v_{2}=V-\frac{\Delta v}{2},\qquad u_{1}=U+\frac{\Delta u}{2},\qquad u_{2}=U-\frac{\Delta u}{2}.

(6)

As before, we let
h(v)=\cosh(v/a)
and define

h_{1}=h(v_{1}),\qquad h_{2}=h(v_{2}),\qquad F=\cosh\!\big(\tfrac{\Delta v}{a}\big)-\cos(\Delta u).

The equations of motion for two vortices of strengths
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
may be written as

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{4\pi ah_{1}^{2}}\frac{\sin(\Delta u)}{F},\qquad\dot{v}_{2}=-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{4\pi ah_{2}^{2}}\frac{\sin(\Delta u)}{F},

(7)

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{4\pi a^{2}h_{1}^{2}}\frac{\sinh(\Delta v/a)}{F}+\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{4\pi a^{2}h_{1}^{2}}\tanh\!\big(\tfrac{v_{1}}{a}\big),

(8)

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{2}

\displaystyle=+\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{4\pi a^{2}h_{2}^{2}}\frac{\sinh(\Delta v/a)}{F}+\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{4\pi a^{2}h_{2}^{2}}\tanh\!\big(\tfrac{v_{2}}{a}\big).

(9)

Passing to
(V,\Delta v,U,\Delta u)
using

\dot{V}=\frac{\dot{v}_{1}+\dot{v}_{2}}{2},\qquad\dot{\Delta v}=\dot{v}_{1}-\dot{v}_{2},\qquad\dot{U}=\frac{\dot{u}_{1}+\dot{u}_{2}}{2},\qquad\dot{\Delta u}=\dot{u}_{1}-\dot{u}_{2},

we obtain the closed system

\displaystyle\dot{V}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\Delta u)}{8\pi aF}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{h_{1}^{2}}-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{h_{2}^{2}}\right),

(10)

\displaystyle\dot{\Delta v}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\Delta u)}{4\pi aF}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{h_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{h_{2}^{2}}\right),

(11)

\displaystyle\dot{U}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi a^{2}}\left[-\frac{\sinh(\Delta v/a)}{F}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{h_{1}^{2}}-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{h_{2}^{2}}\right)+\frac{\Gamma_{1}\tanh(v_{1}/a)}{h_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\Gamma_{2}\tanh(v_{2}/a)}{h_{2}^{2}}\right],

(12)

\displaystyle\dot{\Delta u}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sinh(\Delta v/a)}{4\pi a^{2}F}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{h_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{h_{2}^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{4\pi a^{2}}\left[\frac{\Gamma_{1}\tanh(v_{1}/a)}{h_{1}^{2}}-\frac{\Gamma_{2}\tanh(v_{2}/a)}{h_{2}^{2}}\right].

(13)

Equations (
10
)–(
13
) provide an exact representation of the two–vortex dynamics in collective and relative variables. The terms proportional to
F^{-1}
correspond to the geometric generalization of the planar Biot–Savart interaction, while the terms involving
\tanh(v/a)
arise entirely from curvature-induced self-interaction. Unlike the planar case, the conformal factor
h^{2}(v)
prevents a complete decoupling between collective and relative degrees of freedom: the relative dynamics depends explicitly on
V
through
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
. This coupling is a direct manifestation of the nonuniform geometry of the catenoid.
The system admits two conserved quantities. The Hamiltonian may be written as

H=\frac{\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}}{4\pi}\log\!\Big(\cosh\!\big(\tfrac{\Delta v}{a}\big)-\cos(\Delta u)\Big)-\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[\Gamma_{1}^{2}\log h(v_{1})+\Gamma_{2}^{2}\log h(v_{2})\right],

(14)

with
v_{1,2}=V\pm\Delta v/2
. In addition, rotational invariance
u_{i}\mapsto u_{i}+\theta
yields the conserved momentum

J=\Gamma_{1}S(v_{1})+\Gamma_{2}S(v_{2}),\qquad S(v)=\frac{a}{2}v+\frac{a^{2}}{4}\sinh\!\big(\tfrac{2v}{a}\big),

(15)

so that
dH/dt=0
and
dJ/dt=0
. These invariants constrain the dynamics and, in principle, allow a reduction of the system to a single quadrature. The reduction proceeds as follows. Fixing the value of the momentum,

J=J_{0}
, gives

J_{0}=\Gamma_{1}S\!\left(V+\frac{\Delta v}{2}\right)+\Gamma_{2}S\!\left(V-\frac{\Delta v}{2}\right).

(16)

For fixed
J_{0}
this equation determines the collective coordinate

V
implicitly as a function of the relative separation,

V=V(\Delta v;J_{0}),

(17)

whenever the local inversion of (
16
) is valid. Thus

v_{1}(\Delta v)=V(\Delta v;J_{0})+\frac{\Delta v}{2},\qquad v_{2}(\Delta v)=V(\Delta v;J_{0})-\frac{\Delta v}{2}.

Next, fixing the energy
H=E
gives

4\pi E=\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}\log F-\Gamma_{1}^{2}\log h(v_{1})-\Gamma_{2}^{2}\log h(v_{2}).

(18)

For
\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}\neq 0
, this yields

F=\exp\!\left(\frac{4\pi E}{\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}}\right)h(v_{1})^{\Gamma_{1}/\Gamma_{2}}h(v_{2})^{\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}}.

(19)

Since

F=\cosh\!\left(\frac{\Delta v}{a}\right)-\cos(\Delta u),

we obtain

\cos(\Delta u)=\cosh\!\left(\frac{\Delta v}{a}\right)-\exp\!\left(\frac{4\pi E}{\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}}\right)h(v_{1})^{\Gamma_{1}/\Gamma_{2}}h(v_{2})^{\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}}.

(20)

For convenience, let us also define

\mathcal{C}(\Delta v)\equiv\cosh\!\left(\frac{\Delta v}{a}\right)-\exp\!\left(\frac{4\pi E}{\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}}\right)h(v_{1})^{\Gamma_{1}/\Gamma_{2}}h(v_{2})^{\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}},

(21)

where
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are understood as functions of
\Delta v
through
(
16
). Then

\cos(\Delta u)=\mathcal{C}(\Delta v),\qquad\sin(\Delta u)=\epsilon\sqrt{1-\mathcal{C}^{2}(\Delta v)},

(22)

with
\epsilon=\pm 1
fixed by the initial condition and changing sign at turning points. Substituting (
22
) into (
11
), the relative meridional
motion obeys the single first-order equation

\dot{\Delta v}=\frac{\epsilon}{4\pi a\,F_{E}(\Delta v)}\left[\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{h^{2}(v_{1})}+\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{h^{2}(v_{2})}\right]\sqrt{1-\mathcal{C}^{2}(\Delta v)},

(23)

where

F_{E}(\Delta v)=\exp\!\left(\frac{4\pi E}{\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}}\right)h(v_{1})^{\Gamma_{1}/\Gamma_{2}}h(v_{2})^{\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}}.

(24)

Equivalently, the dynamics is reduced to the quadrature

t-t_{0}=\epsilon\int_{\Delta v_{0}}^{\Delta v(t)}\frac{4\pi a\,F_{E}(\xi)\,d\xi}{\left[\Gamma_{2}h^{-2}(v_{1}(\xi))+\Gamma_{1}h^{-2}(v_{2}(\xi))\right]\sqrt{1-\mathcal{C}^{2}(\xi)}}.

(25)

This is the desired one-dimensional reduction. Once
\Delta v(t)
is
obtained from (
25
), the remaining variables follow by
reconstruction:

V(t)=V(\Delta v(t);J_{0}),\qquad\Delta u(t)=\arccos\mathcal{C}(\Delta v(t)),

(26)

with the branch chosen consistently with (
22
). The mean
azimuthal coordinate is then obtained from

U(t)-U_{0}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\dot{U}\big(V(s),\Delta v(s),\Delta u(s)\big)\,ds,

(27)

where
\dot{U}
is given in (
12
). Thus the two–vortex problem
on the catenoid is Liouville integrable: the conserved momentum removes
the collective meridional coordinate, the conserved Hamiltonian removes
the relative angle, and the remaining motion is governed by the single
quadrature (
25
).

III.1
Exact Rigidly Rotating Solution: Symmetric Two–Vortex Case

We now identify an exact solution corresponding to a symmetric co–rotating configuration of two identical vortices, for which

\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma>0.

We consider the antipodal ansatz

v_{1}(t)=v_{2}(t)=V(t),\qquad\Delta u(t)=u_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t)=\pi.

(28)

Under this ansatz, the relative separation vanishes,

\Delta v=0,\qquad h_{1}=h_{2}=h(V),\qquad F=\cosh(0)-\cos\pi=2.

Substituting into the meridional equations (
11
), we obtain

\dot{v}_{1}=\dot{v}_{2}=0,

(29)

so that

V(t)=V_{0},

(30)

and the vortices remain on a fixed latitude of the catenoid. The azimuthal equations (
13
) reduce to

\dot{u}_{1}=\dot{u}_{2}=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi a^{2}h^{2}(V_{0})}\tanh\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right),

(31)

since the interaction term proportional to
\sinh(\Delta v/a)
vanishes identically. Thus both vortices rotate with the same angular velocity

\Omega(V_{0})=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi a^{2}}\tanh\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right)\sech^{2}\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right).

(32)

It follows that

u_{1}(t)=u_{1}(0)+\Omega(V_{0})t,\qquad u_{2}(t)=u_{2}(0)+\Omega(V_{0})t,

(33)

with the initial condition
u_{1}(0)-u_{2}(0)=\pi
. Consequently,

\Delta u(t)=\pi,

(34)

and the antipodal separation is preserved exactly. The resulting motion is a rigid rotation:

v_{1}(t)=v_{2}(t)=V_{0},\qquad u_{1}(t)=u_{1}(0)+\Omega(V_{0})t,\qquad u_{2}(t)=u_{1}(t)-\pi.

(35)

This describes a pair of vortices rotating uniformly along a circular latitude of the catenoid. The rotation is entirely curvature controlled: in the planar limit, or at the throat
V_{0}=0
, the angular velocity vanishes. For this solution, the conserved quantities simplify to

H=\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{4\pi}\log 2-\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{2\pi}\log h(V_{0}),

(36)

and

J=2\Gamma S(V_{0}),\qquad S(V_{0})=\frac{a}{2}V_{0}+\frac{a^{2}}{4}\sinh\!\left(\frac{2V_{0}}{a}\right).

(37)

Thus the conserved momentum fixes the latitude
V_{0}
, while the Hamiltonian determines the corresponding energy of the rigidly rotating pair. The dependence of the rotation rate on latitude admits a direct geometric interpretation. The Gaussian curvature of the catenoid is

K(V)=-\frac{1}{a^{2}\cosh^{4}(V/a)},

(38)

with derivative

K^{\prime}(V)=\frac{4}{a^{3}}\frac{\sinh(V/a)}{\cosh^{5}(V/a)}.

(39)

Using these expressions, the angular velocity (
32
) can be rewritten as

\Omega(V)=\frac{\Gamma}{16\pi}\,\frac{K^{\prime}(V)}{\sqrt{-K(V)}},

(40)

showing that the rigid rotation is controlled by the curvature gradient rather than by the curvature itself. The maximum rotation follows from

\frac{d\Omega}{dV}=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi a^{3}}\sech^{2}\!\left(\frac{V}{a}\right)\left[1-3\tanh^{2}\!\left(\frac{V}{a}\right)\right],

(41)

so that the nontrivial extrema occur at

\tanh\!\left(\frac{V_{*}}{a}\right)=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}.

(42)

In particular,
\Omega(V)
vanishes at the throat
V=0
, where
K^{\prime}(0)=0
, and changes sign across it. The location of the extrema can be obtained explicitly. From the condition

\tanh(V_{*}/a)=\pm 1/\sqrt{3},

we find

V_{*}=\pm a\,\operatorname{arctanh}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)=\pm\frac{a}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{\sqrt{3}-1}\right)=\pm\frac{a}{2}\ln(2+\sqrt{3}).

(43)

Thus the maximal magnitude of the angular velocity occurs at a finite distance from the throat, symmetrically located at
V=\pm V_{*}
. In particular, the rotation is not strongest at the point of maximal curvature (
V=0
), but rather where the curvature varies most rapidly. This provides a direct geometric characterization of the rigidly rotating state: the dynamics is governed by the spatial gradient of curvature, with the extrema marking the loci of maximal curvature-induced rotation. In the asymptotic region
|V|\gg a
, we find

\Omega(V)\sim\frac{\Gamma}{\pi a^{2}}\,\mathrm{sgn}(V)\,e^{-2|V|/a},

(44)

reflecting the exponential decay of both the curvature and its gradient. Thus the co–rotating state provides a direct realization of curvature–gradient–driven motion, with maximal rotation occurring away from the throat and vanishing dynamics in the asymptotically flat regions.

Linear Stability of the Symmetric Rigidly Rotating State

We now examine the linear stability of the symmetric antipodal solution. For identical co–rotating vortices,
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma>0
, we perturb the rigidly rotating state according to

V(t)=V_{0}+\delta V(t),\qquad\Delta v(t)=\eta(t),\qquad\Delta u(t)=\pi+\phi(t),

(45)

where
|\delta V|
,
|\eta|
, and
|\phi|
are small. To linear order,

\sin(\Delta u)=\sin(\pi+\phi)\simeq-\phi,\qquad F=\cosh(\eta/a)-\cos(\pi+\phi)\simeq 2.

(46)

Expanding Eqs. (
10
), (
11
), and (
13
) about the symmetric state gives

\dot{\delta V}=0,\qquad\dot{\eta}=-\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi a}\sech^{2}\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right)\phi,\qquad\dot{\phi}=-\frac{3\Gamma}{4\pi a^{3}}\sech^{2}\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right)\tanh^{2}\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right)\eta.

(47)

Thus the collective meridional perturbation is neutral at linear order, while the relative perturbations form a hyperbolic pair. Eliminating
\phi
gives

\ddot{\eta}=\lambda^{2}\eta,

(48)

with

\lambda^{2}=\frac{3\Gamma^{2}}{16\pi^{2}a^{4}}\sech^{4}\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right)\tanh^{2}\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right),

(49)

so that

\lambda=\frac{\sqrt{3}\Gamma}{4\pi a^{2}}\sech^{2}\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right)\left|\tanh\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right)\right|.

(50)

In terms of the rigid-rotation frequency (
32
), this takes the compact form

\lambda=\sqrt{3}\,|\Omega(V_{0})|.

(51)

Since
\lambda^{2}>0
for
V_{0}\neq 0
, the linearized dynamics admits an exponentially growing mode, implying that the symmetric co–rotating state is linearly unstable away from the throat. At
V_{0}=0
, one has
\Omega=\lambda=0
, and the instability becomes marginal at linear order.

III.3
Numerical verification of the linear instability

We now verify the above linear stability predictions by direct numerical integration of the full two–vortex equations of motion on the catenoid. Throughout this section we consider two identical vortices with
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma>0
and fix the geometric parameter
a=1
without loss of generality. The symmetric rigidly rotating state is initialized at a prescribed latitude
V_{0}>0
, with small perturbations chosen to excite the unstable eigenmode of the linearized system. Specifically, we take

V(0)=V_{0},\qquad\Delta v(0)=\eta_{0},\qquad\Delta u(0)=\pi+\phi_{0},

(52)

where
|\eta_{0}|\ll 1
. To isolate the growing mode, the initial angular perturbation is chosen according to the linear eigenvector relation

\phi_{0}=-\frac{\lambda}{A}\,\eta_{0}=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{a}\tanh\!\left(\frac{V_{0}}{a}\right)\eta_{0},

(53)

with

A=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi a}\sech^{2}(V_{0}/a).

In terms of the original vortex coordinates, this corresponds to

v_{1}(0)=V_{0}+\frac{\eta_{0}}{2},\qquad v_{2}(0)=V_{0}-\frac{\eta_{0}}{2},

u_{1}(0)=\frac{\pi+\phi_{0}}{2},\qquad u_{2}(0)=-\frac{\pi+\phi_{0}}{2}.

The equations of motion are then integrated numerically over a time interval
t\in[0,t_{f}]
, with
t_{f}
chosen sufficiently large to resolve the exponential growth regime while remaining within the validity of the linear approximation. At each time step we extract the relative variables

\eta(t)=v_{1}(t)-v_{2}(t),\qquad\phi(t)=\Delta u(t)-\pi,

(54)

and compare them with the linear predictions

\eta_{\rm lin}(t)=\eta_{0}e^{\lambda t},\qquad\phi_{\rm lin}(t)=\phi_{0}e^{\lambda t}.

(55)

Figure
2
(a) shows the time evolution of
\eta(t)
and
\phi(t)
. The numerical solution clearly exhibits the hyperbolic structure predicted by the linearized equations: one mode grows exponentially while the other decays, with the two remaining variables determined via the eigenvector relation. In Fig.
2
(b) we plot
\log|\eta(t)|
, which displays a linear dependence on time over an extended interval, confirming exponential growth. A linear fit yields a numerical growth rate
\lambda_{\rm fit}
in excellent agreement with the theoretical prediction
\lambda
, with relative error below
10^{-3}
. However note that because the reduced system conserves both energy and angular momentum, the nonlinear dynamics remains globally bounded: perturbations grow exponentially along the unstable manifold but remain confined to invariant energy and rotational momentum contours.

We now develop an analytic description of a generic co–rotating pair of identical vortices on the catenoid, with

\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma>0.

In contrast to the symmetric antipodal configuration, the dynamics is fully two–dimensional in the relative variables and exhibits a combination of meridional oscillation and azimuthal drift. It is convenient to work in the collective and relative variables introduced in previous section,

V=\frac{v_{1}+v_{2}}{2},\qquad\Delta v=v_{1}-v_{2},\qquad U=\frac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2},\qquad\Delta u=u_{1}-u_{2},

so that

v_{1,2}=V\pm\tfrac{\Delta v}{2}.

For equal strengths, the Hamiltonian reduces to

H=\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{4\pi}\log\!\Big(\cosh\!\big(\tfrac{\Delta v}{a}\big)-\cos(\Delta u)\Big)-\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{4\pi}\Big[\log h(v_{1})+\log h(v_{2})\Big],

(56)

while the conserved momentum simplifies to the explicit form

J=\Gamma\Big(S(v_{1})+S(v_{2})\Big)=\Gamma\left[aV+\frac{a^{2}}{2}\sinh\!\left(\frac{2V}{a}\right)\cosh\!\left(\frac{\Delta v}{a}\right)\right].

(57)

Equation (
57
) provides an explicit algebraic relation between the collective coordinate
V
and the relative separation
\Delta v
at fixed
J=J_{0}
. Thus, unlike the general unequal–strength case, the collective meridional coordinate can be eliminated exactly without inversion ambiguities, yielding

V=V(\Delta v;J_{0}).

Substituting this relation into the Hamiltonian, one obtains an effective one–degree–of–freedom description. The energy constraint
H=E
gives

\cos(\Delta u)=\cosh\!\left(\frac{\Delta v}{a}\right)-C_{E}\,h(v_{1})h(v_{2}),\qquad C_{E}=\exp\!\left(\frac{4\pi E}{\Gamma^{2}}\right),

(58)

so that the relative angle is determined algebraically by
\Delta v
. Writing

\sin(\Delta u)=\epsilon\sqrt{1-\mathcal{C}^{2}(\Delta v)},\qquad\mathcal{C}(\Delta v)=\cos(\Delta u),

the meridional dynamics reduces to a single first–order equation

\dot{\Delta v}=\frac{\epsilon\Gamma}{4\pi a}\frac{h^{-2}(v_{1})+h^{-2}(v_{2})}{F_{E}(\Delta v)}\sqrt{1-\mathcal{C}^{2}(\Delta v)},

(59)

where

F_{E}(\Delta v)=C_{E}\,h(v_{1})h(v_{2}).

This defines a one–dimensional integrable flow with turning points determined by
|\mathcal{C}(\Delta v)|\leq 1
. A key feature of the equal–strength case is the emergence of a nontrivial drift of the collective azimuthal coordinate. Averaging the individual azimuthal velocities yields

\dot{U}=\frac{\Gamma}{8\pi a^{2}}\left[-\frac{\sinh(\Delta v/a)}{F}\Big(\frac{1}{h^{2}(v_{1})}-\frac{1}{h^{2}(v_{2})}\Big)+\frac{\tanh(v_{1}/a)}{h^{2}(v_{1})}+\frac{\tanh(v_{2}/a)}{h^{2}(v_{2})}\right],

(60)

which is generically nonzero. This term represents a curvature--induced drift that has no analogue in the planar problem

The geometric content of the motion is most transparent in the standard
\mathbb{R}^{3}
embedding of the catenoid,

X(u,v)=\big(a\cosh(v/a)\cos u,\;a\cosh(v/a)\sin u,\;v\big).

The instantaneous chord distance between the vortices is then

d_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}=a^{2}\Big[h^{2}(v_{1})+h^{2}(v_{2})-2h(v_{1})h(v_{2})\cos(\Delta u)\Big]+(\Delta v)^{2},

(61)

which provides a natural geometric measure of the pair separation.
. Using (
58
), the chord distance can be expressed entirely in terms of
\Delta v
and the conserved quantities.

Equations (
59
) and (

) show that the generic co–rotating motion consists of an oscillatory evolution in the relative coordinate
\Delta v(t)
, coupled to a secular azimuthal drift
U(t)
. The oscillation arises from the effective one–dimensional dynamics constrained by the energy constraint, while the drift is driven by curvature through the asymmetric weighting of the two vortices in the conformal factor
h(v)
. In particular, even when the relative motion is bounded, the pair undergoes a persistent rotation around the axis of the catenoid.

Thus, in contrast to the rigidly rotating symmetric state, the generic co–rotating pair exhibits a mixed dynamics combining integrable oscillations in separation with curvature–induced transport along the azimuthal direction. The resulting dynamics is illustrated in Fig.

The conservation of
H
and

IV.1
Verification of the reduced quadrature dynamics and azimuthal drift

To validate the analytic reduction developed above, we compare the full numerical evolution of a generic equal–strength co–rotating pair with the reduced one–dimensional theory implied by the conserved quantities. Throughout this test we consider
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma
and fix parameters
a=1
and
\Gamma=1
.
The initial conditions are taken to be

(u_{1}(0),v_{1}(0))=(0,0),\qquad(u_{2}(0),v_{2}(0))=\left(\frac{\pi}{3},\frac{\pi}{4}\right),

corresponding to

V_{0}=\frac{v_{1}(0)+v_{2}(0)}{2},\quad\Delta v_{0}=v_{1}(0)-v_{2}(0),\quad\Delta u_{0}=u_{1}(0)-u_{2}(0).

The full dynamics is obtained by direct numerical integration of the equations of motion, yielding
u_{i}(t)
and
v_{i}(t)
, from which we extract the relative variables

\Delta v(t)=v_{1}(t)-v_{2}(t),\qquad\Delta u(t)=u_{1}(t)-u_{2}(t).

The reduced theory follows from the conservation of the Hamiltonian and momentum,

H=\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{4\pi}\log\!\big(\cosh(\Delta v/a)-\cos(\Delta u)\big)-\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{4\pi}\big[\log h(v_{1})+\log h(v_{2})\big],

J=\Gamma\left(aV+\frac{a^{2}}{2}\sinh\!\left(\frac{2V}{a}\right)\cosh\!\left(\frac{\Delta v}{a}\right)\right),

which determine
V=V(\Delta v)
and the effective interaction factor

F_{E}(\Delta v)=\exp\!\left(\frac{4\pi H_{0}}{\Gamma^{2}}\right)h(v_{1})h(v_{2}).

Eliminating
\Delta u
using

\cos(\Delta u)=\cosh\!\left(\frac{\Delta v}{a}\right)-F_{E}(\Delta v),

the relative dynamics reduces to the first–order equation

\dot{\Delta v}=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi a\,F_{E}(\Delta v)}\left[\frac{1}{h^{2}(v_{1})}+\frac{1}{h^{2}(v_{2})}\right]\sin(\Delta u),

(62)

with the branch of
\sin(\Delta u)
fixed dynamically. In practice,
V(\Delta v)
is obtained by solving
J(V,\Delta v)=J_{0}
, and the reduced right–hand side is evaluated along the trajectory using the instantaneous sign of
\sin(\Delta u(t))
. We first compare
\dot{\Delta v}(t)
computed from the full numerical solution with the analytic prediction. As shown in Fig.
4
, the two are indistinguishable over the entire time interval, demonstrating that the quadrature reduction exactly captures the dynamics, including the correct branch structure across turning points. This provides a stringent verification of the Liouville integrability of the system.

A key consequence of the reduction is that the mean azimuthal coordinate

U(t)=\frac{u_{1}(t)+u_{2}(t)}{2}

(63)

is not an independent dynamical variable, but is completely determined by
\Delta v(t)
. Its evolution is governed by

\dot{U}=\frac{\Gamma}{8\pi a^{2}}\left[-\frac{\sinh(\Delta v/a)}{F}\!\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(v_{1}/a)}-\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(v_{2}/a)}\right)+\frac{\tanh(v_{1}/a)}{\cosh^{2}(v_{1}/a)}+\frac{\tanh(v_{2}/a)}{\cosh^{2}(v_{2}/a)}\right],

(64)

with
F=\cosh(\Delta v/a)-\cos(\Delta u)
and
V=V(\Delta v)
.
Thus the azimuthal drift can be reconstructed by quadrature,

U(t)=U(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{U}\big(\Delta v(s);H,J\big)\,ds,

(65)

where
\mathcal{U}
denotes the right-hand side of Eq. (
64
). We verify this prediction by comparing both the instantaneous drift rate
\dot{U}(t)
and the reconstructed
U(t)
with the full numerical solution. As shown in Fig.
5
, the reduced theory reproduces both quantities with excellent accuracy over long times. This demonstrates that the observed secular azimuthal drift is not an independent degree of freedom, but arises entirely from the constrained one–dimensional dynamics governed by
(H,J)
.

The two–vortex analysis above shows that curvature generically produces an

natural to ask whether the same mechanism survives in the many-vortex problem.
As a first indication, we briefly present a representative numerical experiment

the natural many-body continuation of the two-vortex azimuthal drift described
above: the internal vortex separations remain bounded, but the center of the
packet is transported along the azimuthal Killing direction.

To quantify the collective motion, we define the cluster center by

two-vortex drift derived above, but the many-body problem introduces additional
shape-dependent corrections controlled by the internal distribution of
vortices. In particular, pairwise cancellations familiar from the planar

VI
Conclusion

We have developed a Hamiltonian formulation of point-vortex dynamics on a
catenoid and used it to obtain an explicit reduction of the co-rotating
two-vortex problem. Starting from the global catenoid parametrization, we
derived the Green’s function, the interaction Hamiltonian, the symplectic form,
and the associated equations of motion in the coordinates
(v,u)
. The geometry
enters the dynamics in two distinct ways: through the pairwise interaction
kernel and through the curvature-induced Robin term. The latter produces a
self-induced azimuthal velocity proportional to

\tanh(v/a)\sech^{2}(v/a)
and is responsible for several effects absent in the
planar problem.

The rotational symmetry of the catenoid yields a conserved momentum
J
, in
addition to the Hamiltonian
H
. For two vortices, these two invariants allow a
Liouville reduction of the dynamics. The momentum constraint determines the
collective meridional coordinate as a function of the relative separation,
while the energy constraint determines the relative azimuthal angle. The
remaining relative motion is governed by a single quadrature. This reduction
shows explicitly that the two-vortex dynamics on the catenoid is integrable,
although the collective and relative degrees of freedom do not decouple in the
same way as in the planar problem because of the nonuniform conformal factor of
the surface.

For two identical co-rotating vortices, we identified an exact antipodal
solution in which both vortices remain at a fixed latitude and rotate rigidly
around the symmetry axis. The angular velocity is

\Omega(V)=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi a^{2}}\tanh(V/a)\sech^{2}(V/a).

This motion is entirely curvature induced. It vanishes at the throat and in
the asymptotically flat regions, and its extrema occur at

V=\pm(a/2)\ln(2+\sqrt{3})
. Equivalently, the angular velocity can be written
in terms of the Gaussian curvature as

\Omega(V)=\frac{\Gamma}{16\pi}\frac{K^{\prime}(V)}{\sqrt{-K(V)}}.

Thus the rigid rotation is controlled by the curvature gradient rather than by
the curvature itself.

We also analyzed the linear stability of this symmetric rotating state. The
collective meridional perturbation is neutral at linear order, while the
relative perturbations form a hyperbolic pair. The instability rate is

\lambda=\sqrt{3}\,|\Omega(V_{0})|,

so the same curvature-induced rotation that drives the rigid motion also fixes
the exponential growth rate of the unstable mode. Direct numerical integration
of the full nonlinear equations confirms this prediction and reproduces the
growth rate with high accuracy for sufficiently small perturbations.

Beyond the symmetric solution, we studied generic equal-strength co-rotating
pairs. In this case the relative separation undergoes bounded oscillations,
while the mean azimuthal coordinate exhibits a secular drift. Using the
Hamiltonian and momentum constraints, we reconstructed both the relative
meridional motion and the mean azimuthal drift from the reduced one-dimensional
dynamics. The numerical simulations agree with the reduced theory, including
the branch changes at turning points. This confirms that the observed drift is
not an additional independent degree of freedom, but is completely determined
by the conserved quantities and the reduced relative motion.

Finally, we presented a preliminary many-vortex calculation showing that a
localized same-sign cluster remains compact while its center drifts
azimuthally around the catenoid. This provides evidence that the
curvature-induced transport mechanism identified for vortex pairs persists in
the collective dynamics of vortex clusters, with additional corrections arising
from the internal shape of the packet. A systematic reduced theory of such
many-vortex cluster motion, together with detailed numerical tests, will be
developed in future work
samanta2026
.

Taken together, these results show that the catenoid provides a minimal
geometry in which curvature-induced vortex transport, exact integrability of
the two-body problem, and instability of symmetric co-rotating states can be
studied analytically. The explicit dependence of the dynamics on the curvature
gradient suggests a broader organizing principle for vortex motion on
nonuniform curved surfaces.
