Cardiovascular Health Profiles in Pregnant, Postpartum, and Nonpregnant Reproductive-Aged Women Using Life’s Essential 8 Scores
Authors: Zara Latif, Justin D. Mark, Tracy T. Makuvire, Jose L. Lopez Batista, Estefania Oliveros, Emily S. Lau, Garima Sharma, Ersilia M. DeFilippis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdverse pregnancy outcomes are a major driver of high maternal mortality in the United States. There are limited data on cardiovascular health (CVH) in reproductive-aged women nationally.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to assess CVH in pregnant, postpartum, and nonpregnant women of reproductive age using the American Heart Association Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) score.MethodsWe performed serial cross-sectional analysis of reproductive-aged women 20 to 44 years of age in the United States participating in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey from 2015 to 2023. Overall CVH scores (0-100) were calculated using the LE8 core components in nonpregnant women and women with any pregnancy-related condition (defined as currently pregnant, pregnant within 1 year of the survey, and/or breastfeeding). Population weighted analyses were used to compare CVH scores between groups.ResultsAmong 3,992 participants (representing 46.9 million U.S. women), 10.7% were pregnant, breastfeeding, or postpartum. Overall, the mean LE8 score was 66 corresponding to moderate CVH, with similar scores among women with any pregnancy-related condition compared to nonpregnant women (65 vs 66, P = 0.20). Pregnant women (62) had the lowest LE8 scores compared to postpartum (67), breastfeeding (70), and nonpregnant women (66), respectively, P < 0.001. In addition, high-sensitive C-reactive protein levels were significantly elevated in pregnant women (4.68 mg/dL) compared to postpartum (2.39 mg/dL), breastfeeding (2.40 mg/dL), and nonpregnant women (2.06 mg/dL) (P = 0.006).ConclusionsReproductive-aged women in the United States demonstrated moderate CVH, with pregnant women exhibiting lower LE8 scores compared to postpartum and nonpregnant women. These findings highlight modifiable targets for interventions to reduce adverse pregnancy outcomes and future cardiovascular risk.

The United States has one of the highest maternal mortality rates among high-income countries.1 Adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) such as preeclampsia, preterm birth, and small for gestational age infants are major contributors to maternal mortality.2 Suboptimal cardiovascular (CV) risk factors are associated with higher APOs and a higher risk of developing CV disease (CVD) in the future.2 Importantly, there is a large knowledge gap among women regarding the association of CVD and APOs.3 Poor control of CV risk factors such as pre-pregnancy insulin resistance confers an increased risk to developing gestational diabetes.4 Comparably, pre-pregnancy obesity and elevated blood pressure (BP) increases the risk of preeclampsia and stillbirth.5,6 Despite the strong association between CV risk factors and APOs, few studies have comprehensively assessed CV health (CVH) among women of reproductive age in the United States.7 Numerous studies have examined isolated risk factors rather than an integrated framework for evaluating overall CVH.8 Prior studies have evaluated overall CVH in pregnant women using the American Heart Association (AHA) Life’s Simple 7 scores, however, data from these studies ranged from 1999 through 2014 with a need for contemporary data.9,10

Life’s Essential 8 (LE8), introduced by the AHA in 2022, provides a comprehensive framework for assessing CVH across 8 domains that, when optimized, are associated with better CVH.11 These include diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, healthy sleep, weight, blood lipids, blood glucose, and BP. In an AHA presidential advisory, a scoring algorithm ranging from 0 to 100 points for each metric was proposed with higher scores corresponding to better CVH.11 LE8 scoring allows both risk factor-specific and overall CVH scores, enabling comparisons across populations and highlighting areas most in need of intervention. Applying this framework to evaluate the CVH of women of reproductive age can fill a critical gap in the literature and inform strategies to reduce preventable maternal deaths. Therefore, in this study, we evaluated CVH using the LE8 scoring system among U.S. women, comparing pregnant, recently pregnant and/or breastfeeding, and nonpregnant women of reproductive age (ages 20-44).

Methods

Study sample

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a continuous, cross-sectional survey of the U.S. population conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics using a complex, clustered, stratified, multistage probability design.12 Data and analytic guidance are publicly available. Data are collected in 2-year cycles. Participants complete in-home interviews and undergo standardized physical examinations and laboratory testing at mobile examination centers. This study was exempt from Institutional Review Board given the use of deidentified data. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies. For this analysis, we pooled 3 NHANES cycles: 2015 to 2016, 2017–March 2020 (prepandemic), and 2021 to 2023.

Demographic and social characteristics

Demographic variables were self-reported during home interviews and included age, race/ethnicity, health insurance coverage, education, employment status, food security, and household poverty. According to the NHANES protocol, self-reported race and ethnicity was categorized as non-Hispanic Asian, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, Mexican American, or other Hispanic. Household poverty was calculated as the ratio of monthly family income to poverty levels as defined by the Department of Health and Human Services guidelines and categorized as <1.30, 1.50 to 3.50, and >3.50.13

Pregnancy status

Pregnancy status was assessed among all women aged 20 to 44 years in the Reproductive Health questionnaire. The reproductive health questionnaire collects data on menstrual and pregnancy history, current pregnancy and breastfeeding status, number of live births, use of oral contraceptives or hormone replacement therapy, having tried for a year to become pregnant, and seeking medical help with fertility.14,15 Data were also collected on history of hysterectomy as well as gestational diabetes. Nonpregnant women were compared to women with any pregnancy-related condition (defined as currently pregnant, currently breastfeeding, or postpartum women who were pregnant in the previous year).12 For the primary analysis, women were only counted once. To avoid overlapping exposure groups in the secondary analysis, women were subsequently categorized into mutually exclusive reproductive status groups defined as: currently pregnant, postpartum not breastfeeding, breastfeeding, and nonpregnant.

Health factors and laboratory measures

Comorbid conditions and prescription medication use were obtained from standardized interviews. Prior conditions such as myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, stroke, heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, liver disease, and cancer were included. Laboratory and physical measures included fasting plasma glucose, hemoglobin A1C, systolic and diastolic BP, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) were obtained. Depression symptoms were assessed with the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). Scores were categorized as none/minimal (0-4), moderate (5-9), or severe (≥10).16,17

Cardiovascular health assessment

CVH was assessed using the LE8 scoring system, consisting of 4 health behaviors (diet, physical activity, smoking, and sleep) and 4 health factors (body mass index [BMI], non-HDL cholesterol, blood glucose, and BP). A scale of 0 to 100 was used for each individual component according to the AHA’s suggested algorithm.11 These scores were individually calculated for each of the 8 metrics, summed, and divided by 8 to obtain a composite LE8 score ranging from 0 to 100. A score of 80 to 100 is considered high CVH; a score of 50 to 79 considered moderate CVH; and a score of 0 to 49 is considered low CVH.11 LE8 scores were measured separately for pregnant, breastfeeding, postpartum, and nonpregnant participants. The primary analysis compared LE8 scores between those with a pregnancy-related condition (pregnant/breastfeeding/postpartum) to nonpregnant participants. Secondary analysis compared LE8 scores among all subgroups.

For the 2015 to 2016 and 2017–March 2020 cycles, complete LE8 scores were calculated using all 8 components. For the 2021 to 2023 cycle, dietary data were not yet made available per NHANES. Imputation was performed using a surrogate LE8 score that was calculated by averaging the 7 available component scores from 2015 to 2023 (physical activity, smoking, sleep, BMI, non-HDL cholesterol, hemoglobin A1c, and BP) and scaling to an eight-component equivalent using average diet scores extrapolated from 2015 to 2020.

Statistical analysis

Analyses incorporated the NHANES complex survey design, accounting for strata, primary sampling units, and COVID-period–adjusted pooled weights to reflect differing cycle lengths (2, 3.2, and 2 years, respectively; total 7.2 years). We used interview weights for pregnancy prevalence, mobile examination center’s exam weights for hemoglobin A1C, and fasting subsample weights for fasting glucose. Pooled weights were calculated as appropriate per NHANES guidance. Continuous variables, including LE8 scores, were compared between pregnant and nonpregnant participants using Student’s t-test for normally distributed variables and Mann-Whitney U test for skewed distributions. Categorical variables were compared with chi-square tests. All pregnancy-stratified analyses were limited to women aged 20 to 44 years with nonmissing pregnancy status with variances estimated using Taylor series linearization.

Adjusted survey-weighted linear regression models evaluated differences in total LE8 CVH scores by reproductive status accounting for the complex sampling design to provide nationally representative estimates. Participants were first classified as either pregnant vs not pregnant, and then into a three-level pregnancy group (currently pregnant, breastfeeding, or postpartum). Covariates were selected a priori using a directed acyclic graph-informed framework and included age, NHANES-categorized race/ethnicity, income-to-poverty ratio as an indicator of socioeconomic status, and survey cycle, given their plausible associations with both reproductive status and CVH.

Results are expressed as beta (β) coefficients with corresponding 95% CIs, and a 2-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Standard linear regression assumptions were evaluated. Variance inflation factors demonstrating low multicollinearity (mean variance inflation factor = 1.66) and visual inspection of residual histograms and residual-versus-fitted plots demonstrating no major deviations from normality or homoscedasticity.” Analyses were performed with Stata 17 (StataCorp LLC).

Results

Baseline characteristics

In the pooled NHANES sample, a total of 27,827 participants with complete data were identified. We excluded those self-identifying as male (n = 11,820); we also excluded women outside the age range specified of 20 to 44 years (n = 9,008), resulting in a final cohort of 6,999 women. After excluding those with missing pregnancy status (n = 3,007), the final analytic sample comprised of 3,992 women of reproductive age representing a weighted sample of 46,890,088 adults (Figure 1). Health factor control

Pregnant/postpartum women had significantly lower fasting blood sugar (93.9 vs 100 mg/dL; P < 0.001) and hemoglobin A1C levels (5.2% vs 5.4%; P < 0.001) as compared to nonpregnant women (Table 2). Systolic (114 vs 110 mm Hg; P < 0.001) and diastolic BPs (69 vs 65 mm Hg; P < 0.001) were higher in the nonpregnant cohort compared to pregnant/postpartum women. Pregnant/postpartum women had higher total cholesterol levels (197 vs 180 mg/dL; P < 0.001), direct HDL (62 vs 57; P = 0.002), and triglyceride levels (111 vs 83; P = 0.023) compared to nonpregnant women. However, LDL was similar between groups (109 vs 102 mg/dL; P = 0.40). Pregnant/postpartum women had similar activity levels per week, but increased sleep hours (8.1 vs 7.7 hours; P < 0.001) compared to nonpregnant women. Life’s Essential 8 scores

The mean total CVH (LE8) score was 65.8 for the overall cohort from 2015 to 2020 (Table 3). Total CVH score was similar in pregnant/postpartum combined cohort compared to nonpregnant women (65 vs 66; P = 0.235) (Figure 2). When examining components of the score, there were no differences in the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension diet score, physical activity, tobacco or nicotine exposure, sleep health, BMI, or BP scores (Table 3). Pregnant/postpartum women had significantly lower blood lipid scores due to elevated lipid levels compared to nonpregnant women (68 vs 77; P < 0.001). Blood glucose scores were lower in nonpregnant individuals compared to pregnant/postpartum women (93 vs 96; P < 0.001). Discussion

In this study, we used a large national data set to provide critical insights into the CVH profiles of reproductive-aged women (20-44 years) in the United States using the AHA LE8 scores. First, we found that overall CVH was moderate among this population of women of reproductive age, with lower scores for pregnant women compared to breastfeeding, postpartum, and nonpregnant participants, suggesting that the antepartum period may be a critical window into maternal CVH health and also that prevention efforts should start early.2,18,19 Second, pregnant participants demonstrated worse CVH health compared to postpartum and nonpregnant participants, driven largely by worse lipid, BMI, and physical activity scores, in addition to elevated hs-CRP levels. The dynamic and physiological changes of both lipids and BMI during pregnancy can likely account for the scores observed. These findings remained evident after adjusting for race and socioeconomic status. Finally, over 25% of reproductive-aged women had moderate to severe depression, highlighting the need to consider mental health as an integral component of CVH management during the reproductive years.17 Taken together, these results demonstrate important factors impacting CVH health among reproductive-aged women in the United States and highlight the opportunity to reframe pregnancy and the reproductive period as a window for early CV risk identification, intervention, and ultimately prevention (Central Illustration).Central IllustrationLife’s Essential 8 Scores in Reproductive-Aged WomenIn this study from NHANES of nearly 4,000 females of reproductive age, pregnant women had the lowest LE8 scores as compared to postpartum, breastfeeding and nonpregnant women. Life’s Essential 8 Scores in Reproductive-Aged Women

Our study findings showing predominantly moderate CVH profiles observed among pregnant, breastfeeding, postpartum, and nonpregnant women which aligns with prior reports. In an earlier NHANES analysis from 1999 to 2014, approximately 61% of pregnant women in the United States had moderate CVH, as assessed using the Life’s Simple 7, with score distributions closely mirroring our present study.9 Our analysis, which extends this work to a contemporary NHANES cohort and incorporates the updated LE8 framework, further underscores the persistent challenge of achieving optimal CVH in women during their reproductive years. Previous research has shown that poor CVH health at the start of pregnancy is associated with APOs, and disparities persist in suboptimal CVH profiles by race, ethnicity, geography, and social determinants of health.8,18, 19, 20, 21 Regardless of pregnancy status, we found that Black women had increased likelihood of lower LE8 scores and worse CVH. These findings are concordant with prior analyses with 1 study showing 35 million excess deaths among Black women compared to White women in data from 1999 to 2020.22 Screening women for AHA LE8’s in the pre-pregnancy and postpartum period presents an opportunity for early recognition and intervention in CVH health.

The suboptimal CVH profiles we observed in pregnant women, particularly in BMI, lipids, and physical activity, likely reflect both pre-pregnancy risk factor burden and the physiologic demands of the antepartum period. Women with high pre-pregnancy risk factors such as elevated BP, insulin resistance, or family history of diabetes, are more likely to develop gestational diabetes during pregnancy.2 Meanwhile, those with elevated BP or obesity are more likely to develop preeclampsia and gestational hypertension.2,5,23 BMI is an important component in the LE8 score and should be interpreted cautiously in pregnant patients. Although weight gain is an expected physiologic change during pregnancy, excessive weight gain in pregnancy above the recommended weight gain guidelines is associated with an increased CV mortality and all-cause mortality.24 One study estimated a significant proportion of pregnant individuals in the United States exceeded the recommended weight gain guidelines.25 Monitoring excess weight gain during pregnancy is important for reducing APOs and mitigating risk of future CVD.

Pre-pregnancy lipid levels are associated with the risk of gestational diabetes and hypertensive diseases of pregnancy.26,27 The lipid levels in the pregnancy group, however, must be interpreted in the appropriate context. To support fetal development, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL increase throughout gestation and peak near term which is a normal physiologic response.28, 29, 30 This may explain why in our study, the average total cholesterol was significantly higher in pregnant/postpartum women compared to nonpregnant participants. However, when further stratified by currently pregnant, postpartum, and breastfeeding status, there was some improvement in lipid profiles, most significantly triglycerides, after delivery. For patients with pre-existing lipid profile alterations, these changes during pregnancy may lead to adverse outcomes for the mother and offspring and so it is imperative to ensure they normalize in the postpartum period.31 Given that women were pregnant at the time of exam and the timing of the exam is unknown with respect to gestational age, it is unclear how these levels in the pregnant cohort differed from their pre-pregnancy lipid profiles. Thus, it is important to monitor the physiological adaptations of pregnancy throughout the peripartum period to identify women with residual high future risk of adverse CVD outcomes.

Physical activity, exercise, and sleep are other important elements of CVH in the antepartum period. The American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology recommends at least 30 minutes of moderate intensity exercise every day, for at least 3 to 4 days a week during pregnancy to maintain optimal CVH.18 However, in our study, pregnant participants had lower physical scores compared to nonpregnant women, consistent with previous research showing that women are more likely to reduce physical activity in response to pregnancy.32,33 However, encouragingly, minutes of moderate and vigorous exercise did increase in the postpartum population. In prior studies, achieving 150 to 300 minutes of moderate intensity activity per week was shown to reduce the risk of gestational diabetes, gestational weight gain, and postpartum depression.34 In addition, higher levels of physical activity before and during pregnancy were associated with 25% reduction in the odds of preterm delivery.35 Adequate sleep has also been shown to have significant benefits during pregnancy.36 In our cohort, both pregnant and nonpregnant participants had the recommended amount of sleep. Sufficient sleep duration has been associated with reduced risk of several maternal and fetal adverse events including preeclampsia and gestational diabetes.37

Dynamic changes in inflammation are observed in pregnancy due to hormonal and immunological adaptations to promote maternal-fetal implantation.38 These changes promote fetal well-being but also alter maternal immune response and may predispose women to diseases. In our cohort, we noted significantly elevated hs-CRP levels in pregnant participants. High hs-CRP has been linked to an increased risk of CVD in multiple studies.39 In the Women’s Health Study, hs-CRP was compared to LDL levels and was found to be a stronger predictor of CV events than LDL.40 In addition, hs-CRP has also been studied in pregnancy and has been linked to the development of gestational diabetes and hypertensive diseases of pregnancy.41,42 Notably, hs-CRP levels did decrease significantly in the postpartum period. Further analysis to understand the hs-CRP averages on a population level can help identify the most at-risk populations. Intensive cardiac risk factors optimization in women with elevated hs-CRP is one way to mitigate the risk of future cardiac events in this population. Future analyses investigating the relationship between hs-CRP levels and APO are needed to investigate the possibility of hs-CRP acting as an intermediate link between CVH and APO.

Lastly, depression is a well-established risk factor for CAD and has been linked to adverse CV events.43 In our analysis, moderate to severe depression was present in 29% of the cohort with overall similar prevalence in women with any pregnancy-related condition and nonpregnant women. However, in subgroup analyses, the prevalence of moderate and severe depression was highest in the postpartum group. In prior studies, depression was associated with 1.77 times higher risk of developing CAD compared to those without any depressive symptoms.44 In addition, depression has also been associated with an increased risk of preterm birth and low birth weight.45 Given that nearly one-third of reproductive-aged women in the United States experience moderate to severe depression, prioritizing resource allocation for effective management, ensuring access to adequate support systems, and clarifying the underlying drivers of these high rates are critical public health initiatives.

Strengths and limitations

There are several important limitations to consider. Data from NHANES are cross-sectional which limits the ability to provide longitudinal data on individual participants and limits information regarding disease severity. In addition, NHANES is a self-reported survey which can result in several forms of biases. However, for this analysis, we used objective lab data to assess several variables such as blood glucose and lipid control. Still, data regarding timing of examination during pregnancy (ie gestational age and trimester) are not available in NHANES or data regarding antepartum pregnancy complications as well as neonatal outcomes. However, the addition of postpartum women and breastfeeding women is a strength of our analysis and provides some insights into how changes in pregnancy may evolve shortly after delivery. Given the challenges with studying the pregnant population on a national scale, our sample size and data provide important insights on the health of this population.

Conclusions

Reproductive-aged women in the United States demonstrated overall moderate CVH using the AHA’s LE8 scoring system, with similar CVH scores between pregnant/postpartum women and nonpregnant women. High depression prevalence in both groups and elevated hs-CRP levels in the pregnant cohort highlight additional important risk factors which play a critical role in maternal health and outcomes. Although some of the cardiometabolic changes to BMI, lipid levels, and glucose may improve after delivery, ensuring normalization and addressing residual risk are imperative. These findings can guide future studies to understand drivers and design interventions with the goal of ultimately improving CVH and reducing maternal mortality.
