Phylogenetic and evolutionary insights into Oedogoniales from nine newly sequenced chloroplast genomes
Authors: Yu Li, Qingyu Dai, Guoxiang Liu, Qian Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe order Oedogoniales (Chlorophyceae, Chlorophyta) comprises over 600 described species, divided into three genera—Oedogonium, Oedocladium, and Bulbochaete—based on the presence of branches and hairs. Current classification relies primarily on morphological traits, yet the complex morphological features make species identification challenging. Moreover, limited molecular data have hindered phylogenetic analyses, leaving the phylogeny of Oedogonium problematic. A previous study proposed a new taxonomic classification of the genus Oedogonium based on basal cell shape, splitting it into two sections: section Globosum and section Elongatum. In the present study, we sequenced nine chloroplast (cp.) genomes of Oedogoniales, including four from section Globosum and five from section Elongatum, and performed comparative analyses to elucidate the phylogenetic and evolutionary relationships between these two sections.ResultsThe nine newly sequenced Oedogoniales cp. genomes exhibited highly conserved structures and gene numbers, identical to those previously reported for this order. Nevertheless, variations in genome size, AT content, introns, repeats, and some structural rearrangements were observed. Phylogenetic analyses based on 64 cp. protein‑coding genes, using Bayesian inference and ASTRAL species trees constructed from amino acid and nucleotide datasets, consistently supported the division of Oedogoniales into two sections according to basal cell shape, with the genus Oedocladium clustering within section Elongatum. Evolutionary analysis revealed that 27 genes showed significant differences in dN and dS between the two sections, and three genes—psbA, atpA, and psbC—were identified as undergoing rapid evolution and positive selection. These findings suggest that species of section Globosum experienced rapid radiation and have retained ancestral morphological characteristics since their divergence from the ancestral lineage.ConclusionsOur results enrich the data on cp. genomes of the Oedogoniales. The availability of these cp. genomes can help in understanding the cp. genome characteristics and resolve phylogenetic and evolutionary relationships of the order Oedogoniales.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09188-0.Keywords: Oedogoniales, Oedogonium, Chloroplast genome, Phylogeny, Evolution

Introduction

The order Oedogoniales(Chlorophyceae, Chlorophyta) comprises three genera: Oedogonium Link ex Hirn, Oedocladium Stahl, and Bulbochaete Agardh [1–4]. More than 600 species have been described from this order, predominantly inhabiting freshwater environments worldwide. At the genus level, the presence of branches and hairs serves as key diagnostic traits: Oedogonium is characterized by simple, unbranched filaments; Oedocladium exhibits branched filaments; and Bulbochaete produces hairs with bulbous bases [4–13]. During the past few years, phycologists have used different characteristics as criteria for dividing the three genera [5–10]. Based on his extensive field investigations and specimen collections across China, Jao [10], in his monograph A Monograph of the Oedogoniales of China, proposed that the type of sexual differentiation should be regarded as the primary taxonomic character for all three genera of the Oedogoniales. Mrozińska [5] proposed that the number of spermatozoids of the antheridial cell should be chosen as the criterion in taxonomical classification below the rank of genera for Oedogonium and Bulbichate.

Traditionally, the taxonomy of the Oedogoniales has been predominantly based on morphological characters, and molecular phylogenetic studies on this order have remained limited. The main findings from existing studies indicated that the genus Oedogonium might not be monophyletic, and morphological characteristics may not define phylogenetic groups; Oedocladium formed a separate clade within Oedogonium, whereas Bulbochaete was relatively distant from the other two genera [14–19]. However, these conclusions were drawn from data based on a very limited number of species. Using chloroplast genome protein‑coding genes [20, 21], mitogenome protein-coding genes [22], and single-copy orthogroups derived from transcriptomes [23], Xiong et al. provided evidence that Oedogonium is polyphyletic. A subsequent phylogenetic analysis of 47 Oedogonium specimens from China using 18 S rDNA, ITS, and rbcL gene sequences further supported the polyphyly of Oedogonium, with Oedocladium and Bulbochaete clustering within the Oedogonium clade [24]. Based on these phylogenetic results and a comparison of the most important morphological characteristics used in traditional taxonomy, the authors proposed dividing the genus Oedogonium into two sections: section Globosum (species with spherical or sub-hemispherical basal cells) and section Elongatum (species with elongated basal cells) [24]. Whlie, the phylogenetic studies of Oedogoniales still mainly based on one or a few molecular markers, this approach retains inherent limitations, implying that the gene tree may not always be equivalent to the species tree.

Chloroplast genomes evolve slowly and are maternally inherited, making them highly suitable for molecular evolution and phylogenetic studies [25–29]. Consequently, plastomes are increasingly employed in investigations of green algal evolution and phylogeny. For example, phylogenomic analysis of complete chloroplast genomes confirmed the polyphyly of the genus Klebsormidium and its type species K. flaccidum, and placed Streptofilum as a distinct early‑diverging lineage sister to the clade containing Klebsormidiophyceae and Phragmoplastophyta [30]. Low-light adaptation in the green alga Ostreobium quekettii has resulted in a compact, gene-dense chloroplast genome, slower evolutionary rates, and stronger purifying selection on photosynthetic genes [31]. However, only 12 cp. genomes are currently published for the order Oedogoniales, including 8 from genus Oedogonium and 4 grom Oedocladium; adding more cp. genomes from this group would greatly facilitate deeper understanding of its phylogeny and evolutionary processes.

In this study, we sequenced nine additional cp. genomes of Oedogoniales and performed comparative analyses together with 11 previously reported Oedogoniales cp. genomes including 7 from genus Oedogonium and 4 from Oedoclaium. Using common protein-coding genes, we conducted phylogenetic and evolutionary analyses to further explore the phylogenetic placement and evolutionary processes of the order Oedogoniales, with a particular focus on the two sections. The results of this study will enhance our understanding of the phylogenetic and evolutionary relationships within the Oedogoniales.

Methods

Sampling, culture conditions, DNA extraction, and morphological observation

Nine Oedogoniales strains were isolated from water or soil samples in China and have been deposited in the Freshwater Algae Culture Collection at the Institute of Hydrobiology (strain FACHB collection), Wuhan, Hubei, China. Among them, four strains (FACHB-980, FACHB-994, FACHB-999, FACHB-1003) have been previously reported [19], the remaining five (FACHB-3893–FACHB-3897) are newly isolated and the sample collection details were listed in Supplementary Table S1 All strains were cultured in liquid BG11 medium at 25 °C under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Genomic DNA was extracted using a Universal DNA Isolation Kit (Axygen, Suzhou, China) [56]. Morphological observationswere conducted using an Olympus BX53 light microscope (Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an Olympus DP80 digital camera and CellSens software (Tokyo, Japan).

Library preparation, sequencing, genome assembly, and annotation

The procedures for library preparation, sequencing, genome assembly, and annotation followed our previously described protocols [20, 21] with minor modifications. Briefly, paired-end (PE150) libraries were prepared using the NEB Next Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) and sequenced on a DNBSEQ-T7 platform (BGI) by Novogene (Beijing, China). Raw reads were trimmed using SOAPnuke [32] and assembled using SPAdes [33]. Chloroplast (cp.) contigs were identified by BLAST (e‑value ≤ 1e⁻⁵ against Chlorophyta cp. genomes), GC content (< 45%), and sequencing depth (> 100×). Contig order was resolved by BWA-MEM [34] and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Cp genome annotation was performed with GeSeq [35] using previously reported Oedogoniales cp. genomes as references, followed by manual refinement using BLAST. tRNA genes were identified with tRNAscan‑SE [36]. Intron boundaries and subgroup affiliations were determined by comparing with intron‑less homologs and modeling secondary structures using RNAweasel [37–39]. Repeats (> 30 bp) were detected with Vmatch and masked with RepeatMasker. Genome maps were drawn using OrganellarGenomeDRAW [40]. The annotated cp. genome sequences have been deposited in GenBase [41] in National Genomics Data Center [42], Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences/China National Center for Bioinformation, under accession number C_AA132389.1 to C_AA132397.1 that is publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/genbase.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analyses were performed using concatenated amino acid and nucleotide sequences of 64 cp. protein‑coding genes, including atpA, atpB, atpE, atpF, atpH, atpI, cemA, chlB, chlL, chlN, clpP, ftsH, petB, petD, petG, petL, psaA, psaB, psaC, psaJ, psaM, psbA, psbB, psbC, psbD,psbE, psbF, psbH, psbI, psbJ, psbK, psbL, psbM, psbN, psbT, psbZ, rbcL, rpl14, rpl16, rpl2, rpl20, rpl23, rpl32, rpl36, rpl5, rpoA, rpoBb, rpoC1, rps11, rps12, rps14, rps18, rps19, rps2, rps3, rps4, rps7, rps8, rps9, tufA, ycf1, ycf12, ycf3, ycf4. Alignments were generated with MAFFT 7.0 [43] for amino acids and with MEGA7 [44] with the MUSCLE algorithm [45] for nucleotides. Ambiguous sites were removed using trimAl 1.2 [46] with the parameter gt = 1. Supermatrix concatenation was done with PhyloSuite [47], producing a 73,248‑bp nucleotide alignment and a 24,296‑aa amino acid alignment. Partitioning and evolutionary models were selected using PartitionFinder 2 [48](Supplementary Table S2). Bayesian inference was performed with MrBayes v3.2.6 [49] (four chains, 3 million generations, sampling every 1,000 generations, burn‑in 1,000). Maximum likelihood trees were inferred with RAxML [50] under GTRGAMMAI (nucleotide) and PROTGAMMA GTR (amino acid). Coalescent‑based species trees were reconstructed using ASTRAL [51]. Whole‑cp‑genome synteny was analyzed with Mauve v2.3.1 [52], and average nucleotide identity (ANI) was calculated using FastANI [53].

Evolutionary analysis

Rates of nonsynonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) substitution were estimated with the CODEML program in PAML v4.9 [54] under the ML model (runmode = − 2, CodonFreq = 2), based on 54 chloroplast protein-coding genes. Comparisons between species groups were made using the two‑tailed Wilcoxon rank‑sum test with false discovery rate (FDR) correction [55].

To detect positive selection, the branch‑site model (model = 2, Nsites = 2) [56] was applied, with the five species of section Globosum as the foreground branch. The null model assumed dN/dS = 1 (fix_omega = 1, omega = 1), while the alternative model allowed dN/dS > 1 (fix_omega = 0, omega = 2). Likelihood ratio tests were performed, and P‑values were corrected for multiple testing (FDR). Sites under positive selection were identified using the Bayes empirical Bayes (BEB) method. Genes with FDR‑adjusted P < 0.05 were considered to be under positive selection.

Furthermore, the basal cells of these four strains were noted to belong to section Globosum. The remaining strain, FACHB-3897, was identified as Oe. capillare (Fig. 1e-g).

General characteristics and comparison of Oedogonium cp. genomes

The chloroplast (cp.) genome characteristics of the nine newly sequenced Oedogoniales species were listed in (Table 1). Genome sizes varied between 163,127 bp (Oe. brevicingulatum FACHB‑999 and Oe. sp. FACHB‑994) and 218,922 bp (Oe. sp. FACHB‑3896). A typical circular quadripartite structure was observed in all nine genomes (Supplementary Figs. 1–9), consisting of an LSC region (75,411 − 104,752 bp), an SSC region (42,659 − 57,956 bp), and two IR regions (19,868 − 40,778 bp). Overall AT content was similar across the nine cp. genomes, with only minor interspecific variation, ranging from 69.51% (strain FACHB‑3896) to 71.90% (Oe. brevicingulatum FACHB‑999 and Oe. sp. FACHB‑994). The proportion of coding sequences also showed variation, with values between 51.4% (O. carolinianum) and 66.08% (Oe. crispum). Compared with the Oedocladium species, the cp. genomes of the six Oedogonium species were moderately more compact. All cp. genomes encoded 68 protein‑coding genes and three rRNA genes. Minor differences in tRNA content were detected: all nine genomes encoded 21 types of tRNAs, but with slight copy‑number variations. Specifically, Oe. nodulosum and Oe. capillare (strain FACHB‑3897) each possessed three additional trnR copies (two in the IR regions and one in the LSC region), while Oe. sp. (strain FACHB‑1003) contained four additional trnR copies (two in the IRs and two in the LSC). No obvious differences in repeat content were observed among the nine cp. genomes.

Synteny analysis and average nucleotide identity analysis

To compare gene order, we employed ProgressiveMauve with Oe. cardiacum as the reference genome (Fig. 2). Across the 20 Oedogoniales cp. genomes (16 Oedogonium and 4 Oedocladium taxa), a total of 19 locally collinear blocks (LCBs) were detected. The 20 genomes were largely syntenic; notably, Oe. nodulosum (FACHB‑980) and Oe. sp. (FACHB‑994) shared particularly high sequence similarity. However, some short LCBs showed evidence of rearrangements and inversions. For instance, in Oe. capillare (FACHB‑3897), two large fragments (totaling ~ 76.8 kb) were found to be rearranged and inverted. The first fragment (< 57 kb) carried ccsA, psbZ, psbA, ftsH, trnF-GAA, trnR-CCG, and psbC; the second (< 19 kb) contained other LSC‑located genes. In Oe. sp. (FACHB‑1003), a small inversion (< 3 kb) involving trnR-TCG and petD was identified.

Synteny comparison of Oedogoniales algae chloroplast genomes using progressiveMauve. The coloured syntenic blocks are local collinear blocks; blocks above the centre line indicate they are on the same strand, and blocks below the center line indicate they are on the opposite strand

We calculated the average nucleotide identity (ANI) for the 20 Oedogoniales species using FastANI (Fig. 3). Five species characterized by spherical or sub‑hemispherical basal cells (strains FACHB‑3893, FACHB‑3894, FACHB‑3895, FACHB‑3896, and FACHB‑3311) displayed high mutual ANI values. Moreover, Oe. sp. (strain FACHB‑1003) demonstrated high ANI with the three O. carolinianum strains (FACHB‑2453, NC_031510, and KX507373.1). High ANI values were also found between Oe. sp. (FACHB‑994) and O. brevicingulatum (FACHB‑999) (100% ANI), as well as between O. dentireticulatum (MW250871.1) and Oe. capillare (FACHB‑3897). Three additional strains - Oe. capilliforme (MW250874.1), Oe. cardiacum (EU677193.1), and Oe. cardiacum (NC_011031.1)—shared high similarity, with ANI values exceeding 96.0%; the two Oe. cardiacum strains showed 100% ANI.

IR expansion and contraction

We compared the IR boundary regions across the nine Oedogoniales cp. genomes (Supplementary Fig. 10). IR sizes varied considerably: the largest (approximately 40,000 bp) was found in Oe. sp. FACHB‑3896, while the smallest (19,867 bp) occurred in Oe. sp. FACHB‑3894. All nine genomes shared an identical set of four protein‑coding genes, three tRNAs, and three rRNAs within their IRs. Nevertheless, species‑specific additions were noted. For example, Oe. capillare FACHB‑3897 and FACHB‑1003 each gained an extra trnR(cct) between psbA and rbcL; Oe. sp. FACHB‑3896 acquired two additional rpoB copies; and Oe. nodulosum FACHB‑980 possessed both two extra rpoB and an extra trnR(ccg). Except for Oe. sp. FACHB‑1003 and FACHB‑3897, the IRa region of the other seven species included a portion of the 5′ end of ccsA, with species‑specific lengths: 608 bp in four strains (FACHB‑3894, ‑3895, ‑3896, ‑3893); 387 bp in Oe. nodulosum FACHB‑980; 398 bp in Oe. sp. FACHB‑994 and Oe. brevicingulatum FACHB‑999; and 390 bp in Oe. cardiacum. By contrast, in Oe. sp. FACHB‑1003, ccsA was located 162 bp from the IRa boundary, whereas in Oe. capillare FACHB‑3897, a 389‑bp segment of the ccsA 3′ end resided within the IRb region. The four boundary regions were highly conserved across the nine genomes, with only minor variations. The LSC/IRb junctions (JLBs) fell within trnR in four strains (FACHB‑3894, ‑3895, ‑3896, ‑3893), with 6 bp of the gene’s 3′ end extending into the IR. In Oe. capillare FACHB‑3897, the JLB occurred inside ccsA, while in the other four genomes it was situated between trnR(ccu) and psbA. The IRb/SSC boundaries were consistently located between trnL(caa) and psaA in all nine genomes, and the SSC/IRa junctions all fell within rpoA.

The IRa/LSC junctions (JSAs) exhibited species‑specific variation. Specifically, the JSA was located between psbA and ccsA in Oe. sp. FACHB‑1003, whereas it occurred between psbA and trnR in Oe. capillare FACHB‑3897. In the other seven strains, the JSA was situated within ccsA, with a portion of the gene’s 5′ end extending into the IRa region. The lengths of these ccsA extensions were species‑dependent: 608 bp in four strains (FACHB‑3894, ‑3895, ‑3896, ‑3893); 387 bp in Oe. nodulosum FACHB‑980; 398 bp in Oe. sp. FACHB‑994 and Oe. brevicingulatum FACHB‑999; and 390 bp in Oe. cardiacum.

Phylogenetic analysis and adaptive evolution analysis

Phylogenetic analyses based on 64 cp. protein-coding genes conducted by Bayesian analyses with amino acid and nucleotide datasets revealed two kinds of phylogenetic trees showing almost same results (Figs. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 11), both indicating all these Oedogoniales clusted into two clades with high support values. In the first clade, the species with elongated basal cells clustered with the Oedocladium species, and in the second clade, the species all showed spherical or sub-hemispherical basal cells. And the species trees by Astral based on the two datasets also supported the two clades consisted by species with spherical or sub-hemispherical basal cells and elongated basal cells (Figs. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 12), while for the Oedocladium, the two results showed a little difference. For the genus Oedocladium, Bayesian inference and ASTRAL analyses revealed slight differences in the phylogenetic placement of O. prescottii and O. carolinianum, both of which clustered within Oedogonium. The species tree inferred from the nucleotide dataset placed all Oedocladium species within a small clade together with Oedogonium species. A similar topology was obtained from the amino acid dataset, which was largely congruent with the Bayesian inference results based on both amino acid and nucleotide datasets.

Evolutionary analysis

Based on the maximum likelihood (ML) analysis of 64 chloroplast protein-coding genes, we compared dN and dS values between two groups within the Oedogoniales: species possessing elongated basal cells versus those with spherical or sub‑hemispherical basal cells. Across all strains, the five spherical/sub‑hemispherical‑celled strains displayed higher dN and dS values than their elongated‑celled counterparts (Fig. 6). A subsequent comparison between the two morphological groups identified 27 genes with significant differences (Supplementary Table S3), where FDR‑adjusted P‑values for both dN and dS were below 0.05. We then applied the branch model implemented in PAML to compare dN/dS ratios between the two groups, designating the five spherical/sub‑hemispherical‑celled species as the foreground branch. This analysis revealed that 12 genes were undergoing rapid evolution (Supplementary Table S4).

Boxplots of the non-synonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) substitutions for each species of the twenty Oedogoniales strains. For each species, the box represents values between quartiles, outliers are shown as black points, and black lines inside the box show median values

We employed the branch‑site model to detect positive selection, comparing the null and alternative models. Under the null model, the foreground branch was assumed to evolve with dN/dS = 1, whereas the alternative model allowed certain sites on the foreground branch to have dN/dS > 1 (positive selection). When the five species with spherical or sub-hemispherical basal cells were designated as the foreground branch, three genes-psbA, atpA, and psbC-exhibited FDR-adjusted P-values below 0.05 (Supplementary Table S5). Bayes empirical Bayes (BEB) analysis further suggested that psbA may contain positively selected sites, with the 81st histidine (His) residue showing a posterior probability > 99% (Supplementary Table S6). Nevertheless, because functional site information for closely related species (e.g., Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and Stigeoclonium helveticum) was currently unavailable in UniProt, the candidate positively selected sites in psbA require further validation.

Discussion

In this study, we sequenced the chloroplast genomes of nine Oedogoniales species. Two strains were successfully assigned to existing species: FACHB-980 was identified as Oe. nodulosum, and FACHB-999 as Oe. brevicingulatum. All remaining strains (FACHB‑3893–3896, possessing spherical or sub‑hemispherical basal cells; and FACHB‑1003, FACHB‑994, FACHB‑3897, with elongated basal cells) had unbranched filaments, a characteristic feature of the genus Oedogonium. However, species‑level identification was not possible for these seven strains because complete sexual characteristics were absent.

Comparative analysis of the nine Oedogoniales cp. genomes showed that their structure and gene content are highly conserved. The five newly sequenced Oedogonium cp. genomes possessed a quadripartite organization identical to that of previously reported Oedogoniales cp. genomes, with no structural alterations. Total genome length varied markedly (163,127–218,922 bp), a difference likely attributable to IR region contraction/expansion and variation in non‑coding sequence length, such as introns [57]. Moreover, all nine cp. genomes contained nearly identical numbers of protein‑coding genes and rRNAs, but minor differences in tRNA content were detected. Intron content differed among the nine genomes, especially in group I introns, mainly due to variation within psbA and the presence of open reading frames (ORFs) in this gene. Regarding GC content, the five strains with spherical or sub‑hemispherical basal cells exhibited relatively higher values (more than 30%). Additionally, Oe. brevicingulatum (FACHB‑999) shared the same genomic features as Oe. sp. (FACHB‑994). Synteny analyses revealed a relatively high degree of collinearity across the nine cp. genomes; nevertheless, certain rearrangements and inversions were observed within short locally collinear blocks (LCBs) in Oe. capillare (FACHB‑3897) and Oe. sp. (FACHB‑1003). Specifically, an inversion of less than 3 kb was detected in Oe. sp. (strain FACHB-1003), consistent with earlier reports [20, 21] describing the same inversion in two O. carolinianum strains. This finding was further corroborated by FastANI analysis, which demonstrated high similarity between strain FACHB‑1003 and the three O. carolinianum strains. Taken together, these results suggest that strain FACHB‑1003 should be reclassified as O. carolinianum. Moreover, because Oe. brevicingulatum (FACHB‑999) showed 100% ANI and identical genomic characteristics with Oe. sp. (FACHB‑994), strain FACHB‑994 should be identified as Oe. brevicingulatum.

Moreover, strains with spherical or sub-hemispherical basal cells exhibited high similarity among themselves compared with other species, supporting the taxonomic classification of the genus Oedogonium into two sections, section Globosum and section Elongatum, as proposed in a previous study [24].

The IR regions represent the most conserved segments of chloroplast genomes, and frequent expansions and contractions at the SSC/IR and LSC/IR junctions serve as evolutionary signals that reflect interspecific relationships [58–62]. Here, only minor variations were detected at these boundaries among the nine Oedogoniales species. Notably, strain FACHB‑3896 possessed the longest IR region (an expansion), whereas the other strains displayed IR contractions. Previous studies have indicated that IR expansion and contraction often contributed to variations in genome size and can provide valuable information for phylogenetic and genome evolution analyses [59, 60, 63]. Furthermore, gene conversion during speciation is considered a potential mechanism underlying small-scale IR expansions or contractions [59, 60, 64–66].

Phylogenetic analyses based on 64 cp. protein-coding genes, using Bayesian inference and ASTRAL species trees derived from both amino acid and nucleotide datasets, all supported the division of the Oedogoniales into two well-supported clades, consistent with previous studies [24]. Specifically, the genus Oedogonium was split into two sections—section Globosum (species with spherical or sub-hemispherical basal cells) and section Elongatum (species with elongated basal cells). The genus Oedocladium clustered within section Elongatum, although its exact phylogenetic position showed minor discrepancies among the different analyses, likely due to limited representative taxa for this group and differences in evolutionary rates between amino acid and nucleotide sequences. Nevertheless, consistent with previous phylogenetic results based on 18 S rDNA or cp. protein-coding genes, Oedocladium was resolved into two clades. Increased sample sizes are expected to substantially improve phylogenetic resolution [21, 23, 24].

In this study, substitution rates were evaluated using the maximum likelihood (ML) method implemented in PAML, which is currently considered one of the most accurate approaches for estimating substitution rates [67–69]. Statistical analyses revealed significant differences in cp. protein-coding genes between the two sections of Oedogoniales—species with spherical or sub-hemispherical basal cells versus those with elongated basal cells—suggesting that genes in species with spherical or sub-hemispherical basal cells experienced increased mutation rates during evolution. Branch and branch-site model analyses further identified atpA, psbA, and psbC as rapidly evolving genes under positive selection in species with spherical or sub-hemispherical basal cells, with the 81st histidine (His) residue of psbA identified as a positively selected site. Positively selected genes are known to play critical roles in environmental adaptation and speciation [70–74]; therefore, understanding their evolutionary history is essential for elucidating the divergence of the two sections. According to Jao [10], based on extensive sampling across China, Oedogonium comprises approximately 440 species, of which 33 possess sub-hemispherical or nearly spherical basal cells. These 33 species share several common characteristics, including relatively small filaments (typically less than 10 μm; in width), subdepressed-globose oogonia, circumcision, and smooth oospore walls—features that have been considered primitive in phenetic and cladistic taxonomic studies [75, 76]. We hypothesized that these species with primitive characteristics may have undergone rapid radiation, with a relatively short differentiation period that did not allow for the full accumulation of morphological differences, thereby retaining ancestral morphological traits. This hypothesis is further supported by the high ANI values observed among strains with spherical or sub-hemispherical basal cells, reflecting high molecular similarity. Such a pattern of molecular divergence within a morphologically conserved lineage, often referred to as cryptic speciation, has been increasingly documented in bryophytes [77–83]. Future studies incorporating comprehensive morphological characteristics, particularly sexual traits, from a broader range of Oedogoniales species will be essential for clarifying taxonomic issues and understanding the evolutionary history of this group.

Conclusion

We determined the cp. genomes of nine Oedogoniales strains and found their overall structure and gene content to be relatively conserved, despite variations in size, AT content, introns, repeats, and rearrangements. Phylogenetic analyses of 64 cp. protein‑coding genes consistently divided the Oedogoniales into two sections (Globosum and Elongatum) based on basal cell shape, with Oedocladium placed within Elongatum. Evolutionary analysis revealed 27 genes with significant dN/dS differences between sections, and three (psbA, atpA, psbC) showed signs of rapid evolution and positive selection. These results imply that Globosum species underwent rapid radiation and have retained ancestral morphology since diverging from their common ancestor.
