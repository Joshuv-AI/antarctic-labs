Age‐Dependent Prognostic Significance of Preoperative Transthyretin After Curative Resection for Stage I–III Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Ayane Kawata, Yuji Miyamoto, Hiro Nakamura, Takahiko Akiyama, Kota Arima, Katsuhiro Ogawa, Yukiharu Hiyoshi, Masaaki Iwatsuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPreoperative malnutrition and systemic inflammation are established determinants of adverse outcomes in colorectal cancer (CRC). Transthyretin (TTR), a rapid‐turnover hepatic protein, reflects both nutritional and inflammatory status; however, its clinical utility may be influenced by age‐ and sex‐related variability. This study evaluated the prognostic significance of preoperative TTR in patients undergoing curative resection for stage I–III CRC.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 536 patients who underwent curative resection for stage I–III CRC between 2014 and 2024 at a single institution was analyzed. Sex‐specific TTR cutoffs were derived using survival classification and regression tree analysis (20.9 mg/dL for men; 16.95 mg/dL for women), and patients were stratified into low‐ and high‐TTR groups. The primary endpoint was recurrence‐free survival (RFS). Survival outcomes were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis, multivariable Cox proportional hazards modeling, and age‐stratified analyses.ResultsPreoperative TTR levels were lower in women and demonstrated a progressive decline with increasing age. Patients with low TTR exhibited significantly inferior RFS compared with those with high TTR (log‐rank p < 0.0001). In multivariable analysis, low preoperative TTR remained an independent predictor of worse RFS (HR 2.407, 95% CI 1.625–3.566; p < 0.001). Age‐stratified analyses demonstrated that the prognostic impact of low TTR was most pronounced in younger patients and progressively attenuated with advancing age.ConclusionsPreoperative TTR represents an independent prognostic biomarker following curative resection for stage I–III CRC. Its prognostic utility is age‐dependent, with greatest relevance in younger patients, supporting interpretation within an age‐ and sex‐specific framework.Keywords: colorectal neoplasms, decision trees, prognosis, survival analysis, transthyretin

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health burden. According to the 2022 global cancer statistics, CRC is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide, accounting for approximately 1.9 million new cases (9.6% of all cancers) and nearly 900 000 deaths annually (9.3%) [1]. Projections indicate that by 2040, the global incidence will increase to 3.2 million new cases, with mortality reaching 1.6 million deaths per year [2]. Despite substantial advances in cancer screening, early detection, surgical techniques, and systemic therapies, the incidence and mortality of CRC continue to rise globally. This trend reflects the combined influence of lifestyle changes, demographic shifts, and persistent disparities in access to healthcare [1, 2, 3]. In this context, precise identification of patients at elevated risk of poor outcomes is essential to guide risk stratification and optimize therapeutic strategies. CRC is frequently accompanied by nutritional impairment, which arises from tumor‐related factors such as bowel obstruction, malabsorption, and systemic inflammation. In advanced disease, patients often present with preexisting malnutrition driven by cancer‐associated inflammation and cachexia [4]. Increasing evidence supports the critical role of nutritional status as a determinant of both short‐term surgical outcomes and long‐term prognosis.

Several studies have demonstrated that preoperative nutritional indices, including the prognostic nutritional index (PNI), nutritional risk index (NRI), geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI), and controlling nutritional status (CONUT) score, are significantly associated with postoperative outcomes and survival in patients undergoing curative resection for CRC [5, 6, 7, 8]. These findings underscore the importance of integrating comprehensive nutritional assessment and optimization into perioperative management.

Transthyretin (TTR) is a transport protein synthesized primarily in the liver and choroid plexus that binds thyroxine (T4) and retinol‐binding protein (RBP), which carries vitamin A (retinol) [9]. Owing to its short half‐life of approximately 2 days, TTR is classified as a rapid‐turnover protein and has been widely used as a sensitive marker of acute changes in nutritional status. Emerging evidence suggests that TTR is also associated with prognosis in gastrointestinal malignancies [10]. For example, Zhang et al. reported that reduced preoperative TTR levels are associated with poorer long‐term outcomes following CRC resection [11]. Similarly, Sato et al. demonstrated that low TTR levels are linked to adverse short‐term postoperative outcomes in older patients aged ≥ 80 years undergoing curative surgery for CRC [12]. However, TTR concentrations are substantially influenced by demographic factors, particularly age and sex, which may confound its prognostic interpretation and contribute to inconsistencies in prior studies.

In the present study, the long‐term prognostic significance of preoperative TTR was evaluated in patients undergoing curative resection for stage I–III CRC, with TTR levels classified using sex‐specific thresholds. The objective was to clarify the clinical utility of preoperative serum TTR and to identify patient subgroups in which TTR serves as a robust prognostic indicator.

Methods

Patient Selection

Consecutive patients who underwent primary surgical resection for CRC at Kumamoto University Hospital (Kumamoto, Japan) between December 2014 and April 2024 were retrospectively identified and reviewed. Eligible patients met the following inclusion criteria: histologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma, pathological stage I–III disease, curative resection, and availability of preoperative serum TTR measurements. Patients were excluded if they had stage IV disease, missing preoperative TTR data, or underwent simultaneous resection for synchronous malignancies. This retrospective, single‐center study was approved by the institutional review board of Kumamoto University Hospital (approval no. 1047) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data Collection

Clinical and pathological data were extracted from electronic medical records, including age, sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA‐PS), body mass index (BMI), primary tumor location (right‐sided colon, left‐sided colon, rectum), preoperative treatment, laboratory parameters, surgical approach, pathological findings, postoperative treatment, and recurrence‐free survival (RFS). Blood test results obtained within 7 days prior to surgery were used for serological analyses. Tumor location was anatomically classified as follows: right‐sided colon (cecum to transverse colon), left‐sided colon (descending colon to sigmoid colon), and rectum (rectosigmoid junction to anal canal). Tumor staging was determined according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) 9th edition TNM classification. Recurrence was defined as radiologically confirmed disease during postoperative surveillance. RFS was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of documented recurrence or death without evidence of recurrence.

Preoperative Laboratory Measurements

All preoperative laboratory variables were measured from blood samples collected within 7 days before surgery as part of routine clinical assessment. Serum TTR concentrations were obtained from these samples. Given the influence of sex on TTR levels, sex‐specific cutoffs for RFS were determined using survival classification and regression tree analysis, yielding optimal thresholds of 20.9 mg/dL for men and 16.95 mg/dL for women. Patients were accordingly categorized into TTR‐low and TTR‐high groups. The neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated as the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count, the platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR) as the platelet count divided by the lymphocyte count, and the PNI as 10 × serum albumin (g/dL) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (/mm3). For primary analyses, high NLR, high PLR, and low PNI were defined using established cutoffs of 5, 300, and 45, respectively, in accordance with previously published reports.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and proportions and were compared using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Time‐to‐event outcomes were evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For Cox proportional hazards analysis of RFS, a multivariable model was constructed incorporating age group, BMI, ASA‐PS, tumor location, histology, pathological T stage, lymph node metastasis, preoperative treatment, preoperative TTR (low vs. high), NLR (cutoff 5), PLR (cutoff 300), PNI (cutoff 45), and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), with variable selection performed using stepwise regression based on the Akaike information criterion. As a sensitivity analysis, the primary analyses were repeated after restricting the cohort to patients with stage II–III disease. Time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves for RFS were generated at 6‐month intervals from 6 to 60 months using the timeROC package for each biomarker (TTR, NLR, PLR, and PNI). The integrated area under the curve (iAUC) for each marker was calculated as the mean of time‐dependent AUC values across this interval, and its sampling distribution was estimated using 1000 bootstrap resamples.

Results

Among 1005 patients who underwent primary tumor resection for CRC, 536 cases (53%) met the predefined inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (Figure S1). The median follow‐up duration for RFS was 45.1 months (range, 3–91 months).

Trends in Preoperative Serum Transthyretin Levels

The distribution of preoperative TTR levels according to sex and age is presented in Figure 1. TTR concentrations demonstrated substantial variation across demographic groups. Female patients exhibited consistently lower TTR levels compared with male patients (Figure 1a), and a progressive decline in TTR was observed with increasing age (Figure 1b). These findings indicate that preoperative TTR levels are strongly influenced by demographic characteristics, underscoring the importance of interpreting TTR values within an age‐ and sex‐specific context.

Recurrence‐Free Survival According to TTR Status

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that patients in the TTR‐low group had significantly worse RFS compared with those in the TTR‐high group (log‐rank p < 0.0001; Figure 2). In multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis, low preoperative TTR remained an independent predictor of inferior RFS (HR 2.407, 95% CI 1.625–3.566; p < 0.001). Additional factors independently associated with worse RFS included higher ASA‐PS, rectal tumor location, non‐tubular histology, T4 disease, and lymph node metastasis, whereas BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 was associated with improved RFS (Table 2). Similar findings were observed in a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with stage II–III disease (Figure S2 and Tables S1 and S2).

Subgroup Analysis of the Prognostic Impact of Preoperative TTR

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the association between low preoperative TTR and reduced RFS was broadly consistent across most clinicopathological strata. Significant effect modification was observed for age group (p for interaction = 0.017) and one additional factor presented in the forest plot (p for interaction = 0.023), whereas no statistically significant interactions were identified for other variables (Figure 3). Age‐stratified Kaplan–Meier analyses revealed a stepwise attenuation in the prognostic impact of TTR from the Young group to the Old group and further to the Very Old group (Figure S3). Major tumor‐related factors, including tumor depth and lymph node metastasis, did not differ significantly between the TTR‐Low and TTR‐High groups within each age group (Tables S3–S5). In age‐stratified multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis, low preoperative TTR remained an independent predictor of inferior RFS in the Young group (HR 6.685, 95% CI 2.845–15.71; p < 0.001), but not in the Old or Very Old groups (Tables S6–S8).

In the bootstrap‐based iAUC analysis (Figure 5), predictive performance varied among evaluated variables. Preoperative TTR demonstrated favorable discriminative ability for RFS relative to several clinical and hematologic markers, with its bootstrap distribution consistently exceeding the non‐informative threshold of 0.5. Age‐stratified analyses further indicated that the predictive performance of TTR was more pronounced in younger patients than in older individuals.

Discussion

In this study, low TTR was independently associated with inferior RFS in patients with stage I–III CRC. A sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with stage II–III disease yielded results consistent with those of the overall cohort, suggesting that the prognostic significance of preoperative TTR was not solely attributable to the inclusion of stage I patients. Notably, this association demonstrated clear age dependency, with the strongest prognostic impact observed in younger patients and progressive attenuation in older individuals. These findings indicate that the clinical utility of preoperative TTR is not uniform across age groups and may be particularly informative in younger populations.

TTR is a transport protein synthesized primarily in the liver and choroid plexus that binds T4 and RBP [9]. Owing to its short half‐life of approximately two days, it is considered a sensitive indicator of acute changes in nutritional status. TTR concentrations are influenced by multiple physiological factors. Sex hormones modulate hepatic TTR synthesis, resulting in lower baseline levels in women compared with those in men [13]. In addition, hepatic protein synthesis is suppressed in the setting of chronic inflammation, and reductions in TTR have been shown to correlate with elevated C‐reactive protein levels [14]. Accordingly, TTR reflects both nutritional status and systemic inflammatory burden.

Previous studies have identified an association between low preoperative TTR and adverse outcomes following CRC resection. Zhang et al. reported that decreased TTR levels are linked to poorer long‐term outcomes; however, that analysis did not account for age‐ and sex‐related variability in TTR concentrations [11]. Similarly, Sato et al. demonstrated an association between low TTR and worse short‐term postoperative outcomes in elderly patients [12]. By incorporating sex‐specific classification and evaluating age‐stratified effects, the present study extends these findings and demonstrates that the prognostic significance of TTR is concentrated in younger patients.

A plausible explanation for this age‐dependent effect is that, in younger individuals, low TTR more specifically reflects cancer‐related malnutrition and inflammation, given higher physiological baseline levels. In contrast, in older populations, reductions in TTR may result from a combination of age‐related physiological decline, immune senescence, chronic inflammatory states, frailty, and comorbid conditions, thereby limiting its specificity as a cancer‐related biomarker [15, 16, 17, 18]. Therefore, the prognostic signal attributable to TTR may be more readily discernible in younger patients than in older cohorts.

The interplay between malnutrition, inflammation, and oncologic outcomes is well established. Malnutrition impairs host anti‐tumor immunity and has been associated with reduced tumor‐infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) density and diminished immune competence in gastrointestinal malignancies [19, 20, 21]. Conversely, tumor progression promotes the release of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL‐6 and TNF‐α, which drive catabolic pathways and contribute to cachexia, sarcopenia, and immunosuppression [22, 23, 24]. Thus, the association between low TTR and poor RFS observed in this study is consistent with previous evidence linking nutritional and inflammatory status to oncologic outcomes. Importantly, no interventional studies to date have demonstrated that correction of low TTR directly improves anti‐tumor immunity or oncologic outcomes, and the causal relationship remains to be established.

Given that TTR reflects both nutritional and inflammatory status, its clinical utility should be considered in relation to established inflammatory and nutritional markers. Although markers such as NLR, PLR, and PNI are widely used in clinical practice and can be readily derived from routine blood tests, TTR may provide complementary prognostic information because of its relatively short half‐life and sensitivity to changes in nutritional and inflammatory status. Accordingly, TTR should not be regarded as a replacement for existing clinical assessments or biomarkers, but rather as a complementary tool.

From a clinical standpoint, these findings support the incorporation of preoperative TTR into routine nutritional and prognostic assessment in patients undergoing curative resection for CRC, particularly among younger individuals. In this subgroup, recognition of low preoperative TTR may help identify patients at higher risk of recurrence and guide decisions regarding perioperative nutritional assessment and postoperative surveillance intensity. However, whether nutritional interventions aimed at improving TTR levels can directly influence oncological outcomes remains unclear and warrants further investigation. In older patients, TTR should be interpreted within the broader context of comprehensive geriatric and nutritional evaluation, rather than as an isolated prognostic indicator.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this was a retrospective single‐center study, and the potential for selection bias cannot be excluded. Second, although multivariable adjustment was performed, residual confounding may persist given that TTR is influenced by multiple physiological and clinical factors. Third, detailed data regarding preoperative nutritional interventions and comprehensive nutritional assessments were not available, and direct comparisons with other nutritional indices were limited. Therefore, validation in external multicenter cohorts and prospective investigations is required.

In conclusion, preoperative serum TTR represents a potentially valuable prognostic biomarker for RFS in patients with CRC, with the greatest clinical relevance observed in younger individuals. However, its prognostic significance varies according to patient characteristics, particularly age, and should therefore be interpreted within an individualized clinical context rather than as a universal indicator.
