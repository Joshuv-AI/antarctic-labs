Typical periodic optimization for dynamical systems: Symbolic dynamics
Wen Huang, Oliver Jenkinson, Leiye Xu and Yiwei Zhang

Abstract
. We develop a new theory of maximizing sets in dynamical systems, for the study of ergodic optimization in systems with weak hyperbolicity but where the Mañé cohomology lemma does not hold. This leads to new solutions of the Typical Periodic Optimization problem in the Lipschitz category: existence of an open dense set of Lipschitz functions such that each member has a unique maximizing measure and this measure is periodic (an equi-distribution on a single periodic orbit). The theory yields a structural theorem, that isolates the part of the system responsible for any robust non-periodic optimization. The structural theorem is developed further in the setting of symbolic dynamics: given any shift space, for typical Lipschitz functions the maximizing measure is shown to be either periodic or supported on the Markov boundary of the shift space. It follows that Contreras’ Typical Periodic Optimization theorem [ 21 ] for shifts of finite type can be extended to a wide class of shift spaces, including every sofic shift. The structural theorem is used to provide the first known example of a shift space where Typical Periodic Optimization fails despite periodic measures being dense in the set of all invariant measures.

In what follows, for a given triple

For this it will be convenient to write

Notation 3.8
.

If

\beta\left(Z,T,f\right)=\max_{m\in\mathcal{M}(Z,T)}\int f|_{Z}\,dm=\max_{m\in\mathcal{M}(Z,T)}\int f\,dm\,,

\mathcal{M}_{\max}(Z,T,f)=\left\{m\in\mathcal{M}(Z,T):\int f\,dm=\beta\left(Z,T,f\right)\right\}\,.

The following is straightforward:

Lemma 3.9
.

If
(X,T,f)\in\mathfrak{X}
,
and

is a closed non-empty
T
-invariant set, then

(a)

\beta(Z,T,f)\leq\beta(X,T,f)
.

(b)

(X,T,f)
if and only if
\beta(Z,T,f)=\beta(X,T,f)

(c)

If

\mathcal{M}_{\max}(Z,T,f)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\max}(X,T,f)
.

Proof.

(a) Immediate from the definitions, since
\mathcal{M}(Z,T)\subseteq\mathcal{M}(X,T)
.

(b) If
Z

(X,T,f)
then there exists
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(Z,T)

\int f\,d\mu=\beta(X,T,f)

\beta(Z,T,f)=\max_{m\in\mathcal{M}(Z,T)}\int f\,dm\geq\int f\,d\mu=\beta(X,T,f)

However

Conversely, if
\beta(Z,T,f)=\beta(X,T,f)

\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\max}(Z,T,f)\subseteq\mathcal{M}(Z,T)

\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\max}(Z,T,f)

∎

4.
Completely maximizing sets

The following
is a strengthening of the notion of a maximizable set:

Definition 4.1
.

For
(X,T,f)\in\mathfrak{X}
,
a closed non-empty
T
-invariant set
Y

is called

completely maximizing
for
(X,T,f)
(or simply for
f
)
if

\mathcal{M}(Y,T)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\max}(f)\,,

(4.1)

in other words if every invariant measure with support inside
Y

is
f
-maximizing.

We record the following straightforward consequence of the definition:

Lemma 4.2
.

If
(X,T,f)\in\mathfrak{X}
then

(a)

Any completely maximizing set for
f
is maximizable for
f
.

(b)

Any closed non-empty
T
-invariant subset
of a completely maximizing set for
f
is itself
a completely maximizing set for
f
.

Remark 4.3
.

In general, a completely maximizing set need not exist: for example if
X
is dynamically minimal but supports exactly two ergodic invariant measures
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
, then for any
f\in C(X)
with
\int f\,d\mu\neq\int f\,d\mu^{\prime}
,
the set
X
is maximizable, and indeed minimax, though not completely maximizing.
But
X

is the only closed non-empty
T
-invariant subset, so there are no completely maximizing sets for
f
.

Any closed non-empty invariant set is completely maximizing for some continuous function
(e.g. a constant), so
a completely maximizing set
need not be dynamically minimal.
As noted in Remark
3.3
,
a minimax set need not be dynamically minimal either.
However, a set which is both minimax and completely maximizing must be dynamically minimal:

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
(X,T,f)\in\mathfrak{X}
.
If
Y
is minimax for
f
, and also completely maximizing for
f
, then
Y
is dynamically minimal.

Proof.

Let
Y^{\prime}
be any non-empty closed
T
-invariant subset of
Y
.
By Lemma
4.2
(b),
Y^{\prime}
is completely maximizing for
f
.
In particular, there exists an
f
-maximizing measure
\mu
with
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\subseteq Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
.
But
Y
is minimax, so necessarily
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)=Y
, from which it follows that
Y^{\prime}=Y
.
So any non-empty closed
T
-invariant subset of
Y
must equal
Y
; in other words,
Y
is dynamically minimal.
∎

The following strong notion of maximizing set allows
the precise characterisation of maximizing measures

For

(a)

the support of the unique invariant probability measure.

5.
Maximizable families

In this section, we consider the space
{\rm Lip}(X)
of Lipschitz real-valued functions on
X

Collections of invariant measures will define subsets of

Notation 5.1
.

Let
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
.
For a subset
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(X,T)
, we write

{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T):=\left\{f\in{\rm Lip}(X):\mathcal{M}_{\max}(f)\cap\mathcal{N}\neq\emptyset\right\}\,,

{\rm Lip}_{\subseteq}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T):=\left\{f\in{\rm Lip}(X):\mathcal{M}_{\max}(f)\subseteq\mathcal{N}\right\}\,.

When

we write

{\rm Lip}_{Z}(X,T):={\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{M}(Z,T)}(X,T)=\left\{f\in{\rm Lip}(X):Z\text{ is $f$-maximizable}\right\}\,,

{\rm Lip}_{Z}^{\subseteq}(X,T):={\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{M}(Z,T)}_{\subseteq}(X,T)=\left\{f\in{\rm Lip}(X):\mathcal{M}_{\max}(f)\subseteq\mathcal{M}(Z,T)\right\}\,.

Remark 5.2
.

In the sequel, the subset
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(X,T)
will be chosen as either

\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{M}(Z,T)
or
\mathcal{N}={\mathcal{P}er}(Z,T)
(the set of periodic measures on
Z
, cf. Definition
6.1
), where
Z\subseteq X
is a closed invariant subset.

The following lemmas are straightforward:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
.
A closed
T
-invariant subset
Z\subseteq X
is non-empty
if and only if
{\rm Lip}_{Z}(X,T)
is non-empty.

Proof.

If
Z=\emptyset
then
\mathcal{M}(Z,T)=\emptyset
, so

Z
is not
f
-maximizable for any
f\in{\rm Lip}(X)
, i.e.
{\rm Lip}_{Z}(X,T)=\emptyset
.
If
Z\neq\emptyset
then
Z
is
f
-maximizable for constant functions, so
{\rm Lip}_{Z}(X,T)\neq\emptyset
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
.
If
\mathcal{N}
is a closed subset of
\mathcal{M}(X,T)
,
then
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)
is a closed subset of
{\rm Lip}(X)
.
In particular, if
Z\subseteq X
is a closed

-invariant subset, then

{\rm Lip}_{Z}(X,T)
is a closed subset of
{\rm Lip}(X)
.

Proof.

Suppose

, with

in
{\rm Lip}(X)
, so in particular
f_{i}\to f
in the topology of

There exist

-maximizing measures
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{N}
, and by passing to a subsequence we may assume that

\mu_{i}\to\mu\in\mathcal{N}
.
But

and noting that

then letting

\int f\,d\mu\geq\int f\,dm
for all

so

But

, therefore

, in other words

is closed.
∎

The notion of maximizable set
has the following important generalisation:

f\in{\rm Lip}(X)
there exists
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
that
is
f
-maximizable.

Maximizable families
\mathcal{Z}
for dynamical systems
(X,T)
will be a key tool in the development
that follows: if
\mathcal{Z}
is
non-trivial
(i.e. does not contain
X
itself)
then in some cases it is possible to deduce properties of
(X,T)
from properties of the members of
\mathcal{Z}
.
A first step is the straightforward observation that every maximizable family induces a covering of
{\rm Lip}(X)
:

Lemma 5.6
.

If
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
, with maximizable family
\mathcal{Z}
, then

{\rm Lip}(X)=\bigcup_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}{\rm Lip}_{Z}(X,T)\,.

(5.2)

When the maximizable family is countable, a key consequence is the following:

Lemma 5.7
.

If
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
,
and
\mathcal{Z}=\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a countable maximizable family, then

\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}int\left({\rm Lip}_{Z_{i}}(X,T)\right)

is dense in
{\rm Lip}(X)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{Z}=\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is maximizable,
Lemma
5.6
gives

{\rm Lip}(X)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm Lip}_{Z_{i}}(X,T)
.
Since
{\rm Lip}(X)
is a Baire space, and each
{\rm Lip}_{Z_{i}}(X,T)
is closed,
the Baire Category Theorem means that the union of their interiors is dense in
{\rm Lip}(X)

(see e.g.
[
1
, Thm. 3.34]
).
∎

For certain
\mathcal{N}
, in particular
if
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{M}(Z,T)
, the sets

{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X)
and
{\rm Lip}_{\subseteq}^{\mathcal{N}}(X)
have a common interior, a result that will be useful later.

Lemma 5.8
.

For
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
,
if
\mathcal{N}
is a closed convex subset of
\mathcal{M}(X,T)

then

int\left({\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\right)=int\left({\rm Lip}_{\subseteq}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\right).

Proof.

If
\mathcal{N}=\emptyset
then
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)=\emptyset={\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}_{\subseteq}(X,T)
, so the required equality holds.

Suppose that
\mathcal{N}\neq\emptyset
.
Now
{\rm Lip}_{\subseteq}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\subseteq{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)
,
so

int\left({\rm Lip}_{\subseteq}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\right)
is contained in
int\left({\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\right)
.
To prove the reverse inclusion

int\left({\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\right)\subseteq int\left({\rm Lip}_{\subseteq}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\right)
,
note that it is equivalent to showing that

int\left({\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\right)\subseteq{\rm Lip}_{\subseteq}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)
.
To prove this inclusion,
suppose
for a contradiction that there exists

f\in int\left({\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\right)\setminus{\rm Lip}_{\subseteq}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)
.

So there exists
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\max}(f)\setminus\mathcal{N}
.
Since
\mathcal{N}
is a non-empty closed convex subset of the locally convex space
\mathcal{M}(X,T)
, and
\mu\notin\mathcal{N}
,
there is a continuous linear functional
l

(defined on the space of signed measures on
X
equipped with the weak
∗
topology)
that strongly separates
\mathcal{N}
and
\mu
(see
[
1
, Cor. 5.59]
).
Identifying
l
with some
\phi\in C(X)
,
this means
there exists
\delta>0
such that
\int\phi\,d\mu=3\delta
and
\int\phi\,d\nu\leq 0
for all
\nu\in\mathcal{N}
.
Since
{\rm Lip}(X)
is uniformly dense in
C(X)
,
by the Stone-Weierstrass theorem,
there exists
\psi\in{\rm Lip}(X)
with
\|\phi-\psi\|_{C(X)}\leq\delta
,
so
\int\psi\,d\mu\geq 2\delta\geq\delta+\int\psi\,d\nu
for all
\nu\in\mathcal{N}
.
It follows that

\int(f+\epsilon\psi)\,d\mu\geq\delta\epsilon+\int(f+\epsilon\psi)\,d\nu
for all
\nu\in\mathcal{N}
,
\epsilon>0
.
So if
\epsilon>0
then
\mathcal{N}
is disjoint from
\mathcal{M}_{\max}(f+\epsilon\psi)
,
and therefore
f+\epsilon\psi\notin{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)
,
thus contradicting the fact that

f
is in the interior of
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)
.
∎

The following important result relates denseness in a space of restricted Lipschitz functions to denseness in an open set of Lipschitz functions defined on the whole space
X
.

Theorem 5.9
.

(Subsystem Reduction)

Suppose
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
, with
Z
a closed
T
-invariant subset of
X
.
Let
U
be a non-empty open subset of
{\rm Lip}(X)
such that
Z
is maximizable for all
f\in U
.
If
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(X,T)
is such that
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{M}(Z,T)}(Z,T)
is dense in
{\rm Lip}(Z)
,
then
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\cap U
is dense in
U
.

Proof.

Let
f\in U
, and choose
\delta>0
small enough that

\{g\in{\rm Lip}(X):\|f-g\|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}<\delta\}\subseteq U\,.

(5.3)

Since
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{M}(Z,T)}(Z,T)
is dense in
{\rm Lip}(Z)
, there exists
h_{\delta}\in{\rm Lip}(Z)
with
\|h_{\delta}\|_{{\rm Lip}(Z)}<\delta

such that
f|_{Z}+h_{\delta}\in{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{M}(Z,T)}(Z,T)
,
and so
there exists
\mu\in\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{M}(Z,T)
such that

\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\max}(Z,T,f|_{Z}+h_{\delta})\,.

(5.4)

By McShane’s extension theorem
[
51
]
, the Lipschitz function
h_{\delta}
can be extended to a
function

\widehat{h}_{\delta}\in{\rm Lip}(X)
with the same Lipschitz norm (see
[
78
, Thm. 1.33]
),
so
\|\widehat{h}_{\delta}\|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}=\|h_{\delta}\|_{{\rm Lip}(Z)}<\delta
,
and therefore (
5.3
) implies that

f+\widehat{h}_{\delta}\in U\,.

(5.5)

The fact that
f+\widehat{h}_{\delta}\in U
means that
Z
is maximizable for
f+\widehat{h}_{\delta}
.
Consequently, Lemma
3.9
(b) and (c) give that
\beta(Z,T,f|_{Z}+h_{\delta})=\beta(X,T,f+\widehat{h}_{\delta})
,
and

\mathcal{M}_{\max}(Z,T,f|_{Z}+h_{\delta})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\max}(X,T,f+\widehat{h}_{\delta})\,.

(5.6)

Combining (
5.4
) and (
5.6
) gives that

\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\max}(X,T,f+\widehat{h}_{\delta})
.
Since we also have that

\mu\in\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{M}(Z,T)\subseteq\mathcal{N}
,
it follows that

f+\widehat{h}_{\delta}\in{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\,,

(5.7)

so
(
5.5
) and (
5.7
) give

f+\widehat{h}_{\delta}\in{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\cap U\,.

(5.8)

Since
f
was an arbitrary member of
U
, and
\|\widehat{h}_{\delta}\|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}<\delta
can be chosen arbitrarily small, it follows from (
5.8
) that

{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\cap U
is dense in
U
.
∎

6.
Typical periodic optimization and the structural theorem

In this section, we begin the
study of the main theme of the article, typical periodic optimization.
In particular, the tools developed in
Sections
3
,
4
and
5

will be applied so as to establish a key structural theorem (Theorem
6.10
).

Definition 6.1
.

For
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
,
if
x\in X
is periodic with least period
n
, then its orbit
\{x,\ldots,T^{n-1}(x)\}

supports a unique
T
-invariant probability measure, namely

\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\delta_{T^{j}(x)}\,,

and any such
\mu
will be called a
periodic measure
. We define

{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T):=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X,T):\mu\text{ is periodic}\right\},

the set of periodic measures for the dynamical system
(X,T)
.

The following definition was discussed in Section
1
:

Definition 6.2
.

Given
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
,
a continuous function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is said to have the
periodic optimization
property
if there exists
m\in{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
such that

\int f\,dm>\int f\,d\mu\text{ for all }\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X,T)\setminus\{m\}.

The periodic optimization property leads to the following definition:

Definition 6.3
.

A dynamical system
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
has the
typical periodic optimization (TPO)

property
if there is an open dense subset
P\subseteq{\rm Lip}(X)
such that every
f\in P
has the periodic
optimization property.
For brevity, we may simply say that
(X,T)

has TPO
.

Remark 6.4
.

Note that TPO is stronger than saying that Lipschitz functions with the periodic optimization property are generic,
i.e. constitute a residual subset of
{\rm Lip}(X)
.

If
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}_{\,!}(X,T)
is defined to be the set of
all
Lipschitz functions with
the periodic optimization property, then we can express

{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}_{\,!}(X,T):=\bigcup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)}{\rm Lip}^{\{\mu\}}_{\subseteq}(X,T).

(6.1)

In general
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}_{\,!}(X,T)
need not be an open subset of
{\rm Lip}(X)
, so its interior

P(X,T):=int\left({\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}_{\,!}(X,T)\right)

will play the role of the set
P
from Definition
6.3
, i.e. if
(X,T)
has TPO
then
P(X,T)
will be dense in
{\rm Lip}(X)
.

There are two other open subsets of
{\rm Lip}(X)
that, for a given
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
, are naturally connected to periodic optimization.
The first of these, denoted by
P_{-}(X,T)
, is given by considering the interiors
of the sets
{\rm Lip}^{\{\mu\}}_{\subseteq}(X,T)
that appear
in (
6.1
), and then taking their union

P_{-}(X,T):=\bigcup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)}int\left({\rm Lip}^{\{\mu\}}_{\subseteq}(X,T)\right)=\bigcup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)}int\left({\rm Lip}^{\{\mu\}}(X,T)\right),

(6.2)

where we note that the second equality in (
6.2
)
is by Lemma
5.8

with
\mathcal{N}=\{\mu\}
.
The set
P_{-}(X,T)
consists of those Lipschitz functions with the so-called
locking property

(as defined in
[
6
,
7
]
, cf.
[
9
,
38
]
for related usage of the term
locking
).
By comparison of (
6.1
) and (
6.2
), the open set
P_{-}(X,T)
is a subset of
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}_{\,!}(X,T)
,
and hence also a subset of
P(X,T)=int\left({\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}_{\,!}(X,T)\right)
.

By contrast there is also a
superset

P_{+}(X,T)
of
P(X,T)
that may be regarded as a natural open subset of
{\rm Lip}(X)
in the context of TPO. To describe
P_{+}(X,T)
we first say that
f
has the
weak periodic optimization
property if at least one of its maximizing measures is periodic, i.e. if there exists
m\in{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
such that

\int f\,dm\geq\int f\,d\mu
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(X,T)
, and then define

{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
as the set of all Lipschitz functions with the weak periodic optimization property.
The set
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
can be expressed as

{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T):={\rm Lip}^{{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)}(X,T)=\bigcup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)}{\rm Lip}^{\{\mu\}}(X,T),

(6.3)

and we define

P_{+}(X,T):=int\left({\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)\right).

Clearly
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}_{\,!}(X,T)
is a subset of
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
,
therefore

P(X,T)=int\left({\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}_{\,!}(X,T)\right)

is a subset of

P_{+}(X,T)=int\left({\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)\right)
. Summarising, we have:

P_{-}(X,T)\subseteq P(X,T)\subseteq P_{+}(X,T)\subseteq{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T).

(6.4)

To investigate the extent to which periodic optimization is typical, it would be most satisfactory if the natural
open sets

P_{-}(X,T)
,
P(X,T)
,
P_{+}(X,T)
all have the
same closure
in
{\rm Lip}(X)
,
and indeed this is the case.
Although

\overline{P_{-}(X,T)}=\overline{P(X,T)}=\overline{P_{+}(X,T)}

does hold for arbitrary dynamical systems
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
,
a general proof
is a little protracted for our purposes,
so in the following we give a short proof valid in the case
that
T
is Lipschitz
(which is sufficient for the
specific classes of systems studied in Section
8
onwards),
and briefly indicate a route to the more general result.

Lemma 6.5
.

If
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}

then

\overline{P_{-}(X,T)}=\overline{P(X,T)}=\overline{P_{+}(X,T)}=\overline{{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)}.

(6.5)

Proof.

Let us suppose that
T
is Lipschitz, so each iterate
T^{i}
is Lipschitz as well, in other words there exist
K_{i}>0
such that

d(T^{i}(x),T^{i}(x^{\prime}))\leq K_{i}d(x,x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
In view of (
6.4
), to prove (
6.5
) it suffices to show that

{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)\subseteq\overline{P_{-}(X,T)}.

(6.6)

Suppose that
f\in{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
. So there exists a periodic
f
-maximizing measure
\mu
, with corresponding orbit
Q:=\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
of cardinality
q\in\mathbb{N}
, say.
Note that if
g\in{\rm Lip}(X)
,
x\in X
, then

S_{q}g(x)-q\int g\,d\mu\leq|g|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}d(x,Q)\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}K_{i},

(6.7)

since if
y\in Q
is such that
d(x,y)=d(x,Q):=\min_{z\in Q}d(x,z)
then the lefthand side of
(
6.7
) equals
\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}(g(T^{i}x)-g(T^{i}y))
, which is readily bounded using the
Lipschitz constants for
g
and the
T^{i}
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, define
f_{\epsilon}:=f-\epsilon d(\cdot,Q)
, and

U_{\epsilon}:=\left\{g\in{\rm Lip}(X):|g|_{{\rm Lip}(X)}\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}K_{i}<\epsilon/2\right\}.

(6.8)

We claim that
\mu
is the unique
(f_{\epsilon}+g)
-maximizing measure for all
g\in U_{\epsilon}
.
This means that the open set
V_{\epsilon}:=\{f_{\epsilon}+g:g\in U_{\epsilon}\}
is a subset of

int({\rm Lip}^{\{\mu\}}(X,T))
, and since
f
is the limit, in the Lipschitz topology, of members of

\cup_{\epsilon>0}V_{\epsilon}
, it follows that

f\in\overline{int({\rm Lip}^{\{\mu\}}(X,T))}
.
Since

P_{-}(X,T)=\bigcup_{m\in{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)}int\left({\rm Lip}^{\{m\}}(X,T)\right)

(cf. (
6.2
)),
we see that

f\in\overline{P_{-}(X,T)}
, so (
6.6
) is proved.

It remains to justify the claim that
\mu
is the unique
(f_{\epsilon}+g)
-maximizing measure
for all
g\in U_{\epsilon}
, in other words that if

\nu\in\mathcal{M}(X,T)\setminus\{\mu\}
then

\int(f_{\epsilon}+g)\,d\nu<\int(f_{\epsilon}+g)\,d\mu.

(6.9)

For this first note that, in view of (
6.7
), (
6.8
),

\frac{1}{q}S_{q}g(x)-\int g\,d\mu\leq\frac{1}{q}\frac{\epsilon}{2}d(x,Q)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}d(x,Q)\ \text{ for all }x\in X.

(6.10)

The
T
-invariance of
\nu
, together with (
6.10
), gives

\int g\,d\nu=\int\frac{1}{q}S_{q}g\,d\nu=\int\left(\frac{1}{q}S_{q}g-\int g\,d\mu\right)\,d\nu+\int g\,d\mu\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\int d(\cdot,Q)\,d\nu+\int g\,d\mu,

(6.11)

and (
6.11
) implies

\int(f_{\epsilon}+g)\,d\nu\leq\int f\,d\nu-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\int d(\cdot,Q)\,d\nu+\int g\,d\mu<\int f\,d\nu+\int g\,d\mu,

(6.12)

where the final inequality uses that
\nu\neq\mu
, therefore
\int d(\cdot,Q)\,d\nu>0
.
Now
\mu
is a maximizing measure for both
f
and
f_{\epsilon}
, so

\int f\,d\nu\leq\int f\,d\mu=\int f_{\epsilon}\,d\mu
, and combining this with (
6.12
) gives the required
inequality (
6.9
).

For general
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
, i.e. without the Lipschitz assumption on
T
,
the approach of
[
7
]
can be used
to show that if

f\in{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)

then
f\in\overline{P_{-}(X,T)}
,
by first noting that if
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)\cap\mathcal{M}_{\max}(f)
then there exists
C_{\mu}>0

such that
W(\mu,\nu)\leq C_{\mu}\int d(\cdot,\operatorname{supp}(\mu))\,d\nu
,
where
W
denotes the Wasserstein distance (see Definition
12.1
) on
\mathcal{M}(X)
,
from which it follows that for
\epsilon>0
the functions

f_{\epsilon}:=f-\epsilon\,d(\cdot,\operatorname{supp}(\mu))
lie in

int({\rm Lip}_{\subseteq}^{\{\mu\}}(X,T))
, and hence that

f\in\overline{int({\rm Lip}_{\subseteq}^{\{\mu\}}(X,T))}\subseteq\overline{P_{-}(X,T)}
.
∎

In view of Lemma
6.5
, various characterisations of
typical periodic optimization
can be summarised as follows:

Corollary 6.6
.

For
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

(X,T)
has the TPO property.

(b)

P(X,T)
is dense in
{\rm Lip}(X)
.

(c)

{\rm Lip}_{\,!}^{{\mathcal{P}er}}(X,T)
is dense in
{\rm Lip}(X)
.

(d)

P_{+}(X,T)
is dense in
{\rm Lip}(X)
.

(e)

{\rm Lip}^{{\mathcal{P}er}}(X,T)
is dense in
{\rm Lip}(X)
.

(f)

P_{-}(X,T)
is dense in
{\rm Lip}(X)
.

Proof.

Note that (b) implies (a) because
P(X,T)
is open,
and (a) implies (b) since
P(X,T)
is the largest open set with the property that all its members have the periodic optimization property.
The equivalence of (b), (d), (e) and (f) is immediate from Lemma
6.5
,
and the equivalence of (c) to these follows because

P(X,T)\subseteq{\rm Lip}_{\,!}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)\subseteq{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
.
∎

Clearly, if
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
has no periodic orbits then it does not have TPO.
In fact a necessary condition for
TPO is that periodic measures form a dense
subset of the ergodic measures:

Lemma 6.7
.

If
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
has
TPO
then
{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
is weak
∗
dense in
\mathcal{E}rg(X,T)
.

Proof.

If
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
has the typical periodic optimization property, then
P(X,T)
is dense in
{\rm Lip}(X)
.
Therefore
P(X,T)
is dense in
C(X)
, with respect to the uniform topology on
C(X)
, since

{\rm Lip}(X)
is densely embedded in
C(X)
.
This denseness means, by
[
59
, Lem. 3.4]
,
that the set

\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{E}rg(X,T)\cap\left(\cup_{f\in P(X,T)}\mathcal{M}_{\max}(f)\right)

is weak
∗
dense in
\mathcal{E}rg(X,T)
.

Now each
f\in P(X,T)
is such that
\mathcal{M}_{\max}(f)
consists of a single periodic measure,
so
\mathcal{U}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
.
It follows that
{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
is weak
∗
dense in
\mathcal{E}rg(X,T)
, as required.
∎

Remark 6.8
.

For TPO to hold, it is not necessary that
{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
be dense in all of
\mathcal{M}(X,T)
:
see e.g. the (topologically transitive) shift space described in
Example
8.12
.

Theorem
5.9
and Corollary
6.6
together have the following consequence:

Corollary 6.9
.

Let
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
,
with
Z
a closed
T
-invariant subset of
X
.
Let
U
be a non-empty open subset of
{\rm Lip}(X)
such that
Z
is maximizable for all
f\in U
.
If
(Z,T)
has TPO, then

P(X,T)\cap U
is dense in
U
.

Proof.

The TPO for
(Z,T)
means that

{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(Z,T)={\rm Lip}^{{\mathcal{P}er}(Z,T)}(Z,T)
is dense in
{\rm Lip}(Z)
, by Corollary
6.6
.
Define
\mathcal{N}={\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
, so that
\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{M}(Z,T)={\mathcal{P}er}(Z,T)
,
and
{\rm Lip}^{{\mathcal{P}er}}(X,T)={\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)
.
Theorem
5.9

gives that

{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{N}}(X,T)\cap U
is dense in
U
,
in other words
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)\cap U
is dense in
U
.
But
P(X,T)
is dense in
{\rm Lip}^{\mathcal{P}er}(X,T)
, by Corollary
6.6
,
so
P(X,T)\cap U
is dense in
U
, as required.
∎

Combining Lemma
5.7
and Corollary
6.9
,
we obtain the following key structural theorem:

Theorem 6.10
.

(Structural Theorem)

Suppose
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
.
Let
\mathcal{Z}=\{Z_{0}\}\cup\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a countable maximizable family
such that
(Z_{i},T)
has TPO for all
i\geq 1
.
Then the open set

P(X,T)\cup int\left({\rm Lip}_{Z_{0}}(X,T)\right)

is dense in
{\rm Lip}(X)
.

Proof.

For each
i\geq 0
, define the open set

U_{i}:=int({\rm Lip}_{Z_{i}}(X,T))\,.

Since
\mathcal{Z}
is countable and maximizable,
Lemma
5.7
implies that

\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}U_{i}\ \text{ is dense in }{\rm Lip}(X)\,.

7.
The subset closing property

While the development so far has been in the context of general topological dynamical systems,
henceforth we shall be interested in restricting attention to systems satisfying particular assumptions.
We introduce the following weak hyperbolicity condition, that will be useful in the sequel:

Definition 7.1
.

Suppose
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
.
A non-empty subset
A\subseteq X
has the
closing property
relative to
(X,T)
if there exists
C_{A}>0

such that for all
x\in A
, if
T^{n}(x)\in A
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then there exists a periodic point
z=T^{n}(z)\in X
such that

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d\left(T^{i}(x),T^{i}(z)\right)\leq C_{A}\,.

(7.1)

A closed
T
-invariant subset

is said to have the
subset closing property
if
there exists a non-empty subset
A\subseteq Y
, where
A
is open in
Y
,
such that

has the closing property relative to
(X,T)
.

Remark 7.2
.

If
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
is open and distance-expanding, or a hyperbolic homeomorphism with local product structure
(such as an Axiom A diffeomorphism), then every closed invariant
Y\subseteq X
has the subset closing property:
the existence of the periodic point is a consequence of Anosov’s closing lemma
(see e.g.
[
44
, Thm. 6.4.15]
,
[
73
, Cor. 4.3.7]
), and the exponential instability of orbits
in such systems (cf.
[
44
, Cor. 6.4.17]
) implies the uniform bound (
7.1
).
Beyond uniform hyperbolicity, various other systems have (non-periodic) invariant sets with the subset closing property: some of these will be useful in Section
8
, in the context of symbolic dynamics.

The significance of the subset closing property is in connection with minimax sets.
By combining the Minimax Birkhoff Bound (Proposition
3.7
),
and the Completely Maximizing Criterion (Proposition
4.8
),
we are able to derive the existence of a completely maximizing set:

Theorem 7.3
.

Suppose
(X,T)\in\mathfrak{D}
,

If

is a minimax set for

then

is completely maximizing for

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\beta(f)=0

To prove the theorem it suffices to show that

\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{x\in Y}S_{n}f(x)<\infty\,,

(7.2)

since in this case the fact that
Y
is minimax for
f
implies that

Y
is completely maximizing for
f

by Proposition
4.8

Since

has the subset closing property, there exists a non-empty subset

,
and has the closing property relative to
(X,T)
. Let

be as in
the Minimax Birkhoff Bound
(Proposition

),
and let

be as in Definition

.

Let

.
If

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(T^{i}(x))\leq N_{A}\|f\|_{C(X)}\,.

(7.3)

If on the other hand

its maximum element,

We will derive an upper bound on the sum

9
9

9

If

\sum_{i=n_{1}}^{n_{2}-1}f(T^{i}(x))=0

\sum_{i=0}^{n_{1}-1}f(T^{i}(x))

Firstly, since

\sum_{i=0}^{n_{1}-1}f(T^{i}(x))\leq N_{A}\|f\|_{C(X)}\,.

(7.4)

Secondly, since

Combining (

Since (

∎

8.
Symbolic dynamics

Up until now we have developed a theory of countable maximizable families, with application to the TPO problem,
in the setting of rather general dynamical systems.
For the remainder of this article, however, our attention will be focused on

dynamical systems.
For convenience we consider one-sided shift spaces, and will in particular
exploit the fact that Contreras’ TPO Theorem
(see Lemma

Analogues of all results in Sections

Notation 8.1

(Topology, metric, shift map, subshifts)

In general, the presence of a synchronizing word has consequences regarding periodic points:

Lemma 8.17
.

Suppose

11.

.

Proof.

Suppose

is finite.
All words in
\mathbb{W}(\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y))
of length strictly greater than
\max S
contain the magic symbol
\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
, whereas no words in
\mathbb{W}(Y)
contain
\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
.
So if
y\in Y

|w|>\max S
, then
w\notin\mathbb{W}(\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y))
, so
y\notin\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)
.
Therefore
Y\cap\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)=\emptyset
.
∎

Corollary 11.15
.

Let
Y
be a shift space.
If
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}
is finite then
\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)
is sofic.

Proof.

A shift space is sofic if and only if its Markov boundary is empty, by
Lemma

, so it suffices to show that
\partial_{M}\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)=\emptyset

By Lemma

(b),

\partial_{M}\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)=\partial_{M}Y_{\mathbb{W}_{S}(Y)}\subseteq Y\cap Y_{\mathbb{W}_{S}(Y)}=Y\cap\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y),

but

\partial_{M}\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)=\emptyset

∎

Corollary 11.16
.

If
y
is any sequence on some alphabet

, and
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}
is finite,
then the corresponding
(S,y)
-gap subshift of
[
68
]
is sofic.

Proof.

If
Y
denotes the shift orbit closure of

, then the
(S,y)
-gap subshift is
\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)
,
and the result follows from Corollary

.
∎

We can now show that for

subset

, the corresponding length-constrained interspersion is a TPO-preserving operator:

Theorem 11.17
.

If

is any shift space with TPO, and

,
then
\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)
has TPO.

Proof.

If

\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)
is sofic, by Corollary

, so
\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)
has TPO.
If

is infinite, then
\mathbb{W}_{S}(Y)
is

by Lemma

, as required.
∎

We now focus on the problem of realising all shift spaces
Y
as Markov boundaries
of shift spaces
X
(where
X
has a synchronizing word).
Note that by
Proposition
11.6

(cf. also Corollaries
11.7
and
11.12
),
this problem is already solved in the case where
Y
does not have a synchronizing word.
In the case of general
Y
, the solution to the problem will use length-constrained interspersion, though with the following stronger assumption on the set
S
:

Definition 11.18
.

An infinite subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
with its distinct elements
enumerated as
s_{1}<s_{2}<\ldots
,
will be called
superlinear
if

s_{n+1}-s_{n}<s_{n+2}-s_{n+1}\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(11.9)

Theorem 11.19
.

If
Y
is a shift space, and
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}
is superlinear, then
the
S
-interspersion
\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)
is a shift space with
Markov boundary equal to
Y
; that is,
\partial_{M}(\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y))=Y
.

Proof.

Let
X:=\mathfrak{I}_{S}(Y)
.
Note that
Y\subseteq X
, and that
Y
is precisely the set of sequences in
X
that do not contain the symbol
\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
.
By Lemma
11.2
(a),

\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
is a synchronizing word for
X
,
so any words containing it do not belong to
\mathbb{W}_{\infty}(X)
,
thus

\partial_{M}X\subseteq Y
.
It remains to show that

Y\subseteq\partial_{M}X
,
in other words that

\mathbb{W}(Y)\subseteq\mathbb{W}_{\infty}(X).

(11.10)

For this, let
w
be an arbitrary word in
\mathbb{W}(Y)
.
There exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that if

n>N
then there exists
v^{(n)}\in\mathbb{W}(Y)
such that
v^{(n)}w\in\mathbb{W}(Y)
and

|v^{(n)}w|=s_{n}+1.

Now
\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(n)}w\in\mathbb{W}(X)
, and we wish to consider the follower set
F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(n)}w)
.
We claim that the length constraint
in the definition of
X
means that

F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(n)}w)
contains a word
u\in\mathbb{W}_{s_{n+1}-s_{n}}(X)

such that

u_{s_{n+1}-s_{n}}=\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt\quad\text{and}\quad u_{i}\neq\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt\text{ for }1\leq i\leq s_{n+1}-s_{n}-1.

We now prove this claim.
Since
v^{(n)}w\in\mathbb{W}_{s_{n}+1}(Y)
, it must appear as the prefix of a length-
s_{n+1}
word in
\mathbb{W}(Y)
;
that is, there exists

u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{W}_{s_{n+1}-s_{n}-1}(Y)
such that
v^{(n)}wu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{W}_{s_{n+1}}(Y)
.
The definition of
X
then means that
\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(n)}wu^{\prime}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt\in\mathbb{W}(X)
.
Defining
u=u^{\prime}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
, we then see that

u\in\mathbb{W}_{s_{n+1}-s_{n}}(X)
,
u\in F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(n)}w)
,
u_{s_{n+1}-s_{n}}=\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
,
and
u_{i}=u^{\prime}_{i}\neq\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
for
1\leq i\leq s_{n+1}-s_{n}-1
, so indeed
u
satisfies the requirements of the claim.

On the other hand,
F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(n)}w)
does not contain any words of the form

v=v^{\prime}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
where
|v^{\prime}|<s_{n+1}-s_{n}-1
(the argument is similar to the one above, again using the length constraint).

Now let us compare
F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(n)}w)
to
F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(m)}w)
, for
m>n>N
, and argue that these sets are distinct.
From the above,
F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(n)}w)
contains some word
u=u^{\prime}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
where
|u|=s_{n+1}-s_{n}
,
but
F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(m)}w)
does not contain any word of the form
v=v^{\prime}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt

where
|v|<s_{m+1}-s_{m}
.
Now
s_{n+1}-s_{n}<s_{m+1}-s_{m}
since
S
is superlinear and
m>n
,
therefore
u
does not belong to
F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(m)}w)
,
and so the sets
F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(n)}w)
and
F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(m)}w)
are distinct.

So
F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(n)}w)\neq F_{X}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptv^{(m)}w)

for all
m>n>N
,
therefore the cardinality of the set

\{F_{X}(vw):v\in P_{X}(w)\}

is infinite,
and hence
w\in\mathbb{W}_{\infty}(X)
.
But
w\in\mathbb{W}(Y)
was arbitrary, so

\mathbb{W}(Y)\subseteq\mathbb{W}_{\infty}(X)
, which is the required inclusion (
11.10
),
so the result is proved.
∎

In the language of operators on the class
\mathfrak{S}
of all shift spaces, Theorem
11.19

can be interpreted in terms of right-inverses of the Markov boundary operator:

Corollary 11.20
.

If
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}
is superlinear, then
the
S
-interspersion map
\mathfrak{I}_{S}:\mathfrak{S}\to\mathfrak{S}
is a right-inverse
to the Markov boundary map
\partial_{M}:\mathfrak{S}\to\mathfrak{S}
, i.e.
\partial_{M}\circ\mathfrak{I}_{S}={\rm id}_{\mathfrak{S}}
.

Proof.

Immediate from Theorem
11.19
.
∎

Remark 11.21
.

The superlinear length-constrained interspersions

of Corollary

are not the only right-inverses
to the Markov boundary map: for example the context free shift

was shown, in the proof

While Theorem

has TPO whenever

does, the advantage of Theorem

is in allowing the construction of eventually sofic shifts of

,
via iterated interspersion (thereby answering the two questions posed at the start of this section):

Corollary 11.22
.

Suppose
Y
is a sofic shift, and

.

S_{i}
is a superlinear subset of

.

(or equivalently Corollary

),

∎

.

Suppose

is a sofic shift,

, and

.

, by choosing

.
∎

Remark 11.24
.

The example in Section

Let

denote the magic symbol
for

,

Specific examples of specimen shifts can be used to provide similarly specific examples of eventually specimen shifts.
A generalisation of Corollary

,
and a particular instance of Theorem

, is the following:

Corollary 11.28
.

Suppose
Z
is a dynamically minimal non-uniquely ergodic shift space.
Suppose

is a superlinear subset of
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, for each

.
For each

,

is an eventually specimen shift space,

By Corollary

,
\mathfrak{I}(Z)
is specimen.

simply note that if

then there is a (magic) symbol appearing in certain sequences belonging to

.
∎

12.
Shift spaces without typical periodic optimization

A shift space
X
without any periodic orbits clearly does not have TPO.
More generally,
if
X
has
periodic orbits but

{\mathcal{P}er}(X,\sigma)
is not weak
∗
dense in
\mathcal{E}rg(X,\sigma)
, then
Lemma
6.7
implies that
X
does not have TPO.
For example if
X\subseteq\{0,1,2,3\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is defined as the shift orbit closure of
the set of sequences
of the form

01^{|w^{(1)}|+1}w^{(1)}01^{|w^{(2)}|+1}w^{(2)}01^{|w^{(3)}|+1}w^{(3)}\ldots

where each
w^{(i)}\in\mathbb{W}(Z)
for some
dynamically minimal and uniquely ergodic
non-periodic subshift
Z\subseteq\{2,3\}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
then
X
has
synchronizing word
0, and the unique ergodic measure supported by
Z\subseteq X

cannot be approximated by periodic orbit measures, since the cylinder set
[1]
is given mass zero by the former measure, and mass
1/2
by all of the latter measures; this
X
does not have TPO.

In this section we show that, even if
periodic measures are dense in the set of ergodic measures,
indeed even if they are dense in the set of all invariant measures,
then typical periodic optimization need not hold
(see Theorem
12.4
below, and cf. Theorem
1.2
in Section
1
).
For simplicity of exposition, we will do this by constructing a specific shift space
X
,
which we call the
magic Morse shift
, by augmenting the well known Morse shift
with certain sequences containing an additional (synchronizing) symbol.

Let
\theta
denote the
Morse substitution
on the alphabet
\{0,1\}
(see e.g.
[
66
, p. 7]
), given by
\theta(0)=01
,
\theta(1)=10
,
and extending in the usual way to a morphism of
\{0,1\}^{*}
by setting
\theta(a_{1}\ldots a_{k})=\theta(a_{1})\ldots\theta(a_{k})
.
The
Morse sequence

\omega=0110100110010110\ldots

is the fixed point of
\theta
beginning with the symbol 0
(see e.g.
[
66
]
for properties of the Morse sequence, which appeared in
Morse
[
62
]
, having previously been studied by Prouhet
[
65
]
and Thue
[
72
]
).
For
n\geq 0
, if the length-
2^{n}
word
\theta_{n}
is defined by

\theta_{n}=\theta^{n}(0),

with the convention that
\theta^{0}(0)=0
, then each
\theta_{n}
is a prefix of
\omega
.
The
Morse shift
is defined to be the shift orbit closure
Z=\overline{\{\sigma^{n}(\omega):n\geq 0\}}
,
and is well known
to be dynamically minimal and uniquely ergodic.
Let
\lambda
denote the Morse measure, i.e. the unique
\sigma
-invariant measure supported by
Z
.

Introducing a new symbol
\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt\notin\{0,1\}
, define the
magic Morse shift

X

to be the closure in
\{0,1,\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt\}^{\mathbb{N}}
(which as usual is equipped with the metric
d=d_{\alpha}
, for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
)
of the set of all points of the form

\sigma^{n}(\theta_{n_{1}}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt\,\theta_{n_{2}}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt\cdots),

where
n\geq 0

and
(n_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence
of natural numbers.
In other words,
X
is the
\mathbb{V}
-interspersion of
Z
(cf. Definition
11.1
),
where
\mathbb{V}=\{\theta_{n}:n\geq 0\}\subseteq\mathbb{W}(Z)
, with magic symbol
\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
.
In particular,

\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
is
a synchronizing word for
X
,
by Lemma
11.2
(a),
and
Z=\partial_{M}X\subseteq X
by Corollary
11.7
,
since
Z
is dynamically minimal but non-periodic,
and
\mathbb{V}
is
Z
-descriptive.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in X
, define the
empirical measure

E_{n}(x)
by

E_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\delta_{\sigma^{i}(x)}\,,

noting that this is a probability measure, but is
\sigma
-invariant only when
\sigma^{n}(x)=x
.

A convenient tool in the following analysis will be the Wasserstein distance
(see e.g.
[
74
,
75
]
)
on the set of Borel probability measures:

Definition 12.1
.

For a compact metric space
(Y,d)
, the
Wasserstein distance

W
on
\mathcal{M}(Y)
is defined by

W(\mu,\nu)=\max\left\{\int\phi\,d\mu-\int\phi\,d\nu:\phi\in{\rm Lip}(Y),|\phi|_{{\rm Lip}(Y)}\leq 1\right\}.

(12.1)

The Wasserstein distance
is consistent with the weak
∗
topology, and for our purposes the following simple properties
will be useful:

Lemma 12.2
.

If
(Y,d)
is a compact metric space, the Wasserstein distance
W
satisfies

(a)

If
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(Y)
then
W(\mu,\nu)\leq\text{diam}(Y)
.

(b)

If
x,y\in Y
then
W(\delta_{x},\delta_{y})=d(x,y)
.

(c)

If
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
a_{i}\geq 0
for
1\leq i\leq n
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=1
, and
\mu_{i},\nu_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(Y)
for
1\leq i\leq n
,
then

W\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\mu_{i},\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\nu_{i}\right)\leq\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}W(\mu_{i},\nu_{i}).

Proof.

These follow easily from
(
12.1
).
∎

Recalling that
X
is equipped with the metric
d=d_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
a key estimate will be on the Wasserstein distance between the Morse measure
\lambda
and certain empirical measures:

Lemma 12.3
.

If
\lambda
is the Morse measure, and
x=\theta_{n}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptx^{\prime}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x^{\prime}\in X
,
then

W(E_{2^{n}}(x),\lambda)\leq\left(\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\frac{1}{2^{n}},

(12.2)

and

W(E_{2^{n}+1}(x),\lambda)\leq\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\frac{1}{2^{n}+1}.

(12.3)

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\lambda
is the weak
∗
limit

\lim_{M\to\infty}E_{2^{n}M}(\omega)
, and we can write

E_{2^{n}M}(\omega)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}E_{2^{n}}\left(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)\right),

(12.4)

so that if it can be shown that

W\left(E_{2^{n}}(x),E_{2^{n}}\left(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)\right)\right)\leq\left(\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\frac{1}{2^{n}}

(12.5)

for
0\leq m\leq M-1
, then (
12.4
), (
12.5
) and Lemma
12.2
give

W\left(E_{2^{n}}(x),E_{2^{n}M}(\omega)\right)\leq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}W(E_{2^{n}}(x),E_{2^{n}}\left(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)\right))\leq\left(\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\frac{1}{2^{n}},

for all
M\in\mathbb{N}
, and hence (
12.2
) follows from the fact that
\lambda=\lim_{M\to\infty}E_{2^{n}M}(\omega)
.

To prove (
12.5
), each of the
2^{n}
points in the support of
E_{2^{n}}(x)

will be paired with a nearby point in the support of

E_{2^{n}}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega))
, with the distance between these points
equal to the Wasserstein distance between the corresponding Dirac measures, by Lemma
12.2
(b).
Specifically, writing
\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\ldots
, so that

\omega=\theta^{n}(\omega)=\theta^{n}(\omega_{1})\theta^{n}(\omega_{2})\cdots

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
we see that
the length-
2^{n}
prefix of

\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)

is either
\theta^{n}(0)
or
\theta^{n}(1)
,
for all
m\geq 0
.

In the case that
\theta^{n}(0)=\theta_{n}

is the length-
2^{n}
prefix of

\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)
, since
\theta_{n}
is also the length-
2^{n}
prefix of

then

d(\sigma^{i}(x),\sigma^{i}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)))\leq\alpha^{2^{n}-i}\quad\text{for }0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1,

and therefore

W\left(E_{2^{n}}(x),E_{2^{n}}\left(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)\right)\right)\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}d(\sigma^{i}(x),\sigma^{i}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)))\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha^{2^{n}-i}<\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{1}{2^{n}},

which in particular implies (
12.5
).

In the case that
\theta^{n}(1)

is the length-
2^{n}
prefix of

\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)
,
since

\theta^{n}(1)=\theta^{n-1}(1)\theta^{n-1}(0)
and
\theta_{n}=\theta^{n-1}(0)\theta^{n-1}(1)
,
we see that
\sigma^{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega))
and
x

share the same length-
2^{n-1}
prefix
\theta^{n-1}(0)=\theta_{n-1}
,
and

\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)
and
\sigma^{2^{n-1}}(x)

share the same length-
2^{n-1}
prefix
\theta^{n-1}(1)
.
Consequently,

d\left(\sigma^{i}(x),\sigma^{i}(\sigma^{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)))\right)\leq\alpha^{2^{n-1}-i}\quad\text{for }0\leq i\leq 2^{n-1}-1,

(12.6)

and

d\left(\sigma^{i}(\sigma^{2^{n-1}}(x)),\sigma^{i}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega))\right)\leq\alpha^{2^{n-1}-i}\quad\text{for }0\leq i\leq 2^{n-1}-1,

(12.7)

so the bounds (
12.6
), (
12.7
),
together with
an argument analogous to the one above, give

W\left(E_{2^{n-1}}(x),E_{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)))\right)<\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}

(12.8)

and

W\left(E_{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{2^{n-1}}(x)),E_{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega))\right)<\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}.

(12.9)

Now

E_{2^{n}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}E_{2^{n-1}}(x)+\frac{1}{2}E_{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{2^{n-1}}(x))

and

E_{2^{n}}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega))=\frac{1}{2}E_{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega))+\frac{1}{2}E_{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega))),

so

\displaystyle W\left(E_{2^{n}}(x),E_{2^{n}}\left(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}W\left(E_{2^{n-1}}(x),E_{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega)))\right)+\frac{1}{2}W\left(E_{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{2^{n-1}}(x)),E_{2^{n-1}}(\sigma^{m2^{n}}(\omega))\right),

and therefore (
12.8
), (
12.9
) yield the required inequality (
12.5
).

Noting that
E_{2^{n}+1}(x)=\frac{2^{n}}{2^{n}+1}E_{2^{n}}(x)+\frac{1}{2^{n}+1}E_{1}(\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004ptx^{\prime})
, it follows from Lemma

(c)

(see Lemma

(a))
gives
(
12.3
).
∎

Theorem 12.4
.

The magic Morse shift
X
does not have TPO, though its periodic measures are weak
∗
dense in the set of all invariant
measures.

Proof.

To show that the TPO property does not hold,
let
\rho
be any periodic measure on
X
.
There exists a periodic word
u
for
\rho
of the form
u=\theta_{n_{1}}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt\,\theta_{n_{2}}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt\ldots\theta_{n_{k}}\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
,
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}

Since the symbol
\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt
does not appear in the words
\theta_{n_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{n_{k}}

then
\rho([\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt])=k/|u|

Writing

\int d(\cdot,Z)\,d\rho\geq\frac{k}{|u|}=\rho([\kern 0.20004pt\flat\kern 0.20004pt]).

(12.10)

Writing

(c) gives

W(\rho,\lambda)\leq\frac{1}{|u|}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(2^{n_{j}}+1)W(E_{m_{j}}(y^{(j)}),\lambda)\leq\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\frac{k}{|u|},

(12.11)

and (

), (

One method of showing that the periodic measures on
