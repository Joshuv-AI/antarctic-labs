LiFT: Does Instruction Fine-Tuning Improve In-Context Learning for Longitudinal Modelling by Large Language Models?
Iqra Ali, Talia Tseriotou, Mahmud Elahi Akhter, Yuxiang Zhou, Maria Liakata

Abstract
Longitudinal NLP tasks require reasoning over temporally ordered text to detect persistence and change in human behavior and opinions. However, in-context learning with large language models struggles on tasks where models must integrate historical context, track evolving interactions, and handle rare change events. We introduce LiFT, a longitudinal instruction fine-tuning framework that unifies diverse longitudinal modeling tasks under a shared instruction schema. LiFT uses a curriculum that progressively increases temporal difficulty while incorporating few-shot structure and temporal conditioning to encourage effective use of past context. We evaluate LiFT across five datasets. Models trained on longitudinal tasks with different levels of temporal granularity are tested for generalisability on two separate datasets. Across models with different parameter sizes (OLMo (1B/7B), LLaMA-8B, and Qwen-14B), LiFT consistently outperforms base-model ICL, with strong gains on out-of-distribution data and minority change events.

3
Methodology

We introduce
LiFT
(Longitudinal Instruction Fine-Tuning), a framework for improving language models’ ability to reason over temporally structured sequences. Standard instruction fine-tuning and in-context learning treat examples largely as independent instances and therefore fail to model temporal dependencies across events. LiFT addresses this by combining several mechanisms for temporal learning. First, we formulate longitudinal prediction as sequence-conditioned inference over temporally ordered histories (§
3.1
). Second, we introduce a sequential prompting framework that constructs training examples using rolling historical contexts (§
3.3.1
). Third, we apply curriculum-based instruction fine-tuning, introducing tasks of increasing temporal complexity to stabilize training and improve few-shot adaptation (§
3.3.2
). Fourth, we incorporate a temporal–label conditioning module that injects timestep, relative position, and label signals into token representations (§
3.5
). These components are trained jointly using LoRA-based parameter-efficient adaptation and a multi-objective loss combining language modeling, classification, and history-aware supervision.
Figure
1
provides an overview of LiFT, organized into two modules: an IFT Builder and an IFT Trainer. The builder converts temporal datasets into longitudinal instruction prompts with rolling histories and few-shot demonstrations, while the trainer injects temporal and label embeddings and fine-tunes a base language model using LoRA adapters and the multi-objective loss. The full training workflow is described in
Algorithm

, which constructs stage-wise curricula from temporally ordered data and performs instruction fine-tuning sequentially across stages.

3.1
Problem Formulation

Let a timeline (sequence) be a temporally ordered set

H=\{(x_{1},t_{1}),\ldots,(x_{m},t_{m})\}
with
t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{m}
and a current item

(x_{m+1},t_{m+1})
. The goal is to predict an output
y
for the current item conditioned on the history:

\hat{y}=f(H,x_{m+1})\quad\text{or}\quad\hat{z}=g(H,x_{m+1})

3.3
Longitudinal Prompting Framework

This section introduces the prompting framework used for LiFT.

3.3.1
Sequential Prompting

For temporally structured datasets, data is grouped by a sequence key (e.g., timelineID, topic, or userID) and sorted chronologically. Each training instance consists of a rolling historical context paired with the current observation. To satisfy fixed-context constraints, historical content is iteratively truncated from the oldest side until:

\texttt{tokens}(\texttt{header}\oplus\texttt{<hist>}~\texttt{history}~\texttt{</hist>}\oplus\texttt{<curr>}~\texttt{current})\leq\texttt{MAX\_TOKENS}

We additionally prefix history lines with relative time indices (e.g.,
t-3
,
t-2
,
t-1
).

Curriculum-Based Instruction Tuning

Each training example is rendered as a structured prompt:

<instruction> I </instruction>

\oplus
<few-shot>
\mathcal{D}_{k}
</few-shot>

\oplus
<query> X </query>

We train the model using a three-stage curriculum that progressively increases task difficulty (Figure

Stage 2: Longitudinal Rumor Stance (LRS) introduces temporally ordered social media discussions with evolving user stances and noisier language. Stage 3: Moments of Change (TalkLife) consists of longitudinal mental-health timelines where signals of change are sparse and often implicit, requiring inference over subtle linguistic cues. The number of in-context demonstrations grows from 1

2
\rightarrow
3 shots.

Base Model and Tokenization

We initialize from an instruction-tuned causal language model (Olmo, Llama, Qwen) as the framework is model-agnostic. The tokenizer vocabulary is extended with structured control and task tokens:

x_{m+1}

If the base tokenizer does not define a padding token, we add
<|pad|>
. We freeze base LM weights and insert LoRA adapters into attention and MLP projection modules.

Training proceeds in three sequential stages, each with a fixed LoRA rank and learning rate.

We use LoRA scaling
\alpha=2r
and dropout
0.05
. When moving to a higher-rank stage, we load the previous adapter weights with rank-resize initialization and freeze the previously learned rank slice so that only newly added ranks update.

3.5
Temporal–Label Conditioning Module

We augment token embeddings with a temporal–label conditioning module that encodes four signals: absolute position
p_{t}
, relative position-to-end
r_{t}
, global label id
z_{t}
, and absolute timestep id
\tau_{t}
. The resulting embeddings:

\mathbf{e}_{t}=(e^{\text{abs}}_{t},\;e^{\text{rel}}_{t},\;e^{\text{lbl}}_{t},\;e^{\text{time}}_{t})

are concatenated and projected:

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{MLP}(\mathbf{e}_{t}),

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle=x_{t}+Wu_{t}

The global label id is stamped onto output tokens, while the timestep id is broadcast per instance. The projected embedding is injected additively into the LM input space. We construct
\texttt{input\_ids}=\texttt{tok(prompt)}\oplus\texttt{tok(response)}\oplus\texttt{[eos]}
and derive three supervision signals: (i) prompt-level next-token CE computed only outside
<HIST>
regions, (ii) focal LM loss applied exclusively to output tokens (response + EOS), and (iii) a global classification objective obtained by stamping the global label id over the output span and pooling the final hidden state at the last stamped position.

3.6
Training

The total loss is a weighted sum of four terms:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{CE}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}+\lambda_{\text{out}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{focalLM}}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\text{cls}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{focalCls}}+\lambda_{\text{hist}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{histCls}}

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}
is standard next-token cross-entropy on prompt tokens excluding history spans.
\mathcal{L}_{\text{focalLM}}
is a focal language modeling loss applied only to output tokens, while
\mathcal{L}_{\text{focalCls}}
is focal cross-entropy over the shared global label head with optional class-balanced weights. These losses are chosen to jointly optimize generative instruction-following and robust event prediction:
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}
stabilizes prompt conditioning,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{focalLM}}
focuses learning on difficult output tokens, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{focalCls}}
improves classification under class imbalance by emphasizing hard examples. Finally,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{histCls}}
is an auxiliary focal classification loss computed from a history-only pooled representation (mean of hidden states over
<HIST>
tokens), encouraging the model to extract predictive signal from historical context. Curriculum learning is implemented as sequential training over builder-generated stages. Stage 1 uses structured dialogure with behavioural intent labels, with a context length window of
\sim
142 tokens per timeline, AnnoMI with
k{=}1
, Stage 2 uses temporally ordered social media discussion with a context length window of
\sim
216 tokens per timeline, LRS with
k{=}2
, and finally Stage 3 uses longitudinal social media timelines with context length window of
\sim
1.0K tokens per timeline TalkLife with
k{=}3
where
k
denotes the number of in-context demonstrations included in the prompt. This ordering follows increasing contextual complexity and event sparsity (Section
3.8
,
Table
4
). AnnoMI provides structured conversational supervision with dense behavioral labels, enabling stable initialization. LRS introduces noisier social media discussions with evolving stance dynamics across timelines. TalkLife presents the most challenging setting with sparser change events.

3.7
Mechanistic Interpretability Analysis

We conduct three complementary analyses on OLMo-7B using TalkLife to understand
what
LiFT learns beyond standard IFT. We test whether the improvements observed in

Section
4

arise from better use of temporally ordered history, improved few-shot contextualization, or generic capacity gains.

Probing:
We perform layer-wise probing to measure where task-relevant information becomes linearly decodable. For each layer, we extract representations from four prompt regions: history (
hist_mean
), few-shot demonstrations (
fewshot_mean
), the current post (
curr_mean
), and the final decision-facing token (
last_curr
). Linear probes (StandardScaler + Logistic Regression with class balancing) are trained with 5-fold stratified cross-validation to predict the gold label. This measures
linear recoverability
of label information from different prompt regions.

Attention analysis:
We analyze how the prediction position routes attention across prompt regions (
instruction
,
fewshot
,
hist
,
curr
,
other
). We further compute normalized within-history recency profiles using relative temporal bins (
rel_t
) to test whether models preferentially attend to more recent history items.

Activation patching on
<HIST>
:
We perform activation patching on the history span to test causal dependence on historical context. For 150 stratified test examples, we create corrupted variants by shuffling history posts while preserving the
<HIST>
span. Corrupted hidden states are patched to clean runs at selected layers, and we measure changes in macro-F1, logits, margins, and prediction flips. This analysis gives causal evidence for the model’s reliance on temporally structured history.

Table

reports macro-F1 across five datasets under 0-, 1-, and 3-shot prompting.
Base models struggle with minority change labels:
This is especially evident on TalkLife and AnnoMI, where minority change categories are sparse. For example, OLMo-1B achieves only .044/.044/.040 macro-F1 on TalkLife across 0/1/3-shot settings and .250/.211/.262 on AnnoMI, indicating difficulty distinguishing subtle change behaviors from responses.
LiFT consistently improves macro-F1:
Across all datasets and models, LiFT substantially improves performance.
Few-shot prompting provides additional gains:
In-context demonstrations further improve results when combined with LiFT. For instance, OLMo-7B increases from .283 to .333 on Reddit and from .551 to .578 on LRS when moving from 0-shot to 3-shot prompting. LLaMA-8B shows similar improvements, rising from .454 to .521 on Reddit and from .311 to .526 on AnnoMI.
Imbalanced datasets benefit the most:
The largest gains occur on datasets with stronger imbalance or subtle temporal transitions. On TalkLife, OLMo-7B improves from .056/.050/.059 to .104/.121/.123, while Qwen-14B improves from .209/.201/.286 to .249/.272/.321 across 0/1/3-shot settings. These results indicate that LiFT helps models better identify sparse change signals.
Generalization to held-out datasets:
Although AnnoMI, LRS, and TalkLife are used during training, improvements transfer to held-out datasets.

6
Conclusion

We present an instruction fine-tuning framework (LiFT) to improve
in-context learning
for longitudinal modelling by LLMs. Across datasets, domains, and model scales, longitudinal IFT consistently outperforms base-model ICL under identical prompts and demonstration budgets, with particularly strong gains on minority change events and collapse-prone settings. Interpretability analyses show that these improvements arise from greater reliance on temporally structured history rather than stronger local classification. Probing, attention analysis, and activation patching reveal that the proposed IFT makes historical context more informative, selectively attended, and causally necessary for prediction.

7
Limitations

Our interpretability analyses provide evidence that instruction fine-tuning improves the use of temporally structured history, they do not fully explain all internal mechanisms responsible for the observed improvements. Finally, our models are evaluated in controlled experimental settings and have not been tested in real-world applications. Future work should explore broader datasets, longer temporal horizons, and more robust evaluation protocols before considering deployment in sensitive domains.

8
Ethical Considerations

This work involves datasets derived from conversational and mental health–related timelines. Although several datasets used in our experiments are publicly available and anonymized, research involving mental health signals requires careful ethical consideration. The models developed in this study are intended strictly for research purposes and should not be used for clinical diagnosis, mental health assessment, or decision-making without appropriate professional oversight. The TalkLife dataset contains sensitive and personal user-generated content. Appropriate ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) prior to accessing and processing the data. Access to the dataset was granted through a research licensing agreement with TalkLife following approval of the submitted research proposal. In accordance with these requirements, all data were anonymized and handled using appropriate sensitive data management procedures. To further protect user privacy, all examples reported in this paper are paraphrased rather than reproduced verbatim. Model training and data processing were conducted on secure servers with authorized user-only access. Due to the sensitive nature of the data and the potential risks of misuse, the labeled TalkLife dataset and the trained models are not intended for public release. Furthermore, the Terms of Service of TalkLife state that user data may be shared for research purposes under appropriate safeguards. This study was conducted in compliance with those terms, and access to the TalkLife dataset was obtained through a formal research license agreement. All other datasets used in this work are publicly available and were accessed in accordance with their respective usage policies.

Finally, large language models may inadvertently learn biases present in training data. When applied to sensitive domains such as mental health, these biases could lead to misleading or harmful interpretations. Any future deployment of such systems should therefore involve rigorous ethical review, fairness evaluation, and appropriate safeguards to protect user well-being and privacy.
