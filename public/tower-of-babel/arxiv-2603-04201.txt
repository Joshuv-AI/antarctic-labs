On the defect in the generalized Grunwald–Wang problem
David Harari and Tamás Szamuely

Abstract




1.
Introduction

Let
\displaystyle K
be a field,
\displaystyle T
a set of valuations of
\displaystyle K
,
\displaystyle M
a finite étale commutative group scheme over
\displaystyle K
. Write
\displaystyle K_{v}
for the completion of
\displaystyle K
at
\displaystyle v
and consider the product of restriction maps

(r_{v})_{v\in T}:\,H^{1}(K,M)\to\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M)

for
\displaystyle v\in T
. In this setting equip each cohomology group with the discrete topology and
define
\displaystyle\overline{H^{1}(K,M)}
as the closure of the image of
\displaystyle H^{1}(K,M)
in the product. When
\displaystyle T
is finite, this is just the image of
\displaystyle H^{1}(K,M)
, and when
\displaystyle T
is infinite, we have

\overline{H^{1}(K,M)}=\{(\alpha_{v})\in\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M)\mid{(\alpha_{v})}_{v\in S}\in{\rm Im}{\left(r_{v}\right)}_{v\in S}\,\,\forall S\subset T,|S|<\infty\}

by definition of the product topology.

We say that the
Grunwald-Wang theorem holds for
\displaystyle K
,
\displaystyle T
and
\displaystyle M
if

\overline{H^{1}(K,M)}=\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M).

The classical case is of course when
\displaystyle K
is a number field,
\displaystyle M={\bf Z}/n{\bf Z}
and
\displaystyle T
is finite. As is well known, under these conditions the Grunwald–Wang theorem holds unless we are in the so-called
special case
(see e.g.
[
10
]
, Theorem 9.2.8). The special case arises when
\displaystyle{\rm Gal}(K(\mu_{2^{r}})|K)
is not cyclic, where
\displaystyle 2^{r}
is the largest power of 2 dividing
\displaystyle n
. The smallest counterexample (that of Wang
[
12
]
) is for
\displaystyle K={\bf Q}
,
\displaystyle M={\bf Z}/8{\bf Z}
and
\displaystyle T
consisting only of the 2-adic valuation on
\displaystyle{\bf Q}
.

Apparently already Hasse had the insight that the theorem should hold more generally (for
\displaystyle M={\bf Z}/n{\bf Z}
,
\displaystyle T
finite and in the non-special case) in the above general context of valued fields. His approach was worked out in detail by Lorenz and Roquette in
[
6
]
. However, it was Saltman’s method via generic Galois extensions
[
11
]
and its reinterpretation by Colliot-Thélène and Sansuc in
[
3
]
that opened the way for giving quick proofs of positive results on the Grunwald–Wang problem in a general setting. In fact, in (
[
3
]
, Theorem 8.4) it is proven that the Grunwald–Wang theorem holds in the above general context for finite
\displaystyle T
and a large class of
\displaystyle M
incuding
\displaystyle M={\bf Z}/n{\bf Z}
in the non-special case (see also Corollary
2.3
below).

In this note we focus on another, less studied aspect of the problem: that of the size of the quotient
\displaystyle\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M)\right)/\overline{H^{1}(K,M)}
. In the classical case when
\displaystyle K
is a number field the Poitou–Tate duality theorem implies that this quotient is always finite. For
\displaystyle M={\bf Z}/n{\bf Z}
one can even say more: the above quotient has order at most 2 (for the case of finite
\displaystyle T
see e.g. Theorem 9.2.3 (ii) in
[
10
]
; the general case is proven by similar arguments and passing to the limit). In light of this it is therefore natural to ask:

Questions 1.1
.

(1)

For general
\displaystyle K
,
\displaystyle T
and
\displaystyle M
is the quotient
\displaystyle\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M)\right)/\overline{H^{1}(K,M)}
always finite?

(2)

When the quotient is finite, is its order bounded independently of
\displaystyle T
?

A negative answer for question (2) implies a negative answer for question (1) for infinite
\displaystyle T
but not necessarily for finite
\displaystyle T
. Therefore it is convenient to split question (1) in two, for finite and for infinite
\displaystyle T
. It should also be noted that for
\displaystyle M={\bf Z}/n{\bf Z}
the abelian group
\displaystyle\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},{\bf Z}/n{\bf Z})\right)/\overline{H^{1}(K,{\bf Z}/n{\bf Z})}
is always of exponent 2 (see Corollary
2.4
below), so in that case we are asking about dimensions of
\displaystyle{\bf F}_{2}
-vector spaces.

Though the answers to the above questions can be positive in several interesting cases besides that of number fields, we shall see that in general the answer is no for both questions, even when
\displaystyle K
is a rational function field over a field
\displaystyle k
of characteristic 0 and
\displaystyle T
is a set of discrete valuations coming from closed points of
\displaystyle{\bf P}^{1}_{k}
. Regarding the first question, we shall show:

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a field
\displaystyle k
of characteristic zero such that for every finite
set
\displaystyle T
of discrete valuations of
\displaystyle K:=k(t)
coming from rational points of
\displaystyle{\bf P}^{1}_{k}

the diagonal map

H^{1}(K,{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z})\to\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z})

has infinite cokernel, provided
\displaystyle T
has at least 2 elements.

In the above theorem the field
\displaystyle k
is of infinite transcendence degree over
\displaystyle{\bf Q}
. In fact, we shall see that for
\displaystyle k
a finitely generated extension of
\displaystyle{\bf Q}
and
\displaystyle T
as in the theorem the quotient
\displaystyle\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M)\right)/\overline{H^{1}(K,M)}
is always finite for a finite étale commutative
\displaystyle k
-group scheme
\displaystyle M
. However, Question
1.1
(2) has a negative answer even for
\displaystyle K={\bf Q}(t)
or
\displaystyle{\bf Q}_{2}(t)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\displaystyle k
be
\displaystyle{\bf Q}
or
\displaystyle{\bf Q}_{2}
, and let
\displaystyle T
be a finite
set of discrete valuations of
\displaystyle K:=k(t)
coming from closed points of
\displaystyle{\bf P}^{1}_{k}
.
The cokernel of the diagonal map

H^{1}(K,{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z})\to\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z})

can have arbitrarily large dimension over
\displaystyle{\bf F}_{2}
as
\displaystyle T
varies.

In fact, in the case
\displaystyle k={\bf Q}_{2}
we shall find examples where the
\displaystyle v\in T
come from rational points.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
\displaystyle k={\bf Q}
(resp.
\displaystyle k={\bf Q}_{2}
).
If
\displaystyle T
is an infinite set of discrete valuations of
\displaystyle K
coming
from closed points of
\displaystyle{\bf P}^{1}_{\bf Q}
(resp. from rational points
of
\displaystyle{\bf P}^{1}_{{\bf Q}_{2}}
), the quotient
\displaystyle\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z})\right)/\overline{H^{1}(K,{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z})}
can be infinite.

As a by-product of the above result, we also obtain:

Corollary 1.5
.

In the case
\displaystyle k={\bf Q}_{2}
the subgroup
\displaystyle\cyrille X^{2}_{\omega}(\mu_{8}^{\otimes 2})\subset H^{2}(K,\mu_{8}^{\otimes 2})
consisting of elements with trivial image in

\displaystyle H^{2}(K_{v},\mu_{8}^{\otimes 2})
for all but finitely many
\displaystyle v\in{\bf P}^{1}_{k}
is infinite.

The interest in the groups
\displaystyle\cyrille X^{2}_{\omega}(\mu_{m}^{\otimes 2})
stems from the fact that they control weak approximation on quotients of semisimple simply connected
\displaystyle K
-groups by finite abelian constant subgroups. Whether they can be nontrivial was asked by M. L. Nguyen in (
[
8
]
, Question 3 of Section 3).

We shall prove the above statements in more general form in Sections
3
and
4
.

2.
Preliminaries

Our main tool in studying Questions
1.1
is Saltman’s method in the adaptation of Colliot-Thélène and Sansuc
[
3
]
. If
\displaystyle M
is a group of multiplicative type over a field
\displaystyle K
, (
[
3
]
, Proposition 1.3) gives an exact sequence

(1)

1\to M\to F\to P\to 1

with
\displaystyle F
a flasque and
\displaystyle P
a quasi-trivial torus, called a
flasque resolution of
\displaystyle M
.
Recall that a torus
\displaystyle F
is
flasque
if its cocharacter
group

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=6.83331pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=7.81946pt}

\displaystyle\displaystyle F

satisfies
\displaystyle H^{1}(H,{\mathchoice{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 7.09259pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\lower 7.09259pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=6.83331pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=7.81946pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\displaystyle\displaystyle F$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 7.09259pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\lower 7.09259pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\textstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=6.83331pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=7.81946pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\displaystyle\textstyle F$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 6.40926pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\lower 6.40926pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=4.78334pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=6.14795pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptstyle F$}\crcr}}}}{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{\raise 5.9537pt\hbox{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\lower 5.9537pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\vrule width=0.0pt,height=3.41667pt\vrule height=0.0pt,width=5.20493pt}$}}}}\cr\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptscriptstyle F$}\crcr}}}}})=0
for
every subgroup
\displaystyle H
of the Galois group of a finite Galois field
extension that splits
\displaystyle F
, and a torus
\displaystyle P
is
quasitrivial
if its character module is a permutation module.
Here if
\displaystyle M
splits (i.e. becomes diagonalizable) over a finite Galois extension
\displaystyle L|K
, then so do
\displaystyle F
and
\displaystyle P
.

The following lemma, which will serve several times, is a slight variant of an argument in the proof of (
[
3
]
, Proposition 8.4).

Lemma 2.1
.

In the above situation let
\displaystyle T
be a finite set of discrete valuations of
\displaystyle K
and let
\displaystyle K_{v}
be the completion of
\displaystyle K
with respect to
\displaystyle v\in T
. We have a canonical isomorphism

{\rm Coker}(H^{1}(K,M)\to\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M))\cong{\rm Coker}(H^{1}(K,F)\to\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},F)).

Proof.

Consider the commutative exact diagram

\begin{CD}F(K)@>{}>{}>P(K)@>{}>{}>H^{1}(K,M)@>{}>{}>H^{1}(K,F)@>{}>{}>0\\
@V{}V{}V@V{}V{}V@V{}V{}V@V{}V{}V\\
\prod_{v\in T}F(K_{v})@>{}>{}>\prod_{v\in T}P(K_{v})@>{}>{}>\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M)@>{}>{}>\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},F)@>{}>{}>0\end{CD}

where the zeros on the right come from the fact that
\displaystyle P
has trivial first cohomology over any extension of
\displaystyle K
by Hilbert’s Theorem 90 and Shapiro’s lemma. We endow the groups
\displaystyle F(K_{v})
and
\displaystyle P(K_{v})
with the
\displaystyle v
-adic topology.
The first map in the lower row has open image by the implicit function theorem and the second vertical map has dense image by the weak approximation lemma. The lemma follows by a diagram chase.
∎

The following obvious consequence will be crucial for our constructions.

Corollary 2.2
.

If
\displaystyle{\rm Coker}(H^{1}(K,M)\to\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M))\neq 0
, then
\displaystyle H^{1}(K_{v},F)\neq 0
for some
\displaystyle v\in T
.

The lemma also yields the result of Colliot-Thélène and Sansuc mentioned in the introduction.

Corollary 2.3

(
[
3
]
, Proposition 8.4 (ii))
.

The Grunwald–Wang theorem holds when
\displaystyle M
is a group of multiplicative type that becomes diagonalizable over a metacyclic extension of
\displaystyle K
.

Recall that a finite group is called metacyclic if its
\displaystyle p
-Sylow subgroups are cyclic.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle M
is split by a metacyclic extension, so is
\displaystyle F
. But then by a theorem of Endo and Miyata
\displaystyle F
is a direct summand in a quasi-trivial torus, hence
\displaystyle H^{1}(K,F)=0
. The same argument gives
\displaystyle H^{1}(K_{v},F)=0
for all
\displaystyle v
. Now apply the lemma.
∎

In fact, with a little more work, Colliot-Thélène and Sansuc get the vanishing of the groups
\displaystyle H^{1}(K_{v},F)
under the weaker assumption that
\displaystyle M_{K_{v}}
is split by a metacyclic extension, which suffices for the Grunwald-Wang property. For us what is important from this discussion is the following corollary:

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle n
be an integer not divisible by the characteristic of
\displaystyle K
.
If
\displaystyle\sqrt{-1}\in K
, then the Grunwald–Wang theorem holds for
\displaystyle M={\bf Z}/n{\bf Z}
. In general the abelian group
\displaystyle{\rm Coker}(H^{1}(K,{\bf Z}/n{\bf Z})\to\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},{\bf Z}/n{\bf Z}))
is of exponent 2.

Proof.

For the first statement, note that
\displaystyle{\bf Z}/n{\bf Z}
as a group of multiplicative type is split by the Galois extension
\displaystyle K(\mu_{n})|K
. The
\displaystyle p
-Sylow subgroups of
\displaystyle{\rm Gal}(K(\mu_{n})|K)
are always cyclic for
\displaystyle p\neq 2
and also for
\displaystyle p=2
when
\displaystyle\sqrt{-1}\in K
(‘we are not in the special case’), so the previous corollary applies. The second statement follows by a restriction-corestriction argument for the extension
\displaystyle K(\sqrt{-1})|K
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Though we shall not need it, we remark for the sake of completeness that when
\displaystyle K
has characteristic
\displaystyle p>0
, the Grunwald–Wang theorem holds for finite étale
\displaystyle p
-torsion
\displaystyle M
. This is proven by the same arguments as in the case of global function fields (see
[
10
]
, Theorem 9.2.5).

3.
The general construction

In this section we assume
\displaystyle k
is a field of characteristic 0, and let
\displaystyle M
be a finite étale commutative
\displaystyle k
-group scheme. As in (
1
), we choose a flasque resolution

1\to M\to F\to P\to 1

over
\displaystyle k
. Recall from (
[
3
]
, Proposition 1.3) that though the flasque torus
\displaystyle F
in the above resolution is not unique, it is unique up to multiplication by a quasi-trivial torus. Since
\displaystyle H^{1}
of a quasi-trivial torus is trivial, the group
\displaystyle H^{1}(k,F)
depends only on
\displaystyle M
and not on the choice of flasque resolution. Therefore the assumptions in the following theorem are unambiguous.

Theorem 3.1
.

With notation as above, set
\displaystyle K:=k(t)
and
let moreover
\displaystyle T
be a set of discrete valuations of
\displaystyle K
coming from rational points of
\displaystyle{\bf P}^{1}_{k}
.

The quotient

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M)\right)/\overline{H^{1}(K,M)}

is infinite under either of the following conditions:

(1)

The group
\displaystyle H^{1}(k,F)
is infinite and
\displaystyle T
has at least 2 elements.

(2)

The group
\displaystyle H^{1}(k,F)
is nontrivial and
\displaystyle T
is infinite.

Recall that the group
\displaystyle H^{1}(k,F)
is known to be finite for many types of fields
\displaystyle k
(see the next section) but may be infinite (see Section 8 of
[
2
]
for several examples, one of which we’ll use below).

We thank Jean-Louis Colliot-Thélène for simplifying our already quite simple proof.

Proof.

Assume
\displaystyle T
is finite. By Lemma
2.1
we then have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M)\right)/

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{H^{1}(K,M)}\cong\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},F)\right)/\overline{H^{1}(K,F)}\cong

\displaystyle\displaystyle\cong{\rm Coker}\,(H^{1}(K,F)/H^{1}(k,F)\to\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},F)\right)/H^{1}(k,F)).

Here the group
\displaystyle H^{1}(K,F)/H^{1}(k,F)
is trivial because the natural map
\displaystyle H^{1}(k,F)\to H^{1}({\bf A}^{1}_{k},F)
is an isomorphism (e.g. by
[
3
]
, Lemma 2.4) and the map
\displaystyle H^{1}({\bf A}^{1}_{k},F)\to H^{1}(K,F)
is surjective by flasqueness of
\displaystyle F
(
[
3
]
, Theorem 2.2 (i)).

So we are left with the quotient
\displaystyle(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},F))/H^{1}(k,F)
.
Let
\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}_{v}
be the ring of integers of
\displaystyle K_{v}
; it has residue field
\displaystyle k
as
\displaystyle v
comes from a rational point. As
\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}_{v}
is complete and

\displaystyle F
is flasque, we
have

H^{1}(k,F)\simeq H^{1}({\mathcal{O}}_{v},F)\simeq H^{1}(K_{v},F),

the first isomorphism by (
[
7
]
, Remark III.3.11 (a)) and the second by combining the main result of
[
9
]
with

[
3
]
, Theorem 2.2 (i)). Thus if
\displaystyle H^{1}(k,F)
is infinite, assertion (1) follows by applying the lemma with
\displaystyle S=F
. If we only know that
\displaystyle H^{1}(k,F)
is nontrivial, then the same argument shows that the size of the quotient is unbounded as we increase
\displaystyle T
, whence assertion (2).∎

As a first consequence, we derive Theorem
1.2
from assertion (1) above.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We start with a field
\displaystyle k_{0}
such that in a flasque resolution

1\to{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z}\to F_{0}\to P_{0}\to 1

over
\displaystyle k_{0}

the flasque torus
\displaystyle F_{0}
satisfies
\displaystyle H^{1}(k_{0},F_{0})\neq 0
. For instance

\displaystyle k_{0}={\bf Q}_{2}
has this property thanks to Wang’s counterexample and Corollary
2.2
. Now
by (
[
2
]
, Example 8.4)
there exists an (infinitely generated) field extension
\displaystyle k
of
\displaystyle k_{0}
such that

\displaystyle H^{1}(k,F)
is infinite, where
\displaystyle F
is the base change of
\displaystyle F_{0}
to
\displaystyle k
. Moreover, if
\displaystyle P
is the base change of
\displaystyle P
to
\displaystyle k
, then

\displaystyle 0\to{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z}\to F\to P\to 0

is a flasque resolution over
\displaystyle k
by (
[
3
]
, Proposition 1.4). Now apply Theorem
3.1
(1).
∎

4.
Arithmetic base fields

In the setup of the previous section assume moreover
\displaystyle k
is a finite extension of
\displaystyle{\bf Q}_{p}
or a finitely generated extension of
\displaystyle{\bf Q}
. In this case the groups
\displaystyle H^{1}(K_{v},F)
are finite for a flasque
\displaystyle k
-torus
\displaystyle F
in view of (
[
1
]
, Theorem 3.2) in the
\displaystyle p

[
1
]
, Theorem 3.2) combined with (
[
3
]

the groups
\displaystyle\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M)\right)/\overline{H^{1}(K,M)}
are finite for finite
\displaystyle T

\displaystyle M
. However, for infinite
\displaystyle T
this may not be the case. We start with the local case:

Corollary 4.1
.

For
\displaystyle K={\bf Q}_{2}(t)
,
the groups
\displaystyle\left(\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z})\right)/\overline{H^{1}(K,{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z})}
are infinite if
\displaystyle T
is an infinite set of rational points of

\displaystyle{\bf P}^{1}_{k}

Proof.

Again by Wang’s counterexample and Corollary
2.2
the flasque torus
\displaystyle F
in a flasque resolution of
\displaystyle{\bf Z}/8{\bf Z}
over
\displaystyle{\bf Q}
satisfies
\displaystyle H^{1}({\bf Q}_{2},F)\neq 0

(2).
∎

This proves Theorem

and its corollary in the case
\displaystyle k={\bf Q}_{2}
. Note that when
\displaystyle K={\bf Q}_{p}(t)
with
\displaystyle p>2
, the quotient in the corollary is trivial (’we are not in the special case’).

We can also derive Corollary
1.5
. Recall the context: for
\displaystyle i>0
and a finite étale commutative
\displaystyle k
-group scheme
\displaystyle M
we denote by
\displaystyle\cyrille X^{i}_{\omega}(M)
(resp.
\displaystyle\cyrille X^{i}(M)
) the subgroup of classes in
\displaystyle H^{i}(K,M)
whose image is trivial in
\displaystyle H^{i}(K_{v},M)
for all but finitely many (resp. for all) closed points
\displaystyle v
of
\displaystyle{\bf P}^{1}_{k}
. Let us restate the corollary:

Corollary 4.2
.

For
\displaystyle K={\bf Q}_{2}(t)
the group
\displaystyle\cyrille X^{2}_{\omega}(\mu_{8}^{\otimes 2})
is infinite.

Proof.

For
\displaystyle M
as above we have an exact sequence

(2)

0\to\overline{{H^{1}(K,M)}}\to\prod_{v}H^{1}(K_{v},M)\to\cyrille X^{2}_{\omega}({\rm{Hom}}(M,\mu_{n}^{\otimes 2}))^{D}\to\cyrille X^{2}({\rm{Hom}}(M,\mu_{n}^{\otimes 2}))^{D}\to 0,

where the superscript

denotes
\displaystyle{\bf Q}/{\bf Z}
-linear dual and in the product
\displaystyle v
runs over all closed points of
\displaystyle{\bf P}^{1}_{k}
. This follows after passing to the direct limit over all finite sets
\displaystyle T
of
\displaystyle v
as above from the exact sequence

H^{1}(K,M)\to\prod_{v\in T}H^{1}(K_{v},M)\to\cyrille X^{2}_{T}({\rm{Hom}}(M,\mu_{n}^{\otimes 2}))^{D}\to\cyrille X^{2}({\rm{Hom}}(M,\mu_{n}^{\otimes 2}))^{D}\to 0

proven in more general form in (
[
5
]
, Lemme 2.2). Here the group
\displaystyle\cyrille X^{2}({\rm{Hom}}(M,\mu_{n}^{\otimes 2}))
is finite and dual to
\displaystyle\cyrille X^{2}(M)
by (
[
4
]
, Theorem 1.4). It remains to apply the previous corollary.
∎

Remarks 4.3
.

(1)

The previous two corollaries hold not just for 8 but for any
\displaystyle 2^{r}
with
\displaystyle r\geq 3
.

(2)

Consider still the case
\displaystyle K={\bf Q}_{2}(t)
and the flasque torus
\displaystyle F
in the proof of Corollary
4.1
By (
[
4
]
, Theorem 4.3 (b)) we have an exact sequence

0\to\overline{H^{1}(K,F)}\to\prod_{v}H^{1}(K_{v},F)\to\cyrille X^{2}_{\omega}(F^{\prime})^{D}\to\cyrille X^{1}(F)\to 0

analogous to (
2
), where

is the dual torus to

. Again the last group is known to be finite (

, Theorem 2.4), so Corollary

implies that
\displaystyle\cyrille X^{2}_{\omega}(F^{\prime})

different from that of (

]
, Prop. 4.5).

We finally turn to the proof of Theorem
1.3
in the case of
\displaystyle{\bf Q}(t)
. We need the following lemma which must be well known:

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\displaystyle k
be an infinite field, and let
\displaystyle L
be a finite separable extension of
\displaystyle k
.
There exist infinitely many closed points of
\displaystyle{\bf A}_{k}^{1}
with
residue field isomorphic to
\displaystyle L
.

Proof.

As the case
\displaystyle L=k
is obvious, we can assume
\displaystyle[L:k]\geq 2
. Since
\displaystyle L|k
is separable, there are only finitely many intermediate fields
\displaystyle k\subsetneq k^{\prime}\subsetneq L
(e.g. by Galois theory applied to the Galois closure of
\displaystyle L
). The Weil restriction
\displaystyle{\bf R}_{L|k}{\bf A}_{L}^{1}
is isomorphic to
\displaystyle{[L:k]}
-dimensional affine space, and the
\displaystyle{\bf R}_{k^{\prime}|k}{\bf A}_{k^{\prime}}^{1}
with
\displaystyle k^{\prime}
as above give rise to a finite collection of proper closed subvarieties of smaller dimension. As
\displaystyle k
is infinite, their open complement
has infinitely many
\displaystyle k
-points, which then correspond to closed points of
\displaystyle{\bf A}^{1}_{k}
with residue field
\displaystyle L

Proof of Theorem

Remark 4.5
.

A direct generalization of the above argument shows the following statement. Suppose
\displaystyle k
is a field of finite type over its prime field and
\displaystyle M
is a finite étale commutative
\displaystyle k
-group scheme for which the Grunwald–Wang property fails for some finite set of discrete valuations of
\displaystyle k

\displaystyle M
