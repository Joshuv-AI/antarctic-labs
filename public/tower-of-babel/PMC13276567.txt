Assessment of Moringa oleifera thermoreversible gel as a supplement to nonsurgical periodontal therapy in Stage III periodontitis – A randomized controlled clinical study
Authors: Ramamurthy Shanmugapriya, Sekar Ramya, Vargheese Sheeja, Rajendran Reshmaa, Subramaniam Deepa, Sekar Priyadarsini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease resulting from microbial biofilm and host immune responses, leading to progressive loss of periodontal attachment. Although scaling and root planing (SRP) remains the primary nonsurgical treatment, adjunctive therapies may enhance clinical and biochemical outcomes. This study evaluated the efficacy of a thermoreversible Moringa oleifera (MO) gel as an adjunct to SRP in Stage III periodontitis.Material and methodsThirty patients (90 sites) with Stage III periodontitis were randomly assigned to three groups: Group I – SRP alone, Group II – SRP + tetracycline fibers, and Group III – SRP + 5% MO gel. Clinical parameters and gingival crevicular fluid biomarkers (periostin, nitric oxide [NO], and total antioxidant capacity [TAOC]) were assessed at baseline and two months post-treatment. Data were analyzed using ANOVA followed by post-hoc comparisons.ResultsAll groups showed significant clinical improvement (p < 0.001). Adjunctive groups demonstrated greater reductions in plaque, gingival, and bleeding indices than SRP alone. The MO group showed a significantly higher increase in periostin levels compared with the tetracycline group (p = 0.029) and the largest reduction in NO concentrations (p < 0.001). TAOC increased across all groups, with numerically higher yet nonsignificant values in the MO group.ConclusionThe Moringa oleifera thermoreversible gel exhibited clinical and biochemical efficacy comparable to tetracycline fibers, suggesting its potential as a safe, natural adjunct in nonsurgical periodontal therapy. Larger, long-term studies are recommended to validate these outcomes.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a multifactorial chronic inflammatory condition characterized by the destruction of periodontal supporting tissues, triggered by complex interactions between pathogenic microorganisms and the host immune response.1 The microbial biofilm acts as the primary etiological factor, initiating an immune-inflammatory cascade that often results in collateral tissue damage rather than bacterial injury alone.2 Scaling and root planing (SRP) remains the cornerstone of nonsurgical periodontal therapy, aiming to mechanically disrupt and remove the subgingival biofilm. However, complete elimination of pathogenic bacteria is often limited in deep periodontal pockets and within the protective biofilm matrix, necessitating adjunctive therapeutic approaches to enhance clinical outcomes.3

To overcome these challenges, local drug delivery (LDD) systems have been introduced, providing targeted, sustained release of antimicrobial or anti-inflammatory agents directly into periodontal sites.4 Among various delivery platforms, thermoreversible gels have gained attention due to their sol-to-gel transition at physiological temperatures, allowing easy application, prolonged retention at the diseased site, and controlled drug release.5

Moringa oleifera Lam., commonly known as the drumstick tree, has been extensively studied for its wide pharmacological and therapeutic spectrum. Rich in flavonoids, phenolic acids, glucosinolates, isothiocyanates, alkaloids, vitamins, and essential minerals, Moringa exhibits potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and tissue-protective effects.6 Recent studies have demonstrated its successful incorporation in biocompatible pharmaceutical formulations, such as in-situ gels, oleogels, and encapsulated systems, supporting its potential as a bioactive plant-based therapeutic option.7

Periostin, a matricellular protein predominantly found in collagen-rich tissues, plays a pivotal role in maintaining periodontal ligament integrity and promoting tissue repair. In periodontitis, a decrease in periostin expression correlates with disease severity and attachment loss, whereas its upregulation is associated with periodontal healing following nonsurgical or surgical therapy.8 Despite the extensive pharmacological exploration of Moringa oleifera, limited evidence exists regarding its influence on periostin expression in the context of periodontal healing.9

Given these insights, the present randomized controlled clinical trial aimed to evaluate the adjunctive effectiveness of a 5% thermoreversible Moringa oleifera aqueous extract gel in combination with scaling and root planing in patients diagnosed with Stage III Grade B periodontitis. This investigation focused on assessing its short-term impact on clinical periodontal parameters and gingival crevicular fluid biomarkers, thereby exploring the potential role of Moringa oleifera as a natural agent supporting periodontal healing.

Methodology

This randomized controlled clinical trial utilized a three-arm split-mouth design, with each participant receiving all three interventions (1:1:1 allocation ratio across three quadrants) to evaluate adjunctive therapies to scaling and root planing (SRP) for Stage III periodontitis. Trial setting and eligibility

This single-centre trial was conducted at the Department of Periodontics, Saveetha Dental College, Chennai, India, recruiting otherwise healthy adults with Stage III Grade B periodontitis from January to April 2025. Grade B was assigned based on: (i) no evidence of rapid bone loss within the prior five years; (ii) estimated bone loss/age ratio of 0.25–1.0; (iii) moderate case complexity with interdental CAL ≥5 mm; and (iv) absence of current smoking and well-controlled or absent systemic risk factors, per the 2017 World Workshop Classification.10,11 Participants required ≥20 natural teeth and ≥5 mm interdental clinical attachment loss in ≥3 non-adjacent sites per quadrant. Exclusions encompassed pregnancy or lactation, current smoking, systemic diseases, and periodontal treatment or antibiotic/anti-inflammatory use within the preceding 6 and 3 months, respectively.

Interventions

All sites underwent supragingival prophylaxis and full-mouth subgingival SRP using ultrasonic and hand instruments in a single visit. Group I (control): SRP alone. Group II: SRP + tetracycline fibers (Perio AB Plus®, Group Pharmaceuticals, India) inserted per manufacturer's instructions. Group III: SRP +5% Moringa oleifera thermoreversible gel.

Gel preparation protocol: The 5% Moringa oleifera thermoreversible gel was prepared in-house and is not commercially available. Fresh Moringa oleifera leaves were shade-dried, powdered, and a 5% aqueous extract was prepared by maceration (5 g powder in 100 mL cold deionized water, stirred for 24 h, filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper). To prepare 50 mL of thermoreversible gel using the cold method, 9.25 g of Poloxamer 407 (19% w/v; Sigma Aldrich, Mumbai, India) was slowly added to 40 mL of cold deionized water (4–5°C) under constant stirring and refrigerated for 4–5 h to yield a clear homogeneous solution. Subsequently, 0.75 g of Carbopol 934 (0.2% w/v; Sigma Aldrich, Mumbai, India) dissolved in 10 mL of cold water was incorporated and mixed thoroughly. Finally, 2.5 mL of the 5% Moringa oleifera aqueous extract was added and gently mixed to yield the final formulation.7,12

Gelation temperature: The formulation undergoes sol-to-gel transition at 34 ± 0.5°C, within the physiological intraoral temperature range of 36–37°C, ensuring flowability at room temperature for syringe delivery and gelation upon contact with periodontal pocket tissues.12 The prepared gel was stored at 4°C and administered as a sol via a periodontal syringe for in-pocket gelation.

In-vitro release characteristics: Drug release from this formulation was time-dependent in prior in-vitro validation: 29.35% at 1 h, 86.28% at 4 h, and 98.60% at 8 h under sink conditions at 37°C in phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), with drug content stability of 98.32% at 30 days when stored at 4°C.12

Rationale for concentrations: Poloxamer 407 at 19% w/v represents the minimum concentration demonstrating thermoreversibility at 36°C; Carbopol 934 at 0.2% w/v provides mucoadhesive properties without compromising gel flow, as established in prior optimization studies.12,13 The 5% extract concentration was selected based on prior validated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity data.7 All sites received periodontal dressings following intervention for uniformity.

Outcomes

Pre-specified primary clinical outcomes included Plaque Index (PI), Gingival Index (GI), Sulcus Bleeding Index (SBI), Probing Pocket Depth (PPD), and Clinical Attachment Level (CAL), measured as change from baseline at 2 months using a UNC-15 periodontal probe. Secondary biochemical outcomes comprised periostin (POSTN) measured via ELISA (RayBio®, USA), Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAOC) by FRAP assay, and Nitric Oxide (NO) by colorimetric assay kit, from gingival crevicular fluid (∼2 μL/site, PBS-diluted, stored at −80°C until analysis). Adverse events were systematically assessed at each follow-up visit by clinical inspection for local tissue reactions and structured participant questioning regarding pain, burning, or swelling.

Sample size

G∗Power 3.1.9.7 calculated 30 participants (90 sites, 3 sites/patient) for 95% power (1–β = 0.95), α = 0.05, based on expected PPD reduction differences, accounting for split-mouth correlations; no interim analyses or stopping rules were applied.

Randomization and allocation concealment

For each enrolled participant, three eligible quadrants (each containing ≥1 site with PPD ≥5 mm) were randomized to one of three interventions using a block randomization scheme (block size = 3), ensuring each participant received all three interventions in a randomly assigned quadrant order. Allocation sequences were generated prior to trial commencement by an independent statistician using a computer-generated random number table. Allocation concealment was achieved using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared and stored by a non-clinical coordinator. On the day of treatment, the treating periodontist opened envelopes in sequence immediately before commencing each quadrant's intervention, consistent with CONSORT 2010 recommendations.14

Blinding and implementation

The outcome examiner (UNC-15 measurements, GCF collection; intraclass correlation coefficient >0.85 on 10% repeat sites) was blinded to group allocations. Participants and therapists were unblinded due to the nature of interventions, but visual similarity was ensured via uniform periodontal dressings. Data analysts remained blinded until primary analysis completion.

Statistical methods

SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., USA) was used for all analyses. The unit of analysis was the periodontal site; each of the 30 participants contributed exactly one site per treatment group (one site per randomized quadrant), yielding 30 sites per group (n = 90 total). In the split-mouth design, each participant serves as their own control, inherently accounting for subject-level confounding. Paired t-tests assessed intragroup changes from baseline to two months within each group. One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc correction compared intergroup differences at equivalent time points (p < 0.05). Exact p-values are reported to three decimal places; values below 0.001 are reported as p < 0.001 per convention. Cohen's d effect sizes were calculated for key primary and biochemical outcome comparisons to supplement p-value reporting.15 This statistical approach is consistent with published split-mouth periodontal clinical trials employing comparable designs.16,17 A formal mixed-effects model was not employed; this is acknowledged as a limitation. No missing data handling was required as all 30 participants completed the study.

Results

All thirty participants (aged 21–60 years) completed the clinical trial. At baseline, there were no statistically significant differences among the three groups in any of the clinical or biochemical parameters, confirming initial comparability. Minor variations were observed in periostin and nitric oxide levels but were not statistically significant (Table 1). No adverse events were recorded in any treatment group during the two-month observation period. Following two months of treatment, significant intragroup improvements were noted across all groups in clinical parameters, including Plaque Index (PI), Gingival Index (GI), Sulcular Bleeding Index (SBI), Probing Pocket Depth (PPD), and Clinical Attachment Level (CAL). However, the magnitude of improvement varied among the treatment groups.

Both adjunctive therapy groups, Group II (SRP + tetracycline) and Group III (SRP + Moringa oleifera gel), demonstrated significantly greater reductions in PI, GI, and SBI scores compared to SRP alone (Group I) (p < 0.001). PPD reductions were also more pronounced in Groups II and III, with post-treatment means of 3.73 ± 0.69 mm and 3.73 ± 0.58 mm, respectively, compared with 4.90 ± 0.30 mm for Group I (p < 0.001; Cohen's d for Group III vs. Group I = 1.98, indicating a large effect size). CAL improved correspondingly, with post-treatment means of 4.53 ± 0.69 mm (Group II) and 4.53 ± 0.58 mm (Group III) versus 5.70 ± 0.30 mm (Group I) (p < 0.001; Cohen's d = 1.87).

Biochemical analysis revealed an increase in periostin (POSTN) levels post-treatment in all groups, consistent with periodontal healing responses following mechanical debridement. The Moringa group exhibited a significantly higher rise in periostin concentration (72.75 ± 7.64 ng/mL; p = 0.029; Cohen's d = 0.72) compared to the tetracycline group, suggesting a biochemical environment more supportive of healing-associated periostin expression. Total antioxidant capacity (TAOC) improved across all groups, with Group III showing the numerically highest values post-treatment (921.80 ± 67.30 μmol/L), though the intergroup difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.843).

Nitric oxide (NO) levels, a marker of oxidative and nitrosative stress, decreased significantly post-treatment in all groups. The greatest reduction was observed in Group III (53.83 ± 12.69 μmol/L), followed by Group II (59.27 ± 11.80 μmol/L), both showing statistically significant differences compared to the SRP-alone group (p < 0.001; Cohen's d for Group III vs. Group I = 1.20). These biochemical shifts are associatively consistent with a reduction in local inflammatory and oxidative burden.

Overall, while both adjunctive modalities improved clinical and biochemical outcomes compared to SRP alone, the Moringa oleifera thermoreversible gel demonstrated favorable effects in enhancing periostin expression and reducing nitric oxide levels, reflecting shifts in oxidative defense and the local biochemical milieu that are supportive of periodontal tissue healing. The collective findings suggest that the adjunctive use of a Moringa oleifera thermoreversible gel with nonsurgical periodontal therapy is associated with improved short-term clinical recovery and favorable biochemical modulation compared to SRP alone.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to assess and compare the short-term clinical and biochemical effects of three treatment approaches in Stage III Grade B periodontitis: SRP alone, SRP combined with tetracycline local drug delivery (Group II), and SRP combined with Moringa oleifera thermoreversible gel (Group III). The findings suggest that the adjunctive use of a 5% Moringa oleifera thermoreversible gel in combination with nonsurgical periodontal therapy may provide additional short-term clinical and biochemical benefits compared to SRP alone. The observed improvements in plaque control, gingival inflammation, bleeding on probing, and probing depth, together with favorable shifts in periostin, nitric oxide, and antioxidant markers, are consistent with the potential of Moringa as a biologically active, plant-derived adjunct in periodontal management. The greater reductions in PI, GI, SBI, PPD, and CAL in the tetracycline and Moringa groups compared with SRP alone reflect the added benefit of local adjunctive therapy in disrupting subgingival biofilm and modulating the local inflammatory milieu. The finding that Moringa gel performed comparably to tetracycline in most clinical parameters suggests that phytochemical-rich formulations may approach the efficacy of conventional antimicrobial agents when delivered locally in a sustained manner. The sol–gel transition behavior of the thermoreversible system likely contributed to prolonged retention in the periodontal pocket and enhanced contact time between the active constituents and diseased tissues.

Moringa oleifera has been shown to inhibit inflammatory markers in lipopolysaccharide-activated human macrophages, reducing mRNA expression of IL-6, IL-1β, NF-κB, PTGS2, and TNF-α through suppression of IκB-α phosphorylation 18, 19, 20, 21, 22. These properties are broadly consistent with the clinical anti-inflammatory effects observed in the present study, though direct mechanistic evidence from the current clinical context is associative rather than causal.

The increase in periostin levels across all groups is consistent with periodontal healing following mechanical debridement, as periostin is closely associated with periodontal ligament remodeling and connective tissue repair.8,15 The relatively greater rise in periostin in the Moringa group (p = 0.029; Cohen's d = 0.72) is consistent with a biochemical environment supportive of healing-associated matrix remodeling processes, possibly through improved redox balance and dampening of proinflammatory pathways. It is important to note, however, that periostin changes in this study represent an associative biochemical finding consistent with a milieu more conducive to tissue repair, rather than direct histological evidence of periodontal regeneration. Although direct molecular interactions between Moringa phytoconstituents and periostin expression remain to be elucidated, the current pattern aligns with a local environment more favorable to fibroblast function, collagen turnover, and matrix organization.23

Moringa oleifera leaves contain a broad spectrum of bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, carotenoids, glucosinolates, and isothiocyanates, conferring antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities.6,24 Experimental work has demonstrated that Moringa extracts can scavenge free radicals, inhibit lipid peroxidation, and attenuate proinflammatory mediators.7,25 The significant reduction in nitric oxide levels and improvement in TAOC observed in the Moringa group are consistent with these documented properties and suggest a local rebalancing of oxidative and nitrosative stress within periodontal tissues. These biochemical markers are recognized as indirect, associative indicators of inflammatory status and oxidative balance; they do not provide direct evidence of tissue regeneration or attachment gain beyond what clinical measurements indicate.26

Previous reviews and experimental studies have highlighted the diverse pharmacological profile of Moringa oleifera, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and wound-healing effects 6, 7, 8,19. Phytochemical investigations have identified multiple therapeutic constituents in Moringa leaves exhibiting antimicrobial activity against oral pathogens that could complement mechanical plaque control.27,28 Toxicological evaluations of aqueous leaf extracts have suggested a favorable safety margin in animal models, supporting biocompatibility for local use, though long-term human safety data in intraoral applications remain limited.29

Our clinical findings are broadly consistent with, and add important human data to, the existing experimental literature on Moringa oleifera in periodontitis and oral inflammation. Wang et al. demonstrated that Moringa oleifera leaf extract reduced inflammation and alveolar bone resorption in a ligature-induced rat periodontitis model by modulating the p38α/MAPK14–OPG/RANKL pathway.16 Sahrakary et al. reported that Moringa leaf extract significantly lowered gingival IL-1β and TNF-α levels in a rat model.30 A recent narrative review concluded that Moringa extracts show promise in managing oral inflammatory conditions through antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.31 Clinical work on Moringa-based dentifrices has demonstrated significant reductions in plaque and gingival indices.17 Comparisons with tetracycline in the present study suggest MO is not statistically superior overall but offers comparable benefits, potentially without the risk of antibiotic resistance induction.

Limitations

This study has some important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the two-month follow-up period is short for assessing long-term clinical attachment changes or definitive periodontal healing. Second, histological assessments were not performed, precluding any tissue-level conclusions. And, the Moringa oleifera aqueous extract used in this study was not subjected to formal phytochemical standardization or quantification of active marker compounds such as total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, and quercetin-3-glucoside. The biological activity of plant extracts is known to vary based on geographical origin, harvest season, and extraction parameters.6,32 Finally, the statistical analysis employed paired t-tests and one-way ANOVA, which, while appropriate and well-established for split-mouth trials with one site per patient per group,33,34 did not employ formal mixed-effects modelling; future larger trials should consider linear mixed models for a more rigorous treatment of intra-subject correlations.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled clinical study demonstrates that a 5% Moringa oleifera thermoreversible gel, when used as an adjunct to scaling and root planing in Stage III Grade B periodontitis, is associated with significant short-term clinical improvement and favorable shifts in periodontal biochemical markers, with outcomes comparable to tetracycline local delivery. The enhanced periostin expression, reduced nitric oxide levels, and improved total antioxidant capacity observed in the Moringa gel group are consistent with a healing-conducive local periodontal environment. These findings should be interpreted as preliminary short-term evidence; definitive conclusions regarding periodontal regeneration require long-term randomized controlled trials with larger cohorts, histological endpoints, and standardized extract characterization.
