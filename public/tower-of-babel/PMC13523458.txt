Next-generation CAR-NK cell therapy: engineering strategies, translational challenges, and future perspectives in cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Minoo Bakhtiari, Hamid Nickho, Mahmoud Mahmoudi, Fahimeh Lavi Arab, Akram Hoseinzadeh, Rasoul Baharlou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural killer (NK) cells play a central role in innate immune surveillance and represent a promising platform for next-generation cancer immunotherapy. Recent advances in chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) engineering have accelerated the development of CAR-NK cell therapies as potentially safer and more accessible alternatives to CAR-T cell therapy. Compared with CAR-T cells, CAR-NK therapies demonstrate lower risks of cytokine release syndrome and graft-versus-host disease while offering the potential for allogeneic “off-the-shelf” applications. Preclinical and early-phase clinical studies have shown encouraging antitumor activity of CAR-NK cells in both hematological malignancies and solid tumors. However, several translational challenges remain, including limited in vivo persistence, insufficient tumor trafficking, immunosuppressive tumor immune microenvironments, manufacturing variability, and restricted long-term clinical data. To overcome these barriers, emerging strategies such as CRISPR-based gene editing, cytokine-armored CAR constructs, multiplex engineering, checkpoint blockade combinations, and oncolytic virus-based approaches are being actively investigated. This review provides an updated overview of CAR-NK cell biology, engineering strategies, current clinical progress, major translational challenges, and future therapeutic opportunities. In addition, we discuss evolving combinatorial and next-generation engineering approaches that may improve persistence, safety, and therapeutic efficacy, ultimately facilitating the broader clinical translation of CAR-NK immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12935-026-04389-x.Keywords: CAR-NK cells, Cancer immunotherapy, Gene editing, Combination therapy

Introduction

Natural killer cells are critical components of the innate immune system that play essential roles in immune surveillance against malignant transformation and viral infection [1, 2]. Unlike adaptive immune cells, NK cells exert rapid cytotoxic activity without requiring prior antigen sensitization or major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-restricted antigen recognition, making them attractive candidates for cancer immunotherapy [3, 4]. Their intrinsic ability to recognize and eliminate stressed or transformed cells through a balance of activating and inhibitory receptor signaling has positioned NK cells as promising effector platforms for adoptive cellular therapy [5]. Over the past decade, substantial advances in cancer immunotherapy have transformed the therapeutic landscape of hematological malignancies and, more recently, solid tumors. Among these approaches, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-engineered immune cells have emerged as one of the most powerful strategies for redirecting immune effector cells toward tumor-associated antigens [6, 7]. CAR-T cell therapy has demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy in relapsed or refractory B-cell malignancies and has achieved several regulatory approvals worldwide [8, 9]. Nevertheless, important clinical limitations continue to restrict the broader applicability of CAR-T therapies, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) in allogeneic settings, antigen escape, manufacturing complexity, and limited efficacy against solid tumors [10–13].

To overcome these challenges, increasing attention has focused on the development of CAR-engineered NK (CAR-NK) cells as next-generation cellular immunotherapeutic platforms. Compared with CAR-T cells, CAR-NK therapies may offer several potential advantages, including lower risks of severe CRS and GvHD, preservation of intrinsic NK-cell-mediated cytotoxicity, reduced likelihood of long-term malignant transformation, and greater feasibility for allogeneic “off-the-shelf” manufacturing [14–17]. In addition, NK cells can eliminate tumor targets through both CAR-dependent and CAR-independent mechanisms, potentially reducing vulnerability to antigen-loss-mediated immune escape [18].

Recent advances in genetic engineering, viral and non-viral transduction systems, cytokine-armored CAR constructs, multiplex genome editing, and induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived NK platforms have substantially accelerated the translational development of CAR-NK therapies [19–21]. Encouraging findings from preclinical investigations and early-phase clinical trials have demonstrated promising antitumor activity of CAR-NK cells in both hematological malignancies and solid tumors [22–24]. However, several important barriers continue to limit their broader clinical implementation, including insufficient in vivo persistence, impaired tumor trafficking, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments (TME), manufacturing variability, and limited long-term clinical data [25–27].

In this review, we provide an updated and comprehensive overview of CAR-NK cell therapy, focusing on NK-cell biology, CAR engineering strategies, manufacturing platforms, current preclinical and clinical applications, major translational challenges, and emerging next-generation therapeutic approaches. We further discuss novel engineering strategies designed to improve persistence, tumor infiltration, safety, and antitumor efficacy, highlighting the evolving role of CAR-NK cells as promising candidates for future cancer immunotherapy.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive and structured literature search was conducted to identify relevant studies investigating CAR-NK cell therapy and its applications in cancer immunotherapy. Electronic databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov were systematically searched for studies published between January 2018 and February 2025. The search strategy incorporated combinations of the following keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms: “CAR-NK”, “chimeric antigen receptor natural killer cells”, “NK cell engineering”, “cancer immunotherapy”, “hematologic malignancies”, “solid tumors”, “tumor microenvironment”, “clinical trial”, “immune evasion”, and “combination therapy”.

Eligible studies included peer-reviewed original articles, preclinical investigations, translational studies, clinical trial reports, and high-impact review articles published in English. Particular emphasis was placed on studies addressing NK-cell biology, CAR engineering strategies, genetic modification platforms, tumor-targeting mechanisms, therapeutic efficacy, clinical outcomes, manufacturing approaches, and translational challenges associated with CAR-NK therapy. Ongoing and completed clinical trials were additionally identified and cross-referenced using ClinicalTrials.gov and relevant CAR-NK-related search terms.

Studies lacking sufficient scientific relevance, methodological rigor, or translational significance were excluded from the final evaluation. Reference lists of selected articles were also manually screened to identify additional high-relevance studies. The collected literature was subsequently categorized and analyzed according to major thematic areas, including NK-cell biology, CAR design and engineering, manufacturing strategies, clinical applications, tumor microenvironment-associated resistance mechanisms, and emerging next-generation therapeutic approaches.

NK cell sources for CAR engineering

The selection of an appropriate NK-cell source represents one of the most critical determinants influencing the safety, scalability, persistence, manufacturing feasibility, and antitumor efficacy of CAR-NK cell therapy. Various NK-cell platforms have been explored for adoptive immunotherapy, each possessing distinct biological characteristics, engineering compatibility, expansion potential, and translational limitations [28–30]. The ideal source for CAR-NK generation should combine potent cytotoxic activity, high proliferative capacity, genetic manipulability, prolonged in vivo persistence, favorable safety profiles, and compatibility with large-scale standardized manufacturing approaches [29, 30].

Currently, the major NK-cell sources used for CAR engineering include peripheral blood-derived NK (PB-NK) cells, umbilical cord blood-derived NK (UCB-NK) cells, iPSC- or human embryonic stem cell (hESC)-derived NK cells, immortalized NK-cell lines such as NK-92, cytokine-induced memory-like (CIML) NK cells, and hematopoietic stem cell (HSC)-derived NK platforms [30, 31]. Each platform offers unique translational advantages and challenges that critically influence therapeutic performance and clinical applicability.

Peripheral blood-derived NK cells

Peripheral blood remains one of the most commonly utilized and clinically accessible sources for NK-cell isolation and CAR engineering [28]. PB-NK cells are typically obtained through leukapheresis followed by immunomagnetic enrichment and ex vivo cytokine-mediated expansion. These cells possess strong intrinsic cytotoxic activity, mature effector phenotypes, and robust antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), making them attractive candidates for adoptive immunotherapy [32].

PB-NK-derived CAR platforms have demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity against both hematological malignancies and solid tumors in preclinical and early clinical investigations [33, 34]. Importantly, peripheral blood-derived NK cells retain native activating receptor repertoires, allowing simultaneous CAR-dependent and CAR-independent tumor elimination mechanisms [35, 36]. However, several translational limitations remain. PB-NK cells frequently exhibit donor-to-donor variability, relatively limited ex vivo expansion capacity, short in vivo persistence, and comparatively low viral transduction efficiency, particularly when using conventional lentiviral systems [37–39]. These challenges continue to complicate large-scale manufacturing and standardization of PB-NK-based therapeutic products.

Umbilical cord blood-derived NK cells

Umbilical cord blood has emerged as an attractive alternative source for CAR-NK engineering due to its immediate availability, lower immunogenicity, and favorable allogeneic compatibility [32]. UCB-derived NK cells generally display high proliferative potential, reduced risk of GvHD, and improved suitability for “off-the-shelf” manufacturing approaches [40]. Furthermore, cord blood units can be cryopreserved and banked, thereby facilitating rapid clinical accessibility and industrial-scale production.

Several studies have demonstrated promising therapeutic activity of UCB-derived CAR-NK cells targeting CD19-positive hematological malignancies [40, 41]. Notably, CAR-NK platforms incorporating IL-15 signaling and inducible caspase-9 suicide systems have shown improved persistence and favorable safety profiles in preclinical and clinical settings [40–42]. Nevertheless, UCB-NK cells may exhibit relatively immature phenotypes and lower baseline cytotoxicity compared with peripheral blood-derived NK cells, often requiring extensive cytokine activation and ex vivo expansion to achieve optimal antitumor functionality [32].

iPSC- and hESC-derived NK cells

Induced pluripotent stem cell-derived NK cells and hESC-derived NK cells have recently attracted substantial attention as highly scalable and genetically programmable CAR-NK platforms [30, 43]. Unlike primary NK cells, pluripotent stem cell-derived NK platforms permit extensive genetic engineering prior to differentiation, enabling precise insertion of CAR constructs, multiplex genome editing, cytokine-armored modifications, and safe-harbor transgene integration strategies [43, 44].

Importantly, iPSC-derived NK cells provide a highly standardized and renewable source for manufacturing homogeneous allogeneic CAR-NK products at industrial scale [30]. Li et al. demonstrated that mesothelin-targeted CAR-iPSC-NK cells incorporating NK-specific signaling domains exhibited significantly enhanced antitumor activity and prolonged survival in ovarian cancer models compared with conventional CAR constructs utilizing CD28-41BBζ signaling [30]. Similarly, hESC-derived CAR-NK cells targeting mesothelin effectively suppressed ovarian tumor progression and prolonged survival in xenograft models [45].

Despite these advantages, pluripotent stem cell-derived NK platforms remain associated with several translational concerns, including differentiation complexity, prolonged manufacturing timelines, potential genomic instability, and regulatory challenges related to stem-cell-derived products [43]. Long-term safety evaluation and optimization of differentiation protocols therefore remain important priorities for future clinical translation.

NK-92 cell platforms

The NK-92 cell line represents one of the most extensively investigated immortalized NK-cell platforms for CAR engineering [46, 47]. NK-92 cells exhibit high proliferative capacity, relative phenotypic homogeneity, ease of genetic manipulation, and favorable compatibility with viral and non-viral engineering systems [37, 48]. These characteristics make NK-92 cells highly attractive for standardized large-scale manufacturing and rapid generation of CAR-engineered products.

Multiple preclinical studies have demonstrated potent antitumor activity of CAR-NK-92 cells targeting HER2, GD2, EGFR, mesothelin, GPC3, and CD33 across hematological malignancies and solid tumors [47, 49–51]. Moreover, NK-92-based platforms generally demonstrate higher transduction efficiency compared with primary peripheral blood-derived NK cells [37].

However, a major limitation of NK-92 cells is their malignant origin, necessitating irradiation prior to clinical infusion to prevent uncontrolled in vivo proliferation [47]. Although irradiation substantially improves safety, it simultaneously restricts long-term persistence and proliferative capacity after administration. Consequently, NK-92-derived therapies may require repeated dosing schedules to maintain therapeutic efficacy [47]. In addition, NK-92 cells lack CD16 expression, thereby limiting native ADCC-mediated functionality unless genetically modified.

Ytokine-induced memory-like NK cells

Recent advances in NK-cell biology have identified CIML NK cells as highly promising candidates for next-generation CAR-NK engineering [52–54]. These cells are generated through short-term preactivation with cytokines such as IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18, leading to enhanced metabolic fitness, prolonged persistence, augmented IFN-γ production, and superior antitumor responsiveness upon subsequent stimulation [55].

Compared with conventional NK cells, memory-like NK cells exhibit improved proliferative potential and sustained cytotoxicity within immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments [53]. Emerging evidence further suggests that CIML-NK cells may significantly enhance the efficacy of CAR-engineered NK platforms by improving in vivo expansion, persistence, and resistance to exhaustion [52–54]. Their ability to maintain elevated ADCC additionally supports combination strategies with monoclonal antibodies and multispecific immunotherapeutic platforms.

Hematopoietic stem cell-derived and cryopreserved NK platforms

Hematopoietic stem cell-derived NK cells and cryopreserved NK-cell banking systems have emerged as important components of scalable allogeneic immunotherapy strategies [30, 56]. HSC-derived NK platforms provide opportunities for controlled differentiation, standardized expansion, and large-scale production of functionally consistent NK-cell populations suitable for clinical applications.

Cryopreservation technologies are particularly important for the development of universally accessible “off-the-shelf” CAR-NK products, enabling centralized manufacturing, quality control, transportation, and repeated dosing strategies [56]. Several iPSC-derived and cord blood-derived NK platforms have demonstrated favorable post-thaw viability and preserved cytotoxic functionality following cryostorage [30]. Nevertheless, cryopreservation-associated alterations in metabolic activity, receptor expression, migratory capacity, and cytotoxic function remain important technical challenges that require further optimization.

Collectively, each NK-cell source possesses distinct translational strengths and limitations that may significantly influence CAR engineering efficiency, manufacturing scalability, persistence, safety, and therapeutic performance. Accordingly, careful selection and optimization of NK-cell platforms remain fundamental for the successful clinical translation of next-generation CAR-NK therapies.

CAR architecture and engineering evolution

The therapeutic efficacy of CAR-NK cells is fundamentally influenced by the structural design of the CAR construct and the intracellular signaling pathways activated following antigen recognition [57, 58]. Advances in CAR engineering have progressively transformed NK-cell-based immunotherapy from early proof-of-concept platforms into highly sophisticated next-generation cellular therapeutics with enhanced specificity, persistence, safety, and antitumor functionality [59]. Although many CAR designs were initially adapted from CAR-T cell platforms, accumulating evidence indicates that optimal CAR signaling architecture for NK cells differs substantially from that of T lymphocytes due to the unique biological properties and activation mechanisms of NK cells [60, 61].

Structural components of chimeric antigen receptors

A typical CAR construct consists of four principal structural domains: an extracellular antigen-recognition domain, a hinge or spacer region, a transmembrane domain, and one or more intracellular signaling domains [61, 62]. The extracellular antigen-binding region is most commonly composed of a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) derived from monoclonal antibodies, enabling specific recognition of tumor-associated surface antigens independent of MHC-mediated antigen presentation [63, 64]. This characteristic significantly broadens the spectrum of targetable malignancies and reduces susceptibility to tumor-mediated MHC downregulation.

The hinge or spacer domain provides structural flexibility and influences the accessibility of the CAR molecule to target antigens located at varying distances from the tumor-cell membrane [57, 65]. Spacer composition may substantially affect immune synapse formation, antigen engagement, and signaling efficiency. Commonly utilized spacer regions include fragments derived from CD8α, CD28, and immunoglobulin G (IgG)-based domains [65, 66].

The transmembrane domain anchors the CAR molecule within the cellular membrane and contributes to receptor stability and signaling modulation [67]. Frequently used transmembrane regions include CD3ζ, CD28, CD8α, and NKG2D-derived domains. Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that NK-cell-specific transmembrane domains may improve receptor stability and downstream activation in CAR-NK platforms compared with conventional T-cell-derived constructs [43].

The intracellular signaling region represents the functional core of the CAR construct and plays a critical role in determining activation strength, persistence, cytokine secretion, metabolic fitness, and antitumor efficacy [57]. Early CAR constructs primarily utilized CD3ζ signaling domains alone; however, subsequent generations incorporated additional co-stimulatory domains to enhance proliferation and sustained cytotoxic activity [68]. Evolution of CAR generations

CAR technology has evolved considerably over the past three decades, resulting in the development of increasingly sophisticated receptor platforms with improved therapeutic functionality [68, 72]. First-generation CARs consisted of an scFv linked solely to the CD3ζ intracellular signaling domain [68]. Although these constructs successfully mediated antigen-specific cytotoxicity, they demonstrated limited proliferation, poor persistence, and insufficient in vivo antitumor activity due to the absence of co-stimulatory signaling [72].

To overcome these limitations, second-generation CARs incorporated a single co-stimulatory domain, most commonly CD28 or 4-1BB, in combination with CD3ζ signaling [73]. These constructs significantly improved cellular expansion, cytokine secretion, persistence, and antitumor efficacy, thereby contributing to the clinical success of CAR-T therapies and subsequent adaptation to CAR-NK platforms [74, 75].

Third-generation CARs further integrated two co-stimulatory domains, such as CD28 and 4-1BB or CD28 and OX40, to augment signaling intensity and functional persistence [76]. Several preclinical investigations have demonstrated enhanced cytotoxicity and cytokine production using third-generation CAR-NK constructs targeting HER2, GD2, and mesothelin-expressing tumors [51, 77, 78].

Fourth-generation CARs, often referred to as “armored CARs” or TRUCKs (T cells redirected for universal cytokine-mediated killing), were subsequently engineered to express immunomodulatory cytokines, chemokines, or immune-enhancing molecules following antigen engagement [79]. In CAR-NK systems, cytokine-armored constructs incorporating IL-15 have attracted particular interest due to their capacity to enhance proliferation, persistence, survival, and resistance to functional exhaustion [41, 42, 80].

More recently, next-generation CAR platforms incorporating inducible activation systems, suicide switches, logic-gated circuits, dual-antigen targeting strategies, and multiplex genome editing technologies have emerged as promising approaches to improve tumor specificity and reduce on-target/off-tumor toxicity [69–71].

NK-specific signaling domains and functional optimization

Although many CAR constructs were initially designed for T-cell activation, NK cells possess distinct intracellular signaling pathways that may require alternative co-stimulatory strategies for optimal therapeutic activity [43, 81]. Accordingly, increasing efforts have focused on integrating NK-specific signaling domains into CAR-NK platforms to improve activation efficiency and persistence.

Among these, DAP10 and DAP12 are particularly important adaptor proteins involved in NK-cell activation downstream of receptors such as NKG2D and activating killer immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs) [82]. Incorporation of DAP10- or DAP12-associated signaling domains into CAR constructs has been shown to enhance NK-cell cytotoxicity, cytokine secretion, and metabolic fitness [83].

Similarly, the co-stimulatory receptor 2B4 (CD244) has demonstrated important synergistic effects in CAR-NK engineering by improving activation, proliferation, and persistence while maintaining favorable safety profiles [84]. Li et al.. reported that mesothelin-targeted CAR-NK cells incorporating NKG2D-2B4 signaling domains exhibited superior antitumor activity compared with conventional CD28-41BBζ CAR constructs [30].

Additional NK-associated signaling molecules, including DNAM-1, OX40, and NKG2D-derived intracellular domains, have also demonstrated potential to improve immune synapse formation, enhance resistance to tumor-induced suppression, and sustain cytotoxic functionality within hostile tumor microenvironments [43, 81].

cytokine-armored CAR-NK platforms

Limited in vivo persistence remains one of the major barriers restricting the clinical efficacy of CAR-NK therapy [85, 86]. Consequently, substantial efforts have focused on engineering cytokine-supported CAR-NK platforms capable of sustaining proliferation and survival following infusion. Among cytokines investigated in CAR-NK engineering, IL-15 has emerged as the most extensively studied and clinically promising candidate [80, 85, 87]. IL-15 plays critical roles in NK-cell homeostasis, activation, proliferation, mitochondrial fitness, and long-term persistence [42]. CAR-NK constructs expressing membrane-bound IL-15 or IL-15 superagonist systems have demonstrated significantly prolonged in vivo persistence and enhanced antitumor efficacy in both hematological malignancies and solid tumors [80, 87].

Additional cytokines including IL-12, IL-18, IL-21, and IL-27 have also been explored as components of cytokine-armored CAR-NK systems to improve resistance against immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments and enhance inflammatory cytokine production [52–54]. However, excessive cytokine activation may increase risks of systemic toxicity, functional exhaustion, and activation-induced cell death, necessitating careful optimization of cytokine expression systems and inducible regulatory platforms.

Smart and next-generation CAR-NK systems

Recent advances in synthetic biology and genome engineering have accelerated the development of highly sophisticated CAR-NK platforms capable of adaptive and programmable antitumor responses [88, 89]. Dual-targeting and tandem CAR systems have been designed to simultaneously recognize multiple tumor-associated antigens, thereby reducing the likelihood of antigen escape and improving tumor specificity [90–93].

Adaptor CAR systems represent another innovative strategy in which CAR-NK activation is mediated through intermediary bispecific adaptor molecules that bridge the CAR receptor and tumor-associated antigens [94]. This modular platform provides greater therapeutic flexibility and may reduce on-target/off-tumor toxicity by enabling controlled and reversible CAR activation.

Logic-gated CAR systems utilizing “AND”, “OR”, and “NOT” signaling circuits have additionally emerged as promising approaches for improving discrimination between malignant and healthy tissues [89]. Furthermore, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated multiplex genome editing has enabled simultaneous insertion of CAR constructs together with deletion of inhibitory receptors, checkpoint molecules, or exhaustion-associated pathways to optimize NK-cell functionality [43, 44].

Collectively, continuous advances in CAR architecture, NK-specific signaling optimization, cytokine support systems, and programmable synthetic biology platforms are rapidly reshaping the landscape of CAR-NK immunotherapy. Rational engineering of these next-generation constructs will likely play a central role in overcoming current translational barriers and improving the clinical efficacy of CAR-NK therapies across hematological malignancies and solid tumors.

Genetic engineering and manufacturing strategies for CAR-NK cells

Efficient genetic modification and scalable manufacturing remain among the most critical determinants influencing the clinical translation and therapeutic success of CAR-NK cell therapy [48, 95]. Unlike T lymphocytes, NK cells exhibit intrinsic resistance to exogenous gene delivery, relatively short in vitro lifespan, and increased susceptibility to activation-induced dysfunction during prolonged expansion processes [39, 95]. Consequently, optimization of gene-transfer platforms and manufacturing workflows has become essential for generating clinically effective CAR-NK products with adequate purity, persistence, functionality, and scalability.

A wide range of viral and non-viral engineering approaches have been investigated to enhance CAR delivery efficiency and improve the production of standardized NK-cell-based therapeutics [37, 39, 48]. The choice of engineering platform substantially influences transgene stability, manufacturing complexity, genomic safety, expansion kinetics, and clinical feasibility.

Viral gene delivery systems

Viral transduction remains one of the most widely utilized approaches for stable CAR integration and long-term transgene expression in NK cells [95]. Among viral systems, retroviral and lentiviral vectors are the most extensively investigated platforms in both preclinical and clinical CAR-NK studies.

Retroviral vectors

Retroviral vectors were among the earliest systems used for CAR engineering and have demonstrated stable transgene integration and sustained CAR expression in activated immune cells [48]. These vectors provide relatively high gene-transfer efficiency in proliferating cells and have been successfully employed in several CAR-NK platforms targeting hematological malignancies and solid tumors [37].

However, retroviral systems possess important limitations, including insertional mutagenesis risk, dependence on active cellular proliferation for efficient integration, and restricted transgene packaging capacity [48]. In addition, retroviral transduction efficiency in primary NK cells frequently remains lower than that observed in activated T cells, thereby complicating large-scale clinical manufacturing [39].

Lentiviral vectors

Lentiviral vectors have emerged as one of the preferred platforms for CAR-NK engineering due to their ability to transduce both dividing and non-dividing cells while providing relatively stable long-term transgene expression [39, 48]. Lentiviral systems are associated with improved biosafety profiles compared with conventional γ-retroviral vectors and permit delivery of more complex genetic constructs, including cytokine-armored CAR systems and multiplex engineering platforms [43]. Several studies have demonstrated successful lentiviral generation of CAR-NK cells targeting CD19, HER2, mesothelin, and GPC3 antigens [77, 80, 96]. Nevertheless, primary NK cells remain intrinsically resistant to lentiviral transduction because of antiviral defense mechanisms, limited receptor availability, and innate immune sensing pathways [39]. Various optimization strategies, including pseudotyping approaches, cytokine preactivation, spinoculation techniques, and the use of transduction enhancers, have therefore been explored to improve lentiviral gene-transfer efficiency in NK cells [38, 39].

Adenoviral and adeno-associated viral (AAV) systems

Adenoviral and adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors have also been investigated as alternative gene-delivery platforms for NK-cell engineering [97]. These systems generally exhibit favorable safety characteristics and reduced risks of insertional mutagenesis because of their predominantly episomal transgene persistence. However, transient expression profiles, limited packaging capacity, and relatively lower transduction efficiency in NK cells have restricted their broader clinical implementation [97].

Non-viral engineering approaches

To overcome the limitations associated with viral systems, increasing attention has focused on non-viral gene-transfer technologies capable of improving biosafety, reducing manufacturing costs, and facilitating scalable CAR-NK production [98].

Electroporation and mRNA-based engineering

Electroporation-mediated mRNA delivery has emerged as a highly attractive approach for transient CAR expression in NK cells because it avoids genomic integration and substantially reduces the risk of insertional mutagenesis [44, 98]. mRNA-engineered CAR-NK cells can be generated rapidly and exhibit favorable safety profiles, particularly in early-phase exploratory studies.

Several investigations have demonstrated efficient transient expression of CAR constructs following mRNA electroporation, leading to enhanced cytotoxicity against hematological malignancies and solid tumors [98]. Moreover, mRNA-based engineering permits repeated modification and flexible optimization of CAR designs without permanent genomic alteration. However, transient CAR expression may necessitate repeated cell administrations to maintain therapeutic efficacy, thereby potentially limiting long-term persistence and sustained tumor control [44].

Transposon-based systems

Transposon technologies, particularly Sleeping Beauty and PiggyBac systems, have gained substantial interest as cost-effective non-viral alternatives for stable CAR integration [59]. These platforms enable relatively large transgene insertion capacity while avoiding many biosafety concerns associated with viral vectors.

PiggyBac-based engineering systems have demonstrated promising CAR expression and expansion efficiency in NK-cell platforms [59]. Nevertheless, concerns regarding genomic instability, variable integration profiles, and lower engineering consistency compared with optimized viral systems continue to limit broader clinical adoption.

CRISPR/Cas9 and genome editing technologies

Recent advances in genome editing have significantly expanded the therapeutic potential of CAR-NK engineering [43, 44]. CRISPR/Cas9 technology enables precise insertion of CAR constructs together with targeted disruption of inhibitory receptors, checkpoint molecules, and exhaustion-associated pathways that may limit NK-cell functionality within tumor microenvironments.

Several studies have explored CRISPR-mediated deletion of genes including cytokine-inducible SH2-containing protein (CISH), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), TIGIT, and NKG2A to enhance NK-cell persistence, metabolic fitness, and antitumor activity [43]. Furthermore, multiplex genome editing approaches allow simultaneous incorporation of cytokine-support systems, suicide switches, chemokine receptors, and enhanced signaling domains into CAR-NK platforms.

Genome editing additionally facilitates safe-harbor transgene insertion strategies designed to improve expression stability and minimize insertional mutagenesis risk [44]. Despite these promising advances, concerns regarding off-target genomic effects, editing-associated cellular stress, manufacturing complexity, and regulatory oversight remain important translational challenges requiring further optimization.

Expansion and manufacturing strategies

Large-scale expansion and standardized manufacturing of CAR-NK cells remain major prerequisites for successful clinical translation [30, 56]. Unlike CAR-T products, NK-cell manufacturing must overcome several additional challenges, including lower proliferative capacity, shorter lifespan, and reduced transduction efficiency [38].

Ex vivo expansion protocols commonly utilize cytokines such as IL-2, IL-15, IL-21, and feeder-cell-based systems to promote NK-cell proliferation and functional activation [42, 55]. K562-based feeder platforms expressing membrane-bound cytokines and co-stimulatory molecules have demonstrated substantial improvements in NK-cell expansion efficiency [38]. However, feeder-dependent manufacturing introduces additional regulatory complexity and potential variability between production batches.

The emergence of iPSC-derived NK platforms and cryopreserved allogeneic NK-cell banking systems has significantly improved opportunities for scalable “off-the-shelf” manufacturing [30, 43]. These approaches enable generation of standardized and functionally homogeneous CAR-NK products with improved logistical feasibility and broader clinical accessibility.

Nevertheless, multiple manufacturing challenges continue to limit large-scale commercialization of CAR-NK therapy, including donor variability, cryopreservation-associated functional impairment, limited post-thaw persistence, high production costs, and difficulties maintaining consistent potency across manufacturing batches [30, 56]. In addition, regulatory requirements related to genetic modification, product sterility, genomic safety, and quality control remain important considerations for future industrial translation.

Toward standardized and next-generation manufacturing platforms

Ongoing advances in automated bioreactor systems, closed-system manufacturing technologies, artificial intelligence-guided production optimization, and multiplex genome engineering are expected to substantially improve the scalability and reproducibility of CAR-NK cell manufacturing [43]. Emerging universal donor platforms and standardized cryopreserved NK-cell products may further accelerate the transition of CAR-NK therapy from individualized experimental approaches toward broadly accessible clinical immunotherapy.

Collectively, continuous improvements in genetic engineering technologies and manufacturing strategies are rapidly advancing the feasibility of CAR-NK therapy as a scalable next-generation cellular immunotherapeutic platform. Optimization of these processes will remain essential for improving persistence, safety, affordability, and widespread clinical implementation of CAR-NK-based treatments. Genetic engineering strategies in CAR-NK versus CAR-T cell platforms

Comparative landscape of CAR-NK and CAR-T cell therapies in cancer immunotherapy

Research aimed at expanding the therapeutic applications of CAR-T cells across various malignancies, including solid tumors, has progressed substantially in recent years [101–103], particularly following the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of CD19-targeted CAR-T cells as the first clinically approved CAR-based immunotherapy [104]. Although CAR-T cell therapy has demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy in several hematological malignancies [101], important biological and translational limitations continue to restrict its broader therapeutic applicability, especially in solid tumors [102].

One of the major concerns associated with CAR-T-cell therapy is the occurrence of on-target/off-tumor toxicity resulting from recognition of target antigens expressed not only on malignant cells but also on healthy tissues [105]. In addition, severe treatment-related adverse effects have been reported, including neurological toxicities such as acute cerebral edema, which in certain cases may rapidly progress to fatal neurological complications following CD19-CAR-T-cell administration [106]. Cytokine release syndrome also represents one of the most significant toxicities associated with CAR-T-cell therapy [105]. Evidence suggests that CRS may become life-threatening because of the rapid and excessive systemic release of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-6, following CAR-T-cell activation and expansion [103].

Additional complications associated with CAR-T-cell therapy include GvHD in allogeneic settings [107], tumor lysis syndrome, and diverse neurological manifestations [108, 109]. To reduce the risk of GvHD, several strategies aimed at inhibiting endogenous T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling are currently under investigation, including transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALEN), CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing, zinc finger nucleases, endonuclease-based approaches, and receptor blockade strategies employing various inhibitory proteins [110, 111].

In contrast to CAR-T cells, CAR-engineered natural killer cells have emerged as promising next-generation immunotherapeutic platforms with potentially improved safety profiles and broader allogeneic applicability [15–17, 112]. A pioneering clinical trial evaluating CD33-targeted CAR-NK-92 cells in patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) demonstrated that administration of nearly five billion irradiated CAR-NK-92 cells per patient was not associated with significant treatment-related adverse effects, despite limited therapeutic efficacy [113]. Similarly, other early clinical investigations involving “off-the-shelf” CAR-NK platforms have produced encouraging results in patients with relapsed or refractory CD19-positive leukemia and lymphoma [113]. Importantly, infused NK cells were shown to persist in vivo for relatively prolonged periods, in some cases for at least 12 months following administration [33].

Several biological characteristics distinguish NK cells from T cells and contribute to the growing interest in CAR-NK-based therapies. Unlike CAR-T cells, allogeneic NK cells do not require complete human leukocyte antigen (HLA) matching prior to administration [110]. Moreover, NK cells generally do not induce GvHD and, under certain conditions, may even suppress activated alloreactive T cells while preserving graft-versus-tumor activity [111]. Nevertheless, some investigations have suggested that activated NK cells may contribute to GvHD development depending on their activation status and cytokine exposure. For example, NK cells stimulated using IL-15 and 4-1BB ligand may exhibit functional characteristics distinct from resting NK cells or IL-2-stimulated NK cells, potentially leading to variable clinical outcomes [114]. Conversely, adoptive transfer of adaptive NK cells in combination with IL-2 has been proposed to exert protective effects against GvHD through expansion of regulatory immune-cell populations [115].

Another major advantage of CAR-NK-cell therapy involves its comparatively favorable cytokine profile. Cytokine levels following NK-cell infusion are generally more moderate than those observed during severe CAR-T-associated CRS [116]. Consequently, CAR-NK-cell therapy may help overcome some of the major toxicities associated with CAR-T treatment, particularly CRS. Since infused CAR-NK cells do not typically undergo prolonged uncontrolled expansion over several weeks, the probability of severe CRS appears substantially reduced [117]. In addition, cytokine-producing NK cells predominantly release GM-CSF and IFN-γ, whereas activated CAR-T cells produce higher levels of TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6, which are strongly implicated in severe inflammatory toxicities [117].

Importantly, the prolonged persistence of CAR-T cells may also increase the theoretical risk of malignant transformation or long-term autoimmune complications, whereas NK cells generally decline after exerting their antitumor effects [117]. However, although reduced persistence may improve safety, it may simultaneously limit long-term therapeutic durability and sustained tumor control in certain clinical settings.

In the context of solid tumors, both CAR-T and CAR-NK therapies face substantial barriers, including poor tumor trafficking, antigen heterogeneity, limited infiltration, and highly immunosuppressive TME. Nevertheless, several intrinsic biological properties of NK cells, including MHC-independent cytotoxicity, innate tumor recognition, and reduced alloreactivity, may provide important translational advantages in solid tumor immunotherapy. Furthermore, CAR-NK platforms demonstrate greater compatibility with scalable “off-the-shelf” manufacturing approaches and multiplex genome engineering strategies, thereby supporting the development of next-generation universal cellular immunotherapies.

Collectively, although CAR-T-cell therapy currently remains more clinically advanced, CAR-NK-cell therapy has emerged as a highly promising complementary platform with distinct safety, manufacturing, and translational advantages. Ongoing advances in cytokine-armored CAR systems, genome editing technologies, chemokine receptor engineering, and combinatorial therapeutic strategies are expected to further enhance the clinical efficacy and broader applicability of CAR-NK-based immunotherapy. Recent evidence suggests that NK-cell-based immunotherapy may be particularly advantageous in tumors characterized by profound immunosuppressive microenvironments and impaired endogenous NK-cell activity, including oral squamous cell carcinoma and several other solid malignancies [124]. To address current limitations, various engineering strategies have been developed, including chemokine receptor modification to improve tumor homing [118–120], cytokine-armored CAR constructs, and adaptor CAR systems activated through bispecific molecules that bridge CAR-NK cells with tumor-associated antigens [94]. These approaches may improve tumor-specific cytotoxicity while reducing immune evasion and minimizing on-target/off-tumor toxicity [121, 122].

Car-NK cell therapy in hematological malignancies

Among hematological malignancies, CD19 and CD20 remain the most extensively investigated targets for CAR-NK-cell therapy, particularly in B-cell lymphomas and leukemias [106]. Preclinical and early clinical studies have demonstrated that CD19-CAR-NK cells exhibit considerable antitumor activity and may offer several advantages over conventional CAR-T-cell therapies, including improved safety profiles and greater feasibility for allogeneic “off-the-shelf” applications [113]. Encouraging outcomes observed with CD19-CAR-T-cell therapy in hematological cancers have further supported the development of CD19-targeted CAR-NK platforms as promising therapeutic alternatives for lymphoid malignancies [34, 113].

Additional targets have also emerged in hematological cancers. In multiple myeloma (MM), CAR-NK cells have been developed against CS1 and CD138 antigens [106]. CS1, a glycoprotein highly expressed on myeloma cells, has attracted significant attention because of its selective overexpression in malignant plasma cells [125]. Importantly, CS1-CAR-NK cells have demonstrated the ability to effectively eliminate MM cells both in vitro and in vivo [113]. Moreover, cancer stem cell-like populations associated with MM exhibit markedly elevated CS1 expression, rendering them particularly susceptible to CS1-CAR-NK-mediated cytotoxicity [113]. These findings suggest that CS1-targeted CAR-NK therapy may contribute not only to tumor elimination but also to prevention of disease recurrence through eradication of myeloma stem-like cells.

In ALL, CD7 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target because of its involvement in lymphoid proliferation and immune-cell interactions [28]. Conversely, CD33 represents an important target in AML because of its regulatory role in several cellular processes and its expression in leukemic blasts [126]. Clinical investigations evaluating CAR-NK-cell therapies targeting CD33 (NCT02944162) and CD7 (NCT02742727) are currently ongoing; however, the number of active CAR-NK clinical studies in hematological malignancies remains relatively limited [127].

Overall, available evidence provides encouraging preliminary support regarding the safety and therapeutic potential of CAR-NK-cell therapy in hematological cancers. Nevertheless, most clinical studies remain in early-phase development (phase I or II), indicating that CAR-NK immunotherapy is still in the relatively early stages of clinical translation. Several important challenges continue to limit broader clinical implementation, including the need to improve targeted antitumor efficacy [128–130], prolong CAR-NK-cell persistence and survival [86, 131], and overcome the immunosuppressive effects of the tumor microenvironment [132, 133].

CAR-NK cell therapy in solid tumors

Translation of CAR-NK-cell therapy into solid tumors has gained increasing attention in recent years because of the intrinsic biological properties of NK cells, including MHC-independent cytotoxicity, reduced alloreactivity, and favorable safety characteristics. Various tumor-associated antigens have been explored in preclinical solid tumor models, including mesothelin (MSLN), HER2, GD2, GPC3, EGFR, and others.

Mesothelin-targeted CAR-NK-cell therapy has shown promising activity across multiple solid malignancies. In gastric cancer models, MSLN-targeted NK-92-derived CAR-NK cells significantly enhanced cytokine secretion, including IFN-γ, GM-CSF, granzyme B, and perforin, resulting in effective tumor-cell elimination, improved NK-cell infiltration, and prolonged survival in xenograft models [134]. Similarly, in colorectal cancer, MSLN-targeted CAR-NK cells demonstrated selective cytotoxicity against MSLN-positive tumor cells together with improved homing capability, enhanced tumor penetration, and significant inhibition of tumor growth [135].

MSLN-directed CAR-NK approaches have also demonstrated encouraging findings in AML and cervical cancer models. Peripheral blood-derived CIML NK cells engineered with MSLN-targeted CAR constructs exhibited enhanced cytotoxicity and significantly improved tumor control in AML xenograft models [136]. In cervical cancer, third-generation MSLN-CAR-NK-92 cells displayed substantial degranulation and high cytotoxic activity against tumor cells, supporting their potential therapeutic relevance in mesothelin-positive malignancies [137].

In ovarian cancer, human embryonic stem cell-derived MSLN-CAR induced NK (iNK) cells demonstrated potent antitumor activity through enhanced IFN-γ and TNF-α secretion and elevated CD107a expression, resulting in suppression of tumor progression and prolonged survival in ovarian tumor-bearing animal models [96]. These findings provide important insights regarding the potential future clinical translation of hESC-derived CAR-iNK-cell platforms for ovarian cancer treatment.

In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), GPC3-targeted CAR-NK cells demonstrated substantial cytotoxicity against GPC3-positive HepG2 cells and significantly inhibited tumor growth in vivo [138]. Importantly, variations in GPC3 isoform expression may influence therapeutic responses, suggesting that detailed characterization of antigen isoforms could further improve CAR-NK-cell efficacy and patient outcomes.

Collectively, current evidence supports the growing therapeutic potential of CAR-NK-cell therapy in both hematological malignancies and solid tumors. Preclinical investigations and emerging early-phase clinical studies have demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity together with favorable safety characteristics across multiple cancer types. Nevertheless, several biological challenges—including limited persistence, insufficient tumor infiltration, antigen heterogeneity, and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments—continue to restrict broader therapeutic efficacy and require further optimization before widespread clinical implementation can be achieved.

Clinical translation and safety considerations of CAR-NK cell therapy

The rapid evolution of genetic engineering technologies has substantially expanded the therapeutic potential of CAR-NK-cell therapy. Nevertheless, the increasing use of genome-editing platforms and complex cellular engineering strategies has simultaneously raised important safety considerations regarding unintended off-target genetic alterations and their potential biological consequences [156]. Consequently, safety optimization has become a central component in the clinical translation of CAR-NK-cell products, particularly in the context of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing.

Recent technological advances have significantly improved the precision and safety profile of gene-editing approaches used in cellular immunotherapy. In particular, transient delivery of highly accurate Cas9 ribonucleoprotein complexes through electroporation has markedly reduced the risk of unintended genomic modifications [157–160]. In parallel, the development of highly sensitive next-generation sequencing technologies capable of detecting rare off-target events has provided an additional layer of safety monitoring for genetically engineered lymphocytes intended for clinical application. Collectively, these advances have strengthened the translational feasibility of CAR-NK-cell therapy and supported the development of increasingly sophisticated engineered NK-cell platforms.

Current clinical experience and ongoing clinical trials of CAR-NK cells

The first clinical application of CAR-modified NK cells was reported in 2018 [161]. In a phase I clinical trial (NCT02944162), investigators administered up to 5 × 10^9 CD33-targeted CAR-NK-92 cells to patients with relapsed or refractory AML [162]. This third-generation CAR construct incorporated CD28 and 4-1BB co-stimulatory domains and demonstrated a favorable safety profile without major treatment-related toxicities [163]. The unfavorable outcomes of the study were primarily linked to the short longevity of the irradiated NK-92 cells in vivo, as the levels of CAR-NK diminished to undetectable levels within a week after infusion [80, 164].

A phase 1/2 clinical trial (NCT02944162) evaluated irradiated CD33-CAR-NK-92 cells in three patients with relapsed or refractory AML, with doses approaching 5 × 10⁹ cells per infusion. Although clinical efficacy was limited, no dose-limiting toxicities or severe treatment-related adverse events were reported, suggesting a favorable short-term safety profile for high-dose NK-92 cell administration [164]. Participants exhibited no notable adverse responses following administration of up to five billion NK-92 cells, suggesting that high-dose NK-92 infusion was well tolerated [164].

At present, ClinicalTrials.gov lists multiple ongoing clinical trials investigating CAR-NK-cell therapies across both hematological malignancies and solid tumors [165]. Among these studies, a considerable proportion focuses on hematologic cancers, while an increasing number are evaluating CAR-NK-cell strategies in solid tumors. Frequently investigated targets include CD19, ROBO1, and CD22 [165]. Most currently registered studies are early-phase clinical trials, including phase I, early phase I, and phase I/II investigations, reflecting the relatively early stage of clinical development of CAR-NK-cell therapy.

Geographically, the majority of CAR-NK clinical trials have been conducted in China, followed by the United States, Germany, and Singapore, underscoring the expanding global interest in NK-cell-based immunotherapeutic platforms [165]. Importantly, several ongoing studies have demonstrated encouraging preliminary findings regarding feasibility, safety, and biological activity.

One particularly promising strategy involves administration of CAR-NK cells in combination with lymphodepleting chemotherapy and cytokine-supportive engineering approaches. Recent clinical observations have reported favorable therapeutic responses in a substantial proportion of treated patients, while CAR-NK cells incorporating IL-15 within the CAR construct demonstrated prolonged in vivo persistence exceeding 12 months in certain cases [166]. These findings suggest that cytokine-armored CAR-NK constructs may partially overcome one of the major limitations traditionally associated with NK-cell therapy, namely insufficient persistence.

In addition, an ongoing clinical study in Germany is currently evaluating HER2-targeted CAR-NK-92 cells in patients with glioblastoma (NCT03383978), further expanding the translational exploration of CAR-NK-cell therapy into solid malignancies [167]. Emerging clinical platforms and future directions for CAR-NK trials

Recent years have witnessed a substantial expansion in early-stage clinical trials investigating diverse CAR-NK-cell platforms [168]. These studies are evaluating a broad spectrum of target antigens, NK-cell sources, and CAR-engineering strategies aimed at improving antitumor efficacy, persistence, and scalability.

Importantly, a major shift has occurred in the translational focus of CAR-NK-cell therapy. Importantly, the clinical development landscape of CAR-NK-cell therapy is rapidly evolving from early proof-of-concept studies toward increasingly sophisticated and disease-adapted therapeutic platforms. Although initial clinical investigations predominantly focused on hematological malignancies, recent translational efforts have progressively expanded toward solid tumors, reflecting growing confidence in the safety and engineering flexibility of CAR-NK-cell systems [168]. Simultaneously, next-generation strategies involving multiplex genome editing, cytokine-armored constructs, and enhanced activation signaling modules are being incorporated to improve persistence, functional durability, and therapeutic scalability.

One representative example is NKX101, a donor-derived CAR-NK product engineered to express a modified NKG2D activating receptor coupled with OX40, CD3ζ, and IL-15 signaling domains [169, 170]. Unlike conventional CAR platforms that target a single tumor-associated antigen, NKX101 is designed to exploit the broad stress-ligand recognition capacity of NKG2D, a key activating receptor involved in NK-cell-mediated immune surveillance, which may enable recognition of a wider spectrum of malignant cells [2, 171]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated encouraging antileukemic activity, and NKX101 is currently undergoing phase I clinical evaluation in patients with relapsed or refractory AML and high-risk myelodysplastic syndrome (NCT04623944) [166].

Another innovative platform under clinical investigation involves a multifunctional CAR-NK construct expressing a non-cleavable form of CD16a together with a CD19-targeted CAR, NKG2D, CD3ζ, 2B4, and IL15 receptor fusion domains. This candidate is currently being evaluated in patients with advanced B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma and chronic lymphocytic leukemia in combination with anti-CD20 therapy (NCT04245722) [165]. Although clinical evidence remains limited, preclinical studies have shown that multiplex-engineered CAR-NK platforms incorporating additional stimulatory and persistence-enhancing elements may achieve promising antitumor activity and could potentially overcome some limitations associated with conventional CAR-T-cell therapies [80, 169].

FT538 represents another important next-generation CAR-NK platform currently under clinical evaluation for relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (NCT04614636) [165]. This engineered product incorporates biallelic CD38 knockout to prevent fratricide associated with anti-CD38-based therapies such as daratumumab. Consequently, CD38-deficient CAR-NK cells may offer unique therapeutic advantages, particularly in patients who have not previously received anti-CD38-directed treatment [172].

Collectively, the expanding diversity of CAR-NK-cell clinical platforms reflects the rapid evolution of the field toward increasingly sophisticated and customizable cellular immunotherapies. Ongoing and future clinical trials are expected to provide critical insights into the optimal design, safety, persistence, and therapeutic efficacy of CAR-NK-cell therapy across a broad range of malignancies. Limitations of current clinical evidence and remaining translational challenges

Although early clinical investigations of CAR-NK-cell therapy have demonstrated encouraging safety profiles and promising preliminary efficacy, several important limitations continue to restrict definitive interpretation of current findings [40, 173].

Most available studies involve relatively small patient cohorts, frequently including fewer than 30–50 participants, thereby limiting statistical power and generalizability of the observed outcomes [40, 174, 175]. Furthermore, follow-up durations remain relatively short in most trials, preventing comprehensive evaluation of long-term persistence, durability of clinical responses, and delayed toxicities associated with engineered NK-cell therapies [59, 173].

An additional challenge arises from the substantial heterogeneity among clinical studies. Variability in NK-cell sources—including peripheral blood, umbilical cord blood, induced pluripotent stem cells, and NK-92-derived platforms—as well as differences in CAR design, lymphodepletion regimens, cytokine support strategies, and manufacturing protocols complicate direct comparison across trials [176–178]. Moreover, many investigations enroll heavily pretreated patients with advanced disease, potentially confounding interpretation of therapeutic efficacy [174, 175].

Importantly, long-term safety concerns remain incompletely understood, particularly regarding genomic stability, potential off-target effects associated with gene-editing technologies, and the possibility of delayed immune-mediated toxicities [179–181]. In addition, several manufacturing-related challenges—including large-scale expansion, product consistency, cost-effectiveness, and standardization of production workflows—continue to limit widespread clinical implementation of CAR-NK-cell therapies [177, 178, 182–184].

Taken together, these limitations emphasize the necessity for larger multicenter randomized clinical trials with prolonged follow-up periods to more accurately define the therapeutic value, long-term safety, and real-world applicability of CAR-NK-cell therapy [176]. Furthermore, harmonization of clinical trial design, manufacturing standards, and outcome reporting will be essential for enabling meaningful cross-study comparisons and accelerating the clinical translation of next-generation CAR-NK-cell platforms.

Challenges and limitations of CAR-NK cell therapy

Despite the considerable therapeutic promise of CAR-NK cell therapy, multiple biological, technical, and translational barriers continue to limit its widespread clinical implementation. Although CAR-NK cells demonstrate important advantages over CAR-T-cell therapy, including lower risks of CRS, neurotoxicity, and GvHD, several unresolved challenges continue to affect their persistence, trafficking, manufacturing feasibility, and efficacy in solid tumors [185, 186].

One of the principal limitations of CAR-NK-cell therapy is the relatively transient lifespan of NK cells following adoptive transfer. While this limited persistence contributes to the improved safety profile of CAR-NK therapy, it may simultaneously compromise sustained antitumor responses and long-term therapeutic durability [185]. In addition, uncertainty regarding the capacity of NK cells to efficiently migrate toward and infiltrate tumor tissues has historically represented a major obstacle limiting the broader development of CAR-engineered NK-cell therapies, particularly in solid tumors [187].

Expansion, activation, and functional enhancement of NK cells

One of the earliest and most important technical challenges in CAR-NK-cell therapy involves achieving sufficient ex vivo expansion and activation of NK cells for therapeutic administration. In many cases, NK cells isolated from a single donor are insufficient to achieve clinically relevant cell numbers, thereby necessitating specialized and prolonged in vitro expansion protocols [188, 189]. Conventional expansion approaches commonly require 2–3 weeks of culture in the presence of cytokines such as IL-2, IL-15, and anti-CD3 antibodies [190]. Importantly, combined stimulation with IL-2 and IL-21 has demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy compared with IL-2 alone because of its enhanced capacity to promote NK-cell proliferation and activation [191].

Irradiated K562-mbIL15-41BBL feeder cells have shown considerable utility in supporting large-scale expansion of primary NK cells under clinical conditions [192]. Nevertheless, achieving a stable and reproducible source of donor-derived NK cells remains challenging. Furthermore, complete depletion of contaminating T cells prior to NK-cell expansion is essential to minimize the risk of adverse complications such as GvHD [192]. Since K562 feeder cells are themselves malignant in origin, their complete elimination prior to patient administration is also critically important [192].

Several bioprocessing platforms have been investigated for NK-cell cultivation, including flasks, Teflon bags, continuous-flow devices, stirred-tank bioreactors, and automated systems such as the Miltenyi Prodigy platform [193]. Among these approaches, hybrid bioreactor systems capable of supporting continuous culture conditions may offer particular advantages for large-scale NK-cell expansion because they facilitate cost-effective manufacturing while enabling substantial cellular proliferation using lower volumes of culture media [194].

Following expansion, NK cells generally require cryopreservation prior to clinical administration [195]. However, limitations associated with large-scale cellular manufacturing continue to complicate implementation of dose-escalation strategies in clinical settings [196].

Cryopreservation, storage, and transportation challenges

The preservation and transportation of NK-cell products remain major technical obstacles in CAR-NK-cell therapy. Compared with T cells and many other immune-cell populations, NK cells exhibit markedly greater sensitivity to cryopreservation and thawing procedures, particularly following cytokine-mediated activation [197]. Post-thaw viability varies substantially, and cryopreservation frequently results in diminished cytotoxic functionality [198].

Activated NK cells also demonstrate pronounced sensitivity to reduced temperatures, making maintenance of near-physiological conditions critically important during storage and transportation processes [198]. Consequently, preserving NK-cell functionality throughout shipping procedures remains particularly challenging for CAR-NK-cell products [199]. In addition, elevated cellular densities during transportation may accelerate nutrient depletion and alter pH and glucose concentrations, thereby impairing cellular performance and viability [200]. Optimization of transport conditions and cell density therefore remains essential for maintaining the quality and therapeutic efficacy of CAR-NK-cell products.

CAR Gene delivery and transduction strategies in NK cells

Efficient introduction of CAR constructs into NK cells represents another major challenge in the development of CAR-NK-cell therapy. Both viral and nonviral approaches have been investigated for CAR transduction; however, viral vectors remain the most widely utilized systems because of their relatively stable genomic integration and transduction efficiency [201].

Retroviral vectors exhibit relatively high transduction efficacy but are associated with important safety concerns, including insertional mutagenesis, oncogenesis, and other adverse genomic effects [95]. Lentiviral vectors demonstrate lower risks of insertional mutagenesis and have shown promising results in umbilical cord blood-derived NK cells [202]. Nevertheless, transduction efficiency in peripheral blood-derived NK cells frequently remains below 20%, thereby significantly limiting manufacturing efficiency [192]. Blocking intracellular antiviral defense pathways has been proposed as a potential strategy to improve lentiviral transduction efficiency in NK cells while maintaining acceptable safety profiles [202].

Increasing concerns regarding the cost and biosafety risks associated with viral vectors have accelerated interest in nonviral transduction strategies. Among these approaches, Sleeping Beauty (SB) transposon systems have attracted growing attention because of their relative simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and favorable safety characteristics [203]. Preclinical and clinical studies involving SB-engineered CAR-T cells have demonstrated encouraging therapeutic outcomes and acceptable safety profiles [204, 205]. However, application of SB transposon systems in primary NK cells remains limited because of low transduction efficiency and reduced cellular viability following electroporation procedures [205, 206]. Additional studies are therefore required to determine whether SB-mediated gene transfer can become a clinically feasible strategy for enhancing CAR expression in NK cells.

mRNA-based transfection strategies have also emerged as attractive alternatives for CAR-NK engineering because they may improve safety while reducing manufacturing complexity and cost [207]. Studies using xenograft tumor models have demonstrated substantial cytotoxic activity in mRNA-transfected NK cells, supporting the therapeutic potential of this approach [204]. Furthermore, transient CAR expression achieved through mRNA electroporation may reduce risks associated with prolonged on-target/off-tumor toxicity [95]. Nevertheless, the transient nature of mRNA-mediated CAR expression remains a major limitation, as receptor expression typically persists for only a few days following transfection [95]. Consequently, additional optimization strategies are required to improve the durability and long-term therapeutic efficacy of mRNA-engineered CAR-NK cells.

Challenges of CAR-NK cell therapy in solid tumors

Despite encouraging outcomes in hematological malignancies, the efficacy of CAR-NK-cell therapy in solid tumors has remained comparatively limited [208]. This discrepancy is largely attributable to the highly complex and heterogeneous nature of solid tumors and their associated TMEs. In solid malignancies, CAR-NK cells must successfully traffic through the circulation, infiltrate tumor tissues, survive within suppressive TMEs, and maintain cytotoxic functionality despite numerous inhibitory factors [209].

Interestingly, several solid tumors—including ovarian, breast, and prostate cancers—appear more amenable to CAR-NK-cell therapy because of their relative anatomical accessibility and the feasibility of targeting selected tumor-associated antigens such as PSCA in prostate cancer [210–213]. Nevertheless, multiple major barriers continue to limit therapeutic efficacy in solid tumors.

Antigen heterogeneity and limited target availability

A critical step in CAR design involves identification of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) that are highly and consistently expressed on malignant cells while exhibiting minimal expression in healthy tissues. However, many TAAs expressed in solid tumors are also present at varying levels in essential normal tissues, thereby increasing the risk of on-target/off-tumor toxicity [214].

In addition, antigen expression frequently varies among distinct tumor-cell subclones within the same malignancy because of clonal evolution and adaptive immune evasion mechanisms that reduce TAA expression [215, 216]. To address these limitations, bispecific CAR systems containing dual scFvs have been developed to improve targeting specificity through simultaneous recognition of multiple tumor antigens. Such approaches have demonstrated promising results in prostate cancer models [214].

Physiological and anatomical barriers

Physical barriers within solid tumors significantly impair infiltration of CAR-modified immune cells. Several alternative delivery approaches—including intraperitoneal administration, localized injections, and ultrasound-guided regional delivery—have therefore been investigated to improve local tumor access [217, 218].

Regional delivery of CAR-T cells into pleural cavities has demonstrated superior persistence and antitumor efficacy compared with systemic intravenous administration in pleural tumor models [218]. Similarly, localized administration strategies may reduce the therapeutic doses required for effective treatment while improving regional immune-cell persistence [219].

Focused ultrasound combined with intravenously administered microbubbles has also been investigated as a strategy to facilitate migration of anti-HER2 CAR-NK-92 cells across the blood-brain barrier in metastatic breast cancer models involving cerebral metastases [218, 220]. This approach substantially improved NK-cell delivery into brain tissues while minimizing adverse cellular effects.

Impaired tumor trafficking and infiltration

Efficient migration of CAR-NK cells into tumor tissues remains one of the major determinants of therapeutic efficacy in solid tumors. Enhanced infiltration has been observed in CAR-NK cells engineered to express chemokine receptors compatible with chemokines released by tumor cells [221]. For example, increased CXCR3 and CXCR2 expression has been associated with improved migration toward chemokine-producing tumors together with enhanced adhesion and cytotoxic functionality [222].

Similarly, overexpression of CXCR4 in anti-EGFRvIII CAR-NK cells significantly improves chemotactic responses toward glioblastoma-derived CXCL12/SDF-1α, thereby enhancing tumor regression and improving survival outcomes in preclinical models [9, 222].

Furthermore, oncolytic viruses can induce local inflammatory responses and promote the recruitment of immune effector cells through remodeling of the TME. Consequently, their dual mechanism of action involves both direct tumor cell lysis and enhancement of antitumor immunity, potentially creating a more favorable immune landscape for tumor control [223–225]. Notably, combined treatment using anti-EGFR CAR-NK cells together with oncolytic virotherapy significantly improved antitumor efficacy and survival in preclinical metastatic breast cancer brain metastasis models compared with either treatment alone [222].

Tumor microenvironment-mediated immunosuppression

Although NK cells demonstrate potent cytotoxicity under controlled in vitro conditions, their antitumor activity is often attenuated following transfer into immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments [226]. Regulatory T cells, MDSCs, and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) contribute to NK-cell dysfunction through multiple suppressive mechanisms, thereby limiting antitumor efficacy and facilitating tumor progression [226–228].

In addition, tumors produce multiple immunosuppressive mediators including IL-10, TGF-β, arginase, and PD-1-associated inhibitory pathways [229]. Among these factors, TGF-β plays a particularly central role in suppressing NK-cell cytotoxicity and downregulating activating receptors such as CD16 and NKG2D [230, 231].

Several strategies targeting TGF-β signaling have therefore been explored. Combined administration of TGF-β kinase inhibitors with NK-cell therapy preserves cytotoxic functionality and receptor expression [230, 232–234]. Moreover, galunisertib and fresolimumab—agents targeting TGF-β signaling pathways—have demonstrated favorable safety and tolerability profiles in patients with solid tumors [235, 236].

Additional engineering approaches include hybrid CAR systems incorporating extracellular TGF-β receptor domains linked to intracellular NKG2D signaling components, dominant-negative TGF-β receptors, and SMAD3 knockdown strategies, all of which enhance NK-cell resistance to TME-mediated suppression and improve antitumor activity [23, 236, 237].

Metabolic dysregulation within the tumor microenvironment

The metabolic landscape of the TME is characterized by hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, acidosis, and accumulation of immunosuppressive metabolites, all of which impair NK-cell functionality [238, 239]. Hypoxic conditions promote tumor progression and angiogenesis while simultaneously suppressing expression of NK-cell activating receptors including NKp30, NKp44, NKp46, and NKG2D [240].

CD73-mediated accumulation of adenosine and arginase production further suppress NK-cell cytotoxicity under hypoxic conditions [241]. Importantly, blockade of CD73 has been shown to enhance migration and antitumor activity of NKG2D-CAR-NK cells in lung cancer models, thereby improving therapeutic responses within suppressive TMEs [242].

Immune checkpoint-mediated NK-cell exhaustion

Tumor cells evade immune surveillance through expression of checkpoint molecules that suppress activation of immune effector cells [241, 243]. For example, TIGIT antagonizes CD226 signaling and thereby inhibits NK-cell cytotoxicity [244]. Similarly, PD-1-positive NK cells exhibit reduced proliferation and impaired antitumor functionality compared with PD-L1-positive activated NK cells [245].

Checkpoint blockade strategies targeting PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4, TIGIT, and LAG3 have therefore emerged as promising approaches for restoring exhausted immune-cell populations and improving long-term antitumor responses [244, 246]. Furthermore, genetically engineered NK-92 cells coexpressing high-affinity IL-2 variants, CD16, and PD-L1-targeted CAR constructs demonstrate enhanced perforin and granzyme secretion together with potent cytotoxicity against multiple solid tumor cell lines, including lung, breast, and gastric cancers [247].

Collectively, although CAR-NK-cell therapy represents a highly promising and rapidly evolving immunotherapeutic platform, substantial challenges related to persistence, manufacturing, trafficking, antigen heterogeneity, and TME-mediated immunosuppression continue to limit its broader clinical success. Continued advances in synthetic biology, cytokine engineering, genome editing, checkpoint modulation, and combinatorial therapeutic strategies are expected to play central roles in overcoming these barriers and accelerating the future clinical translation of CAR-NK-cell therapy.

Emerging strategies and future directions in CAR-NK cell therapy

Despite substantial advances in CAR-NK cell therapy, multiple biological and technical barriers continue to restrict its broader clinical translation, particularly in the context of solid tumors [176, 211, 218, 248–251]. In recent years, however, major progress in synthetic biology, gene editing, immunometabolism, and bioengineering has led to the emergence of innovative strategies designed to overcome these limitations and enhance the therapeutic efficacy, persistence, and manufacturability of CAR-NK-cell products.

Overcoming cryopreservation-associated dysfunction

Cryopreservation remains one of the major technical bottlenecks in CAR-NK-cell therapy because post-thaw NK cells frequently exhibit reduced viability, impaired cytotoxicity, and diminished metabolic fitness [252, 253]. Conventional cryoprotective agents such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) may induce osmotic stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, membrane destabilization, and oxidative injury, collectively contributing to post-thaw functional impairment [252–255].

To address these challenges, recent investigations have focused on the development of optimized serum-free and xeno-free cryopreservation formulations supplemented with membrane-stabilizing compounds, antioxidants, and metabolic protective agents [254–256]. Furthermore, several post-thaw recovery approaches have demonstrated encouraging results, including cytokine-mediated preactivation using IL-2 and IL-15 as well as metabolic reprogramming strategies aimed at restoring NK-cell bioenergetic functionality [257, 258].

Emerging cryobiological technologies—including controlled-rate freezing systems and ice recrystallization inhibitors—have also shown considerable potential for improving cellular recovery, preserving membrane integrity, and enhancing post-thaw cytotoxic activity [252, 253]. Collectively, these advances may substantially improve the feasibility of large-scale manufacturing and distribution of “off-the-shelf” CAR-NK-cell products.

Optimization of gene delivery and transduction efficiency

Efficient genetic engineering of NK cells remains a major challenge because NK cells exhibit intrinsic resistance to viral entry and transgene integration [259]. Recent evidence indicates that pharmacological modulation of cholesterol biosynthesis using statins may improve lentiviral transduction efficiency by enhancing membrane fluidity and facilitating viral fusion and cellular uptake [259, 260].

In parallel, increasing attention has been directed toward nonviral gene-delivery systems as safer and potentially more cost-effective alternatives to conventional viral vectors. Among these approaches, the SB transposon platform has emerged as a promising strategy because of its relative simplicity, lower manufacturing complexity, and improved biosafety profile compared with retroviral systems [177, 178, 260, 261]. Additionally, substantial progress has been achieved in electroporation-based transfection technologies and nanoparticle-mediated gene-delivery systems, both of which may improve transduction efficiency while minimizing cellular toxicity and preserving NK-cell viability [252, 253]. These developments are particularly important for facilitating scalable manufacturing and improving the clinical applicability of next-generation CAR-NK-cell platforms.

Enhancing NK-cell expansion, persistence, and resistance to exhaustion

Limited in vivo persistence and functional exhaustion remain among the principal barriers restricting durable clinical responses following CAR-NK-cell therapy [262, 263]. To overcome these limitations, several innovative engineering strategies have been developed to enhance NK-cell proliferation, survival, and metabolic fitness.

Engineered feeder-cell systems expressing membrane-bound cytokines and costimulatory ligands—including IL-15, IL-21, and 4-1BBL—have demonstrated considerable efficacy in promoting robust ex vivo NK-cell expansion and enhancing cytotoxic functionality [264, 265]. In addition, incorporation of cytokine support directly into CAR constructs, particularly IL-15 knock-in systems, has significantly improved CAR-NK-cell persistence and survival in vivo [42, 266, 267]. Recent genome-engineering approaches have further focused on targeting intracellular inhibitory regulators associated with NK-cell exhaustion. For example, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated disruption of CISH has been shown to enhance NK-cell metabolic activity, improve responsiveness to cytokine signaling, and augment antitumor functionality while reducing exhaustion phenotypes [268]. Collectively, these strategies highlight the growing importance of immunometabolic engineering in optimizing the long-term therapeutic efficacy of CAR-NK-cell therapy.

Remodeling the tumor microenvironment and improving tumor homing

The immunosuppressive TME remains one of the most important barriers limiting CAR-NK-cell efficacy, particularly in solid tumors [176, 248, 269]. Multiple suppressive factors within the TME impair NK-cell trafficking, infiltration, activation, and persistence, thereby reducing overall therapeutic effectiveness.

To improve tumor homing, CAR-NK cells have been genetically engineered to express chemokine receptors such as CXCR2 and CXCR4, which enhance migration toward tumors producing corresponding chemokine ligands [270]. These modifications substantially improve tumor infiltration and intratumoral accumulation of CAR-NK cells in preclinical models.

Additional strategies have focused on overcoming TME-mediated immunosuppression through resistance to inhibitory cytokines such as TGF-β. Dominant-negative TGF-β receptor engineering and genome-editing approaches targeting suppressive signaling pathways have demonstrated encouraging efficacy in preserving NK-cell cytotoxicity under highly suppressive conditions [269, 270]. Moreover, combinatorial therapeutic approaches involving immune checkpoint inhibitors, TME-modulating agents, and cytokine-armored CAR constructs have shown synergistic enhancement of CAR-NK-cell antitumor activity in both preclinical and early clinical investigations [176, 248, 269]. These findings suggest that integrated multimodal strategies may be essential for maximizing CAR-NK-cell efficacy in advanced solid malignancies.

Future perspectives and translational outlook

Rapid advances in synthetic biology, genome editing, biomaterials engineering, and systems immunology are substantially accelerating the evolution of CAR-NK-cell therapy toward next-generation precision immunotherapeutic platforms. Emerging technologies—including multiplex genome engineering, logic-gated CAR systems, armored CAR constructs, adaptor CAR platforms, and iPSC-derived universal NK-cell products—are expected to significantly improve therapeutic specificity, persistence, scalability, and safety.

Importantly, the integration of bioengineering approaches with immunometabolic modulation and TME-targeting strategies may ultimately enable CAR-NK cells to overcome many of the limitations currently restricting their clinical efficacy. Continued innovation in these areas is therefore anticipated to play a central role in establishing CAR-NK-cell therapy as a broadly applicable and clinically transformative modality for both hematological malignancies and solid tumors [176].

Combination strategies to enhance CAR-NK cell therapy

Despite the favorable safety profile and promising antitumor activity of CAR-NK-cell therapy, its clinical efficacy—particularly in solid tumors—remains constrained by several biological and translational barriers, including limited in vivo persistence, insufficient tumor trafficking, antigen heterogeneity, and the profoundly immunosuppressive TME [185, 211, 250, 271]. Consequently, rational combinatorial approaches have emerged as an essential strategy to enhance CAR-NK-cell functionality through synergistic and complementary mechanisms capable of overcoming these limitations [186, 211].

Importantly, recent advances in immunoengineering and multimodal cancer therapy have demonstrated that combining CAR-NK cells with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immune checkpoint blockade, cytokine support systems, gene-editing technologies, and oncolytic virotherapy can substantially augment antitumor efficacy. These integrated strategies not only improve CAR-NK-cell trafficking, activation, and persistence, but also remodel the TME into a more immunologically permissive environment [176, 272, 273].

Radiotherapy-induced Immune modulation and enhancement of CAR-NK cell trafficking

Radiotherapy (RT) is increasingly recognized as a potent immunomodulatory modality capable of reshaping the TME in favor of immune effector-cell activity [218, 274]. Beyond its direct cytotoxic effects on tumor cells, ionizing radiation induces multiple immunological alterations that may substantially improve the therapeutic efficacy of adoptively transferred CAR-NK cells.

One important mechanism involves the upregulation of stress-induced ligands, including MHC class I chain-related proteins A/B (MICA/B) and UL16-binding proteins (ULBPs), on irradiated tumor cells [15, 211, 218, 274]. These ligands interact with activating receptors such as NKG2D expressed on NK cells, thereby enhancing NK-cell-mediated cytotoxicity and tumor recognition. In addition, RT stimulates the secretion of chemokines including CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, which facilitate NK-cell recruitment, trafficking, and infiltration into tumor tissues [249, 250, 275].

Radiotherapy also contributes to transient normalization of abnormal tumor vasculature, reduction of hypoxia, and enhancement of antigen presentation, collectively improving immune accessibility within the TME [176, 218, 274]. Moreover, radiation-induced immunogenic cell death (ICD) promotes the release of danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including ATP, HMGB1, and calreticulin, which amplify local inflammatory signaling and support activation of innate and adaptive immune responses [276, 277].

Recent preclinical investigations have demonstrated substantial synergistic interactions between RT and NK-cell-based immunotherapy. Combination strategies involving RT and adoptive CAR-NK-cell transfer significantly improved tumor control and prolonged survival in experimental models of glioblastoma and hepatocellular carcinoma [83, 218, 274]. These findings suggest that RT may function not only as a cytoreductive modality but also as an important immunological sensitizer capable of enhancing CAR-NK-cell therapeutic efficacy.

Chemotherapy and immunogenic reprogramming of the tumor microenvironment

In addition to their direct cytotoxic activity, several chemotherapeutic agents possess important immunomodulatory properties that may synergize with CAR-NK-cell therapy [186, 278]. Certain chemotherapy regimens can selectively reduce immunosuppressive cellular populations within the TME, particularly Tregs and MDSCs, thereby partially restoring NK-cell functionality and antitumor immune activity [265, 278].

Furthermore, chemotherapy-induced cellular stress frequently enhances tumor susceptibility to NK-cell-mediated killing through upregulation of activating ligands and death receptors on malignant cells [278, 279]. Importantly, several chemotherapeutic agents induce immunogenic cell death, characterized by release of DAMPs such as ATP, HMGB1, and calreticulin, which enhance dendritic-cell activation, antigen presentation, and subsequent immune stimulation [278, 280, 281].

Preclinical and translational studies have reported improved antitumor outcomes when NK-cell-based therapies are combined with agents including cyclophosphamide and doxorubicin [265, 278, 280]. In many cases, lymphodepleting chemotherapy administered prior to CAR-NK-cell infusion may additionally improve engraftment and transient persistence of infused cells by reducing endogenous immune competition and increasing availability of homeostatic cytokines. Collectively, these findings indicate that appropriately selected chemotherapy regimens may function as immunological conditioning platforms capable of enhancing CAR-NK-cell efficacy while simultaneously modulating the suppressive TME.

CRISPR/Cas9-based genetic engineering to overcome inhibitory signaling pathways

The emergence of CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing technology has substantially expanded the engineering potential of CAR-NK-cell therapy by enabling precise and multiplexed genetic modifications designed to improve persistence, metabolic fitness, and resistance to immunosuppressive signaling [60, 274, 282, 283].

One major application involves targeted disruption of inhibitory immune regulators such as PD-1, T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), and cytokine-inducible SH2-containing protein (CISH). Knockout of these inhibitory pathways has been shown to significantly enhance NK-cell cytotoxicity, cytokine production, metabolic activity, and resistance to functional exhaustion [268, 282–284].

In parallel, knock-in engineering strategies have enabled insertion of cytokine-expression cassettes, including IL-15, as well as chemokine receptors such as CXCR2 and CXCR4, thereby improving CAR-NK-cell proliferation, survival, and tumor homing capacity [60, 119, 178]. These modifications may partially overcome one of the major limitations of NK-cell therapy, namely insufficient in vivo persistence.

Recent preclinical studies have demonstrated that CRISPR-engineered CAR-NK cells exhibit prolonged persistence and superior antitumor efficacy in xenograft tumor models relative to conventionally engineered NK-cell platforms [60, 274, 282, 283]. Nevertheless, concerns regarding genomic stability, off-target editing, and manufacturing standardization remain important considerations for future clinical translation.

Combination with immune checkpoint blockade and cytokine support

Checkpoint-mediated immunosuppression within the TME represents a major mechanism limiting NK-cell activity in solid tumors. Accordingly, combination strategies integrating CAR-NK cells with immune checkpoint inhibitors have attracted substantial attention as a means to restore NK-cell functionality and overcome tumor-induced immune exhaustion [119, 278, 285]. Blocking inhibitory pathways such as PD-1/PD-L1, NKG2A, TIGIT, and LAG3 has been shown to enhance NK-cell activation, cytokine secretion, proliferation, and tumor-cell killing [119, 278, 286]. Importantly, checkpoint inhibition may synergize with CAR-mediated signaling by simultaneously augmenting activating pathways while preventing exhaustion-associated suppression.

Alongside checkpoint modulation, cytokine support strategies have emerged as another major approach for enhancing CAR-NK-cell persistence and therapeutic durability. Among these cytokines, IL-15 plays a particularly critical role in NK-cell homeostasis, proliferation, survival, and metabolic maintenance [287, 288]. Consequently, IL-15 has been extensively incorporated into cytokine-armored CAR constructs to improve in vivo persistence and antitumor activity [50, 85, 280, 289].

Early-phase clinical studies investigating IL-15-expressing CAR-NK platforms have demonstrated encouraging safety profiles together with preliminary evidence of enhanced persistence and therapeutic efficacy, particularly in hematological malignancies [40, 41, 280]. These findings support continued development of multifunctional CAR-NK constructs integrating cytokine support and checkpoint resistance mechanisms.

Emerging combination strategies and novel mechanisms of synergy

Recent advances in combinatorial immunotherapy have highlighted several additional strategies capable of synergistically enhancing CAR-NK-cell efficacy and overcoming resistance mechanisms within the TME [176, 272, 273].

One particularly promising approach involves combining CRISPR-engineered CAR-NK cells with immune checkpoint blockade. Genetic disruption of inhibitory molecules such as PD-1, TIGIT, or CISH substantially enhances NK-cell activation and metabolic fitness [60, 268, 290, 291]. When these engineered CAR-NK cells are combined with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, synergistic antitumor activity has been observed through simultaneous reversal of immune exhaustion and restoration of cytotoxic signaling pathways [292].

Another rapidly evolving strategy involves integration of oncolytic viruses (OVs) with CAR-NK-cell therapy. Oncolytic viruses selectively infect and lyse malignant cells while simultaneously reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment [313–316]. Viral-mediated tumor destruction promotes local inflammatory responses, enhances antigen release, and induces secretion of chemokines such as CXCL9 and CXCL10, thereby facilitating CAR-NK-cell trafficking and infiltration into tumor tissues [293–296]. In addition, OVs increase tumor sensitivity to NK-cell-mediated cytotoxicity through upregulation of stress-induced ligands and activation of type I interferon signaling pathways [295, 296]. Preclinical investigations evaluating OV/CAR-NK combination therapy have demonstrated substantially improved tumor control compared with either modality alone, particularly in difficult-to-treat solid tumors.

Radiotherapy-based combination strategies likewise continue to demonstrate significant synergistic potential through enhancement of tumor immunogenicity, promotion of antigen presentation, normalization of tumor vasculature, and induction of immunogenic cell death [276, 277]. Similarly, cytokine-supported approaches utilizing IL-15-armored CAR-NK cells have shown superior persistence and enhanced antitumor efficacy in both preclinical and early clinical studies [289, 297].

Collectively, these emerging combinatorial strategies illustrate how rational integration of gene editing, checkpoint modulation, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, cytokine engineering, and virotherapy may synergistically overcome current translational limitations of CAR-NK-cell therapy (Refer to Figure. 2). Continued optimization of treatment sequencing, dosing regimens, manufacturing approaches, and long-term safety assessment will be essential for maximizing the clinical potential of these next-generation combinatorial immunotherapeutic platforms [211, 218, 271].

Mechanisms of synergy in CAR-NK combination therapy. Combinatorial therapeutic strategies substantially enhance the antitumor efficacy of CAR-NK-cell therapy through complementary and synergistic mechanisms. Immune checkpoint blockade restores NK-cell cytotoxicity, reverses immune exhaustion, and improves persistence. Oncolytic viruses promote tumor-cell lysis, increase antigen release, and remodel the tumor immune microenvironment by inducing inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that facilitate NK-cell trafficking and infiltration. Radiotherapy enhances tumor immunogenicity through upregulation of stress-induced ligands, promotion of antigen presentation, vascular normalization, and induction of immunogenic cell death. IL-15 support further augments CAR-NK-cell proliferation, metabolic fitness, survival, and resistance to immunosuppressive signaling within the tumor microenvironment. Collectively, these integrated approaches improve CAR-NK-cell infiltration, persistence, and cytotoxic activity, ultimately resulting in enhanced and more durable antitumor responses [272, 273]

Discussion

Recent advances in the understanding of NK-cell immunobiology have substantially accelerated the development of next-generation cellular immunotherapies for cancer treatment. Among the most promising of these approaches is CAR-NK-cell therapy, which combines the intrinsic antitumor properties of NK cells with the antigen specificity conferred by CAR engineering. One of the major advantages of NK-cell-based immunotherapy is the ability of NK cells to exert antitumor activity without requiring strict HLA compatibility between donor and recipient, thereby facilitating the development of allogeneic “off-the-shelf” therapeutic products with broader clinical accessibility [125]. In contrast to autologous CAR-T-cell therapy, this characteristic offers important translational advantages, including reduced manufacturing time, improved scalability, and potentially lower treatment costs.

The principal objective of CAR-NK-cell engineering is to establish highly efficient and controllable activation systems capable of enhancing NK-cell cytotoxicity while simultaneously improving tumor specificity and minimizing treatment-related toxicities [298]. Over recent years, substantial progress has been achieved in optimizing CAR architecture, cytokine support systems, genome-editing technologies, and NK-cell expansion platforms. Collectively, these innovations have significantly improved the therapeutic potential of CAR-NK cells and expanded their applicability across both hematological malignancies and solid tumors.

Nevertheless, despite encouraging preclinical and early clinical findings, several critical biological and translational barriers continue to limit the widespread clinical implementation of CAR-NK-cell therapy. Among the most important limitations are insufficient in vivo persistence, impaired trafficking and infiltration into solid tumors, antigen heterogeneity, functional exhaustion, and the highly immunosuppressive TME [185, 211, 250, 271]. In addition, challenges related to large-scale manufacturing, cryopreservation-associated dysfunction, variable transduction efficiency, and standardization of clinical-grade production workflows remain incompletely resolved.

Importantly, growing evidence suggests that the future success of CAR-NK-cell therapy will likely depend on the development of multifunctional and combinatorial engineering strategies capable of simultaneously addressing several of these barriers. Emerging approaches involving cytokine-armored CAR constructs, chemokine receptor modification, immune checkpoint disruption, metabolic reprogramming, and multiplex genome editing have demonstrated substantial promise in enhancing NK-cell persistence, tumor homing, and resistance to TME-mediated immunosuppression [211, 268, 282–284, 299].

Furthermore, combinatorial therapeutic strategies integrating CAR-NK cells with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immune checkpoint blockade, oncolytic viruses, and immunometabolic modulators may provide synergistic antitumor effects by reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment and restoring immune responsiveness [176, 272, 273]. These approaches are particularly important in solid tumors, where physical barriers, hypoxia, immunosuppressive cytokines, and inhibitory checkpoint pathways collectively impair NK-cell functionality.

An additional area of growing interest involves the development of advanced synthetic biology platforms and next-generation switchable CAR systems. Engineering CAR-NK cells with multi-antigen recognition capacity may reduce tumor immune escape caused by antigen loss or tumor heterogeneity while improving therapeutic specificity and maintaining acceptable safety thresholds [300]. Similarly, chimeric switch receptors capable of converting inhibitory signals into activating pathways represent an innovative strategy to overcome TME-mediated suppression. For example, incorporation of extracellular domains derived from inhibitory receptors such as TGF-βR or IL-10R fused to intracellular co-stimulatory signaling domains may transform immunosuppressive cytokine signaling into activating stimuli that enhance NK-cell cytotoxicity within tumors [89, 301–303].

Recent investigations have also highlighted the emerging role of engineered NK-cell-derived exosomes as potential therapeutic mediators within the TME. Exosomes released from genetically modified CAR-NK cells may carry immunologically active proteins, RNA molecules, and signaling mediators capable of modulating tumor progression, angiogenesis, metastasis, and immune evasion pathways [304–308]. Such approaches may ultimately provide cell-free adjunctive immunotherapeutic platforms with improved tissue penetration and reduced toxicity profiles.

In parallel, advances in nanotechnology and biomaterial-based delivery systems may further improve the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-NK-cell therapy by enhancing tumor targeting, remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment, and facilitating controlled cytokine or drug delivery within tumor tissues. These integrated bioengineering strategies may substantially improve both the clinical applicability and durability of NK-cell-based immunotherapies in the future.

Despite these encouraging developments, several important considerations remain unresolved. Long-term safety data concerning extensive genome editing, multiplex engineering, and prolonged cytokine exposure remain limited. Moreover, optimization of treatment sequencing, dosing regimens, and combinatorial therapeutic protocols will require further investigation through large-scale multicenter clinical trials. Harmonization of manufacturing procedures, quality-control standards, and clinical trial reporting will likewise be essential for accelerating regulatory approval and broader clinical implementation.

Overall, CAR-NK-cell therapy represents one of the most promising emerging platforms in cancer immunotherapy. The field is rapidly evolving from relatively simple CAR-engineered constructs toward highly sophisticated multifunctional cellular systems capable of resisting immunosuppression, improving tumor infiltration, and sustaining durable antitumor activity. Continued integration of immunology, synthetic biology, genome engineering, biomaterials science, and translational oncology will likely play a pivotal role in shaping the next generation of CAR-NK-cell therapeutics.

Ultimately, successful clinical translation of CAR-NK-cell therapy will depend not only on technological innovation but also on the establishment of multidisciplinary collaborative frameworks involving immunologists, oncologists, bioengineers, translational scientists, and regulatory experts. Such coordinated efforts will be critical for transforming current experimental advances into safe, effective, and broadly accessible clinical therapies for patients with both hematological malignancies and solid tumors.

Future perspectives and unresolved challenges

Despite substantial progress in CAR-NK-cell engineering and early clinical translation, several biological and translational challenges continue to limit the broad clinical applicability of CAR-NK-cell therapy, particularly in solid tumors [309]. One of the most important unresolved issues is the incomplete understanding of the TME across different malignancies. Increasing evidence indicates that TMEs cannot simply be classified as “hot” or “cold,” but instead represent highly heterogeneous and dynamic ecosystems composed of distinct immune, stromal, metabolic, and vascular components that variably influence therapeutic responsiveness [309, 310]. Consequently, a more comprehensive characterization of tumor-specific TMEs will be essential for the rational design of next-generation CAR-NK-cell therapies.

Recent studies have identified several emerging molecular targets that may improve future CAR-NK-cell strategies in specific malignancies. For example, tumor necrosis factor-α-inducible protein 8-like 2 (TNFAIP8L2) has recently been recognized as an important negative regulator of immune homeostasis. Elevated TNFAIP8L2 expression has been associated with poor prognosis in chromophobe renal cell carcinoma and clear cell renal cell carcinoma, suggesting its potential utility as a future therapeutic target or biomarker for CAR-NK-cell-based renal cancer therapy [311]. Similarly, increased levels of soluble PD-L1 in HCC have been proposed as clinically relevant biomarkers that may influence response to immunotherapeutic interventions [312, 313]. Additional targets, including death receptor 4/5 (DR4/5) in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and HLA-G as an emerging immune checkpoint molecule expressed in several malignancies, may further expand the repertoire of targetable pathways for future CAR-NK-cell engineering [314, 315].

Importantly, tumor antigen expression frequently evolves during disease progression and metastatic dissemination, contributing to therapeutic heterogeneity and immune escape [146, 316, 317]. For instance, B7-H3 expression appears substantially higher in metastatic tumors than in localized disease, while aggressive breast cancer subtypes characterized by elevated B7-H3 and low PD-L1 expression may exhibit an “immune-armored cold tumor” phenotype associated with resistance to conventional immunotherapy [318, 319]. These observations underscore the urgent need for reliable predictive biomarkers capable of characterizing the immunological and metabolic landscape of the TME in individual patients to support precision-guided CAR-NK-cell therapy (Fig. 3).

Prospective strategies and future directions for next-generation CAR-NK-cell therapy. The tumor microenvironment remains one of the major barriers limiting the efficacy of CAR-NK-cell therapy, owing to the presence of immunosuppressive cytokines, immune checkpoint molecules, hypoxia, metabolic dysregulation, and tumor-derived extracellular vesicles. Emerging next-generation CAR-NK engineering strategies aim to overcome these barriers through multiplex CAR designs, cytokine armoring, chemokine receptor modification, immune checkpoint disruption, metabolic reprogramming, and synthetic switch-receptor systems capable of converting inhibitory signals into activating pathways. Genetically engineered CAR-NK cells may also release immunologically active exosomes that modulate tumor progression, angiogenesis, metastasis, and immune evasion. Integration of nanotechnology, biomaterial-assisted delivery systems, genome editing, and combinatorial immunotherapeutic approaches may further enhance CAR-NK-cell trafficking, persistence, and antitumor functionality. Collectively, these multidisciplinary strategies are expected to improve the therapeutic efficacy, safety, and clinical applicability of next-generation CAR-NK-cell immunotherapy

Future engineering strategies will likely focus on the development of multifunctional and logic-gated CAR-NK-cell platforms capable of dynamically responding to complex TMEs. Advanced inducible promoter systems may enable tightly regulated expression of cytotoxic mediators such as perforin and granzyme B, thereby improving antitumor activity while minimizing unintended toxicities. Furthermore, incorporation of synthetic biology approaches—including switch receptors, conditional activation systems, and multiplex CAR architectures—may improve tumor specificity, reduce antigen escape, and enhance resistance to immunosuppressive signaling within the TME [89, 300].

Particularly promising are chimeric switch-receptor strategies that convert inhibitory signals into activating signals. In these systems, extracellular domains derived from suppressive receptors such as TGF-βR or IL-10R are fused to intracellular co-stimulatory signaling domains, thereby transforming immunosuppressive cytokine signaling into stimulatory activation pathways [89, 301–303]. Such approaches may substantially enhance CAR-NK-cell persistence and cytotoxicity within highly suppressive tumor environments. In parallel, engineering CAR-NK cells to secrete immunomodulatory exosomes or soluble decoy receptors may further contribute to remodeling the TME, restoring immune recognition, and inhibiting tumor-promoting pathways including angiogenesis, metastasis, and immune evasion [304–308, 320, 321].

Another emerging area involves metabolic and microenvironmental adaptation of engineered NK cells. Inspired by adaptive mechanisms observed in hypoxia-resistant microorganisms and tumor-tropic bacteria, future CAR-NK-cell platforms may be engineered to maintain functional activity under hypoxic, nutrient-deprived, and acidic TME conditions commonly observed in solid tumors. Such metabolic reprogramming strategies could significantly improve NK-cell survival, infiltration, and sustained cytotoxicity within hostile tumor niches.

Despite these advances, several major translational obstacles remain unresolved. One of the most critical limitations is the relatively short in vivo persistence of infused NK cells, which may restrict durable antitumor immunity and limit the development of long-term immunological memory [322, 323]. Gene-editing approaches, cytokine-armored CAR constructs, and stem-cell-derived NK-cell platforms may help overcome these challenges by enhancing persistence, proliferative capacity, and functional durability. Additionally, safety remains a central concern, particularly regarding off-target toxicity, excessive immune activation, and long-term genomic stability. Consequently, incorporation of inducible suicide switches and precision-engineered logic-gated activation circuits will likely become increasingly important for improving therapeutic controllability and minimizing unintended adverse effects [324]. Finally, the future of cellular immunotherapy may increasingly involve integrated multi-cellular therapeutic platforms combining CAR-engineered NK cells with CAR-T cells, macrophages, or other immune effectors to exploit complementary antitumor mechanisms [325].

Such combinatorial and highly customizable immunotherapeutic systems may ultimately provide more durable and broadly effective anticancer responses across both hematological malignancies and solid tumors.

Collectively, continued advances in synthetic biology, genome engineering, immunometabolic reprogramming, biomarker-guided patient stratification, and combinatorial immunotherapy are expected to substantially accelerate the clinical maturation of CAR-NK-cell therapy. Future-generation CAR-NK platforms will likely evolve toward safer, more durable, and highly adaptable cellular therapeutics capable of overcoming the complex biological barriers that currently limit successful treatment of advanced malignancies.
