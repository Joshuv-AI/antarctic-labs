 SUMMARY OF TESTING

A) Objectives

 The principal objective of this report was to summarize the testing experience on
the DM1200 WESP. Further objectives were to provide descriptions of the history of all
modifications and maintenance, methods of operation, problems and unit failures, and
performance while processing a variety of feeds, and while employing a variety of
operating methods, and to provide comparisons to the Hanford Tank Waste Treatment
and Immobilization Plant (WTP) high level waste (HLW) and low activity waste (LAW)
WESP designs.

B) Test Exceptions

 This report is a summary of previously reported test results. While the source
reports were subject to various Test Exceptions, there were no Test Exceptions specific to
this report.

C) R&T Testing Conditions

 Several tests have been conducted with simulants of WTP HLW and LAW
streams on the DuraMelter® 1200 (DM1200) and prototypical off-gas system since its
installation in early 2001 and the present. Throughout these tests, the off-gas system
components were operated under conditions intended to mimic WTP operating
conditions, as known at the time, in order to permit the expected performance of each
unit to be assessed. The melter and off-gas system were evaluated in the course of
producing over 180 metric tons of glass from HLW feed simulants at a maximum steady
state rate of 1450 kg/m2/day and 83 metric tons of glass from LAW feed simulants at a
maximum steady state rate of 2500 kg/m2/day.

 All of the tests were performed on the DM1200 melter system. The DM1200
HLW Pilot Melter is a Joule-heated melter with Inconel 690 electrodes. The melter shell
is water-cooled and incorporates a jack-bolt thermal expansion system. The footprint of
the melter is approximately 8 ft by 6.5 ft with a 4 ft by 2.3 ft air-lift discharge chamber
appended to one end; the melter shell is almost 8 ft tall. The melt surface area and the
melt pool height are approximately 32 percent and 57 percent, respectively, of the
corresponding values for the full-scale HLW melter. The discharge riser and trough are
full-scale to verify pouring performance. The surface of the glass pool is about 1.2 m2,
and the volume is about 849 liters, corresponding to about 2 metric tons of glass. The
feed system consists of a mix tank and a feed tank, both of which are 750-gallon
polyethylene tanks with conical bottoms that are fitted with mechanical agitators. The
feed tank is also fitted with baffles to improve mixing, and calibrated load cells that are

 7

electronically monitored to determine the feed rate to the melter. The feed is introduced
into the melter using an air-displacement-slurry (ADS) pump, which is the present
baseline for the RPP-WTP. Feed from the ADS pump flows into the melter through a
prototypic un-cooled feed nozzle that is located above the center of the glass pool. The
melter and entire off-gas treatment system are maintained under negative pressure by two
Paxton external induced draft blowers. This negative pressure is necessary to direct the
gases from the melter to the prototypical off-gas system. The off-gas treatment system
consists of a submerged bed scrubber (SBS); a wet electrostatic precipitator (WESP); a
high-efficiency mist eliminator (HEME for HLW only), a high-efficiency particulate air
(HEPA) filter; a thermal catalytic oxidation unit (TCO); a NOx removal system (SCR); a
packed-bed caustic scrubber (PBS); and a second HEME. A sulfur impregnated activated
carbon column was installed between the HEPA and the TCO for the later tests. The
second HEME is used to limit entrained particle carryover into the balance of the VSL
ventilation system; the PBS and the second HEME are not part of the WTP HLW off-gas
train, which effectively ends at the SCR.

 Specific testing conditions varied according to the specific Test Plans and are
listed in each of the referenced reports. Test conditions for the WESP also varied during
testing and are detailed in the body of this report.

D) Results and Performance Against Objectives

 The effectiveness of the WESP at removing particulate ranged from
decontamination factor (DF) values of about 4 up to about 300. A principal factor
affecting measured performance was the inclusion or exclusion of the WESP deluge
procedure during the exhaust sampling period. The average DF values for samples
including and excluding the deluge are 26 and 110, respectively. Other factors possibly
affecting WESP performance include aging of the unit and the use of the inlet spray with
city water. Comparison of the first (inlet spray off or using recycled sump fluid) and last
set of tests (inlet spay using city water) measuring WESP performance show a decrease
in DF values from 55 - 302 to 24 - 67. One possible reason for the early excellent
performance was the low pH of sump solutions (0.3 - 6) caused in part by the lack of
dilution from continuous spraying of city water. Internal WESP components after these
tests were observed to be remarkably clean due to the acidic conditions. Highly acidic
solutions in the WESP may be more effective than the deluge used in subsequent tests for
keeping the internal WESP components clean, thus improving the performance of the
WESP.

 Given the number of tests conducted, few deposits formed on the WESP internals
due presumably to the effectiveness of the deluge in removing collected solids. At no
time during testing did solid accumulations impede the exhaust flow through the WESP
or the removal or spraying of process solutions.

 8

 All major WESP failures occurred while processing feeds containing LAW
Envelope A and C simulants. No significant failures occurred while processing feeds
containing HLW simulants or LAW Envelope B simulants. These WESP failures,
therefore, appear to be related to certain compositional characteristics of the waste and its
associated off-gas stream rather than the manner in which the WESP was operated.

 Time required for restoring power after a deluge varied with feed type and inlet
spraying. In the later tests, the inlet spray was turned off at the same time the power was
turned off immediately prior to the deluge, and turned back on only after the operator
observed stable electrical conditions in the WESP. This procedure resulted in a decrease
of power-down time from an average of 38 minutes (ranging from 6 to 97 minutes) with
the spray on, to an average of 10 minutes (ranging from 4 to 31 minutes) with the spray
off for tests in which HLW feed was processed.

 However, restoring power to the WESP after the deluge while processing LAW
feed was considerably more difficult, regardless of which procedure was used. Power
interruptions, including the time required to restore power, averaged 30 minutes when the
spray was turned off for tests with LAW feed. During the most recent LAW tests,
restoring power to the WESP often took several hours, even with additional deluges
intended to remove any residual material on the electrodes. Once power was restored, the
current and voltage were less than typically observed. By the end of the test, power could
not be restored to the unit after deluges. However, the WESP performed normally during
tests with feed containing HLW simulants conducted immediately afterwards, indicating
that there was no mechanical fault with the unit. These observations and the association
of the difficulty in restoring power after the deluge suggest that the issue is related to
components unique to the SBS exhaust stream generated while processing LAW
Envelope A and C. As compared to the LAW Envelope B feeds, these feeds are
compositionally more disparate (higher in terms of nitrates, nitrites, halides) from the
HLW feeds.

 The designs of the WTP HLW and LAW WESPs are very similar to that of the
DM1200 WESP. The WTP HLW WESP has nearly the same collector area per unit
off-gas flow rate and residence time as the DM1200 WESP. However, the collector area
per unit gas flow rate and the residence time for the WTP LAW WESP are approximately
50% larger than those of the DM1200 WESP. As a result, this difference should be taken
into consideration when operational information from the DM1200 WESP during LAW
testing is used or applied to the WTP LAW WESP.

 The most significant difference in the DM1200 and WTP WESP designs is that
the WTP electrode assemblies are suspended by rods and ceramic insulators, which are
located well away from the off-gas flow. By utilizing high air purge rates around the
insulators, it is expected that the WTP WESP will be less subject to the type of electrode

 9

shorting and downtime that was experienced in the DM1200 WESP during the processing
of the LAW simulants. However, while the isolation of the electrical connections and the
introduction of purge air in the WTP design may well prevent the electrical problems
encountered with the DM1200 WESP, they also introduce additional uncertainties due to
the lack of testing data under these conditions. The WTP protocol includes inlet spraying
for LAW feeds but not for HLW feeds. The WTP HLW protocol also includes a deluge
from the bottom of the WESP, which has not been tested on the DM1200 WESP. It is
also not clear how rapidly effective power will be restored to the WTP WESP after
deluging.

E) Quality Requirements

 This work is a summary of previously reported test results, the quality assurance
requirements for each of which are provided in the original reports. This work was
conducted under a quality assurance program that is in place at the VSL that is based on
NQA-1 (1989) and NQA-2a (1990) Part 2.7. This program is supplemented by a Quality
Assurance Project Plan for RPP-WTP work that is conducted at VSL. Test and procedure
requirements by which the testing activities are planned and controlled are also defined in
this plan. The program is supported by VSL standard operating procedures that were used
for this work. This work was not subject to DOE/RW-0333P or the requirements of the
WTP QAPjP for environmental testing. However, several of the original tests were
subject to the requirements of the WTP QAPjP, as noted in the original test reports.

F) Simulant Use

 This summary covers testing that employed a wide range of HLW and LAW
simulants formulated based on various waste bases, as detailed in the respective original
test reports. References for each waste simulant are provided in each source report. The
HLW waste simulants processed were AZ-101 [5-7, 11, 12], AZ-102 [8, 13],
C-106/AY-102 [9, 12, 13, 19], and C-104/AY-101 [10]. The LAW Sub-Envelope
simulants processed were C1 [15], A1 [16], B1 [17], A2 [19], and A and C [18].

G) Issues and Follow-On Tests

 Further testing would be useful to determine whether the apparent degradation in
DF performance over time was due to changes in operating parameters or degradation of
the unit. Further testing would also be useful to determine the origin of the WESP failures
with LAW feeds and whether or not the design differences implemented in the WTP
WESP will prevent such failures.

 SECTION 1.0
 INTRODUCTION

 The operational requirements for the River Protection Project – Hanford Tank
Waste Treatment & Immobilization Plant (RPP-WTP) High Level Waste (HLW) melter
system and the specific range of HLW melter feed constituents impose a number of
challenges to the off-gas treatment system. The system operates with a highly radioactive
feed and therefore must be very robust to limit the need for remote maintenance. The
system must effectively control and remove solid particulate matter over a wide range of
particle sizes and physical characteristics; control and remove acid gases such as SOx,
HCl, NOx in the off-gas stream; and destroy volatile organic constituents (VOCs) to
concentration levels below those imposed by regulatory requirements. In particular, the
level of radioactivity released as sub-micron particulates, notably cesium, must be
controlled to ensure an acceptable low release. A baseline design for the RPP-WTP off-
gas system was developed by BNFL, Inc. in Part B-1. It includes a submerged bed
scrubber (SBS), a wet electrostatic precipitator (WESP), and a high efficiency mist
eliminator (HEME) as the principal components [1]; a catalytic oxidation unit was added
on the basis of test data that showed that the melter alone would not provide sufficient
destruction of organics [2]. Essentially the same process train is also used for the low
activity waste (LAW) off-gas treatment system [3]. This combination of off-gas
components, while conceptually reasonable, had not been tested in an integrated manner
with a waste glass melter. Accordingly there was a need to evaluate the performance and
design features of this combination of off-gas control components with a melter system
that is representative of the proposed RPP-WTP HLW vitrification system. Pilot scale
equipment testing is required to assure that the design functions as required for the RPP-
WTP HLW vitrification systems. To address this need, the RPP-WTP deployed a one-
third of full scale HLW pilot melter system at the Vitreous State Laboratory (VSL) of
The Catholic University of America (CUA) in Washington, DC. The DuraMelter® 1200
(DM1200) system was used to address a variety of objectives including collection of data
on processing rates for HLW waste compositions, and evaluation of the effectiveness of
the prototypical off-gas system components in treating exhaust from the vitrification of
both the LAW and HLW simulated waste streams. Since the commissioning of the
DM1200 in January of 2001 [4, 5], the DM1200 off-gas system has been evaluated in the
treatment of exhaust from a wide range of HLW and LAW simulated waste streams [5-
19].

 This report provides a summary of the history and performance of the WESP as a
part of the off-gas system of the DM1200 HLW pilot melter facility at VSL. Included are
the design specifications, a detailed description of the unit, a complete history of
operation, listing of all modifications and repairs made to the unit, description of all unit
failures, summary of the amounts and chemical compositions of process effluents
generated by the WESP, measured particle removal efficiencies of the WESP, and

 SECTION 2.0
 WESP DESCRIPTION AND DESIGN

2.1 Principle of Operation

 The WESP targets submicron particulate removal. The SBS immediately
upstream of the WESP removes the coarse particulate, the HEME immediately
downstream of the WESP (HLW off-gas train only) removes mist from the WESP
exhaust, and the high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filtration units complete the
particulate removal in the primary off-gas system. In the WESP, the saturated SBS off-
gas is introduced with an optional concurrent, low velocity water spray into a large
collector grid. The collector grid consists of an array of tubes, each with a central rod
electrode along its length. A high voltage is applied between the wall of each tube and the
central rod electrode. The high water content in the off-gas raises its dielectric constant to
a relatively uniform high level, which smoothes out possible fluctuations due to changes
in particulate resistivity and loading. The high voltage (about 25-32 kV) applied to the
ionizing rods is increased until an ionization corona is just created, causing a flow of
electrons across the space between the rods and the collector surface. The WESP is then
operated at a slightly lower voltage. Entrained particles and water droplets passing
through this electric field are ionized and electrostatically attracted to the (grounded)
collector plate walls (or the central rods, depending on their charge), where they coalesce
and are washed into the WESP sump by an intermittent downward water spray. Hence,
the key operating parameter affecting WESP performance is the voltage and, secondarily,
the current.

 In the DM1200 system, an air operated diaphragm (AOD) pump periodically
transfers the slurry of fine particulates collected in the sump to the sample and storage
tanks based on the liquid level in the WESP sump.

2.2 WESP Specifications

 The WESP installed in the DM1200 off-gas system is based on the original design
input from BNFL, Inc. obtained during Part B-1; the unit was sized to reflect the
expected gas flow rates from the DM1200 melter. Bids were solicited from several
vendors from which the final selections were made. The WESP was procured from Clean
Gas Systems (CGS) of Ronkonkama, NY. The cost of the WESP unit for the DM1200
off-gas system was significantly reduced by using Fiber Reinforced Plastic (FRP) instead
of metal for the WESP outer vessel, which is not prototypical of the WTP design. As a
consequence, an additional intermediate blower was needed immediately after this WESP
unit since it was not designed to operate at more than -30 in.WG (-7.5 kPa) pressure.

2.3 DM1200 WESP Design Details
 2.3.1 Particulate Removal/Electrical Systems

 Exhaust flows from the SBS into the horizontal inlet nozzle located on the lower
portion of the WESP shell. The gas then flows upwards within the WESP vessel and
passes through a perforated diffuser plate and ten hexagonal electrode channels where
particulate removal occurs. The treated gas then exits through a nozzle in the top plate of
the WESP vessel. A liquid spray nozzle is situated at the inlet and sprays water at about 2
gallons per hour. The liquid flow mixes with the incoming gas in sufficient quantity to
ensure a wet film on the hexagonal collecting electrodes. The diffuser plate in the WESP
shell ensures a uniform flow of gas through each of the electrode collectors. Ten
hexagonal collector channels, each consisting of six, 5-ft. (1.52 m) X 3 inch (9 cm) steel
plates surround central high voltage electrodes. The collecting electrodes are grounded.
The high-voltage electrode is a long rod with star shaped projections. These projections,
shown in Figure 2.4, are located about every 6 in. (152 mm) along the rods and provide
regions of high electric field along the electrodes. The system voltage and current are
manually adjusted through an external power supply rated at 2 kW with capability of
electrical potential in excess of 60,000 volts. Each of the high-voltage electrode rods is
held in place and centered within its respective channel by a crisscrossing network of
stainless steel beams located above the collecting electrode area in the upper crossing
FRP channel (see Figures 2.5 and 2.6) within the WESP vessel. The support grid extends
to the ends of this pipe and is electrically insulated from the FRP shell by plastic or
ceramic spacers, as shown in Figure 2.7. The grid is connected to the external power
supply where voltage and current are regulated. The high-voltage electrode rods
terminate in a metallic grid of beams situated just below the hexagonal channel entrance.
The high-voltage rods are thus both rigidly mounted and also accurately centered in the
hexagonal channels.

 Particulate-laden gas passes through the high-voltage electric field between the
electrodes and the collector surface. The particulate is ionized within this field
whereupon it becomes attracted to the collecting electrode (or the central rods, depending
on the charge). The moisture layer on the electrode surface serves the dual purpose of
maintaining a constant resistivity for the moistened particulate (which greatly enhances
collection efficiency) and also preventing the particulate from rigidly adhering to the
metallic surfaces.

 14

 There is an optimum operating voltage for the WESP for any given particulate
composition and flux. In the DM1200 system, this is manually established using the
WESP power supply. In general, the higher the voltage across the electrodes, the greater
the collection efficiency, provided the voltage is below a certain threshold value.
Excessively high voltages can ionize the gas molecules and lead to both mechanical
degradation of the electrode surface, if operated under these conditions for an extended
period, as well as reduced collection efficiency. This is known as corona discharge.
Preliminary testing of the WESP at the VSL showed that the optimum voltage for this
system was likely to be about 22,000 to 25,000 volts; however, the range determined with
actual melter off-gas during melter tests was about 5 kV higher. High operating
efficiency can be achieved in the WESP using the highest operating voltage/current
without causing “corona” discharge. These electrical parameters can be optimized with
testing both independently and fully integrated with melter operations. The preliminary
testing of the WESP was conducted with voltage/current settings recommended by the
manufacturer (CGS) while operating under a no-flow, non-integrated mode of operation.

 2.3.2 Liquid Removal System

 It is necessary to periodically flush (deluge) the WESP collecting surfaces with
fresh filtered water. This flush washes the particulate collected on the electrodes into a
collection sump located in the bottom 2-ft (0.6 m) of the WESP FRP vessel. The water is
distributed from a nozzle located directly above the collector electrodes. The standard
deluge consisted of about 40 gallons of city water over 3 to 5 minutes. This deluge was
conducted daily during most of the tests. As a minimum, the manufacturer recommends
about a 10-15 minute flush following an operating campaign to prevent drying out of the
particulate on the collector surface.

 Since the WESP internal chamber is operated under a slight vacuum, it is
necessary to provide a loop seal at the liquid blow-down connection from the sump. The
WESP vessel sump has a sight glass to provide a direct indication of liquid level within
the vessel. A differential pressure gauge is used to measure the pressure drop across the
WESP.

3.1 Chronology of WESP Usage and Maintenance

 The WESP installed in the DM1200 off-gas system has been used in all DM1200
tests since the commissioning of the melter system in January 2001. The WESP has
treated exhaust from the vitrification of over a million and half pounds of feed producing
over half a million pounds of glass. A wide range of simulated HLW and LAW streams
were treated under a variety of melter conditions including high bubbling rates and
elevated melt temperatures. During the five-year testing period, melter feeds were spiked
with various compounds including hazardous organic compounds, cesium, selenium,
halides, and sulfates, which are not fully retained in the glass or removed by the SBS and
therefore present appreciable fluxes to the WESP. A complete chronology of WESP
operation, maintenance, and modification is given in Table 3.1. A summary of the testing
and modifications is provided below.

 • No processing problems over 53 days of operation with HLW AZ-101 feed
 and vendor-supplied ceramic grid supports.
 • Failure of connector at start of LAWC tests; replaced with AL6XN connector
 followed by 7-day test.
 • Changed mode of continuous 2 gal/hr spray from recirculation of sump
 solution to clean city water.
 • Unit inoperable after 2 days of LAWA tests; replaced vendor-supplied 6"
 ceramic grid supports with 8.5" Chlorinated Polyvinyl Chloride (CPVC) grid
 supports; 5-day test completed.
 • Glass-filled polytetrafluroethylene (PTFE) grid supports installed.
 • No major processing problems over 100 days of operation with various HLW
 and LAWB feeds.
 • Glass-filled PTFE grid supports replaced with Ultra High Molecular Weight

 • No major processing problems over 39 days of operation with HLW AZ-101
 feed.
 • Unit inoperable after 5 days of LAWC tests; UHMWPE grid supports found
 to have melted; replaced with chlorotrifluoroethylene (CTFE) supports
 • No major processing problems over 36 days of operation with HLW feeds
 • Difficulty in maintaining current during LAW MACT scoping test; inability to

 Since commissioning in January 2001 and during the production of over
580,000 lbs of glass from about 1.5 million lbs of feed, the WESP has performed well
except for three incidents during tests processing LAW Envelope A and C simulants (see
Section 3.3). Maintenance activities relating to the power supply, power cables, and
connectors occurred outside of the WESP vessel and therefore were not reflective of
exposure to the SBS exhaust stream. Flow blockages of either exhaust air or draining of
process fluids did not occur at any time during the tests. Inspections after each test
revealed minimal solid deposits on the WESP elements, suggesting the effectiveness of
the deluge at removing adhering solids. In one instance, the deluge routinely conducted at
the end of each test was inadvertently omitted, which resulted in a hard coating remaining
on the elements [12]. This coating was removed by recirculating a solution composed of
15 gallons of water, 1 gallon of hydrochloric acid, a half-gallon of nitric acid, 500 grams
of sodium phosphate and 250 grams of sodium pyrophosphate decahydrate. While the
specific proportions chosen were somewhat arbitrary, the general ingredients were
selected based on the use of mixtures of acids and phosphates in various metal cleaners
and rust and scale removers. While possible, it is not known if nitric acid alone would
have removed the coating.

3.2 Monitored Parameters

 The parameters monitored and recorded electronically through testing are
summarized in Table 3.2. The inlet and outlet gas temperatures, differential pressure
across the WESP, and the WESP current and voltage were measured and recorded
electronically every two minutes during all the tests. The WESP exhaust wet gas flow,
which was recorded manually during all sampling periods, was also electronically
monitored and recorded every 2 minutes beginning in July of 2002. The WESP inlet gas
temperature is determined by the manner in which the SBS is operated. SBS outlet
(WESP inlet) temperatures ranged from a target low of about 25°C in early tests to a
target high of about 50°C in latter tests. WESP outlet gas temperatures were one to two
degrees higher than the inlet gas temperatures due presumably to a combination of the
electrical power dissipated and the general increase in ambient temperature with height in
the melter room and the height of the WESP unit. In commissioning tests, the differential
was greater as a result of the lack of water spraying at the air inlet. Periodic downward
spikes in the WESP outlet temperature result from the daily deluge of the WESP. The
SBS is controlled to provide constant sump temperatures and, therefore, constant exhaust
temperatures during each test; consequently, temperature fluctuations other than those
related to the deluge were uncommon. No differences in WESP performance were
discerned as a result of the different WESP gas temperatures. The pressure drop across
the WESP was small, typically ranging between 1.5 and 2.5 in. water column (W.C.).
The WESP wet exhaust gas flow rate typically ranged between 200 and 250 scfm. The
flow rate was a function of the feed rate, required vacuum on the melter, as well as
dilution by the film cooler and control air system.

3.3 Failures and Malfunctions

 All major WESP failures occurred while processing feeds containing LAW
Envelope A and C simulants. No significant failures occurred while processing feed
containing HLW simulants or LAW Envelope B simulants. On three occasions over the
lifetime of the WESP, there was a complete inability to establish the normal electrical
operating conditions in the WESP. On two of these occasions, either the ceramic [16] or
UHMWPE grid supports [18] were found to have collapsed, causing the grid to be in
contact with the sides of the vessel. The failed support from one of these tests is shown
in Figure 3.3. On the third occasion, the degradation in electrical condition was more
gradual and associated with the deluge (see Section 3.2) [19]. Inspections after the test
showed no damage to the internal components of the WESP, including the CTFE grid
supports. Video taken during a pretest run with the same feed appeared to show corona
discharges in the vicinity of the grid supports. However, the WESP performed normally
during tests with feed containing HLW simulants conducted immediately after
completing tests with feed containing LAW simulants, indicating that there was no

mechanical fault with the unit (as also confirmed by subsequent inspections). These
observations and the association of the difficulty in restoring power after the deluge (see
Section 3.2) suggest that the issue is related to components unique to the SBS exhaust
stream generated while processing LAW Envelope A and C. It is perhaps worth noting
that, as compared to the LAW Envelope B feeds, the LAW A and C feeds, because of
their higher waste loadings, are compositionally more disparate (higher in terms of
nitrates, nitrites, halides) from the HLW feeds.

 SECTION 4.0
 WESP SOLUTIONS AND DEPOSITS

4.1 WESP Solutions

 The amount of liquid accumulated in the WESP during each test is determined by
the rate at which the inlet air is sprayed with city water and the frequency of the
40-gallon deluge that is used to clean the WESP elements. A complete list of all the
solutions removed from the WESP during each test is provided in Table 4.1. During the
first three sets of tests [5, 6, 15], both the deluge and the spraying of city water were not
used and, therefore, no net accumulation of liquids occurred. In some of these tests, sump
liquids were recirculated to the inlet exhaust spray nozzle [6, 15] and in others, there was
no inlet exhaust spraying [5, 6]. During these tests, small amounts of process fluids were
added to the WESP to maintain a minimum volume in the WESP due to evaporation or
misting. There was a one- to two-degree temperature increase in the gas flowing through
the WESP and therefore evaporation, not condensation, of liquids was likely to occur. In
fact during the initial tests [5], evaporation from the sump required the addition of
process water to maintain a constant volume. After the introduction of the inlet spray
water targeted at 2.0 ± 0.2 gph and the 40-gallon deluge, the volume of water removed
from the WESP was between zero and fifteen percent higher than the amount of water
provided by the spray and deluge [7-13, 16-19]. The additional water removed from the
WESP may be due to improved collection and/or reduced evaporation of water droplets
in the SBS exhaust stream in the WESP as a result of the inlet spray.

 The solids and chemical content of the WESP solutions are dependent on the
melter feed composition, feed processing rate, the deluge frequency, and manner in
which the inlet air is sprayed. Chemical analysis of WESP solutions from the end of
many of the tests is provided in Table 4.2. Analytical methods used include direct current
plasma – atomic emission spectroscopy (DCP-AES) for most of the elements, atomic
absorption for cesium, and ion chromatography for halides, sulfate, nitrate, and nitrite.
Also included in the table are analyses of solutions taken before and after deluges.

 The WESP solution pH values were dependent on the amount of dilution with city
water, either by spraying inlet exhaust air or deluging, and the amount of volatile
components in the feed that form acid gases. The WESP inlet air was not sprayed or
deluged with city water (see Tables 4.1 and 5.2) in early tests with HLW simulants [5, 6],
resulting in high concentrations of acidic species and low pH values (0.4 to 6). In
subsequent tests conducted with similar feeds while spraying and deluging with city
water, the sump solutions were typically neutral to mildly basic (pH: 7 to 9) [7-13].
Solutions from tests conducted with LAW simulants were acidic (pH: 0.3 to 4) [15-17],
with the lowest pH occurring for tests with feed containing the highest concentrations of

 20

constituents such as nitrates and organic carbon that form acid gases. Tests conducted
with HLW feed simulants containing high concentrations of organic carbon, selenium,
and nitrates also resulted in acidic solutions (pH: 2-5) [12, 13].

 The chemical composition and solids content of WESP solutions are controlled by
the same factors as the pH, as well as the efficiency of the upstream SBS. The SBS
removes the majority of the coarse particles, which tend to be mostly insoluble materials.
The particles remaining in the exhaust stream to be treated by the WESP are, therefore,
mostly fine and water soluble. There was a near total absence of suspended material
measured in many of the pre-deluge blow-down solutions. Higher, but still low
concentrations of suspended material were found in post-deluge solutions; this suspended
material was presumably material that was dislodged from the electrodes. Tests with very
high concentrations of dissolved solids tended to have higher suspended solids due to
precipitation. Dissolved solids concentrations in WESP solutions ranged from a few
hundred to almost thirty two thousand mg/l. The highest concentrations of measured
dissolved solids were in solutions from tests that did not use city water at the inlet spray
[5, 6, 15] and tests processing LAW simulants. Processing of HLW C-106/AY-102
simulants resulted in higher dissolved solids content in WESP solutions due to selenium
in waste and the ineffectiveness of the SBS in removing selenium from the exhaust
stream [9, 12, 13]. Volatile salts (alkali halides, borates, sulfates, and selenates) carried
over from the SBS, nitrates originating from gaseous nitrogen oxides in the exhaust
stream, and the elements in city water are the major constituents in the WESP solutions.
In tests with C-106/AY-102 simulants [9, 12, 13], selenium is the principal constituent in
the WESP blow-down solutions. Cesium was measured in all WESP solutions in all tests
processing feed that contained cesium. The observed solution chemistry supports the
expectation that the majority of the coarser, less-soluble species were removed by the
SBS leaving predominantly highly soluble species for accumulation in the WESP.

4.2 Estimates of Accumulations in WESP Fluids

 Estimates of elemental accumulations in the WESP blow-down solutions for
select tests are provided in Table 4.3. The accumulation totals are the product of the
chemical analyses and the total accumulated liquid. Single sample concentrations from
the end of each test were used for the estimates. The accumulations given, therefore, are
mostly upper estimates since the concentration values were taken near the end of tests
and the concentrations certainly increased over the course of the test segment. They do
not include any solid residue on the WESP element or sump (see Section 4.3). The
accumulations estimated from blow-down data are also compared to estimates calculated
from test average emissions data as percent of feed for select tests. Estimates of
accumulations in WESP solutions for all glass forming feed components are less than one
percent and in most samples much less than one percent. Conversely, more than one of
the volatile minor feed components such as halogens, sulfur, selenium, and arsenic were

 21

present in the WESP solutions. For all but one test, selenium and sulfate were the only
compounds to have accumulations in WESP greater than one kilogram and percent of
feed accumulations greater than seven percent. High accumulations of sodium, chlorine,
and nitrogen oxides were measured in tests with LAW Envelope A simulants [16] due in
part to high concentrations of these components in the feed. It is also of note that up to
two and half percent of feed cesium was measured in WESP solutions. Agreement
between the two methods of estimating accumulations in the WESP (analyzed solution
concentration × accumulated liquid amounts vs. feed percent calculated from emissions
data) was excellent for most constituents in most tests. Both estimation methods indicate
little feed accumulation in the WESP. Some of the calcium, fluorine, and magnesium in
the WESP solutions originated from city water used to constantly spray the WESP and
conduct the deluge, which would not be reflected in the exhaust sampling estimates. The
element with the biggest disparity between the two estimation methods is iodine. Given
the acidic nature of the WESP sump solutions, very little of the feed iodine is expected to
accumulate in the WESP sump solutions; analyses of the sump solutions support this
notion. However, the measured iodine emission rates in and out of the WESP were not
always equal. The reason for this disparity is not known but such an effect would arise if
the iodine emissions were somewhat episodic in nature because of the large differences in
the sampling durations at the WESP inlet and outlet.

4.3 WESP Deposits

 The WESP was inspected after each test and samples were collected when
sufficient solids were present inside the WESP. A list of solid samples collected and
analysis performed on these samples is provided in Table 4.4. Given the number of tests
conducted, few deposits formed on the WESP internals due presumably to the
effectiveness of the deluge in removing collected solids. At no time during testing did
solid accumulations impede the exhaust flow through the WESP or the removal or
spraying of process solutions. Each deposit collected was no more than 26 grams. The
compositional analysis of the solids indicates two different origins: iron from the
corrosion of the stainless steel WESP internals and elements removed from the exhaust
stream by the WESP. Elements from the latter origin comprise a larger portion of the
deposits and include Te, Se, alkali metals, Pb, Cd, and Cr. Both the small amount of
deposited material and the frequent inspection indicate that corrosion of the WESP
internals was minor. This maybe due in part to dilution with city water from the deluge
and inlet spray thus decreasing the acidity of the sump solutions. Continuous operation in
the recycle mode may result in higher corrosion rates, particularly while processing LAW
waste streams.

 SECTION 5.0
 WESP PARTICULATE REMOVAL PERFORMANCE

 Exhaust samples were taken at sampling points located between various off-gas
system components using 40-CFR-60 Methods 3, 5, and 29 to determine particulate
removal efficiency of these components, including the WESP. Sampling durations were
one to three hours for the SBS exhaust, whereas sampling durations of up to 36 hours
were required for the WESP exhaust due to the low particulate concentration. Teflon
filters were used to allow for analysis of all feed components. The majority of the off-gas
analyte concentrations were derived from laboratory data on solutions extracted from air
samples (filters and various solutions) together with measurements of the volume of air
sampled. The volume of air sampled and the rate at which it can be sampled are defined
in 40-CFR-60 and SW-846. Isokinetic sampling, which entails removing gas from the
exhaust at the same velocity that the air is flowing in the duct (40-CFR-60, Methods 1-5),
was used. Total particulate loading was determined by gravimetric analysis of the
standard particle filter and of probe-rinse solutions. Downstream of the particulate filter
in the sampling train are iced impingers with acidic (5% concentrated nitric acid plus
10% hydrogen peroxide) and basic (2 N sodium hydroxide) solutions. The analysis of
these solutions permits the determination of total gaseous emissions of several elements,
notably halides and sulfur. An additional dissolution procedure was required for analysis
of ruthenium in particulate samples [12]. Undissolved ruthenium particles were filtered
from the nitric/hydrofluoric acid digestate, fused with sodium hydroxide, and dissolved
with hydrochloric acid for analysis. Results from all inorganic isokinetic samples taken at
the WESP outlet are provided in Table 5.1, including test, feed type, melt pool bubbling
rate, WESP elemental emission rates, concurrent SBS emission rates, and WESP
decontamination factors (DFs). Notice the distinction that is made between constituents
sampled as particles and as "gas". The "gaseous" constituents are operationally defined as
those species that are scrubbed in the impinger solutions after the air stream has passed
through a 0.3 µm heated filter. Impinger solutions from off-gas sampling were analyzed
for all of the elements in the feed but only halides, boron, selenium, and sulfur were
detected. The amount of particulate collected on sampling filters was not sufficient to
conduct both metals and total halide analysis and, therefore, sample analyses only
account for gaseous chlorine and fluorine.

 The effectiveness of the WESP at removing particulate ranged from DF values of
about 4 up to about 300, as summarized in Table 5.2. A principal factor affecting
measured performance was the inclusion or exclusion of the WESP deluge procedure
during the exhaust sampling period. During the deluge, the power to the WESP is off and
therefore exhaust flows through the unit untreated. The time period during which the
power is off is variable depending on conditions in the WESP (see Section 3.2). The
average DF values for samples including and excluding the deluge are 26 and 110,

 23

respectively. A similar five fold increase in efficiency was observed in samples from an
individual test processing LAW Envelope B simulants taken with and without the deluge
(DFs of 9.5 vs. 51). Other factors possibly affecting WESP performance include aging of
the unit and the use of the inlet spray with city water. Comparison of the first [6] and last
[11] set of tests measuring WESP performance show a decrease in DF values from
55 - 302 to 24 - 67. In both sets of tests, sampling was not conducted during the deluge
and although both tests used HLW AZ-101 simulants, the feed used in the first set of tests
contained selenium and tellurium, which are presumably harder to remove from the
exhaust stream. One possible reason for the early excellent performance was the low pH
in sump solutions (0.3 - 6) caused in part by the lack of dilution from continuous spraying
of city water. Internal WESP components after these tests were observed to be
remarkably clean due to the acidic conditions. Highly acidic solutions in the WESP may
be more effective than the deluge used in subsequent tests for keeping the internal WESP
components clean, thus improving the performance of the WESP.

 The analyzed composition of WESP emissions (excluding oxygen, carbon, nitrate,
and nitrite) was primarily attributable to the inclusion of volatile components in the feed.
Volatiles, such as halides and selenium, constitute the vast majority of WESP emissions,
even though these elements comprise less than one percent of most feeds on an oxide
basis. For example, selenium, which constitutes only half a percent of the glass produced
from C-106/AY-102 simulants, constitutes about half to three quarters of the WESP
emissions [9, 12]. In most tests with iodine in the feed, it constituted the majority of
WESP emissions as a result of low retention in the glass and poor iodine removal in the
SBS and WESP [7-10, 17]. Emissions during many tests with HLW AZ-101 simulants
were very different from those during C-106/AY-102 tests due to the lack of several
volatile elements, mainly selenium and iodine. In these tests with HLW AZ-101 feed,
alkali metals, boron, and sulfur comprised most of the WESP emissions. Emissions from
tests with LAW simulants have higher concentrations of sodium, sulfur, and chlorine due
to higher feed concentrations of these elements. Low but measurable amounts of cesium
were observed in the particulate from all tests with LAW simulants [15-17] and one test
with HLW simulants [12]. Many of the HLW simulants did not contain cesium and
therefore the WESP removal of cesium cannot be evaluated for all waste compositions.
Impinger solutions from off-gas sampling were analyzed for all of the elements in the
feed but only halides, boron, selenium, and sulfur were detected. Much of the boron and
selenium measured in the impinger solutions was probably not in the gaseous form but as
very fine particulate that passed through the 0.3-µm heated filter.

 SECTION 6.0
 COMPARISON OF DM1200 OFF-GAS SYSTEM
 WESP TO WTP DESIGN

 The design of the WTP HLW and LAW WESPs are very similar to that of the
DM1200 WESP. Specifically, the off-gas enters near the bottom of the WESP, passes
through a baffle plate and up through the collector grid and electrodes to remove
particulate matter. The off-gas then exits near the top of the WESP (see Figures 6.1 and
6.2). Unlike the DM1200 WESP, the collector tubes for the WTP LAW and HLW
WESPs are round and there are significantly more tubes in the WTP WESPs than the
DM1200 WESP (see Figures 6.3 and 6.4). The latter is a natural consequence of the
significantly higher process flow rates in the WTP facilities. In addition, the electrode
projections in the WTP WESPs are slightly larger (1.25 inches in diameter) and are
spaced much closer together (2 inches) than in the DM1200 WESP (see Figure 6.5).
Finally, the most significant difference in the WESP designs is that the WTP electrode
assemblies are suspended by rods and ceramic insulators, which are located well away
from the off-gas flow. By utilizing high air purge rates around the insulators, it is
expected that the WTP WESP will be less subject to the type of electrode shorting and
downtime that was experienced in the DM1200 WESP during the processing of the LAW
simulants (see Section 3.3).

 Table 6.1 provides a comparison of the design parameters for the DM1200 and
the WTP WESP units. It is interesting to note that the WTP HLW WESP has nearly the
same collector area per unit off-gas flow rate and residence time as the DM1200 WESP.
Therefore, operational information obtained from the DM1200 WESP during HLW
testing will be directly applicable and scaleable to the WTP HLW WESP. However, the
collector area per unit flow rate and the residence time for the WTP LAW WESP are
approximately 50% larger than those of the DM1200 WESP, which should make the
DM1200 data conservative for the LAW WESP. As a result, this difference should be
taken into consideration when operational information from the DM1200 WESP during
LAW testing (such as the overall WESP DF and solids loading on the collector plates
over time) is used or applied to the WTP LAW WESP.

 The differences between the DM1200 and WTP WESP designs are anticipated to
alleviate many of the problems observed during DM1200 tests; however, the changes
also introduce uncertainty in predicting the performance of the WTP WESP. Thus while
the isolation of the electrical connections and the introduction of purge air in the WTP
design may well prevent the electrical problems encountered with the DM1200 WESP,
they also introduce additional uncertainties due to the lack of testing data under these
conditions. There are other differences between the units which could also contribute to
these uncertainties: The WTP HLW protocol includes a deluge from the bottom of the

 25

WESP, which has not been tested on the DM1200 WESP, and the WTP protocol includes
inlet spraying for LAW feeds but not for HLW feeds. It is also not clear how rapidly
effective power will be restored to the WTP WESP after deluging. Some of the
uncertainties could be addressed by testing on the current DM1200 WESP, including the
effect of spraying the inlet exhaust with city water. However, many of the uncertainties
can only be addressed by prolonged testing with the actual WTP WESP units or by
modifying the DM1200 WESP to include more prototypical features.

 SECTION 7.0
 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

 The WESP installed in the DM1200 off-gas system has been used in all DM1200
tests over the five-year testing period since the commissioning of the melter system. The
WESP has treated exhaust from the vitrification of over a million and half pounds of feed
producing over half a million pounds of glass. A wide range of simulated HLW and
LAW streams were treated under a variety of melter and off-gas system operating
conditions. From these tests, a large body of data has been collected on the effectiveness
of the WESP in removing particles from the exhaust stream as well as identifying
required maintenance activities and failure modes.

 The effectiveness of the WESP at removing particulate ranged from DF values of
about 4 up to about 300. A principal factor affecting measured performance was the
inclusion or exclusion of the WESP deluge procedure during the exhaust sampling
period. The average DF values for samples including and excluding the deluge are 26 and
110, respectively. Other factors possibly affecting WESP performance include aging of
the unit and the use of the inlet spray with city water. Comparison of the first (inlet spray
off or using recycled sump fluid) and last set of tests (inlet spray using city water)
measuring WESP performance show a decrease in DF values from 55 - 302 to 24 - 67.
One possible reason for the early excellent performance was the low pH of sump
solutions (0.3 - 6) caused in part by the lack of dilution from continuous spraying of city
water. Internal WESP components after these tests were observed to be remarkably clean
due to the acidic conditions. Highly acidic solutions in the WESP may be more effective
than the deluge used in subsequent tests for keeping the internal WESP components
clean, thus improving the performance of the WESP.

 Given the number of tests conducted, few deposits formed on the WESP internals
due presumably to the effectiveness of the deluge in removing collected solids. At no
time during testing did solid accumulations impede the exhaust flow through the WESP
or the removal or spraying of process solutions.

 All major WESP failures occurred while processing feeds containing LAW
Envelope A and C simulants. No significant failures occurred while processing feed
containing HLW simulants or LAW Envelope B simulants.

 Time required for restoring power after a deluge varied with feed type and inlet
spraying. In the later tests, the inlet spray was turned off at the same time the power was
turned off immediately prior to the deluge, and turned back on only after the operator
observed stable electrical conditions in the WESP. This procedure resulted in a decrease
of power-down time from an average of 38 minutes (ranging from 6 to 97 minutes) with

 27

the spray on, to an average of 10 minutes (ranging from 4 to 31 minutes) with the spray
off for tests in which HLW feed was processed.

 Restoring power to the WESP after the deluge while processing LAW feed,
however, was considerably more difficult, regardless of which procedure was used.
Power interruptions, including the time required to restore power, averaged 30 minutes
when the spray was turned off for tests with LAW feed. During the most recent LAW
tests, restoring power to the WESP often took several hours, even with additional deluges
intended to remove any residual material on the electrodes. Once power was restored, the
current and voltage were less than typically observed. By the end of the test, power could
not be restored to the unit after deluges. However, the WESP performed normally during
tests with feed containing HLW simulants conducted immediately afterwards, indicating
that there was no mechanical fault with the unit. These observations and the association
of the difficulty in restoring power after the deluge suggest that the issue is related to
components unique to the SBS exhaust stream generated while processing LAW
Envelope A and C. As compared to the LAW Envelope B feeds, these feeds are
compositionally more disparate (higher in terms of nitrates, nitrites, halides) from the
HLW feeds.

 The designs of the WTP HLW and LAW WESPs are very similar to that of the
DM1200 WESP. The WTP HLW WESP has nearly the same collector area per unit
off-gas flow rate, and residence time as the DM1200 WESP. However, the collector area
per unit flow rate, and the residence time for the WTP LAW WESP are approximately
50% larger than those of the DM1200 WESP. As a result, this difference should be taken
into consideration when operational information from the DM1200 WESP during LAW
testing is used or applied to the WTP LAW WESP.

 The most significant difference in the DM1200 and WTP WESP designs is that
the WTP electrode assemblies are suspended by rods and ceramic insulators, which are
located well away from the off-gas flow. By utilizing high air purge rates around the
insulators, it is expected that the WTP WESP will be less subject to the type of electrode
shorting and downtime that was experienced in the DM1200 WESP during the processing
of the LAW simulants. However, while the isolation of the electrical connections and the
introduction of purge air in the WTP design may well prevent the electrical problems
encountered with the DM1200 WESP, they also introduce additional uncertainties due to
the lack of testing data under these conditions. The WTP protocol includes inlet spraying
for LAW feeds but not for HLW feeds. The WTP HLW protocol also includes a deluge
from the bottom of the WESP, which has not been tested on the DM1200 WESP. It is
also not clear how rapidly effective power will be restored to the WTP WESP after
deluging.

 Further testing would be useful to determine whether the apparent degradation in
DF performance over time was due to changes in operating parameters or degradation of

the unit. Further testing would also be useful to determine the origin of the WESP failures
