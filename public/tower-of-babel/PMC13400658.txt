Radiocapitellar elbow arthroplasty—a registry study on indications, types, and short-term survival in the Netherlands
Authors: A Prkić, AA Macken, LN van Steenbergen, B The, D Eygendaal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiocapitellar arthroplasty is a treatment option for pathology of the radial side of the elbow that cannot be managed arthroscopically and for which total elbow arthroplasty is not indicated. The available literature on radiocapitellar arthroplasty is limited. The aim of this study was to evaluate the survival, indications, and surgical approaches based on data from the Dutch Arthroplasty Register.MethodsThe Dutch Arthroplasty Register contained 33 radiocapitellar arthroplasties performed between 2014 and 2024, with a median follow-up of 6.5 years (range, 1–10 years).ResultsNine arthroplasties required revision (27%), most commonly due to radial component loosening within 3 years after surgery. Post-traumatic osteoarthritis was the most common indication for radiocapitellar arthroplasty. Half of the patients (17/33) had previous surgery. A lateral approach with detachment of the lateral ulnar collateral ligament was most commonly used. The reported, estimated 10-year overall survival rate was 60%.ConclusionDespite the relatively high revision rate, radiocapitellar arthroplasty may still have a role within clinical trials with strict inclusion criteria and structured follow-up. Limited implant availability currently represents a barrier to clinical use. In all cases, the revision rate should be discussed with the patients before surgery.Keywords: Radiocapitellar arthroplasty, Elbow arthroplasty, Revision rate, Survival, Registry study, Observational study

Several treatment options exist for radiocapitellar osteoarthritis. Besides nonsurgical management, arthroscopic débridement and resection or interposition arthroplasty, prosthetic arthroplasty has emerged as an option.2,11,22 In the past 2 decades, 2 types of prosthesis have been developed and implemented on a wider scale: the UNI-Elbow (Stryker, Selzach, Switzerland) and the Lateral Resurfacing Elbow (LRE; LRE Systems, Oxford, United Kingdom).7,13,17 The UNI-Elbow is designed to replace both the radial head and the capitellum, whereas the Lateral Resurfacing Elbow is meant to be a resurfacing of the radial head and capitellum. Therefore, radial head resection is required when implanting the UNI-Elbow, and the UNI-Elbow implant may be more suitable in fracture cases, as seen in Figure 1. The Lateral Resurfacing Elbow, however, requires less bone resection, and revision of the radial component to a radial head prosthesis is still possible. Clinical studies on these implants reported satisfactory patient-reported outcomes.7,13,17 Nevertheless, these prosthesis types are currently scarcely available, largely due to the financial and regulatory burden associated with Medical Device Regulation (MDR) requirements.

Due to the rare use of the radiocapitellar arthroplasty, limited data are available in the current literature. Nevertheless, recent systematic reviews include studies varying from case reports to a series of 31 patients, with an accumulated maximum of 105 patients.13,17 Limited conclusions can be drawn from these data, and little is known about the indications and implant survival of the current implant designs. Therefore, the aim of this study is to review the survival, indications, and techniques of radiocapitellar arthroplasty in the Netherlands in a relatively large cohort by using population-based registry data. This design also circumvents bias from design centers and the developer's own series and avoids potential double-use of cohorts.

Materials and methods

Data on radiocapitellar arthroplasties were retrieved from the nationwide Dutch Arthroplasty Register (LROI).3 Data are collected using a standardized form for all elbow arthroplasties, which is completed after surgery. The register collects data on elbow arthroplasties since January 2014, and data collection is encouraged by the Dutch Orthopaedic Association (Nederlandse Orthopaedische Vereniging, NOV). Trauma surgeons are also encouraged to report to the LROI.

The database contains demographic data such as age and gender as well as procedure and implant characteristics. Primary and revision surgery are linked. Deaths are retrieved from Vektis.24 The research dataset was pseudonymized, and the cases cannot be traced to a specific patient, surgeon, or hospital. Due to privacy and traceability reasons, prosthesis type was not possible to be specified in the data. The dataset is updated every year and available in the next year for research purposes after a written application has been approved by the LROI's research council.

Given the nature of this research, institutional review board approval was waived.

Analysis

Demographic, surgical, and outcome data are presented descriptively because the sample size was too small to permit reliable statistical testing. Implant survival is estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Survival time was estimated as the time from the primary procedure up to the revision procedure, death of the patient, or end of follow-up (January 1, 2024).

Results

From the LROI, 33 radiocapitellar prostheses were identified between January 2014 and December 2024. Surgeries were performed between 2014 and 2018 and in 2022. From 2019 to 2021, no surgeries were performed due to implant availability issues. The most common indication was post-traumatic osteoarthritis (39%).

Follow-up time ranged between 1 and 10 years, with a median of 6.5 years. All surgeries were performed by orthopedic surgeons. One hospital performed 15 procedures, 5 hospitals performed 2, and 8 hospitals performed 1 procedure.

Demographic details are listed in Table I. Eleven men and 22 women were included; most radiocapitellar prostheses were implanted in patients aged 50 to 70 years (58%). More than half of the patients underwent previous surgery on the affected elbow before radiocapitellar arthroplasty, both arthrotomies and arthroscopies.

The overall 5-year revision-free implant survival is 78% (95% confidence interval: 60%-89%) and the 10-year revision-free implant survival is 59.8% (95% confidence interval: 30-84%) with 9 revisions (Fig. 2). Patients who died during follow-up were censored in the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Of the 9 revisions, 2 revisions occurred in the first year and 7 in the first 3 years of follow-up. Interestingly, 6 out of these 7 were revised due to the loosening of the radial component. A revision is registered as an event in the LROI when another implant is implanted, or during explantation.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of all radiocapitellar arthroplasties over time. In blue the 95% confidence intervals.

When stratified by patient age, 4 out of 11 arthroplasties performed in patients under 50 years were revised due to radial component loosening, one because of malalignment and one because of a periprosthetic fracture. Two prostheses were removed at revision procedure, one was partially revised and one was scored as “other revision.”

In the patient group of 50 to 59 years, 3 were revised due to radial head component loosening and one because of concurrent instability. From the patients between 60 and 69 years, 2 underwent revision surgery, with one because of radial component loosening and the other was scored as “other revision.” Above 70 years, no revisions were observed.

Concerning prosthesis types, from the 9 revisions, certainly at least 7 were UNI-Elbow arthroplasties. Due to the small patient numbers and possible traceability in the dataset, detailed analysis was neither possible nor meaningful.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first registry study on radiocapitellar arthroplasties. We report a revision rate comparable to, or higher than, that reported in previous studies, with a survival at 5 years of 78% (95% confidence interval: 60%-89%).1,4, 5, 6,9,10,15,23 The only study with a similarly high revision rate was the study performed by Spross et al,20 who studied the UNI-Elbow arthroplasty. Bigazzi et al1 reported 2 out of 7 patients with radial component loosening, which also represents a relatively high failure rate in approximately 4 years' time. In their studies, loosening of the radial component was also the most common mode of failure. In another study, Bigazzi et al5 reported better outcomes for the LRE when used when no radial head deformity was present and the UNI-Elbow when a radial head deformity was present before surgery.

Despite the high revision percentage, radiocapitellar arthroplasty may still have a role in carefully selected cases, especially in the form of a clinical trial. Trauma cases with fractures of the capitellum that are not suited for internal fixation, or cases of focal osteoarthritis, seem to be the best cohorts for inclusion.15,18 It is possible that radiocapitellar arthroplasty prevents further destruction of the joint in radiocapitellar osteoarthritis, avoiding the need for total elbow arthroplasty.14 However, of our cases with osteoarthritis as an indication for surgery, one had a removal of the prosthesis because of radial component loosening and one a revision of the radial head component. Therefore, it is mandatory to remind that the LRE is intended as a resurfacing arthroplasty in normal anatomy and the UNI-Elbow for replacement of the radial head when necessary.5

Concerning the revisions, 8 were from the hospital with the largest volume (8 revisions out of 15 prostheses in total, 53% revisions) and one from a hospital with one reported procedure. These revisions may represent part of a “prosthesis cascade,” as described by Hackl, and could potentially delay total elbow arthroplasty, which has well-known limitations in younger and more active patients.8,16 The patient-reported outcomes, however, are generally positive.7,13,17 The UNI-Elbow could be used as a revision of a failed LRE arthroplasty.6

Of the 7 revisions performed within 3 years (21%), 6 were because of radial implant loosening. This might reflect the importance of implant positioning and sizing. Initial micromotion of these short-stemmed implants could have predisposed to early loosening. This is observed in double the frequency of the radial head implant loosening, compared to isolated radial head arthroplasty in the Netherlands.12 Overstuffing, overlengthening, and malposition are more likely because a good reference of the opposite side is missing.

Infection was not reported as a cause for revision. Due to potentially incomplete reporting of removals of the implant, it might be underreported. Only revisions in which at least one of the components is replaced are accounted for in the registry, even though “removal” is an option to report as well. There were also no reported cases of polyethylene wear as a reason for revision.

Inherent to of the nature of this study, there are several limitations. As mentioned, the revision rate might be underreported as explantation alone or irrigation and débridement is not necessarily reported. Only cases in which at least one component is replaced are reported. Also, in contrast to the study of Bigazzi et al1, radiographs are not included so asymptomatic loosening is not scored in the registry. However, we see a trend similar to other studies that have followed a complete cohort. Furthermore, the arthroplasties performed before 2014 are not included in the register, as data collection on elbow implants started from January 2014, limiting the cohort size and follow-up time.

Another point to be addressed is that the availability of radiocapitellar implants is restricted, mostly due to the MDR Act. Therefore, between 2019 and 2022, no surgeries were performed. For manufacturers, this kind of niche implants seem not commercially interesting and therefore not worth the investment to make them MDR-cleared. This trend is worrisome, as innovation and niche implants are limited for those in need but also for revision surgery.21

Lastly, the number of patients is not large enough to perform meaningful detailed statistical analyses, apart from the survival analysis. Other registries do not report radiocapitellar arthroplasties in their reports, so unfortunately an international collaborative registry study is not feasible. However, the current cohort is slightly larger than the largest individual study.5 Moreover, the registry study design is a more accurate representation of clinical practice. A multinational cooperation or registration option could detect problems with implants sooner, even with relatively low numbers.19 Otherwise, (small) in vivo studies with radio stereometric analysis could provide information on implant migration, subsidence, and loosening more correctly.

Conclusion

In this retrospective observational registry cohort study of radiocapitellar arthroplasties, a high revision percentage (27%) was reported after 10 years. It is the first national, non-developer-based real-world assessment of survivorship, in contrast to the predominantly single-center observational evidence currently available.

This is mainly due to radial component loosening within three years. The most common indication was post-traumatic osteoarthritis (39%), and the most common surgical approach was the lateral approach with detachment of the lateral ulnar collateral ligament. In selected patients, most preferably in a clinical trial with clear inclusion criteria and follow-up, the radiocapitellar arthroplasty may be considered as a treatment option in the treatment of radiocapitellar osteoarthritis or as a possibility to postpone a total elbow arthroplasty. However, availability of the implants is an issue.
