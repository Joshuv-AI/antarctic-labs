Protective efficacy of Lactobacillus plantarum BS22 against Eimeria tenella and Eimeria acervulina co-infection in broiler chickens
Authors: Xinhui Zhang, Xuan Zhou, Lidan Wang, Xiao Tan, Yan Zeng, Yue Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAvian coccidiosis, caused by Eimeria spp., is a major constraint on global poultry production, and mixed-species infections—particularly Eimeria tenella and Eimeria acervulina—are common and more pathogenic in the broiler breeding. Rising concerns over anticoccidial drug resistance and residues have stimulated interest in probiotics as alternative or complementary control strategies. This study evaluated the protective efficacy of Lactobacillus plantarum BS22 (LP-BS22), a chicken-derived strain with confirmed in vitro antimicrobial activity and stability, against this clinically prevalent mixed infection. Seventy-two broiler chickens were randomly assigned to four groups: uninfected control (CG), probiotic control (LP-BS22), co-infected model (DIG), and probiotic-treated co-infected group (LP-BS22+DIG). Efficacy was assessed via growth performance, oocyst output, lesion scoring, and histopathology. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, the expressions of intestinal tight junction (TJ)-related zonula occluden-1 (ZO-1) and claudin 5 (CLDN5) proteins as well as pro-inflammatory (interleukin [IL]-6) and regulatory (IL-10 and IL-22) cytokines were also quantified. The results demonstrated that prophylactic administration of LP-BS22 significantly mitigated growth retardation, reduced fecal oocyst shedding, and lowered intestinal lesion scores compared to the untreated DIG group (P < 0.05). Histologically, the probiotic treatment alleviated intestinal villus atrophy and crypt hyperplasia. Mechanistically, LP-BS22 preserved intestinal barrier integrity by preventing the downregulation of ZO-1 and CLDN5; furthermore, the probiotic modulated the immune response by suppressing pro-inflammatory IL-6 while upregulating regulatory IL-10 and the tissue-repair cytokine IL-22 (P < 0.05). The calculated anticoccidial index (ACI) ranged from 126.76 to 133.46, classifying the efficacy as moderate. These findings indicated that LP-BS22 conferred protection against mixed Eimeria infections by enhancing mucosal barrier function and regulating inflammation, positioning it as a promising sustainable strategy for coccidiosis control in broiler production.Keywords: Coccidiosis, Probiotics, Intestinal barrier, Cytokines, Anticoccidial performance

Introduction

Avian coccidiosis, an enteric infection induced by protozoa of the genus Eimeria, represents a critical challenge to the sustainability of the global poultry industry (Pal et al., 2024; El-Saadony et al., 2025; Mathis et al., 2025). This disease is characterized by enteritis, malabsorption, and potential mortality, culminating in devastating global economic deficits estimated at over £12.9 billion annually (Blake, 2025). While there are multiple species of Eimeria responsible for chicken coccidiosis, Eimeria tenella and Eimeria acervulina are of particular clinical importance due to different pathogenic characteristics and economic consequences (Zhou et al., 2023; Badri et al., 2024; Qiao et al., 2024; Bafundo et al., 2025). Specifically, the highly virulent E. tenella triggers profound cecal tissue damage and hemorrhagic diarrhea, whereas E. acervulina, though less pathogenic, primarily affects the small intestine and severely compromises feed conversion (Blake et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024). Because the apicomplexan life cycle—comprising merogony, gametogony, and sporogony—facilitates rapid oral-fecal transmission, prevalence of chicken coccidiosis is exacerbated by intensive rearing conditions (Mesa-Pineda et al., 2021; Ahmad et al., 2023).

Epidemiological surveillance underscores the ubiquity of chicken coccidiosis, with an overall global infection rates estimated at 44.3%, though prevalence varies by regions (e.g., 80.5% in the Western Pacific versus 30.8% in South-East Asia) (Badri et al., 2024). Crucially, a recent survey in China covering 15 provinces revealed an infection rate of 86.1%, with multi-species infections being the norm. The most prevalent combination identified was E. tenella co-infecting with E. acervulina (39.0%) (Liao et al., 2024). Despite the prevalence of this specific mixed infection, current prevention and control measures remain problematic. Usually, prevention relies on vaccination, but the live or attenuated vaccines suffer from unstable immunogenicity, residual virulence, and strain specificity (Zaheer et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Liao et al., 2024). Furthermore, the therapeutic reliance on anticoccidial drugs has also led to widespread drug resistance, residue accumulation in meat products, and environmental contamination (Chapman and Rathinam, 2022; Kandeel et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Consequently, there is an urgent need for alternative strategies that are both sustainable and effective against mixed infections.

Probiotics, particularly those isolated from indigenous chicken gut flora, represent a promising alternative (da Silva et al., 2024; El-Saadony et al., 2025). Strains capable of modulating the mucosal immune system and maintaining microbial homeostasis have shown efficacy against enteric pathogens, including Escherichia, Salmonella, Campylobacter, Clostridium and even Eimeria (da Silva et al., 2024; El-Shall et al., 2024). Specifically, Lactobacillus plantarum has been demonstrated significant potential in avian health by enhancing intestinal barrier function and competitively excluding pathogens (An et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024, 2025). More importantly, increased studies have highlighted the anticoccidial potential of this species; for instance, L. plantarum CMU-FP002 can alleviate caecal coccidiosis induced by E. tenella in broilers (Kasornpikul et al., 2009); L. plantarum strains P8 and HW1 are able to improve growth performance by reducing oocyst shedding in Eimeria-challenged broilers models (Wang et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023). Furthermore, several studies employing recombinant L. plantarum strains expressing Eimeria antigens also show their efficient protections against Eimeria challenges by activating more stronger host humoral, mucosal, and T cell-mediated immune responses (Liu et al., 2020a; Liu et al., 2020b).

However, the efficacy of probiotics is highly strain-specific (Jankiewicz et al., 2023; Rahmannia et al., 2024). Our research group recently isolated a novel strain, L. plantarum BS22 (LP-BS22; CCTCC: M 2013010), from the chicken intestine. We previously demonstrated that LP-BS22 possesses superior tolerance to bile acid and low pH, and can restore microbial homeostasis in broilers exposed to aflatoxin B1 (Zeng et al., 2018). Despite these promising robust characteristics, information regarding the therapeutic efficacy of LP-BS22 against coccidiosis remains limited. Crucially, there is a scarcity of data regarding the efficacy of probiotics under the clinically relevant condition of concurrent E. tenella and E. acervulina co-infection. To address this gap, the present study was designed to investigate the anticoccidial potential and therapeutic benefits of LP-BS22 in broiler chickens subjected to a dual-species challenge.

Materials and methods

Animals

Ninety-two one-day-old Arbor Acres (AA) broilers, selected for uniform initial body weights, were sourced from Sichuan Daheng Poultry Breeding Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China). These chickens were housed in sterilized stainless steel cages equipped with wire-mesh floors specifically designed to prevent re-infection through oocyst recycling (Mohsin et al., 2025). All facilities were maintained under stringently sanitized and coccidia-free conditions at suitable temperature and humidity levels under a regulated 12-h light/dark photoperiod (Ke et al., 2025). The chickens were provided with feed and water ad libitum. The basal diets were strictly devoid of any antibiotic or anticoccidial additives. Prior to the initiation of experimental interventions, all chickens underwent a 7-day acclimation phase.

Preparation of parasites

Sporulated oocysts of E. tenella (XJ strain) and E. acervulina (BJ strain) were obtained from the National Animal Protozoa Laboratory, China Agricultural University (Beijing, China) and were amplified in 20 AA broilers (n = 10 per species) maintained at another separated facility under 22 ± 2 °C and 60% relative humidity as described elsewhere (Li et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023; Ke et al., 2025). In brief, each chicken was orally inoculated with either 1,000 E. tenella or 5,000 E. acervulina oocysts. Fecal samples were subsequently gathered during the respective shedding periods: 6-9 post-infection (dpi) for the E. tenella group and 5-7 dpi for E. acervulina group. Oocysts were isolated and purified from feces using a saturated NaCl flotation. Following fecal homogenization and mixture with saturated NaCl solution, the resulting suspension was filtered sequentially through 40- and 100-mesh sieves. Then, the supernatant was collected and washed three times with distilled water (ddH2O) through centrifugation at 1,500 g for 10 min to remove residual salt and precipitate the oocysts. Fresh oocysts were incubated in 2.5% (w/v) potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) at 27°C for 24-48 h to induce sporulation. Finally, the sporulated oocysts were treated with sodium hypochlorite for purification, counted using the McMaster technique as described elsewhere (Yadav et al., 2020), and stored in a solution of 2.5% K₂Cr₂O₇ at 4°C until required.

Preparation of LP-BS22

The strain LP-BS22, originally isolated from the intestine of healthy broiler chickens, was initially identified via 16S rRNA gene sequencing and morphological assessment following cultivation on de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) agar. A phylogenetic tree was constructed utilizing the neighbor-joining (NJ) algorithm and validated by 1,000 bootstrap replicates. To characterize its growth dynamics, the purified LP-BS22 was incubated in MRS broth at 37°C for 24 h, with optical density measurements (OD600) taken at 2 h intervals to plot the growth curve.

The antagonistic activity of LP-BS22 was evaluated against Escherichia coli ATCC 11775 and Salmonella choleraesuis ATCC 13312. Both indicator pathogens were activated in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth and spread on MRS agar plates at final concentrations of 10⁵ or 10⁶ CFU/mL. LP-BS22 was subsequently co-inoculated at matching cell densities. Following a 24-h incubation at 37°C, antagonistic efficacy was assessed by recording the growth inhibition of the indicator strains. All trials were conducted in triplicate. To prepare the strain for experimental application, bacterial cultures were harvested by centrifugation (4,000 rpm, 4°C, 10 min) and the resulting pellets were washed twice using sterile physiological saline. The collected cells were then mixed 1:1 with a trehalose-based cryoprotectant solution (5% glycerol, 10% skimmed milk powder, 10% trehalose), frozen at −20°C overnight, and lyophilized under vacuum for 24 h. The final lyophilized product was preserved at −80°C and applied in experiments within one week. Viability was confirmed by culture in MRS broth prior to gavage.

Experimental design

Following environmental acclimation, 72 chickens were randomized into four distinct groups (n = 18 per group). Treatments were administered via gavage as follows: (1) the control group (CG) received 1 mL of PBS; (2) the dual infection group (DIG) received 1 mL of PBS containing a 1:1 mixture of E. tenella (2.5 × 10⁴) and E. acervulina (2.5 × 10⁴) sporulated oocysts as described elsewhere (Jenkins et al., 2009; Mustafa et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023); (3) the LP-BS22 control group (LP-BS22+CG) received 1 mL of PBS containing lyophilized LP-BS22 at a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/g; and (4) the LP-BS22 dual infection group (LP-BS22+DIG) received the same LP-BS22 dosage (1 × 10⁸ CFU/g) followed by the Eimeria challenge described for the DIG group. Daily administration of LP-BS22 to one-day-old AA began at the start of the acclimation period. After one 7-day continuously administration of LP-BS22, the chickens were subject to Eimeria challenge. During the procedure, eighteen chickens in each group were subdivided into three cages (n = 6 per cage) and assigned to three distinct sampling time points at 0, 5, and 10 dpi for sample collection and performance assessment. The oocyst excretions were assessed by collecting daily feces after challenge. Briefly, the feces were sampled individually from the chickens ranging from 0 to 10 dpi (Table S1), and fecal oocyst excretion per chicken was determined using the McMaster method and expressed as oocysts per gram (OPG). Performance was evaluated by monitoring body weight (BW) and calculating the viscera index (VI), defined as visceral weight divided by body weight (Zhang et al., 2016). At the same time, the duodenum and cecum were also removed to measure intestinal length and to score macroscopic lesions according to Johnson and Reid (Johnson and Reid, 1970). To ensure anatomical consistency, the intestinal samples were excised from the same defined locations: the midpoint of the duodenal loop and the middle section of the ceca, regardless of the severity of gross lesions. Tissue samples from these intestinal regions were also either fixed in paraformaldehyde for histopathological examination or immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent analysis of tight junction (TJ)-related proteins and inflammatory cytokines. H&E staining

After rinsing the fixed duodenal and cecal tissues with ddH2O, these samples were dehydrated in ethanol, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Tissue slices at 4 µm thickness were prepared, mounted onto glass slides, and deparaffinized in xylene, followed by rehydration in gradient ethanol and staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Morphological evaluations were conducted utilizing an Olympus light microscope integrated with Olympus MicroSuite Imaging software (Center Valley, PA, USA). The measurement of villus length (V), crypt depth (C), villus length/crypt depth (V/C) ratio, and intestinal thickness were performed by the CaseViewer system (3D HISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). A total of ten intact, well-oriented villi coupled with their crypts per slide were quantified to calculate the V/C values as described previously (Sallam et al., 2021; Alagbe et al., 2023).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA isolation was performed on duodenal and cecal tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, approximately 25 mg of tissue samples were homogenized in 500 µL of TRIzol, followed by phase separation via chloroform addition and centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The RNA in the upper aqueous phase was then precipitated with isopropanol, washed with 75% ethanol, and dissolved in RNase-free water. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized utilizing the PrimeScriptTM RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa) on a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Following template quantification, the mRNA abundance of tight junction proteins (zonula occluden-1 [ZO-1] and claudin 5 [CLDN5]) and inflammatory cytokines (interleukin (IL)-6 [IL-6], IL-10, and IL-22) was assessed via qRT-PCR using the SYBR Green qPCR Kit (Accurate Biotech, Changsha, China). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, NM_204305) served as the internal control to normalize relative gene abundance (Zhou et al., 2023). Specific primer sequences were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software (Premier Biosoft, Palo Alto, CA), synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and provided in Table 1. The reaction mixtures were prepared in a 20 µL total volume comprising 10 µL of 2 × PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix (Accurate Biotech), 2 µL of cDNA, 0.4 µL of both forward and reverse primers (10µ M each), and 7.2 µL of nuclease-free water. The amplification protocol was initiated with a 30 s activation phase at 94°C. This was succeeded by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 5 s and a combined annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). To ensure analytical precision, assays were performed in technical triplicates. The relative transcription levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method (Livak et al., 2001).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Duodenal and cecal samples were processed by cryogenic grinding followed by homogenization in ice-cold T-PER tissue protein extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Following extraction, the Coomassie Plus Protein Assay Reagent Kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA) was applied to quantify the total protein content of the lysates. The concentrations of TJ-related proteins (ZO-1, CLDN5) and cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, IL-22) were then evaluated using commercial chicken-specific ELISA kits (Anruikebio, Chengdu, China) strictly according to the supplier’s protocols. Standard curves were generated by plotting OD at 450 nm versus known standard concentrations using a SpectraMax M2 plate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), and the final concentrations of the target proteins were extrapolated from the generated standard curves.

Anti-coccidial index (ACI)

To assess the anticoccidial efficacy of LP-BS22, the anti-coccidial index (ACI) was computed for all treatment groups. Using the equation described by Wang et al. (2025a), the ACI was determined as: ACI = (Survival rate + relative weight gain rate) - (oocyst value + lesion score). The resulting scores were categorized according to the scale reported by Kim et al. (2024), defining efficacy as inactive (<120), moderate (120–160), good (160–180), or excellent (>180).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with results expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). For multiple group comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.

Results

Biological characteristics of LP-BS22

The LP-BS22 isolate was identified as L. plantarum based on its morphological and molecular characteristics. Colonies on MRS agar were medium-sized, raised, milky white, and circular with regular edges, and the cells were Gram-positive, rod-shaped, and arranged singly or in chains (Fig. 2A). 16S rRNA sequencing (1,458 bp) confirmed that this isolate shared the highest sequence similarity to reference L. plantarum strains and clustered LP-BS22 within the L. plantarum clade (Fig. 2B). Growth kinetics analysis revealed that LP-BS22 entered the logarithmic growth phase at 4 h post-inoculation, reached the stationary phase at 16 h, and maintained stability without a decline phase across the 48-h culture period (Fig. 2C). Crucially, the antimicrobial assay demonstrated that LP-BS22 exerted significant inhibitory effects against pathogenic E. coli and S. choleraesuis at concentrations of 1 × 10⁵ and 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL, respectively, with a more pronounced antagonism observed against S. choleraesuis (Fig. 2D).

The effect of LP-BS22 on clinical symptom changes

To evaluate the clinical protection conferred by LP-BS22, we monitored BW, VI, and oocyst outputs in chickens co-infected with E. tenella and E. acervulina. As shown in Table 2, DIG suffered severe growth retardation. At 5 dpi, DIG exhibited an average weight gain rate and a relative weight gain rate of only 46.74% and 49.98% of CG, respectively. In contrast, LP-BS22 treatment largely attenuated this growth depression, and the BW of LP-BS22+DIG did not differ significantly from that of CG at 5 dpi (P > 0.05), and its average weight gain rate and relative weight gain rate reached 86.28% and 93.43%, respectively. Notably, LP-BS22 administration in uninfected chickens (LP-BS22+CG) achieved the highest average weight gain rate (98.00%) and relative weight gain rate (107.70%), confirming the inherent growth-promoting potential of the strain. Similar trends in BW and weight gain indices were observed at 10 dpi (Table 2).

Consistent with the observed growth trends, the impact of LP-BS22 on the VI is detailed in Table 3. The DIG exhibited significantly elevated indices for the heart, liver, and spleen compared to the uninfected CG (P < 0.05). These findings reflect substantial organ stress and pathological hypertrophy induced by the co-infection. Probiotic administration reversed this effect, as the VI in LP-BS22+DIG was comparable to that of CG and even that of LP-BS22+CG (Table 3). Besides, duodenal and cecal lengths were significantly reduced in both infected groups (DIG and LP‑BS22+DIG) compared with CG and LP‑BS22+CG (P < 0.05), but shortening was more severe in DIG than in LP‑BS22+DIG (Table 3), supporting protective effect of LP‑BS22 on intestinal integrity.

The effect of LP-BS22 on intestinal lesions

Given that E. acervulina and E. tenella predominantly infect the duodenum and cecum (Weng et al., 2024), gross lesions in these segments were examined (Fig. 4A) and quantified (Table 4). At 5 dpi, DIG displayed classic white, step-like lesions in the duodenum and severe hemorrhage in the cecum. By 10 dpi, irregular cecal cores and thickened walls were prevalent in chickens of DIG. Quantitatively, in the DIG, the duodenal lesion scores were 3.33±0.26 and 2.17±0.21 at 5 and 10 dpi, respectively (corresponding lesion values 33.3 and 21.7), and cecal lesion scores of DIG were 3.83±0.21 and 2.50±0.28 (lesion values 38.3 and 25.0). However, LP-BS22 treatment significantly attenuated this pathology. LP-BS22+DIG presented only minor punctate lesions and occasional mild bleeding, resulting in significantly lower lesion values in the duodenum (26.7 and 15.0) and cecum (28.3 and 21.7) compared to DIG (P < 0.05) at 5 and 10 dpi (Table 4). No lesions were observed in CG or LP-BS22+CG at any time point. Combined, these results confirmed that LP-BS22 significantly attenuated intestinal damage triggered by the concurrent infection of E. tenella and E. acervulina co-infection in broilers.

Microscopic analysis (H&E) corroborated the macroscopic findings (Fig. 4B). At 5 dpi, the DIG group exhibited severe mucosal disruption in both duodenum and cecum. These segments were characterized by extensive epithelial desquamation, enterocyte shedding, and pronounced lymphocytic infiltration within the lamina propria. High-magnification assessment revealed that the mucosal layers were heavily parasitized by multiple developmental stages of Eimeria: numerous immature schizonts were embedded within the epithelium, accompanied by clusters of mature macrogamonts and microgamonts, along with newly formed unsporulated oocysts. This massive intracellular replication resulted in severe mucosal erosion and localized hemorrhage, particularly in the cecum. By 10 dpi, DIG progressed toward chronic pathological remodeling; for instance, the duodenal lesions featured marked crypt hyperplasia and cystic dilatation, with residual unsporulated oocysts lodged in the mucosa, while the cecal tissue showed persistent inflammatory infiltration and compensatory thickening of the muscularis mucosae and intestinal wall. In contrast, LP-BS22+DIG showed significant mucosal restoration, characterized by relatively continuous epithelial layers and successfully restricted parasite proliferation. In addition, morphometric analysis also confirmed that LP-BS22 treatment preserved the duodenal villus height/crypt depth ratio and prevented the pathological thickening of the cecal mucosa observed in DIG (P < 0.05; Fig. 4C-F), further supporting the protective role of LP‑BS22 on intestinal morphology and barrier structure.

The effect of LP-BS22 on TJ-related protein genes

To understand the mechanism underlying mucosal protection, we chosen and analyzed the expressions of two representative TJ-associated proteins, ZO-1 a cytoplasmic plaque protein, and CLDN5, a primary transmembrane protein essential for paracellular barrier integrity (Zhou et al., 2023). In DIG, mRNA expression of these markers in the duodenum and cecum was diminished at 5 and 10 dpi. Notably, ZO-1 and CLDN5 both demonstrated a more substantial and statistically significant downregulation compared to both control cohorts (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A,B). Conversely, LP-BS22 administration effectively prevented such declines. In LP-BS22+DIG, ZO-1 mRNA expression levels in duodenum were similar to that of CG and LP-BS22+CG at both time points, and CLDN5 expression also approached CG levels by 10 dpi (P > 0.05; Fig. 5A,B). These transcriptional changes were mirrored at their protein levels as well. ELISA measurements demonstrated that ZO-1 and CLDN5 protein contents were decreased in DIG, regardless duodenum or cecum, compared with CG and LP-BS22+CG, whereas LP-BS22+DIG maintained higher levels than DIG and were comparable to controls, particularly in the cecum (Fig. 5C,D). Together, these results indicated that LP-BS22 mitigated both Eimeria-induced disruption of tight junction components and helped maintain intestinal barrier integrity.

The effect of LP-BS22 on inflammation-related cytokines

To further determine whether the protective effect of LP‑BS22 was also associated with modulation of intestinal inflammatory responses, we quantified the expressions of the pro-inflammatory (IL-6) and regulatory (IL-10 and IL-22) cytokines. For pro-inflammatory responses, as shown in Fig. 6A, DIG exhibited an upregulated IL-6 expression, particularly in the cecum at 10 dpi (P < 0.05). However, LP-BS22 treatment significantly dampened this response and maintained IL-6 levels comparable to two control groups (P < 0.05). Within regulatory responses, the expressions of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 and tissue-repair cytokine IL-22 were markedly elevated in the duodenum of both infected groups (DIG and LP-BS22+DIG) (P < 0.05) but their expressions in the cecum were similar to those of CG and LP-BS22+CG (Fig. 6B,C). Additionally, LP-BS22+DIG exhibited sustained or higher expressions of IL-22 compared to DIG, particularly at earlier time points. Such trends were consistent across mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6D-F), suggesting that LP-BS22 might limit excessive inflammation (e.g., IL-6) while potentially support tissue repair pathways (e.g., IL-22).

Anti-coccidial index of LP-BS22

Finally, the comprehensive therapeutic efficacy was evaluated using the ACI, which integrated survival, weight gain, lesion scores, and oocyst output (Table 5). Using duodenal lesions as the reference, LP-BS22+DIG achieved ACI values of 130.03 (5 dpi) and 133.46 (10 dpi). Similarly, using cecal lesions as the reference, the ACI values were 128.43 and 126.76, respectively. All calculated ACI values fell within the range of 120 ≤ ACI < 160 (Table 5), classifying the therapeutic effect of LP-BS22 against mixed E. tenella and E. acervulina infection as “moderate” protection, in agreement with the aforementioned improvements in growth performance, reduction in oocyst shedding, alleviation of intestinal lesions and preservation of barrier and immune parameters.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that LP-BS22, a strain isolated from the chicken intestine, conferred significant protection against the clinically relevant co-infection of E. tenella and E. acervulina in broiler chickens. Although current single-species infections are often studied, co-infection is the norm in field conditions and typically results in exacerbated pathology (Zhou et al., 2023; Badri et al., 2024; Blake, 2025). Our results indicated that prophylactic administration of LP-BS22 mitigated growth retardation, reduced oocyst shedding, and alleviated intestinal lesions. Mechanistically, these protective effects appeared to be mediated by the maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity (e.g., ZO-1, CLDN5) and the modulation of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-10, and IL-22) toward a reparative profile.

The efficacy of a probiotic is fundamentally linked to its ability to survive and colonize the host gut (Byakika et al., 2019). Unlike many commercial strains derived from dairy products, LP-BS22 is host-specific (chicken-derived), which likely enhances its colonization potential, as described in the congeneric L. johnsonii (Johnson et al., 2023). Our in vitro characterization confirmed that LP-BS22 possesses high stability (no decline phase over 48 h) and strong antagonistic activity against enteric pathogens like S. choleraesuis and E. coli. This aligns with findings by Zeng et al., who suggested that LP-BS22 promotes microbial homeostasis via competitive exclusion (Zeng et al., 2018). We postulate that the robust growth kinetics of LP-BS22 allow it to occupy mucosal niches prior to Eimeria challenge, thereby establishing a “defensive line” that limits parasitic invasion. Interestingly, this “defensive line” hypothesis has been supported by recent studies on probiotic Lactobacillus species. For example, Yang et al. (2024) demonstrated that a pre-emptive administration of L. plantarum can modify mucosal niches by create a lower pH environment that restricts the proliferation of acid-sensitive pathobionts, thereby preventing dysbiosis-induced mucosal inflammation and oxidative stress. Because Eimeria parasites require a disrupted, inflamed gut environment (often referred to as a “pathological ecological niche”) for optimal asexual replication, the suppression of these pathobionts could effectively hinder schizogony and subsequent tissue damage (Yang et al., 2024; Mohsin et al., 2025). Additionally, L. plantarum may occupy mucosal attachment sites, physically hindering Eimeria sporozoite invasion into epithelial cells following challenge (Ritzi et al., 2014).

Clinically, the most economically significant result in the poultry breeding was the preservation of growth performance (Korver et al., 2023). Co-infection with E. tenella and E. acervulina typically causes severe weight loss due to malabsorption and anorexia. However, the LP-BS22-treated group (LP-BS22+DIG) maintained body weight comparable to the uninfected control group (CG). This observation is consistent with the findings of Wang et al. (2021) and Chen et al. (2022), who demonstrated that Lactobacillus supplementation enhances feed conversion efficiency and nutrient bioavailability, even in the presence of pathogenic stress. Furthermore, we observed a significant reduction in oocyst output and a one-day delay in peak excretion in the LP-BS22-treated group. Similar oocyst dynamics have been documented in other Eimeria challenge models following Lactobacillus administration or intervention (Behnamifar et al., 2019; Gaboriaud et al., 2021; Gong et al., 2022; Mohsin et al., 2022). For instance, Wang et al. (2025b) reported that oral administration of L. plantarum significantly reduced oocyst shedding in mice infected with E. falciformis. Furthermore, several studies have indicated that the assembly of the host intestinal microbiota likewise influences the shedding and peak excretion timing of Eimeria oocysts. For example, Gaboriaud et al. (2021) demonstrated that germ-free broilers exhibited a 3.5-day delay in the maturation of second-generation schizonts and subsequent gametocyte formation, which consequently led to a significantly reduced oocyst output following E. tenella challenge. Similarly, a one-day delay in peak oocyst shedding was observed in mice with antibiotic-mediated depletion of gut microbiota after E. falciformis infection. These findings, to some extent, support the premise that a Lactobacillus-modulated gut microbiota can suppress Eimeria burdens in the host. Despite the fact that the LP-BS22 treatment fell short of absolute sterilization, its capacity to limit parasitic fecundity remains a critical finding. Consistent with the results reported by Liu et al., 2020a, Liu et al., 2020b, such suppression is vital for mitigating the risk of environmental contamination and horizontal transmission in poultry houses.

At the tissue level, LP-BS22 treatment significantly reduced the severity of gross lesions and histopathological damages, such as villus atrophy and hemorrhage. The underlying mechanism for this tissue protection appears to be twofold: barrier reinforcement and immune modulation. Firstly, our data showed that LP-BS22 prevented the downregulation of tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and CLDN5) observed in untreated infected chickens (DIG). Since Eimeria invasion compromises the paracellular seal, leading to “leaky gut” and secondary bacterial translocation (Pham et al., 2021), the maintenance of these proteins suggests that LP-BS22 physically strengthens the epithelial barrier. It is speculated that organic acids (e.g., lactic acid) secreted by the probiotic may stimulate epithelial proliferation and tight junction assembly (Gao et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2023), though metabolomic verification is required to confirm this hypothesis. Secondly, LP-BS22 exerted a distinct immunoregulatory effect. Eimeria infections typically trigger an intense inflammatory response, characterized by high IL-6 levels, which contributes to tissue damage (Zhou et al., 2023). We observed that LP-BS22 significantly suppressing the pro-inflammatory mediator IL-6, while concurrently enhancing the production of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 and the tissue-repair cytokine IL-22. IL-22 is critical for mucosal healing and epithelial regeneration (Wei et al., 2020; Villablanca et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2026). The shift toward an IL-10/IL-22 dominant profile suggested that LP-BS22 limited immunopathology, allowing the host to tolerate the infection while repairing tissue damage. Interestingly, this regulatory pattern, characterized by the activation of the IL-10/IL-22 axis, closely aligns with the host immunoregulatory responses reported in E. maxima and E. falciformis infection models (Sun et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025b). Thus, this protective cytokine profile may represent a conserved mechanism, potentially triggered by host-microbe signaling pathways during the mucosal colonization of L. plantarum (Cui et al., 2024). Future research aimed at evaluating the downstream molecules of this host-microbe signaling pathway will help elucidate the regulatory network of LP-BS22 against E. tenella and E. acervulina co-infection in chickens.

The overall therapeutic potential was summarized by the ACI. The ACI for LP-BS22 ranged from 126.76 to 133.46, classifying it as “moderately effective”. While this is lower than the ACI reported for some recombinant L. plantarum expressing Eimeria antigens (e.g., RON2 and MIC2) (Liu et al., 2020a; Zhang et al., 2024), it must be noted that our study utilized a severe dual-species challenge model, which induced greater pathology than single infections. Moreover, the advantages of LP-BS22 lie in its operational practicality: unlike live vaccines, it requires no cold chain and poses no risk of residual virulence. The fact that LP-BS22 promoted growth in uninfected chickens (relative weight gain >100%) further positions it as a viable dual-purpose feed additive—acting as a growth promoter in healthy flocks and a therapeutic buffer during outbreaks.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the experimental infections involved only E. tenella and E. acervulina under controlled laboratory conditions. Field coccidiosis often involves additional Eimeria species (e.g. E. maxima or E. necatrix) and concurrent bacterial or fungal pathogens (Weng et al., 2024; Mousa et al., 2024), which may influence LP‑BS22 efficacy. Second, while we measured tight junction markers and key cytokines, we did not directly assess changes in the intestinal microbiota or LP‑BS22 colonization, nor did we identify the metabolites or host signaling pathways (e.g., ERK or NF-κB pathways; c.f., Zhu et al., 2023; Zang et al., 2024) responsible for the observed effects. Third, the study design primarily addressed prophylactic/early-intervention use (LP‑BS22 administration began before infection), and its efficacy when started after the onset of clinical coccidiosis remains to be determined. Future research should therefore include (i) evaluation of LP‑BS22 in more complex multi-species and naturally acquired infections, ideally under field conditions; (ii) characterization of its impact on the intestinal microbiome and metabolome; (iii) elucidation of the signaling pathways linking LP‑BS22 to tight junction regulation and cytokine modulation; and (iv) assessment of its potential use in combination with reduced anticoccidial drug doses or vaccines.

In summary, this study confirmed that the chicken-derived probiotic LP‑BS22 confers effective but biologically relevant protection against experimental co-infection with the most prevalent E. tenella and E. acervulina in broilers. LP‑BS22 improved growth performance, reduced oocyst shedding and intestinal lesions, preserved tight junction protein expression and shifted local cytokine responses toward a less pro-inflammatory, more reparative profile. These attributes make LP-BS22 a promising probiotic candidate for the development of sustainable, antibiotic-freeinterventions to control avian coccidiosis.
