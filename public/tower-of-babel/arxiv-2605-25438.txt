Coding Beyond Your Training: Claude Code and the Technological Frontier of Software Developers Thanks: Preliminary draft, May 2026. Comments welcome.
Alexander Quispe

Abstract
We study whether adoption of an AI coding assistant causally expands the technological frontier of individual software developers. We exploit the staggered rollout of Claude Code across GitHub between May 2025 and January 2026 in a panel of 5,838 developers observed monthly over 28 months, with treatment defined by the developer’s first Claude-co-authored commit and not-yet-treated developers as controls. Using the doubly robust 12 estimator, we find positive and significant effects on monthly commits ( + + 41), repositories contributed to ( + + 1.5), distinct programming languages used ( + + 0.83), Shannon language entropy ( + + 0.14), newly-used languages ( + + 0.31), and cumulative lifetime languages ( + + 0.51). The cumulative-languages effect grows with time since adoption, matching a Bayesian-learning model in which AI provides free signals about unfamiliar technologies and lowers the switching barrier. Results are robust to two stricter activity filters. The estimates document a sharp, persistent shift in developer behavior coincident with AI adoption; identification limits prevent a strict causal claim and we outline an agenda for cleaner tests.

Introduction

A skilled Python developer rarely writes production Rust. A
data scientist trained on R and SQL rarely ships an iOS application
in Swift. The boundaries of an individual’s programming-language
portfolio are sticky, set by the human-capital investments made
during training and early career, and difficult to cross even when the
underlying problem-solving ability would transfer. The same observation
extends to industry sectors: a developer specialized in fintech
rarely ships a healthcare application, regardless of how transferable
her general engineering skills might be. These boundaries are
plausibly costly: they prevent skilled individuals from contributing
to projects that need their general abilities but happen to be coded
in an unfamiliar language or oriented to an unfamiliar domain.

Generative AI coding assistants such as GitHub Copilot, Cursor, and
Claude Code may dissolve some of these boundaries. The assistants
generate code, explain syntax, and debug errors in any language the
underlying model has seen during training. From the user’s perspective,
this provides a form of
skill substitution
: where she once
needed deep proficiency to write Rust, she can now produce working
Rust by describing what she wants. If the substitution is sufficiently
good, the developer’s effective production function expands to include
languages and domains previously beyond her individual reach. This
paper asks whether that expansion is empirically detectable.

We exploit the staggered rollout of Claude Code across the GitHub
developer population between May 2025 and January 2026 to estimate the
causal effect of AI assistant adoption on six measures of an
individual developer’s technological frontier. We construct a panel
from 7.8 million Claude-co-authored commits paired with the full
public contribution history of a stratified sample of 5,838
developers over 28 months, capturing every public repository
contribution and language usage at monthly frequency.
The treatment date is the developer’s first Claude-co-authored
commit; the comparison group consists of not-yet-treated developers
who adopt later in the rollout window. We estimate group-time
average treatment effects via the doubly robust

12
estimator with one-month anticipation.

We organize the empirical investigation through a Bayesian learning
model of skill-frontier choice adapted from

21
. In the model, developers hold prior
beliefs about their productivity in each programming language with
high precision on languages they have used and low precision on
languages they have not. Risk aversion makes them reluctant to switch
to an unfamiliar language, generating a
switching barrier

proportional to the difference in precision across known and unknown
languages. AI assistants enter the model as a free signal channel
that raises precision on unfamiliar languages even when the developer
is not actively using them. This shrinks the switching barrier and
expands the set of languages that clear the entry threshold. The model
yields five testable propositions: AI increases language, sector, and
repository counts; the effect is larger for specialists than for
generalists; and the treatment effect grows with time since adoption
as further threshold crossings accumulate.

The empirical results confirm three of the model’s predictions on the
margins we can measure. All six outcomes register positive and
statistically significant effects at the adoption month. Monthly
commits rise by 40.7 (a 191% increase over the pre-adoption mean of
21.3); the number of distinct repositories rises by 1.5; the
number of programming languages used rises by 0.83 against a
pre-adoption mean of 0.63. Shannon language entropy, a measure that
weights both the count and the balance of the language mix, rises by
0.14. The number of
newly
-used languages — languages not
used by the same developer in any earlier month — rises by 0.31.
Crucially for the dynamic prediction of Proposition

5
, the effect on cumulative-lifetime-languages

grows
over event-time horizon: the simple aggregated ATT
(0.59) exceeds the instantaneous ATT (0.51), and the event-study
profile is monotonically increasing. The pattern matches the model’s
prediction that AI-mediated precision accumulation should keep crossing
new entry thresholds over time, not stabilize at adoption.

The results are robust to two stricter sample restrictions —
keeping only developers active in
\geq 50\%
of pre-treatment months
(
N
= 1,620) and only developers active in
\geq 6
pre-treatment
months (
N
= 2,672) — with effect sizes qualitatively
identical and pre-trend coefficients essentially flat for five of six
outcomes. Pre-trends are non-trivial only for the
mechanical cumulative-languages measure, where the cumulative
construction generates an unavoidable upward drift in calendar time.

We are explicit about the central identification threat. Claude
adoption is voluntary, and the timing of adoption is plausibly
correlated with the timing of a developer’s decision to start a new
project in an unfamiliar language. A developer who decides to ship
a Rust application may install Claude
because
the language is
unfamiliar, and her first Claude commit will be mechanically
contemporaneous with her first Rust commit. The staggered DiD design
addresses cohort heterogeneity and TWFE negative-weighting concerns

(
19
;
15
)
but does
not resolve this reverse-causal selection problem. We treat the
estimates as documenting a
sharp, persistent shift in developer
behavior coincident with Claude adoption, quantitatively consistent
with the model’s mechanism
, rather than as a settled causal claim.
Section
8
outlines three paths toward stronger
identification — exogenous-shock searches, conditional parallel
trends with rich covariates, and placebo treatments at fake adoption
dates — that we leave for the full paper.

The paper contributes to three literatures. First, the empirical
economics of generative AI in knowledge work

(
25
;
11
;
24
;
14
;
16
)
: we move from task-level or
short-run experimental measurement to a longitudinal observational
design that admits dynamic effects, and shift the outcome
of interest from raw productivity to the diversity and breadth of
the developer’s technological portfolio. Second, the literature on
skill-biased technological change

(
1
;
2
;
5
;
4
)
: we document a third margin alongside displacement and
reinstatement, namely the within-worker expansion of the set of
tasks a given worker can perform. Third, the user-innovation tradition

(
29
;
6
;
9
)
: our developer-level evidence speaks to how
the introduction of AI restructures the feasible set of user
innovators on open-source platforms.

2
Related Literature

The paper sits at the intersection of three literatures: the empirical
economics of generative AI in the workplace, the theory of skill-biased
technological change and skill acquisition, and the difference-in-differences
methods literature.

2.1
Empirical effects of generative AI on knowledge work

A growing body of empirical evidence documents productivity effects of
generative AI assistants on knowledge workers.
24

report a 37% reduction in task time and an increase in output quality
among professional writers exposed to ChatGPT in a controlled experiment.

11
use a field experiment with customer-
support agents and find a 14% productivity increase concentrated in the
bottom skill quintile.
16
document a “jagged
technological frontier” in consultancy tasks — AI helps for some tasks
and hurts for others, with implications for task allocation.

Within software development specifically,
25
use a
randomized experiment with GitHub Copilot and find that treated developers
complete a coding task 55% faster than controls.

14
extend this with three field experiments at
Microsoft, Accenture, and a major US bank, finding 26% mean productivity
gains with substantial heterogeneity.
3
provide
descriptive evidence on how Claude assistance reshapes the formation of
coding skills, and
23
measure productivity impacts
in experienced open-source developer workflows.

Our contribution to this literature is twofold. First, we move from

task-level
or short-run experimental measurement to a

longitudinal observational design
on a large developer panel, which
admits dynamic effects (Proposition
5
) that experimental
designs are typically too short to detect. Second, where prior work
emphasizes raw productivity outcomes (task completion time, lines of
code, bug rates), we focus on the
technological frontier
: the
diversity of languages, repositories, and domains the developer engages
with. This shifts the question from “does AI make developers faster?”
to “does AI expand what developers can do?”

2.2
Skill-biased technological change and AI as a general-purpose
technology

Our findings speak directly to the broader question of how AI reshapes
the structure of human capital. The task-based framework of

1
;
2
decomposes
automation’s labor-market impact into a
displacement
effect (capital
substituting for labor in existing tasks) and a
reinstatement

effect (new tasks created at the frontier).

4
document the empirical creation of new work over
1940–2018. Our results are consistent with a third margin: AI tools
expand the
set of tasks a given worker can perform
, neither
displacing nor reinstating but instead reshaping individual
production functions.

The general-purpose-technology (GPT) tradition

(
10
;
20
;
27
)

predicts that GPTs diffuse slowly and reorganize complementary tasks
across sectors.
17
provide a labor-market-impact
classification of large language models in this tradition. Our
developer-level evidence offers a microcosm of GPT diffusion: developers
are early adopters of the GPT in their own work, and we observe direct
within-individual expansion of the task set.

The Bayesian-learning framework we adopt in Section
3

builds explicitly on
21
, who model
learning-by-doing as Bayesian updating about an unknown latent
productivity. Our extension introduces a parallel signal channel
operating at near-zero cost on unfamiliar technologies — the AI
assistant. The framework relates to the literature on strategic
experimentation
(
7
;
18
)
but
treats the signal arrival as exogenous rather than as a costly
information-acquisition choice.

2.3
User innovation and open-source software

The observation that ordinary users innovate, and that open
communities can scale this innovation, is associated with the
research program of
28
;
29
.

6
formalize the shift from producer
innovation to user and open collaborative innovation. Open-source
software is the canonical empirical setting:

22
and
9
document
the motivations and innovation outputs of open-source developers.

Our paper contributes to this literature by examining how a new tool —
the AI coding assistant — restructures the feasible set of user
innovators. If the language and repository portfolios of individual
developers expand as we document, the implications for open-source
collaboration networks are non-trivial: more developers active in
more languages and more repositories should change the matching
of contributors to projects and the structure of cumulative innovation

(
9
)
. The companion peer-effects work

(
13
)
examines the network dimension explicitly.

Difference-in-differences with staggered adoption

The recent econometrics literature on staggered DiD designs is

19
,

15
,

,

26
, and

3
A Bayesian Learning Model of AI-Driven Skill Frontier Expansion

To organize the empirical investigation we develop a Bayesian model of
language choice and skill acquisition, adapted from
21
.
The model formalizes a single mechanism: AI coding assistants supply
free signals about unfamiliar technologies, reducing the uncertainty that
deters developers from working outside their domain of expertise. We derive
five propositions that map directly onto the empirical specifications in
Section
5
.

3.1
Setup

There are
K\geq 2
programming languages indexed by
k
and a continuum of
developers
i\in[0,1]
. Each developer has a latent productivity

\theta_{ik}\in\mathbb{R}
in language
k
that captures her ability to produce
output in that language conditional on full proficiency. The developer
does not observe
\theta_{ik}
. She holds Normal prior beliefs:

\theta_{ik}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_{ik,0},\;\sigma^{2}_{ik,0}\right),\qquad\pi_{ik,t}\equiv 1/\sigma^{2}_{ik,t}.

(1)

We refer to
\pi_{ik,t}
as the developer’s
precision
(or

expertise
) in language
k
at date
t
. Each developer’s

known
languages
\mathcal{K}_{i}
have high initial precision

\pi_{ik,0}=\bar{\pi}
for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
, and her
unknown

languages
\mathcal{U}_{i}=\{1,\ldots,K\}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{i}
have low
initial precision
\pi_{ik,0}=\underline{\pi}
with

\underline{\pi}\ll\bar{\pi}
.

3.2
Learning by doing

When the developer uses language
k
in period
t
she observes a noisy
signal of her productivity,

x_{t}=\theta_{ik}+\varepsilon_{t},\qquad\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}),

(2)

with
\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}
the signal noise. Bayes’ rule on the
conjugate Normal–Normal model delivers closed-form updates: precision
accumulates additively,

\pi_{ik,t+1}=\pi_{ik,t}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}},

(3)

and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior and the
signal,

\mu_{ik,t+1}=\frac{\pi_{ik,t}\,\mu_{ik,t}+(1/\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon})\,x_{t}}{\pi_{ik,t+1}}.

(4)

Expertise grows linearly with experience; unused languages remain at their
prior precision indefinitely.

3.3
The switching decision

Developers value mean productivity but dislike uncertainty. Following
the standard mean-variance benchmark, the risk-adjusted payoff of using
language
k
in period
t
is

U_{ik,t}=\mu_{ik,t}-\frac{\rho}{2\,\pi_{ik,t}},

(5)

where
\rho>0
is the coefficient of risk aversion. The developer
switches from her current language
k
to a new language
k^{\prime}
if and only
if
U_{ik^{\prime},t}>U_{ik,t}
, which rearranges to the

switching condition

\mu_{ik^{\prime},t}-\mu_{ik,t}\;>\;\frac{\rho}{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{\pi_{ik^{\prime},t}}-\frac{1}{\pi_{ik,t}}\right)\;\equiv\;\Delta_{ik^{\prime}k,t}.

(6)

The right-hand side
\Delta_{ik^{\prime}k,t}
is the
switching barrier
.
It is large precisely when the developer is much less expert in
k^{\prime}

than in
k
: low
\pi_{ik^{\prime},t}
and high
\pi_{ik,t}
generate a high
barrier. This is the formal counterpart of the well-documented stickiness
of programming-language portfolios.

3.4
AI as a signal generator

We introduce AI assistance through a single modeling primitive. When the
developer has access to an AI coding assistant of capability
A
, she
receives
n_{A}\equiv n(A)>0
additional signals about
every

language each period, whether or not she is using it:

\tilde{x}_{t,j}=\theta_{ik}+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t,j},\qquad\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{A}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,n_{A}.

(7)

These signals are interpreted as AI-generated code samples, explanations,
debugging traces, and project scaffolding for languages the developer has
not committed to use. Under Equation (
7
), the precision
update for any language is

\pi_{ik,t+1}\;=\;\pi_{ik,t}\;+\;\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathds{1}\left[i\text{ uses }k\text{ at }t\right]\;+\;\frac{n_{A}}{\sigma^{2}_{A}}.

(8)

The crucial change relative to Equation (
3
) is that
precision in
unused
languages now grows: AI lets the developer
learn about Rust while writing Python. After
T
periods of passive
exposure, the precision of an unfamiliar language
k^{\prime}
rises from

\underline{\pi}
to
\underline{\pi}+T\,n_{A}/\sigma^{2}_{A}
. The
switching barrier in Equation (
6
) shrinks correspondingly.

3.5
Propositions

The model environment of Equations
(
1
)–(
8
) yields the following
testable predictions. Proofs are collected in
Appendix
A
. Throughout,
\tau_{i}
denotes the date at
which developer
i
adopts AI;
N_{it}
denotes the number of languages
in her portfolio at
t
;
R_{it}
denotes the number of repositories;
and
S_{it}
denotes the number of distinct sectors.

Proposition 1

(Language diversification)
.

For any developer
i
adopting AI at date
\tau_{i}
and any horizon

s>0
, the expected number of languages in her portfolio post-adoption
exceeds the counterfactual without AI:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[N_{i,\tau_{i}+s}\right]\;>\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[N_{i,\tau_{i}+s}\,\middle|\,\text{no AI}\right].

Proposition 2

(Sector diversification)
.

Augment the model with sector-specific productivity components

\theta^{S}_{is}
that are also subject to Bayesian updating. Then for
any
s>0
:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[S_{i,\tau_{i}+s}\right]\;>\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[S_{i,\tau_{i}+s}\,\middle|\,\text{no AI}\right].

Proposition 3

(Repository expansion)
.

Each repository requires both language-specific and sector-specific
proficiency. The expected number of repositories the developer
contributes to satisfies

\mathbb{E}[R_{i,\tau_{i}+s}]>\mathbb{E}[R_{i,\tau_{i}+s}\mid\text{no AI}]
for all

s>0
.

Proposition 4

(Specialist advantage)
.

Let
\Delta N_{it}
denote the treatment effect on language count. The
effect is larger for specialists (
|\mathcal{K}_{i}|\leq 2
) than for
generalists (
|\mathcal{K}_{i}|\geq 3
):

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta N_{it}\,\middle|\,\text{specialist}\right]\;>\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta N_{it}\,\middle|\,\text{generalist}\right].

Proposition 5

(Dynamic treatment effect)
.

The treatment effect on language count is increasing in time since
adoption:
\partial\mathbb{E}[\Delta N_{i,\tau_{i}+s}]/\partial s>0
for

s\geq 0
. Equivalently, the event-study coefficients exhibit a

growing
, not flat, dynamic profile.

The intuition for Proposition
4
is the concavity of
the precision channel: the reduction in the uncertainty penalty from
adding
\delta\equiv n_{A}T/\sigma^{2}_{A}
units of precision,

\rho\delta/[2\underline{\pi}(\underline{\pi}+\delta)]
, is decreasing
in the baseline precision
\underline{\pi}
. Specialists have a larger
mass of unknown languages clustered near the entry threshold; the same
exogenous precision shock crosses more of them. Proposition

5
reflects continued precision accumulation: each post-
adoption period adds
n_{A}/\sigma^{2}_{A}
precision to every unfamiliar
language, so additional thresholds are crossed over time.

Propositions
1
,
3
, and

5
drive the main empirical specifications in
Section
5
and the results reported in
Section
6
. Propositions
2
and

4
require ingredients (NAICS sector classification
and pre-adoption portfolio measures) that we develop in a companion
analysis; the empirical implementation is left for future work.

4
Data

The empirical analysis combines two complementary data sources scraped from
the public GitHub event stream: the universe of Claude-co-authored commits
between January 2025 and January 2026, and the full monthly contribution
history of a stratified developer sample over a 28-month window
(January 2024 through April 2026). We describe each in turn, then summarize
the resulting analysis panel.

4.1
Source: Claude-co-authored commits

Claude Code, released in beta in February 2025 and at general availability
in May 2025, attaches a co-author trailer of the form

Co-Authored-By: Claude
to every commit it produces.
1
1

1

The
trailer is a Git convention by which any number of additional authors can
be associated with a commit; co-authors appear in the commit metadata and
are surfaced on GitHub. The Claude trailer carries a model identifier,
e.g.
claude-opus-4
or
claude-sonnet-4
, which we use
descriptively but do not exploit for identification.
Because this trailer
is machine-readable and propagates to GitHub’s public event API, every
commit produced with Claude assistance can be detected and aggregated at
scale. We harvest 7,786,771 such commits across the 13-month window
of Claude availability, covering 185,517 distinct authors.

For each commit we record the SHA, the repository (owner/name), the commit
and authored timestamps, the committer and author identities (login,
email), the commit message, and the detected Claude model. We discard
1.6 million commits with missing
author_login
, which are
predominantly bots and squash-merge artifacts.

4.2
Sample construction

We define a developer’s
adoption date
as her first Claude-co-authored
commit. This gives us 185,517 staggered adoption events distributed
across the 13-month window: 1,557 developers adopt in 2025 Q1, 11,894
in Q2, 46,700 in Q3, 73,151 in Q4, and 52,215 in 2026 Q1. The mass
of adoptions in late 2025 reflects the public launch of Claude Code in
May 2025 and the subsequent expansion of its free tier.

From this population we construct two analysis samples. The treated sample
contains 5,000
early adopters
: developers whose first Claude
commit falls in Q2 or Q3 of 2025. Within this window we further require at
least five Claude-co-authored commits to exclude one-time experimenters,
and we drop GitHub bot accounts identified by standard naming patterns
(e.g.
[bot]
,
dependabot
,
renovate
). The
eligible pool is 36,718 developers; we draw 5,000 stratified
proportionally by adoption month and equally across three intensity tiers
(5–10 commits, 11–50, 50+) to ensure variation in both treatment timing
and commitment.

The control sample contains 5,000
not-yet-treated
developers:
authors who adopt Claude in 2025 Q4 or 2026 Q1 under the same eligibility
filters and stratification scheme. During the treated cohort’s adoption
window of April–September 2025, the control developers have zero Claude
usage. This choice of comparison group is the standard recommendation in
the
12
framework for staggered designs and is
robust to selection on observable trends: the controls are eventual
adopters and therefore arguably similar in unobserved tastes to the
treated group, simply with a later adoption date.

4.3
Developer-month panel

To estimate treatment effects we need each developer’s full public
contribution history, not only her Claude-assisted commits. We re-fetch
the monthly contribution record for all 10,000 sampled developers via the
GitHub GraphQL
contributionsCollection
endpoint, querying 28
monthly windows from January 2024 through April 2026. For each
(developer, month, repository) cell we record the number of commits, the
repository’s primary language (as classified by GitHub Linguist), the
distribution of bytes across all detected languages, the owner type
(user vs. organization), and repository metadata (topics, stargazer
count, fork status, archive status).

4.4
Outcome variables

From the (developer, month, repository) records we construct six monthly
outcome variables at the developer level. Three are extensive-margin
counts:
N_{it}
is the number of distinct primary languages contributed
to in month
t
;
R_{it}
is the number of distinct repositories; and the
monthly commit count is the developer’s total commits over the month.
Three further variables capture language diversity directly:

H_{it}
is the Shannon entropy of the developer’s commits across
languages in month
t
,

H_{it}=-\sum_{k}p_{itk}\log(p_{itk})
where
p_{itk}
is the share
of commits in language
k
;
\Delta N_{it}^{\text{new}}
is the number
of languages first used in month
t
(not used by the same developer in
any earlier month); and
C_{it}
is the cumulative count of distinct
languages ever used through month
t
. The two diversity-with-novelty
measures
\Delta N_{it}^{\text{new}}
and
C_{it}
are the most direct
empirical counterparts to the model’s portfolio expansion mechanism.

5
Empirical Strategy

The empirical task is to estimate the causal effect of Claude Code
adoption on each of the six outcomes defined in Section
4
.
Two features of the setting dictate the choice of estimator. First,
treatment is
staggered
: developers adopt Claude at different
months between May 2025 and January 2026. Second, treatment effects are
plausibly
heterogeneous
across cohorts and time horizons: an
April 2025 adopter has had different exposure (to model upgrades, pricing
changes, and accumulated learning-by-doing) than a January 2026 adopter
at the same event time. Conventional two-way fixed-effects (TWFE)
specifications are known to be biased under these conditions

(
19
;
15
;
26
)
.

5.1
Group-time average treatment effects

Let
i
index developers,
t
index months (coded
1,2,\ldots,28
for
January 2024 through April 2026), and
G_{i}
denote the period in which
developer
i
first uses Claude. Developers who never adopt have

G_{i}=\infty
; developers in our sample all eventually adopt, with the
not-yet-treated developers playing the role of the comparison group at
each
(g,t)
cell. Let
Y_{it}(0)
denote the potential outcome for
developer
i
in period
t
in the absence of treatment, and let

Y_{it}(g)
denote the potential outcome for a developer first treated
in period
g
. The
group-time average treatment effect
is

ATT(g,t)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,Y_{it}(g)-Y_{it}(0)\,\middle|\,G_{i}=g\,\right],\qquad t\geq g.

(9)

Each
ATT(g,t)
is identified under three assumptions. Conditional
parallel trends requires

\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0)-Y_{i,t-1}(0)\mid G_{i}=g]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(0)-Y_{i,t-1}(0)\mid G_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}]
, where
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is the set of
not-yet-treated comparison cohorts at period
t
. Limited treatment
anticipation rules out
ATT(g,t)\neq 0
for
t
far below
g
; we
permit one month of anticipation given the setup time required to install
and configure Claude Code. An overlap condition ensures that the
propensity score
P(G_{i}=g\mid X_{i})
is bounded away from zero and one
on the comparison support.

We estimate (
9
) by the doubly robust (DR) estimator, which
combines a propensity-score weighting and an outcome regression and is
consistent if either is correctly specified

(
12
)
. The not-yet-treated control group widens
the comparison pool relative to the never-treated alternative and is
robust to compositional changes at later horizons. Standard errors are
computed by 1,000 multiplier-bootstrap iterations clustered at the
developer level, and uniform confidence bands are reported alongside
pointwise intervals throughout.

5.2
Aggregation: event-study and simple ATTs

The matrix
\{ATT(g,t)\}
is high-dimensional. We aggregate along two
dimensions following
12
. The

event-study aggregation
averages
ATT(g,t)
across cohorts at
each event time
e=t-g
:

ATT(e)=\sum_{g}\omega_{g}(e)\,ATT\!\left(g,\,g+e\right),\qquad\omega_{g}(e)\geq 0,\ \textstyle\sum_{g}\omega_{g}(e)=1,

(10)

with weights proportional to cohort size at event time
e
. This
collapses the
(g,t)
matrix into a dynamic treatment-effect profile,
the central figure of the paper. The
simple aggregation
averages

ATT(g,t)
across all post-treatment
(g,t)
cells with weights
proportional to cohort-period sizes:

ATT^{\mathrm{simple}}=\sum_{g,t\geq g}\omega_{g,t}\,ATT(g,t).

(11)

ATT^{\mathrm{simple}}
is interpretable as a single-number summary of
the average treatment effect.

Pre-trends

Identification rests on the conditional parallel-trends assumption.

12

8
Discussion: Identification Threats

The estimates in Section
6
are precisely identified

within
the Callaway–Sant’Anna framework, given the maintained
parallel-trends assumption. The harder question is whether that
assumption is plausible in this setting. We take the threats in turn,
distinguishing those the staggered DiD design addresses from those it
does not.

8.1
What the staggered DiD design handles

Three threats are mechanically managed by the estimator. First,

treatment effect heterogeneity across cohorts
: April 2025
adopters faced a different version of Claude, a different price
schedule, and a different stock of accumulated learning-by-doing than
January 2026 adopters. TWFE specifications can produce sign-flipped
estimates under such heterogeneity
(
19
)
;
the cohort-time decomposition in Equation (
9
) sidesteps
this by estimating cohort-specific effects and aggregating with
non-negative weights. Second,
negative weighting
on
already-treated comparison units: we restrict the comparison group to
the not-yet-treated, ensuring weights are bounded below by zero. Third,

calendar-time trends
: shocks to GitHub aggregate activity that
hit both groups equally (e.g. new platform features, weekend
seasonality, public holidays) are absorbed by the period-specific

ATT(g,t)
baseline.

8.2
What the design does not handle: selection on time-varying
unobservables

The central threat is that Claude adoption is voluntary and
self-selected. A developer does not adopt Claude at random; she adopts
because something about her work situation has changed. If that
something also affects the outcomes — the languages she uses, the
repositories she touches — then the parallel-trends assumption fails
and the estimated ATT confounds the treatment effect with the
selection mechanism.

The most plausible version of this concern is a
reverse-causal

story at the project level. A developer decides to start a new
project — perhaps because of a new job, a personal-interest pivot, or
a client requirement — in a programming language she does not know
well. Because the project is in an unfamiliar language, she installs
Claude Code to assist. Her first Claude commit, our treatment date,
is mechanically simultaneous with the language-portfolio expansion we
measure. In this scenario, Claude does not
cause
the
diversification; both are downstream consequences of the same
upstream decision to start a new project.

The pattern of the t=0 coefficient is consistent with this concern.
Across all six outcomes the largest effect is the contemporaneous one,
which is exactly what reverse causation predicts: the underlying shock
hits both treatment and outcome at the same moment. The

dynamic
treatment effect in cumulative languages
(Figure
6
, Proposition
5
) is
harder to explain by reverse causation alone, since it requires the

stock
of distinct languages to keep rising at later horizons.
But the bulk of the t=0 effect is open to the alternative interpretation.

8.3
The reversed Ashenfelter dip

The classical Ashenfelter dip refers to outcomes that fall just before
treatment — workers’ earnings declining in the months before entering
job training, for example. We observe the opposite: monthly commit
activity, language usage, and repository contribution all rise
modestly in the single pre-period month
e=-1
. The

12
estimator with one-month anticipation
absorbs this rise into the treatment window, but the underlying
pattern suggests that the treatment timing is endogenous: the
developer’s overall activity ramps up in the month preceding her first
Claude commit, consistent with the start-of-a-new-project narrative.

8.4
Robustness specifications and what they rule out

The stricter pre-period activity filters reported in Section

7
address one specific selection concern: that
low-pre-activity developers are mechanically pulled into the sample by
the act of starting any post-period work. The results survive under
both filters with similar magnitudes for five of six outcomes and a
larger attenuation only for the mechanical cumulative-languages
measure. This rules out one class of selection stories but not the
class above.

8.5
What would be needed for a causal claim at top venues

Three credible paths toward stronger identification, in order of
desirability. First, an
exogenous source of variation
in Claude
adoption: regional rollouts of the free tier, pricing-change events,
or eligibility cutoffs in the institutional subscription program would
permit instrumental variables or regression-discontinuity designs.
Second,
conditional parallel trends
estimated with a richer
covariate set: pre-period commit history, language-portfolio measures,
account age, organizational affiliation. The
xformla
argument
of the Callaway–Sant’Anna estimator implements this directly and is a
natural next step. Third,
placebo treatments
: assigning fake
adoption dates six or twelve months earlier and rerunning the
estimator should find null effects under a valid design; non-zero
placebo effects would diagnose the central concern.

8.6
Honest framing

In the absence of an exogenous shock, the most defensible
interpretation of the results is associational rather than causal.
The estimates document a
sharp, persistent shift in developer
behavior coincident with Claude Code adoption, in directions
quantitatively consistent with the AI-as-signal mechanism
formalized in Section
3
. The pattern is robust to
sample-restriction perturbations and is internally consistent with
the model’s prediction of growing dynamic effects on cumulative
language diversity. Whether the underlying mechanism is the
uncertainty-reduction channel of Proposition
1
or
a self-selection process that drives both adoption and outcomes will
require the identification work outlined above to resolve.

9
Conclusion

Generative AI coding assistants are diffusing rapidly across the
software-development workforce. This paper takes one of the first
observational looks at what changes in a developer’s behavior on the
day she adopts such a tool, using a panel of 5,838 GitHub developers
observed monthly across 28 months and the rollout of Claude Code as
the natural experiment. The picture is consistent. Adoption is
associated with sharp, persistent increases in monthly commit volume
(+40 commits), the number of repositories contributed to (+1.5), the
number of programming languages used (+0.83), the entropy of the
language mix (+0.14), the number of newly-used languages (+0.31), and
the cumulative count of lifetime languages (+0.51). The last of these,
crucially, grows further over time, exactly as predicted by a Bayesian
learning model in which AI provides a free signal channel about
unfamiliar technologies.

The shift in within-worker production functions implied by these
magnitudes is substantial. A representative treated developer in the
adoption month makes roughly twice as many commits, doubles the
number of repositories she touches, and uses approximately one
additional programming language relative to her own pre-adoption
mean. The composition of her output shifts, not just the volume:
treated developers begin working in languages they had never used
before at a rate of roughly one new language at adoption, with
continuing accumulation thereafter. If these effects are causal, the
implication is that AI coding assistants are not merely a productivity
multiplier within a developer’s existing technological boundary but a

structural shift
in the boundary itself — the technological
frontier of the individual developer expands.

We are explicit about the limit of the current design. Claude
adoption is voluntary; the timing of adoption is plausibly
correlated with the timing of decisions to start projects in
unfamiliar languages. The Callaway–Sant’Anna estimator handles
cohort heterogeneity and TWFE bias but does not resolve this
reverse-causal selection. The most defensible reading of the
results is therefore associational rather than strictly causal: we
document a sharp, persistent, dynamic shift in developer behavior
coincident with adoption and quantitatively consistent with the
AI-as-signal mechanism formalized in Section
3
.
A full causal claim would require an exogenous source of variation
in adoption — regional rollouts of free-tier eligibility,
pricing-change events, or institutional-subscription cutoffs — or
a conditional parallel-trends specification with rich pre-period
covariates. Both are natural next steps.

The findings have implications beyond the developer population we
study. If AI tools enable individual workers to operate in domains
previously outside their core human capital, the labor-market
implications of AI may be less about
substitution
of capital for
labor than about
reallocation
of labor across tasks within
individuals. The returns to language-specific or domain-specific
human capital may fall; the returns to general problem-solving ability
and domain knowledge may rise. The matching of workers to projects
should change. In open-source software specifically, the structure
of collaboration networks — which contributors connect to which
projects through which languages — should shift as individual
portfolios expand. Companion work using the same source data

(
13
)
examines the peer-influence dimension of this
mechanism on company-level collaboration networks.

Two extensions deserve emphasis. First, the model predicts that
specialists — developers using few pre-adoption languages — should
benefit more from AI than generalists (Proposition

4
). The test requires partitioning the sample by
pre-adoption portfolio size and is reserved for the full paper.
Second, the sector-diversification prediction (Proposition

2
) requires a NAICS classification of each
repository, which we develop separately. Both tests would discipline
the AI-as-skill-substitution hypothesis against alternatives and
either strengthen or qualify the causal interpretation we currently
offer associationally. We close with the observation that, if the
AI-driven expansion of the developer’s technological frontier is
real and is broadly representative of how generative AI is reshaping
knowledge work, the policy and welfare consequences are large enough
to merit the identification work it will take to settle them.

1

Define the entry condition for language

\mu_{ik^{\prime},t}-\frac{\rho}{2[\underline{\pi}+(t-\tau_{i})\,n_{A}/\sigma^{2}_{A}]}\;>\;\mu_{ik^{\prime},t}-\frac{\rho}{2\underline{\pi}}.

The entry condition is therefore satisfied for a weakly larger set of

holds whenever the cross-sectional distribution of

[\bar{U}_{t}-c_{\text{entry}}+\rho/(2(\underline{\pi}+\delta)),\bar{U}_{t}-c_{\text{entry}}+\rho/(2\underline{\pi})]

\mathbb{E}[N_{i,\tau_{i}+s}]>\mathbb{E}[N_{i,\tau_{i}+s}\mid\text{no AI}]

.
\square

\underline{\pi}
and therefore a larger
mass of candidate languages in the activation zone. A generalist
(
|\mathcal{K}_{i}|\geq 3
) has already entered the languages with
the most favorable
\mu_{ik^{\prime}}
values, so the marginal unknown
language for the generalist has lower
\mu_{ik^{\prime}}
or partial

\pi>\underline{\pi}

\square

Proof of Proposition
5

The unknown-language precision after
s
periods of AI exposure is

\pi_{ik^{\prime},\tau_{i}+s}=\underline{\pi}+s\,n_{A}/\sigma^{2}_{A}
,
which is strictly increasing in
s
. The mass of languages in the
activation zone is increasing in the precision difference

\pi_{ik^{\prime},\tau_{i}+s}-\underline{\pi}=s\,n_{A}/\sigma^{2}_{A}
.
Differentiating
\Delta N
with respect to
s
yields a positive
sign. Hence the expected treatment effect on language count is
strictly increasing in time since adoption,

\partial\mathbb{E}[\Delta N_{i,\tau_{i}+s}]/\partial s>0
, for
s\geq 0
.
At a sufficiently long horizon the effect saturates as the
uncertainty penalty approaches zero, but the rate of accumulation
is positive throughout the empirically-relevant range.
\square

We exclude developers whose login matches any of the following

Stratification scheme

The treated sample of 5,000 developers is allocated proportionally
across the six adoption-month strata (April through September 2025).
Within each month, the allocation is divided equally across three
intensity tiers:
low
(5–10 Claude commits),
medium

(11–50), and
high
(
\geq 51
). The control sample of 5,000
not-yet-treated developers follows the same stratification scheme,
proportional across two adoption-quarter strata (2025 Q4 and
2026 Q1) and equal across the three intensity tiers within each
quarter.

GraphQL fetch

Each developer’s monthly activity is retrieved by querying the GitHub
GraphQL API’s
user.contributionsCollection
endpoint with

from

to
parameters set to the first and last
calendar dates of each month. Within each window we retrieve up to

) and the top 15
languages per repository ordered by byte size. The fetch is

tracking. The full panel of 280,000 (developer, month) cells
