Viable Spores Can be Obtained and Germinated from Commercially Obtained Dried Morel (Morchella) Mushrooms
Authors: Anton Kubala, Ivy J Mutai, Samantha Mclean, Catherine E D Rees
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMorel mushrooms (Morchella sp.) are highly prized edible mushrooms that are still difficult to cultivate despite great interest and advancements made in recent years. Obtaining genetic material of morel mushrooms is necessary to carry out research but may be challenging depending on the region, time of the season, budget, or local legislation. This study assessed whether ascospores could be recovered and germinated from retail dried morel products and whether resulting isolates could be taxonomically identified. Four packets of dried morels from different suppliers were rehydrated, homogenized, and processed to recover spore suspensions. Hemocytometer counts yielded mean spore titers of 1.50 × 105 (A), 4.24 × 106 (B), 5.86 × 106 (C), and 3.51 × 105 (D) spores mL−1. Aliquots were plated on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) with chloramphenicol and incubated at 24 °C; colony counts indicated cultivable fractions corresponding to c. 2.2% germination for sample A and ∼10–20% for samples B–D. Representative colonies were subcultured, DNA was extracted, and ITS barcodes were generated by Sanger sequencing. Isolates from samples A, B, and D matched Morchella sextelata, while isolates from sample C matched Morchella eximia. The findings demonstrate that viable Morchella ascospores can persist through commercial drying and that dried products constitute a low‑cost source of cultivable material. Discrepancies between sequencing results and product labels highlighted possible country of origin concerns but were considered outside the scope of this work and were not exhaustively investigated.Keywords: Morchella, morel mushroom, dried mushrooms, strain isolation, spores, food origin

Introduction

True morels (Morchella spp.) are Ascomycota that belong to the family Morchellaceae (class Pezizomycetes). They are some of the most sought after and highly prized edible mushrooms reaching reported market prices of >150 US$ kg−1 of dried morels in China [1] while fresh, undried, morels foraged in the USA would be sold for ∼$70 kg−1 [2]. This places them in a similar category to other gourmet wild mushrooms such as porcini (Boletus edulis) and chanterelles (Cantharellus cibarius). Their popularity is in large part due to their exquisite savory flavor as well as associated health benefits, which have been used as part of traditional Chinese medicine for thousands of years. Apart from being rich in protein, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals, organic acids, and fiber they also contain a wide range of bioactive compounds such as polysaccharides, phenolics, and ergosterols [3,4]. These compounds have been found to possess anti-inflammatory, immune protective, and antioxidative effects as well as anti-cancer and anti-cardiovascular disease properties [3–6]. Cultivation of morel mushrooms once thought impossible has advanced greatly in recent decades [4,7]. However, cultivation is still considered difficult with most of the world’s cultivated morels being grown in covered outdoor beds [7,8] with China being by far the largest producer of commercially grown morels [7]. Attempts at indoor year round cultivation have produced mixed results. While some successful attempts at indoor cultivation are reported [9–11], indoor cultivation is generally considered challenging and the only morel species currently used for commercial indoor cultivation is Morchella rufobrunnea [8,9,12]. Therefore, many of the morel mushrooms consumed in Europe and North America are still foraged in the wild, which introduces ecological problems of overharvesting and damage to natural populations and forest biomes, and also economic issues of high prices due to limited supply as well as fluctuating and diminishing harvests [4,7]. Morel mushroom fruiting bodies or ascocarps are notorious for spoiling quickly and, despite advances in freezing technology and frozen morels becoming more common on the market, dried morels are still the most common form in which morel mushrooms are accessible to consumers making up ∼60% of the global market [4,13]. More research into methods for year round reliable cultivation of morel mushrooms is thus required to ensure consistent supply of these sought after mushrooms without damaging natural populations and has the potential to be a very lucrative industry [7,8]. One of the principal challenges of conducting research on morel mushrooms is the procurement of genetic material. This can be done in a variety of ways with the easiest being purchase of Morchella mycelium from culture collections or other specialized commercial entities that will guarantee purity and identity of the strain. This is however expensive as culture collections or commercial entities may charge hundreds of pounds for samples. While morel strains are available from institutions such as the Westerdijk Institutes CBS or the Leibnitz Institutes DSMZ culture collection other large culture collections like the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) or the UK Health Security Agency culture collection did not, at the time of writing (10/2025), have any Morchella species available for purchase. These factors all constrain the number of species and strains available to work on depending on region and budget. An alternative is isolation of strains from wild harvested ascocarps, which is substantially less expensive and can yield a broader representation of species and local genotypes [14]. Wild isolation, however, has practical constraints: investigators must be located within regions where Morchella spp. occur, collect during the brief seasonal window when ascocarps are present, and invest considerable field effort to locate and process fresh specimens for axenic culture—tasks that are time consuming and dependent on variable phenology and habitat specificity [14]. As ascospore resistance to desiccation is well documented for a range of ascomycete taxa [15–17] this work explored an additional option of obtaining morel genetic material through extracting and culturing spores from commercially obtained dried morel ascocarps. This would allow individuals to access a wide range of species and strains of both cultivated and wild foraged morel mushrooms at a fraction of the cost of procuring strains from commercial entities or strain collections as well as not being dependent on regional and seasonal availability of morel mushrooms for isolating new strains.

Materials and methods

Growth media and diluents

Dilutions were carried out using sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), while Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA; Difco, Omagh, UK) with 100 mg L−1 chloramphenicol (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was used for the cultivation of Morchella mycelium throughout this work.

Fungal samples used in this work

This work was carried out on four commercially obtained dried morel mushroom products. They were either obtained from supermarkets or purchased on the internet. Countries of origin stated on the product or by the supplier could not be independently verified. Fungal barcoding

Fungal barcoding was carried out using ITS1f and ITS4 primers (sequences given in Table 2). PCR mixture was composed of 4 µL of 5x FIREPOL master mix (Bento Bioworks Ltd., London, UK), 12 µL of water, 1 µL ITS1f forward and 1 µL of ITS4 reverse primer as well as 2 µL of DNA template. PCR conditions were as follows: 15 min of initial denaturation at 95 °C followed by 32 cycles of 30 s denaturation at 95 °C, 30 s of annealing at 55 °C and 60 s of extension at 72 °C before a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were run on a 1.5% agarose gel containing SYBR safe DNA stain (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). When visualized, a single large band of ∼ 400–600 bp was visible for each sample. The band was excised and DNA was extracted from the gel slice using Qiagen QIAquick Gel Extraction kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Extracted and purified DNA was sent for sanger sequencing with commercial sequencing service (Source BioScience, Nottingham, UK). Species identification was performed using NCBI blast.

Results

Extraction of Morchella spores from dried Morchella ascocarps

Four packets of dried morel mushrooms (A, B, C, and D) were obtained from online and commercial retailers (see Table 1 and Figure 1). From each sample, 2 g of dried morel ascocarps were weighed and rehydrated in 20 mL PBS for 10 min before being processed in a stomacher at medium speed for 2 min. The resulting suspension was pipetted off and allowed to stand for 2 min allowing the larger debris fragments to settle out while the finer particles, including spores, stayed suspended. Supernatant was carefully pipetted off and was centrifuged at 4000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting grey-brown pellet was resuspended in 1 mL PBS. The presence of Morchella sp. ascospores was confirmed using light microscopy (Figure 2) and serial dilutions were prepared using PBS. The number of spores in suspension was determined by counting ascospores using a hemacytometer yielding an average (n = 3) titer of 1.50 × 105 (A), 4.24 × 106 (B), 5.86 × 106 (C), and 3.51 × 105 (D) spores per ml (see Figure 3).

Germinating Morchella spores and determining viability

To determine spore viability, 100 µL samples of dilutions of spore extracts were plated (n = 3) onto SDA agar plates containing 100 mg L−1 chloramphenicol. Agar plates were incubated in the dark at 24 °C for 48 h. Plates were visually inspected using a stereomicroscope and colony morphology was confirmed to match Morchella sp. (see Figure 4). Morchella sp. colonies were counted and the colony-forming unit (cfu) mL−1 was calculated with samples containing on average 3.33 × 103 (A), 8.47 × 105 (B), 9.1 × 105 (C), and 3.33 × 104 (D) cfu mL−1 (Figure 3). Average percentage germination rate of the extracted Morchella spores was calculated using the formula:germination rate=number of colonies countednumber of spores counted×100 and it was found that spores extracted from dried morel samples B, C, and D had germination rates between ∼10 and 20% while only 2.2% of spores of sample A would germinate (Figure 5).

Fungal barcoding and species identification

To confirm the identification of isolates of presumptive Morchella sp., three individual colonies exhibiting Morchella typical colony morphology were isolated, subcultured onto fresh SDA + chloramphenicol plates and incubated at 24 °C for five days (Figure 6). Mycelium was scraped off the agar and DNA was extracted using Zymo Fungal DNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo, Irvine, CA). DNA was quantified using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA), and ITS1 and ITS2 rDNA regions were PCR amplified using ITS1f/ITS4 primers (PCR conditions and primer sequences given in Table 1). Sequencing using ITS1f/ITS4 primers was carried out using commercial sanger sequencing service (Source BioScience, Nottingham, UK) with all isolates of samples A, B, and D being found to be M. sextelata while isolates of sample C were found to be most similar to M. eximia (Table 3).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that viable Morchella ascospores can be recovered from commercially available dried morel ascocarps and that a measurable fraction of those spores will germinate quickly on standard mycological agar. The observed spore titers and cfu counts indicate that dehydration and commercial drying processes reduce but do not eliminate ascospore viability, and that viability varies substantially among products with factors such as age of the sample, processing- and storage conditions and method of dehydration likely playing a role [4,7]. Ascospore resistance to desiccation is well documented for a range of ascomycete taxa, and the variable germination rates observed here are consistent with differential ascospore tolerance to dehydration and with the influence of processing parameters (temperature, drying duration) and storage conditions on spore viability [3,4]. The commercial drying process may protect some spores (e.g., by rapid dehydration that arrests metabolism and limits damage) while destroying others (through prolonged heat exposure or oxidative stress). Characterizing the processing parameters of commercial products and experimentally testing controlled drying regimes would clarify which factors most strongly predict residual viability. No information could be obtained on the drying conditions employed when producing the commercial dried morels used in this study. Drying conditions of mushrooms can vary widely with drying temperatures usually ranging between 50 and 60 °C but reaching up to 80 °C in some commercial operations [18]. It is therefore reasonable to assume that success rates when isolating morel strains from dried products can vary significantly between producers and products. It also cannot be ruled out that certain dried morel products will not allow successful germination of spores due to high heat drying or potential gamma irradiation to extend shelf life or as a phytosanitary measure during shipping [19,20]. Despite significant differences in germination rates, morel strains were successfully germinated and isolated from all four tested products, representing both European and Chinese sources. Apart from identification from dried specimens being fundamentally challenging, commercially available dried morel mushrooms are frequently wild foraged with harvests from different regions and producers combined [7]. This necessitates species identification using fungal barcoding, multi-locus sequencing or similar genetic identification tools to ascertain the species or strain of the Morchella isolate in question with any degree of certainty [11,14]. Three of the four sampled dried morel products listed a species on the packaging: M. conica (sample A), M. esculenta (sample C), and either/both for sample B. ITS barcoding of cultivated isolates did not corroborate these labels: samples A, B, and D yielded only Morchella sextelata, while sample C was identified as Morchella eximia. These results also cast doubt on the declared countries of origin, since M. sextelata and M. eximia are primarily reported from North America and are not regarded as native European taxa. Recent reviews of commercial Morchella production note that large-scale plantings, particularly in China, are dominated by a limited set of taxa including M. sextelata, M. eximia, and M. importuna [7,21]. Bosnia and Herzegovina, the country of origin claimed for samples A and C, is known for wild foraging but shows little or no documented commercial morel cultivation [7,22] making the presence of a Chinese morel cultivar unlikely. The countries of origin stated on packaging and by suppliers could not be independently verified or traced back and this was considered outside the scope of this work. Similarly, evaluation of product authenticity and provenance is beyond the scope of the present study; however, a dedicated follow-up using a larger, geographically diverse sample set and expanded molecular markers (e.g., ITS plus LSU or protein-coding loci) is planned. Limitations of this study include the small number of commercial products sampled, the lack of information regarding drying conditions and origin countries of samples as well as the sole reliance on ITS sequencing for species identification. The incubation conditions used for germination and culture establishment (24 °C on SDA containing 100 mg L−1) chloramphenicol) could reasonably favor recovery of some taxa over others and may under-represent viable but slow-growing or nutritionally fastidious strains. However, the complete lack of intra sample genetic variation, at least at the species level, makes this less likely. Recovering viable spores from dried material provides a practicable, low-cost and low-tech route to obtain Morchella genetic material compared with purchasing authenticated cultures from specialized collections or spawn suppliers, which are costly and often limited in availability and diversity [7]. At the same time, the method overcomes the limitations encountered when wild collecting, i.e., regional and seasonal exclusivity as well as being time and labor intensive while providing no guarantee that Morchella specimens will be found. Spore extraction from dried commercial products therefore has real potential to widen the diversity of strains and species available to researchers, educators, and small-scale producers as well as citizen scientists and could accelerate strain discovery and help research into cultivation and commercialization of morel mushrooms. However, species confirmation of isolates using molecular tools is as necessary a part in strain isolation from dried mushrooms as from wild collected ones. Additionally, due to the prevalence of cultivated mushrooms on the market obtaining a sample of dried wild foraged morel mushrooms with a large variety of wild collected species and isolates may require significant product research and selection and was not achieved in this work. Future work will address two linked objectives. The first objective is to isolate Morchella from commercially obtained, dried wild foraged specimens, prioritizing samples with high expected species and strain level diversity. The second objective is to investigate the origin and authenticity of commercially sold dried morels by resolving discrepancies between the species declared on packaging and the results of ITS fungal barcoding, and by assessing the reliability of stated countries of origin.

Conclusions

Extraction and germination of viable Morchella spores from dried commercial morels offers a pragmatic, low‑cost avenue to expand access to genetic diversity for research and development allowing members of the scientific community to participate in morel research and development regardless of geographical location and with minimal budget and equipment. This approach however is likely not a substitute for authenticated culture collections when strain certainty, regulatory compliance, and repeatable production are required.
