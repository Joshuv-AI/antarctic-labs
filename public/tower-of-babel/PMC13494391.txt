Proper Usage of the Terms “Turbulent” and “Laminar” in Color-Flow Doppler
Authors: Curt G DeGroff
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Congratulations to Shirvani et al.1 for their interesting case report on finding via color-flow Doppler a rare case of extranodal-only cardiac Rosai-Dorfman disease (cRDD), a condition characterized by nonmalignant proliferation of histiocytes in extranodal cardiac sites. The case report explains how cRDD was suspected in a patient serendipitously during a routine echocardiogram that demonstrated increased velocities in one of the pulmonary veins, which led to further testing that finally led to the diagnosis of cRDD.

The issue I have with this case report is in the authors’ use of the term “turbulence.” There are 2 statements in the case report1 that raise concerns:

“Turbulent pattern of color-flow Doppler should raise suspicion for PV stenosis.”

“The finding of turbulent PV flow on color-flow Doppler should raise suspicion of PV stenosis with flow velocities >1.1 m/s indicating possible obstruction.”

The term “turbulence” should be used with caution in color-flow Doppler. The mosaic color patterns one sees on color-flow Doppler do not necessarily indicate the presence of turbulence. Color-flow Doppler encodes multiple fluid dynamic, and mosaic patterns may arise from a spectrum of flow conditions. These include laminar flow with velocity gradients, disturbed (nonturbulent) flow characterized by shear, separation, or curvature-related effects, complex but organized flow patterns such as swirl or recirculation, and velocity variance. Velocity variance represents a true physical dispersion of velocities within a sampled region but not necessarily turbulent flow. In addition, nonturbulent mosaic color patterns can also be produced from device settings and device phenomena (e.g., aliasing and backscatter-related spectral widening, respectively).

This then leads to the challenge of how to efficiently refer to the mosaic color patterns one sees on color-flow Doppler. I keep coming back to the terminology “disturbed flow” in place of “turbulent flow.” It's not perfect, but it does help acknowledge the complexity of turbulence along with color-flow Doppler's lack of ability to recognize and measure it accurately.
