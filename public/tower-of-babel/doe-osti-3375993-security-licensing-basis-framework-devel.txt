 ABSTRACT
 This report summarizes a technology-inclusive and performance-based
method to determine the physical security licensing basis for a commercial
nuclear reactor under the proposed 10 CFR 73.100 for Part 53 licensees. The
method focuses on identifying security functions, the contributing security
systems and programs to meet those functions, and identifying security events
that will provide the foundation for the security licensing basis. This includes a
risk-informed performance-based defense-in-depth adequacy method. This
method can also be employed to justify performance-based alternative measures
to traditional security requirements found in 10 CFR 73.55.

 Security Licensing Basis Framework Development
 1. INTRODUCTION
 The advent of advanced nuclear reactors presenting increased safety design and performance, coupled
with advances in risk-informed and performance-based licensing has spurred the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) to propose a performance-based rulemaking for the licensing of commercial nuclear
power plant security against the design basis threat (DBT) of radiological sabotage.
 Using NRC guidance on development of a performance-based regulation, a performance-based
framework is presented here to meet the proposed rulemaking language found in Rulemaking NRC-2019-
0062, “10 CFR Part 53: Risk-Informed, Technology-Inclusive Regulatory Framework for Advanced
Reactors” draft text for Title 10 of the Code of Federal Regulations (10 CFR) 73.100. Additionally, this
method could be used to justify alternative measures and exemptions from the existing 10 CFR 73.55 and
may assist in meeting the proposed rule language and interpretations found in NRC-2017-0227,
“Alternative Physical Security Requirements for Advanced Reactor” and NRC-2024-0167, “Law
Enforcement Response in Power Reactor Physical Protection Programs.”
 This report presents a method to develop and maintain a licensing basis for commercial nuclear power
physical security that is performance-based, scaled to the security risks of the facility, and allows licensee
flexibility in meeting the licensed requirements to demonstrate reasonable assurance of protection against
the DBT of radiological sabotage. The licensing basis will be centered around the development of several
bounding security scenarios that will drive the determination of performance metrics, while being
backstopped by the traditional NRC-observed force-on-force (FOF) performance testing. The number of
security scenarios should scale to the size of the facility (both in number of units and number of potential
adversary pathways) and the number of radiological sabotage targets. The security scenarios will
represent (1) an adversary timeline to cause a high radiological consequence that exceeds the dose limits
expected by the regulator and (2) a site protective strategy timeline that may rely on onsite response,
offsite response, or a blend of the two to neutralize the adversary or end the security event consistent with
the adversary interference precluded time (AIPT) concept.
 The use of defined scenarios with performance parameters associated with protective strategy
detection, delay, and response timeline would allow flexibility by the licensee to meet these parameters
individually or holistically within the licensing basis of the facility. The licensed performance times
would allow variations and changes that meet the same performance metric, and allow the licensing of an
established margin within the physical security system.
 The result of a licensee adopting this framework would be a licensing basis that provides operational
flexibility, and clear indication of when the licensing basis is affected (when the parameters are impacted
beyond the licensed values). This distinction would allow straightforward modifications and adjustments
under §50.54(p) or the proposed § 53.1565(d)(4), and a clear demarcation when a license amendment is
required consistent with § 50.90 or the proposed § 53.1510.
 2. BACKGROUND
 The arena of physical protection of special nuclear material at nuclear power reactors has been one of
responsiveness to threat environment impacts. The NRC security orders of the 2000s spurred by the 2001
terrorist attacks led to the codification of the new requirements in 10 CFR 73.55 and other pertinent
sections. The application of high-security principles was applied ad hoc to existing nuclear power
facilities to meet the requirements of protection against the DBT of radiological sabotage. The “New
Reactors” timeframe of the late 2000s through early 2010s brought new reactor designs, some of which
considered security within the design of the facility using the existing requirements found under 10 CFR
73.55. The NRC sought greater development of performance evaluation within physical security as early

as 2006 as evidenced by the later-canceled SECY-06-0204, “Security Assessment Requirements for New
Nuclear Power Reactor Designs” proposed rulemaking.
 Reflecting the development of four advanced reactor types in the 1990s, the NRC issued the 1998
Commission Policy Statement on the Regulation of Advanced Reactors. The policy statement focused on
early engagement between stakeholders, the design characteristics of advanced reactor designs, including
safety, reliability, and efficiency, and the need to provide timely feedback. The policy statement also
included points related to physical security, including the incorporation of physical security into the
design features of the plant, regularly improved guidance on security features, and improved threat
assessment approvals.
 The NRC security orders of the 2000s spurred by the 2001 terrorist attacks led to the codification of
the new requirements in 10 CFR 73.55 and other pertinent sections. The application of high-security
principles was applied ad hoc to existing nuclear power facilities to meet the requirements of protection
against the DBT of radiological sabotage. The “New Reactors” timeframe of the late 2000s through early
2010s brought new reactor designs, some of which considered security within the design of the facility
using the existing requirements found under 10 CFR 73.55. The NRC sought greater development of
performance evaluation within physical security as early as 2006 as evidenced by the later-canceled
SECY-06-0204, “Security Assessment Requirements for New Nuclear Power Reactor Designs” proposed
rulemaking.
 The advent of generation IV reactor designs seeking commercial licensing spurred Congress and the
NRC to support additional efforts to make the licensing process reflect the Nuclear Energy Innovation
and Modernization Act (NEIMA) of 2019. This provided new transparency requirements for the NRC.
The 2019 NEIMA Act had bipartisan support, setting the groundwork for new Part 53 licensing pathway
for advanced reactors. The key objectives of NEIMA are modernizing the NRC’s licensing process to
reflect a technology that includes a risk-informed and performance-based regulatory framework. The goal
is to improve cost transparency and external expertise in new nuclear technologies and promote the use of
innovative designs. The provision of NEIMA focuses on the use of risk-informed and performance-based
licensing evaluations, which
 In July 2024, the Accelerating Deployment of Versatile, Advanced Nuclear for Clean Energy
(ADVANCE) Act was signed into law. The ADVANCE Act covers a wide range of NRC activities with
the intent of providing “reasonable and targeted” measured to reduce the regulatory cost imposed on the
advanced reactor industry. The act focuses on including implementation initiatives for efficient, timely
and predictable license application reviews, expedited review processes, and developing strategies and
guidance for microreactors. The result of NEIMA is the advancement of several NRC performance-based
rulemakings, including specific considerations for the performance-based licensing of physical security.
 2.1. Physical Security Rulemakings
 To comply with legislation, requests from industry, and NRC-initiated goals to increase the use of
performance-based regulation, the NRC has initiated three rulemakings over the last several years.
 NRC-2017-0227, “Alternative Physical Security Requirements for Advanced Reactors” was initiated
in 2017 to provide a pathway to credit offsite response in response to a security event. The draft proposed
rule for comment was published October 23, 2024 and provided an alternative pathway for compliance
with 10 CFR 73.55 if the licensee met performance parameters associated with the postulated offsite
release resulting from a security event. Accompanying the draft proposed rule language were two draft
regulatory guides (DGs), DG-5071, “Target Set Identification and Development for Nuclear Power
Reactors,” (draft Regulatory Guide 5.81 Rev. 2), and NRC, DG-5072, “Guidance for Alternative Physical
Security Requirements for Small Modular Reactors and Non-Light-Water Reactors,” (Draft Regulatory
Guide 5.90), July 2024. DG-5071 would provide updated guidance on the determination of radiological
sabotage for advanced nuclear power reactors as a radiological release from any source. DG-5071 sets out
the expectations for consequence analysis to support radiological sabotage determinations and the

 2

methods to determine and support adversary interference preclusion time, offsite response, and the
crediting of operator actions during a security event.
 NRC-2019-0062, “10 CFR Part 53: Risk-Informed, Technology-Inclusive Regulatory Framework for
Advanced Reactors” provides an alternative performance-based framework in the draft 10 CFR 73.100
for Part 53 licensees to license the physical security of a commercial nuclear power plant. In addition,
DG-5076, “Guidance for Technology-Inclusive Requirements for Physical Protection of Licensed
Activities at Commercial Nuclear Plants,” (Draft Regulatory Guide 5.97) was also released to provide
guidance to meet the proposed 10 CFR 73.100.
 NRC-2024-0167, “Law Enforcement Response in Power Reactor Physical Protection Programs” is an
interpretive rulemaking that would provide the justification for the use of offsite response for any (not just
advanced) commercial nuclear power plants licensed under 10 CFR 50 or 52.
 These three rulemakings open the door to many performance-based methods of compliance for
licensees. The proposed 10 CFR 73.100 provides the clearest and broadest possibility of allowing
performance-based methods to meet the proposed regulation. The proposed guidance in DG-5076 did not
provide a robust description on how to meet the proposed rule in a performance-based manner and often
provided prescriptive expectations for redundancy in functionality. This report examines NRC guidance
on the development of performance-based regulation and applies it to the proposed rulemakings above
(predominately 10 CFR 73.100) to create a performance-based method to identify and maintain the
physical security licensing basis, providing flexibility for licensees and transparency and traceability for
regulators.
 2.2. Performance-based Regulation
 NRC published guidance for the development of a performance-based alternative to prescriptive
regulations in NUREG/BR-0303, “Guidance for Performance-Based Regulation.” The intent of
NUREG/BR-0303 was to inform the NRC staff in a consistent manner to consider a performance-based
framework and discuss the best practices and issues with developing performance-based regulations.
 NUREG/BR-0303 lay out a five-step process to provide information to define a performance-based
approach:
1. Defining the regulatory issue and its context
2. Identifying the safety functions
3. Identifying safety margins
4. Selecting performance parameters and criteria
5. Formulating a performance-based alternative.
 Section 3 of this report will follow the above steps to define the information needed to define the
performance-based framework that is ultimately captured in Section 3.5.
 An important aspect of performance-based regulation is the identification of performance parameters,
indicators that can demonstrate the performance of the function and allow issues to be identified and
corrective actions to be taken to reduce or eliminate delicacies. Figure 1 is from NUREG/BR-0303 and
outlines how the objectives hierarchy relate to the fulfillment of a fundamental objective. In this case, we
will consider it fulfillment of security functions.

  3. PERFORMANCE-BASED PHYSICAL SECURITY FRAMEWORK
 This framework follows the process described in NUREG/BR-0303 to define the regulatory issue, the
fundamental objectives, performance parameters, margins, and ultimately describing the performance-
based alternative. This framework was designed to demonstrate compliances with the draft proposed
10 CFR 73.100 and also provide a basis for performance-based alternatives to 10 CFR 73.55.
 3.1. Defining the Regulatory Issue and Context
 Physical security requirements have traditionally been prescriptively determined, with a performance-
based assessment (limited-scope or FOF exercises) to ensure compliance. The NRC security program
regulations found in 10 CFR 73.55 provide minimum expectations for the implementation of a physical
security plan and the methods of compliance. New commercial reactor facilities pose new deployment
and operational paradigms, and the NRC has sought to provide flexibility to reactor designers through the
development of performance-based physical security rulemakings. The proposed Part 53 and Alternative
Physical Security Requirements for Advanced Reactors rulemakings provide alternative methods of
compliance for protection against the DBT of radiological sabotage, without prescriptive requirements.
The NRC-proposed methods that could be leveraged to meet the proposed rule in DG-5072 and DG-5076
did not define the specific framework to license and maintain compliance with the proposed rule
language. Therefore, a need arises to frame physical protection program compliance within a
performance-based security framework found in the proposed rulemaking in a holistic manner that can

 5

justify initial licensing, support continued compliance under operation, while allowing flexibility by the
operator in meeting the performance-based requirements.
 3.2. Identifying the Security Functions
 When designing and defining security requirements it is important to identify the functions that
makeup a physical protection system (PPS). A PPS consists of three key functions detection, which
includes assessment and communication, delay, and response. PPSs have been described as a three-legged
stool. For a PPS to be effective at mitigating any nuclear security event, the system must be equipped
with detection, delay, and response. If the PPS is missing one of these three functions or one of these
functions is inadequate, the PPS will fail at its intended function of mitigating nuclear security events.
This is similar to a three-legged stool with a missing or broken leg.

Figure 3. Functions of a PPS.

 The following sections will discuss and identify the functions of a PPS and the associated
performance requirements that are used to evaluate the effectiveness of these functions.
3.2.1. Physical Protection System
 A PPS must be designed with security goals in mind to ensure that the PPS mitigates or prevents an
undesired act. In nuclear security, the ultimate goal of a security system is to prevent the theft of nuclear
material and to mitigate or prevent an act of sabotage that could lead to a radiological release. The
descriptions of PPS functions will be described in the context of preventing a radiological release from a
small modular reactor or microreactor. The figure below highlights the basic functions of a PPS.

 6

Figure 4. PPS functions.

 It is important to note that the three functions of detection, delay, and response can be implemented
with technology, people, or procedures. Often, a PPS is designed and implemented by using the three
functions and a combination of technology, people, and procedures to achieve the desired outcome of the
PPS. The three functions of a PPS are seen as subsystems of a PPS. A PPS is considered a complex
system and requires a systems engineering approach.
3.2.2.      Detection
 Intrusion detection systems (IDSs) are used for many purposes. IDSs consist of exterior intrusion
detection sensors, interior intrusion detection sensors, alarm assessment, contraband detection, access
controls, and alarm communication and display (AC&D) systems. There are many factors that can be
evaluated in detection that can be used as performance indicators for a PPS. These factors include:
• Probability of detection
• Probability of assessment
• Assessment time
• Communication time.
 Additionally, many regulations and best practices have been in place to evaluate the performance of
detection systems. These regulatory requirements will be referenced throughout this section.
3.2.3.      Delay
 Delay barriers and systems play an important role in the overall effectiveness of a PPS. Delay barriers
can be used to slow down an adversary to increase the overall adversary task time to complete an act of
sabotage, and used to channel adversaries into advantageous locations that can improve the ability of the
response force to neutralize the adversary. Therefore, delay barriers can be beneficial to both the
probability of interruption and neutralization.
 Delay can be any barrier, distance, or element that an adversary must defeat, cross, or bypass to gain
access to a system, structure, and component (SSC) location that if sabotaged could lead to a radiological
release. An important note when evaluating delay times is that delay times can only be calculated as part

 7

of the total adversary attack timeline if the delay barrier occurs after detection. For delay barriers to
increase the adversary task time, the PPS must first be able to detect (sense and assess) that an adversary
is attempting to attack the facility and attempt to cause a radiological release.
 Figure 5 below is a hypothetical example of how delay barriers are incorporated into the overall
adversary task timeline. As can be seen from the figure below, each element, barrier, or space that the
adversary must cross is calculated as part of the overall adversary task timeline. In the scenario below,
fences, doors, moving between security layers, accessing the target, and sabotaging the target are
incorporated into the overall adversary task timeline.

Figure 5. Example of delay and adversary timelines.

 An important factor to consider in delay is that the delay is only effective after detection has occurred.
If detection has not occurred, then it is not possible for the PPS and the PPS personnel to know that the
adversary is attempting to penetrate the facility. Delay timelines are only accounted for in the overall PPS
timeline when it occurs after detection.
3.2.4.      Response
 The response force is the third critical function of a PPS. Response plays the critical role of the
facility being able to interrupt and neutralize an adversary force before they complete their malicious act.
The response force must interrupt the adversary and then neutralize the adversary for the system to be
effective. Interruption is the successful arrival of the response force at an appropriate location with
enough responders to stop the adversary. Neutralization is the ability of the response force after
interruption to prevent completion of the adversary action. Neutralization is achieved when the response
force kills, captures, or causes the adversary to flee before the adversary can complete the task. The
overall effectiveness of the response force depends on many factors, including communication training,
equipment, experience, adversary capabilities, tactics, knowledge, procedures, and use-of-force policies.
 The response force must have reliable and timely communication from a central alarm station (CAS)
operator about a potential nuclear security event, a description of the cause of the security event, and

 8

potential movements of the adversary that may have caused the alarm. Without communication from the
CAS about an alarm generated at the facility, responders cannot be activated to interrupt the adversary
force, and without interruption, the response force cannot neutralize the adversary force. Additional
information, such as descriptions of adversaries from the CAS and movements of the adversaries, can
help to improve the effectiveness of the response force to interrupt and neutralize the adversary forces.
This communication also ensures that the response force arrives to the correct location to interrupt and
neutralize adversaries.
 Once the response force is notified of alarms, the responders can then move to a location to interrupt
the adversary before they complete their malicious act. This total time from response notification to
arrival at the interruption location is a key factor in determining the effectiveness of the PPS.
 Once the responders arrive at the interruption point, there are additional factors that can influence the
overall effectiveness of the response force to neutralize the adversary force. These measures include the
equipment of the responders and the adversaries, knowledge of tactics, physical fitness, training, and use-
of-force policies. The first factor is equipment used, if the response is less equipped and has fewer
responders than the adversary, the responders may be less effective at neutralizing the adversary force.
Knowledge of tactics plays a key role in the responders’ ability to execute contingency plans and response
plans to nuclear security events to ensure the adversaries can be neutralized. Physical fitness and training
play an important role in the response force’s ability to execute the contingency plans to respond to a
nuclear security event. Training also ensures that the responders have the ability with their equipment to
properly neutralize the adversary. The final factor that can influence the effectiveness of the response
force is the use-of-force policies that are in place for the responders. Use-of-force for armed responders at
nuclear facilities can be based on the legal authority of the responders, rules governing the conditions for
the use-of-force, and the types of methods that can be implemented for use-of-force.

 3.3. Selecting Performance Parameters and Criteria
 Following NUREG/BR-0303, the selection of performance parameters that satisfy the viability
guidelines and regulatory issue as described. NUREG/BR-0303 states:
 Generally, a performance-based regulatory alternative needs to:
 1. allocate performance across relevant functions, systems, or barriers, in order to assess
 whether the target safety objectives are satisfied
 2. then implement that allocation of performance which entails identifying the steps to
 be taken by licensees and/or NRC to make the performance allocation “come true” in practice
 …Part of implementation is confirmation of ongoing performance.
 The Advisory Committee on Reactor Safeguards recommends setting the performance criteria as high
as a level as practicable to allow flexibility. The intent is that the performance parameters define the
success of the system and identify opportunities for corrective action.
 Corrective action could be temporary (compensatory measures) until the performance meets the
criteria, a change to the facility design or operational procedures to address the inadequacy of the current
facility to meet the licensed performance parameters, or a license change justified through analysis to
establish new performance parameter values.
 The following is a discussion of physical security hierarchy, and how performance parameters can be
identified that tie to regulatory compliance.
Hierarchy
 The regulatory framework guidance provided in NUREG/BR-0303 adapts well to the space for the
proposed performance-based physical security licensing framework given in the 10 CFR Part 53
rulemaking § 73.100, “Technology-inclusive requirements for physical protection of licensed activities at
commercial nuclear plants against radiological sabotage.”
 § 73.100(b)(1) provides the general performance objective as:
 The licensee must establish, implement, and maintain a physical protection program and a
 security organization, which will have as their objective to provide reasonable assurance that
 activities involving special nuclear material are not inimical to the common defense and security
 and do not constitute an unreasonable risk to the public health and safety.
 § 73.100(b)(2) defines that § 73.100(b)(1) is satisfied by the prevention of radiological sabotage and
that capability must be maintained at all times, with defense in depth (DID), to achieve the performance
requirements.
 § 73.100(b)(3) provides performance requirements for the physical protection program in (i) through
(vi) for intrusion detection, intrusion assessment, security communication, security response vehicle
bomb assaults, and access control portals.
 § 73.100(b)(4) through (12) provide other supporting or programmatic requirements for the security
of the facility.

 The irreversible release timing defines the requirement for the site security timeline. The site security

3.3.1. Irreversible Release Timing
 The parameter associated with the irreversible release timing will ultimately define the required
performance of the PPS sum of parts associated with detection (including assessment), delay, and
response (communication and interdiction). This parameter results from the target set analysis as
described in DG-5071.
 Target sets are the site-specific combinations of equipment and actions whose sabotage by an
adversary leads to an unacceptable offsite release. a The target set identification process will screen out
those targets that do not meet the definition of a target set (could not result in a release in exceedance of
the dose reference values), or those targets that are beyond the capability of the DBT adversary. The
remaining list of target combinations are called “achievable targets” and are defined as those targets that
must be considered in the protective strategy, with each target set having an irreversible release time.
 The irreversible release time for each target set is determined from the shortest time from the initial
adversary action to the point that represents the last point that operator actions can reverse sabotage
actions to prevent a significant release, and then that time walked back to the point operators need to
initiate their actions. The initial irreversible release time is computed though the determination of
adversary traversal times, breach times, and action times to complete the sabotage of all target elements,
plus the progress of the sabotage event until the event is deemed irreversible. Operator actions to reverse a
significant release may involve actions that prevent a release or mitigate the consequences below required
dose consequence values. For sabotage events that are irreversible or unmitigable, the irreversible release
timing would be the point of sabotage of the last target element of a target set. Initial irreversible release
time determinations should not include any active components of the physical protection program (i.e.,
response) to provide a baseline determination for the screening of target sets against the site security
bounding time.
 The physical protection program could implement earlier detection, delay elements, and response
personnel to extend the irreversible release timeframe from the values determined from the target set
analysis. Modifications to facility safety systems, structures, and components, or operational emergency
procedures may also extend the irreversible release time.
 The neutralization time is the time associated with the detection, delay, and response of the physical
protection program and represents the time from detection of an adversary to the conclusion of a security
event through neutralization of adversary forces. This timeframe defines the site-only response to prevent
radiological sabotage. Prevention of sabotage through delay and any iteration of offsite response can be
defined though the AIPT.
 The AIPT time can be determined following guidance in DG-5072 to determine the timeframe that
offsite response can conservatively be assumed to arrive at the licensed site and control the facility,
allowing operator recovery actions to occur if necessary, as a bounding value for the entire site. The site
protective strategy must be designed to protect the site until this occurs, either through delay and or
neutralization of the adversary. Shorter individual target set and security event security bounding times
can also be determined following DG-5072 to inform specific security event timelines.

a. DG-5071 specifically defines a target set for non-LWRs and SMRs as “the minimum combination of equipment, operator

3.3.2. Site Security Timeline
 The site security timeline is the site response to the detection of adversary action. This timeframe is
composed of the elements of detection (including assessment and communication), delay, and response.
The site protective strategy may rely on site passive and active delay features (to include response
personnel) to the facilitate the response by the offsite response forces to arrive and secure the site. The
site security timeline is concluded when the adversaries are neutralized or the security bounding time has
elapsed and determines the end of the security event.
 This parameter is thus comprised by the sum of detection, delay, and response. These parameters can
be treated and maintained separately. Treated separately, these values can be monitored closely for
performance indicators.
3.3.2.1.      Detect
 Detection is the process in which a physical sensor in the deployed environment first senses a change
in the environment. Once this sensor identifies this change, a signal is sent through a communication
network to the AC&D system that a CAS operator is staffing. Once the signal arrives, an alarm is reported
to the CAS and should be audibly and visibly announced to the CAS operator. Once the CAS operator
receives the alarm notification, they use video feeds, either live or recorded from closed-circuit television
(CCTV) cameras. The video feeds then allow the CAS operator to determine whether the sensor has
alarmed due to an adversary incursion or due to a nuisance or a false alarm. Once the assessment is made,
the CAS operator communicates the cause of the alarm to the appropriate person in the security
organization to respond to the alarm cause. The figure below shows the process that is used for alarms to
be generated and assessed in the CAS. This process utilizes people, technology, and procedures to

considered a successful adversary attack scenario. This applies for all potential targets and the time it

individuals. If a breach only requires a small penetration to cause a release or cause water to drain, then
the breach may not be as large as the breach necessary for people to pass through.

adversary to bypass barriers at the facility. Bypassing a barrier considers an adversary that is not
attempting to penetrate through a barrier but circumvent the barrier. An example of this is adversaries
who face a chain-link fence at a facility. The adversaries may not attempt to breach or penetrate the fence,
but may choose to climb the fence or tunnel underneath the fence. These times similarly can be collected
through performance testing, databases, or standards.

target at the facility that could lead to a radiological release. Once all these times have been identified,
they can be incorporated to develop the adversary task timeline and then used for comparison against the
PPS timeline. Determining delay times plays an important role in evaluating the overall effectiveness of
the PPS.

3.3.2.3.      Response
 There are key performance parameters that are used to evaluate the effectiveness of the response
force. These factors are also integral in evaluating the overall effectiveness of the PPS. Evaluation of the
response force is used in both the probability of interruption and the probability of neutralization. Both
are key factors in evaluating the overall effectiveness of the PPS. Unlike detection and delay, testing and
collection of response force performance criteria is taken from conducting tests of the responders for a
facility based on the protection strategy, targets to be protected, and contingency plans implemented to
defend the facility against a DBT attack.
 Testing of the response force can be conducted through limited-scope performance tests (LSPT), time
motion studies (TMS), alarm response assessment performance test (ARAPT), enhance limited-scope
performance tests (ELSPT), and FOF exercises. Additionally, tests also include testing of responder
proficiencies with weapons and the use of combat simulation tools. These methods can be utilized to
evaluate the performance of the responders and within overall evaluations of the effectiveness of the
systems.
 One of the first performance metrics utilized to evaluate the response force is communication time
from the CAS to the responders, to respond to a nuclear security event at the facility. This time is used to
determine how fast the response force is notified of a potential security event and begins to start the
response ready time. Once the response force is communicated to about the cause of a security alarm at
the facility, the response team must gather equipment (if needed) and begin other necessary steps for
interrupting the adversary force. Depending on the strategy implemented, another time that must be
evaluated is the time that it takes the response force to then deploy and arrive at a location where they can
interrupt the adversary force.
 These above times for the response force can be evaluated by the facility using many of the testing
methods described above. LSPTs can be utilized to determine specific times and proficiencies of
responders for gathering and donning equipment needed in a security event. These tests can include
proper loading of a weapon, proper methods for charging a weapon, etc. LSPTs are meant to test specific
portions of the response force and response procedures. A TMS can be utilized to determine specific
response times from a starting location to a finishing location.
3.3.2.4.      AIPT
 The AIPT represents the time for an offsite response force to respond and secure the site against an
adversary. We can treat the AIPT time as the end of the security event, similar to neutralization; as at that
point, any operator actions may occur unimpeded. The AIPT methodology would apply for a private
response force or local law enforcement. DG-5072 Appendix C proposes the framework to determine the
site-specific AIPT for a facility. These steps would include:
1. Alarm assessment and communication time
2. Response time (travel and assembly)
3. Mission preparedness time (briefing, planning, and any rehearsals)
4. Mission execution time
5. Additional action time (operator actions).
 For this framework, step 5 would not be included within the AIPT determination, thus the AIPT
would be a site-wide value associated with securing the facility. The operator actions associated with
preventing or mitigating the release would be included in the target-set-specific irreversible release timing
values.

3.3.3. Detection Probability and Confidence
 Probability of detection (PD) is the likelihood that an adversary action can be sensed and assessed.
Probability of detection is a key performance metric for a PPS and is directly impactful to the probability
of interruption. Probability of detection is determined by both the sensors probability of sensing (PS) and
the likelihood that the alarm station operator can correctly assess the cause of the alarm (PA). The
equation to calculate the probability of detection can be seen below.
 𝑃𝑃𝐷𝐷 = 𝑃𝑃𝑆𝑆 𝑥𝑥 𝑃𝑃𝐴𝐴
 Probability of sensing is a function of the sensor’s performance that is used in the operational
environment. The probability of sensing is the likelihood that the sensor identifies a change in the
physical environment based on the sensor characteristics and performance. It is important to note that
while probability of detection is defined as a function of probability of sensing and probability of
assessment, probability of assessment is generally assumed to be one. This assumption has been made on
the basis that probability of assessment is extremely difficult to evaluate. The reason for this is the
following factors play into a determining probability of assessment:
• AC&D configuration
• Recorded video feeds (auto call up or self-selected)
• Pre and post-alarm recorded video
• Integrated systems or separated systems
• Human factors.
 These factors make probability of assessment extremely difficult to determine and site-specific. This
has resulted in probability of assessment to be assumed as one, and therefore, probability of detection is
often simplified to be taken as the probability of sensing for an individual sensor.
 The U.S. DOE requires sensors to have a 90 percent probability of detection with a 95 percent
confidence. b In addition to this probability of sensing, the DOE requires that this be proven annually in
accordance to performance testing standards. The NRC requires that sensors also have a 90 percent
probability of detection at a 95 percent confidence level. c. The NRC Regulatory Guide (RG) 5.44 requires
that sensors be operationally tested once every 7 days and that performance tests be conducted every 90-
days. Once these testing requirements are known, each sensor must then be evaluated to determine
whether it meets this 90 percent probability of detection at a 95 percent confidence level, which is also
known as 90/95 testing. Generally, to determine whether a sensor meets this 90/95 rule, 30 tests are
conducted across three different adversary defeat methods. These adversary defeat methods include
walking, crawling, and running. However, sensors should be tested for all potential adversary defeat
methods to include jumping. Once these 30 tests have been conducted, the probability of detection can be
determined. The table below outlines the number of trials and the number of failures that allows the user
to identify the probability of detection based on the confidence level. As can be seen from the table

of this, these types of performance tests are generally called a 30-for-30 test.

3.3.4.2. Probability of Neutralization
 Probability of neutralization (PN) is the likelihood that the response force is able to neutralize the
adversary before their malicious act can be accomplished. Neutralization can occur when the adversary
decides to give up their tasks and retreat from the facility, when the adversary is unable to complete their
intended tasks, when the adversary is arrested by the response force, or when the response force kills or
renders all adversaries unable to complete the malicious act of sabotage.

numerical table to compare probabilities of neutralization. For high-security applications, such as nuclear
power facilities, this method is not recommended.

number of responders involved in the engagement. There are many assumptions incorporated into a
simple numerical table. These assumptions include but are not limited to:
• Responders and adversaries are trained the same
• Responders and adversaries have the same weapon systems (e.g., 5.56-mm ammunition)
• Responders and adversaries have the same protective equipment (e.g., body armor)
• Responders and adversaries engage each other from the same shooting posture (e.g., crouching,
 prone, standing, running)

 The process for using combat simulation tools is:
1. Modeling Adversary Behavior:
 - Adversary Profiles: Simulation tools often include predefined adversary profiles that describe
 potential attackers’ capabilities, tactics, and strategies. These profiles can be customized to reflect
 specific threats relevant to a facility.
 - Attack Scenarios: Users can create various attack scenarios based on the adversary profiles,

 theft of nuclear material or sabotage).
2. PPS Modeling:
 - Facility Layout: The simulation tool incorporates a detailed representation of the nuclear facility,
 including its layout, security features (e.g., fences, cameras, guards), and critical assets (e.g.,
 reactors, storage areas).
 - Security Measures: Users can input various security measures and protocols in place, such as
 access control, surveillance systems, and response force capabilities.
3. Simulation Engine:
 - Event Simulation: The core of the tool is a simulation engine that executes the modeled scenarios.
 It simulates the interactions between adversaries and the PPS, taking into account the timing,
 probabilities of detection, and response actions.
 - Stochastic Processes: Many tools use stochastic processes to account for the inherent
 uncertainties in both adversary behavior and security responses. This allows for the modeling of
 random events, such as detection failures or delays in response.
4. Data Analysis and Metrics:
 -  Performance Metrics: The simulation generates various performance metrics, such as the
 probability of detection, probability of neutralization, and overall effectiveness of the PPS against
 the simulated attacks.
 - Statistical Analysis: Users can analyze the results statistically to understand the likelihood of
 success for different attack scenarios and the effectiveness of various security measures.
5. Visualization and Reporting:
 - Graphical User Interface (GUI): Most simulation tools feature a user-friendly GUI that allows
 users to visualize the facility layout, adversary movements, and security responses in real-time.
 - Reporting Tools: After running simulations, the tools often provide detailed reports summarizing
 the findings, including strengths and weaknesses of the PPS, recommended improvements, and
 potential vulnerabilities.
 Once the scenarios have been simulated, a statistically significant number of times the overall
probability of neutralization can be determined. It is important that while the number of simulations must
be statistically significant, the combat simulations should be based on identified and credible adversary
attack scenarios that have been developed. Credible adversary attack scenarios should be developed
within the bounds of the DBT and the capabilities that are present and can be used by the response force.

 3.4. Identifying the Security Margins
 A necessary component in a performance-based framework is margin in performance to
consequences. Fundamentally, a standard in physical security is the planning and application of
compensatory measures, a baseline application of DID, and a margin to consequence. In this framework,
this margin can also be manifested and quantified in two additional ways: (1) DID of the PPS and (2)
timeline margin within the site security timeline to consequence.
3.4.1. Compensatory Measures
 One of the many factors considered in the design of a PPS is the proper design and placement of
compensatory measures into the PPS. Compensatory measures are additional security measures to include
technologies, procedures, and policies that are put in place to mitigate vulnerabilities due to primary PPS
component failures. According to 10 CFR 73.55, licensees must:
• Implement compensatory measures upon discovery or suspicion that any access control device may
 have been compromised e
• Implement compensatory measures that ensure the effectiveness of onsite physical protection
 program when there is a failure or degraded operations of security-related components f
• Identify criteria and measures to compensate for degraded or inoperable equipment, systems and
 components; compensatory measures must provide a level of protection that is equivalent to the
 protection that was provided by the degraded or inoperable equipment, systems or components;
 compensatory measures must be implemented within specific timeframes necessary. g
 Additionally, DG-5076 describes that compensatory measures should be applied for IDSs and
intrusion assessment systems. Additionally, DG-5076 states:
• “The licensee shall establish and implement timely compensatory measures for degraded or
 inoperable security SSCs to meet the requirements in 10 CFR 73.100. Compensatory measures shall
 provide a level of protection that is equivalent to the protection that was provided before the
 degradation or inoperability of the security systems, equipment, or components.”
• “The licensee shall document processes and procedures and maintain records for implementing the
 corrective actions; compensatory measures; and maintenance, inspection, testing, and calibration of
 security SSCs.”
 When designing a PPS, compensatory measures are a key factor in the overall designing and planning
for a PPS. Compensatory measures can be implemented with additional technologies or through the use
of onsite security personnel. For example, when a sensor at the perimeter of a facility is taken offline for
maintenance, loses power, or fails for any reason, a compensatory measure is put in place which could
include:
• Using an additional line of sensors that performs at the same probability of detection as the first line
 of sensors. (In application, this looks like two lines of detection along the perimeter if regulatory
 requirements only specify one line of detection).
• Placing a member of the security team (i.e., armed security officer at the location) to attack as a form
 of detection at the location.
• Using CCTV camera or pan-tilt-zoom cameras overlooking the down sensor location with a member
 from the alarm station to constantly watch the area.

3.4.2. Defense-in-Depth Analysis
 The proposed 10 CFR 73.100 provides some requirements pertaining to DID:
 73.100 (b)(2)(ii): Provide defense in depth in achieving performance requirements through
 the integration of engineered systems, administrative controls, and management measures.
 73.100 (b)(3)(iv) Security Response: The physical protection program must be designed to
 provide timely security response to interdict and neutralize adversary attacks up to and including
 the design basis threat of radiological sabotage. The physical protection program must be
 designed to provide layers of security response, with each layer assuring that a single failure does
 not result in the loss of capability to neutralize the design basis threat adversary. Structures,
 systems, and components relied on for delay functions must be designed to allow for timely
 security responses to adversary attacks with adequate defense in depth. h
 Thus, the proposed requirements indicate that detection, delay, and response elements exhibit DID,
and DID for security has not been well defined. Taking the above language at its word, the offsite
response to any site could be interpreted to meet the requirements of response DID to neutralize the
adversary. However, a robust understanding of DID needs to be mutually understood to appropriately
scale the performance-based framework to prevent overly conservative assumptions of DID equating to
redundancy.

 It would be the most appropriate to provide a flexible framework for licensees to choose their own

3.4.2.1. Redundancy Method
 The most conservative method to demonstrate DID is redundancy. Though this may be impractical
for some aspects of the PPS (e.g., a redundant armed responder at every post), a licensee may demonstrate
DID through redundancy for some elements of the PPS. Redundant detection and assessment hardware,
alarm stations, locks, or delay barriers may factor within the planned PPS for other reasons (e.g., easy
compensatory measure recovery, operational program execution) and may be credited for their
contribution to DID. This simple method is not performance-based but provides a simple way to
demonstrate compliance.
3.4.2.2. Sensitivity Analysis Method
 Current methods of demonstration of DID within security use a sensitivity analysis. Individual
elements of the PPS are deleted, and the impact of their loss is realized through modeling and simulation,
tabletop exercises (TTXs), or limited-performance testing. The PPS is tested to see if it is still effective
without that PPS element.
 This method of DID demonstration is also conservative, as loss of PPS elements would be addressed
through compensatory measures, and security should not consider random failures during security events.
3.4.2.3. Risk-Informed and Performance-Based DID Adequacy Method
 Within this performance-based framework, sensitivity analysis can be quantified and provides a
method to risk-inform DID determination. Sensitivity analysis could highlight those elements of the PPS
whose loss contributes the greatest negative impact to the security of the site. In the framework proposed
here, the impact could be quantified by changes to the site security timeline. In this way, the loss of those
PPS elements would be quantified, and the application of DID elements could be risk-informed according
to the time contributions.
 Those PPS elements that contribute longer extensions to the site security timeline could have DID
applied to reduce the impact of the PPS element. In this way, security could be aligned with the methods
endorsed by NRC in RG 1.233 of the Nuclear Energy Institute (NEI) 18-04 Rev. 1, Licensing
Modernization Project (LMP) methodology.

Programmatic DID
 Programmatic DID is described in NEI 18-04 Rev. 1 that it “provides a means to incorporate special
treatment i while designing, manufacturing, constructing, operating, maintaining, testing, and inspecting
the plant and the associated processes to ensure there is reasonable assurance that the predicted
performance can be achieved throughout the lifetime of the plant.” Programmatic DID scales treatment
(e.g., inspections, testing) to the risk associated with the component. Security programs have previously
applied conservative prescriptive requirements to the testing requirements associated with security SSCs.
Under the performance-based framework, performance-based testing frequencies and success criteria
could be used by the licensee to propose alternative risk-informed and performance-based criteria based
on the risk contribution according to the sensitivity analysis. Those security elements with DID security
timelines greater than the irreversible release timeline should have additional programmatic requirements
to ensure functionality. This may be fulfilled by increased maintenance, testing, for SSCs, or higher
performance requirements or training for programmatic PPS components.

security is already regarded as high, given the expectation of preventing radiological sabotage, and
performance of detection, delay, and response elements. The security program is required to consider
random failures and deficiencies and apply temporary measures to meet the security function in a
degraded condition. Compensatory measures are applied to any function of the security program in a
degraded condition and allows flexible temporary resolution of these issues. The security program applies
compensatory measures in a DID manner to fulfill the function of reasonable assurance against
radiological sabotage.

3.4.3. Timeline Margin
 Following the explanation of irreversible release timing provided in Section 3.3.1, the concept of
security margin is given here. Given the security timeline shown in Figure 17 where there is a time delay
between the target sabotage event and the irreversible release timing, the security margin can be defined
as the time difference between when security event ends and the irreversible release timing. The larger
this margin is, the more confidence can be had that an undesirable release can be prevented considering
all the uncertainties in the attack and response scenarios. In a single attack scenario, the margin is a
singular value. However, if this scenario is repeated multiple times accounting for uncertainties in the
attack speed, detection probability, delay period, communication and assessment, and neutralization
probability, the margin will have an uncertainty band in which some of the margin may be negative
indicating the failure of physical protection.
 The security margin is a useful concept in the risk-informed performance-based security framework
because it informs licensees on various flexible ways to meet the required timeframe of the irreversible
release timing. For example, licensees may increase margin and therefore protection confidence by:
1. Delaying the irreversible release timing. This may be done by utilizing preventive safety measures
 after a component is sabotaged to slow down the progression of process events that leads to
 irreversible damage, for example by derating reactor power or putting it in a hot standby mode,
 thereby shifting irreversible release timing to the right in Figure 11.
2. Improving security response capabilities to shift the ending of security event to the left of Figure 11.
 This may be done by refining strategies and positioning for a faster neutralization thereby shortening
 the armed engagement period, reducing response team travel time for a faster deployment that also
 helps with strategic positioning prior to engagement, reducing intrusion assessment time, and/or
 sensor positioning to allow earlier detections.
 These various methods can be implemented in combination with each other and be tested using
computer modeling and simulations for many number of times to ensure a sufficient security margin is
achieved. A goal for the security program is a percent of margin compared to the irrevesible release
timeline of at least 10%. Such a goal provides confidence in the site protective strategy for protecting
against the DBT of radiological sabotage. An example would be a irreversible release timing of 60 min.
The site protective strategy results in neutralization of the adverary at 40 min, giving a margin of 20 min,
or 33% (20 min/60 min).

 3.5. Formulating a Performance-Based Alternative
 The following is a demonstration of a performance-based method of compliance consistent with the
above approach. This method is applicable for the determination of the performance-based licensing of
PPS detection, delay, and response, either as individual elements or for an entire PPS.
3.5.1. Target Sets
 The licensee will determine the target sets through the target set determination process, as described
in RG 5.81 Rev. 1 or DG-5071 as Rev. 2 when published. Allowed operator actions will be applied to
determine the list of achievable target sets. This will include the determination of the timeline associated
with each target set. Target sets that are deemed the most desirable and those with the shortest timelines
would likely be the best candidates to evaluate for the licensing basis. It is noted that all achievable
targets would fall within the requirements for protection and may be selected for demonstration during an
audit or inspection action by the regulator.
3.5.2. Design Physical Protection System Detection and Communication
 The PPS design of the detection, assessment, and communication will support determining the
communication to offsite response organizations. Once the detection and communication timeline is
established, the AIPT can be determined. Iterations on the design of SSCs and procedures can lead to
updates to the AIPT and should be captured through the PPS design.

 34

3.5.3. Offsite Response Timeline
 The site detection and communication and offsite response timeline will need to be determined to
establish the site AIPT, as well as any event-specific security bounding time to support PPS design. The
AIPT would be determined according to the guidance given in DG-5072.
3.5.4. Licensing Basis Security Scenarios
 The initial PPS design can be started early in the design phase, before full target set characterization
consistent with RG 5.81. Initial delay values (not crediting any programmatic response) can, with the
target set timelines, determine the initial values for the irreversible release timing. The design of the PPS
using the Design Evaluation and Process Outline (DEPO) methodology will then update the irreversible
release timing associated with the target sets with each iteration of the PPS design.
 The licensing basis security scenarios can be determined from the irreversible release timing from
each individual target set and then down-selected given the following considerations:
• Shortest target set time to release (as determined as part of the target set identification process).
• Most desirable target sets.
• Directional attacks from different sides of the facility in manner best for adversary (e.g., stealth,
 shortest path, greatest cover or concealment). This would inform bounding values for adversary
 traversal times through passive security barriers and task times.
• Attack methods that employ variations to the DBT, to include number, function, and methods of
 adversaries, as well as the use of applicable vehicles and explosives, cyberattacks, and insider actions.
 This would inform bounding values for adversary traversal times through passive security barriers
 and task times.
 Ten should be the minimum number of representative irreversible release timeline scenarios to form
the licensing basis security scenarios. The scenarios should be representative of different DBT adversary
attributes, targets within the facility, and methods. This number of representative security events should
be increased when accounting for:
• Larger facilities (i.e., 300 MWe to 1 GWe +) that have a greater number of adversary pathways
• Multimodule facilities
• More than two radiological sabotage targets
 The resulting list of licensing basis security scenarios and the associated irreversible release times
will be representative of the PPS response for the purposes of licensing. As part of the PPS design, the
parameters and programmatic requirements will be determined for the detection probability and
confidence. The initial maintenance and testing requirements should be determined according to
manufacturing requirements and supported by available operational data. As site-specific operational data
is gained, site maintenance and testing requirements should be updated to support the licensed
performance parameters associated with detection probability and confidence as necessary through the
lifetime of the facility, as well as the training and qualification requirements to support probability of
neutralization.

3.5.5.1. Component Testing
 Component testing is testing of individual security technologies, personnel, or procedures that are
used within a PPS. Generally, testing of personnel and procedures is considered LSPTs as well. During
the design and subsequent operation of a facility acceptance testing and performance testing, respectively,
should be conducted.
 When considering licensing of a nuclear facility, acceptance testing plays a key role in determining
whether the security technologies described within the security plan meet the effectiveness of regulatory
requirements and perform at a level that ensures the PPS will operate at its designed effectiveness level.
Acceptance testing considers evaluating the components of a PPS for the relevant functions the
component is to perform. For example, exterior sensors should be evaluated for their overall probability
of detection for the DBT, the nuisance alarm rate, and false alarm rate. These tests will confirm that the
component can operate within the regulatory requirements and ensure that when the component is placed
into the operational PPS it will function as stated. Once the equipment is installed, these acceptance tests
are conducted to ensure regulatory compliance and compliance with the security plan developed for the
facility. For guidance on conducting these tests, please refer to Section 3.2.2 and 3.2.3.
 Additionally, a component level test may also include evaluation of the response force’s ability to
specifically engage adversaries during a security event. These tests can be conducted by using weapon
platforms that the response force can use within its system and test the overall portability of hit (i.e., the
probability that the responder can hit the target at a certain range over a specified number of rounds being
shot at the target). These tests can be used to ensure that the responder training is adequate to engage the
adversary force and that the responders can successfully engage an adversary at a given range within the
contingency plan that is developed by the site.

3.5.5.2. Limited-Scope Performance Testing
 LSPTs can be used to evaluate people and procedures used within the PPS. An LSPT can be used to
evaluate individual components of a process or the process itself that is being used as part of the PPS or
the PPS strategy. LSPTs can be beneficial in evaluating portions of the response time, the process for
conducting contraband searches, the process for conducting vehicle searches, and processes for allowing
personnel and vehicles into the facility once they have been searched. An LSPT may require an armed
responder to pick up their weapon and load the ammunition magazine into the rifle. This LSPT confirms
that the armed responder is knowledgeable about loading their weapon and the time is recorded to gather
the total task time that is used in the overall response timeline to respond to a nuclear security event.
Additionally, these tests can be used to evaluate the ability of an armed security officer to use a handheld
metal detector to conduct contraband searches of individuals as they enter the facility.
 Another category of this evaluation is ELSPTs that are used to test multiple parts of a plan, procedure,
and technologies and encapsulates subsystem testing of the PPS. An example of an ELSPT could
encompass activating a sensor in the field, ensuring the sensor alarms in the CAS, ensuring the CAS
operator assesses the cause of the alarm, ensuring the CAS operator communicates the cause of the alarm,
and finally timing and ensuring accuracy of these alarm causes. Another example is using a radio dispatch
from the RTL to the response force, the response force getting their equipment ready, moving to respond
to the radio callout, and timing and ensuring adequacy in every step of the process. These tests are used to
ensure that the times used to implement a response to a nuclear security event and ensure all functions of
the response to a security event are within the specified time limits to implement an effective response.
3.5.5.3.      Path Analysis
 Path analysis tools are used to evaluate the total adversary attack timeline and the response timeline
and determine the probability of interruption for an identified adversary attack scenario. Path analysis
tools use much of the information that is collected through component level tests and LSPTs. This
information is used to identify whether ample time exists for the response force to interrupt an adversary
force before they can achieve their malicious act. Path analysis software tools can be used to identify the
probability of interruption for various adversary attack vectors such as the most vulnerable path (the path
with the lowest probability of interruption), the fastest path (the path with the shortest over all adversary
task timeline), and the stealthiest path (the path where the adversary has the lowest cumulative probability
of detection). By using path analysis tools, a facility can ensure that its probability of interruption meets
design standards and the response force can adequately interrupt the adversary force. These identified
adversary pathways can also be used to inform other validation and evaluation tools, such as TTXs,
combat simulations, and FOF exercises.
3.5.5.4. Tabletop Exercises and Combat Simulations
 TTXs can be used to evaluate and validate various areas of a PPS. A TTX can be used to evaluate the
site’s response strategies, communications, and procedures against identified and outlined adversary
attack scenarios. TTX tools exist within three-dimensional software tools that can be used to record a
TTX or can be carried out using facility blueprints and maps on paper.

 37

Figure 19. Screenshot of Scribe3D TTXs recorder tool. j

 These tools are used to assess how the PPS will respond to a security event at a facility. A TTX
generally starts with a pre-identified adversary attack scenario that may have been developed or informed
by the results of path analysis or developed by a mock adversary team. It is important that the adversary
attack scenario developed for a TTX be consistent with the DBT and any other regulatory bounds for the
adversary team. The TTX is conducted in a turn-based fashion, allowing the adversary team and response
team to make moves according to the adversary attack scenario and the contingency plans developed for
the facility. Because a TTX is conducted using these setup adversary attack scenarios and the contingency
plans developed for the facility, these exercises can be used to evaluate the overall effectiveness of the
developed security plans and contingency plans that have been implemented. These exercises also can be
used to evaluate the communications that are used during a security event and how the communication
works between groups at the facility. TTXs can be a valuable way for the facility to evaluate that its
security and contingency plans are effective against an adversary attack scenario. A TTX can be very
beneficial when the facility’s data is used in the TTX. This includes adversary task times, probabilities of
detection, and response force probabilities of the hit for the weapon platforms they are using. By
conducting these component level tests and subsystem level tests, the data can be used to ensure accuracy
in a TTX.
 Another tool that can be used to evaluate or validate a PPS is combat simulation tools. Combat
simulation tools can be used to simulate adversary attack scenarios and the PPS response to those
adversary attack scenarios and include simulated combat engagements between the adversary team and
the response team. Combat simulation tools can ultimately be used to calculate the probability of
neutralization for a PPS. Combat simulation tools utilize the data that is generated from continuous
testing as well as human behavior modeling to determine the outcome of a particular security event that
occurs at the site. The combat simulation tool is used to evaluate the developed adversary attack scenarios
in a Monte-Carlo fashion over a statistically significant total number of scenarios to determine the overall
probability of neutralization. Combat simulation tools can also be used to evaluate the effectiveness of a
contingency plan and the security plan for the facility to ensure these plans are adequate at mitigating and
defending the facility against an adversary attack. A listing of current NRC technical letter reports on
modeling and simulation tools can be found in Appendix A.

3.5.5.5. Force-on-Force Exercises
 One of the final and ultimate evaluations that should be conducted by a facility are FOF exercises. A
FOF exercise is a realistic scenario-based drill with a trained mock adversary force that attempts to attack
the facility according to a developed adversary attack scenario and the response of the PPS to defend
against the adversary attack scenario. These exercises use simulated weapons and simulated explosives
for both the response force and adversary force. These FOF exercises are meant to evaluate the facility’s
security plan and contingency plans in live action and determine whether the plans, procedures,
technologies, and personnel used to implement these plans are effective. FOF exercises are recommended
to be the final evaluation tool that is used to ensure the system is effective at mitigating adversary attack
scenarios.
 FOF exercises can be very costly to implement at nuclear facilities due to the need for a response
team to participate in the exercise, a response team that is needed to actively defend the facility against
adversary attacks at the nuclear facility, controllers for the FOF, and evaluators for the FOF. Because of
this, it is recommended that FOFs be conducted based on other types of analysis that are conducted. For
example, during the evaluation process discussed above, the use of TTXs and combat simulation tools
may show some adversary attack scenarios that the system has a low probability of neutralization to
defend against, an adversary pathway with a significantly low probability of interruption, or that the tools
created a new adversary attack scenario that the facility has not evaluated. These scenarios, if utilized in
an FOF, can help ensure that the PPS and its associated plans and measures are effective at protecting the
facility against the most concerning adversary attacks that the facility may face. Using this process and
methodology for a structured approach to testing and data collection can ensure that an FOF encapsulates
all the known information about the facility and protective strategies to evaluate the effectiveness of the
security system.
3.5.5.6. Corrective Action
 One important factor of the evaluation and validation process for a PPS is the compensatory measure
and corrective action process that is put in place to mitigate vulnerabilities or deficiencies within the PPS.
A corrective action is implemented to mitigate a vulnerability or deficiency in a process, procedure,
technology, or plan for the site. It is important to note the difference between compensatory measures and
corrective actions that are taken based on the vulnerability or deficiency that is seen through the
evaluation and validation process. An example of this process is when a microwave sensor fails to ensure
the required probability of detection at the required confidence level during a performance test. An
immediate compensatory measure should be put in place to mitigate any potential vulnerabilities due to
the failure of this sensor. The compensatory measure could include placing an armed security officer at
the location of the microwave, or the site could task a pan-tilt-zoom or fixed camera to the location where
an alarm station operator could be used to backfill for the detection. While these measures may be
implemented by the facility to counter a potential vulnerability temporarily, the facility must correct the
deficiency to ensure the effectiveness of the PPS. This requires the facility to implement a corrective
action to permanently fix the deficiency. In this example, the corrective action is to conduct maintenance
or replace the microwave sensor in the field.
 Compensatory measures and corrective actions can also be implemented for personnel and procedures
that are used within the security plan and contingency plan. An example is when an armed security officer
fails to conduct a vehicle search correctly when a LSPT is conducted. A compensatory measure for this
action may be to replace the armed security officer that failed the test with another qualified officer that
has not failed the test. The corrective action for this example may be to conduct training with the armed
security officer and conduct another evaluation after the training to ensure the officer correctly conducts
the vehicle search.
 It is important that the facility implements compensatory measures and corrective actions in a timely
manner once identified. Vulnerabilities and deficiencies can be identified through any of the above-

 39

mentioned evaluation and validation steps. By implementing compensatory measures and corrective
actions in a timely manner, the facility can ensure it adequately mitigates vulnerabilities to the facility.
3.5.6. Security Margin
 To support the parameters determined for the PPS, the margin of the PPS is characterized through
DID and timeline margin. These analyses support the determination of acceptability of the proposed
design to regulatory requirements associated with performance and DID.
3.5.6.1. Defense-in-Depth Analysis
 The DID of the designed PPS should be selected by the licensee for each component of the PPS. The
three methods of DID demonstration may be applied by a licensee to different elements of the PPS to
provide a holistic view of PPS DID.
 For example, some elements (e.g., alarm stations, communication) may be best addressed through
functional redundancy. These SSCs and programmatic elements may have redundancy built into the
design and operation of the facility. Some licensees may choose to have full detection redundancy to
address possible issues with compensatory measure implementation.
 Some security program elements may best demonstrate DID through a sensitivity analysis. This may
be the best method for passive delay features or response elements.
 The performance-based method of DID demonstration may take additional resources to justify design
decisions but provides a pathway to demonstrate the least conservative approach for compliance. The
application of programmatic DID can provide methods to apply special treatments to those security
program elements identified as the most sensitive in the DID analysis and provide a performance-based
method to apply increase performance-applications.
3.5.6.2. Timeline Margin
 For each of the irreversible release timelines compared against the site security timeline, the margin
should be characterized. Lesser margin can be licensed into the facility to provide operational flexibility
in meeting the required timeframe to prevent radiological sabotage.
3.5.7. Documentation
 It would be necessary for the licensee to document the analyses that support the parameter
determinations. Such analyses should be included within a technical report, available for regulatory
inspection as part of a license determination audit, and maintained at the licensed site for inspection
throughout the license.
 The parameters of performance (bounding irreversible release timings, site security timelines,
detection probability and confidence, and probability of effectiveness) should be recorded within the site
security plan as commitments of the license. In this way, the licensee will have flexibility to meet the
licensed performance parameters. This would allow smaller changes to occur without regulatory
notification, changes that impact descriptions within the security plan to easily occur under the
10 CFR 50.54(p) or the proposed § 53.1565(d)(4), framework, and changes that impact the performance
of the security plan to require approval under a § 50.90 or the proposed § 53.1510license change process.
 4. CONCLUSION
 The NRC has developed proposed physical security performance-based rulemakings that provide the
opportunity for a new method of demonstrating compliance for nuclear security at commercial power
reactors. The performance-based rulemakings will allow for greater flexibility by a licensee to meet the
high-level goals of protection of the public health and safety from the consequences associated with the
DBT of radiological sabotage. The performance-based security licensing basis determination framework
proposed here provides a clear and traceable method to demonstrate and maintain compliance while

 40

allowing operational flexibility. This framework also provides a method to demonstrate the justification
for alternative methods of compliance with the prescriptive requirements currently found in § 73.55.
