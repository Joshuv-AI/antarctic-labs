Combination of p-STAT5, CRLF2 and copy number alterations as a potential indicator of a high-risk subgroup of acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Eman O Rasekh, Maha Saleh, Marwa Hanafy, Shimaa Basha, Abdallah M Almuslimani, Youssef Madney, Raafat Abd El-Fattah, Azza M Kamel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSignal transduction and transcription activation5 (STAT5) drives leukemic cell survival and treatment resistance in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), linking its activation to poor prognosis and highlighting it as an important biomarker and therapeutic target. We hypothesized that integrating STAT activation, cytokine receptor-like factor2 (CRLF2) overexpression, and copy number alterations (CNAs) affecting B-cell differentiation, cell-cycle control, and proliferation could define a high-risk ALL subtype, possibly including Philadelphia (Ph) like ALL.MethodsThe study included 115 precursor-B-ALL patients, comprising 88 (76.5%) children and 27 (23.5%) adults, 66 males (57.4%) and 49 females (42.6%). Expressions of p-STAT3, p-STAT5, and CRLF2 were assessed by flow cytometry. Forty patients, including 29 with positive marker expression, of whom 18 lacked any of the recurrent genetic translocations, were further tested for CNAs by Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA).Resultsp-STAT3, p-STAT5, and/or CRLF2 were expressed in 36 (31.3%) patients. p-STAT3 was positive in 6 (5.2%) patients, p-STAT5 in 27 (23.4%) patients, and CRLF2 overexpression was detected in 15 (13.04%) patients. Thirty/forty patients (75%) had one or more CNAs abnormalities, including deletions or duplications. p-STAT5 was detected in 6/7 (85.7%) patients with IKZF1 deletion. Higher day 42 MRD was significantly associated with IKZF1 deletion (p = 0.04). Adult patients with positive p-STAT3, p-STAT5, and/or CRLF2 overexpression had shorter overall survival (P < 0.01) and disease-free survival (P < 0.001).ConclusionThe expression of p-STAT5 and/or CRLF2 is probably associated with dismal prognosis in adults. A combined assessment of CRLF2, p-STAT5, and CNAs might identify a high-risk subset of ALL, potentially encompassing Ph-like ALL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05294-5.Keywords: ALL, p-STAT5, p-STAT3, CRLF2, Flow cytometry, CNAs

Introduction

The progress in the therapeutics of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is tremendous, leading to the development of risk-adjusted treatment strategies with markedly improved survival rates, especially in children [1, 2]. However, there are subtypes of ALL, with certain phenotypic and/or molecular characteristics, that still have a dismal prognosis [3, 4].

The Janus kinase/signal transduction and transcription activation (JAK/STAT) pathway is a key intracellular signaling cascade, broadly expressed and essential for multiple biological processes, including immune regulation, cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [5]. STAT5 plays a pivotal role in the normal development of lymphoid and myeloid lineages, but its aberrant activation contributes to leukemia progression through interactions with the ATP-binding region. In hematologic malignancies, abnormal activation of STAT3 and STAT5 has been strongly linked to dysregulated gene expression and chromatin remodeling [6]. Additionally, STAT5B activation has been identified as a major driver of BCR::ABL1-mediated leukemogenesis in Philadelphia (Ph) positive ALL and possibly in BCR::ABL1 negative Ph-like ALL [7]. Collectively, these findings underscore the multifaceted role of STAT5 in leukemia and highlight the clinical relevance of its mutations [8].

CRLF2 encodes the cytokine receptor-like factor 2, which heterodimerizes with the interleukin-7 receptor α chain (IL7Rα) to form the receptor for thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) [9]. Genomic alterations activating this pathway including JAK mutations and rearrangements, IKZF1 alterations, IL7R mutations, and CRLF2 rearrangements, lead to downstream STAT activation and are associated with poor prognosis in ALL.

Individual copy number alterations (CNAs) such as deletions, duplications, or amplifications of genes regulating B-cell differentiation, cell cycle control, proliferation, and transcription are increasingly recognized as important markers for risk stratification in ALL. Frequently involved genes include CDKN2A/B, IKZF1, PAX5, RB1, ETV6, BTG1, EBF1, and those located within the PAR1 region (CRLF2, CSF2RA, IL3RA) [10].

Targeting the JAK-STAT, PI3K/mTOR, and BCL2 signaling pathways with novel therapies has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical studies, both as single agents or in combination [11]. Furthermore, integrating intracellular signaling biomarkers, detected by flow cytometry, with measurable residual disease (MRD) assessment provides a more comprehensive approach to predict patient outcomes and refine treatment strategies [12].

In the present work, we studied the activation patterns of STAT3 and STAT5, along with the CRLF2 overexpression, in precursor B-ALL patients using flow cytometry. Additionally, we included evaluation of CNAs of genes involved in B-cell differentiation, cell cycle regulation, and proliferation, by Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA), to assess their complementary role alongside signal transduction molecules in marker-positive patients as a hypothesis-generating approach for a high-risk group, likely including Ph-like ALL.

Material and methods

Patients

The study included 115 newly diagnosed precursor-B ALL patients enrolled between December 2021 and February 2023, 88 patients (76.5%) were pediatrics (including 2 infants (< 1 year) and 86 children (≥ 1 year- < 18 years), 27 (23.5%) adults, 66 males (57.4%) and 49 females (42.6%) with an age range of 0.14–65 years and a median of 6 years. All patients presented to the outpatient clinics of the Pediatric and Medical Oncology Departments at the National Cancer Institute (NCI), Cairo University. The study was conducted following the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the NCI-IRB [No: 202101-03-02001]. Written informed consents were obtained from the patients or their guardians. Clinical trial number is not applicable.

Methods

Patients were diagnosed with precursor B-ALL based on standard clinical, hematological and immunophenotypic criteria. Hematological investigations included complete blood count (CBC), bone marrow (BM) morphology, cytochemistry, immunophenotyping, conventional cytogenetics, and molecular studies.

Immunophenotyping

Whole BM staining was performed on fresh samples using monoclonal antibodies and a 10-color BD FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data acquisition and analysis were conducted using BD FACSDiva™ software version 8.0.2. At least 10,000 events were acquired and a cluster of 50 cells is required with a diagnostic sensitivity of 0.5%. Immunophenotyping and MRD detection were performed using surface and cytoplasmic staining as previously described [13].

p-STAT3, p-STAT5, and CRLF2 staining

Details of the monoclonal antibodies used for p-STAT3, p-STAT5, and CRLF2 detection are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Panels used were distributed in two tubes:

p-STAT3FITC-p-STAT5PE-CD10PE-PC5.5-CD34PC7-CD19APC-CD45KO

Philadelphia-positive ALL patients harboring the BCR::ABL fusion gene were used as positive controls, whereas mature lymphocytes served as internal negative controls. The blast population was identified based on low side scatter and weak CD45 expression with a range of 35–95%.

Marker expression was assessed on gated CD19 + , CD19 + /CD10 + , or CD19 + /CD10 + /CD34 + populations according to marker expression in each case and reported as percentage positivity from the sequentially gated blast population (Fig. S1). All markers were considered positive when ≥ 20% of blast cells expressed the antigen above the negative control, except for CD34, p-STAT3, and p-STAT5 for which a cutoff of ≥ 10% was used [14]. Overexpression of CRLF2 was defined as ≥ 10% of positive cells above the negative lymphocyte population [15].

Multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA)

According to DNA availability and quality, some patients were selected for further analysis with MLPA; these included patients with positive marker expression and lack of recurrent translocations, patients with positive BCR::ABL as positive control and some patients with negative marker expression.

Genomic DNA was extracted from BM samples using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Detection of CNAs was performed using the SALSA MLPA Probemix P335-C2 ALL-IKZF1 kit (MRC Holland, Amsterdam, Netherlands), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This assay targets multiple genes implicated in cell cycle regulation and B-cell differentiation, including IKZF1, EBF1, CDKN2A/B, PAX5, ETV6, BTG1, RB, and the PAR1 region.

Each MLPA run included three reference DNA samples from healthy male individuals for data normalization. Capillary electrophoresis was conducted on the 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Data analysis was carried out using Coffalyser.Net software (MRC Holland). According to the manufacturer’s instructions; copy number status was interpreted based on the intensity ratio thresholds.

Therapy

Children received treatment per St. Jude Total XV protocol [16], infants per Interfant-99 [17], and adults according to German or Dana-Farber protocols [18, 19].

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 27. Numerical data were summarized as medians and ranges, while categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Continuous variables with normal distribution (age and hemoglobin level) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the student’s t-test. Continuous variables with non-normal distribution (total leukocyte count (TLC) and platelet count (PLT) were presented as median (range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables (bone marrow cellularity, immunophenotype, CD34 expression, molecular abnormalities, cytogenetic risk group, and MRD) were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were < 5.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death or last follow-up, and disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from the end of treatment to relapse or death. OS and DFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. Multivariate analysis was performed using the Cox regression model to identify independent prognostic factors, with hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals reported. All tests were two-tailed, and p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Copy number alterations

According to the availability and quality of DNA, 40 patients were tested for CNAs to integrate proteomic and genomic data and identify protein signatures linked to recurrent genetic events. Among them, 29 patients had positive marker expressions: 4 had p-STAT3, 23 had p-STAT5, and 13 had CRLF2 overexpression. Eleven marker-negative patients were included. Recurrent translocations were detected in 16 patients, including seven with t(9;22), four with t(12;21), three with t(1;19), and two with t(4;11). Eighteen of the 29 marker-positive patients lacked any of the recurrent translocations.

Of the 40 patients tested, 30 (75%) had at least one genetic abnormality, including deletions or duplications in genes such as IKZF1, PAX5, CDKN2A/B, ETV6, PAR1 region genes, JAK2 (exon 23), BTG1, RB, and EBF1. A total of 73 alterations were identified including 30 deletions and 43 duplications. The most frequent deletions were CDKN2A/B (25.0%), IKZF1 (17.5%), and PAX5 (17.5%); no deletions were detected in the PAR1 region. Duplications were most frequent in the PAR1 region, involving IL3RA (20.0%), P2RY8 (15.0%), CRLF2 (12.5%), and CSF2RA (12.5%). Other frequent duplications included BTG1 (22.5%) and ETV6 (15.0%), Fig. 1B.

CDKN2A/B deletions were monoallelic in 6/10 (60%) patients and biallelic in 4 (40%) patients. IKZF1 complete deletions (exons 1–8) were detected in 2/7 (28.6%) patients, while partial deletions spanning exons 1–7 or 1–6 were each detected in two children, and one child had a deletion involving exons 1–5. All deletions in PAX5 (7/7), ETV6 (3/3), JAK2 exon 23 (1/1), and EBF1 (1/1) were monoallelic.

Six of the seven patients (85.7%) with IKZF1 deletion had positive p-STAT5 expression, including three patients who had the t(9;22) p190) and one patient with t(9;22) p210 along with CRLF2 overexpression. Detailed characterization of the 40 patients analyzed by MLPA is presented in Table 3.

Response to treatment

Early death occurred in 16 patients, primarily from septic shock. By day 42, 96/99 patients achieved complete remission (CR). Three children achieved CR later; two of them relapsed, one had t(9;22) p190, CDKN2A/B, and PAX5 deletions, and the other had an IKZF1 deletion, both were p-STAT5 positive. The third had no detected abnormalities and was alive at the end of the study. The MRD was assessed on day 15 in 99 patients and on day 42 in 92 patients. On day 15, 40/99 (40.4%) patients had MRD < 0.1%, and 59/99 (59.6%) patients had MRD between 0.1 and < 10%. On day 42, 81/92 (88%) patients had MRD < 0.01%, and 11/92 (12%) patients had MRD ≥ 0.01%. MRD was not associated with marker expression, Table 2. Within the group of the patients studied for CNAs, a higher MRD on day 42 was significantly associated with IKZF1 deletion (P = 0.04), but not with CDKN2A/B deletions, CSF2RA/IL3RA, or CRLF2 duplications.

Overall survival

The follow-up period ranged from 0.1 to 34 months with a median of 23 months. Pediatric patients had a significantly better OS with 77% survival at 2 years versus 33% in adults (P < 0.001). Marker expression did not affect survival in children, whereas adults with positive p-STAT3, p-STAT5, and/or CRLF2 expression had inferior OS with a median of three months and zero % survival at one year compared to a 19-month median OS and 68% survival at one year in marker-negative adults (P < 0.01), Table 4, Fig. 2A.

In the group of patients studied, CNAs had no significant impact on survival. High TLC (≥ 50 × 10⁹/L) was significantly associated with shorter OS (P = 0.021). Disease-free survival

In adult patients, the median DFS was 17 months, whereas in pediatric patients, it was not reached. Pediatric patients had an 83% disease-free survival (DFS) rate at one year and 79% at two years, compared with 62% at one year and 39% at two years in adult patients (P < 0.001).

Marker-positive adult patients had an inferior DFS; patients with p-STAT3, p-STAT5, and/or CRLF2 overexpression had a median DFS of three months and zero% survival at one and two years, compared to a median DFS of 25 months, 81% DFS at one year, and 51% DFS at two years in marker-negative adults (p < 0.001), Fig. 2B. No significant impact of marker expression on DFS was detected in the overall cohort or pediatric patients (P = 0.208, P = 0.335, respectively) Table 4.

In the group of patients studied for CNAs, DFS was not significantly affected by IKZF1 or CDKN2A/B deletions, or by CSF2RA/IL3RA and CRLF2 duplications. Whereas high TLC (≥ 50 × 10⁹/L) and PB blasts > 70% were associated with inferior DFS in the overall cohort (P = 0.007 and P = 0.022, respectively). Among pediatric patients,female sex (P = 0.041), high TLC ≥ 50 × 10⁹/L (P = 0.013), and splenomegaly (P = 0.043) were significantly associated with shorter DFS.

In multivariate analysis, adult patients (> 18 years) had a five-fold higher risk of recurrence compared to pediatric patients (HR: 5.4; 95% CI: 2.4–12.5, P < 0.001). Whereas in the pediatric group, TLC ≥ 50 × 10⁹/L was the only independent predictor of DFS; pediatric patients with TLC ≥ 50 × 10⁹/L had a six-fold higher risk of recurrence (HR: 6.2, 95% CI: 1.9–20.4, P = 0.003).

Discussion

In the present study, we aimed to investigate whether the interplay of STAT activation, CRLF2 overexpression, and CNAs in genes involved in B-cell differentiation, cell cycle regulation, and proliferation may define an ALL subtype associated with poor prognosis. p-STAT3, p-STAT5, and CRLF2 expressions were assessed by flow cytometry; MLPA was tested in selected cases mainly those with p-STAT3, p-STAT5 and/or over expression of CRLF2. BCR::ABL positive cases were included as positive control for both flow cytometry and MLPA assays.

There is currently no consensus regarding the cutoff value for p-STAT5 expression measured by flow cytometry. Xu et al. [20] applied a 20% threshold to distinguish high from low expression.

However, this value was empirically selected, and their study was published in 2024 after the completion of our patient recruitment. In our cohort, BCR::ABL-positive cases, used as positive control, demonstrated p-STAT5 levels ranging from 10.1% to 28.2%. Therefore, we adopted a 10% cutoff for our analysis.

MLPA was tested in 40 selected cases, including 29 patients expressing p-STAT3, p-STAT5, and/or CRLF2 overexpression. Eighteen out of the 29 lacked any of the recurrent translocations; some of these patients could belong to the Ph-like subtype. Nevertheless, this possibility should be interpreted with caution, as confirmation of Ph-like ALL requires comprehensive molecular genetic testing.

Our results revealed that p-STAT3 was expressed in 6.8% of pediatric patients and not detected in adults. p-STAT5 was positive in 25% of pediatric and 18.5% of adult patients, which is lower than the 38.5% previously reported [20]. CRLF2 overexpression was detected in 13.04% of patients, which is lower than the 25.2% reported in a mixed Brazilian cohort [21]. Among adults, 18.5% had CRLF2 overexpression, also lower than the 41.3% reported in a Mexican cohort [22]. In pediatric patients, CRLF2 overexpression was detected in 11.4%, which is higher than the 6% reported in one study [23], but lower than the 20.6% reported in another study [24]. These discrepancies might reflect differences in the frequency of ALL subtypes associated with STAT activation and/or CRLF2 overexpression.

It is worth mentioning that some of our patients exhibited CRLF2 overexpression without detectable p-STAT5. Although this may sound paradoxical, given that STAT5 lies downstream of CRLF2 in the signaling pathway, the relationship between CRLF2 expression and STAT5 phosphorylation is not absolute. Steeghs et al. [25] reported that TSLP-induced STAT5 phosphorylation occurred in JAK2R683S-mutated cells but not in JAK2 fusion–positive cells, indicating that this activation is context-dependent. Additionally, Hasan et al. [26] observed no significant difference in basal p-STAT5 levels between CRLF2-rearranged and CRLF2 wild-type leukemias in a mouse model.

In the present study, no significant associations were detected between marker expression and clinical parameters such as age, sex, lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, or splenomegaly; this finding is consistent with previous studies [20, 21]. In contrast, a significant association between CRLF2 overexpression and splenomegaly (P = 0.012) was previously reported [27], suggesting a role in extramedullary infiltration. Similarly, no associations were detected between the marker expression and hematological parameters, including hemoglobin, TLC, platelet count, or blast percentage in PB or BM, confirming earlier results [20]. In contrast, associations of CRLF2 overexpression with hyperleukocytosis (P = 0.0002) and thrombocytopenia (P = 0.005) in adults lacking recurrent cytogenetic abnormalities were previously reported [27].

In the present study, no significant associations were detected between marker expression and IPT, CD13/CD33 aberrancy, CD34%, or DNA index; these findings are consistent with previous studies [20, 21]. In contrast, associations of CRLF2 overexpression with CD34%, CD13%, and CD33% were previously reported [28]. Additionally, Kamel et al. [29] reported a significant association between CD34% and p-STAT5 expression (P < 0.01) in a chronic myeloid leukemia cohort.

In the present study, no significant associations were detected between p-STAT3 or p-STAT5 expression and molecular genetic translocations or genetic risk groups. These findings are consistent with previous reports [30, 31]. Additionally, no associations were detected between marker expression and MRD or the achievement of CR; these findings are consistent with previous studies [20, 30]. As expected, p-STAT5 expression was higher in BCR::ABL-positive patients, supporting its role as a therapeutic target in Ph + ALL, particularly in relapsed or tyrosine kinase inhibitor-resistant patients.

Among the 40 patients tested for CNAs, CDKN2A/B deletion was the most frequent abnormality, followed by deletions in IKZF1 and PAX5. These findings are consistent with previous studies [32, 33]. The most frequent co-deletions detected were CDKN2A/B and PAX5 as previously reported [33], and the most frequent duplication was in the PAR1 region as reported by Agarwal et al. [34].

In the present study, IKZF1 deletions were significantly linked to age over 18, aligning with findings in older pediatric groups [35]. IKZF1 deletions have been linked to high-risk features, such as older age, high TLC, and persistent MRD [36]. In the present study, IKZF1 deletion was the only CNA significantly associated with higher day 42 MRD (p = 0.040) in the patients studied for CNAs, supporting its utility in risk stratification [36, 37].

In the present study, no significant associations were detected between CNAs and marker expression as previously reported (20,38). However, earlier studies in both adults (P = 0.001) [27] and children (P = 0.003) [29] reported a link between CRLF2 overexpression and IKZF1 deletion. Of the eight patients with PAR1 region duplication detected in the present study, five had CRLF2 overexpression, as previously reported [30]. Variations between studies may be attributed to focusing on adults or pediatrics. The combined analysis of signaling markers, recurrent translocations, and CNAs performed in the present study supports the multifactorial pathogenesis of ALL as previously reported [20, 38–40]. It may help identify a high-risk ALL subtype; it could be that the combination of marker expression and CNAs may help identify Ph-like patients, a subgroup that is known to be associated with worse survival outcomes. This hypothesis should be confirmed in future studies by including sequencing to verify such a diagnosis.

In the present study, five/seven patients with BCR::ABL fusion gene, had secondary deletions in IKZF1, CDKN2A/B, PAX5, or ETV6. Among them, three patients died and two experienced early relapses, suggesting a contribution of these lesions to disease progression. This finding is consistent with Van Outerstep et al. [41], who reported that concurrent deletions in IKZF1 and CDKN2A/B or PAX5 were associated with resistance to ex vivo imatinib treatment, indicating mechanisms of tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance beyond the ABL1 domain mutations.

In the present study, the expression of p-STAT3, p-STAT5, or CRLF2 overexpression was significantly associated with shorter OS and DFS in adults (P < 0.001), but not in pediatric patients. This is consistent with Xu et al. [20], who reported p-STAT5 expression as a predictor of poor OS (p = 0.018) and DFS (p = 0.001) in adults, and with Chiaretti et al. [27], who reported that CRLF2 overexpression is associated with inferior outcomes (P = 0.015). In contrast, Juárez-Velázquez et al. [42] linked CRLF2 overexpression to reduced OS in pediatric patients. The lack of prognostic impact in our pediatric cohort may be due to the relatively short follow-up period in a group of patients expected to have longer survival, unlike adults, whose shorter OS/DFS allowed for clearer outcome associations.

However, given the small number of the adult group in the present study, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

In the present study, deletions in IKZF1 or CDKN2A/B and duplications in CRLF2 or CSF2RA/IL3RA had no significant effect on OS or DFS, likely due to the limited number of patients studied for CNAs. Previous studies have shown associations between IKZF1 deletion and inferior survival or increased the risk of relapse [20, 43].

In agreement with earlier reports [44, 45], children had significantly better OS and DFS than adults. High TLC was associated with worse OS and DFS, and a high blast count was linked to shorter DFS [45].

Ph-like ALL is a subset of ALL that shares a gene expression profile with Ph-positive ALL but lacks the BCR::ABL fusion gene. Like Ph-positive ALL, the Ph-like ALL subtype is characterized by diverse molecular alterations that converge on tyrosine kinase activation, including the JAK/STAT pathway, resulting in the expression of p-STAT5. This has dual clinical implications. First, a subset of patients can benefit from FDA-approved tyrosine kinase inhibitors, a strategy already in clinical use. Second, detecting tyrosine kinase activation could serve as a preliminary screening marker, with confirmatory sequencing applied in positive patients. In addition, it would be of interest to explore whether activated STAT3 and STAT5 are linked to increased leukemia stem cell (LSC) frequency at diagnosis and higher rates of MRD positivity. Such associations could highlight multiple therapeutic targets and ultimately improve the prospects for cure.

The strength of this study is that it introduces the hypothesis that combining p-STAT5 expression, CRLF2 overexpression, and CNAs may serve as a potential screening approach to identify cases that could belong to the Ph-like ALL subgroup. However, definitive identification of Ph-like ALL requires comprehensive molecular analyses; validation of this hypothesis may provide a practical preliminary screening strategy for identifying candidates for further molecular testing.

The main limitations of the study are the small sample size of the adult group and the short follow-up period for the pediatric group. Additionally, MLPA was performed on a selected subset of patients according to DNA availability and quality; this might have introduced some selection bias. Future studies should include all consecutively diagnosed fusion gene–negative ALL patients, irrespective of p-STAT5 status, to ensure that the addition of p-STAT5 assessment to CRLF2 overexpression reliably captures the full spectrum of Ph-like ALL cases. Such an approach would minimize selection bias and allow a more accurate evaluation of the diagnostic and prognostic utility of this combined strategy.

In conclusion, the present study further highlights p-STAT5 as a potential adverse prognostic marker in adult patients with ALL; validation in a larger cohort is required. The prognostic significance of p-STAT5 in pediatric patients warrants longer follow-up to allow definitive assessment. Additionally, the combined evaluation of p-STAT5 expression, CRLF2 expression, and CNAs may delineate a high-risk subset of ALL with unfavorable outcomes. Notably, such findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, as accurate identification of Ph-like ALL requires detailed molecular analyses. Cases exhibiting this profile should therefore undergo further sequencing studies to confirm the diagnosis and to identify potential therapeutic targets. Targeting alterations in signaling pathways, including activated STATs, mainly STAT5, and gene copy number variations, may represent promising venues for developing precision medicine-based therapeutic strategies.
