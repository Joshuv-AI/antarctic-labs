Highly Efficient
Exciton Modulation in MoSe2/PdSe2 Heterostructures

Petr Rozhin, Muhammad Sufyan Ramzan, Emma Contin, Danae Katrisioti, Till Weickhardt, Micol Bertolotti, Nouha Loudhaief, Bing Wu, Zdeněk Sofer, Takashi Taniguchi, Kenji Watanabe, Leonardo Puppulin, Caterina Cocchi, Ioannis Paradisanos, Giancarlo Soavi, Stefano Dal Conte, Giovanni Antonio Salvatore, Domenico De Fazio

Abstract

We report a pronounced
∼6-fold enhancement of
room-temperature
A-exciton emission in a type-I MoSe2/PdSe2 van
der Waals heterostructure, yielding a photoluminescence quantum yield
of 6%, as compared to ∼1% for as-exfoliated monolayer MoSe2. This unusual enhancement is accompanied by strong quenching
of the B exciton, consistent with interlayer electronic coupling that
redistributes exciton populations toward the radiative A-exciton channel.
Power- and temperature-dependent measurements reveal a suppression
of exciton–exciton annihilation and a crossover to quenched
emission at a low temperature, indicating a redistribution of exciton
relaxation pathways. Photoluminescence excitation spectroscopy further
reveals a broadband enhancement spanning 450–725 nm. These
results demonstrate that interlayer electronic coupling can be used
as an efficient means to redirect exciton populations toward radiative
channels, enhancing emission efficiency in two-dimensional semiconductors
without chemical modification or significant strain.

Strong, stable
light emission
is essential for optoelectronic devices, such as light-emitting diodes
(LEDs), lasers, and single-photon sources. Efficient
emission occurs when excitons recombine radiatively, consistently
producing photons, rather than being lost to heat, defect trapping, phonon-assisted
decay, and Auger recombination.
,
 Suppression of these non-radiative pathways increases the photoluminescence
quantum yield (PLQY), defined as the ratio of emitted photons to absorbed
photons, thereby improving the efficiency of light-emitting devices
and reducing their power consumption, both key aspects in modern photonic
components.
−

Transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), such as MoS2, MoSe2, WS2, and WSe2, have emerged
as promising platforms for next-generation optoelectronics owing to
their unique properties, including a direct bandgap in the monolayer
limit, strong photoluminescence (PL), large exciton binding energies (typically ∼300–600
meV),
,
 pronounced spin–orbit coupling, mechanical flexibility, and the ability to form van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures with
novel functionalities.
,
 Furthermore, their atomic thickness
makes their optical response highly sensitive to the surrounding dielectric
environment.
,
 While this sensitivity enables
powerful control of excitonic properties through dielectric engineering,
it also makes emission strongly dependent on defects, substrate disorder, and interface
quality.

Several approaches have
been employed to enhance the PLQY of monolayer
TMDs, including encapsulation,
−

 chemical passivation,
,,
 and strain engineering.
,
 Encapsulation with dodecanol passivates Se vacancies and suppresses
substrate-induced non-radiative recombination, enabling PLQY values
from 6.2% up to ∼22% at 5 K in MoSe2. In addition, the thickness of the hexagonal
boron nitride (hBN) encapsulation layer can control the exciton radiative
rate via the Purcell effect (where the local optical environment alters
the spontaneous emission rate of excitons). Chemical treatments, such as bis­(trifluoromethane) sulfonimide (TFSI)
passivation, have also been used to increase the PLQY of MoS2 from ∼0.8% to over 95% by reducing defect-mediated recombination. Strain engineering exploits spatially graded
strain fields to funnel excitons toward regions of minimum bandgap,
with reported PL intensity increasing by a factor of ∼2 in
MoS2 and ∼2.8 in WSe2.
,
 However, these approaches often rely on chemical treatments that
degrade rapidly under ambient exposure
,
 or on uniform
tensile strain that drives a direct-to-indirect bandgap transition,
thereby suppressing PL. Additionally,
achieving high PL via hBN encapsulation typically requires vacuum
annealing at ∼1000 °C, a
temperature incompatible with the use of pre-patterned electrodes,
polymer substrates, and post-integration device architectures. These
limitations motivate the need for PLQY control through non-invasive
interlayer engineering.

Forming vdW stacking enables tuning
of electronic and optical properties
through interlayer electronic coupling rather than solely through dielectric screening as in encapsulation. In type-I heterostructures, both the conduction
band minimum (CBM) and valence band maximum (VBM) of one layer lie
within the gap of the other layer, confining electrons and holes to
the same material and enhancing radiative recombination. In contrast, type-II heterostructures exhibit
staggered band alignment, where the CBM and VBM reside in different
layers, spatially separating electrons and holes and typically reducing
the PL intensity. The PL enhancement in type-I heterostructures is
typically attributed to exciton funneling, whereby carriers generated
in the wider bandgap layer transfer into the narrower bandgap emissive
layer and accumulate there.
,
 This mechanism has
been widely reported in systems combining an emissive TMD, such as
MoSe2 or WS2, with a non- or weakly emissive
material, for example, in FePS3/MoSe2 (showing
a ∼20-fold enhancement of room-temperature PL emission from
monolayer MoSe2) and PbI2/WS2 (exhibiting a ∼2-fold PL enhancement
relative to bare monolayer WS2) heterostructures.

Interestingly, an unusual scenario has been
demonstrated in a type-I
heterostructure in which a strongly absorbing, non-luminescent narrow-gap
SnSe2 multilayer underlies a wider-gap monolayer MoS2. In that system, the MoS2 PL is enhanced by up to 14-fold via a mechanism named Förster
resonance energy transfer (FRET), that is, non-radiative transfer
of the energy of photoexcited electron–hole pairs in SnSe2 to the MoS2 monolayer through near-field dipole–dipole
coupling. In type-I band alignments,
one would expect carrier funneling into the narrower gap layer, which
should quench emission from the wider bandgap TMD by depleting its
photoexcited carriers. In this sense, MoS2/SnSe2 represents an unusual situation, as a strong interlayer energy transfer
outweighs the PL loss pathways associated with charge transfer. This
result established a general design principle: pairing a strongly
absorbing, weakly emissive narrow-gap layer (SnSe2) with
a highly luminescent wider gap TMD monolayer (MoS2) can
enhance rather than quench emission from the TMD. However, FRET efficiency
in that system relies critically on the near resonance between the
direct transition of SnSe2 and the excitonic gap of MoS2, a condition that is not easily generalizable to other material
combinations.

Here, we fabricate a vdW heterostructure combining
a narrow-gap
PdSe2 multilayer with a monolayer of MoSe2.
PdSe2 exhibits strong broadband absorption but weak PL.
,
 Unlike SnSe2, PdSe2 does not exhibit a strong
spectral resonance with the dominant excitonic transitions of MoSe2, placing the system in a regime governed by interlayer electronic
hybridization rather than dipole–dipole energy transfer. Its
narrow-gap electronic structure and strong polarizability enable interactions
beyond dielectric screening, in contrast to hBN or SiO2. MoSe2 is selected for its strong room-temperature emission and well-resolved A and B excitons (∼200
meV splitting),
,
 enabling sensitive tracking of
exciton relaxation pathways. We investigate
how interlayer coupling in a MoSe2/PdSe2/hBN
vdW heterostructure modifies exciton relaxation and PLQY. We observe
a pronounced enhancement of the MoSe2 A-exciton PL, with
the extracted PLQY increasing from ∼1% to 6%.

Our approach
exploits interlayer coupling in a vdW heterostructure,
preserving the intrinsic material properties. In addition to enhanced
A-exciton emission, the heterostructure exhibits strong B-exciton
quenching, which we attribute to interlayer hybridization of MoSe2 with PdSe2 due to the enabling of additional relaxation
pathways. As a result, exciton populations are redistributed toward
the lower energy A exciton, where radiative recombination is favored,
leading to an enhanced A-exciton emission. These findings demonstrate
that PdSe2 can serve as an effective platform for engineering
exciton dynamics and enhancing the PL efficiency in two-dimensional
semiconductors without significant strain or chemical functionalization.
Enhanced A-exciton PL in MoSe2/PdSe2 arises
from interlayer coupling that requires near-resonant valence-band
alignment and low substrate density of states at the A-exciton energy,
ensuring efficient radiative recombination.

We begin our analysis
with the inspection of the optical image
of a MoSe2/PdSe2/hBN heterostructure, which
is shown in a. The individual hBN, PdSe2, and MoSe2 flakes,
as well as the overlapping MoSe2/PdSe2/hBN heterostructure
area, are outlined by yellow dashed lines, light blue dashed lines,
red dashed lines, and overlapping polygons, respectively. The thickness
and layer quality of PdSe2 and MoSe2 were characterized
by Raman spectroscopy and atomic force microscopy (AFM) in Figures S1 and S2,
respectively. The sample consists of a monolayer trigonal prismatic
phase MoSe2 flake partially overlapping a hexalayer (6L)
PdSe2 flake on a hBN substrate. At this thickness, PdSe2 retains a finite bandgap of ∼1.0 eV while exhibiting
strong broadband absorption that increases with the layer number. In contrast, monolayer PdSe2 has
a larger bandgap (∼1.3 eV)
,
 and a weaker
band offset with MoSe2,
,
 limiting interlayer
electronic coupling. On the other hand, thicker PdSe2 flakes
approach semimetallic behavior (bulk bandgap of ∼0.03 eV) and would possess a higher density of states,
which, in turn, could promote non-radiative recombination. In the following, we distinguish between two
regions of the same MoSe2 monolayer: (i) the heterostructure
(HS) region, where MoSe2 lies on PdSe2, and
(ii) the reference monolayer (1L) region, where MoSe2 lies
directly on hBN without PdSe2 underneath. Both regions,
therefore, contain 1L MoSe2 but differ in the presence
or absence of the PdSe2 layer.

To clarify the nature of interlayer electronic
interactions in
this system, we performed ab initio simulations on
a model system, including 1L MoSe2 stacked on tetralayer
(4L) PdSe2 (Figure S3). The
4L PdSe2 model was adopted as a computationally efficient
approximation of the experimentally investigated 6L PdSe2 because of their similar electronic structures (Figure S4). In particular, the band-edge positions and work
functions differ by less than ∼0.1 eV (Table S1), indicating that the interlayer hybridization mechanism
is not expected to be significantly affected by the reduced PdSe2 thickness. The electronic structure of the 1L MoSe2/4L PdSe2 interface exhibits an almost vanishing indirect
bandgap, with both VBM and CBM dominated by PdSe2 states
(b). The highest
occupied states with a MoSe2 character are buried in the
valence band of PdSe2, between the VBM and the VBM–1
(the energy state located directly below the VBM) at the Γ point.
The interaction with the underlying PdSe2 layer downshifts
the uppermost valence bands of MoSe2 by about 100 meV relative
to the isolated 1L. Moreover, close to the K valley of pristine MoSe2 (inset of b), where the A and B excitons originate, there are signatures
of strong hybridization with PdSe2 bands. The strength
of this interlayer electronic hybridization and the magnitude of the
density of states are discussed in the “Quantifying interlayer
hybridization” subsection of the Supporting Information (see Figures S5 and S6). This hybridization generates mixed electronic
states that are expected to quench excitonic resonances in MoSe2. Beyond opening new optical transition channels, this state
mixing is anticipated to be particularly pronounced for the B exciton,
stemming from an even deeper valley interacting with the PdSe2 valence region. Such interlayer coupling modifies the relaxation
pathways of photoexcited carriers, enabling additional non-radiative
interlayer relaxation channels, which compete against the intrinsic
B → A intralayer exciton relaxation. A schematic illustration
of the proposed carrier relaxation pathways, differentiating electron
and hole dynamics under both resonant and non-resonant excitation,
is provided in Figure S7. This alters exciton
population dynamics, leading to a redistribution of carriers between
A-exciton emission and interlayer decay channels, which may contribute
to the enhanced A-exciton emission highlighted below.

To validate
this interpretation, we performed control calculations
on an analogous 1L MoS2/4L PdSe2 HS (Figure S8a). In this case, the valence states
of MoS2 are energetically deeper and do not hybridize as
strongly with the valence bands of PdSe2. Consequently,
exciton quenching is less pronounced, consistent with equivalent control
measurements (Figure S8b and c).

To investigate how band alignment affects excitonic emission, we
performed PL measurements of the HS region. a shows a pronounced enhancement of the A-exciton
PL in the HS region. Under 514 nm excitation, the A-exciton PL intensity
increases by a factor ∼6× relative to the 1L region. Assuming
comparable optical absorption at the excitation wavelength and identical
light extraction efficiencies, we estimate an external PLQY of approximately
6% for the HS, using the reported internal PLQY of 1% for as-exfoliated
1L MoSe2 as a benchmark. Notably,
while the absolute PL intensity of the 1L is higher under 633 nm excitation
due to its proximity to the A-exciton resonance, the relative enhancement
factor decreases to ∼4× (Figure S9). This suggests that the HS-induced enhancement is more pronounced
under off-resonant excitation conditions.

In type-I TMD HSs, charge carriers transfer from
the wider bandgap
layer to the narrower bandgap emissive layer, resulting in enhanced
radiative recombination.
,
 Although DFT calculations
indicate a type-I band alignment for the MoSe2/PdSe2 HS (b), the observed PL enhancement occurs in wider bandgap MoSe2. This contradicts the expected carrier funneling mechanism,
as injection into narrower gap PdSe2 would deplete the
MoSe2 exciton population and suppress emission. Similarly,
while dielectric screening from a dielectric substrate like hBN can
influence the excitonic behavior of TMDs, it fails to account for
the PL enhancement observed in this HS. The specificity of this effect
is underscored by the fact that PdSe2 does not enhance
PL in MoS2 (Figure S8b and c), ruling out generic substrate-induced passivation. Instead, we
attribute the enhancement to specific electronic interactions between
PdSe2 and MoSe2. With support for this interpretation, b reveals a strong
quenching of the B-exciton emission in the HS region, consistent with
the interlayer hybridization predicted by our DFT calculations. Furthermore,
the minimal blue shift (∼3 meV) and the absence of a clear trion (charged exciton) enhancement suggest that charge-transfer-induced doping
is negligible. Consequently, neither simple carrier funneling nor
doping variations can be considered the dominant drivers of the observed
PL intensity modification.

To prove the robustness of our analysis,
we examined the consistency
of this behavior across the HS. As shown in a, the PL emission is uniform across the
entire HS region, demonstrating the spatiotemporal spectral homogeneity
of the A-exciton PL. The PL mapping in b further confirms low B-exciton population
throughout the HS area.

To further probe the underlying
mechanisms of the
observed A-exciton
enhancement and B-exciton quenching, we investigated the dependence
of the PL intensity on excitation power and temperature. Because 1L
MoSe2 forms the top layer of the stack (MoSe2/PdSe2/hBN), the incident laser power is absorbed directly
by MoSe2 without prior attenuation in both regions. Furthermore,
the absence of trion emission and the minimal spectral shift of ∼3
meV confirm that effective carrier densities remain comparable between
the two regions. The data presented in are well-described by a power-law dependence
(I ∝ P
α).
As shown in a, the PL intensity of both the 1L region and the HS region increases
sublinearly with excitation power, yielding exponents of α ≃
0.599 and 0.738, respectively. An exponent significantly smaller than
unity (α < 1) in the 1L region typically indicates that relaxation
dynamics are dominated by exciton–exciton annihilation (EEA),
a process whereby two excitons interact and one or both recombine
non-radiatively, reducing the PL intensity at high excitation densities. The increase of the exponent to α ≃
0.738 in the HS region suggests a suppression of EEA, consistent with
interlayer hybridization modifying exciton relaxation pathways.

At cryogenic temperatures, b reveals two distinct regimes. In the low-power
region,
the PL intensity scales nearly linearly with excitation power, yielding
exponents of α ≃ 1.08 for the 1L region and α ≃
1.13 for the HS region. At higher excitation powers, the response
becomes sublinear, with α ≃ 0.71 and 0.85, respectively,
which is attributed to EEA at high carrier densities. Importantly, unlike the room-temperature behavior, the
large PL enhancement disappears at 78 K, with the HS region exhibiting
lower intensity than the 1L region. This observation indicates that
the mechanisms responsible for the A-exciton enhancement are strongly
temperature-dependent, consistent with a temperature-dependent redistribution
of exciton relaxation dynamics that favors radiative recombination
at intermediate temperatures (∼200–300 K) and weakens
at low temperatures (<200 K).

By comparison, the 1L A-exciton PL increases monotonically

This is directly supported by time-resolved (TR) transient reflectivity
measurements (Figure S11), which reveal

Independent of this early time process, the reduced PL intensity in

scattering, which can limit dark–bright

 thereby reducing the repopulation of the radiative A-exciton state.
This behavior is hence not governed by EEA effects. To further probe

To further probe
how interlayer interactions affect exciton generation
and recombination, we performed photoluminescence excitation (PLE)
spectroscopy on the HS. Unlike conventional PL measurements, PLE examines
how the excitation energy influences the emission intensity, providing
insight into absorption pathways, exciton relaxation, and interlayer
coupling. PLE spectroscopy reveals a
consistent broadband PL enhancement of emission from the HS region
across the entire excitation energy range compared to the 1L region
(a). Although
excitation near 700 nm, corresponding to the MoSe2 B-exciton
resonance, produces the highest absolute PL intensity due to stronger
intrinsic absorption, the PL enhancement persists across the full
spectral range (∼450–725 nm) rather than being confined
to a specific transition. The wavelength-dependent PL enhancement
factor extracted from the PLE spectra (b) reveals the largest PL enhancement at
shorter excitation wavelengths, exceeding ∼3.5 near 450 nm
while decreasing to approximately ∼1.5 in the ∼550–650
nm range but remaining greater than unity. Unlike the PL quenching
across the whole range observed in MoS2/PdSe2 (Figure S13), the MoSe2-based
HS exhibits a divergent excitation response, demonstrating that interfacial
band alignment dictates the transition between emission enhancement
and suppression.

In this work, we have
shown that interlayer coupling
in MoSe2/PdSe2 HSs enables strong enhancement
of A-exciton
emission in 1L MoSe2, increasing the PLQY from ∼1%
in the reference 1L to 6% at room temperature. Our method exploits
interlayer coupling in a fully assembled heterostructure, enhancing
emission while preserving the intrinsic properties of the 1L. The
enhancement is accompanied by pronounced B-exciton quenching, reduced
EEA, and a broadband increase in excitation efficiency. The observation
that emission is enhanced in the wider bandgap layer, together with
the broadband PLE response and temperature-dependent crossover to
quenching, indicates that the effect does not arise from simple exciton
funneling or a single-resonant process. Instead, the combined PL,
broadband excitation PLE measurements, temperature-dependent PL, and
DFT calculations indicate a mechanism in which interlayer electronic
interactions redistribute exciton populations and modify relaxation
pathways.

These findings establish interlayer coupling as a
means to control
exciton dynamics and enhance radiative recombination in two-dimensional
semiconductors, offering a general strategy for improving light emission
in vdW HSs. The enhanced A-exciton PL in MoSe2/PdSe2 arises from favorable interlayer coupling, which requires
two criteria: (i) the 1L VBM lies near a high-density-of-states region
of the narrow-gap substrate, enabling hybridization (the ∼100
meV offset in MoSe2/PdSe2 meets this, while
MoS2/PdSe2 does not), and (ii) the substrate
has a low density of states at the A-exciton energy, preventing excessive
transfer and allowing radiative recombination to dominate. Because
these criteria depend on band alignment and density of states, which
can be tuned through material selection and stacking in a wide range
of vdW HSs, they open opportunities for systematic design of materials
with controlled exciton dynamics and improved light emission.
