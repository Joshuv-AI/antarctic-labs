 1. Introduction

1.1 ENERGY TO COMMUNITIES (E2C) PROGRAM OVERVIEW

The Energy to Communities (E2C) program is a U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)-funded technical
assistance program designed to provide technical support to communities transitioning to clean energy
and more sustainable energy economies. One offering under E2C is the "Expert Match" technical
assistance program, through which DOE national laboratories and other partners provide 40–60 hours of
technical support on a topic where expert assistance is critical to the community making an informed
clean energy decision.

1.2 PUNTA GORDA, FL WATER TREATMENT FACILITY OVERVIEW

The Punta Gorda water utility serves as the primary provider of potable water for the city and surrounding
unincorporated areas of Charlotte County, Florida, catering to a population of over 40,000 residents. The
utility operates a dual-plant system that includes a reverse osmosis water treatment plant and a service
water treatment facility, supported by a network of intake and freshwater wells. With an emphasis on low-
cost water and resilience, the utility is exploring innovative solutions, including solar energy projects, to
reduce reliance on diesel-powered generators and enhance its operational efficiency amid challenges such
as flooding in low-lying areas and the need for emergency preparedness during natural disasters,
especially hurricanes.

During previous hurricanes, flooding and loss of power have caused significant challenges maintaining
water supply and water quality to Punta Gorda. If transportation and electric infrastructure is unavailable,
it can be challenging to fuel diesel generators for the treatment facilities. As part of this analysis,
alternative forms of backup energy, such as batteries and solar panels, will undergo a techno-economic
analysis for both grid-connected and emergency situations.

 2. ANALYSIS APPROACH AND OBJECTIVES

2.1 OBJECTIVES

This project encompasses a comprehensive microgrid pre-feasibility study, which includes meetings to
engage stakeholders, particularly from the Punta Gorda water utility. The study aims to outline the
anticipated benefits, costs, and maintenance requirements while communicating these aspects in
accessible language for policymakers and the public. There are opportunities to explore various
technologies to integrate solar energy, battery storage, and existing generator systems, to both reduce
costs and maintain reliable operation following hurricanes.

In the microgrid analysis, there will be consideration for six scenarios based off of two load profiles:

 1. Today’s facility loading optimized for finances only (no resilience).

 2. Today’s facility loading optimized for finances and surviving a 5-day outage during peak load
 days.

 3. Today’s facility loading optimized for finances and surviving a 7-day outage during peak load
 days.

 4. Today’s facility loading optimized for finances and surviving a 10-day outage during peak load
 days.

 5. Future facility loading optimized for finances and surviving a 5-day outage during peak load
 days.

 6. Future facility loading optimized for finances and surviving a 7-day outage during peak load
 days.

In order to perform the analysis on these six scenarios, the ORNL team has leveraged the NREL REopt
tool.

2.2 REOPT OVERVIEW

The ORNL team used NREL’s REopt techno-economic analysis tool (https://reopt.nrel.gov/tool) for
estimating the optimum DER asset sizes for a combination of solar photovoltaic (PV), battery energy
storage system (BESS), and diesel generator. The REopt tool takes the hourly or 15-minute load interval
data, location, and tariff information of the facility as its input to determine the most economically
optimum solution for the facility. Additional constraints such as space constraints, energy export
constraints, clean energy goals, and resilience considerations to enable the facility to sustain a certain
duration of outage can also be added.

 3. INPUTS AND RESULTS

3.1 INPUTS AND ASSUMPTIONS

3.1.1 Site space availability

 Figure 3: Potential PV sites

The water treatment facility sits on roughly 166 acres of land. Through previous study, three potential
solar sites were identified with sizes of 42, 44, and 18 acres. Two of the sites are indicated in Figure 3.
The facility is planning to expand in the near future; however these available sites should be unaffected.

 4

For the purposes of this analysis, the three potential solar sites are assumed to operate as one large solar
array as opposed to many smaller arrays.

3.1.2 Load data

Load data was provided at a 15-minute resolution for one year across all metering points serving the
water treatment facility. There are a few points which show zero consumption which can be attributed to
outage in most cases. Overall, the data quality was high and was sufficient for modelling.

The first modelling case was based off of the provided load data, which will act as the baseline. In this
case, the critical load is considered to be 100% of the nominal load.

The second modelling case considers future upgrades of the plant, which will roughly double the load by
adding additional treatment facilities. In this case, the critical load is considered to be half of the nominal
load, making it the same amount of critical load as in the baseline case.

 Figure 4: Provided load data

3.1.3 Technology inputs and assumption

All cases include 3.25 MW of diesel gensets plus 10,000 gallons of fuel reserve as a baseline. These
assets already exist at the facility.

3.1.3.1 Solar PV technology assumptions

The solar PV installations are considered to be only ground-mount solar which can fit on 110 acres, which
is roughly the total size of available land identified in a previous study. The total size of the solar array
was not limited by the optimization, but instead limited by available land area. Based on Florida Power &
Light’s electricity programs, the maximum size for a net-metered DER installation is 2 MW. The PV was
not capped at 2MW, but the net metering credit drops to zero once the for any amount produced over 2
MW. Other PV considerations were left as default values within REopt.

 5

3.1.3.2 Battery Energy Storage System assumptions

Under Florida Power & Light policies, behind the meter battery storage systems are allowed to charge
from the grid at the cost defined by the site’s utility rate. No minimum or maximum battery capacity was
defined, instead the optimization was allowed to operate across a wide range of energy storage options.

3.1.4 Utility rate and financial assumptions

3.1.4.1 Financial assumptions

Default financial settings were used in REopt. This includes a 25-year analysis period, a discount rate,
electricity cost escalation rate, and fuel cost escalation rate.

3.1.4.2 Utility rate

Based on utility bills, the site operates using Florida Power & Light’s General Service Large Demand –
Time of Use (GSLDT-1) rate. It is assumed that the rate is applied across the aggregation of every meter
on the site, instead of each meter individually.

Additionally, based on public rate documents, Florida Power & Light provides full retail rate net metering
up to 2 MW. Any energy generation beyond the annual consumption of the site is compensated at 0.03
$/kWh.

3.2 RESULTS

The optimization results from REopt are summarized in Table 1. In all cases, the battery is controlled
optimally to reduce the peak load while maintaining the resiliency targets. Note that the optimization in
all cases does not recommend additional diesel gensets.

The scenarios range from a baseline condition that optimizes solely for cost reduction to more complex
conditions that incorporate the need for resilience during outages. The scenarios specifically examine both
current and future load data, assessing scenarios with outage durations of 5, 7, and 10 days, as well as the
implications of doubling the load during these outages.

In terms of solar photovoltaic (PV) and battery sizing, the baseline scenario (Scenario 1) involves a
modest PV requirement of 207 kW and a battery storage size of 71 kW / 174 kWh. However, as the
scenarios progress to incorporate outages, battery sizes increase notably, especially in Scenario 4, which
requires 3,667 kWh to meet the energy demands of a 10-day outage, resulting in an upfront capital cost of
over $7.4 million. Expectedly, the battery size increases significantly as the outage time increases. Note
that the analysis for Scenarios 1-4 were performed assuming that 100% of all loads are critical. If fewer
loads are served during outage time, the facility can either 1) survive longer for the same size battery, or
2) reduce the size of the battery to save on capital cost.

The financial implications of these scenarios vary significantly as well. While the baseline scenario shows
a positive net present value (NPV) of $71,766, the NPV turns negative in scenarios with longer outage
durations, particularly in Scenario 4 where it drops to -$1,474,126. The analysis demonstrates that there is
a breakpoint between the 5-day and 7-day outage preparedness scenarios where the net present value goes
from positive to negative, underscoring the cost of resilient grid operations.

 6

Lifecycle capital costs also increase with outage duration, reflecting the greater investment needed for
resilience measures. Utility costs in the first year decrease with increasing outage time, due to the
increased size of PV and battery allowing the site to shift more load off-peak and generate more of its
own electricity.

 4. CONCLUSION

The analysis of the Punta Gorda water utility's energy scenarios demonstrates the interplay between
resiliency and financial investment. The varying scenarios highlight the necessity of not just optimizing
for immediate cost reduction but also for long-term sustainability and preparedness for outages of
differing durations. As the required sizing for solar photovoltaic systems and battery storage significantly
escalates with longer outage durations and increased loads, the capital costs also rise sharply, particularly
in the most demanding scenarios.

The results indicate the baseline approach provides a positive net present value which shifts negatively
when considering longer outage scenarios, emphasizing the costs associated with resilient operations.
However, the first-year utility costs demonstrate potential savings from generating electricity on-site.
Ultimately, these findings underscore the importance of a comprehensive and resilient energy strategy
that balances cost considerations with the vital need for reliable service during emergencies. As the Punta
Gorda utility moves forward, these insights should inform future investments and operational strategies to
enhance resilience against unforeseen challenges while remaining economically viable.
