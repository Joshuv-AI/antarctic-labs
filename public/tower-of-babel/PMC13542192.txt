Attention problems statistically mediate the association between mobile phone addiction and mental health and procrastination in Chinese college students
Authors: Lidan Hu, Lejing Guan, Zipei Chen, Lidan Sun, Danny Junyi Tan, Yunxia Hong, Kai Shao, Xiantian Ma, Guannan Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMobile phone addiction is a significant public health issue among college students, associated with poor mental health and procrastination, yet the underlying mechanisms involving attention problems are not well understood. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among college students in Zhejiang Province. Linear regression models were employed to assess the relationships between mobile phone addiction, mental health, and procrastination. Statistical mediation analysis was performed to evaluate the role of attention problems. Data from 755 college students were analyzed. After adjusting for covariates, mobile phone addiction scores were significantly associated with poorer mental health (β = 0.44, p < 0.001) and more severe procrastination (β = 0.23, p < 0.001). In the pathway from mobile phone addiction to mental health, the indirect effect via attention problems (0.159, 95% CI 0.104–0.225) accounted for 36.55% of the total effect, indicating statistical indirect effects consistent with partial mediation. Similarly, in the pathway from mobile phone addiction to procrastination, attention problems showed a statistically significant indirect effect (0.076, 95% CI 0.041–0.115) accounting for 33.33% of the total effect. In this cross-sectional, self-report sample of Chinese college students, attention problems consistent with a partial mediating role in the associations between mobile phone addiction and both mental health issues and procrastination. The results generate testable hypotheses for preventive interventions targeting attention problems. Introduction

Mobile phones have become ubiquitous among college students, serving as essential tools for both academic and social activities. While these devices offer considerable convenience, their pervasive use has raised concerns regarding potential dependence and addiction1. Indeed, a 2018 report by the McKinsey Research Institute indicated that over 80% of Chinese college students exhibited signs of mobile phone dependence2. This pattern of compulsive use, often termed problematic mobile phone use or smartphone addiction in the literature, is widely recognized as a behavioral addiction characterized by a loss of control and continued use despite negative consequences3–5, and sharing key similarities with substance addiction or gambling disorder.

As a population particularly vulnerable to this modern behavioral addiction, college students are increasingly reported to experience a range of associated psychological and behavioral issues6. Consequently, mobile phone addiction represents a significant concern in higher education7, as extensive research links it to adverse outcomes including altered brain structure8, impaired cognitive function9, and diminished academic performance10.

Among these diverse outcomes, mental health impairments are of paramount concern. Mental health is commonly assessed using scales measuring depression, anxiety, and stress. Previous studies have shown that mobile phone addiction significantly predicts anxiety, while anxiety itself may also contribute to increased dependency on mobile phones11, suggesting a bidirectional relationship. Furthermore, beyond emotional distress, cognitive and behavioral correlates are critically involved. Emerging evidence links excessive mobile phone use to symptoms associated with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)12,13, broadly categorized as attention problems. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition marked by persistent inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that are inconsistent with developmental level and impair daily functioning in academic, occupational, and social contexts14. A core deficit in ADHD involves executive dysfunction15, which is also closely associated with procrastination—manifested as difficulties in time management, organization, and task prioritization—a behavior shown to be exacerbated by problematic mobile phone use16.

From a theoretical perspective, the cognitive-behavioral theory suggests that excessive mobile phone use may lead to attention depletion and impaired executive functions by occupying cognitive resources and causing frequent task switching. This impaired cognitive state, theoretically, will reduce an individual’s ability to regulate emotions and manage tasks, and may subsequently manifest as an exacerbation of mental health symptoms and an increase in procrastination behaviors. Drawing on this framework, we propose that attention problems serve as a mediating pathway: mobile phone addiction may deplete attentional resources, which in turn exacerbates mental health symptoms and procrastination.

Therefore, this cross-sectional study aims to examine the associations between mobile phone addiction and mental health and procrastination in a large sample of Chinese college students. More importantly, we investigate the potential statistical mediating role of attention problems in these associations, testing the hypothesis that attention deficits serve as a psychological pathway linking mobile phone addiction to its negative outcomes.

Methods

Study population and data collection

A cross-sectional study was conducted among undergraduate students at a comprehensive university in Hangzhou, China, between November 1 and December 31, 2022. A convenience sampling method was employed for participant recruitment. The electronic questionnaire was developed using the Wenjuanxing platform (www.wjx.cn), and a Quick Response code linking to the survey was distributed via WeChat class groups and posted in high-traffic areas on campus (e.g., canteens, dormitories). University administrators facilitated survey dissemination to ensure all enrolled students had access to the questionnaire. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, with detailed information about the study purpose provided at the beginning of the questionnaire.

The inclusion criteria for participants were full-time undergraduate students aged 18 to 25. Participants were excluded from the final analysis if they met any of the following criteria: (1) incomplete responses; (2) logical inconsistency; or (3) showed a clear pattern of straight-lining in their responses. Of the 864 questionnaires initially received, 109 were excluded based on these quality control criteria, resulting in 755 valid responses for analysis, yielding a valid response rate of 87.38%. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, National Clinical Research Center for Child Health (Approval No.: 2023-IRB-0275-P-01). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional guidelines.

Definition of key terms

To make the research variables clear and actionable, this study has clearly defined the following core terms.

Mobile Phone Addiction refers to an individual’s excessive dependence on mobile phones, inability to control the urge to use them, accompanied by withdrawal symptoms and functional impairments3–5. In this study, the Mobile Phone Addiction Tendency Scale (MPATS) compiled by Xiong Jie et al.17. was used for measurement. The higher the total score, the more severe the tendency of mobile phone addiction.

Attention Problems in this study refers to the difficulties that individuals encounter in maintaining attention, suppressing distractions, working memory, and executive functions, similar to the core symptoms of Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)12,13, but not a clinical diagnosis. It was measured using the Adult Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder Self-Report Scale (ASRS), with a higher total score indicating more prominent attention problems.

Mental Health refers to the severity of negative emotional symptoms such as depression, anxiety and stress that an individual has experienced in the past week. It is measured using the 21-item Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS), with higher scores indicating poorer mental health.

Procrastination refers to the tendency of an individual to unnecessarily delay the initiation or completion of tasks in academic or daily activities. It is measured using the General Procrastination Scale (GPS)18, with a higher total score indicating more severe procrastination behavior.

Questionnaire development and measurements

The questionnaire was developed by a panel of experts in health education and health promotion. It comprised five sections: 1) general information, 2) mobile phone use, 3) mental health status, 4) procrastination, and 5) attention problems. The questionnaire underwent multiple rounds of discussion and a pilot test. A pre-test was administered to 63 undergraduate students from various academic years, followed by brief face-to-face interviews with 15 participants to gather feedback. The final version was revised accordingly to enhance clarity and feasibility for online administration.

General information included gender, age, body mass index (BMI), residence, parental education, monthly household income, sleep routine, self-rated sleep quality, self-rated academic pressure, self-rated popularity, and screen time. BMI = weight(kg)/height2 (m2). BMI classification is based on World Health Organization standards for Asian populations. The four indicators—sleep routine, self-rated sleep quality, self-rated academic pressure and self-rated popularity—are based on the participants’ self-assessments of their cognitive levels and do not have clear cut-off values.

Mobile phone use was assessed using the MPATS developed by Xiong et al.17. The 16-item scale measures four dimensions: withdrawal symptoms, salience, social comfort, and mood changes. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (very inconsistent) to 5 (very consistent), with higher total scores indicating a greater tendency toward mobile phone addiction. In this study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.894).

Mental health was evaluated using the 21-item Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS). Respondents rate each item on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (did not apply to me at all) to 3 (applied to me very much or most of the time). Higher scores indicate more severe symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress. The scale showed excellent internal consistency in this sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.953).

Procrastination was measured using the GPS18, which has been validated in Chinese populations19. The scale comprises 20 items assessing procrastination in academic and daily activities. Items are rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree), with ten items reverse-scored. Total scores range from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating more severe procrastination. The internal consistency in this study was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.710).

Attention problems were screened using the ASRS. The 18-item scale uses a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (never) to 5 (very often) and consists of two subscales: working memory and inhibition. The first six items are considered particularly predictive of ADHD symptoms. Higher total scores indicate more significant attention problems. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in previous studies20,21. In this study, it showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.873).

Importantly, we did not collect data on participants’ medical history (e.g., prior diagnosis of ADHD, depression, or anxiety disorders) or current/past use of psychoactive medications (e.g., stimulants, antidepressants). This absence is a limitation addressed in the Discussion.

Statistical analysis

We conducted the descriptive analysis. The normality was tested. For normally-distributed continuous variables, means and standard deviations were calculated, and for non-normally distributed variables, medians and interquartile ranges were calculated. Numbers and percentages were calculated for the categorical variables. Independent samples t-tests (for two groups) and one-way ANOVA (for ≥ 3 groups) were used to assess the differences of MPATS, DASS, GPS, ASRS scores across subgroups. Levene’s test was used to assess homogeneity of variance. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to examine bivariate relationships among MPATS, DASS, GPS, and ASRS.

Regarding the mediation analysis, we employed the classic framework proposed by Baron and Kenny22,23. This approach involves a series of linear regression models to evaluate the relationships between the independent variable (IV), mediator (M), and dependent variable (DV). Specifically, we tested: (1) the total effect of the IV on the DV (path c); (2) the effect of the IV on the mediator (path a); and (3) the effects of both the mediator (path b) and the IV (direct effect, path c’) on the DV in a combined model. The indirect effect was calculated as the product of paths a and b (a × b), and the total effect was decomposed as c = c’ + a × b.

In the application of our study, we used linear regression models to assess the associations between smart phone use, mental health and procrastination. In the crude model, we included age and MPATS score as independent variables. Additionally, we added other covariates that were selected with statistical significance in the last step to the adjusted model. We assessed the attenuation (or increase) of the original association of MPATS with DASS and GPS scores respectively in the adjusted model by controlling other covariates to evaluate the total effect. The total effect represented the effects of mobile phone addiction on mental health and procrastination without a mediator. Then we added attention problems to evaluate the direct effect which represented the effects of problematic mobile phone use on mental health and procrastination through the mediator. Mediation effects were computed from the regression coefficients, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the indirect effects are reported. An indirect effect was considered statistically significant if the confidence interval did not include zero24. To be noted, we assessed key assumptions of linear regression, including linearity, multicollinearity, normality of residuals, and homoscedasticity, and found them to be satisfactory25. In addition, covariates were selected for inclusion in the regression models based on their potential to act as confounders, defined as variables significantly associated with both the exposure and outcome variables, while not being on the causal pathway25,26. Given the cross-sectional design, the term ‘mediation’ is used here in a statistical sense (testing of indirect effects). No causal claims are intended or can be inferred. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0. The statistical significance was indicated by p < 0.05.

ASRS in the relationships between MPATS and DASS as well as GPS

To test our primary hypothesis, we examined the mediating role of attention problems (ASRS) in the relationships between mobile phone addiction (MPATS) and both mental health (DASS) and procrastination (GPS). As illustrated in Fig. 1, the results supported a partial mediation model in both pathways.

For the association between MPATS and DASS (Fig. 1a), the total effect was significant (β = 0.435). Critically, this effect was partitioned into a significant direct effect (β = 0.276) and a significant indirect effect mediated by ASRS (β = 0.159), which accounted for 63.45% and 36.55% of the total effect, respectively. This indicates that attention problems support partial mediation (over one-third) of the link between mobile phone addiction and poorer mental health.

Similarly, for the pathway from MPATS to GPS (Fig. 1b), the total effect was significant with a β equal to 0.228, and a significant indirect effect via ASRS was observed with a β equal to 0.076, accounting for 33.33% of the total effect. The direct effect (β = 0.152) was accounting for 66.67% of the total effect.

The detailed breakdown of all path coefficients, effects, and their CIs are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Overall, these findings provide statistical evidence of indirect effects consistent with our hypothesis that attention problems play a mediating role. However, due to the cross-sectional design, these results should be interpreted as correlational, not causal.

Discussion

The pervasive use of mobile phones has been increasingly associated with a spectrum of physical and mental health concerns27–29. Our study contributes to this body of evidence by showing a significant positive association between mobile phone addition and both poorer mental health status and increased procrastination among Chinese college students. More importantly, the results provided evidence supporting the role of attention problems as a statistical mediator in these relationships. The mediation analysis indicated that attention problems accounted for 36.55% of the total effect of mobile phone use on mental health and 33.33% of its total effect on procrastination. These findings are consistent with a cognitive pathway, providing a more nuanced understanding of how mobile phone addiction may be linked to adverse psychological and behavioral outcomes, although causal inference requires longitudinal repetition.

Our finding that mobile phone addiction is positively correlated with depression, anxiety, and stress is consistent with prior research30–32. The literature suggests that mobile phone addiction can diminish life and relationship satisfaction, which may subsequently exacerbate depressive symptoms33,34. A longitudinal study of changes in smart phone addiction and depressive symptoms among college students suggests boredom and emotional loneliness were positively correlated with both smart phone addiction and depressed symptoms35. Furthermore, our results indicated that the severity of mobile phone addiction varied significantly across subgroups with different self-rated sleep quality, aligning with existing studies36. Individuals with the higher levels of mobile phone addiction tend to poorer sleep quality and shorter sleep time, potentially leading to daytime dysfunction and emotional disturbances37.

A significant association was also observed between mobile phone use and procrastination. As hypothesized, a higher level of mobile phone dependence was linked to a greater likelihood of procrastination among college students. This finding corroborates previous research13,38. We posit that smartphones may serve as a readily available source of immediate gratification, offering an escape from reality and a means to relieve psychological pressure. This pleasure-driven engagement can foster dependence and facilitate the avoidance of tasks, thereby directly contributing to procrastinatory behavior, which in turn can negatively impact academic performance and daily functioning, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates psychological distress9.

A growing body of evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between mobile phone addiction and attention deficits: mobile phone addiction may act as a risk factor for developing attention problems, while individuals with pre-existing attention deficits may be more vulnerable to developing addictive phone use patterns33,39. Our analysis aligns with this notion, revealing significant connections among smartphone addiction, attention problems, and poor mental health outcomes. Previous studies, including one on South Korean adolescents, have similarly found strong correlations between smartphone addiction scores and ADHD symptoms33,40.

The relationship between ADHD and mental health is well-documented. For instance, children diagnosed with ADHD at an early age exhibit an increased risk of developing depression in adolescence41. A Mendelian randomization study explored the genetic associations between ADHD and six psychiatric disorders, and suggested that individuals with ADHD may have an increased risk for certain psychiatric disorders, which could support the argument that ADHD symptoms potentially precede other mental health issues42. Individuals with ADHD often find themselves in stressful situations due to their symptoms. This can create a vicious cycle where stress exacerbates ADHD symptoms, which in turn leads to more stress. Another systematic review and meta-analysis imply that ADHD’s presence is associated with an increased risk of developing other mental health disorders, supporting the notion that attention deficits may contribute to or exacerbate other psychiatric conditions43. At the same time, some studies have provided evidence that individuals with ADHD symptoms tends to have deficit executive function. Executive function, the high-level cognitive processes involved in goal-directed behavior, are thought to be impaired in attention deficit disorder15,44. ADHD is closely associated with procrastination, as difficulties with time management, organization, and prioritization of tasks are characteristic of the condition. This can lead to a cycle of avoidance and procrastination, further impairing productivity and exacerbating feelings of anxiety and stress45. As a result, those with deficit executive function had a higher likelihood of poor academic performance, which may lead to negative emotional reaction (e.g. depression, anxiety and stress)46. This is in agreement with findings from other studies that people with ADHD symptoms have difficulties in emotion regulation. The correlations between attention deficit and depression, anxiety, stress, and procrastination are complex and intertwined. In light of this complex interplay, our findings position attention problems as a partial mediator in the pathway from mobile phone addiction to adverse mental health and behavioral outcomes. The observed partial mediation of ADHD symptoms in the relationship between mobile phone addiction and procrastination is understandable, given that individuals with executive function deficits are inherently more prone to procrastination.

Taken together, previous studies have examined the pairwise relationships among mobile phone addiction, attention problems, mental health, and procrastination. For example, some studies found that mobile phone addiction is associated with symptoms of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)12,13. Other studies linked ADHD symptoms to depression and procrastination41–43. However, to the best of our knowledge, few studies have simultaneously tested attention problems as a single mediator linking mobile phone addiction to two distinct outcomes (mental health and procrastination) within an integrated model. The present study represents an initial effort to address this gap. It investigated a relatively large sample of college students using standardized, validated scales to comprehensively assess mobile phone addiction and its correlates. By identifying significant associations and highlighting statistical indirect effects consistent with a mediating role of attention problems, this cross-sectional study serves as a crucial preliminary step, generating valuable hypotheses and providing a solid foundation for future longitudinal research. Importantly, given the cross-sectional design, the novelty of our findings does not extend to causal conclusions. Future experimental or longitudinal studies are needed to examine the temporal dynamics of these relationships.

Several limitations of the present study should be considered. Firstly, the use of a convenience sample from a single university may limit the generalizability of our findings. Although the survey was distributed through official channels to ensure broad access, the overrepresentation of first-year students (43.36%) in our final sample suggests the presence of response bias, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Secondly, because of the cross-sectional design, by their nature, capture data at a single point in time, which constrains our ability to infer temporal sequences or causality between variables, obtained results cannot interpret the cause-and-effect relationships and longitudinal studies are needed. Third, we did not collect data on participants’ medical history (e.g., prior diagnosis of ADHD, major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders) or current/past use of psychoactive medications (e.g., stimulants, antidepressants, anxiolytics). Such unmeasured factors could act as confounders. For instance, a student with undiagnosed ADHD might score higher on both ASRS and MPATS, artificially inflating the mediation effect. Similarly, medication use could improve attention and reduce addiction tendencies, biasing our estimates. Future studies should incorporate clinical interviews and medication records to address this limitation. Fourthly, the categorization of screen time, particularly the “3–7 h/day” range, may be considered broad. While this classification allowed for adequate group sizes for statistical comparison, it might mask nuanced differences within this substantial time range. Future research would benefit from employing more granular measures of screen time. Lastly, some of the scales were self-rated (e.g. sleep routine and sleep quality). While clear and concise questions, providing instructions for completion, ensuring the anonymity and confidentiality of responses to encourage participation, these self-test forms still introduce measurement bias in both exposure and outcome, leading to non-differential errors and ultimately biased analysis results.

Conclusions

In summary, this cross-sectional, self-report study found significant positive associations between mobile phone addiction and both poorer mental health and greater procrastination among Chinese college students. Our mediation analysis revealed statistical indirect effects via attention problems, accounting for approximately one-third of the total effects. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that attention problems partially explain these associations. However, because of the cross-sectional design and the absence of data on medical conditions and medication use, causal claims cannot be made, and the term ‘mediation’ is used here in a statistical sense only. Future longitudinal or experimental research with clinical assessments is required to test temporal and causal dynamics. Despite these limitations, our results generate testable hypotheses and inform potential targets (i.e., attention problems) for preventive interventions.
