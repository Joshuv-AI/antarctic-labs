Statistical regularity and linear response of Mather Measures for Tonelli Lagrangian systems Thanks: Statements and Declarations: The authors declare no competing interests and no data were generated or analyzed in this study.
Alfonso Sorrentino, Jianlu Zhang, Siyao Zhu

Abstract
. We study the statistical regularity of Mather measures associated with C 1 C^{1} perturbations of a Tonelli Lagrangian. When the unperturbed Mather measure is supported on a quasi-periodic torus with a Diophantine frequency, we establish Hölder continuity of the perturbed Mather measure with respect to the perturbation parameter. The Hölder exponent is shown to depend explicitly on the Diophantine index of the frequency. We also discuss the possibility of achieving Lipschitz regularity using KAM theory.

1.
Introduction

The Aubry-Mather theory provides a comprehensive description of the minimizing measures for Tonelli Lagrangian systems. A central concept is that of
statistical stability
, which concerns how these Mather measures, as elements of the space of probability measures, change under perturbations of the system. This notion is intimately linked to quantitative estimates of the impact of perturbations on the system’s dynamics.

In this article, we consider two common types of perturbations:

(1)

Mañé’s perturbation

[
27
]
:
L_{\varepsilon}(x,v)=L(x,v)+\varepsilon f(x)
with
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
and
f\in C(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
.

(2)

Cohomological perturbation

[
28
]
:
L_{c}(x,v)=L(x,v)-\langle c,v\rangle
with
c\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and
|c|\ll 1
.

In suitable topologies,
L_{\varepsilon}\to L
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and
L_{c}\to L
as
c\to{0}
. Our goal is to investigate the regularity of the associated Mather measures
\mu_{\varepsilon}
and
\mu_{c}
as functions of the parameters
\varepsilon
and
c
. To make this precise, we equip the space of Borel probability measures on the tangent bundle
T\mathbb{T}^{n}
, that we denote
\mathcal{P}(T\mathbb{T}^{n})
, with the so-called
1
-Wasserstein distance (see
[
27
]
for an alternative choice of topology).

We say the map
\varepsilon\mapsto\mu_{\varepsilon}
(respectively,
c\mapsto\mu_{c}
) is
statistically stable
if it is continuous at
0
(or
{0}
). The notion of
statistical regularity
refines this by providing a modulus of continuity. Naturally, one aims for stronger forms of regularity, such as Hölder or Lipschitz continuity, or even differentiability (the so called
linear response
). These are more challenging to obtain and typically require more detailed dynamical information about the perturbed system.

Remark 1.1
.

For uniformly hyperbolic systems, this program is well-developed, with results on Lipschitz regularity and, in some cases, differentiability with respect to perturbations (see
[
1
,
20
]
). In such systems, it is also known that generic Mather measures are supported on a unique hyperbolic periodic orbit
[
9
]
. However, Tonelli Lagrangian systems are generally not uniformly hyperbolic, and much less is known about the regularity of their Mather measures. The famous Mañé conjecture posits that for generic Lagrangians, the Mather measure is ergodic and supported on a unique hyperbolic periodic orbit. While significant progress has been made from a perturbative viewpoint
[
18
,
19
,
9
,
10
]
, this conjecture remains largely open.

In this paper, we focus on the two classes of perturbations mentioned above. Under the key assumption that the unperturbed Mather measure of
L
is supported on a quasi-periodic torus with a non-resonant frequency, we establish Hölder continuity for the perturbed Mather measures. Since such a quasi-periodic torus is not hyperbolic, this result provides a first step toward understanding the statistical stability of non-hyperbolic systems.

Structure of the paper.
This article is organized as follows:

•

In Section

we gather the necessary preliminary material that will be used throughout the rest of the article.

•

In Section

•

In Section

2.1.
Notation and setting

Throughout this paper,
\mathbb{T}^{n}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{Z}^{n}
denotes the flat
n
-dimensional torus, equipped with the standard Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
. The tangent and cotangent bundles over
\mathbb{T}^{n}
are denoted by
T\mathbb{T}^{n}
and
T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{n}
, with coordinates
(x,v)
and
(x,p)
, respectively. For both,
\pi:T\mathbb{T}^{n}\text{ (resp. }T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{n}\text{)}\to\mathbb{T}^{n}
is the canonical projection. The Euclidean norm is naturally inherited by the fibers.

C(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
denotes the space of continuous functions with the sup norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. The spaces of
k
-times continuously differentiable and Lipschitz continuous functions are denoted by
C^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and
C^{0,1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
. We denote by
C_{c}(T\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
the space of continuous functions on
T\mathbb{T}^{n}
with compact support.

A vector
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
non-resonant
if

\langle k,\omega\rangle\neq 0\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{{0}\}.

More strongly,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
Diophantine with exponent

\tau\geq n-1
if there exists a constant
\sigma>0
such that

(1)

|\langle\omega,k\rangle|\;\geq\;\sigma|k|^{-\tau},\quad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{{0}\}.

Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,\tau}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the set of such vectors.

Remark 2.1
.

For an irrational number
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, the Diophantine exponent corresponding to it can be characterized as

(2)

\displaystyle\tau(\nu)\;=\;\sup\left\{\gamma\geq 0:\liminf_{m\to+\infty}m^{\gamma}\,\|m\nu\|_{\mathbb{Z}}=0\right\}

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{Z}}
denotes the distance to the nearest integer.

2.2.
Viscosity solutions

Consider a Hamiltonian
H:T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that is continuous, convex, and superlinear in the momentum variable
p
. Its Lagrangian conjugate
L:T\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by the Fenchel transform

L(x,v):=\sup_{p\in T_{x}^{*}\mathbb{T}^{n}}\bigl\{\langle p,v\rangle-H(x,p)\bigr\}.

For such a Hamiltonian, there exists a unique constant
c(H)\in\mathbb{R}
, called the
ergodic constant
or
Mañé critical value
, such that the Hamilton-Jacobi equation

(3)

H(x,du)=c(H),\quad x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}

admits a viscosity solution
[
17
,
28
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

A function
u\in C(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
is a
viscosity subsolution
of (
3
) if, for every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
and every
C^{1}
function
\varphi
such that
u-\varphi
has a local maximum at
x_{0}
, we have
H(x_{0},d\varphi(x_{0}))\leq c(H)
. It is a
viscosity supersolution
if for every local minimum of
u-\varphi
at
x_{0}
, we have
H(x_{0},d\varphi(x_{0}))\geq c(H)
. A function that is both a subsolution and a supersolution is a
viscosity solution
.

In the following, all solutions, subsolutions, and supersolutions are understood with respect to this critical value
c(H)
unless otherwise stated.

A key concept for non-smooth subsolutions is that of a
reachable gradient

[
6
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
u
be a viscosity subsolution of (
3
). A vector
p\in T_{x}^{*}\mathbb{T}^{n}
is a
reachable gradient
of
u
at
x
if there exists a sequence
\{x_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus\{x\}
such that
u
is differentiable at each
x_{k}
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}(x_{k},du(x_{k}))=(x,p)
. The set of all reachable gradients of
u
at
x
is denoted by
D^{*}u(x)
.

2.3.
Aubry-Mather theory for low-regularity Lagrangians

We now assume
L
is only continuous.
For every
t>0
, we define the
minimal action function

h_{t}:\mathbb{T}^{n}\times\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

h_{t}(x,y):=\inf_{\gamma}\int_{-t}^{0}L\big(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s)\big)+c(H)\,ds,

where the infimum is taken over all absolutely continuous curves
\gamma:[-t,0]\to M
satisfying
\gamma(-t)=x
and
\gamma(0)=y
. The
Peierls barrier
is defined as

h^{\infty}(x,y):=\liminf_{t\to+\infty}h_{t}(x,y);

it turns out to be well posed for any

The

\mathcal{A}:=\{x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}:h^{\infty}(x,x)=0\}.

[

For any

\int_{a}^{b}L(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))+c(H)\,ds+h^{\infty}(\gamma(b),\gamma(a))=0.

For any critical subsolution

The

is then defined as the intersection

where the intersection is taken over all subsolutions

is well-defined.

Lemma 2.5
.

The canonical projection
\pi:\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathcal{A}

Proof.

This result has already been established in Theorem B.14 of

For each critical subsolution

By combining this property with the definition of static curve, one deduces that, for almost every differentiability point

At a non-differentiable point

Definition 2.6

(Mather measure)

A probability measure

Remark 2.8
.

If
H
(and hence

) is
C^{2}
and strictly convex, it is called a Tonelli Hamiltonian/Lagrangian. In this case, Mather measures are invariant under the Euler-Lagrange flow
[
28
]

Before concluding this section, we recall the
1
-Wasserstein distance (also known as the Kantorovich-Rubinstein distance). For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(T\mathbb{T}^{n})
, it is defined as

(8)

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}\times\mathbb{T}^{n}}d(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of couplings. A useful dual formulation is

(9)

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\psi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\left\{\int\psi\,d\mu-\int\psi\,d\nu\right\},

where
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
denotes 1-Lipschitz functions. See
[
31
]
for details.

Remark 2.9
.

This distance is related to the so-called Monge’s problem.
Given two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on metric spaces
X
and
Y
, respectively, and a cost function
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
, Monge’s problem consists in finding a measurable map
T:X\to Y
that pushes
\mu
forward to
\nu
(i.e.
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
) and minimizes the total cost

(10)

\inf_{T_{\#}\mu=\nu}\int_{X}c(x,T(x))\,d\mu(x).

A transport map (i.e., a map achieving the infimum) may not exist in general, which motivates Kantorovich’s relaxation or Kantorovich’s problem:
let
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
be the set of probability measures
\pi
on
X\times Y
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. The relaxed problem reads

(11)

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y).

Wasserstein distance arises naturally when the cost function is chosen as a power of the metric. More specifically,
for
p\in[1,\infty)
, let
\mathcal{P}_{p}(X)
be the space of Borel probability measures on
(X,d)
having finite
p
-th moment.
The
p
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(X)
is given by:

(12)

W_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\left(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times X}d(x,y)^{p}\,d\pi(x,y)\right)^{1/p}.

3.
Statistical regularity of the Mather measures for Mañé’s Lagrangians

We now consider a family of Mañé’s Lagrangians
L_{\delta}:T\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined by

(13)

L_{\delta}(x,v):=\frac{1}{2}\left|v-V_{\delta}(x)\right|^{2},

where
\delta\geq 0
is a small parameter and
V_{\delta}:\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a family of Lipschitz vector fields satisfying the following assumptions:

(A1)

V_{0}(x)=\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a non-resonant vector.

(A2)

\omega
satisfies one of the following two conditions:

(i)

\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,\tau}
is a finite Diophantine vector;

(ii)

\omega=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})
is a vector whose components
\omega_{i}
are all algebraic for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

(A3)

For
0\leq\delta\ll 1
, we have

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lvert V_{\delta}(x)-\omega\rvert\leq\delta.

It is worth noting that, whether (i) or (ii) in Assumption
(A2)
holds, the corresponding vectors always form a prevalent subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Indeed, for case (i), if
\tau>n-1
, the Diophantine set
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,\tau}
has asymptotically full Lebesgue measure as
\sigma\to 0
.
For case (ii), Lemma 2.10 in
[
22
]
shows that the set of non-resonant vectors whose components are algebraic also has full measure. We also point out that for
n=2
, thanks to
Roth’s theorem
(see
[
7
]
), any non-resonant vector
\omega
with all algebraic components necessarily satisfies a finite Diophantine condition, i.e.,
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,\tau}
for suitable
\sigma\in[1,2)
; however, for
n\geq 3
, we do not know whether (ii) implies (i).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}
denote the set of projected Mather measures associated with
L_{\delta}
.
For any
\mu_{\delta}\in\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}
, one has

(\varphi_{\delta}^{t})_{\#}\mu_{\delta}=\mu_{\delta},\qquad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R},

where
\varphi_{\delta}^{t}:\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}^{n}
denotes the flow generated by the ODE

(14)

\dot{x}=V_{\delta}(x),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

In other words, every
\mu_{\delta}\in\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}
is invariant under the flow
\varphi_{\delta}^{t}
. Consequently,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}
is nonempty.

Proof.

Step 1: Existence of Mather measures.

Since
L_{\delta}
is of Mañé type, its critical value is
c(H_{\delta})=0
, i.e.,
\inf_{\tilde{\mu}\in\mathfrak{P}_{0}}\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}L_{\delta}\,d\tilde{\mu}=0
. For any initial point
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
, there exists an integral curve
\gamma:[0,T]\to\mathbb{T}^{n}
solving equation (
14
) with
\gamma(0)=x
.
Using this curve
\gamma
, we define a probability measure
\tilde{\mu}_{\gamma}
on the tangent bundle
T\mathbb{T}^{n}
by

\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}\psi(x,v)\,d\tilde{\mu}_{\gamma}(x,v):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\psi(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\,dt,

for any bounded measurable function
\psi:T\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
.

We have

\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}|v|\,d\tilde{\mu}_{\gamma}(x,v)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|\,dt=\frac{\ell_{g}(\gamma)}{T}<+\infty,

where
\ell_{g}(\gamma)
denotes the Riemannian length of
\gamma
, which is finite since
\gamma
is absolutely continuous on the compact interval
[0,T]
.
Moreover, for any
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
,

\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}df_{x}(v)\,d\tilde{\mu}_{\gamma}(x,v)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))\,dt=\frac{f(\gamma(T))-f(\gamma(0))}{T}.

Taking a sequence
T_{k}\to\infty
, by the compactness of the space of probability measures and the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there exists a subsequence
\tilde{\mu}_{\gamma_{k}}
converging in the weak
∗
topology to a probability measure
\tilde{\mu}_{\infty}
.
Moreover, since
\gamma(t)
is an integral curve of (
14
), we have

\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}L_{\delta}(x,v)\,d\tilde{\mu}_{\infty}(x,v)=0,

which implies
\tilde{\mu}_{\infty}
is a minimizing Mather measure associated with
L_{\delta}
.
Therefore, the Mather measure set is nonempty, i.e.,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}\neq\emptyset
.

Step 2: Graph property of the Mather set.

Since
u\equiv 0
is a smooth solution of the Mañé-type Hamilton-Jacobi equation, the Aubry set
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}
satisfies

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}\subset\mathcal{L}(0)=\{(x,V_{\delta}(x))\mid x\in\mathcal{A}_{\delta}\}.

By Lemma
2.7
, the Mather set is contained in the Aubry set. Together with the inclusion above, we conclude that

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\delta}=\{(x,V_{\delta}(x))\mid x\in\mathcal{M}_{\delta}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\delta}\}.

Step 3: Invariance of projected Mather measures.

For any
\mu_{\delta}\in\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}
, the graph property of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\delta}
implies that

(15)

\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}\Phi(x,v)\,d\tilde{\mu}_{\delta}(x,v)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\Phi\big(x,V_{\delta}(x)\big)\,d\mu_{\delta}(x),\quad\forall\Phi\in C_{c}(T\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R}).

Since
\tilde{\mu}_{\delta}
is a closed measure, for any
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
we have

(16)

\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}d\phi_{x}(v)\,d\tilde{\mu}_{\delta}(x,v)=0.

By taking
\Phi(x,v)=d\phi_{x}(v)
in (
15
) and combining with (
16
), it follows that

(17)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}d\phi_{x}\big(V_{\delta}(x)\big)\,d\mu_{\delta}(x)=0,\quad\forall\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R}).

Let
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
. We compute

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi\,d\big((\varphi_{\delta}^{t})_{\#}\mu_{\delta}-\mu_{\delta}\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\big(\phi(\varphi_{\delta}^{t}(x))-\phi(x)\big)\,d\mu_{\delta}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d}{ds}\phi(\varphi_{\delta}^{s}(x))\,ds\,d\mu_{\delta}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\int_{0}^{t}D\phi(\varphi_{\delta}^{s}(x))\cdot V_{\delta}(\varphi_{\delta}^{s}(x))\,ds\,d\mu_{\delta}(x),

where we have applied the fundamental theorem of calculus, the chain rule, and the definition of the flow
\varphi_{\delta}^{t}
.

Set
\phi_{s}(x):=\phi(\varphi_{\delta}^{s}(x))
for each fixed
s\in[0,t]
.
By (
17
) applied to
\phi_{s}
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}D\phi_{s}(x)\cdot V_{\delta}(x)\,d\mu_{\delta}(x)=0.

Thus,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi\,d\big((\varphi_{\delta}^{t})_{\#}\mu_{\delta}-\mu_{\delta}\big)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}D\phi_{s}(x)\cdot V_{\delta}(x)\,d\mu_{\delta}(x)\,ds=0.

Since this holds for every
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, we conclude that
\mu_{\delta}
is invariant under the flow
\varphi_{\delta}^{t}
.

Moreover, by the density of
C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
in
\mathrm{Lip}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
with respect to the uniform norm and the dominated convergence theorem, the same conclusion extends to all Lipschitz test functions:

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi\,d\big((\varphi_{\delta}^{t})_{\#}\mu_{\delta}-\mu_{\delta}\big)=0,\quad\forall\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R}).

The proof of Lemma
3.1
is now complete.
∎

Due to Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A3)
,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}
(resp.
\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}
) may contain multiple elements, while
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
(resp.
\mathfrak{M}_{0}
) is a singleton. As we are concerned with the Wasserstein distance between any Mather measure of
L_{\delta}
and that of
L_{0}
, we shall, without risk of confusion, write
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}
(resp.
\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}
) to indicate an arbitrary choice of measure from the respective set.

3.1.
Upper bound

In this subsection, we establish an upper bound for the Wasserstein distance between Mather measures under perturbations. Our analysis relies on a quantitative ergodic lemma, which will serve as a key technical ingredient:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
F\in C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
with
n\geq 2
.

(i)

[Theorem 3 in
[
24
]
] Let
\omega=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,\tau}
.
There exists a constant
C=C\bigl(n,\sigma,\tau,\|F\|_{C^{0,\alpha}}\bigr)
such that

\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}F(x+\omega t)\,dt-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}F({x})\,d{x}\right|\leq C\,T^{-\frac{\alpha}{\tau+n}}.

(ii)

[Proposition 2.12 in
[
22
]
] Let
\omega=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be non-resonant with
\omega_{i}
algebraic for
i=1,\ldots,n
. For every
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, there exists a constant
C=C(n,\omega,\|F\|_{C^{0,\alpha}},\varepsilon)
such that:

\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}F(x+\omega t)\,dt-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}F({x})\,d{x}\right|\leq C_{\varepsilon}T^{-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+n-1+\varepsilon}}.

(iii)

[Proposition 2.12 in
[
22
]
] If
n=2
,
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
, and the Diophantine index
\tau_{\omega}=1
, then there exists a constant
C=C(\tau,\|F\|_{C^{0,\alpha}})
such that:

\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}F(x+\omega t)\,dt-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}F({x})\,d{x}\right|\leq CT^{-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}.

Theorem 3.3

(Hölder continuity)
.

Assume that Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A3)
hold.
Then there exist constants
l\in(0,1)
and
C=C(n,\omega)
such that, for all
\delta>0
sufficiently small,

W_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta})\leq C\,\delta^{\,l},\qquad W_{1}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta})\leq C\,\delta^{\,l}.

The exponent
l
depends only on
n
and
\omega
(see Remark
3.4
for a discussion on its possible values).

Proof.

We present the proof under the first case of Assumption
(A2)
together with
(A3)
; the other case can be treated similarly. By the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality formula (see Remark
2.9
), we have

W_{1}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta})=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\left|\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}f\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}-\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}f\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}\right|.

By the graph property of Mather measures, we can rewrite the right-hand side as

W_{1}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta})=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x,V_{0}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}_{0}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x,V_{\delta}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\right|.

To decompose the difference, we add and subtract the quantity
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x,V_{0}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}
, obtaining

\displaystyle W_{1}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\Bigg|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x,V_{0}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}_{0}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x,V_{0}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x,V_{0}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x,V_{\delta}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\Bigg[\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x,V_{0}(x))\,d(\mathfrak{M}_{0}-\mathfrak{M}_{\delta})\right|

(18)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\left|f(x,V_{0}(x))-f(x,V_{\delta}(x))\right|\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\Bigg].

By Assumption
(A3)
, the second term in (
3.1
) is bounded above by
\delta
. Hence, we obtain the following inequality:

(19)

W_{1}\big(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}\big)\leq W_{1}\big(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\big)+\delta.

By Lemma
3.1
, we have the following equalities:

\mathfrak{M}_{0}=(\varphi_{0}^{t})_{\#}\mathfrak{M}_{0},\quad\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}=(\varphi_{\delta}^{t})_{\#}\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}

for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then by the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality formula, for any
T>0
,

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f\,d\mathfrak{M}_{0}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\Bigg|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f\,d\mathfrak{M}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f\circ\varphi_{0}^{t}\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\,dt

(20)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\big(f\circ\varphi_{0}^{t}-f\circ\varphi_{\delta}^{t}\big)\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\,dt\Bigg|

Note that every probability measure on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
can be approximated in the weak topology by a convex combination of Dirac measures. More precisely, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exist points
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
and weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\geq 0
with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=1
such that

\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\phi(x_{i})\right|\leq\varepsilon,\quad\forall\phi\in C(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R}).

Since
\omega
is non-resonant, the function
t\mapsto f(x+\omega t)
is quasi periodic
2
2

2

We call a function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
quasi periodic, if there exists a function
\psi:\mathbb{T}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a non-resonant vector
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, such that
\phi(x)=\psi(x\cdot\nu)
.

. Using Lemma
3.2
, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f\,d\mathfrak{M}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x+\omega t)\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f\,d\mathfrak{M}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}f(x_{i}+\omega t)\,dt\right|+\varepsilon

(21)

\displaystyle\quad\leq CT^{-\beta}+\varepsilon,

where
\beta=\frac{\alpha}{\tau+n}\in(0,1)
is a constant. Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we conclude

\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f\,d\mathfrak{M}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}f(x+\omega t)\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\,dt\right|\leq CT^{-\beta}.

By equation (
14
), we have

\varphi_{\delta}^{t}(x)=x+\int_{0}^{t}V_{\delta}(\varphi_{\delta}^{s}(x))\,ds.

Together with
\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1
, this implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}|f\circ\varphi_{0}^{t}(x)-f\circ\varphi_{\delta}^{t}(x)|\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}(x)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\int_{0}^{t}|\omega-V_{\delta}(\varphi_{\delta}^{s}(x))|\,ds\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}(x)\,dt.

Using Assumption
(A3)
, we have

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}|f\circ\varphi_{0}^{t}-f\circ\varphi_{\delta}^{t}|\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\int_{0}^{t}\delta\,ds\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta}\,dt\leq\frac{T\delta}{2}.

Substituting the estimates from (
3.1
) and (
22
) into inequality (
20
), we derive the following bound:

(23)

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta})\leq C_{1}T^{-\beta}+C_{2}T\delta,

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are constants.

Since (
23
) holds for any
T>0
, we can minimize the function

g(T):=C_{1}T^{-\beta}+C_{2}T\delta.

The minimum point of
g(T)
is given by

T_{\text{min}}=\tilde{C}\delta^{-\frac{1}{\beta+1}}

for some constant
\tilde{C}>0
. Evaluating
g(T_{\text{min}})
, we have

g_{\text{min}}\leq C_{3}\delta^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}}.

Substituting this into (
23
), we deduce that

W_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta})\leq C\delta^{\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}}.

Substituting the above inequality into inequality (
19
), we finally obtain

W_{1}\big(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta}\big)\leq C\delta^{\,l},\quad\text{with }l=\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\in(0,1).

Here,
l
is a constant depending on
n
and
\omega
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

(i)

The Hölder exponent for the
W_{1}
-distance of the Mather measure
strongly depends
on the convergence rate provided by Lemma
3.2
. When
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,\tau}
, we have
\beta=\alpha/(\tau+n)
and the exponent
l
is given by

l=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\tau+n}.

For Lipschitz observables (
\alpha=1
), this simplifies to
l=1/(1+\tau+n)
. In particular, if
n=2
and
\tau=1
, this yields
l=1/4
.

(ii)

If
\omega
is non-resonant and each component
\omega_{i}
is algebraic, then by Schmidt’s subspace theorem,
\omega
is Diophantine with some exponent
\tau
. For such
\omega
, the exponent
l
can be made arbitrarily close to
1/(n+1)
by taking
\varepsilon
small in Lemma
3.2
(ii), namely
the exponent
l
in Theorem
3.3
improves to

(24)

\displaystyle l=\frac{1}{n+\varepsilon+1},

where
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrarily small.

(iii)

In fact, it is sufficient to estimate
|V_{\delta}-V_{0}|
restricted to the projected Mather set
\mathcal{M}_{\delta}
in (
3.1
) and (
22
), and the proof remains valid.

3.2.
Lower bound in dimension 2

In this subsection, we obtain a lower bound for the
W_{1}
-distance between the Mather measures in the case
n=2
. The reason we specifically choose
n=2
is that Dirichlet’s approximation theorem guarantees that for any irrational
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist infinitely many pairs
(q,p)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
q>0
such that

(25)

\left|\nu-\frac{p}{q}\right|\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}}.

On the other hand, the Diophantine exponent of
(\nu,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is at least
1
, which matches the approximation rate in (
25
). This yields a sharp lower bound for the Wasserstein distance when
\nu
has Diophantine exponent
1
.

Remark 3.5
.

For
n>3
, there is a gap between the minimal Diophantine index and the exponent in the Dirichlet’s approximation (see
[
8
]
), therefore it is not possible to get an analogue of Diophantine approximation to the frequency. Nonetheless, in subsection
3.3
we still provide a possible lower bound under special arithmetic conditions.

Definition 3.6

(Physical measure)
.

Let
\varphi_{V}^{t}:\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}^{n}
be the continuous-time flow generated by a Lipschitz vector field
V:\mathbb{T}^{n}\to T\mathbb{T}^{n}
. For any
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
and
T>0
, define the empirical measure

\mu_{T}^{x}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\delta_{\varphi_{V}^{t}(x)}\,dt
,
where
\delta_{y}
denotes the Dirac measure at
y\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
.
An invariant Borel probability measure
\mu
for
\varphi_{V}^{t}
is called a
physical measure
if the set

\mathcal{B}(\mu):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}:\lim_{T\to\infty}\mu_{T}^{x}=\mu\text{ in the weak* topology}\right\}

has positive Lebesgue measure. The set
\mathcal{B}(\mu)
is called the
basin of attraction
of
\mu
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
n=2
and
\omega=(1,\nu)
, where
\nu
is Diophantine with exponent
1<\tau(\nu)<\infty
.
Then, for any
0<r<\tau(\nu)
, there exist a sequence
\{\delta_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

with
\delta_{j}>0
,
\delta_{j}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
and a corresponding sequence of
vector field
V_{\delta_{j}}
satifying

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}|V_{0}-V_{\delta_{j}}(x)|=\delta_{j},

such that

(26)

W_{1}\bigl(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{j}}\bigr)\geq c\,\delta_{j}^{1/(r+1)}\qquad\text{for all }j\in\mathbb{N}.

for some constant
c>0
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\nu<1
. For any
r<\tau(\nu)
, the definition of
\tau(\nu)
implies the existence of infinitely many integers
k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
and
p_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\left|\nu-\frac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{k_{n}^{r+1}}.

We set
V_{\delta_{n}}(x):=(1,p_{n}/k_{n})
for all
x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Then
\delta_{n}=|V_{\delta_{n}}-V_{0}|\leq 1/k_{n}^{r+1}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

For each rational
p_{n}/k_{n}
, the corresponding flow on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
admits a periodic orbit. Introduce the global transversal section

\Sigma:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}^{2}:\ x=0\}.

The associated Poincaré map
P_{n}:\Sigma\to\Sigma
is a rigid rotation given by

P_{n}(y)=y+\frac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}\bmod 1.

Consequently, the periodic orbit of the flow intersects
\Sigma
at exactly
k_{n}
distinct points. In the fundamental domain
(0,1)\times(0,1)
, the closed orbit decomposes into
k_{n}
parallel line segments:

l_{i}:=\bigl\{(x,y)\in(0,1)^{2}:\ y=\tfrac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}x+\tfrac{i}{k_{n}}\bmod 1\bigr\},\quad i=0,1,\dots,k_{n}-1.

We endow each
l_{i}
with normalized arc-length measure and define the probability measure

\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}}:=\frac{1}{k_{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{k_{n}-1}\mathfrak{m}|_{l_{i}},

supported on the periodic orbit. To obtain a lower bound for
W_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}})
, consider the function

\psi(x):=\min_{0\leq i\leq k_{n}-1}\operatorname{dist}(x,l_{i}),

where
\operatorname{dist}(x,l_{i})
denotes the Euclidean distance to
l_{i}
in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Since
\psi
is the minimum of finitely many distance functions, it is
1
-Lipschitz. Therefore, by Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality,

W_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}})\geq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\psi\,d\mathfrak{M}_{0}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\psi\,d\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\psi\,d\mathfrak{M}_{0},

because
\psi
vanishes on
\operatorname{supp}(\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}})
.

The distance between consecutive lines
l_{i}
and
l_{i+1}
is
d_{n}=1/(k_{n}\sqrt{1+(p_{n}/k_{n})^{2}})
. A direct computation gives

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\psi\,d\mathfrak{M}_{0}=k_{n}\int_{0}^{d_{n}}\min(t,d_{n}-t)\,dt=\frac{d_{n}^{2}}{4}=\frac{1}{4k_{n}^{2}\bigl(1+(p_{n}/k_{n})^{2}\bigr)}.

Since
p_{n}/k_{n}
is bounded, there exists
c>0
such that

W_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}})\geq\frac{c}{k_{n}}.

Because
\delta_{n}\leq k_{n}^{-(r+1)}
, we obtain
k_{n}\leq\delta_{n}^{-1/(r+1)}
, and thus

W_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}})\geq c\,\delta_{n}^{1/(r+1)}.

To ensure that
\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}}
is indeed a physical measure for a smooth perturbation, we construct a smooth vector field
V_{\delta_{n}}
satisfying the given bound and having the periodic orbit as an attractor (this construction is inspired by Proposition 18 in
[
21
]
).
For each strip between
l_{i}
and
l_{i+1}
, define the midline

m_{i}:=\{(x,y)\in(0,1)^{2}:y=\tfrac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}x+\tfrac{i+1/2}{k_{n}}\bmod 1\}.

Perturb the vector field in the vertical direction:

V_{\delta_{n}}(x,y):=\bigl(1,\tfrac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}+\delta\,g(x,y)\bigr),

where
\delta>0
is small,
g:(0,1)\times(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function, positive above
m_{i}
, negative below
m_{i}
, and vanishing on each
l_{i}
and
m_{i}
, with appropriate monotonicity. More specifically, for each
i
:

•

g(x,y)<0
for
y\in(\tfrac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}x+\tfrac{i}{k_{n}},\tfrac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}x+\tfrac{i+1/2}{k_{n}})
, monotone increasing there,

•

g(x,y)>0
for
y\in(\tfrac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}x+\tfrac{i+1/2}{k_{n}},\tfrac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}x+\tfrac{i+1}{k_{n}})
, monotone decreasing there,

•

g(x,\tfrac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}x+\tfrac{i}{k_{n}})=g(x,\tfrac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}x+\tfrac{i+1/2}{k_{n}})=g(x,\tfrac{p_{n}}{k_{n}}x+\tfrac{i+1}{k_{n}})=0
,

•

g
is smoothed near the endpoints to ensure
C^{\infty}
regularity.

For sufficiently small
\delta>0
, this construction makes each
l_{i}
an attracting invariant curve.
Consequently, the time averages of almost every orbit converge to a measure supported entirely on
\bigcup_{i}l_{i}
, which coincides with the previously defined measure
\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}}
.
Thus
\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}}
is a physical measure and satisfies the lower bound (
26
).
This completes the proof of Lemma
3.7
.
∎

Based on Lemma
3.7
, we obtain a lower bound on the
W_{1}
-distance of the Mather measures:

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
n=2
and
V_{0}=\omega=(1,\nu)
, where
\nu
is Diophantine with exponent
1\leq\tau(\nu)<\infty
.
Then, for any
0<r<\tau(\nu)
, there exist a sequence
\{\delta_{j}>0\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
tending to
0
as
j\to\infty
and a corresponding sequence of
vector field
V_{\delta_{j}}
satifying

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}|V_{0}-V_{\delta_{j}}(x)|=\delta_{j},

such that

W_{1}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\,\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{j}}\bigr)\geq c\,\delta_{j}^{1/(r+1)},

where
c>0
is a constant independent of
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

We lift the projected Mather measure
\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}}
from Lemma
3.7
to the tangent bundle:

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{n}}:=\frac{1}{k_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\left(\mathfrak{m}|_{l_{i}}\otimes\delta_{V_{\delta_{n}}}\right),

where
\mathfrak{m}|_{l_{i}}
is the normalized Lebesgue measure on the line
l_{i}
, and
\delta_{V_{\delta_{n}}}
is the Dirac measure at the constant velocity
V_{\delta_{n}}=(1,p_{n}/k_{n})
. The unperturbed Mather measure is
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}:=\mathfrak{m}\otimes\delta_{V_{0}}
, with
V_{0}=(1,\nu)
. By the graph property,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{n}}
is indeed the Mather measure associated with
L_{\delta_{n}}
, since it is supported on the graph of the flow induced by
V_{\delta_{n}}
.

To estimate
W_{1}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{n}})
, consider an optimal transport map
T(x,v)=(T_{1}(x),T_{2}(x,v))
pushing
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
to
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{n}}
, where
T_{1}(x)
maps
x
to its nearest point on
\bigcup_{i}l_{i}
, and
T_{2}(x,v)\equiv V_{\delta_{n}}
, which replaces the constant velocity
V_{0}
with the perturbed vector
V_{\delta_{n}}
. Then

W_{1}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{n}})=\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{2}}|T(x,v)-(x,v)|\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\sqrt{|T_{1}(x)-x|^{2}+|V_{\delta_{n}}-V_{0}|^{2}}\,dx.

Using
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\geq(a+b)/\sqrt{2}
for
a,b\geq 0
, we get

W_{1}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{n}})\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|T_{1}(x)-x|\,dx+|V_{\delta_{n}}-V_{0}|\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(W_{1}(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{n}})+\delta_{n}\right).

Applying Lemma
3.7
yields the desired lower bound:

W_{1}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{n}})\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1}{4}\delta_{n}^{\frac{1}{r+1}}+\delta_{n}\right)\sim\mathcal{O}\left(\delta_{n}^{\frac{1}{r+1}}\right)

as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

In the theorem above, the lower bound exponent is
1/(r+1)
, which cannot be less than
1/2
(since
\tau(\nu)\geq 1
). Meanwhile, Theorem
3.3
provides an upper bound exponent
l\leq 1/3
for the case
\tau=1,n=2
. Thus there is a gap between the exponents obtained from the upper and lower bounds.

3.3.
Lower bound in higher dimensions (
n>2
)

The previous argument suggests a possible lower bound for
n>2
under additional arithmetic assumptions. While a general lower bound is not available, we present a result under a restrictive arithmetic condition.

Let the non-resonant vector be

\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{n-1},1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and assume that each component
\omega_{k}
has Diophantine exponent
\tau(\omega_{k})=1
for
k=1,\dots,n-1
.

For each component
\omega_{k}
, choose a sequence of rational approximants

\frac{p_{k,m}}{q_{k,m}},\qquad p_{k,m},q_{k,m}\in\mathbb{Z},\ q_{k,m}>0,

and define the perturbed velocity vector

V_{\delta_{m}}:=\left(\frac{p_{1,m}}{q_{1,m}},\,\frac{p_{2,m}}{q_{2,m}},\,\dots,\,\frac{p_{n-1,m}}{q_{n-1,m}},\,1\right).

Let
T_{m}=\operatorname{lcm}(q_{1,m},\dots,q_{n-1,m})
, and set
Q_{m}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}q_{k,m}
. Since

0<{\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}q_{k,m}}\leq T_{m}\leq\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}q_{k,m}\leq\bigl(\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}q_{k,m}\bigr)^{n-1},

then
Q_{m}\leq T_{m}\leq Q_{m}^{n-1}
. Define

\delta_{m}:=\max_{1\leq k\leq n-1}\left|\omega_{k}-\frac{p_{k,m}}{q_{k,m}}\right|.

Proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
3.8
, we obtain
a lower bound for the Wasserstein distance between the Mather measures:

(27)

W_{1}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{m}}\bigr)\;\geq\;C\Bigl(W_{1}\bigl(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{m}}\bigr)+|V_{\delta_{m}}-V_{0}|\Bigr),

where
C>0
denotes a constant independent of
m
. With the above choice of rational approximations, one can construct periodic orbits whose associated Poincaré sections are divided into
T_{m}
equal parts within a single period. Therefore,

(28)

W_{1}\bigl(\mathfrak{M}_{0},\mathfrak{M}_{\delta_{m}}\bigr)\;\geq\;\frac{C}{T_{m}^{1/(n-1)}}\;\geq\;\frac{C}{Q_{m}}.

On the other hand, the velocity difference is bounded, i.e.,

(29)

\delta_{m}\;\leq\;|V_{\delta_{m}}-V_{0}|\;\leq\;\sqrt{n-1}\,\delta_{m}.

Combining (
27
), (
28
), and (
29
), we obtain

(30)

W_{1}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{m}}\bigr)\;\geq\;C\bigl(Q_{m}^{-1}+\delta_{m}\bigr),

where the right hand side tends to zero as
m\rightarrow+\infty
.

Because each
\omega_{k}
has Diophantine exponent
1
, we can choose the approximants so that
\delta_{m}\sim Q_{m}^{-2}
. Consequently,

W_{1}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{0},\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\delta_{m}}\bigr)\geq C\delta_{m}^{1/2}.

This gives a Hölder exponent of
1/2
under these specific arithmetic conditions, which is larger than the exponents obtained in the two-dimensional case. This suggests that the regularity of Mather measures may degrade as the dimension increases, even under favorable arithmetic conditions.

4.
Statistical regularity of Mather measures for Tonelli Hamiltonians under perturbations

Motivated by the results obtained in the previous section, we now turn to a more general class of Tonelli Hamiltonian systems. We consider the parametrized Hamilton–Jacobi equation

(31)

H_{\varepsilon}(x,du_{\varepsilon}(x))=\alpha(\varepsilon),\qquad x\in\mathbb{T}^{n},

where
\alpha(\varepsilon)
denotes the ergodic constant associated with
H_{\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
(
m\in\mathbb{N}
).

We add the following assumptions:

(A4)

H_{\varepsilon}
remains Tonelli for
\varepsilon
with sufficiently small norm. Moreover,
H_{\varepsilon}\to H_{0}
in the
C^{2}
topology as
|\varepsilon|\to 0
.

(A5)

Equation (
31
) associated with
\varepsilon=0
admits a classical solution
u_{0}\in C^{1,1}
, such that

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\omega}:=\{(x,du_{0}(x)):x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\}

is a KAM torus with frequency
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,\tau}
.

Definition 4.1

(KAM torus)
.

A torus
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\omega}
is called a
KAM torus
for
H_{0}
with frequency
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if it is invariant under the Hamiltonian flow
\varphi_{H_{0}}^{t}
, and there exists a diffeomorphism
\psi:\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}^{n}
such that for any
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
,

\psi\!\big(\pi\circ\varphi_{H_{0}}^{t}(x,du_{0}(x))\big)=\psi(x)+\omega t\pmod{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}.

Equivalently, the following diagram commutes:

(32)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\omega}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\omega}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{T}^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{T}^{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{H_{0}}^{t}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi\circ\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi\circ\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{\omega}^{t}}

We recall several standard results from weak KAM theory that will be used repeatedly. We refer to
[
15
,
17
,
3
,
12
,
13
]
for the proofs.

Lemma 4.2
.

For the Hamilton–Jacobi equation (
3
) with a Tonelli Hamiltonian
H
, we have the following results.

(1)

Any critical subsolution is differentiable on
\mathcal{A}
, and it is in fact a solution of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation (
3
) there.

(2)

Any two critical subsolutions differ by an additive constant on each connected component of the Aubry set; the constant may differ between different components.

(3)

There exists a
C^{1,1}
critical subsolution of (
3
) which is strict outside the Aubry set
\mathcal{A}
.

(4)

The map
\pi^{-1}:\mathcal{A}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
is Lipschitz.

Among the various perturbations of Tonelli Hamiltonians, two types are of most interest for our analysis (as already mentioned in the Introduction):

(I)

Cohomological parametrization

H_{c}(x,p)=H(x,p+c),\qquad c\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})

introduced by Mather in 1991
[
28
]
, where
|c|\ll 1
;

(II)

Mañé’s potential perturbation

H_{\varepsilon}(x,p)=H(x,p)+\varepsilon f(x),\qquad 0\leq\varepsilon\ll 1,

proposed by Mañé in 1992
[
26
]
with
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
.

We will respectively analyze these two cases and investigate the stability of the Mather measures. Throughout this section, we denote by
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)
,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon)
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(c)
the sets of Mather measures associated with
H
,
H_{\varepsilon}
and
H_{c}
, respectively.
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)
is a singleton due to assumption (
A5
).

4.1.
Case I: Cohomological perturbations

In this subsection, we study (
31
) in the case where it takes the following form:

(33)

H\big(x,du_{0}(x)\big)=\alpha(0),

together with its perturbation

(34)

H\big(x,c+du_{c}(x)\big)=\alpha(c),

where
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|c|\ll 1
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that Assumptions (
A4
) and (
A5
) hold.
For the Hamilton–Jacobi equation of type (I), we have

(35)

W_{1}\big(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(c),\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\big)\leq C\,|c|^{\,\ell},

where
\ell=\frac{1}{\,n+\tau+1\,}
and

C=C(n,\omega,H_{0})>0
is a constant.

Proof.

By the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality formula (see Remark
2.9
), we have

W_{1}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(c),\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\right)=\sup_{\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(T\mathbb{T}^{n})}\left\{\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(c)-\int_{T\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\right\}.

Let
u_{c}
denote the viscosity solution of (
34
), and let

\mathcal{A}(c)
be the associated projected Aubry set. By
Lemma
4.2
, the Mather set is contained in the Aubry set,
and both

du_{c}
and
du_{0}
are well defined on
\mathcal{A}(c)
and

\mathcal{A}(0)
.
Then by the graph property of Mather measures, the above formula reduces to

\displaystyle W_{1}\!\big(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(c),\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\Bigg\{\int_{\mathcal{A}(c)}\phi\!\big(x,\partial_{p}H\big(x,du_{c}(x)+c\big)\big)\,d\mathfrak{M}(c)

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathcal{A}(0)}\phi\!\big(x,\partial_{p}H\big(x,du_{0}(x)\big)\big)\,d\mathfrak{M}(0)\Bigg\},

where
\mathfrak{M}(c)
and
\mathfrak{M}(0)
denote the projected Mather measures associated with

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(c)
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)
, respectively.
For notational convenience, we write

V_{c}(x):=\partial_{p}H\!\big(x,du_{c}(x)+c\big),\quad x\in\mathcal{A}(c),

and

V_{0}(x):=\partial_{p}H\!\big(x,du_{0}(x)\big),\quad x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}.

By Lemma
4.2
, it suffices to consider these vector fields on the Aubry set:

\displaystyle W_{1}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(c),\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\left\{\bigg[\int_{\mathcal{A}(c)}\phi\big(x,V_{c}(x)\big)\,d\mathfrak{M}(c)-\int_{\mathcal{A}(c)}\phi\big(x,V_{0}(x)\big)\,d\mathfrak{M}(c)\bigg]\right.

(36)

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt\left.+\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi\big(x,V_{0}(x)\big)\,d\mathfrak{M}(c)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi\big(x,V_{0}(x)\big)\,d\mathfrak{M}(0)\bigg]\right\}.

We estimate the two bracketed terms separately. For the first term in inequality (
4.1
), since

\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
and
H
is smooth, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}(c)}\big|\phi(x,V_{c}(x))-\phi(x,V_{0}(x))\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}(c)}\big|V_{c}(x)-V_{0}(x)\big|

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}(c)}\big|du_{c}(x)+c-du_{0}(x)\big|.

Here and in what follows,
C
denotes a positive constant that may change from line to line, but it is independent of
c
.

By Theorem 2.1 of
[
25
]
, for any sufficiently small
\epsilon_{0}>0
,
the viscosity solution
u_{c}
of (
34
) satisfies the following estimate
at all points where it is differentiable:

\big|c+du_{c}-du_{0}\big|\leq C\,\|c\|^{1-\epsilon_{0}}.

We immediately obtain

(37)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}(c)}\|V_{c}(x)-V_{0}(x)\|\leq C\,|c|^{1-\epsilon_{0}}.

Hence, we have

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi(x,V_{c}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}(c)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi(x,V_{0}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}(c)\leq\mathcal{O}(|c|^{1-\epsilon_{0}}).

For the second term in inequality (
4.1
), note that it can be written as

\sup_{\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi(x,V_{0}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}(c)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi(x,V_{0}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}(0)\;=\;W_{1}\!\left(\mathfrak{M}(c),\,\mathfrak{M}(0)\right).

By (3) of Lemma
4.2
,
there exists a
C^{1,1}
critical subsolution
\omega_{c}
of (
31
) which is strict outside the projected Aubry set. Although such a subsolution could be chosen rather random, we can impose
\omega_{c}(x_{0})=0
at a fixed point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
and due to the superlinearity of
H(x,p)
, all possible
\omega_{c}
are uniformly Lipschitz and then uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Moreover, due to (1) of Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
2.7
, the multiplicity of
\omega_{c}
makes no ambiguity on
\mathcal{M}
. This allows us to define the Lipschitz vector field

(38)

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{c}:\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\widehat{V}_{c}(x)=\partial_{p}H_{c}\!\big(x,d\omega_{c}(x)\big),

which yields a Mañé-type modified Lagrangian

(39)

L_{\mathrm{sub}}^{c}(x,v):=\frac{1}{2}\,\big|v-\widehat{V}_{c}(x)\big|^{2}.

We denote by
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathrm{sub}}(c)
the set of Mather measures associated with
L_{\mathrm{sub}}^{c}
; then by part (1) of Lemma
4.2
, we actually have
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(c)\subset\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\rm sub}(c)
. By the triangle inequality for
W_{1}
, inserting the projected Mather measure

\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{sub}}(c)
associated with the Mañé-type Lagrangian
L_{\mathrm{sub}}
, we have

W_{1}\!\left(\mathfrak{M}(c),\mathfrak{M}(0)\right)\leq W_{1}\!\left(\mathfrak{M}(c),\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{sub}}(c)\right)+W_{1}\!\left(\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{sub}}(c),\mathfrak{M}(0)\right).

By Lemma
4.2
, we have
V(c)=W(c)
on
\mathcal{A}(c)
. Hence the first term on the right-hand side vanishes:

W_{1}\!\left(\mathfrak{M}(c),\,\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{sub}}(c)\right)=0.

For the second term, using (
37
) and Theorem
3.3
, we have

W_{1}\!\left(\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{sub}}(c),\mathfrak{M}(0)\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\!\left(|c|^{\ell_{2}}\right)

for some
\ell_{2}\in(0,1)
. This proves the desired bound for the second term in inequality (
4.1
). By choosing
\epsilon_{0}
sufficiently small and combining estimate (
37
) with the bound obtained in Remark
3.4
, we deduce that (
35
) holds with

\ell=\frac{1}{\,n+\tau+1\,}.
This completes the proof.∎

Remark 4.4
.

In
[
5
]
, Walter Craig proposed the following open problem:

For a Tonelli Lagrangian
L(x,v)
and the associated
\alpha
-function
\alpha:c\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
, how can we relate the regularity of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(c)
(with respect to

As we can see, Theorem
4.3
partially answers this problem by establishing the Hölder continuity of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(c)
with respect to

when

.

4.2.
Case II: Mañé’s perturbations

In this subsection,
we consider perturbed Hamiltonians given by

H_{\varepsilon}(x,p)=H(p)+\varepsilon f(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{T}^{n},

with
0\leq\varepsilon\ll 1
, where
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Tonelli Hamiltonian that depends only on the momentum variable
p
, and
f:\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{2}
-smooth perturbation.

Theorem 4.5
.

Suppose that Assumptions (
A4
) and (
A5
) hold, and let
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
.
Then there exists a modulus of continuity
\Theta:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
with
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\Theta(r)=0
such that

W_{1}\!\bigl(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon),\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\bigr)\;\leq\;\Theta(\varepsilon).

Proof.

According to the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality formula (see Remark
2.9
), we have

W_{1}\!\bigl(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon),\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\bigr)=\sup_{\Phi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(T\mathbb{T}^{n})}\left\{\int\Phi\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon)-\int\Phi\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\right\}.

By the graph property of Mather measures (Lemma
4.2
), each Mather measure is supported on a Lipschitz graph. Hence there exist vector fields

V_{\varepsilon}(x):=\partial_{p}H_{\varepsilon}\big(x,du_{\varepsilon}(x)\big),\qquad V_{0}(x):=\partial_{p}H_{0}\big(x,du_{0}(x)\big),

defined on the respective projected Aubry sets. Therefore the dual formula reduces to

W_{1}\!\bigl(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon),\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\bigr)=\sup_{\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\Bigg\{\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}\phi(x,V_{\varepsilon}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}(\varepsilon)-\int_{\mathcal{A}_{0}}\phi(x,V_{0}(x))\,d\mathfrak{M}(0)\Bigg\}

and then, adding and subtracting an intermediate term,

\displaystyle W_{1}\!\bigl(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon),\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\bigr)\leq{}

\displaystyle\sup_{\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\Bigg\{\Big[\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}\big(\phi(x,V_{\varepsilon})-\phi(x,V_{0})\big)\,d\mathfrak{M}(\varepsilon)\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi(x,V_{0})\,d\mathfrak{M}(\varepsilon)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi(x,V_{0})\,d\mathfrak{M}(0)\Big]\Bigg\}.

The first bracket is clearly bounded by

\Big|\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}\big(\phi(x,V_{\varepsilon})-\phi(x,V_{0})\big)\,d\mathfrak{M}(\varepsilon)\Big|\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}\big|V_{\varepsilon}(x)-V_{0}(x)\big|.

The second bracket equals the
W_{1}
-distance between the projected Mather measures
\mathfrak{M}(\varepsilon)
and
\mathfrak{M}(0)
; hence

\sup_{\phi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi(x,V_{0})\,d\mathfrak{M}(\varepsilon)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\phi(x,V_{0})\,d\mathfrak{M}(0)\Big\}=W_{1}\!\bigl(\mathfrak{M}(\varepsilon),\mathfrak{M}(0)\bigr).

By Lemma
4.2
,
there exists a
C^{1,1}
critical subsolution
\omega_{\varepsilon}
of (
31
) which is strict outside the projected Aubry set.
This allows us to define the Lipschitz vector field

(40)

\displaystyle W_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad W_{\varepsilon}(x)=\partial_{p}H_{\varepsilon}\!\big(x,d\omega_{\varepsilon}(x)\big),

which yields a Mañé-type modified Lagrangian

(41)

L_{\mathrm{sub}}^{\varepsilon}(x,v):=\frac{1}{2}\,\big|v-W_{\varepsilon}(x)\big|^{2}.

We denote by
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\varepsilon)
the set of Mather measures associated with
L_{\mathrm{sub}}^{\varepsilon}
; then by part (1) of Lemma
4.2
, we actually have
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon)\subset\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{\rm sub}(\varepsilon)
.
By introducing the Mañé-type Lagrangian
L_{\mathrm{sub}}
in (
41
) and the associated projected Mather measure
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{sub}}(\varepsilon)
, and combining Lemma
4.2
and Theorem
3.3
,
there exist a constant
C>0
independent of
\varepsilon
and a constant
\ell\in(0,1)
such that

W_{1}\bigl(\mathfrak{M}(\varepsilon),\mathfrak{M}(0)\bigr)\leq C\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}\big|V_{\varepsilon}(x)-V_{0}(x)\big|^{\ell}.

Combining the two estimates, we have

W_{1}\!\bigl(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon),\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)\bigr)\leq C\,\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}\bigl|\partial_{p}H_{\varepsilon}(x,du_{\varepsilon}(x))-\partial_{p}H_{0}(x,du_{0}(x))\bigr|^{\ell}.

In the following, we estimate

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}\bigl|\partial_{p}H_{\varepsilon}(x,du_{\varepsilon}(x))-\partial_{p}H_{0}(x,du_{0}(x))\bigr|.

Since the Hamiltonian
H_{0}
is smooth in the momentum variable, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
\varepsilon

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}\bigl|\partial_{p}H_{\varepsilon}(x,du_{\varepsilon}(x))-\partial_{p}H_{0}(x,du_{0}(x))\bigr|\leq C\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}|du_{\varepsilon}(x)-du_{0}(x)|.

Notice that for every
x
where
u_{\varepsilon}
is differentiable we have
du_{\varepsilon}(x)=D^{*}u_{\varepsilon}(x)
; hence

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}|du_{\varepsilon}(x)-du_{0}(x)|\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}d_{H}\bigl(D^{*}u_{\varepsilon}(x),du_{0}(x)\bigr),

where

A,B\subset T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{n}

in

.
Combining the above inequalities yields

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}}\bigl|\partial_{p}H_{\varepsilon}(x,du_{\varepsilon}(x))-\partial_{p}H_{0}(x,du_{0}(x))\bigr|\leq C\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}d_{H}\bigl(D^{*}u_{\varepsilon}(x),du_{0}(x)\bigr),

In the following, we claim that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}d_{H}(D^{*}u_{\epsilon}(x),du_{0}(x))=0.

To see this, for any
p\in D^{*}u_{\epsilon}(x)
, by weak KAM theory there exists a

-calibrated curve
\gamma_{\epsilon}:(-\infty,0]\to\mathbb{T}^{n}

\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}(x)-u_{\epsilon}(\gamma_{\epsilon}(-t))=\int_{-t}^{0}L_{\epsilon}(\gamma_{\epsilon}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{\epsilon}(s))\,ds\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

By Theorem 4.3.8 in

-calibrated curve satisfies

\dot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\partial_{p}H_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t),du_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t))\bigr),\qquad-\infty<t<0.

By the calibration identity (

) and the superlinearity of

, the family

is uniformly bounded and equi-Lipschitz. Hence, any limit curve of
\gamma_{\varepsilon}

and
u_{0}

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}d_{H}\bigl(D^{*}u_{\varepsilon}(x),\{du_{0}(x)\}\bigr)=0,

.

We define

\theta(\epsilon):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}d_{H}(D^{*}u_{\epsilon}(x),du_{0}(x)),\qquad\epsilon\geq 0,

and then set

\eta(r):=\sup_{0\leq s\leq r}\theta(s),\qquad r\geq 0.

By construction,
\eta
is nondecreasing and satisfies
\eta(r)\geq\theta(r)
for all
r\geq 0
.
Since
\eta:[0,\varepsilon_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is monotone increasing and bounded, we define

\Theta(r):=\int_{0}^{1}\eta\bigl(r(1+t)\bigr)\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad r\geq 0,

where
\varphi\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
is a nonnegative mollifier with
\int_{0}^{1}\varphi=1
. It is clear that
\Theta
dominates
\eta
. Indeed, since
r(1+t)\geq r
for all
t\in[0,1]
and
\eta
is monotone, we have
\eta(r(1+t))\geq\eta(r)
; hence

\Theta(r)\;=\;\int_{0}^{1}\eta\bigl(r(1+t)\bigr)\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\;\geq\;\int_{0}^{1}\eta(r)\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\;=\;\eta(r).

Monotonicity follows immediately. Whenever
0\leq r_{1}<r_{2}
, one has
r_{1}(1+t)<r_{2}(1+t)
for every

, and therefore
\eta(r_{1}(1+t))\leq\eta(r_{2}(1+t))
. Integrating against
\varphi
gives
\Theta(r_{1})\leq\Theta(r_{2})
.
To establish continuity, fix
r_{0}>0
. Since
\eta
has at most countably many discontinuities, the set

N=\{t\in[0,1]:\;r_{0}(1+t)\text{ is a discontinuity of }\eta\}

has Lebesgue measure zero. For
r_{n}\to r_{0}
, the functions
h_{n}(t):=\eta(r_{n}(1+t))
converge pointwise to
h(t):=\eta(r_{0}(1+t))
on
[0,1]\setminus N
. By Egorov’s theorem, for any
\delta_{1}>0
there exists a measurable set
E
with
m(E)<\delta_{1}
such that the convergence is uniform on
F:=[0,1]\setminus E
. On the other hand, since
\varphi\in L^{1}([0,1])
, the absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral ensures that there exists
\delta_{1}>0
such that if
m(A)<\delta_{1}
then

\int_{A}\varphi(t)\,dt<\frac{\varepsilon}{4M},\qquad M=\sup_{[0,\varepsilon_{0}]}\eta.

Therefore, for
|r-r_{0}|
sufficiently small, one obtains

|\Theta(r)-\Theta(r_{0})|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\eta(r(1+t))-\eta(r_{0}(1+t))\bigr)\varphi(t)\,dt\right|\leq I_{F}+I_{E},

where

I_{F}:=\int_{F}|\eta(r(1+t))-\eta(r_{0}(1+t))|\varphi(t)\,dt,\quad I_{E}:=\int_{E}|\eta(r(1+t))-\eta(r_{0}(1+t))|\varphi(t)\,dt.

On
F
, uniform convergence gives

I_{F}\leq\sup_{t\in F}|\eta(r(1+t))-\eta(r_{0}(1+t))|\cdot\int_{F}\varphi(t)\,dt\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot 1=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

On
E
, boundedness of
\eta
yields

I_{E}\leq 2M\int_{E}\varphi(t)\,dt\leq 2M\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{4M}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Hence
|\Theta(r)-\Theta(r_{0})|<\varepsilon
, proving continuity at
r_{0}>0
.

Next we show that
\Theta
is continuous at
0
. Note that for
0\leq r\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon_{0}}{2}
,

0\leq\Theta(r)\leq\eta(2r)\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\eta(2r).

Since
\eta(s)\to 0
as
s\to 0^{+}
, we conclude that
\Theta(r)\to 0
as
r\to 0^{+}
. Setting
\Theta(0)=0
ensures continuity at
0
. Consequently,
\Theta:[0,\varepsilon_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is continuous, nondecreasing, satisfies
\Theta(r)\geq\eta(r)
for all
r
, and
\Theta(r)\to 0
as
r\to 0^{+}
. Therefore, up to a multiplicative constant,
\Theta^{\ell}
can be taken as the required continuous modulus, which proves Theorem
4.5
.∎

5.
Lipschitz regularity and Linear response under the KAM theory

In this section, we start with a Tonelli Hamiltonian
H\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
of the form
H=H(p)
, satisfying
\nabla H(0)=\omega
, where
\omega
is a fixed Diophantine vector. Consequently,
u_{0}\equiv\text{const}
is a smooth solution of

H(du_{0})=\alpha(0)

and

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\omega}:=\{(x,0)\mid x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\}

is a KAM torus with frequency
\omega
. We then perturb
H_{0}
by

(43)

H_{\varepsilon}(x,p)=H(p)+\varepsilon f(x),\quad|\varepsilon|\ll 1

with
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and consider the corresponding Hamilton–Jacobi equation

(44)

H_{\varepsilon}\bigl(x,du_{\varepsilon}+c_{\varepsilon}\bigr)=\alpha(\varepsilon)

for suitable
c_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{R}
. Our goal, by applying classical KAM theory, is to select
c_{\varepsilon}
so that for each sufficiently small
|\varepsilon|\ll 1
, there exists a perturbed KAM torus

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\omega}^{\varepsilon}:=\{(x,\,c_{\varepsilon}+du_{\varepsilon}(x))\mid x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\}

which remains conjugate to the linear flow on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
with frequency
\omega
. For each
\varepsilon
, the set
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon)
is a singleton, and so is
\mathfrak{M}(\varepsilon)
. We are interested in the linear response of the Mather measure. More precisely, we say that
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon)
admits a linear response if the limit

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon)-\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)}{\varepsilon}

exists in the sense of weak convergence of measures. In this case, the family of measures expands as

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon)=\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)+\varepsilon\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}+o(\varepsilon).

Equivalently, for any test function
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(T\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, one has

(45)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\int g\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(\varepsilon)-\int g\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}(0)}{\varepsilon}=\int g\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}.

Lemma 5.1

(KAM theorem)
.

Suppose
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\sigma,\tau}
and
\|f\|_{C^{2\tau+3+\delta}(\mathbb{T}^{n},\mathbb{R})}\leq 1
for arbitrary
\delta>0
. There exists a positive constant
\varepsilon_{0}:=\varepsilon_{0}(\sigma,\tau,\|H\|_{C^{2}})<1
such that for any
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})

defined in (

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\omega}^{\varepsilon}=\{(x,p_{\varepsilon}(x))\mid x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\}.

Precisely, there exists a diffeomorphism

(46)

\psi_{\varepsilon}:=\operatorname{id}+\varepsilon\psi_{1}(\cdot,\varepsilon):\mathbb{T}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}^{n}

with

for some constant
\kappa_{1}:=\kappa_{1}(\varepsilon_{0})>0

such that the graph in (
32

for any

and

p_{\varepsilon}(x)=c_{\varepsilon}+du_{\varepsilon}(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}

Proposition 5.2

Suppose that assumptions (

Theorem 5.3

(Linear response)
.

Assume that (
A4
) and (

Suppose that

is the smooth potential associated with the perturbed Hamiltonian (

admits a linear response; i.e., for any

\displaystyle\int g\,d\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{1}g(x,\omega),\psi_{1}(x,\varepsilon)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{{\bf 0}\}}\frac{\langle\partial_{2}g(x,\omega),\partial_{pp}H(0)k\rangle}{\langle k,\omega\rangle}f_{k}\,e^{i\langle k,x\rangle}

\displaystyle\quad+\langle\partial_{2}g(x,\omega),\partial_{pp}H(0)c_{1}\rangle,

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}
,
\psi_{1}(x,\varepsilon)
,
c_{1}

46
), (

f_{k}

f
for

Proof.

By the KAM theorem (Lemma

), the Hamilton–Jacobi equation (
44
) admits a smooth solution
u_{\varepsilon}
for every
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Moreover, there exists a diffeomorphism
\psi_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{T}^{n}\to\mathbb{T}^{n}

\psi_{\varepsilon}

\mathbb{T}^{n}

\omega

Since the Mather measures are supported on Lipschitz graphs, we introduce the vector fields

and

V_{0}(x):=\partial_{p}H(0)=\omega,\qquad x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}.

If we expand

u_{\varepsilon}=u_{0}+\varepsilon u_{1}+o(\varepsilon),\qquad c_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon c_{1}+o(\varepsilon),

Writing the Fourier expansion
