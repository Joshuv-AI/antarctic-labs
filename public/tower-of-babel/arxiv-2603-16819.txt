Inadmissible representations of the tree automorphism group
Nicolas Monod

Abstract
. The automorphism group of a regular locally finite tree is shown to admit irreducible Banach representations that are not admissible. The dense subspace of smooth vectors contains no algebraically irreducible component.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Context

Classically, a linear representation of a group
G
on a Banach space
V
is called irreducible if there is no
G
-invariant closed proper subspace
{V_{0}\subseteq V}
; proper means
V_{0}\neq 0,V
.

In this note, we call this
topologically irreducible
to distinguish it from the following. The representation is
algebraically irreducible
if there is no
G
-invariant proper subspace
{V_{0}\subseteq V}
at all, with
V
viewed merely as a vector space.

A straightforward Baire argument shows that algebraic irreducibility can never happen when
V
is an infinite-dimensional Banach space and
G
is countable, or more generally totally disconnected locally compact second countable and the representation is continuous.

That setting includes notably
p
-adic algebraic groups.

Nevertheless, algebraic irreducibility plays a crucial role in the very rich representation theory of such groups because one can replace the Banach space
V
with the subspace of
smooth
vectors
{V}_{\mathrm{sm}}\subseteq V
, namely those with an open stabilizer (which in this setting coincides with the “
K
-finite” vectors). Whenever algebraically irreducible smooth representations can be classified, this allows
some
classification results, most notably when
V
is a Hilbert space and the representation is unitary, see e.g.
[
BZ76
, §4.21]
and
[
JL70
, p. 30]
.

In this context, the key concept is
admissibility
, namely the requirement that open subgroups
U<G
have only a finite-dimensional space
V^{U}
of fixed vectors. Admissibility is the gateway to classification, when classification is possible. A cornerstone of the study of representations of reductive
p
-adic groups is to prove admissibility of irreducible representations, see e.g.
[
Ren10
, VI.2.2]
.

1.2.
Tree groups

Early on, it was recognized that the automorphism group
G={\rm Aut}(T)
of a regular tree
T
of finite valency
\geq 3
offers a very interesting non-linear analogue to
p
-adic groups. One of the reasons is that
T
occurs as the Bruhat–Tits building
[
BT67
,
BT72
]
of rank one
p
-adic groups when the valency is
p+1
; and yet
{\rm Aut}(T)
is much larger than an algebraic group. The representation theory of
{\rm Aut}(T)
was initiated by Cartier
[
Car73a
,
Car73b
]
and the unitary case studied in great detail in
[
FTN91
]
. Indeed the unitary dual is completely known; this follows from a complete description by Olshanskii of all algebraically irreducible smooth representations
[
Ol′77
]
. Again, this depends crucially on the fact that they are all admissible, which was proved earlier by Olshanskii
[
Ol′75
]
.

The situation is very different for topological irreducibility:

Theorem A
.

Let
G
be the automorphism group of a regular tree of finite valency
{\geq 3}
.

There exists topologically irreducible continuous
G
-representations on a Banach space
V
that are not admissible.

Moreover, the subspace
{V}_{\mathrm{sm}}
of smooth vector does not contain any non-zero algebraically irreducible subrepresentation.

In particular, the classification of algebraically irreducible smooth representations gives no information at all about
V
. By contrast, all topologically irreducible unitary
G
-representations are admissible, see e.g.
[
FTN91
]
. Very rencently, the corresponding statement has been established by Viola
[
Vio26
]
in the more general setting of Hilbert space representations preserving a sesquilinear form of any finite index.

Two remarks are in order.

The first is that
A
is a non-Archimedean analogue of Soergel’s result
[
Soe88
]
that
\mathbf{SL}(2,\mathbf{R})
admits inadmissible topologically irreducible continuous representations. Although the techniques are different because
G={\rm Aut}(T)
does not give rise to Harish-Chandra modules, the strategy is parallel in that we “induce” from counter-examples to the invariant subspace problem.

The second remark is, therefore, that Soergel’s own two remarks apply: the hard work is done by Enflo and Read to provide these counter-examples, and the idea to induce them is attributed to Vogan.

Given the connection to the invariant subspace problem, it is of keen interest to investigate which Banach spaces
V
can occur in
A
. Leveraging the work of Read, we obtain:

Theorem B
.

In
A
, one can arrange that
V
is isometrically isomorphic to its own bidual. Alternatively, one can choose
V=L^{1}
.

The usual convention is that
L^{1}
denotes a classical Lebesgue space, for instance
L^{1}([0,1])
or
L^{1}(\mathbf{R})
, which are isomorphic as Banach spaces.

2.2.
The representations

Given a Banach space
W
, we make two choices in order to define the Banach space
V=L^{p}(\partial T,W)
. First, we choose a basepoint
x_{0}
and consider the corresponding measure
\mu=\mu_{x_{0}}
as above. Secondly, we choose the exponent
1\leq p<\infty
.

With these choices, the space
V=L^{p}(\partial T,W)
of Bochner
p
-summable
W
-valued function classes on
\partial T
is a Banach space and is separable as soon as
W
is so. We refer to the monograph
[
DU77
]
for very thorough background information on these Bochner spaces. A basic fact is that the step functions, namely measurable function (classes) taking only finitely many values, form a dense subspace of
V
, see e.g. §II.2 in
[
DU77
]
. The duality theory for
V
is more subtle and will be discussed in the proof of
B
.

Given an invertible continuous linear operator
\tau\colon W\to W
of the Banach space
W
, we define a
G
-representation
\pi
on
V
by the “induction” formula

(ii)

\big(\pi(g)v\big)(\xi)=\tau^{B_{\xi}(x_{0},gx_{0})}\,v(g^{-1}\xi)\kern 8.53581pt\text{for}\kern 8.53581ptg\in G,v\in V.

For any given
g
, the kernel
B_{\xi}(x_{0},gx_{0})
takes only finitely many values because the triangle inequality bounds its absolute value by
d(x_{0},gx_{0})
. Therefore, there is a bound on the norm of the operators
\tau^{B_{\xi}(x_{0},gx_{0})}
occurring for the given
g
. It follows that
\pi(g)v
remains in
L^{p}(\partial T,W)
and that
\pi(g)
defines a continuous linear operator on
V
. A similar argument shows that the representation
\pi
is continuous in the sense that for every
v\in V
, the orbital map
g\mapsto\pi(g)v
is norm-continuous as a map
G\to V
.

By construction, the
K
-representation on
V
for
K=\Fix_{G}(x_{0})
is simply a translation representation, so that the
K
-fixed vectors
V^{K}
are isometrically identified with
W
as constant maps.

2.3.
Fixed vectors

So far, we made no assumptions at all on the Banach space
W
nor on the operator
\tau\colon W\to W
, except that
\tau
is supposed invertible so that
\tau^{B_{\xi}(x_{0},gx_{0})}
is defined. In this generality, we can establish the following, where as always
q+1\geq 3
is the degree of the tree.

Proposition 1
.

Let
V_{0}\subseteq V
be any non-zero closed
G
-invariant subspace and let
x_{0},x_{1}
be two adjacent vertices of
T
.

Then
V_{0}
admits a non-zero vector fixed by
\Fix_{G}(\{x_{0},x_{1}\})
.

Furthermore,
V_{0}
admits a non-zero vector fixed by
\Fix_{G}(x_{0})
unless
q
or
-q
is an eigenvalue of
\tau
.

We clarify that
\Fix_{G}(S)
denotes the

S\subseteq T

In the above statement, the fact that
x_{0}
is the vertex that we chose for the measure
\mu=\mu_{x_{0}}
is convenient, but not essential. Indeed, any automorphism sending
x_{0}
to any other vertex would intertwine the entire picture.

We shall repeatedly use the following fact: given

and a compact subgroup
U<G
, the integral

\int_{U}\pi(k)v\,\mathrm{d}k

with respect to the normalized Haar measure on
U

U
-fixed vector of
V
. The point is that this is a well-defined Bochner integral since the representation is orbitally continuous.

Proof of
1
.

Recall that a subtree
S
of

is either a boundary vertex (i.e. adjacent to a vertex not in

Examples of finite complete subtrees include the trees reduced to one vertex, or to two adjacent vertices, or the regular closed

Since the restriction of
\pi

We can assume

Among all finite complete subtrees, we choose

Remark 2
.

There is only one point in the above proof where we used that
V_{0}
is closed in a Banach space, namely to argue by integration that
V_{0}
contains a non-zero vector fixed by some compact open subgroup
U<G
. All other integrals in the proof where of the following form: given two compact open subgroups

, we integrate

In conclusion, the entire statement of

is a
G
-invariant vector subspace containing a non-zero smooth vector.

We have now collected enough properties of

Theorem 4
.

Let
W
be a Banach space admitting a linear operator
\alpha
without invariant closed proper subspace and of norm
\|\alpha\|<2q^{1/2}
.

Then the
G
-representation on

.

Proof of
4
.

Let
V_{0}\subseteq V
be a non-zero closed
G
-invariant subspace. By density of the step functions, it suffices to prove that
V_{0}
contains all vectors of the form
w\boldsymbol{1}_{C}
where
w\in W
and
C\subseteq\partial T
ranges over a basis of the topology of
\partial T
. Such a basis is provided by the collection of all “half-trees”

C=\Big\{\xi\in\partial T:B_{\xi}(x,y)=1\Big\}\kern 8.53581pt\text{where}\kern 8.53581ptx,y\in T\text{ with }d(x,y)=1.

Note that the other “half”
C^{\prime}=\partial T\smallsetminus C
corresponds to the only other value
B_{\xi}(x,y)=-1
; see
Figure

1
. By
G
-invariance of
V_{0}
, it suffices to establish this in the special case where
x=x_{0}
.

Choose
g\in G
such that
gx_{0}=y
. Given
w^{\prime}\in W
, the definition (
ii
) gives

\pi(g)\left(w^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\partial T}\right)=\tau(w^{\prime})\,\boldsymbol{1}_{C}+\tau^{-1}(w^{\prime})\,\boldsymbol{1}_{C^{\prime}}

and therefore

(viii)

\pi(g)\left(w^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\partial T}\right)-\tau^{-1}(w^{\prime})\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\partial T}=\big(\tau(w^{\prime})-\tau^{-1}(w^{\prime})\big)\,\boldsymbol{1}_{C}.

3
implies that
V_{0}
contains all elements of the form
w^{\prime\prime}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\partial T}
with
w^{\prime\prime}\in W
. In particular,
V_{0}
contains both
w^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\partial T}
and
\tau^{-1}(w^{\prime})\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\partial T}
. By
G
-invariance it also contains
\pi(g)\left(w^{\prime}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\partial T}\right)
and thus we conclude from (
viii
) that
V_{0}
contains, for any
w^{\prime}\in W
, the element
\big(\tau(w^{\prime})-\tau^{-1}(w^{\prime})\big)\,\boldsymbol{1}_{C}
.

Therefore, it only remains to show that any
w\in W
is of the form
\tau(w^{\prime})-\tau^{-1}(w^{\prime})
; that is, to show that
\tau-\tau^{-1}
is onto. Actually that map is even invertible: this follows from the spectral mapping theorem because the function
\varphi-\varphi^{-1}
does not vanish on
\mathbf{D}_{2q^{1/2}}
. In fact it never vanishes because its domain does not contain
\pm(q+1)
.
∎

At this point,
A
follows from the negative solution to the invariant subspace problem.

Proof of
A
.

Enflo
[
Enf76
,
Enf87
]
and Read
[
Rea84
]
have proved that there exists an infinite-dimensional Banach space
W
and a continuous linear operator
\alpha\colon W\to W
without any invariant closed proper subspace. If needed, we rescale
\alpha
to ensure
\|\alpha\|<2q^{1/2}
.

Then the
G
-representation
\pi
on
V=L^{p}(\partial T,W)
is topologically irreducible by
4
and inadmissible by
3
.

For the additional statement, consider the subspace
{V}_{\mathrm{sm}}
of smooth vectors and suppose that it contains a non-zero algebraically irreducible subrepresentation
V_{1}\subseteq{V}_{\mathrm{sm}}
. In view of
2
, we can still apply
1
and deduce
V_{1}^{K}\neq 0
. On the other hand, Olshanskii proved (Theorem 1 in
[
Ol′75
]
) that algebraically irreducible
G
-representations are admissible, so that
V_{1}^{K}
is finite-dimensional. (In the present case, this can also be deduced from the fact that
(G,K)
is a Gelfand pair, using that
V_{1}
must have countable algebraic dimension. In fact this argument amounts to a special case of Olshanskii’s Lemma 3 in
[
Ol′75
]
.) Now the argument of
3
shows that
\alpha
preserves a finite-dimensional subspace of
W
, which is absurd.
∎

We can obtain more Banach-theoretic information on
V
from information on
W
provided by the extensive work of Read.

Proof of
B
.

The quasi-reflexive Banach space
W
discovered by James
[
Jam51
]
is isometrically isomorphic to its bidual
W^{**}
. Read proves in
[
Rea89
]
that this space admits continuous linear operator without invariant closed proper subspace. We apply
A
to this situation, with the additional restriction
p>1
. We only need to justify that
V=L^{p}(\partial T,W)
is also isometrically isomorphic to its own bidual (but we point out that it is not quasi-reflexive).

Write
p^{\prime}
for the Hölder conjugate
p^{\prime}=p/(p-1)
. Integration over
\partial T
yields a canonical isometric embedding of
L^{p^{\prime}}(\partial T,W^{*})
into
V^{*}
, where
∗
denotes the Banach dual. This embedding is an isometric isomorphism if and only if
W^{*}
has the Radon–Nikodým property, see Theorem 1 of §IV.1 in
[
DU77
]
. On the other hand, every separable dual enjoys the Radon–Nikodým property, see Theorem 1 of §III.3 in
[
DU77
]
. The dual
W^{*}
is separable; this holds for any space whose dual is separable: see Banach, Théorème 12 of §XI.9 in
[
Ban32
]
. Here,
W^{**}
is separable because
W
is separable from its very definition.

In summary,
V^{*}
can be identified isometrically with
L^{p^{\prime}}(\partial T,W^{*})
. Since
p^{\prime}<\infty
and
p^{\prime\prime}=p
, we can repeat exactly the same Radon–Nikodým arguments to identify
V^{**}
with
L^{p}(\partial T,W^{**})
. By James’s theorem, this implies that
V^{**}
is isometrically isomorphic to
V
.

Concerning
V=L^{1}
, we obtain it from another of Read’s results. He proved in
[
Rea85
]
that
W=\ell^{1}(\mathbf{N})
also admits a continuous linear operator without invariant closed proper subspace. This time, we apply
A
with
p=1
; Fubini’s theorem implies that
V=L^{1}(\partial T,\ell^{1}(\mathbf{N}))
can be canonically identified with
L^{1}(\partial T\times\mathbf{N})
. Since
\partial T\times\mathbf{N}
is a countably generated non-atomic measure space, we obtain indeed the standard Lebesgue space
L^{1}
.
∎

Of course with
p=2
we obtain a Hilbert space
V=L^{2}(\partial T,W)
if
W
itself is a Hilbert space; the original invariant subspace problem concerns that case, but remains notoriously unsolved.
