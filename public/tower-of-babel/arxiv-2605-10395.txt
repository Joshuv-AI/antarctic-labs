Sharp feature-learning transitions and Bayes-optimal neural scaling laws in extensive-width networks
Minh-Toan Nguyen, Jean Barbier

Abstract
We study the information-theoretic limits of learning a one-hidden-layer teacher network with hierarchical features from noisy queries, in the context of knowledge transfer to a smaller student model. We work in the high-dimensional regime where the teacher width k k scales linearly with the input dimension d d – a setting that captures large-but-finite-width networks and has only recently become analytically tractable. Using a heuristic leave-one-out decoupling argument, validated numerically throughout, we derive asymptotically sharp characterizations of the Bayes-optimal generalization error and individual feature overlaps via a system of closed fixed-point equations. These equations reveal that feature learnability is governed by a sequence of sharp phase transitions: as data grows, teacher features become recoverable sequentially, each through a discontinuous jump in overlap. This sequential acquisition underlies a precise notion of effective width k c k_{c} – the number of learnable features at a given data budget n n – which unifies two distinct scaling regimes: a feature-learning regime in which the Bayes-optimal generalization error ε BO \varepsilon^{\rm BO} scales as n 1 / ( 2 ​ β ) − 1 n^{1/(2\beta)-1} , and a refinement regime in which it scales as n − 1 n^{-1} , where β > 1 / 2 \beta>1/2 is the exponent of the power-law feature hierarchy. Both laws collapse to the single relation ε BO = Θ ⁡ ( k c ​ d / n ) \varepsilon^{\rm BO}=\Theta(k_{c}d/n) . We further show empirically that a student trained with Adam near the effective width k c k_{c} achieves these optimal scaling laws (up to a small algorithmic gap), and provide an information-theoretic account of the associated scaling in model size.

Neural scaling laws.

Neural scaling laws have been studied from two broad perspectives. A first line of work operates in the non-feature-learning regime – kernel methods, random feature models, or linear regression – and derives scaling laws from spectral assumptions on the data covariance. Several of these works obtain scaling laws in both data size and model size
5
;
10
;
32
, or characterize how the optimal model size scales with compute budget
35
. A second line of work introduces genuine feature learning, either through abstract task models
29
;
34
;
23
;
12
or through concrete neural network models in the multi-index or sublinear-width regime
2
;
36
;
22
;
20
. The quantization model of
34
is
phenomenologically close to our framework: it postulates that
networks learn discrete units of knowledge sequentially, in order of
their contribution to loss, and that model capacity caps the number of
learnable units. These properties instead emerge from first principles in
our model.

Our closest predecessor
20
derives data-size scaling laws in the multi-index regime
k=\Theta(1)
(numerically:
k=10
,
d=1000
, vs.
k=100
,
d=200
here), using a hand-crafted rather than standard generalization error, and restricted to activations with
\mu_{2}\neq 0
; ours are complementary, covering
\mu_{2}=0
and
hence all odd activations. On the algorithmic side, the optimal scaling laws in
20
are achieved by AMP, later extended in
19
to activations with
\mu_{2}\neq 0
and even to even functions with
\mu_{2}=0
: for AMP, the data-limited exponent acquires a multiplicative correction from a noise-sensitive exponent defined therein, while the information-theoretic exponents are
conjectured to be unchanged. Despite all these differences, these three works obtain the same BO data-size scaling exponent, suggesting universality across activation classes and width regimes.

Bayes-optimal learning of extensive-width networks.

In the linear data regime
n=\Theta(d)
, the BO problem reduces to linear

For quadratic activations at

A note on rigor.

The extensive-width regime
k=\Theta(d)
of Bayesian neural networks is particularly challenging to analyze, with only few rigorous results in simpler settings, see e.g.
13

hypothesized Gaussianity of so-called cavity fields – techniques standard in statistical physics that are proved rigorously in simpler related
models
37
;
7
;
9
and
conjectured to extend here.
They rely on concentration properties of the posterior; in particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\langle(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}_{j})^{2}\right\rangle=O(d^{-1})\ \text{ for }i\neq j,\qquad\mathbb{E}\left\langle(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}_{i}-q_{i})^{2}\right\rangle=O(d^{-1}),

(5)

for
\mathbf{W}^{1}\sim P(\,\cdot\mid\mathcal{D})

across all parameter regimes, with excellent agreement already at

Decomposition of the Bayes-optimal error.
Equation (

Bayes-optimal scaling law.
With power-law readouts with

For

Scaling laws for SGD-trained student.

4
Derivation of the main results

Our main results rely on a crucial statistical equivalence between the learning problem in the neural network model from the perspective of a single feature and a simpler “high-sample rate generalized linear model”; it is derived in Appendix
B
.

Result 3
.

Let
\sigma
be a non-constant, non-even, differentiable function such that
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
are square-integrable w.r.t. the standard Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}
(we do not need here to restrict to the case
\mu_{2}=0
). Let
n,d,\Delta
be in the regime
n\gg d\gg 1
, and define
\lambda:=\frac{n}{d\Delta}
. Consider the problem of inferring the signal
\mathbf{w}^{0}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
from the observations
\mathbf{Y}=(y_{\mu})_{\mu=1}^{n}
given by:

\displaystyle y_{\mu}=\sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\right)+\sqrt{\Delta}\,z_{\mu},\quad\mu\in[n],

(11)

with inputs
\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
and i.i.d. noise variables
z_{\mu}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Let
\mathbf{w}^{1}
be a sample from the posterior
P(\mathbf{w}\mid\mathbf{Y})
. Then, recalling definitions (
4
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\langle(\mathbf{w}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}-m_{\sigma}(\lambda))^{2}\right\rangle=o_{d}(1).

(12)

In particular, when the information exponent of
\sigma
is at least
3
(Appendix
A
), the model exhibits a sharp phase transition at
\lambda_{\sigma}
:
m_{\sigma}(\lambda)=0
if
\lambda<\lambda_{\sigma}
, whereas
m_{\sigma}(\lambda)>0
if
\lambda>\lambda_{\sigma}
(Appendix
D
).

We now provide the main steps of our heuristic derivation of Result
1
based on the leave-one-out argument and Gaussian assumptions.

Feature overlaps and Bayes-optimal error.

Recall that
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
denotes the posterior mean w.r.t.
P(\mathbf{W}\mid\mathcal{D})
, and
\mathbf{W}^{1}
is a posterior sample. Fix
i\in[k]
. The leave-one-out approach relies on isolating the contribution of feature
\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0}
in the model (
1
):

y_{\mu}^{\rm out}=v_{i}\,\sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\right)+R_{\mu i}+\sqrt{\Delta}\,z_{\mu},\qquad R_{\mu i}:=\sum_{j\neq i}v_{j}\,\sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\right).

(13)

Decompose
R_{\mu i}
into its posterior mean and a posterior fluctuation that acts as an effective noise:

R_{\mu i}=\langle R_{\mu i}\rangle+(R_{\mu i}-\langle R_{\mu i}\rangle)
. The posterior mean
\langle R_{\mu i}\rangle
is a deterministic function of the dataset
\mathcal{D}
that can be absorbed into the observation
y_{\mu}^{\rm out}\to y_{\mu}^{\rm out}-\langle R_{\mu i}\rangle
without changing the posterior. We thus focus on the effective noise, whose variance is

\langle(R_{\mu i}-\langle R_{\mu i}\rangle)^{2}\rangle\sim\sum_{j\neq i}v_{j}^{2}(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}(q_{j})):=\varepsilon_{-i}
. This together with the relation below,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm BO}\sim\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}^{2}(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}(q_{i})):=\varepsilon.

(14)

are derived for a special case – and assumed to hold in general – in Appendix
F
.

We make the additional assumption that, with fixed
i
while
\mu
varies, the effective noises behave like independent Gaussian variables
– this central limit theorem heuristic, standard in the cavity method
37
, was tested numerically and verified a-posteriori via the excellent match between our theory and experiments, as well as via the analytical match with the equations of
6
in the dense readout case. Combining the effective noise with the original label noise yields a single effective Gaussian noise of variance
\Delta+\varepsilon_{-i}
, so that (
13
) (after subtracting the deterministic
\langle R_{\mu i}\rangle
) takes the form

\text{observation}_{\mu}=v_{i}\sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\right)+\sqrt{\Delta+{\varepsilon_{-i}}}\,\text{noise}_{\mu}.

(15)

By Result
3
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}_{i}\simeq m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{nv_{i}^{2}/d}{\Delta+\varepsilon_{-i}}\Big)\simeq m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{nv_{i}^{2}/d}{\Delta+\varepsilon}\Big)

(16)

where in the last step we used
\Delta+\varepsilon_{-i}=\Delta+\varepsilon+O(1/k)
. Together with the equation for
\varepsilon
, this closes the system of fixed-point equations (
6
).

Asymptotic Bayes-optimal error.
From (
14
), and using
q_{i}=0
for
i>k_{c}
, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm BO}\sim\sum_{i\leq k_{c}}v_{i}^{2}(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}(q_{i}))+\sum_{k_{c}<i\leq k}v_{i}^{2}g_{\sigma}(1).

The second sum, which is the error due to non-learnable features, is
\Theta(\sum_{k_{c}<i\leq k}v_{i}^{2})
. On the other hand, from the fixed point equations (
6
), we have
q_{i}=m_{\sigma}({\rm snr}_{i})
, where recall that
{\rm snr}_{i}=(n/d)v_{i}^{2}/(\Delta+\varepsilon)
. For any
i\leq k_{c}
, feature
i
is learnable, so
{\rm snr}_{i}>\lambda_{\sigma}
. The first sum becomes

\displaystyle(\Delta+\varepsilon)\frac{d}{n}\sum_{i\leq k_{c}}{\rm snr}_{i}\,\big(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}({\rm snr}_{i})\big),

which is
\Theta(k_{c}d/n)
, since
\Delta+\varepsilon=\Theta(1)
and there exist absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
x(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}(x))\in[c_{1},c_{2}]
for all
x>\lambda_{\sigma}
(Appendix
D
).

From the derivation, each learnable feature contributes
\Theta(d/n)
to the BO error, while each non-learnable feature
i
contributes
\Theta(v_{i}^{2})
.

5
Conclusion and limitations

We studied the information-theoretic limits of learning a
one-hidden-layer teacher network in the extensive-width regime

k=\Theta(d)
, a setting that captures large-but-finite-width
networks and sits strictly between the multi-index and NTK/infinite-width extremes. We provided a heuristic leave-one-out argument yielding closed
fixed-point equations for the individual feature overlaps
(q_{i})

and the Bayes-optimal error
\varepsilon^{\rm BO}
. A single
activation-dependent SNR threshold
\lambda_{\sigma}
governs feature
learnability: as
n
grows, features cross it one by one, producing
a staircase of sharp phase transitions in the learning curve. For
a power-law hierarchy of features with exponent
\beta>1/2
, the resulting effective width

k_{c}\leq k
unifies the two empirically observed scaling regimes via

\varepsilon^{\rm BO}=\Theta(k_{c}d/n)
: a feature-learning regime in
which
k_{c}
grows with
n
, and a refinement regime in which
k_{c}=k

is saturated and the parametric rate
n^{-1}
is recovered. An

Adam
-trained student near
k_{c}
tracks these Bayes-optimal
exponents up to a small residual gap on the weakest features,
consistent with an algorithmic threshold

\lambda_{\sigma}^{\rm algo}>\lambda_{\sigma}
.

The main limitations of our work are that derivations rest on unproved cavity assumptions
– Gaussian cavity fields and overlap concentration – and the
restriction
\mu_{2}=0
. Tackling general activations in the extensive-width regime
k=\Theta(d)
is a technical challenge that has been considered only recently
6
. Closing the
\mu_{2}

gap
20
;
33
, proving the
leave-one-out reduction, extending to multi-layer
architectures
6
, and characterizing
compute-optimal scaling
28
are natural
next steps.

Hermite expansion and information exponent

The
\ell
-th Hermite polynomial is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{He}_{\ell}(x):=(-1)^{\ell}e^{x^{2}/2}\frac{d^{\ell}}{dx^{\ell}}e^{-x^{2}/2},\quad\ell\geq 0.

(17)

\mathrm{He}_{\ell}
is a polynomial of degree
\ell
with leading coefficient
1
. A first few examples are
\mathrm{He}_{0}(x)=1,\mathrm{He}_{1}(x)=x,\mathrm{He}_{2}(x)=x^{2}-1
,
\mathrm{He}_{3}(x)=x^{3}-3x
. Any
\sigma\in L^{2}(\mu)
(
\mu
is the standard Gaussian measure) admits the
Hermite expansion

\sigma(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{\ell}}{\ell!}\mathrm{He}_{\ell}(z),

(18)

in which the
\ell
-th
Hermite coefficient
of
\sigma
is given by
\mu_{\ell}=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\sigma(z)\mathrm{He}_{\ell}(z)
.

An important quantity is the
information exponent
of
\sigma

[
3
]
, defined as

k_{\star}(\sigma):=\min\{\ell\geq 1:\mu_{\ell}\neq 0\}.

(19)

Appendix B
Heuristic derivation of Result
3

We provide here a heuristic derivation of Result
3
. We start by deriving the fixed point equation for
m_{\sigma}(\lambda)
. Assuming
\mathbf{w}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}\simeq q
where
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}^{1}
is a posterior sample, we will show that
q/(1-q)=\lambda\,g^{\prime}_{\sigma}(q)
. Fix
i\in[d]
and focus on the estimation of
w^{0}_{i}

\displaystyle y_{\mu}=\sigma\left(w^{0}_{i}x_{\mu i}+A_{\mu i}\right)+\sqrt{\Delta}\,z_{\mu},\quad\mu\in[n],

where
A_{\mu i}=\sum_{j\neq i}w_{j}^{0}x_{\mu j}
. Since
w^{0}_{i}x_{\mu i}
is small compared to
A_{\mu i}
, we can expand the model around
A_{\mu i}
and obtain

\displaystyle y_{\mu}

\displaystyle\simeq\sigma(A_{\mu i})+\sigma^{\prime}(A_{\mu i})w^{0}_{i}x_{\mu i}+\sqrt{\Delta}z_{\mu},\quad\mu\in[n].

We claim that
\sigma(A_{\mu i})
and
\sigma^{\prime}(A_{\mu i})
can be replaced by their posterior averages
\langle\sigma(A_{\mu i})\rangle
and
\langle\sigma^{\prime}(A_{\mu i})\rangle
: the fluctuations around the average contribute additional noise, negligible against the channel noise with variance
\Delta
. This is because, if
\Delta=\Theta(1)
, then since
n\gg d
, the overlap for
\mathbf{w}^{0}
is almost
1
, making the fluctuations of
\sigma(A_{\mu i})
and
\sigma^{\prime}(A_{\mu i})
of order
o(1)
, and thus negligible compared to
\Delta
. On the other hand, if
\Delta\gg 1
, since the fluctuations of
\sigma(A_{\mu i})
and
\sigma^{\prime}(A_{\mu i})
are bounded, they remain negligible compared to
\Delta
.

Subtracting
\langle\sigma(A_{\mu i})\rangle
yields the effective scalar model

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}_{\mu}=\langle\sigma^{\prime}(A_{\mu i})\rangle\,w^{0}_{i}\,x_{\mu i}+\sqrt{\Delta}\,\widetilde{z}_{\mu},\quad\mu\in[n],

which is equivalent to a single Gaussian channel for
w^{0}_{i}
with effective signal-to-noise ratio

\displaystyle r_{i}:=\frac{1}{\Delta}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\langle\sigma^{\prime}(A_{\mu i})\rangle^{2}x_{\mu i}^{2}.

Suppose that
r_{i}
concentrates around its mean. Using
\mathbb{E}[x_{\mu i}^{2}]=1
together with the approximate independence between
x_{\mu i}^{2}
and
\langle\sigma^{\prime}(A_{\mu i})\rangle
, then replacing
A_{\mu i}
by
\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu}
inside the bracket (an
O(d^{-1/2})
correction), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[r_{i}]\sim\frac{n}{\Delta}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\langle\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu})\rangle^{2}\right]=\lambda d\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\langle\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu})\rangle^{2}\right].

Computing
\mathbb{E}[\langle\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu})\rangle^{2}]
.

By Lemma
4
below,

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\simeq}}\langle\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\rangle+\sqrt{1-q}\,\xi,\qquad\xi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Since
\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
, the average term is Gaussian with variance
\|\langle\mathbf{w}\rangle\|^{2}\simeq q
. We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\simeq}}\;\sqrt{q}\,\xi_{0}+\sqrt{1-q}\,\xi_{1},\qquad\xi_{0},\,\xi_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\xi_{0}
is measurable with respect to the data and
\xi_{1}
captures the posterior uncertainty. We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\langle\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu})\rangle^{2}]\simeq\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{0}}(\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{1}}\sigma^{\prime}(\sqrt{q}\xi_{0}+\sqrt{1-q}\xi_{1}))^{2}=g_{\sigma^{\prime}}(q)=g^{\prime}_{\sigma}(q)

where the second equality follows from Proposition
5
.

Summarizing the previous arguments, for each
i
, the posterior of
w_{i}
is asymptotically that of a Gaussian channel with signal
w_{i}^{0}
and SNR
\lambda dg^{\prime}_{\sigma}(q)
. Assuming independence across individual channels, the posterior of
\mathbf{w}
is asymptotically that of a Gaussian channel with signal
\mathbf{w}^{0}
and the same SNR. This channel admits a simple SNR-overlap relation,
\mathrm{snr}/d=q/(1-q)
, yielding the fixed point equation

\displaystyle q/(1-q)=\lambda g^{\prime}_{\sigma}(q).

This equation may have multiple solutions and thus does not uniquely determine
q
. A unique characterization of the overlap is instead achieved by maximizing a large deviation rate function
I(q)
, defined informally via

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\ln\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{w}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}\simeq q)\simeq I(q).

The fixed point equation is then simply
I^{\prime}(q)=0
. Integrating both sides of
q/(1-q)-\lambda g^{\prime}_{\sigma}(q)=0
suggests that, up to an affine transform, the rate function is

\displaystyle\lambda g_{\sigma}(q)+q+\ln(1-q),

(20)

which yields the unique characterization of the overlap given by
m_{\sigma}(\lambda)
via its argmax. Importantly, this integration argument relies on the specific form of the fixed point equation: the left-hand side
q/(1-q)
has a clear interpretation as the SNR of a Gaussian channel expressed in terms of the overlap, and it is this structure that makes this work. Note that integrating arbitrary equivalent form of the fixed point equation, such as
q=(1-q)\lambda g^{\prime}_{\sigma}(q)
, does not recover the correct rate function. The resulting function (
20
) is called a “replica symmetric potential” in physics parlance, and can also be guessed by taking the joint high-sample-rate, high-noise limit, i.e.
\alpha=n/d\to\infty
and
\Delta\to\infty
with
\alpha/\Delta\to\lambda
fixed, in the replica symmetric potential for the standard generalized linear model
[
7
]
.

Lemma 4
.

(informal result by Talagrand
[
37
]

Consider a probability measure
\mu=\mu_{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
the average with respect to

\displaystyle\langle(\mathbf{w}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{2}-q)^{2}\rangle=o_{d}(1)

(21)

\displaystyle\langle(\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2}-\rho)^{2}\rangle=o_{d}(1).

(22)

Let
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
independent of
\mu
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\simeq}}\langle\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}\rangle+\sqrt{1-q}\,\xi,\qquad\xi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

We applied this lemma when
\mu
is the posterior distribution in the current model. The conditions (
21

Appendix C
Estimating the linear part via linear regression

We provide here the justification that the activation
\sigma(x)
can be replaced by
\sigma(x)-\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}x
without changing the asymptotic behavior of the posterior.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\mu_{0}=0
, since this is a constant that can be subtracted from
y_{\mu}^{\rm out}
. We will show that
\mathbf{s}^{0}=\mathbf{W}^{0\top}\mathbf{v}
can be estimated with vanishing error with the following estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\mu_{1}}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{y}.

(23)

Writing
\mathbf{y}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mu_{1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{s}^{0}+\boldsymbol{\xi},\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{W}^{0}\mathbf{X})+\sqrt{\Delta}\mathbf{z},

we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{s}^{0}+\frac{1}{n\mu_{1}}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}.

Since
n\gg d
, we have
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}/n\simeq\mathbf{I}_{d}
which is a basic property of Wishart matrices. Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{s}^{0}\simeq\mathbf{s}^{0}.

(24)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\xi_{\mu}\mathbf{x}_{\mu}

\displaystyle\simeq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},z}[(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{W}^{0}\mathbf{x})+\sqrt{\Delta}z)\mathbf{x}],\quad\text{since }n\gg d

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{W}^{0}\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{x}],\quad\text{since }\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}z]=0

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}].

Recall the following basic property of Hermite polynomials, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{He}_{\ell}(z_{1})\mathrm{He}_{\ell^{\prime}}(z_{2})]=0

for all
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
and
(z_{1},z_{2})
jointly Gaussian, each with variance
1
. This implies
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x})x_{j}]=0
, or
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x})\mathbf{x}]=0
. Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}\simeq 0.

(25)

Combining (
24
) and (
25
),
\hat{\mathbf{s}}\simeq\mathbf{s}^{0}
, so the linear contribution can be perfectly estimated and removed; the residual posterior on
\mathbf{W}_{0}

then matches that of the model with
\tilde{\sigma}
in place of
\sigma
.

Appendix D
Properties of
g_{\sigma}
and
m_{\sigma}

Proposition 5
.

Let be a non-constant, differentiable function and assume
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mu)
. Then the functions
g_{\sigma}
and
m_{\sigma}
defined in (
3
), (
4
) satisfy the following properties:

1.

g_{\sigma}
has the expansion

\displaystyle g_{\sigma}(x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{\ell}^{2}}{\ell!}x^{\ell}

(26)

where
\mu_{\ell}=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\sigma(z)\mathrm{He}_{\ell}(z)]
is the
\ell
-th Hermite coefficient of
\sigma
. As a consequence,
g_{\sigma}
is strictly increasing in
[0,1]
.

2.

m_{\sigma}
is non-decreasing,
1-m_{\sigma}(x)\sim c/x
as
x\rightarrow\infty
for some constant
c>0
. Consequently, there exists
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
x(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}(x))\in[c_{1},c_{2}]
for all
x>\lambda_{\sigma}

3.

If
k_{\star}(\sigma)\geq 3
, there exists
\lambda_{\sigma}>0
,
q_{\sigma}>0
such that
m_{\sigma}(\lambda)=0
if
\lambda<\lambda_{\sigma}
and
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\lambda_{\sigma}^{+}}m_{\sigma}(\lambda)=q_{\sigma}
.

4.

g^{\prime}_{\sigma}=g_{\sigma^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

1. Recall
Mehler’s formula
: for
(z_{1},z_{2})
a centered Gaussian vector with unit variances and correlation
x\in[-1,1]
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{He}_{\ell}(z_{1})\mathrm{He}_{m}(z_{2})]=\delta_{\ell m}\,\ell!\,x^{\ell},\qquad\ell,m\geq 0.

(27)

Writing
\sigma=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{\mu_{\ell}}{\ell!}\mathrm{He}_{\ell}
in
L^{2}(\mu)
and using the orthogonality (
27
), we obtain, for every
x\in[-1,1]
,

\displaystyle g_{\sigma}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\sigma(z_{1})\sigma(z_{2})]=\sum_{\ell,m\geq 0}\frac{\mu_{\ell}\mu_{m}}{\ell!\,m!}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{He}_{\ell}(z_{1})\mathrm{He}_{m}(z_{2})]=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{\mu_{\ell}^{2}}{\ell!}\,x^{\ell}.

Justification of the interchange: we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell,m}\frac{|\mu_{\ell}\mu_{m}|}{\ell!\,m!}\,|\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{He}_{\ell}(z_{1})\mathrm{He}_{m}(z_{2})]|=\sum_{\ell}\frac{\mu_{\ell}^{2}}{\ell!}\,|x|^{\ell}\leq\sum_{\ell}\frac{\mu_{\ell}^{2}}{\ell!}=\|\sigma\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\infty

for
|x|\leq 1
, so Fubini applies. The series (
26
) therefore converges absolutely on
[-1,1]
, and in particular at
x=1
.

2. Recall that

\displaystyle m_{\sigma}(\lambda)=\arg\max_{q\in[0,1)}F(\lambda,q),\quad F(\lambda,q)=\lambda g_{\sigma}(q)+q+\ln(1-q).

Monotonicity.
Consider
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}\geq 0
. Let
q_{1}=m_{\sigma}(\lambda_{1})
,
q_{2}=m_{\sigma}(\lambda_{2})
. By optimality of
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
,

\displaystyle F(\lambda_{1},q_{1})\geq F(\lambda_{1},q_{2}),

\displaystyle F(\lambda_{2},q_{2})\geq F(\lambda_{2},q_{1}).

Adding the two inequalities and simplifying,

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\big(g_{\sigma}(q_{1})-g_{\sigma}(q_{2})\big)\geq 0.

Since
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
, this implies
g_{\sigma}(q_{1})\geq g_{\sigma}(q_{2})
. Since
g_{\sigma}
is strictly increasing (Part 1), we have
q_{1}\geq q_{2}
. We conclude that
m_{\sigma}
is non-decreasing in
[0,\infty)
.

Asymptotic behavior.
Let
q=m_{\sigma}(\lambda)
. Then
q
satisfies
q/(1-q)=\lambda g^{\prime}_{\sigma}(q)
. When
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, we have
q\rightarrow 1
. From
q=(1-q)\lambda g^{\prime}_{\sigma}(q)
, as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, we have
1\sim(1-q)\lambda g_{\sigma}^{\prime}(1)
, which gives
m_{\sigma}(\lambda)=q\sim 1-c/\lambda
, where
c=1/g^{\prime}_{\sigma}(1)
.

Boundedness of
\kappa(x):=x(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}(x))
on
(\lambda_{\sigma},\infty)
. From the asymptotic of
m_{\sigma}
, we have
\kappa(x)\rightarrow 1
as
x\rightarrow\infty
. Fix a
\epsilon>0
. There exists
M>0
such that
\kappa(x)\in[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]
for all
x\geq M
. For
x\in(\lambda_{\sigma},M]
,
\kappa(x)
is bounded above by
Mg_{\sigma}(1)
and bounded below by
\lambda_{\sigma}(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}(M))>0
.

3. We first observe that
q=0
is always a local maximum of
F(\lambda,q)
for every
\lambda\geq 0
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{q}^{2}F(\lambda,q)

\displaystyle=\lambda g^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}(q)-\frac{1}{(1-q)^{2}},

which gives
\partial_{q=0}^{2}F(\lambda,q)=-1<0
, since
g^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}(0)=0
as
k_{\star}(\sigma)\geq 3
. Next,
q=0
is the global maximum if and only if

\displaystyle\lambda g_{\sigma}(q)+q+\ln(1-q)<0,\quad\forall q\in(0,1),

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lambda<\lambda_{\sigma}:=\inf_{q\in(0,1)}h(q),\quad h(q)=\frac{-q-\ln(1-q)}{g_{\sigma}(q)}.

(28)

Let
q_{\sigma}=\arg\inf_{q\in(0,1)}h(q)
. Since
h(q)>0
for all
q\in(0,1)
,
\lim_{q\rightarrow 0^{+}}h(q)=\lim_{q\rightarrow 1^{-}}h(q)=\infty
, it follows that
q_{\sigma},\lambda_{\sigma}
are well-defined and
\lambda_{\sigma}\in(0,\infty)
. It also follows from the definition of
q_{\sigma}
that
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\lambda_{\sigma}^{+}}m_{\sigma}(\lambda)=q_{\sigma}
.

4. Expand

\sigma(z)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}a_{\ell}\mathrm{He}_{\ell}(z),\qquad a_{\ell}=\mu_{\ell}/\ell!.

Using
\mathrm{He}_{\ell}^{\prime}(z)=\ell\,\mathrm{He}_{\ell-1}(z)
and the assumption
\sigma^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mu)
(so the term-by-term differentiation is justified in
L^{2}
), we obtain

\sigma^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}a_{\ell}\ell\,\mathrm{He}_{\ell-1}(z).

Mehler’s formula gives

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{He}_{\ell}(z_{1})\mathrm{He}_{m}(z_{2})]=\delta_{\ell m}\,\ell!\,x^{\ell}\quad\text{for}\quad(z_{1},z_{2})\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\begin{bmatrix}1&x\\
x&1\end{bmatrix}\right).

Therefore,

g_{\sigma}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\sigma(z_{1})\sigma(z_{2})]=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}a_{\ell}^{2}\,\ell!\,x^{\ell},

so

g^{\prime}_{\sigma}(x)=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}a_{\ell}^{2}\,\ell!\,\ell\,x^{\ell-1}.

On the other hand,

g_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{\prime}(z_{1})\sigma^{\prime}(z_{2})]=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}a_{\ell}^{2}\,\ell^{2}\,(\ell-1)!\,x^{\ell-1}=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}a_{\ell}^{2}\,\ell!\,\ell\,x^{\ell-1}.

Hence
g^{\prime}_{\sigma}(x)=g_{\sigma^{\prime}}(x)
for
x\in[0,1]
.
∎

Appendix E
Proof of features decoupling in the dense readouts case

Let us prove the neuron decoupling – first identity in (
5
) – for the special case
\sqrt{k}\mathbf{v}=\mathds{1}
; we conjecture that this strategy with additional care extends to more general readout distributions. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\langle(\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}_{j})^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big\langle\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\sum_{l,m=1}^{d}w^{0}_{il}w^{1}_{jl}w^{0}_{im}w^{1}_{jm}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big\langle\sum_{l,m}[\mathbf{W}^{0\top}\mathbf{W}^{0}]_{lm}[\mathbf{W}^{1\top}\mathbf{W}^{1}]_{lm}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\langle\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{W}^{0\top}\mathbf{W}^{0}\mathbf{W}^{1\top}\mathbf{W}^{1})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\langle\|\mathbf{W}^{0\top}\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{F}^{2}\rangle\mathbb{E}\langle\|\mathbf{W}^{1\top}\mathbf{W}^{1}\|_{F}^{2}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{W}^{0\top}\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{F}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{W}^{0}\mathbf{W}^{0\top}\|_{F}^{2}=k.

This sum contains
k
equal terms of order
O(1)
and other
k(k-1)
“off-diagonal” terms that are equal by statistical exchangeability. This implies that the off-diagonal terms are of order
O(k^{-1})=O(d^{-1})
as claimed.

Appendix F
More details on the derivation of the main result

We fill in some details of the derivation of the main result. First, we show that

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm BO}\sim\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}^{2}\bigl(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}(q_{i})\bigr)

(29)

holds in the dense readout, interpolation regime, while leaving a complete proof for future work.

Since the BO error is half of Gibbs error, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm BO}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}^{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})}[(y_{1}-y_{0})^{2}]

where

\displaystyle y_{0}=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\sigma(\mathbf{W}^{0}\mathbf{x}),\quad y_{1}=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\sigma(\mathbf{W}^{1}\mathbf{x})

and
\mathbf{W}^{1}
is drawn from the posterior
P(\mathbf{W}\mid\mathcal{D})
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[(y_{1}-y_{0})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[y_{0}^{2}]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[y_{1}^{2}]-2\mathbb{E}[y_{0}y_{1}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[y_{1}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[y_{0}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}v_{i}v_{j}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x})\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{x})]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}v_{i}v_{j}g_{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j})=g_{\sigma}(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[y_{0}y_{1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}v_{i}v_{j}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x})\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x})]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}v_{i}v_{j}g_{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}_{j}).

Sue to the concentration of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[(y_{0}-y_{1})^{2}]
with respect to
(\mathcal{D},\mathbf{W}^{0},\mathbf{W}^{1})
, and since
B
is negligible compared to
A
, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm BO}\sim\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[(y_{0}-y_{1})^{2}]=\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}^{2}(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}_{i}))}_{A}-\underbrace{\sum_{i\neq j}v_{i}v_{j}g_{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}_{j})}_{B}

It suffices to show that
B
is negligible compared to
\varepsilon^{\rm BO}
, since this implies

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm BO}\sim A\sim\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}^{2}(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}(q_{i}))

Since
g_{\sigma}(x)=O(x^{3})
when
k_{\star}(\sigma)\geq 3
, each
g_{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}_{j})
, with
i\neq j
, is of order
d^{-3/2}
. Moreover, when
\mathbf{v}
is dense, the entries
v_{i}
are of order
k^{-1/2}
, it follows that
B=O(kd^{-3/2})=O(d^{-1/2})
. In the dense readout, interpolation regime,
\varepsilon^{\rm BO}=\Theta(1)
. We conclude
B\ll\varepsilon^{\rm BO}
.

The derivation for the variance of
R_{\mu i}
follows verbatim, since
R_{\mu i}
only differs from
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_{\mu})
by a single term
v_{i}\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\mu})
.

For general case, we give here some insights that may help to prove (
29
). Let us denote
Q_{ij}:=\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{w}^{1}_{j}
.

1.

\sum_{i}Q_{ij}^{2}=\sum_{j}Q_{ij}^{2}=1
. This identity is useful for bounding
B
when
n
is very large, as in this regime the overlaps
Q_{ii}\simeq q_{i}
are close to
1
, which suppresses the off-diagonal terms.

2.

Experimentally, we observe that
Q_{ij}
for
i\neq j
are
asymptotically uncorrelated
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(B)\lesssim\sum_{i\neq j}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}\mathrm{Var}(Q_{ij}^{3}),

This improves upon the pessimistic upper bound we used in the dense readout, interpolation regime.

Appendix G
Optimal scaling laws for
\beta>1/2

Recall that the effective SNR for feature
i
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{snr}_{i}=\frac{(n/d)v_{i}^{2}}{\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO}}.

Consequently, the weakest feature (indexed by
k
) is learnable whenever
\mathrm{snr}_{k}>\lambda_{\sigma}
, which in turn requires
n\gtrsim k^{2\beta}d
. Using
k^{2\beta}d
as the threshold, we distinguish two regimes for
n
, in addition to the standing assumption that
n\gg d
:

•

When
n\ll k^{2\beta}d
, the weakest feature is not learnable, so
k_{c}<k
.
From

\displaystyle\mathrm{snr}_{k_{c}}>\lambda_{\sigma}>\mathrm{snr}_{k_{c}+1}\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\frac{(n/d)v_{k_{c}}^{2}}{\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO}}>\lambda_{\sigma}>\frac{(n/d)v_{k_{c}+1}^{2}}{\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO}},

we obtain
k_{c}=\Theta((n/d)^{1/(2\beta)})
, which implies
1\ll k_{c}\ll k
. Recall from Result
1
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm BO}=\Theta\Big(\frac{k_{c}d}{n}\Big)+\Theta\Big(\sum_{i>k_{c}}v_{i}^{2}\Big).

From the asymptotics of
k_{c}
, the first term in this decomposition is
\Theta((n/d)^{1/(2\beta)-1})
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sum_{i>k_{c}}v_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Theta\Big(\sum_{i>k_{c}}i^{-2\beta}\Big)=\Theta\Big(\int_{k_{c}}^{k}x^{-2\beta}\,dx\Big)

\displaystyle=\Theta\Big(k_{c}^{1-2\beta}-k^{1-2\beta}\Big)=\Theta\Big(k_{c}^{1-2\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Theta((n/d)^{1/(2\beta)-1}).

Thus, the second term in the decomposition is comparable to the first one. We conclude
\varepsilon^{\rm BO}=\Theta((n/d)^{1/(2\beta)-1})
, which scales as
n^{\frac{1}{2\beta}-1}
when
k,d
are fixed.

•

When
n\gg k^{2\beta}d
, all the features are learnable:
k_{c}=k
. The second term in the error decomposition vanishes, so
\varepsilon^{\rm BO}=\Theta(kd/n)
, which scales as
n^{-1}
for fixed
k,d
.

It is worth noting from the preceding argument that when
d\ll n\ll k^{2\beta}d
, the first term in the error decomposition is comparable to the second. As
n
increases, additional features become learnable, causing the first term to grow while the second decreases. Consequently, as long as
n\gg d
, the second term is either comparable to or negligible relative to the first. Therefore, the asymptotics for Bayes-optimal error simplifies to
\Theta(k_{c}d/n)
for all
n\gg d
.

Appendix H
Extensive-width, near-interpolation regime

We derive here the results in the table of Section
3
. Recall that in the interpolation regime, we have
k\sim\gamma d
and
n\sim\alpha d^{2}
, where
\gamma,\alpha>0
are fixed parameters.

Ultra-sparse readout.
In this case, the vector
\mathbf{v}
has only finitely many nonzero components. Assume
v_{1}\geq\dots\geq v_{p}>0
with
p=\Theta(1)
, while
v_{p+1}=\dots=v_{k}=0
. For
i\leq p
, recall that the effective signal-to-noise ratio of
\mathbf{w}_{i}
is
\mathrm{snr}_{i}=(n/d)v_{i}^{2}/(\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO})
. Since

and

. Therefore, feature
i
is learnable for every

. Using (
7

.

For the remaining readout cases (dense, power-law, and exponentially decayed), recall that when

,

\displaystyle\frac{(n/d)v_{k_{c}}^{2}}{\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO}}>\lambda_{\sigma}>\frac{(n/d)v_{k_{c}+1}^{2}}{\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO}}

In each of these cases, consecutive readout coefficients satisfy

. Combined with

, the above inequality yields

(30)

Now let us deal with each case

Power-law,
\beta>1/2
. In this case
v_{i}^{2}=\Theta(i^{-2\beta})
. Combined with (

), this gives

,

By the asymptotics of
k_{c}
, both terms on the right-hand side are

Power-law,

Power-law,

In the near-interpolation regime, certain exact results, such as the leading constants in the asymptotic behavior and the locations of phase transitions, can be computed explicitly as consequences of Result

. Although these results are not the primary focus of this paper, they may be of independent interest to some readers. We collect them here.

Result 6
.

Consider the near-interpolation regime with a dense readout
\mathbf{v}
, such that the empirical distribution of the entries of
\sqrt{k},\mathbf{v}
converges to a limiting distribution
P_{v}
in the infinite-dimensional limit. Define

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon^{\rm BO}

(32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\mathsf{v})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{|I_{\mathsf{v},\delta}|}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathsf{v},\delta}}q_{i}

(33)

where
I_{\mathsf{v},\delta}=\{i\in[k]:\sqrt{k}v_{i}\in[\mathsf{v}-\delta,\mathsf{v}+\delta]\}
. Then we have

1.

(\varepsilon,\mathcal{Q}(\mathsf{v}))
satisfies the fixed-point equations

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\mathsf{v})

\displaystyle=m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha\mathsf{v}^{2}}{\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big)

(34)

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=g_{\sigma}(1)-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{v}\sim P_{v}}[\mathsf{v}^{2}g_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Q}(\mathsf{v}))].

(35)

2.

Let
\alpha_{c}(\mathsf{v})=\sup\{\alpha\geq 0:\mathcal{Q}(\mathsf{v})=0\}
, then

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}(\mathsf{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{v}^{2}}\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma\Big(\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1)-\mathbb{E}_{v\sim P_{v}}\Big[v^{2}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\lambda_{\sigma}v^{2}}{\mathsf{v}^{2}}\Big)\Big]\Big).

In particular, if
\max_{i}v_{i}
converges to a limiting value
\mathsf{v}_{\max}
, then the first phase transition–corresponding to the feature with the largest readout–occurs at

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma(\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1))}{\mathsf{v}_{\max}^{2}}.

(36)

Proof.

1. Fix
i\in I_{\mathsf{v},\delta}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{(\mathsf{v}-\delta)^{2}}{k}\leq v_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{(\mathsf{v}+\delta)^{2}}{k}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{n(\mathsf{v}-\delta)^{2}}{kd(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{nv_{i}^{2}}{d(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\leq\frac{n(\mathsf{v}+\delta)^{2}}{kd(\Delta+\varepsilon)}

Applying the non-decreasing function
m_{\sigma}
on all sides of the previous inequality, we have

\displaystyle m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{n(\mathsf{v}-\delta)^{2}}{kd(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big)\leq q_{i}\leq m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{n(\mathsf{v}+\delta)^{2}}{kd(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big).

The last inequality holds for any
i\in I_{\mathsf{v},\delta}
, which implies

\displaystyle m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{n(\mathsf{v}-\delta)^{2}}{kd(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big)\leq\frac{1}{|I_{\mathsf{v},\delta}|}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathsf{v},\delta}}q_{i}\leq m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{n(\mathsf{v}+\delta)^{2}}{kd(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big).

Taking
d\rightarrow\infty
in the last inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha(\mathsf{v}-\delta)^{2}}{\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big)\leq\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{|I_{\mathsf{v},\delta}|}\sum_{i\in I_{\mathsf{v},\delta}}q_{i}\leq m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha(\mathsf{v}+\delta)^{2}}{\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big).

Taking
\delta\rightarrow 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\mathsf{v})=m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha\mathsf{v}^{2}}{\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big)

(37)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm BO}\sim\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}^{2}(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}(q_{i})),

taking the limit
d\rightarrow\infty
, and using a similar argument in the derivation of (
37
), we obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon=g_{\sigma}(1)-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{v}\sim P_{v}}[\mathsf{v}^{2}g_{\sigma}(\mathcal{Q}(\mathsf{v}))]

(38)

2. From the definition of
\alpha_{c}(\mathsf{v})
and equation (
37
), using the fact that
\lambda_{\sigma}=\sup\{\lambda\geq 0:m_{\sigma}(\lambda)=0\}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{c}(\mathsf{v})\mathsf{v}^{2}}{\Delta+\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\sigma}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}(\mathsf{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{v}^{2}}\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{v}^{2}}\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma(\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1)-\mathbb{E}_{v\sim P_{v}}[v^{2}g_{\sigma}\circ\mathcal{Q}(v)])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{v}^{2}}\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma\Big(\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1)-\mathbb{E}_{v\sim P_{v}}\Big[v^{2}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha_{c}(\mathsf{v})v^{2}}{\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big)\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{v}^{2}}\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma\Big(\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1)-\mathbb{E}_{v\sim P_{v}}\Big[v^{2}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\lambda_{\sigma}v^{2}}{\mathsf{v}^{2}}\Big)\Big]\Big)

If
\mathsf{v}=\mathsf{v}_{\max}
, then for any
v\in\mathrm{supp}(P_{v})
, we have
\lambda_{\sigma}v^{2}/\mathsf{v}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\sigma}
, so
m_{\sigma}(\lambda_{\sigma}v^{2}/\mathsf{v}^{2})=0
. The formula simplifies to

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}(\mathsf{v}_{\max})=\frac{\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma(\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1))}{\mathsf{v}_{\max}^{2}}.

∎

Result 7
.

Consider the near-interpolation regime with power-law readouts with exponent
\beta
, i.e.

\displaystyle v_{i}^{2}=\frac{i^{-2\beta}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}j^{-2\beta}},\quad i\in[k].

1.

If
k_{c}<k
and
i=\left\lfloor xk_{c}\right\rfloor
for some
x\in(0,1)
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}q_{i}=m_{\sigma}(\lambda_{\sigma}x^{-2\beta}):=\phi(x)

(39)

In other words, the overlaps
(q_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
in the infinite dimension limit can be compactly described by a limiting profile
\phi
.

2.

For
\beta>1/2
, let

\displaystyle\bar{k}_{c}=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}k_{c}d^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}},\quad\bar{\varepsilon}^{\rm BO}=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon^{\rm BO}d^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}+1},

(40)

Then, letting
z(\beta):=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i^{-2\beta}
and since
\varepsilon=0
,

\displaystyle\bar{k}_{c}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{\sigma}z(\beta)\Delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}

(41)

\displaystyle\bar{\varepsilon}^{\rm BO}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{k}_{c}^{1-2\beta}}{z(\beta)}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}(\phi(x))}{x^{2\beta}}dx+\frac{g_{\sigma}(1)}{(2\beta-1)}\right).

(42)

3.

For
\beta<1/2
, let

\displaystyle\bar{k}_{c}=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}k_{c}/k,\quad\varepsilon=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon^{\rm BO}.

(43)

Then
\varepsilon
is the unique solution in
(0,\infty)
of

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\sigma}(1)-\varepsilon}{1-2\beta}=\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2\beta}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha(1-2\beta)}{\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}x^{-2\beta}\Big)dx

(44)

and

\displaystyle\bar{k}_{c}=\Big(\frac{(1-2\beta)\alpha}{\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}

(45)

Moreover, in the thermodynamic limit, all features are learnable if and only if
\alpha>\alpha_{\star}
, where

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{\star}}{\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma}=\frac{\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1)}{1-2\beta}-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{g_{\sigma}(\phi(x))}{x^{2\beta}}\,dx,

(46)

Derivation.
1. We have

\displaystyle\frac{nv_{k_{c}+1}^{2}}{d(\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO})}<\lambda_{\sigma}<\frac{nv_{k_{c}}^{2}}{d(\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO})}.

(47)

From the inequality on the left, it is clear that
k_{c}\to\infty
as
d\to\infty
. This implies
\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}v_{k_{c}}/v_{k_{c}+1}=1
, which yields

\displaystyle\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{nv_{k_{c}}^{2}}{d(\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO})}=\lambda_{\sigma}.

(48)

Next, let
x\in(0,1)
and
i=\left\lfloor xk_{c}\right\rfloor
. Recall that

\displaystyle q_{i}\simeq m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{nv_{i}^{2}}{d(\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO})}\Big)

(49)

Sending
d\rightarrow\infty
on both sides of (
49
), and using the fact that

\displaystyle\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{nv_{i}^{2}}{d(\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO})}=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\frac{nv_{k_{c}}^{2}}{d(\Delta+\varepsilon^{\rm BO})}\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{v_{k_{c}}^{2}}=\lambda_{\sigma}x^{-2\beta},

(50)

we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}q_{i}=m_{\sigma}(\lambda_{\sigma}x^{-2\beta})=\phi(x)

(51)

2. From (
48
),
straightforward calculations yield

\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}k_{c}\,d^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{\sigma}z(\beta)(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}.

(52)

Next, using
q_{i}\simeq\phi(i/k_{c})
for
i\leq k_{c}
, the error contributed by learnable features is

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq k_{c}}\frac{i^{-2\beta}}{z(\beta)}(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}\circ\phi(i/k_{c}))

\displaystyle=k_{c}^{1-2\beta}\frac{1}{k_{c}}\sum_{i\leq k_{c}}\frac{(i/k_{c})^{-2\beta}}{z(\beta)}(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}\circ\phi(i/k_{c}))

(53)

\displaystyle\simeq k_{c}^{1-2\beta}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{-2\beta}}{z(\beta)}(g_{\sigma}(1)-g_{\sigma}(\phi(x)))dx.

(54)

where in the last step, averaged sum over
i\leq k_{c}
is asymptotically the integral over
[0,1]
.

On the other hand, since
q_{i}=0
as
i>k_{c}
, the error that comes from non-learnable features is

\displaystyle\sum_{i>k_{c}}v_{i}^{2}g_{\sigma}(1)\simeq g_{\sigma}(1)\sum_{i>k_{c}}\frac{i^{-2\beta}}{z(\beta)}\simeq\frac{g_{\sigma}(1)}{(2\beta-1)z(\beta)}k_{c}^{1-2\beta}

(55)

This implies
\varepsilon^{\rm BO}\propto d^{\frac{1}{2\beta}-1}
, so
\varepsilon=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon^{\rm BO}=0
. Plug this value of
\varepsilon
to the previous formula, we obtain the results for
\beta>1/2
.

3. We have

\displaystyle v_{i}^{2}=\frac{i^{-2\beta}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}j^{-2\beta}}\simeq(1-2\beta)i^{-2\beta}k^{2\beta-1}

(56)

From (
48
), straightforward calculations yield

\displaystyle\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}k_{c}/k=\Big(\frac{(1-2\beta)\alpha}{\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}

(57)

In this case it is possible for all features to be learnable. Let us denote

\displaystyle\alpha_{\star}=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{c}(k)

(58)

where we recall that
\alpha_{c}(k)
is the threshold of
\alpha
for the feature
k
to be learnable. Let
\varepsilon
be the BO error at this value of
\alpha
. We have

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}(k)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\lambda_{\sigma}(\Delta+\varepsilon)}{dv_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\sigma}}{dv_{k}^{2}}\Big(\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}^{2}g_{\sigma}(q_{i})\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\sigma}}{dv_{k}^{2}}\Big(\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}^{2}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha_{c}(k)dv_{i}^{2}}{\Delta+\varepsilon}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\sigma}}{dv_{k}^{2}}\Big(\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}^{2}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\lambda_{\sigma}\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{v_{k}^{2}}\Big)\Big)

Using the fact that
v_{k}^{2}\sim(1-2\beta)/k
,
v_{i}^{2}/v_{k}^{2}=(i/k)^{-2\beta}
and
v_{i}^{2}\sim(1-2\beta)i^{-2\beta}k^{2\beta-1}
, the last expression can be written as a Riemann sum, which in the limit
d\rightarrow\infty
gives

\displaystyle\alpha_{\star}=\frac{\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma(\Delta+g_{\sigma}(1))}{1-2\beta}-\lambda_{\sigma}\gamma\int_{0}^{1}\frac{g_{\sigma}(\phi(x))}{x^{2\beta}}\,dx,

(59)

Let us derive the fixed-point equation for
\varepsilon
. We have

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=g_{\sigma}(1)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}^{2}g_{\sigma}(q_{i})

\displaystyle\sim g_{\sigma}(1)-(1-2\beta)k^{2\beta-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{-2\beta}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha dv_{i}^{2}}{\Delta+\varepsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle\sim g_{\sigma}(1)-(1-2\beta)k^{2\beta-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{-2\beta}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha(1-2\beta)}{\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}(i/k)^{-2\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle\sim g_{\sigma}(1)-(1-2\beta)\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(i/k)^{-2\beta}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha(1-2\beta)}{\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}(i/k)^{-2\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle\sim g_{\sigma}(1)-(1-2\beta)\int_{0}^{1}x^{-2\beta}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{\alpha(1-2\beta)}{\gamma(\Delta+\varepsilon)}x^{-2\beta}\Big)dx

which gives the fixed point equation for
\varepsilon
.

Appendix K
Numerical methods

K.1
Solving the fixed point equations

From the system of fixed point equations, we obtain the following equation for
\varepsilon
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon=g_{\sigma}(1)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}^{2}g_{\sigma}\circ m_{\sigma}\Big(\frac{nv_{i}^{2}/d}{\Delta+\varepsilon}\Big).

(60)

This can be quickly solved by a standard numerical method such as bisection.

K.2
HMC simulation

The theoretical predictions for the overlaps are validated by HMC simulation. In general, sampling from the posterior is difficult since the posterior landscape can be extremely complicated: with random initialization, it can take exponentially long to reach the true equilibrium. However, if the initialization lies in the same basin of attraction as the equilibrium state, convergence is achieved with reasonable computational cost. We initialize such that the first
k_{c}
rows coincide with the ground truth, and the remaining rows are random. Note that the HMC does not validate the prediction of
k_{c}
itself.

K.3
Numerical evaluation of Bayes-optimal error

Directly evaluating the Bayes-optimal error from its definition is computationally expensive. Indeed, computing
\mathbb{E}[y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{W}\mid\mathcal{D})]
requires drawing many independent samples from the posterior distribution
P(\mathbf{W}\mid\mathcal{D})
, and each sample requires a computational time on the order of
10^{2}
seconds on a GPU. To overcome this difficulty, we compute the Bayes-optimal error through
Gibbs error
, defined as

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm Gibbs}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathbf{W}^{0},\mathbf{W}^{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})}(y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{W}^{0})-y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{W}^{1}))^{2},

(61)

where
\mathbf{W}^{1}
is a sample of
P(\mathbf{W}\mid\mathcal{D})
. It is known that
\varepsilon^{\rm BO}=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\rm Gibbs}
. This identity is a straightforward generalization of the identity
\mathbb{E}(X-X^{\prime})^{2}=2\mathbb{E}(X-\mathbb{E}X)^{2}
for any i.i.d. random variables
X,X^{\prime}
. In practice, one only needs
10
-
10^{2}
samples of
(\mathcal{D},\mathbf{W}^{0},\mathbf{W}^{1})
due to small fluctuations with respect to these variables, while averaging over
\mathbf{x}
requires
10^{4}
-
10^{5}
samples, each of which can be generated at small cost. Computing the optimal error via Gibbs error is much more efficient, as for each fixed
(\mathcal{D},\mathbf{W}^{0})
, only one posterior sample from
P(\mathbf{W}\mid\mathcal{D})
is needed.

K.4
Training with SGD

We use the
Adam
optimizer implemented in
Pytorch
to train the student network. We note here some important points in the training

1.

The student’s features are normalized after each epoch, preventing their norms from growing excessively and resulting in more stable and well-behaved training dynamics.

2.

Performance degrades when the student’s readout is fixed to match the teacher during training, as the student is more likely to get stuck in local minima. In contrast, when the student readout is learnable, it still recovers that of the teacher, and the possibility for the readout to change during training helps the student escape local minima.
