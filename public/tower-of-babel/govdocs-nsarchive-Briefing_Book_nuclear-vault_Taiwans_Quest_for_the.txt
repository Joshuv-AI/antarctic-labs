<div class="node__content clearfix"><div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Washington, D.C., January 10, 2019 –</strong> From the late 1960s until the late 1980s, U.S.

government officials worried that Taiwanese leaders might make a “fundamental decision” to develop nuclear weapons.

Documents published today for the first time by the National Security Archive illustrate Washington’s efforts to keep tabs on military and scientific research and to intervene when they believed that Taiwan’s nuclear R&amp;D had gone too far.</p><p>Today’s posting builds on and adds documentary detail to an important recent publication by the Institute for Science and International Security (ISIS), David Albright and Andrea Stricker’s <a href="http://isis-online.org/books/detail/taiwans-former-nuclear-weapons-program-nuclear-weapons-on-demand/15">Taiwan’s Former Nuclear Weapons Program: Nuclear Weapons on Demand</a>.

Their book provides a comprehensive account of the nuclear program, in part by drawing on documents from the National Security Archive.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1">[1]</a></p><p>A key moment in the Albright-Stricker history is the January 1988 exfiltration by the CIA of a senior official Chang Hsien-yi who was embedded in Taiwan’s nuclear establishment and wanted to stop activities that he believed could endanger his country.

Albright and Stricker provide the first detailed account in English of Chang’s whistle-blowing.

While Chang’s relationship with the CIA remains deeply classified in government files, today’s posting includes declassified documents on the consequences of his actions.</p><hr><p>&nbsp;</p><h3 class="rtecenter">Taiwan’s Nuclear Weapons Research and Development, 1966-1988</h3><p class="rtecenter"><strong>By William Burr</strong></p><p>From the late 1960s into the late 1980s, U.S.Albright and Stricker provide the first detailed account in English of Chang’s whistle-blowing.

While Chang’s relationship with the CIA remains deeply classified in government files, today’s posting includes declassified documents on the consequences of his actions.</p><hr><p>&nbsp;</p><h3 class="rtecenter">Taiwan’s Nuclear Weapons Research and Development, 1966-1988</h3><p class="rtecenter"><strong>By William Burr</strong></p><p>From the late 1960s into the late 1980s, U.S.

government officials worried about the possibility that the Taiwanese government, officially known as the Republic of China [ROC], was secretly committed to acquiring a capability to produce a small number of nuclear weapons.

According to a recently declassified telegram from September 1978, published today by the National Security Archive for the first time, the U.S.

ambassador to the ROC supported a proposal to station a few U.S.

scientists at nuclear installations in Taiwan.

While it was not known for sure if President Chiang Ching-kuo had made a “fundamental decision” to develop a weapons capability, “if the ROC were to move in this direction they would have to conceal their effort from us at all costs.” Although U.S.

scientists “could not absolutely preclude a clandestine ROC effort ….

their presence would make such an effort much more difficult, by forcing still greater clandestinity.”</p><p>The proposal to station a few U.S.

nuclear scientists in Taiwan never came to pass, but for years before and after 1978 U.S.

government officials were watching for signs of a clandestine program to develop a nuclear weapons capability.

U.S.

diplomats and intelligence officers became aware of Taiwan’s nuclear interests not long after China first tested a nuclear device at Lop Nur in October 1964.

Seeking a deterrent against a Chinese attack, the Taiwanese military, with the support of President Chiang Kai-shek, played a leading role in the nuclear program.diplomats and intelligence officers became aware of Taiwan’s nuclear interests not long after China first tested a nuclear device at Lop Nur in October 1964.

Seeking a deterrent against a Chinese attack, the Taiwanese military, with the support of President Chiang Kai-shek, played a leading role in the nuclear program.

Military interest in a nuclear deterrent continued when Washington began to normalize relations with Beijing in 1971 and then broke formal diplomatic relations with Taipei altogether in 1978.</p><p>U.S.

government officials believed that even a few nuclear weapons would worsen Taiwan’s security situation and complicate U.S.

efforts to normalize relations with Beijing while maintaining economic and security ties with Taipei.

The latter’s dependence on Washington for security support gave the U.S.

an influential role in Taiwan’s nuclear decisions whenever CIA and State Department officials became aware of problematic activities.</p><p>The military-controlled Chung Shan Institute of Science and Technology (CSIST) directed nuclear weapons research since its founding in 1966.

CSIST played a controlling role in the Institute for Nuclear Energy Research (INER), which conducted weapons research and development.

According to one of the declassified records published today, a Taiwanese official explained that INER was a “daughter” of the Atomic Energy Council that had been “married” to the Chung Shan Institute.CSIST played a controlling role in the Institute for Nuclear Energy Research (INER), which conducted weapons research and development.

According to one of the declassified records published today, a Taiwanese official explained that INER was a “daughter” of the Atomic Energy Council that had been “married” to the Chung Shan Institute.

That relationship enabled CSIST secret payments of salary supplements to INER staffers without any civilian oversight.</p><p>Inspiring today’s posting is a recent publication by the Institute for Science and International Security (ISIS), David Albright and Andrea Stricker’s <a href="http://isis-online.org/books/detail/taiwans-former-nuclear-weapons-program-nuclear-weapons-on-demand/15">Taiwan’s Former Nuclear Weapons Program: Nuclear Weapons on Demand</a>, which provides a comprehensive account of the nuclear program, in part by drawing on documents from the National Security Archive.

A key moment in this history was in January 1988, when the Central Intelligence Agency exfiltrated a senior Taiwanese official, Chang Sen-i, deputy director of the Institute for Nuclear Energy Research (INER).

Chang had warned the CIA that Taiwan had a major clandestine nuclear program in the works but the Agency did not believe he would be safe when the U.S.

started demanding that the ROC stop work at suspect sites that only a few people, such as Chang, could identify.

Albright and Stricker provide the first detailed account in English of Chang’s whistle-blowing.</p><p>U.S.

government documents about Chang Sen-i’s work as a CIA source remain classified; so do documents about the secret installations and activities that Taiwan closed in early 1988 in response to U.S.

pressure.

Nevertheless, some records about the consequences of Chang’s revelations have been declassified and are published today.pressure.

They include documents about the shipment of heavy water from the Taiwan Research Reactor (TRR) and the withdrawal of Taiwan’s military from nuclear research and the separation of INER from the Chung Shan Institute.</p><p>Today’s posting includes significant material on the U.S.

and Taiwanese nuclear activities that the National Security Archive has collected since the last major <a href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nukevault/ebb221/index.htm">posting</a> on the issue in 2007.

Many were declassified in collections at the National Archives in response to requests by the National Security Archive.

Some have even been declassified in the weeks since the publication of the Abright-Stricker book.

They include:</p><ul><li>State Department intelligence reports from the 1960s on prospects for Taiwanese nuclear weapons capabilities and plans to purchase nuclear technology</li><li>A report from 1971 by a U.S.

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p></p><p>According to INR analysts, the "shock" of the 1964 Chinese nuclear test convinced President Chiang kai-Shek to order the creation of the Chung Shang Institute of Science to "investigate the feasibility and advisability of research and development leading to the manufacture of nuclear weapons." The Taiwanese government recognized the difficulty of carrying out a weapons program but wanted to be ready to execute one in the event of "profound changes in the international scene." Therefore, "studies of the feasibility of manufacturing nuclear weapons appear to be long-range directed ten years or more into the future."</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p></p><p>This recently declassified report provides perspective on the U.S. government's objections to chemical reprocessing in Taiwan and the problems in assuring IAEA safeguards in Taiwan. For Van Doren, the best outcome would be that a reprocessing plant not be "built in ROC at this time." Not only did it make no "economic sense," it would help prevent a nuclear weapons capability and should China take over Taiwan "it would acquire a valuable, free chemical reprocessing plant." A thorny problem was the role of IAEA safeguards. The future of the trilateral U.S.-Taiwan-IAEA trilateral agreement was in doubt because Taiwan had been expelled from the IAEA, to be eventually replaced by the PRC as part of the process of normalizing Beijing's international relations. The agreement would permit continuing IAEA inspection of the Canadian-supplied Taiwan Research Reactor, then under construction, but the question was whether Beijing would compel the Agency to withdraw from the agreement with Taiwan.</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p></p><p>Reports and rumors that Taiwan was trying to purchase a reprocessing plant were widespread in European nuclear energy circles during 1972. This message mentions Taiwanese discussions with potential British, Belgian, French, and West German suppliers. Bonn had a leg up in the negotiations because it could provide a "guarantee of delivery" while the other governments were wary of objections from Beijing. The U.S. position was that Taiwan "must be content" with a non-reprocessing "status quo" unless it built a capability with "indigenous technical expertise and industrial capability." The problem was that if Taiwan was determined to have a reprocessing capability was it better for suppliers to work out a safeguarded arrangement than to "run [the] risk" of Taiwan "finding other ways of realizing [its] objective with uncertainty about controls." Within weeks, the State Department authorized the embassy in Taiwan to convey its <a href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB20/docs/doc03.pdf">objections</a> to Taiwanese interest in building a reprocessing plant</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p></p><p>This recently declassified telegram was originally<a href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nukevault/ebb221/T-12.pdf"> released</a> to the National Security Archive in excised form, with the names of an IAEA official and an inspector withheld. Mentioned in the document were Leslie Thorne, chief of the Far East division of the Agency's Safeguards Department, and inspector Herbert Frittum. Meeting in Tokyo with ACDA official Joerg Menzel, a member of the U.S. team that had visited Taiwan a few weeks earlier, Thorne told Menzel about his visit to the Taiwan Research Reactor (TRR) and inspector Frittum's discovery of an unsafeguarded exit port at the fuel pond. The discovery brought up questions about the effectiveness of IAEA safeguards, but Agency inspectors believed that the problem reflected "lack of ROC cooperation" more than any problems with the safeguards themselves.<br><br>An issue that was unexplained in the telegram was the presence of "special fuel rods," which according to the Albright-Strickner account, the U.S. team also noticed. They were segmented so that they could be cut more easily in the cooling pond for eventual chemical reprocessing.<br><br>The discovery of the exit port raised questions about the possible diversion of spent fuel for secret reprocessing because the "irradiation and discharge of nine fuel rods had not been declared to the IAEA." The discoveries and discrepancies quickly led the U.S. to press Taiwan to shut down the TRR temporarily and to agree to restrictions on fuel cycle research and development work that could facilitate a weapons option.</p></div>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p></p><p>Galen Stone, the chief of the U.S. mission to the IAEA, reported on his discussion of the TRR problem with IAEA Director General Sigvard Eklund. <div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p></p><p>After Galen Stone's private meeting with Eklund (see previous document), Deputy Director General for Safeguards Rudolph Rometsch met privately with Joerg Menzel to discuss the Agency's safeguards at the TRR. Rometsch confirmed that Taiwan had not included the exit port in the design information that it had submitted to the Agency but "gave no explanation as to why the IAEA had not known about the port" (implying that it should have been noticed during the 1976 inspection). Rometsch "did not comment on previous IAEA safeguards effectiveness at all during 90-minute conversation." He did, however, observe that the special fuel rods "put safeguards at TRR and the significance of [the] now defunct reprocessing laboratory in new perspective." The implication was that the fuel rods could have been cut up into small enough pieces to be processed at the one-cell reprocessing laboratory which had apparently been <a href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nukevault/ebb221/T-14.pdf">closed</a> under U.S. pressure.<br><br>Stone reported that Rometsch did not seem to be aware of the discrepancies in the fuel rod count that the IAEA's Leslie Thorne had noticed. Moreover, Rometsch did not confirm Thorne's statement that the Agency had approved the transfer of spent fuel to dry storage, but in fact that would happen in the coming months.</p></div>

One of the purposes was to permit the U.S. to "continue exporting the many commodities ordered by CSIST that are subject to control because of their potential significance to nuclear weapons activities." The U.S. experts also wanted to discuss with Taiwanese officials their plans for the relationship between INER and CSIST and the AEC, respectively. The detailed itinerary included a visit to the Lungtan area, the suspected site for explosives research related to nuclear weapons.</p></div>
