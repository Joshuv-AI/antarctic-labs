Are Semantic Networks Associated with Idea Originality in Artificial Creativity? A Comparison with Human Agents CCS: Human-centered computing Empirical studies in HCI CCS: Human-centered computing HCI theory, concepts and models
Umberto Domanti, Lorenzo Campidelli, Sergio Agnoli, Antonella De Angeli

Abstract
. The application of generative artificial intelligence in Creativity Support Tools (CSTs) presents the challenge of interfacing two black boxes: the user’s mind and the machine engine. According to Artificial Cognition, this challenge involves theories, methods, and constructs developed to study human creativity. Consistently, the paper investigated the relationship between semantic networks organisation and idea originality in Large Language Models. Data was collected by administering a set of standardised tests to ChatGPT-4o and 81 psychology students, divided into higher and lower creative individuals. The expected relationship was confirmed in the comparison between ChatGPT-4o and higher creative humans. However, despite having a more rigid network, ChatGPT-4o emerged as more original than lower creative humans. We attributed this difference to human motivational processes and model hyperparameters, advancing a research agenda for the study of artificial creativity. In conclusion, we illustrate the potential of this construct for designing and evaluating CSTs.

Creativity is a complex aspect of human cognition with wide-ranging implications for individual, societal, and technological progress

(

based on the ability to produce original and effective products
(

. Despite the extensive application of this definition in many disciplines, Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) research has long lacked conceptual clarity in defining what creativity means
(

)
. Furthermore, the application of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) to creative tasks has added further terminological complexity. As a result, the word creativity is now used indifferently to denote human and machine performance, as well as their interaction
(

However, psychology suggests substantial differences between human and artificial creativity in intentionality and authenticity

. Consistently, the study reported in this paper has highlighted differences in the distribution of mean original ideas produced by humans and machines (Figure

). Human scores are distributed along a larger range of responses. On the contrary, machine scores are centred on the average performance of humans cutting out both lower and higher creative ideas
(

The question of whether GenAI can be creative has stimulated a stream of research comparing the performance of machines and humans using psychological tests
(

. The results are contradictory, with some studies showing better machine performance
(

, equal performance

. Despite their differences, this research shares an implicit product-centred definition of creativity

. GenAI is considered creative because it generates outcomes that would be defined creative if produced by humans
(

. Inevitably, this definition is bound to have a limited impact on the design and development of rapidly evolving obscure models and algorithms. The product-centred definition acknowledges the occurrence of creative outputs but cannot explain how they were generated. The question is no longer whether GenAI can be creative or which model generates more creative outputs
(

)
. The pertinent question is:
what are the mechanisms that could lead to artificial creativity?
Only this answer will provide designers of Creativity Support Tools, CSTs

The distinction between human and artificial creativity

;

. The ultimate challenge is how to interface two different types of black-boxes: the human mind and nondeterministic systems. In this fussy context, the outcome of the interaction is difficult to predict
(

Such complexity requires psychological knowledge
(

(

. To overcome this gap, the current paper investigates artificial creativity by applying theories and methods of creative cognition
(
17
)
. Accordingly, creativity is the
process
that emerges from the combination of basic neurocognitive mechanisms (memory, attention, and control) in divergent and convergent thinking. The paper focuses on the association between semantic memory network (the representation of the organisation of the conceptual units in memory) and idea originality in a divergent task. Psychology has demonstrated that more flexible networks are associated with higher originality
(
70
;
71
)
.
Therefore, the specific research question addressed in this paper is:

RQ:

What is the relationship between semantic networks and idea originality in ChatGPT-4o
?

To answer it, we conducted an experiment comparing human and artificial originality. The machine data was collected by using the chat interface of GPT-4o, which reproduced a complete black box example, as no information on model hyperparameters was available. The human sample included 81 psychology students divided into higher and lower creative individuals. Results confirmed the expected association between semantic networks and originality only with respect to higher creative individuals. Consistently, ChatGPT-4o was less original than higher creative humans,
who also displayed a more flexible semantic memory network. However, the expected association did not manifest in the comparison with lower creative individual. Despite having a more rigid network, ChatGPT-4o emerged as more original than them.

The contribution of this paper to the CHI community is empirical, operational, and methodological. To the best of our knowledge, the paper provides the first
empirical
evidence of an association between semantic networks (an indication of the creative process) and idea originality (an indication of the creative product). The relationship mirrored human creativity only with regard to the comparison with higher creative individuals. This unexpected outcome inspired new fascinating research directions for the study of artificial creativity in HCI. In short, the paper has the following organisation. Section
2
presents the related work about creativity in HCI and psychology, with the aim to seed a ground for our research agenda. Section
3
presents the method of the study advancing best psychological practices. Section
4

5

6

7
concludes the manuscript with a call for further research on artificial creativity.

2.2.
Creativity as a Cognitive Process

To move beyond product-oriented research
(
97
)
, we follow the definition of creativity as the dynamic interplay between inconclusiveness (when originality or effectiveness has not yet been reached) and achievement (the fulfilment of both originality and effectiveness). In other words, creativity requires “potential” originality and effectiveness
(
38
)
. The term potential allows the shift from a focus on the product to a focus on the process. It acknowledges the existence of countless instances in which the value of a product was not initially understood but was rediscovered and recognised later, as well as cases in which the outcome itself is not primarily important
(
39
)
.

As illustrated in Figure
2
, the creative process emerges from basic neurocognitive abilities: memory, attention, and cognitive control
(
17
)
. They converge into two generative modalities: convergent and divergent thinking
(
63
)
. Convergent thinking considers all available information to assemble it in the most compact form
(
54
)
and possibly arrive at a single result through integration
(
78
)
. In contrast, divergent thinking is an exploratory cognitive mode that aims to generate multiple solutions or ideas for a problem or task. The current paper investigates the relationship between semantic memory network as a basic mechanism of the creative process and idea originality as an indicator of divergent creative outcomes.

2.3.
Divergent Creativity

Previous works have demonstrated how the divergent creative potential of LLMs is improving over time
(
113
)
. Furthermore, research suggests that LLMs reflect typical human patterns in idea generation, such as the serial order effect, whereby later responses tend to be more novel than earlier ones
(
14
;
58
;
108
)
. Other authors have suggested that it is possible to enhance LLM creativity by incrementally prompting the model more forcefully to generate original responses across interactions
(
59
)
. Most of these studies investigated artificial creativity using experimental divergent tests.

As a reliable indicator of real world achievements
(
69
;
100
)
, divergent thinking can be assessed through a set of tasks, including verbal and visual stimuli
(
111
, TTCT;)
, ambiguous figures
(
61
, FIQ;)
and the production of words that are as different from each other as possible
(
83
, DAT;)
. A common verbal test is the Alternate Uses Task
(
63
, AUT;)
, which requires generating as many alternative uses as possible for common objects (e.g. a brick) within a fixed time. Responses provide a measure of fluency (the number of generated ideas), flexibility (the number of different categories of ideas), elaboration (the level of idea detail), and originality (the idea novelty and uncommonness). Historically, originality has been assessed based on interrater agreement between at least two expert judges on a Likert scale
(
3
)
. Recently, however, deep neural network-based LLMs have been trained to perform this task. An example is OCSAI (Open creativity scoring with artificial intelligence), which achieved a correlation
r=0.81
with human raters
(
85
)
.

Concerns on AI reliability.

The findings of experimental studies comparing humans and LLMs on divergent thinking tasks are contradictory, likely reflecting methodological and procedural differences, each with its own strengths and limitations. Table
1
provides an overview starting from the articles reviewed by Gilhooly
(
58
)
and including only published papers which underwent peer-review by July 2025. Most of these studies addressed originality, with a preference for the AUT. Based on such comparison, some experiments have shown that humans outperform machines
(

;
108

, while others have demonstrated that machines have higher divergent thinking capabilities than humans
(
47
;
64
;
68

. The inconsistency can be explained by several factors. These include procedural differences in the collection of human and machine data; cognitive, affective, and motivational variations in the human sample; and the evolving capabilities of LLMs. Furthermore, at a conceptual level, the comparison between humans and machines is oversimplified.

As reported in Table
1
, some studies revealed that the machine performance outperformed human average, but others noted how a small portion of humans (the higher creative individuals) consistently outperformed the machine
(
74
;
65
;
61
)
. Therefore, this paper adopted an experimental design that includes Higher Creative Humans (HCH) and Lower Creative Humans (LCH). Within this methodology, we posit that HCH will outperform ChatGPT-4o (GPT) in originality, while GPT is expected to be more original than LCH (
H1.1
).

2.4.
Semantic Memory

Memory plays a crucial role in human creativity
(
17
;
16
)
. However, the study of the memory-creativity link is complex as there are different types of memory (working, semantic, episodic, short/long-term) and different modalities of creative thinking (e.g., divergent and convergent). According to the MemiC framework, the role of memory in the creative ideation stages extends from the search for memory and the construction of candidate ideas to the evaluation of novelty and effectiveness
(
16
)
. In particular, the role of semantic memory in creative thinking has recently been explored. Recent research has highlighted how semantic memory structures and the ability to access these structures are fundamentally related to creative performance across the lifespan
(
42
;
40
;
41
;
25
)
.

The structure of semantic memory can be represented as a network and analysed based on mathematical graph theory
(
105
)
. The network consists of nodes (the basic unit of the system) and edges (the semantic associations between two concepts). Data to construct these networks is generally collected by verbal production tasks
(
70
;
77
)
. They require participants to generate as many words as possible belonging to a given semantic category or related to a target word, within a fixed amount of time. In the resulting network, each node represents a category exemplar (e.g., tiger), and the edges represent associations between two exemplars. In other words, these associations constitute the tendency to generate an exemplar
b
(lion) when
a
(tiger) is produced.

A large body of psychological research has shown how the semantic network of higher creative individuals differs from that of lower creative individuals
(
18
;
66
;
70
;
71
;
86
)
. As summarised in Table
2
, the former is characterised by (a) lower distance to traverse from one node to another, ASPL; (b) higher connectivity between conceptual nodes, CC; and (c) fewer sub-communities within the network, Q
(
70
;
71
)
. Moreover, higher creative individuals appear to have a more resilient and flexible network, which is more resistant to targeted “attacks” than lower creative individuals, R
(
72
)
. Thus, a robust and flexible structure enables the activation of a broader range of associations, thereby increasing the possibility of generating new ideas
(
72
)
.

Despite semantic network flexibility being a reliable indicator of human creative performance, it remains unclear how such structures are represented in LLMs
(
46
;
74
)
. Moreover, much uncertainty exists about the association between network organisation and idea originality in artificial creativity. To the best of our knowledge, only one published study has investigated the difference in semantic network structures between humans and different LLMs
(
115
)
. Human data was obtained from a random selection of 30 participants in the animal category of the semantic fluency database reported in
(
116
)
. LLM data was extracted by instructing the LLMs to play specific roles related to 30 common occupations (e.g., pretend that you are a chef) before performing the task. This procedure was intended to enrich the diversity of the machine responses
(
101
)
. The results showed that the semantic network of LLMs has higher ASPL, lower CC, and higher Q compared to humans
(
115
)
. Despite creativity not being a variable of the study, the authors concluded that “LLMs have poorer creativity and flexibility compared to humans”
(
115
, p. 6)
.

The current study was designed to explicitly study the association between semantic networks and originality in artificial vs. human creativity. Following Artificial Cognition
(
110
)
and expanding the study of Wang et al.
(
115
)
, we expected GPT to have significantly different semantic networks than HCH and LCH, given the expected direction of idea originality stated in
H1.1.
We tested this hypothesis with structural and percolation analysis using the measures described in Table
2
. Consistently, we hypothesise that GPT would display a more flexible network compared to LCH and the reverse compared to HCH (
H1.2
).

3.1.
Participants and Design

Human responses were obtained from the dataset collected by Campidelli et al.
(
25
)
. The sample analysed in the present study included 81 psychology students (mean age = 20.3 years, SD = 1.71, men = 12, women = 69). Machine data was obtained on the 6th and 7th of March 2025 by using ChatGPT-4o chat interface
1
1

1

https://chat.openai.com
with the “Plus” subscription package
(
113
)
. This interface neither displayed nor allowed for the modification of the values of the hyperparameters. Based on the mean originality scores of each human participant, the human sample was median split into (a) LCH and (b) HCH. Accordingly, the experiment compared the networks and creative performances of three groups: GPT, LCH, and HCH.

3.2.
Procedure

The experiment with human participants took place in a room free of acoustic and visual distractors and was conducted by an expert researcher. After signing informed consent, they sat in front of an empty desk and were asked to complete two different verbal production tasks and the AUT. The target words were printed on a sheet of paper, which remained on the desk for the entire duration of the tasks. The responses were recorded with an audio recorder and manually transcribed by one of the experimenters. The procedure was repeated by creating a new separate chat for each of the 81 ChatGPT-4o participants (Appendix
A
)
(
113
)
. All human and machine responses were merged, anonymised, sorted in alphabetic order within each object, and finally scored for originality by two expert coders, blind to the experimental goals and the origin of the data, as recommended by Silvia et al.
(
106
)
. The interrater reliability was high, ICC = .93.

3.3.
Experimental Tasks

Appendix
A
presents the instructions given to humans and prompts received by ChatGPT-4o across three tasks. The Verbal Fluency Test and the Free Association Task were used to construct semantic networks. Finally, the AUT was used to estimate originality. The Verbal Fluency Test required participants to produce as many words as possible belonging to the “animals” and “fruit and vegetables” categories within one minute for each target
(
60
)
. The Free Association Task required participants to verbally produce as many words as possible associated with “building” and “notebook” within one minute for each target
(
81
)
. The presentation order of the verbal production tasks was randomised. For the AUT, human participants were asked to generate as many original alternative uses as possible for “shoe” and “fork” within 3 minutes for each target. ChatGPT-4o was prompted with the same instruction provided to human participants (Appendix
A
). Appendix
B
provides examples of interactions (i.e., prompts and outputs), for both humans and ChatGPT-4o. The databases containing all responses for each task and the scripts for the analyses are available in the Supplementary Materials.

3.4.
Semantic Networks

The network estimation followed the procedure and pipelines provided by Christensen et al.
(
33
)
. The entire verbal production database was translated from Italian to English and pre-processed with the R packages
SemNetDictionaries
and
SemNetCleaner
. The cleaned data was entered into a binary response matrix with participants in rows and words in columns. Each cell was filled with 1 if the participant had produced the word and 0 if they had not. The networks were estimated using the
SemNet
package and applying the correlation-based method
(
33
)
. The cosine similarity function was employed to estimate the edges between nodes and compute the association profiles of the responses. To control for confounding factors (e.g., different numbers of nodes or edges among groups), the matrices only included responses that were provided by at least two participants in each group
(
22
;
42
, see, for example,)
. Furthermore, in line with the literature on network construction
(
31
;
33
;
115
;
42
;
77
;
80
;
32
)
, the Triangulated Maximally Filtered Graph (TMFG) was adopted to minimise noise and spurious association by creating different networks with the same number of nodes and edges
(
33
;
31
;
80
)
. This method aims to maximise the strength of node associations while maintaining the extant network planar to be depicted graphically
(
31
)
. TMFG represents the only consolidate alternative to the Planar Maximally Filtered Graph, PMFG
(
73
;
112
;
80
)
. Generally, the two methods align with one another
(
33
)
, but TMFG is more efficient than PMFG
(
80
)
. The resulting networks of GPT, LCH, and HCH were compared using structural and percolation analyses.

5.
Discussion

This study investigated the association between semantic networks and idea originality in ChatGPT-4o and a sample of 81 psychology students divided into higher and lower creative individuals. In the following subsections, we discuss the results in comparison with research on human creativity
(
17
;
115
;
70
)
and then reflect on the implications of artificial creativity
(
98
)
for CSTs design
(
104
;
52
)
.

5.1.
Hypotheses testing

Extending Artificial Cognition
(
110
)
with research on creative cognition
(
17
;
115
;
70
)
, the study tested the hypothesis of a relationship between
the semantic network of ChatGPT-4o and its idea originality
(H1)
. To address it, we tested two sub-hypotheses related to the analysed constructs (Table
6
).

5.1.1.
Main Effects on Idea Originality and Network Flexibility

Results supported
H1.1
related to idea originality. They showed that ChatGPT-4o was less original than higher creative humans but more original than lower creative humans (Table
6
). The effect emerged consistently, considering both the total production and the originality peaks. Differently, the results of the structural and percolation analyses only partially confirmed
H1.2
related to network flexibility. Consistent with previous studies
(
71
;
70
)
, higher creative humans were characterised by a more organised, flexible, and robust semantic memory network compared to lower creative humans. Furthermore, as expected, the network of ChatGPT-4o was found to be less flexible than that of higher creative humans. However, the same pattern also emerged in the comparison with lower creative individuals, who appeared to be more flexible than the machine.

To summarise, ChatGPT-4o semantic network was characterised by more distances, weaker local interconnectivity and higher modularity compared to those of both lower and higher creative humans. This configuration indicates higher internal rigidity and less cohesion, which reflects the sparser connections between concepts in different sub-communities
(
115
;
70
)
. It suggests an increased difficulty in “navigating” the semantic structures to reach remote concepts and to create original associations between them
(
13
;
16
)
. This conclusion was confirmed by the percolation analysis. It showed that the semantic network of ChatGPT-4o broke apart faster as its intensity thresholds increased.

5.1.2.
Association between Idea Originality and Network Flexibility

Our main hypothesis
(H1)
stated that the semantic network flexibility provides an indication of idea originality in LLMs. Results revealed that higher creative humans consistently outperformed ChatGPT-4o in terms of originality. This result supports psychological literature suggesting that highly creative individuals, who are characterised by a less spread out, less segregated, more flexible and robust semantic memory network, exceed AI in creative tasks
(
46
;
74
)
. However, despite a more rigid and segregated network, ChatGPT-4o consistently exceeded lower creative humans in idea originality (Table
6
).

These results seem to suggest independence between semantic network structures and creative performance in machines. Yet, they may be better understood in light of the Entropy Modulation Theory of Creative Exploration
(
67
)
. The authors of this theory suggest that even when the semantic memory structure of groups of individuals is equivalent, higher creative ones navigate the network faster by increasing activation variance or lowering production threshold. While in humans this capacity reflects executive control, in ChatGPT-4o it may be mimicked by different hyperparameters of the model, such as temperature, top_p, max tokens, frequency and presence penalty.

Temperature, for example, defines the level of randomness of text generation, with lower values producing more deterministic responses, and higher values increasing unpredictability
(
88
;
45
)
. However,
preliminary results suggest a weak effect of temperature on creative outputs, despite it being described as the creativity
parameter in the literature
(
88
)
.
The effect of the other hyperparameters remains largely unexplored and much uncertainty still exists
(
45
)
.
In this study, we tested ChatGPT-4o using its chat interface. Accordingly, although its network was more rigid, ChatGPT-4o may have emerged as more original than lower creative humans because this model configuration allowed the generation of original alternative uses without sacrificing coherence with respect to the AUT prompt.

Another explanation for these inconsistencies could be related to the impact of affective and motivational processes on human creative performance
(
5
;
49
;
74
;
6
;
79
;
37
;
2
)
. While we might assume that ChatGPT-4o consistently performed the task at the best of its incredibly fast computational capabilities
(
74
)
, lower creative humans might have experienced a higher cognitive effort. Furthermore, they were likely more affected by scarce motivation to do the task, low self-efficacy, external pressure of being evaluated, time constraints, or cognitive fatigue than higher creative humans. Consequently, even with a less flexible network, ChatGPT-4o performed the test consistently at its best capacity and generated more original responses than participants with lower creative abilities. Finally, these inconsistent results could be due to the impact of the ChatGPT-4o speed in processing large data structures
(
74
)
. Even if its network was not flexible, its rapidity in navigating complex information structures might have led to the retrieval of remote associations at a pace that compensated for its structural limitations.

5.2.
Limitations and Future Directions

This paper provides a first step in linking semantic networks and artificial creativity. The novelty of the approach encompasses a number of limitations, which limit the generalisability of our results. These pertain to the data sample, the study procedure, and the complexity of the creativity construct.

5.2.1.
Data Sample

A main limitation concerns the type of human sample and the reliance on only ChatGPT-4o. The human participants were psychology students, who may have been more intrinsically motivated to perform the tasks because of their academic background as compared to other individuals. Since motivation is a key dimension of the creative process
(
49
;
5
;
79
;
6
;
74
;
37
)
, it is possible that different participants would have led to different results. Furthermore, we only tested ChatGPT-4o using its chat interface
(
113
)
. This choice tested an extreme black-box that improved ecological validity as it represents the typical interface employed by most users. However, the chat interface does not allow to know and modify the values of the model hyperparameters. Future research is needed to compare different models while manipulating hyperparameters, in order to investigate their effects on the correlation pattern between semantic network flexibility and originality.

5.2.2.
Study Procedure

Following a strict application of Artificial Cognition
(
110
)
and consistent with
(
65
;
61
;
64
;
47
;
113
)
, we prompted ChatGPT-4o with the same instructions given to human participants. Yet, research suggests that prompting plays a fundamental role in the potential creativity of GenAI
(
84
)
. There is evidence that LLMs can be incrementally enforced to generate original responses across interactions
(
59
)
and creative outcomes are more likely to occur during roleplay
(
62
;
101
)
. Furthermore, both humans and ChatGPT-4o were given the same time constraints (Table
7
), but we controlled it only for the human sample. Although it has been suggested that ChatGPT may reflect the serial order effect typical of human creativity
(
14
;
108
;
58
)
, no rigorous investigation has yet examined how time limitation affects both machine and human performance. Altogether, these considerations highlight the urgency of research on how prompting affects the production of artificial creativity.

5.2.3.
Construct Complexity

Following previous research, we investigated divergent thinking through the AUT focusing on originality
(
108
;
11
;
10
;
65
;
74
)
. AUT is “regularly used in work on AI creativity”
(
10
, p. 2)
(Table
1
). Nevertheless, divergent thinking represents only one aspect of the multi-componential construct that is creativity
(
38
;
61
)
, and originality is only one requirement of a creative idea
(
98
)
. Due to its popularity in research, AUT responses may have been included in the training databases of the machine
(
58
;
65
)
. However, as Haase & Hanel
(
65
)
noted, this possibility does not imply that these words would appear in the generated output. Future research should extend to more ecological methods to assess creativity, including real world activities, and non-verbal, visual, or figurative performance. Additional creativity measures (i.e., flexibility, elaboration) should also be considered to comprehensively investigate both human and artificial creativity. Finally, future studies should clarify the the directionality of the association between network flexibility and creativity.
Altogether, these limitations provide directions for future research on artificial creativity.

6.
Artificial Creativity

Given all the limitations acknowledged in the previous section, we now reflect on the relevance of artificial creativity
(
99
;
98
)
for CSTs design and evaluation
(
52
;
104
)
. Following
(
98
;
99
)
, artificial creativity refers to the potential of a machine to provide output that if generated by humans would be defined as creative. Building on this definition, we provide two operational insights (OPI) and three methodological recommendations (MER) for a systematic research agenda on artificial creativity in HCI.

OPI1

Artificial Creativity is Average Performance
: The mean originality scores of ChatGPT-4o on the AUT reflected the average human performance
(
45
)
, but cut out lower and higher creative individuals. Therefore, the machine outcome was good enough, but it could not achieve excellence. This result supports and explains previous qualitative research showing how at first encounter artists tended to perceive artificial creativity as inadequate, constrained, or even constraining
(
62
;
44
;
28
;
57
;
48
)
. In particular, they were concerned about a risk of homogenisation which threatened their professional identity and personal style. It was feared that GenAI could make creativity less authentic
(
8
)
and elaborated
(
10
)
, fostering convergence towards similar average results
(
50
;
75
;
7
;
24
)
. On the other hand, this average definition suggests that GenAI could be beneficial, specifically for less creative individuals, whose output becomes more novel, enjoyable, and useful when supported by AI
(
50
)
. Therefore, it is of primary importance to consider the user experience and skills when introducing users to artificial creativity. Regardless of their background, all users require new interfaces to bridge human and artificial creativity. Therefore, designers of human-AI systems should consider the average nature of artificial creativity when designing interfaces connecting human and artificial creativity to ensure that the former benefits from rather than being hindered by the interaction
(
35
;
75
)
.

OPI2

Artificial Creativity is a Process, not only a Product
: Artificial creativity expands and connects HCI and psychological research by advancing a dynamic definition of artificial creativity
(
38
)
. Not only does this definition address the question of whether AI can be creative, but it also attempts to understand the processes embedded in AI mechanisms. The current paper provides preliminary evidence of a partial association between idea originality (as an indication of the product) and semantic networks (as an indication of the process) in ChatGPT-4o. The results confirmed the expectation derived by human cognition
(
70
;
71
)
of a relationship between originality and network flexibility only with respect to the comparison with higher creative individuals. Therefore, the potentiality of generating original ideas may not be strictly associated with the semantic structure, but certain hyperparameters may partly compensate for the rigidity of the network and enable the production of original responses. This papers offers HCI researchers a structured methodology for systematically addressing artificial creativity. Future research should also focus on supporting different stages of the creative process
(
39
;
99
;
27
)
, and how their use affects users’ performance in subsequent tasks with and without them
(
10
;
75
)
.

MER1

Human Creativity Provides Methodological Scaffolding
: Research on Artificial Cognition draws parallels between the AI black-box and the human mind — the original black box
(
110
)
. In sharp contrast with the influential Media Equation Theory
(
94
)
, artificial creativity does not assume a similarity between machine and human behaviour. On the contrary, this paper provides evidence of differences in idea originality and its association with semantic memory structure between human and artificial creativity. The evidence enriches related work describing higher level psychological differences in drivers, experiences and norms
(
98
;
99
;
1
)
. The framework of creative cognition
(
17
)
provides concepts, methods, and theories to approach the study of artificial creativity. From a design perspective, it addresses the interface at a micro-level, directly connecting machine hyperparameters and human executive functions. In this paper we have analysed semantic memory, future research should address attention and control (Figure
2
), as relevant dimensions of both human and artificial creativity.

MER2

Collecting Reliable Human Baselines
: The human sample used as a baseline is of critical importance for the study of artificial creativity. From a cognitive perspective, indeed, creativity emerges as the expression of personal abilities, which vary across individuals and along the lifespan
(
98
)
. Furthermore, the natural black box is affected by hidden cognitive functions, motivations, and experiences, all of which can affect creative performance. Consequently, an average representation of creativity only applies to the machine, but it fails to explain the richness of the human counterpart (Figures
1
and
3
). It makes limited sense to talk about the average creativity of humankind. Consequently, it needs to be better characterised taking into account possibilities and limitations. The median split applied in this study offers a simple way to obtain a finer-grained operationalisation of creativity for future research. Other individual and contextual factors also need careful consideration. They relate to participants’ recruitment and experimental design.

MER3

Artificial Creativity Provides Metaphors for Design
: Over 20 years ago, Shneiderman
(
104
)
suggested that “Graphics composition tools show the enormous power of software to enable more people to be more creative ”
(
104
, p. 119)
. Consistently, the metaphor of semantic network opens up stimulating design trajectories for HCI research. We can imagine new CSTs allowing different types of users (artists and researchers) to visualise and manipulate the semantic networks of the model. By modifying these networks as creative interfaces, artists may explore new semantic spaces which may lead to seed incidents at the core of their masterpieces
(
48
)
. There is evidence indeed that experimental artists engage in experiments with extensive data curation
(
26
)
, subverting conceptual spaces, forcing the machine to fail and hallucinate
(
21
;
20
)
, or introducing deliberate frictions in tangible interactions
(
43
)
. Experimental artists may value the tool as a medium for transformational practices
(
20
;
26
;
21
)
. Scientists, on the other hand, may exploit the interactive visualisation of semantic networks to support a systematic program of research on artificial creativity. They could manipulate prompts, hyperparameters, and tasks to understand their effect on the system output. This research requires different methods and sensibilities when testing humans or machines. Current research has paid extensive attention to the prompt provided to the machine
(
84
;
59
)
. This paper instead highlighted methodological considerations for user research.

7.
Conclusion

To conclude, we go back to the question posed in the introduction about the
the mechanisms that could lead to artificial creativity.
Extending the framework of Artificial Cognition
(
110
)
to account for creativity
(
17
)
, this paper provides initial evidence of the role of semantic networks on idea originality in LLMs using the the Alternate Uses Task. We demonstrated that this role differed according to the human sample used for the comparison. Higher creative individuals were more original than ChatGPT-4o, which, despite its rigidity, emerged as more original than lower creative individuals.
Taken together, the evidence extends previous studies comparing human and artificial creativity by providing operational and methodological advances. Finally, we provide methodological guidance for a more rigorous investigation of artificial creativity in the field of HCI.
