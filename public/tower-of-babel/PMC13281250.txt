Estimating dietary intake and nutrient adequacy among adults in Saudi Arabia: a population-based assessment
Authors: Omar Alhumaidan, Shihana Alakeel, Sarah Alkhunein, Ghadir Fallata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon‑communicable diseases (NCDs) pose a significant health challenge in Saudi Arabia, where 18.95% of adults report at least one chronic condition, including diabetes (9.1%), hypertension (7.9%), and cardiovascular diseases (1.5%). This burden highlights the need for reliable, national‑level estimation of dietary intake and nutrient adequacy to inform evidence‑based nutrition policy. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate dietary intake, nutrient exposure, and adequacy among Saudi adults using an updated, locally developed probabilistic model (NNC‑v2).MethodsThis modeling study was developed a model using nationally published data from Saudi Arabia starting in 2010. The model was designed to generate probabilistic estimates that account for uncertainty and variability by linking food supply data to population-level intake and nutrient exposure. Model parameters were specified as statistical distributions rather than fixed values, allowing variability and uncertainty to propagate through the estimation process. Age-related intake adjustment and routine variation were incorporated within the model structure. Nutrient adequacy was evaluated using a Monte Carlo–based Average Requirement cut-point approach (10,000 iterations). Predictive performance was assessed indirectly by comparing model-derived food expenditure with national survey data.ResultsGrains estimated intake (mean 278 g/day), followed by protein foods (120 g/day). Median energy intake was 2399 kcal/day in males and 2366 kcal/day in females. Estimated intake levels were evaluated against the Average Requirement (AR) values of 320 µg/day for folate and 75–90 mg/day for vitamin C. Among individuals with intake below the AR, the mean intake gaps were approximately 97 µg/day for folate and 27 mg/day for vitamin C. Magnesium intake was evaluated relative to AR values of 350 mg/day for males and 255 mg/day for females. Model-estimated monthly food expenditure closely aligned with national data (7% difference in 2018; 2% in 2023).ConclusionsNNC-v2 offers a probabilistic framework for estimating dietary intake and nutrient adequacy at the national level in Saudi Arabia within the available data sources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01341-0.

Background

According to the World Health Organization, non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as cardiovascular diseases and cancer, are among the leading causes of death globally, accounting for approximately 41 million deaths annually and representing approximately 71% of the total global mortality [1]. This substantial burden is driven by the complex interplay of biological, behavioral, and environmental factors that interact in a nonlinear manner, making it challenging to address each component in isolation [2]. Comprehensive and integrated approaches that consider the interconnected nature of these factors are required for effective public health responses. Key influencing factors include individual physical characteristics, dietary patterns, physical activity levels, and psychological and social factors, as well as the policy and regulatory framework governing the food system [3].

Building on this understanding of the multifactorial nature of NCDs, diet emerges as a central element because of its direct and indirect influence on disease risk or through the prevailing national dietary patterns [2, 3]. Thus, public health strategies often adopt integrated approaches that combine regulation, behavioral change, and environmental interventions to reduce nutritional risk exposure and improve overall food systems [2].

National nutrition policies and programs are pivotal to these efforts [4, 5], operating through two complementary tracks. The first track indirectly influences consumer behavior by regulating the food industry and markets, while the second provides direct support through programs, such as those targeting maternal and child nutrition [4, 5].

These policies are often informed by the national Food-Based Dietary Guidelines (FBDGs), which are grounded in scientific evidence and present user-friendly formats to guide consumers [6–8]. FBDGs also support policy planning by outlining ideal dietary patterns and highlighting gaps in the actual consumption data [9, 10].

Understanding national dietary patterns, either through direct measurements or estimations, is critical in developing evidence-based nutrition policies, programs, and dietary guidelines aimed at improving nutritional outcomes [11]. This process involves two main components: (1) identifying and characterizing dietary patterns and (2) evaluating health and nutritional adequacy [12–14]. Data sources, tools, and analytical approaches vary with the objectives of each component.

Food balance sheet data, such as those provided by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), offer estimates of food availability to identify and describe national dietary patterns [15]. Additional sources, including the Global Dietary Database (GDD) [16] and Euromonitor [17], provide data on the consumption of packaged and unpackaged food products. These sources are particularly valuable in settings where recurring national dietary surveys are lacking and thus provide a useful alternative for dietary assessment. Such data are analyzed using computational models designed to characterize national dietary patterns, such as the Global Expanded Nutrient Supply (GENuS) and Food and Agriculture Biomass Input–Output (FABIO) models [18, 19]. In Saudi, the National Nutrition Consumption Model (NNC) developed by the Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) serves this purpose [20].

Several tools and methodologies are used to assess dietary patterns in terms of health and nutritional adequacy. Index-based methods, such as the Healthy Eating Index (HEI-2015), the Chinese Healthy Eating Index (CHEI), and the Saudi Healthy Eating Index (S-HEI) [21–23] compare current dietary patterns with national dietary guidelines and assign scores based on the degree of alignment [24, 25]. The Estimated Average Requirement (EAR) cut-point method, which assesses the nutrient adequacy of individual dietary components based on average requirement thresholds, is also widely used [26, 27].

As previously discussed, the original version describes the dietary pattern in Saudi Arabia by converting food supply data into dietary intake estimates using probabilistic modeling [20]. However, it lacks crucial features, including the weekly variability in consumption, the influence of aging on dietary intake, and disaggregation by age group. Additionally, it did not include features for evaluating nutrient adequacy using the EAR cut-point or index-based method.

Given the importance of studying dietary patterns and leveraging recent methodological advancements, this study aims to estimating dietary intake and nutrient adequacy among adults in Saudi Arabia. This requited to develop an updated version of the NNC—National Nutrition Consumption Model Version 2 (NNC-V2). This model enables the description and evaluation of the current dietary pattern in Saudi Arabia between 2018 and 2025 and projects trends through 2030. In addition, the model will provide probabilistic representations of the food supply and intake stratified by age and sex, enabling a more detailed and policy-relevant understanding of the national dietary landscape.

Methods

Study design overview

This modeling study developed an updated model utilizing nationally published data from Saudi Arabia, commencing in 2010. The study employs a probabilistic approach, the study leverages data to define statistical distributions and integrate them into the model's computational methodology. This approach is intended to improve the model’s capacity to manage uncertainty across the food system, from supply to consumption. It effectively captures weekly fluctuations in food consumption and accounts for age-related differences in intake [28]. These elements were not addressed in the initial version, which converted food supply to consumption without adjustments for age or weekly variations [20].

The computational modeling approach in this study is primarily based on the supply level (S), which is first converted into an unadjusted consumption estimate (F) using a supply-to-consumption coefficient derived from empirical data. The probabilistic approach represents each input variable through statistical distributions from empirical data rather than fixed values [32, 33]. Each model iteration randomly samples values from input distributions to solve the system of equations [34]. This process repeats across iterations, with each representing a plausible scenario based on defined distributions. We performed 10,000 iterations to ensure stable output distributions and capture model estimate variability.

For example, assume that the estimated daily intake of fruits in the adult population follows a log-normal distribution with a mean of 100 g/day and a standard deviation of 50 g, as derived from observed data. In a fixed-point framework, the model would assign 100 g/day to all individuals and proceed with exposure calculations using this single value. However, this ignores both variability in intake across individuals and uncertainty in the parameter estimate. Within the probabilistic framework, each Monte Carlo iteration samples a different intake value from the specified distribution, for instance, 65 g/day in one iteration, 118 g/day in another, and 210 g/day in a third, reflecting the right-skewed nature commonly observed in dietary data. The sampled intake is then multiplied by the corresponding nutrient concentration to estimate exposure for that iteration. Over 10,000 iterations, this process generates a full distribution of exposure outcomes rather than a single estimate.

The results form an output distribution reflecting uncertainty in the food system. The model uses distributions and iterations to enable meaningful interpretation of likely scenarios and risk ranges in supply and consumption estimates [35].

Figure 2 shows three core estimation processes: (1) forecasting food supply, (2) estimating dietary consumption, and (3) adjusting intake for age and weekly variability. Study data and population

Analysis focused on the adult population (≥ 18 years) of Saudi Arabia at the national level. Where the modeled results of the model derived from aggregated secondary data sources rather than individual-level dietary surveys. Population figures were obtained from the General Authority for Statistics (GASTAT) [29], while food supply data were extracted from FAOSTAT [15]. Historical consumption patterns were informed by the GDD and Euromonitor datasets, which capture both packaged and non-packaged food categories [16, 17]. Food composition data were obtained from standardized food composition tables and adapted to reflect locally relevant food items and fortification practices [30].

All data inputs were harmonized to ensure consistency in units, reference periods, and population coverage before integration into the probabilistic modeling framework. The study period primarily covered observed data between 2010 and 2018 for model parameterization, with supply estimation extended to 2023 and projected through 2030.

Food supply modeling

Food supply was modeled using a random walk algorithm, which projects the supply for the upcoming year based on the current year while considering historical data [36]. These data indicate the magnitude of changes stemming from random, system-wide factors that are difficult to isolate without complicating the model. The random walk approach also facilitates the representation of possible scenarios as multiple iterations, enabling the model to present the expected supply as a distribution with means and 95% confidence intervals (CI; 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, respectively). This reduces reliance on deterministic estimates [36].

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${DC}_{i}$$\end{document} is the probability of food consumption during the day. The unadjusted food consumption is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{i}$$\end{document} which is the result of multiple \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${FS}_{i}$$\end{document} with the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{t+1}$$\end{document} and conversion from tons/year/population to grams/day/capita. The conversion was carried out using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y$$\end{document}, the ton-to-gram factor (1,000,000). Additionally, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${B}_{pop}$$\end{document}, the population factor representing the average population between 2010 and 2018, was used to calculate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${FS}_{i}$$\end{document}. The model was adjusted for weekly variations in consumption patterns to determine final modeled intake estimates using Eqs. (10) and (11).

The assumption of independence between food-group distributions reflects the statistical constraints of nationally aggregated data rather than an underlying causal structure. Accordingly, within-individual dietary correlations, including substitution or compensation patterns, cannot be derived from aggregated probability distributions, and each food group is therefore modeled independently in accordance with the available data structure.

The model is developed to align with population-level aggregated data, following a supply-to-consumption framework in which consumption patterns are inferred from aggregated supply. As such, the resulting outputs represent modeled population-level outcomes rather than individual-level dietary relationships.

Within this context, interrelationships between food groups are not specified, as they cannot be identified from the current aggregated data structure. Consequently, scenario-based analyses requiring such relationships were not undertaken.If detailed individual-level data become available in future work, the framework can be extended to support individual-level simulations, enabling the incorporation of interdependent patterns across food groups.

Regarding the accuracy of the data sources at this stage, GASTAT and Euromonitor were used directly because they serve as the primary documentation sources for retail statistics, primarily including packaged food and single food ingredients (such as oil and sugar). Regarding the GDD data in Saudi Arabia, which included most non-packaged foods, analysis was conducted against the FAO Food Balance Sheets, indicating limited observable bias in terms of over- or underestimation [37].

As shown in Fig. 2, the model result can be adjusted from general intake into age-specific intake for individuals aged 18 and above to account for the effect of age. This adjustment was performed using Eq. 12:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${AdjNNC}_{Ai}={FET}_{i}-({FET}_{i}\times FA \times \Delta Age).$$\end{document}

Where the intake at age 18, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${FET}_{i}$$\end{document} serves as a reference, and an age adjustment factor (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FA$$\end{document}) is applied for each year thereafter until the target age \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A$$\end{document}. Thus, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta Age$$\end{document} is the difference between target age \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A$$\end{document} and age 18.

The inclusion of this age-adjustment factor was necessary within this framework to convert the relative reductions reported in meta-analyses into a comprehensive correction applied to national intake estimates [28]. Given that Saudi GDD data do not consider age-related declines, resulting in intake patterns that increase with age [16], contrary to meta-analytic evidence [28]. This partially adjusts for this structural issue and aligns the estimates with the anticipated age-related pattern.

Therefore, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FA$$\end{document} was derived from the findings of a meta-analysis that highlighted age-related declines in dietary intake [28]. The meta-analysis reported a cumulative decrease of approximately 20% in food intake between the ages of 27 and 70 for male, and 14% for female. The age adjustment component was derived from published meta-analysis evidence due to the absence of relevant local data. Given that this evidence is not population-specific, a conservative consensus-based approach was adopted, whereby the meta-analysis estimates were represented using a uniform distribution. This formulation reflects the non-local nature of the data while accounting for the associated uncertainty and maintaining consistency with the available evidence.

In addition, the available studies reported aggregated total intake effects rather than food-group. Therefore, to maintain consistency with empirically observed data and to avoid introducing unsupported assumptions, a uniform adjustment factor was applied across all food groups [28]. Thus, a cumulative decline in the meta-analysis was converted into an annualized rate of reduction, which was subsequently applied using Eq. Modeling of nutrient exposure assessment

After obtaining the values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${NNC}_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${AdjNNC}_{Ai}$$\end{document}, the model assessed exposure by combining these values with nutrient concentration data or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{i}$$\end{document} from food composition tables. This was calculated using Eqs. (16) and (17).16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{All age}:{E}_{i}= {NNC}_{i}\times {C}_{i},$$\end{document}17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{Specific age}: {E}_{i}= {AdjNNC}_{Ai}\times {C}_{i}.$$\end{document}

This procedure was applied to each food group, and we subsequently aggregated the resulting exposure of each group to nutrients to obtain the total exposure. When the assessment determines the exposure of the subgroup, the equation is modified, as in Eq. (18).18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S{E}_{i}= {SG}_{i}\times {C}_{i}.$$\end{document}

Notably, instead of using a single nutrient profile per food group as the value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{i}$$\end{document}, this model incorporates the distribution of nutrient concentrations obtained from the Widdowson and McCance’s food tables [30].

Suppose that a food group contains items with nutrient concentrations of 50–150 mg; in that case, the model applies a uniform distribution rather than relying on a single mean value. In estimating the probabilistic exposure, each iteration randomly selects a value from this distribution under the assumption that any item within the group can be consumed. This approach allows representation of diverse scenarios, where one individual may consume only foods at the lower or upper end of the concentration range, whereas another may consume a balanced mix. This variability is reflected across all the iterations. Nutrient composition data from Widdowson and McCance’s food tables [30] were used to minimize the influence of outliers, with the 25th and 75th percentiles applied as bounds for uniform distribution (Table 2). This is mathematically expressed as follows:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{i}\sim U({\mathrm{min}}_{C}, {\mathrm{max}}_{C}).$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{min}}_{C}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{max}}_{C}$$\end{document} are the nutrient concentrations below 25% and 75% of food items in the food composition table, respectively. The uniform distribution was selected because national dietary surveys and within–food-group item-level consumption shares are not available, precluding the construction of consumption-weighted mixtures. Accordingly, a uniform distribution bounded by the interquartile range was applied as a conservative and internally consistent assumption at the food-group level. To evaluate the assumption that nutrient composition can be represented by a uniform distribution, a comparative test was conducted against the observed distributions in the food composition table. Exposure was estimated under both specifications assuming a fixed consumption of 50 g. The resulting distributions were compared based on decile estimates. The findings showed agreement between the two approaches across deciles, with the uniform distribution yielding comparable results to those obtained using the observed data. Based on this agreement, the uniform distribution was adopted as a simplifying assumption, as it provides a reasonable representation of the full dataset. Detailed results are presented in Appendix 2.

Since Widdowson and McCance’s food tables were used as an external food composition database in this study, the database was first reviewed to remove culturally irrelevant items, such as pork products and foods not available in the Saudi market based on import and market availability data (FAO food balance sheet and Euromonitor) [15, 17]. The remaining items were then adjusted according to national fortification regulations to reflect Saudi-specific foods, market conditions, and mandatory standards [38, 39]. All selected items were reviewed and harmonized for cultural relevance by two independent researchers, and the full list of included foods with their specifications is provided in Appendix 2.

Modeling dietary adequacy assessment

Nutrient intake adequacy was evaluated using a Monte Carlo–based cut-point framework previously developed and validated by de Lauzon [26], which was formally compared with both conventional probability approach and deterministic approaches. Within this framework, nutrient requirements were treated stochastically rather than as fixed cut-points. During each simulation iteration, a requirement value was randomly sampled from a distribution centered on the Average Requirement (AR). The requirement distribution was constructed by applying a coefficient of variation (CV) of 15% to represent inter-individual variability, consistent with the referenced methodology. All value that representing the AR for adults was extracted from by Allen et al. [31].

It is worth noting that the conventional probability approach typically applies nutrient-specific CVs, with requirement variability defined separately for each nutrient based on available evidence [26, 27, 31]. In contrast, the Monte Carlo–based framework proposed and evaluated by de Lauzon [26] applies a standardized CV of 15% across nutrients. In their comparative evaluation, this specification produced estimates comparable to those obtained using both the nutrient-specific probability approach and the deterministic cut-point method. Accordingly, a CV of 15% was consistently applied to all nutrients in the present study following the referenced methodology.

Comparative analysis of modeled results and observed data

To evaluate the predictive performance of the model against observed real-world data, a direct validation using individual dietary intake surveys was not feasible due to the absence of nationally representative food consumption survey data in Saudi Arabia. Consequently, a conventional comparison between predicted and directly measured intake could not be conducted.

Due to the limitations of the available data, direct accuracy validation was not feasible. Therefore, this study employed an indirect comparative analysis, which is used as an indicative measure rather than a formal accuracy criterion. This alternative approach employed an indirect comparative analysis using nationally representative food expenditure data reported by the GASTAT databases [29].

In the 2018 National Expenditure and Budget Survey, the reported mean monthly per‑capita food expenditure was 516 SAR, while the more recent 2023 survey, which followed the same methodology, showed an increase to 825 SAR [29].

The comparative analysis using this data, the Model-predicted consumption quantities for each food group were converted into monetary values by integrating these estimates with observed national food price data. This allowed the derivation of a model-implied monthly food expenditure per capita, which was subsequently compared with the corresponding observed expenditure reported in food expenditure data surveys. The underlying premise of this comparative analysis is that, if the model adequately represents consumption volumes, combining these modeled volumes with observed price data would yield expenditure estimates within a comparable range to observed household spending. This comparison is intended to provide an indicator at the level of model variables under conditions where direct accuracy testing is not feasible. However, it does not constitute an assessment or validation of model accuracy. Formal validation would require detailed, disaggregated data with consistent agreement across multiple data points, particularly at the individual level rather than aggregated data, which is not available in the present study.

Discussion

Key findings

The updated NNC-v2 model was structured as a probabilistic framework integrating food supply estimation, supply-to-consumption conversion, age adjustment, and nutrient exposure assessment within a unified simulation process. Key parameters were represented as statistical distributions, and estimates were generated through iterative modeling. Intake and adequacy were therefore expressed as population-level distributions rather than single-point values. The model incorporated probabilistic input data, age-adjusted consumption patterns, and historical food supply trends, while accounting for age-related changes in intake and weekly variation in consumption.

Food supply estimates between 2018 and 2023 showed stable ranges across major food groups, with projected values through 2030 remaining within similar intervals. Grains accounted for the largest share of total supply, followed by dairy, fruits, and vegetables. No substantial shifts in overall supply structure were observed during the study period.

Daily intake estimates followed a similar pattern. Grain and protein foods represented the largest components of intake across age groups. Fruit and vegetable intake remained comparatively lower. Absolute intake declined gradually with age. Cereals contributed the largest share of carbohydrate intake and a notable share of protein intake, while oils contributed the largest proportion of total fat intake.

The adequacy assessment evaluated estimated nutrient intake relative to the AR across nutrients. Intake levels for phosphorus, selenium, vitamin B12, and iron were evaluated relative to their AR values. For vitamin C, magnesium, vitamin B6, and vitamin A, intake levels among some individuals were below the corresponding AR values. Intake gaps were observed for folate and vitamin C in both sexes. For magnesium, the intake gap differed between males and females, whereas calcium and manganese also showed intake gaps relative to their AR values. Evaluation of the magnitude of these intake gaps would require studies examining the relationship between nutrient intake levels and deficiency-related health outcomes in order to determine their potential health significance.

Using a de Lauzon Monte Carlo–based cut-point framework previously developed and validated to evaluate energy and sodium intake is methodologically inappropriate, as these nutrients are not assessed against deficiency thresholds in the same manner as micronutrients [26, 27, 31]. Accordingly, the evaluation of energy imbalance and sodium intake relative to recommended intake levels and values associated with increased risk of adverse health outcomes was not included in the present analysis. These aspects will be addressed in future research directions, where risk-factor–based assessment approaches will be explored to evaluate potential health implications associated with excessive intake.

Model performance compared to observed data

The validation suggests that the model showed reasonable predictive performance, with differences between predicted and observed expenditures falling within a modest range. These results indicate that the model may approximate real consumption patterns under the current data constraints. Compared to the earlier version publish in 2024, which showed higher error, the updated model appears to benefit from incorporating probabilistic uncertainty [20].

However, several limitations remain. The validation relies on an indirect approach, and only two observation points were available because the National Expenditure and Budget Survey is conducted every five years. In addition, the official expenditure data are aggregated, which limits the ability to perform detailed validation at the food‑group level.

Additionally, the supply-to-consumption conversion used in the model, the study could not verify these coefficients directly and relied on comparing final predicted consumption expenditure with national data. While this approach allows model assessment under current constraints, future work should validate the coefficients against external sources like household purchases or dietary surveys and incorporate uncertainty into model outputs.

Despite these limitations, this indirect approach provides a practical means of evaluating model performance in the absence of national dietary survey data and offers a more informative benchmark than relying on food balance sheets alone. Future validation using dedicated dietary survey data would further strengthen the model’s external validity.

Model implications

This pattern may inform future modeling studies assessing the impact of the Saudi mandatory calorie menu labeling issued by the SFDA on dietary intake. This modeling study utilizes the Regulatory Impact Assessment Model, which has been validated for Saudi Arabia [40], and contributes to the evidence on consumer responses to calorie labeling policies [41, 42].

Among the individuals who exceeded the AR for sodium (1500 mg/day), 47% each of males and females had intake levels slightly above this threshold. On average, the intake exceeded the requirements of 421 mg for males and 431 mg for females. These levels are relatively close to the AR threshold; however, they emphasize the value of continued monitoring and awareness to promote a balanced sodium intake across the population. Notably, the sodium content of bread products considered in this analysis was based on the regulatory limit set by the SFDA in accordance with Gulf Standard GSO 2362:2021, which specifies a maximum of 400 mg per 100 g [37].

The findings of this model can support the future development of sodium benchmark levels for additional food categories. This can be achieved through a follow-up modeling study that applies the risk assessment approach proposed by the World Health Organization (WHO) to simulate the potential impact of sodium limits on population intake patterns [43].

Over 40% of the population had an intake below the AR for magnesium and manganese. Over 40% of the population had intake levels that fell below the AR for vitamin A, vitamin E, niacin, vitamin B6, folate, and vitamin C. The most pronounced inadequacies were observed in folate and vitamin C, where over 90% of both sexes had intake levels that were below the AR, with a mean deficit exceeding 95 µg for folate and 27 mg for vitamin C.

Nutrients with a prominent gap between intake levels and established AR, such as folate, may warrant consideration for fortification strategies to be explored. These studies could incorporate a Tolerable Upper Intake Level to ensure safety and apply nutrient risk assessment methodologies to evaluate the potential benefits and risks associated with such regulatory strategies [44, 45].

Advantages and disadvantages of the study approach

The revised model is structured around a probabilistic estimation framework that was consistently applied across all stages of the analysis, including the AR, supply estimates, consumption rates, exposure calculations, and nutrient concentrations. Each parameter was represented as a probability distribution, and multiple iterations were conducted to reflect the potential variability. This methodological shift allows the model to account for uncertainty in dietary and food system dynamics, thereby avoiding reliance on single-point estimates that may underrepresent inherent variation. Unlike the previous version of the model, which considered variables such as food composition as fixed inputs without accounting for internal variability, the current version adopts distribution-based estimations that reflect natural variation within these parameters [20].

Despite evaluating the overall predictive performance of the model, there are inherent limitations in the validation process. The estimated daily expenditure, calculated by combining predicted consumption with price data, was compared with the actual national expenditure reported by GASTAT, which is available only as an aggregate total across all food groups. This restricts the ability to assess accuracy at the level of individual food groups. In addition, the supply forecasting component could not be independently validated because the available supply data (2010–2018) were also used for model parameterization, preventing a out‑of‑sample assessment.

For the age-related relative decrease was derived from international meta-analytic evidence rather than nationally representative Saudi data [28]. While this approach provides a methodologically consistent and generalized estimate, it may not fully capture population-specific patterns. The availability of context-specific data would allow for more precise estimation tailored to the target population.

Furthermore, the model does not incorporate geographic disaggregation; thus, estimations are conducted at the national level, and the regional heterogeneity of dietary exposure is not captured. This is a limitation in applying the model to sub-national policy assessments. The same limitation applies child-specific estimations due to that no parameters and data relate children were not included in this application of the model. The computational framework could theoretically accommodate; however, this was not implemented because of the absence of robust data and validated intake indicators for this age group. Particularly, the lack of reliable food expenditure data for children limits the ability to verify or calibrate the exposure of this subgroup. Therefore, estimates for children were excluded to avoid uncertain or potentially misleading outputs. These aspects may be incorporated in future extensions of the model, contingent on the availability of appropriate data and validated estimation methods.

Comparison of model results with other estimates and surveys

The first version of the model produced a median intake of approximately 3,306 kcal with a 95% uncertainty interval of 2,100–4,880 kcal [20]. This high-level estimate reflects the use of the previous Saudi reference values (2,207 kcal for males and 1,700 kcal for females) [46] and the structure of the original model, which did not account for weekly variability in consumption and relied on fixed‑point nutrient concentrations [20].

In contrast, the updated probabilistic framework used in NNC‑V2 incorporates variability in both consumption and nutrient concentrations. This resulted in a median energy intake of 2,396 kcal, with an uncertainty interval of 1,268–3,758 kcal. The distribution indicates the presence of higher‑intake subgroups on the right tail of the distribution and lower‑intake groups on the left, which aligns with patterns observed in international dietary surveys where population means often cluster around ~ 2,000 kcal with right‑skewed distributions [47–49]. This pattern is more consistent with NNC‑V2 than with the original model and supports the use of a probabilistic approach. Similar alignment was observed in the contribution of major food groups, which was overstated in the original model compared with values reported in international datasets [47].

Regarding national surveys, a Saudi assessment of 10,735 participants in 2013 reported mean intakes of 70 g/day for fruits, 111 g/day for vegetables, 147 g/day for poultry meat, and 219 ml/day of milk [50]. The NNC‑V2 estimates showed comparable patterns, with median values of 79 g for fruits, 63 g for vegetables, and 122 g for poultry meat, noting that the model outputs represent median consumption.

According to the Saudi Healthy Plate (SHP‑2024), the recommended daily amount of fruits and vegetables for energy levels between 2,200–2,400 kcal is 400–500 g/day [51]. In the current model, even the estimated 97.5th percentile reached only 218 g/day, which remains below national recommendations [51]. These findings represent modeled estimates intended to support general policy considerations. Future specialized dietary surveys would allow direct verification of these patterns, and the model can guide such efforts by identifying food groups with the highest likelihood of deviation from recommended levels, thereby optimizing resources and survey design.

The FAO Food Balance Sheet serves as the sole source for food supply data in Saudi Arabia and supports indicators like undernourishment [15]. Published figures indicate that energy intake was 3,704 kcal in 2018 and 3,659 kcal in 2023. Although FAO reports offer general per-capita availability figures based on the assumption of an evenly distributed population, our probabilistic framework translates supply into estimated consumption and nutrient exposure. As demonstrated in the model, this approach yielded a median energy intake of 2,396 kcal, with an uncertainty range of 1,268–3,758 kcal. This method generates distributions that reflect population variability and highlight groups that may fall below or exceed physiological needs, a detail not apparent from aggregated FBS indicators alone.

Conclusions

The second version of NNC-v2 was developed using a probabilistic approach that incorporates input distributions and iterative simulations to estimate dietary intake and nutrient adequacy using a distribution-based approach. This version improves on the original model by integrating age-related variability and weekly consumption fluctuations and applying the AR cut-point method using full distributions of intake and requirement. The results demonstrated stable trends in the food supply between 2018 and 2023, with projected consistency through 2030. Notably, deficits were identified in the intake of certain nutrients, such as folate. These findings may serve as a basis for informing national nutrition policies, guiding food fortification strategies, and which may support regulatory assessments of the health and economic impacts of nutrient inadequacy.
