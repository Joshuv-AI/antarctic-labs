Concurrent inferior vena cava syndrome and spontaneous retroperitoneal hemorrhage secondary to inferior vena cava filter thrombosis, treated with large bore mechanical thrombectomy: a case report
Authors: Basil Zia Khan, Andrew Wigham, Daniel Kearns
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInferior Vena Cava (IVC) filters are an established treatment option to prevent morbidity and mortality in patients who are at high risk for pulmonary embolus and unable to receive pharmacological anticoagulation. As their use increases, we are now seeing an increasing incidence of associated complications; IVC thrombosis is one of them and can be challenging to treat. Our report describes a rare case of a 55-year-old male patient with active metastatic lung cancer presenting with concurrent inferior vena cava syndrome and spontaneous retroperitoneal hemorrhage due to an occlusive IVC filter-associated thrombus. The case demonstrates successful treatment with large bore mechanical thrombectomy. Our case aims to provide insight into a rare yet complex complication that can occur due to IVC filter use. Our hope is that this case increases awareness of how to successfully manage concurrent IVC filter thrombosis and resultant retroperitoneal hemorrhage with large bore mechanical thrombectomy.Keywords: thrombosis, inferior vena cava filter, retroperitoneal hemorrhage, large bore mechanical thrombectomy

Summary

Inferior vena cava filter (IVCF) placement is a routinely performed procedure within interventional radiology. Current guidance suggests that an IVCF should be placed in patients with known venous thromboembolic disease and an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation.1 Several studies suggest an incidence rate of 2%-30% for IVCF-related thrombosis.2,3 Concurrent IVCF thrombosis and hemorrhage is extremely rare, with only a handful of cases reported in literature. We present a case of IVCF-associated thrombosis causing concurrent venous bleeding and IVC syndrome. We describe how this was diagnosed and successfully treated with large bore mechanical thrombectomy. This report also investigates current literature on such a rare occurrence.

Clinical presentation

A 55-year-old male patient presented to the emergency department (ED) with collapse, severe periumbilical/left iliac fossa pain, and bilateral lower limb edema 4 days following resection of a brain metastasis from an Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR)-positive lung adenocarcinoma. On examination there was rebound tenderness within the left iliac fossa, a palpable mass in the left lower quadrant and bruising. The patient was anticoagulated on apixaban for a previous pulmonary embolism. This had been withheld prior to neurosurgery in keeping with local protocols and an IVCF was placed (Cook Celect; Cook Medical). Post operatively the patient was started on a split treatment dose of Heparin based on weight as per our local protocol which was continued until presentation to the ED.

Investigations/imaging findings

A CT Abdomen/Pelvis was performed to exclude/identify surgical causes. This was performed in the porto-venous phase as per our local protocols. The CT demonstrated a large left retroperitoneal hematoma measuring approximately 54 × 105 mm with an occlusive IVC thrombus extending below the filter to both common iliac veins (see Figure 1). There were dense foci within the hematoma which were equivocal for an active bleeding point given the absence of a multiphase study and no discrete vessel supply (see Figure 2). A second CT was performed a few hours later due to persistent hypotension despite adequate fluid resuscitation. This consisted of 3 phases (non-contrast, arterial and delayed) to increase the probability of identifying an active bleeding point. The repeat CT demonstrated no interval growth in the hematoma or active contrast extravasation. The patient continued to deteriorate clinically and became nonresponsive to fluid resuscitation. The hemoglobin level remained normal and did not drop. This was not in keeping with the extent of the hematoma. Given the out-of-hours presentation and short period of deterioration further imaging was not performed to assess the lower limb veins.

Differential diagnosis

A multi-disciplinary approach involving the surgical, intensive care, hematological and interventional radiology teams was undertaken. Whilst initially the differential for hypotension and a retroperitoneal hematoma included an active arterial bleed it was later determined that the retroperitoneal hematoma was secondary to venous bleeding given the absence of an arterial blush or rapidly enlarging hematoma on serial imaging. This was felt to be the case due to increased venous backpressure from the occlusive IVC thrombus resulting in a perforated venous vessel. The persistent hypotension was felt to be due to a severe reduction in venous return to the heart resulting in obstructive shock/IVC syndrome. The reduced preload meant fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support were not effective in achieving hemodynamic improvement.

Treatment

The presentation of concurrent hemorrhage and complete IVC thrombosis presented a dilemma in terms of treatment. Systemic or catheter directed thrombolysis was not suitable options given the bleed and recent neurosurgery. Surgical management would be extremely high-risk due anesthetic and operative risks. A joint decision was made to proceed with mechanical thrombectomy under conscious sedation. The rationale for selecting this treatment option was that thrombectomy would relieve the IVC obstruction therefore reducing venous hypertension and subsequently preventing further venous back bleeding. This would also allow increased return to the heart thus alleviating the obstructive shock. Complications of mechanical thrombectomy included vessel rupture/damage, pulmonary emboli and failure.

A 24 Fr DrySeal sheath (Gore) was inserted into the right common femoral vein (CFV) using ultrasound guidance. A total of 2500 units of heparin was administered during the procedure. The T24 FlowTriever system (Inari) was used for thrombectomy. A total of 6 aspirations were performed with excellent thrombus clearance and restoration of lower body venous return. There was immediate systolic blood pressure improvement from 90 to 150 mmHg. Post thrombectomy venograms demonstrated a patent IVC with prompt passage of contrast to the heart. No active contrast extravasation was seen to suggest an active bleed. After flow was re-established, a decision was made to leave the IVCF in situ and retrieve it at a later date due to an increased risk of clot propagation to the lungs. Closure of the CFV access site was achieved with a combination of manual compression and a purse string suture. Figure 3 highlights imaging from the procedure and a CT performed the following day. Hematology advice was sought regarding post procedural anticoagulation. A joint decision was made to continue split treatment heparin dosing based on patient weight as per our local protocols. The rationale being that the bleed occurred secondary to venous hypertension which had now been treated thus changing the risk/benefit ratio. The patient was felt to be more at risk of rethrombosis rather than a further bleed.

Outcome/follow-up

The patient made a good recovery from this episode and was discharged from hospital 4 days later. Following 6 weeks of anticoagulation with split treatment dose Heparin, they successfully underwent removal of the IVCF. A CT performed 5 months following thrombectomy confirmed a patent IVC and resolution of the hematoma (see Figure 4). Unfortunately, the patient died 5 months later due to progressive metastatic disease.

Discussion

Complete occlusive thrombosis of IVCF resulting in obstructive shock is a rare complication. This alongside a concurrent retroperitoneal hematoma is even rarer. A comprehensive literature search of electronic databases (PubMed, Google Scholar) was performed using the keywords “IVC Filter,” “IVC Thrombosis,” “Hemorrhage” and “Mechanical thrombectomy” and demonstrated 3 relevant case reports where authors faced concurrent bleeding and IVCF related thrombosis.

The first is by Misono.4 The author describes a case of an IVCF-related thrombosis and subsequent caval rupture following initiation of therapeutic anticoagulation. Their patient had a long-term IVCF and symptoms of abdominal pain and bilateral lower limb swelling. Hypotension only occurred post-initiation of therapeutic anticoagulation rather than on presentation as seen in our case. The author proceeded with large bore mechanical thrombectomy using the 24 Fr FlowTriever System (Inari Medical) however they subsequently performed venoplasty across the filter and deployed an Abre self-expanding stent (Medtronic). It was their opinion that the long-term filter resulted in stenosis of the IVC which led to thrombosis resulting in the initial presentation. The author attributed the hemorrhage secondary to anticoagulation and increased venous back pressure. Unlike Misono, our patient had a much shorter IVCF indwelling period and did not have an IVC stenosis. We can hypothesize that in both cases occlusion of the IVC alongside anticoagulation predisposed patients to an increased bleeding risk due to higher venous back pressures and hypercoagulable blood.

The second relevant case is by Tseng et al5 The authors report that their patient presented with an IVCF-related thrombosis and a retroperitoneal hematoma. They were admitted with collapse and required cardiopulmonary resuscitation. CT confirmed a lumbar vein feeding into the hematoma and non-occlusive clots extending from the filter to the common iliac veins. Instead of opting for primary mechanical thrombectomy the authors performed embolization of the bleeding lumbar vein with immediate positive results. Despite stabilizing the hemoglobin and improving the hemodynamic status their patient reported of scrotal pain and left lower limb swelling 2 days following the embolization. This required treatment with mechanical thrombectomy using the AngioVac cannula and system (AngioDynamica). Whilst they retrieved significant clot, the long-term infrarenal filter was unretrievable. Their patient was commenced on Enoxaparin with a view to starting Warfarin; however, the patient was lost to follow up. A key difference between our cases is that Tseng et al’s patient suffered from hemorrhagic shock and had imaging evidence of a bleeding point. In contrast our patient was thought to be suffering from obstructive shock despite the presence of a hematoma. This was supported by the stable hemoglobin and size of hematoma on serial imaging. Between Tseng et al, our case and Misono we can hypothesize that IVCF-related thrombosis can lead to obstructive/hemorrhagic shock or a degree of both. We found that in cases of obstructive shock patients were less responsive to fluid resuscitation or vasopressor support as compared with hemorrhagic shock. This feature can help tailor whether to focus treatment on removing the obstruction (eg, vessel thrombosis) or stopping the bleed (eg, bleeding vessel).

Finally, Ludwig et al6 reported a case of concurrent IVC rupture and IVCF-related thrombosis. Their patient presented with severe atraumatic back pain, lower limb swelling and hematuria on a background of IVCF (Trap Ease, Cordis) insertion 3.5 years prior for recurrent DVTs. Ultrasound, CT and MR imaging confirmed a retroperitoneal hematoma and thrombosis of the IVC extending to the femoral veins. Despite a mild reduction in hemoglobin the authors opted for conservative treatment in view of hemodynamic stability (stopped concurrent anticoagulation). CT imaging at 3 and 4 months revealed a shrinking hematoma. They report that the lower limb swelling whilst initially worsened improved with medical management (furosemide). They successfully restarted the patient on therapeutic anticoagulation 4 months following admission. This case differs from all 3 by means of management. Whereas all our patients share similar pathologies the range in severity highlights the unpredictability of IVCF-related complications. This emphasizes the need to investigate, diagnose and treat based on clinical history, disease progression and imaging findings.

It is interesting to note that most cases in current literature report long-term IVCFs. We find that only Gao et al7 and our case demonstrate a short history from IVCF insertion to presentation with acute obstructive shock.

A retrospective review by Li et al10 investigated the outcomes of 15 acutely unwell patients with symptomatic IVCF-related thrombosis undergoing large bore mechanical thrombectomy. They reported a technical success rate of 73% with 100% improvement in symptoms. The 30-day mortality rate was 13% (1 intra-procedural death due to PE and 1 post-operatively due to malignancy complications). Similar to our case Li et al’s review demonstrates that large bore mechanical thrombectomy is a safe and effective option for treating occlusive IVCF-related thrombosis.

In conclusion, IVCF-related thrombosis and concurrent hemorrhage are rare occurrences presenting both a diagnostic and treatment challenge. We hope our report provides an example of how this can be successfully diagnosed and treated.
