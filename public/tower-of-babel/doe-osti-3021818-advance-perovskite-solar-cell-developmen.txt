EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The goal of this SETO project (DE-EE0010238, Advance perovskite solar cell development and
stability using in-line electrochemical methodologies) is to develop and demonstrate a
disposable, non-destructive electrochemical sensor prototype for i) reactive defect
characterization in lead halide perovskite solar cell layers during in-line manufacturing (i.e., roll-
to-roll) and ii) assessing quality, stability, and durability of device layers under stressors (i.e.,
humidity, light). This simple analytical tool will simultaneously offer a fast, automated, and
reliable solution for in-line quality control, enabling the emerging industry to measure, correct,
and control uniformity, quality, stability, durability and surface characteristics during processing,
which is needed to characterize and study suboptimal solar cells and modules to realize the SETO
goal of the levelized cost of electricity of $0.03/kWh by 2030.
State-of-the-art multi-model characterization capabilities, and novel inline sensors, are needed
to monitor formation of (electro)chemically relevant defects, resulting from intermolecular
interactions, interfacial and physical processes (drying, crystal formation, surface tension
changes) during high-throughput manufacturing operations across length and time scales, and to
characterize device-transport layer interactions. Critically, defect concentrations (< 1017 cm-3) are
typically well below levels of detection for many spectroscopic, electrical, and computational
approaches, with often limit spatial and temporal resolution. Electrochemical approaches offer
advantages of simplicity, low cost, and speed, while simultaneously being amenable to
characterizing electronic structure and defect quantification, even at multiple length scales.
Additionally, these approaches allow for uniquely high sensitivities and can readily distinguish
changes in semiconductor materials under stressors, including temperature, humidity, light, O2
and voltage bias. Ultimately, characterization and understanding of the (electro)chemical
mechanisms of defect generation and propagation, especially as perovskite layers are forming,
will be the enabling key to commercializing perovskite solar cell technologies.
This SETO project has made significant advances to define an innovative electrochemical probe
to be used as a quality control sensor for in-line solution-processed semiconductor films and
stacks. (1) We demonstrated that spectroelectrochemical technologies replace the need for
photoelectron spectroscopy for band edge characterization; spectroelectrochemical approaches
demonstrated increased energy resolution (10 mV or less), more reliable and less expensive
alternative. (2) The addition of redox probes to the electrolyte allows for the accurate and reliable
assessment of defect distributions in semiconductor films. Including reactive ion species with the
electrolytes defines electrochemical reactions within semiconductor films and provides new
insights into ion migration. (3) Benchmarking the electrochemical technique with perovskite
stacks provided by the well-known and established perovskite company First Solar Inc. improves
credibility for quality control. (4) Standard photoluminescence gives us information about the
bulk perovskite photophysics mainly influenced by its crystalline features and grain boundaries,
while the electrochemistry focuses on surface chemistry that assess important interfaces within
PSC stacks. (5) Measuring the valence band bleach and acceptor defect distributions in discretely
sampled perovskite films as a function of exposure time under aging conditions has shown
evidence of predicting their long-term stability in a device setup.

1 Background
  To achieve the current U.S. goal of 100% clean electricity by 2035, the electricity supply
produced from solar energy needs to increase from 4% today to 40%.1 Only the creation of a
robust domestic solar manufacturing supply chain will meet the demand for solar modules and
components to reach $0.02 kWh-1 levelized cost of electricity (LCEO) by 2030. At present,
perovskite-based solar technologies are the most competitive commercial technology, but
specific challenges must be addressed regarding efficiency, stability and decomposition,
manufacturing, recyclability as well as validation and bankability.2 As forecasted by the Energy
Materials Corporation, 4 GW/yr of electricity can be generated from perovskite solar modules
manufactured using only one roll-to-roll (R2R) assembly line in which all layers are consecutively
deposited.3 This manufacturing approach will make the cost of producing solar modules less than
half the cost per watt of existing solar panel manufacturing methods, which will be critical to
achieve this ambitious LCEO goal. Therefore, the key is to invest in efficient, stable and recyclable
PV technologies that have improved form factors compared to conventional platforms.
  Organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite materials (ABX3, where A = Cs+, CH3NH3+ (MA), or HC(NH2)2+
(FA); B = Pb2+ or Sn2+; and X = Cl-, Br-, or I-) exhibit outstanding optoelectronic properties, including
high optical absorption, high carrier mobility, long diffusion lengths, but can, unlike traditional
semiconductors, be solution processed at moderate annealing temperatures, making them
amenable to large-area, inexpensive, and fast manufacturing on flexible substrates. Despite the
rapid increase in perovskite solar cell (PSC) efficiencies, issues concerning high speed
manufacturing at scale, hysteresis in J-V behavior, mechanical, thermal, and chemical stability,
and environmental stressors limit service lifetimes and their practical use. 4 The main barrier for
their commercial viability is the characterization and removal of unintentionally formed reactive
defects during manufacturing that limit performance and long-term stability.5 Large-scale
manufacturing necessitates continuous quality control to monitor for any small changes in
material properties that ultimately lead to suboptimal performance, especially at long times
under stress. State-of-the-art multi-model characterization capabilities are needed to monitor
formation of (electro)chemically defects, resulting from intermolecular interactions, interfacial
and physical processes (drying, crystal formation, surface tension changes) during high-
throughput manufacturing operations across length and time scales, and to characterize device-
transport layer interactions. The lack of in-line manufacturing characterization methods that are
able to detect these defects that limit long-term stability and durability of materials-to-module
components are a critical part to reach commercialization of this perovskite technology.6 Existing
approaches are limited to optical probes, including ellipsometry and photoluminescence (PL),
which do not correlate well with device performance and stability.7 Ideal in-line sensors should
assess defects in individual materials and in multilayer device stacks in short response times (i.e.
faster response times provide higher test coverage),8 under operating condition, and provide
information about electronic structure (i.e. defect energetics), chemical identification (i.e. which
are the most mobile and reactive species), and quantification.
  To address these needs, we have developed an extremely sensitive electrochemical operando
approach to accurately characterize electronic energy levels and quantify reactive defects using
a “stick and peel” solid-state sensor.9 The approach leverages the advantages of (spectro)-

risk, high reward opportunity.

2 Project Objectives
The objective of this project is to develop and demonstrate a simple, easily implemented and
scalable, non-destructive prototype electrochemical probe for the: i) characterization of reactive
defects in PSC layers during in-line manufacturing (e.g., roll-to-roll coating) and ii) assessing
quality, stability, and durability of device layers under operando conditions and with added
external stressors (i.e., humidity, light, and temperature), by monitoring time-dependent
changes to reactive defect speciation, energetics and concentrations, for low defect density PALs.
 Since this is a SETO SIPS project, there will only be a single budget period in which we have
defined three distinct tasks and four milestones.
Objective 1: Demonstrate characterization of valence and conduction band energetics from
onsets of electrochemical activity, followed by the defect quantification with the probe
technology, using a high-sensitivity differential pulse voltametric (DPV) approach, in device-
relevant low defect density PALs (e.g., a methyl-ammonium (MA)-free, Cs0.17FA0.83Pb(Br0.17I0.83)3.
Reactive defect quantification near valence and near conduction bands will be demonstrated for
a series of PALs and for samples prepared independently by a partner (Milestone 1).
Objective 2: Correlate the stability of optimized PALs by measuring time-dependent changes in
reactive defect density and energetics using accelerated aging conditions by integrating the
electrochemical probe into an environmental chamber (Milestone 2).
Objective 3: Demonstrate full in-line processing compatibility of the electrochemical probe to
test the quality (spatial heterogeneity) of large area Cs0.17FA0.83Pb(Br0.17I0.83)3 films by multi-point
probing and the air stability of these PALs by time-dependent probing under “environmental
stress” and/or annealing conditions (Milestone 3).
  The end goal of this project will be to deliver a prototype, in-line electrochemical probe
technology that can be easily integrated into quality control systems for large-area thin films in
the high-throughput manufacturing of perovskite-based opto-electronic/energy conversion
platforms marked (Milestone 4).

3 Project Results and Discussion
 3.1 Band Edge Quantification
 Reproducible quantification of the transport energetics (i.e., transport levels for holes and
electrons on the energy band diagram) of Cs0.17FA0.83Pb(Br0.17I0.83)3 (or Cs0.17FA0.83) perovskite
films is done using a spectroelectrochemical approach that tracks the bleach of the optical gap
when holes or electrons are injected into valence or conduction band states. Electrochemically,
we achieve this by using the three-electrode cell described in Figure 2A, which uses a solid
electrolyte without a redox probe, as previously reported.9, 10, 17 Briefly, the three-electrode cell
shown in Figure 2A consists of a working electrode (WE) material stack, perovskite/ITO/glass,
interfaced with a solid electrolyte/ionic liquid (SE/IL) “top contact” comprised of a chemically
inert polymer with low oxygen transport (poly(vinylidene ﬂuoride) - PVDF) and 1-ethyl-3-
methylimidazolium bis(triﬂuoromethyl-sulfonyl)imide ([EMIM][TFSI]) as an ionic liquid (IL) that
equilibrates electrochemically with the perovskite interface,18 an Ag/AgCl reference electrode
(RE) embedded in the SE/IL with a reference potential of 0.1 V (versus the Fc/Fc + redox couple,
ca. -4.8 eV vs local vacuum level)19-21 and a transparent ITO counter electrode (CE). This
calibration of the reference electrode is crucial for benchmarking the perovskite on the energy
scale under device-relevant bias. The potential-dependent UV-vis spectra are shown in Figure 2B
referenced versus the pristine perovskite film.

 3.2 Acceptor Defect Quantification
  To measure acceptor states near the VBM in Cs.17FA.83, we use a similar electrochemical cell as
displayed in Figure 1A and add a hole capturing Fc/Fc+ probe to the SE/IL as described in Figures
5A and 5B. Like for the spectroelectrochemical bleach experiment, the oxidative window is
systematically increased, but now instead of the absorption, the current is monitored. 17
However, simple cyclic voltammetry methods are not able to detect acceptor defect states in the
Cs.17FA.83 film below a concentration threshold of 1016 cm−3. To improve the sensitivity that
enables us to measure lower acceptor defect concentrations, the non-Faradaic background
needs to be decreased and energy resolution improved; in many electrochemical systems,
contributions from these non-Faradaic effects are effectively reduced using potential pulse
approaches such as differential pulse voltammetry (DPV), which is one of the most common
pulsed waveforms. The procedure consists of small voltage pulses superimposed on a linear
voltage ramp, with flexibility in choice of pulse magnitude (amplitude), pulse width (duration)
and time between pulses (pulse period).17

 Acceptor defect characterization requires the extraction of electrons within these defect states
during oxidation and subsequently reducing in the return sweep, with good energetic resolution.
Thus, the approach consists of a linear potential ramp with linear sweep voltammetry (or LSV)
that oxidizes the defects and subsequently run DPV on the return sweep (shown in Figure 5C and
D) to reduce acceptor defects back using a more sensitive process with better energy resolution
for detection. As a note, we emphasize that in the DPV scan, as in the LSV sweep, mainly surface
defects participate in the detected reversible processes. Running LSV/DPV scans on the Cs.17FA.83
films as described in literature17 cannot distinguish Faradaic defect peaks within the band gap,
which is demonstrated in Figure 5C, while if we surpass the VBM, we characterize other Faradaic
contributions linked to the reverse Fc+ reduction in Figure 5D. As a last step, we went back to our
initial input parameters for the DPV scan and found that an increase in the input amplitude of
the pulse from 0.05 mV to 0.08 mV further improves the sensitivity.
  In Figure 6A, we focus on the Faradaic contributions in the band gap by looking at the recorded
reverse scans by systematically increasing to oxidative window to the VBM. When comparing
Figure 5C and 6A, we see that a Faradaic defect peak appears when increasing the input
amplitude. For defect quantification, a Fc calibration procedure measures the change in
electrochemical response as a function of known Fc concentrations in the SE/IL. Based on this

 3.3 Donor Defect Quantification
  Quantification of donor defect concentrations within the Cs.17FA.83 perovskite necessitates a
redox molecule that captures electrons near the conduction band and stabilizes the reductive
region of the perovskite material. We selected 1,4-benzoquinone (or BQ) that forms a radical
anion at a reduction potential of -0.25 V vs Ag/AgCl (-4.45 eV vs local vacuum) and reduces further
to the dianion at -0.7 V vs Ag/AgCl (-4.0 eV vs local vacuum) as shown in Figure 7A. With the
formation of the dianion at cathodic potentials at the CBM, electron transfer to the BQ probe is
preferred over reduction of the lead cation. The reversibility, diffusivity, and stability of the BQ.-

 For the detection of donor defects, the approach is to first reduce defect states using a linear
potential ramp with LSV and subsequently run DPV on the return sweep for the oxidation
process. LSV/DPV scans on the Cs.17FA.83 perovskite films following the parameters described in
literature17 distinguish a Faradaic defect peak recorded in the reverse scan within the band gap,
which is demonstrated in Figure 7B. For donor defect quantification, we must run a BQ
calibration procedure to measure the change in electrochemical response as a function of known
BQ concentrations. We determine an average donor defect concentration of 5.06 × 10 16 ± 4.8 ×
1016 cm−3 at an energy level of -3.87 eV vs local vacuum (-0.83 V vs Ag/AgCl) below the conduction
band edge measured at -3.78 eV vs local vacuum (-0.92 vs Ag/AgCl) in Figure 7C. Looking back at

the defect peak in Figure 3, we notice the perovskite film spectroelectrochemically bleaches at a
similar energy level (i.e. -3.86 eV). Additionally, we note that the BQ reduction starts around -0.9

  In conclusion, we have demonstrated that the combination of a linear sweep and differential
pulse voltametric (LSV/DPV) approach has provided sensitivities to characterize defect densities
in the range of 1017 to 1015 cm-3 and even lower by considering increased pulse amplitudes. These
long electrochemical experiments use consecutive increasing potential windows to measure
electrochemical responses across a wide potential range to get an idea of the complete band
diagram of the semiconductor material. However, defect quantification does not necessarily
require scanning across the complete band diagram, instead running one LSV/DPV scan where
the potential window is kept within the band gap of the material is enough to measure defect
concentrations. Each CV scan takes about 1 minute per scan, while each LSV/DPV scan takes
about 5 minutes, but the process can be accelerated by changing the initial parameters with the
caveat that energy resolution will be potentially decreased.

 3.4 Connect Defect Quantification to Photoluminescence
  To make a connection between photoluminescence (PL) performance and band diagrams
measured using voltametric LSV/DPV scans without raising the issue of reproducibility across
perovskite films, we decided to go back to results obtained using the triple cation Cs.05FA.79MA.16

 3.5 Defect Origin Assessment
  Halide-specific defects were quantified in Figure 9 via the inclusion of [EMIM+][I-] and
[EMIM+][Br-] (i.e., iodides and bromides) and compared directly with hole-capturing Fc probe.
We demonstrate the oxidation current density close to the VBM (i.e., 0.76 V vs Ag/AgCl) increases
when the halide ions are present, which is hypothesized to be associated with localized defect
states. These defect states are assumed to be due to the oxidation of the specific halide species
from within the bulk of the perovskite film at the ITO/perovskite interface due to ion migration
processes. In other words, when halides are added, a halide migratory pathway is created
through the bulk of the perovskite film to the ITO substrate, which results in electrochemical
halide activity in the band gap in the reverse scans (i.e., C1,I, C2,I and C1,Br peaks). In the case of
iodides, we generate more acceptor defect states (i.e., peak C1,I), which indicates that these
acceptor defects are chemically identified as iodide vacancies or interstitials. The C2,I peak around
-0.3 V vs Ag/AgCl has previously been identified as the reduction of triiodide which was formed
in the forward scan.23 Similarly, The C2,Br peak describes the reduction of tribromide formed in

Page 16 of 27

the forward scan.23 This complementary experiment using the intentional addition of halide
probes confirms that redox-active defects in perovskites are predominantly associated with
iodide anions.

 3.7 Reactive Defect Progression to Define Perovskite Stability
  To measure defect progression in LHP films using an environmental chamber, we need to
reduce risk by running control experiments to ensure the approach is feasible. We check the long-
term stability of the electrolyte, redox probe and perovskite in an inert environment. The
summary of these results explains why this is a valid approach for assessment of long-term
stability in perovskite materials.
 3.7.1 Check the validity of the electrochemical approach
  The stability of the probe-filled electrolyte is measured in an inert environment using a
glass/ITO/SE/IL + Fc/Ag/AgCl RE/ITO/glass device stack by running repeated cyclic voltametric
scans, which is also used to characterize stability in the field of organic electronics. 27 The
electrolyte remains active for more than 1 week with a very small shift in the reference potential
of 60 mV as seen in Figure 10A and B. In Figure 10C and D, we notice the reaction is reversible,
but not all Fc+ is continuously reduced back, which explains why we see the drop in absolute
current.28 As a result, a calibration curve or fresh electrolytes are recommended for each
measurement.

Figure 10. The (A) first and (B) last five CVs measured using a device stack of glass/ITO/SE/IL + Fc/Ag/AgCl
RE/ITO/glass kept in an inert atmosphere with (C) the reference potential and (D) current recorded vs time.

  Finally, five cyclic voltammograms within the band gap of the Cs0.17FA0.83 are recorded every 30
minutes using a device stack of glass/ITO/Cs0.17FA0.83/SE/IL + Fc/Ag/AgCl RE/ITO/glass that is kept
in an inert environment for 20 hours as shown in Figure 11A and B. The Cs0.17FA0.83 films are stable
under inert conditions when a potential range within the band gap is used, while wider potential

ranges induce bias-induced ion migration. We want to emphasize that none of the measurements
currently used to detect defect states in perovskite materials using the electrochemical probe
have a duration exceeding 6 hours.

which we know can be reproducibly made within the same batch, that have been corroded for
increased exposure times (going from 15 mins to 120 mins) under 60 °C and 5%RH in an
environmental chamber.

4 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
  We have achieved significant accomplishments under this SETO project to establish a non-
destructive prototype electrochemical probe technology as the potential for an in-line quality
control tool for high-throughput manufacturing of perovskite solar cells.
 • Electrochemical technology replaces photoelectron spectroscopy for band edge
 characterization. The VBM of Cs0.17FA0.83Pb(Br0.17I0.83)3 through spectroelectrochemical
 bleaching is -5.65 eV, which is in good agreement with ultraviolet photoelectron
 spectroscopy (UPS) which demonstrates a VBM at -5.82 eV. The CBM from
 spectroelectrochemical bleaching is -3.78 eV and cannot be measured using inverse
 photoemission spectroscopy (IPES) due to degradation issues. We estimate an electronic
 gap of 1.87 eV, which is slightly larger than the optical gap of 1.65 eV.

 • Electrochemical probe for reliable defect characterization. We demonstrated the
 reproducible characterization of donor (and acceptor) defect states in LHP films. Acceptor
 defect concentrations in Cs0.17FA0.83 perovskite films range between 1014-1015 cm-3 (10x
 lower than Cs.05FA.79MA.16 and 1000x lower than MAPbI3. Donor defect concentrations
 range between 1016 -1017 cm-3 (same as Cs.05FA.79MA.16). To measure lower defect
 concentrations, we have established the use of an LSV/DPV approach with increased
 input amplitude to improve the sensitivity of our methodology.
 • Electrochemical detection of ion migration. By adding iodide anions to the SE/IL, the
 acceptor defect density increases, confirming acceptor defects are explained by the
 presence of iodide vacancies or interstitials in the perovskite. Electrochemically, a halide
 migratory pathway is created through the perovskite bulk towards the underlying ITO
 substrate resulting in increased halide redox activity.
 • Better than standard PL characterization. Lifetime variation across perovskite films
 confirms the large standard deviations on defect concentrations, which means that
 reproducibly manufacturing LHP films through solution processing remains an issue.
 However, PL remains a bulk property influenced by mainly crystalline features within the
 perovskite material, while the band gap electrochemistry focuses mostly on surface
 chemistry. It is this surface chemistry that defines the interfaces within PSC stacks, which
 remains the most critical issue in solar cell manufacturing.
 • Benchmark electrochemical probe as a quality control system. The electrochemical
 approach has been benchmarked against device-relevant PAL stacks provided by First
 Solar Inc. to reproducibly quantify acceptor defects. We were also able to study the
 influence of processing methodologies and the addition of transport and blocking layers
 to improve device performance.
 • Define the stability and durability of perovskite films. Using repeated cyclic voltametric
 scans, we show that the probe-filled SE/IL electrolyte is chemically stable over a week and
 that even perovskite films are stable under inert conditions when using a potential
 window within the band gap. Measuring the valence band bleach and acceptor defect

Page 23 of 27

 distributions in discretely sampled LHP films as a function of exposure time under aging
 conditions allows us to predict their long-term stability.
 • Develop capability to measure heterogeneity in perovskite films. We demonstrate the
 automatic detection of defect densities on large samples to define heterogeneity and
 assess quality in perovskite films.

6 Path Forward
  This simple and novel electrochemical characterization methodology opens the possibility to
investigate the stability and assess the quality of new materials under different aging conditions.
 • Defect quantification of perovskites in tandems. We need a holistic interface
 characterization approach to greatly reduce development costs to device manufacturers
 of perovskite-based tandems. The idea is to extend this approach to study the stability in
 Pb-Sn narrow (NBG; <1.2 eV) and Pb-based wide bandgap (WBG; >1.65 eV) metal halide
 perovskite absorbers to accelerate tandem cell development and optimization. This could
 be combined with a focus on additive engineering to improve the intrinsic stability within
 the narrow bandgap subcell. Slight compositional changes have indeed important
 consequences for the electronic structure and long-term stability. The idea is to extend
 the performance metrics to PL lifetime and shift, absorption, and defect density.
 • Study defect progression under select aging conditions. Knowing that the choice of aging
 conditions changes the degradation speed of perovskite active layers and device
 structures, this electrochemical approach can be useful to measure and define defect
 progression in perovskite films and stacks. Specific aging conditions can be studied to
 define components that are more detrimental to the perovskite material.
 • Very versatile methodology to be employed in other fields of research. We posit that
 this approach could characterize a much broader range of semiconductor materials,
 including thin films of organic and inorganic semiconductors, semiconductor and metal
 quantum dot thin films, and material blends and partial or full device stacks, where the
 SE/IL functions as a removable top contact that rapidly electronically equilibrates. This
 characterization platform will be crucial for in-line (scalable process) assessment and
 improvement of perovskite optoelectronic and energy conversion device stabilities and
 durabilities, which remain the primary impediment to commercialization and widespread
 market penetration of these technologies.
