Nanoscopic Observation of Structural Changes After First‐Firing Behavior in W/GeTe/W Ovonic Threshold Switches Using AFM Tip–Based Local Biasing
Authors: Young‐Min Kim, Su‐Bong Lee, Siwon Park, Jong‐Souk Yeo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAn Ovonic threshold switch (OTS) is a promising selector device for three‐dimensional crossbar memory arrays due to its simple two‐terminal architecture and unique electrical properties. Despite extensive studies, the switching mechanisms of OTS devices under an electric field (EF) and thermal driving forces remain ambiguous. In this work, a local biasing approach using atomic force microscopy is employed to spatially confine the switching region in Te‐rich Ge‐Te‐based OTS devices. EF simulations are used to evaluate stable switching in two types of local biasing systems. Following local switching, transmission electron microscopy reveals nanocrystalline formation at the interface between the top electrode and the switching layer, providing insights into structural changes and the first‐firing (FF) phenomenon. This interfacial crystallization enhances local conductivity and reduces the effective thickness of the amorphous layer from 32 to 24 nm, lowering the voltage required for subsequent threshold switching to 1.27 V relative to the 1.72 V for the initial FF behavior. The study demonstrates the feasibility of directly observing switching at the nanometer scale in targeted regions, offering a clear interpretation of the underlying mechanism of OTS switching and highlighting the critical role of the crystalline/amorphous interface in facilitating localized electron transport.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of artificial intelligence technologies has generated vast volumes of data, approaching several hundred zettabytes [1], thereby necessitating the development of highly integrated memory devices. Phase change materials (PCMs) are promising candidates for next‐generation non‐volatile memory and neuromorphic computing because they enable rapid and reversible switching between crystalline and amorphous states [2]. To overcome the spatial limitations of conventional memory devices, three‐dimensional cross‐point architectures (3D XPoint) have been developed to achieve high memory density [3]. However, the nanometer‐scale spacing between adjacent cells can cause unwanted leakage currents, leading to read and write errors [4]. To suppress such sneak currents, several types of selectors have been explored, including Schottky diodes, metal–insulator transition devices [5], Ovonic threshold switch (OTS) selectors [6], and electrochemical‐metallization‐based threshold switches [7]. Among these, OTS selectors have attracted significant attention due to their excellent compatibility with PCM‐based memory architectures and their potential for high‐density integration. The electrical nonlinearity of the OTS was first reported in the 1960s by Stanford R. Ovshinsky [8]. This phenomenon involves a reversible transition between a high‐resistance state (HRS) and a low‐resistance state (LRS) once the applied voltage exceeds a characteristic threshold voltage (V th). Although extensively studied, the underlying physical mechanism remains incompletely understood. Several models have been proposed, including electronic excitation through electron tunneling [9], thermally assisted formation of metastable conductive filaments or local structural rearrangements [10], and other electric field (EF)‐driven switching processes [11, 12]. Chalcogenide thin‐film‐based OTS selectors typically feature amorphous switching layers characterized by broken symmetry, point defects, and chemical impurities. These heterogeneities result in a random distribution of trap sites within the device, leading to stochastic switching events across different regions [13]. Consequently, accurately locating the active switching region in conventional OTS structures is challenging. This lack of spatial localization complicates the direct observation of structural or electronic changes during switching, thereby limiting a comprehensive understanding of the OTS mechanism. Recent efforts have explored OTS devices with nanoparticle structures to spatially confine the switching region, enabling more localized investigation of fundamental conduction behavior [14].

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) employs a nanometer‐scale probing tip that can apply localized voltage to the sample surface. In addition, various AFM‐based modules enable the analysis of multiple surface properties, facilitating detailed investigation of switching‐induced changes [15, 16, 17]. This approach allows the random switching behavior associated with the amorphous state to be confined and analyzed within a defined region. In this study, a Te‐rich Ge‐Te (GT) film was used as the switching layer. An optimal structure for local biasing was designed using EF distribution simulations. Current mapping with AFM confirmed that no unexpected structural or electrical changes occurred outside the targeted switching region. To minimize excessive local EF enhancement, a thin metal layer (∼20 nm) was introduced between the AFM tip and the switching layer, resulting in an EF distribution comparable to that produced by planar electrodes. Notably, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed the formation of a crystalline structure at the interface between the top electrode (TE) and the switching layer at the locally biased point. This interfacial crystallization effectively reduces the thickness of the amorphous switching layer, thereby lowering the voltage required to induce switching. First‐principles calculations further revealed that the charge density is redistributed at the crystalline/amorphous interface. This interfacial charge redistribution facilitates electron transport, thereby lowering the energy required to trigger switching. These findings suggest that the observed interfacial phenomena may provide important insights into the first‐firing (FF) process that occurs during the initial switching of OTS devices.

Results and Discussion

Simulation of Electric Field Distribution Under Various Local Bias Conditions

The general architecture of OTS devices is based on a metal–insulator–metal (MIM) sandwich structure. Typically, the chalcogenide‐based OTS thin film is deposited by a co‐sputtering process (Figure 1a), which allows for the tuning of the electrical properties through precise compositional control. OTS selectors are integrated into 3D XPoint memory arrays to suppress leakage currents through unselected cells. The amorphous chalcogenide layer of an OTS selector contains various types of randomly distributed structural defects. This randomness alters the electronic structure [18] and introduces inhomogeneities in the electrical properties of the film. Additionally, as shown in Figure S1, the diffusion or penetration of electrode materials into the soft amorphous switching layer can lead to non‐uniformity in the effective thickness of the conductive path. The protrusion phenomenon of metal material in the MIM structure is commonly reported and detrimentally affects device reliability and performance [19]. This phenomenon occurs stochastically within the switching layer, making it difficult to predict the formation and location of the conductive path. As a result, it becomes challenging to control or precisely localize the current paths formed during switching in conventional symmetric MIM structures. To address this, an asymmetric MIM structure was designed to enable localized voltage application using an AFM with a nanometer‐scale probing tip (Figure 1b). However, this configuration also induces significant mechanical and electrical stress within the thin film due to the strong EF concentration beneath the AFM tip.

To evaluate the influence of the asymmetric MIM structure on the EF distribution, the configuration shown in Figure 1c was designed for EF simulations under various local biasing conditions. COMSOL simulations were performed to provide a qualitative assessment of the EF distribution in the HRS before the FF process, rather than the EF distribution change during the switching operation. Furthermore, steady‐state electrostatic calculations were performed with thermal coupling intentionally excluded to confirm only the EF distribution according to voltage application. The 3D geometry of the MIM structure was obtained by revolving a simplified two‐dimensional cross‐section. Two structural configurations were considered: one in which the AFM tip acts directly as the TE, and another in which the AFM tip is coupled with a thin metal layer. The thickness of the thin metal layer between the AFM tip and the switching layer was set to 20 nm. Since the objective of this simulation was to investigate the effect of geometric parameters, simulations were performed in a conductivity range (10−5–103 S/m) including the reported parameters of Te‐based materials [18] and the experimentally derived conductivity values (σ ≈ 1.77 × 10− 3–6.37 × 10− 2 S/m) estimated from the effective contact radius (5–30 nm) of the AFM tip used in this study. Under all conductivity conditions, the normalized EF distribution profiles maintained morphological consistency. This confirms that the spatial pattern of the EF distribution is governed primarily by the structural geometry of the AFM tip and thin W layer rather than the absolute conductivity of the switching layer (Figure S2). In addition, Figure S3 presents the EF distribution for various W interlayer configurations. The simulation results exhibit that, despite the introduction of a thin W layer, the EF remains concentrated beneath the AFM tip. Furthermore, the localized EF strength can be modulated to a level comparable to that of devices with a blanket electrode through varying the thickness of the W layer.

Figure 2a illustrates the local biasing system without a TE between the AFM tip and the switching layer. EF distributions were simulated at distances of 0.1, 10.0, and 19.9 nm from the AFM tip within the switching layer. The resulting EF profiles beneath the tip are shown in Figure 2b. As expected, the EF is most concentrated directly beneath the AFM tip compared to the surrounding regions. Notably, the EF intensity becomes significantly higher near the edges of the tip at the 0.1 nm layer. This enhancement arises because the AFM tip has a much smaller radius of curvature (on the order of nanometers) than a conventional blanket electrode (typically micrometers to millimeters), which increases the local charge density at the tip apex. Consequently, the nanoscale contact area amplifies the EF beneath the AFM tip due to edge effects [20, 21, 22]. The three‐phase boundary among the switching layer, AFM tip, and air further enhances the EF near the air interface due to the lower permittivity of air compared to the surrounding materials. Figure 2c shows the EF strength along a line from the center to the edge of each layer. In layers located 10.0 and 19.9 nm from the AFM tip, the maximum EF is observed at the center region. In contrast, at the 0.1 nm layer, the EF at the tip edge is approximately 36% higher than at the center. Such localized field enhancement can induce switching in unintended regions or generate excessive stress, making precise control of the switching location challenging.

Figure 2d shows the simulated EF distribution for the local biasing system incorporating a thin TE between the AFM tip and the switching layer (hereafter referred to as the ‘combined electrode’). Unlike the previous structure without the planar TE, the EF tomography results in Figure 2e indicate a consistent EF distribution across all layers. In particular, the EF profile at the 0.1 nm layer beneath the AFM tip (Figure 2f) shows that the maximum EF occurs at the center region. Although the EF difference between the center and edge is smaller than in the previous structure, it remains evident that the strongest EF is concentrated beneath the AFM tip. Furthermore, the EF magnitude under the tip can be influenced by the properties of the amorphous chalcogenide thin film. The amorphous structure of the chalcogenide thin film makes it susceptible to deformation under external stresses, such as mechanical pressure applied by the AFM tip, due to structural disorder [18]. These local deformations can induce inhomogeneities, such as protrusions, within the thin film. However, excessive deformation can lead to structural failure. In our experimental conditions, the contact force of ∼80 nN is used as the threshold condition for achieving stable and reproducible current signals without excessive structural changes. At this force level, structural deformation is estimated to be on the sub‐nm scale, which is insufficient to induce structural failure but may change the atomic configuration of the GT film. Since the structure and the morphology of amorphous thin films affect their electronic structure and charge trapping capacity [23], such structural rearrangement induced by AFM tip contact may contribute to the formation of preferential conductive channels by modifying the distance between traps within the GT films. These combined effects of mechanical and electrical interactions can cause topographic irregularities beneath the AFM tip during contact [24], which enhances the local EF and facilitates the observation of switching behavior in the targeted region.

Electrical Characterization and Structural Analysis of OTS Devices Under Local Bias using AFM Tip Electrode

EF distribution simulations confirmed that switching can be induced by concentrating the EF in a localized region under the designed local biasing systems. In Figure 3, the switching behavior and associated structural changes are experimentally analyzed using a local biasing system in which the AFM tip serves as the TE.

Electrical and structural analysis of the GT thin film after local biasing using an AFM tip electrode. (a) I–V curves of the thin film during the local biasing process. (b) Surface topography and current mapping after local biasing. (c) Cross‐sectional HRTEM image of the thin film at the locally biased region. (d) Cross‐sectional HRTEM image of the thin film at the non‐biased region. (e) Schematic of the structural changes after local switching.

Figure 3a shows a schematic of the AFM tip/GT/W local biasing system and the corresponding current–voltage (I–V) characteristics over three consecutive voltage sweeps (0 V → 3 V → 0 V). During the first direct current (DC) sweep, a rapid current increase occurs at approximately 1.73 V, indicating the switching ON behavior. Subsequent sweeps exhibit ohmic behavior, suggesting that a structural change in the GT film has induced a permanent transition to a high‐conductivity state. Under this condition, the calculated EF across the GT switching layer during the FF process reaches 85 MV/m, which exceeds the critical field (∼56 MV/m) required to induce crystallization in Te‐based phase‐change materials [25, 26]. The crystallization timescale for such phase‐change materials typically ranges from hundreds of nanoseconds to microseconds [27]. Because the DC sweep duration in AFM measurements is on the order of milliseconds, excessive energy is applied, leading to thermal effects such as Joule heating. Furthermore, the absence of an electrode layer to act as a heat sink can amplify the local thermal effects, contributing to localized crystallization of the switching layer. Changes in electrical characteristics under local biasing are also reflected in surface topography and current mapping, obtained via contact‐mode AFM scans over regions containing the locally biased point (Figure 3b). The scans were performed at 0.1 V to prevent unintentional switching. This voltage value was chosen to be much lower than V th based on electrical characteristics measured using a conventional probe station on an OTS device with the same composition (Figure S4). At the locally biased point, which exhibits ohmic behavior after DC sweeps, a rise in surface height is observed, accompanied by increased local conductivity. Such surface‐height changes during conductive AFM (C‐AFM) measurements in contact mode are commonly observed in chalcogenide thin films due to their mechanical softness [28].

To further investigate the effects of the local switching process, the vertical structure and material composition of the stacked thin films were analyzed using cross‐sectional high‐resolution TEM (HRTEM). In the biased region, nanocrystalline structures with d‐spacings of 0.387 and 0.296 nm were observed within the switching layer (Figure 3c). As shown in Table S1, these correspond to the (100) and (202) planes of the hexagonal and rhombohedral crystalline phases of c‐GT, which are commonly found in phase‐change materials [29]. The coexistence of different crystalline structures within the same thin film reflects the heterogeneous nature of the original amorphous layer. Consequently, the local electrical conductivity is higher than in the amorphous phase due to the presence of these crystalline domains. These TEM observations are consistent with the surface and conductivity changes detected in the previous AFM analysis. In contrast, the non‐biased region exhibits an amorphous thin film with a ring‐shaped fast Fourier transform pattern, as shown in Figure 3d. These observations indicate that applying voltage locally using only the AFM tip can generate an excessively strong internal EF, potentially causing permanent structural changes within the switching layer (Figure 3e). This behavior is consistent with the trends predicted by the previous EF distribution simulations.

Electrical Characterization and Structural Analysis of OTS Devices Under Local Bias using AFM Tip with Thin Metal Electrode

Another local biasing system, comprising an AFM tip combined with a thin W layer (AFM tip and W/GT/W), was experimentally investigated. The I–V curve in Figure 4a exhibits electrical behavior distinct from that observed in Figure 3a. Specifically, a V th shift from 1.72 to 1.27 V (∼26%) is observed during repeated local switching, without a transition to ohmic behavior. This trend resembles the linear I–V characteristics commonly observed in conventional device structures (Figure S5). Such changes are attributed to degradation phenomena arising from repetitive switching operations in many OTS devices [30, 31]. Correspondingly, the topography and current mapping after DC sweeps show negligible changes in the locally biased region (Figure 4b), indicating that the volatile characteristics of the OTS devices are well preserved in this local biasing system. Consistent with the previous EF distribution simulations, the designed system effectively controls the excessive EF enhancement associated with direct local biasing. Additionally, the thin W electrode, which has higher thermal conductivity than binary GT compounds [32, 33, 34], likely helps suppress unintended crystallization caused by local heating.

Panoramic cross‐sectional TEM images (Figure 4c) reveal internal changes in the GT thin film at the locally biased region. In Figure 4d, a nanostructure exhibiting coexistence of nanocrystalline and amorphous phases is observed in the switching layer induced by the local biasing system using the AFM tip with a thin W electrode. In contrast, amorphous regions are widely distributed throughout the GT thin film (Figure 4e). However, a nanocrystalline structure with 1 nm scale is also observed near the top W electrode, which is expected to be a crystalline structure generated during the deposition process rather than local biasing. Because the spatial distribution of the crystalline structure is not correlated with the switching region. The nanocrystalline structures in Figure 4d extend over approximately 8 nm of the switching layer near the TE and exhibit the same d‐spacings as those observed in Figure 3c. This partial crystallization reduces the thickness of the amorphous region, as shown in Figure 4f, thereby altering the device's electrical properties. Notably, V th of OTS devices is proportional to the switching layer thickness [35, 36, 37, 38]. Analysis of the TEM images confirms that the amorphous region thickness decreases from 32 to 24 nm (∼25%), which is consistent with the observed V th reduction. However, it is necessary to verify the changes by performing more DC sweeps to determine whether the crystalline structure is formed as a direct result of an immediate failure rather than FF effects. As shown in Figure S6, repetitive volatile OTS switching was confirmed during further DC sweeps, suggesting that the nanocrystalline structure at the interface is not formed by an immediate failure phenomenon. Failure is observed only after stress accumulation from successive DC sweeps, which is consistent with the gradual crystallization tendency of the remaining amorphous region due to accumulated stress from repetitive voltage application.

Many challenges in MIM‐based devices arise from unstable interfaces with high interfacial energy, where different materials or phases coexist [39, 40, 41]. When an external EF is applied across the dielectric layer in a MIM structure, potential energy is stored within the dielectric, which can induce changes in the atomic structure. This energy accumulation also occurs in the chalcogenide layer of OTS devices, which exhibits high electrical resistance prior to switching. The stored energy is distributed over the region where the EF is present, and its density can be quantified as energy per unit volume. For a linear dielectric material, the energy density (UEF ) associated with an applied EF is given by Equation (1) [42, 43]: (1) UEF=12ε0εrELocal2 here, εo is the vacuum permittivity (8.85 × 10−12 F/m), εr is the relative permittivity of the dielectric material, and ELocal is the magnitude of the locally applied EF. Detailed parameters of the local biasing system can be found in Table S2. For this local biasing system, the calculated energy density is approximately 266 J/cm3, which corresponds to 0.093 eV per atom—about four times higher than the thermal energy at 300 K (0.0259 eV) and comparable to the energy of atoms at temperatures exceeding the highest melting point [44] of the GT binary system. Despite this high energy density, the local biasing system is believed to maintain stable switching behavior by suppressing excessive temperature rise, facilitated by the thin W electrode, which has a thermal conductivity roughly three orders of magnitude higher than that of air.

Computational Analysis of Energy States at the Crystalline/Amorphous Interface

Understanding the behavior of the GT thin film in regions where crystalline and amorphous phases coexist is critical. To investigate this, first‐principles calculations were performed to determine the energy states within the thin film.

Figure 5a shows a GT supercell containing a crystalline/amorphous interface. A vacuum region is included in the supercell to avoid interactions between the periodic cells [45]. The projected density of states (PDOS) of Ge and Te in the interface region exhibits electronic states near the Fermi level (E F), indicating the formation of interfacial mid‐gap states (Figure 5b). These mid‐gap states are induced by structural defects, such as dangling bonds and vacancies [46]. Such structural discontinuities and bond distortions commonly occur at crystalline/amorphous interfaces and can contribute to the formation of localized intermediate mid‐gap states. Specifically, Te 5p orbitals dominate in these mid‐gap states. This indicates that electrons participating in the threshold switching process are likely to originate from Te 5p orbitals. Furthermore, the emergence of interfacial electronic states near the E F may provide energetically favorable pathways for carrier excitation during threshold switching operation. The upper image of Figure 5c presents the charge density difference at the crystalline/amorphous interface before and after applying an EF of 0.01 V/Å. In the visualization, yellow and blue isosurfaces represent regions of positive and negative charge differences, respectively. Notably, the yellow regions are concentrated near the crystalline/amorphous interface, indicating enhanced charge redistribution in this region under the applied EF. These interfacial characteristics are further confirmed through the planar average value (PAV) of electrostatic potential and charge density difference (Δρ) graph in the lower panel of Figure 5c. Rapid decreases in the PAV are observed at atomic positions due to the influence of atomic nuclei. In the crystalline region, the PAV shows periodic oscillations reflecting the ordered atomic arrangement, whereas the amorphous region exhibits irregular fluctuations. An interfacial region representing the transition between these two behaviors is observed within the range of approximately 53–68 Bohr. In addition, the Δρ profile exhibits oscillatory behavior near the interface, suggesting local electrostatic perturbation and electronic inhomogeneity in this region. These results indicate that the crystalline/amorphous interface provides preferential pathways for localized carrier transport and EF concentration, thereby contributing to the reduction of V th after FF operation.

The crystalline/amorphous interface in the GT thin film is expected to play a critical role in forming conductive pathways. Figure 5d–f schematically illustrate each state during the FF operation. The MIM structure commonly has two interfaces near the bottom and top electrodes, respectively. Following the deposition of TE on the GT film, the top surface of the GT switching layer is subjected to residual stress induced by high sputtering power (Figure S7). When W atoms with high energy are deposited on a soft chalcogenide film, the residual stress can be accumulated on the film surface. It is known that amorphous chalcogenides such as GT and GST alloys exhibit stress relaxation that affects electrical properties [47]. This instability is particularly pronounced near the top of the switching layer, as the metal electrode is deposited with high energy during sputtering. High DC sputtering power onto the GT film causes energetic deposition effects such as atomic peening [48], which can induce the formation of structural defects [49]. These stress‐induced defects lower the energy barrier for structural rearrangement; consequently, the energy generated during the FF process preferentially promotes nanocrystallization near the TE. In this context, the concentrated EF during localized switching provides the necessary energy to relax the unstable interface, thereby driving the transition toward nanocrystalline domains representing a lower free energy state compared to the kinetically stressed amorphous phase. After the removal of the local bias, these stable structures persist, resulting in the formation of a crystalline/amorphous interface. The interface exhibits locally concentrated charge density, creating a preferred pathway for current during subsequent switching operations. While this concentrated charge density provides a preferred pathway for current, it may also generate an internal EF that partially hinders the formation of a conductive channel. However, in the sub‐threshold regime, electron emission is typically governed by the Poole–Frenkel (PF) equation, which depends exponentially on the applied EF [50]. External EF‐driven PF emission rapidly dominates carrier transport, which may easily appear in the interfacial region where charge density is higher than in other regions. Furthermore, as the trap density increases in the PF equation, the current density also increases. Therefore, it is expected that relatively more traps caused by defects, such as grain boundary mismatches, will occur in the crystalline/amorphous interface layer compared to other homogeneous areas. This facilitates switching at voltages below the initial threshold, which likely contributes to the observed decrease in V th following the FF process.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates the structural changes in GT‐based OTS devices following the FF operation using a local biasing system. In conventional devices with symmetric electrodes, it is challenging to pinpoint the active switching region and observe the associated structural transformations. The combined electrode–induced local biasing architecture introduced here overcomes this limitation, enabling targeted analysis of structural changes after switching. The local biasing system was optimized through EF distribution simulations, with the optimized design incorporating a combined electrode consisting of an AFM tip and a thin ∼20 nm W layer. The energy accumulated in the GT switching layer during local biasing was estimated to be approximately 0.093 eV per atom, roughly four times greater than the thermal energy at 300 K.

After local switching, a nanocrystalline structure was observed at the interface between the top W electrode and the GT switching layer. This structural change near the top electrode is attributed to the relaxation of residual stress and defects induced by energetic deposition effects, which lowers the energy barrier required for structural rearrangement during the FF process. DFT calculations show that the presence of the crystalline/amorphous interface hosts mid‐gap states dominated by Te 5p orbitals. Notably, the Δρ is significantly enhanced at this interfacial region, indicating a high degree of charge redistribution when an external EF is applied. These interfacial states and the concentrated charge density provide energetically favorable pathways for electron transport. These results support the observed decrease in V th following the FF process and provide insights into the switching mechanism of OTS devices. Furthermore, our findings provide an analytical framework for understanding stochastic switching of amorphous thin‐film‐based OTS devices. By elucidating microscopic origins of structural changes resulting from switching behavior, this approach suggests new research directions for optimizing the reliability of devices as well as for engineering applications that require modulating stochasticity, such as a true random number generator [51]. Consequently, the analysis of the switching region using the local bias method is expected to be a powerful tool for elucidating the stochasticity in practical OTS devices based on observational results.

Experimental Section

Fabrication of GT‐Based OTS Devices

The tungsten (W) bottom electrode was deposited on a 100 nm SiO2 substrate by DC sputtering under a working pressure of 3 × 10−3 Torr and a target power of 300 W. An amorphous GT layer (∼20 nm) was deposited on the W layer using Ge (99.999%) and Te (99.999%) single targets via radio frequency co‐sputtering under a working pressure of 1 × 10−2 Torr and target powers of 25 and 15 W, respectively. To prevent crystallization of the chalcogenide thin film due to thermal stress during deposition, the GexTe1−x layer was deposited in five steps of 4 nm each. X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirmed that the composition of GexTe1‐x corresponds to x = 0.17 (Figure S8). Subsequently, a TE layer consisting of W was deposited under the same conditions as the bottom electrode deposition (DC sputtering, working pressure of 3 × 10−3 Torr, target power of 300 W) to fabricate the two‐terminal OTS devices. For general electrical characterization, the TE thickness was 100 nm, while a ∼20 nm TE was used for local biasing experiments. Prior to all deposition processes, the base pressure was confirmed to be 1 × 10−7 Torr. Device patterns were defined using maskless lithography (Nanosystem Solutions, Inc., UK) with AZ‐5214E photoresist, achieving a resolution of 1 µm.

Local Biasing System Optimization using Electric Field Simulation

The local biasing system was optimized by analyzing EF distributions for different biasing configurations using COMSOL Multiphysics v6.2 (COMSOL Inc., Burlington, USA) with the Electric Currents module. Steady‐state EF distributions were simulated using the following equations: (2) ∇·J=Q,J=σE+Je,E=−∇V.

In these equations, J is the current density, Q is the electric charge density, σ is the electrical conductivity, E is the EF, Je ​ is the current density from external sources, and V is the electric potential. To evaluate only the qualitative EF distribution, the EF distribution is simulated under 2 V conditions similar to the experimental V ff (∼1.7 V) without considering thermal effects.

Local Biasing System

The local biasing system was employed to precisely target the switching region within the chalcogenide thin film. Local voltage was applied using an XE‐100 atomic force microscope (Park Systems, Korea) equipped with a CDT‐NCHR conductive tip (Park Systems, Korea). Prior to applying the local voltage, the tip scanned the surface in contact mode to obtain topographic images. Simultaneously, current mapping was performed to characterize the electrical properties by measuring the current flow between the tip and the sample at a low bias of 0.1 V. Local biasing was conducted at a set point of ∼80 nN to minimize structural damage induced by physical contact.

Analysis of Crystalline Structure

The local switching region was identified using contact‐mode AFM. A thin cross‐sectional sample for HRTEM was prepared via focused ion beam milling. Structural analysis was performed using a JEM‐ARM 200F NEOARM (JEOL Ltd., Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV.

First‐Principles Calculation Simulation

First‐principles simulations were performed using the SIESTA package within the Materials Square platform. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange‐correlation functional was employed. The crystalline/amorphous GT supercell was constructed using 96 atoms. The amorphous GT structure was generated using a melt‐and‐quench procedure: melting from 300 to 1100 K over 50 ps, followed by quenching from 1100 to 300 K over 15 ps under NVT conditions (constant number of particles N, volume V, and temperature T) while maintaining a fixed crystalline GT region. A vacuum layer of 20 Å was applied along the z‐axis on both sides to avoid interactions between periodic images. The relaxation of the crystalline/amorphous GT cell was carried out with a force threshold of 0.02 eV/Å. The GT cell under an applied EF of 0.01 V/Å was also relaxed using a force threshold of 0.02 eV/Å. All relaxation processes applying EF were conducted using the ‘Slab.DipoleCorrection’ condition. Electronic structure calculations, including the DOS and local charge density difference, employed a 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k‐point mesh and a real‐space mesh cutoff of 300 Ry.
