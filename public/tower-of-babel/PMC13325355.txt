Lead-Free Sr3MCl3 (M = Sb, P) Perovskite Solar Cells: from First-Principles Calculations to Recombination-Aware Device Simulations
Authors: Chokri Saidi, Mourad Debbichi, Noureddine Chaaben
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, first-principles calculations were performed to explore structural, mechanical, electronic, carrier transport, and optical properties of strontium-based perovskites (Sr3MCl3 (M = Sb, P)) using density functional theory (DFT). These calculations reveal that both materials are thermodynamically and mechanically stable. Employing the GGA-PBE functional, they possess a direct-band-gap semiconductor behavior with an energy of 1.704 eV (Sr3SbCl3) and 1.677 eV (Sr3PCl3). The analysis of the density of states (DOS) further corroborates the semiconducting behavior. Carrier mobility calculations indicate that electron/hole mobilities of 89.15/110.52 cm2/V·s are achieved for Sr3SbCl3 and 137.16/100.60 cm2/V·s for Sr3PCl3. In the visible region, a light absorption coefficient above 105 cm–1 is reached for both materials, highlighting their suitability as an absorber layer (AL) in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). Considering band-to-band recombination (radiative and Auger), SCAPS-1D was used to conduct an inquiry into the photovoltaic performance of various devices, integrating different electron and hole transport layers (ETL/HTL): Ag/FTO/ETL/uniform-AL/HTL/Ni. Among all configurations examined in this study, the Ag/FTO/IGZO/uniform-AL/Cu2O/Ni architecture achieves the highest photovoltaic performance parameters, upon optimization of AL thickness, AL-doping concentration, AL-bulk and interface defect densities, radiative recombination coefficient, and series/shunt resistances. The Sr3SbCl3-based PSC (Device I) attains a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of ∼25.80%, with an open-circuit voltage (V OC) of 1.31 V, a short-circuit current density (J SC) of 21.82 mA/cm2, and a fill factor (FF) of 90.27%, whereas the Sr3PCl3-based PSC (Device II) achieves a PCE of approximatively 26.22%, with V OC = 1.28 V, J SC = 22.71 mA/cm2, and FF = 90.09%. Finally, replacing the single uniform-AL with a graded-Sr3Sb1–x P x Cl3 AL (Device III), adopting linear and parabolic graded physical parameters, does not demonstrate marked improvements in device performance. Consequently, this study positions Sr3MCl3 (M = Sb, P) perovskites as alternatives to lead-based ALs, which can constitute a suitable pathway for real-time experimentation of PSC.

Introduction

In conjunction with demographic expansion, remarkable industrialization has intensified global energy demand, leading to the depletion of natural resources, climate change, and significant strain on the environment. , Therefore, the transition toward renewable energy resources is becoming an international priority. Owing to its abundance, environmental friendless, and scalability, solar energy stands out among various renewable energy options including wind and hydro. , Solar cells have the ability to directly convert solar radiation into electrical energy, providing a promising, clean, and increasingly cost-effective solution. In this context, various solar cell technologies have been studied, starting with crystalline silicon (Si) solar cells, followed by thin film (CdTe, CdS, CIGS, CIS, CZTS), organic, multijunction, tandem, and dye-sensitized solar cells. Despite their excellent performance, the indirect band gap of Si, high and complex fabrication feasibility, the deficiency of indium (In), gallium (Ga), and tellurium (Te), and the toxicity of cadmium (Cd) limit further development. Addressing these limitations, extensive efforts have been focused on the development of new alternative materials that can be applied as absorber layers in solar cells. As a result, lead (Pb) halide perovskites with the APbX3 (A = Cs+, CH3NH3 +, CH­(NH2)2 + and X = I–, Br–, Cl–) molecular formula have emerged as revolutionary materials in solar energy owing to their exceptional optoelectronic and charge transport properties including broad and strong light absorption (>105 cm–1), tunable band gap, longer charge diffusion length (>1 μm), and high carrier mobility. , Since 2009, the PCE of lead-halide perovskite-based solar cells has increased from 3.8 to 27% today. , Nevertheless, PSCs often suffer from drawbacks such as issues of long-term stability with respect to temperature, light, humidity, and oxygen, as well as Pb toxicity, stimulating work in identifying alternative host perovskite materials. As such, there is strong interest in developing Pb-free perovskite materials that can absorb a broad spectral region of sunlight and then retain or increase this impressive PSC efficiency. − Inorganic Pb-free halide perovskites, with the A3MX3 general formula, where A stands for a larger size inorganic divalent cation, M for a smaller trivalent metallic cation, and X for a halogen anion, have been considered to address these needs. In fact, several candidates have been explored using DFT calculations, such as Ba3MX3 (M = N, P, As, Sb and X = F, Cl, Br, I), Mg3SbBr3, Mg3PI3, Sr3PI3, etc. − Specifically, these compounds exhibit a considerable absorption coefficient (>105 cm–1), thermodynamic stability, and an optimal band gap within the range of 0.8–2.0 eV, allowing PCEs surpassing 25%. − Thanks to these key specifications, the focus has shifted toward the design of a more efficient PSC device by integrating these compounds as either a single or a double AL between an ETL and an HTL.

Among these, because of its nontoxicity, eco-friendliness, earth abundance, and easy extraction, strontium (Sr)-based perovskites (Sr3MX3, where M = N, P, As, Sb, Bi; X = F, Br, I) stand out as affordable options in the A3MX3 category. According to the DFT calculations, they feature a cubic phase with the space group Pm3̅m (no. 221) and a direct band gap ranging from 1.15 to 1.908 eV using GGA-PBE (1.55 to 2.49 eV with HSE). − Using primarily SCAPS-1D, recent studies explored their capacity to serve as ALs in PSCs. In this regard, the combination of appropriate ETLs and HTLs was found to be the dominant factor in boosting the PCE of these emerging devices. According to recent findings by Ghosh et al., the SCAPS-1D studies on the Ag/FTO/SnS2/Sr3AsX3(X = F, Cl, Br)/Au configuration without HTLs led to a maximum PCE of 20.89%. These authors also investigated the incorporation of Sr3PI3 in the Al/FTO/SnS2/Sr3PI3/Ni structure and reported a high performance of 27.32%. Moreover, keeping the same ETL and without HTL, Harun-Or-Rashid’s and Rahman’s research teams explored a single PSC using Sr3SbI3 and Sr3NCl3 perovskites as ALs, achieving 26.93 and 18.11% as the maximum PCE, respectively. On the other hand, when employing MoO3 as an HTL and SnS2 as an ETL, the Sr3SbI3-based PSC attained an exceptional PCE of 33.25%, as demonstrated by Reza et al. Along the same lines, Gupta et al. reported a PCE of 23.55% by combining CuSCN as an HTL with SnS2 as an ETL in the FTO/ETL/Sr3NCl3/HTL/FB configuration. Furthermore, using other ETLs (TiO2, ZnO, WS2, etc.) and HTLs (V2O5, MASnBr3, CBTS, etc.), perovskite materials like Sr3SbI3, Sr3NCl3, and Sr3BiI3 were also assessed as a single AL to enhance the performance of PSC and achieve at most a PCE of 27.34%. − To further enhance the performance of PSC, these perovskite materials have also been incorporated as double ALs. In the study, using the FTO/SnS2/Ca3SbI3/Sr3SbI3/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au device structure, a PCE of 32.74% was achieved. Lately, Benahmedi et al. demonstrated that PCS, incorporating both Ca3BiI3 and Sr3BiI3, achieved a PCE of 28.37%. Moreover, a high PCE of 32.46% was reported by Reza et al. when combining Sr3PBr3 and Sr3NCl3 in the Al/FTO/IGZO/Sr3PBr3/Sr3NCl3/Cu2O/Au proposed device structure. Moreover, another efficient strategy has been widely reported to enhance the solar cell efficiency, which can be realized via the intentional introduction of a graded perovskite band gap configuration. This approach was selected based on its well-established advantages over nongraded configurations, including the creation of an internal built-in electric field that enhances carrier collection, the suppression of interfacial recombination, and improved energy level alignment at the absorber interfaces.

It has been reported that the integration of phosphorus (P) and antimony (Sb) allows for efficient, lead-free, and robust perovskite configurations. This has encouraged researchers to investigate the suitability of Sr3MCl3 (MP, Sb) as an AL in PSCs. Asadul Islam Shimul and collaborators explored the performance of Sr3PCl3-based PSC. Using ZnO as an ETL and various HTLs such as Cu2O, CBTS, MoO3, and CuI, their results underscore FTO/ZnO/Sr3PCl3/Cu2O/Ni as the best device configuration, producing a PCE of 26.67%. In addition, this research team analyzed indium gallium zinc oxide (IGZO) as an ETL in the proposed FTO/IGZO/Sr3PCl3/Cu2O/Ni structure, attaining a PCE of 26.66%. In a similar vein, Rahmana et al. examined, without an HTL, various ETLs including WS2, CdS, SnS2, and ZnS in order to optimize Sr3PCl3-based PSC. Among the tested configurations, Al/FTO/SnS2/Sr3PCl3/Au achieved a maximum PCE of 18.58%. On the other hand, Elboughdiri et al. conducted an in-depth analysis to evaluate the application of different ETLs (CdS, PCBM, SnS2, and ZnO) and without an HTL in Sr3SbCl3-based PSC. Their simulation outcomes revealed that the FTO/SnS2/Sr3SbCl3/Au-based device yielded the highest PCE of 16.63%. However, despite possessing noticeable performance approaching the Shockley–Queisser (SQ) limit of 28.64% for absorbers having a band gap of 1.7 eV, these SCAPS-1D simulations do not account for recombination losses, including radiative and Auger recombination. Saidarsan et al. reinforced this limitation by establishing that even a small change in the radiative recombination coefficient can have a significant impact on device performance. This emphasizes the requirement of incorporating realistic recombination coefficients as input parameters for SCAPS-1D simulations. In addition, despite efforts to assess the performance of Sr3MCl3(MP, Sb)-based PSC, to the best of our knowledge, involving both ELTs and HTLs at the same time has not been completely searched out yet through simulation. Considering these gaps, this motivated us to conduct additional research into properties of these lead-free perovskite materials and SCAPS-1D simulations by accounting for recombination losses.

Therefore, in the present study, using first-principles DFT calculations, the structural, mechanical, optical, and electrical properties of Sr3MCl3 (MP, Sb) perovskite materials are comprehensively investigated. Their band gap energy (Eg), effective density of states (Nc and Nv), effective masses, absorption coefficient (α), static dielectric permittivity (ε), and carrier mobilities (μe and μh) are determined. Unlike previous studies, the DFT-derived perovskite properties are used as input parameters to SCAPS-1D to simulate device performance by considering recombination losses. In conjunction with different ETLs (In2S3, SnS2, IGZO, CdS, WS2, ZnO) and various HTLs (Cu2O, CuO, CuI, MoO3, Spiro-MeOTAD, CBTS), Sr3MCl3 (MP, Sb) perovskites are integrated as single uniform-ALs (Devices I and II) or novel graded-Sr3Sb1–x P x Cl3 ALs (Device III) in Ag/FTO/ETL/AL/HTL/Ni adopting an n+–n–p–p+ device configuration. Subsequently, optimization of key parameters including AL thickness, AL-doping concentration, AL-bulk and interface defect densities, recombination rates, and series/shunt resistances is employed to enhance the efficiency of the designed PSC.

Computational Method

First-Principles DFT Calculations of the Perovskite Absorber Layer

The structural and optoelectronic properties of the perovskite absorber layer (AL) were investigated via first-principles DFT calculations, as implemented in the VASP. In this approach, the exchange–correlation energy was treated within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) framework using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional, while valence–core interactions were described using the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method. It is worth noting that these DFT calculations performed at the GGA-PBE level are well known to systematically underestimate the material band gap (Eg) relative to experimental values. More sophisticated approaches such as hybrid functionals (e.g., HSE06) or the many-body perturbation theory (GW) provide more accurate absolute band gap values, but at significantly higher computational costs. Consequently, the PBE-derived Eg values reported in this work should be regarded as lower bounds on the true optical band gaps, and emphasis is placed on the relative trends and variations rather than on absolute values. To ensure calculation accuracy, a plane-wave cutoff energy of 520 eV was applied. The Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid of the first Brillouin zone was sampled with 6 × 6 × 6 for structural relaxations and 10 × 10 × 10 for electronic property calculations. The atoms in the cell were relaxed until the total energy change was below 10–5 eV and residual forces on each atom were smaller than 0.01 eV/Å.

Devices Simulation via SCAPS-1D

Simulation Method

Device simulations were performed using a one-dimensional Solar Cell Capacitance Simulator, SCAPS-1D (version 3.3.12), developed by M. Burgelman et al. This program is a powerful numerical simulation tool that enables the in-depth understanding, design, and optimization of high-efficiency solar cells. In these equations, J n and J p represent the current densities of electrons and holes, respectively. D n and D p are the diffusion coefficients, while n and p denote electron and hole densities, respectively. The mobilities of electrons and holes are given by μ n and μp, respectively. The electrostatic potential is represented by ψ, and q denotes the electronic charge. Device Structure

In this study, the configuration of the perovskite-based solar cell (PSC), shown in Figure a, follows a conventional n + –n–p–p + design, which enhances charge separation and reduces recombination losses by creating multiple built-in electric fields at each junction. It consists of a sequence of layers arranged according to doping concentrations: an n +-type transparent conductive oxide (FTO) layer, an n-type electron transport layer (ETL), a p-type perovskite absorber layer (AL), and a p +-type hole transport layer (HTL). Silver (Ag) and nickel (Ni) were employed as the front and back metal contacts, respectively. The work functions (Φ) of Ag and Ni are 4.52 and 5.35 eV, respectively. In the Ag/FTO/ETL/AL/HTL/Ni device configuration, the ETL is selected from six common materials (In2S3, SnS2, IGZO, CdS, WS2, ZnO), while six other materials (Cu2O, CuO, CuI, MoO3, Spiro-MeOTAD, CBTS) are investigated to identify the most suitable HTL. The AL, positioned between the ETL and HTL, consists of a uniform Sr3SbCl3 perovskite (Device I), a uniform Sr3PCl3 perovskite (Device II), or a graded Sr3Sb1–x P x Cl3 perovskite (Device III). It is worth mentioning that the ETL and HTL materials were selected based on four key criteria: (i) widespread use in the SCAPS-1D simulation literature, (ii) appropriate band gap and band alignment with the Sr3M­(M = Sb, P)­Cl3 absorber layer, (iii) lead-free and nontoxic composition, and (iv) experimental availability and low fabrication costs. Subsequently, among the investigated ETL and HTL candidates, the optimal transport layers were determined through systematic calculation of the conduction band offset (CBO) at the ETL/absorber interface and the valence band offset (VBO) at the absorber/HTL interface.

(a) Schematic configuration of the proposed perovskite-based solar cell (PSC). (b) Working mechanism of the Ag/FTO/ETL/AL/HTL/Ni device. (c) Energy level alignment in the proposed devices with various ETLs and HTLs.

Figure b illustrates the operating mechanism of the PSC. Upon light absorption and exciton dissociation within the perovskite AL, the AL/ETL interface blocks holes while allowing electrons to flow toward the electrode. In contrast, the HTL/AL interface efficiently transports holes while restricting electron flows. The energy level alignment of each ETL, HTL, and perovskite AL is depicted in Figure c. The simulated devices are evaluated under standard AM 1.5G illumination (1 sun) with an incident power density of 1000 W·m–2, at room temperature (300 K), and a frequency of 1.0 × 106 Hz. This default setting frequency is relevant only for alternating current (AC) simulations and does not influence the J–V or EQE measurements. In line with the recommendations of Saidarsan et al., the device characteristics are analyzed by considering band-to-band recombination (radiative and Auger). Therefore, the total recombination rate in our SCAPS-1D simulation study is expressed as the combination of all three recombination mechanisms (eq ), contributing simultaneously to carrier losses: 6 Rtotal=Rradiative+RAuger+RSRH where radiative and Auger electron/hole band-to-band recombination are modeled through non-zero coefficients C r (cm3/s) and Cn /C p (cm6/s), respectively, and trap-assisted Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination is accounted for through the trap densities N t (cm–3) introduced in the bulk and at the interfaces, along with their associated capture cross sections (σ n/σp). For the FTO layer, ETL, and HTL, both C r and C p are set to zero, while for the perovskite absorber layer, they are set to 3.0 × 10–11 cm3·s–1 and 1.0 × 10–29 cm6·s–1, respectively. – The bulk and interface defects were modeled as single neutral deep-level states located at 0.6 eV above the valence band maximum (VBM). This energy level was specifically chosen to represent deep-level traps acting as dominant Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination centers within all layers and at the ETL/AL and AL/HTL heterointerfaces.

As external lumped parameters, the series resistance (R S) and shunt resistance (R sh) are set to 2 Ω·cm2 and 5000 Ω·cm2, respectively. Structural Properties

Analyzing the structural properties of a compound is an essential first step before exploring its other characteristics. For this purpose, the studied Sr3MCl3 (M = Sb, P) perovskite structures were structurally optimized to determine the lattice parameters corresponding to their lowest-energy and most stable configurations. Each unit cell contains seven atoms, with Sr cations located at 3c (0.5, 0, 0.5), P/Sb cations at 1b (0.5, 0.5, 0.5), and Cl anions at 3d (0, 0.5, 0). To quantify the structural robustness of Sr3MCl3 (M = Sb, P), the mechanical properties were assessed by computing the elastic constants from stress–strain relations, obtained using Hessian matrices along each Cartesian direction. Based on Voigt notation and cubic symmetry of the A3MX3 perovskite materials, only three independent elastic constants, C 11, C 12, and C 44, are required to describe their mechanical behavior. In this case, Born and coauthors , have reported the conditions of mechanical stability, which are defined as C11−C12>0,C11+2C12>0,C44>0

As Table shows, both compounds satisfy the prescribed Born’s stability conditions, demonstrating their significant mechanical stability. Furthermore, based on the computed elastic constants C ij , several mechanical parameters like bulk modulus (B), shear modulus (G), Young’s modulus (Y), Poisson’s ratio (ν), and Pugh’s ratio (B/G) were calculated for both compounds, as summarized in Table . These outcomes illustrate that the Sr3PCl3 compound possesses the highest values of B, Y, and G compared to Sr3SbCl3. This reveals that Sr3PCl3 is the stiffest material among the studied compounds, demonstrating its greatest resistance to both deformation and compression. The other critical mechanical parameters for materials are ν and B/G. According to the standard criteria reported in the ref, both perovskite materials are brittle under ambient conditions because all of them have ν less than 0.26 and B/G below 1.75.

Electronic Properties

A comprehensive understanding of perovskite material’s electronic properties, like band structure and density of states (DOS), are essential for exploring its practical use as an AL in solar cells.

After structural optimization, the electronic band structures of Sr3PCl3 and Sr3SbCl3 materials were examined at ambient conditions along the Γ–X–M−Γ–R–X path of the first Brillouin zone (Figure a,b). The Fermi level (E f ) is set at 0 eV for precise band gap determination. It can be seen that, for both perovskite materials, the conduction band minimum (CBM) and valence band maximum (VBM) are located at the high-symmetry Γ point in the Brillouin zone, thereby validating their direct band gap nature. As illustrated in Table , the calculated band gap energies are 1.704 eV for Sr3SbCl3 and 1.677 eV for Sr3PCl3, highlighting the suitability of these materials for photovoltaic applications. Indeed, solar cells utilizing these perovskite materials as ALs are expected to achieve an efficiency limit of 29%, as reported by Shockley–Queisser. Additionally, replacing Sb with P slightly reduces the band gap due to the higher electronegativity of P, which raises the valence band edge. Subsequently, the total and partial density of states (TDOS and PDOS) are investigated to offer significant insights into the electronic configuration of materials and thus into individual atomic orbitals’ contribution to their band structures. Figure a,b, displays the calculated TDOS and PDOS spectra for Sr3MCl3 (M = Sb, P) perovskites over a limited energy range from −5 to +5 eV. The TDOS spectra indicate that both studied compounds have a semiconducting behavior.

Electronic band structure with DOS for (a) Sr3SbCl3 and (b) Sr3PCl3 perovskites.

Moreover, they display distinct peaks in both the valence and conduction bands, signifying a high density of electronic states available in these regions. In fact, Sr3PCl3 has an effective density of states of 1.940 × 1019 electrons/eV in the valence band (N V) and 6.563 × 1018 electrons/eV in the conduction band (N C), whereas Sr3SbCl3 has an N V of 1.680 × 1019 electrons/eV and an N C of 7.625 × 1018 electrons/eV. On the other hand, from the PDOS spectra for both perovskite materials, the conduction band predominantly originates from Sr d-orbitals, whereas the valence band is dominated by Sb/P p-orbitals, with minor contributions from Cl. These findings indicate a robust covalent interaction between Sr–Cl and Sr–Sb/P, and its significant role in the conduction process.

It has been reported that strain technology plays a crucial role in adjusting the band gap and thus enhancing visible light absorption, which are critical for improving photovoltaic performance. ,,− For this reason, the band gap energy changes of Sr3MCl3 (M = Sb, P) perovskites under uniaxial and biaxial strains were evaluated within the stable range from −4 to +4%, as shown in Figure a,b. It is worth noting that, after relaxation, the direct band gap nature remains stable throughout the applied strain range.

Calculated band gap energy as a function of applied uniaxial and biaxial strain for (a) Sr3SbCl3 and (b) Sr3PCl3.

As depicted in these figures, the band gap for both perovskite materials is significantly influenced in response to the amount of applied uniaxial and biaxial strains. It increases monotonically from compression to tension due to the shifting of the VBM and CBM from the Fermi level energy. , In fact, for both compounds, under uniaxial compressive strain (0 to −4%), the band gap energy can be reduced to a value of 1.45 eV, while it is reduced to 1.36 eV under biaxial compressive strain. According to Shockley and Queisser, for PSCs with these band gap values, a maximum PCE of 33% can be reached. As a result, the biggest band gap change for both perovskites is obtained when biaxial strain is applied. This suggests that biaxial strain is more effective than uniaxial strain for tuning the band gap.

Optical Properties

Investigating the optical properties of Sr3MCl3 (M = Sb, P) perovskites is an additional crucial step to assess their suitability for optoelectronic and photovoltaic applications. Consequently, frequency-dependent dielectric function ε(ω), absorption coefficient α­(ω), reflectivity R(ω), and energy loss function L(ω) were calculated.

According to Cohen’s and Ehrenreich’s equation, , the key optical parameter ε­(ω) contains two components: a real part ε1(ω) and imaginary part ε2(ω), as expressed by eq : 12 ε(ω)=ε1(ω)+iε2(ω)

The real part can be obtained through the application of the Kramers–Kronig transformation, while the imaginary part can be achieved using momentum matrix components. Figure a depicts the ε1(ω) and ε2(ω) of Sr3SbCl3 and Sr3PCl3 for energy range up to 11 eV. As we know, the real part ε1(ω) of the dielectric function is used to gain insights into the polarization and dispersion effects. The most significant factor is its static value, ε1(ω = 0), which represents the electronic component of the dielectric function. For Sr3SbCl3 and Sr3PCl3, the values of ε1(ω = 0) are 3.28 and 3.45, respectively. These findings are in accordance with Pen’s model, predicting that a smaller band gap energy leads to a larger ε1(ω = 0). Moreover, from this static value, ε1(ω) starts to increase until reaching its maximum value. After that, it declines, generating several peaks, which indicates a significant light absorption capacity within this spectral range. It is noted that a metallic behavior, identified by a negative ε1(ω) value, appears beyond 7.5 eV and 8.1 for Sr3SbCl3 and Sr3PCl3, respectively. On the other hand, the imaginary part of the dielectric function is known to offer insights into the electronic band gap of materials, such as the interband transition energy near the Fermi level. As depicted in this figure, the values of ε2(ω) reveal a wide absorption range, where the first prominent peaks are located at a photon energy of 2.71 eV for both materials. It is from this energy that charge carriers begin to migrate from the valence band to the conduction band. At energy values above 10 eV, both Sr3SbCl3 and Sr3PCl3 become transparent.

Another key parameter is the optical absorption coefficient α­(ω), which assesses the material’s capability to absorb light energy, giving information about its potential usefulness for efficient solar cells. According to Figure b, for both perovskites, α­(ω) shows comparable features to the imaginary part of the dielectric function. α­(ω) starts to increase from an energy value of 1.5 eV until reaching its first maximum (2 × 105 cm–1) at 2.75 eV, which is indispensable for solar cell applications.

The optical reflectivity R(ω) quantifies the amount of reflected light from a material surface when it is exposed to electromagnetic radiation such as visible light. As shown in Figure c, it is clear that both Sr3SbCl3 and Sr3PCl3 have lower reflectivity in the UV–visible range and can capture the most incident photon, approving their utilities as ALs in solar cells. Optimization of the Uniform Perovskite Absorber Layer (AL)

The AL plays a crucial role in determining the performance of PSCs by enhancing photon absorption and facilitating efficient photogeneration of electron–hole (e–h) pairs. Achieving high power conversion efficiency (PCE) requires the AL to have a suitable band gap, optimal thickness, appropriate doping density, and low bulk and interfacial defect densities. Therefore, optimizing the perovskite AL is essential to identify effective strategies for improving device performance.

Impact of Thickness

Barbé and Tietze Max reported that both the diffusion length and lifetime of photogenerated carriers strongly influence the efficiency of solar cells. To investigate this effect in our devices, the AL thickness was varied from 0.1 to 3 μm for Device I (Sr3SbCl3) and Device II (Sr3PCl3). Figure a–d illustrates the corresponding variations in PSC performance metrics as a function of AL thickness.

From Figure d, a significant efficiency improvement is observed as the AL thickness increases up to 1.2 μm. For Device I, which employs Sr3SbCl3 as the AL, the PCE rises from 10.50 to 24.50%, whereas Device II, using Sr3PCl3 as the AL, exhibits a slightly higher PCE, increasing from 11.00 to 25.00%. This enhancement for both devices can be attributed to improved light absorption, which increases the generation rate of charge carriers. As a result, J SC also rises (Figure b), directly contributing to higher power output. Beyond 1.2 μm, the PCE saturates due to additional loss mechanisms, such as enhanced recombination and reduced carrier collection efficiency. These mechanisms can explain the slight decrease in V OC for both devices, as shown in Figure a. Meanwhile, the FF remains almost unchanged (85.7–86.9%), which can be attributed to the fact that the charge transport and series resistance remain nearly unaffected despite the increase in absorber thickness (Figure c). Consequently, an AL thickness of 1.2 μm was selected as the ideal value for further optimization steps.

Impact of Doping and Bulk Defect Densities

After optimizing the AL thickness, the influence of both acceptor density N A (AL-NA) and bulk defect density N t (AL-Nt) in the absorber layer (AL) on the PSC performance metrics (V OC, J SC, FF, and PCE) was systematically investigated for Device I (Sr3SbCl3) and Device II (Sr3PCl3). Contour plots were generated by varying AL-NA and AL-Nt from 1011 to 1020 cm–3, as shown in Figure a–h. As illustrated, both devices follow similar trends under varying AL-NA and AL-Nt, with only minor differences in their optimal responses. As shown in Figure a,e, the maximum V OC values are nearly identical (1.31 V for Device I and 1.28 V for Device II), achieved when AL-NA < 1016 cm–3 and AL-Nt < 1015 cm–3. Nevertheless, with increasing AL-Nt, V OC gradually declines to 1.07 and 1.05 V, respectively. A similar behavior is observed for J SC (Figure b,f): it peaks at 21.70 mA/cm2 for Device I and 22.70 mA/cm2 for Device II when AL-NA ≈ 1016 cm–3 and AL-Nt < 1015 cm–3, beyond which it declines. FF shows a slightly different behavior (Figure c,g). For Device I, FF sustains a maximum of 86.24% at AL-NA ≈ 1017 cm–3 and AL-Nt < 1016 cm–3, whereas it reaches a comparable maximum (86.01%) for Device II, only when both AL-NA and AL-Nt remain below 1016 cm–3. Beyond these limits, FF drops sharply. Consequently, the PCE is maximized at 24.50% (Device I) and 25.01% (Device II) under optimal conditions of AL-NA = 1016 cm–3 and AL-Nt < 1015 cm–3, as illustrated in Figure d,h. Beyond these threshold values, two distinct recombination mechanisms become simultaneously dominant: (i) SRH recombination, driven by the sharp decrease in carrier lifetime with increasing AL-Nt, and (ii) Auger band-to-band recombination, which intensifies with increasing AL-NA. The coexistence and dominance of these two recombination mechanisms under these specific conditions result in significant performance degradation, − as evidenced by the sharp decline in J SC, V OC, FF, and PCE observed in our simulations. Therefore, the adjusted values of AL-NA (1016 cm–3) and AL-Nt (1014 cm–3) are identified as optimal parameters for maximizing device efficiencies.

Impact of Interface Defect Density

After determining the optimal thickness and the best combination of N A and N t in the absorber layer, attention is turned to the role of interfacial properties. The quality of the absorber layer/charge transport layer (AL/CTL) interfaces is a decisive factor for device operation, as it governs both the rectifying behavior of the junction and the overall performance of the PSC.

Since interface defect densities (Nt-interface) are difficult to quantify accurately in real devices, they are systematically varied over a wide range in the simulations. This approach allows for a qualitative assessment of the impact of interface quality on the photovoltaic parameters (V OC, J SC, FF, and PCE) and provides valuable insights into the sensitivity of both Devices I and II to interfacial imperfections. Accordingly, the Nt-interface values were changed from 109 cm–3 to 1015 cm–3 at an AL thickness of 1.2 μm for both Devices I and II. The simulated J–V characteristics and the corresponding PCE for both Devices I and II are presented in Figure a–f as a function of Nt-interface density.

Effect of interface defect density (Nt-interface) on PCE and J–V characteristics at the IGZO/AL and AL/Cu2O interfaces: (a–c) Device I and (d–f) Device II.

It can be seen from Figure a,d that, under front illumination, the AL/Cu2O interface exhibits much higher sensitivity to increasing defect density (109–1015 cm–3) compared to the IGZO/AL. At the AL/Cu2O interface, the PCE decreases substantially from 24.62 to 19.19% (a 22% reduction) for Device I and from 25.10 to 21.67% (a 13% reduction) for Device II. On the other hand, at the IGZO/AL interface, only a marginal decline is observed, with PCE dropping from 24.62 to 24.38% (≈1% reduction) for Device I and from 25.01 to 24.50% (≈2% reduction) for Device II. These results indicate that, for both devices, (i) the front interface (IGZO/AL interface) exhibits a relatively low recombination rate of the photogenerated carriers compared to that at the rear interface (AL/Cu2O). Previous studies have reported that the ETL/perovskite interface has a greater impact on the device parameters than the perovskite/HTL interface. This has been ascribed to the direction of illumination rather than the interface nature. , Conversely, Omer et al. reported notable sensitivity of the AL/HTL interface in other perovskite devices employing SnO2 as an ETL and spiro-MeOTAD as an HTL, particularly when illumination is applied from the SnO2 side. This effect was attributed to asymmetry in the electric potential near the interfaces, leading to a higher potential drop at the SnO2 side compared to the opposite one. (ii) Device I is more sensitive to defects at the rear interface, whereas Device II demonstrates greater robustness, particularly at the AL/Cu2O junction. Since the only difference between the two devices lies in the AL, Sr3SbCl3 in Device I and Sr3PCl3 in Device II, this contrasting behavior can be attributed to the intrinsic physical properties of the absorber material, most notably the VBO with Cu2O (see Table ). The larger VBO in Device I (−0.254 eV) impedes hole transport and enhances recombination at the rear interface, while the smaller VBO in Device II (−0.190 eV) enables more efficient hole extraction and thereby ensures greater interfacial robustness. Moreover, as shown in Figure c,f, at the rear interface (AL/Cu2O), both V OC and J SC decrease with increasing Nt-interface. However, at the front interface (IGZO/AL), as shown in Figure b,e, these parameters remain nearly constant. This confirms that the characteristics of the AL/Cu2O interface are predominantly influenced by the defect density, significantly affecting device efficiency. Consequently, based on this analysis, an Nt-interface density of 1010 cm–3 at both IGZO/AL and AL/Cu2O interfaces appears to be optimal for further investigation, as it yields the best performance observed so far in both devices.

Impact of Radiative Recombination

Previous simulation studies have often neglected intrinsic recombination pathways, such as radiative and nonradiative Auger processes, even though these mechanisms are fundamental in defining the realistic efficiency limits of perovskite solar cells. In this work, we explicitly incorporate both effects to provide a more accurate assessment of device behavior under practical operating conditions. Specifically, we investigate how variations in the radiative recombination coefficient (C r) influence key photovoltaic parameters (V OC, J SC, FF, and PCE) while keeping the Auger electron/hole capture coefficients (C n /C p) at a low value of 1.0 × 10–29 cm6·s–1 for both devices. Hence, C r was varied from 10–15 to 10–1 cm3·s–1, and its impact on both devices’ performances is presented in Figure a–d. As shown in Figure a, all performance parameters for both devices remain essentially unchanged in the range from 10–15 to 10–13 cm3·s–1, with a slight decrease in V OC from 1.406 to 1.403 V for Device I and from 1.376 to 1.374 V for Device II. However, when C r is further increased to 10–9 cm3·s–1, a significant drop in V OC is observed, decreasing from 1.40 to 1.22 V for Device I and from 1.37 to 1.19 V for Device II. This behavior originates from the fact that an increase in C r directly enhances the thermal generation rate of carriers at equilibrium. According to the detailed balance principle, this leads to a higher rate of spontaneous photon emission, which manifests as an increase in the dark saturation current density (J 0), ultimately reducing V oc as expressed in eq : 15 VOC=nkBTqln⁡(JSCJ0+1) where kBTq designates the thermal voltage, while n represents the diode ideality factor. Noting that n = 1 corresponds to dominant radiative (band-to-band) recombination, n = 2 indicates dominant SRH trap-assisted recombination, and intermediate values reflect mixed recombination mechanisms.

Dependence of (a) V OC, (b) J SC, (c) FF, and (d) PCEs of both devices on the radiative recombination coefficient (C r).

As illustrated in Figure d, the decrease in VOC is accompanied by a sharp decline in PCE, from 26.54 to 22.63% for Device I and from 27.47 to 22.98% for Device II. Meanwhile, J SC and FF are only weakly affected, as shown in Figure b,c. These results highlight the necessity of selecting a reliable Cr coefficient from the literature to ensure physically realistic simulations. At very low defect densities and Cr values, the overall recombination rate becomes unrealistically small, which can artificially boost device performance beyond the Shockley–Queisser (SQ) limit. To avoid such artifacts, a value of 1.0 × 10–11 cm3·s–1 was adopted for both perovskite absorber layers in this study.

Effect of Series and Shunt Resistances

The series resistance (R S), defined as the sum of the resistances of the functional layers (ETL, AL, and HTL) and the front and back metallic contacts in the solar cell configuration, is a key parameter limiting the PCE. In our investigation, R S was varied from 0 to 6 Ω·cm2 for both Devices I and II, while the shunt resistance (R sh) was fixed at 5 × 103 Ω·cm2 throughout the analysis (see Figure S2a–d). Figure S2d,c shows that V OC and J SC remain nearly constant as R S increases. Among the two devices, Device I exhibits the highest V OC and lowest J SC, compared to Device II, which displays the opposite trend. By contrast, Figure S2b,a reveals that an increase in R S significantly reduces both FF and PCE. These findings agree with previous studies, , which have shown that elevated R S impedes current flow and consequently degrades overall solar cell performance. Therefore, minimizing R S is essential to mitigate its detrimental effects on FF and PCE, ultimately improving device efficiency.

Another key parameter influencing PCE is the shunt resistance (R sh), which originates primarily from point defects and grain boundary defects in the AL film, as well as from mismatches at AL/CTL interfaces. These imperfections serve as primary pathways for charge recombination in PSCs, thereby contributing to efficiency losses. In this study, the impact of R sh was systematically evaluated over the range from 101 to 107 Ω·cm2 while keeping R S constant at 0.1 Ω·cm2 for both optimized Devices I and II, as depicted in Figure S2e–h. As shown in Figure S2h, V OC increases with R sh up to approximately 102 Ω·cm2 and then stabilizes with minimal change beyond 103 Ω cm2. The J SC, presented in Figure S2g, exhibits a negligible change across the entire R sh range for both Devices I and II. On the other hand, PCE and FF (Figure S2e–f) improve markedly as R sh increases up to 103 Ω cm2, after which both parameters plateau. These results highlight the importance of maximizing R sh and minimizing R S to achieve optimal device PSC performance. Thus, R S and R sh were fixed at 0.1 Ω cm2 and 106 Ω·cm2, respectively, in the optimized device configurations.

Hence, the efficiency increases with P concentration, reaching its maximum for PSC with P-rich compositions (x∼1). The graded absorber did not improve the PCE compared to the uniform devices; in fact, the highest PCE was observed for one of the uniform-layer devices. Nevertheless, a more systematic study of graded absorber profiles could identify conditions under which the performance of graded Sr3Sb1–x P x Cl3-based perovskite solar cells might be enhanced.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study presents an in-depth investigation of the structural, mechanical, and optoelectronic properties and photovoltaic performance of the cubic crystal system of Sr3MCl3 (MP, Sb)-based PSC using first-principles DFT calculations in combination with SCAPS-1D simulations. All DFT calculations were carried out using the VASP code within the GGA-PBE method. Based on the obtained results, both compounds are structurally, thermodynamically, and mechanically stable. It is also found that these perovskite materials have brittle behavior. Moreover, the electronic characteristics (band structure and DOS) analysis of Sr3MCl3 (MP, Sb) reveals that they are semiconductors with a direct band gap. Accordingly, the computed band gap energies are 1.704 eV for Sr3SbCl3 and 1.667 eV for Sr3PCl3. By calculating the carrier mobility, it is found that the electron/hole mobilities are 89.15/110.52 cm2/V·s for Sr3SbCl3 and 137.16/100.60 cm2/V·s for Sr3PCl3.They also possess high optical absorption in the visible region, exceeding 105 cm–1, underscoring their suitability to be integrated as absorber layers (ALs) in solar cells. Then, numerical simulations were performed to investigate the performance of PSCs utilizing Sr3MCl3 (MP, Sb) as single uniform-ALs (Devices I and II) or a graded-Sr3Sb1–x P x Cl3 AL (Device III) with various ETLs and HTLs. This simulation study leads to finding Ag/FTO/IGZO/uniform-AL/Cu2O/Ni device design as an optimal combination. The simultaneous variation of AL thickness, AL-doping concentration, and AL-bulk and interface defect densities yields a PCE of approximately 25.80% for Sr3SbCl3 and 26.22% for Sr3PCl3 at an optimal AL thickness of 1.2 μm. Furthermore, analyses of the influences of the radiative recombination coefficient (C r) and series (R S)/shunt (R sh) resistances emphasize the necessity of appropriate input parameters (C r = 10–11 cm3·s–1, R S = 0.1 Ω cm2, and R sh = 106 Ω·cm2). Finally, the use of graded-Sr3Sb1–x P x Cl3 AL-based PSC does not lead to a significant improvement in PCE. These findings offer valuable insights for future experimental and computational investigations, supporting the development of environmentally sustainable and efficient solar energy technologies.
