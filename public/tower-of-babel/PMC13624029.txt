Introduction

The General Linear Model (GLM) has been the cornerstone of fMRI analysis for over two decades (Friston et al., ). In the standard two-level approach, first-level models estimate activation parameters per run, and second-level models treat subject-averaged estimates as independent observations for population inference. This framework underpins most neuroimaging software (Cox, ; Friston et al., ; Woolrich et al., ).

However, the GLM has important limitations for hierarchical fMRI data. It treats repeated measurements as independent, potentially inflating false positive rates (Mumford & Nichols, ). The summary-statistics approach is asymptotically equivalent to a full mixed-effects model only when first-level variance estimates are homogeneous (Mumford & Nichols, ). Additionally, averaging across runs discards within-subject variability that may contain meaningful information about neural processes or individual differences (Garrett et al., ; Noble et al., ).

Linear Mixed-Effects (LME) models address these issues by explicitly modeling both fixed effects and random effects (Bates et al., ), offering proper correlation modeling, variance component estimation via the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and incorporation of run-level covariates. Several studies have explored LME for fMRI: Beckmann et al. () demonstrated improved sensitivity with hierarchical models in FSL; Mumford and Nichols () characterized when summary statistics are suboptimal; Chen et al. () developed 3dLME in AFNI. More recently, reproducibility studies have highlighted large individual differences in task-evoked reliability (Caceres et al., ; Elliott et al., ; Noble et al., ; Zuo & Xing, ).

Despite these important contributions, LME adoption in routine fMRI practice remains limited, and the obstacles are as much practical as statistical. Robust implementations exist in R, but Python-based neuroimaging workflows lack a validated, benchmarked LME component that can be dropped into an existing pipeline; the computational cost, convergence behaviour, and implementation fidelity of such a component have not been systematically documented. Two substantive questions are also unresolved: existing comparisons have not isolated the sources of divergence between GLM and LME, whether sensitivity differences arise from hierarchical variance decomposition, from run-level covariates, or from both, and whole-brain voxelwise ICC mapping has rarely been performed at the scale afforded by datasets such as the Human Connectome Project (HCP).

The primary contribution of this paper is a tool. We describe and release a validated, benchmarked, containerised LME workflow for volumetric task fMRI, and then use it as a case study to address the two substantive questions above. Methods

Dataset

We analyzed data from 143 unrelated healthy adults (aged 22–35) from the HCP S1200 release (Van Essen et al., ) (Table ). All participants provided informed consent under protocols approved by Washington University in St. Louis. Framewise displacement (FD) was computed per run following Power et al. (), converting the rotational parameters—stored in degrees in the HCP movement regressor files—to millimetres of displacement on a 50 mm sphere. Across the 286 runs, mean FD was 0.1604 ± 0.0606 mm (range 0.0717–0.5141 mm). Runs were excluded if >20% of frames exceeded FD > 0.5 mm; three runs met this criterion, belonging to two subjects (one in both runs, one in a single run). Statistical Analyses

First-Level GLM

For each run, a GLM was estimated with regressors for fearful faces and neutral shapes (convolved with canonical HRF), 12 motion regressors (three translations, three rotations, and their temporal derivatives), and cosine high-pass filtering (128 s cutoff), with AR(1) noise modeling. The resulting design matrix contains 16 columns; the pipeline verifies after fitting that all 12 motion regressors are present and aborts otherwise (Sect. “”). The contrast of interest was [fear > neut], yielding 283 beta maps.

Second-Level GLM

Beta maps were averaged across retained runs per subject, then entered into a one-sample t-test (n = 142). Maps were thresholded at FDR q < 0.05 (primary) and Z > 3.1 uncorrected (secondary). The second-level GLM was restricted to the same group mask as the LME (Sect. “”), so that both methods are evaluated over an identical voxel set with the same Benjamini–Hochberg denominator.

Reproducibility Infrastructure

Three properties of the released pipeline are design decisions rather than implementation details. Each addresses a failure mode that produces results which cannot be attributed to a stated method, and which typically leaves no trace in the output.

Explicit Backend Declaration

The fitting engine must be named by the caller (LME_BACKEND=statsmodels or pymer4); if it is unavailable the pipeline raises an error rather than substituting another, and the resolved engine is written to every output record.

Versioned Outputs

Each step takes a run tag appended to all output filenames, so a second execution cannot overwrite the first, and figures are generated from an identically tagged file set.

Environment Manifests

Each step writes a JSON manifest recording the resolved backend and package versions, R and lme4 versions where applicable, CPU model, memory, mask voxel count, and all analysis parameters.

Additionally, the first-level model verifies its own design matrix after fitting and aborts if the expected motion regressors are absent, and the group-level scripts log the Benjamini–Hochberg denominator, converting two classes of silent degradation into explicit failures.

Region-of-Interest Analysis

Twenty-one ROIs were selected from FSL’s Harvard-Oxford atlases (Desikan et al., ), comprising cortical emotion and cognitive regions and subcortical structures (see Table for the full list). Masks were thresholded at 25% probability and resampled to the 2 mm analysis grid. Mean beta values were extracted per subject and run (283 observations/ROI).

Computational Benchmark

Benchmarking used synthetic data (β = 0.3, ICC ≈ 0.2) with systematic variation of subjects (20–200) and voxels (100–10,000). Wall-clock time, memory, and convergence were measured. All analyses reported in this paper were executed on a single cloud instance with 12 Intel Xeon (Cascade Lake) vCPUs and 64 GB RAM under Ubuntu 24.04, with BLAS threading pinned to one thread per worker so that parallelism is controlled solely by the job scheduler.

Voxelwise Analysis

The group mask was computed from the unsmoothed beta maps, retaining voxels with finite non-zero values in ≥ 50% of runs, followed by binary opening, and was then intersected with the standard MNI152 brain mask. This yielded 215,265 voxels, shared by the GLM and both LME models. The mask must be derived before smoothing: applying the same criterion to smoothed maps yields 356,749 voxels, the excess being a halo of non-zero values spread past the cortical boundary by the 5 mm kernel. Since the number of tests is the Benjamini–Hochberg denominator, this affects the significance threshold and every reported percentage.

Both LME models were fit at each voxel, in chunks of 10,000 across 8 parallel workers with checkpointing. Quality control flagged non-converged, non-finite, or extreme values (|z| > 500, ICC ∉ [0,1]), and the number of finite p-values entering the FDR step is logged per execution. Variance components are bounded below at zero, so where no between-subject variance is estimable the ICC reaches the boundary and is reported as zero rather than indicating a convergence failure.

Simulation Analyses

Three simulations were conducted: (1) False positive rate: 1,000 null datasets confirmed adequate Type I error (GLM: 0.045 [0.034, 0.060]; LME: 0.052 [0.040, 0.068]). (2) RI vs. RS model comparison: random-intercept preferred by AIC in 95.4% of 196 comparisons; 3% RS singularity. (3) ICC bootstrap stability: with two runs, ICC SD = 0.081 (RMSE = 0.081), improving to 0.055 with three runs, 0.038 with five, and 0.026 with ten.

Group-Level Activation

Both methods identified a highly similar network including bilateral amygdala, anterior insula, inferior frontal gyrus, dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, and visual cortex (Fig. ). Over the shared mask of 215,265 voxels at FDR q < 0.05, the GLM identified 108,627 significant voxels against 74,004 for the full LME (ratio 1.47× ; Dice = 0.769); at Z > 3.1 the ratio was 1.58× . Discussion

Main Findings

The primary contribution of this work is a validated and benchmarked LME pipeline for volumetric task fMRI, together with the reproducibility properties described in Sect. “”. The case study demonstrates what the tool makes visible. By introducing an intermediate LME model that retains the hierarchical structure but omits the run covariate, we show that the commonly observed sensitivity difference between these frameworks is not an inherent consequence of mixed-effects modeling, but rather reflects the absorption of systematic run-related variance. This reframes the GLM–LME comparison: when no run-level covariates are modeled, the two approaches yield nearly identical results (r = 0.9942; Dice = 0.993), suggesting that previous reports of reduced LME sensitivity may have conflated two distinct effects. This decomposition is not specific to run effects, it generalises to any run-level covariate, and we suggest it as a routine diagnostic when comparing statistical frameworks. Additionally, the whole-brain ICC maps reveal a pronounced subcortical–cortical gradient in within-subject reliability, with implications for the design of brain–behavior correlation studies and biomarker development.

The complete set of case-study findings is: (1) under equalized FDR correction over a shared mask, the full LME identifies 1.47× fewer significant voxels, but an intermediate model without the run effect is almost indistinguishable from the GLM (Dice = 0.993), attributing the difference to the run covariate; (2) ROI effect sizes are concordant, with LME consistently lower (mean |d| 0.542 vs. 0.429); (3) within-subject variability is substantial (mean voxelwise ICC = 0.199) and spatially heterogeneous; (4) run effects are significant in 13/21 ROIs and all 13 survive adjustment for framewise displacement, with motion controlled at both analysis levels; (5) both methods show excellent stability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal}
 \usepackage{amsmath}

 \usepackage{amsfonts}
 \usepackage{amssymb}

The Role of the Run Covariate

A central insight is that the GLM-LME sensitivity difference is driven by the run fixed effect, not by hierarchical variance decomposition. The intermediate LME produced near-identical results to GLM (r = 0.9942), while the full LME with run effect showed moderate divergence (r = 0.9425). The run covariate absorbs systematic phase-encoding-related variance, reducing apparent activation extent. Researchers should consider whether run effects represent nuisance variance to control or are incidental to the research question. The pipeline supports this decision directly: fitting both specifications and comparing them is a single additional command, and the resulting difference quantifies how much of the apparent activation depends on run-level structure.

When to Use LME vs. GLM

GLM is preferable for single-run designs or rapid exploratory analysis. Our intermediate model analysis further suggests that when run-level covariates are not of interest, the GLM remains a practical and nearly equivalent choice, as hierarchical variance decomposition alone produces negligible divergence from standard summary statistics (r = 0.9942). LME is preferable when multiple runs are available and within-subject variability is of interest, when run-level covariates need explicit modeling, when conservative inference is prioritized, or when ICC-based reliability is relevant to research questions.

Generalizability and Extensibility

Since the pipeline is the primary contribution, its scope of application deserves explicit statement.

Other Tasks and Atlases

The workflow applies without modification to any task for which run-level contrast maps can be produced; the task name, condition labels, and contrast definition are configuration values rather than code. Likewise, the ROI module accepts any probabilistic or labelled atlas that can be resampled to the analysis grid, the Harvard-Oxford atlas and its 25% threshold being parameters. The emotion task was chosen here because its two opposite phase-encoding runs are the minimum structure that makes both the ICC and the run covariate identifiable.

Runs Per Subject

The specification generalises directly to more than two runs, and favourably: ICC precision improves from SD = 0.081 with two runs to 0.026 with ten, and with three or more the random-slope model becomes viable (Sect. “”). Subjects contributing a single run are retained and inform the fixed effects, but not the within-subject variance component.

External Datasets

Two dataset-specific conventions, neither of them statistical, are the most likely points of failure outside HCP: the file layout of the motion regressors, and the unit convention for rotational parameters (HCP stores degrees, fMRIPrep radians). The pipeline searches candidate paths and takes the rotation unit as an explicit argument, raising errors rather than proceeding with degraded inputs.

Surface and CIFTI Data

The implementation operates on volumetric NIfTI. Extending it to CIFTI requires replacing only the masking and map-writing layer, since the statistical core takes an observations-by-vertices matrix and is indifferent to spatial representation.

Computational Scaling

Cost is linear in voxels and approximately linear in subjects over the range tested (Fig. ), so the figures in Sect. “” extrapolate reasonably; memory is dominated by the observations-by-voxels matrix and controlled by chunk size.

Limitations

Key limitations include: (1) single contrast and dataset (HCP emotion task, 142 healthy adults)—the pipeline is designed for generality (Sect. “”) but has been validated empirically only here; (2) two runs per subject limit ICC precision and preclude random-slope models, and in the lowest-reliability regions the between-subject variance estimate reaches its lower boundary; (3) random-intercept-only specification; (4) univariate (voxel-by-voxel) analysis; (5) reliance on ICA-FIX without explicit physiological noise modeling; (6) spatial correlation may partially violate FDR independence assumptions; (7) stability analyses were ROI-level only; and (8) volumetric rather than surface-based analysis.

A further limitation is inherited from the acquisition protocol rather than from our design. The HCP acquires the LR run before the RL run for every subject, without counterbalancing. The “run effect” we report is therefore confounded with acquisition order, and may partly reflect practice, adaptation, or fatigue in addition to phase-encoding direction. Our data cannot separate these contributions; doing so would require a counterbalanced acquisition. This does not affect the decomposition result, the run covariate absorbs systematic run-level variance whatever its origin, but it does qualify the mechanistic interpretation, and the term is better read as “run/order effect”.

Conclusions

We have presented a validated, benchmarked, containerised pipeline for linear mixed-effects analysis of task fMRI. The implementation agrees with the R reference standard to the sixth decimal place, recovers known parameters across five variance regimes, converges on 100% of voxels, and completes a whole-brain analysis of 215,265 voxels in under six hours on twelve cores at 19× the per-voxel cost of the GLM. It declares its fitting backend explicitly, versions its outputs, and records the environment that produced every result.

Applied to the HCP emotion task, the pipeline shows that GLM and LME produce highly concordant activation patterns, and that the sensitivity difference between them is attributable to the run covariate rather than to hierarchical modeling: an intermediate model retaining the hierarchy but omitting the run term is almost indistinguishable from the GLM. LME provides additional information that summary statistics discard—spatially heterogeneous ICC maps and run effects that survive motion adjustment at two levels, at a computational cost that is manageable for whole-brain analysis. Rather than replacing the GLM, LME is a complement, most valuable when multiple runs are available and within-subject variability is of interest.

The decomposition strategy the pipeline implements, fitting full, intermediate, and standard models over an identical voxel set, generalises beyond run effects to any run-level covariate, and we offer it as a reusable diagnostic for attributing sensitivity differences between statistical approaches in neuroimaging.
