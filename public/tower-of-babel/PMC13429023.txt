The gastrointestinal microbiome and constituent short-chain fatty acids: a narrative review of an underexplored axis in acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Cate V. Cheney, Elyse C. Page, David T. Yeung, Deborah L. White
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) is an aggressive malignancy of lymphoid progenitors, and remains the most commonly diagnosed hematological cancer in the pediatric population. Although 5-year overall survival rates now exceed 90%, standard-of-care therapies are associated with substantial acute and long-term toxicities, underscoring the need for novel and supportive strategies that preserve treatment efficacy whilst reducing dose-limiting side effects. Increasing evidence linking the microbiome to therapeutic response and toxicity in oncology highlights the potential relevance of microbial-derived metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), in hematological malignancies. This review critically examines the emerging, yet limited, evidence supporting a role for SCFAs in ALL, integrating mechanistic insights from metabolic, immunological, and oncological studies to propose biologically plausible pathways of involvement. Specifically, we suggest how SCFAs may influence treatment response, mitigate therapy-related toxicity, reduce treatment-related morbidity and modulate early-life factors associated with ALL risk. Whilst direct ALL-specific evidence remains sparse, we propose that SCFAs represent a compelling and underexplored axis for microbiome-ALL research, and we aim to stimulate targeted experimental and clinical investigations to define their therapeutic potential.

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), resulting from clonal expansion of abnormal lymphoid progenitors, is the most commonly diagnosed hematological malignancy in children [1]. ALL may arise in primitive B or T-lymphocytes, with B-ALL accounting for 80–85% of ALL cases, and T-ALL 15–20% [2]. Symptoms at diagnosis include fatigue, anemia, bruising, and frequent infections due to marrow failure [3].

Advances in treatment have improved survival outcomes, with > 85% of pediatric ALL patients now achieving > 10-year overall survival; however, aggressive treatment has long-term side effects that significantly impair quality of life and survivorship [4]. Despite the high survival rates observed in pediatric patients, adolescents and young adults (AYA) diagnosed with ALL are recognized as a vulnerable population [5]. AYA outcomes are markedly lower (30–40%) compared to those observed in children, attributed to high rates of relapse [5]. Intense pediatric protocols show improved response rates and are well-tolerated in AYA (15–39-year-olds), however, 3-year overall survival is lower in adolescents receiving these treatments (74.9%) than in pediatric patients (93.4%) [5]. In the adult population, 5-year leukemia-free survival rates remain at ~ 30–50%, with high relapse rates and low chances of second remission [6].

Gastrointestinal Microbiota

The gut microbiome consists of over 2000 species of bacteria, fungi, viruses, and archaea [7]. A healthy microbiome is mainly composed of Bacteroidota and Bacillota (termed Bacteriodetes and Firmicutes respectively, prior to 2021) [8, 9]. These commensal phyla play several symbiotic roles, including metabolite synthesis, digestion, and immune development/maturation [10]. Approximately 5–20% of the body’s immune cells reside within the gastrointestinal tract [11, 12]. Within this subset, 70% of the gastrointestinal tract’s immune population is comprised of lymphocytes [11, 12]. Interestingly, the gut is also home to a large population of plasma antibody-producing cells, with approximately 70% of the body’s plasma cell population residing in the GI tract, making it the largest compartment of the body concerning humoral immunity [11, 12].

Despite increasing investigations, there is no clear consensus as to whether microbial colonization of infants begins in utero or is established during the process of birth [13]. It is known, however, that neonates possess a distinct microbial signature, with the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium longum particularly high upon receipt of breast milk [14]. Importantly, infants born by caesarean display a distinct reduction of Bacteroides, resulting in altered immune stimulatory potential [15]. Weaning onto solid food represents one of the most significant microbiome restructuring events, and is responsible for maturation of three major phyla; Bacillota, Bacteroidota and Actinomyetota [16]. Healthy colonization of the gut microbiome benefits children by priming the immune system, increasing tolerance to foreign antigens, and aiding in the conversion of indigestible dietary components to metabolites [17]. Differences in gastrointestinal taxa are noted in infants and young children who develop life-long diseases associated with a disordered immune response [e.g. asthma [18], type-1 diabetes [19] and eczema [20]]. This dysregulation appears to be associated with the SCFA-producing organism Bifidobacterium longum [21, 22]. After 4 years of age, the microbiome stabilizes with 68, 57 and 63 core microbiota members identified in age cohorts 5–17, 17–64 and 65 and older, respectively [23].

Converging with microbiome composition, immune system compartments fluctuate over the human lifespan. High-dimensional cytometry analysis of peripheral blood samples collected from birth to 75 years of age demonstrates immune system immaturity in early life [24]. Low levels of double-negative T cells, memory T and B cells and NK cells are observed in infants. The total B cell population is nearly 7% in newborns, increasing to 20% in 7- to 12-month-olds and remaining high until it declines to 8–13% at 5 years of age. Inversely, T cells are identified to be low in early childhood, reaching adult levels at around 6 years of age [24].

Alterations in the balance of commensal and pathogenic microbiota give rise to dysbiosis, a state encompassing a decrease in bacterial diversity accompanied by the overgrowth of opportunistic microbes [25]. Within the microbiome, alpha (α) diversity indicates the overall richness or evenness of a sample, fundamentally, the number and relative abundance of each taxon [26]. Beta (β) diversity refers to the variability in composition between samples collected from the same environment, such as different anatomical locations in the small intestine [26].

Established connections between the gastrointestinal microbiome and oncology

Microbial-mediated resistance

Evidence has shown that the microbiome can alter responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy [27–30]. The mechanisms of microbial-mediated resistance involve DNA repair, epigenetic regulation and suppression of apoptosis [27]. For instance, Streptomyces spp. and Raoultella planticola can deactivate the chemotherapeutic drug doxorubicin by deglycosylation under anaerobic conditions [28]. Murine studies have demonstrated reduced therapeutic effects of combination immunotherapy in antibiotic-treated and germ-free animals compared to controls with an intact commensal profile [27]. Commensal microbial species Akkermansia muciniphila, Fecalibacterium spp. and Bifidobacterium spp., have been observed to potentiate the anticancer effects of immunotherapy [29]. Negative clinical outcomes associated with antibiotic administration and immune checkpoint blockade therapy have been identified in renal, urothelial, and lung cancer [30]. Despite these associations, antibiotics may simply be a surrogate for immunocompromised patients who would have been non-responsive to therapy irrespective of antibiotic use.

Modulation of therapeutic toxicity

In addition to mechanisms of treatment resistance, microbiota composition also influences treatment outcomes through modulation of therapeutic toxicity [31, 32]. The healthy microbiome indirectly mediates the effects of methotrexate toxicity, with adverse reactions significantly increased after administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, implicating a protective effect of a healthy microbiome [31]. This response is mediated by Toll-Like Receptor 2 signaling, and toxicity is resolved upon restoration of the gut microbiota [31]. Conversely, bacterial enzymes β-glucuronidases are responsible for converting inactive forms of chemotherapy irinotecan, into their active form, leading to severe dose-limiting intestinal toxicities [32]. These examples of heterogeneous responses to therapy, as mediated by the gut microbiome composition, require a personalized approach to mitigate toxicity, considering chemotherapy/immunotherapy regimens and the need for microbial alteration to ensure minimal resistance and toxicity.

Microbiome therapeutics in oncology

Fecal microbiota transplant (FMT) is a method of directly modulating microbiota composition [33]. First successfully performed in 1958 for treating pseudomembranous colitis, FMT involves introducing healthy donor fecal suspension into the gastrointestinal tract of the recipient, aiding in the restoration of healthy gut microbiota [7]. Post-2013, FDA approval for the treatment of recurrent Clostridium difficile infections, FMT use has rapidly expanded. With alterations in microbiota composition described in various cancers, FMT is proposed as a supportive oncology therapy to improve primary treatment outcomes. Phase II efficacy in oncology has yet to be established, with unresolved challenges including optimal dosing, marked interindividual variability, pathogenic risk in immunocompromised patients and limitations to therapeutic scalability [34–36]. Development of bacterial consortia for oncological FMT is complicated by heterogeneous patient responses. Microbiome profiles of ‘responders’ and ‘non-responders’, in the context of immunotherapy efficacy, fail to converge on a reproducible microbial signature predictive of treatment success [37–40]. Larger cohorts have not resolved this issue [38], and some studies instead identify distinct signatures in non-responders, supporting the concept of ‘unhelpful’ microbes [39]. A functional, rather than taxonomic, focus on microbiota associated with response may improve scalability and translatability by eliminating reliance on donor feces, reducing risk in immunocompromised patients and enabling less invasive therapies. Safety concerns surrounding FMT in immunocompromised patients are significant, with clinical considerations encompassing the transmission of live microorganisms to immunosuppressed patients [41, 42]. Despite this, a 2018 systematic review of the efficacy and safety of FMT in immunocompromised patients showed similar adverse event rates to immunocompetent patients, with 87% of immunocompromised individuals’ recurrent C. difficile infection resolved after the first treatment [43].

Current connections between the gastrointestinal microbiota and ALL

Growing evidence implicates the gut microbiome in the development, prognosis and treatment responses of ALL. Current studies demonstrate significant dysbiosis in children undergoing ALL treatment compared with healthy controls (Table 1). In children diagnosed with ALL, changes in alpha diversity appear to be variable; however, beta diversity is consistently distinct between ALL and healthy fecal samples, both pre- and post-chemotherapy [44]. At diagnosis, ALL patients were observed to harbor an increased abundance of gram-positive anaerobic bacteria, specifically Streptococcaceae and Clostridiaceae communities [45]. Additionally, the abundance of gram-negative Bacteroidota decreases upon the induction of chemotherapeutic protocols [44]. Bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization identified genus Blautia and Lactococcus as risk factors for ALL, with genus Slackia a protective factor [46]. All studies used DNA sequencing or microarray assays of the 16 S rRNA region of the prokaryotic ribosome. Studies by Liu, 2020 [47] and Liu, 2021 [48] excluded patients exposed to antibiotics in the three months before fecal sampling, whereas all other studies included in Table 1 accounted for antibiotic usage in their analysis. Liu, 2020 also excluded patients with Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) as assessed using the Rome III criteria, as IBS has been shown to influence gut microbiota [49].

Expanding research also suggests a correlation between microbiota dysbiosis and chemotherapy complications in ALL patients [50]. Increased populations of opportunistic pathogens are observed in patients with ALL, and baseline microbiota diversity and composition may be independent predictors of infection risk during induction chemotherapy [51]. A predominance of opportunistic pathogens also predicts higher infection risks after therapy cessation (> 6 months) in patients with ALL [45]. Key bacterial genera associated with treatment failure include Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Ralstonia, Lactococcus, and Enterococcus [45].

Features of the microbiome contributing to ALL pathogenesis

Select ALL cases in the pediatric population likely originate from genomic alterations acquired in utero, giving rise to a vulnerable ‘pre-leukemic’ population of hematopoietic precursors [52]. Evidence for these pre-natal mutations includes rare pre-natal leukemia diagnoses, concordant twin studies and retrospective investigation on birth samples [52–54]. Twin studies have provided compelling data that concordant leukemia is due to a clonal origin in utero. This is supported by the presence of prenatally acquired initiating genomic alterations, for example, ETV6::RUNX1 [53] and TCF3::ZNF384 [52, 54], with a concordance rate of leukemia reported between 5 and 25% in monozygotic twins. However, as demonstrated in mouse models, many primary events are insufficient to promote leukemic transformation in all carriers [55]. Therefore, additional post-natal mutations are required to drive oncogenesis.

Greave’s two-hit model of Precursor B-ALL (Pre-B-ALL) postulates a critical role for maturation of the gastrointestinal microbiome in conjunction with the adaptive immune system in the genesis of ALL in pediatric populations [56]. Depending on a variety of epidemiological factors, as highlighted in Fig. 1, maturation of the gut microbiome and the consequent priming of the immune system pose a possible avenue for intervention in early childhood. The two-hit model of childhood Pre-B-ALL posits that overt leukemic genesis occurs after the acquisition of a secondary mutative genomic lesion after birth [56]. The specific mechanistic pathways of this second lesion are unknown; however, a dysregulated immune response to common respiratory pathogens is thought to trigger inflammatory cytokines that expand pre-leukemic clones and prompt secondary mutations [57]. Twin studies support the two-hit model in children aged 2–15, as latency periods confer post-natal disease initiation [52]. Furthermore, supporting this theory, early induction of innate immune responses by Toll-like receptors was observed to reduce leukemic penetrance in Eµ-Ret and E2A-PBX1 transgenic murine models [58]. Ex-vivo experiments have also indicated that exposure of ETV6::RUNX1+ Pre-B-cells to the bacterial endotoxin lipopolysaccharide triggers malignant transformation in the absence of IL-7, mimicking the setting of an inappropriate immune response [59].

In 2021 Vicente-Dueñas et al. [60], demonstrated that a genetic predisposition to B-ALL, as modelled in Pax5+/− mice, shapes a distinct dysbiotic gut microbiome. Within this model, the murine immune system is affected by heterogeneous Pax5 deletion, with gastrointestinal lymphoid tissue significantly reduced, shaping a microbiome distinctly different from that of wild-type (WT) mice in both specific pathogen-free and contaminant-free facilities. The major microbial difference between WT and Pax5+/− predisposed animals was a lack of commensal bacteria; specifically, members of the family Rikenellaceae and genus Oscillospira were undetectable in Pax5+/− mice. Building upon Greaves’ hypothesis, this study demonstrates that antibiotic-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis triggers leukemia in predisposed mice, even in the absence of infectious stimuli, further cementing the role of gut microbiota dysbiosis in ALL development [60]. A more recent study contradicts this observation, demonstrating that cytomegalovirus infection in early infancy produces a protective effect against hyperdiploid B-ALL in mice [61]. In this scenario, pre-leukemic cell depletion is observed to occur via STAT4 and IL12p40-mediated innate immune responses, supporting a potential inhibitory effect of early-life infections on B-ALL progression [61].

Known microbiome disruptors in ALL

Chemotherapy

Owing to the absence of targeted therapeutics for the majority of ALL subtypes, high-dose chemotherapy is the cornerstone of ALL treatment. Chemotherapy is a known disruptor of the gastrointestinal microbiome, significantly altering the compositional profile of the gut microbiota [62]. Studies on microbiome disturbances in pediatric ALL patients demonstrate a decrease in the commensal phyla Bacteroidota, facilitating an increase in pathogenic communities of Streptococcaceae and Clostridiaceae [45].

Antibiotics

Antibiotics are frequently administered prophylactically or therapeutically to ALL patients to prevent or treat bacterial infections. Antibiotic use disrupts the ecological balance of the gastrointestinal microbiota, leading to reduced microbial diversity and the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria [63]. A cross-sectional analysis of pediatric ALL patients diagnosed in Pakistan shows that antibiotic usage is most commonly due to positive blood culture findings for gram-negative bacterial isolates [64]. Commonly adopted antibiotics in ALL treatments, ampicillin, ciprofloxacin and cefotaxime demonstrate high resistance rates [64].

At diagnosis, ALL patients showed an alteration in beta diversity, regardless of whether antibiotics were administered in the month before fecal sampling, as determined by Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) [65]. Interestingly, alpha diversity disruption is reduced in patients exposed to antibiotics [65]. Additionally, the impact of antibiotics may be minimal during chemotherapeutic treatment because of the effect of chemotherapeutics on altering gut microbiota composition. Species evenness, as indicated by Shannon or Chao1 alpha diversity, for ALL patients who reported taking antibiotics in the two months following diagnosis or during chemotherapy did not significantly differ from those who did not take any antibiotics during this period in a 2019 case-control cohort study [66].

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation

Hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) is a last-line therapeutic for pediatric patients with minimal residual disease positivity at the end of consolidation therapy, commonly employed in children with high-risk lesions [67]. Conditioning regimens administered before stem cell transplantation, including radiation therapy, also contribute to microbiota injury [68, 69].

Smaller than gut microbes: a look into the metabolites they produce

Adjusting for disease-related factors such as genomic subtype and risk stratification, ALL treatment outcomes of standardized chemotherapy protocols are variable, suggesting a contribution from host factors [70]. Gut microbiota composition and the attendant immune response may contribute to these inter-individual disease responses, a relationship that may be exploited therapeutically by modulating the gut microbiota [71–73]. A core contribution of the symbiotic relationship between gut microbiota and host physiology is the production of a diverse array of metabolites and small molecules [74]. Common microbial metabolites can be categorized into: short-chain fatty acids, amino acids and derivatives, bile acids, and vitamins (Table 2) [74].

Generation of dietary fiber microbial metabolites

Short Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs) are immuno-modulatory metabolites derived from microbial fermentation of non-digestible fiber and dietary carbohydrates throughout the intestines [75]. A part of the larger family of fatty acids (FAs), SCFAs occur as saturated monocarboxylic FAs with up to five carbon atoms in a single chain. Owing to a short hydrophobic carbon chain and the presence of a hydrophilic carboxyl group (Fig. 2), approximately 95% of SCFAs passively diffuse across the epithelial barrier into colonic epithelial cells as their preferred energy substrate [76]. Within the human colon, microbial fermentation of polysaccharides achieves a 300 mmol/day production, with only 10 mmol/day secreted [77]. The principal volatile SCFAs generated are acetate (C2), propionate (C3) and butyrate (C4), which are typically observed at a ratio of 60:25:15 [78]. Low concentrations of total SCFAs are found in the hepatic portal blood (375 ± 70 µmol/l), liver (148 ± 42 µmol/l), and peripheral blood (79 ± 22 µmol/l), as measured by gas chromatography of post-mortem samples obtained from six adults [79].

Upon contact with the immune system, SCFAs interact with lymphocytes via membrane transporters (MCT1 and SMCT1), facilitating movement into the cytoplasm, or externally, through G-Protein Coupled Receptors (GPR41 and 43), also referred to as Free Fatty Acid Receptors 2 and 3 (FFAR) (Fig. 3) [80]. Once inside the cell, SCFAs act upon Class 1 Histone Deacetylases (HDACs), exerting an inhibitory effect [81]. HDACs can either directly regulate innate immune responses through epigenetic inhibition of gene transcription by the removal of acetyl groups, leading to transcriptional repression or indirect modulation of transcription factors [81]. In malignant cells, treatment with HDAC inhibitors leads to an accumulation of hyperacetylated histones, resulting in altered gene expression [82]. This therapeutically altered gene expression leads to the induction of apoptotic pathways, altered cell proliferation and perturbed migration [82]. Extracellularly, SCFA activation of FFAR2 leads to alterations in cytokine production within intestinal epithelial cells, promoting IL-18 production to regulate intestinal inflammatory homeostasis [83]. With increasing age, SCFA production decreases, with adults greater than 59 years having diminished production, compared to younger adults and children [84]. This is hypothesized to be a result of a microbiome deficit in SCFA producers or reduced metabolic activity of the existing microbiome [84]. Both hypotheses are assumed to be modulated by reduced dietary fiber intake and lower colonic abundance of fermentable substrates. A recent randomized trial has observed that SCFA production can be rescued in older adults (59–87 years) upon supplementation of indigestible dietary fiber, inulin [84].

Posited applications of SCFAs in ALL

SCFAs exert anticancer effects by targeting apoptosis, autophagy, metabolism, cell cycle, migration/invasion and gene expression in a variety of cancer types, including breast cancer [85], hepatocellular cancer [86], bladder cancer [87], colorectal cancer [88], and myeloid leukemia [89]. However, this connection has yet to be robustly investigated in an ALL-specific context. Microbial metabolites are hypothesized to be relevant in ALL through implications in improving the efficacy of adoptive T-cell therapies, decreasing treatment side effects, and reducing the risk of septic complications [86].

Screening of histone deacetylase inhibitors has identified mild anti-tumor activity of valproic acid, a synthetic derivative of 8-carbon SCFA valeric acid, in NOD/SCID models of B-ALL [90]. However, there is a lack of innate microbial production of valproic acid, low luminal and serum levels of valeric acid, and limited data on the SCFA pro-drug in the context of leukemia [91]. Similarly, a novel application for butyric acid pro-drug, pivaloyloxymethyl butyrate (AN-9), was investigated in the early 2000’s, with selective toxicity to T-ALL cancer cell lines and primary patient material identified [92]. Leukemic cells were 2- to 3-fold more sensitive to AN-9 compared to normal hematopoietic progenitors, however, the use of AN-9 has not translated into clinical ALL treatment [92]. Here we postulate the benefit of SCFAs, acetate, propionate and butyrate as supplementary therapeutics to improve the efficacy of primary therapies and reduce on-treatment side effects, as well as suggesting a role for SCFAs in the prevention of ALL genesis.

Improving the efficacy of primary therapeutics

Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR)-T cells are a personalized form of immunotherapy that utilizes a patient’s own altered T-cells to target cancerous cells. CAR-T cells are increasingly used to treat several cancers – especially blood cancers [93]. Clinical trials for relapsed pediatric ALL patients treated with CD-19 CAR-T cells showed that approximately half of the enrolled children survived long enough to receive stem cell transplants, with 60% of this cohort achieving 5-year disease-free survival [94]. However, further improvements are needed to minimize toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANs), and to improve the durability of response [85]. SCFAs can modify responses to immunotherapy, providing a possible pathway for improving the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapies through epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming [86]. Luu et al. 2021, recently investigated the effects of SCFA supplementation on CD8 + CAR-T cell efficacy in a C57BL/6 murine model. They reported improved CD8 + effector cell function as a result of butyrate supplementation, with observed increases in IFNγ and IFNα leading to increased anti-tumor activity [87]. Acetate and propionate were not identified to enhance the activity of CD8 + CAR-T cells in this study. Interestingly, these findings were independent of FFAR2/3 activation and relied solely on HDAC inhibition and metabolic reprogramming. This work indicates that the SCFA membrane transporters SCMT1 and MCT1 are actionable targets for improving responses to CAR-T cell therapies. Supporting this, butyrate pre-treatment of cytotoxic CAR-T cells has demonstrated enhanced antigen-specific anti-tumor activity through increased mTOR function [87].

A beneficial combinatorial effect of SCFA butyrate with both standard chemotherapy and targeted therapies has been demonstrated both in vitro and in vivo [95–97]. For example, the in vitro addition of sodium butyrate to AML cell lines KG-1 and SKNO-1 enhances Venetoclax efficiency through upregulation of pro-apoptotic factors, inducing cell death via synergistic cleavage of the caspase substrate poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) [95]. In vivo, oral administration of butyrate has been demonstrated to enhance the anti-tumor efficacy of 5-fluorouracil in murine models of colorectal cancer [96]. With increased efficacy attributed to the modulation of PINK1/Parkin signaling through enhanced ROS-mediated mitophagy and restoration of intestinal microbiota diversity [96]. Propionate has also demonstrated potential synergy with PD-1 immunotherapy in colorectal cancer [97].

Prevention of septic morbidity/mortality in ALL

As a ‘success story’ in modern oncology, ALL standard-of-care treatment affords approximately 90% survival in pediatric patients [98]. Despite this, infectious complications, specifically septicemia, remain the major cause of mortality in patients diagnosed with hematological malignancies [99]. Neutropenia, lymphopenia and damage to the barrier functions of gut and respiratory epithelia contribute significantly to febrile neutropenia (FN), sepsis, and opportunistic infections [100]. Antimicrobials used to prevent or combat infections may further adversely alter the balance of microbiota, which can, in turn, lead to infectious complications. SCFA combination (67.5 mM acetate, 25.9 mM propionate, and 40 mM butyrate) supplementation has been shown to repair abnormal gut microbiota in C57BL/6 mice treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics, with efficacy equal to fecal microbiota transplant [101]. Furthermore, SCFA supplementation protects against organ damage and improves survival in cecal ligation and puncture pre-clinical models of sepsis [101].

FN is a severe complication of myelosuppressive chemotherapy, characterized by low neutrophil granulocyte levels and loss of normal immune function, increasing vulnerability to infection and leading to fatal sequelae. Bacterial bloodstream infections are the most common cause of FN [100], with prolonged FN increasing the risk of invasive fungal infections and associated morbidities. Fluoroquinolones are the most commonly employed antibiotic prophylaxis to prevent FN in patients undertaking intensive anti-leukemic chemotherapy [102]. Antibiotic treatment, however, is considered one of the main initiators of microbiome dysbiosis in these patients. Foundational studies have observed that fluoroquinolone levofloxacin prophylaxis in cancer patients is associated with an increased risk of Clostridioides difficile infection, Candida colonization and multidrug-resistant bacteriemia [103, 104]. In a hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT) setting, the baseline gut microbiota composition distinctly differs between patients who develop FN and those who do not. The bacterial taxa associated with an absence of FN in the HCT setting were Prevotella, Ruminococcus, Dorea, and Collinsella, known for their ability to ferment plant carbohydrates into metabolites [105]. Furthermore, there was a distinct expansion of Faecalibacterium at baseline in patients who didn’t develop major infections compared to those who did [105]. These results suggest a protective effect of commensal bacteria and associated fermented microbial metabolites against FN development and infective complications. Changes in gut microbiota after induction therapy have also been identified as predictors of neutropenia in pediatric patients [106]. Multicenter observation of pediatric patients with newly diagnosed ALL identifies a diminished abundance of commensal bacteria, paired with Enterococcus overgrowth, is associated with delayed neutrophil reconstitution [106]. Patients experiencing prolonged neutropenia were identified to have significantly elevated levels of neutrophil signaling markers (CXCL1, CXCL8 and G-CSF) during induction therapy, strongly correlated with beta diversity on day 29 of treatment [106]. In vivo, studies on neutrophil homeostasis during Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection in C57BL/6 mice demonstrated enhanced neutrophil mobilization associated with oral butyrate supplementation [107]. Butyrate increased the expression of CXCL2 in lung macrophages, a chemokine ligand responsible for promoting neutrophil infiltration. Interestingly, despite an increase in neutrophil mobilization, butyrate-treated animals were less efficient in clearing bacterial infections due to a reduction in secondary granule enzyme and oxidase components, leading to impaired bactericidal activity [107]. Within the context of treatment-induced septic morbidity in patients undergoing anti-leukemic therapy, the potential of SCFA supplementation to mediate neutropenia requires additional investigation in translational ALL models.

A major cause of bloodstream infection in patients undergoing chemotherapy is the translocation of pathogenic bacteria from the gastrointestinal microbiome into systemic circulation [108]. Chemotherapy-induced intestinal mucositis is characterized by crypt loss and impairment of absorptive and barrier functions [109]. SCFA, specifically butyrate, have a well-characterized role in supporting the functional integrity of the gastrointestinal lining [110]. Frequently explored in murine models of chemically induced gastrointestinal colitis, butyrate reduced intestinal permeability and maintained tight junctions through GPR109a activation [111]. Further supporting barrier function, butyrate suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in lipopolysaccharide-induced macrophages in co-culture of Caco-2 (intestinal epithelium) and RAW246.7 (macrophage) cells [111]. In murine models of AML, oral butyrate supplementation was observed to reverse intestinal barrier damage through upregulation of Claudin-1 and reduction in Claudin-2 in both mRNA and protein expression [89]. This regulation leads to an increase in tight junctions and a decrease in water channel formation between gut endothelial cells, mitigating endotoxin translocation across the gut barrier [89].

Decreasing short-term side effects

Intensified treatment for ALL significantly reduces the risk of relapse and leukemia-related death, particularly in children [112]. Therefore, it is crucial to focus on minimizing the toxic side effects of treatment without compromising efficacy. A previously published consensus defined fourteen severe acute toxic effects related to ALL treatment, highlighting priorities to be addressed [113]. Five of these fourteen acute toxic effects are proposed here to potentially have effective interventional targets through modulation of the gut microbiota and treatment with SCFAs. At the time of this review, SCFA supplementation has not been studied in an ALL context, pre-clinically or clinically. Therefore, we suggest a role for SCFAs in the mitigation of treatment side effects through available reported evidence of efficacy in other pathophysiological disease models.

Osteonecrosis. Highly prevalent within the AYA cohort, osteonecrosis (ON) is the death of bone tissue due to a lack of vascularization, leading to pain, restricted range of motion, and loss of mobility [114]. The incidence of symptomatic ON was reported to be 15.65% in one cohort of children after ALL treatment, and asymptomatic ON may have an incidence as high as 51.8% [114]. Steroid exposure is a risk factor for ON, however, recent evidence suggests that composition and metabolomic profiles of the gastrointestinal microbiota may also be contributory [115]. Steroid-induced ON patients have significantly reduced Lactobacillus spp [115]. Consistent with prior research, Chen et al. 2022 supports the association between the loss of Lactobacillus and the development of steroid-induced ON, providing a mechanistic insight into the microbiome’s protective effect [116]. In vivo, experiments showed that weekly oral administration of Lactobacillus animalis protected mice from the development of glucocorticoid-induced ON, and exerted bone-protective effects in healthy control mice [116]. In vitro, this protective effect was observed to result from bacterial extracellular vesicles (EV) providing strong proangiogenic effects and osteogenic differentiation upon incubation of human microvascular endothelial cells with escalating doses of L.animalis-EV [116]. In summary, Lactobacillus has been shown to mitigate steroid-induced ON by enhancing angiogenesis, promoting osteogenesis and reducing vascular cell apoptosis [115]. While direct evidence in ALL is unavailable, we posit that restoration of Lactobacillus populations in ALL patients may be beneficial to reduce the burden of treatment-induced osteonecrosis in AYA patient cohorts.

Arterial Hypertension. During the first few months of anti-leukemic treatment, arterial hypertension is common and associated with posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome [113]. In healthy, normotensive adult populations, 500 mg of oral propionate administered twice daily is associated with a mild reduction in blood pressure [117]. Within experimental models of murine hypertension, cardioprotective effects are observed upon administration of a high-fiber diet supplemented with 100 mmol/L butyrate or acetate [118]. Investigation into a gut microbiota profile associated with hypertension identified that a diet lacking in resistant starch is associated with a dysbiotic microbiome in the presence of a mild hypertensive stimulus [118]. Furthermore, in pre-clinical studies, propionate-supplemented drinking water (200 mmol/L) modulated effector T-cell levels in AngII-induced hypertensive NMRI mice, attenuating vascular inflammation [119]. Cardioprotective effects of SCFAs are mediated via GPR43/GPR109A receptors, which modulate blood pressure-lowering L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-Dopa) levels [118]. Anticancer pro-drugs of butyric acid have also demonstrated a protective effect against doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity in pre-clinical murine models [120].

Seizures. Seizures during ALL therapy may be an isolated side effect, or occur secondary to infection, CNS disturbance, metabolic changes or ICANS [121]. The role of the microbiota-gut-brain axis in neurological diseases is associated with the bidirectional relationship between intestinal and central nervous system homeostasis. Within the field of epilepsy, SCFAs have demonstrated key roles in the maturation of microglia, permeability of the blood-brain barrier and stress responses [122]. Neurological in vivo experimentation showed that intragastric injection of butyrate (10 mg/kg) an hour before seizure induction, by intraperitoneal injection of pentylenetetrazol (PTZ), reduced seizure score and increased the latency period in male Kunming mice [123]. Pre-treatment with butyrate significantly prevented PTZ-associated reduction in NAD+ levels as assessed by ELISA of purified mitochondria isolated from hippocampal tissue [123]. Furthermore, propionate shows strong efficacy in the same pre-clinical model, demonstrating successful reduction of seizure intensity and increased latency periods [124]. In vitro research has suggested that moderate levels of endogenous norepinephrine have anticonvulsant effects [125]. In PC12 cells, butyrate has been shown to degrade tyrosine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme in the catecholaminergic pathway responsible for norepinephrine production [126]. Collectively, current data obtained from neurological studies raise the possibility that SCFA may influence seizure susceptibility in ALL, potentially through effects on NAD+ homeostasis and norepinephrine signalling.

Peripheral neuropathy. Peripheral motor and sensory neuropathy in ALL patients are commonly associated side effects of vincristine treatment [113]. Vincristine-induced peripheral neuropathy is mechanistically driven by canonical activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-1β, known to sensitize nociceptors [127]. In models of obesity-induced inflammation with heightened activation of NLRP3, butyrate supplementation reduced mRNA transcripts for NLRP3, caspase-1 and IL-1β [128]. This antioxidant effect of butyrate has been recapitulated in cardiology research showing that butyrate significantly decreases NLRP3 inflammasome formation in wild-type mice [129]. Furthermore, butyrate has been demonstrated to directly decrease levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β [130]. Similar to butyrate, propionate has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects on the peripheral nervous system via histone modification [131]. The binding of propionate to FFAR3 receptors on Schwann cells and dorsal root ganglia has neuroprotective effects, significantly reducing H2O2-induced cell death, compared to controls. Translated in vivo, Sprague-Dawley rats treated with 150 mM propionate oral gavage demonstrate significant protection of axonal integrity when exposed to oxidative stress [131]. However, these results are challenged by recent work demonstrating activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by Butyrate under inflammatory conditions [132]. We suggest that SCFAs may mitigate peripheral neuropathy in ALL, with a putative mechanism involving neuronal protection against oxidative stress.

Hyperlipidemia. Hyperlipidemia is defined as the presence of excess lipids in the blood and is a high-risk factor for metabolic diseases [133]. In ALL patients, severe hyperlipidemia is graded as a triglyceride/cholesterol concentration greater than 20 times the upper normal limit [113, 134]. In vivo studies in transgenic mice lacking apolipoprotein-E, which is involved in fat metabolism, demonstrate significant hyperlipidemia when maintained on a high-fat diet (HFD) [134]. Sodium Butyrate supplementation at 200 and 400 mg/kg markedly decreased this HFD-induced elevation of total cholesterol, through the reduction of non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [134]. Reductions in serum cholesterol are observed to occur through butyrate-mediated upregulation of ABCA1 expression via the Sp1 pathway, accelerating reverse cholesterol transport and allowing excess lipids to be excreted into bile. We raise the possibility that SCFAs may be useful in the mitigation of hyperlipidemia on ALL treatment through the upregulation of hepatic lipid metabolism and clearance mechanisms.

Preventing ALL genesis through healthy immune maturation

A notable biological feature of many childhood B-ALL subtypes, as discussed earlier in this review, is the presence of a latent pre-leukemic stage. Recent reported increases in B-ALL incidence appear associated with modern lifestyle changes and environmental exposures [135]. Epidemiological studies correlate exposure to infectious agents during infancy and the transformation from pre-disposing clones into an overt leukemic state [56]. Practical approaches to validate proposed epidemiological theories require complex murine models of ALL that can emulate disease initiation.

The Pax5+/- [136] and Sca1-ETV6::RUNX1 [137] models of B-ALL development have been instrumental in validating the interaction between infection and ALL, and further, the gut microbiome and ALL. When housed in a specific pathogen-free environment, these murine models do not develop ALL, despite harboring a genetic predisposition. Exposure to infectious agents, however, resulted in 22% of Pax5+/- and 10% of Sca1-ETV6::RUNX1 mice developing overt disease [136, 137]. The underlying mechanisms for infectious transformation of pre-leukemic clones are hypothesized to involve significant immunological stress capable of triggering leukemic conversion. In both the Pax5+/- and Sca1-ETV6::RUNX1 murine models, exposure to pathogens induces expansion of aberrant B-cell precursor compartments within the bone marrow [136, 137].

Isolated from infectious stimuli, perturbation of gastrointestinal dysbiosis also demonstrates a transformative ability, suggesting that dysbiosis can constitute a separate form of immune stress capable of triggering B-ALL [60]. We postulate that promotion of healthy immune maturation and microbial eubiosis may protect against leukemic transformation in predisposed individuals. This hypothesis is supported by results from a New Zealand cohort study demonstrating the protective role of healthy microbial colonization, with exposure to plant diversity during early childhood associated with a reduced risk of ALL [138].

SCFAs promote healthy immune maturation, improve adaptive responses and regulate autoimmunity. SCFA regulation of immune maturation occurs via G protein-coupled receptor signalling and/or histone deacetylase activity. Concluding remarks and future directions

This review highlights the current evidence for the role of microbial diversity in Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia and suggests a prompt investigation of a SCFA-leukemia axis, supported by available data external to hematological disease.

The gut microbiota and attendant immune system appear to be the key modifiable risk factor for B-ALL development and pre-, intra- and post-therapy side effects. Contemporary studies have revealed a distinct microbial signature in pediatric ALL patients, with a significant reduction in beta-diversity both pre- and post-therapy [44–48, 51, 66, 139]. However, these aforementioned studies have not identified causal connections between gastrointestinal dysbiosis and ALL pathophysiology. Current investigations into the role of the microbiome in ALL are subject to several key limitations [44–48, 51, 66, 139]. Many clinical cohorts remain small and isolated to the pediatric population, limiting the generalizability of findings. In addition, substantial heterogeneity exists in sampling timepoints across studies, particularly in relation to diagnosis, therapeutic protocol phases and cross-study comparison. The widespread use of prophylactic antibiotics and ablative chemotherapy regimens represents a major confounding factor, making it difficult to disentangle disease-associated microbial signatures from treatment-induced alterations. Furthermore, a large proportion of studies rely on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, which provides limited taxonomic resolution and lacks functional insight into mechanistic possibilities [140]. Combined with the relative scarcity of integrated metabolomic analyses, such as SCFA quantification, the ability to link microbiota compositional changes with functional consequences is negatively impacted. To reveal the key interactions between the gastrointestinal microbiome and ALL, there must be a focus on larger, longitudinal and multi-omics studies paired with the development of robust pre-clinical gnotobiotic models. As potential candidates for predictive biomarkers, quantification of key microbes constituting ALL-specific microbial signatures and related metabolomics in patients will provide opportunities for the development of tailored therapies for each age group.

Short-chain fatty acids are an underexplored pathway in the gastrointestinal microbiome–leukemia axis. Extrapolating findings from various disease states highlights a potential for the use of SCFAs in increasing the efficacy of oncological immunotherapies [86–97], mediating various side-effects frequently observed in ALL patients [115–120, 122–124, 126, 128–132, 134], and assisting in healthy immune development in early childhood [60, 138]. Due to microbiome research in hematological oncology being in its early stages, SCFAs are not at the forefront of research priorities; however, we suggest investigation into their potential to benefit the ALL patient population. Currently, the role of SCFAs in ALL can only be extrapolated from ALL bacterial diversity data [44–48, 51, 66, 139], pre-clinical investigation in other hematological/oncological malignancies [60, 89] and microbial metabolite research in non-malignant pathophysiology [115–120, 122–124, 126, 128–132, 134]. As literature surrounding hematology and microbiology remains sparse and heterogeneous, differences in study design, patient population and analytical methodologies must be considered in the variability of reported findings. With such a lack of pre-clinical and clinical investigation of SCFA mechanisms in ALL, it is imperative to prioritize well-designed longitudinal human and mechanistic studies to clarify our stated hypotheses and assess the clinical relevance of SCFA in ALL.
