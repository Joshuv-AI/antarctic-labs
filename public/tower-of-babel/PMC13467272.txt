Three Years of Co-Testing 2020–2022: Correlation of Diagnostic Colposcopy with Histology in Cases of Normal Cytology and Persistent High-Risk HPV
Authors: Arjola Xhaja, André Ahr, Ilona Zeiser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction Since the introduction of organised cervical cancer screening, abnormal findings have been referred for diagnostic colposcopy in accordance with defined algorithms. The algorithms are primarily based on the HPV test results. A colposcopy is recommended even in cases where the cytology is normal but HPV persists. CIN2+ lesions are also detected in the process. The aim of this study is to investigate the causes of normal cytological results followed by CIN2+ lesions. Patients and MethodsAll cytological, HPV, and resultant histological test results from the primary screening, recorded in our database between 2020 and 2022, were analysed. The CIN2+ lesions with normal cytology were re-evaluated with regard to the quality of the cell material.ResultsA total of 884201 preventive cytology tests and 5002 corresponding histology results from the years 2020–2022 were evaluated. In the normal groups I/II-a, 49 CIN2+ lesions were documented. After revision, 71% (35 cases) remained in group I/II-a. The revision resulted in 16% (8 cases) being reclassified into group 0, and in 12% (6 cases) showing minor discrepancies.ConclusionDue to the persistence of high-risk HPV, CIN2+ lesions were diagnosed via colposcopy and biopsy despite normal cytology results. These lesions account for a small proportion of the total population. The main reason for the discrepancy was that the cell material was difficult to assess. In the majority of cases where discrepancies were found, but the cell material was otherwise easy to assess, dysplastic cells were absent. This shows that the real problem here lies in the pre-analytical stage. It is possible that the dysplastic areas may not be reached during the swab collection. Systematic reporting of specimen quality and a separate category for HPV-positive/cytologically unremarkable findings in MN III could improve diagnostic management.

Notes

The cytological findings were recorded using Munich Nomenclature III (MN III). These were indicated in brackets in the Bethesda System (TBS). In this text, human papillomaviruses (HPV) always refer to high-risk (HR) types; co-testing refers to the simultaneous performance of cytological diagnosis and HPV testing; CA: Computer-assisted; DC: Digital cytology; PS: Primary screening, CPS: Cytology-based primary screening, PSC: Primary screening via co-testing; ‘histology’ in the text refers to histological findings; CC is the abbreviation for cervical cancer, EC for endometrial cancer and C for other cancers.

Introduction

Since the introduction of cytological screening for the early detection of cervical cancer in 1971, conventional cytology has been the central standard in Germany. Its widespread use has significantly reduced the incidence of invasive cervical cancer, highlighting the effectiveness of cytological diagnostics in screening 1 .

On 1 January 2020, a new preventative programme was introduced under the Guideline on Organised Cancer Screening (oKFE-RL). The previously opportunistic approach has evolved into a partially organised screening programme. In addition to cytology, women aged 35 and over now also receive an HPV test combined with cytology (co-testing). Abnormal findings are referred for diagnostic colposcopy using defined algorithms 2 .

These algorithms are primarily based on the results of HR-HPV testing and differ significantly from previous risk-adapted recommendations. According to the Munich Nomenclature III (MN III), prompt evaluation via diagnostic colposcopy is largely restricted to cytological findings suggestive of CIN2+ (HSIL+) 3 . The S3 guideline on ‘Prevention of Cervical Cancer’ also suggests, in many cases, repeating cytological examination initially, possibly supplemented by biomarkers such as p16/Ki-67 4 . The oKFE-RL guidelines, however, recommend a diagnostic colposcopy after three months even if HR-HPV positivity is present with normal or low-grade cytological changes. This change to the prevention strategy has a direct impact on clinical practice. Out of 884201 preventive cytological diagnoses, 43179 (4.9%) were categorised as findings in groups II-a (NILM) to V (cervical cancer), which frequently resulted in a colposcopic examination. A total of 37643 (4.3%) cases were from groups II and IIID1, which are known for their high regression tendency ( Table 1 ). As a result, the number of referrals to dysplasia clinics rose considerably. In many of these instances, cytological follow-up would have sufficed in accordance previous recommendations, as the risk of CIN3+ in this cohort is low.

The limitations of colposcopy are well known: Compared with cytology, it has lower sensitivity and specificity, which is why colposcopy is not suitable as a screening method 5 . Nevertheless, even in cases that are cytologically unremarkable but HR-HPV-positive (groups I/II-a), CIN2+ lesions can be detected using colposcopy-guided biopsies 6 .

The aim of this study is to investigate the causes of normal cytological findings in primary screening followed by the detection of CIN2+. Based on these findings, potential strategies for optimising cytology-based screening will be identified. The extensive data available in the laboratory system, with histologically confirmed correlations, provide a suitable basis for this investigation.

Patients and methods

Data basis and legal framework

The data were collected at CytoMol, a routine cytology and molecular biology laboratory in Frankfurt am Main, which specialises in cervical cancer prevention. Since the introduction of the new cancer early detection guidelines (oKFE-RL) in 2020, co-testing has been standard for women aged 35 and over:

Patient groups and data storage

The analysed smears originated from approximately 450 gynaecological practices. Around 80% of these practices are located within a 200-kilometre radius of Frankfurt; the remainder are dispersed throughout the country. The representation of urban and rural practices, as well as solo and group practices, was consistent with the national average. Between 2020 and 2022, the volume of laboratory work increased by approximately 13%. Data were recorded in a certified IT system (Nexus Zytologie, Donaueschingen, Germany), in which all histological findings have been continuously linked to cytological diagnoses since 2014 (maximum interval: 12 months). The statistical analysis was conducted using exported Excel spreadsheets (pivot analysis, CUBES).

High-risk HPV positivity was strongly age-dependent

Among women aged 35 to 37, the percentage of high-risk HPV positivity was approximately 10%. This percentage decreased with age, reaching the lowest level of 3.3% in the 81–90 age group ( Fig. 1 ).

A total of 5002 histological findings were recorded in connection with the primary screening programme conducted between 2020 and 2022. 868 (17%) were from the CPS group ( Table 2 ) and 4134 (83%) from the PSC cohort ( Table 3 ). Both tables depict the correlation between cytological diagnoses and the corresponding histological findings in CPS ( Table 2 ), as well as the HPV results for PSC ( Table 3 ). The clarification rate in the CPS group was considerably lower than that in the PSC group and showed a high proportion of high-grade findings in the histology.

41% (356 cases) were CIN3, 0.8% (7 cases) each were AIS and squamous cell carcinomas, one case was cervical adenocarcinoma, and one case was endometrial carcinoma; 24.2% (210 cases) showed no evidence of a CIN lesion ( Table 2 ).

In the 4134 histological samples from the PSC cohort, the distributions were more varied: 33% (1376 cases) showed no evidence of CIN, 19% (784 cases) were CIN1, 14% (561 cases) were CIN2, 29% (1185 cases) were CIN3 lesions, 0.6% (25 cases) were AIS, 3% (111 cases) were squamous cell carcinomas, and 0.7% (31 cases) were cervical adenocarcinomas. In 61 cases (1.5%), EC and other carcinomas (e. g., of the vulva and vagina) were diagnosed.

Of the 1376 histological samples without CIN, 89.5% were HPV-positive. Of the 784 CIN1, 561 CIN2, and 1185 CIN3 cases, an average of 97.5% were HPV-positive. 96% of the 25 cases of AIS and 81% of the 31 cases of cervical adenocarcinoma were HPV-positive. Of the 111 squamous cell carcinomas of the cervix, 102 (92%) tested positive for HPV. Among the 61 cases of EC and other carcinoma, only 10 (16.4%) tested positive for HPV. These were predominantly cases of squamous cell carcinoma of the vulva and vagina.

Of the 884201 cytological results, 97.3% (860291) were normal. The cytological group I (NILM) included 95% (840139) of the cases. In cases where cytological findings were normal but there was a significant medical history or persistent HPV positivity, these cases were classified as Group II-a (NILM); this accounted for 2.3% (20152) of cases. Cases that could not be technically analysed accounted for 0.1% (833) of the total and were assigned to Group 0.

Only 2.6% (23027) of all cytology smears showed abnormalities, the majority of which (2%) were low-grade changes, classified as groups II/IIID1 (ASCUS/LSIL). Only 0.6% (5536) of all findings were categorised as belonging to the high-risk cytological group IIID2+ (HSIL+) ( Table 1 ).

Groups I and II-a (NILM) were investigated in only 0.01% and 0.6% of cases, respectively. In this group, CIN3+ lesions were identified either based on clinical findings or due to the persistence of high-risk HPV. The rate of diagnostic follow-up immediately after the PS increased markedly with the severity of the cytological group and reached a peak of 80% in group V.

While in Groups II-p and g the proportion of CIN3+ lesions (lesions requiring treatment) was 9% in both cases, in Groups IVb–V, the proportion reached 90% to 100%, with almost all histological findings confirming CIN3 or carcinoma. These results demonstrate the high specificity of cytology, particularly in high-risk groups ( Table 1 ).

12 (67%) of 18 samples that were easily assessable (6 CIN2, 5 CIN3, and 1 CC) contained no dysplastic cells upon re-examination. In six cases (33%; 2 CIN2 and 4 CIN3), the easily assessable specimens were reassigned to a higher-grade group upon review. The reason for the discrepancy was attributed to the cell morphology. In half of the cases, the issue was the interpretation of metaplastic (small) cells; in the other half, it was the interpretation of cell clusters, particularly glandular ones, which were perceived and assessed differently by various cytologists.

In 25 cases (2 CIN2, 4 CIN3, and 19 ECC), the cell profiles were difficult to assess. The main reason in all two CIN3 and EC cases in this group was the presence of atrophic cells, with endocervical cells that were either absent or not clearly identifiable. Significant inflammatory-haemorrhagic or cytolytic cell patterns, and the use of lubricants or gels, etc., are the reasons for the discrepancy in two CIN2 and two CIN3 cases. In 23 (92%) of the cases involving cell material of uncertain quality, the cytological diagnosis remained in Group I following review. Only in two cases of CIN2 did the second review result in an unclear Group 0/II-p classification.

The category ‘unassessable cell material’ comprises six cases (2 CIN2, 2 CIN3, and 2 EC). In this category, re-evaluation of the cytological diagnosis is not possible due to severe cytolysis or a haemorrhagic cell patterns. A second examination revealed a Group 0/II-p here. The MN III 3 states: ‘Inadequate specimen quality is also to be classified as group 0 in MN III, with the reasons stated.’ Classification into Groups I to V indicates the suitability of the specimen. Limitations on the assessability of the findings must be specified: insufficient cell material, inadequate fixation, extensive artificial or severe degenerative cellular changes, or significant cell overlap caused by epithelial cells, leukocytes, or erythrocytes.’ Apart from the description of limited assessability in the report text, there is no additional field on the cytology form that would allow the submitter to classify the specimen as being of limited quality or to conduct a statistical analysis for quality assurance purposes at first glance.

The main reason for these discrepancies was the limited or unassessable cell material (n = 31) (63%), particularly due to atrophic, inflammatory, or cytolytic changes, which made it difficult to reach a definitive cytological diagnosis. Dysplastic cells were absent in most specimens with clear and easily assessable cell patterns. These results show that the real problem here lies in the pre-analytical stage.

A comparison of the 34- and 35-year-old age groups provided an indication of how the detection rate in the laboratory has improved ( Fig. 2 ). The number of 34-year-olds who took part in the screening was 30756, while that of 35-year-olds was 32380. This represents a 5.3% increase. While the annual screening using conventional cytology (+CA) detected only 53 cases of CIN3 and one carcinoma among 34-year-olds, 203 cases of CIN3 and 10 carcinomas were found among 35-year-olds, who underwent co-testing with HPV and thin-layer cytology (TLC) for the first time.

Discussion

In a well-screened population such as in Germany, where the prevalence of cervical cancer is intrinsically very low, maintaining a highly specific screening test is advisable. The goal is to accurately identify the small remaining group with the highest likelihood of disease development. When used in co-testing, HPV testing enhances sensitivity and aids routine diagnosis, particularly in cases where cytology reaches its limits (atrophic, haemorrhagic-degenerative, and inflammatory cell patterns).

The transition in screening methods is especially noticeable at the age threshold of 34/35 years. The detection rate for CIN3+ in the PSC cohort of 35-year-olds exceeds that in the CPS group of 34-year-olds. The reasons for this vary.

The number of 35-year-olds screened was approximately 5.28% higher than that of 34-year-olds; thus, more lesions were detected.

Part of the increase can be attributed to the general rise in laboratory volume and the increased number of referrals (including some ‘imported’ lesions in new patients or those previously not part of our screening population).

With the commencement of co-testing, internal cytological practices have evolved: Cytological technicians now specifically focus on HPV-positive specimens. These are now referred to cytologists much more frequently than before, even in instances of low-grade cellular changes. This resulted in an increase in the percentage of smears screened by doctors.

The data provided above illustrate that implementing new diagnostic methods in routine practice enhances the accuracy of cytological diagnosis. This is illustrated in the CC and PSC tables ( Table 3 and Table 5 ), in which almost all CIN2+ lesions were assigned to high-risk cytological groups (Group IIID2, p/IV, ASC-H/HSIL+). In a 2020 internal validation study, we compared TIS with cytology supported by digital tools. The digitally assisted cytology was more sensitive and specific compared to TIS; more CIN2+ lesions were identified with this method 9 .

The question of how many CIN3+ lesions are found in a specific cytological group depends directly on the quality of the smear (was the lesion sampled?), the cytological method used in routine practice, and the internal quality of reporting.

In low-risk groups such as II-a (NILM) or II-p (ASCUS), only a few lesions are anticipated. In our population, the detection rate for CIN3+ in primary screening and the overall cohort 10 11 remains below 10% in the II-p (ASCUS) group and about 13% in the IIID1 (LSIL) group. Nationwide data from Germany depict a comparable scenario 8 . The evaluation would differ if 20–30% of low-grade findings had CIN3+; then, the diagnostic approach would need a comprehensive reassessment 1 . Overall, the available techniques should complement each other rather than compete: Each screening method has its distinct strengths and weaknesses in regular practice.

The registry study by the Working Group on Cervical Pathology and Colposcopy 6 documents that, within the II-p (ASCUS) category, 54.8% of cases showed minor changes upon colposcopy; histologically, 10.5% were determined to be CIN3. In the same study, colposcopically defined IVa-p (HSIL severe dysplasia) showed major changes in 79.8% of cases; however, histology confirmed CIN3+ in only 56.5% of cases. The discrepancy of 43.5% is surprisingly high in the IV-p (HSIL) group, exceeding that in the low-risk II-p (ASCUS) group. Another single-centre colposcopy study without documentation of colposcopic findings reported a histological prevalence of 8% CIN3 in stage II-p (ASCUS) and 63.6% CIN3 in stage IVa-p (HSIL); after conisation, the proportion of CIN3 increased to 70.14% 12 . In both studies, it remains uncertain whether the results ought to be deemed a limitation or a success from a histological perspective.

Immediate colposcopic/biopsy assessment in HPV-positive patients without a cytological diagnosis would result in a substantial loss of specificity and, in the long term, lead to overdiagnosis. For CIN2 lesions, there is also a risk of under-treatment.

The criteria outlined in textbooks (metaplasia, immature cells, glandular cell clusters) can be difficult to identify in everyday practice; assessment in such cases is more subjective and reliant on experience. If discrepancies are mainly attributed to cellular morphology (e.g., cell clusters, metaplasia, small cells), additional staining with p16/Ki-67 can enhance the final cytological diagnosis 13 14 .

Limitations

This is a single-centre, retrospective analysis based on routine data from a large, technology-focused private laboratory. We employ modern techniques and continuously evaluate them (some of which have been published), which, given the substantial volume of real-time cases, allows for a detailed analysis of the screening methods. A certain degree of variation in data collection is unavoidable; notably, numerous histological findings from dysplasia clinics at university centres are absent. Despite considerable efforts, a proportion of cytological findings remain without a colposcopic or biopsy correlate and could not, therefore, be included in the analysis.

With the current Nexus system, it is not feasible to track individual cytological groups dynamically over time; for example, it is not possible to determine what proportion of Group I/II-a (NILM) cases with persistent HPV-HR positivity in 2020 progressed to IIID2+ (HSIL+) by 2021/2022.

Conclusion

In a population with a low prevalence of the disease, we benefit from a highly specific, stepwise approach.

During the primary screening, very few CIN2+ lesions were identified in the group with normal cytology and HPV-HR positivity.

Analysis of these lesions indicates that, in the vast majority of cases, the main cause of discrepancy was the limited or complete inability to assess the cell pattern due to inflammation, blood, cytolysis, gel, etc. In cases where the cell profile allowed for an optimal assessment, we found no dysplasia even upon review. In such cases, the dysplastic areas are not accessed during the biopsy. A very small number of cases were considered false negative upon review.

When used in co-testing, HPV testing enhances sensitivity and aids routine diagnosis, particularly in cases where cytology reaches its limits (atrophic, haemorrhagic-degenerative, and inflammatory cell patterns).

The quality of the specimens could be more effectively described by adding a checkbox labelled ‘limited assessability.’ This would also enable statistical evaluation of quality defects in such specimens, even when discrepancies exist. Furthermore, the definition of a new cytological group with normal cytology but a positive HPV test (proposed Group I-h) would facilitate the identification of this patient cohort in routine laboratory practice and follow-up treatment according to the algorithm. It would also provide clearer guidance for all parties involved in the investigation and diagnosis of cervical lesions.

Einleitung

Seit der Einführung des zytologischen Screenings zur Früherkennung des Zervixkarzinoms im Jahr 1971 stellte die konventionelle Zytologie in Deutschland den zentralen Standard dar. Durch ihre breite Anwendung konnte die Inzidenz des invasiven Zervixkarzinoms deutlich gesenkt werden, was die Effektivität der zytologischen Diagnostik im Screening unterstreicht 1 .

Am 01.01.2020 trat mit der Richtlinie zur organisierten Krebsfrüherkennung (oKFE-RL) ein neues Vorsorgekonzept in Kraft. Das bislang opportunistische Vorgehen wurde in ein teilweise organisiertes Screening überführt. Neben der Zytologie kommt bei Frauen ab 35 Jahren nun der HPV-Test in Kombination mit der Zytologie (Co-Testung) zum Einsatz. Auffällige Befunde werden anhand definierter Algorithmen einer Abklärungskolposkopie (AKK) zugeführt 2 .

Diese Algorithmen orientieren sich primär an den Ergebnissen der HPV-HR-Testung und weichen in wesentlichen Punkten von bisherigen risikoadaptierten Empfehlungen ab. Nach der Münchner Nomenklatur III (MN III) ist eine zeitnahe Abklärungskolposkopie im Wesentlichen auf zytologische Befunde mit Verdacht auf CIN2+ (HSIL+) beschränkt 3 . Auch die S3-Leitlinie „Prävention des Zervixkarzinoms“ empfiehlt in vielen Fällen zunächst eine erneute zytologische Kontrolle, gegebenenfalls ergänzt durch Biomarker wie p16/Ki-67 4 . Die oKFE-RL sieht hingegen bereits bei HPV-HR-Positivität mit unauffälligen oder geringgradigen zytologischen Veränderungen eine Abklärungskolposkopie nach 3 Monaten vor. Diese Änderung des Vorsorgekonzeptes hat eine direkte Auswirkung auf die klinische Praxis. Von 884201 präventiven zytologischen Diagnosen entfielen 43179 (4,9%) auf Befunde der Gruppen II-a (NILM) bis V (cervical cancer), die häufig eine kolposkopische Untersuchung nach sich zogen. Allein 37643 (4,3%) Fälle gehörten den Gruppen II und IIID1 an, Befunde mit bekanntermaßen hoher Regressionsneigung ( Tab. 1 ). Damit stieg die Zahl der Überweisungen zu Dysplasie-Sprechstunden erheblich an. In vielen dieser Fälle wäre nach bisherigen Empfehlungen eine zytologische Verlaufskontrolle ausreichend gewesen, da das Risiko für CIN3+ in dieser Kohorte gering ist.

Stark entzündlich-hämorrhagische oder zytolytische Zellbilder und Benutzung von Gleitmittel, Gel etc. sind der Grund der Diskrepanz in 2 CIN2- und 2 CIN3-Fällen. In 23 (92%) der Fälle mit bedingt beurteilbarem Zellmaterial blieb die zytologische Diagnose nach Überprüfung in Gruppe I, nur in 2 Fällen von CIN2 ergab die Zweitsicht eine unklare Gruppe 0/II-p.

Die Kategorie „nicht beurteilbares Zellmaterial“ umfasst 6 Fälle (2 CIN2, 2 CIN3 und 2 EC). In dieser Kategorie ist die Reevaluation der zytologischen Diagnose wegen starker Zytolyse oder hämorrhagischem Zellbild nicht möglich. Die Zweitsicht ergab hier eine Gruppe 0/II-p. In der MN III 3 steht folgendes Zitat: „Eine unzureichende Präparatequalität ist auch in der MN III mit Angabe der Gründe als Gruppe 0 zu klassifizieren. Die Einordnung in die Gruppen I bis V dokumentiert die Brauchbarkeit des Präparates. Einschränkungen der Beurteilbarkeit müssen benannt werden: zu wenig Zellmaterial, unzureichende Fixierung, ausgedehnte artefizielle oder schwere degenerative Zellveränderungen, starke Zellüberlagerung durch Epithelzellen, Leukozyten oder Erythrozyten .“ Außer der Beschreibung der eingeschränkten Beurteilbarkeit im Befundtext gibt es kein zusätzliches Feld auf dem Zytologie-Schein, das auf den ersten Blick auch für den Einsender erlaubt, eine eingeschränkte Präparatequalität einzuordnen und für Fragen der Qualitätssicherung eine statistische Auswertung durchzuführen.

Diskussion

In einer gut gescreenten Population wie in Deutschland, in der die Prävalenz des Zervixkarzinoms per se sehr niedrig ist, sollte ein hochspezifischer Screeningtest beibehalten werden. Ziel ist es, die kleine Restgruppe mit der höchsten Erkrankungswahrscheinlichkeit zuverlässig zu identifizieren. Die HPV-Testung erhöht in der Co-Testung die Sensitivität und unterstützt die Routinediagnostik insbesondere dort, wo die Zytologie an Grenzen stößt (atrophische, hämorrhagisch-degenerative, entzündliche Zellbilder).

Besonders deutlich wird der Wandel der Screeningmethoden an der Altersgrenze 34/35 Jahre . Die Finderate für CIN3+ in der PSK-Kohorte der 35-Jährigen ist höher als in der PSZ-Gruppe der 34-Jährigen. Die Gründe dafür sind unterschiedlicher Natur.

Die gescreente Zahl der 35-Jährigen war um ca. 5,28% höher als die der 34-Jährigen; entsprechend wurden mehr Läsionen gefunden.

Die Frage, wie viele CIN3+-Läsionen sich in einer bestimmten zytologischen Gruppe finden, hängt direkt von der Abstrichqualität (wurde die Läsion erfasst?), von der eingesetzten zytologischen Methode in der Routine sowie von der internen Befundungsqualität ab.

In Niedrigrisikogruppen wie II-a (NILM) oder II-p (ASCUS) ist entsprechend nur mit wenigen Läsionen zu rechnen. In unserer Population liegt die Finderate für CIN3+ im Primärscreening und in der Gesamtkohorte 10 11 in der II-p-(ASCUS-)Gruppe unter 10% und in der Gruppe IIID1 (LSIL) bei etwa 13%. Deutschlandweite Daten zeigen ein ähnliches Bild 8 . Anders wäre die Bewertung, wenn 20–30% der niedriggradigen Befunde CIN3+ aufwiesen; dann müsste die Diagnostik grundsätzlich hinterfragt werden 1 . Insgesamt sollten sich die verfügbaren Methoden ergänzen, nicht verdrängen: Jede Screeningmethode hat in der Routine ihre spezifischen Stärken und Schwächen.

Die Registerstudie der Arbeitsgemeinschaft Zervixpathologie und Kolposkopie 6 berichtet in II-p (ASCUS) kolposkopisch von 54,8% Minor Changes; histologisch wurden 10,5% CIN3 festgestellt. In derselben Studie wurden bei IVa-p (HSIL severe dysplasia) kolposkopisch in 79,8% Major Changes beschrieben; histologisch bestätigten sich jedoch nur in 56,5% CIN3+. Die Diskrepanz von 43,5% ist in der IV-p-(HSIL-)Gruppe unerwartet hoch und übersteigt jene in der Niedrigrisikogruppe II-p (ASCUS). Eine weitere monozentrische Kolposkopiestudie ohne kolposkopische Befunddokumentation berichtet histologisch 8% CIN3 in II-p (ASCUS) und 63,6% CIN3 in IVa-p (HSIL); nach Konisation stieg der CIN3-Anteil auf 70,14% 12 . In beiden Studien bleibt offen, ob die Ergebnisse auch als Limit oder Erfolg aus histologischer Perspektive zu werten sind.

Eine unmittelbare kolposkopisch/bioptische Abklärung bei HPV-positiven Patientinnen ohne zytologische Diagnose würde viel an Spezifität verlieren und langfristig zu Überdiagnostik führen. Bei CIN2-Läsionen bestünde zudem das Risiko einer Untertherapie.

In Lehrbüchern beschriebene Kriterien (Metaplasie, unreife Zellen, glanduläre Zellverbände) sind im Alltag teils schwer erkennbar; die Beurteilung ist in solchen Fällen stärker subjektiv und erfahrungsabhängig. Wenn Diskrepanzen primär in der Zellmorphologie begründet sind (z. B. Zellverbände, Metaplasie, kleine Zellen), kann eine Zusatzfärbung mit p16/Ki-67 die zytologische Enddiagnose verbessern 13 14 .

Limitationen

Es handelt sich um eine monozentrische, retrospektive Analyse aus der Routine eines großen, technisch geprägten privaten Labors. Wir setzen moderne Techniken ein und evaluieren diese fortlaufend (teils publiziert), was durch das große „real-time“-Fallaufkommen eine differenzierte Analyse der Screeningmethoden erlaubt. Eine gewisse Varianz/Fluktuation in der Datensammlung bleibt unvermeidbar; insbesondere fehlen zahlreiche histologische Befunde aus den Dysplasie-Sprechstunden universitärer Zentren. Trotz intensiver Bemühungen verbleibt ein Anteil zytologischer Befunde ohne kolposkopisch/bioptisches Korrelat und konnte daher nicht in die Auswertung einfließen.

Mit dem aktuellen Nexus-System ist die dynamische Nachverfolgung einzelner zytologischer Gruppen über die Zeit nicht möglich; z. B. lässt sich nicht ermitteln, welcher Anteil der Gruppe I/II-a (NILM) mit persistierender HPV-HR-Positivität im Jahr 2020 sich bis 2021/2022 zu IIID2+ (HSIL+) entwickelt hat.

Schlussfolgerung

In einer Bevölkerung mit niedriger Krankheitsprävalenz profitieren wir von einem hochspezifischen, stufenweisen Vorgehen.

Während des Primärscreenings wurden unter der Gruppe mit unauffälliger Zytologie und HPV-HR-Positivität nur sehr wenige CIN2+-Läsionen festgestellt.

Die Analyse dieser Läsionen zeigt, dass in der überwiegenden Mehrheit dieser Fälle die Hauptursache der Diskrepanz die begrenzte oder keine Beurteilbarkeit des Zellbildes durch Entzündung, Blut, Zytolyse, Gel etc. war. In den Fällen, wo das Zellbild eine optimale Beurteilung zeigte, endeckten wir auch bei der Revision keine Dysplasien. In diesen Fällen werden die dysplastischen Areale bei der Zellentnahme nicht erreicht. Eine sehr kleine Anzahl der Fälle wurde bei der Zweitsicht als falsch negativ gestuft.

Die HPV-Testung erhöht in der Co-Testung die Sensitivität und unterstützt die Routinediagnostik insbesondere dort, wo die Zytologie an Grenzen stößt (atrophische, hämorrhagisch-degenerative, entzündliche Zellbilder).

Eine Vereinfachung zur Beschreibung der Präparatequalität wäre durch die Ergänzung eines ankreuzbaren Feldes „eingeschränkt beurteilbar“ zu erreichen. Dadurch wäre auch eine statistische Auswertung von Qualitätsmängeln solcher Präparate auch bei Diskrepanzen möglich. Außerdem würde die Definition einer neuen zytologischen Gruppe mit unauffälliger Zytologie, aber positivem HPV-Test (Vorschlag Gruppe I-h) die Identifizierung dieser Patientenkohorte in der Laborroutine- und Folgebehandlung gemäß dem Algorithmus erleichtern und würde mehr Klarheit für alle Beteiligten in der Abklärung und Diagnostik der zervikalen Läsionen bringen.
