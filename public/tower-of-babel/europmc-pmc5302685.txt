THE VIRULENCE OF TUBERCULOUS VIRUSES.*
By W. T. MUNRO, M.D., D.P.H., Medical Superintendent, Fife and
Kinross (Glenlomond) Sanatorium, and W. M. CUMMING, M.B.,
D.P.H., Assistant Medical Superintendent, late Assistant, Bacterio-
logical Department, University College, Dundee.
One HUNDRED "viruses" of tubercle bacilli grown from the
sputum in 100 patients of Glenlomond Sanatorium have been
examined by cultural methods,1 and two of these gave
dysgonic growths or conformed to the bovine type so far as
growth characteristics were concerned. All were examined by
serological methods, and all were agglutinable by the three sera
prepared by the injection into rabbits of an ether acetone
extracted emulsion of three organisms of the series. The three
strains used for the preparation of the agglutinating sera were
typical " human " viruses. All the organisms also were capable
of absorbing the agglutinins from the type sera.
At the same time one of us (W. M. C.)2 grew direct from
glandular lesions of animals 25 viruses from 28 specimens of
tuberculous glands, and conjointly we obtained 63 viruses from
human surgical lesions. Of these surgical viruses many of them
Were grown from patients in Glenlomond Sanatorium, while
other viruses were obtained by one of us (W. M. C.) from
specimens of pus sent to the Bacteriological Laboratory,
University College, Dundee, purely for examination and report.
A reference to Table I. shows the number of eugonic and
dysgonic viruses among each group isolated (a) from sputum ;
(^) from animal sources; (c) from human " surgical" lesions.
Eugonic strains are luxuriantly growing cultures and
practically mean a growth of human type bacilli. Dysgonic
strains are poorly growing cultures and practically mean a
growth of bovine type bacilli.

All the viruses obtained from surgical sources and from animal
sources were likewise tested serologically by the type sera
obtained by the injection into rabbits of eugonic viruses from
sputa. All were agglutinated, and all were capable of absorbing
the agglutinins from the type sera. Thus it was shown that
tuberculosis as it occurs in the human subject is caused by a
bacillus which, notwithstanding cultural variation and difference
in pathogenicity, is serologically monotypical to that found in
other mammals. This work has been confirmed by G. S.
Wilson.7 It remained to test the virulence of these organisms,
and the present article deals with the results obtained in testing
the virulence of a representative proportion of these organisms,
both dysgonic and eugonic.

Various animals react differently to the two mammalian
types of tubercle bacilli. Some, such as the common fowl, are
almost totally resistant to either type of bacillus. Others, such
as the ox and the rabbit, are highly susceptible to the bovine
type and highly resistant to the human type; while the guinea-
pig and monkey are susceptible to both.
(3) A. S. Griffith divides animals species into three groups
according to the way in which they react to the tubercle bacillus
of human and bovine types respectively.
(1) The first group comprises animals highly susceptible
to both types, and includes such animals as rhesus
and other monkeys, the guinea-pig, etc.

(2) The second group comprises animals which possess a
fairly high and equal resistance to both types, and
includes the rat, the mouse, and the dog.
(3) The third group is the differentiating group, susceptible
to infection with the bovine type, but highly resistant
to infection with the human type. This group
includes a number of species, among which are the
ox, goat, pig, sheep, horse, cat, and rabbit. The
rabbit is the animal, which on account of its
cheapness and convenience, and the fact that it
rarely is the subject of spontaneous tuberculosis,
is most commonly used for the purpose under
consideration. It is said to distinguish with less
accuracy than the goat or the ox, and on this
account may mislead, but for practical purposes it is
the most suitable animal.
Choice of Method of Inoculation and Dose of Inoculum.
?There are available to us four methods of inoculation?
feeding, subcutaneous, intraperitoneal, and intravenous. All
these methods have been tried out by various observers.
Feeding with bovine T.B. causes tuberculosis with a considerable
degree of certainty, doses of 10 mgrms. being sufficient.
Subcutaneous injection with bovine T.B. causes tuberculosis
which is progressive and that to a fatal issue; but the disease is
not so rapid as when the intravenous route is used, and, as a
rule, after 1 mgrm. bovine T.B., the average duration of life is
between two and three months. Intraperitoneal injections of
bovine T.B. are always fatal to the rabbit, and with very small
doses. Intravenous injections of bovine T.B. are also fatal, and
in very small doses, o-oi mgrm. killing in about four weeks'
time.
Intravenous injection is easy, the dose can be fairly
accurately measured, and the end result is fairly sharply
defined.
We decided to proceed with rabbits of approximately
1000 grms. weight, and to give 001 mgrm. T.B. by the intra-
venous route. In general we found that the bovine or dysgonic
virus killed these animals about the thirtieth day or thereby.
Those inoculated with o-oi mgrm. T.B. eugonic or human
type were not killed by this dose. In the series under
discussion, those rabbits which had been inoculated with

Lesions produced.?We decided only to observe macroscopic
lesions. Speaking generally, we found the bovine or dysgonic
type caused a generalised tuberculosis when inoculated intra-
venously in doses of o-oi mgrm., the tuberculosis being
progressive and causing the death of the animal. The
superficial lymph glands were enlarged, especially those of the
axilla and groin. Park4 considers these lesions as being
pathognomonic of bovine infection. The lungs were massively
affected. Sometimes they were much collapsed and the
tubercles might be small and closely packed together, or large
confluent areas were found. There was no tendency to break
down and no evidence of ulceration. The bronchial lymph
glands were enlarged and showed tubercles. The spleen was
commonly enlarged; sometimes very much so, and usually
thickly set with grey tubercles. The liver was sometimes
grey with tubercles and always showed some. The kidneys
showed numerous small tubercles and the abdominal lymph
nodes were slightly enlarged, and in some cases showed
tubercles. The whole picture was one of a progressive wide-
spread generalised tuberculosis.
What concerned us chiefly in assessing the virulence was
the ability of the virus to cause progressive lesions, and the
degree of generalisation was assumed to be an index of the
virulence.
Human, i.e., eugonic, viruses injected in the same way
produce no disease at all, or lesions of moderate severity in the
lungs and occasionally in the kidneys, but never a progressive
generalised tuberculosis.

Subcutaneous injection in doubtful cases has been resorted
to. Griffith has stated authoritatively that the subcutaneous
injection of human T.B. in doses of 10 mgrms. does not cause
fatal tuberculosis, whereas 10 mgrms. bovine T.B. produces a
fatal generalised tuberculosis in the rabbit. The two dysgonic
viruses from sputum have been examined in this way.

invites attention is the small group which show a marked
gradation in virulence from that of the typical bovine bacillus
producing such a rapidly fatal generalised tuberculosis in rabbits,
to the type of human bacillus which seems incapable of producing
disease in these animals in a dose of 001 mgrm. As already
mentioned, various observers have remarked on this feature, and
attention is directed to collected statistics on the frequency of
bovine infections in the human subject and to the remarks of
Calmette.
Calmette8 says "that in all these cases there is no certainty
whatsoever that one is not dealing with bacilli whose bovine
origin is relatively close. It may always be argued that these
human strains virulent for cattle had original characters which
were poorly defined or unstable, and that this is the reason why
the characters are still uncertain, and why between the human and
bovine types as defined there exists a whole series of intermediaries
which approach more or less either to the one or the other."
It must be a truth that infection will take place with a
frankly bovine or a frankly human bacillus, but it may be that
a bovine infection in the human subject adapts itself through
a series of generations to a human environment, and when the
infection has become visceral the organism found conforms
more to the human type.

Study of these figures shows that (i) bovine bacilli are very
rarely obtained from pulmonary lesions, but (ii) that they are
frequently obtained from glandular lesions ; (iii) that by selection
according to age groups and lesions one can show a distinct
age and tissue incidence of bovine infection. One may be
tempted to infer from these facts :?
(1) That it is possible that a frankly bovine virus may
become modified by prolonged stay in the human
subject, and if this were so it is probable many
bovine viruses will be recovered from the lesions of
young children, as indeed is the case. It would be
rash, however, to conclude therefrom that the bovine
virus does, in an appreciable number of instances,
become so modified.
(2) If it could be demonstrated unequivocally that naturally
acquired tuberculosis in man is, as is suggested by
some, always lymphatic in the first instance, and only
later takes on a visceral distribution, then a survey
of tuberculous lymph glands would give a better
index of the source of tuberculous infection in man
than would the investigation of material from any
other lesion. Unfortunately, unequivocal evidence
of the validity of this hypothesis has not so far
been adduced. If the hypothesis were valid, then
one would expect to find that the series of cultures
made from glands would exhibit a larger number
of strains of intermediate virulence than similar series
from other sources.
While the figures dealt with in the present communication
are too small to permit of conclusions being drawn, it is worthy
of note that they do not indicate a marked predominance of
cultures of intermediate virulence associated with glandular
lesions.
