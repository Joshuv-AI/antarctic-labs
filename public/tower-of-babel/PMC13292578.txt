Tracheal intubation in the critically ill: beyond the airway, toward hemodynamic precision
Authors: Jay Prakash, Tonny Veenith, Shio Priye, Bodhisatwa Choudhuri
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with interest the recent narrative review by Kotani et al., which highlights tracheal intubation in critically ill patients as a complex physiological event extending beyond airway instrumentation alone [1]. By emphasizing the sequential hemodynamic insults associated with induction, apnea, positive-pressure ventilation, and post-intubation care, the review provides an important framework for understanding peri-intubation cardiovascular instability and the concept of the physiologically difficult airway [1–3].

We believe this framework may be further strengthened through a physiology-based approach that classifies patients according to dominant physiological vulnerabilities during the peri-intubation period. Although preload dependence, vasoplegia, and right ventricular (RV) dysfunction are discussed, these states are not explicitly integrated into a bedside decision model. Such an approach may facilitate more individualized peri-intubation management.

Importantly, not all patients requiring tracheal intubation exhibit substantial hemodynamic compromise; some may undergo intubation primarily because of isolated respiratory failure. Moreover, overlapping physiological disturbances frequently coexist—for example, septic shock may involve vasoplegia, relative hypovolemia, metabolic acidosis, and evolving RV dysfunction simultaneously. Therefore, this framework should identify the dominant actionable derangement in patients at increased risk of peri-intubation cardiovascular instability rather than assign discrete categories.

Not all peri-intubation hypotension is mechanistically identical. Patients with vasoplegia, occult hypovolemia, RV failure, cardiogenic dysfunction, or severe metabolic acidosis differ substantially in physiological vulnerability [3–5]. In vasoplegia, abrupt loss of sympathetic tone following induction may favor pre-emptive vasopressor support. In preload-dependent states, positive-pressure ventilation may markedly reduce venous return despite apparently preserved pre-induction blood pressure. Similarly, worsening hypoxemia, hypercapnia, or airway pressures may precipitate RV decompensation [4, 6].

Severe metabolic acidosis warrants particular consideration. Patients with profound acidosis represent a recognized subgroup within the physiologically difficult airway, because loss of compensatory hyperventilation may precipitate severe acidemia and cardiovascular collapse [4, 7, 8]. Conditions such as diabetic ketoacidosis, septic lactic acidosis, toxic ingestions, and fulminant shock rely on compensatory respiratory effort for survival. Sedation, paralysis, or brief apnea may, therefore, result in catastrophic deterioration. In such patients, minimizing apnea and rapidly matching pre-intubation minute ventilation may be critical.

Another area deserving emphasis is point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) as a peri-intubation hemodynamic assessment tool. Focused POCUS may rapidly identify major abnormalities, including ventricular dysfunction, RV dilation, severe hypovolemia, pericardial pathology, or pulmonary edema, although feasibility depends on operator expertise and available resources [9]. Such information may better guide individualized use of fluids, vasopressors, inotropes, and ventilatory strategies.

We agree that the post-intubation period remains among the highest-risk phases of airway management. Hemodynamic instability frequently persists beyond tube placement because of sedation, reduced venous return, altered RV loading, and positive-pressure ventilation. Successful peri-intubation management should, therefore, be defined not only by airway placement but also by preservation of cardiovascular stability and oxygen delivery throughout the peri-intubation continuum.

To further translate physiology into bedside practice, we propose a simplified physiology-based framework summarizing major physiological vulnerabilities and targeted peri-intubation strategies (Table 1). Incorporating phenotype-based assessment, severe metabolic acidosis, and focused POCUS may help improve individualized and safer airway management in critically ill patients. As critical care increasingly embraces precision medicine, peri-intubation management should similarly evolve from protocol-driven interventions toward physiology-guided decision-making. Such an approach may improve not only procedural success but also meaningful patient outcomes.
