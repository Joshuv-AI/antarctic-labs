Distribution for nonsymmetric V-monotone position operators
Adrian Dacko 1 , {}^{1,\lx@orcidlink{0009-0004-9386-4445}{\orcidlogo}}, Lahcen Oussi 2 , {}^{2,\lx@orcidlink{0000-0001-9804-0761}{\orcidlogo}}

Abstract
. We investigate the vacuum distribution of a family of partial sums of nonsymmetric position operators, depending on a real parameter λ \lambda , and acting on the discrete Fock space in the framework of V-monotone independence. We analyze the combinatorics of the moments of this distribution, and using its Cauchy–Stieltjes transform, we determine its exact form, consisting of a unique atom and an absolutely continuous part. Finally, we present computer-generated graphs that illustrate the distribution for several values of the intensity parameter λ \lambda .

Introduction

In this paper, we study the vacuum distribution of a sum of noncommutative random variables, namely

(1.1)

S_{N}(\lambda)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{A_{i}^{+}+A_{i}^{-}}{\sqrt{N}}+\lambda A_{i}^{\circ}\right)\text{,}\qquad N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\text{, }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\text{.}

where
A_{i}^{+}
,
A_{i}^{-}
and
A_{i}^{\circ}
, for
i\in\mathbb{N}_{+}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
, are creation, annihilation, and conservation operators, respectively, acting on a V-monotone analog of the full Fock space, called the
V-monotone Fock space
.

A
noncommutative (or quantum) probability space
is a pair
\left(\mathcal{A},\varphi\right)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a unital
C^{*}
-algebra with unit
\mathbf{1}
, and
\varphi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
is a
state
, that is, a linear functional such that
\varphi(\mathbf{1})=1
and
\varphi(aa^{*})\geq 0
. Self-adjoint elements
a\in\mathcal{A}
are called
noncommutative random variables
.
A
distribution
of a noncommutative random variable
a\in\mathcal{A}
is a probability measure
\mu_{a}
on
\mathbb{R}
such that

\varphi(a^{n})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{n}\mu_{a}(\textnormal{d}x)\text{}

for each positive integer
n
. One can show that such a measure exists and is uniquely determined by the moment sequence
\left(\varphi(a^{n})\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
Let
(\mathcal{A}_{N},\varphi_{N})_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
be noncommutative probability spaces and let
(a_{N})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a family of random variables such that
a_{N}\in\mathcal{A}_{N}
for each
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. We say that
a_{N}

converges in distribution
to a random variable
a\in\mathcal{A}
as
N\to\infty
, if we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\varphi_{N}(a_{N}^{n})=\varphi(a^{n})\text{}

for all positive integers
n
, and we write
a_{N}\overset{d}{\to}a
. Since all random variables are compactly supported here, the above condition is equivalent to the fact that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x)\mu_{a_{N}}(\textnormal{d}x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x)\mu_{a}(\textnormal{d}x)\text{}

for each continuous bounded function
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
For more general tools and background on noncommutative probability and related topics, we refer the reader to
[
8
,
9
,
12
,
15
,
19
,
24
]
and references therein.

There are several notions of independence in this framework, the universal ones being: free independence of Voiculescu
[
23
]
, monotone independence of Muraki
[
14
]
, and Boolean independence
[
22
]
. There are other notions of noncommutative independence, where some of them are mixtures of these natural ones. In the present paper, we develop and focus on the theory of the notion of independence called
V-monotone
, introduced in
[
5
]
. It can be considered as a combination of monotone and antimonotone independence, which is best seen on a Fock space level. We now recall the definition of this independence.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
be a noncommutative probability space and let
(\mathcal{A}_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of subalgebras of
\mathcal{A}
, where
(I,\leq)
is a totally ordered set. We assume that there exists a family
(\mathbf{1}_{i})_{i\in I}
such that for each
i\in I
, the random variable
\mathbf{1}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
is an
inner unit
of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{1}_{i}a=a\mathbf{1}_{i}=a
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
.

Let
\mathrm{w}(I)
be the set of all finite words over the (possible infinite) alphabet
I
, i.e.,

\mathrm{w}(I)\coloneqq\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}I^{n}\text{,}

where
I^{0}\coloneqq\{()\}
contains only the empty word. Define

\mathcal{V}_{n,m}(I)\coloneqq\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in I^{n}:i_{1}>\cdots>i_{m}<\cdots<i_{n}\}\text{,}\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\text{, }m\in[n]\text{,}

where
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, with the convention that
\mathcal{V}_{1,1}(I)=I
, and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, set

\mathcal{V}_{n}(I)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\left\{()\right\}&\text{if $n=0$,}\\
\bigcup_{m=1}^{n}\mathcal{V}_{n,m}(I)&\text{if $n>0$.}\end{cases}

For
i\in I
and
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in\mathcal{V}_{n}(I)
, we use the notation

i\sim(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\text{ if }(i,i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in\mathcal{V}_{n+1}(I)\text{ and }(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\sim i\text{ if }(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n},i)\in\mathcal{V}_{n+1}(I)\text{.}

Definition 1.1
.

We say that the family
(\mathcal{A}_{i})_{i\in I}
is
V-monotonically independent
with respect to
\varphi
, if for any
i\in I
, we have
\varphi(\mathbf{1}_{i})=1
, and for any sequence
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in I^{n}
such that
i_{k}\neq i_{k+1}
for
k\in[n-1]
and for any
a_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{i_{n}}
, the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

\varphi(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})=0
if
\varphi(a_{1})=\cdots=\varphi(a_{n})=0
,

(ii)

for any
j\in[n]
we have

\varphi(a_{1}\ldots a_{j-1}\mathbf{1}_{i_{j}}a_{j+1}\ldots a_{n})=\begin{cases}\varphi(a_{1}\ldots a_{j-1}a_{j+1}\ldots a_{n})&\text{if $j\leq r$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

whenever
\varphi(a_{1})=\cdots=\varphi(a_{j-1})=0
and
r\in[n]
is such that
\mathcal{V}_{r}(I)\ni(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})\sim i_{r+1}
(without the condition with ‘
\sim
’ for
r=n
).

We say that random variables from a family
(a_{i})_{i\in I}
are V-monotone independent if there exists a V-monotone independent family of algebras
(\mathcal{A}_{i})_{i\in I}
such that
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
for each
i\in I
.

For more details on V-monotone independence, see
[
5
]
. Throughout this paper, we only consider
I=\mathbb{N}
with the canonical total order.

In many models of noncommutative independence, one can define an associated Poisson distribution with the intensity parameter
\lambda>0
. A Poisson limit theorem, also known as the law of small numbers, can be formulated as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\{a_{N,i}:N\in\mathbb{N}_{+},i\in[N]\}
be a family of operators with the following properties:

(i)

a_{N,1}\ldots,a_{N,N}
are self-adjoint,
independent
, and identically distributed random variables from a noncommutative probability space
(\mathcal{A}_{N},\varphi_{N})
for each
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,

(ii)

there exist constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that for each
i\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and any positive integer
n
, we have

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}N\varphi_{N}\left(a_{N,i}^{n}\right)=a^{n}b\text{.}

Then

\frac{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\overset{d}{\to}a\cdot\gamma(\lambda)\text{,}

where
\gamma(\lambda)
is a random variable with the Poisson distribution with intensity
\lambda>0
.

In noncommutative probability, this result was established, among other things, for free
[
21
, Theorem 4]
, Boolean
[
22
, Theorem 3.5]
, and monotone independence
[
14
, Theorem 4.1]
. There are other noncommutative analogs of the law of small numbers, for example
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
10
,
17
]
. Basically, in this type of Poisson limit theorem, one determines the weak limit (as
N\to\infty
) of the
N
th convolution power of the binomial distributions for a single trial (i.e., Bernoulli distributions) with the probability of success
\lambda/N
(certain generalization in
[
17
]
).

The combinatorics of limit moments of the Poisson distribution, which are expressed by means of certain class of partitions related to the notion of independence, is also studied. For instance, in the case of free independence, we have the class of noncrossing partitions (called admissible in
[
21
]
) and in monotone probability we have monotone noncrossing partitions (Muraki
[
14
]
mentions a kind of noncrossing partitions, but for a more precise description, see
[
10
, Theorem 8.1]
for
m=1
).

The second approach to the Poisson limit theorem, which we take here, is motivated by
[
12
, Theorem on p. 74]
. The construction using operator sum of type (
1.1
) was first established in the noncommutative case by Muraki
[
13
]
for monotone independence (see also Crismale et al.
[
3
]
for weakly monotone Fock space), by Oussi and Wysoczański
[
18
]
for bm-independence, and by Oussi for free independence
[
16
]
. In this paper, we extend the construction to the setting of noncommutative V-monotone independence by constructing an appropriate model on the associated discrete V-monotone Fock space. This leads to a new family of Poisson-type distributions reflecting the combinatorial and algebraic features of V-monotone independence.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we review some elementary information about noncrossing partitions of a finite set.

In Section
3
, we recall the definition of V-monotone Fock space and certain operators that act on it. Some properties of these operators are given, and their mixed moments are studied. These mixed moments are closely related to partitions, with a focus on V-monotonically labeled noncrossing partitions.
Section
4
contains our main results, the V-monotone Poisson-type limit theorem on the V-monotone Fock space. We study the vacuum distribution of the operator
S_{N}(\lambda)
. Its exact and limit moments are given combinatorially by means of V-monotone labeled noncrossing partitions.
In Section
5
, we investigate the moment generating function of the operator
S_{N}(\lambda)
with its limit distribution as
N\to\infty
.
In Section
6
, using the Stieltjes inversion formula, we obtain the limit measure
\mu_{\lambda}
of the nonsymmetric V-monotone operator
S_{N}(\lambda)
. Finally, using the
matplotlib.pyplot
module in Python 3, we generate some graphs illustrating the limit distribution for several values of intensity
\lambda
, and the position and weight of its only atom as functions of
\lambda
.

2.
Combinatorics of noncrossing partitions and V-monotone labelings

In this section, we review some foundational combinatorial concepts related to noncrossing partitions, with a particular focus on V-monotone labelings. These tools play a key role in the results discussed later in the paper. For further details, we refer the reader to
[
15
]
.

By a
partition
of the set
[n]\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, we mean a collection
\pi=\{B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{k}\}
of its nonempty and disjoint subsets whose union is
[n]
. Elements of a partition are called
blocks
. The set of all partitions of
[n]
will be denoted by
\mathcal{P}(n)
. A partition
\pi=\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\}
is called
noncrossing
if there are no elements
a<c<b<d
such that
\{a,b\}\subset B_{i}
and
\{c,d\}\subset B_{j}
for some
i\neq j
. The set of all noncrossing partitions of
[n]
will be denoted by
\mathcal{NC}(n)
(sometimes we write
\mathcal{NC}(n,k)
to denote its subset containing all partitions with exactly
k
blocks). A block
B\in\pi
is called
a singleton
if it contains exactly one element, i.e.,
|B|=1
, where
|.|
denotes cardinality.

The elements of a block
B\in\pi
are called
legs
. For a block
B\in\pi
that is not a singleton, the rightmost leg of
B
is defined as the greatest element in
B
, the leftmost leg is the least element, and the remaining legs are called middle legs. We denote by
\mathfrak{ml}\left(B\right)
the number of middle legs in the block
B
and by
\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi\right)
the total number of middle legs in the partition
\pi
.
A noncrossing partition
\pi=\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\}\in\mathcal{NC}(n,k)
in which every block satisfies
|B_{i}|\geq 2
for
i=1,\ldots,k
will be denoted by
\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n)
. We write
\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n,k)
when specifying that the partition has exactly
k
blocks.

We say that a block
B
of a partition
\pi
is
inner
with respect to a block
B^{\prime}\in\pi
and we write
B^{\prime}\prec_{\pi}B
if
l_{q-1}<\min B\leq\max B<l_{q}
for some
q\in[p]
, where
B^{\prime}=\{l_{0}<\cdots<l_{p}\}
for
p\in\mathbb{N}
. In that case, we say that
B^{\prime}
is
outer
with respect to
B
. If a block
B\in\pi
has no outer blocks, then it is called an
outer block
in
\pi
.
There is a natural partial order on the blocks of
\pi
, induced by the above relation, i.e.,
B^{\prime}\preceq_{\pi}B
if and only if
B^{\prime}\prec_{\pi}B
or
B^{\prime}=B
.

3.
Discrete V-monotone Fock space and related operators

In this section, we recall the definition of the discrete V-monotone Fock space
[
5
, Definition 6.6]
, which extends monotone and antimonotone Fock space constructions by incorporating “V-shaped” sequences. We also introduce the associated creation, annihilation and conservation operators acting on this space, and establish some of their fundamental properties that are essential for the analysis of the distribution of (
1.1
).

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space with an orthonormal basis
\{\Omega\}\cup\{e_{i}:i\in I\}
, where
\Omega
is the vacuum vector. Let

e_{\mathbf{i}}=\begin{cases}\Omega&\text{for $\mathbf{i}=()$,}\\
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{V}}(\mathbf{i})\cdot e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{n}}&\text{for $\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in I^{n}$, $n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}$,}\end{cases}

where
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{V}}\colon\mathrm{w}(I)\to\{0,1\}
is the characteristic function of the set

\mathcal{V}(I)\coloneqq\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{V}_{n}(I)\text{,}

where
\mathcal{V}(I)
is the set of all finite words over the alphabet
I
.

Definition 3.1

(Discrete V-monotone Fock space)
.

The
discrete V-monotone Fock space
, denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{vm}}^{\mathrm{d}}
, is defined as the direct sum of one-dimensional Hilbert spaces

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{vm}}^{\mathrm{d}}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{V}(I)}\mathbb{C}\,e_{\mathbf{i}}\text{,}

equipped with the canonical inner product. Recall that the set of indices
\mathcal{V}_{n}(I)
consists of all multi-indices
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in I
satisfying the V-monotone ordering condition. The vacuum state
\varphi
on
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{vm}}^{\mathrm{d}}
is given by

\varphi(a):=\langle a\,\Omega,\Omega\rangle,\quad a\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{vm}}^{\mathrm{d}}).

For each
i\in I
, we define the
V-monotone (left) creation operator

A_{i}^{+}
associated with
e_{i}
by the continuous linear extension of

A_{i}^{+}\,e_{\mathbf{i}}=e_{(i,\mathbf{i})}\text{,}\quad\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{V}(I)\text{,}

where
(i,\mathbf{i})
is the concatenation of the words
(i)
and
\mathbf{i}
.

Its adjoint, the
V-monotone (left) annihilation operator

A_{i}^{-}
associated with
e_{i}
, is given by the continuous linear extension of

A^{-}_{i}\,e_{\mathbf{i}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{for $\mathbf{i}=()$,}\\
\delta_{i,j}e_{\mathbf{i}_{0}}&\text{for $\mathbf{i}=(j,\mathbf{i}_{0})\in\mathcal{V}(I)$,}\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{i}_{0}\in\mathcal{V}(I)
and
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta. The
V-monotone conservation operator

A_{i}^{\circ}
associated with
e_{i}
is defined by its action on the vacuum and tensor basis vectors as the continuous linear extension of

A^{\circ}_{i}\,e_{\mathbf{i}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{for $\mathbf{i}=()$,}\\
\delta_{i,j}e_{\mathbf{i}}&\text{for $\mathbf{i}=(j,\mathbf{i}_{0})\in\mathcal{V}(I)$.}\end{cases}

The creation and annihilation operators are mutually adjoint, i.e.,

(A_{i}^{+})^{*}=A_{i}^{-},

and the conservation operator
A_{i}^{\circ}\coloneqq A^{+}_{i}A^{-}_{i}
is self-adjoint. Using the above definitions of operators, one can establish some of their basic properties.

Lemma 3.2
.

The creation, annihilation and conservation operators satisfy the following relations

•

A_{i}^{-}A_{j}^{+}=A_{i}^{\circ}A_{j}^{+}=A_{i}^{-}A_{j}^{\circ}=A_{i}^{\circ}A_{j}^{\circ}=0,i\neq j
,

•

A_{i}^{+}A_{i}^{+}=A_{i}^{-}A_{i}^{-}=A_{i}^{\circ}A_{i}^{-}=A_{i}^{+}A_{i}^{\circ}=0
,

•

(A_{i}^{\circ})^{m}A_{i}^{+}=A_{i}^{+}
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Using the same idea as for the bm-case
[
18
]
and
[
5
, Proposition 6.7]
, one can prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3.3
.

The algebras
\mathcal{A}_{i}\coloneqq\mathrm{alg}\{A_{i}^{+},A_{i}^{-}\}
generated by the creation and annihilation operators are V-monotone independent in the probability space
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is the
C^{*}
-algebra of all bounded operators in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{vm}}^{d}(\mathcal{H})
.

In this paper, we want to study the V-monotone Poisson-type limit theorem for a sequence of operators (
1.1
). In order to compute their exact and limit moments, we now study mixed moments of the form

\varphi\left(A^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{i_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot A^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{i_{n}}\right)\text{,}

where
\mathbf{i}=\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\right)\in I^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\left(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}\right)\in\{-,0,+\}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

First, we provide a necessary condition for sequences
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
for which a mixed moment of the above form is nonzero.

Definition 3.4
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathcal{R}_{n}
the set of all sequences
(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})\in\{-,\circ,+\}^{n}
that meet the following conditions:

(i)

\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{1})+\cdots+\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{n})=0
,

(ii)

\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{1})+\cdots+\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{l})\leq 0
, for all
l\in[n]
,

(iii)

\varepsilon_{l}=-
whenever
\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{1})+\cdots+\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{l-1})=0
, for all
l\in[n]
(for
l=1
, the empty sum equals
0
by convention),

where

\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{l})=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if $\varepsilon_{l}=-$,}\\
0&\text{if $\varepsilon_{l}=\circ$,}\\
1&\text{if $\varepsilon_{l}=+$,}\end{cases}

for
l\in[n]
. For
n=0
, we put
\mathcal{R}_{0}=\{()\}
, i.e., the singleton of the empty word.

Proposition 3.5
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
\varphi\left(A^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{i_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot A^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{i_{n}}\right)\neq 0
then
(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, be the closed linear subspace of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{vm}}^{\mathrm{d}}
spanned by all tensors of length
k
(we put
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathbb{C}\,\Omega
and
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\{0\}
for all negative
k
). Consider an operator
b=A^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{i_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot A^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{i_{n}}
. Let us first observe that the image of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
under
b
is a subspace of

\mathcal{H}_{k+\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{1})+\cdots+\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{n})}\text{,}

which can be easily proved by induction on
n
using only the definitions of creation, conservation, and annihilation operators.

Assume that
(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})\notin\mathcal{R}_{n}
. We consider three cases. If Condition (i) does not hold, then
b\,\Omega\perp\Omega
and the moment vanishes. If Condition (i) holds but Condition (ii) fails, then
r=\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{l+1})+\cdots+\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{n})<0
for some
l\in[0,n-1]
and
A^{\varepsilon_{l+1}}_{i_{l+1}}\cdots A^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{i_{n}}\,\Omega\in\mathcal{H}_{r}=\{0\}
. This means
b\,\Omega=0
and the moment also vanishes. If Conditions (i) and (ii) hold, but Condition (iii) fails, then
\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{1})+\cdots+\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{l-1})=0
and
\varepsilon_{l}\neq-
for some
l\in[n]
, which means
\varepsilon_{l}=\circ
. Since
\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{l})+\cdots+\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{n})=0
, we have

A^{\varepsilon_{l}}_{i_{l}}A^{\varepsilon_{l+1}}_{i_{l+1}}\ldots A^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{i_{1}}\,\Omega=A^{\varepsilon_{l}}_{i_{l}}\,(\alpha\Omega)=\alpha A^{\circ}_{i_{l}}\,\Omega=0\text{,}

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, and
b\,\Omega=0
. Then the mixed moment vanishes, which proves the proposition.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The set
\mathcal{R}_{n}
corresponds to a certain subset of the set of all Motzkin paths of length

[

Proposition 3.7
.

There is a natural bijection between sets
\mathcal{R}_{n}

\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n)

Proof.

For

()\mapsto\emptyset
. For

\mathcal{R}_{1}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(1)=\emptyset

(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
and let
m\in[n]
be the smallest number such that
\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{1})+\cdots+\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{m})=0
. Note that
\varepsilon_{1}=-
and
\varepsilon_{m}\neq\circ
from (iii). Then
\varepsilon_{m}=+
from (iii) and
\varepsilon\neq-
from (ii). For that reason, the sequence
(\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m-1})
(possibly empty) satisfies the properties (i) and (ii), but not necessarily (iii). Let
\{l_{1}<\cdots<l_{p-1}\}\subseteq[2,m-1]
for some
p\in[m]
(
\emptyset
for
p=1
) be the set of all integers
l
for which
\varepsilon_{l}=\circ
although
\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{2})+\cdots+\textnormal{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{l-1})=0
(by convention, an empty sum equals
0
). We put
l_{0}=1
and
l_{p}=m
, and define the set
B=\{l_{0}<l_{1}<\cdots<l_{p-1}<l_{p}\}
. For each
q\in[p]
, we have
(\varepsilon_{l_{q-1}+1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{l_{q}-1})\in\mathcal{R}_{l_{q}-1-l_{q-1}}
and
(\varepsilon_{m+1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})\in\mathcal{R}_{n-m}
. These sequences correspond to unique partitions
\pi^{\prime}_{q}\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(l_{q}-1-l_{q-1})
,
q\in[p]
, and
\pi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n-m)

Definition 3.9

4.
V-monotone Poisson type limit theorem

In this section, we study the V-monotone Poisson-type limit theorem for the sequence of operators (
1.1
), that is,

S_{N}(\lambda)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{G_{i}}{\sqrt{N}}+\lambda A_{i}^{\circ}\right)\text{,}\qquad N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\text{,}\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\text{,}

where
G_{i}:=A_{i}^{+}+A_{i}^{-}
is a Gaussian operator. We investigate its exact and limit moments

m_{n}(\lambda,N)\coloneqq\varphi\left(S_{N}^{n}(\lambda)\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{n}(\lambda)\coloneqq\lim_{N\to\infty}m_{n}(\lambda,N)\text{,}

respectively, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 4.1
.

The limit measure for
\lambda=0
is the standard V-monotone Gaussian distribution (see
[
5
, Corollary 6.8]
). The exact form of this measure was obtained in
[
6
]
.

The moments of
S_{N}(\lambda)
can be expressed combinatorially. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, let
\mathcal{LV}_{N}^{2+}(n)
be the set of all V-monotonically labeled partition
(\pi,\mathrm{L})
such that
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n)
and
\mathrm{L}\colon\pi\to[N]
. We denote by
\mathcal{LV}_{N}^{2+}(n,k)
the set of all
(\pi,\mathrm{L})\in\mathcal{LV}_{N}^{2+}(n)
such that
\pi
has exactly
k\in\mathbb{N}
blocks. Moreover, we put
\mathcal{LV}_{N}^{2+}(0)=\mathcal{LV}_{N}^{2+}(0,0)=\{\emptyset\}
. Note that
\mathcal{LV}_{N}^{2+}(n,k)=\emptyset
if
n>k=0
,
n<2k
, or
N=0
(with
(n,k)\neq(0,0)
).

We first establish the formula for the exact moments.

Theorem 4.3
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the moments of
S_{N}(\lambda)
with respect to
\varphi
are given by

(4.1)

m_{n}(\lambda,N)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{|\mathcal{LV}_{N}^{2+}(n,k)|}{N^{k}}\cdot\lambda^{n-2k}\text{,}

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. We have

m_{n}(\lambda,N)=N^{-n/2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\{-,\circ,+\}^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{n}}\left(\sqrt{N}\lambda\right)^{|\{l\in[n]:\varepsilon_{l}=\circ\}|}\varphi\left(A^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{i_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot A^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{i_{n}}\right)\text{.}

with
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})
and
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
. For fixed
\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{n}
and each
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\{-,\circ,+\}^{n}
, there exists at most one
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n)
such that
A^{\varepsilon_{1}}_{i_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot A^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{i_{n}}=b_{\mathbf{i}}^{\pi}
. Moreover, the total number of middle legs in partition
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}
is
\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi\right)=n-2\cdot|\pi|
. Combining these facts with Propositions
3.5
,
3.7
and Lemma
3.13
, we get

m_{n}(\lambda,N)=N^{-n/2}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n)}\left(\sqrt{N}\lambda\right)^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi\right)}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{n}}\varphi\left(b_{\mathbf{i}}^{\pi}\right)=\sum_{(\pi,\mathrm{L})\in\mathcal{LV}_{N}^{2+}(n)}\frac{\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi\right)}}{N^{|\pi|}}\text{,}

which yields (
4.1
).
∎

\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+4/N}}\right)\delta_{\frac{\lambda-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+4/N}}{2}}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+4/N}}\right)\delta_{\frac{\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+4/N}}{2}}\text{,}

for any
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

Our limit theorem is almost identical to the law of small numbers (Poisson limit theorem). The only difference is that in our case the first moment vanishes, whereas in the law of small numbers it is asymptotically equal to
\lambda/N
.

Proposition 4.8
.

The family of operators
\{a_{N,i}:N\in\mathbb{N}_{+},i\in[N]\}
has the following properties:

(i)

a_{N,1}\ldots,a_{N,N}
are self-adjoint, V-monotonically independent, and identically distributed random variables with mean
0
,

(ii)

there exist constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that for each
i\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and any natural
n\geq 2

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}N\varphi\left(a_{N,i}^{n}\right)=a^{n}b\text{.}

Proof.

The V-monotonically independence follows directly from Proposition
3.3
. The conditions for moments, with
a=\lambda
and

, are implied by (

), in which we replace
\lambda
by
\sqrt{N}\lambda
.
∎

Definition 4.9
.

By an
ordered partition
of a set

, we mean a sequence of blocks
\pi=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{k})
such that
\widehat{\pi}=\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
. Each block
B_{j}
has its unique
position
in
\pi
, i.e., the
order
of
B_{j}
which is the number
j\in[k]
, hence the name ‘ordered partition’. Note that for every partition
\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\}
there exist exactly
k!
ordered partitions that have the same blocks. Note also that the ordered partition
\pi
can be seen as a labeled partition
(\widehat{\pi},\mathrm{L})
, where
\mathrm{L}\colon\widehat{\pi}\to[k]
assigns to each block its position in
\pi
.

The set of all ordered partitions of
X
will be denoted by
\mathcal{OP}(X)
. For noncrossing ordered partitions, we use the same notation as for noncrossing partitions, with the prefix
\mathcal{O}
:
\mathcal{ONC}(n)
,
\mathcal{ONC}^{2}(n)
,
\mathcal{ONC}^{2+}(n)
,
\mathcal{ONC}^{2+}(n,k)
, etc., for
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
. For example,
\mathcal{ONC}^{2+}(n,k)
stands for the set of all noncrossing ordered partitions of the set
[n]
that have exactly
k
blocks, each of them having at least two legs.

For
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathcal{OV}^{2+}(n)
and
\mathcal{OV}^{2+}(n,k)
the set of all ordered partitions
\pi
of
\mathcal{ONC}^{2+}(n)
and
\mathcal{ONC}^{2+}(n,k)
, respectively, which are V-monotonically labeled as labeled partitions
(\widehat{\pi},\mathrm{L})
. This kind of ordered partitions will be called
V-monotone partitions
.

Theorem 4.11
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
n
th limit moment of
S_{N}(\lambda)
with respect to
\varphi
is given by

(4.3)

m_{n}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{|\mathcal{OV}^{2+}(n,k)|}{k!}\cdot\lambda^{n-2k}\text{,}

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. For a partition
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n)
, we denote by
\mathcal{LV}_{N}(\pi)
and
\mathcal{OV}(\pi)
the set of all
(\pi^{\prime},\mathrm{L})\in\mathcal{LV}_{N}^{2+}(n)
such that
\pi^{\prime}=\pi
and the set of all
\pi_{0}\in\mathcal{OV}^{2+}(n)
such that
\widehat{\pi}_{0}=\pi
, respectively. We rewrite (
4.1
) as

(4.4)

m_{n}(\lambda,N)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n)}\frac{|\mathcal{LV}_{N}(\pi)|}{N^{|\pi|}}\cdot\lambda^{n-2|\pi|}\text{.}

It suffices to prove that for each
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}
, we have

(4.5)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|\mathcal{LV}_{N}(\pi)|}{N^{|\pi|}}=\frac{|\mathcal{OV}(\pi)|}{|\pi|!}\text{.}

If
\mathrm{L}\colon\pi\to[N]
is not injective, then it has at most
|\pi|-1
different values and therefore there are at most
\mathrm{o}\left(N^{|\pi|}\right)
non-injective labelings.

Otherwise, there exists a unique ordered partition
\pi_{0}\in\mathcal{ONC}^{2+}(n)
with the same blocks as
\pi
, such that the block
B\in\pi
is on
r
th position in
\pi_{0}
if and only if
\mathrm{L}(B)
is the
r
th smallest number in the range of
\mathrm{L}
, for some
r\in[|\pi|]
. Since
(\pi,\mathrm{L})\in\mathcal{LV}_{N}^{2+}(n)
if and only if
\pi_{0}\in\mathcal{OV}^{2+}(n)
, there are exactly
|\mathcal{OV}(\pi)|\cdot{N\choose|\pi|}
injective V-monotone labelings
L\colon\pi\to[N]
. Therefore, we have

|\mathcal{LV}_{N}(\pi)|=|\mathcal{OV}(\pi)|\cdot{N\choose|\pi|}+\mathrm{o}\left(N^{|\pi|}\right)\text{,}

which leads to

(4.6)

m_{n}(\lambda)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n)}\frac{|\mathcal{OV}(\pi)|}{|\pi|!}\cdot\lambda^{n-2|\pi|}\text{,}

and, consequently, (
4.5
) holds.
∎

5.
Moment generating function

In this section, we derive the moment generating functions of the exact and limit distributions of

, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, that is,

M_{\lambda,N}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}m_{n}(\lambda,N)z^{n}\text{, }N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{\lambda}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}m_{n}(\lambda)z^{n}\text{, }N\to\infty\text{.}

We start with
S_{N}(\lambda)
for fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. The moment generating function is given recursively.

Theorem 5.1
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the moment generating function of the operator
S_{N}(\lambda)
has the form

(5.1)

M_{\lambda,N}(z)=P_{N,N+1}(z,\lambda)\text{,}\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\text{, $\lvert z\rvert$ small enough,}

where
\left(P_{N,l}:N\in\mathbb{N}\text{, }0\leq l\leq N+1\right)
is a family of functions

P_{N,l}\colon\left\{(z,\lambda)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:\lvert z\rvert<\min\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4\lvert\lambda\rvert}\right)\right\}\to\mathbb{C}\text{}

satisfying the following recurrence

(5.2)

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{P_{N,l}(z,\lambda)}=\\
&\begin{cases}1-\dfrac{z^{2}}{N}\cdot\left(\dsum\limits_{i=1}^{l-1}P_{N,i}(z,0)+\dsum\limits_{i=l+1}^{N}P_{N-i,0}(z,0)\right)&\text{for $\lambda=0$,}\\
1-\dfrac{z}{\lambda N}\cdot\left(\dsum\limits_{i=1}^{l-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{1-\lambda zP_{N,i}(z,\lambda)}-1\right)+\dsum\limits_{i=l+1}^{N}\left(\dfrac{1}{1-\lambda zP_{N-i,0}(z,\lambda)}-1\right)\right)&\text{for $\lambda\neq 0$,}\end{cases}\end{split}

for
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
l\in[0,N+1]
. We adopt the convention that, for
l\in\{0,1,N,N+1\}

0
, which means that

is constant and equals
1

where

is the number of all legs in
\pi

\mathcal{LV}_{N}(\pi)
was defined in the proof of Theorem

), where for a noncrossing partition

5.1

Indeed,

\left\lvert\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n)}\frac{c_{N,l}(\pi)}{N^{|\pi|}}\lambda^{n-2|\pi|}\right\rvert\leq\max(1,\lvert\lambda\rvert^{n})\cdot\left(\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}(n)}1\right)\leq\max(1,\lvert\lambda\rvert^{n})\cdot C_{n}\text{,}

5.2

Lemma 5.8
.

The function
s\mapsto P(x,\lambda;s)
fulfills the following integral equation:

(5.10)

\left(\sqrt{(1-\lambda x)^{2}-2(1-s)x^{2}}-\frac{x}{\lambda}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\textnormal{d}t}{1-\lambda xP(x,\lambda;t)}-s\right)+\lambda x\right)P(x,\lambda;s)=1\text{,}

where
s\in[0,1]
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, and
0<x<1/\lvert\lambda\rvert
.

Proof.

Let
n\left(\pi\right)
and
\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi\right)
be the numbers of all legs and all middle legs in
\pi
, respectively. For
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}
, we have
\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi\right)=n\left(\pi\right)-2\cdot|\pi|
. Define

Q(x,\lambda;s)=\sum_{\pi\in NC^{2+}}Q_{\pi}(s)\cdot\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi\right)}x^{n\left(\pi\right)}

and let us first prove that the following system of integral equations is fulfilled:

(5.11)

P(x,\lambda;s)=1+\frac{x}{\lambda}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\textnormal{d}t}{1-\lambda xP(x,\lambda;t)}+\int_{s}^{1}\frac{\textnormal{d}t}{1-\lambda xQ(x,\lambda;t)}-1\right)P(x,\lambda;s)\text{,}

and

(5.12)

Q(x,\lambda;s)=1+\frac{x}{\lambda}\left(\int_{s}^{1}\frac{\textnormal{d}t}{1-\lambda xQ(x,\lambda;t)}-(1-s)\right)Q(x,\lambda;s)\text{.}

According to Definition
5.4
, we have

\displaystyle P(x,\lambda;s)=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}}P_{\pi}(s)x^{n\left(\pi\right)}\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi\right)}=1+\frac{x}{\lambda}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}(x\lambda)^{p}

\displaystyle\cdot\sum_{\pi_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\pi_{p}^{\prime},\pi^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{NC}^{2+}}\Big(\int_{0}^{s}P_{\pi_{1}^{\prime}}(t)x^{n\left(\pi_{1}^{\prime}\right)}\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi_{1}^{\prime}\right)}\cdots P_{\pi_{p}^{\prime}}(t)x^{n\left(\pi_{p}^{\prime}\right)}\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi_{p}^{\prime}\right)}\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{1}Q_{\pi_{1}^{\prime}}(t)x^{n\left(\pi_{1}^{\prime}\right)}\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi_{1}^{\prime}\right)}\cdots Q_{\pi_{p}^{\prime}}(t)x^{n\left(\pi_{p}^{\prime}\right)}\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi_{p}^{\prime}\right)}\textnormal{d}t\Big)P_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}(s)x^{n\left(\pi^{\prime\prime}\right)}\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi^{\prime\prime}\right)}\text{.}

Applying Fubini’s theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle P(x,\lambda;s)=

\displaystyle\,1+\frac{x}{\lambda}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}(x\lambda)^{p}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\prod_{q=1}^{p}\left(\sum_{\pi_{q}^{\prime}}P_{\pi^{\prime}_{q}}(t)x^{n\left(\pi^{\prime}_{q}\right)}\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi^{\prime}_{q}\right)}\right)\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{1}\prod_{q=1}^{p}\left(\sum_{\pi_{q}^{\prime}}Q_{\pi^{\prime}_{q}}(t)x^{n\left(\pi^{\prime}_{q}\right)}\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi^{\prime}_{q}\right)}\right)\textnormal{d}t\Bigg)\cdot\sum_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}P_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}(s)x^{n\left(\pi^{\prime\prime}\right)}\lambda^{\mathfrak{ml}\left(\pi^{\prime\prime}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,1+\frac{x}{\lambda}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}(x\lambda)^{p}\left(\int_{0}^{s}P^{p}(x,\lambda;t)\textnormal{d}t+\int_{s}^{1}Q^{p}(x,\lambda;t)\textnormal{d}t\right)\cdot P(x,\lambda;s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,1+\frac{x}{\lambda}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\textnormal{d}t}{1-x\lambda P(x,\lambda;t)}+\int_{s}^{1}\frac{\textnormal{d}t}{1-x\lambda Q(x,\lambda;t)}-1\right)\cdot P(x,\lambda;s)\text{.}

The prove of (
5.12
) is similar, so we leave it to the reader.

To obtain (
5.10
), let us substitute

y(s)=\int_{s}^{1}\frac{dt}{1-x\lambda Q(x,\lambda;t)}-(1-s)

in (
5.12
). Multiplying it by
\lambda x(y^{\prime}(s)-1)
, we get

y^{\prime}(s)=\lambda x\cdot(y^{\prime}(s)-1)+\frac{x}{\lambda}y(s)y^{\prime}(s)\text{,}

obtaining the following initial value problem:

\begin{cases}(1-\lambda x-\dfrac{x}{\lambda}y)y^{\prime}=-\lambda x\text{,}\\
y(1)=0\text{,}\end{cases}\qquad y=y(s)\text{, }s\in[0,1]\text{.}

This initial value problem leads to the quadratic equation

-\frac{x}{2\lambda}y^{2}+(1-\lambda x)y-\lambda x(1-s)=0\text{,}

which, according to the initial condition and the fact that
0<x<1/\lvert\lambda\rvert
, has the following solution

y(s)=-\frac{\lambda}{x}\sqrt{(1-\lambda x)^{2}-2(1-s)x^{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{x}-\lambda^{2}\text{.}

Replacing

\int_{s}^{1}\frac{\textnormal{d}t}{1-\lambda xQ(x,\lambda;t)}=y(s)+(1-s)\text{}

in (
5.11
), after elementary transformations, we get (
5.10
), which finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

Using (
5.12
), we can also get

Q(x,\lambda;s)=\frac{1}{\lambda x+\sqrt{(1-\lambda x)^{2}-2(1-s)x^{2}}}\text{,}

which satisfies

Q(x,\lambda,s)=Q\left(x\sqrt{1-s},\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1-s}};0\right)\text{.}

It is worth mentioning that
x\mapsto Q(x,\lambda;0)
is the moment generating function of the limit distribution of the monotone counterpart of the operator
S_{N}(\lambda)
(see
[
13
, Section 5]
with a different notation).

Now, we are in a position to establish the moment generating function of the operator
S_{N}(\lambda)
. It is expressed in terms of the function
T_{0}\colon[0,\infty)\mapsto\mathbb{R}
given by

(5.13)

T_{0}(s)=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\bigg(\arctan\bigg(\frac{2s-1}{\sqrt{3}}\bigg)-\frac{\pi}{6}\bigg)-\frac{\ln(s^{2}-s+1)}{2}\text{,}

which can be written as the following integral:

(5.14)

T_{0}(s)=\int_{s}^{1}\dfrac{t}{t^{2}-t+1}\,\textnormal{d}t\text{.}

From that, we can easily see that
T_{0}
is decreasing and its range is
(-\infty,T_{0}(0)]
, where
T_{0}(0)=\sqrt{3}\pi/9
and the limit of
T_{0}(s)
as
s\to\infty
is
-\infty
. We define
T_{0}^{-1}\colon(-\infty,\sqrt{3}\pi/9]\to[0,\infty)
as the inverse of
T_{0}
.

Theorem 5.10
.

The moment generating function
M_{\lambda}
of the partial Poisson distribution with the parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is given by

(5.15)

\frac{1}{M_{\lambda}(x)}=\lambda x+(1-\lambda x)\cdot T_{0}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{(1-\lambda x)^{2}}{(1-\lambda x)^{2}-2x^{2}}\right)\right)\text{}

for
x>0
small enough.

Proof.

Let us first reduce the integral equation (
5.10
) to an initial value problem, proceeding similarly while solving (
5.12
). Namely, we substitute

(5.16)

y(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{dt}{1-x\lambda P(x,\lambda;t)}-s\text{,}

we multiply both sides by
\lambda x(y^{\prime}(s)+1)
, and we subtract
\lambda xy^{\prime}(s)
, obtaining

\begin{cases}\left(\sqrt{(1-\lambda x)^{2}-2(1-s)x^{2}}-\dfrac{x}{\lambda}y\right)y^{\prime}=\lambda x\text{,}\\
y(0)=0\text{,}\end{cases}\qquad y=y(s)\text{, }s\in[0,1]\text{.}

We now change coordinates

\xi=\frac{\lambda}{x}\sqrt{(1-\lambda x)^{2}-2(1-s)x^{2}}\text{,}\qquad u=\frac{\lambda}{x}\sqrt{(1-\lambda x)^{2}-2(1-s)x^{2}}-y(s)\text{.}

The inverse mapping is given by

s=\dfrac{\xi^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}-\dfrac{(1-\lambda x)^{2}}{2x^{2}}+1\text{,}\qquad y=\xi-u(\xi)\text{,}

with
\xi\geq 0
. We differentiate with respect to
\xi
, obtaining the following ODE:

\frac{x}{\lambda}\cdot u\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\xi}\cdot(1-u^{\prime})=\lambda x\text{,}

where
u^{\prime}=u^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}\xi}u(\xi)
. Therefore, the initial value problem takes the form

(5.17)

\begin{cases}uu^{\prime}-u=-\xi\text{,}\\
u(\xi_{0})=\xi_{0}\text{,}\end{cases}

where
\xi_{0}=\lambda/x\cdot\sqrt{(1-\lambda x)^{2}-2x^{2}}
, with
u=u(\xi)
and
\xi
between
\xi_{0}
and
\lambda/x-\lambda^{2}
. The ODE here is an Abel ordinary differential equation of the second kind, whose general solution, according to
[
20
, eq. 1.3.1.2.]
, has the parametric form

\xi=Ce^{T_{0}(t)}\text{,}\quad u=Cte^{T_{0}(t)}\text{,}\quad t\in\mathbb{R}\text{.}

The curve describing our solution must pass through the point
(\xi_{0},\xi_{0})
. Since
u=t\xi
, this can only hold for
t=1
for the suitable curve. Therefore,
C=\xi_{0}
since
T_{0}(1)=0
(see (
5.14
)) and the solution of (
5.17
) is given by

u(\xi)=\xi T_{0}^{-1}\left(\ln\left(\frac{x\xi}{\lambda\sqrt{(1-\lambda x)^{2}-2x^{2}}}\right)\right)\text{.}

In fact, we need only
M_{\lambda}(x)=P(x,\lambda;1)
, which, due to (
5.16
), can be obtained from

\frac{1}{P(x,\lambda;1)}=\lambda x\left(1+\frac{1}{y^{\prime}(1)}\right)\text{,}

where, using (
5.17
), the derivative of
y
can be expressed by

y^{\prime}(s)=\frac{\textnormal{d}\xi}{\textnormal{d}s}-\frac{\textnormal{d}u}{\textnormal{d}\xi}\cdot\frac{\textnormal{d}\xi}{\textnormal{d}s}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\xi(s)}(1-u^{\prime}(\xi(s)))=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{u(\xi(s))}\text{.}

That yields

\frac{1}{P(x,\lambda;1)}=\lambda x\left(1+\frac{u(\xi(1))}{\lambda^{2}}\right)=\lambda x+(1-\lambda x)\cdot T_{0}^{-1}\left(\ln\left(\frac{1-\lambda x}{\sqrt{(1-\lambda x)^{2}-2x^{2}}}\right)\right)\text{,}

which equals to (
5.15
) for
x>0
small enough. This proves our theorem.
∎

.

For
\lambda=0

the moment generating function for the standard V-monotone Gaussian distribution

6.
Limit measure

In this section, we obtain the limit measure
\mu_{\lambda}
of the nonsymmetric V-monotone position operator
S_{N}(\lambda)
using the Stieltjes inversion formula. Let
G_{\lambda}\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus\text{supp}(\mu_{\lambda})\to\mathbb{C}
be the Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of the limit measure. Of course,
\mu_{\lambda}
has a compact support since the radius of convergence of its moment generating function fulfills (
5.5
), which can be proven similarly as the aforementioned inequality. Using the formula

G_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{x}M_{\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\text{,}

we get

(6.1)

\frac{1}{G_{\lambda}(x)}=\lambda+(x-\lambda)\cdot T_{0}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{2}{(x-\lambda)^{2}-2}\right)\right)\text{}

for
x>0
big enough. Note that

(6.2)

G_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{G_{0}(x-\lambda)}{1+\lambda G_{0}(x-\lambda)}\text{,}\quad x>0\text{ big enough,}

where
G_{0}
is the Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of the standard V-monotone Gaussian distribution obtained and analytically extended in
[
6
]
.
Let us now recall all the necessary definitions and statements required to write the aforementioned transform. In the definitions below, we use the principal value of the logarithm and argument, i.e.,

(6.3)

\textnormal{Log}\,z=\ln R+i\varphi\text{,}\qquad\text{for $z=Re^{i\varphi}$ with $R>0$ and $-\pi<\varphi\leq\pi$,}

which is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
. Let
\textnormal{Arg}\,\,z=\Im\textnormal{Log}\,z
.

The suitable analytic extension of
T_{0}^{-1}
plays a crucial role in the analytic extension of
G_{0}
.

Definition 6.1
.

Consider a strip
D=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:-\pi/2\leq\Im w\leq 0\}
and define
T^{-1}\colon D\to\mathbb{C}
by

(6.4)

T^{-1}(w)=\begin{cases}T_{0}^{-1}(w)&\text{ if $w\in(-\infty,\sqrt{3}\pi/9]$, }\\
g(\eta,\xi)&\text{ if $w=H(\eta,\xi)\in D\setminus(-\infty,\sqrt{3}\pi/9]$, for $(\eta,\xi)\in\Theta$, }\end{cases}

where
\Theta=\{(\eta,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:-\pi\leq\eta\leq 0\text{ and }\xi<-\eta\}
is an infinite trapezoid and the functions
g,H\colon\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
are defined by

g(\eta,\xi)=\frac{\sqrt{3}i(e^{-\sqrt{3}(\eta+\xi)+i\xi}-1)}{1-e^{-2\sqrt{3}(\eta+\xi)}}+\frac{1+\sqrt{3}i}{2}

and

\begin{split}H(\eta,\xi)=&-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}\left(\textnormal{Arg}\,\left(e^{-\sqrt{3}(\eta+\xi)+i\xi}-1\right)-\textnormal{Arg}\,\left(g(\eta,\xi)-\frac{1-\sqrt{3}i}{2}\right)+2\left\lceil\frac{\xi-\pi}{2\pi}\right\rceil\pi+\frac{\pi}{6}\right)\\
&\ -\frac{\ln(\lvert g^{2}(\eta,\xi)-g(\eta,\xi)+1\rvert)}{2}+\frac{i\eta}{2}\text{.}\end{split}

It was showed
[
6
, Theorem 3.4]
that
T^{-1}
is a continuous extension of
T_{0}^{-1}
which is analytic on the topological interior of
D
. This is the inverse of some function
T
which is some extension of
T_{0}
. This
T
is not relevant here, however.

The following proposition was given in
[
6
, Proposition 2.6]
.

Proposition 6.2
.

The function
W\colon(\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\mathbb{R})\setminus\{-\sqrt{2},0,\sqrt{2}\}\mapsto\mathbb{C}
, given by

(6.5)

W(z)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{Log}\,\bigg(1+\dfrac{2}{z^{2}-2}\bigg)&\text{if $z\notin(0,\sqrt{2})$,}\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\bigg(\dfrac{2}{2-z^{2}}-1\bigg)-\dfrac{i\pi}{2}&\text{if $z\in(0,\sqrt{2})$,}\end{cases}

is a continuous extension of the function

x\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{2}{x^{2}-2}\right)

which is analytic on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

To apply the Stieltjes inversion formula, we first use the continuous extension of
G_{0}
(which is analytic on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
) to the maximal possible subset of
\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\mathbb{R}

[
6
, Definition 5.3]
.

Definition 6.3
.

Let

\gamma_{0}=\frac{2}{e^{2\sqrt{3}\pi/9}-1}

and let
G\colon\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\big(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\mp\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}\}\big)\to\mathbb{C}

(6.6)

G(z)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{zT^{-1}(W(z))}&\text{for $\Re z\geq 0$, $z\neq 0$, $z\neq\sqrt{2}$,}\\

\dfrac{\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{6}i}{4}&\text{for $z=\sqrt{2}$,}\end{cases}

and by
G(z)=-\overline{G(-\overline{z})}
, for

.

The result below was also proven in

]
(see the proof of Theorem 5.4).

Theorem 6.4
.

The function
G
has the following properties:

(1)

it is a continuous extension of
x\mapsto G_{0}(x)
(
\lambda=0
in (
6.1
)),

(2)

its restriction to

is analytic and is the Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of the standard V-monotone Gaussian distribution, which is supported on

.

We now find the Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of the limit distribution of

.

Proposition 6.5
.

The Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of the measure
\mu_{\lambda}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, has the form

(6.7)

G_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{G(z-\lambda)}{1+\lambda G(z-\lambda)}\text{}

for
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

Proof.

Since
G
restricted to
\mathbb{C}_{+}
is the Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of
\mu_{0}
, its imaginary part is negative and therefore, since
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the RHS of (
6.7
) is well-defined. Combining Theorem
6.4
with (
6.2
), we get our assertion.
∎

Corollary 6.6
.

The family of measures
\mu_{\lambda}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, fulfills the following condition

\mu_{-\lambda}(A)=\mu_{\lambda}(-A)\text{,}

for any Borel
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, where
-A=\{-a:a\in A\}
.

Proof.

We use the Stieltjes inversion formula. Fix
\lambda
and let
f_{1},f_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be the real and the imaginary part of
(x,y)\mapsto G(x+iy)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
y>0
, respectively. By the definition of
G
, we have

f_{1}(-x,y)=-f_{1}(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad f_{2}(-x,y)=f_{2}(x,y)\text{}

for any
x+iy\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Using this fact, we get

\begin{split}\Im G_{-\lambda}(x+iy)&=\frac{f_{2}(x+\lambda,y)}{(1-\lambda f_{1}(x+\lambda,y))^{2}+\lambda^{2}f_{2}^{2}(x+\lambda,y)}\\
&=\frac{f_{2}(-x-\lambda,y)}{(1+\lambda f_{1}(-x-\lambda,y))^{2}+\lambda^{2}f_{2}^{2}(-x-\lambda,y)}=\Im G_{\lambda}(-x+iy)\text{.}\end{split}

From the above equality, for any continuous bounded
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\begin{split}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x)\mu_{-\lambda}(\textnormal{d}x)&=-\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x)\cdot\Im G_{-\lambda}(x+iy)\textnormal{d}x\\
&=-\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(-x)\cdot\Im G_{\lambda}(x+iy)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(-x)\mu_{\lambda}(\textnormal{d}x)\text{,}\end{split}

which leads to the desired conclusion.
∎

Due to the above corollary, from now on we will consider
\lambda>0
only. Recall that
\mu_{0}
is the standard V-monotone Gaussian distribution, established in
[
6
]
. In order to use the Stieltjes inversion formula, using (
6.7
), we extend
G_{\lambda}
continuously to the maximal possible subset of
\mathbb{C}_{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 6.7
.

For
\lambda>0
, the equation

(6.8)

1+\lambda G(x)=0\text{,}\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\mp\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}\}\text{}

has exactly one solution
a(\lambda)<-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
.

Proof.

From Definition
6.3
, we see that
x\mapsto\Re G(x+iy)
is an odd function for any
y\geq 0
, whereas
x\mapsto\Im G(x+iy)
is even. Let us first show that the function
\Im G
is negative on
(-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}})
, which implies that (
6.8
) has no solutions on this interval.

By (
6.6
), we have
\Im G(0)<0
and therefore we consider
0<x<\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
only. The reader can check that the image of this interval under
W
is
(\mathbb{R}-i\pi/2)\cup(\sqrt{3}\pi/9,\infty)
, which follows directly from (
6.5
). That means
T(W(x))=g(\eta,\xi)
for some
(\eta,\xi)\in\Theta
, see (
6.4
). From
[
6
, Lemma 3.8]
, we have
\Im g(\eta,\xi)>0
, hence
\Im G(x)<0
for
x\in(-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}})
.

Let us first show that
G
is strictly decreasing for
x>\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
and takes all the values from
(0,\infty)
. First, note that the image of
(\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\infty)
under
W
is
(0,\sqrt{3}\pi/9)
, which follows directly from the definitions of
W
and
\gamma_{0}
, and
T^{-1}(W(x))=T_{0}^{-1}(W(x))
. Moreover,
x\mapsto W(x)
is decreasing for
x>\sqrt{2}
. Since
T_{0}
is decreasing by the definition, so is
T_{0}^{-1}
, therefore
x\mapsto G(x)
is decreasing by (
6.6
). We have
T_{0}(0)=\sqrt{3}\pi/9
and
T_{0}(1)=0
,
T_{0}
is decreasing and continuous, therefore the image of the increasing function
x\mapsto T_{0}^{-1}(W(x))
under
(\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\infty)
is
(0,1)
, which implies that

(6.9)

G(x)\to\begin{cases}\infty&\text{as $x\to(\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}})^{+}$,}\\
0&\text{as $x\to\infty$,}\end{cases}

i.e., the image of
(\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\infty)
under
G
is
(0,\infty)
. This implies two facts. First, (
6.8
) has no solutions for
x>\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
, since
\lambda>0
. Moreover, the function
G
restricted to
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}]
is a real-valued odd function, therefore it is strictly decreasing for

and the image of this interval is

∎

Corollary 6.8
.

The injective function
a\colon(0,\infty)\to(-\infty,-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}})
which is defined as the solution of (

) fulfills the following equation:

(6.10)

\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}+\lambda a+a^{2}}{a^{2}-2}\right)=\arctan\left(\frac{a+2\lambda}{\sqrt{3}a}\right)+\frac{\pi}{6}\text{,}\quad a<-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}\text{,}

where

.

Fix
\lambda>0
. The implicit form (
6.10
) of
a
follows directly from (
6.8
). Since
G
restricted to
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}]
is an odd function and

, we have

-\frac{\lambda}{a}=\frac{1}{aG(a)}=-\frac{1}{aG(-a)}=T_{0}^{-1}(W(-a))\text{,}

∎

Lemma 6.9
.

The Cauchy–Stjeltjes transform of
\mu_{\lambda}
defined on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\textnormal{supp}\,\mu_{\lambda}
has one simple pole
z=a+\lambda
. Moreover, the residue of the transform at the pole is

(6.11)

C_{\lambda}=\frac{\lambda\left(2-a^{2}(\lambda)\right)}{\lambda^{2}a+2\lambda+2a}\text{}

with
0<C_{\lambda}<1
.

Proof.

Consider an open disk
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{+}
centered at
a+\lambda
with the radius
0<R\leq-(a+\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}})
. Let
f
be the analytic continuation of

G_{\lambda}\restriction\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Im z\geq 0\text{ and }0<\lvert z-(a+\lambda)\rvert<R\}\text{}

to
D\setminus\{a+\lambda\}
. We can use the Riemann–Schwarz reflection formula to define
f
, since
x\mapsto G(x-\lambda)
is real-valued and negative for
x<\lambda-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
. Since
1/f
is analytic on
D
,
f
is meromorphic. The function
f
is of course a restriction of the Cauchy–Stieltjes transform of
\mu_{\lambda}
(defined on the completion of
\textnormal{supp}\,\mu_{\lambda}
) and has a pole at
z=a+\lambda
. Let us compute the limit of
(z-a-\lambda)f(z)
as
z\to a+\lambda
. Due to the meromorphicity of
f
, we can consider only real
z
and we substitute
x=\lambda-z
. The limit is equal to

(6.12)

\begin{split}\lim_{x\to-a}\frac{-(x+a)G(-x)}{1+\lambda G(-x)}=\lim_{x\to-a}\frac{(x+a)G(x)}{1-\lambda G(x)}=G(-a)\lim_{x\to-a}-\frac{1}{\lambda G^{\prime}(x)}=-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}G^{\prime}(-a)}\text{}\end{split}

Let us compute the derivative of
G
for real
x>\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
:

\begin{split}G^{\prime}(x)=\left(\frac{1}{xT^{-1}(W(x))}\right)^{\prime}=-G^{2}(x)\left(T^{-1}(W(x))+\frac{xW^{\prime}(x)}{T^{\prime}(T^{-1}(W(x)))}\right)\text{.}\end{split}

Using the identity
T^{-1}(W(-a))=-\lambda/a
, we have

\lim_{z\to a+\lambda}(z-a-\lambda)G_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{1}{-\lambda/a-aW^{\prime}(-a)/T^{\prime}(-\lambda/a)}\text{.}

Since

T^{\prime}(u)=-\frac{u}{u^{2}-u+1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad W^{\prime}(x)=\frac{2}{x(2-x^{2})}\text{,}

we have

(6.13)

\lim_{z\to a+\lambda}(z-a-\lambda)G_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{\lambda\left(2-a^{2}\right)}{\lambda^{2}a+2\lambda+2a}=C_{\lambda}\text{.}

Let
N
and
D
be the numerator and denominator of
C_{\lambda}
, respectively. Since
a<-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
, we have
N<0
. If it held

N-D=-a(\lambda^{2}+\lambda a+2)\leq 0\text{,}

we would have
\lambda^{2}+\lambda a+2\leq 0
, i.e.,
\lambda^{2}+\lambda a+a^{2}\leq a^{2}-2
. Since
\lambda^{2}+\lambda a+a^{2}
and
a^{2}-2
are always positive, the LHS of (
6.10

Define

G_{\lambda}(z)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{G(z-\lambda)}{1+\lambda G(z-\lambda)}&\text{for $z\neq\lambda\mp\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}$,}\\

Theorem 6.10
.

The measure
\mu_{\lambda}
,
\lambda>0
, has the form

\mu_{\lambda}(\textnormal{d}x)=\frac{\lambda\left(2-a^{2}\right)}{\lambda^{2}a+2\lambda+2a}\cdot\delta_{a+\lambda}(\textnormal{d}x)-\frac{\Im G_{\lambda}(x)}{\pi}\,\textnormal{d}x\text{,}

where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac measure at
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the absolutely continuous part of the measure is supported on
[\lambda-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\lambda+\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}]
.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda>0
. The continuity of
G
, needed to apply the Stieltjes inversion formula, follows from the fact that

\lim_{z\to\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}}1/G(z)=0\text{,}\qquad\Im z\geq 0\text{,}

which was shown in the proof of
[
6
, Theorem 5.4]
. Due to Lemma
6.9
, the point
a+\lambda
is an isolated point of
\textnormal{supp}\,\mu_{\lambda}
and it is an atom of the measure with the weight
C_{\lambda}
(see
[
8
, Proposition 1.104]
).

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function with a compact support such that
a+\lambda\notin\textnormal{supp}\,\phi
. For such function we have

\lim\limits_{y\to 0^{+}}\phi(x)\Im G_{\lambda}(x+iy)=\phi(x)\Im G_{\lambda}(x)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a+\lambda\}
, which implies that

\lim\limits_{y\to 0^{+}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x)\Im G_{\lambda}(x+iy)\textnormal{d}x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(x)\Im G_{\lambda}(x)\textnormal{d}x\text{.}

The convergence above is guaranteed by the Lebegue’s dominant convergence theorem with the majorant

\lvert\phi(x)\rvert\cdot\max_{z}\lvert G_{\lambda}(z)\rvert\text{,}

where the maximum is taken over
\textnormal{supp}\,\phi+i\cdot[0,1]
from which it follows that
\mu_{\lambda}
restricted to
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a+\lambda\}
equals
-1/\pi\Im G_{\lambda}(x)\textnormal{d}x
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6.11
.

The function
\lambda\mapsto a(\lambda)
has the following properties:

(1)

a^{\prime}(\lambda)=-C_{\lambda}
and thus
a
is decreasing,

(2)

\lim\limits_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}a(\lambda)=-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
and
\lim\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}a(\lambda)=-\infty
,

(3)

-\lambda-\min(\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},2/\lambda)<a(\lambda)<-\max(\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}},\lambda)
.

Proof.

From the Proposition
6.7
,
G\restriction\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\sqrt{2+\lambda_{0}},\sqrt{2+\lambda_{0}}]
is an odd function. Then

a^{\prime}(\lambda)=\left(G^{-1}\left(-1/\lambda\right)\right)^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{G^{\prime}\left(G^{-1}\left(1/\lambda\right)\right)}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{G^{\prime}(-a)}=-C_{\lambda},

where the last equality follows from (
6.12
). Moreover, since
0<C_{\lambda}<1
,
a(\lambda)
is decreasing, and this proves (
1
). The second statement follows from (
6.9
) since
a(\lambda)=G^{-1}\left(-1/\lambda\right)
. Now, since
a(0^{+})=\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}
and
a^{\prime}(\lambda)\in(-1,0)
, we have

-\lambda-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}<a(\lambda)<-\sqrt{2+\gamma_{0}}.

From the proof of Lemma
6.9
, we obtain
\lambda^{2}+\lambda a+2>0
. Combining it with (
6.10
) as in the proof, we get
a+\lambda<0
, which leads to

-\lambda-\frac{2}{\lambda}<a(\lambda)<-\lambda.

This proves the last statement.
