Biomechanical impact of odontogenic cyst–associated impacted mandibular third molars on mandibular angle fragility: a finite element analysis
Authors: Selman Arslan, Meltem Özden Yuce, Ugur Tekin, Sevtap Gunbay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImpacted mandibular third molars are recognized as predisposing factors for mandibular angle fractures. When associated with odontogenic cystic lesions, progressive bone loss may further compromise structural integrity; however, quantitative biomechanical evidence remains limited.This study aimed to evaluate the effect of cyst-associated impacted mandibular third molars on mandibular angle fragility using finite element analysis.MethodsA comparative three-dimensional finite element study.The primary predictor variable was cyst volume associated with an impacted mandibular third molar (no cyst [Model 1], small cyst [Model 2], medium cyst [Model 3], and large cyst [Model 4]).The primary outcome variables were maximum principal stress (P1), minimum principal stress (P3), and von Mises equivalent stress measured in the mandibular angle region.Loading direction (ipsilateral, contralateral, symphyseal), cortical surface location (buccal vs lingual), and predefined anatomical measurement points were included as biomechanical covariates.Four standardized mandibular finite element models were constructed with identical material properties and mesh parameters. A static 2000 N load was applied under three loading conditions. Stress values were recorded from twelve predefined cortical points in the mandibular angle region. Absolute stress magnitudes and percentage differences relative to the control model were compared to assess volume-dependent effects.ResultsCyst-associated models demonstrated volume-dependent stress amplification compared with the control model. Under ipsilateral loading, tensile stress in selected cortical regions increased several-fold in the largest cyst model, with marked elevation of both tensile and compressive stress magnitudes. Contralateral and symphyseal loading produced localized stress increases, although to a lesser extent. The mandibular angle, particularly cortical regions adjacent to the cyst, exhibited progressive mechanical weakening as cyst size increased.ConclusionOdontogenic cystic lesions associated with impacted mandibular third molars significantly reduce the biomechanical resistance of the mandibular angle in a volume-dependent manner. Early detection and appropriate management of cystic pathology may help prevent trauma-related angle fractures.

Introduction

Despite the mandible being the largest and strongest bone of the facial skeleton, it is subject to a high frequency of fractures in the context of maxillofacial trauma, a consequence of its anatomical position and structural characteristics. American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) database analysis highlighted that fractures of the mandible constitute the predominant form of isolated facial fractures [1]. Within the mandible, the angle is a region particularly prone to fracture because of its reduced bone mass and biomechanical stress concentration. Numerous studies have suggested that the presence of impacted mandibular third molars further weakens the region, serving as a predisposing factor for angle fractures. Furthermore, the risk of an angle fracture is also dependent on the M3 position [2, 3].

Odontogenic cystic lesions are frequently associated with impacted mandibular third molars [4]; these lesions, including dentigerous cysts, odontogenic keratocysts, and less common entities such as calcifying odontogenic cysts, often develop asymptomatically and may reach considerable size before clinical detection. Expansion within the mandibular bone can result in the thinning of the cortical plates, the resorption of the roots of adjacent teeth, displacement, and, in advanced cases, pathological fractures [5, 6].

Several clinical and biomechanical studies have consistently shown that the presence of impacted mandibular third molars increases the likelihood of mandibular angle fractures, with patients harboring third molars exhibiting approximately two- to three-fold higher risk compared to those without [2, 3, 7]. The presence of odontogenic cystic lesions may exacerbate this risk, yet evidence remains limited. Only a small number of clinical reports and retrospective case series have described pathological fractures associated with cystic lesions around third molars [8, 9]. Given the ethical and practical limitations of in vivo studies, biomechanical models are crucial for advancing our understanding of how cystic lesions alter mandibular fragility.

Due to the inherent limitations of conducting experiments on human subjects, finite element analysis (FEA) has emerged as a valuable computational method for simulating complex biomechanical conditions. Initially developed in engineering disciplines, FEA has been widely adopted in dental research for evaluating implant stability, fracture risk, and fixation strategies [10]. FEA facilitates precise simulation of mandibular geometry, material properties and load application, thereby enabling assessment of stress distribution under various conditions that cannot be directly studied in patients. Research utilising FEA has demonstrated that impacted third molars have the capacity to increase stress concentration in the mandibular angle during traumatic loading [11–13]. Nevertheless, the biomechanical impact of odontogenic cystic lesions associated with third molars remains to be the subject of comprehensive investigation.

The significance of this research lies in its capacity to serve as a key link between clinical observations and biomechanical evidence. While clinicians frequently encounter impacted third molars associated with cystic lesions, the decision to perform preventive extraction remains controversial. Proponents of the surgical approach posit that the procedure itself serves to mitigate the potential for complications, including but not limited to cyst formation, infection, and pathological fracture [14]. Conversely, concerns have been raised regarding the risks of inferior alveolar nerve injury and surgical morbidity during removal [15]. The use of biomechanical data can provide objective evidence to guide such clinical decisions.

The objective of the present study was to evaluate the effect of odontogenic cystic lesions associated with impacted mandibular third molars on mandibular angle fragility using FEA. The present study sought to clarify how these pathological entities alter stress distribution and fracture risk. To this end, three-dimensional models of the mandible with and without cystic lesions of varying sizes were constructed, and they were then subjected to simulated traumatic forces. The hypothesis was formulated that the presence of cystic lesions would result in a significant increase in stress concentration in the mandibular angle region, thereby compromising structural integrity and predisposing to fracture.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

The present study was carried out at the Faculty of Dentistry, Ege University, in collaboration with Ay Tasarım Ltd. Co. (Ankara, Turkey). Due to ethical considerations regarding the use of patient-specific data, no CBCT scans from actual individuals were utilized in this study. The mandibular model used in this study was constructed based on standardized anatomical geometry derived from previously published datasets and validated anatomical references, rather than patient-specific CT data. This approach was intentionally selected to enable controlled comparison between models while minimizing inter-individual anatomical variability. Although patient-specific models may provide higher anatomical fidelity, the use of a standardized model is a commonly accepted approach in comparative finite element studies.This approach enabled the simulation of a standardized anatomical structure without exposing patients to additional radiation or requiring individual consent.As this study was based solely on computational finite element modeling and did not involve human participants, patient-specific data, or animal experimentation, institutional review board approval was not required.

Meshing and material characteristics

The standardized mandibular model was transferred to 3D-Doctor software (Able Software Corp.), where geometric refinement and segmentation procedures were performed. No patient-specific CT data were used in this process. The models were then processed in VRMesh software (VirtualGrid Inc.) and subsequently imported into Algor software (Autodesk Inc.) as surface images in the .stl format.

The finite element models were discretized using three-dimensional tetrahedral elements generated through automatic meshing algorithms. A sufficiently refined mesh density was selected to ensure numerical stability and realistic stress distribution. All models were generated using identical meshing parameters and element characteristics to ensure consistency of comparative stress analysis between models. A formal mesh convergence analysis was not performed; however, mesh refinement was maintained at a level considered sufficient for stable stress distribution patterns in mandibular finite element simulations.

All materials were modeled as homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic, which represents a common simplification in finite element studies of the mandible for comparative analysis.The material properties assigned to cortical bone, cancellous bone, teeth, and soft tissues were obtained from previously published experimental and finite element studies and represent commonly accepted values in the literature. Explicit contact interactions between adjacent teeth were not separately defined in the present models. All anatomical structures were modeled as continuous bonded interfaces to enable standardized comparative stress analysis between cyst conditions.

In the present study, four mandibular models were generated. All models were constructed with identical structures, including tooth positions, enamel, dentin, periodontal ligament, cortical bone, cancellous bone, cystic epithelial wall, cystic fluid, and masticatory muscles. On both sides, the mandibular third molars were modelled as Pell–Gregory class III C, with equal amounts of surrounding bone [13].

Four distinct finite element models were developed to simulate the varying effects of cystic lesions in the mandibular region.

The lesion geometries used in this study were designed as simplified representations of progressive cyst-associated bone defects rather than exact simulations of the biological growth kinetics of a specific odontogenic cyst subtype. The selected expansion patterns were based on the radiological characteristics commonly reported in odontogenic cysts associated with impacted mandibular third molars, including progressive bone resorption, cortical thinning, and buccolingual expansion within the posterior mandible [4, 5, 16].

To account for the influence of masticatory muscles, muscle forces were simulated using spring elements that resisted tensile forces but not compression. The stiffness coefficients were defined as follows: anterior temporalis 14 N/mm, posterior temporalis 13 N/mm, masseter 16.35 N/mm, medial pterygoid 15 N/mm, and lateral pterygoid 12 N/mm.

The mandibular condyles were constrained in all translational directions to simulate articulation with the temporal bone, while allowing realistic stress transfer within the mandibular body.

Loading conditions

Each model was subjected to a 2000 N load applied through cylindrical contact areas (1 cm in diameter) at the symphysis, ipsilateral, and contralateral regions (Fig. 2). The selected loading magnitude and application sites were based on previously published finite element studies investigating mandibular angle fragility under traumatic conditions. Ipsilateral, contralateral, and symphyseal loading directions were selected to represent clinically relevant patterns of force transmission during mandibular trauma.

Results

The obtained values were systematically compared across the four models in order to determine the influence of cyst size and location on mandibular stress distribution. Differences between ipsilateral–contralateral and buccal–lingual cortical regions were examined to identify areas of stress concentration and possible fracture susceptibility This approach enabled the detection of localized stress concentration zones, which may indicate regions of increased susceptibility to fracture or deformation. These trends were consistently observed across different regions and loading conditions, indicating a progressive and model-dependent increase in stress distribution.Principal stress values are presented as signed values throughout the Results section. Accordingly, positive values indicate tensile stress, whereas negative values indicate compressive stress; therefore, more negative P3 values reflect greater compressive stress magnitude.

In all four models, twelve identical points were designated on the buccal and lingual cortical bone of the right and left mandibular angle regions. Peak maximum principal stress (P1), minimum principal stress (P3), and von Mises stress values were recorded at each predefined point under all loading conditions. Differences between models were evaluated by comparing absolute stress magnitudes and calculating relative percentage changes from the control model (Model 1) to cyst-associated models (Models 2–4). Particular attention was given to regions demonstrating progressive stress concentration with increasing cyst volume, especially those adjacent to the cystic cavity and the inferior border of the mandibular angle, as these areas were considered biomechanically relevant to fracture initiation.

Ipsilateral loading

Under ipsilateral loading, the most pronounced stress escalation was observed in the right mandibular angle.

In the buccal cortex, P1 demonstrated substantial increases with enlarging cyst volume. The highest P1 value was recorded at Region 2, rising from 2.88 MPa in Model 1 to 22.99 MPa in Model 4, corresponding to an approximate 698% increase. Similarly, Region 5 exhibited an increase from 3.19 MPa to 22.29 MPa (~ 598% increase).

P3 values also increased in magnitude. In Region 1, P3 changed from − 42.92 MPa to − 92.59 MPa, indicating a marked increase in compressive stress magnitude (Table 2).

Overall, although contralateral loading produced relevant stress amplification, the magnitude and spatial extent were less pronounced than in ipsilateral loading.

Symphyseal loading

Symphyseal loading generated intermediate stress responses.

Discussion

This study evaluated the biomechanical impact of odontogenic cystic lesions associated with impacted mandibular third molars on mandibular angle fragility using finite FEA. The results demonstrated that the presence of cystic lesions significantly increased stress concentration in the mandibular angle, with greater lesion volumes correlating with higher fragility. Ipsilateral loading produced the most pronounced weakening effect, reflecting the typical pattern of trauma observed in clinical settings. Ipsilateral impact transfers force directly through the structurally compromised angle region where cyst-induced cortical thinning reduces resistance to bending and shear stresses. Consequently, higher tensile and compressive peaks are observed locally compared to contralateral or symphyseal loading. In the present models, stress amplification with increasing cyst size was evident, with peak tensile values in selected regions increasing several-fold from Model 1 to Model 4. Additionally, the buccal cortex of the mandibular angle was identified as a vulnerable site under all loading conditions. This may be due to its relatively thin cortical structure and its role as the primary tensile surface during lateral bending of the mandible [17]. This biomechanical behaviour suggests that cyst-associated weakening of the buccal cortex could be a critical point at which mandibular angle fractures begin to form under traumatic loading. This provides a plausible explanation for the fracture patterns observed clinically in this region.

To improve the interpretability of the findings and to align with clinically relevant fracture patterns, specific regions of interest (ROI) were considered in the present analysis. Based on the observed stress distribution patterns, stress concentrations were predominantly localized in areas adjacent to the cystic cavity, particularly in the proximal region of the inferior wall of the lesion and along the inferior border of the mandibular angle. These regions represent biomechanically vulnerable zones where fracture initiation is more likely to occur. Focusing on these localized stress concentration areas allows a more clinically meaningful interpretation of fracture risk compared to global assessment of stress distribution [18].

Some researchers have advocated prophylactic extraction of impacted mandibular third molars to reduce the risk of infection and mandibular fracture, whereas others argue that removal of asymptomatic third molars may be unnecessary and could increase the risk of condylar fractures [7].Antic et al., in a finite element study, reported that the presence of impacted mandibular third molars decreases the risk of condylar fracture while increasing susceptibility to angle fractures [13].Similarly, Bezerra et al. demonstrated altered stress distribution patterns in the mandibular angle when third molars were present [19]. Takada et al., using FEA, observed localized stress concentration around impacted third molar roots under traumatic loading [20]. The present findings are consistent with these observations and further extend the existing literature by demonstrating that cyst-associated volumetric bone loss amplifies stress concentration beyond that observed with impacted teeth alone. While the prophylactic removal of asymptomatic third molars remains controversial, the current results suggest that cystic pathology represents an additional biomechanical risk factor that may justify earlier surgical intervention.

Odontogenic cystic lesions, particularly dentigerous cysts and odontogenic keratocysts, are the most common pathological entities associated with impacted third molars [21]. These lesions typically expand slowly and may cause cortical thinning, root resorption, displacement of adjacent teeth, or pathological fractures. Clinical reports have described mandibular fractures arising from cystic lesions; however, comprehensive biomechanical investigations remain limited [22, 23]. The present study provides quantitative support for these clinical observations by demonstrating a clear relationship between cyst volume and stress amplification within the mandibular angle. As cyst dimensions increased, both tensile and compressive stresses intensified, indicating progressive deterioration of mechanical resistance.

FEA has been widely validated as a reliable method for simulating mandibular biomechanics. Vollmer et al. demonstrated a strong correlation between experimental mechanical testing and finite element predictions, supporting its application in fracture risk assessment [24]. More recent studies have employed FEA to evaluate implant biomechanics, orthognathic surgical planning, and fixation strategies for mandibular fractures [10]. The present study extends these applications by focusing specifically on cyst-associated structural weakening of the mandibular angle, an area that has received limited biomechanical attention. By modeling progressive volumetric bone loss, our approach provides a controlled framework for isolating the mechanical consequences of cyst expansion.

Previous finite element studies have investigated stress distribution in the mandible under various conditions. For instance, Liu et al. reported that impacted third molars significantly influence stress concentration patterns in the mandibular angle under traumatic loading conditions [11]. Similarly, biomechanical analyses of cystic mandibular defects have demonstrated that residual bone thickness plays a critical role in determining fracture susceptibility [25].

Previous finite element studies have also demonstrated that mandibular strength significantly decreases following surgical removal of cystic lesions associated with impacted third molars. Murakami et al. reported a substantial increase in von Mises stress after virtual removal of the cyst and tooth, particularly in the buccal cortical bone surrounding the defect area, indicating an increased risk of pathological fracture. Furthermore, they identified specific regions of stress concentration around the fenestrated area, supporting the concept that fracture risk is closely associated with localized stress accumulation rather than global stress distribution [18].

However, most existing studies have evaluated either impacted third molars or cystic defects independently. In contrast, the present study specifically investigates cyst-associated impacted mandibular third molars and analyzes the combined effect of increasing cyst volume under different loading conditions. This approach provides a more comprehensive biomechanical assessment of mandibular fragility in clinically relevant scenarios.

From a clinical perspective, the management of asymptomatic impacted mandibular third molars remains controversial. Mercier and Precious emphasized the need to balance potential benefits of removal against surgical morbidity, while Dodson discussed contemporary decision-making strategies in the absence of symptoms [14, 26]. The present findings do not advocate routine prophylactic extraction; however, they suggest that the presence of cystic pathology represents a distinct biomechanical risk factor. The marked stress amplification observed in larger cyst models indicates that progressive bone loss may substantially reduce resistance to traumatic forces. Therefore, when radiographic evidence of cyst formation is present, early surgical management may be considered to prevent further structural weakening of the mandibular angle.

It should be noted that the evaluation of fracture risk cannot rely solely on relative increases in stress values. Cortical bone exhibits tensile strength generally ranging between approximately 90–135 MPa and compressive strength between approximately 150–205 MPa [27]. In the present study, peak tensile stress values observed in the largest cyst model approached or exceeded the lower range of reported cortical bone tensile strength in certain localized regions, particularly under ipsilateral loading conditions. Finite element stress concentrations, however, cannot be interpreted as direct predictors of fracture occurrence, and these findings should instead be considered indicators of biomechanical vulnerability. Although progressive stress amplification was observed with increasing cyst volume, the distribution and magnitude of peak stresses varied substantially according to loading direction and anatomical region. Some localized regions approached cortical bone strength thresholds, whereas others remained considerably lower despite similar cyst dimensions. Therefore, the present findings do not support the definition of a single clinically meaningful cutoff value for cyst size or fracture risk within the limitations of this finite element model.

Although a high-magnitude traumatic load of 2000 N was applied in the present simulations, stress distribution within the mandible was not uniform and varied substantially according to anatomical region and loading direction. Previous biomechanical and finite element studies have similarly demonstrated that mandibular stress transfer is highly heterogeneous and influenced by mandibular geometry, cortical thickness, and loading direction [11, 17, 28]. Because the applied force was distributed through defined contact areas and transferred throughout the mandibular structure, localized stress magnitudes remained relatively lower in some regions while marked stress concentration developed in biomechanically vulnerable areas adjacent to the cystic cavity and mandibular angle. Therefore, the observed stress patterns were considered biomechanically consistent with previously reported mandibular load-transfer behavior under traumatic conditions.

Furthermore, bone is a complex, anisotropic, and heterogeneous structure, and fracture initiation is influenced by multiple factors beyond stress magnitude alone, including local geometry, loading direction, and microstructural characteristics. Therefore, stress concentration should be interpreted as an indicator of biomechanical vulnerability rather than a direct predictor of fracture occurrence.

From a clinical perspective, the findings of this study suggest that fracture risk is not solely dependent on defect size but is also influenced by the direction and type of loading as previous finite element studies have demonstrated that loading conditions, including occlusal state and impact direction, significantly alter stress distribution patterns in the mandible [28].

Consistent with previous biomechanical studies, residual bone height has been identified as a critical determinant of mandibular strength, with reduced residual height significantly increasing stress concentration and susceptibility to pathologic fracture. Furthermore, regions of maximal stress concentration have been shown to correspond closely with actual fracture sites, emphasizing the importance of localized stress analysis in predicting fracture risk [29].

Similarly, finite element analyses of cystic mandibular lesions have demonstrated that progressive cyst enlargement leads to thinning of the surrounding cortical bone and a marked increase in localized stress and strain energy density around the lesion. These stress concentrations, generated by both tensile and compressive components, are predominantly localized in the vicinity of the cyst and may represent potential sites of fracture initiation [25].

In particular, ipsilateral loading conditions produced the highest stress concentrations in the mandibular angle, indicating a greater susceptibility to fracture under trauma originating from the same side. From a biomechanical perspective, the present findings further indicate that fracture risk is influenced not only by cyst size but also by the direction of loading. In particular, ipsilateral loading conditions produced the highest stress concentrations in the mandibular angle, suggesting increased susceptibility to fracture under trauma originating from the same side. Additionally, variations between buccal and lingual cortical stress patterns indicate that fracture initiation sites may differ depending on loading direction and anatomical configuration.

Furthermore, stress distribution patterns varied between buccal and lingual cortical regions, suggesting that fracture initiation sites may differ depending on loading direction and anatomical configuration. These findings highlight the importance of considering trauma direction, occlusal conditions, and individual anatomical variability when assessing fracture risk in patients with cyst-associated impacted third molars.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations

The use of linear elastic analysis represents a simplification of actual mandibular biomechanics, particularly under high-magnitude loading conditions where nonlinear material behavior may occur. However, because all models were constructed using identical material assumptions and loading parameters, the comparative stress distribution patterns observed between models remain biomechanically meaningful. Future studies incorporating nonlinear analysis may provide more realistic simulation of mandibular fracture behavior.

The loading conditions applied in this study represent high-magnitude external forces simulating traumatic scenarios. In clinical practice, pathological fractures may also occur under physiological or occlusal loading, particularly following surgical removal of cystic lesions and impacted third molars. Therefore, future studies incorporating occlusal loading conditions and post-extraction models may provide additional clinically relevant insights into cyst-associated mandibular weakening.

In addition to these considerations, certain modeling assumptions should also be acknowledged. The cyst geometries used in this study were simplified and idealized to represent progressive cyst-associated volumetric bone loss rather than the exact biological growth behavior of a specific odontogenic cyst subtype. Therefore, the primary aim of the model was to evaluate the biomechanical consequences of progressive structural weakening under standardized conditions while minimizing anatomical variability between models. The selected expansion patterns were based on radiological characteristics commonly reported in odontogenic cysts involving impacted mandibular third molars, including cortical thinning, expansile bone resorption, and mesiodistal growth tendency within the posterior mandible.

Bone tissue was modeled as homogeneous and isotropic, whereas actual mandibular bone exhibits anisotropic and heterogeneous characteristics that may influence absolute stress magnitudes.In addition, all materials were assumed to be linearly elastic. This simplification does not fully reflect the complex anisotropic and viscoelastic behavior of biological tissues, particularly muscles and fluids.In particular, the cystic content and surrounding soft tissues may demonstrate time-dependent viscoelastic behavior under physiological conditions. However, linear elastic assumptions are commonly adopted in comparative mandibular finite element analyses to enable numerical stability and standardized comparison between models.Although no formal validation or sensitivity analysis was performed, all models were constructed using identical material properties, mesh parameters, and boundary conditions. Therefore, the comparative differences observed between models can be attributed primarily to variations in cyst size rather than modeling assumptions.

Future research should focus on patient-specific models generated from cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) to enhance anatomical fidelity. Incorporating dynamic loading and muscle forces would further approximate real clinical scenarios. Prospective clinical studies correlating radiographic cyst size with fracture outcomes could also validate and expand upon the biomechanical evidence presented here.

Recent advances in computational biomechanics have introduced more sophisticated fracture modelling approaches, such as the extended finite element method (XFEM), which enables simulation of crack initiation and propagation in dental and maxillofacial structures. Recent studies have successfully applied XFEM in dental biomechanics and implant-related fracture analyses [30, 31]. However, the present study was designed primarily to evaluate stress distribution patterns and biomechanical weakening associated with cyst-related bone loss rather than direct fracture propagation behaviour. Future investigations incorporating XFEM-based approaches may provide additional insight into crack initiation and fracture progression in weakened mandibular structures.

Conclusion

This FEA study demonstrated that odontogenic cystic lesions associated with impacted mandibular third molars significantly compromise the biomechanical integrity of the mandibular angle. Larger cyst volumes resulted in greater stress concentration and higher fragility, particularly under ipsilateral traumatic loading. These findings underscore the importance of early detection and preventive management of impacted third molars with cystic pathology to reduce the risk of trauma-related mandibular fractures. Although increasing cyst size was associated with higher stress concentrations, a definitive clinical threshold for fracture risk could not be established within the scope of this FEA. Future studies integrating biomechanical findings with clinical outcomes are needed to define clinically meaningful cutoff values.
