Evaluation of augmented reality for interstitial brachytherapy optimisation: a prospective phantom-based study protocol and preliminary results
Authors: Artur J. Chyrek, Adam Kluska, Grzegorz Bielęda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterstitial brachytherapy enables highly conformal tumor irradiation while limiting exposure of surrounding tissues, but its successful execution requires advanced spatial orientation and manual precision. Augmented reality (AR) may support these procedures by providing three-dimensional visualisation of treatment planning data within the procedural environment. Each phantom undergoes CT imaging and treatment planning with the Oncentra Brachy system. Implantations are performed using a conventional freehand technique and an AR-assisted approach. The preliminary results support the feasibility of AR-assisted implantation and justify completion of the full experimental protocol and subsequent clinical validation.

Introduction

Interstitial brachytherapy remains one of the core components of modern radiation oncology, enabling precise delivery of high radiation doses directly to the tumor while minimizing exposure to surrounding healthy tissues. Despite its clinical effectiveness, this technique requires a high level of manual precision and advanced spatial planning skills, which poses a significant barrier to widespread adoption and standardisation in clinical practice [1–3].

In response to these challenges, recent years have seen growing interest in the application of augmented reality (AR) as a supportive tool in both medical education and real-time procedural guidance. AR systems allow the projection of virtual anatomical or planning information onto the re-al-world environment, improving the user’s spatial perception and decision-making. In a systematic review, Eckert et al. [4] emphasized the growing number of AR applications across a broad range of medical specialties, including surgery, orthopedics, interventional radiology, and neurosurgery, where AR has been shown to improve anatomical understanding, reduce error rates, and facilitate training.

Several pioneering reports have described the use of AR in brachytherapy, primarily in educational and intraoperative settings. Krempien et al. [5] demonstrated that projection-based AR systems can provide high guidance accuracy (< 2 mm) during needle implantation while eliminating the need for rigid frames or repeated switching between the surgical field and screen-based displays. Similarly, the study by Liu et al. [6] illustrated that holographic AR guidance using Iodine-125 seed implantation for brain metastases can not only improve procedural safety, but also enhance anatomical understanding by enabling real-time shared visualisation among interventional team members.

Despite its promise, the use of AR in brachytherapy remains an underexplored area. In their 2024 review, Ferioli et al. [1] emphasized the lack of prospective studies and the need for protocol standardisation and technology validation in procedure-specific oncological contexts. In particular, there is a shortage of experimental studies evaluating the impact of AR on key procedural parameters such as procedure duration and implantation accuracy, especially under realistic but controlled conditions.

In response to this gap, the present study aims to evaluate the effect of an AR system on the time and precision of interstitial applicator placement in brachytherapy, using high-fidelity tissue-mimicking phantoms and real treatment plans. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantitatively compare conventional and AR-assisted implantation methods under standardized and reproducible experimental conditions.

Objectives

The primary objective of this experimental study is to evaluate the effectiveness of an AR guidance system in optimising interstitial brachytherapy procedures. Specifically, the study will compare the accuracy of applicator placement and total procedure time between conventional and AR-assisted implantation techniques.

Secondary objectives include assessing the feasibility of AR system integration with standard treatment planning software and exploring its potential for procedural standardisation and future clinical application.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This is a prospective, controlled, single-centre experimental study designed to assess the impact of an AR guidance system on procedural performance in interstitial brachytherapy. The study will compare two implantation techniques — conventional freehand and AR-assisted — using custom-designed phantoms under standardized laboratory conditions. The project will be conducted at the Greater Poland Cancer Center, in collaboration with a multidisciplinary team comprising radiation oncologists, a medical physicist, and an electroradiology technologist.

Phantom design and treatment planning

The study will use 34 custom-designed, tissue-equivalent phantoms developed specifically for this project. These phantoms replicate the mechanical properties of soft tissues and incorporate critical anatomical analogs, such as bone-like structures and major blood vessels, to reflect key constraints relevant to interstitial brachytherapy. Each phantom (Fig. 1) is reusable and allows for four independent implantation procedures, yielding a total of 136 procedures (68 performed with AR assistance and 68 using the conventional technique).

Within the Oncentra Brachy (Elekta) treatment planning system (TPS), the phantom geometry was designed as a hexagonal structure with varied edge lengths to facilitate orientation during holographic alignment with the physical model. A virtual mold was generated and fabricated using 3D printing [polylactic acid (PLA) material]. Additionally, anatomical features mimicking critical organs were incorporated to simulate a clinical setting. The mold was then filled in layers with soft plastic, blended with pigments to ensure opacity — thereby preventing the operator from visually tracking the needle path during the procedure. After processing, the material retains the physical characteristics of human soft tissue.

The soft plastic mixture used in this study consists primarily of polyvinyl chloride (PVC), combined with plasticizers such as phthalates or phosphates to achieve the desired flexibility and radiographic properties [7]. To better simulate tissue density, small quantities of gypsum were added to increase electron density and computed tomography (CT) visibility. Embedded within the phantom are small volumetric structures that imitate target volumes, maintaining uniform mechanical properties (i.e., no perceptible changes in needle resistance during insertion). All phantoms will undergo CT imaging, and treatment plans will be generated using the TPS, based on phantom geometry (Fig. 2). Plans will be developed by an experienced medical physicist and standardized with respect to the number of needles, insertion depth, and target location.

Augmented reality system

For the AR-assisted group, the operator uses a Microsoft HoloLens 2 head-mounted display integrated with a customized mixed reality platform based on the HOLO BR system (HOLO Vectors) (Fig. 3). The system imports DICOM data from the TPS. A key aspect is not only the automatic reconstruction but also the extraction of additional data from DICOM RTSTRUCT and DICOM RT Plan files. These files contain information about the positions of structures reconstructed in the TPS (positions of the multi-contour boundaries describing the contours from TPS in the same coordinate system as CT). This is crucial because the positions of the volumetric discs are determined not only by imaging data but also by comprehensive clinical planning information (visual information alone is insufficient). Furthermore, it is vital to visualise the trajectories of the catheters designed in TPS on the hologram. Most previously reported AR systems have relied exclusively on imaging data without additional components dedicated to interventional radiotherapy. This system allows visualisation of three-dimensional anatomical structures and applicator trajectories, reconstructed from DI-COM RT data and overlaid on the real-world view of the phantom (Fig. 4). The AR environment enables intuitive spatial interaction, such as translation, scaling, and transparency adjustment. Manual alignment is performed using predefined surface landmarks and visual cues. The AR system is compatible with Oncentra Brachy TPS and supports seamless import of treatment plan data.

Procedure workflow

In the conventional freehand arm, applicator placement will be performed based on printed treatment plans placed next to the phantom. These materials will include schematic drawings of applicator entry points presented in 4 different planes and angulations, as well as written information about the required insertion depth for each needle. Three implantation needles will be available next to the phantom. At the moment of stopwatch activation, the operator will see the treatment plan for the first time and will be required to measure the needles and perform the implantation solely based solely on the printed documentation.

In the AR-assisted arm, no printed plans will be available during the procedure. Instead, the treatment plan will be visualised using a head-mounted AR display. At the start of each procedure, the holographic phantom model will be intentionally oriented in a way that does not reveal the planned needle trajectories (e.g., a bottom-up view). Only after activation of the stopwatch will the operator be allowed to manipulate the hologram and become familiar with the treatment plan.

The holographic model will be positioned adjacent to the physical phantom according to the op-erator’s preference. Due to technical limitations of the investigated system, rigid fixation of the hologram directly onto the real phantom will not be possible; therefore, the virtual model will serve as a spatial reference rather than as a direct overlay. During the procedure, the operator will be able to freely adjust the hologram, including rotation, scaling, freezing its position, hiding individual phantom layers, or displaying selected or all needle trajectories simultaneously.

Each implantation will be based on an individual treatment plan. No AR-assisted procedure will have a corresponding conventional procedure using the same plan, and vice versa. This design is intended to minimize learning effects related to plan memorization and to better reflect the variability encountered in real-world interstitial brachytherapy procedures.

All implantations will be carried out by trained clinicians with experience in interstitial brachytherapy. The study design assumes the involvement of operators with different levels of prior experience in the use of AR technology. Half of the procedures will be performed by an operator with prior experience in AR-assisted implantation, while the remaining procedures will be carried out by an operator at an early stage of AR adoption.

Participants will perform an equal number of procedures in both study arms. The order of procedures will be randomised to minimize bias. The room environment (including lighting, temperature, and noise) will be standardised across all experimental sessions.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoints are:

— procedure duration, defined as the time from the initial handling of the applicator to the completion of the final needle insertion;

— procedure duration, defined as the time from the initial handling of the applicator to the completion of the final needle insertion;

— implantation accuracy, assessed as the spatial deviation between planned and actual needle positions, measured in millimetres.

— implantation accuracy, assessed as the spatial deviation between planned and actual needle positions, measured in millimetres.

Following each implantation, the phantom will undergo CT scanning. The resulting images will be registered with the corresponding treatment plan, and needle trajectories will be reconstructed and analysed using image fusion software. Deviations in entry point, angle, and depth will be quantified.

Sample size and statistical analysis

The study will analyse a total of 136 implantation procedures (n = 136), with 68 performed using AR and 68 using a conventional technique. Sample size was determined based on an a priori power calculation (80% power, α = 0.05), ensuring sufficient sensitivity to detect statistically significant differences in both primary outcomes.

Data will be summarized using descriptive statistics and presented as mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons will be performed using paired t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, depending on the distribution of values (assessed via Shapiro–Wilk test). Statistical significance will be set at p < 0.05. Results will be visualised using boxplots and scatterplots with 95% confidence intervals.

The present manuscript additionally reports preliminary results from a pilot phase of the study, comprising the first 12 AR-assisted and 12 conventional freehand implantation procedures. These pilot data were analysed independently to assess feasibility, explore potential trends, and validate the planned study methodology. Given the limited sample size and the independent nature of the pilot measurements, group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The full study, including all 136 procedures, is ongoing and will be analysed separately upon completion.

Results and discussion

Preliminary results — pilot phase

The preliminary analysis included 12 paired comparisons, comprising 24 procedures: 12 AR-assisted and 12 conventional freehand implantation procedures. All procedures were completed successfully, and no technical failures of the AR system were observed.

The mean procedure time was 179 seconds for the conventional freehand technique and 214.5 seconds for the AR-assisted technique. The difference did not reach statistical significance (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.068).

The mean spatial deviation between the planned and actual needle-tip positions was 7.15 mm in the conventional freehand group and 6.14 mm in the AR-assisted group. Although a trend toward improved implantation accuracy was observed with AR guidance, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.166).

In addition to quantitative measurements, qualitative observations were made during the pilot phase. Direct manual manipulation within the holographic field occasionally resulted in partial occlusion of the operator’s hands by virtual content, which could disrupt hand–eye coordination and depth perception. Consequently, the holographic visualisation was primarily used as a navigational and planning reference, while the actual needle insertion was performed outside the holographic image.

Although no formal statistical analysis of learning effects was performed due to the limited number of pilot procedures, exploratory inspection suggested increasing familiarity with the AR system across successive procedures. A tendency toward shorter procedure times in the AR-assisted arm was observed in both operators. Given the small number of observations, these findings should be interpreted as qualitative and hypothesis-generating rather than as statistically supported conclusions.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its rigorous experimental design, which allows direct comparison between conventional and AR-assisted interstitial brachytherapy procedures under controlled and reproducible conditions. The use of high-fidelity tis-sue-mimicking phantoms incorporating soft-tissue analogs and critical structures enhances the realism and reproducibility of the experimental setting.

Standardized treatment planning and systematic assessment of both procedural time and implantation accuracy provide objective outcome measures with clear relevance for interventional radiotherapy practice.

Another important strength is the use of a clinically compatible AR platform capable of importing and visualising treatment planning data. This facilitates potential translation of the proposed workflow into real-world clinical settings. In addition, the interdisciplinary composition of the study team, including radiation oncologists, medical physicists, and electroradiologists, supports methodological robustness and procedural realism.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the preliminary analysis is based on a limited number of pilot procedures and is not intended to support definitive statistical conclusions. Second, although the custom-designed phantoms reproduce key geometric and mechanical characteristics relevant to interstitial brachytherapy, they cannot fully reflect biological variability, tissue heterogeneity, or motion encountered in clinical practice. Finally, the experimental setting was intended primarily for technical and procedural evaluation and was not designed to formally quantify patient-related outcomes or full clinical workflow integration. These aspects fall beyond the scope of the present experimental phase and warrant further dedicated investigation in clinical settings.

Conclusions

This prospective phantom-based study provides initial quantitative data on the application of AR guidance in interstitial brachytherapy under controlled experimental conditions. The preliminary findings confirm the feasibility of integrating three-dimensional visualisation with treatment planning data and indicate a trend toward improved implantation accuracy without a relevant prolongation of the procedure.

Beyond numerical outcomes, the study yields practical insights into procedural workflow and operator interaction with the proposed system, highlighting both its technical potential and usability-related aspects. These observations underline the importance of structured validation of AR-based solutions before broader clinical application.

The presented protocol, together with the preliminary results, establishes a foundation for further experimental and clinical investigations aimed at refining AR-guided implantation procedures and exploring their potential role in procedural standardisation and training.
