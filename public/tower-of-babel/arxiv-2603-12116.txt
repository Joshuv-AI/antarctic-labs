Twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev modules
Joseph Muller, Chia-Fu Yu

Abstract
. In this expository paper, given a field K K and an automorphism σ ∈ Aut ⁡ ( K ) \sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(K) , we give a self-contained proof of the classification of finite dimensional K K -vector spaces equipped with two operators F F and V V , respectively σ \sigma -linear and σ − 1 \sigma^{-1} -linear, such that F ​ V = V ​ F = 0 FV=VF=0 . This classification was originally due to the combined results of Gelfand and Ponomarev (1968), and of Kraft (1975). Following a recent suggestion of Chai (2025), we reworked their classification in light of the notion of Kraft quivers.



Introduction

for all

-modules of finite dimension over

[

Anhang

”, p. 69.

is the arithmetic Frobenius. In this case,

Let us sum up the main results. Following

Theorem A

(Theorem
4.2
)
.

Let
M
be a twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev module. There exist

•

a Kraft quiver
\Gamma
whose connected components are pairwise non-isomorphic, and all of whose circular connected components have no repetitions, and

•

a strict
\sigma
-linear representation
(U,\rho)
on
\Gamma
,

such that
M\simeq M(\Gamma,U,\rho)
as
K[F,V]_{\sigma}
-modules. If
\Gamma^{\prime}
and
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
are another Kraft quiver and strict
\sigma
-linear representation meeting the same conditions, then there exists an isomorphism
\Gamma\simeq\Gamma^{\prime}
making
(U,\rho)
isomorphic to
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
.

The proof of Theorem
A
is almost identic to the proof of Gelfand and Ponomarev, which covers the integrality of Chapter 2 of
[
3
]
in the case
(K,\sigma)=(\mathbb{C},\mathrm{id})
. To this extent, we claim no originality regarding our proof of Theorem
A
, but we hope that our treatment based on the use of Kraft quivers makes the arguments clearer and more systematic. Besides, one major upshot of introducing Kraft quivers is that it allows us to classify explicitely the twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev modules which are
indecomposable
(i.e. which are not the direct sum of two proper
K[F,V]_{\sigma}
-submodules).

Theorem B

(Theorem
4.5
)
.

Let
M
be an indecomposable twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev module. Then
M
is either of the first kind or of the second kind.

•

If
M
is of the first kind, there exists a connected linear Kraft quiver
\Gamma
, unique up to isomorphism, such that
M\simeq M(\Gamma,\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma})
.

•

If
M
is of the second kind, there exists a connected circular Kraft quiver with no repetitions
\Gamma
and a strict indecomposable
\sigma
-linear representation
(U,\rho)
such that
M\simeq M(\Gamma,U,\rho)
. The datum
(\Gamma,U,\rho)
is unique up to isomorphism.

We refer to the body of the text for the notions of

Kraft quivers (Definition

\sigma
-linear relations

1.1.
Basic definitions

In this section, unless stated otherwise,
K

\sigma
is any automorphism of
K

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}

\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}\circ\sigma_{1}

Definition 1.1
.

A
\sigma
-linear relation

(x,y)\in B

(\lambda x,\sigma(\lambda)y)\in B

In other words, a
\sigma

M\oplus M^{\sigma}
, where
M^{\sigma}:=M\otimes_{K,\sigma}K
. This definition was seemingly first considered in the Appendix of
[

]
, and the special case where
K
is a perfect field of positive characteristic and
\sigma
is the Frobenius automorphism was used in the main body of loc. cit. When
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
, we recover the notion of
linear relations
which was, as far as we can tell, originally

In
[

]
, the author mentions unpublished lecture notes of Puppe where the notion of linear relations was already used in the context of spectral sequences.
introduced independently in
[

]
and in

]
.

Example 1.2
.

There are two standard examples of
\sigma
-linear relations.

•

Let
f
be a
\sigma
-linear endomorphism of
M
. Its graph

\Gamma_{f}:=\{(x,f(x))\,|\,x\in M\}

is a
\sigma
-linear relation on
M
. In particular, the identity
\mathrm{id}
on
M
defines a linear relation which we denote
\mathbf{1}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{id}}
.

•

Let
N
be a subspace of
M
. Then

\theta_{N}:=N\oplus\{0\}

is a
\sigma
-linear relation on
M
for every automorphism
\sigma
of
K
. When
N=M
, we omit the subscript and write
\theta:=\theta_{M}
. When
N=\{0\}
, we write
\mathbf{0}:=\theta_{\{0\}}
and refer to it as the
trivial relation
on
M
.

In analogy with morphisms, if
B
is a
\sigma
-linear relation on
M
and
(x,y)\in B
, we will say that
x
is a
preimage
of

by
B
, or that

is an

of
x
by
B
. We will often write
x\xrightarrow{B}y
instead of

. When
B
is clear from the context, we will also write

instead.

Definition 1.3
.

For
i=1,2
, let
\sigma_{i}
be an automorphism of
K
and let
B_{i}
be a
\sigma_{i}
-linear relation on
M
. The
product
or
composition
of
B_{2}
and
B_{1}
is the
\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}
-linear relation given by

B_{2}B_{1}=\{(x,z)\in M\oplus M\,|\,\exists y\in M,x\xrightarrow{B_{1}}y\text{ and }y\xrightarrow{B_{2}}z\}.

For example, we have
\mathbf{1}B=B\mathbf{1}=B
for every
\sigma
-linear relation
B
. It is easy to check that composition is associative, and that we have
\Gamma_{g}\Gamma_{f}=\Gamma_{g\circ f}
whenever
f
and
g
are
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
-linear endomorphisms of
M
. If
(B_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
are
\sigma_{i}
-linear relations on
M
, the composition
B_{n}\cdots B_{1}
is the
\sigma_{n}\cdots\sigma_{1}
-linear relation consisting of all pairs
(x_{0},x_{n})\in M\oplus M
such that there exists
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}\in M
satisfying
x_{i}\xrightarrow{B_{i}}x_{i+1}
for all
0\leq i\leq n-1
. We will write

x_{0}\xrightarrow{B_{1}}x_{1}\xrightarrow{B_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{B_{n-1}}x_{n-1}\xrightarrow{B_{n}}x_{n}

in order to describe such a situation. When
B_{1}=\cdots=B_{n}=B
, we write
B^{n}
for the product
BB\cdots B
and we will drop the superscripts to write
x_{0}\to\cdots\to x_{n}
instead.

Proposition 1.4
.

For
1\leq i\leq 3
, let
\sigma_{i}
be an automorphism of
K
and let
B_{i}
be a
\sigma_{i}
-linear relation on
M
. If
B_{1}\subseteq B_{2}
, then
B_{3}B_{1}\subseteq B_{3}B_{2}
and
B_{1}B_{3}\subseteq B_{2}B_{3}
.

Here,
\subseteq
denotes the inclusion as subgroups of
M\oplus M
. Equivalently, we have
B_{1}\subseteq B_{2}
whenever
x\xrightarrow{B_{1}}y
implies
x\xrightarrow{B_{2}}y
.

Proof.

Let
(x,z)\in B_{3}B_{1}
. There exists some
y\in M
such that

x\xrightarrow{B_{1}}y\xrightarrow{B_{3}}z.

It follows that
x\xrightarrow{B_{2}}y\xrightarrow{B_{3}}z
so that
(x,z)\in B_{3}B_{2}
as required. Likewise, one may easily check that
B_{1}B_{3}\subseteq B_{2}B_{3}
.
∎

Definition 1.5
.

Given a
\sigma
-linear relation
B
on
M
, its
converse
is the
\sigma^{-1}
-linear relation given by

B^{\#}:=\{(x,y)\in M\oplus M\,|\,(y,x)\in B\}.

In other words,
x\xrightarrow{B^{\#}}y\iff y\xrightarrow{B}x
. Clearly, we have
(B^{\#})^{\#}=B
and if
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are two
\sigma
-linear relations on
M
, then we have
(B_{2}B_{1})^{\#}=B_{1}^{\#}B_{2}^{\#}
. If
f
is a
\sigma
-linear automorphism of
M
, then
\Gamma_{f}^{\#}=\Gamma_{f^{-1}}
.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
B
be a
\sigma
-linear relation. We define four subspaces of
M
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dom}(B)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in M\,|\,\exists y\in M,x\xrightarrow{B}y\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ker}(B)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in M\,|\,x\xrightarrow{B}0\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(B)

\displaystyle:=\{y\in M\,|\,\exists x\in M,x\xrightarrow{B}y\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Indet}(B)

\displaystyle:=\{y\in M\,|\,0\xrightarrow{B}y\}.

We refer to these subspaces respectively as the domain, kernel, image and indeterminacy of
B
. Clearly, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ker}(B)\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Indet}(B)\subseteq\mathrm{Im}(B).

Moreover,
B
induces a
\sigma
-linear isomorphism

\mathrm{Dom}(B)/\mathrm{Ker}(B)\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{Im}(B)/\mathrm{Indet}(B).

Alternatively, one can also define these four subspaces by means of composition of relations. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\theta B

\displaystyle=\theta_{\mathrm{Dom}(B)},

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}B

\displaystyle=\theta_{\mathrm{Ker}(B)},

\displaystyle\theta B^{\#}

\displaystyle=\theta_{\mathrm{Im}(B)},

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}B^{\#}

\displaystyle=\theta_{\mathrm{Indet}(B)}.

In particular, it follows that
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\#})=\mathrm{Im}(B)
and
\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\#})=\mathrm{Indet}(B)
. By replacing
B
with
B^{\#}
, one obtains similar statements for the image and the indeterminacy of
B^{\#}
.

Example 1.7
.

If
f
is a
\sigma
-linear endomorphism of

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dom}(\Gamma_{f})

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ker}(\Gamma_{f})

.

A

-linear relation

\mathrm{Ker}(B)=\mathrm{Dom}(B)

For instance, the relation

Lemma 1.9
.

For
1\leq i\leq 2
, let
\sigma_{i}

1.2.
Stable domain, kernel, image and indeterminacy

We start with a definition.

Definition 1.14
.

Let
B
be a
\sigma
-linear relation on
M
. We define four subspaces of
M
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})

\displaystyle:=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{Dom}(B^{n}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{Ker}(B^{n}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})

\displaystyle:=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{Im}(B^{n}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{Indet}(B^{n}).

We refer to these subspaces respectively as the stable domain, stable kernel, stable image and stable indeterminacy of
B
. For every
n\geq 1
, we have

\mathrm{Ker}(B^{n})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}(B^{n+1})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B^{n+1})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B^{n}),

from which it follows that
\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
. By replacing
B
with
B^{\#}
, we also have
\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})\subseteq\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})
.

Proposition 1.15
.

Let
B
be a
\sigma
-linear relation on
M
.

(1)

If
\dim\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})<\infty
, then

\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Indet}(B)\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}).

(2)

If
\dim\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})<\infty
, then

\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(B)\subseteq\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})\subseteq\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty}).

Proof.

Point (2) follows from point (1) by replacing
B
with
B^{\#}
. We prove (1). Since
\mathrm{Indet}(B)\subseteq\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
, the first inclusion is obvious. In order to prove the second inclusion, we claim that it is enough to prove

\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Indet}(B)\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}).

Indeed, let us assume that this inclusion is already proved for all
\sigma
-linear relations
B
such that
\dim\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})<\infty
, and with respect to any automorphism
\sigma
of
K
. Let
x\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
. Thus we have
x\in\mathrm{Indet}(B^{n})
for some
n\geq 1
. Using our assumption with respect to the
\sigma^{n}
-linear relation
B^{n}
, we have

\mathrm{Dom}((B^{n})^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Indet}(B^{n})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}((B^{n})^{\infty}).

It is clear that
\mathrm{Dom}((B^{n})^{\infty})=\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
and that
\mathrm{Ker}((B^{n})^{\infty})=\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
, from which it follows that
x\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
as desired.

Now let
x\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Indet}(B)
. Let
n:=\dim\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
. Since
x\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{n})
, there exists
x_{0}=x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in M
such that

x=x_{0}\to x_{1}\to\cdots\to x_{n}.

Moreover, we know that
0\to x
. It follows that
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
. Thus, these vectors can not be linearly independent. Let
m\leq n
be the smallest integer such that the vectors
x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}
are linearly dependent. We can write

\mu_{m}x_{m}+\cdots+\mu_{0}x_{0}=0,

for some scalars
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{m}\in K
that are not all identically equal to
0
. By minimality of
m
, we must have
\mu_{m}\not=0
. Consider

\lambda_{i}:=\sigma^{-i-1}\left(\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{m}}\right),\qquad\forall\,0\leq i\leq m-1.

By additivity and
\sigma
-linearity of
B
, we have

\displaystyle 0\to x_{0}

\displaystyle\to x_{1}+\sigma(\lambda_{m-1})x_{0}

\displaystyle\to x_{2}+\sigma^{2}(\lambda_{m-1})x_{1}+\sigma(\lambda_{m-2})x_{0}

\displaystyle\to\cdots

\displaystyle\to x_{m}+\sigma^{m}(\lambda_{m-1})x_{m-1}+\cdots+\sigma^{2}(\lambda_{1})x_{1}+\sigma(\lambda_{0})x_{0}.

By construction,
x_{m}+\sigma^{m}(\lambda_{m-1})x_{m-1}+\cdots+\sigma(\lambda_{0})x_{0}=0
. Thus, we have proved that
x_{0}\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{m})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
, which concludes the proof.
∎

We provide a counterexample to Proposition
1.15
(1) when
\mathrm{Inder}(B^{\infty})
has infinite dimension. Let
M=K^{\mathbb{N}}
and for
i\geq 1
, denote by
e_{i}=(\delta_{i,j})_{j\geq 1}
the canonical basis (
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta). Let
B
be the
\sigma
-linear relation generated by the pairs
(0,e_{1})
and
(e_{i},e_{i+1})
for all
i\geq 1
. It is clear that
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})=\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})=M
,
\mathrm{Indet}(B)=\mathrm{Span}_{K}\{e_{1}\}
and
\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})=\{0\}
.

1.3.
The decomposition theorem

The contents of this section will only be used in the proof of Lemma
3.36
and can be skipped at first.

In this section, we recall the decomposition theorem for
\sigma
-linear relations
B
. We discuss two statements, a “weak” decomposition theorem for the restriction of
B
to the subspace
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
(version 1) or to
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})+\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})
(version 2), which we assume to be finite dimensional, and a “strong” decomposition theorem for the whole of
B
, provided that the ambient space
M
is finite dimensional.

The weak version is, as far as we can tell, due to
[
4
]
(see Zerlegungssatz in Section 7 of the Appendix). However in loc. cit. the author assumes that the whole of
M
is finite dimensional and restricts the relation to
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
only, so that our versions 1 and 2 offer a mild generalization.

The strong version is the main theorem of
[
7
]
, which deals with linear relations but which can easily be generalized to its
\sigma
-linear version, and for which we provide only a statement without proof.

Finally, we note that the hypotheses of both versions of the weak decomposition theorems are automatically satisfied when
M
is finite dimensional.

Theorem 1.16

(Weak decomposition - Version 1)
.

Let
B
be a
\sigma
-linear relation such that
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
and
\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
are finite dimensional, and assume that

B(\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty}))\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})+\mathrm{Inder}(B).

There exist a subspace
S\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})
and a
\sigma
-linear automorphism
T:S\xrightarrow{\sim}S
such that

(1)

\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})=S\oplus\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
,

(2)

B_{|\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})}=B_{|S}\oplus B_{|\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})}
,

(3)

B_{|S}=\Gamma_{T}
.

Proof.

We decompose the proof in several steps, following
[
4
]
and
[
2
]
.

Step 1:
We prove that
B
induces a
\sigma
-linear automorphism

\overline{T}:\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}).

Let
x_{0}\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
, and let
y
be an image of
x_{0}
. By hypothesis, we can write
y=x_{1}+z
where
x_{1}\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
and
z\in\mathrm{Indet}(B)
. It follows that
x_{0}\to x_{1}
, and we consider the associated coset
[x_{1}]\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
. We claim that
[x_{1}]
does not depend on the choice of
y
. That is, let
y^{\prime}\in M
be another image of
x_{0}
, and write
y^{\prime}=x_{1}^{\prime}+z^{\prime}
as above. It follows that
y-y^{\prime}=(x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime})+(z-z^{\prime})\in\mathrm{Indet}(B)
. By difference, we have

x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Indet}(B)\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}),

where we used Proposition
1.15
. Thus
[x_{1}]=[x_{1}^{\prime}]
, so we have a well-defined map
x_{0}\mapsto[x_{1}]
. It is easy to check that it factors through an injective
\sigma
-linear morphism

\overline{T}:\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}),

and surjectivity follows as
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
is finite dimensional by assumption.

Step 2:
Fix a linear section
\iota:\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})\to\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
of the natural quotient map. We prove that there exists a linear map
f:\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})\to\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
such that

\forall\,[x]\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}),\qquad\iota([x])+f([x])\to\iota(\overline{T}([x]))+f(\overline{T}([x])).

Fix a
K
-basis
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})
of
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
, and let
1\leq i\leq d
. By construction, there exists
z_{i}\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
such that
\iota(e_{i})\to\iota(\overline{T}(e_{i}))+z_{i}
. Define a
\sigma
-linear map
\delta_{0}:\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})\to\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
by setting
\delta_{0}(e_{i})=z_{i}
and extend it by
\sigma
-linearity.

Since
\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{Ker}(B^{n})
is finite dimensional, there must be some
m\geq 1
such that
\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})=\mathrm{Ker}(B^{m})
. In particular, for every
1\leq i\leq d
we can find vectors
z^{i}_{1},\ldots,z^{i}_{m-1}
such that

\delta_{0}(e_{i})=z_{i}\to z^{i}_{1}\to\cdots\to z^{i}_{m-1}\to 0.

For
1\leq j\leq m-1
, define a
\sigma^{j+1}
-linear map

\delta_{j}:\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})\to\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}),\quad e_{i}\mapsto z^{i}_{j}

and extend it by
\sigma^{j+1}
-linearity. In doing so, we have constructed a family of maps
(\delta_{j})_{j\geq 1}
from
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
to
\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
such that
\delta_{j}
is
\sigma^{j+1}
-linear, and

\displaystyle\forall\,[x]\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}),

\displaystyle\iota([x])

\displaystyle\to\iota(\overline{T}([x]))+\delta_{0}([x]),

\displaystyle\forall\,0\leq j\leq m-1,

\displaystyle\delta_{j}([x])

\displaystyle\to\delta_{j+1}([x]),

where we put
\delta_{m}:=0
. The desired linear map
f:\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})\to\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
is given by

f([x]):=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\delta_{j}(\overline{T}^{-j-1}([x])).

Indeed, we have

f(\overline{T}([x]))-\delta_{0}([x])=\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}\delta_{j+1}(\overline{T}^{-j-1}([x])).

By construction, for all
0\leq j\leq m-2
we have
\delta_{j}(\overline{T}^{-j-1}([x]))\to\delta_{j+1}(\overline{T}^{-j-1}([x]))
. Moreover we have
\delta_{m-1}(\overline{T}^{-m}([x]))\to 0
. By addition, it follows that

\forall\,[x]\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}),\qquad f([x])\to f(\overline{T}([x]))-\delta_{0}([x]).

Besides, we also have
\iota([x])\to\iota(\overline{T}([x]))+\delta_{0}([x])
. By addition, it follows that

\iota([x])+f([x])\to\iota(\overline{T}([x]))+f(\overline{T}([x])),

as desired.

Step 3:
We define

S:=(\iota+f)(\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}))

and prove that it meets all the requirements.

It is clear that
S\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
. Besides, if
x\in S
, it is easy to check that
x=\iota([x])+f([x])
. Then, by construction of
f
, for every
n\geq 1
we have

\hskip-20.00003pt\begin{split}\iota(\overline{T}^{-n}([x]))+f(\overline{T}^{-n}([x]))&\to\iota(\overline{T}^{-n+1}([x]))+f(\overline{T}^{-n+1}([x]))\\
&\to\cdots\to\iota(\overline{T}^{-1}([x]))+f(\overline{T}^{-1}([x]))\to x,\end{split}

from which it follows that
x\in\mathrm{Im}(B^{n})
. Thus
S\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})
.

We prove (1). If
x\in S
, since
x=\iota([x])+f([x])
it is clear that
[x]=0\implies x=0
, thus
S\cap\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})=\{0\}
. Now let
x\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
, and define
s:=\iota([x])+f([x])\in S
. Since
[s]=[\iota([x])]=[x]
, it follows that
x=s+(x-s)\in S\oplus\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
as desired.

We prove (2) and (3). According to (1), the quotient map
x\mapsto[x]
induces a linear isomorphism
\varphi:S\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})/\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
. We consider
T:=\varphi^{-1}\circ\overline{T}\circ\varphi
, which is a
\sigma
-linear automorphism of
S
. By construction, we have
x\to T(x)
for all
x\in S
, thus
\Gamma_{T}\subseteq B_{|S}
.

Now consider
(x,x^{\prime})\in B_{|\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})}
. By (1), we can decompose
x=s+z
and
x^{\prime}=s^{\prime}+z^{\prime}
where
s,s^{\prime}\in S
and
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
. Since
T^{-1}(s^{\prime})\to s^{\prime}
, we have

(s-T^{-1}(s^{\prime}))+z\to z^{\prime}.

Since
z^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
, it follows that
(s-T^{-1}(s^{\prime}))+z\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
, so that
s-T^{-1}(s^{\prime})=0
by (1). Hence
s^{\prime}=T(s)
, we have
s\to s^{\prime}
and thus
z\to z^{\prime}
. This concludes the proof of (2) and (3).
∎

Theorem 1.18

(Weak decomposition - Version 2)
.

Let
B
be a
\sigma
-linear relation such that
M^{\prime}:=\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})+\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})
is finite dimensional, and assume that there exists some
m\geq 1
such that
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})=\mathrm{Dom}(B^{m})
. Let
N=\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})+\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
. There exist a subspace
S\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})
and a
\sigma
-linear automorphism
T:S\xrightarrow{\sim}S
such that

(1)

M^{\prime}=S\oplus N
,

(2)

B_{|M^{\prime}}=B_{|S}\oplus B_{|N}
,

(3)

B_{|S}=\Gamma_{T}
.

Proof.

Let us check that the hypotheses of Theorem
1.16
are satisfied. Clearly
\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
and
\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
are finite dimensional. Then, let
y\in B(\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty}))
so that there exists some
x_{0}\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
with
x_{0}\to y
. We can find vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m+1}
such that

x_{0}\to x_{1}\to x_{2}\to\cdots\to x_{m+1}.

It follows that
x_{1}\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{m})=\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
and
y-x_{1}\in\mathrm{Indet}(B)
. Thus
B(\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty}))\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})+\mathrm{Indet}(B)
as desired.

Thus, we can apply the Version 1 of the weak decomposition, giving rise to a subspace
S
together with a
\sigma
-linear automorphism
T
satisfying (1), (2) and (3) of Theorem
1.16
. We will prove the Version 2 by using the same subspace
S
. Note that (3) already follows from the Version 1.

We prove (1). Let
x\in S\cap N
, where
N
is as in the statement. Write
x=z_{1}+z_{2}
where
z_{1}\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
and
z_{2}\in\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
. It follows that

z_{2}=x-z_{1}\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty}),

where we used Proposition
1.15
. Thus
x\in S\cap\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})=\{0\}
. Therefore we have
S\cap N=\{0\}
.

Now let
y\in\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})
. We can find vectors
x_{0},\ldots,x_{m-1}
such that

x_{0}\to x_{1}\to\cdots\to x_{m-1}\to y.

It follows that
x_{0}\in\mathrm{Dom}(B^{m})=\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})
. Let
s_{0}:=\iota([x_{0}])+f([x_{0}])\in S
, so that
x_{0}=s_{0}+z_{0}
for some
z_{0}\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
. We can find vectors
z_{1},\ldots,z_{r}
for some

such that

z_{0}\to z_{1}\to\cdots\to z_{r}\to 0.

Up to adding zeroes at the end of this sequence, we can assume
r\geq m
. Thus, we have
x_{0}\xrightarrow{B^{m}}y
and
x_{0}\xrightarrow{B^{m}}T^{m}(s_{0})+z_{m}
. It follows that

Moreover, we have

z_{m}=y-T^{m}(s_{0})-t\in\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})\cap\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})\subseteq\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty}),

where we used Proposition
1.15
. All in all, we have
y=T^{m}(s_{0})+t+z_{m}
, which proves that
\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})=S\oplus\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
. It follows that

M^{\prime}=\mathrm{Dom}(B^{\infty})+\mathrm{Im}(B^{\infty})=S\oplus N.

We prove (2). First observe that
B(N)\subseteq N
. Indeed, let
y\in B(N)
so that we have
x\to y
for some
x\in N
. Write
x=x_{1}+x_{2}
where
x_{1}\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
and
x_{2}\in\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
. There exists some
y_{1}\in\mathrm{Ker}(B^{\infty})
such that
x_{1}\to y_{1}
. It follows that
x_{2}\to y-y_{1}
, which implies that
y-y_{1}\in\mathrm{Indet}(B^{\infty})
. Thus
y\in N
.

Now let
(x,y)\in B_{|M^{\prime}}
. We can decompose
x=s+n
and
y=s^{\prime}+n^{\prime}
where
s,s^{\prime}\in S
and
n,n^{\prime}\in N
. Since
s\to T(s)
, we have
n\to(s^{\prime}-T(s))+n^{\prime}
. Thus,
(s^{\prime}-T(s))+n^{\prime}\in B(N)\subseteq N
, which implies that
s^{\prime}=T(s)
. Eventually it follows that
n\to n^{\prime}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

.

Let

.

A

•

\mathscr{V}
is a finite set,

•

\mathscr{E}
is a subset of
\mathscr{V}\times\mathscr{V}
,

•

\mathrm{label}:\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{A}

The set
\mathscr{V}
consists of the

of
\Gamma
, and the set
\mathscr{E}
consists of the
directed edges
or

of
\Gamma

Definition 2.2

Definition 2.3
.

We say that

Definition 2.4
.

For

, let

\mathscr{A}
. A

\displaystyle(f(v_{1}),f(v_{2}))\in\mathscr{E}_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{label}_{1}((v_{1},v_{2}))=\mathrm{label}_{2}(f(v_{1}),f(v_{2})).

and

Definition 2.7

Connected Kraft quivers fall into two disjoint categories, the linear ones and the circular ones.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\Gamma=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E},\mathrm{label})
be a Kraft quiver, and write
n=\#\mathscr{V}
.

(1)

We say that
\Gamma
is a
connected linear Kraft quiver
if there exists an ordering
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
of its vertices such that

\mathscr{E}^{\#}=\{(v_{i},v_{i+1}),1\leq i\leq n-1\}.

(2)

We say that
\Gamma
is a
connected circular Kraft quiver
if there exists an ordering
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
of its vertices such that

\mathscr{E}^{\#}=\{(v_{i},v_{i+1}),1\leq i\leq n\},

where
v_{n+1}:=v_{1}
.

We say that a Kraft quiver
\Gamma
is
linear
(resp.
circular
) if all its connected components are connected linear (resp. connected circular) Kraft quivers.

Remark 2.10
.

A Kraft quiver is both linear and circular if and only if it is empty, that is, its set of vertices is empty.

A Kraft quiver with a single vertex is trivially connected. It is linear if it contains no edge, and it is circular if it contains a loop.

Lemma 2.11
.

Any non-empty connected Kraft quiver is either linear or circular.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E},\mathrm{label})
be a non-empty connected Kraft quiver. We may assume that
n:=\#\mathscr{V}\geq 2
. The connectedness of
\Gamma
forces every vertex to be the tail or the head of at least one arrow, and
\Gamma
can not contain any loop of the form
(v,v)
for some
v\in\mathscr{V}
.

Case 1:
there exists
v_{1}\in\mathscr{V}
which is connected to exactly one single arrow in
\Gamma
. Let us denote the corresponding arrow in
\mathscr{E}^{\#}
by
E_{1}
, so that we have
E_{1}=(v_{1},v_{2})
or
(v_{2},v_{1})
for some
v_{2}\in\mathscr{V}
. Up to replacing
\Gamma
with
\Gamma^{\mathrm{op}}
, we may assume that
E_{1}=(v_{1},v_{2})
. Assume that there are pairwise distinct vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{j+1}\in\mathscr{V}
for some
j\geq 1
such that
E_{i}:=(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in\mathscr{E}^{\#}
for all
1\leq i\leq j
. By point (1) of Definition
2.8
, the vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{j}
can not be the head nor the tail of any other arrow besides
E_{1},\ldots,E_{j}
. Assume that
j<n-1
. If
v_{j+1}
was the head or the tail exactly one single arrow, then
v_{1},\ldots,v_{j+1}
would form a proper connected component in
\Gamma
, which is a contradiction. Thus, there must be some arrow
E_{j+1}\in\mathscr{E}^{\#}
connected to
v_{j+1}
and such that
E_{j+1}\not=E_{j}
. Since
v_{j+1}
is already the head of
E_{j}
, it must be the tail of
E_{j+1}
by construction of
\Gamma^{\#}
and point (3) of Definition
2.8
. Let us write
E_{j+1}=(v_{j+1},v_{j+2})
for some
v_{j+2}\in\mathscr{V}
. Then
v_{j+2}
must be different from
v_{1},\ldots,v_{j+1}
. By induction, we have found pairwise distinct vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
such that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in\mathscr{E}^{\#}
for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
. Then point (1) of Definition
2.8
implies that there can not be any other arrow in
\mathscr{E}^{\#}
besides
E_{1},\ldots,E_{n-1}
, so that
\Gamma
is linear.

Case 2:
all vertices in
\mathscr{V}
are connected to exactly two distinct arrows in
\Gamma
. Fix
v_{1}\in\mathscr{V}
. Then
v_{1}
is either the head and the tail of two arrows in
\mathscr{E}^{\#}
with the same label, or the common head or tail of two arrows in
\mathscr{E}^{\#}
with different labels. In all cases, one of the two arrows connected to
v
corresponds to an arrow
E_{1}\in\mathscr{E}^{\#}
of the form
E_{1}=(v_{1},v_{2})
. Assume that there are pairwise distinct vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{j+1}\in\mathscr{V}
for some
j\geq 1
such that
E_{i}:=(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in\mathscr{E}^{\#}
for all
1\leq i\leq j
. By point (1) of Definition
2.8
, the vertices
v_{2},\ldots,v_{j}
can not be the head or the tail of any other arrow besides
E_{1},\ldots,E_{j}
. Let
E_{j+1}\not=E_{j}
be the second arrow in
\mathscr{E}^{\#}
connected to
v_{j+1}
. Necessarily,
v_{j+1}
is the tail of
E_{j+1}
. Let us write
E_{j+1}=(v_{j+1},v_{j+2})
. We have
v_{j+2}\not=v_{1}
, otherwise
v_{1},\ldots,v_{j+1}
would form a proper connected component in
\Gamma
, which is a contradiction. Thus
v_{j+2}
is distinct from
v_{1},\ldots,v_{j+1}
. By induction, we have found pairwise distinct vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
such that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in\mathscr{E}^{\#}
for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
. Let
E_{n}\not=E_{n-1}
denote the second arrow in
\mathscr{E}^{\#}
which is connected to
v_{n}
. Then the head of
E_{n}
can not be any vertex other than
v_{1}
, so that
\Gamma
is circular.
∎

Consequently, any Kraft quiver
\Gamma
can be uniquely decomposed as a disjoint union

\Gamma=\Gamma_{\mathrm{lin}}\sqcup\Gamma_{\mathrm{circ}},

where
\Gamma_{\mathrm{lin}}
(resp.
\Gamma_{\mathrm{circ}}
) is the disjoint union of all the linear (resp. circular) connected components of
\Gamma
. We call
\Gamma_{\mathrm{lin}}
the
linear part
of
\Gamma
, and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{circ}}
the
circular part
of
\Gamma
.

of the vertices

.

Let

2.2.
Connected Kraft quivers and words

Non empty connected Kraft quivers can be entirely described in terms of words in the alphabet
\mathscr{A}^{\#}=\{F,V^{\#}\}
. More precisely, linear Kraft quivers correspond to finite words, and circular Kraft quivers correspond to (infinite) periodic words.

A finite word (in the alphabet
\mathscr{A}^{\#}
) is a sequence

w=w_{m}w_{m-1}\cdots w_{2}w_{1},

where
m\geq 0
and
w_{i}\in\mathscr{A}^{\#}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
. The integer
m
is called the
length
of the word, and is also written
l(w):=m
. The unique word of length
0
is called the empty word, and sometimes denoted by
\emptyset
. We denote by
W
the set of finite words. It is a monoid under the operation of concatenation, namely if
w=w_{m}\cdots w_{1}
and
w^{\prime}=w^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}}\cdots w_{1}^{\prime}
then

ww^{\prime}:=w_{m}\cdots w_{1}w^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}}\cdots w_{1}^{\prime}\in W.

Of course, we have
l(ww^{\prime})=l(w)+l(w^{\prime})
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
w=w_{m}\cdots w_{1}
be a finite word. We define a directed graph
\Gamma(w)^{\#}=(\mathscr{V}(w),\mathscr{E}(w)^{\#},\mathrm{label}^{\#})
labeled by
\mathscr{A}^{\#}
as follows:

•

\mathscr{V}(w)=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m+1}\}
is a set with
m+1
elements,

•

\mathscr{E}(w)^{\#}=\{(v_{i},v_{i+1})\,|\,1\leq i\leq m\}
,

•

for
E_{i}:=(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in\mathscr{E}(w)^{\#}
, we have

\mathrm{label}^{\#}(E_{i})=w_{i}.

The graph
\Gamma(w)^{\#}
is the converse of a connected linear Kraft quiver uniquely determined up to isomorphism, which we denote by
\Gamma(w)
.

Definition 2.17
.

An
infinite word
in the alphabet
\mathscr{A}^{\#}
is an infinite sequence
\widetilde{w}=\cdots w_{t}w_{t-1}\cdots w_{1}
of letters with
w_{i}\in\mathscr{A}^{\#}
for all
i\geq 1
. If
w^{\prime}=w_{m}^{\prime}\cdots w_{1}^{\prime}\in W
is a finite word, then

\widetilde{w}w:=\cdots w_{t}w_{t-1}\cdots w_{1}w_{m}^{\prime}\cdots w^{\prime}_{1}

is again an infinite word. For
j\geq 0
, the
j
-shift of
\widetilde{w}
is the infinite word
\widetilde{w}[j]:=\cdots w_{t}w_{t-1}\cdots w_{j+1}
. It is characterized by the identity

\widetilde{w}=\widetilde{w}[j]w_{j}\cdots w_{1}.

An infinite word
\widetilde{w}
is said to be
periodic
if there exists
t\geq 1
such that
w_{i+t}=w_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
. In this case, the smallest such integer
t
is called the
period
of
\widetilde{w}
, and we write
[w]
instead of
\widetilde{w}
, where
w:=w_{t}\cdots w_{1}\in W
.

Remark 2.18
.

We put the stress on the fact that with our conventions, whenever we write
[w]
it is understood that
w
has no repetition, so that the period of
[w]
is
l(w)
. For instance, we do not allow ourselves to write
[FV^{\#}FV^{\#}]
instead of
[FV^{\#}]
.

Definition 2.19
.

Let
[w]
be a periodic word and let
m
be a multiple of the period
t=l(w)
of

. We define a directed graph
\Gamma([w],m)=(\mathscr{V}([w],m),\mathscr{E}([w],m)^{\#},\mathrm{label}^{\#})
as follows:

•

\mathscr{V}([w],m)=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
is a set whose elements
v_{i}
are indexed by
i\in\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
,

•

\mathscr{E}([w],m)^{\#}=\{(v_{i},v_{i+1})\,|\,\forall\,1\leq i\leq m\}
,

•

for
E_{i}=(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in\mathscr{E}([w],m)^{\#}
, we have

\mathrm{label}^{\#}(E_{i})=\widetilde{w}_{j},

where
1\leq j\leq t
is the residue of
i
modulo
t
.

The graph
\Gamma([w],m)^{\#}
is the converse of a connected circular Kraft quiver, uniquely determined up to isomorphism, which we denote by
\Gamma([w],m)

The direct sum of two

Notation 2.24

We introduce the twisted free

Definition 2.25

Let

The proof is the same as

Remark 2.29

It follows that if

Let us now consider a connected circular Kraft quiver
\Gamma=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E},\mathrm{label})
, together with a strict
\sigma

(U,\rho)
. Fix an ordering
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
such that
\mathscr{E}^{\#}=\{(v_{i},v_{i+1}),i\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\}
. For each

, let

\sigma
-linear isomorphism

\phi_{v_{i}}=\begin{cases}\rho_{E_{i}}&\text{if }\mathrm{label}(E_{i})=F,\\
\rho_{E_{i}}^{-1}&\text{if }\mathrm{label}(E_{i})=V.\end{cases}

Definition 2.30
.

With the notations above, the

of

-linear automorphism

Proposition 2.31
.

Let
\Gamma=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E},\mathrm{label})
be a non-empty connected circular Kraft quiver and let
n=\#\mathscr{V}=\#\mathscr{E}>0
. Let
(U,\rho)
and
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
be two strict
\sigma
-linear representations of
\Gamma
. The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

The representations
(U,\rho)
and
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
are isomorphic.

(2)

For every

, the monodromy operators
\Phi_{v}
and
\Phi^{\prime}_{v}
at

for
(U,\rho)
and
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})

(3)

For some

, the monodromy operators
\Phi_{v}
and
\Phi^{\prime}_{v}
at
v
for
(U,\rho)
and
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
respectively are conjugate.

Here, we say that
\Phi_{v}
and
\Phi^{\prime}_{v}
are conjugate if there exists a linear isomorphism
h:U_{v}\xrightarrow{\sim}U^{\prime}_{v}
such that

\Phi^{\prime}_{v}=h\circ\Phi_{v}\circ h^{-1}.

Proof.

The implication (2)
\implies
(3) is trivial.

We prove (1)
\implies
(2). Let
f:(U,\rho)\xrightarrow{\sim}(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
be an isomorphism, and let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
be an ordering of the vertices of
\Gamma
such that
\mathscr{E}^{\#}=\{(v_{i},v_{i+1}),i\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\}
. For all

, let
\phi_{v_{i}}
and
\phi^{\prime}_{v_{i}}
be the
\sigma
-linear isomorphisms defined above for to
(U,\rho)
and
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
respectively. By definition, we have

for all

\Phi_{v_{i}}=f_{v_{i}}^{-1}\circ\Phi_{v_{i}}^{\prime}\circ f_{v_{i}},

so that
\Phi_{v_{i}}
and
\Phi^{\prime}_{v_{i}}
are conjugate.

We prove (3)
\implies
(1). Let

such that

for some

. Put

, and for

.

In other words, given a connected circular Kraft quiver

The structure of the set on the right-hand side may depend heavily on the choice of

. We give two classical examples.

Proposition 2.32
.

Assume that

and that
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
. Conjugacy classes of finite dimensional

-vector spaces equipped with an automorphism correspond bijectively to finite sets of the form

This amounts to writing the automorphism in its Jordan normal form. The number of Jordan blocks is

∎

Proposition 2.33

Assume that

3.
Twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev modules of the first and second kinds

In Definition
2.25
, we have seen how to build a twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev module out of any
\sigma
-linear representation of a Kraft quiver. In this section, we will study the converse. Namely, given such a module
M
, how can we produce a Kraft quiver
\Gamma
together with a
\sigma
-linear representation
(U,\rho)
such that

? As it turns out, the process does not depend much on the choice of
(K,\sigma)
, so that most of the proofs here will be directly adapted from
[
3
]
. We will use notations and definitions introduced in Section
1
without recalling most of them.

Remark 3.1
.

We warn the reader who might be interested in reading the original proofs in
[
3
]
that in loc. cit., the authors use the symbol
\subset
for
proper
inclusions. This differs from our notations, as we use
\subsetneq
instead.

3.1.
Stabilized sequence and elementary intervals

Let
M
be a twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev module. Recall that
W
denotes the monoid of finite words in the alphabet
\mathscr{A}^{\#}=\{F,V^{\#}\}
as in Section
2.2
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
w_{1},w_{2}\in W
. We say that
w_{1}
is a
prefix
(resp. a
suffix
) of
w_{2}
if there exists some
w\in W
such that
w_{2}=w_{1}w
(resp.
w_{2}=ww_{1}
).

We consider the
\sigma
-linear relations
\Gamma_{F}
and
(\Gamma_{V})^{\#}
on
M
in the sense of Section
1
. By abuse of notations, we still write
F
and
V^{\#}
instead of
\Gamma_{F}
and
(\Gamma_{V})^{\#}
. Any word
w=w_{m}w_{m-1}\cdots w_{1}
of length
m
gives rise to a
\sigma^{m}
-linear relation
D(w)
on
M
. We call
monomial
(in
F
and
V^{\#}
) any
\sigma^{m}
-linear relation
D
on
M
of the form

D=F^{m_{1}}(V^{\#})^{m_{2}}\cdots F^{m_{r-1}}(V^{\#})^{m_{r}},

where
r\geq 1
, and
m_{1},m_{r}\geq 0
while
m_{i}>0
for
1<i<r
, and
m=\sum m_{i}
. With
r=1
and
m_{1}=0
, we also consider
\mathbf{1}
as a monomial. The set of monomials is denoted
\Sigma
. The application
w\mapsto D(w)
defines a surjective morphism of monoids

, where

is equipped with the composition of relations. In general, this application is not injective. For instance, if
\Gamma=\Gamma(V^{\#}F)
and

, one may check that
D(FV^{\#}FV^{\#})=D(FV^{\#})
.

Remark 3.3
.

With our notations, formulas of the form “

” make sense. On the left-hand side,

is seen as a word in

, whereas on the right-hand side it is seen as a monomial in

. We hope that context is sufficiently clear throughout the exposition so that no confusion arises.

Remark 3.4
.

Since
FV=0
on
M
, we have
\mathrm{Dom}(V^{\#})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}(F)
.

Definition 3.5
.

The
stabilized sequence
of

is the set

\mathscr{F}_{M}=\{\mathrm{Ker}(D),D\in\Sigma\}\cup\{\mathrm{Dom}(D),D\in\Sigma\}.

Thus

is a set consisting of certain subspaces of

. As it turns out, it is actually a flag.

Theorem 3.6

(
[
3
]
Theorem 1.1)
.

The stabilized sequence
\mathscr{F}_{M}
is finite, non-empty, and there exists a unique numbering
\mathscr{F}=\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{s}\}
of its elements such that

\{0\}=\beta_{0}\subsetneq\beta_{1}\subsetneq\beta_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\beta_{s-1}\subsetneq\beta_{s}=M.

We call
interval
any pair
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
of
K
-subspaces of
M
such that
\alpha_{1}\subseteq\alpha_{2}
. The intervals of the form
(\beta_{i},\beta_{i+1})
for

are called the

of

. Before proving the Theorem, we start with a Lemma.

Lemma 3.7

(
[
3
]
Lemma 1.1 and
[
2
]
Lemma 6.3.2)
.

Let
w_{1},w_{2}\in W
be two words. Then, up to exchanging
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
if necessary, exactly one of the following assertions hold:

(1)

w_{1}=wV^{\#}w_{0}
and
w_{2}=w^{\prime}Fw_{0}
for some words
w,w^{\prime},w_{0}\in W
. In this case the interval
(\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{2}),\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{2}))
follows
(\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{1}),\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{1}))
, that is,

\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{2})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{2}),

(2)

w_{2}=ww_{1}
for some word
w\in W
. In this case the interval
(\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{2}),\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{2}))
is sandwiched inside
(\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{1}),\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{1}))
, that is,

\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{2})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{2})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{1}).

Proof.

Let
w_{0}
denote the maximal common suffix of
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, so that
w_{1}=w_{1}^{\prime}w_{0}
and
w_{2}=w_{2}^{\prime}w_{0}
for some possibly empty words

. Then either
w_{1}^{\prime}
or

is the empty word, either both of them are non-empty. The former corresponds to (2) and the latter corresponds to (1).

Assume first that both
w_{1}^{\prime}
and
w_{2}^{\prime}
are not empty. The maximality of
w_{0}
implies that, up to exchanging
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, we have

and

for some words

. By Lemma

, we have

\mathrm{Dom}D(wV^{\#})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(V^{\#})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}(F)\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(w^{\prime}F).

In particular, we have
\mathrm{Dom}D(wV^{\#})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(w^{\prime}F)
. By Proposition
1.4
, multiplying on the right with

preserves the inclusion, so that
\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{2})
. We deduce that

\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{2})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{2}),

Assume now that
w_{1}^{\prime}
or
w_{2}^{\prime}
is empty. Up to exchanging
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, we may assume that
w_{1}^{\prime}
is empty. It follows that
w_{2}=w_{2}^{\prime}w_{1}
. By Lemma
1.9
, we readily have

\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(w_{2})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{2})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w_{1}).

∎

3.6
.

From now on, we will always write

(1)

\mathrm{Ker}(D)\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(V^{\#}D)\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}(FD)\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}(D).

We define a subset

Proposition 3.9

(

3
]

For

w\not=w^{\prime}
implies that
(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w),\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw))\not=(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w^{\prime}),\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw^{\prime}))
.

Proof.

Let us consider some
\beta_{i}\in\mathscr{F}_{M}
lying in between
\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w)
and
\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw)
, that is,

\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w)\subseteq\beta_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw).

We must prove that one of these two inclusions is in fact an equality. There is a word
v\in W
such that
\beta_{i}
is equal to
\mathrm{Ker}D(v)
or
\mathrm{Dom}D(v)
. Assume first that
w
is not a suffix of
v
. Since we have
\mathrm{Ker}D(w)\subseteq\beta_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w)
, by distinguishing cases in Lemma
3.7
with respect to
w
and
v
, we find out that
\beta_{i}=\mathrm{Ker}D(w)
or
\beta_{i}=\mathrm{Dom}D(w)
. The former implies that
\beta_{i}=\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w)
and the latter implies that
\beta_{i}=\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw)
. Thus, let now assume that we can write
v=v^{\prime}w
for some word
v^{\prime}\in W
.

Case 1:

v^{\prime}
is the empty word. If
\beta_{i}=\mathrm{Ker}D(v)=\mathrm{Ker}D(w)
, then we get
\beta_{i}=\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w)
. If
\beta_{i}=\mathrm{Dom}D(v)=\mathrm{Dom}D(w)
, then we get
\beta_{i}=\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw)
.

Case 2:

v^{\prime}=v^{\prime\prime}V^{\#}
for some word
v^{\prime\prime}\in W
. In this case
V^{\#}w
is a suffix of
v
so that we are in Case (2) of Lemma
3.7
, that is,

\mathrm{Ker}D(V^{\#}w)\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(v)\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(v)\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w).

It follows that
\beta_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w)\subseteq\beta_{i}
, so that this is in fact an equality.

Case 3:

v^{\prime}=v^{\prime\prime}F
for some word
v^{\prime\prime}\in\Sigma
. In this case
Fw
is a suffix of
v
so that we are in Case (2) of Lemma
3.7
, that is,

\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw)\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(v)\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(v)\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(Fw).

It follows that
\beta_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw)\subseteq\beta_{i}
, so that there is an equality.

This proves that the interval
(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w),\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw))
is elementary. Let us now consider some
w^{\prime}\in W_{1}
with
w\not=w^{\prime}
. If
w
and
w^{\prime}
are in Case (1) of Lemma
3.7
, then clearly the intervals
(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w),\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw))
and
(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w^{\prime}),\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw^{\prime}))
are distinct. Thus, up to exchanging
w
and
w^{\prime}
, we may assume that
w
is a suffix of
w^{\prime}
. Since
w\not=w^{\prime}
, we actually have that
V^{\#}w
or
Fw
is a suffix of
w^{\prime}
.

Case 1:

w^{\prime}=w_{1}V^{\#}w
for some word
w_{1}\in W
. By Lemma
3.7
with respect to
w^{\prime}
and
V^{\#}w
, we have

\mathrm{Ker}D(V^{\#}w)\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(w^{\prime})\subsetneq\mathrm{Dom}D(w^{\prime})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w).

It follows that
\mathrm{Dom}D(w^{\prime})
separates the intervals
(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w),\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw))
and
(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w^{\prime}),\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw^{\prime}))
. Namely, we have

\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w^{\prime})\subsetneq\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw^{\prime}))\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w^{\prime})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w)\subsetneq\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw)).

Case 2:

w^{\prime}=w_{1}Fw
for some word
w_{1}\in W
. By Lemma
3.7
with respect to
w^{\prime}
and
Fw
, we have

\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw)\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(w^{\prime})\subsetneq\mathrm{Dom}D(w^{\prime})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(Fw).

It follows that
\mathrm{Ker}D(w^{\prime})
separates the intervals
(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w),\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw))
and
(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w^{\prime}),\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw^{\prime}))
. Namely, we have

\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w)\subsetneq\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw)\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}D(w^{\prime})\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w^{\prime})\subsetneq\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw^{\prime}).

Thus, the Proposition is proved.
∎

Definition 3.10

Theorem 3.15

(
[
3
]
Theorem 4.1 and
[
2
]
Lemma 6.5.2)
.

Assume that
W_{1}\not=\emptyset
. The graph
\Gamma(M,1\mathrm{st})^{\#}
is a connected tree with a unique root, and such that each vertex is the tail of at most two arrows and the head of at most one arrow.

Proof.

First let us show that any prefix of a word of the first kind is again of the first kind. Namely, assume that
w_{1}\preceq w_{2}
and
w_{2}\in W_{1}
. Let us write
w_{2}=w_{1}w
for some word
w\in W
. If we had
\theta D(V^{\#}w_{1})=\mathbf{0}D(Fw_{1})
then multiplying by
D(w)
on the right would yield
\theta D(V^{\#}w_{2})=\mathbf{0}D(Fw_{2})
, that is,
\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w_{2})=\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{2})
, which is a contradiction. Thus
w_{1}\in W_{1}
must be of the first kind.

Since
W_{1}
is not empty, it contains some word
w
and also all of its prefixes. In particular, the empty word
\emptyset
belongs to
W_{1}
, and constitutes the unique root of
\Gamma(M,1\mathrm{st})^{\#}
. Since the empty word is a prefix of every word, it also follows that
\Gamma(M,1\mathrm{st})^{\#}
is connected. If
w\not=\emptyset
is a word of the first kind, it has a unique immediate prefix, thus it is the head of a single arrow in
\Gamma(M,1\mathrm{st})^{\#}
. Besides, if an arrow has
w
as its tail, its head is either equal to
wV^{\#}
or to
wF
. Thus,
w
is the tail of at most two arrows. Since each successor of
w
has length strictly higher than the length of
w
, the graph
\Gamma(M,1\mathrm{st})^{\#}
has no cycle hence is a tree.
∎

(U^{1\mathrm{st}},\rho^{1\mathrm{st}})

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
w_{1},w_{2}\in W_{1}
.

(1)

Assume that
w_{2}=w_{1}F
. Then
F
induces a
\sigma
-linear injection

\rho_{(w_{2},w_{1})}^{1\mathrm{st}}:\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{2})/\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w_{2})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{1})/\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w_{1}).

(2)

Assume that
w_{2}=w_{1}V^{\#}
. Then
V
induces a
\sigma^{-1}
-linear surjection

\rho_{(w_{1},w_{2})}^{1\mathrm{st}}:\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{1})/\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w_{1})\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{2})/\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w_{2}).

Proof.

Assume first that
w_{2}=w_{1}F
. It is easy to check that

\displaystyle F^{-1}(\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{1}))=\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{2}),

\displaystyle F^{-1}(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w_{1}))=\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w_{2}).

Indeed, for the first equality, we have
x\xrightarrow{D(Fw_{2})}0
if and only if there exists some
y\in M
such that
x\xrightarrow{F}y\xrightarrow{D(Fw_{1})}0
. This, in turn, is equivalent to
x\in F^{-1}(\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{1}))
. For the second equality, given
x\in M
, there exists some
z\in M
with
x\xrightarrow{D(V^{\#}w_{2})}z
if and only if there exists some
y,z\in M
such that
x\xrightarrow{F}y\xrightarrow{D(V^{\#}w_{1})}z
. This, in turn, is equivalent to
x\in F^{-1}(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w_{1}))
. It follows that the restriction of
F
defines a
\sigma
-linear map
\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{2})\rightarrow\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{1})
, which induces an injective map upon taking the quotients on both sides.

The case
w_{2}=w_{1}V^{\#}
is similar once we prove the equalities

\displaystyle V(\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{1}))=\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw_{2}),

\displaystyle V(\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w_{1}))=\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w_{2}).

∎

,

•

,
\rho^{1\mathrm{st}}_{E}
is the

-linear) morphism defined in Lemma

Proposition 3.18
.

The module
\mathrm{gr}^{1\mathrm{st}}(M):=M(\Gamma(M,1\mathrm{st}),U^{1\mathrm{st}},\rho^{1\mathrm{st}})
associated to
(U^{1\mathrm{st}},\rho^{1\mathrm{st}})
in the sense of Definition
2.25
is a twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev module of the first kind.

Proof.

First, we must prove that
FV=VF=0
on
\mathrm{gr}^{1\mathrm{st}}(M)
. But this is obvious by construction, since the morphisms
\rho_{E}^{1\mathrm{st}}
are induced by the operators
F
or
V
on
M
depending on whether
E
is an
F
-arrow or a
V
-arrow respectively. Then, we must prove that all the elementary intervals of
\mathrm{gr}^{1\mathrm{st}}(M)
are of the first kind. Given a word
w\in W
, we write
D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w)
for the monomial induced by
w
on
\mathrm{gr}^{1\mathrm{st}}(M)
. For
w\in W_{1}
, let us show by induction on
l(w)
that

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ker}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(Fw)=U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{w}\oplus\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w).

We note that the proof is formal, that is, it does not depend on the definition of
U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{w}
, but only on the shape of the graph
\Gamma(M,1\mathrm{st})
and the injectivity/surjectivity of
\rho^{1\mathrm{st}}_{E}
.

Step 1:
We claim that for every
w\in W_{1}
,
\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w)
is a direct sum of
U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v}
’s for
v
running through a certain subset of
W_{1}
. Indeed, first notice that for every subset
I\subseteq W_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle F^{-1}\left(\bigoplus_{v\in I}U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v}\right)=\sum_{v\in I}F^{-1}(U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v}),

\displaystyle V\left(\bigoplus_{v\in I}U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v}\right)=\bigoplus_{v\in I}V(U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v}).

The right equality is obvious since any vertex of
\Gamma(M,1\mathrm{st})
is the head of at most one
V
-arrow, and the left equality holds because

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(F)=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{1}\text{ s.t.}\\
vF\in W_{1}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{vF,v}^{1\mathrm{st}}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{vF,v}^{1\mathrm{st}})\subseteq U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v}.

Moreover, given any
v\in W_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle F^{-1}(U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Ker}(F)&\text{if }vF\not\in W_{1},\\
\mathrm{Ker}(F)\oplus U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{vF}&\text{if }vF\in W_{1},\end{cases}

\displaystyle V(U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }vV^{\#}\not\in W_{1},\\
U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{vV^{\#}}&\text{if }vV^{\#}\in W_{1}.\end{cases}

Here, we use the surjectivity of
\rho^{1\mathrm{st}}_{E}
whenever
E=(v,vV^{\#})
is a
V
-arrow. Besides, we have

\mathrm{Ker}(F)=U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{\emptyset}\oplus\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{1}\text{ s.t.}\\
vV^{\#}\in W_{1}\end{subarray}}U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{vV^{\#}}.

Indeed, the injectivity of
\rho^{1\mathrm{st}}_{E}
whenever
E=(vF,v)
is an
F
-arrow forces the kernel of
F
to be reduced to the sum of the
U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v}
for
v\in W_{1}
which is not the tail of any
F
-arrow. Such
v
correspond precisely to the empty word
\emptyset
and to all words
v\not=\emptyset
of the first kind whose last letter is a
V^{\#}
.

Now, if
w=\emptyset
is the empty word then
\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w)=\mathrm{Dom}(\mathbf{1})=\mathrm{gr}^{1\mathrm{st}}(M)
is the sum of all the
U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v}
’s, so the claim holds. Assume that it holds for all
w\in W_{1}
with
l(w)=k
for some
0\leq k<m
, and let
w\in W_{1}
with
l(w)=k+1
.

Assume first that
w=w^{\prime}F
for some word
w^{\prime}
which is necessarily of the first kind. Thus, we have
\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w)=F^{-1}(\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w^{\prime}))
. By induction,
\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w^{\prime})
is a direct sum of certain
U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v}
’s. By our discussion above, it follows that
\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w)
itself can be written as such a direct sum.

Assume now that
w=w^{\prime}V^{\#}
for some
w^{\prime}\in W_{1}
. We have
\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w)=(V^{\#})^{-1}(\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w^{\prime}))=V(\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w^{\prime}))
. Again, by induction and the discussion above, the claim follows.

Step 2:
We prove (
2
) by induction on
l(w)
. First, observe that

\mathrm{Dom}(V^{\#})=\mathrm{Im}(V)=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in W_{1}\text{ s.t.}\\
vV^{\#}\in W_{1}\end{subarray}}U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{vV^{\#}}.

In particular, we see that

\mathrm{Ker}(F)=U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{\emptyset}\oplus\mathrm{Dom}(V^{\#}),

which is precisely (
2
) for
w
equal to the empty word. Now, let us assume that (
2
) is known for all
w\in W_{1}
of length
k
for some
0\leq k<m
, and let
w\in W_{1}
have length
k+1
. Assume first that
w=w^{\prime}F
for some word
w^{\prime}\in W_{1}
. By induction, we have

\mathrm{Ker}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(Fw)=F^{-1}(\mathrm{Ker}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(Fw^{\prime}))=F^{-1}\left(U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{w^{\prime}}\oplus\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w^{\prime})\right).

By step 1,
F^{-1}
preserves the sum on the right-hand side so that we have

\mathrm{Ker}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(Fw)=F^{-1}(U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{w^{\prime}})+F^{-1}(\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w^{\prime}))=U_{w}^{1\mathrm{st}}+\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w).

Here, we used the fact that
\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w)
contains
\mathrm{Ker}(F)
. It remains to show that the sum is direct. So let
x\in U_{w}^{1\mathrm{st}}\cap\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w)
. Then
\rho_{w,w^{\prime}}^{1\mathrm{st}}(x)\in U_{w^{\prime}}^{1\mathrm{st}}\cap\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w^{\prime})=\{0\}
by induction. Since
\rho_{w,w^{\prime}}^{1\mathrm{st}}
is injective, we have
x=0
as desired.

Assume now that
w=w^{\prime}V^{\#}
for some word
w^{\prime}\in W_{1}
. By induction we have

\mathrm{Ker}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(Fw)=V(\mathrm{Ker}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(Fw^{\prime}))=V\left(U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{w^{\prime}}\oplus\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w^{\prime})\right).

By Step 1,
\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w^{\prime})
is a direct sum of some
U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{v}
’s. By induction, this sum does not involve
U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{w^{\prime}}
. Thus, we have

\mathrm{Ker}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(Fw)=V(U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{w^{\prime}})\oplus V(\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w^{\prime}))=U_{w}^{1\mathrm{st}}+\mathrm{Dom}D^{1\mathrm{st}}(V^{\#}w),

which concludes the proof of Step 2.

Since
U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{w}\not=\{0\}
for all
w\in W_{1}
, we deduce that all the monomials of the form
D^{1\mathrm{st}}(w)
for
w\in W_{1}
are monomials of the first kind in
\mathrm{gr}^{1\mathrm{st}}(M)
. Besides, if
w\in W_{1}
then the corresponding elementary interval
(\beta_{i}^{1\mathrm{st}},\beta_{i+1}^{1\mathrm{st}})
of the first kind in
\mathrm{gr}^{1\mathrm{st}}(M)
satisfies

\beta_{i+1}^{1\mathrm{st}}/\beta_{i}^{1\mathrm{st}}=U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{w}.

Since
\mathrm{gr}^{1\mathrm{st}}(M)=\bigoplus_{w\in W_{1}}U^{1\mathrm{st}}_{w}
, by dimensions we have already exhausted all the elementary intervals of
\mathrm{gr}^{1\mathrm{st}}(M)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.19

]

Corollary 3.20
.

Let
\Gamma=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E},\mathrm{label})
be a connected linear Kraft quiver together with a strict
\sigma
-linear representation
(U,\rho)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dom}D(Fw)=\mathrm{Dom}D(w).

∎

By Proposition

Theorem 5.3. For
[w]\in W_{2}

S_{w}\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w)^{\infty}\cap\mathrm{Im}D(w)^{\infty}
which is a supplement to

We claim that these subspaces can be chosen compatibly within every equivalence class in

.

Lemma 3.36

(
[
3
]
Theorem 5.3)
.

Let
[w]\in W_{2}
and let
t:=l(w)
. Let us write
w=w_{t}\cdots w_{1}
. The subspaces
S_{w(j)}
for
0\leq j<t
can be chosen so that

\begin{cases}F(S_{w(j)})=S_{w(j+1)}&\text{if }w_{j+1}=F,\\
V(S_{w(j+1)})=S_{w(j)}&\text{if }w_{j+1}=V^{\#}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We can choose
S_{w}
arbitrarily, then determine suitable
S_{w(j)}
’s for
0<j<t
from our initial choice. Let
(e_{i}^{w})_{1\leq i\leq d}
be a basis of
S_{w}
, where
d:=\dim S_{w}
. By construction, there exists unique vectors
f_{i}^{w}\in S_{w}
such that
e_{i}^{w}\xrightarrow{D(w)}f_{i}^{w}
for all
1\leq i\leq d
. Since
D(w)=D(w_{t})\cdots D(w_{1})
, by definition of the composition of relations, we can find vectors
e_{i}^{w(j)}
for
1\leq j\leq t-1
such that for all
i
,

e_{i}^{w}\xrightarrow{D(w_{1})}e_{1}^{w(1)}\xrightarrow{D(w_{2})}\cdots\xrightarrow{D(w_{t-1})}e_{1}^{w(t-1)}\xrightarrow{D(w_{t})}f_{i}^{w}.

We define
S_{w(j)}:=\mathrm{Span}_{K}\{e_{i}^{w(j)},1\leq i\leq d\}
. Let us show that these spaces
S_{w(j)}
meet all the requirements. By construction, it is obvious that we have

\begin{cases}F(S_{w(j)})=S_{w(j+1)}&\text{if }w_{j+1}=F,\\
V(S_{w(j+1)})=S_{w(j)}&\text{if }w_{j+1}=V^{\#}.\end{cases}

For
j=t-1
, this follows from the fact that
(f_{i}^{w})_{1\leq i\leq d}
is also a basis of
S_{w}
. Thus all we have to prove is that
S_{w(j)}
satisfies the conditions (1), (2) and (3) in Theorem
1.16
. We have

\displaystyle[w(j)]=[w]w_{t}\cdots w_{j+1},

\displaystyle[w]=[w(j)]w_{j}\cdots w_{1}.

In particular, we deduce that

(9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dom}D(w(j))^{\infty}

\displaystyle=D(w_{t}\cdots w_{j+1})^{-1}\mathrm{Dom}D(w)^{\infty},

(10)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty}

\displaystyle=D(w_{t}\cdots w_{j+1})^{-1}\mathrm{Ker}D(w)^{\infty},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dom}D(w)^{\infty}

\displaystyle=D(w_{j}\cdots w_{1})^{-1}\mathrm{Dom}D(w(j))^{\infty},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ker}D(w)^{\infty}

\displaystyle=D(w_{j}\cdots w_{1})^{-1}\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty}.

Since we have
e_{i}^{w(j)}\xrightarrow{D(w_{t}\cdots w_{j+1})}f_{i}^{w}\in S_{w}\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w)^{\infty}
for every
1\leq i\leq d
, it follows that
S_{w(j)}\subseteq\mathrm{Dom}D(w(j))^{\infty}
. Let us now consider
x\in S_{w(j)}\cap\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty}
. There is some
y\in\mathrm{Ker}D(w)^{\infty}
such that
x\xrightarrow{D(w_{t}\cdots w_{j+1})}y
. Thus, if we decompose
x=\sum\lambda_{i}e_{i}^{w(j)}
for some scalars
\lambda_{i}
’s, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{-j}(\lambda_{i})e_{i}^{w}\xrightarrow{D(w_{j}\cdots w_{1})}x\xrightarrow{D(w_{t}\cdots w_{j+1})}y.

Since
S_{w}
satisfies (2) of Theorem
1.16
, we deduce that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{-j}(\lambda_{i})e_{i}^{w}=0
so that all the
\lambda_{i}
’s are
0
. Thus
S_{w(j)}\cap\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty}=\{0\}
. By a similar reasoning, it is easy to prove that the vectors
e_{i}^{w(j)}
are linearly independent. Thus
\dim S_{w(j)}=d
, which is also the the codimension of
\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty}
in
\mathrm{Dom}D(w(j))^{\infty}
according to Corollary
3.32
. Thus, we have

\mathrm{Dom}D(w(j))^{\infty}=S_{w(j)}\oplus\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty},

as desired. Before continuing, let us fix some notations. For
1\leq i\leq d
, we can decompose
f_{i}^{w}=\sum_{k}\mu_{ik}e_{k}^{w}
for some scalars
\mu_{ik}
. We define
f_{i}^{w(j)}:=\sum_{k}\sigma^{j}(\mu_{ik})e_{k}^{w(j)}\in S_{w(j)}
. The vectors
(f_{i}^{w(j)})_{1\leq i\leq d}
form another basis of
S_{w(j)}
. Moreover, we have
f_{i}^{w}\xrightarrow{D(w_{j}\cdots w_{1})}f_{i}^{w(j)}
for all
1\leq j\leq t-1
.

Let us now consider
x,y\in\mathrm{Dom}D(w(j))^{\infty}
such that
x\xrightarrow{D(j)}y
. We can decompose
x=x_{1}+x_{2}
and
y=y_{1}+y_{2}
with
x_{1},x_{2}\in S_{w(j)}
and
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty}
. Let us write
y_{1}=\sum\lambda_{i}f_{i}^{w(j)}
for some scalars
\lambda_{i}
’s. We have

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{-t}(\lambda_{i})e_{i}^{w(j)}\xrightarrow{D(w_{t}\cdots w_{j+1})}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{-j}(\lambda_{i})f_{i}^{w}\xrightarrow{D(w_{j}\cdots w_{1})}y_{1}.

By taking the difference, we deduce that

x-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{-t}(\lambda_{i})e_{i}^{w(j)}\xrightarrow{D(w(j))}y_{2}.

Thus
x-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{-t}(\lambda_{i})e_{i}^{w(j)}\in D(w(j))^{-1}\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty}=\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty}
. It follows that
x_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{-t}(\lambda_{i})e_{i}^{w(j)}
, and therefore
x_{1}\xrightarrow{D(w(j))}y_{1}
. This proves that

D(w(j))_{|\mathrm{Dom}D(w(j))^{\infty}}=D(w(j))_{|S_{w(j)}}\oplus D(w(j))_{|\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty}}.

It remains to prove that
D(w(j))_{|S_{w(j)}}
is the graph of a
\sigma^{t}
-linear automorphism of
S_{w(j)}
. By what precedes, we already saw that
D(w(j))_{|S_{w(j)}}
has domain and image equal to
S_{w(j)}
. It only remains to prove that the kernel and the indeterminacy of
D(w(j))_{|S_{w(j)}}
are zero. If
x\in S_{w(j)}
is in the kernel, then in particular
x\in\mathrm{Ker}D(w(j))^{\infty}
, so that
x=0
. Let us now consider some
y\in S_{w(j)}\cap\mathrm{Indet}D(w(j))
. Let us write
y=\sum\lambda_{i}e_{i}^{w(j)}
for some scalars
\lambda_{i}
’s. Since
0\xrightarrow{D(w(j))}y
, we can find some

such that

0\xrightarrow{D(w_{t}\cdots w_{j+1})}z\xrightarrow{D(w_{j}\cdots w_{1})}y.

In particular,

. Now, by taking the difference we also have

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{-j}(\lambda_{i})e_{i}^{w}-z\xrightarrow{D(w_{j}\cdots w_{1})}0.

In particular,
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{-j}(\lambda_{i})e_{i}^{w}-z\in\mathrm{Ker}D(w)

. We have just proved that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{-j}(\lambda_{i})e_{i}^{w}\in S_{w}\cap N_{w}
. But this intersection is zero according to the stronger condition (1) of Theorem

, which is also satisfied by

. Therefore, all the
\lambda_{i}
’s are zero, so that
\mathrm{Indet}D(w(j))_{|S_{w(j)}}=\{0\}
. This concludes the proof.

For any

4.
The classification of twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev modules

In this last section, we give a summary of the results we have been building up so far.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
M
be a twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev module. There is a decomposition

M=M_{1}\oplus M_{2},

where
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are submodules of the first and of the second kind respectively. Moreover,
M_{1}
(resp.
M_{2}
) decomposes further as a direct sum of submodules which are isomorphic to modules associated to strict
\sigma
-linear representations of linear (resp. circular) connected Kraft quivers.

Proof.

Let
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
be given by Theorems
3.24
and
3.37
. All we have to prove is the decomposition of
M
into the direct sum of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. Let us write
D^{1}
,
D^{2}
and
D^{1\cap 2}
for the homomorphisms of monoids, sending a word
w\in W
into the associated monomial acting respectively on
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
and on
M_{1}\cap M_{2}
. For every
w\in W
, we have

D^{1\cap 2}(w)=D^{1}(w)_{|M_{1}\cap M_{2}}=D^{2}(w)_{|M_{1}\cap M_{2}}.

Since
M_{2}
is of the second kind, for every
w\in W
we have
\mathrm{Dom}D^{2}(V^{\#}w)=\mathrm{Ker}D^{2}(Fw)
, so that the same equality holds for
D^{1\cap 2}
. Likewise, since
M_{1}
is of the first kind, for every
w\in W
we have
\mathrm{Ker}D^{1}(w)^{\infty}=\mathrm{Dom}D^{1}(w)^{\infty}
, so that the same equality holds for
D^{1\cap 2}
. In other words,
M_{1}\cap M_{2}
has no elementary interval, thus must be zero. Eventually, since we have

\displaystyle\dim M

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\dim\beta_{i+1}/\beta_{i}=

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in W_{1}}\dim\mathrm{Ker}D(Fw)/\mathrm{Dom}D(V^{\#}w)+\sum_{[w]\in W_{2}}\dim\mathrm{Dom}D(w)^{\infty}/\mathrm{Ker}D(w)^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\dim M_{1}+\dim M_{2},

we conclude that
M=M_{1}\oplus M_{2}
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
M
be a twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev module. There exists

•

a Kraft quiver
\Gamma
whose connected components are pairwise non-isomorphic, and all of whose circular connected components have no repetitions, and

•

a strict
\sigma
-linear representation
(U,\rho)
on

making

Proof.

Let
M=M_{1}\oplus M_{2}
as in Theorem
4.1
. Both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
decompose as the direct sum of modules associated to connected Kraft quivers which are linear or circular respectively. We build
\Gamma
by taking the disjoint union of all these Kraft quivers, together with the induced
\sigma
-linear representation
(U,\rho)
.

Assume that
M\simeq M^{\prime}
are two isomorphic Gelfand-Ponomarev modules. The choice of an isomorphism
\varphi
transforms the stabilized sequence

into

, thus preserves the sets

and
W_{2}
of words of the first and second kinds. By construction, for

can be constructed from the same Kraft quivers, and the isomorphism

∎

Corollary 4.3

Assume that

We can classify indecomposable twisted Gelfand-Ponomarev modules as well.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected Kraft quiver, which has no repetition if it is circular. Let
(U,\rho)
be a strict
\sigma
-linear representation of
\Gamma
. Then
M(\Gamma,U,\rho)
is indecomposable if and only if
(U,\rho)
is indecomposable.

Proof.

If
(U,\rho)
decomposes in a non-trivial sum of two
\sigma
-linear representations, then it is clear that
M(\Gamma,U,\rho)
decomposes as a non-trivial sum as well. Thus, we may now assume that
(U,\rho)
is indecomposable. Towards a contradiction, assume that
M:=M(\Gamma,U,\rho)
decomposes into a non-trivial direct sum
M=M^{\prime}\oplus M^{\prime\prime}
. By Theorem
4.2
, there exists Kraft quivers
\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
, and
\sigma
-linear representations
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
and
(U^{\prime\prime},\rho^{\prime\prime})
such that
M^{\prime}\simeq M(\Gamma^{\prime},U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
and
M^{\prime\prime}\simeq M(\Gamma^{\prime\prime},U^{\prime\prime},\rho^{\prime\prime})
. Define a Kraft quiver
\Gamma^{\mathrm{new}}
with a
\sigma
-linear representation
(U^{\mathrm{new}},\rho^{\mathrm{new}})
as follows. For each pair
\Gamma^{\prime}_{i}\simeq\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{j}
of isomorphic connected components of
\Gamma^{\prime}
and of
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
,
\Gamma^{\mathrm{new}}
has a single connected component
\Gamma^{\mathrm{new}}_{k}
isomorphic to
\Gamma^{\prime}_{i}\simeq\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{j}
, bearing a
\sigma
-linear representation which is the direct sum of
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
and
(U^{\prime\prime},\rho^{\prime\prime})
restricted to
\Gamma^{\prime}_{i}
and
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}_{j}
respectively. Else, for each connected component of
\Gamma^{\prime}
(resp.
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
) which is not isomorphic to any connected component of
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
(resp.
\Gamma^{\prime}
),
\Gamma^{\mathrm{new}}
has a single connected component isomorphic to it, bearing a
\sigma
-linear representation which is isomorphic to the restriction of
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
(resp. of
(U^{\prime\prime},\rho^{\prime\prime})
).

The Kraft quiver
\Gamma^{\mathrm{new}}
together with its
\sigma
-linear representation specified above meet the conditions of Theorem
4.2
. By unicity,
\Gamma
must be isomorphic to
\Gamma^{\mathrm{new}}
. Since
\Gamma
is connected, both
\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
must be connected and isomorphic to each other. But then,
(U,\rho)
must be isomorphic to
(U^{\mathrm{new}},\rho^{\mathrm{new}})
which is the direct sum of
(U^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
and
(U^{\prime\prime},\rho^{\prime\prime})
. This is a contradiction.
∎
