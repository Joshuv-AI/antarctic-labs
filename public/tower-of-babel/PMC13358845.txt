Improving dermatological adverse events from amivantamab plus lazertinib: lessons from the COCOON trial
Authors: Tess Gillham, Federico Monaca, Raffaele Califano
Published: 2026

Introduction

The treatment landscape for epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has evolved dramatically over the last decade with the development of third generation EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as Osimertinib. The efficacy of EGFR TKIs is limited by the eventual development of tumour resistance mechanisms, with median overall survival (OS) of approximately three years in pivotal trials (1,2). First-line intensification strategies such as platinum-pemetrexed plus osimertinib (FLAURA 2 regimen) or amivantamab plus lazertinib (MARIPOSA regimen) are superior in terms of progression free survival (PFS) and OS over single agent osimertinib and are recommended options by international guidelines (3,4).

The phase III FLAURA 2 trial evaluated osimertinib with or without platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy in treatment-naïve patients with advanced EGFR-mutant NSCLC (5). The addition of chemotherapy significantly improved median PFS [25.5 vs. 16.7 months; hazard ratio (HR) 0.62] and delayed the emergence of resistance, at the cost of increased treatment-related toxicity. Grade ≥3 adverse events were more frequent in the combination arm (64% vs. 41%), driven predominantly by haematological toxicity and chemotherapy-related gastrointestinal effects, while rates of EGFR-related dermatological toxicity were similar between arms (38% vs. 19% any grade, 10% vs. 3% grade ≥3) (5).

The phase 3 MARIPOSA study compared intravenous amivantamab, a bispecific monoclonal antibody targeting EGFR and MET, in combination with lazertinib, a third generation oral EGFR TKI, versus osimertinib in treatment naïve EGFR mutant NSCLC (6). Amivantamab plus lazertinib significantly improved PFS (23.7 vs. 16.6 months; HR 0.70; P=0.0002), and median OS (not reached vs. 36.7 months; HR 0.75; P=0.005) in the amivantamab-lazertinib group (7). However, 75% of patients in the amivantamab-lazertinib arm experienced grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), including rash (17%), paronychia (12%) and dermatitis acneiform (8%). Venous thromboembolism occurred in 37% of patients and infusion-related reactions in 63%. Overall, TRAEs resulted in dose interruptions in 37% of patients, dose reductions in 23%, and treatment discontinuation in 5%.

Dermatological toxicities have long been recognised as an on-target effect of EGFR inhibition and have been observed across multiple generations of EGFR-targeted therapies. Amivantamab has demonstrated higher incidence and severity of dermatological toxicity compared to EGFR-TKIs alone (8). Rates may be further increased with amivantamab-based combinations, which may limit tolerability in some patients. Dermatological toxicities have traditionally been managed reactively, with interventions initiated on or after the development of symptoms. However, increasing evidence supports a proactive approach (8).

The phase 2 COCOON study evaluated whether proactive, enhanced dermatological prophylaxis can sufficiently mitigate dermatological toxicity in patients receiving amivantamab-lazertinib.

Study design and results

COCOON is a phase 2, open-label, multicentre, randomised study, comparing enhanced dermatological management (COCOON DM) with standard of care (SoC) DM, in patients receiving amivantamab-lazertinib with newly diagnosed locally advanced or metastatic EGFR-mutant NSCLC (9).

All patients received weekly intravenous amivantamab and once daily oral lazertinib, as well as prophylactic anticoagulation during the first 4 months of treatment; 199 patients were randomised 1:1 to COCOON DM or SoC DM. The COCOON DM regimen consisted of oral doxycycline or minocycline (weeks 1–12); clindamycin 1% on scalp (weeks 13–52); chlorhexidine 4% on nails; and ceramide-based moisturizer on the body and face. Patients in the SoC DM arm received treatment according to local clinical practice, and there was no standard protocol.

The primary endpoint was the incidence of grade ≥2 dermatological adverse events of interest (DAEIs) by week 12, including but not limited to rash, dermatitis acneiform, pruritus and paronychia. A secondary endpoint of patient reported outcomes were measured with a validated questionnaire assessing functional, emotional and physical impact of skin conditions on quality of life.

COCOON demonstrated a 44% relative reduction in the incidence of grade ≥2 DAEIs by 12 weeks with COCOON DM (42%) versus SoC DM (75%) [odds ratio (OR) 0.24; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.13–0.45; P<0.0001]. The median time to the first occurrence of a grade ≥2 DAEI was 3.5 months for participants who received COCOON DM and 1.0 month for participants who received SoC DM. The benefit observed at 12 weeks was maintained after 6 months of amivantamab-lazertinib, with a significant reduction in number of participants with grade ≥2 DAEIs (41% vs. 76%). There was no significant difference in rates of paronychia, although rates of DAEIs affecting the scalp, face and body were reduced (Table 1).

No new safety signals were observed with amivantamab-lazertinib, in keeping with the toxicity profile seen in MARIPOSA. There was no increase in liver toxicity or infections in the COCOON DM arm, suggesting prolonged tetracycline use is safe.

Discussion

The COCOON trial demonstrates that dermatological toxicity associated with amivantamab-lazertinib therapy can be effectively reduced in frequency and severity through proactive DM. This standardised regimen demonstrates maintained efficacy at 6 months, and is non-invasive, readily available and relatively inexpensive. The protocol appears to be feasible and safe, with no additional safety signals observed.

This evidence is aligned with previous findings showing that a proactive approach towards dermatological toxicity can improve outcomes. A randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial evaluating 69 patients on EGFR inhibitor monotherapy showed that prophylactic topical chloramphenicol and prednisolone reduced incidence of protocol-specified significant rash from 48% to 14%, compared with aqua cream alone (10). In a small retrospective study (n=7), starting prophylactic tetracycline, emollients, and hygiene measures 14 days before amivantamab monotherapy was associated with reduced dermatological toxicity, although interpretation is limited by the retrospective, non-randomised design and small sample size (8).

Similarly, a post-hoc analysis of trial data of patients with endocrine tumours treated with EGFR inhibitors showed that prophylactic vs. reactive use of oral antibiotics and topical agents was associated with significantly lower incidence of rash, which, in turn, led to fewer dose delays and modifications (11). Although most studies are consistent with the efficacy of prophylactic topical therapies, data regarding prophylactic oral antibiotics are heterogenous. In a randomised, double-blind trial of 65 patients treated with gefitinib or cetuximab, prophylactic oral tetracycline did not prevent rash, lessen severity, or improve quality of life (12). Conversely, a meta-analysis identified oral antibiotics as the most effective strategy for preventing grade ≥2 acneiform dermatitis (13). Notably, long-term tetracycline use has been associated with liver toxicity, gastro-intestinal disturbance, arthralgia and photosensitivity (14), although the COCOON study did not demonstrate any additional safety signals with prophylactic tetracyclines.

In this study, the COCOON DM regimen did not result in a significant difference in the rates of paronychia, implying limited benefit from the inclusion of chlorhexidine 4% for nail care. This finding highlights the need for further research to define effective strategies for the prevention and management of nail-specific toxicities. Nonetheless, the authors propose that nail antisepsis may confer indirect benefit by reducing the transfer of pathogens from the nails to other cutaneous sites, potentially mitigating broader dermatological complications.

In the SoC arm, 98% of patients received at least one reactive dermatological treatment: topical steroids (83%), topical anti-infectives (67%), oral antibiotics (61%), and oral steroids (15%). Despite the large numbers of patients receiving reactive DM, 75% of patients in the SoC group experienced grade 2 or higher DAEIs at 12 weeks. These findings indicate that, although the medications used in the SoC arm overlapped substantially with those in the COCOON DM protocol, dermatological toxicity remains problematic when treatment is delivered reactively rather than proactively. The management of EGFR-associated dermatological toxicities is associated with significant clinical burden, including the need for additional hospital visits, dermatology referrals, dose adjustments, and other resource-intensive interventions, potentially impacting overall healthcare costs (15).

Several important limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the COCOON trial. As an open-label, phase II study, the potential for reporting and assessment bias cannot be excluded, particularly for dermatological endpoints that may be subject to clinician interpretation and patient perception. In addition, the relatively modest sample size and limited duration of follow-up restrict the strength and maturity of the conclusions that can be drawn, particularly for longer-term outcomes. Critically, the study was not designed or powered to evaluate oncological endpoints, and there are no data demonstrating an impact on progression-free or OS. While improved tolerability and reduced dermatological toxicity may plausibly support better treatment tolerability, fewer dose modifications, and prolonged drug exposure, this cannot be assumed to translate into improved clinical outcomes. Further studies incorporating evaluation of adherence and survival outcomes are needed to define the broader clinical impact of proactive DM in this setting.

The COCOON trial demonstrates that structured, proactive DM can meaningfully mitigate the frequency and severity of skin toxicities associated with amivantamab-lazertinib. By improving tolerability, this approach supports better adherence and treatment duration, which may have the potential to translate into improved long-term outcomes for patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC, although further prospective evaluation is required. Nonetheless, proactive DM represents a pragmatic and low-burden strategy to optimise treatment delivery, and improve patient experience in EGFR-mutant NSCLC.
