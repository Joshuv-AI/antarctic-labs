Abstract
. Fine-tuning is now the primary method for adapting large neural networks, but it also introduces new integrity risks. An untrusted party can insert backdoors, change safety behavior, or overwrite large parts of a model while claiming only small updates. Existing verification tools focus on inference correctness or full-model provenance and do not address this problem. We introduce Fine-Tuning Integrity (FTI) as a security goal for controlled model evolution. An FTI system certifies that a fine-tuned model differs from a trusted base only within a policy-defined drift class. We propose Succinct Model Difference Proofs (SMDPs) as a new cryptographic primitive for enforcing these drift constraints. SMDPs provide zero-knowledge proofs that the update to a model is norm-bounded, low-rank, or sparse. The verifier cost depends only on the structure of the drift, not on the size of the model. We give concrete SMDP constructions based on random projections, polynomial commitments, and streaming linear checks. We also prove an information-theoretic lower bound showing that some form of structure is necessary for succinct proofs. Finally, we present architecture-aware instantiations for transformers, CNNs, and MLPs, together with an end-to-end system that aggregates block-level proofs into a global certificate.



1.
Introduction

Fine-tuning has become the predominant method for adapting large neural networks to new tasks, domains, and safety regimes
(
Anisuzzaman et al. 2025
)
(
Han et al. 2024
)
. Rather than training models from scratch, practitioners start from a pre-trained base model and apply additional gradient updates on datasets that are often proprietary or sensitive
(
Xin et al. 2024
)
. This workflow appears across model families including transformer-based language models
(
Dong et al. 2024
)
, vision architectures
(
Kim et al. 2025
)
, and large-scale recommendation systems
(
Hu et al. 2025
)
. Fine-tuning substantially reduces the cost of deployment, yet it also creates a new attack surface. An adversary controlling the fine-tuning pipeline may inject backdoors while claiming only innocuous modifications
(
Raghuram et al. 2024
)
, remove safety-aligned components or alignment modules without disclosure
(
Jain et al. 2024
)
, replace large portions of the model while misrepresenting the extent of drift, or produce a model that passes validation but contains strategically placed poisoned regions
(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
. These risks matter most in regulated or high-stakes applications such as content moderation, code generation, and financial decision support, where auditors and customers need clear guarantees that the deployed model remains within a certified family.

1.1.
Fine-Tuning Integrity

We study the problem of
fine-tuning integrity
(FTI): given a trusted base model and a fine-tuned model, can a prover generate a succinct, zero-knowledge proof that the fine-tuned model differs from the base only within a policy-defined drift class? A policy might stipulate that certain layers must remain unchanged, that other layers may deviate only through low-rank adapters such as LoRA-style updates, that the overall parameter drift must have Frobenius norm at most
\varepsilon
, or that only
k
parameters or parameter blocks may change, reflecting sparse updates.

An FTI proof system must satisfy: (1) the verifier learns only that the drift obeys the policy, and nothing about the weights themselves; (2) the proof is succinct, with complexity governed by the structure of the drift (rank, sparsity, norm budget) and at most polylogarithmic in the size of the model; and (3) any policy violation leads to rejection, except with negligible probability.

Generic zero-knowledge proof systems could encode the full drift relation as a circuit
(
Lavin et al. 2024
)
, but this produces proof sizes and prover costs essentially linear in the parameter count. Such approaches quickly become infeasible for models containing billions of parameters, and they fail to exploit the structural regularities of contemporary fine-tuning practices.

1.2.
Our Approach and Contributions

We adopt a structural view of fine-tuning. The model is decomposed into parameter blocks such as layers, channels, and attention heads. Each block is assigned a drift class that specifies the admissible modifications. We consider three drift classes: norm-bounded, rank-bounded, and sparse. These classes capture the fine-tuning patterns commonly used in practice
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
and provide the algebraic structure required for succinct proofs. We then develop
Succinct Model Difference Proofs
(SMDPs), they are zero-knowledge proofs that certify whether the difference between two models lies within a specified drift class. We present three constructions in this framework.

(1)

Norm-bounded SMDPs via random projections (NBDP).
Using concentration of measure, we build a protocol in which the verifier checks random projections of the drift. The verifier’s cost depends on the norm bound and desired statistical accuracy, not on the full model dimension.

(2)

Low-rank SMDPs via matrix polynomial commitments (MRDP).
Weight matrices are encoded as bivariate polynomials, and low-rank drift corresponds to sums of separable components. A random evaluation test yields a certificate whose complexity depends only on the rank constraint.

(3)

Sparse SMDPs via streaming-style linear proofs (SDIP).
By combining linear sketching with zero-knowledge sum checks, we prove that only a bounded number of coordinates differ between the two models. Any additional non-zero drift is detected with high probability.

For each construction we prove soundness and zero-knowledge, and we analyze its succinctness. We also establish a lower bound: without structural constraints on drift, any non-interactive proof with negligible soundness error must have size at least
\Omega(n)
for a model with
n
parameters. This result motivates the use of structured drift classes. We then show how to instantiate our constructions for transformers, CNNs, and MLPs, and how to compose block-level SMDPs into a complete FTI system suitable for practical fine-tuning pipelines.

Scope.

Our objective is not to address all aspects of model auditing or behavioral verification. Instead, we aim to develop a foundational cryptographic framework, FTI & SMDPs, to demonstrate efficient and meaningful instantiations for drift classes that are prevalent in practice.

2.
Background and Threat Model

2.1.
Fine-Tuning in Practice

We briefly review common fine-tuning patterns used with modern neural architectures.

Norm-regularized fine-tuning.

Training recipes often constrain the magnitude of updates via weight decay, gradient clipping, or explicit regularization.This suggests policies of the form

\|W^{*}-W_{0}\|_{\mathbb{R}}\leq\epsilon

Low-rank adapters.

LoRA and related methods introduce low-rank update matrices
A,B
to existing weight matrices
W_{0}

(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, yielding

W^{*}=W_{0}+AB,

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d^{\prime}}
for some small rank
r
. Variants such as Q-LoRA adapt quantized models but preserve the low-rank structure.

Sparse updates.

Prefix-tuning and soft prompts add learnable vectors only at particular positions. Some adapters modify only a small subset of neurons or channels, leading to sparse drift
(
Xu 2023
)
. In adversarial settings, an attacker may deliberately localize changes to a small subset of parameters to evade naive consistency checks.

These patterns motivate our focus on norm, rank, and sparsity as drift structures.

Why Norm, Rank, and Sparsity?

The three drift classes we consider are not arbitrary choices; they correspond directly to the dominant fine-tuning paradigms in modern deep learning. Weight decay and gradient clipping enforce norm constraints on parameter updates, making norm-bounded drift a natural certificate for regularized fine-tuning. LoRA and related adapter methods
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
produce updates that are low-rank by construction, so rank-bounded drift captures the algebraic signature of adapter-based methods. Prefix tuning, soft prompts, and sparse adapters modify only a small subset of parameters, yielding sparse drift patterns
(
Xu 2023
)
.

Beyond this correspondence with training methods, these three properties also carry diagnostic value for distinguishing benign from adversarial modifications. Empirical studies of backdoor attacks
(
Raghuram et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
)
indicate that injecting backdoors or removing safety guardrails typically requires coordinated changes across multiple layers or large-magnitude perturbations to specific weight subspaces, resulting in updates with larger norm, higher effective rank, or denser support than those produced by standard fine-tuning with common hyperparameters. Conversely, benign fine-tuning updates tend to have small Frobenius norm relative to the base weights, low effective rank due to the low-dimensional structure of gradient updates on overparameterized models, and sparse modification patterns when adapter-based methods are used.

Together, norm, rank, and sparsity cover a wide spectrum of realistic fine-tuning policies. They also provide algebraic structure that is directly exploitable for constructing succinct proofs: norm bounds interact with random projections via concentration inequalities, rank constraints translate into polynomial degree conditions, and sparsity enables compressed linear sketches. This combination of practical relevance and mathematical tractability is what makes these three drift classes a natural foundation for the FTI framework.

2.2.
Threat Model

We assume a
provider
produces a base model
W_{0}
, and a
deployer
fine-tunes it to
W^{*}
on some data, under a policy that constrains the allowable drift between
W_{0}
and
W^{*}
. A
verifier
has access to commitments to
W_{0}
and
W^{*}
, the policy description, and a purported drift proof.

Adversary goal.

The adversary aims to produce a fine-tuned model that passes integrity checks while having made modifications beyond the declared policy. Concrete adversarial objectives include inserting backdoors into the model, removing safety guardrails or alignment modules, or making unauthorized large-scale weight changes that exceed the permitted drift class. The adversary controls the fine-tuning pipeline and can run arbitrary training or manipulation procedures, observe all intermediate weights, choose arbitrary training data, and craft the proof using any computationally feasible strategy.

Victim.

The victim is the model deployer, auditor, or end-user who relies on the FTI certificate to trust that the model has been fine-tuned within policy bounds. For example, an enterprise customer receiving a fine-tuned model from a cloud provider must trust that the provider did not exceed the agreed-upon modification scope. Similarly, a regulatory auditor must verify that a deployed model remains within a certified family relative to an approved base model.

Attack scenario.

Consider a concrete attack flow: a cloud fine-tuning service receives a base model
W_{0}
and a policy stipulating that only LoRA-style updates with rank
r\leq 4
are permitted. The service claims to have applied compliant fine-tuning and produces commitments
C_{0},C^{*}
along with an SMDP proof
\pi
. In reality, the service has made substantially larger modifications, perhaps inserting a backdoor that requires a rank-
20
perturbation or rewriting entire layers. The auditor receives the commitments and proof and must detect this violation. FTI guarantees that the auditor will reject the proof with overwhelming probability whenever the true drift exceeds the declared policy.

Scope and limitations of FTI.

FTI certifies parametric drift bounds; it does not certify behavioral or semantic safety. A model could still behave unexpectedly or produce harmful outputs within the permitted drift bounds, since small parameter perturbations in nonlinear architectures can induce disproportionate functional changes. FTI is therefore complementary to behavioral testing, red-teaming, and output monitoring. Its role is to narrow the space of possible modifications, not to replace semantic evaluation.

Trust assumptions.

The commitment to the base model
W_{0}
is assumed to be correct and publicly available. The verifier trusts the commitment scheme (binding and hiding properties) and the source of public randomness used in the protocols. No trusted hardware is required in our core constructions. The verifier is computationally bounded and does not see raw weights, but can run polynomial-time verification algorithms. We focus on the cryptographic integrity of the parameter drift relation; system-level concerns such as side-channel attacks, implementation bugs, or compromised randomness sources are outside our current scope but are discussed in Section
10
.

3.
Fine-Tuning Integrity

3.1.
Model Representation and Drift Classes

We work over the field of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
. A neural network is modeled as a structured collection,

W=\big(W^{(1)},W^{(2)},\dots,W^{(L)}\big),

where each block
W^{(i)}
is an element of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times d_{i}^{\prime}}
.
The dimensions
(d_{i},d_{i}^{\prime})
depend on the architecture and may correspond to matrices, tensors flattened into matrices, or vectors represented as
d_{i}\times 1
blocks.

Let
W_{0}
denote the base model parameters and
W^{*}
the fine-tuned parameters.
We define the
drift
at block
i
as

\Delta^{(i)}=W^{*(i)}-W_{0}^{(i)},

and the overall drift as
\Delta=\{\Delta^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{L}
.

A
drift class

\mathcal{F}
is a set of admissible drift tensors. Policies are expressed by specifying, for each block
i
, a local drift class
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, and setting

\mathcal{F}=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\mathcal{F}_{i}.

In this work we focus on three canonical drift classes:

•

Norm-bounded drift.

For a matrix
X
, we define the
\ell_{2}
norm as:

\|X\|_{2}=\max_{\|v\|_{2}=1}\|Xv\|_{2}.

A norm-bounded policy restricts the drift of a parameter block to stay within a prescribed radius, formally, for a parameter block
i
,

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{norm}}^{(i)}(\epsilon_{i})=\{\Delta^{(i)}:\|\Delta^{(i)}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{i}\}.

•

Rank-bounded drift.

For matrix blocks,

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rank}}^{(i)}(r_{i})=\{\Delta^{(i)}:\operatorname{rank}(\Delta^{(i)})\leq r_{i}\}.

This captures low-rank adapter updates.

•

Sparse drift.

For vectorized parameters, with
\|\cdot\|_{0}
denoting the number of nonzero entries,

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sparse}}^{(i)}(k_{i})=\{\Delta^{(i)}:\|\Delta^{(i)}\|_{0}\leq k_{i}\}.

Additional drift classes, such as block-sparse updates or group-norm-bounded updates, can be represented as compositions of these basic primitives.

3.2.
Commitments

We assume a binding and hiding commitment scheme
(
Damgård and Nielsen 2002
)

\mathsf{Com}:\{0,1\}^{\ast}\times\{0,1\}^{\ast}\to\{0,1\}^{\lambda},

where
\lambda
is the security parameter, and we write
C=\mathsf{Com}(m;r)
for the commitment to message
m
with randomness
r
. In practice,
\mathsf{Com}
may be a Merkle-based vector commitment or a polynomial commitment.

The provider publishes a commitment
C_{0}
to the base parameters
W_{0}
, and the deployer publishes
C^{*}
committing to
W^{*}
.

3.3.
FTI Security Definitions

We now define FTI at the level of a generic proof system for drift compliance.

Definition 3.1 (Drift compliance relation).

Given a drift class
\mathcal{F}
, define the NP relation

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{F}}=\big\{

\displaystyle((C_{0},C^{*},\mathcal{F}),W_{0},W^{*}):C_{0}=Com(W_{0};r_{0}),

\displaystyle C^{*}=Com(W^{*};r^{*}),\ W^{*}-W_{0}\in\mathcal{F}\big\}.

Definition 3.2.

[Fine-Tuning Integrity (FTI)]
A proof system
\Pi=(\mathsf{Prove},\mathsf{Verify})
satisfies FTI for drift class
\mathcal{F}
if it satisfies:

•

Completeness.

For all
(W_{0},W^{*})
with
W^{*}-W_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
, if
C_{0}=\mathsf{Com}(W_{0};r_{0})
and
C^{*}=\mathsf{Com}(W^{*};r^{*})
, then

\Pr\big[\mathsf{Verify}(C_{0},C^{*},\mathcal{F},\pi)=1\big]=1,

where
\pi\leftarrow\mathsf{Prove}(W_{0},W^{*},\mathcal{F})
.

•

Soundness.

For any PPT adversary
\mathcal{A}
that outputs
(C_{0},C^{*},\mathcal{F},\pi)
,

\displaystyle\Pr\big[

\displaystyle\mathsf{Verify}(C_{0},C^{*},\mathcal{F},\pi)=1\ \wedge

\displaystyle\neg\left(\exists W_{0},W^{*}:((C_{0},C^{*},\mathcal{F}),W_{0},W^{*})\in R_{\mathcal{F}}\right)\big]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

•

Zero-knowledge.

There exists a PPT simulator
\mathcal{S}
such that for all
(W_{0},W^{*})
with
W^{*}-W_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
the distributions

(C_{0},C^{*},\mathcal{F},\pi)\quad\text{and}\quad(C_{0},C^{*},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{S}(C_{0},C^{*},\mathcal{F}))

are computationally indistinguishable.

•

Succinctness.

The verifier’s running time and the proof size are bounded by

\mathsf{poly}(\lambda,\text{complexity}(\mathcal{F}),\log|W_{0}|),

where
|W_{0}|
is the total model dimension and
\text{complexity}(\mathcal{F})
captures drift structure, e.g., norm threshold
\epsilon
, rank
r
, and sparsity
k
in
3
.

This abstract definition leaves open how to instantiate
\Pi
. We address this via SMDPs.

3.4.
Succinct Model Difference Proofs

We now introduce a primitive that captures the core cryptographic task underlying FTI.

Definition 3.3 (Succinct Model Difference Proof (SMDP)).

Let
W_{0}
,
W^{*}
be two model parameter tensors and
\mathcal{F}
a drift class. An SMDP for
\mathcal{F}
is a proof system
\mathsf{SMDP}_{\mathcal{F}}=(\mathsf{Prove},\mathsf{Verify})
such that:

•

the prover takes
(W_{0},W^{*},\mathcal{F})
as input;

•

the verifier takes
(C_{0},C^{*},\mathcal{F})
as input;

•

the system satisfies correctness, soundness, zero-knowledge, and succinctness as defined for FTI
3.2
, with respect to the relation
R_{\mathcal{F}}
.

4.
SMDPs for Fundamental Drift Classes

We construct SMDPs for three fundamental drift classes: norm-bounded drift, low-rank drift, and sparse drift. These classes serve as basic components from which more elaborate drift policies can be assembled.

4.1.
Norm-Bounded SMDPs via Random Projections (NBDP)

Intuition.

The key insight is that random projections approximately preserve norms, a consequence of the Johnson-Lindenstrauss phenomenon. Instead of checking the full
n
-dimensional drift vector, the verifier checks a small number of random projections. If the drift norm exceeds the bound, at least one projection will detect this violation with high probability, because the projected value will exceed a calibrated threshold. Conversely, if the drift is within bounds, concentration inequalities guarantee that all projections remain below the threshold. The projections reveal no information about the actual weight values, preserving zero-knowledge. Succinctness follows because the number of projections depends only on the norm bound and the desired confidence level, not on the model dimension.

We first describe the SMDP construction for norm-bounded drift.
We focus on a single parameter block, multiple blocks can be handled by independent instances.

4.1.1.
Setup and Drift Class

Let
W_{0},W^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{\prime}}
be the parameter matrices of a block, and let

\Delta=W^{*}-W_{0}.

For a bound
\epsilon>0
, the norm-bounded drift class is

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{norm}}(\epsilon)=\big\{\,\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{\prime}}:\|\Delta\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\,\big\},

where the spectral norm of any rectangular matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{\prime}}
is

\|X\|_{2}=\max_{\|v\|_{2}=1}\|Xv\|_{2}.

When commitments require a vector input, we use the vectorization

\mathrm{vec}(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{dd^{\prime}},

which stacks the columns of
X
in a fixed order.

We assume a binding and hiding vector commitment scheme
\mathsf{Com}

(
Catalano and Fiore 2013
)
and a common source of randomness for generating public projection vectors.

4.1.2.
Random Projections

Let
m
be the number of random projections.
For each
i\in[m]
, let

r_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}^{dd^{\prime}}

be an independently sampled Rademacher vector, interpreted in the same vectorized order as
\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)
.

Define the projection

z_{i}=\langle r_{i},\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)\rangle.

We recall a standard concentration bound.

Lemma 4.1 (Hoeffding concentration
(
Pelekis and Ramon 2017
)
).

Let
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{\prime}}
and let
r\in\{-1,+1\}^{dd^{\prime}}
have independent Rademacher entries.
Then for any
t>0
,

\Pr\big[\,|\langle r,\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)\rangle|>t\,\big]\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}}\right),

where the Frobenius norm is

\|\Delta\|_{F}=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d^{\prime}}\Delta_{i,j}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Since
\|\Delta\|_{2}\leq\|\Delta\|_{F}
, the same inequality holds for
\|\Delta\|_{2}
.
In particular, if
\|\Delta\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
, then

\Pr\big[\,|z_{i}|>t\,\big]\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}\right).

4.1.3.
Protocol Description

Fix parameters
\epsilon>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Set

m=\Theta\big(\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta)\big),

and define

\tau=\epsilon\sqrt{2\log(2m/\delta)}.

Public parameters.

The public parameters specify the commitment scheme
\mathsf{Com}
, the number of projections
m
, the projection vectors
r_{1},\dots,r_{m}
, and the threshold value
\tau
.

Commitments.

The prover holds the base parameters
W_{0}
and the fine-tuned parameters
W^{*}
.
To commit to these tensors, the prover samples randomness
r_{0}
and
r^{*}
and publishes

C_{0}=\mathsf{Com}(\mathrm{vec}(W_{0});r_{0}),\qquad C^{*}=\mathsf{Com}(\mathrm{vec}(W^{*});r^{*}).

Proof generation.

The prover computes the drift matrix
\Delta=W^{*}-W_{0}
.
For each projection vector
r_{i}
, the prover computes

z_{i}=\langle r_{i},\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)\rangle.

To commit to each
z_{i}
, the prover samples fresh randomness
s_{i}
and computes

C_{z_{i}}=\mathsf{Com}(z_{i};s_{i}).

For every index
i
, the prover then produces:

(1)

a zero-knowledge proof
\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{lin}}
that the committed value
z_{i}
satisfies

z_{i}=\langle r_{i},\mathrm{vec}(W^{*})\rangle-\langle r_{i},\mathrm{vec}(W_{0})\rangle

relative to the commitments
C_{0}
,
C^{*}
, and
C_{z_{i}}
;

(2)

a zero-knowledge range proof
\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{rng}}
that
|z_{i}|\leq\tau
.

The prover outputs

\pi=\big(\{C_{z_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{m},\;\{\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{lin}},\,\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{rng}}\}_{i=1}^{m}\big).

Verification.

Given
(C_{0},C^{*},\epsilon,\delta)
and
\pi
, the verifier:

(1)

reconstructs
r_{1},\dots,r_{m}
and
\tau
;

(2)

verifies each
\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{lin}}
against
C_{0}
,
C^{*}
, and
C_{z_{i}}
;

(3)

verifies each
\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{rng}}
against
C_{z_{i}}
and
\tau
;

(4)

accepts if and only if all verifications succeed.

4.1.4.
Security Analysis

Theorem 4.2 (FTI-soundness of NBDP).

Assume that
\mathsf{Com}
is binding and that the linear-relation and range-proof systems used in NBDP are sound.
Let
\epsilon>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, and choose
m
and
\tau
as above.
Then any PPT adversary that outputs a proof accepted by the verifier with non-negligible probability must either break one of the underlying primitives or satisfy

\|\Delta\|_{2}\leq(1+\gamma)\epsilon

with probability at least
1-\delta
over the verifier’s randomness, for some absolute constant
\gamma>0
.

Proof sketch.

Suppose
\|\Delta\|_{2}>(1+\gamma)\epsilon
.
By Lemma
4.1
and the relationship between
\|\Delta\|_{2}
and
\|\Delta\|_{F}
, each projection
z_{i}
exceeds
\tau
in magnitude with probability at least a constant
c>0
depending only on
\gamma
.
With
m
independent projections, the probability that all
|z_{i}|\leq\tau
is at most
\delta
for the specified choice of
m
.
Given the binding property of
\mathsf{Com}
, the soundness of the linear-relation and range-proof systems, an adversary cannot produce valid proofs unless the committed
z_{i}
satisfy both the linear identities and the bounds.
Thus an accepting proof implies either that the underlying primitives are broken or that all projections lie within the threshold, which, by the concentration bound, forces
\|\Delta\|_{2}\leq(1+\gamma)\epsilon
except with probability at most
\delta
.
∎

Zero-knowledge follows from the hiding property of
\mathsf{Com}
together with the zero-knowledge guarantees of the linear and range-proof sub-protocols.

Theorem 4.3 (Succinctness).

For fixed
\epsilon
and
\delta
, the verifier’s running time and the proof size are
O(m)
up to polylogarithmic factors in the security parameter.
More precisely, both are bounded by

\mathsf{poly}\big(\lambda,\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta)\big),

and are independent of the dimensions
d
and
d^{\prime}
.

Proof sketch.

The proof consists of
m
commitments
C_{z_{i}}
and
m
pairs of auxiliary proofs
(\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{lin}},\pi_{i}^{\mathrm{rng}})
.
Each auxiliary proof has size
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
, independent of
d
and
d^{\prime}
, so the total proof size is
O(m\cdot\mathsf{poly}(\lambda))
.
Verification requires checking
m
linear proofs and
m
range proofs, each costing
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
time.
No step iterates over the matrix entries, all checks operate on committed scalars and short proofs.
Thus the verifier’s running time is
O(m\cdot\mathsf{poly}(\lambda))
, independent of
d
and
d^{\prime}
.
∎

4.2.
Low-Rank SMDPs via Matrix Polynomial Commitments (MRDP)

Intuition.

We encode each weight matrix as a bivariate polynomial, so that matrix rank translates into algebraic degree constraints. Specifically, a low-rank update decomposes into a sum of separable polynomial terms, each being a product of a univariate polynomial in
X
and a univariate polynomial in
Y
. The verifier checks this structure via random evaluation points, leveraging the Schwartz-Zippel lemma: if the drift is not genuinely low-rank, the polynomial identity required for verification would need to hold at a random point, which happens with negligible probability over a large field. Succinctness arises because the verifier only checks
O(r)
polynomial openings at a single random point, independent of the matrix dimensions.

We next consider drift classes that constrain the rank of matrix-valued parameters, motivated by low-rank adapters.

4.2.1.
Setup and Drift Class

Let
W_{0},W^{*}
denote the base and fine-tuned parameter blocks, and define the drift matrix
\Delta=W^{*}-W_{0}
.
For a prescribed rank bound
r
, the drift class is

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rank}}(r)=\{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{\prime}}:\operatorname{rank}(\Delta)\leq r\}.

To use polynomial commitments, we represent each real-valued entry by a fixed-point encoding into a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
All polynomial arithmetic and commitment operations
(
Libert 2024
)
(
You et al. 2023
)
are performed over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
For notational simplicity in the discussion that follows, we write
W_{0},W^{*}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d\times d^{\prime}}
, with the understanding that they arise from encoding the underlying real-valued matrices.

4.2.2.
Polynomial Encoding of Matrices

Given
W\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d\times d^{\prime}}
, we associate the bivariate polynomial

P_{W}(X,Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\sum_{j=0}^{d^{\prime}-1}W_{i,j}X^{i}Y^{j}.

The drift polynomial is

P_{\Delta}(X,Y)=P_{W^{*}}(X,Y)-P_{W_{0}}(X,Y).

If
\operatorname{rank}(\Delta)\leq r
, then there exist vectors
a_{k}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
and
b_{k}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d^{\prime}}
such that

\Delta=\sum_{k=1}^{r}a_{k}b_{k}^{\top}.

Define the univariate polynomials

f_{k}(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}(a_{k})_{i}X^{i},\qquad g_{k}(Y)=\sum_{j=0}^{d^{\prime}-1}(b_{k})_{j}Y^{j}.

This yields a separable decomposition

P_{\Delta}(X,Y)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}f_{k}(X)g_{k}(Y).

4.2.3.
Commitment and Protocol

Commitment.

The prover commits to the polynomials
P_{W_{0}}
,
P_{W^{*}}
and to each
f_{k}
and
g_{k}
using the polynomial commitment scheme.
Let
C_{W_{0}},C_{W^{*}},C_{f_{k}},C_{g_{k}}
denote these commitments.

Random point test.

The verifier samples a random point
(x,y)
uniformly from
\mathbb{F}_{q}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
The prover opens

P_{W_{0}}(x,y),\quad P_{W^{*}}(x,y),\quad f_{k}(x),\quad g_{k}(y)\quad\text{for }k=1,\dots,r

together with corresponding opening proofs.
The verifier then checks

P_{W^{*}}(x,y)-P_{W_{0}}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{r}f_{k}(x)g_{k}(y).

4.2.4.
Soundness

Lemma 4.4 (Schwartz–Zippel

(
Moshkovitz 2010
)
).

Let
Q(X,Y)
be a nonzero polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of total degree at most
D
.
Then

\Pr_{(x,y)\leftarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}}[\,Q(x,y)=0\,]\leq\frac{D}{q}.

Theorem 4.5 (FTI-soundness of MRDP).

Assume the polynomial commitment scheme is binding and its opening proofs are sound.
Let
\Delta
be the true drift.
If the MRDP verifier accepts with non-negligible probability over its choice of
(x,y)
, then with probability at least
1-D/q
we have
\operatorname{rank}(\Delta)\leq r
, where
D
is the degree of
P_{\Delta}
.

Proof sketch.

If
\operatorname{rank}(\Delta)>r
, then for any choice of polynomials
f_{k}
and
g_{k}
the difference

Q(X,Y):=P_{\Delta}(X,Y)-\sum_{k=1}^{r}f_{k}(X)g_{k}(Y)

is a nonzero polynomial.
The verifier’s check is equivalent to
Q(x,y)=0
at a randomly sampled
(x,y)
.
By the Schwartz–Zippel lemma, this holds with probability at most
D/q
.
An adversary whose proof is accepted with significantly higher probability must either force
Q
to be the zero polynomial, which implies a genuine rank-
r
decomposition of
\Delta
, or break the binding or soundness of the commitment scheme.
∎

Zero-knowledge follows from the hiding properties of the polynomial commitments and the zero-knowledge guarantees of their opening proofs.

Theorem 4.6 (Succinctness).

For fixed rank
r
, the verifier’s running time and the proof size scale as
O(r)
up to polylogarithmic factors in the security parameter, i.e. both are bounded by

\mathsf{poly}(\lambda,r),

These costs do not depend on the matrix dimensions
d
or
d^{\prime}
.

Proof sketch.

The prover commits to the bivariate encodings of
W_{0}
and
W^{*}
, and to the
2r
univariate polynomials
f_{k}
,
g_{k}
.
The verifier samples a single evaluation point and checks
O(r)
opened values with their commitment proofs.
Each opening proof has size and verification time
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
, with no dependence on
d
or
d^{\prime}
.

Thus the total proof size and verification time are
O(r\cdot\mathsf{poly}(\lambda))
, independent of the ambient matrix dimensions.
∎

4.3.
Sparse SMDPs via Streaming Interactive Proofs (SDIP)

Intuition.

Sparsity means that only
k
out of
n
coordinates change. We use random linear sketches that compress the drift vector while preserving information about its support. The prover commits to the sparse support set and the corresponding nonzero drift values, and the verifier checks consistency via streaming-style linear checks: a random inner product with the full parameter vectors must match the inner product computed from the declared sparse drift. Any undisclosed nonzero coordinate would cause a mismatch that is detected with high probability over the random sketch, since a nonzero vector has negligible chance of being orthogonal to a random vector. The proof size depends on the sparsity bound
k
and the number of repetitions, not on the ambient dimension
n
.

We now address drift classes that constrain the number of nonzero parameter updates.

4.3.1.
Setup and Drift Class

For the sparse-drift construction, we work with a vectorized form of all model parameters.
Let

\mathrm{vec}(W_{0}),\mathrm{vec}(W^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

denote the concatenation of all matrix entries in a fixed order.
We write these as
W_{0},W^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
when convenient.
For a sparsity bound
k
, the drift class is

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sparse}}(k)=\{\,\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\Delta\|_{0}\leq k\,\}.

To use commitment schemes, each real-valued entry is encoded into a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
through a fixed-point representation, after which all commitments and algebraic checks occur over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

4.3.2.
Sparse Witness Encoding

Let
S\subseteq[n]
denote the support of nonzero entries of
\Delta
with
|S|\leq k
.
Define
\Delta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\Delta^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}\Delta_{i}&\text{if }i\in S,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The prover must demonstrate that:

(1)

the support size satisfies
|S|\leq k
,

(2)

the relation
W^{*}=W_{0}+\Delta^{\prime}
holds on all supported indices,

(3)

no additional drift exists outside
S
, so that
\Delta=\Delta^{\prime}
.

Directly checking these equalities at all coordinates would require linear time, so we use random linear checks.

4.3.3.
Random Linear Checks

The verifier samples a random vector
r\leftarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and defines

T=\langle r,\Delta^{\prime}\rangle.

The prover must convince the verifier that

\langle r,W^{*}\rangle-\langle r,W_{0}\rangle=T.

If
\Delta\neq\Delta^{\prime}
, then the difference vector
h=\Delta-\Delta^{\prime}
is nonzero, and

\langle r,h\rangle=0

holds with probability only
1/q
for uniformly random
r
.

Lemma 4.7.

Let
h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be nonzero.
For a uniform random
r\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,

\Pr[\,\langle r,h\rangle=0\,]=\frac{1}{q}.

Proof.

Since
h\neq 0
, there is an index
j
with
h_{j}\neq 0
.
Conditioning on all coordinates of
r
except
r_{j}
, the inner product
\langle r,h\rangle
is an affine function of
r_{j}
with nonzero coefficient.
This equation has exactly one solution in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, so for uniform
r_{j}
the probability that the inner product is zero is
1/q
.
∎

Repeating the check with independent challenges
r^{(1)},\dots,r^{(t)}
reduces the error probability to
q^{-t}
.

4.3.4.
Zero-Knowledge Sparse Index Proofs

To avoid revealing the support
S
, the prover uses a binary indicator vector
u\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with
u_{i}=1
if and only if
i\in S
.
The prover commits to
u
and
\Delta^{\prime}
and proves the following properties without revealing
S
:

•

\sum_{i}u_{i}\leq k
,

•

\Delta^{\prime}_{i}=0
whenever
u_{i}=0
,

•

W^{*}_{i}=W_{0,i}+\Delta^{\prime}_{i}
for all indices with
u_{i}=1
,

•

for each
j
, the value
T^{(j)}
equals
\langle r^{(j)},\Delta^{\prime}\rangle
.

4.3.5.
Protocol Description

Public parameters.

These include the vector and scalar commitment schemes and the challenge count
t
.

Commitments.

The prover publishes

C_{0}=\mathsf{Com}(W_{0};r_{0}),\qquad C^{*}=\mathsf{Com}(W^{*};r^{*}).

Proof generation.

The prover chooses a support set
S
, constructs
\Delta^{\prime}
and
u
, commits to both, and produces:

(1)

a proof
\pi^{\mathrm{cnt}}
that
\sum_{i}u_{i}\leq k
,

(2)

a proof
\pi^{\mathrm{supp}}
that
\Delta^{\prime}_{i}=0
whenever
u_{i}=0
,

(3)

a proof
\pi^{\mathrm{eq}}
that
W^{*}=W_{0}+\Delta^{\prime}
on indices with
u_{i}=1
.

For each
j=1,\dots,t
:

(1)

the verifier samples
r^{(j)}\leftarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,

(2)

the prover computes
T^{(j)}=\langle r^{(j)},\Delta^{\prime}\rangle
and commits to it,

(3)

the prover generates a proof
\pi^{(j)}_{\mathrm{lin}}
that

\langle r^{(j)},W^{*}\rangle-\langle r^{(j)},W_{0}\rangle=T^{(j)}.

Verification.

The verifier checks
\pi^{\mathrm{cnt}}
,
\pi^{\mathrm{supp}}
,
\pi^{\mathrm{eq}}
and all linear proofs
\pi^{(j)}_{\mathrm{lin}}
, and accepts if all verifications succeed.

4.3.6.
Security Analysis

Theorem 4.8 (FTI-soundness of SDIP).

Assume the commitment schemes are binding and all sub-protocols are sound.
If the verifier accepts with probability greater than negligible, then with probability at least
1-q^{-t}
over the random challenges, the drift satisfies
\|\Delta\|_{0}\leq k
.

Proof sketch.

If
\|\Delta\|_{0}>k
, then any claimed support
S
with
|S|\leq k
omits at least one nonzero entry of
\Delta
, so
\Delta-\Delta^{\prime}\neq 0
.
By Lemma
4.7
, each linear check detects this discrepancy with probability at least
1-1/q
.
The probability that all
t
checks fail to detect it is at most
q^{-t}
.
Binding commitments and sound sub-protocols ensure that any accepting proof must satisfy all linear checks, so except with probability
q^{-t}
, we must have
\Delta=\Delta^{\prime}
with
|S|\leq k
, which implies
\|\Delta\|_{0}\leq k
.
∎

Zero-knowledge follows from hiding commitments, the use of the indicator vector
u
in place of the explicit support
S
, and the zero-knowledge guarantees of the sub-protocols.

Theorem 4.9 (Succinctness).

For fixed sparsity bound
k
and challenge count
t
, the verifier’s running time and the proof size are

\mathsf{poly}(\lambda,k,t,\log n),

independent of
n
beyond logarithmic factors.

Proof sketch.

The verifier checks a constant number of counting and support-consistency proofs over at most
k
active coordinates, and performs
t
linear checks.
Each check involves only scalar openings and field operations and has cost
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
.
The dependence on
n
enters only through index encoding and hashing, which contributes at most logarithmic factors.
Thus both verification time and proof size are bounded by
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda,k,t,\log n)
.
∎

5.
Lower Bound: Necessity of Structured Drift

In this section we show that structure is essential for obtaining succinct fine-tuning integrity proofs in an information-theoretic setting.
If the allowed drift is unstructured, then any non-interactive proof system achieving statistical FTI must have communication cost
\Omega(n)
, where
n
is the number of parameters.
This does not contradict the existence of succinct computationally-sound SNARKs for arbitrary drift classes, but it shows that without algebraic assumptions or trusted setup, succinctness requires structure such as norm, rank, or sparsity.
Our constructions in Sections
4
use such structure to avoid this barrier.

5.1.
A Statistical FTI Model

We adopt a statistical variant of FTI in which both soundness and zero-knowledge hold against unbounded adversaries.
For this section we work over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, since information-theoretic lower bounds are most naturally stated in discrete domains.
We consider a single vector-valued parameter block

W\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},

and define the fine-tuned parameters as
W^{*}=W_{0}+\Delta
for a drift vector
\Delta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
The argument extends to multi-block models by concatenation.

Definition 5.1 (Statistical FTI).

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be a drift class.
A non-interactive proof system
\Pi=(\mathsf{Prove},\mathsf{Verify})
achieves
statistical FTI
for
\mathcal{F}
if:

•

Completeness.

For all
W_{0}
and all
\Delta\in\mathcal{F}
, if
W^{*}=W_{0}+\Delta
then

\Pr[\mathsf{Verify}(W_{0},W^{*},\pi)=1]=1,

where
\pi\leftarrow\mathsf{Prove}(W_{0},W^{*})
.

•

Statistical soundness.

For every (possibly unbounded) prover,

\Pr\big[\mathsf{Verify}(W_{0},W^{*},\pi)=1\ \wedge\ (W^{*}-W_{0})\notin\mathcal{F}\big]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

•

Statistical zero-knowledge.

There exists a simulator whose output is statistically close to

\mathsf{Prove}(W_{0},W^{*})

for all
(W_{0},W^{*})
with
(W^{*}-W_{0})\in\mathcal{F}
.

We call
\Pi

succinct
if its proof length satisfies
|\pi|=o(n\log q)
.

We omit commitments in this model and let the verifier see both
W_{0}
and
W^{*}
.
This only strengthens the lower bound.

5.2.
Unstructured Drift and One-Way Communication

To model drift with no exploitable structure, we consider all vectors within a Hamming ball of radius
t
.

Definition 5.2 (Hamming-ball drift class).

For
t\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, define

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(t)=\{\,\Delta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}:d_{H}(\Delta,0)\leq t\,\},

where
d_{H}
denotes Hamming distance
(
Norouzi et al. 2012
)
.

When
t=\alpha n
for a constant
\alpha<1/2
, this drift class is exponentially large and contains no algebraic structure that could support compression.
Statistically certifying membership in such a set is closely related to distinguishing small from large Hamming distance, which is an inherently communication-intensive task.

This connection is captured by a standard one-way communication problem.
Alice receives
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, Bob receives
y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and they are promised that

d_{H}(x,y)\leq t\qquad\text{or}\qquad d_{H}(x,y)\geq 3t,

for some
t\leq n/4
.
Alice sends a single message to Bob, who must determine which case holds.
Any protocol solving this problem with small error requires
\Omega(n)
bits of one-way communication
(
Kumar 2008
)
.

If a succinct statistical FTI proof existed for
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(t)
, producing proofs of length
o(n)
, then such proofs could be used to construct a one-way protocol for this promise problem with communication
o(n)
, contradicting the
\Omega(n)
lower bound.
Thus unstructured drift cannot admit succinct statistical FTI.

5.3.
Lower Bound Theorem

Theorem 5.3 (Information-theoretic lower bound for unstructured drift).

Let
t=\alpha n
for a constant
\alpha<1/4
and let
\varepsilon_{s},\varepsilon_{c}<1/3
.
Suppose there exists a non-interactive proof system achieving statistical FTI for
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(t)
with soundness error at most
\varepsilon_{s}
, completeness error at most
\varepsilon_{c}
, and proof size
|\pi|=o(n)
.
Then there exists a one-way communication protocol for the promise Hamming-distance problem with communication
o(n)
and error at most
\varepsilon_{s}+\varepsilon_{c}
.
This contradicts the
\Omega(n)
lower bound.
Hence no succinct statistical FTI scheme exists for
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(t)
.

Proof sketch.

Given
(x,y)
for the promise problem, interpret them as
(W_{0},W^{*})
and let Bob compute
\pi=\mathsf{Prove}(W_{0},W^{*})
.
Bob sends
\pi
to Alice, who runs
\mathsf{Verify}
.

If
d_{H}(x,y)\leq t
, then
y-x\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(t)
and completeness implies acceptance with probability at least
1-\varepsilon_{c}
.
If
d_{H}(x,y)\geq 3t
, then
y-x\notin\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Ham}}(t)
and soundness implies rejection with probability at least
1-\varepsilon_{s}
.

Thus the protocol solves the promise problem with error at most
\varepsilon_{s}+\varepsilon_{c}
using communication
|\pi|=o(n)
, contradicting the
\Omega(n)
lower bound.
∎

5.4.
Discussion

Theorem
5.3
shows that, without algebraic structure, statistical FTI requires linear-size proofs.
Our SMDP constructions avoid this barrier by exploiting structures: (1) norm, (2) rank, (3) sparsity, and by relying on computational assumptions to obtain succinct zero-knowledge proofs.

6.
Architecture-Aware Instantiations

The SMDP constructions in Section
4
apply to generic vector- or matrix-valued parameter blocks.
This section shows how to instantiate them for concrete neural architectures and how to compose block-level proofs into end-to-end fine-tuning integrity.
Our goals are:
(i) to demonstrate that the drift classes defined earlier align with common fine-tuning regimes, and
(ii) to illustrate how a verifier decomposes a full model into blocks, assigns each block a drift policy, and composes the resulting SMDPs.

We focus on three representative families: transformers, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs), but the same methodology extends to other architectures.

6.1.
SMDPs for Transformer Models

Consider a decoder-only transformer with
L
layers and model dimension
d
.
Each layer
\ell
contains:

•

a multi-head self-attention block with query, key, value, and output matrices

W_{Q}^{(\ell)},\ W_{K}^{(\ell)},\ W_{V}^{(\ell)},\ W_{O}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

•

a feedforward block with

W_{1}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\mathrm{ff}}},\qquad W_{2}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{ff}}\times d},

•

normalization parameters such as scale and bias vectors.

The model also includes a token embedding matrix
E\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times d}
and, depending on implementation, positional embeddings and output projections.

Block decomposition.

In the FTI framework each of the following is treated as a separate parameter block:

•

attention projections
W_{Q}^{(\ell)},W_{K}^{(\ell)},W_{V}^{(\ell)},W_{O}^{(\ell)}
,

•

feedforward matrices
W_{1}^{(\ell)},W_{2}^{(\ell)}
,

•

embedding matrix
E
and any untied output weights,

•

normalization parameters for each layer.

Fine-tuning patterns.

Common transformer fine-tuning regimes admit natural drift structure:

•

LoRA-style updates correspond to low-rank drift,

•

feedforward updates often stay small in norm,

•

prefix tuning induces sparse changes in embedding rows,

•

normalization parameters typically exhibit small-magnitude drift.

These patterns align directly with rank, norm, and sparsity drift classes.

Rank-bounded drift in attention.

For each attention matrix a policy may enforce

\Delta_{Q}^{(\ell)}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rank}}(r_{Q}),\quad\Delta_{V}^{(\ell)}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rank}}(r_{V}),

capturing rank-
r
LoRA adapters.
The MRDP scheme in Section
4.2
applies per projection matrix or even per head by decomposing
W
into
(d\times d_{h})
submatrices.
Verification remains
O(r)
per block.

Norm-bounded drift in feedforward layers.

Feedforward updates can be constrained by

\|\Delta_{1}^{(\ell)}\|_{F}\leq\epsilon_{1}^{(\ell)},\qquad\|\Delta_{2}^{(\ell)}\|_{F}\leq\epsilon_{2}^{(\ell)}.

The NBDP construction certifies these bounds independently of the width
d_{\mathrm{ff}}
.

Sparse drift in embeddings/prefixes.

Prefix tuning or soft prompts modify only a small set of rows in
E
.
If at most
k_{E}
rows change, then

\Delta_{E}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sparse}}(k_{E}).

SDIP verifies row-sparsity with proof size depending on
k_{E}
and logarithmically on the vocabulary size.

Norm-bounded drift in normalization parameters.

Normalization drifts are typically small, and a policy may require

\|\Delta_{\mathrm{LN}}^{(\ell)}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{LN}}^{(\ell)}.

NBDP applies directly to these low-dimensional vectors.

Example transformer policy.

A platform may enforce:

(1)

rank-bounded drift for all attention matrices,

(2)

norm-bounded drift for feedforward and normalization layers,

(3)

sparse drift for embeddings.

The global admissible drift set is then

\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Trans}}=\Big(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rank}}(r)\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{norm}}(\epsilon)\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{norm}}(\epsilon_{\mathrm{LN}}^{(\ell)})\Big)\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sparse}}(k),

with block-level MRDP, NBDP, and SDIP proofs aggregated into a full FTI proof.

6.2.
SMDPs for Convolutional Networks

A convolutional layer has kernel parameters

W^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}^{(\ell)}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}^{(\ell)}\times K_{h}^{(\ell)}\times K_{w}^{(\ell)}},

which can be grouped per layer or per output channel.

Channel-wise sparsity.

Pruning and lightweight tuning often alter only a small number of output channels.
If at most
k_{\ell}
channels are modified, the drift satisfies

\Delta^{(\ell)}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sparse}}(k_{\ell}),

with sparsity measured at the channel level.
The SDIP construction certifies this structure with complexity determined by
k_{\ell}
.

Norm-bounded kernel updates.

A policy may constrain the overall magnitude of kernel updates by requiring

\|\Delta^{(\ell)}\|_{F}\leq\epsilon_{\ell},

with analogous bounds imposed per channel if desired.
After vectorizing the kernels, the NBDP construction certifies these norm constraints succinctly.

Low-rank drift in
1\times 1
convolutions.

A
1\times 1
convolution is equivalent to a matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}}
.
If fine-tuning restricts updates to a low-rank subspace, MRDP verifies the rank bound.

Example CNN policy.

A CNN policy may combine:

(1)

channel-sparsity constraints

(2)

kernel norm bounds

(3)

rank bounds for
1\times 1
layers

Each component is certified by SDIP, NBDP, or MRDP.

6.3.
Instantiating SMDPs for MLPs and Feedforward Networks

An
L
-layer MLP with widths
(d_{0},\dots,d_{L})
has matrices

W^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}\times d_{\ell-1}}.

Each matrix forms a block with an associated drift class.

Norm-bounded drift.

Regularized tuning often restricts updates to

\|\Delta^{(\ell)}\|_{F}\leq\epsilon_{\ell},

which NBDP certifies succinctly.

Low-rank drift in bottleneck layers.

In architectures with bottlenecks or adapter modules, updates are parameterized as low-rank products.
MRDP verifies that drift remains rank-bounded.

Sparse neuron updates.

Policies that allow changing only
k_{\ell}
rows of a weight matrix induce row-sparse drift,

\Delta^{(\ell)}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sparse}}(k_{\ell}),

and SDIP verifies this property.

6.4.
Discussion: Composing Block-Level SMDPs

Across all architectures the workflow is the same:

(1)

Decompose the model into parameter blocks.

(2)

Assign each block a drift class
\mathcal{F}_{i}
reflecting desired constraints and expected fine-tuning behavior.

(3)

Instantiate SMDPs for each
\mathcal{F}_{i}
using NBDP, MRDP, or SDIP.

(4)

Aggregate block-level proofs into a global FTI proof for the product class
\mathcal{F}=\prod_{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}
.

The SMDP constructions depend on drift structure rather than model size.
This yields a uniform methodology: the cryptographic mechanisms are architecture-independent, yet the chosen decomposition and policies adapt to the architecture and deployment constraints.
We detail the policy language and proof aggregation strategy in Section
7
.

7.1.
Policy Language

A verifier declares a
fine-tuning policy

\mathcal{P}
that assigns an admissible drift class to each parameter block.
Let
W=\{W^{(i)}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
denote the model, where
\mathcal{I}
indexes blocks such as layers, attention heads, convolutional channels, or embedding rows.
A policy is a mapping

\mathcal{P}:i\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{i},

where each
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is chosen from

\{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{norm}}(\epsilon),\;\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rank}}(r),\;\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sparse}}(k),\text{ and their intersections}\}.

Composed drift classes.

A policy may simultaneously constrain magnitude, rank, and sparsity:

\mathcal{F}_{i}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{norm}}(\epsilon_{i})\cap\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rank}}(r_{i})\cap\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sparse}}(k_{i}).

The global drift class is the product

\mathcal{F}=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{F}_{i}.

7.2.
End-to-End Workflow

An FTI-enabled model release proceeds in four phases:

(1) Model decomposition and commitments.

The provider commits to each block:

C_{0}^{(i)}=\mathsf{Com}(W_{0}^{(i)};r_{0}^{(i)}),\qquad C^{*(i)}=\mathsf{Com}(W^{*(i)};r^{(i)}),

and aggregates
\{C_{0}^{(i)}\}
,
\{C^{*(i)}\}
into Merkle roots (
C_{0}
,
C^{*}
) to obtain concise commitments to the full model.

(2) Block-level proofs.

For each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, the prover constructs

\pi_{i}=\mathsf{SMDP}_{\mathcal{F}_{i}}(W_{0}^{(i)},W^{*(i)}),

using NBDP, MRDP, SDIP, or a composition thereof as dictated by
\mathcal{P}
.

(3) Proof aggregation.

The prover outputs

\Pi=\{(\pi_{i},i,\mathcal{F}_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},

optionally augmented with aggregated signatures or batched opening proofs to reduce overhead.
This binds each
\pi_{i}
to its block index and to the corresponding drift class.

(4) Verification.

Given
\Pi
and the model commitments
(C_{0},C^{*})
, the verifier: (i) authenticates
C_{0}^{(i)}
and
C^{*(i)}
through Merkle inclusion proofs; (ii) verifies each block proof
\pi_{i}
against its commitments and drift class
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, and (iii) accepts if and only if every block satisfies its assigned policy.

7.3.
Composability Across Fine-Tuning Stages

If a model evolves through a sequence

W_{0}\to W_{1}\to\cdots\to W_{T},

the system supports chained proofs:

\Pi_{t}:W_{t-1}\to W_{t}\text{ satisfies policy }\mathcal{P}_{t}.

This enables provenance tracking: a failure in any link invalidates all subsequent descendants.
Chaining also allows revocation or rollback by removing or rejecting a specific
\Pi_{t}
.

7.4.
Security Guarantees

By Theorem
5.3
, drift structure is necessary for succinctness in statistical settings.
The integrated system preserves:

•

Soundness.

Each SMDP is sound for its block-level relation; since blocks are independent, global soundness follows by a union bound.

•

Zero-knowledge.

ZK holds via standard hybrid arguments, as block proofs reveal nothing about drift outside their declared structure.

•

Succinctness.

The global proof size scales with the structural complexity of drift (rank, sparsity, allowed norms) rather than with the model dimension.

Together, these properties yield a scalable and practical mechanism for certifying fine-tuning integrity across complex architectures.

8.
Implementation and Evaluation

We implemented a prototype of our FTI system that integrates commitment backends, the NBDP, MRDP, and SDIP constructions, and a proof aggregator with a policy interpreter.
The prototype is designed to isolate the cryptographic cost of fine-tuning integrity from the underlying machine learning workload, so that proof size and verification time can be measured directly.

8.1.
Implementation Components

Commitment backends.

Vector-valued parameters are committed using a Merkle-tree-based
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
vector commitment instantiated with SHA-256.
This yields linear-time commitment, logarithmic size opening proofs, and small memory overhead. Matrix- and polynomial-valued objects in MRDP are handled using a polynomial commitment backend: KZG over BLS12-381 via the
arkworks
library
(
Wahby and Boneh 2019
)
, which provides constant-size openings, logarithmic verification time, and efficient batch evaluation.
Zero-knowledge in both cases is obtained via the Fiat–Shamir transform with domain-separated transcripts.

The proof aggregator compresses the raw output of NBDP, MRDP, and SDIP into a single structured proof artifact.

4.3\times
and verification time by about
7.8\times

8.2.

We evaluate the prototype along four axes: correctness under benign fine-tuning, detection of malicious drift, proof size and verification latency, and end-to-end feasibility on large models.

Detection of malicious drift.

Finally, we test whether violations of the drift policy are reliably detected.
If the rank of an attention update exceeds the allowed bound
r=8
, MRDP fails with probability greater than
0.99999
under random verifier challenges.
SDIP detects any extra nonzero drift coordinate beyond the claimed sparsity with false-negative probability below
10^{-10}
.
For norm-bounded policies, any drift whose norm exceeds the declared bound by more than ten percent causes NBDP to reject with confidence at least
1-\delta
for
\delta<2^{-30}
.
These experiments confirm that the SMDP schemes behave as predicted by the theoretical soundness guarantees, and that violations of structured drift constraints are caught with very high probability.

9.
Related Work

Research on zero-knowledge proofs for machine learning has focused on certifying correctness of inference or training. Systems such as zkML, CryptFlow, and Falcon
(
Al-Zubaidie and Tregi 2025
)
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
(
Kumar et al. 2020
)
encode the full forward pass and sometimes gradients inside a SNARK or MPC protocol, resulting in substantial prover overhead
(
Peng et al. 2025
)
. These approaches guarantee that model outputs are computed faithfully but do not constrain how models may evolve. In contrast, our work targets a different objective: certifying that fine-tuned models remain within a structured drift class, without re-executing or proving the full computation.

A complementary literature studies watermarking, provenance, and secure parameter updates. Watermarking and ownership verification methods embed identifiable patterns into weights or predictions
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
(
Mohanty 1999
)
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
, while provenance systems
(
Gierend et al. 2024
)
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
track lineage through metadata or trusted infrastructure. Mechanisms for privacy-preserving parameter updates
(
Hunt et al. 2018
)
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
focus on selective disclosure in federated learning. These approaches, however, do not provide zero-knowledge guarantees, do not achieve succinctness, and do not enforce structural constraints such as norm-, rank-, or sparsity-bounded drift. Our constructions fill this gap by providing cryptographic assurances about how much, and in what structured way a model has changed.

Our techniques also build on advances in succinct proofs for linear algebra, streaming verification, and random projections. MRDP leverages polynomial-commitment-based proofs for matrix rank and linear operations
(
Boneh et al. 2020
)
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
, while SDIP draws from space efficient streaming verification frameworks
(
Block et al. 2021
)
(
Pappas and Papadopoulos 2024
)
. NBDP connects classical concentration results
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
to cryptographic ZK settings, enabling norm-bounded guarantees via random projections. Motivated by emerging empirical work on safety drift and backdoor risks during fine-tuning
(
Zhu et al. 2023
)
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
, we develop structured, enforceable proofs of controlled model change. Finally, our lower-bound analysis relates to classical communication-complexity results
(
Kushilevitz 1997
)
and impossibility bounds for assumption-free SNARKs
(
Brinkmann et al. 2013
)
, giving an FTI-specific formulation of why structure is necessary for succinct statistical proofs.

10.
Discussion and Limitations

Semantic vs. Parametric Integrity.

FTI constrains how much model
parameters
may drift, but it does not directly bound
semantic
or behavioral changes.
Nonlinear architectures can exhibit disproportionate functional shifts even under small parameter perturbations.
Bridging this gap, by developing semantic drift metrics, robustness-aware constraints, or hybrid guarantees that relate parameter displacement to output behavior, remains an important direction for future work.

Expressiveness of Drift Classes.

Our framework focuses on norm-bounded, rank-bounded, and sparse drift, which align with common fine-tuning regimes such as LoRA, prefix tuning, and structured pruning.
However, some techniques rely on richer algebraic structures, including block-circulant matrices, Kronecker-factored subspaces, or hardware-aware sparsity patterns.
Supporting such classes without losing succinctness will likely require new polynomial encodings or vector-commitment abstractions that better capture these structures.

Prover Cost and Cryptographic Assumptions.

Although proofs are succinct and verification is fast, prover cost remains significant for very large models.
GPU-accelerated arithmetic and distributed proving help, but scaling to frontier models with 70B-500B parameters remains an engineering challenge.
Furthermore, MRDP currently relies on KZG commitments and thus on a structured reference string; while IPA or STARK-style commitments eliminate trusted setup, they increase proof sizes and verifier costs, suggesting an inherent trade-off between transparency and efficiency. We note that the KZG trusted setup can be replaced with universal or transparent alternatives. IPA-based commitments, as used in Halo and similar systems
(
Bünz et al. 2018
)
, eliminate the trusted setup at the cost of larger proofs and slower verification. STARK-based approaches offer full transparency but with further proof-size overhead. For deployment scenarios where trust in a setup ceremony is unacceptable, these alternatives provide a viable path, and our SMDP constructions can be adapted to work with any extractable polynomial commitment scheme. The choice between KZG, IPA, and STARK backends is thus a deployment-time decision that does not affect the core structure of our protocols.

Normal versus Adversarial Drift.

An important question is how benign fine-tuning differs structurally from adversarial modification. Empirical studies show that standard fine-tuning with common hyperparameters produces weight updates with predictable structural properties: small Frobenius norm relative to the base weights, low effective rank due to the low-dimensional structure of gradient updates, and sparse modification patterns when using adapter-based methods. In contrast, adversarial modifications such as backdoor insertion
(
Raghuram et al. 2024
)
or safety removal
(
Jain et al. 2024
)
typically require coordinated changes across multiple layers or large-magnitude perturbations to specific weight subspaces. FTI exploits this structural gap: by requiring proofs that updates conform to realistic drift classes, it raises the bar for adversaries who must now either (a) confine their attack to the permitted drift class, significantly limiting attack effectiveness, or (b) exceed the drift bounds and risk detection. While we do not claim that all attacks are detectable—an adversary operating within the permitted drift class may still cause undesirable behavior—FTI narrows the attack surface and complements behavioral testing and monitoring.

System and Policy-Level Risks.

Our guarantees assume correct implementations of primitives and secure randomness.
Malicious randomness, timing leakage, or misconfigured policies fall outside our current threat model.
Future work includes integrating stronger RNG validation, hardware isolation, semantic drift monitors, and support for interactive or online fine-tuning workflows.

Despite these limitations, FTI offers a principled foundation for cryptographically auditable fine-tuning pipelines and a practical path toward accountable model evolution.

10.1.
Choosing Policy Parameters

Selecting appropriate policy parameters is critical for balancing security against usability. We provide guidance for each drift class.

Norm bound
\varepsilon
.

The norm bound should be calibrated to the expected magnitude of benign fine-tuning updates. Practitioners can estimate this by running reference fine-tuning experiments on representative data and measuring
\|W^{*}-W_{0}\|_{F}
across parameter blocks. A common heuristic is to set
\varepsilon
to two to three times the observed norm of legitimate updates, providing a margin that accommodates natural variance across runs while remaining tight enough to detect adversarial modifications. For models fine-tuned with weight decay coefficient
\lambda_{\mathrm{wd}}
, the expected drift norm can also be estimated analytically from the learning rate, number of steps, and dataset size.

Rank bound
r
.

For LoRA-based fine-tuning,
r
should match or slightly exceed the adapter rank used in training, which is typically
r\in\{4,8,16,32\}
depending on the model size and task complexity. Setting
r
too low rejects legitimate updates and forces re-proving after minor adjustments; setting
r
too high weakens the integrity guarantee by allowing an adversary to make richer modifications. When the fine-tuning method is known in advance, the rank bound can be set precisely. When it is not, a conservative choice of
r=2r_{\mathrm{train}}
provides a reasonable safety margin.

Sparsity bound
k
.

The sparsity bound should reflect the number of parameters expected to change. For prefix tuning,
k
equals the number of prefix tokens multiplied by the embedding dimension. For full fine-tuning of selected layers,
k
equals the total parameter count of those layers. For adapter-based methods that modify only specific modules,
k
should be set to the total number of parameters in the adapted modules plus a small margin for numerical effects.

General guidance.

The policy should be agreed upon before fine-tuning begins and should reflect both the intended training procedure and the risk tolerance of the deployer. Tighter bounds provide stronger integrity guarantees but require more careful calibration; overly restrictive policies lead to false rejections that undermine trust in the system. In practice, we recommend an iterative calibration process: run the intended fine-tuning procedure several times, measure the resulting drift statistics, and set policy bounds that comfortably encompass the observed distribution while excluding clearly adversarial modifications.

11.
Conclusion

We introduced
fine-tuning integrity
as a new cryptographic objective for modern neural networks and presented
succinct model difference proofs
(SMDPs) as a foundational mechanism for certifying controlled and verifiable model evolution.
Our constructions support three fundamental drift classes: norm-bounded, low-rank, sparse, and achieve succinct, zero-knowledge guarantees with verifier complexity determined by drift structure rather than model size.
We further established that such structural assumptions are not merely convenient but
necessary
for succinct statistical FTI, and we demonstrated how SMDPs integrate cleanly into real-world architectures and fine-tuning workflows.

Together, these results outline a practical path toward cryptographically auditable fine-tuning pipelines.
We envision FTI as a building block for secure model governance, enabling accountable deployment, provenance tracking, and principled safety constraints.

Future work includes extending SMDPs to richer drift classes, bridging parametric and semantic integrity, and integrating FTI into end-to-end training and deployment infrastructures.
