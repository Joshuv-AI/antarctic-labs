Abstract
Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder caused by neuronal hyperexcitability. Pinacidil (PIN) has emerged as a
potential therapeutic alternative for individuals who discontinue conventional treatment due to adverse effects.
However, its hepatic and renal effects remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the histological effects of PIN on
the liver and kidneys of rats with induced epilepsy. Twenty-six Wistar rats were divided into three groups: Negative
Control (NC) (0.9% NaCl i.p.), Treatment Group (TG) (Pinacidil 5 mg/kg i.p.), and Positive Control (PC) (Diazepam 2
mg/kg i.p.). After 30 minutes, the convulsant agent pentylenetetrazol (80 mg/kg i.p.) was administered. Following
euthanasia and slide preparation, histological sections were evaluated for the presence of mononuclear cells (MC) in
the liver, and for mesangial matrix expansion (MME), vascular hyalinosis (VH), and tubular injury (TI) in the kidneys,
using the Banff and Zhang-Kudose scoring systems. Hepatic analysis revealed no statistically significant difference
between NC and TG regarding MC infiltration (p>0.05); however, TG showed significantly lower infiltration compared
to PC (p<0.05). Renal analysis showed that TG had higher MME and VH scores than NC (p<0.05), with no significant
difference compared to PC (p>0.05). No significant difference in TI was observed between NC and TG (p>0.05),
whereas TG showed lower TI scores than PC (p<0.05). Pinacidil represents a promising therapeutic alternative for
epilepsy without inducing significant lesions in the organs responsible for drug pharmacokinetics.
Keywords: Pinacidil; Histopathology; Kidney; Liver; Epilepsy.

1. Introduction
Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder resulting from neuronal hyperexcitability, predominantly localized in the
hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. The condition represents a significant global health burden, as 50 million people worldwide
are affected by epilepsy. Among these individuals, approximately 70% could live seizure-free with early diagnosis and adequate
treatment. (World Health Organization, 2024)
Individuals with epilepsy exhibit frequent and high-intensity electrical discharges in the central nervous system, which
may trigger clinical manifestations such as vigorous involuntary muscle contractions and transient loss of consciousness. These
characteristic symptoms result from repetitive and uncontrolled neuronal depolarizations caused by alterations in ion channel
function and imbalances between excitatory and inhibitory mechanisms, favoring the propagation of focal or generalized
electrical discharges. (Pereira et al., 2024)
In addition to the characteristic clinical manifestations of epilepsy, affected individuals are also burdened by adverse
reactions to conventional medications, including drowsiness, reduced alertness, and impaired motor coordination. Among these
drugs, benzodiazepines are commonly used for seizure control, as they act as agonists potentiating the action of gamma-

aminobutyric acid (GABA), the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter responsible for suppressing peak electrical discharges.
(McTague et al., 2018)
The cyanoguanidine compound Pinacidil, originally developed in the 1970s, is currently indicated for the treatment of
hypertension due to its ability to promote vascular smooth muscle relaxation, acting as a peripheral vasodilator. Furthermore,
Pereira et al. (Pereira et al., 2024) investigated the feasibility of therapeutic repositioning of Pinacidil for epilepsy based on its
potential mechanism of action. From this perspective, Pinacidil acts as an ATP-sensitive potassium channel opener, exerting its
effects on both pre- and postsynaptic regions of the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, thereby enabling repolarization and
hyperpolarization of neuronal membranes following excitatory depolarization activity. (Neri et al., 2022)
In this context, Pinacidil emerges as a therapeutic alternative for individuals who have discontinued treatment due to
the side effects of conventional therapies. Supporting these findings, Pereira et al. (Pereira et al., 2024) reported that the drug did
not produce motor side effects and, furthermore, promoted increased exploratory activity and locomotion in an animal model,
contributing significantly to seizure control and reduction of disabling adverse reactions.
Despite the therapeutic potential of Pinacidil, no studies have yet evaluated its impact on the renal and hepatic systems.
Given that the circulatory rate of drugs through the kidneys and liver is higher than in other tissues — as these organs are
responsible for the metabolism and excretion of substances — it is essential to investigate possible adverse effects, including
histopathological alterations. (Asconapé, 2014)
Therefore, understanding the chemical interaction of this drug with the primary organs involved in pharmacokinetics is
necessary to support a potential future therapeutic repositioning of Pinacidil, ensuring its efficacy and safety for individuals with
epilepsy. This study aimed to evaluate the histological effects of PIN on the liver and kidneys of rats with induced epilepsy.

2. Methodology
2.1 Experimental Design
This study was a lab one which employed a qualitative approach regarding microscope slide images and, a quantitative
one in relation to numbers (Risemberg et al., 2026; Pereira et al., 2018) regarding the statistical analysis (Costa Neto & Beckman,
2009) and, conducted through the analysis of previously prepared slides containing renal and hepatic tissue samples from 26
male Wistar rats (Rattus norvegicus), aged 4 to 5 weeks. The animals were obtained from ANILAB (Paulínia, SP, Brazil), and
drug administration was performed in partnership with the School of Medicine of the University of Ribeirão Preto (UNAERP),
Guarujá campus. The animals were divided into three groups — Negative Control, Positive Control, and Treatment Group —
and were euthanized 24 hours after experimentation, characterizing acute exposure and effect.
Organ collection was performed following approval by the Research Ethics Committee of the Institute of Health of the
University of Ribeirão Preto. Experimental protocols involving animals were carried out in accordance with the guidelines for
the use of animals in research, following the Brazilian College of Animal Experimentation (COBEA) and the American
Guidelines for Animal Care (2020), avoiding unnecessary sacrifice and suffering of the animals. In addition, the animals were
maintained under controlled conditions (24 ± 2°C) with a 12-hour light-dark cycle and had free access to food and water. Project
approval was granted by the Animal Use Ethics Committee (CEUA-UNAERP) under registration number 007/2024.
2.2 Drug Administration
The animals were divided into three groups: Negative Control (0.9% NaCl saline solution), Treatment Group (5 mg/kg
intraperitoneal Pinacidil), and Positive Control (2 mg/kg intraperitoneal Diazepam), totaling 26 male Wistar rats. Subsequently,
after administration of the saline solution and the drugs to each group, and following a 30-minute interval, the seizure inducer

2.3 Tissue Collection and Preparation
After experimentation and euthanasia, the organs were collected and stored in ALFAC solution (85% 80 GL ethyl
alcohol, 10% formaldehyde, and 5% glacial acetic acid) and kept refrigerated at 5°C. Subsequently, the Histocell Soluções em
Anatomia Patológica Ltda. Laboratory was responsible for slide preparation of the kidney and liver samples, performing the
processes of fixation, dehydration, clearing, embedding, microtomy, and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. The slides were
then made available to UNAERP – Guarujá campus and sent to the Pontifical Catholic University of Minas Gerais (PUC Minas),
Poços de Caldas campus, where the histological analyses were performed for the purpose of this study.
2.4 Hepatic Histopathological Evaluation
This study was responsible for the histopathological analysis of the main organs involved in pharmacokinetics, the liver
and kidneys. Slide analysis was performed at the Cytology Laboratory of PUC Minas, Poços de Caldas campus, using an
Olympus CX41 trinocular microscope and image capture of histological structures with LC Micro software.
Thus, an analysis of mononuclear cell infiltrate was performed in the portal triad and centrilobular vein regions. Prior
to quantification, a standardized pattern for the presence and absence of leukocyte infiltrate was established (Figure 2) by
evaluating histological sections from the Negative Control group, taking into account the baseline presence of resident Kupffer
cells in the hepatic sinusoids and perivascular leukocytes, in order to avoid identification bias. Accordingly, 10 random fields
were examined for portal triads and 10 for central veins, with the number of inflammatory infiltrates identified per animal ranging
from 0 to 10.

2.6 Statistical Analysis
Based on the data collected in the laboratory, statistical analysis was conducted by the authors with the assistance of
the Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) tool Claude Sonnet (Anthropic, version 4.6). Raw data were manually entered into
the platform, and statistical methods were carried out through structured prompts developed by the researchers. The use of this
GAI tool is declared in compliance with CNPq Ordinance No. 2,664/2026. All generated results were reviewed and validated by
the authors prior to incorporation into the manuscript. Data were organized and detailed using the Python programming language
(version 3.11; Python Software Foundation) via Claude Sonnet (Anthropic, 4.6). Normality of the data was assessed using the
Shapiro-Wilk test (Shapiro & Wilk, 1965), which is indicated for samples with n < 50. Given the absence of normality across
multiple groups, the ordinal and discrete nature of the variables, and the unequal sample sizes among groups, a non-parametric
approach was adopted.
For hepatic analyses (centrilobular vein and portal triad), the Positive Control group resulted in n = 5 after exclusion of
animal D9, identified as an outlier by two independent methods (Tukey and Grubbs). For renal analyses (Banff and ZhangKudose scores), the Treatment Group resulted in n = 13 after exclusion of one animal without adequately assessable vascular
structures. For comparisons among groups (Negative Control, Treatment Group, and Positive Control), the Kruskal-Wallis test
(Kruskal & Wallis, 1952) with tie correction was applied. When a significant difference was identified, Dunn's post hoc test
(Dunn, 1964) with Bonferroni correction (k = 3 comparisons; adjusted α = 0.0167) was performed for the planned comparisons:
Negative Control × Treatment and Positive Control × Treatment. In addition, results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation
(SD) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI), calculated using the two-tailed Student's t method. The significance level adopted
was α = 0.05 (two-tailed), and all p-values are reported with full precision.

4. Discussion
The present study aimed to evaluate the histological effects of the drug Pinacidil on the main organs involved in
pharmacokinetics, the liver and kidneys, under induced seizure conditions. In this context, experimental groups were used to
compare histopathological alterations in order to assess the cytotoxic safety of the administered substances, with a focus on the
action of Pinacidil. Thus, the results obtained indicate that this antihypertensive drug did not present relevant alterations when
compared to the Negative Control and Positive Control groups.
The analysis of mononuclear cell infiltrate in the liver, particularly in the portal triad and centrilobular vein regions,
was necessary given that these are the entry and exit routes for substances and metabolites, respectively (Choi, 2025). In this
sense, the analysis of leukocyte infiltrate in portal triads predicts the cytotoxic action of the drug prior to metabolization,
suggesting an immediate immune response to the drug's action upon hepatic entry. Thus, the results obtained indicated that the
action of Pinacidil (TG) had an influence similar to that of the inducer in the Negative Control (NC) group on the hepatic vascular
region. This is consistent with studies indicating that the seizure inducer pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) causes cellular stress by
triggering neuronal hyperexcitability, resulting in momentary hypoxia and cell membrane microlesions. Accordingly, PTZinduced seizures generate cellular damage, which promotes the recruitment of mononuclear inflammatory cells, such as
neutrophils and hepatic macrophages (Kupffer cells), mediated by the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6.
Under this perspective, it is worth noting that the inflammatory profile results were similar for both the portal triads and
centrilobular veins, since no statistically significant difference was found between the Negative Control and Treatment groups,
whereas the inflammatory pattern under Pinacidil administration was lower than that observed under the Diazepam dose in the
Positive Control. Accordingly, the inflammatory profile of the Treatment Group did not differ statistically from the Negative
Control in any of the regions evaluated, indicating that Pinacidil did not induce additional inflammatory recruitment beyond that
observed under baseline conditions.

Furthermore, it is worth considering that the vasodilation promoted by the drug may favor leukocyte diapedesis, given
that the increase in vessel diameter slows blood flow velocity, thereby increasing vascular permeability (Filippi, 2016). However,
the data did not show this effect to a statistically distinct extent from that observed in the Negative Control, indicating no relevant
inflammatory impact during the acute exposure period evaluated in the study. Therefore, the presence of inflammatory cells is
not necessarily an immune response to the chemical component of the drug, but rather a consequence of the vasodilation
mechanism, which allows for the visualization of leukocytes in histological analysis (Moazzam et al., 1997).
In addition to not presenting deficits in motor coordination (Pereira et al., 2024), Pinacidil has a short biological halflife and rapid metabolization (up to 3 hours), which prevents the accumulation of metabolites in the liver and the consequent
inflammatory process evident in the central vein regions (Laher & Hickey, 1985). This differs from benzodiazepines, which have
a half-life of up to 100 hours in the body, favoring the accumulation of the metabolite N-desmethyldiazepam and, consequently,
a hepatic inflammatory response. This tissue-level microscopic finding is confirmed by the results obtained in this study, which
demonstrate a lower degree of perivascular infiltrate in the Treatment Group compared to the Positive Control.
Regarding the findings observed in the renal analyses, it was possible to identify that the action of Pinacidil was similar
to that of medications traditionally used for epilepsy control, such as Diazepam (PC), when analyzing mesangial matrix
expansion and vascular hyalinosis using the Banff Score. In this sense, the evaluation of mesangial matrix expansion was
fundamental for assessing the degree of glomerular capillary compression and reduced filtration resulting from acute drug
exposure. Mesangial cells are activated by injury, plasma proteins, immune complexes, or inflammatory mediators (Daha, 2000).
Furthermore, EMM is an early sign of glomerular injury, since acute drug exposure may cause mesangial edema and consequent
reduction of Bowman's capsule, impacting renal glomerular filtration function. Accordingly, the score results indicated that the
Treatment Group presented greater mesangial matrix alterations than the Negative Control, indicating that the seizure inducer
pentylenetetrazole did not alter glomerular architecture.
In another analysis, Pinacidil and Diazepam showed statistically equivalent effects on glomerular architecture, with no
significant difference between TG and PC in the mesangial expansion scores. Although the mean value for the Treatment Group
(2.07) was numerically lower than that of the Positive Control (2.57), this difference did not reach statistical significance, and it
is therefore not possible to state that Pinacidil caused less glomerular impact than Diazepam. Thus, both drugs presented a similar
mesangial expansion profile in this acute exposure model.
Furthermore, the mechanism of action of Pinacidil allows for vasodilation and a consequent abrupt increase in
hydrostatic pressure within the glomerular capillaries. This sudden pressure increase mechanically stretches the mesangial cells,
which respond temporarily to this stress with increased proliferation, contraction, and synthesis of collagen I and III—
characteristic findings of acute exposure to a vasodilator drug (Thomas & Ford Versypt, 2022).
In addition, vascular hyalinosis occurs as an indirect, long-term consequence of the use of potent vasodilators such as
Pinacidil. However, since this study involved acute drug exposure, the presence of hyalinosis was attributed to acute vascular
injury caused by deep and sudden arteriolar vasodilation. In animal models, this abrupt change in vessel diameter may lead to
the opening of endothelial junctions, allowing infiltration of hyaline material (Clemo et al., 2003; Guionaud, 2015). Thus, the
present results indicate that PTZ does not cause hyalinosis in renal vasculature, but that the drugs used for seizure control cause
mild arteriolar protein extravasation, as demonstrated in the prior histological analysis. Nevertheless, in order to confirm the
severity of this extravasation and its potential impact on the integrity of renal arterioles, a study involving chronic administration
of both the TG and PC drugs is warranted.
In addition to the evaluation of renal vascular structures, tubular analysis was essential, given that ion channels, such as
potassium (K+) channels, are present in cells beyond the central nervous system. In renal tubules, potassium channels play an

increase in solute and fluid influx. In the nephron, mechanosensitive K+ channels have been identified in both the apical and
basolateral membranes (Giebisch, 2001). Accordingly, this study used the Zhang and Kudose scores, adapted for the
histopathological analysis of epithelial denudation, tubular degeneration, and cast formation, which impair proper renal
excretion. In this regard, the results indicate that Pinacidil administration produced alterations similar to those found with the
seizure inducer (PTZ), but statistically different from those found in the Diazepam group, which presented, according to the
score, moderate to severe tubular alterations. Thus, it is worth noting that the Treatment Group exhibited a predominantly
moderate degree of tubular injury, equivalent to that observed in the Negative Control, confirming that Pinacidil did not add
tubular damage beyond that caused by the seizure inducer, PTZ.
Furthermore, it is important to consider that tubular alterations may occur due to elevated production of reactive oxygen
species resulting from PTZ administration. In this regard, since renal tubular cells (especially those of the proximal convoluted
tubule) contain a high density of mitochondria, given their need for energy (ATP) to perform active transport and electrolyte
reabsorption, oxidative stress directly interferes with the mitochondrial membranes of these renal cells, disrupting energy
production and triggering cellular degeneration processes (Geng et al., 2025).
However, these mechanisms could be investigated in greater depth in future studies involving chronic administration
and larger sample sizes, allowing for the assessment of whether the injury patterns observed change as a result of prolonged drug
exposure.

5. Conclusion
The results of the present study demonstrate that acute administration of Pinacidil did not induce relevant
histopathological alterations in the hepatic and renal tissues of Wistar rats subjected to pentylenetetrazole-induced seizures, when
compared to the Negative Control and Positive Control groups under the experimental conditions.
Regarding the hepatic analysis, Pinacidil did not show a statistically significant difference from the Negative Control
in terms of mononuclear cell infiltrate in portal triads and centrilobular veins, indicating the absence of relevant inflammatory
hepatotoxicity. Furthermore, the hepatic inflammatory profile observed in the Treatment Group was statistically lower than that
found in the Positive Control, which may be attributed to the short biological half-life of Pinacidil and the consequent absence
of hepatotoxic metabolite accumulation.
Regarding the renal analysis, although Pinacidil resulted in higher scores for mesangial matrix expansion and vascular
hyalinosis compared to the Negative Control, these alterations were statistically equivalent to those observed with Diazepam, a
drug commonly used in epilepsy therapy. In addition, regarding tubular alterations, the Treatment Group presented scores similar
to those of the Negative Control and significantly lower than those of the Positive Control, indicating a lower impact on renal
tubular architecture.
Therefore, the histopathological findings obtained indicate that Pinacidil presents a tissue safety profile compatible with
its potential therapeutic use in seizure control, without inducing tissue damage greater than that already associated with
conventional benzodiazepine treatment. Nevertheless, further studies involving chronic administration and larger sample sizes
are needed to consolidate the pharmacokinetic safety of the drug in animal models, with the aim of supporting future
investigations into the therapeutic repositioning of Pinacidil for the treatment of epilepsy.
