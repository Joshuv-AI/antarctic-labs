Temporal Routing in Static Networks: The Schedule Completion Problem
Michelle Döring, Niklas Mohrin, George Skretas

Abstract
We introduce the TemporallyEdgeDisjointScheduleCompletion ( TEDSC ) problem in which we need to cover a set of temporal edge demands D D by routing k k temporal walks through a directed static graph while remaining temporally edge disjoint. This problem combines the temporal aspects of train routing and passenger demands with the static nature of real-world rail networks. We present a polynomial time algorithm for TEDSC . Motivated by real world constraints, we next investigate two restricted variants of TEDSC in which each walk can only travel for some bounded distance or time h h . We show that both are tractable when parameterized by k + h k+h , but hard for h h and k + | D | k+\lvert D\rvert . If we fix the underlying network, the two problems exhibit distinct complexities: The distance variant remains 𝖶 ⁡ [ 1 ] {\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1] -hard parameterized by k k even on a path of three vertices whereas the time variant admits an 𝖥𝖯𝖳 \mathsf{FPT} algorithm on any fixed star. Finally, we show how to approximate the number of required walks up to a factor of ( 2 − h − 1 ) (2-h^{-1}) .

Introduction

Transportation networks are a major source of inspiration for graph-theoretic problems.
A prominent example is the real-world problem of scheduling train trips across a network, where each train must be routed between a pair of nodes.
This problem has spurred work in graph theory that accounts for various constraints that may arise.
One of the foundational approaches to modeling this is the
Edge Disjoint Paths
(
EDP
) problem
[
23
,
35
]
.
In this scenario, we are given a graph
G=(V,E)
and pairs of vertices
s_{i},t_{i}
.
The objective is to find a path from
s_{i}
to
t_{i}
for each pair such that no two paths share an edge.
This critical constraint prevents collisions between trains.
However, the
EDP
problem has two deficiencies: (i) its disjointness constraint does not reflect how train tracks are actually used in practice, and (ii) the routing objective is more complex than findings paths between pairs of vertices.
We discuss these two deficiencies in length, and propose how to fix them.

Regarding deficiency (i), the schedule generated by the
EDP
solution assigns each edge to at most one train.
This approach is unrealistic, because a track can accommodate multiple trains, as long as at most one occupies it at any given moment.
Temporal graphs
[
33
]
provide a natural model for this aspect by allowing the same edge to be traversed at different time steps by different trains.
A temporal graph
\mathcal{G}=(G,\lambda)
generalizes a static graph
G
(called the
footprint
) by equipping it with a labeling function
\lambda\colon E(G)\to 2^{\mathds{N}^{+}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
, where
\lambda(e)
is the set of time steps at which the edge
e
is available.
We call the pair
(e,t)
a
temporal edge
.
When traversing
\mathcal{G}
, the time steps of the used temporal edges must be strictly increasing.

In this new setting, we can generalize the
EDP
problem as follows: Given a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
and pairs of vertices
s_{i},t_{i}
, the new objective is to find a path from
s_{i}
to
t_{i}
for each pair, ensuring that no two paths share a temporal edge in
\mathcal{G}
.
This more faithfully captures the time-dependent nature of train conflicts.
However, the model still does not correctly reflect the track availability.
In a real-world network, the tracks are always passable, whereas the labeling function of a temporal graph artificially restricts at which time steps each edge may be used.

Both the static and the temporal model thus have complementary shortcomings.
The static model fails to account for the temporal dynamics of trains traveling along the network, while the temporal approach unnecessarily restricts the availability of edges. To combine the strengths of both methods, we propose working on a static graph
G=(V,E)
and source-destination pairs
(s_{i},t_{i})
, with the objective of finding temporal edge disjoint paths, i.e., no two paths traverse the same edge at the same time step, assuming unit travel time per edge.

In addressing deficiency (ii), it is important to note that
EDP
fails to capture real-world train scheduling in three ways:
(a) trains should serve specific passenger demands along their route, not merely an origin-destination pair;
(b) passengers require connections at particular times, rather than at arbitrary ones;
(c) a train may traverse the same track multiple times throughout the day.
We address all three by extending the problem as follows:
We are given a directed static graph
G=(V,E)
, and a set of demands
D
of the form
(u,v,t)
, where
(u,v)\in E
is an edge and
t\in\mathds{N}^{+}
is a time step.
We denote by
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
the temporal graph where every edge of
G
is available at all time steps until
\Lambda(D)=\max_{(u,v,t)\in D}t
.
The goal is to find the minimum number of temporal walks in
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
such that for every
(u,v,t)\in D
there is a walk that traverses the temporal edge
(u,v,t)
, and no two walks traverse the same temporal edge.
We call this the
Temporally Edge Disjoint Schedule Completion
(
TEDSC
) problem.

Problem 1.1
.

Temporally Edge Disjoint Schedule Completion (
TEDSC
)

\Input
Directed static graph
G
, draft schedule
D
, integer
k\in\mathds{N}
.

\Prob
Does there exist a schedule
S
in
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
with
\lvert S\rvert\leq k
that extends
D
?

The name reflects the structure of the input and the objective:
D
is a
draft schedule
of required trips, each specifying an edge that must be traversed at a particular time, and the goal is to
complete
this draft schedule by finding walks that cover every demanded trip while remaining
temporally edge disjoint
.
We call the resulting set of temporal walks a
schedule
.

TEDSC
directly models the goal of minimizing the number of walks needed to serve all passenger demands.
In practice, however, driver availability is often the binding constraint, as drivers are subject to legal working-time regulations. This motivates two constrained variants:
First, the length-constrained
\ell
-TEDSC
problem, which adds the restriction that every walk can contain at most
h
edges.
Second, the lifespan-constrained
\tau
-TEDSC
problem, where every walk is allowed to be active for no more than
h
time steps, meaning that the time difference between the start and end of a route cannot exceed
h
. Intuitively,
\ell
-TEDSC
upper bounds the amount of time that a train travels on tracks, whereas
\ell
-TEDSC
upper bounds the time spent traveling plus the time spent waiting at stations.

Formally, for a temporal walk
p
we denote by
\ell(p)
the length (number of temporal edges) and by
\tau(p)
the lifespan (number of time steps spanned) of
p
.
The
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
problems are defined like
TEDSC
, but have an additional integer
h
as input and require that all walks
p\in S
satisfy
\ell(p)\leq h
or
\tau(p)\leq h
respectively.

\tau
-TEDSC

We do so by providing a polynomial time algorithm for
TEDSC
and showing that
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
are
\mathsf{NP}

\ell
-TEDSC

\tau
-TEDSC

k
, (b) the size of the draft schedule
\lvert D\rvert
, (c) the upper bound on the length/lifespan of each walk
h
, and (d) the number of vertices of the input graph
n
.
Finally, we provide a
2
-approximation algorithm for
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
.

1.1
Discussion of our results

We begin our study with the
TEDSC
problem, where we provide an algorithm that solves
TEDSC
in polynomial time using a flow-based approach. We use a standard technique from temporal graphs, called
static expansion
, where we expand the graph in time.

Result 1
.

TEDSC
can be decided using one max-flow computation on a network with
\text{O}(n\cdot\lvert D\rvert\cdot(n+k))
vertices and
\text{O}((n+m)\cdot\lvert D\rvert\cdot(n+k))
edges.

While the technique we use is standard in the literature,
TEDSC
imposes a particular problem. Recall that
\Lambda(D)=\max_{(u,v,t)\in D}t
, that is,
\Lambda(D)
is the latest time step that has a demand that needs to be covered by the schedule. The size of the problem input depends on the number of vertices
n
of the graph and the number of demands
\lvert D\rvert
. However, the size of the static expansion depends on
\Lambda(D)
. Therefore, if the demands are spaced sufficiently apart, the size of the static expansion can be exponential in the size of the input. This is why both for
TEDSC
and its constrained variants, we always need to compress the graph we are working with.

We move on to the constrained problems,
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
, where we show that both problems are NP-hard, by providing a reduction from the
3-SAT
problem.

Result 2
.

Both
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard even if
h=5
.

This result is not surprising since both of our problems capture the complexity of edge disjoint paths of bounded length, which is an NP-hard problem. We now want to find out where the boundary between tractability and intractibility lies when we consider natural parameters of the problem. Our goal is to create a panorama of the parameterized complexity of
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
for the following three parameters: (i)
k
which is the number of walks, (ii)
h
which is the length bound and the lifespan bound in the
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
problem, respectively, and (iii)
\lvert D\rvert
, which is the number of demands.

First, note that our
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness result for
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
works for
h=5
.
This implies
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
are
\mathsf{paraNP}
-hard when parameterized by
h
.
We now examine
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
when they are parameterized by both
k
and
\lvert D\rvert
.

Result 3
.

Both
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
parameterized by
k+\lvert D\rvert
are
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hard on planar DAGs.

By tracking the growth of the parameters in the presented reductions, we get two conditional lower bounds based on the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH).

Result 4
.

Unless ETH fails, there is no algorithm that decides
\ell
-TEDSC
or
\tau
-TEDSC
in time
2^{o(n+m+k+\lvert D\rvert)}
or
f(k+\lvert D\rvert)n^{o(k+\lvert D\rvert)}
.

On the positive side, we provide an
\mathsf{XP}
algorithm for
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
parameterized by
k
. We argue that this
\mathsf{XP}
algorithm also works when we parameterize by
\lvert D\rvert
, by showing that we can safely assume that
k<\lvert D\rvert
. Finally, we provide an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm for
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
paramerized by
k+h
.

Result 5
.

Both
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
parameterized by
k
are in
\mathsf{XP}
.

Result 6
.

Both
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
parameterized by
k+h
are in
\mathsf{FPT}
.

The results we have discussed so far complete the parameterized complexity landscape of the three problems for parameters
k
,
h
, and
\lvert D\rvert
.
As we can see, the complexity for
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
seem to be exactly the same, but we claim that this is not the case for all reasonable parameters.
Towards disproving this, we are going to consider a different parameter, the number of vertices
n
of the graph.
We argue that
n
is a reasonable parameter for rail networks since the number of stations in a network can be magnitudes smaller than the number of trips throughout a day.
From a mathematical perspective, parameterizing by
n
usually makes the problem trivial.
However, in our case, the set of demands
D
is also part of the input and, as we will see, this makes a big difference.
In particular, we show the surprising result that
\ell
-TEDSC
is
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hard when we parameterize by
k
even on path graphs with
3
vertices.

Result 7
.

\ell
-TEDSC
is
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hard when parameterized by
k
on path graphs with
3
vertices.

We now prove our claim that the
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
is different by providing an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm for
\tau
-TEDSC
parameterized by
k
on
fixed
(i.e. constant
n
) bidirected star graphs.
A bidirected star graph is a directed graph where we have
n-1
leaf vertices and a center vertex
c
, and
c
has both an incoming and outgoing edge towards every other vertex.

Result 8
.

There is an algorithm that solves
\tau
-TEDSC
on bidirected star graphs in
3^{k}(n\cdot\lvert D\rvert)^{\text{O}(n)}
time.

This result leads to two natural open questions that we leave for future work:
First, can we design an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm that solves
\tau
-TEDSC
when parameterized by
k
for a more general class of graphs than bidirected stars, e.g., bidirected trees?
Second, can the running time be improved to
\mathsf{FPT}
by
n+k
?
While we cannot conjecture whether it is possible or not, we believe that our techniques cannot be easily generalized since they heavily rely on the low diameter structure of bidirected stars.

For our final result, we consider the
Min-
\ell
-TEDSC
and
Min-
\tau
-TEDSC
problems which asks for a schedule
S
of minimum cardinality
\mathrm{OPT}
.
We provide a 2-approximation algorithm for both problems by using min-cost flows.

Result 9
.

There exists an algorithm that, given a
Min-
\ell
-TEDSC
or
Min-
\tau
-TEDSC
instance, computes a schedule
S
with
\lvert S\rvert\leq(2-h^{-1})\mathrm{OPT}
in polynomial time.

1.2
Techniques and Proof Outlines

In this section, we describe the techniques that we use to prove our results. One of the main ingredients that we use throughout the paper is the notion of static expansion from temporal graphs. In order to express the temporal dimension of our input graph
G=(V,E)
, we create a new graph by creating
\Lambda(D)+1
many layers, where each layer contains one vertex copy for each of the
n
vertices. The vertex copy of a vertex
u
in layer
i
, represents vertex
u
at time step
i
. We will refer to such a vertex as time-vertex
(u,i)
. Every vertex in layer
i
has a directed edge to its corresponding copy in layer
i+1
. These edges express the ability of a walk to wait on a vertex for one time step, and we refer to them as waiting edges. Additionally, for every edge
(u,v)\in E
and every layer
i
, we add a directed edge from
(u,i)
to
(v,i+1)
. We refer to these as moving edges.
\cref
fig:static_expansion shows how we construct the static expansion.

Polynomial-time Algorithm for
TEDSC
.

In
\cref
chap:unconstrained, we give a polynomial time algorithm for
TEDSC
by reducing to a flow problem with upper and lower bounds on the flow on each edge.
We construct our static expansion graph based on our input graph
G=(V,E)
and demands
D
.
We add a source vertex
s
connected to the first layer, and a target vertex
z
that is connected to the last layer.
We now add capacity constraints to the edges.
The waiting edges have an upper bound of
\infty
.
For the moving edges that are also demands, we put a lower and upper bound capacity of
1
, while for the rest of them we only put an upper bound capacity of
1
.
The upper bound enforces that our walks will be edge disjoint, while the lower bound enforces that every demand edge will be covered by a walk.

It is well known that for directed networks one can reduce the problem of finding flows with lower bounds to a regular flow problem
[
15
]
which can then be solved by any polynomial max-flow algorithm
[
17
]
.
However, there is one important caveat. This algorithm is only pseudopolynomial in the number of demands
\lvert D\rvert
. In particular, if there are large gaps in time between demands, our static expansion may have exponential number of layers compared to the input. To account for this, we show that we can prune enough layers from the static expansion down to a desirable number while keeping a feasible solution. First, let a time step
t
be called a
relevant
time step if and only if
D
contains a demand edge at time step
t
. We show that if there is a feasible flow solution in the static expansion we just constructed, then there also is a feasible solution if we prune our static expansion, by removing layers such that there are at most
n+2k
layers between any two consecutive relevant time steps. Therefore, we now have a static expansion with at most
\lvert D\rvert(n+2k)
many layers without destroying all feasible solutions.
\cref
fig:gap-compression-intro shows an example of the compression.

and both the reduction in
[
28
]
and our reduction preserve planarity.

n=5

.

.

{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-Hardness of
\ell
-TEDSC
by
k
on Fixed Graphs.

For our final negative result, we show in
\cref
subsec:bin-packing-reduction that
\ell
-TEDSC
is
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hard on path graphs with
3
vertices.
Our reduction is from
UnaryBinPacking
parameterized by the number of bins
[
22
]
.

The transformation from
BinPacking
to
\ell
-TEDSC
is very natural:
A bin of capacity
B
is the same as a walk that can make
B
steps.
In that sense, if an item
i
of size
s(i)
is packed into a bin, then the corresponding walk must travel a distance of
s(i)
.
An easy way to realize this setup is by building a tree whose root initially hosts all walks and whose branches are paths of length
s(i)
. By placing demands at the ends of the paths at sufficiently spaced time steps, the work of traversing the branches is equivalent to placing the corresponding item into the corresponding bin.
As the item sizes are encoded in unary, constructing the branches with
s(i)
vertices is still polynomial in the size of the
UnaryBinPacking
instance.
It also shows that
\ell
-TEDSC
is
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hard even on graphs of constant treewidth.

Next, observe that all branches of the tree are only used once by one walk.
If we place demands at the root of the tree that force all walks to return there in between handling the demands of the items, we can merge the branches of the tree into a single path. The walks are hosted at one end of the path and each item appears as a demand somewhere on the path.
This simplification shows that
\ell
-TEDSC
is also
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hard on graphs of constant pathwidth.

Finally, we can simplify this construction even further by collapsing the entire path into a single edge.
To ensure that each item
i
still requires
s(i)
steps, we add more than one demand.
In particular, there are
2s(i)
demands that direct a walk back and forth on the very same edge.
To ensure that all these demands are fulfilled by the same walk, we introduce one additional vertex that serves as the waiting area for all other walks.

We now discuss our positive results for
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
. Before that, let us point out some useful observations. Whenever a schedule satisfies the lifespan constraint for some value
h
, it also satisfies the length constraint for the same
h
.
Since our goal is to cover demands with a particular number of walks, if the number of walks is larger than the number of demands, we can just trivially assign each demand to a distinct walk. This implies that whenever
|D|\leq k
, we have a trivial yes instance. On the other hand, we can also get some trivial no instances. If
k\cdot h<|D|
, there are too many demands for our walks to cover during their lifespan. We combine these two arguments together and assume that
k<\lvert D\rvert\leq k\cdot h
from now on.

\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
are
\mathsf{FPT}
Parameterized by
k+h
.

In
\cref
sec:fpt-kh, we provide an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm for
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
when parameterized by
k+h
. The general approach for obtaining this result is the same for both problems, where we reduce them to length bounded edge disjoint paths. However,
\ell
-TEDSC
has some extra necessary steps, so we begin by describing the solution for
\tau
-TEDSC
.

First, we guess for each demand which walk is going to cover it.
For every assignment we check what is the lifespan required by each walk to cover its demands.
If the lifespan is too large for any of them, we reject the assignment and move on.
For every non rejected assignment, we construct the static expansion of
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
and a new set of terminal pairs
\mathcal{S}
.
For every walk and every pair
((u_{i},v_{i},t_{i}),(u_{i+1},v_{i+1},t_{i+1}))
of consecutive demands assigned to each walk, we add the terminal pair between the corresponding vertices in the static expansion
((v_{i},t_{i}+1),(u_{i+1},t_{i+1}))
to
\mathcal{S}
.
We can now feed the static expansion with the demand edges removed as well as the set
\mathcal{S}
to the algorithm by
18
that solves length bounded edge disjoint paths.
However, based on our problem formulation, we allow multiple walks to traverse the same waiting edge, whereas the algorithm by
18
does not allow this.
To fix this, we replace every waiting edge with
k
many parallel edges, and then subdivide every edge of the graph.
The subdivision step is important to guarantee that the final product is a simple graph and not a multigraph.

For
\ell
-TEDSC
, the previous approach falters in two ways.
First, the waiting edges of the static expansion do not contribute towards the length of each temporal walk.
This means that while the lifespan of any walk between layers
i
and
j
is always
j-i
, the length of such a walk is not fixed.
To amend this, apart from guessing which demands are covered by each walk, we also guess how many edges a walk uses between two consecutive demands assigned to it.
However, this would mean that our walks may have different lengths, so we also extend the algorithm by
18
to handle heterogeneous length bounds.
Additionally, we use a modified static expansion in which waiting for multiple time steps corresponds to traversing only a single edge.
When guessing, we generate at most
k^{\lvert D\rvert}
assignments of walks to demands, and at most
h^{\lvert D\rvert}
assignments based on how many edges each sub walk uses.
Since we have bounded
k+h
and we can assume that
\lvert D\rvert\leq k\cdot h
, the running time of our algorithm is
\mathsf{FPT}
in
k+h
.

\mathsf{XP}
Algorithm for
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
Parameterized by
k
.

In
\Cref
sec:k-xp, we show that
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
admit an
\mathsf{XP}
-algorithm when parameterized by
k
. Since we showed that
k<\lvert D\rvert
, the algorithm also works when we parameterize by
D
.

Our approach is the same for both problems, where we create a
state graph
. The state graph describes every possible state of the network based on (i) current time step and (ii) the state and position of every walk. Informally, the state graph contains a vertex for every possible combination of the following possibilities: (i) time steps from 1 to
\Lambda(D)+1
, (ii) the set of walks that have not started yet, (iii) the set of walks that are currently active, (iv) the position of the current walks, (v) the remaining length/lifespan of each walk. Formally,
a state is a tuple
(t,U,A,p,c)
where
t\in[\Lambda(D)+1]
is a time step,
U\subseteq[k]
is the set of so-far unused walk ids,
A\subseteq[k]\setminus U
is the set of active walk ids,
p\colon A\to V(G)
is the position profile of the active walks, and
c\colon A\to\mathds{N}
is the capacity profile of the active walks.

Additionally, we add a directed edge from a vertex
s_{1}
to a vertex
s_{2}
of the state graph if it is possible to transition from state
s_{1}
to state
s_{2}
in a valid solution. Informally, we need to make sure that the two states are compatible based on (i) their time steps, (ii) their set of inactive walks, (iii) the position and capacity of their active walks. We also need to make sure that the transition guarantees (a) temporal edge disjointness and (b) all demands are covered. All of these constraints are covered by the following formal definition.
The state graph contains an edge from a state
(t_{1},U_{1},A_{1},p_{1},c_{1})
to a state
(t_{2},U_{2},A_{2},p_{2},c_{2})
if and only if all of the following hold:

1.

t_{2}=t_{1}+1
.

2.

U_{2}\subseteq U_{1}
.

3.

A_{2}\subseteq A_{1}\cup U_{1}
.

4.

For every
i\in A_{2}\setminus A_{1}
we have
c_{2}(i)=h
.

5.

For every
i\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}
it holds that

(a)

p_{1}(i)=p_{2}(i)
or
(p_{1}(i),p_{2}(i))\in E(G)
.

(b)

If
\mathcal{I}
is a
\tau
-TEDSC
instance then
c_{2}(i)=c_{1}(i)-(t_{2}-t_{1})
.

(c)

If
\mathcal{I}
is a
\ell
-TEDSC
instance then
c_{2}(i)=c_{1}(i)-d_{G}(p_{1}(i),p_{2}(i))
.

6.

Let
\mathcal{E}(p_{1},p_{2})=\{(p_{1}(i),p_{2}(i),t_{1})\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}i\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}\land p_{1}(i)\neq p_{2}(i)\}
denote the (multi-)set of temporal edges used in the transition.

(a)

Every temporal edge occurs at most once in
\mathcal{E}(p_{1},p_{2})
.

(b)

For every demand
d=(u,v,t)\in D
with
t=t_{1}
,
d\in\mathcal{E}(p_{1},p_{2})
.

Intuitively, the first condition ensures that state transitions are only forwards in time, the second and third ensure that each walk goes from “unused” over “active” to “finished”, the fourth and fifth ensure that each walk starts with capacity
h
and that the capacity decreases correctly, and the sixth ensures that the walks are temporally edge disjoint and cover all demands.
Note now that a solution for
\ell
-TEDSC
or
\tau
-TEDSC
exists if and only if there exists a path from a state with time step
1
to a state with time step
\Lambda(D)+1
.
However, given an instance
\mathcal{I}
of
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
, constructing the equivalent state graph naively will yield too many vertices for our desired runtime.
This is because the number of time steps
\Lambda(D)+1
can be exponential in
\lvert D\rvert
.
We show that we can compress the state graph, similar to how we compressed the static expansion previously, by having at most
n\cdot k
time steps between any two consecutive relevant time steps.
Furthermore, we limit the set of values that the capacity function
c
in a state takes to only polynomially many.

We want to note that while this approach might seem overkill for obtaining this particular
\mathsf{XP}
result, it is the basis for the following result.

\tau
-TEDSC
is
\mathsf{FPT}
by
k
on Fixed Bidirected Stars.

In
\cref
subsec:bidirected-stars, we show that
\tau
-TEDSC
and
\ell
-TEDSC
are fundamentally different.
Similarly to the previous result, our approach is to construct a state graph.
However, we now want to have
\mathsf{FPT}
running time instead of
\mathsf{XP}
so the state graph is too big.
To combat this, we show that if a solution exists for an instance
\mathcal{I}
, and we restrict our state space only to states where only at most
f(n)
many walks are positioned on leaf vertices at any given time, then a solution can still be found.
We achieve this by employing an exchange argument where we show how a feasible solution can be modified to adhere to the above constraint.
In particular, walks only need to spend a constant number of successive time steps on the leaves of
G
before returning to the center.

Approximation of
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
for
\lvert S\rvert
.

In
\Cref
sec:approx, we provide a
(2-h^{-1})
-approximation algorithm for
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
.
The approximation ratio is with regards to the number of walks needed to cover all demands.
Our approach is to solve these problems by considering the flow network for unconstrained
TEDSC
with suitable costs added to the edges.
In particular, for the
\ell
-TEDSC
, we add a cost of
1
to every moving edge of the static expansion.
For
\tau
-TEDSC
, we add a cost of
1
to
every
edge.
Consider a flow
f
for this network.
The cost generated by any particular walk in the decomposition of
f
is equal to the length or lifespan of that walk, respectively for
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
.
Since the arguments from now on are identical for both problems, we will just argue over the
\ell
-TEDSC
problem.

We make the following claim: Given a
\ell
-TEDSC
instance and a particular value
k
, if the cost of the min-cost flow in the generated graph is larger than
k\cdot h
, then
\ell
-TEDSC
does not admit a solution with
k
walks.
This can be proven by observing the following: For every schedule
S
that is a solution to
\ell
-TEDSC
with
k
walks, the paths of this solution, form a
k
-flow in the min-cost flow network with total cost at most
k\cdot h
.

Let us now describe our algorithm.
For every value of
k
starting from
1
, we compute a min-cost
k
-flow
f
.
As long as the cost of
f
is more than
k\cdot h
, we discard the solution and move on the next value of
k
.
We can safely do this because of the claim we just proved.
Once, we find a flow with cost at most
k\cdot h
, we show how we can decompose the
k
paths provided by
f
into at most
2k-k/h
paths that each have cost at most
h
.

1.3
Outlook and Future Work

In this paper, we have the studied the complexity of
TEDSC
,
\ell
-TEDSC
, and
\tau
-TEDSC
. We have completely explored the parameterized complexity landscape with regards to the parameters
k
,
h
, and
\lvert D\rvert
and showed that the constrained problems behave the same. We also showed that if we consider
n
as a parameter, then the complexity of the two problems is different. One immediate open question is whether our
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm by
k
on fixed bidirected stars can be generalized to trees. However, our proof heavily relies on the structure of star graphs, so we would need a different approach to generalize the result. There are also other natural next steps to consider. If we wanted to make the problem even more realistic, then we can consider extending it in different ways.

First, we are currently assuming that travel time of every edge is
1
, so one natural extension is to assume that different edges have varying travel times. Our negative results obviously extend to this setting, however, it is not clear whether our positive results would still hold.
The second extension is based on the start and end points of the walks. We are currently assuming that the walks can start and end at any vertex. However, in real-world transportation networks, e.g. train systems, only a subset of the stations can serve as depots where the trains can start or end their trip. Therefore, we can extend the problem by assuming that as part of the input we are given a subset of the vertices where the walks can start or end, and study whether our computationally hard variants become tractable.
Finally, we are currently assuming that any number of walks can be at the same station at the same time. This is an oversimplification of the real-world and we can extend the model by having as part of the input, a capacity for each vertex.

1.4
Related work

The problem of connecting pairs of terminal vertices using vertex or edge disjoint paths in static graphs is well studied.
The notions of vertex and edge disjointness are very close as the two problems can be reduced to one another in polynomial time.
In the case that all terminal pairs connect the same two vertices
s
and
t
, Menger’s theorem
[
30
]
tells us that the maximum number of disjoint paths equals the cardinality of the minimum cut.
This is in turn the same as the capacity of the minimum vertex cut, which is the same as the maximum flow from
s
to
t
if all edges have unit capacity.
If, on the other hand, the endpoints of the paths are specified separately for each path, the complexity changes drastically.
The corresponding flow interpretation is that there are multiple so called
commodities
that need flow routed between them.
Multicommodity flow is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete, the first general proof of this was given by Donald Knuth in book by
23
.
Shortly after,
12
showed that the problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard even if there are only two commodities.
Their reduction from
SAT
uses one commodity with a single flow unit to choose variable values and a second commodity with one flow unit for each clause to check the assignment.
Recent work by
3
studies the complexity of two-commodity flow parameterized by structural parameters.
Finally, requiring a single commodity flow to adhere to lower bounds on edges makes the problem
\mathsf{NP}
-hard on undirected graphs
[
21
]
.
On directed networks, this requirement can be reduced to a normal flow problem
[
15
]
.

The complexity of
EDP
parameterized by the number of paths
k
greatly varies between directed and undirected graphs.
In undirected graphs,
35
,
25
give
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms.
In directed graphs though, the problem is notoriously hard.

16
show that in general graphs,
VDP
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard even for
k=2
.
For their reduction from
SAT
, they design a gadget called the
switch
which can be attached to two edges so that later on only one of those two edges can be used.
Importantly, the two paths leave the switch again and can be reused in other parts of the graph, for example to power more switches.
Recently,
4
show that
EDP
parameterized by
k
on Eulerian directed graphs is in
\mathsf{FPT}
.

5
gives a tight lower bound for
EDP
on planar DAGs.

When considering paths of bounded length, Menger’s theorem does not hold anymore
[
1
,
13
]
, which means that finding disjoint paths and cuts are separate problems.

20
study the problem of finding disjoint paths of bounded length.
They give an almost complete classification of the values of
h
for which the problem of finding vertex / edge disjoint paths of length equal / at most
h
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
In particular they show that all variants are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard starting with
h=5
.

18
study bounded length disjoint paths and cuts and give a complete characterization with regards to parameterizing by
k
,
h
, or both.

The general study of temporal paths begins with work by
7
who study shortest temporal walks with varying travel times.

19
study shortest paths in temporal graphs and introduce what we today call the “static expansion” of a temporal graph.
The term “temporal network” was first used by
26
.

28
study the problem of finding temporally disjoint walks connecting the given terminal pairs.
They show that this problem is
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hard when parameterized by
k
.
Notably, in their problem each walk needs to connect a predetermined pair of vertices, whereas in our problem it is not fixed which demand is covered by which walk.
Recent work by
9
studies a similar problem to the one posed here:
Given a temporal graph, can it be exactly covered using
k
temporal walks?
Thus, in their work the temporal network is given in its entirety and the walks cannot move on edges that are not demanded in the input.
In the case of strict travel in directed networks (which is the closest to our setting), they give a polynomial time algorithm.
Finally,
2
introduce a train routing problem where a set of trains should be routed on temporal
s
-
t
-paths while maintaining a safe distance, the headway, between each other.
The concept of headway is a generalization of temporal edge disjointness and also incorporates travel times, whereas in our problem all edges can be traversed in unit time.
They study approximation algorithms for the problem of minimizing the makespan, that is, the latest arrival time among all trains.
Another perspective on the
TEDSC
problem is as a realization problem, that is, a problem where a temporal graph should be constructed so that it fulfills some property.
In our case, the sought graph would be the union of the temporal walks.
While there is much ongoing work on temporal realization problems
[
11
,
29
,
32
]
, we believe that none of it covers the problem studied here.

The problem of finding
flows over time
(also called dynamic flows)
[
14
,
36
]
is tangentially related to the problems studied here.
Particular problems like
Quickest Flow
and
Earliest Arrival Flow
use similar ideas, for example that of a time-expanded network, but optimize in the time domain instead of fixing it like the draft schedule of
TEDSC
.
To the best of our knowledge, no equivalent problem to
TEDSC
has been studied in this area.

Let
\mathds{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
denote the natural numbers including zero and
\mathds{N}^{+}=\mathds{N}\setminus\{0\}
.
Further, for
n,m\in\mathds{N}
we write
[n,m]=\{n,n+1,\dots,m-1,m\}
and
[n]=[1,n]
.
For a graph
G
, we usually abbreviate
n=\lvert V(G)\rvert
and

.
Throughout this work, we assume that the graphs given in problem instances do not contain any isolated vertices, as these are irrelevant for all discussed problems.
This allows us to assume
m\in\Omega(n)
.

2.1
Temporal graphs

A
temporal graph

\mathcal{G}
is a static (directed or undirected) graph
G
equipped with a
labeling function

\lambda\colon E(G)\to 2^{\mathds{N}^{+}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
.
The interpretation of
\lambda
is that an edge
e\in E(G)
is available only at the time steps
\lambda(e)
.
For
t\in\mathds{N}
let
E_{t}(\mathcal{G})=\{e\in E(G)\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}t\in\lambda(e)\}
be the set of edges available at time step
t
.
We call the static graph
G_{t}(\mathcal{G})=(V(G),E_{t}(\mathcal{G}))
the
snapshot
at time step
t
.
Note that knowing the sequence
(G_{t}(\mathcal{G}))_{t\in\mathds{N}}
suffices to fully specify
\mathcal{G}
.
We denote by
\Lambda(\mathcal{G})=\max\{t\in\mathds{N}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}E_{t}(\mathcal{G})\neq\emptyset\}
the
lifetime
of
\mathcal{G}
.

We refer to the combination of a vertex
v\in V(G)
and a time step
t\in\mathds{N}
as a
time-vertex

(v,t)
, and the combination of an edge
e\in E(G)
and a time step
t\in\lambda(e)
as a
time-edge

(e,t)
.
We denote the set of all time-edges of
\mathcal{G}
as
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})
.
A
temporal walk
is a sequence of time-vertices
(v_{0},t_{0}),(v_{1},t_{1}),\dots,(v_{k},t_{k})
such that the sequence of time steps
(t_{i})
is strictly increasing and for each
i\in[k]
the vertices are connected by the corresponding time edge
((v_{i-1},v_{i}),t_{i-1})\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})
.
We denote the set of the time-edges of a walk
p
as
\mathcal{E}(p)
.
Note that the literature distinguishes between
strict
and
non-strict
walks:
The former is the definition given here, whereas the latter requires that the sequence of time steps is only non-decreasing, that is, multiple edges may be traversed at the same time step.
For the rest of this work, we consider only strict temporal walks.
Further, we denote by
\ell(p)=k
the
length
of
p
and by
\tau(p)=t_{k}-t_{0}
the
lifespan
of
p
.
Finally, we define the notion of temporal edge disjointness, which is central to the problem discussed in this work.

Definition 2.2

(TED)
.

Two temporal walks
p
and
q
are
temporally edge disjoint
(TED) if
\mathcal{E}(p)\cap\mathcal{E}(q)=\emptyset
.
Further, a set of temporal walks is TED if its elements are pairwise TED.

A standard technique in the field of temporal graphs
[
26
,
39
,
31
]
is the time-expanded version of
\mathcal{G}
which reduces reachability and other related questions in temporal graphs to similar questions in directed graphs. Here, we define it specifically for undirected strict temporal graphs.

Definition 2.3

(Static expansion)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(G,\lambda)
be a directed temporal graph with strict walks.
The
static expansion

\mathrm{SE}(\mathcal{G})=(V_{\mathrm{SE}},E_{\mathrm{SE}})
is a static directed graph with vertices

V_{\mathrm{SE}}=\{v_{t}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}v\in V,t\in[\Lambda+1]\},

and edges
E_{\mathrm{SE}}=E_{\mathrm{wait}}\cup E_{\mathrm{move}}
, where

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{wait}}

\displaystyle=\{(v_{t},v_{t+1})\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}v\in V(G),t\in[\Lambda]\},

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{move}}

\displaystyle=\{(u_{t},v_{t+1})\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}(u,v)\in E(G),t\in[\Lambda]\}.

We refer to
V_{t}
for some time step
t
as the
t
-th layer
of
\mathrm{SE}(\mathcal{G})
.

2.2
Network flows

A graph
G
equipped with a capacity function
c\colon E(G)\to\mathds{N}
is called a
network
.
Fix two distinct vertices
s,t\in V(G)
called the
source
and the
sink
respectively.
An
s
-
t
-
flow
is a function
f\colon E(G)\to\mathds{N}
satisfying

\displaystyle f(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq c(u,v)

\displaystyle\forall(u,v)\in E(G),

(capacity)

\displaystyle\sum_{(u,v)\in E(G)}f(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(v,w)\in E(G)}f(v,w)

\displaystyle\forall v\in V(G)\setminus\{s,t\}.

(conservation)

We extend the functions
f
and
c
to all of
V(G)\times V(G)
so that for
(u,v)\notin E(G)
we have
f(u,v)=0
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that for all
u,v\in V(G)
, at least one of
f(u,v)
and
f(v,u)
is
0
.
Further, we assume that
f(\cdot,s)=f(t,\cdot)=0
, that is, there is no flow coming into the source
s
and no flow leaving the sink
t
.
All of these assumptions can be enforced in polynomial time.

The
value
of
f
is
\lvert f\rvert=\sum_{v\in V(G)}f(s,v)
.
The problem of finding a flow of maximum value is called
MaxFlow
and has been well studied.
It is possible to solve
MaxFlow
in time
\text{O}(nm)
by using the algorithms by
27
,
34
.

Based on that, one can also solve other variants of this problem.
The most relevant additions are
lower bounds
and
costs
.
In a flow network with lower bounds, every edge
e
does not just specify a capacity but instead a range
r(e)=(l,u)
so that a feasible flow must satisfy
l\leq f(e)\leq u
.
For directed networks, the problem of finding a flow adhering to lower bound can be reduced to the regular
MaxFlow
problem
[
15
]
.
For the second extension every edge
e
has a cost
\mathrm{cost}(e)\in\mathds{R}
and the objective is to find a feasible flow with minimum cost.
Note that the empty flow has cost
0
, but might (a) not be feasible due to lower bounds and (b) not be optimal as costs can be negative.
If the network contains cycles with an overall negative cost, a min-cost flow might not exist.
However, all flow networks studied here are DAGs, so this is not a problem for us.
It is possible to find a minimum-cost flow
f
with a fixed value
\lvert f\rvert=x
in polynomial time
[
38
]
.

2.3
Satisfiability

The
SAT
problem is central to the study of computational complexity.
It is the first problem which was shown to be
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
6
,
24
]
.
An instance of
SAT
is a boolean formula
\varphi
in conjunctive normal form, meaning

•

there are
n
variables
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
;

•

the formula is the conjunction of
m
clauses
\{C_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
, that is,
\varphi=C_{1}\land C_{2}\land\dots\land C_{m}
; and

•

each clause is the disjunction of
k_{i}
literals, that is, for all
j\in[m]
,
C_{j}=\ell_{1}\lor\ell_{2}\lor\dots\lor\ell_{k_{j}}
where each
\ell_{p}=x_{i}
or
\ell_{p}=\lnot x_{i}
for some
i\in[n]
.

The
k
-
SAT
problem is a special case of
SAT
where all
k_{i}\leq k
.
Notably, for all
k\geq 3
,
k
-
SAT
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Assuming
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{P}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{P}$}}{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{P}}}\neq{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{NP}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{NP}$}}{\mathsf{NP}}{\mathsf{NP}}}
means that there cannot be an algorithm that solves
SAT
in time
(n+m)^{\text{O}(1)}
.
However, in the area of parameterized complexity, a stronger assumption is often used that gives rise to much stronger lower bounds: the
Exponential Time Hypothesis
(ETH).
Intuitively, ETH states that there is no algorithm that solves
3-SAT
in
2^{o(n)}(n+m)^{\text{O}(1)}
time.

Conjecture 2.4

(Exponential Time Hypothesis)
.

Let
\delta_{3}
be the infimum of the set of all constants
c
for which there exists an algorithm solving
3-SAT
in time
2^{cn}\cdot{\mathsf{poly}}(n,m)
.
It holds that
\delta_{3}>0
.

By tracking the relations of parameters in parameterized reductions, one can prove lower bounds for other problems too, as we will do in this thesis.
In particular, if the parameter
k
of a parameterized problem is bounded as
\text{O}(n)
, then this implies that under ETH there cannot be an algorithm solving said problem in time
2^{o(k)}
.
While this already allows for strong lower bounds, it turns out that using the so-called
sparsification lemma
, an even stronger variant of ETH holds that allows us to also depend on the number of clauses.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
8
]
)
.

Unless ETH fails, there is no algorithm that solves
3-SAT
in
2^{o(n+m)}
time.

For a more thorough introduction to the topic, we refer the reader to the textbook by
8
.
All statements in this thesis that assume ETH clearly state this assumption upfront.

3
The Schedule Completion Problem

Consider a rail network modeled by a static graph
G
, where each edge represents a track that can be used by at most one train at any given time.
A
draft schedule
is a set
D
of demands, where each demand
(u,v,t)
is a passenger request to travel from
u
to
v
at time step
t
.
We define
\Lambda(D)=\max_{(u,v,t)\in D}t
as the
lifetime
of the draft schedule; when
D
is clear from context we write just
\Lambda
.
We want to operate a small fleet of trains, each following a route through the network, such that all passenger requests are covered.
Each train corresponds to a temporal walk, and since two trains cannot share the same track at the same time, the walks must be temporally edge disjoint.
The
schedule completion problem
asks: how many trains suffice?

Formally, since
G
is a static graph with all edges always available, we model it as the temporal graph
\mathcal{G}(G,D)=\langle G,\lambda_{D}\rangle
where
\lambda_{D}\colon e\mapsto[\Lambda(D)]
.
When
G
and
D
are clear from context, we may abbreviate
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(G,D)
.
We denote the static expansion of this temporal graph by
\mathrm{SE}(G,D):=\mathrm{SE}(\mathcal{G}(G,D))
; deliberately using
\mathrm{SE}(G,D)
to emphasize its dependence on the instance
(G,D)
.
A
schedule
for
D
is a set
S
of TED temporal walks in
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
.
We say that
S

extends

D
if for each demand
(u,v,t)\in D
there is a walk
p\in S
with
((u,v),t)\in\mathcal{E}(p)
.

Problem 3.6
.

Temporally Edge Disjoint Schedule Completion (
TEDSC
)

\Input
Static graph
G
, draft schedule
D
, integer
k\in\mathds{N}
.

\Prob
Does there exist a schedule
S
in
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
with
\lvert S\rvert\leq k
that extends
D
?

Without loss of generality, we assume there is a demand at time step
1
, as the times of all demands can always be shifted down.

3.1
Constrained variants

The general schedule completion problem is a deliberate simplification of the real-world problem: trains can travel arbitrarily far and operate indefinitely.
In practice, both constraints are bounded: the fuel capacity limits the distance a train can travel, and working-time regulations limit the time a train can operate.
We study two variants of
TEDSC
that capture these restrictions, one for each constraint.

The
length-constraint
bounds the number of edges a train may traverse.

Problem 3.7
.

Length-Constrained TED Schedule Completion (
\ell
-TEDSC
)

\Input
Static graph
G
, draft schedule
D
, integers
k,h\in\mathds{N}
.

\Prob
Does there exist a schedule
S
for
D
such that
\lvert S\rvert\leq k
and
\ell(p)\leq h
for all
p\in S
?

The
lifespan-constraint
instead bounds the total active time of a train, i.e., the time between its first and last move.

Problem 3.8
.

Lifespan-Constrained TED Schedule Completion (
\tau
-TEDSC
)

\Input
Static graph
G
, draft schedule
D
, integers
k,h\in\mathds{N}
.

\Prob
Does there exist a schedule
S
for
D
such that
\lvert S\rvert\leq k
and
\tau(p)\leq h
for all
p\in S
?

;

.

3.2
Some observations

The two problems
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
are closely related as the only difference between the length
\ell
and the lifespan
\tau
of a walk is whether its remaining “capacity” decreases whenever it waits on a vertex.
As the lifespan of a temporal walk bounds its length, any schedule satisfying the lifespan constraint for some value
h
also satisfies the length constraint for the same
h
.

Observation 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a
\tau
-TEDSC
instance and let
S
be a feasible schedule.
Then
S
is also a feasible
\ell
-TEDSC
schedule for
\mathcal{I}
.

Recall that the goal of
TEDSC
is to cover multiple demands with each walk.
Our definition captures this by fixing the number of walks
k
that may be used.
However, if an instance permits at least as many walks as demands, this instance can be solved by employing one walk for every demand.

Observation 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a
TEDSC
,
\ell
-TEDSC
, or
\tau
-TEDSC
instance.
If
\lvert D\rvert\leq k
, then
\mathcal{I}
is feasible.

For the constrained variants, a similar bound on the number of demands holds for the other direction.
In particular, if there are few walks that can each travel only a small distance, then they can cover only few temporal edges.
We can thus immediately reject some instances with particularly small
k
and
h
.

Observation 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a
\ell
-TEDSC
or
\tau
-TEDSC
instance.
If
k\cdot h<\lvert D\rvert
, then
\mathcal{I}
is infeasible.

Proof 3.12
.

For
\ell
-TEDSC
, suppose there is a feasible solution
S
.
Every step by any walk covers at most one demand.
Thus, the total length of all walks in
S
is at least
\lvert D\rvert
.
As
\lvert S\rvert\leq k
,

k\cdot h<\lvert D\rvert\leq\sum_{p\in S}\ell(p)\leq k\cdot h,

a contradiction.
For
\tau
-TEDSC
, the same proof works with the additional step of bounding
\ell(p)\leq\tau(p)
in the final inequality.

Based on these two observations, we assume that
k<\lvert D\rvert\leq k\cdot h
in any given instance going forward.
Our final observation holds only for
\ell
-TEDSC
.
Intuitively, if the length bound
h
is very large, then it poses no restriction at all.

Observation 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(G,D,k,h)
be a
\ell
-TEDSC
instance and suppose that
h\geq\lvert V(G)\rvert\cdot\lvert D\rvert
.
Then
\mathcal{I}
is feasible if and only if the corresponding unconstrained
TEDSC
instance
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=(G,D,k)
is feasible.

Proof 3.14
.

The forward direction holds by definition.
Suppose on the other hand that
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is feasible, then there is a schedule
S
of TED walks fulfilling all demands.
Let
p\in S
and
p^{\prime}
be a contiguous subwalk of
p
such that
p^{\prime}\cap D=\emptyset
.
We can assume that
p^{\prime}
is a path, because if
p^{\prime}
would visit a vertex
v
more than once, then we could remove the cycle between the first and last visit of
v
and still have a valid schedule.
With this assumption, no walk can actually be longer than
n\cdot\lvert D\rvert
because each walk can cover at most
\lvert D\rvert
demands and the paths between them have length at most
n
.
Therefore
S
is also a schedule for
\mathcal{I}
.

We thus assume that
h<\lvert V(G)\rvert\cdot\lvert D\rvert
holds in all
\ell
-TEDSC
instances going forward.

4
Solving Unconstrained Schedule Completion

For the unconstrained
TEDSC
, we are given a static graph
G
providing the rail network, a draft schedule
D
containing temporal edges that must be covered, and the number
k
of possible trains. The goal is to find
k
TED walks in
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
covering all temporal edges in
D
.

We can solve this by constructing the schedule completion flow network
(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(G,D),r)
based on the static expansion
\mathrm{SE}(G,D)
of
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
and then computing a feasible flow (
\Cref
subsec:schedule-completion-flow).
However, since the static expansion has
\text{O}(n\cdot\Lambda(D))
vertices and
\Lambda(D)
can be exponential in the number of bits needed to encode the input, this yields only a pseudo-polynomial algorithm.

To achieve polynomial running time, we observe in
\Cref
subsec:gaps-longtime that large
gaps
between consecutive demands can be compressed without affecting feasibility, bounding the flow network to
{\mathsf{poly}}(n,\lvert D\rvert)
vertices.
This gap-observation will also play a central role for our subsequent results.

4.1
Schedule Completion Flow Network

Recall from
\Cref
con:walk-expansion that the static expansion
\mathrm{SE}(G,D)=\mathrm{SE}(\mathcal{G})
is a static directed graph where every static path in
\mathrm{SE}(G,D)
corresponds to a temporal walk in
\mathcal{G}
and vice versa.
By adding a global source
s
and sink
z
, we obtain an augmented graph
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(G,D)
whose
s
-
z
-paths correspond to temporal walks in
\mathcal{G}
, where a feasible flow of value
k
decomposes into
k
paths, one per walk in the completed schedule.
The draft schedule and temporal edge disjointness is encoded as flow ranges: demand edges receive range
(1,1)
, forcing each demand to be covered by exactly one walk and preventing two walks from sharing a temporal edge; non-demand moving edges receive range
(0,1)
, enforcing temporal edge disjointness without requiring coverage.

(0,1)
.

{construction}

[Schedule completion flow network]

Let
(G,D,k)
be an instance of
TEDSC
with lifetime
\Lambda=\Lambda(D)
.
Starting from the static expansion
\mathrm{SE}(G,D)
of the temporal graph
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(G,D)
, we define the augmented graph
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(G,D)
by introducing a global source
s

\mathrm{SE}(G,D)

\displaystyle V(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(G,D))

\displaystyle=V(\mathrm{SE}(G,D))\cup\{s,z\},

\displaystyle E(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(G,D))

\displaystyle=E(\mathrm{SE}(G,D))\cup\bigcup_{v\in V(G)}\{(s,v_{1}),\ (v_{\Lambda+1},z)\}.

Proposition 4.15
.

TEDSC
can be decided using one
MaxFlow
computation on a network with
\text{O}(n\cdot\Lambda)
vertices and
\text{O}((n+m)\cdot\Lambda)
edges.

Proof 4.16
.

Let
(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(G,D),r)
be the schedule completion flow network from
\Cref
con:sc-flow-network.
The graph
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(G,D)
has
\text{O}(n\cdot\Lambda)
vertices and
\text{O}((n+m)\cdot\Lambda)
edges.

Since
G
is directed and we consider temporal walks to be strict,
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(G,D)
is acyclic.
Therefore any feasible
s
-
z
-flow of value
k
in
(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(G,D),r)
decomposes into exactly
k

s
-
z
-paths (never revisiting any time-vertex
v_{t}\in V(G)\times[\Lambda]
), each of which corresponding to a temporal walk in
\mathcal{G}
(possibly revisiting a vertex
v\in V(G)
).
Furthermore, the flow ranges ensure that these walks cover all demands in
D
and are temporally edge disjoint:
Each demand edge
e
has range
(1,1)
, which enforces that the corresponding demand is covered by exactly one walk, and that no two walks use the same temporal edge, i. e., the walks are temporally edge disjoint. Each remaining moving edge has range
(0,1)
, which enforces that no two walks share a temporal edge, but allows for the possibility that some temporal edges are not used by any walk.
Lastly, the waiting edges and the edges from
s
and to
z
are unconstrained, allowing for any number of walks to wait in a vertex, to start at any vertex in the first layer, and to end at any vertex in the last layer.
As a result,
TEDSC
is a YES-instance if and only if a feasible
s
-
z
-flow of value at most
k
exists in
(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(G,D),r)
.
Such a flow can be found in polynomial time with respect to the network size as discussed in
\Cref
sec:flow-prelims.
Note however that this time is pseudo-polynomial: the bound is polynomial in
n
and
\Lambda
, but
\Lambda
can be exponential in the bit-length of the input.

4.2
Gaps and gap-compression

The algorithm from
\Cref
thm:tec-poly-n-tau is super-polynomial only when
\Lambda
is large, which occurs precisely when consecutive demands are timed far apart: for instance,
D=\{(u,v,1),(v,u,2^{n})\}
yields
\Lambda=2^{n}
despite having polynomial encoding size.
We call such large time intervals
gaps
and show that, once a gap is sufficiently long, any placement of the
k
walks at the beginning of the gap can always be extended to any target placement at its end.
This allows us to replace long gaps in the flow network by a single unconstrained biclique layer, bounding the total network size by
{\mathsf{poly}}(n,\lvert D\rvert)
.

Definition 4.17

(Relevant time step)
.

We call a time step
t\in\mathds{N}

relevant
if there are
u,v\in V(G)
such that
(u,v,t)\in D
; otherwise we say
t
is
irrelevant
.

Definition 4.18

(
\gamma
-gap)
.

We call a time interval
I=[t,t+\gamma)
of size
\gamma
an
\gamma
-gap
if all of the time steps in
I
are irrelevant.

Routing
k
walks across a gap is an instance of the
TEDWalks
problem, where the start and end positions of the
k
walks at the two boundaries of the gap form the terminal pairs:

Problem 4.19
.

TemporallyEdgeDisjointWalks (
TEDWalks
)

\Input
Temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
, terminal pairs
\{(s_{i},z_{i})\in V(G)^{2}\}_{i=1}^{k}
.

\Prob
Are there temporally edge disjoint walks connecting the terminals
(s_{i},z_{i})
for all
i\in[k]
?

In general, the
TEDWalks
problem is intractable.

28
show that the related
TemporallyVertexDisjointWalks
problem is
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hard when parameterized by
k
.
The standard vertex-to-edge reduction (splitting each vertex into an in-copy and an out-copy connected by a single edge) transfers this hardness to the edge disjoint variant, which yields:

Proposition 4.20
.

TEDWalks
parameterized by
k
is
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hard.

The
TEDWalks
instances arising from gaps, however, are special: since all time steps in a gap are irrelevant, and
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
makes every edge of
G
available at every time step, the underlying graph in which we are looking for the walks is effectively static throughout the gap.
The two lemmas below make this precise: any connected static subgraph
F\subseteq E(G)
spanning all terminal pairs suffices to route
k
walks TED through a gap, provided the gap is long enough.

Lemma 4.21

(
((n-1)\cdot k)
-gap suffices)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{G}=\langle G,\lambda\rangle,\{(s_{i},z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k})
be an instance of
TEDWalks
.
Suppose that there is a set of edges
F\subseteq E(G)
that connects all terminal pairs and is present at all time steps until
\gamma=(n-1)\cdot k
, that is,
F\subseteq E_{t}(\mathcal{G})
for
t\in[\gamma]
.
Then there is a solution
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
for
\mathcal{I}
.
Furthermore, for all
i\in[k]
,
p_{i}
is a shortest
s_{i}
-
z_{i}
-path in
F
.

Proof 4.22
.

We schedule the walks one after another, so they will be temporally edge disjoint.
For each
i
, let
p_{i}
be a shortest
s_{i}
-
z_{i}
-path in
F
.
We schedule it to start at time step
t_{0}+(i-1)\cdot(n-1)
.
As each path has length at most
n-1
, no two walks overlap in time, and all walks finish before time
t_{0}+(n-1)\cdot k=t_{0}+\gamma
.

The construction of the walks in
\Cref
thm:tedw-n-times-k has most walks waiting on vertices most of the time.
Which on the one hand makes it more robust in the sense that each walk is free to take any path it wants, but on the other hand also means that the overall schedule is not very efficient.
We can significantly decrease the duration
\gamma
for which the edges
F
need to be present by coming up with a more parallelised schedule.

Lemma 4.23

(
(n+2k)
-gap suffices)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be given as in
\Cref
thm:tedw-n-times-k.
Suppose that there is a set of edges
F\subseteq E(G)
that connects all terminal pairs and is present at all time steps until
\gamma=n+2k
.
Then
\mathcal{I}
is feasible.

Proof 4.24
.

Let
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
be an inclusion-maximal, acyclic subset of
F
.
Assume that
F^{\prime}
has one connected component, otherwise the following analysis holds for each component individually.
Pick a vertex
r\in V(G)
so that all terminals have distance at most
n/2
in
F^{\prime}
to
r
.
We construct the walks in two phases:
First, all walks move from their start vertex
s_{i}
to
r
.
Once all walks have arrived at
r
, every walk moves to its target vertex
z_{i}
.
It suffices to show that each phase takes at most
n/2+k
time steps.

First, consider the second phase.
For each
i\in[k]
let
p_{i}
be the unique
r
-
z_{i}
-path in
F^{\prime}
.
By letting the paths leave
r
one time step after another, no walk blocks another, because walks that left before have either arrived at their target vertex or are already further away from
r
.
Thus, the
i
-th temporal walk leaves after
i
waiting at
r
steps then and takes at most
n/2
steps to reach its target vertex.
After
n/2+k
steps, the process has finished.

For the first phase we need to construct temporal walks from each
s_{i}
to
r
.
It suffices to construct TED walks in
F
from
r
to each
s_{i}
and then reverse each walk because the edges of
F
are present at all times until
\gamma
.
Such walks can be constructed using the procedure we just presented for the second phase.

The previous two lemmata show that sufficiently long gaps can be compressed without changing feasibility. We now define the resulting
\gamma
-compressed static expansion and then equip it with the appropriate flow ranges to solve
TEDSC
in polynomial time.
This concept of
\gamma
-compressed constructions will also play a vital role in the remainder of the paper.

Definition 4.25

(
\gamma
-compressed static expansion)
.

Let
(G,D)
be a schedule completion instance.
For every
\gamma
-gap
[t_{1}+1,t_{2})
between two consecutive relevant time steps

and
t_{2}

(t_{1}+2)
-th layer up to and including the
(t_{2}-1)
-th layer from

and connect all vertices from the
(t_{1}+1)
-th layer to all vertices from the
t_{2}
-th layer so that they form a biclique.
The resulting graph is the
\gamma
-compressed static expansion

\mathrm{SE}_{\gamma}(G,D)
.

\Cref

:

5
Hardness of Constrained Schedule Completion

Both
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
capture the complexity of notoriously hard problems.
This is in part because finding edge disjoint paths of bounded length in static graphs is already
\mathsf{NP}
-hard and we thus only need to remove the temporal aspect to capture this hardness.
On the other hand, we can use the length- and lifespan-constraints to enforce a pairing of demands and thus capture the problem of connecting terminal pairs with edge disjoint paths.
Finally, if we allow for a large number of demands, distributing the demands among the walks also captures the
BinPacking
problem, which leads to our most drastic hardness result.

kh-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ell(p_{i})-k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(z_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(s_{i})
steps inside of

We do so using a potential argument:
Suppose that the walks are at the vertices

\varPhi=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(u_{i})
.
When all walks have only traversed the

and are on the

vertices then
\varPhi=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(s_{i})
.
Before the walks leave

\varPhi=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(z_{i})
.
The potential increases exactly when a walk traverses an edge in
V(G)\cup S
.
Therefore, exactly the desired amount of steps are taken.

Claim 3
.

The walks connect the terminal pairs
(s_{i},z_{i})
.

After entering
V(G)
through a path
p_{i}
, a walk must travel inside
V(G)\cup S
for the rest of its lifespan before taking an exit edge.
By construction, the remaining lifespan after entering through

is

, so it must take
\varphi(z_{i})-\varphi(s_{i})
edges inside

As
p_{i}
ends on
s_{i}
, this leads exactly to
z_{i}
and the exit edge to
z_{i}^{\prime}
there.

Claim 4
.

The walks are edge disjoint.

We know that the paths are temporally edge disjoint by assumption.
The previous claims and the chosen times of the demands imply that all walks are constantly moving during their lifespan.
By construction of the demand set, the only time a walk can be on a vertex

is at the time step
\varphi(v)
.
Thus, for every edge inside

, no two walks cross this edge at two different times.
This implies that the paths are edge disjoint and therefore correspond to a solution of the

instance.

\mathsf{XP}
algorithms which would only be ruled out by a corresponding
\mathsf{paraNP}
-hardness result.
To this end, we know by
\Cref
thm:h5-hard that parameterizing by
h
cannot yield an
\mathsf{XP}
algorithm unless
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{P}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{P}$}}{\mathsf{P}}{\mathsf{P}}}={\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{NP}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{NP}$}}{\mathsf{NP}}{\mathsf{NP}}}
.

In this section, we show that both constrained
TEDSC
variants parameterized by
k
are in
\mathsf{XP}
.
By our usual assumption
k<\lvert D\rvert
this algorithm also works for parameterizing by
\lvert D\rvert
.
The main concept of the algorithm is the
state graph

\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I})
of an instance
\mathcal{I}
.
Every vertex of
G
corresponds to a configuration of walks in
G
at a time step
t
.
Edges in the state graph correspond to valid state transitions between two successive time steps.
Similar to before, we compress the state graph to only some bounded number of steps around relevant time steps.

First, we formally define the state graph.
After the definition, we show that traversing this graph corresponds to constructing schedules, which also gives some intuition for the definition.

Definition 6.51
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(G,D,k,h)
be a
\ell
-TEDSC
or
\tau
-TEDSC
instance.
The
state graph

\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I})
is a directed graph where
V(\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I}))
is the set of states of
\mathcal{I}
.
A state is a tuple
(t,U,A,p,c)
where
t\in[\Lambda(D)+1]
is a time step,
U\subseteq[k]
is the set of so-far unused walk ids,
A\subseteq[k]\setminus U
is the set of active walk ids,
p\colon A\to V(G)
is the position profile of the active walks, and
c\colon A\to\mathds{N}
is the capacity profile of the active walks.
The state graph contains an edge from a state
(t_{1},U_{1},A_{1},p_{1},c_{1})
to a state
(t_{2},U_{2},A_{2},p_{2},c_{2})
if and only if all of the following hold:

1.

t_{2}=t_{1}+1
.

2.

U_{2}\subseteq U_{1}
.

3.

A_{2}\subseteq A_{1}\cup U_{1}
.

4.

For every
i\in A_{2}\setminus A_{1}
we have
c_{2}(i)=h
.

5.

For every
i\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}
it holds that

(a)

p_{1}(i)=p_{2}(i)
or
(p_{1}(i),p_{2}(i))\in E(G)
.

(b)

If
\mathcal{I}
is a
\tau
-TEDSC
instance then
c_{2}(i)=c_{1}(i)-(t_{2}-t_{1})
.

(c)

If
\mathcal{I}
is a
\ell
-TEDSC
instance then
c_{2}(i)=c_{1}(i)-d_{G}(p_{1}(i),p_{2}(i))
.

6.

Let
\mathcal{E}(p_{1},p_{2})=\{(p_{1}(i),p_{2}(i),t_{1})\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}i\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}\land p_{1}(i)\neq p_{2}(i)\}
denote the (multi-)set of temporal edges used in the transition.

(a)

Every temporal edge occurs at most once in
\mathcal{E}(p_{1},p_{2})
.

(b)

For every demand
d=(u,v,t)\in D
with
t=t_{1}
,
d\in\mathcal{E}(p_{1},p_{2})
.

Lemma 6.52
.

Every schedule for a constrained
TEDSC
instance
\mathcal{I}
corresponds to a path from some initial state
(1,s)\in V(\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I}))
to a terminal state
(\Lambda+1,s^{\prime})\in V(\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I}))
.

Proof 6.53
.

Suppose that there is such a path
\mathbf{P}
in
\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I})
.
Then this induces a schedule
S
where the temporal edges of each walk
i\in[k]
are exactly those of the form
(p_{1}(i),p_{2}(i),t)
when
i\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}
and
p_{1}(i)\neq p_{2}(i)
.
Throughout the states of
\mathbf{P}
, each walk
i
first is in the unused set
U
, then at some point transitions to the active set
A
, and finally is removed from
A
and never occurs in either of such sets again.
Moreover, as
c(i)
is initially equal to
h
and then decreases according to the rules of
\ell
-TEDSC
/
\tau
-TEDSC
, the length / lifespan of walk
i
is at most
h
.
Finally, the last set of conditions ensures that the walks of
S
are TED and cover all demands
D
.
For the other direction, every schedule
S
induces a corresponding path through
\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I})
with the individual states matching the positions and remaining capacities of the walks at each time step.

Thus, finding such a path is the same as solving
\mathcal{I}
.
However, constructing
\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I})
naively yields too many vertices to still find paths in time
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert^{f(k)}
.
There are two easy changes we need to perform to reduce the number of vertices in
\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I})
:

1.

The number of time steps is
\Lambda+1
, which might be exponential in
\lvert D\rvert
(and thus
k
).
We can fix this by considering only time steps that are relevant or at most
n\cdot k
after a relevant time step (recall that a time step
t
is relevant if there is a demand at
t
).
We then relax
\Cref
item:state-time-delta to instead allow transitions to whichever time step is the next one.
Notably, it does not suffice to consider only
n+2k
time steps after a relevant one because this might disallow some walk to follow a shortest path to its position at the next relevant time step.

2.

The capacity profile
c
should not map to
\mathds{N}
but instead only to a suitable subset of
\mathds{N}
with size polynomial in
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert
.
For
\ell
-TEDSC
this subset is
[0,h]
(see
\Cref
thm:length-h-le-nD).
For
\tau
-TEDSC
it is
\{h-(t-t_{0})\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}t_{0}\in R\}
where
R
is the set of relevant time steps and
t
is the time step of the state.
Intuitively, it never makes sense to start a walk at an irrelevant time step, so at time step
t
all walks should have a remaining lifespan equal to the distance to some time step
t_{0}\in R
.

Theorem 6.54
.

Both
\ell
-TEDSC
and
\tau
-TEDSC
parameterized by
k
are in
\mathsf{XP}
.

Proof 6.55
.

Constructing the state graph
\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I})
for an instance
\mathcal{I}
with the restrictions described above yields a graph of size
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert^{\text{O}(k)}
.
As previously argued, a schedule exists if and only if this graph contains a path from some state
(1,s)
to
(\Lambda+1,s^{\prime})
for some
s,s^{\prime}
.

6.3
\mathsf{FPT}
by
k
on fixed bidirected stars

All our results up to this point suggest that
\tau
-TEDSC
and
\ell
-TEDSC
are equally complex.
In particular, we have not observed any differences in the parameterized complexity of these two problems when parameterized by any of the inherent parameters
k
,
h
, or
\lvert D\rvert
.
We did, however, notice that constructing
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms parameterized by
k+h
requires more work for
\ell
-TEDSC
than for
\tau
-TEDSC
.
Furthermore, the strongest hardness result we saw so far,
\Cref
thm:length-w-hard-on-p3, holds only for
\ell
-TEDSC
and there is no analogue of this reduction for
\tau
-TEDSC
.

We confirm the hunch that
\ell
-TEDSC
is computationally more difficult than
\tau
-TEDSC
.
In particular, we construct an algorithm that eliminates the existence of a hardness result like
\Cref
thm:length-w-hard-on-p3 for
\tau
-TEDSC
, unless
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{FPT}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{FPT}$}}{\mathsf{FPT}}{\mathsf{FPT}}}={\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
.
As
\Cref
thm:length-w-hard-on-p3 is so strong (
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hardness by
k
on graphs with three vertices) we can take many liberties in designing this algorithm.
Formally, we are looking for a running time that is
\mathsf{FPT}
by
k
on fixed graphs of some nontrivial graph class.
To this end, we present an algorithm for
\tau
-TEDSC
of this running time for bidirected star graphs.

Definition 6.56

(Bidirected star graph)
.

A bidirected star graph is a graph
G
with a distinguished
center vertex

c\in V(G)
such that
E(G)=\bigcup_{v\in V(G)\setminus\{c\}}\{(c,v),(v,c)\}
.
The vertices
V(G)\setminus\{c\}
are called the

of
G
.

The algorithm is based on our state graph algorithm from the previous section which was
\mathsf{XP}
by
k
.
We show that there is a function

such that when
G
is a
n
-vertex bidirected star graph and a
\tau
-TEDSC
schedule exists, then this schedule can be modified so that at most
f(n)
walks are placed on the leaves of
G
at the same time.
This means that we can reduce the number of states in the state graph to only those adhering to this constraint.

Lemma 6.57
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(G,D,k,h)

\tau

Let

If

Proof 6.58
.

If

We modify

Let

We first describe how to fix

By repeating this for every

We call the occurrence of a walk entering

Observe that for a fixed leaf

Suppose that the earliest conflict is walk

We modify

As the walks are active at only a finite number of time steps, this process terminates with a conflict-free schedule.

If

Otherwise, we make

We managed to clear the temporal edge

With
\Cref
thm:short-wait-on-leaf we have a schedule in which walks spend only constant time on leaves of

.
This means that at any time step there are the number of walks currently on a particular leaf is also constant.
Using this, we can consider only a subset of states in the state graph

which leads to an improved running time.

Theorem 6.59
.

There is an algorithm that solves
\tau
-TEDSC
on bidirected star graphs in
3^{k}(n\cdot\lvert D\rvert)^{\text{O}(n)}
time.

Proof 6.60
.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(G,D,k,h)
be a
\tau
-TEDSC
instance on a bidirected star
G
whose center vertex is
c
and whose leaves are
L
.
To decide whether
\mathcal{I}
is feasible, we run the algorithm from
\Cref
thm:life-len-k-xp, but instead of constructing the full state graph
\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I})
we consider only a subset of vertices.
In particular, we let
f\colon n\mapsto 4(n-1)
and define
\mathrm{St}^{\star}(\mathcal{I})
as follows:
The graph
\mathrm{St}^{\star}(\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I})
should contain only those states
(t,U,A,p,c)
where
\lvert\{i\in A\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}p(i)\in L\}\rvert\leq f(n)
.
The number of vertices is thus bounded as stated in the theorem:
There are
3^{k}
possible ways to partition
[k]
into
U
,
A
, and
[k]\setminus(U\cup A)
.
Next, there are
k^{f(n)}
choices for which walks
i\in A
have
p(i)\in L
.
Finally, there are
(n-1)^{f(n)}
to distribute these walks among the vertices of
L
.
Recalling that
k<\lvert D\rvert
by
\Cref
thm:k-lt-D, we see that the size of
\mathrm{St}^{\star}(\mathcal{I})
and thus the running time of the algorithm is bounded as stated.

As
\mathrm{St}^{\star}(\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathrm{St}(\mathcal{I})
, the proof of
\Cref
thm:life-len-k-xp already shows that if our algorithm outputs YES then a feasible schedule exists.
For the other direction, it suffices to show that the existence of any feasible schedule implies that a schedule exists which is witnessed by
\mathrm{St}^{\star}(\mathcal{I})
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{I}
is feasible and let

be schedule obtained by
\Cref
thm:short-wait-on-leaf.
As no walk of

stays on a leaf for more than two time steps and at every time step at most

Thus, the schedule

is found by only checking states of

.

7
Approximating the Number of Required Walks

So far we studied the schedule completion problem as a decision problem.
In this section, we switch perspective and investigate the corresponding search problem in which the output of an algorithm should be a schedule using the smallest possible number of walks.

Problem 7.61
.

Min-
\chi
-TEDSC
(
\chi\in\{\ell,\tau\}
)

\Input
Static graph
G
, draft schedule
D
, integer
h\in\mathds{N}
.

\Prob
A schedule
S
that extends
D
, satisfies
\chi(p)\leq h
for all
p\in S
, and is of minimal size
\lvert S\rvert
.

We provide an approximation algorithm with regards to
\lvert S\rvert
.
The main idea for the algorithm is to use the
(n-1)\cdot k
-compressed static expansion
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{(n-1)\cdot k}(G,D)
from
\Cref
def:compressed-static-expansion and ask a slightly more involved question:
Instead of computing a flow of value
k
, we compute a
minimum cost flow
of value
k
for a suitable cost function.
This flow can then be transformed into a schedule of at most
2k
walks.
The cost functions for
\tau
-TEDSC
and
\ell
-TEDSC
are defined as follows.

Definition 7.62

(
\tau
-cost)
.

The
\tau
-cost of an edge
e=((u,t_{1}),(v,t_{2}))
is
c_{\tau}(e):=t_{2}-t_{1}
.

Definition 7.63

(
\ell
-cost)
.

The
\ell
-cost of an edge
e=((u,t_{1}),(v,t_{2}))
is
c_{\ell}(e):=d_{G}(u,v)
.

Observe that the cost of a path according to
c_{\tau}
/
c_{\ell}
in the static expansion equals exactly the lifespan / length of the corresponding temporal walk in
\mathcal{G}
.
In particular, the
\ell
-cost of a waiting edge is 0; the
\ell
-cost of a moving edge in the uncompressed static expansion is 1; and the
\ell
-cost of a moving edge in the compressed static expansion is exactly the distance of the shortest static path between the corresponding vertices in
G
.
This motivates the following lemma.

Lemma 7.64
.

Let
\chi\in\{\ell,\tau\}
and let
S
be a schedule for the
\chi
-TEDSC
instance
(G,D,k,h)
.
Let
P_{S}
be the set of static
s
-
z
-paths in
(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{(n-1)\cdot k}(G,D),r)
from
\Cref
con:compress-gaps corresponding to
S
.
Then
P_{S}
induces a feasible flow
f_{S}
of value
\lvert S\rvert
with

c_{\chi}(f_{S})=\sum_{p\in S}\chi(p)\leq k\cdot h.

This means that if the minimum-cost flow has cost more than
k\cdot h
then there is no feasible schedule.
In the other direction, a flow of cost at most
k\cdot h
does not immediately yield a feasible schedule, since its total cost may be concentrated on only a few paths whose corresponding temporal walks may then have cost larger than
h
.
To deal with this, we expand each compressed path to a path in the uncompressed static expansion
\mathrm{SE}(G,D)
and split it greedily into maximal segments of cost at most
h
, each of which will become one walk in the schedule.

Definition 7.65
.

For a static path
p
in
\mathrm{SE}(G,D)
we define its
segment set

\mathrm{seg}(p)
as follows.
If
c_{\chi}(p)=0
then
\mathrm{seg}(p)=\emptyset
.
Else if
c_{\chi}(p)\leq h
then
\mathrm{seg}(p)=\{p\}
.
Otherwise, let
p^{\prime}
be the longest prefix of
p
that still satisfies
c_{\chi}(p^{\prime})\leq h
, and let
r
be the path obtained by removing the edges of
p^{\prime}
from
p
.
We set
\mathrm{seg}(p)=\{p^{\prime}\}\cup\mathrm{seg}(r)
.

In particular, every edge in
\mathrm{SE}(G,D)
has
c_{\chi}
-cost at most
1
: lifespan-cost is exactly
1
per edge, and length-cost is
0
for waiting edges and
1
for moving edges.
Therefore, every segment
p^{\prime}\in\mathrm{seg}(p)
except the last one satisfies
c_{\chi}(p^{\prime})=h
.
Using this observation, we can bound the number of walks we generate by splitting.

Lemma 7.66
.

Let
f
be a feasible flow of value
k
in
(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{(n-1)\cdot k}(G,D),r)

.
Let
P_{f}
be a set of
k
static

-
z
-paths inducing

and

be the corresponding
k
paths in
\mathrm{SE}(G,D)
obtained by taking each walk in

and (a) removing
s
and

and (b) expanding gaps according to
\Cref
thm:tedw-n-times-k.
Consider the set
W_{f}
of temporal walks in
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
corresponding to
Q_{f}
.
Then the set of segments

W_{f}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{q\in Q_{f}}\mathrm{seg}(q)

viewed as temporal walks in
\mathcal{G}(G,D)
, satisfies
\lvert W_{f}^{\prime}\rvert\leq 2k-\frac{k}{h}
.

Proof 7.67
.

We first expand

to
Q_{f}
.
For each

and each biclique edge
(u,t_{1})\to(v,t_{2})

\Cref
