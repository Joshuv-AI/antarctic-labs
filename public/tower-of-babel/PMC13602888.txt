Azidophosphonamido Rhodium(I) and Iridium(I) Complexes: Synthesis, Properties, and Linkage Isomerism
Authors: Emily L. Trew, Daisy E. Cruz‐Milette, Douglas Turnbull, Paul G. Hayes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA series of azidophosphonamido rhodium(I) and iridium(I) complexes ([ i Pr2P(N3R)NR]MLx; M = Rh, Ir; R = 1‐adamantyl (Ad), mesityl (Mes); L = cyclooctadiene (COD), x = 1, cyclooctene (COE), x = 2, CO, x = 2) were prepared, and their stability and reactivity investigated. In the 1,5‐cyclooctadiene (L = COD) complexes, the azidophosphonamido ligands coordinate in a κ2 ‐N,Nα fashion, in which the PN3 moiety adopts a transoid conformation. Irradiation (λ = 365 nm) resulted in linkage isomerization to the κ2 ‐N,Nβ isomers without loss of N2. Substitution of the COD co‐ligands with CO in the κ2 ‐N,Nα complexes occurred with retention of the transoid conformation in the solid state. Meanwhile, in the analogous bis(cyclooctene)rhodium(I) complex, [κ2‐N,Nα ‐ i Pr2P(N3Mes)(NMes)]Rh(COE)2, the κ2 ‐N,Nα ‐coordinated azidophosphonamido ligand exhibited a cisoid conformation, the first example of a stable cisoid phosphazide in the coordination sphere of a group 9 transition metal. Unlike the COD complexes, irradiation of this species resulted in N2 extrusion, yielding an iminophosphonamide, [κ2‐N,N‐ i Pr2P(NMes)2]Rh(COE)2. These complexes have been characterized by multinuclear NMR spectroscopy and single‐crystal X‐ray diffraction studies.

Introduction

Phosphazides (R3P=N3‐R′) constitute a rare class of compounds that, while not often isolated, are invoked as intermediates in the Staudinger reaction (Scheme 1). They are inherently unstable and prone to release N2 when in the cis configuration, yielding the corresponding phosphinimine/iminophosphorane (R3P=N–R′) [1]. Steric and electronic factors, such as bulky groups on nitrogen and phosphorus, strongly electron donating groups on phosphorus and electron‐withdrawing groups on nitrogen, have been shown to stabilize the trans configuration of the phosphazide group, and thus prevent N2 extrusion [2]. More commonly, coordination to a Lewis acid or metal has been demonstrated to stabilize the trans configuration of phosphazides [1, 3, 4].

Complete and arrested Staudinger reaction producing phosphinimine and trans phosphazide ligands, respectively.

The three nitrogen donors in a phosphazide allow for various coordination modes to metal centers. This can result in linkage isomerization, wherein the nitrogen that is bonded to the central atom changes, often due to steric factors [5]. Recently, phosphazides derived from adducts of Frustrated Lewis Pairs (FLPs) with azides have exhibited thermally or photochemically induced linkage isomerism from κ1‐Nα to κ1‐Nβ coordination (Scheme 2) [6, 7]. Bourissou and coworkers first reported such a phenomenon during the attempted photochemical N2 extrusion from the phosphazide‐borane FLP, ortho‐[( i Pr2PN3Ph)C6H4(BMes2)]; Mes = mesityl = 2,4,6‐Me3C6H2 (Scheme 2, top) [6]. The isomerization also changed the phosphazide conformation from cisoid to transoid and the N=N bond configuration from E to Z [6].

Thermally and photochemically induced linkage isomerism of phosphazides in the literature; Bourissou (top) [6], Slootweg (bottom) [7].

In 2019, Slootweg and colleagues disclosed thermally induced linkage isomerism of the boryl‐substituted phosphazide, [κ1‐Nβ ‐( t Bu2PN3Mes)CH2]BPh2 (Scheme 2, bottom). Interestingly, only the N‐mesityl and N‐tert‐butyl compounds could be isolated; other substituents (e.g., trimethylsilyl) resulted in N2 extrusion, yielding the corresponding phosphinimine [7]. The phosphazide conformation changed from cisoid to transoid, and the N=N bond adopted the less sterically hindered Z conformation [6, 7]. Notably, these are the only examples of phosphazide linkage isomerization yielding Nβ→B coordination. Although photochemically induced reactions have various applications in inorganic chemistry (e.g., metal‐catalyzed alkylation of indenes and ketones [8, 9, 10, 11], including photoisomerization [12, 13] (typically employed to avoid harsh reaction conditions) there is a dearth of studies on complexes containing phosphazide moieties.

Despite the limited number of phosphazide‐containing metal complexes [4, 7, 14, 15], recent advances have demonstrated their potential applications in small‐molecule functionalization and catalysis. The first report of a potassium phosphazide, by Fryzuk and coworkers, was found to undergo a salt metathesis reaction to produce an iron complex [16]. We recently synthesized many phosphazide‐containing thorium complexes, which, unlike their phosphinimine counterparts, were resistant to cyclometallation of aryl substituents. Remarkably, the phosphazide allowed for stabilization of the thorium alkyls and amides, [κ5‐N,Nα,Nα,Nγ,Nγ ‐2,5‐[(4‐ i PrC6H4)N3P i Pr2]2N(C4H2)]Th(CH2SiMe3)3 and [κ5‐N,Nα,Nα,Nγ,Nγ ‐2,5‐[(4‐ i PrC6H4)N3P i Pr2]2N(C4H2)]Th(NHAd)3, respectively, which readily insert CO2 [5, 14].

At present, there is only one known group 9 phosphazide complex, Br2Co[Cy3PN3(CO(C4H3O))]•Et2O (Cy = cyclohexyl = C6H11) [17], and no reaction chemistry of this species has been published. Given the well‐established precedent of rhodium and iridium complexes activating a wide variety of bonds, it is reasonable to anticipate that phosphazide complexes of these metals may mediate a variety of catalytic processes [18].

In 2015, Stasch and coworkers co‐crystallized two dimers that featured lithium‐coordinated azidophosphonamido ligands [R2P(N3R1)(NR1)]– (Figure 1), wherein the phosphazide group (–P=N3R1) was retained from an arrested Staudinger reaction [19]. The azidophosphonamido lithium compounds, Li[Ph2P(NDipp)(N3Dipp)]2 and Li[Ph2P(NDipp)(N3Dipp)][Ph2P(NDipp)2], were minor products isolated after the deprotonation of Ph2(NHDipp)(NDipp) [19]. However, during attempts to prepare a less sterically encumbered mesityl‐substituted azidophosphonamido lithium compound, Li[Ph2P(NMes)(N3Mes)2]2, only the N2‐liberated product, Li[Ph2P(NMes)2]2, was obtained [19]. Later, they synthesized another azidophosphonamido‐containing species, [L2L’2Mg4H4] (L = Ph2PNDipp; L’ = DippNP(N3Ad)Ph2; Dipp = 2,6‐diisopropylphenyl), which was produced via reaction of [LMgH]4 with adamantyl azide [20].

Structure of co‐crystallized azidophosphonamido lithium compounds depicting the azidophosphonamido ligand (green) [19].

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Azidophosphonamido Ligands

Mesityl‐substituted azidophosphonamido ligands were chosen as our starting point in an effort to ascertain if the isolation of azidophosphonamido compounds could be attributed, in part, to the electronic influence of the groups on nitrogen. The proposed route to the azidophosphonamido ligand followed the synthesis of analogous iminophosphonamide ligands [19]; reaction of an aryl‐substituted phosphonamide ( i Pr2PNHAr) with an organic azide (ArN3), followed by deprotonation of N–H with a base, such as KH, to generate the corresponding potassium salt, K[ i Pr2P(NAr)(N3Ar)] (Scheme 3). The reaction between i Pr2PNHMes and MesN3 yielded myriad resonances, ranging from δ 65 to 0 in the 31P NMR spectrum [21], and attempts to isolate any of the major products from the mixture were not successful. Another approach, previously reported by our group, is to first deprotonate the N–H to form the potassium salt, then generate the phosphazide ligand in the K+ coordination sphere via reaction with the chosen organic azide [5, 14]. Following this pathway, the targeted azidophosphonamido salt K[ i Pr2P(NMes)(N3Mes)] (1) was readily obtained (Scheme 3) and found to be thermally robust, with no evidence of decomposition at elevated temperatures (100°C) in solution for one week.

Attempted synthesis of 1, according to iminophosphonamide ligand precedent (top, red box) and utilized route to 1 (bottom, green box).

Hypothesizing that bulky alkyl groups could better electronically stabilize a neutral phosphazide, while still providing substantial steric protection, adamantyl (Ad) moieties were selected. Despite the aforementioned unsuccessful attempts to generate iPr2(N3Mes)(NHMes), combination of iPr2NHAd (2) and AdN3 afforded the neutral azidophosphonamido iPr2(N3Ad)(NHAd) (3) (Scheme 4). Extensive heating of 3 at 60°C in CDCl3 for two weeks yielded no change, indicating the phosphazide moiety is kinetically stable toward extrusion of N2. Deprotonation of 3 with nBuLi generated the anticipated lithium salt Li[iPr2P(NAd)(N3Ad)] (4) (Scheme 4), as confirmed by the loss of the N–H resonance in the 1H NMR spectrum and the appearance of a 7Li NMR signal (δ –1.21).

Synthesis of 2–4.

X‐ray diffraction experiments confirmed that both the neutral ligand 3 and the lithium compound 4 retained an intact phosphazide group (Figure 2). Compound 4 was found to crystallize as a dimer, similar to the systems reported by Stasch and colleagues [19]. The dimer lies on the Wyckoff special position a in the highly symmetric orthorhombic space group Fdd2, with the other half of the dimer being generated through a twofold rotation axis; hence, why only half an equivalent is observed in the asymmetric unit. The phosphazide functionality assumes a transoid conformation in the solid state, akin to its precursor 3 (P1–N2–N3=N4 = 178.7(4)°), albeit with a smaller P1–N2–N3=N4 torsion angle (172.4(2)°, 4) that is attributed to coordination of the ligand to lithium. The geometry about lithium is severely distorted from tetrahedral (τ4 = 0.52, τ′4 = 0.48). The P–N2 distance approaches typical single bond length (1.669(2) Å, typical range: 1.67–1.77 Å) [22]. In contrast, P–N1 exhibits double‐bond character (1.573(2) Å, typical range: 1.47–1.62 Å) [22], suggesting the ligand's negative charge resides primarily on N2.

Synthesis of Azidophosphonamido Rhodium and Iridium Complexes

The azidophosphonamido rhodium and iridium complexes were synthesized by reaction of 1 and 4 with the metal dimers [MCl(COD)]2 (M = Rh or Ir; COD = 1,5‐cyclooctadiene) in benzene, which readily afforded complexes 5 ([κ2‐N,Nα ‐ i Pr2P(N3Mes)(NMes)]Rh(COD)]), 6 ([κ2‐N,Nα ‐ i Pr2P(N3Mes)(NMes)]Ir(COD)]), 7 ([κ2‐N,Nα ‐ i Pr2P(N3Ad)(NAd)]Rh(COD)]), and 8 ([κ2‐N,Nα ‐ i Pr2P(N3Ad)(NAd)]Ir(COD)]) over various reaction times (Scheme 5; 5 and 6: t < 5 min, 7 and 8: t = 16 h). Unique to the mesityl‐substituted complexes 5 (δ 84.1) and 6 (δ 105.7), small upfield signals were detected in both 31P{1H} NMR spectra (δ 64.9 in 5 and δ 77.0 in 6), even when they were synthesized at –35°C in the dark, which were later attributed to linkage isomerization products (vide infra). As a consequence, two successive recrystallizations were required to generate analytically pure samples of 5 and 6.

Synthesis of complexes 5–8.

Unambiguous confirmation of the identity of azidophosphonamido complexes 5–8 was provided by X‐ray diffraction experiments (Figure 3). The phosphazide functionalities in complexes 5–8 exhibit a transoid conformation (P1–N2–N3=N4 torsion angle: 5: 171.6(1)°, 6: 172.0(2)°, 7: 170.8(3)°, 8: 172.4(8)°), and an E N=N bond configuration (N2–N3=N4–C16 torsion angle: 5: 179.7(2)°, 6: 179.7(2)°, 7: 179.6(3)°, 8: 178(1)°), minimizing steric interactions of the mesityl or adamantyl substituents and the COD ligand (Figure 3). The azidophosphonamido ligand binds to rhodium or iridium in a κ2 ‐N,Nα fashion, with slightly distorted square planar geometry (5 and 8: τ4 = 0.12, 6 and 7: τ4 = 0.13) and small N–M–N bite angles (5: 70.65(6)°, 6: 69.94(9)°, 7: 70.20(10)°, 8: 70.2(4)°).

The κ2 ‐N,Nα bonding mode of the ligand can be explained by examination of the P–N distances, wherein the ligand's anionic charge is presumed to reside on Nα, as the P–Nα lengths (5: 1.6635(16), 6: 1.667(2), 7: 1.652(3), 8: 1.655(9) Å) are at the short end of the standard single bond range (1.67–1.77 Å) [22] and the P–N1 distances (5: 1.6052(17), 6: 1.606(2), 7: 1.607(3), 8: 1.623(10) Å) exhibit typical double‐bond character (1.47–1.62 Å) [23], mirroring previously reported azidophosphonamido lithium compounds [19].

Linkage Isomerization of Mesityl‐Substituted Rhodium and Iridium Complexes 5 and 6

While generating complexes 5 and 6, minor impurities were observed as sharp singlets in the 31P{1H} NMR spectra (δ 64.9 in 5 and δ 77.0 in 6). Although initially attributed to the corresponding iminophosphonamide complexes, ( i Pr2P(NMes)2)M(COD), from N2 extrusion, literature reports suggested the possibility of linkage isomerization products (Nβ–M or Nγ–M) with larger metal chelate rings [6, 7]. To probe whether our complexes were indeed amenable to ligand isomerization, both 5 and 6 were independently heated (60°C, 2 days), after which near complete conversion of 5 (to the compound that exhibits a 31P NMR peak at δ 64.9) and no changes in compound 6 were observed. Alternatively, substantial conversion via photochemical irradiation (λ = 365 nm) was observed for both 5 (100% conversion after 16 h) and 6 (80% conversion after 30 h) (Scheme 6). Notably, no N2 gas evolution was observed, corroborating the likelihood of linkage isomer products. In addition, the substantial upfield shifts of the 31P resonances of 9 and 10 were echoed by Bourissou and coworkers (Nα→B at δ 61, to Nβ→B at δ 35) [6].

Thermally or photochemically induced phosphazide linkage isomerization (Nα→M to Nβ→M) of complexes 5 and 6, yielding complexes 9 and 10, respectively.

X‐ray diffraction experiments unequivocally confirmed the solid‐state structures of complexes 9 ([κ2‐N,N β‐ i Pr2P(N3Mes)(NMes)]Rh(COD)) and 10 ([κ2‐N,N β‐ i Pr2P(N3Mes)(NMes)]Ir(COD)) as the Nβ–M linkage isomers (Figure 4). The square planar geometry at the metal centers more closely approaches ideality (9 and 10: τ4 = 0.08) via expansion of the metallocycle. Unlike the linkage isomerization reported by Bourissou [6] and Slootweg [7], the phosphazide moieties in 9 and 10 retain the transoid conformation (P–N–N=N 9: 176.8(2) and 178.4(2)°, 10: 176.3(4)° and 178.1(4)°). Similar to those examples, however [6, 7], the N=N bond configuration changed from E to Z (N–N=N–C 9: 2.7(3) and 3.8(3)°, 10: 0.4(7)° and 6.1(6)°), thus minimizing Mes group and COD ligand steric interactions.

To the best of our knowledge, complexes 9 and 10 are the first reported azidophosphonamido transition metal complexes produced via thermally or photochemically induced linkage isomerization. Furthermore, 9 and 10 are the first crystallographically characterized transition‐metal phosphazide complexes that exhibit the unusual N,Nβ coordination mode. Notably, neither linkage isomerization (thermal or photochemical) nor N2 extrusion was observed in the adamantyl‐substituted complexes 7 and 8, suggesting that the κ2‐N,Nα isomers are both kinetically and thermodynamically favored.

COD Displacement and Reactions With Small Molecules

Although complexes 5–8 appeared to be quite robust, it was uncertain if stoichiometric reactions with said complexes would promote N2 loss. Given the ubiquity of Rh and Ir complexes in various hydrofunctionalization reactions [23], probing the reaction chemistry of complexes 5–8 with various small molecules was of interest. Intriguingly, carbon monoxide (CO), a σ‐donating and π‐accepting ligand, readily displaced COD in complexes 5–8, without N2 extrusion, affording the corresponding carbonyl‐containing species 11, [κ2‐N,Nα ‐ i Pr2P(N3Mes)(NMes)]Rh(CO)2, and 12, [κ2‐N,Nα ‐ i Pr2P(N3Mes)(NMes)]Ir(CO)2, 13, [κ2‐N,Nα ‐ i Pr2P(N3Ad)(NAd)]Rh(CO)2) and 14, [κ2‐N,Nα ‐ i Pr2P(N3Ad)(NAd)]Ir(CO)2, respectively (Scheme 7).

Synthesis of complexes 11–14.

Attempts to crystallize complexes 11 and 12 have so far proven unsuccessful, and thus, the azidophosphonamido coordination modes (e.g., Nα‐M, Nβ‐M, or Nγ‐M) have not been established crystallographically. However, the adamantyl‐substituted carbonyl complexes 13 and 14 readily crystallized as yellow plates from saturated pentane solutions (Figure 5).

Complexes 13 and 14 retained the κ2 ‐N,Nα binding of the azidophosphonamido ligand, as well as the transoid conformation (P–N–N=N 13: 168.8(2)°, 14: 170.5(1)°) and distorted square‐planar geometry (13 and 14: τ4 = 0.15). Paralleling the COD analogues, the longer P–Nα (13: 1.6515(18), 14: 1.6511(17) Å) and shorter P–N1 bonds (13: 1.6133(18), 14: 1.6220(18) Å) imply localization of the ligand's charge on Nα.

Loss of the COD resonances in the 1H NMR spectra of 11‐14, and the appearance of two CO signals in the 13C{1H} NMR spectra (11: δ 188.48 (1 J RhC = 69.5 Hz) and 188.34 (1 J RhC = 70.7 Hz), 12: δ 177.22 and 175.06, 13: δ 191.20 (1 J RhC = 69.7 Hz) and 188.87 (1 J RhC = 70.2 Hz), and 14: δ 180.03 and 175.71), corroborate the displacement of COD by CO. This is further supported by the observation of two νCO stretches in the respective IR spectra of each of the complexes.

In the 31P{1H} NMR spectra of 11 and 12, there is an additional signal (δ 78.36 and 87.35, respectively), which we assumed was minor Nβ–M impurities (9 or 10), reacting with CO. To confirm our hypothesis, we independently added CO gas to a benzene‐d 6 solution of [κ2‐N,N β‐ i Pr2P(N3Mes)(NMes)]Rh(COD) (9). Immediate in situ generation of [κ2‐N,N β‐ i Pr2P(N3Mes)(NMes)]Rh(CO)2 (15) was observed. Notably, complex 15 exhibits the expected signal at δ 78.36 in the 31P NMR spectrum. The chemically inequivalent mesityl signals in the 1H spectrum indicate Cs symmetry, as opposed to C2v , which would be anticipated upon liberation of N2 (Scheme 8).

Synthesis of complex 15.

Interestingly, complex 15 exhibits higher frequency carbonyl IR signals (2004, 2070 cm– 1) than the Nα isomer 11 (1996, 2054 cm– 1). Hence, coordination of the azidophosphonamido ligand through Nα (c.f. Nβ) leads to a more electron rich rhodium centre, a fact that is likely to prove important when pursuing subsequent reaction chemistry.

Given the prevalence of group 9 species that serve as competent hydrogenation [24] and hydrosilylation catalysts [25], the ability of the described Rh(I) and Ir(I) azidophosphonamido COD and CO complexes (5‐14) to mediate such reactions was probed. Unfortunately, no reaction between these complexes and stoichiometric or catalytic quantities of various silanes (H3SiPh, H2SiPh2, and HSiEt3) or H2 was detected, despite testing a wide variety of reaction conditions (e.g., ambient temperature to 70°C, irradiation at 365 nm), and solvents. Likewise, no reaction occurred with 2‐butyne.

A More Reactive Azidophosphonamido Rhodium Complex

Since the azidophosphonamido COD complexes 5‐10 are somewhat limited in terms of their reaction chemistry, we aimed to prepare species that contain cyclooctene (COE) ligands, which were anticipated to be more labile. Specifically, reaction of [RhCl(COE2)]2 with 1 in cold (–35°C) pentane generated the corresponding azidophosphonamido complex 16, [κ2‐N,N‐ i Pr2P(N3Mes)(NMes)]Rh(COE)2 (Scheme 9).

Synthesis of complexes 16 and 17.

Crystals of 16 were grown from a pentane solution of [RhCl(COE2)]2 and 1 stored at –35°C for four days, and its identity was confirmed by X‐ray diffraction experiments (Figure 6). Intriguingly, the phosphazide functionality assumes a cisoid conformation (P–N–N=N = 3.0(2)°). Complex 16, is thus, the first reported azidophosphonamido species to adopt the less stable conformation in the solid state, suggesting that this species may be more susceptible to N2 extrusion. There is a nearly ideal E configuration about the N=N bond (N2–N3=N4–C16 = 179.3(1)°), reducing steric repulsion between the mesityl and isopropyl groups.

Crystals of 16 were found to be stable under reduced pressure at ambient temperature for very short periods of time (ca. 5 min). However, decomposition in benzene solution occurred, even at ambient temperature, giving rise to a 31P{1H} NMR signal at δ 67.0. Given that the cisoid conformation observed in the solid state suggested facile N2 loss from complex 16 (Scheme 1), attempts were made to isolate the anticipated product, [ i Pr2P(NMes)2]Rh(COE)2 (17). Complex 16 was heated (70°C, 16 h) in solution, but only 18% conversion to the new product occurred during this timeframe. Alternatively, 16 was irradiated (λ = 365 nm, 1 h) during which complete loss of the 31P{1H} NMR resonance attributed to 16 was observed, along with concomitant appearance of several new signals (δ 67.0 (s, 29% 17), 61.1 (s, 28%), 51.7 (s, 26%), and 11.8 (s, 16%) (Scheme 9). Yellow/orange plates of 17 grew from this mixture over one week at ambient temperature and X‐ray diffraction experiments confirmed its formulation (Figure 7). Other impurities were unable to be isolated from the mixture; however, it is postulated that there is linkage isomerization (κ2‐N,Nα ⇌ κ2‐N,Nβ ) happening in solution giving rise to the upfield signal at δ 51.7, mirroring what was observed when complexes 5 and 6 converted into 9 and 10, respectively.

X‐ray crystal structure of 17 depicted as 50% displacement ellipsoids. Hydrogen atoms are omitted for clarity. Selected bond angles [°] for 17: P–N1 1.623(4), P–N2 1.615(3), N1–Rh–N2 70.36(11), N1=P–N2 99.83(16), C10–P–C13 106.70(18), P–N1–C1 125.3(3), P–N2–C16 128.7(2).

Key bond angles and distances observed in 16 and 17 are similar, including the N–M–N bond angles (16: 69.94(6)°, 17: 70.36(11)°) as well as the distorted square planar geometry at rhodium (16: τ4 = 0.21, 17: τ4 = 0.20), indicating that N2 extrusion has little effect on the geometry at the metal center. However, unlike complex 16, the P–N bond lengths in 17 are similar (1.615(3) and 1.624(3) Å), indicating delocalization within the iminophosphonamide ligand. Crystals of 17 were found to be unstable under vacuum at ambient temperature, leading to an intractable mixture. As a consequence, 1H and 13C NMR experiments were uninformative due to the vast excess of pentane and benzene‐d 6. Nonetheless, 31P{1H} NMR spectroscopy was utilized to confirm the identity of the crystals as the contaminant in complex 16 that resonated at δ 67.0.

Conclusions

A family of azidophosphonamido ligands and their corresponding rhodium and iridium complexes have been reported. Notably, the first examples of Nβ‐coordinated azidophosphonamidos were isolated through photochemically induced linkage isomerization. While these systems are inert to H2 and silanes, the COD ligand can be displaced with CO. Substituting COD for the much more labile COE ligand leads to rapid loss of N2 upon exposure to UV light (λ = 365 nm), giving the iminophosphonamide complex (17). Future studies aim to exploit the enhanced reactivity of complexes 16 and 17 (e.g., with silanes, dihydrogen, amines, etc.) and compare and contrast the impact that the azido functionality has on such chemistry.

Experimental Section

General Considerations

All manipulations of air‐ and moisture‐sensitive compounds were carried out under strict air and moisture exclusion using a double‐manifold vacuum line or an MBraun Labmaster 130 glove box with an inert (argon) atmosphere. All glassware was stored in an oven at 110°C for a minimum of 16 h or flame‐dried prior to use. Solvents used for air‐sensitive procedures were obtained from an MBraun Solvent Purification System (SPS). Nonhalogenated solvents (benzene, toluene, pentane, heptane, THF, and Et2O) were further dried over 4 Å molecular sieves, degassed by three freeze‐pump‐thaw cycles, then stored in PTFE‐sealed glass vessels over sodium benzophenone ketyl, and distilled into the reaction vessel at the time of use. Solvents used for NMR spectroscopy were prepared as follows: benzene‐d 6 (C6D6) and THF‐d 8 were dried over sodium benzophenone ketyl, and chloroform‐d (CDCl3) was dried over CaH2. Deuterated solvents were degassed via three freeze‐pump‐thaw cycles, distilled into a glass bomb under reduced pressure and temperature, and stored under static vacuum over 4 Å molecular sieves.

The reagents n BuLi (2.5 M in hexanes), KH, 1‐adamantylamine, mesitylamine, chlorodiisopropylphosphine and [RhCl(COE)2]2 were purchased from Sigma Aldrich and used without further purification. The compounds Ph2P(NHMes)(NMes) [26], MesNHP i Pr2 [27], MesN3 [28], Li[Ph2P(NMes)2] [19], AdNHPPh2 [21], [Rh(Cl)(COD)]2 [29], and [IrCl(COD)]2 [30] were prepared using literature procedures. All solid reagents used in air‐ and moisture‐sensitive reactions were dried under vacuum for 48 h at ambient temperature and kept under an inert atmosphere before use. All liquid reagents were transferred via cannula to a glass bomb for storage and degassed via three freeze‐pump‐thaw cycles.

All NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker Avance II 300 MHz spectrometer (300.13 MHz for 1H, 75.47 MHz for 13C, and 121.48 MHz for 31P) or a Bruker Avance III HD 700 MHz spectrometer (700.44 MHz for 1H, 116.59 MHz for 7Li, 176.13 MHz for 13C, and 283.54 MHz for 31P) NMR spectrometer. Experiments were performed at ambient temperature unless otherwise stated. The NMR spectroscopic data is reported in parts per million (ppm). The 1H and 13C chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to SiMe4 through internal 1H and 13C resonance(s) of the employed solvent; benzene‐d 6 (δ 7.16 and 128.0), THF‐d 8 (δ 1.73, 3.58 and 25.4, 67.6), or CDCl3 (δ 7.27 and 77.4). The 7Li NMR spectra were referenced to an external 1 M LiCl sample in D2O (δ 0.0). The 31P spectra were referenced to an external 85% H3PO4 sample in D2O (δ 0.0). The 1H NMR data are reported as follows: chemical shift, multiplicity (s = singlet, d = doublet, t = triplet, q = quartet, sp = septet, dsp = doublet of septets, br = broad, m = multiplet, ov = overlapping), coupling constant(s) (Hz), integration, and assignment. The assignment of 1H and 13C{1H} resonances was supported by 1H–1H COSY, 13C{1H} APT, 13C{1H} DEPT 135, 13C{1H} DEPT 90, 1H–31C HSQC and 1H–13C HMBC experiments. Infrared spectroscopic analysis was conducted on bulk recrystallized compounds employing a Thermo‐Nicolet iS10 FT‐IR spectrometer.

CHNS elemental analyses were collected at the University of Lethbridge using an Elementar Americas Vario MicroCube Analyzer (C, H, N, O, S capabilities). “Universal Combustion Additive,” purchased from Elemental Microanalysis, was added to all standards, blanks, and samples. Vanadium oxide was used as a combustion agent in cases when only the carbon percentages were low (3% deviation from predicted values). Unless otherwise specified, reported yields correspond to those obtained for analytically pure samples. When additional purification was required to generate analytically pure compounds for combustion analysis, both the crude and analytical yields were included. In such cases, the reported crude yield corresponds to material that was utilized for successive synthetic steps and was >98% pure as indicated by multinuclear NMR spectroscopy. (N.B. NMR spectra displayed in the ESI were obtained using these “crude” samples.) It should also be noted that when reported, analytical yields are generally artificially low, as excess crystals grown for X‐ray diffraction experiments are used for this purpose. Those recrystallizations were not carried out under conditions that maximized yield but rather were optimized for the growth of X‐ray quality crystals.

X‐ray Crystallography

To protect the air‐sensitive crystal samples from moisture, they were submerged in Paratone‐N oil under the inert atmosphere of the glove box before being removed and transferred to a microscope slide. Suitable single crystals were selected under the microscope and mounted on MiTeGen cryo‐loops. Data were collected using a Rigaku SuperNova diffractometer equipped with a Pilatus P200 HPC detector, dual X‐ray sources (CuKα, λ = 1.54184 Å; MoKα, λ = 0.71073 Å), and an Oxford Cryostream 800 cooler. Data collection and refinement parameters are provided in Tables S1–S80.

Data collection, integration, scaling, and correction were performed using CrysAlisPro (version 1.171.4x). Empirical absorption corrections were applied using spherical harmonics, implemented in the SCALE3 ABSPACK scaling algorithm. For 8, the crystals exhibited two‐component, non‐merohedral twinning (77:23, rotation by ca. 180° about the c* axis). The reflections for each component were integrated, scaled, and corrected separately, then merged with intensities (I) calculated using statistical weighting scheme and σ(I) calculated using the SHELX‐97 scheme.

Structure solution (ShelXT) [31] and least‐squares refinement (ShelXL) [32] were performed using Olex2 (version 1.5) [33]. Hydrogen atoms were refined isotropically using a riding model (HFIX). For 8, structure solution and initial refinement were performed using the major twin component (HKLF4), whereas final refinement was performed on the merged dataset (HKLF5). Additionally, a global enhanced rigid‐body restraint (RIGU) was applied to 8, and one adamantyl group in 8 exhibited rotational disorder that was modeled in two components with freely refined occupancies (68:32) and similarity restraints (SADI/SIMU) for similar C–C distances and C displacement parameters, respectively.
