Neurophysiological correlates of taVNS-mediated cognitive enhancement
Authors: Myeong Seong Bak, Jin-Sol Lee, Geehoon Chung, In-Seon Lee, Sun Kwang Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the neural mechanisms underlying cognitive enhancement through transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) by analyzing Stroop task performance and electroencephalography (EEG) data.MethodsTwenty-three healthy adults (12 males; aged 19–40) were randomly assigned to either the taVNS or sham group. The Stroop task was administered in three sessions, with taVNS (3 Hz, 0.5 mA for 10 min) applied between sessions. EEG data were collected before and after the experiment, as well as during the Stroop task, for further analysis.ResultsThe taVNS group showed higher accuracy than the sham group under the incongruent condition in Session 3 (p < .05), consistent with differences in signal detection metrics, suggesting changes in decision processes. Across sessions, beta-band power increased during task performance in the sham group, whereas this increase was attenuated in the taVNS group. This effect was most pronounced in the high beta range (20–30 Hz) at the Fp1 channel (FDR-corrected q < 0.05).ConclusiontaVNS attenuated the task-related escalation in high beta-band power typically observed during sustained Stroop performance, thereby mitigating cognitive demand and enhancing response accuracy.SignificanceThese results position taVNS as a promising non-invasive intervention for strengthening cognitive control in healthy individuals.Keywords: Transcutaneous auricular Vagus nerve stimulation, Stroop task, Electroencephalography, Cognitive function, Band power analysis

Introduction

Cognitive control represents a fundamental aspect of human cognition, enabling the deliberate guidance of thought and behavior in accordance with internal goals, particularly when confronted with conflicting information or potent distractions (Tik et al., 2018). This capacity is essential for navigating complex environments and achieving desired outcomes, requiring the rapid and flexible allocation of neural resources to prioritize relevant information while suppressing irrelevant or interfering inputs. However, cognitive control is widely considered to be intrinsically effortful, which often biases individuals towards habitual actions or less demanding tasks (Shenhav et al., 2013; Botvinick and Braver, 2015). Therefore, developing a simple and efficient method to enhance cognitive control, particularly under conditions of conflict, and identifying its neural basis represents a key objective in neuroscience research.

While vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), an established neuromodulation technique, has demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in epilepsy and depression (Groves and Brown, 2005; Nemeroff et al., 2006), emerging evidence indicates its potential to modulate higher-order cognitive processes, such as memory, attention, and executive control (Vonck et al., 2014; Tan et al., 2024). Most mechanistic accounts emphasize the role of neuromodulatory release via afferent vagal input to the brainstem, proposing broad effects on cortical function (Frangos et al., 2015). Nonetheless, few studies have systematically connected these mechanisms to observable patterns of brain activity during active cognitive processing, leaving a gap in our understanding of how peripheral stimulation shapes neural systems in ways that are behaviorally meaningful.

Transcutaneous auricular VNS (taVNS) provides a non-invasive route to engage the same vagal pathways without the need for surgical implantation, thereby increasing both experimental and clinical accessibility (Frangos et al., 2015). Early taVNS studies in healthy individuals have reported modulation of cortical oscillations during rest and low cognitive demand tasks; however, several limitations hinder current understanding (Roux and Uhlhaas, 2014; Wienke et al., 2023). First, most research has focused predominantly on low-frequency bands (theta, alpha) (Raghavachari et al., 2001; Konjusha et al., 2022), overlooking beta activity that closely reflects top-down control and cognitive load (Palacios-Garcia et al., 2021; Lundqvist et al., 2024). Second, most works rely on resting-state recordings (Ricci et al., 2020; Sharon et al., 2021), leaving unsolved the neural impact of taVNS during active conflict resolution. Thus, it remains unclear how taVNS influences the neural dynamics that govern active cognitive control, particularly in situations requiring rapid conflict resolution. Addressing this gap requires task-based paradigms that evoke robust executive demands while enabling concurrent measurement of neural and behavioral responses.

Beta oscillations (13–30 Hz) in fronto-parietal networks are known to signal sustained effort and gate information flow needed for goal maintenance (Buzsaki and Draguhn, 2004; Lundqvist et al., 2024). During demanding cognitive engagement, beta power typically increases, reflecting mounting control costs. Efficient performers show attenuated or more rapidly disengaged beta bursts, suggesting that controlled suppression of beta rhythms may facilitate the allocation of neural resources for adaptive cognitive responses (Cebolla and Cheron, 2019; Konakoglu et al., 2023). Whether taVNS can effectively constrain such beta build-up under high-conflict situations and subsequently improve task performance remains an open question. Answering this question necessitates time-resolved electrophysiological recordings during tasks that robustly engage executive control functions, particularly conflict monitoring, response inhibition, and attentional control (Zysset et al., 2001; Chen et al., 2023).

To clarify the functional impact of taVNS during active cognitive engagement, the present study examined its effects on cortical oscillations and behavioral performance under conditions demanding high executive control. We utilized a Stroop task paradigm in conjunction with electroencephalography (EEG), targeting neural processes associated with conflict monitoring, response inhibition, and attentional control. Departing from resting-state or general arousal effects, our approach captured dynamic neural activity during task execution, with a specific focus on beta-band oscillations that reflect cognitive effort and top-down control. By quantifying beta power changes across task sessions and correlating them with individual performance metrics, this study offers empirical insight into how taVNS modulates neural states to enhance cognitive efficiency.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University (KHSIRB-24-121, 2024). The trial was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service, and a Korean Clinical Trial (KCT) number was assigned (KCT0009998). A total of 23 healthy adults (12 males, 11 females; age range 19–40 years, mean age = 25.39 ± 3.90 years) were recruited. Participants were randomly assigned to the taVNS group (n = 11; 5 males, 6 females; mean age = 25.8 ± 3.8 years) or the sham group (n = 12; 7 males, 5 females; mean age = 24.9 ± 3.6 years). All participants voluntarily provided written informed consent prior to participation. Inclusion criteria included no history of neurological, psychiatric, or significant medical conditions, and no current use of medications.

Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation

The vagus nerve stimulator (NGT-01/Cerogrin, Neurogrin Inc., South Korea; Fig. 1A) used in this study was applied to the cymba conchae of the left ear, where branches of the vagus nerve are located. The stimulation area covered approximately 6 mm in diameter, enabling non-invasive stimulation of the vagus nerve (Fig. 1A). Based on prior research and to minimize potential side effects (Groves and Brown, 2005; Vonck et al., 2014), stimulation was delivered using biphasic square-wave pulses at 3 Hz and 0.5 mA with a 100% duty cycle, resulting in continuous current delivery. The sham control group wore an identical device for the same duration, but no current was delivered (0 mA). Both groups underwent identical procedures in terms of device attachment, session duration, and experimental timing, with electrical stimulation applied only in the active taVNS condition. Each stimulation session lasted 10 min, and stimulation was administered twice during the experiment, resulting in a total stimulation time of 20 min. No stimulation-related adverse events were reported.

Sessions were defined based on task performance periods (Session 1–3).

Color-word matching stroop task

We implemented a color-word matching Stroop task (hereafter, Stroop task) based on Zysset et al. (2001). The task was programmed using a custom Python script to record response accuracy and reaction times. Participants were seated in front of a computer monitor while wearing the EEG device (Fig. 1B–C). Each block began with a 15-s fixation period, followed by three conditions presented in a fixed order (neutral, congruent, and incongruent). Within each condition, 20 stimuli were presented in random order (1.5 s each). Each stimulus consisted of two vertically arranged words: the top word was displayed in colored ink, whereas the bottom word was presented in black. The words denoted color names, and participants were required to determine whether the ink color of the top word matched the semantic meaning of the bottom word. Participants indicated their response by clicking the mouse (left click for match, right click for mismatch). Each block (210 s) consisted of 60 stimuli (20 per condition), and two blocks constituted one session. A total of three sessions were completed. The structure of the Stroop task is illustrated in Fig. 1D, and the overall experimental timeline is shown in Fig. 1E.

Design and procedure

Participants were randomly assigned to either the taVNS group (active stimulation) or the sham group using a computer-generated randomization sequence. The study employed a single-blind design, with participants and the data analyst blinded to group assignment. Participants were seated comfortably and fitted with an EEG headband for continuous recording. After receiving task instructions, they completed the experimental protocol illustrated in Fig. 1E, which consisted of a 5-min pre-stimulation baseline, three Stroop task sessions (210 s each), two stimulation periods (10 min each; taVNS or sham), and a 5-min post-stimulation baseline. EEG was recorded continuously throughout the experiment. Stimulation was delivered to the cymba conchae of the left ear using a taVNS device (Fig. 1A). Baseline recordings were included as part of the experimental protocol but were not analyzed in the primary task-related comparisons.

Behavioral data analysis

Behavioral performance was assessed using reaction time (RT) and response accuracy. RT was defined as the time interval between stimulus onset and participant response and was averaged at the session level for each condition. RT values are reported in milliseconds (ms) without transformation. Accuracy was defined as the proportion of correct responses within each condition and session (range: 0–1), with higher values indicating better performance. To dissociate perceptual sensitivity from potential decision bias, signal detection theory (SDT) metrics were computed, including sensitivity (d') and decision criterion (c). For SDT analyses, hit rates and false alarm rates were adjusted using a log-linear correction (Hautus, 1995) to avoid extreme values (i.e., 0 or 1). Specifically, 0.5 was added to the number of hits and false alarms, and the adjusted counts were divided by the total number of trials plus one (n + 1).

EEG recordings

The EEG recording device used in this study (Smart Wearable, SOSO H&C Inc., South Korea; Fig. 1B) was designed for wearable acquisition of frontal EEG signals. Electrodes were positioned at the frontopolar sites Fp1 and Fp2 according to the international 10–20 system, with a reference electrode placed on the right earlobe. EEG data were continuously recorded at a sampling rate of 250 Hz. Electrode impedance was maintained at ≤50 kΩ, and the amplifier input impedance was ≥10 MΩ. The recorded signals were processed using standard band-pass and notch filtering, and frequency-domain features were extracted. Because frontopolar EEG recordings are susceptible to ocular and muscle artifacts, data were acquired under controlled conditions with real-time monitoring by the examiner. Participants were instructed to minimize movements and remain alert throughout the recording. In line with previous studies, two-channel frontal EEG recordings can support frequency-domain analyses for cognitive and clinical assessment (Choi et al., 2019; Jang et al., 2025). Although the present two-channel configuration does not support spatial interpretation, it is sufficient for assessing relative changes in EEG band power associated with task and session conditions.

Band power analysis of EEG data

Time-frequency decomposition was performed using a complex Morlet wavelet transform adapted from the EEGLAB toolbox (Delorme and Makeig, 2004) and implemented in Python 3.9. Frequencies were linearly sampled from 1 to 50 Hz (50 bins; ∼1 Hz resolution). The Morlet wavelet family used frequency-dependent cycle numbers that were log-spaced from 4 to 10 cycles across the 1–50 Hz range. Convolution was computed in the frequency domain via FFT-based multiplication of the wavelet and signal spectra, followed by inverse FFT and center-cropping to remove edge artifacts (half-wave trimming). The temporal support of the wavelet was fixed at −2 to 2 s. Time-frequency analyses were performed on stimulus-locked EEG data within a predefined time window of 0.2–0.7 s post-stimulus. Power values were averaged across this interval. Power spectral density (PSD) values were expressed as log-transformed power using a 10·log10 transformation, and relative power was used for visualization where appropriate. Baseline data were not included in the present analysis. EEG analyses focused on session-wise beta-band activity during Stroop task performance. Channel-specific analyses were conducted for Fp1, Fp2, and the bipolar derivation (Fp1 – Fp2) to assess potential lateralized effects. The beta band was further divided into low beta (13–20 Hz) and high beta (20–30 Hz) ranges to distinguish frequency-specific effects. Representative PSD plots from one participant per group (sham and taVNS) are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1 for visualization of data quality.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) and Python 3.9 utilizing the scipy library. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), unless otherwise specified. Group differences in behavioral performance and signal detection theory (SDT) metrics were assessed using two-tailed Welch's t-tests. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were estimated using standard methods. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied as appropriate. To examine group × session effects on EEG-derived measures, linear mixed-effects models (MixedLM) were applied with participant included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements. These models included fixed effects for group and session, with Session 1 as the reference level. Regression coefficients (β) represent unstandardized fixed effects for group, session, and their interaction. Data were analyzed at the block level, with two blocks per participant in each session, and this repeated-measures structure was accounted for in the mixed-effects models. Statistical significance was defined as p < .05 for primary comparisons. To control for multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied where applicable, and FDR-adjusted q-values were reported. For visualization of individual trajectories (Fig. 4C), data were additionally summarized at the participant level, with each line representing session-wise changes for a single participant.

Results

Behavioral performance during the color-word matching Stroop task

RT and accuracy were examined across sessions (S1–S3) for each stimulus condition (neutral, congruent, and incongruent) (Fig. 2). RT decreased across sessions (i.e., faster responses) in both groups for all conditions, indicating a general improvement in task performance over time (Fig. 2A). This pattern was consistent across all stimulus conditions. Block-level comparisons further confirmed that RT did not differ between the taVNS and sham groups in any condition or session (Welch's t = −1.59 to −0.04, df = 33.42–43.97, p = .118–.970, Cohen's d = −0.47 to −0.01, all 95% CIs included zero). In contrast, accuracy showed a different pattern (Fig. 2B). While performance was generally high across conditions and sessions, a significant between-group difference emerged in the incongruent condition at Session 3. In the block-level comparison, Session 3 incongruent accuracy was higher in the taVNS group than in the sham group (taVNS: M = 0.980, SEM = 0.007, n = 22; sham: M = 0.935, SEM = 0.017, n = 24; Welch's t(30.73) = 2.42, p = .022, Cohen's d = 0.69, mean difference = 0.044, 95% CI [0.007, 0.082]). All other comparisons were not significant (neutral: p = .384–.659; congruent: p = .086–.886; incongruent Sessions 1–2: p = .222 and .077; Cohen's d = −0.04 to 0.52, all 95% CIs included zero). Overall, these results suggest that although both groups showed comparable improvements in response speed, taVNS was associated with enhanced accuracy specifically under higher cognitive demand (i.e., incongruent condition) at the later stage of task performance.

SDT-derived conflict effects across sessions

To further characterize task performance within a signal detection framework, difference scores were computed for each SDT-related measure (false alarm rate, d', and c) by comparing incongruent trials with neutral and congruent conditions (incongruent – neutral; incongruent – congruent) across sessions (Fig. 3). For the incongruent – neutral comparison (Fig. 3A), the most consistent block-level group differences were observed at Session 3, where the taVNS group showed a smaller false-alarm contrast and higher d' and c contrasts than the sham group (false alarm: M = 0.000 vs. 0.036, Welch's t(35.04) = −2.30, p = .028, Cohen's d = −0.66, mean difference = −0.036, 95% CI [−0.067, −0.004]; d': M = 0.014 vs. −0.424, Welch's t(42.24) = 2.42, p = .020, Cohen's d = 0.70, mean difference = 0.439, 95% CI [0.073, 0.805]; c: M = 0.007 vs. −0.112, Welch's t(43.67) = 2.18, p = .035, Cohen's d = 0.64, mean difference = 0.119, 95% CI [0.009, 0.230]). At Session 2, group differences were also observed for false alarm rate and d', whereas c did not reach significance (false alarm: Welch's t(39.45) = −2.21, p = .033, Cohen's d = −0.64, mean difference = −0.034, 95% CI [−0.064, −0.003]; d': Welch's t(43.79) = 2.05, p = .047, Cohen's d = 0.60, mean difference = 0.390, 95% CI [0.006, 0.775]; c: p = .124).

A similar pattern was observed for the incongruent – congruent comparison (Fig. 3B), with significant block-level group differences at Sessions 2 and 3. At Session 2, the taVNS group showed a smaller false-alarm contrast and higher d' and c contrasts than the sham group (false alarm: M = 0.009 vs. 0.046, Welch's t(36.34) = −2.60, p = .013, Cohen's d = −0.75, mean difference = −0.037, 95% CI [−0.066, −0.008]; d': M = −0.158 vs. −0.618, Welch's t(43.47) = 2.64, p = .012, Cohen's d = 0.77, mean difference = 0.461, 95% CI [0.109, 0.812]; c: M = −0.046 vs. −0.173, Welch's t(43.71) = 2.25, p = .029, Cohen's d = 0.66, mean difference = 0.127, 95% CI [0.013, 0.240]). At Session 3, the same pattern remained evident across all SDT-related measures (false alarm: Welch's t(31.28) = −2.35, p = .025, Cohen's d = −0.67, mean difference = −0.037, 95% CI [−0.069, −0.005]; d': Welch's t(38.56) = 2.57, p = .014, Cohen's d = 0.74, mean difference = 0.440, 95% CI [0.094, 0.787]; c: Welch's t(42.86) = 2.56, p = .014, Cohen's d = 0.75, mean difference = 0.123, 95% CI [0.026, 0.221]). Overall, these findings suggest that taVNS modulates multiple components of decision-making under cognitive conflict, including enhanced perceptual sensitivity (d'), reduced false-alarm responses, and systematic shifts in response bias (c), with effects becoming more pronounced in later sessions.

Task-related high beta-band activity at prefrontal sites

To examine the neural correlates of task performance, beta-band activity at prefrontal channels was analyzed across sessions (Fig. 4). At the Fp1 channel, PSD during task performance showed distinct session-wise differences between groups (Fig. 4A). While the sham group exhibited a gradual increase in beta-range power across sessions, the taVNS group showed a relatively stable profile without progressive elevation. Consistent channel-specific PSD patterns for Fp1, Fp2, and the bipolar derivation are provided in Supplementary Fig. S2. To further characterize these effects, relative beta-band power at Fp1 was quantified by separating low beta (13–20 Hz) and high beta (20–30 Hz) components (Fig. 4B). For relative high beta power, the linear mixed-effects model revealed a significant group × Session 3 interaction (β = −0.00313, SE = 0.00126, z = −2.48, p = .013, FDR-adjusted q = 0.033, 95% CI [−0.00560, −0.00066]). This interaction indicates that the session-dependent increase in high beta relative power from Session 1 to Session 3 was smaller in the taVNS group than in the sham group. Detailed statistical results for band-specific power across frequency ranges are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Full-band relative power profiles across sessions are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3. Session-wise trajectories of high beta power further illustrated these effects (Fig. 4C). At both Fp1 and Fp2, individual-level analyses showed that the sham group exhibited increasing high beta power across sessions. In contrast, the taVNS group showed relatively stable high beta power. Together, these findings indicate that taVNS modulated prefrontal beta-band dynamics during cognitive task performance by attenuating the session-dependent increase in high beta activity observed in the sham condition.

Discussion

Effective performance on the color-word matching Stroop task (hereafter, Stroop task) requires conflict monitoring, response inhibition, and attentional control (Zysset et al., 2001; Chen et al., 2023). In this context, the Stroop effect refers to delayed responses under incongruent conditions, reflecting interference between competing representations that increases cognitive demand. taVNS appears to enhance these cognitive functions (Beste et al., 2016; Tan et al., 2024). Consistent with this, the present study showed a gradual increase in reaction time from neutral to congruent to incongruent conditions (Fig. 2A), suggesting that the task effectively elicited interference-related processing. In addition, the reduction in reaction time across sessions in both groups likely reflects a general practice effect, indicating increased task familiarity over time. However, accuracy changes were observed only under incongruent (high-conflict) conditions. Notably, the taVNS group showed significantly higher accuracy and SDT indices under high-conflict (incongruent) conditions in Session 3, which may indicate enhanced performance beyond practice-related improvements. This pattern may be consistent with a modulation of conflict-related cognitive processes rather than a general practice effect. These findings are consistent with prior research showing that taVNS augments conflict monitoring: for example, Fischer et al. (2018) reported that taVNS in healthy adults improved behavioral adaptation to conflict and increased the N2 event-related potential (ERP) associated with conflict detection. The authors proposed that this effect may be mediated via activation of the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC-NE) system by taVNS, which could heighten the salience of the conflict signal and recruit cognitive control mechanisms. Collectively, these observations suggest that the improved Stroop performance with taVNS likely reflects a synergistic enhancement of conflict monitoring, response inhibition, and attentional control, thereby enabling more efficient cognitive processing under high-conflict conditions.

A key neural finding in the present study was a difference in high beta-band activity (20–30 Hz) during the Stroop task. Beta oscillations in fronto-central regions are known to be involved in top-down cognitive control and to reflect cognitive load or effort over time (Stoll et al., 2016; Palacios-Garcia et al., 2021). taVNS can influence a broad set of neurophysiological systems by activating afferent vagal pathways to the brainstem (Groves and Brown, 2005; Tan et al., 2024). This leads to the release of key neuromodulators—including NE, gamma-aminobutyric acid, acetylcholine, serotonin, and dopamine—which in turn affect cortical oscillatory dynamics and cognitive function. Such widespread neuromodulator release may contribute to a reduction in excessive beta synchrony in frontal cortical networks while promoting a more alert and flexible pattern of brain activity. For instance, Reimer et al. (2016) showed that enhanced LC-NE activity is associated with cortical desynchronization and reduced beta synchrony. Vinck et al. (2015) explicitly measured beta power reductions in high arousal states, directly linking elevated LC-NE activity to diminished beta oscillations. Complementing these findings, Tinkhauser et al. (2017) revealed that adaptive deep brain stimulation targeting dopaminergic circuits can reduce beta oscillatory activity in the subthalamic nucleus. Taken together, these findings underscore that taVNS induces widespread neuromodulatory effects—particularly across noradrenergic and dopaminergic systems—which culminates in a robust reduction in beta oscillatory activity, thereby optimizing cortical processing and enhancing cognitive performance.

Beta-band power typically changes with time-on-task, often increasing gradually during prolonged cognitive effort (Stoll et al., 2016). Consistent with this, in the sham control group of the study, repeated Stroop task sessions led to a build-up of beta power, indicative of mounting cognitive load. In contrast, by the final session, frontal beta power in the taVNS group was significantly lower than in the sham group. The effect was most evident in the high beta band (20–30 Hz), where a significant reduction was observed compared to the sham group. In the low beta range (13–20 Hz), only a trend-level difference was observed. Notably, these differences were more pronounced at Fp1 than at Fp2. The Fp1 electrode is located over the left prefrontal cortex, a region associated with language processing and conflict-related cognitive control (MacDonald 3rd et al., 2000; Zysset et al., 2001). These findings suggest that taVNS may mitigate cognitive fatigue by diminishing excessive beta synchrony, thereby supporting more flexible and efficient cognitive processing. During conflict or when a shift in cognitive set is needed, beta rhythms may require suppression to enable flexible reconfiguration (Zavala et al., 2018). The taVNS-induced beta reduction fits this framework: by preventing an excessive build-up of beta activity, taVNS may promote a more optimal, flexible neural state for cognitive control (Stoll et al., 2016; Fischer et al., 2018; Palacios-Garcia et al., 2021; Konakoglu et al., 2023). Participants receiving taVNS essentially exhibited an “anti-fatigue” neural profile (Reimer et al., 2016), wherein reduced beta activity suggests that their brains were less prone to entering a hyper-synchronized or rigid state, even after extended task exposure. This neurophysiological modulation likely reflects diminished cognitive load and more efficient cognitive processing , in line with their superior task performance.

The taVNS technique employed in this study offers significant practical advantages over conventional VNS approaches by enhancing both safety and ease of application through its non-invasive design, making it accessible for broader clinical and research settings. Additionally, the wireless battery-powered operation allows uninterrupted stimulation for duration exceeding four hours, eliminating constraints associated with wired connections and external control equipment. These features not only enhance usability but also bolster its translational potential in therapeutic and research contexts. The device's capacity to deliver stimulation at variable frequencies and intensities further exemplifies its versatility. In this study, a mild stimulation protocol of 3 Hz and 0.5 mA was utilized (Groves and Brown, 2005; Vonck et al., 2014), demonstrating its utility in modulating neural oscillations. This adaptability underscores its prospective applications in neurological conditions like Alzheimer's disease (Wang et al., 2022), ADHD (Zaehle and Krauel, 2021), and post-stroke cognitive impairment (Peng et al., 2023), where targeted neuromodulation may yield therapeutic benefits.

Several limitations inherent to this study merit consideration. The employed EEG apparatus consisted of a two-channel headband with dry electrodes. Although advantageous in terms of practicality, this configuration offers reduced signal quality relative to wet electrode systems, impedes the application of advanced signal processing techniques, such as independent component analysis, and constrains the precision of spatial localization of neural activity. Within these constraints, the present analyses nonetheless revealed differences between the two frontal channels (Fp1 and Fp2), suggesting lateralized patterns of activity rather than a single aggregated signal. No inference regarding inter-channel connectivity was made. Accordingly, while this configuration may be adequate for frequency-domain analysis of frontal high beta-band (20–30 Hz) activity, interpretation of these findings may need to consider the limited spatial resolution and potential susceptibility to artifacts inherent to the two-channel EEG setup. Additionally, the modest sample size of 23 participants undermines the generalizability of the results and diminishes the statistical power required to detect subtle effects. To partially address these limitations, we applied false discovery rate (FDR) correction and visualized session-wise changes using connected individual data points (Fig. 4). Larger-scale studies will be required to confirm the robustness and generalizability of these findings. Moreover, the present study evaluated only the short-term effects of taVNS on cognitive performance, leaving its long-term effects and sustainability unaddressed. Finally, the Stroop task was specifically focused on attention and executive function, without evaluating other cognitive domains such as learning and memory or spatial processing. Hence, future research should include a broader range of cognitive tasks and analytical approaches (e.g., ERP) to fully understand the scope of taVNS-induced cognitive enhancement.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings suggest that taVNS is associated with changes in cognitive performance, particularly accuracy in the Stroop task, while concurrently attenuating the increase in high beta-band (20–30 Hz) oscillatory power at the Fp1 channel, suggesting modulation of top-down control processes. The attenuation of high beta activity at Fp1 may reflect a neurophysiological mechanism by which taVNS influences cognitive performance. Taken together, this study positions taVNS not only as a promising approach for cognitive enhancement in healthy individuals but also as a potential intervention for neuropsychiatric conditions characterized by executive dysfunction. Large-scale randomized clinical trials are warranted to establish its efficacy across broader populations, including individuals with cognitive impairments such as mild cognitive impairment or early-stage dementia. Moreover, bridging mechanistic insight with translational applications will be critical to advancing personalized neuromodulation strategies.
