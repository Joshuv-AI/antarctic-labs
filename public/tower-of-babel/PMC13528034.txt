Addiction across substance use, gambling, and screen: a scoping review of coping strategies adopted by concerned significant others
Authors: Mélissa Côté, Kim Hardy, Nadine Blanchette-Martin, Catherine Gatineau, Joël Tremblay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA loved one’s addiction to substance use (alcohol or drugs), gambling, or screen use (e.g. gaming, social media) has significant impact on the quality of life of concerned significant others (CSOs), affecting various aspects of their daily lives. As a result, CSOs implement a range of coping strategies to deal with their loved one’s addiction and to try to preserve their personal and family well-being. Several studies have focused on the strategies used by CSOs, but none have provided a comprehensive overview of CSO’s experiences of coping across substance use, gambling, or screen-related addiction contexts.MethodsA scoping review was conducted to provide a comprehensive overview of the coping strategies used by CSOs of adults whose loved ones experience substance use, gambling, or screen-related addiction. It also sought to extend the previous mapping of coping strategies among CSOs of individuals with gambling problems developed by Côté et al. (2018), by broadening its scope to include substance use and screen-related addictions. To do so, a systematic search was conducted across six databases: PsycINFO, Medline, Psychology and Behavioral Sciences Collection, SocIndex, Cinahl Complete, and Health and Psychosocial Instruments.ResultsAmong the 64 included studies, most focused on CSOs of individuals with substance use (64%) or gambling (26%), with fewer addressing co-occurring substance use and gambling (6%) and screen-related addiction (3%). Findings indicate that CSOs rely on a broad range of coping strategies aimed at either influencing the loved one’s addiction-related behaviours or enhancing personal, couple, or family well-being, alongside emerging strategies not previously captured in existing frameworks. Despite some nuances, the results reveal a trend toward uniformity in coping strategies used by CSOs in substance use and gambling contexts. Evidence on coping strategies used by CSOs in screen-related addiction remains more limited, and comparability with substance use and gambling contexts is less established.ConclusionsCSOs demonstrate active engagement in managing the impacts of addiction through coping strategies that address both the recovery process of their loved ones and the preservation of family and relational well-being, highlighting their central role in the broader addiction context.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13011-026-00739-0.

Introduction

The presence of addiction1 related to substance use (alcohol or drugs), gambling, or screens (e.g., gaming, social media), especially in their more severe manifestations, leads to multiple consequences for the person concerned, affecting all areas of their life, whether psychological, physical, professional, academic, occupational, or interpersonal [1–7]. It is therefore not surprising that concerned significant others (CSOs) are greatly affected by this addiction. Based on studies focusing on the loved ones of individuals with addiction to substance [8] or gambling [9–11] use, it appears that between five to ten people may be affected by one person’s addiction.

To date, in the field of addictions, studies focusing on the experience of CSOs have primarily examined those whose loved ones have substance use and gambling problems [3, 5, 12]. Although very few studies specifically address the experience of CSOs whose loved one has screen-related addiction [4, 13, 14], several similarities have been identified across substance use, gambling, and screen-related addictions [15–18]. It would therefore be beneficial to adopt an inclusive approach to CSOs’ experiences of living with a loved one engaged in these addictive behaviors, in line with emerging literature on affected others across addiction types [19, 20].

The way in which CSOs are affected by substance use or gambling by an adult loved one is well documented, whereas much less is known about these experiences in the context of screen-related addiction. The financial sphere is greatly impacted, regardless of the nature of the addiction [3, 5, 21–27]; conflicts emerge due to the time spent around the addiction at the expense of the relationship [3, 5, 28, 29]; and the physical and psychological health of CSOs deteriorates [5, 21, 25, 30]. The finding is that, regardless of the nature of the addiction, CSOs are negatively affected, particularly in the context of substance use and gambling. Although evidence is scarce for screen-related addiction, common underlying mechanisms (i.e. impulsivity, loss of control, obsession) suggest that CSOs may experience similar harms. In response to the significant distress caused by the harms associated with addiction, CSOs implement a range of coping strategies [30–32].

Coping strategies are defined as a set of cognitive and behavioral efforts implemented to manage, tolerate, avoid, or minimize the negative influence of stress on an individual’s physical and psychological well-being [33]. These strategies change over time depending on the impact they have had on changes in the environment causing the stress [33]. For a coping strategy to be considered effective, it must allow the individual to regain their initial balance by reducing the negative impact of stressful events (such as the loved one’s addiction) on their physical and psychological well-being [33]. According to Lazarus and Folkman [33], two general categories of strategies encompass the range of means individuals use to cope with a stressful situation. The first involves problem-focused strategies, meaning acting directly on the source of stress. The second includes emotion-focused strategies, such as avoidance, inner speech regulation, etc.

Orford and colleagues [34] propose the stress-strain-coping-support (SSCS) model, which is more specific to the issues of substance and gambling use. This model is based on the principle that coping strategies play a central role for CSOs affected by their loved one’s addiction [34, 35]. The typology proposed by the authors includes a first group of strategies with a goal similar to that postulated by Lazarus and Folkman, namely the so-called “engaged” strategies aimed at changing the loved one’s addiction by being assertive, controlling, or supportive toward them. The second group, called “tolerant-inactive”, includes strategies that partly fall into Lazarus and Folkman’s second category, such as accepting the addictive behaviors (in order to soothe oneself emotionally), but also encouraging the addictive behaviors. A third group of strategies are referred as “withdrawal” strategies, for example by engaging in activities independent of the loved one with addiction, again in attempt to find emotional relief.

Building on the experience of CSOs of individuals with gambling addiction, Côté et al. [36] revisit the model of Lazarus and Folkman: they provide a more detailed description of the different strategies used and expand the second category by including the improvement and protection of the well-being of the CSO, but also of the couple and the family. This includes emotion-focused strategies, but not exclusively (e.g., financially supporting the loved one). This conceptualization is consistent with a recent scoping review of coping strategies used by CSOs in the context of gambling, which similarly identifies two main goals of these strategies: influencing gambling behaviour and enhancing the wellbeing of CSOs and the family [32].

In a manner similar to Côté et al. [36], Booth et al. [37] distinguish between strategies that are oriented toward the family and those that are focused on the gambler (solving the problem). On the other hand, Le Poire et al. [38] use three categories of coping strategies in response to excessive substance use. The first is called “reinforcement of substance use”, which is very similar to the “tolerant-inactive” category proposed by Orford and colleagues [34], possibly aiming to protect individual and relational well-being by reinforcing excessive substance use. The other two are problem-focused: punishing the excessive substance use (e.g., threatening to leave the relationship or to call the police) or reinforcing alternative behaviors (e.g., organizing activities that do not involve alcohol or drugs, encouraging attendance at Alcoholics Anonymous meetings). Suonpaa [39] proposes four categories of strategies for controlling the stress related to a loved one’s alcohol use: discrete strategies carried out in the absence of the loved one (e.g., attending Al-Anon meetings); strategies aimed at making suggestions or inquiring about the loved one’s use habits (e.g., asking what they did with their friends, what and how much they drank); “reasonable character” strategies that allow the CSO to express their views on the loved one’s addiction (e.g., telling them to be careful with the punch); and finally, sanction strategies aimed at making the loved one understand the seriousness of the situation (e.g., refusing to lend money). Then, Kim-Godwin [40] adds to the classic categories of Lazarus and Folkman [33] (problem-focused vs. emotion-focused) strategies that are religion-centered (e.g., praying, reading the Bible) or focused on meaning-making to cope with substance use (e.g., keeping a gratitude journal), as well as another category involving avoidance or distraction, which closely resembles the third category, withdrawal, proposed by Orford et al. [34]. With a few subtle differences, a broad two-level categorization seems to hold up in understanding the purposes of the strategies used by CSOs.

On the other hand, very few studies have focused on the strategies used by CSOs of individuals with screen-related addiction [41, 42]. Yet, the prevalence of this issue is increasing [43, 44], and specialized addiction treatment centers are increasingly seeing CSOs of individuals with screen-related addiction seeking their services [45]. Moreover, this issue presents several characteristics typical of addictive behaviours [15], and CSOs of individuals affected by substance use, gambling, or screen-related addiction experience similar harms [1–7]. This highlights the need for a comprehensive update of the strategies used by all CSOs of individuals affected by substance use, gambling, or screen-related addictions.

A scoping review will make it possible to comprehensively identify qualitative and quantitative studies in order to answer the following question: What do we know about the coping strategies used by CSOs of adult loved one struggling with substance use, gambling, or screen-related addiction? A second goal of the study is to improve the previous mapping of coping strategies used by gambler’s CSO and developed by Côté et al. [36], to encompass substance and screen use problems. This is why the model will be used as an analytical framework to organize and map the coping strategies identified in the literature.

Method

The scoping review is a method of choice as it allows for a rapid mapping of the relevant literature in a field of interest and an emerging area of research, based on a rigorous process that ensures reproducibility [46]. The entire process was carried out in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR criteria, which guide a methodological and transparent extraction of data [47]. It involves five main steps proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute [48, 49]: (1) identification of research question; (2) identification of relevant studies; (3) selection of studies; (4) data extraction; (5) synthesis of the results from articles; and an optional final step, (6) expert consultation.

Identification of relevant studies

In July 2024, a systematic search was conducted in six databases: PsycINFO, Medline, Psychology and Behavioral Sciences Collection, SocIndex, Cinahl Complete and Health and Psychosocial Instruments. Study selection

The following three filters were directly applied in the databases to carry out an initial selection of studies: (a) peer-reviewed articles, regardless of study design; (b) written in French or English; and (c) published after 1990. Only primary research articles were included, while reviews, theses, conference abstracts, reports, and opinion pieces were excluded. For these reasons, 3,630 references were excluded. Subsequently, the titles and abstracts of the remaining 2,618 references were reviewed by KH under the supervision of JT, MC and NMB, in order to retain only the articles meeting the following inclusion criteria: (a) both the CSO and the loved one with addiction are 18 years of age or older; (b) these two individuals have a significant relationship (parents, spouses, siblings, children, close friends); (c) the loved one presents an addiction to substance, gambling, or screen (as reported by the CSO); and (d) the study focuses on describing the coping strategies used by the CSOs or on the development of a questionnaire addressing the coping strategies used by CSOs. This led to the inclusion of 48 studies. The reference review of each selected study [50] made it possible to add 16 additional articles. Data extraction and result synthesis

The research team first developed a standardized data extraction grid to systematically capture and describe key characteristics of each included study (e.g., country, study design, population) (see Additional File 1). In accordance with our research question, data related to coping strategies used by CSOs were subsequently classified based on the typology developed by Côté et al. [36] by KH under the supervision by JT, MC and NBM. A directed qualitative content analysis [51] was then conducted using this pre-existing conceptual model as a coding framework. Relevant information from the literature was coded according to the model’s categories, allowing for both confirmation and extension of the conceptual framework. The analysis was conducted using NVivo 12.

Expert consultation

This consultation step, initially described as optional by Arksey and O’Malley [46], but increasingly recommended in scoping review methodology [52], was undertaken in the present study to enhance the comprehensiveness and applicability of the findings. Two experts in interventions with CSOs (JT & MC) were involved throughout the process and provided additional coping strategies not identified in the literature but drawn from their clinical experience (identified in Table). This approach is consistent with recommendations to incorporate experiential knowledge in scoping reviews and contributed to strengthening the relevance of the results for clinical practice [53].

Results

Among the 64 articles, 41 (64%) focus on the experience of CSOs with a loved one presenting addiction to substance use, 17 (26%) refer to CSOs of a loved one using gambling, four (6%) concern CSOs of loved ones with both gambling and substance use, and two (3%) concern loved ones with screen-related difficulties. CSOs are exclusively romantic partners in 24 of the selected studies (37.5%). The other studies include varied samples composed of parents (45.1%), siblings (20.3%), adult children, or other CSOs. A total of 75% of the articles provide data on the gender of CSOs, among whom 82% of participants across the reviewed studies are women. The studies were conducted mainly in Europe (n = 25), Oceania (n = 24), and North America (n = 19), with a few conducted in South America (n = 2), Asia (n = 7), and Africa (n = 1). The total exceeds n = 64 because several studies were conducted across more than one country.

The first finding is that all the strategies identified in the literature align well with the two main purposes of the conceptual model by Lazarus and Folkman [33]. In addition, they are adequately classified within the various categories of coping strategies developed in the framework proposed by Côté and colleagues [36]. A few additions help enhance the model by Côté and colleagues [36], either by adding new objectives or by introducing new strategies under an existing objective, resulting in a total of 19 added strategies and three specific objectives. When applicable, the results further highlight potential distinctions in strategies depending on the type of harmful use (substance use, gambling, or screen-based activities). Notably, although only two studies addressed screen-related use, they identified strategies across five of the eight objectives (62.5%) targeting influence on use, and two of the six objectives (33%) related to increase the personal well-being of the partner, couple, or family.

In addition to this new objective supported by a few strategies, three other strategies were added, each inserted under an already existing objective in the classification by Côté et al. [36]. The review of various studies highlighted how CSOs may resort to physical or psychological violence (#7), such as belittling, insulting, threatening, attempting to cause physical harm, or even hitting, with the goal of convincing their loved one to reduce or stop their harmful use (Objective #2)) [21, 23, 28, 30, 39, 58, 61, 63–70]. This finding was mainly reported in studies involving CSOs of individuals with substance use. Another strategy focusing on the importance of letting the loved one experience the natural consequences of their harmful use of … (#12) was added under Objective #5, which is to avoid reinforcing harmful use of …. This finding was predominantly reported in studies examining CSOs of individuals with substance use. For example, some authors [35, 61, 62, 71] suggest that refusing to take care of the loved one when they are intoxicated is sometimes a strategy used by CSOs. Other authors [37], in studies focusing on gambling, propose that CSOs help their loved one identify their own high-risk situations for harmful use of … (#16), for instance by encouraging reflection on what triggers cravings. This strategy was therefore added under Objective #6, i.e., avoiding high-risk situations for the loved one.

Coping strategies aimed at increasing personal, couple, or family well-being

Overall, the strategies identified by various authors and aimed at improving individual, couple, or family well-being could be classified within the model proposed by Côté et al. [36]. However, two additional objectives were added to the second general purpose. The first new objective concerns efforts to care for the family (#12) [5, 22, 27, 38, 40, 58, 59, 64, 67, 69, 72–74], under which three new strategies were identified. First, several studies report that CSOs must take on more responsibilities to compensate for the shortcomings of their loved one (#35) [5, 22, 27, 38, 58, 67, 69, 73, 74]. Then, Kim-Godwin [40], Landon et al. [72] and Orford et al. [64] highlight that some CSOs choose to focus their energy on other family members rather than on the loved one (#36). Finally, expert clinicians emphasize how CSOs strive to ensure that family members suffer as little as possible from the loved one’s harmful use of … (#37), for example, by always giving their children toys as Christmas presents, even if they give second hand ones.

The second added objective concerns improving the quality of the relationship with the loved one (#14) [21, 23, 27, 28, 31, 57, 58, 69, 75]. Four strategies are included here, two of which were simply relocated (#41 and #43). Järvinen-Tassopoulos [76] reports that some partners are willing to trust again if their loved one demonstrates honesty about their urges or relapses, leading to the addition of this strategy (#42). Under the objective aimed at ensuring the financial security of the CSO (objective #12), one strategy was added, namely that CSOs will protect their assets (#38), by refusing to lend money to the loved one, hiding valuables, securing their personal finances, and even placing money in trust for their grandchildren [22, 27, 28, 39, 57, 61, 64, 65, 75, 82, 83, 90, 91].

Finally, under the objective of taking care of the loved one (objective #14), two strategies were added. The first is to avoid letting the loved one experience the natural consequences of their harmful use of … (#46), a finding primarily reported in studies focusing on substance use. More specifically, some CSOs take care of the loved one while they are intoxicated or assume responsibilities that would normally fall to the loved one, such as paying fines or drunk driving, calling their workplace to report their absence, or performing their tasks because they are in treatment [21, 23, 28, 35, 58, 61, 64, 65, 69, 74, 75, 78, 82, 88, 92]. Secondly, CSOs have to deal with crisis situations and sometimes even resort to police or judicial authorities (#47) [5, 21, 24, 28, 31, 37, 57, 60, 72, 78, 85, 93].

Discussion

The objective of the study was to identify the full range of coping strategies used by CSOs of individuals with substance use, gambling, or screen-related addiction and based on these results, to expand the previous model of coping strategies used by CSOs of gambler, to these in the substances or screen harmful use. Two main findings emerge from this study. First, the dual-process model as conceptualized by Folkman and Lazarus [33] and adapted by Côté and colleagues [34] appropriately applies to CSOs’ coping strategies in the context of substance use, gambling, and potentially screen-related addiction, although the latter remains less studied. The second finding is that variations appear to emerge depending on the nature of the relationship between the CSO and the loved one. For instance, the use of certain strategies is more frequent or specific depending on whether the CSO is a partner, a parent, or a friend. Considering these key findings, the present discussion will focus on the modifications made to the model proposed by Côté and colleagues [36], particularly the addition of three new objectives and the distinctions in strategy use according to the type of relationship.

The first added objective relates to the support provided by CSOs in response to changes made by their loved one concerning their harmful use of … (Objective #8), which falls under the first purpose of exerting influence on addiction. This objective highlights the important role CSOs can play in encouraging and supporting their loved one’s change process, thereby promoting the reduction or cessation of addictive behaviors [94–105]. CSOs often complement formal treatment and may act as agents of change in supporting recovery [106–109]. However, evidence suggest that CSOs may find this role more challenging in the context of addictions compared to other mental health conditions. Corrigan et al. [110] report that CSOs are more likely to attribute blame for addiction onset and relapse than in conditions such as schizophrenia. This may be partly explained by the perception that substance use, gambling, or screen-related addiction is voluntary [111–113], leading some CSOs to interpret a lack of change as insufficient commitment or motivation [114]. Conversely, people experiencing addiction emphasize the importance for CSOs of understanding the complex nature of addiction, which for many is a long-term condition marked by fluctuations and relapses [115–121]. Such understanding may enhance CSOs sensitivity to the recovery process and improve the support they provide [114, 121]. Finally, when CSOs maintain hope regarding their loved one’s progress, they are better able to adapt their strategies across stages of recovery [122]. Overall, this recognition of the active and evolving role of CSOs justifies the addition of objective #8 to the Côté et al. model [36].

Under the second purpose (increasing personal, couple, and family well-being), the addition of a second objective, namely, taking care of the family (objective #11), broadens the concept to include the family unit, particularly the well-being of the children. This inclusion reflects the broader harms associated by addiction, including family conflicts, increased risk of violence, and collateral effects observed in minor children [123]. Overall, the findings highlight that CSOs coping strategies are note only individual but also deeply relational and family-oriented, often leading to assume a central caregiving and protective role within the family system [25, 124–126]. In this context, CSOs may prioritize children’s well-being, sometimes at the expense of the couple relationship, and experience a redistribution of responsibilities within the household, particularly when the loved one does not change their harmful behaviours. This shift also reflects broader structural and gendered dynamics, with women often assuming disproportionate caregiving and compensatory roles within the family system [69]. Ultimately, these findings suggest that coping strategies extend beyond managing the addiction itself to include efforts aimed at maintaining minimal family functioning and protecting children, even in contexts of ongoing addiction.

Interpersonal relationships are often significantly affected when addiction is present [3, 22, 114, 127], and CSOs frequently engage in coping strategies aimed at restoring closeness and repairing relational bonds [93]. The inclusion of Objective #13 reflects these relational disruptions, which are often characterized by repeated breaches of trust (e.g., lying, concealment, denial), leading to feelings of betrayal and long-lasting impacts on relational security [30, 77, 128, 129]. As a result, CSOs may experience ongoing uncertainty and worry about relapse and trust, even when the situation appears stabilized [3]. It is thus not surprising that CSOs express the need to implement strategies to maintain or repair the relationship [31]. Overall, these findings highlight that coping strategies in this domain are fundamentally relational and aimed at repairing trust and rebuilding the relationship, rather than solely managing the addictive behaviour itself. This suggests that interventions targeting the relational dimension of addiction, including communication and differentiation between the person and the addiction, may be particularly relevant for supporting recovery processes [65, 98, 125, 130, 131].

The second finding of this scoping review is that coping strategies vary according to the nature of the relationship between CSOs and the loved one. These variations reflect differences in relational proximity and structural living conditions, including cohabitation, division of responsibilities, and financial interdependence [2, 4, 14, 69, 79, 132–135]. Overall, the findings suggest that coping strategies are shaped by the relational position of CSOs within the family system, with these contextual factors influencing their capacity to distance themselves, provide support, or maintain involvement. This supports the need for a more context-sensitive and relationally informed approach to understanding CSOs’ experiences in the context of addiction. For instance, romantic partners and parents may differ in their capacity to distance themselves, partly due to differing relational obligations and social expectations, such as continued financial support or norms surrounding family commitment [2, 124, 135].

Lastly, many authors agree that there is a significant number of similarities in the core set of coping strategies used by CSOs [133, 136–138], across different types of addictive behaviors. Orford [137] suggests that differences in the ability to cope with the situation, as well as in the strategies used, are associated with three aspects: (1) the heavier the burden represented by the addiction, the more difficult it is to cope with it; (2) the more the CSO occupies a family position that reinforces subordination (often linked to cultural components), the more difficult it is to cope; and (3) the more access the CSO has to high-quality social support, the easier it is to deal with their loved one’s addiction. These are the underlying assumptions of the stress–strain–coping–support (SSCS) model [34], which are posited as fundamental and universal for CSOs affected by addiction (of substance, gambling, or screen), in which all CSOs engage in coping strategies in an attempt to restore some balance.

Study outcomes and clinical implications

The main contribution of this study is the enhancement of a cross-addiction nomenclature of coping strategies used by CSOs of individuals with substance use, gambling, or screen-related addiction, offering useful insight for clinical interventions. This scoping review has confirmed, among other things, that the model proposed by Lazarus and Folkman [33] is generalizable to addictions (substance, gambling or screen), as suggested by Côté and colleagues [36]. This classification allows practitioners to better understand the range of coping strategies used by CSOs to deal with their loved one’s addiction. It serves as a useful clinical tool to initiate discussions with CSOs about the full range of behaviors they adopt in response to their loved one’s addiction. Engaging in such discussions helps raise CSOs awareness not only about the influence they may have on their loved one’s behavior but also about the impact on their own well-being and that of their family. Finally, exploring CSOs’ perceptions of the disorder (its origins, its link to motivation for change or lack thereof) is essential to ensuring a comprehensive understanding of their perspective, since coping strategies must be addressed in parallel with the CSOs’ attributions regarding their loved one’s addiction [58].

Limitations and future research

The present study has certain limitations, particularly regarding the study population. Indeed, the majority of the selected studies refer to a Western cultural context, suggesting that some coping strategies may differ in other cultures. Additionally, the samples are predominantly composed of female partners. Tremblay et al. [139] explain this phenomenon by suggesting that men tend to prefer solving their problems on their own, avoid discussing them, and report that their pride would be affected if they asked for help. Furthermore, Hurcom et al. (122) highlight that the coping strategies used by men differ from those used by women. Therefore, more studies focusing on the coping mechanisms of men whose loved one is struggling with a substance use issue would be relevant. This would allow for a better understanding of men’s experiences as CSOs [140].

It is interesting to comprehensively document strategies used by CSOs. However, the finding is that very few studies focus on their effectiveness [77]. Although we are aware of the intention behind the use of a coping strategy (to influence addiction or to protect one’s well-being), its specific effectiveness is poorly documented. Clinical experience leads to the recommendation of certain strategies and the avoidance of others, but the scientific foundations of these recommendations remain weak in the field of addictions [77].

To date, little literature exists on CSOs of adult with screen-related addiction. Although it may be reasonable to suggest that these CSOs use strategies similar to those of CSOs of individuals with substance use or gambling addiction, only two articles were identified to support this observation. It could be relevant to further explore the strategies used by CSOs.

Despite its limitations, this scoping study contributed to the advancement of knowledge on the coping strategies adopted by CSOs of adult loved ones with substance use, gambling, or screen-related addiction. It encourages practitioners to pay attention to these strategies in order to better tailor their interventions.
