Preoperative and perioperative predictors of prolonged hospital stay after uncomplicated elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ramazan Topcu, Turker Kaymak, Mahmut Arif Yüksek, Orhan Aslan, Elif Yiğit Kaymak, İsmail Sezikli, Kaan Canal, Aşkın Kadir Perçem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough laparoscopic cholecystectomy is considered the gold standard for benign gallbladder disease and is typically associated with short hospitalization, a subset of patients experience prolonged length of stay (LOS), increasing healthcare costs and resource utilization. Early identification of patients at risk for extended hospitalization may facilitate perioperative optimization and discharge planning. This study aimed to identify perioperative predictors of prolonged hospital stay following uncomplicated elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy and to evaluate the discriminative performance of a multivariable predictive model.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort study, 718 adult patients who underwent uncomplicated elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy between January 2024 and August 2025 were analyzed. Patients with conversion to open surgery, reoperation, or major postoperative complications were excluded from the primary analysis and evaluated separately as an Extended Cohort (n = 31). Prolonged hospital stay was defined as LOS > 2 days. Preoperative variables included demographic characteristics, ASA score, preoperative imaging findings, and prior biliary interventions, while operative duration and surgeon experience were assessed as intraoperative variables. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Model discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsOf the 718 patients, 263 (36.6%) experienced prolonged hospitalization. In multivariable analysis, increasing age (OR 1.02 per year, 95% CI 1.00–1.03, p = 0.009), higher ASA score (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.05–1.56, p = 0.015), longer operative duration (OR 1.03 per minute, 95% CI 1.02–1.04, p < 0.001), higher preoperative imaging score (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.09–1.43, p = 0.002), and prior ERCP (OR 2.45, 95% CI 1.24–4.84, p = 0.010) were independently associated with prolonged LOS. Surgeon experience was not significantly associated with hospital stay after adjustment. The multivariable model demonstrated good discriminative ability (AUC 0.76), outperforming operative duration alone (AUC 0.59) and the preoperative-only model (AUC 0.69).ConclusionProlonged hospital stay following uncomplicated laparoscopic cholecystectomy is primarily driven by patient characteristics and disease severity rather than surgeon experience. A structured perioperative risk model incorporating clinical, radiological, and intraoperative variables may improve discharge planning and optimize hospital resource utilization. The proposed model applies to uncomplicated elective procedures, whereas complication-related hospitalization represents a distinct clinical entity requiring separate predictive frameworks. Prospective multicenter validation is warranted.

Introduction

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is widely accepted as the gold standard surgical treatment for symptomatic cholelithiasis and other benign gallbladder diseases [1, 2]. Owing to its minimally invasive nature, it offers well-established benefits, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, faster recovery, and earlier return to daily activities [3, 4]. Despite these advantages, a substantial proportion of patients experience a longer-than-expected hospital stay in routine clinical practice, posing challenges for both patient outcomes and healthcare resource utilization.

Length of stay (LOS) is a key indicator of surgical quality and healthcare efficiency. Prolonged hospitalization has been associated with increased healthcare costs, higher risk of nosocomial complications, and reduced patient satisfaction. Identifying patients at risk for extended hospitalization in the preoperative period is therefore critical for optimizing perioperative care, improving discharge planning, and enhancing hospital workflow.

Previous studies have identified several factors associated with prolonged LOS after laparoscopic cholecystectomy, including advanced age, higher comorbidity burden, acute inflammatory findings, and indicators of technical difficulty. In particular, preoperative imaging may provide valuable insights into disease severity and operative complexity, thereby offering an opportunity to anticipate postoperative recovery trajectories. Additionally, prior interventions such as endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography or percutaneous cholecystostomy may reflect more complicated disease courses and potentially influence recovery [5–8].

However, existing literature largely focuses on isolated predictors, and comprehensive models integrating clinical, radiological, and procedural variables remain limited. Furthermore, the influence of surgeon experience on hospital stay remains incompletely understood, with conflicting evidence reported across studies [9–12]. Given that surgical services frequently operate near capacity, even modest increases in LOS can disrupt operating schedules, delay elective procedures, and strain hospital resources, underscoring the need for robust risk stratification strategies.

In this context, the present study aimed to identify preoperative and perioperative predictors of prolonged hospital stay following uncomplicated elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy using a large retrospective cohort and multivariable analysis. Patients with conversion to open surgery, reoperation, or major postoperative complications were excluded from the primary analysis, as these events prolong hospitalization through technical failure mechanisms distinct from patient-related factors. By focusing on uncomplicated cases, we aimed to improve risk stratification and discharge planning in routine clinical practice. Baseline characteristics of excluded patients were additionally analyzed as a separate Extended Cohort to contextualize the study population.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This single-center retrospective cohort study aimed to identify preoperative and selected perioperative predictors of prolonged hospital stay following uncomplicated elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Consecutive adult patients who underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy between January 2024 and August 2025 were identified from the electronic medical record system of Hitit University Erol Olçok Training and Research Hospital.

The primary analysis focused on patients without intraoperative conversion, reoperation, or major postoperative complications, as these events prolong hospitalization through mechanisms distinct from routine perioperative recovery. Patients meeting these exclusion criteria were additionally evaluated as a separate Extended Cohort to contextualize the primary study population and characterize more complex clinical scenarios.

A total of 718 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final primary analysis.

All data were handled confidentially and in compliance with institutional data protection regulations.

Preoperative imaging assessment and scoring

The preoperative imaging score was determined based on five predefined radiological features commonly associated with difficult laparoscopic cholecystectomy, assessed using ultrasonography, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography. The scoring framework was adapted from previously validated radiological and preoperative prediction models described by Siddiqui et al., Vivek et al., and Kumar et al. [13–15].

Each feature was scored as present (1) or absent (0), yielding a cumulative score ranging from 0 to 5, with higher scores reflecting greater inflammatory severity and anticipated operative difficulty.

Although the imaging score has been primarily developed to predict operative difficulty and adverse outcomes, in the present study it was incorporated as a surrogate marker of underlying disease severity and inflammatory burden. This approach allows the model to capture variations in preoperative disease complexity, even among patients without conversion or major postoperative complications.

Surgical technique

All laparoscopic cholecystectomies were performed using a standardized four-port technique. The critical view of safety (CVS) was achieved in all cases prior to division of the cystic duct and cystic artery, in accordance with the Strasberg–Berlin consensus.

Monopolar electrocautery was the primary modality for dissection, while ultrasonic energy devices were selectively used at the discretion of the operating surgeon. Intraoperative cholangiography was not routinely performed.

A silicone round perforated closed-suction drain was routinely placed in all patients at the completion of the procedure as part of the institutional protocol. Drain output was monitored postoperatively, and removal was typically performed on postoperative day 1, and in selected cases on postoperative day 2, when the output was serous and < 50 mL/24 hours.

Although the operative strategy was standardized, minor variations in port placement, traction technique, and energy device selection existed depending on surgeon preference.

Outcome measure

The primary outcome was prolonged hospital stay, defined as hospitalization exceeding two days following laparoscopic cholecystectomy. This binary outcome variable (LOS group) was used in both univariate and multivariate analyses.

Patients were stratified into two groups according to hospital length of stay: short stay (2 days) and prolonged stay (> 2 days). This classification reflects the institutional perioperative pathway, where patients are routinely admitted one day prior to surgery for preoperative preparation, undergo surgery on the following day, and are typically discharged on postoperative day one, resulting in a standard hospitalization duration of 2 days.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed through visual inspection and distribution analysis. Continuous data were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages.

Univariate and multivariate analysis

Univariate and multivariable analyses were performed to identify factors associated with prolonged hospital stay. Continuous variables were compared using the independent samples t-test, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Variables with a p value < 0.10 in univariate analysis, together with clinically relevant factors, were entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model to determine independent predictors of prolonged hospitalization. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model fit and discriminative ability were assessed using pseudo R² statistics and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

To further evaluate the robustness of the findings, additional analyses were performed: In addition, a preoperative-only model was constructed as a sensitivity analysis, including age, ASA score, prior ERCP, history of biliary pancreatitis, and preoperative imaging score, to specifically evaluate the predictive performance of variables available before surgery.

ROC analysis

The discriminative ability of operative time alone and the multivariable model was assessed using ROC curves, with the area under the curve (AUC) used as the primary measure of predictive accuracy.

Threshold analysis

The optimal predicted probability cutoff for prolonged hospitalization was determined using the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated at clinically relevant thresholds.

Subgroup analysis

Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of associations between operative time and prolonged LOS across surgeon experience categories (< 10 years, 10–19 years, ≥ 20 years).

Most patients were classified as ASA II (61.8%), followed by ASA III (26.2%), ASA I (10.3%), and ASA IV (1.7%). The mean preoperative imaging score was 0.8 ± 1.0, with 55.0% of patients scoring 0 and 24.5% scoring 1; only 9.0% had scores of 3 or higher. Regarding surgeon experience, 44.2% of procedures were performed by surgeons with less than 10 years of experience, 22.2% by surgeons with 10–20 years, and 33.4% by those with more than 20 years.

To contextualize the study exclusions, baseline characteristics of the Extended Cohort (n = 31) were compared with the primary study population (Table 1). Patients in the Extended Cohort demonstrated significantly higher preoperative imaging scores (3.13 ± 1.48 vs. 0.8 ± 1.0, p < 0.001), longer operative times (107.6 ± 27.9 vs. 73.8 ± 20.3 min, p < 0.001), and higher rates of prior ERCP (25.8% vs. 5.0%, p < 0.001) and cholecystostomy (12.9% vs. 2.9%, p = 0.015). The distribution of imaging scores differed markedly between cohorts: while 79.5% of the primary cohort scored 0–1, only 16.2% of the Extended Cohort fell within this range, and 45.2% of excluded patients had scores of 4–5 compared with just 2.0% in the primary cohort. Notably, all patients in the Extended Cohort experienced prolonged hospitalization (mean LOS 5.6 ± 2.4 days vs. 2.4 ± 1.1 days, p < 0.001).

Histopathological examination in the primary cohort most commonly demonstrated chronic cholecystitis with cholelithiasis (97.1%), while xanthogranulomatous cholecystitis (1.3%), gallbladder carcinoma (0.8%), and chronic cholecystitis with polyp (0.7%) were less frequent. In contrast, the Extended Cohort showed a substantially higher proportion of xanthogranulomatous cholecystitis (19.4% vs. 1.3%, p < 0.001), reflecting greater inflammatory severity among excluded patients.

The distribution of complications in the Extended Cohort is presented in Table 2. Conversion to open surgery was the most common complication (38.7%), followed by bile leak (32.3%), postoperative bleeding (16.1%), and reoperation (12.9%). Notably, mean preoperative imaging scores were consistently high across all complication subgroups (range, 2.75–3.70). The highest mean imaging score was observed in patients with bile leak (3.70 ± 1.16), whereas patients requiring reoperation had the longest mean hospital stay (8.25 ± 2.50 days). These findings indicate that the excluded patients constituted a distinct high-complexity subgroup characterized by greater disease severity and operative difficulty. Furthermore, the elevated preoperative imaging scores across complication categories suggest that the imaging-based scoring system was able to reflect surgical complexity even among patients with adverse postoperative outcomes.

Patients with prolonged hospitalization were older and had higher perioperative risk profiles, as reflected by significantly greater age (53.1 ± 14.6 vs. 49.7 ± 13.6 years, p = 0.002) and higher ASA scores (2.28 ± 0.67 vs. 2.14 ± 0.60, p = 0.004). Operative duration was significantly longer in the prolonged stay group (80.1 ± 25.0 vs. 70.1 ± 15.9 min, p < 0.001), and preoperative imaging scores were also higher (0.95 ± 1.10 vs. 0.66 ± 0.99, p < 0.001), suggesting increased disease complexity. Among preoperative interventions, both ERCP and percutaneous cholecystostomy were more frequently performed in patients with prolonged hospitalization (8.7% vs. 2.9%, p = 0.001; and 4.9% vs. 1.8%, p = 0.027, respectively), indicating that these interventions may represent markers of a more complicated clinical course. In contrast, sex, history of biliary pancreatitis, and surgeon experience were not significantly associated with hospital stay duration. Overall, these findings identify age, operative duration, imaging severity, and prior biliary interventions as important factors associated with prolonged hospitalization (Table 4).

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, increasing age remained independently associated with prolonged hospital stay (OR 1.02 per year, 95% CI 1.00–1.03, p = 0.009). Higher ASA score was also an independent predictor (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.05–1.56, p = 0.015). Longer operative duration remained strongly associated with prolonged hospitalization (OR 1.03 per minute, 95% CI 1.02–1.04, p < 0.001), while higher preoperative imaging score independently predicted prolonged stay (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.09–1.43, p = 0.002). Prior ERCP was associated with more than twofold higher odds of prolonged hospitalization (OR 2.45, 95% CI 1.24–4.84, p = 0.010). Conversely, sex, biliary pancreatitis, cholecystostomy, and surgeon experience were not independently associated with length of stay after adjustment for confounding variables. Multivariable logistic regression was not performed in the Extended Cohort, as all 31 patients (100%) experienced prolonged hospitalization. Detailed regression results are summarized in Table 5.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy, we identified several independent preoperative and perioperative determinants of prolonged hospital stay, including increasing age, higher ASA score, longer operative duration, a higher preoperative imaging score, and a history of prior ERCP. Importantly, after adjustment for potential confounders, surgeon experience was not independently associated with length of stay, suggesting that patient-related factors and disease severity play a more prominent role than operator seniority in determining postoperative recovery.

An important methodological consideration in this study is the deliberate exclusion of patients with conversion to open surgery, reoperation, or major postoperative complications. Although these events are well-established determinants of prolonged length of stay (LOS), our objective was to identify predictors of prolonged hospitalization in the absence of major technical failure, thereby isolating the effects of patient characteristics, disease severity, and perioperative factors from the dominant influence of overt complications.

This distinction is clinically relevant because prolonged LOS after uncomplicated laparoscopic cholecystectomy likely reflects mechanisms different from complication-driven hospitalization, including delayed recovery, persistent inflammatory response, and reduced physiological reserve. In contrast, patients with conversion, bile leak, or reoperation generally require prolonged hospitalization as a direct consequence of these events, making extended postoperative care clinically apparent early in the perioperative course.

Comparison with the Extended Cohort further supports this approach. Patients with major complications demonstrated markedly higher preoperative imaging scores (mean range 2.75–3.70) than those in the primary cohort (mean 0.8 ± 1.0), with bile leak associated with the highest imaging score (3.70 ± 1.16). Furthermore, all excluded patients experienced prolonged LOS, underscoring the deterministic relationship between major complications and hospitalization duration. These findings suggest that the preoperative imaging score, originally developed to predict operative difficulty, may also reflect underlying disease severity and risk of major complications.

In the primary cohort, where high imaging scores were relatively uncommon, prolonged LOS likely reflected subclinical inflammatory burden and moderate technical complexity insufficient to result in overt complications, yet substantial enough to delay recovery. Accordingly, exclusion of patients with major complications enabled evaluation of factors associated with delayed recovery following technically successful surgery.

The proposed model should be regarded as perioperative rather than purely preoperative. Although preoperative variables formed the basis of risk stratification, inclusion of operative duration improved predictive performance and more closely reflected real-world discharge planning.

Nevertheless, this study design limits generalizability to the full spectrum of laparoscopic cholecystectomy, particularly complex cases with severe inflammation or unexpected intraoperative difficulty. Future studies integrating intraoperative difficulty grading systems and postoperative complications are needed to develop more comprehensive predictive models.

These findings emphasize the multifactorial nature of perioperative outcomes in minimally invasive biliary surgery and support the value of individualized perioperative risk stratification for improving patient counseling and resource planning.

Our observations regarding age and ASA score are consistent with previous literature demonstrating that older patients and those with a greater burden of comorbidities are more likely to experience delayed recovery and prolonged hospitalization [16–18]. Advanced age is commonly associated with reduced physiological reserve, a higher prevalence of chronic conditions, and slower functional recovery, all of which may contribute to extended postoperative monitoring and delayed discharge. Similarly, a higher ASA classification reflects systemic comorbidity and has repeatedly been linked to increased perioperative risk and resource utilization [8].

In our cohort, operative duration emerged as one of the strongest predictors of prolonged length of stay. Longer operative times likely reflect increased technical difficulty, more severe inflammation, or challenging anatomy, which may result in greater tissue manipulation, increased postoperative pain, and the need for closer postoperative observation. This finding supports the notion that operative complexity, even in minimally invasive surgery, serves as a surrogate marker for the postoperative recovery trajectory [19–21].

We also observed that a higher preoperative imaging score was independently associated with prolonged length of stay [22–24]. Although this scoring system has traditionally been used to predict operative difficulty and adverse outcomes, in the present study it was interpreted as a surrogate marker of underlying disease severity and inflammatory burden rather than a direct predictor of complications. This interpretation is supported by the Extended Cohort analysis (Table 2), in which patients with postoperative complications demonstrated markedly higher imaging scores (mean 3.13 ± 1.48) compared with the primary cohort (mean 0.8 ± 1.0). Notably, bile leak was associated with the highest imaging score (3.70 ± 1.16), reinforcing the concept that the score reflects a continuum of disease complexity ranging from subclinical inflammation to overt technical difficulty associated with conversion and major complications.

Within this framework, even in patients without conversion or major postoperative complications, higher imaging scores likely indicate a greater degree of underlying inflammation and more technically demanding dissection. This may result in increased tissue trauma, a more pronounced postoperative inflammatory response, and delayed functional recovery, ultimately contributing to prolonged hospitalization. Therefore, beyond its conventional role in predicting operative difficulty, the preoperative imaging score provides clinically meaningful information that captures disease complexity and independently influences postoperative recovery trajectories.

The independent association between prior endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and prolonged hospital stay is also in line with reports suggesting that patients requiring preoperative biliary intervention represent a subgroup with more complex disease [22, 25, 26]. ERCP may indicate the presence of choledocholithiasis, biliary obstruction, or previous inflammatory episodes, all of which can influence perioperative management and recovery. Recognizing this relationship may help clinicians anticipate the need for closer monitoring and tailored discharge planning.

Interestingly, after adjustment for other variables, surgeon experience was not significantly associated with length of hospital stay. Although more experienced surgeons showed a modest trend toward shorter hospitalization, this association did not remain significant in multivariable analysis. It should be noted that surgeon experience was categorized according to years in practice rather than annual procedural volume—a potentially important distinction, particularly given that laparoscopic cholecystectomy has a relatively short learning curve. Therefore, our analysis may have been underpowered to detect subtle differences in operative efficiency that could influence length of stay.

These findings otherwise suggest that standardized surgical techniques, institutional protocols, and established perioperative care pathways may mitigate variability related to surgeon seniority, and that patient-related factors and disease severity may play a more dominant role than surgeon-related variables in determining short-term recovery following laparoscopic cholecystectomy [6, 9, 27].

In our study, the multivariable model demonstrated good discriminative ability in predicting prolonged hospital stay (AUC 0.76) and showed clear superiority over the model based on operative duration alone (AUC 0.59). Notably, the preoperative-only model showed acceptable but lower discriminative performance compared with the full model.

This highlights that while preoperative factors are valuable for early risk stratification, perioperative variables such as operative duration provide additional predictive value, particularly in the context of discharge planning. Prolonged hospitalization cannot be adequately explained by a single intraoperative parameter; rather, a combined assessment of patient characteristics, disease severity, and perioperative factors provides a more reliable and clinically meaningful risk estimate. Integrating clinical, radiological, and procedural variables offers stronger predictive performance than relying solely on operative time. These findings support the added predictive value of combining clinical, radiological, and perioperative variables for risk stratification and discharge planning following laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Consistent with the literature, multivariable risk models have been shown to predict prolonged hospitalization more accurately than univariate approaches. The combined evaluation of factors such as ASA score, comorbidities, and surgical difficulty improves predictive performance, and in recent years, machine learning–based models have achieved even greater discriminative ability. Our findings support the importance of multifactorial preoperative assessment for clinical decision-making, bed management, and individualized care planning [28–30].

The clinical implications of our findings are noteworthy. Early identification of patients at risk for prolonged hospitalization may enable the implementation of targeted strategies such as enhanced recovery protocols, closer postoperative monitoring, proactive pain management, and more structured discharge planning. In resource-constrained healthcare systems, improving the prediction of hospital length of stay may contribute to more efficient utilization of surgical services and reduction of unnecessary bed occupancy.

From a practical standpoint, our results have several implications for perioperative care and hospital workflow. Early recognition of patients at risk for extended hospitalization may facilitate preoperative coordination of physiotherapy, pain management, nutritional optimization, and social support services, thereby enabling more effective discharge planning and minimizing delays between medical readiness and actual discharge. Risk stratification using variables such as ASA classification, prior ERCP, imaging severity, and operative duration may also support perioperative triage, allowing low-risk patients to be directed toward enhanced recovery pathways or short-stay protocols, while higher-risk individuals may benefit from closer postoperative observation or planned monitoring. Patients with lower ASA scores, no history of ERCP, favorable imaging findings, and shorter operative times may be suitable candidates for early discharge strategies, which are associated with improved patient satisfaction and reduced healthcare costs. Conversely, patients with greater comorbidity burden or indicators of disease complexity may require extended observation due to increased susceptibility to postoperative pain, delayed recovery, or biliary-related events. Incorporating these predictive factors into routine clinical decision-making may improve bed utilization, enhance patient safety, and optimize resource allocation without compromising quality of care.

Finally, several emerging technologies may complement the predictive framework described herein. Artificial intelligence (AI)–based guidance systems may enhance surgical safety in laparoscopic cholecystectomy by supporting real-time anatomical landmark recognition and intraoperative decision-making [31]. However, current evidence remains limited, particularly in complex cases with severe inflammation or adhesions. Complementary approaches such as indocyanine green fluorescence imaging may further assist intraoperative navigation. The overall clinical impact of these technologies warrants further investigation.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective single-center design may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability to other settings with different perioperative practices. Although multiple clinical and radiological variables were included, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as body mass index, detailed cardiovascular comorbidities, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes mellitus, smoking status, discharge practices, social support, and postoperative care cannot be excluded. The definition of prolonged hospitalization (> 2 days) was based on institutional protocols and may not be universally applicable. Operative duration was used as a surrogate marker of surgical difficulty, while the imaging score may be subject to interobserver variability. In addition, standardized intraoperative difficulty grading systems (e.g., Parkland scale) were not routinely available, and therefore important intraoperative factors such as dense adhesions or distorted anatomy could not be fully captured. Surgeon experience was classified according to years of practice rather than procedural volume, which may not precisely reflect technical proficiency. Routine drain usage and standardized discharge practices may also have influenced length of stay as system-level confounders. The predominance of low imaging scores (0–2 in 90% of patients) limits applicability to more complex cases such as severe acute cholecystitis, and 30-day readmission outcomes were not assessed. External validation of the predictive model was not performed.

The most important limitation is the exclusion of patients with conversion to open surgery, reoperation, bile leak, surgical site infection, and other major complications. Although this approach was necessary to minimize confounding from technical failure-related outcomes, it restricts the applicability of the model to uncomplicated elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. The Extended Cohort analysis (Tables 1 and 2) demonstrated that excluded patients had substantially higher disease complexity, reflected by higher imaging scores (3.13 ± 1.48 vs. 0.8 ± 1.0), and included cases of conversion (38.7%), bile leak (32.3%), postoperative bleeding (16.1%), and reoperation (12.9%). All excluded patients experienced prolonged hospitalization (mean 5.6 ± 2.4 days), with reoperation associated with the longest stay (8.25 days) and bile leak with the highest imaging score (3.70). These findings indicate that complication-driven hospitalization represents a fundamentally different clinical trajectory from uncomplicated cases, and should be evaluated using separate predictive frameworks.

Despite these limitations, the study benefits from a relatively large cohort, comprehensive assessment of clinical and radiological variables, and robust statistical analysis, providing meaningful insights into predictors of prolonged hospitalization after laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, prolonged hospital stay after elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy is primarily influenced by patient-related factors and underlying disease complexity rather than surgeon experience. Increasing age, higher ASA score, prior ERCP, longer operative duration, and higher preoperative imaging scores were independently associated with prolonged hospitalization.

These findings suggest that postoperative recovery is shaped by a multifactorial interaction between baseline patient characteristics, inflammatory disease burden, and perioperative factors. In particular, the preoperative imaging score may reflect a continuum of disease complexity that extends beyond its traditional role in predicting operative difficulty and contributes meaningfully to risk stratification of postoperative recovery. Identification of these factors may enhance perioperative risk assessment, support individualized discharge planning, and improve allocation of hospital resources. Further prospective studies are required to validate these findings and to develop comprehensive predictive models incorporating both preoperative and intraoperative variables.
