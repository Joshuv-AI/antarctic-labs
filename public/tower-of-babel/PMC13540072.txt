Screening the optimal parameters for Er: YAG laser debonding of lithium disilicate veneers: an in vitro study
Authors: Xiao Chen, Siqian Wang, Yan Wang, Anni Wu, Ni Dai, Yifan Bai, Yifan Li, Yanjing Zhang, Yuanjie Shao, Yuyi Zhang, Haisheng Lin, Zhennan Deng, Jingsong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStatement of the problemAlthough the efficacy of Er: YAG lasers for debonding all-ceramic restorations has been validated, further research is required to optimize the operational parameters for Er: YAG laser debonding of lithium disilicate veneers and to determine whether laser debonding alters the translucency, hardness, and rebonding strength of lithium disilicate veneers.PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the comprehensive debonding performance of an Er: YAG laser operating at a clinically prevalent power of 4.5 W with three different energy and frequency parameter combinations. The evaluation was performed on IPS e.max CAD lithium disilicate ceramic specimens with two thicknesses (0.5 mm and 1.0 mm), focusing on debonding time and intrapulpal temperature variation. Furthermore, the changes in ceramic rebonding strength, surface morphology, translucency, and Vickers hardness following laser debonding were analyzed, so as to identify the optimal parameters for clinical laser debonding of ceramic veneers.Materials and methodsForty freshly extracted maxillary first premolars were prepared to achieve standardized uniform enamel surfaces. CAD/CAM ceramic blocks were fabricated into two sizes of square specimens: 3 × 3 mm2 square specimens for the assessment of debonding efficiency and rebonding strength, and 10 × 10 mm2 square specimens for translucency and Vickers hardness tests. All specimens were categorized into two groups based on ceramic thickness (0.5 and 1.0 mm). Each thickness group was further subdivided into four subgroups with distinct laser parameters: a non-irradiated control group (E0), 300 mJ/15 Hz (E1), 225 mJ/20 Hz (E2), and 150 mJ/30 Hz (E3). The 3 × 3 mm2 square specimens were bonded to prepared tooth surfaces using Variolink N resin cement and subsequently debonded with an Er: YAG laser according to the corresponding subgroup parameters. After debonding, the specimens were rebonded to the tooth surfaces using the same resin cement. Debonding time and intrapulpal temperature change (∆T) were recorded throughout the procedure. The fracture modes of the veneer bonding interfaces were observed under a stereomicroscope, and shear rebonding strength was measured via a universal testing machine. The 10 × 10 mm2 ceramic specimens were irradiated with the corresponding Er: YAG laser parameters for 30 s. Post-treatment evaluations included observations of surface morphology and quantitative measurements of translucency and Vickers hardness. A two-way analysis of variance was adopted for statistical analysis. The Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated to analyze the correlation between debonding time and intrapulpal temperature variation.ResultsFor specimens with the same thickness, the E3 group exhibited significantly longer debonding time and greater intrapulpal temperature elevation compared with the E1 and E2 groups, whereas no significant differences were detected between the E1 and E2 groups. For the same laser parameters, 1.0 mm-thick veneers showed significantly longer debonding time and higher temperature variation than 0.5 mm-thick veneers. A strong positive correlation was identified between debonding time and ∆T (R² = 0.739). The main failure mode of the adhesive surface was classified as type 1. No significant differences in rebonding strength were observed after debonding. In addition, laser irradiation exerted no remarkable influence on the surface morphology, translucency, or Vickers hardness of lithium disilicate ceramic specimens.ConclusionsAt a constant laser power of 4.5 W, both E1 and E2 parameter settings are applicable for the debonding of 1.0 mm-thick lithium disilicate veneers, while the E2 setting is optimal for 0.5 mm-thick veneers. Er: YAG laser debonding treatment has no significant adverse effect on the rebonding strength, surface morphology, translucency, or hardness of lithium disilicate ceramics.Clinical implicationsThe outcomes of Er: YAG laser debonding for all-ceramic veneers are affected by laser energy, frequency, and veneer thickness. Optimized Er: YAG laser parameters should be selected according to the thickness of all-ceramic veneers. Given the preservation of mechanical strength and translucency after laser debonding, the treated all-ceramic veneers are reusable for clinical application.Keywords: Laser debonding, Er: YAG laser, Lithium disilicate ceramics, Shear bond strength, Translucence, Vickers hardness

Introduction

Public demand for dental aesthetics has grown substantially in recent years. Partial anterior tooth loss, tetracycline tooth staining, scattered anterior interdental gaps, and irregular tooth morphology are common factors compromising dental aesthetics [1, 2]. Among diverse clinical therapeutic options for such defects, all-ceramic veneer restoration has emerged as a reliable treatment modality [3]. Lithium disilicate glass ceramics are currently the most widely used high-strength glass ceramic materials, attributed to their superior biocompatibility, favorable mechanical properties, and excellent aesthetic performance [4].

Multiple adverse factors can shorten the service life of porcelain veneers, including bonding failure, restoration discoloration, microleakage, secondary caries, periodontal disease, and porcelain chipping, which often necessitate veneer removal and replacement [5–7]. Owing to the high adhesive bonding strength between porcelain veneers and tooth surfaces, the removal of aged or defective veneers remains technically challenging with conventional clinical instruments [8]. Clinically, the predominant method for all-ceramic veneer removal relies on high-speed dental handpieces equipped with dental burs, frequently combined with crown removers, which belongs to a destructive removal approach [9]. Given the color similarity among all-ceramic veneers, resin cement, and natural dental tissues, visual differentiation during operation is difficult, inevitably leading to inadvertent enamel damage [10]. Furthermore, the loud noise generated by high-speed handpieces during destructive removal procedures significantly increases patient discomfort and anxiety [11].

Laser technology has provided an innovative alternative for the clinical removal of ceramic restorations [12]. Different types of lasers, including Nd: YAG, Er: YAG, CO, Tm: YAP, diode, and yttrium fiber lasers, have been proven effective for degrading the adhesive layer of ceramic restorations [12]. Existing studies have confirmed that Er: YAG and Er, Cr: YSGG lasers exhibit superior debonding efficiency compared with other laser systems [12–15]. The Er: YAG laser is a hydrodynamic biological laser system with a wavelength of 2940 nm, which precisely matches the highest absorption peak of water [16, 17]. Various debonding mechanisms allow Er: YAG laser energy to degrade adhesive resin cement through thermal ablation, thermal softening, or photoablation [18], which may be accompanied by an increase in temperature. Zach et al. stated that an intrapulpal temperature increase exceeding 5.5 °C may trigger dental pulp cell necrosis [19]. Accordingly, a 5.5 °C temperature rise is widely recognized as the safe threshold for pulpal health. Numerous studies have verified that laser debonding performed at appropriate power levels maintains intrapulpal temperature elevation within a safe physiological range [20–24].

Laser debonding efficacy is governed by multiple variables, including laser parameters (power, frequency, energy, pulse duration), ceramic type, and ceramic thickness [25–30]. Previous studies on the laser removal of orthodontic ceramic brackets, all-ceramic crowns, and veneers have predominantly adopted laser power ranging from 4 to 5.5 W, which is considered safe and efficient for clinical debonding procedures [17]. Despite inconsistent parameter settings across relevant studies, 4.5 W has been the most commonly applied power level [22, 31–34]. To the best of our knowledge, there is a lack of literature on the influence of changes in laser energy and frequency on the debonding effect under fixed power conditions. Therefore, further experimental investigation is warranted to clarify the effects of different energy and frequency combinations on debonding time and intrapulpal temperature changes at this fixed power setting.

For veneers with incomplete seating, minor aesthetic flaws, or slight morphological defects during initial bonding, non-destructive complete removal enables the reuse of original restorations. This approach can effectively reduce patients’ treatment duration and economic burden. However, only a limited number of studies have evaluated the changes in mechanical properties, aesthetic characteristics, and rebonding strength of laser-removed ceramic restorations. Zhang et al. analyzed the surface morphology, mechanical strength, and color stability of ceramics after laser removal at different power outputs [35]. Their results indicated that Er: YAG laser debonding exerted no adverse effects on the mechanical properties or surface morphology of lithium disilicate ceramics, whereas laser irradiation at 5 W significantly altered ceramic translucency. Several other studies reported no significant changes in the rebonding strength of ceramics after Er: YAG laser debonding [36]. In contrast, Abdel Sadek HM et al. found a significant reduction in ceramic rebonding strength following Er, Cr: YSGG laser treatment [27]. These conflicting findings highlight the need for further systematic investigation into the variations in material properties and rebonding performance after laser debonding, which is critical for evaluating the clinical reusability of laser-removed ceramic veneers.

This study aimed to optimize the energy and frequency parameters of a 4.5 W Er: YAG laser for debonding lithium disilicate ceramic veneers of two common clinical thicknesses. The secondary objective was to evaluate the alterations in rebonding strength, surface morphology, translucency, and Vickers hardness of ceramics after treatment with three different laser parameter combinations. The first null hypothesis was that no significant differences in debonding time or intrapulpal temperature elevation would exist between the two ceramic thicknesses under the three laser parameter settings. The second null hypothesis was that Er: YAG laser irradiation would not induce significant changes in the rebonding strength, surface morphology, translucency, or Vickers hardness of lithium disilicate ceramics.

Materials and methods

Tooth selection and preparation

Forty healthy maxillary premolars freshly extracted for orthodontic treatment were collected [Ethics committee approval number: WYKQ2022036] [27]. All of the teeth were examined under a stereomicroscope (Bilang, Shanghai, China, SMZ800N) at 8× magnification to ensure that they were free of caries and cracks. A low-speed diamond cutting machine (Kejing, Shenyang, China, STX 202AQ) was used to remove the teeth roots, and the buccal surfaces were prepared to expose the enamel layer [20]. The specimens were then polished with silicon carbide sandpaper (Blue Dragon, Korea, CW 400, 800, 1200). All processed tooth specimens were stored in distilled water and incubated in a thermostatic water bath (JOANLAB, Zhejiang, China, WB100-1) at 37 °C for no longer than 2 weeks [11].

Specimen fabrication and grouping

Lithium disilicate ceramic blocks (Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein, IPS e.max CAD HT A2 C14) were fabricated into two types of square specimens with different dimensions: 3 × 3 mm2 (n = 40) and 10 × 10 mm2 (n = 40). The 3 × 3 mm2 square specimens were designed to fit the morphological characteristics of prepared tooth surfaces and were bonded to enamel substrates for the assessment of debonding time, intrapulpal temperature variation, and rebonding strength. The 10 × 10 mm2 square specimens were fabricated to adapt to testing equipment and were utilized to evaluate alterations in surface morphology, translucency, and Vickers hardness following 30 s of laser irradiation with different parameter settings.

Both sizes of specimens adopted an identical grouping strategy. All specimens were first categorized into two groups according to ceramic thickness, including 0.5 mm-thick (n = 20) and 1.0 mm-thick (n = 20) groups. Each thickness group was further randomly subdivided into four subgroups (n = 5) with varied laser parameters: 0.5E0 (non-irradiated control), 0.5E1 (300 mJ/15 Hz), 0.5E2 (225 mJ/20 Hz), 0.5E3 (150 mJ/30 Hz), 1.0E0 (non-irradiated control), 1.0E1 (300 mJ/15 Hz), 1.0E2 (225 mJ/20 Hz), and 1.0E3 (150 mJ/30 Hz). All ceramic specimens underwent secondary crystallization sintering at 850 °C in a porcelain furnace (Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein, Programat P310).

Specimen bonding

Ceramic specimens (3 × 3 mm2) were etched with 9.5% hydrofluoric acid (Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein, Variolink N) for 20 s, thoroughly rinsed with running water for 60 s to eliminate residual etchant, and then dried with oil-free air. Silane (Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein, Variolink N) was applied to the ceramic surfaces for 60 s. A 37% phosphoric acid gel etchant (Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein, Variolink N) was evenly applied to the tooth surface for 20–30 s, then rinsed with water and dried with oil-free air. The appearance of a chalky enamel surface was regarded as a sign of successful etching.

An adhesive (Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein, Variolink N) was applied to the etched tooth surfaces and gently scrubbed for 15 s, air-dried for 5 s, and light-cured (3 M, Minnesota, USA, Elipar S10) for 10 s. Resin cement (Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein, Variolink N) was used to bond the ceramic specimens of each group to the prepared teeth. Gentle pressure was applied to ensure proper seating of the ceramic specimens. Light curing was performed for 3 s to achieve a semi-gel state of the luting cement, facilitating the removal of excess cement [27]. An oxygen-blocking agent (Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein, Variolink N) was then applied to the edges of the ceramic specimens, and the resin cement was fully cured under a light-curing unit for 20 s.

After bonding, the ceramic specimens were stored in distilled water and maintained in a constant-temperature water bath at 37 °C (JOANLAB, Zhejiang, China, WB100-1) for one week to simulate the oral aqueous environment and stabilize the bonding interface properties of light-cured resin cement [38, 39].

Laser debonding procedure

Specimens in each subgroup were debonded using an Er: YAG laser system (Fotona, Beijing, China, MO21-5AF/1) with parameter settings listed in Table 1. The laser scanned the ceramic surfaces vertically in an S-shaped pattern until the ceramic specimens detached, ensuring homogeneous irradiation across the entire bonded area.

Debonding time recording

A stopwatch timer (Yisheng, Guangdong, China, YS-810) was used to record the total debonding duration, defined as the time from the initiation of laser irradiation to complete specimen detachment.

Fracture mode evaluation

The bonding surfaces of the specimens and teeth were observed under a stereomicroscope (Bilang, Shanghai, China, SMZ 800 N) at 8× magnification to classify fracture modes, which were categorized into three types based on established criteria [40]. Type 1 referred to adhesive failure between ceramic and resin cement, with residual resin on the tooth surface accounting for more than two-thirds. Type 2 represented adhesive failure between resin cement and tooth enamel, with residual resin on the ceramic bonding surface accounting for more than two-thirds. Type 3 indicated cohesive failure within the resin cement layer, with residual resin distributed nearly equally on both tooth and ceramic surfaces.

Specimen rebonding

After debonding, diamond polishing burs (MANI, Beijing, China, TR-25EF) were used to remove resin cement residues from the tooth surfaces. The debonded ceramic surfaces were cleaned via 50 μm alumina sandblasting at 1 bar (15 psi) pressure for 20 s to eliminate residual cement [27]. All treated ceramics were rebonded to the corresponding teeth following the identical bonding protocol described above.

Shear bond strength (SBS) test

An SBS test was performed on the ceramic specimens (3 × 3 mm2) using a universal testing machine (Instron, Shanghai, China, Model 4465). Specimens were fixed on a customized fixture and aligned to ensure that the loading head was vertically parallel to the bonding interface and closely contacted the ceramic margin. Loading was applied at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. The maximum shear force (F) during the shearing process was recorded.

Translucency parameter (TP) measurement

A spectrophotometer (Sanenshi, Guangdong, China, TS7700) with a 4-mm aperture probe was fully calibrated prior to measurement. Based on the CIE system, translucency parameter was measured and described using three values: L* represented lightness, a* represented red to green, and b* represented blue to yellow. Ceramic specimens (10 × 10 mm2) from each subgroup were measured on two different background substrates, black and white. Surface morphological observation

Specimens of each subgroup (10 × 10 mm2) were examined using a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan, SU8010) at a magnification of ×5000 to assess surface morphological alterations after laser treatment.

Vickers hardness test

Vickers hardness tests were performed with a force of 9.8 N applied for 15 s using a microhardness tester (Zhongyan, Shanghai, China, HVST 1000 C). Each specimen (10 × 10 mm2) was positioned at the center of the measurement table of the digital Vickers hardness tester, and a fully automatic hardness analysis system was used to perform measurements at two locations for each specimen, one at the center and one at the surrounding area. The average value of the two measurements was defined as the final Vickers hardness of the specimen.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software (SPSS, Inc., IBM Corporation, NY, USA). A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to evaluate the independent and interactive effects of ceramic thickness and laser parameter settings on debonding time, intrapulpal temperature elevation, shear bond strength, translucency and Vickers hardness, followed by pairwise comparisons between experimental groups (a = 0.05). Pearson correlation analysis was used to determine the relationship between temperature changes and average debonding time values.

Results

The mean and standard deviation values of debonding time and intrapulpal temperature elevation for all experimental groups are summarized in Table 2. Two-way ANOVA revealed that both ceramic thickness and laser parameter settings significantly affected the debonding time and intrapulpal temperature variation during veneer removal (P < 0.05). The 1.0E3 group exhibited the longest debonding time (10.82 ± 1.08 s), while the 0.5E1 group showed the shortest debonding time (1.88 ± 0.52 s). The maximum intrapulpal temperature elevation (ΔT) was detected in the 1.0E3 group (1.58 ± 0.38 °C), and the minimum ΔT was found in the 0.5E1 group (0.30 ± 0.19 °C). No significant differences in debonding time or temperature elevation were observed between the 0.5E1 and 0.5E2 groups, as well as between the 1.0E1 and 1.0E2 groups (P > 0.05). Two-tailed Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 2) identified a strong positive correlation between average debonding time and intrapulpal temperature elevation (R2 = 0.739), indicating that prolonged laser debonding time was closely associated with increased pulpal temperature within the experimental parameter range.

Discussion

Adjustments to laser parameter settings substantially affect laser debonding efficiency. Philips described that laser energy is inversely proportional to pulse frequency [19]. Accordingly, at a constant frequency, increased laser power delivers higher single-pulse energy and shortens debonding time [25]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear how coordinated changes in pulse energy and frequency influence debonding outcomes under a fixed average laser power.

The present results confirmed that both laser parameter settings and ceramic thickness significantly affected debonding time and intrapulpal temperature elevation, leading to the rejection of the first null hypothesis. Under the fixed average power of 4.5 W, increased pulse frequency accompanied by reduced single-pulse energy prolonged debonding duration and elevated intrapulpal temperature. This phenomenon may be explained by the shortened interval between pulse peaks at higher frequencies, which reduces the natural heat dissipation time of dental tissues, even though individual pulse energy is decreased [16]. The debonding time and ΔT of the 0.5E2 group (2.36 ± 0.50 s and 0.42 ± 0.20 °C) showed no significant difference compared with those of the 0.5E1 group (1.88 ± 0.52 s and 0.30 ± 0.19 °C). The same trend was observed between the 1.0E2 and 1.0E1 groups. This may be because an increase in frequency within a certain range can accelerate the debonding process [36], compensating for the reduced speed caused by lower single-pulse energy.

Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive association between debonding time and intrapulpal temperature elevation, verifying that laser irradiation duration is a critical determinant of pulpal temperature variation. Importantly, the maximum temperature increase in all groups was below the clinically safe threshold of 5.5 °C [19], confirming the relative safety of the 4.5 W laser debonding protocol under conventional water-cooling conditions. No significant differences in debonding time or temperature elevation were detected between the E1 and E2 parameter groups at either ceramic thickness (P > 0.05). This may be because an increase in frequency within a certain range can also accelerate debonding, compensating for the reduced effect caused by decreased energy [41]. With identical laser parameters, 1.0 mm-thick veneers required significantly longer debonding time than 0.5 mm-thick veneers. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Cifuentes et al., who showed that thinner ceramic restorations present lower debonding resistance [30]. This phenomenon can be explained by the mechanism of Er: YAG laser debonding, in which laser energy is transmitted through the ceramic restoration to act on the underlying resin cement, causing adhesive failure [17]. As ceramic thickness increases, less laser energy passes through the material, leading to longer debonding times [29].

Observation under a stereomicroscope revealed that the predominant failure mode at the adhesive interface in all groups was type 1. As reported by Morford et al., laser debonding mainly occurs at the interface between the veneer and the resin adhesive [8˒ 42˒ 43]. A type 3 failure mode was observed in the 1.0E3 group, which may be related to the lower laser energy. Zach et al. proposed that higher pulse energy enables the resin adhesive to rapidly reach the ablation threshold, thereby achieving faster debonding, whereas lower laser energy causes the resin to undergo thermal softening [18, 19]. Tooth carbonization was observed in the 0.5E1 group, indicating that the 300 mJ/15 Hz parameter setting may pose potential thermal damage risks for removing 0.5 mm-thick veneers.

Rebonding strength is a key indicator for evaluating the clinical reusability of laser-removed restorations, especially for veneers removed due to improper initial seating. However, studies investigating the rebonding strength of restorations after laser debonding are limited, and existing findings are inconsistent [27, 36]. The present study found no significant difference in shear bond strength between laser-debonded and control specimens after standardized rebonding, which is in accordance with the results reported by Karagoz-Yildirak M et al. [36]. This favorable outcome may be attributed to the standardized alumina sandblasting procedure that thoroughly eliminates residual resin on ceramic surfaces before rebonding [44]. Scanning electron microscopy confirmed no obvious microcracks or microfractures on laser-irradiated ceramic surfaces, demonstrating the safety of 4.5 W Er: YAG laser debonding for lithium disilicate ceramics. The measurement results of ceramic transparency showed that there was no significant changes in the transparency of the specimens compared to the control group, which may be due to the moderate laser power adopted in this study. Turgut et al. observed reduced translucency in lithium disilicate glass-ceramic veneers after surface treatment with an Er: YAG laser (10 W, 500 mJ) [45], while Kurtulmus Yilmaz et al. reported no change in translucency following irradiation with an Er, Cr: YSGG laser (3 W, 300 mJ) [37]. In our study, 0.5 mm-thick ceramics exhibited significantly higher translucency than 1.0 mm-thick ceramics (P < 0.05), validating that material thickness is a decisive factor affecting the optical properties of lithium disilicate glass-ceramics, which aligns with the conclusions of Sari et al. [29]. Mechanistically, during laser debonding, laser energy is transmitted through the ceramic restorations and acts on the resin cement underneath [16]. The transmittance of laser is related to the translucency of ceramic materials: thinner restorations exhibit greater translucency and higher transmittance [29]. Research on the mechanical stability of laser-treated dental ceramics remains insufficient. Zhang et al. noted a decreasing trend in the hardness of dental ceramic following Er: YAG laser debonding, although intergroup differences were not significant [35]. the present study demonstrated that three different energy-frequency combinations at a fixed 4.5 W power exerted no significant influence on Vickers hardness of lithium disilicate ceramics. This may due to the relatively short duration of the laser irradiation we performed. In conclusion, the second null hypothesis was accepted.

This study had several limitations. First, only a single type of lithium disilicate ceramic and a fixed laser power (4.5 W) were investigated. Second, no artificial aging treatment was performed on the specimens to simulate long-term intraoral service conditions. In addition, this was an in vitro study and did not involve full-contour veneers, which should be addressed in future investigations.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this in vitro study and statistical analysis, the following conclusions are drawn:

At a fixed 4.5 W power output, variations in Er: YAG laser energy and frequency significantly affect the debonding time of lithium disilicate veneers and intrapulpal temperature elevation. Within the parameter range investigated in this study, intrapulpal temperature elevation is positively correlated with laser debonding time, and all measured temperature rises are within the clinically safe threshold. Laser protocols E1 and E2 achieved comparable debonding performance, both of which were significantly superior to E3. Accordingly, optimal laser parameters should be selected based on veneer thickness. For 0.5 mm-thick veneers, E2 is the preferred protocol, while both E1 and E2 are safe for 1.0 mm-thick veneers.
