Prefrontal parvalbumin neurons mediate working memory in a task demand-dependent manner
Authors: Tyler D. Dexter, Meira M. F. Machado, Shahnaza Hamidullah, Daniel Palmer, Ahmed. M. Hashad, Marcus Doyle, Megan Attard, Brian L. Allman, Wataru Inoue, Lisa M. Saksida, Timothy J. Bussey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prefrontal cortex (PFC) is known to mediate working memory processes, although how individual circuit elements within PFC support working memory is much less well understood. Parvalbumin-expressing neurons (PVNs) are inhibitory cells involved in coordinating local communication and facilitate the emergence of high frequency gamma oscillations. PVNs are shown to support various PFC-dependent cognitive functions in rodents, though reports of their involvement in working memory have yielded conflicting results. Here, we implemented in vivo fiber photometry and optogenetics to record and manipulate prefrontal PVN activity during a touchscreen-based test of working memory in mice. We found that PVN involvement is highly dependent on task demands, making a necessary contribution through heightened activity only during high-demand conditions. These findings suggest that previous contradictory findings regarding the role of prefrontal PVNs in working memory may be due to differences in the degree to which the working memory paradigms used taxed cognitive load.

Introduction

Working memory involves a collection of processes that allow fleeting sensory information to be encoded, held mentally available, and used to guide future behavior1. This ability is a fundamental function of the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and allows organisms to interact with their environment without reliance on immediate sensory cues2–5. The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) has been extensively studied in the context of working memory in rodents, and disrupting its activity via physical or chemical perturbations consistently impairs working memory performance6–21. As a temporary storage system, working memory has a limited capacity in terms of the number of items that can be held on-line and the duration over which they can be maintained. The number of to-be-remembered items, and the duration of the retention delay, contribute to the total working memory load, and fMRI studies in humans show enhanced activation of the PFC as working memory load increases22–26. While currently available rodent paradigms do not allow variation in the number of items to be remembered, a recent study showed that exposing rats to extended delays during a delayed non-match to position (DMTP) paradigm significantly increased mPFC activity, suggesting this method is efficient for increasing prefrontal involvement27.

Classic primate studies described the presence of persistently active neuronal ensembles during the delay period in which previously experienced goal-relevant information must be held on-line3,4,28–32. More recent models suggest working memory operations are facilitated by bouts of coordinated neural rhythms that facilitate distinct working memory operations, particularly in the gamma and beta band ranges33–44. Specifically, it has been suggested that bursts of gamma activity driven by bottom-up sensory signals (30–100 hz) and are regulated via anti-correlated beta rhythms, which may harmonize to facilitate the selectivity and stability of working memory content45–47. Synchronized neural oscillations are dependent on precise temporal regulation of excitatory signals, which is provided by GABAergic inhibitory neurons. Parvalbumin-expressing neurons (PVNs), a well-studied subclass of inhibitory neurons, regulate the spike timing of local pyramidal neurons via perisomatic inhibition and are necessary for the emergence of gamma band oscillatory activity48,49. Due to their extensive innervation of local pyramidal neurons and connectivity with other populations of inhibitory neurons, PVNs are part of an integrated circuit of feedforward and feedback inhibition that facilitate the canonical computations of the PFC50–52. Causal evidence supports a diverse role for PVNs in various PFC-dependent cognitive processes, including attention53–55 and cognitive flexibility56–62. In the context of working memory, prefrontal PVNs appear to display task-related, but not information-specific, activity during task performance16,63,64.

Despite these observations, efforts to identify a causal role for PVNs in working memory have yielded conflicting results. Studies that have inhibited prefrontal PVNs during working memory tasks have reported improved performance16, impaired performance56,65, and no effects66. Further, increasing PVN activity during working memory tasks has been reported to either reduce task performance16,67 or have no effect63. Importantly, the variation in behavioral paradigms used to assess working memory makes it challenging to draw definitive conclusions across studies. Of note, optogenetically inhibiting PVNs during T-maze variants has consistently yielded null results63,66. Alternatively, suppressing PVN activity during the delay period of a delayed match-to-position (DMTP) working memory paradigm in which multi-sensory features were integrated led to an impairment in working memory maintenance65. If PFC activity is more greatly recruited under conditions of increased task difficulty, it may be that its components, such as PVNs, become more important as the demands of the task are increased.

To achieve some clarification on this relationship, we characterized the role of prefrontal PVNs during a touchscreen-based working memory task. The trial-unique non-match-to-location (TUNL) task requires mice to retain the location of a stimulus presented in one of six windows over a delay and use this information to select a new stimulus location when it is presented alongside the original sample during the choice stage68,69 (Fig. 1). The use of multiple stimulus locations, as opposed to traditional two-option methods (i.e., two static arms of a maze or two levers), minimizes motor mediating strategies that effectively reduce working memory requirements9. Moreover, we have recently modified this task to include proficiency-driving training, where animals are automatically presented with more challenging trial configurations as they successfully complete trials, within a single session20. As a result, animals are continuously presented with different trials configurations of varying difficulties, and task difficulty can be varied further by altering the length of the delay interval, and the amount of proactive interference. Results

Prefrontal PVN recruitment during working memory is modulated by task difficulty

We first characterized the activity of prefrontal PVNs during working memory performance under varying degrees of task difficulty. Adult male and female PV-Cre mice (n = 7) were trained to high proficiency (see Methods) on the TUNL task prior to surgery. Briefly, mice had to reach greater than 70% accuracy on an S-1 session, which includes all trial configurations and separation distances presented at equal probabilities with a minimum delay. Then, mice received unilateral injections of the AAV-syn-FLEX-jGCaMP7f-WPRE calcium biosensor70 targeted to the mPFC and a fiber optic implant to selectively record PVN dynamics (Fig. 2A, B, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Following a re-baselining period, mice underwent multiple behavioral probe sessions that modulated the delay length and used a constant separation distance of one-window apart between sample and correct choice location (i.e., S1). These included a minimum delay session, where mice could immediately initiate the choice phase following a sample touch by returning to the reward tray, thus minimizing working memory requirements and providing a control condition, and a maximum delay session, which included a 2 s delay following sample touch (Fig. 2C). We have previously observed that extending the delay past 2 s reduces performance to chance level20, and thus used a 2 s delay to evaluate processes associated with working memory. To measure prefrontal PVN activity during task performance, the continuous signal was aligned to behaviorally relevant task events, including sample presentation and choice (Fig. 2D–F). We observed significant increases in PVN activity during the presentation of the sample stimulus, which were independent of delay length (Fig. 2G, H, Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). Moreover, we did not observe any associations between PVN activity at sample presentation and whether mice would go on to make a correct or incorrect choice during either delay session (Fig. 2I–K). We next assessed whether PVN activity was differentially recruited during the delay period of both testing sessions. Here, we analyzed the change in PVN signal, compared to the intertrial interval (ITI) baseline, immediately prior to making a choice (Fig. 2G). Interestingly, we observed that PVN activity was significantly higher during the maximum delay session compared to the minimum delay session (Fig. 2M). We further separated PVN signals based on trial outcome, to evaluate whether there was a relationship between PVN signals and performance on varied delayed sessions (Fig. 2N, O). Although there was an overall effect of delay length, we did not observe any interactions between choice outcome and PVN activity (Fig. 2P). Lastly, PVN activity following choice displayed a bi-directional relationship with trial outcome regardless of delay length, a characteristic that has been previously shown in prefrontal PVN populations71,72. Together, these data suggest a recruitment of prefrontal PVNs at the time of sample presentation, and that PVN activity was sustained during the extended delay sessions, potentially reflecting higher demand on PFC resources. Importantly, these delay signals appeared to be independent of trial outcome, suggesting a relationship with task requirements rather than the successful execution of working memory.

We further examined the relationship between prefrontal PVN activity and task demands by next exposing mice to a probe session that increased the potential for proactive interference. While increasing the retention delay taxes the holding of information available in working memory, introducing competing information leaves working memory vulnerable to interference. Proactive interference is the process by which previous memories interfere with the current memory being accessed, and this can be tested via TUNL by removing the ITI period and having trials occur immediately following one another (Fig. 3A, B). Otherwise, the interference session had the same parameters as a typical baseline session (i.e., Level S-1: All trial separation levels present) with a minimum delay, and importantly, has been shown to be sensitive to global mPFC disruption20,73. As was done during the delay sessions, the continuous signal was aligned to sample presentation and choice, and filtered for trial outcome (Fig. 3C–E). First, we aligned PVN signals to sample presentation and compared activity between a baseline and interference probe session (Fig. 3F–H). We observed significantly higher PVN activity at sample presentation during the interference session, which was not associated with trial outcome, similar to what was observed during the delay sessions (Fig. 3I). Next, we evaluated PVN signals at choice during the interference and baseline sessions, filtering by correct or incorrect choice outcome (Fig. 3J–L). Here, we again observed significantly higher PVN activity during the interference condition. Interestingly, there was also a significant effect of trial outcome, though no interaction with session type. Post hoc analysis revealed that PVN activity during the interference condition was significantly higher during correct, but not incorrect choices. The presence of this effect is of interest, as no differences in PVN signal as a function of choice outcome were observed on either the minimum or maximum delay session. Of note, the interference and baseline sessions comprised all separation levels (S4:S1) occurring at equal and unpredictable probabilities. Alternatively, the delay sessions include a fixed separation level of S1 trials and a single delay length, providing task parameters that were difficult but consistent across trials within a session. Prefrontal PVN inhibition disrupts working memory under conditions of high task demand

After observing significantly sustained PVN activity during the delay period of the maximum delay session, we next aimed to evaluate the consequences of manipulating prefrontal PVNs across incrementally challenging test sessions. First, given that this version of the TUNL task was developed quite recently, we aimed to establish a temporal causative role of the mPFC in task performance. A recent study demonstrated that mPFC pyramidal neurons contribute to all phases (i.e., sample encoding, delay, and retrieval) of a T-maze working memory paradigm21, so we used optogenetic inhibition to target prefrontal pyramidal neurons across task phases on TUNL. To do so, we expressed AAV-CaMKII-ArchT-GFP74 on CAMKII-expressing pyramidal neurons in the mPFC of mice using a within-subjects design (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 2A). Optogenetic inhibition was provided at distinct task phases including the sample presentation, delay (2 s length), and choice periods to assess the temporal contributions of the mPFC. Inhibition of mPFC pyramidal neurons significantly impaired task performance, specifically when photoinhibition was delivered during the delay period, but not during the sample or choice phase. After observing that performance deficits were specific to delay-phase mPFC disruption, we elected to target mPFC PVNs specifically during the delay period. To do so, we crossed mice that contained cre-dependent inhibitory archaerhodopsin (ArchT) with PV-Cre mice to generate offspring that selectively expressed ArchT under the PV promoter, resulting in widespread expression throughout the brain (PV-Arch+) (Fig. 4C). Prior to behavioral manipulations, we first confirmed the effectiveness of optogenetic control over PVNs using ex vivo patch clamp electrophysiology paired with optogenetic stimulation in PV-Arch+ mice (n = 4; 9 cells) (Fig. 4D). As predicted, application of light robustly suppressed PVN action potentials induced by a series of square current pulse injections (Fig. 4D). To determine the effects of prefrontal PVN inhibition during working memory, male and female PV-Arch+ (n = 15) and control PV-Arch- (n = 10) mice were trained until performance was stable on separation stage S-1 prior to surgery for fiber optic implants (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Both groups learned to perform the task above 70% choice accuracy, without significant differences between groups in acquisition (Fig. 4E). Prior to optogenetic manipulations, both groups of mice were exposed to test sessions with increasing delays at separation level S1 to reduce effects of novelty during testing and confirm significant reductions in performance at the maximum delay length (Fig. 4F). Following bilateral fiber optic implantation in the mPFC and task re-baselining, PV-Arch+ and PV-Arch- mice were assessed on the same test sessions (S1: 0 s, 1 s, 2 s delay) with optogenetic inhibition delivered bilaterally to the mPFC pseudorandomly on half of the trials during the delay period (Fig. 4G). One mouse from the PV-Arch- group had to be removed due to headcap damage and thus an inability to finish all testing sessions (n = 15 PV-Arch+ and 9 PV-Arch-). Optogenetic stimulation significantly impaired choice accuracy in PV-Arch+ mice, though only when PVNs were inhibited during the longest delay session (Fig. 4G). Further, there was a significant delay-dependent decrease in accuracy across delay sessions with PVN inhibition in PV-Arch+ mice (one-way RM ANOVA, F(2, 28) = 4.169, p = 0.0260; Dunnett’s multiple comparison’s test, 0 s vs. 2 s: p = 0.0150; 0 s vs. 1 s: p = 0.4731). Importantly, these data indicate that prefrontal PVN inactivation was only detrimental to task performance when the demand on working memory was increased by extending the delay length. Optogenetic inhibition had no effect on task performance in PV-Arch- mice, confirming there were no confounding effects of light bleeding or cre recombinase expression (Fig. 4G). Lastly, PVN inhibition did not significantly affect response latency or reward collection latency, suggesting motor and motivational processes were not affected (Supplementary Fig. 2D).

Gamma and theta activation of prefrontal PVNs differentially affect working memory

After confirming that PVN activity was necessary for optimal working memory performance during increased task demands, we next investigated whether frequency-specific stimulation of prefrontal PVNs during the delay period would affect task performance (Fig. 5A). Previous studies have shown bi-directional effects of stimulating PVNs at distinct frequencies, where higher range gamma (30–40 hz) has been shown to improve aspects of PFC-dependent cognition53–55,58 while lower theta stimulation (5 hz) impairs performance. However, this approach has not yet been applied to the domain of working memory. Previous studies that optogenetically activated PVNs during working memory paradigms provided continuous light stimulation through the delay period and yielded conflicting results regarding the impact on working memory16,63. While one study reported no effects, the latter reported a disruption in task performance, suggesting that stimulating PVN activity outside of normal firing patterns can have detrimental cognitive effects. We have previously shown that local prefrontal oscillatory activity can be modulated in vivo using frequency-specific (30 hz and 5 hz) stimulation of PVNs in our generated PV-ChR2 mice55. Importantly, we were able to confirm that our stimulation procedures at 30 hz entrained local activity to the gamma frequency, whereas 5 hz stimulation introduced aberrant activity across various frequency bands. To provide additional confirmation of efficacy, here we used ex vivo patch clamp electrophysiology and validated that trains of excitatory optogenetic stimulation drive the firing of PVNs at high fidelity. Briefly, light illumination at either 30 hz or 5 hz triggered action potentials in PVN in mPFC slices prepared from PV-ChR2 mice (Fig. 5B). Similar to the inhibition experiments, there were no effects of either manipulation on measures of choice or response latency (Supplementary Fig. 2F). Interestingly, however, we did observe lower accuracy on the no-optogenetic stimulation trials during the 30hz testing session. To assess whether variation in individual performance may be a factor, we separated animals based on “No Light” trial performance, identifying a portion of animals performing significantly lower on the 30 hz control trials (Fig. 5D). Thus, we reanalyzed the data to include performance as a variable. We used a median split analysis to classify animals as higher or lower performers within the respective test session (i.e., 30 hz or 5hz session). While we still observed a significant optogenetic stimulation x frequency interaction, there were not interactions between performance and stimulation protocol. Post hoc analysis showed that 30 hz stimulation significantly improved performance, regardless of whether animals were high or low performers (Fig. 5E), while 5 hz stimulation impaired performance in both groups (Fig. 5F). Thus, the cognitively enhancing and disrupting consequences of 30 hz and 5 hz stimulation, respectively, appear to be robust and were not driven by baseline differences in performance. Moreover, given the interest in prefrontal PVNs as therapeutic targets for cognitive impairments, these findings indicate that gamma range stimulation of PVNs has the potential to improve working memory functioning.

Optogenetic perturbation of prefrontal PVNs increases susceptibility to interference

High interference conditions have been previously shown to disrupt TUNL performance when the mPFC was lesioned or pharmacologically inactivated20,73. Thus, the contributions of prefrontal PVNs to working memory may be expected to extend to conditions under which interference is increased. Moreover, our findings of increased PVN activity at sample presentation during the interference session could suggest a role of this population in filtering interference and/or enhancing target information. To investigate a causal relationship, we assessed PV-Arch+ and PV-Arch- mice on the interference session, in which all task parameters were the same as the calcium imaging experiments. As a result, trials occurred in rapid succession, increasing the propensity of memories from previous trial configurations to disrupt the current trial (Fig. 6A). Photoinhibition was delivered immediately after sample touch for a 0.5 s duration, which was informed by our recordings showing a greater degree of PVN activity recruitment during the interference session at sample presentation. Interestingly, PVN inhibition significantly reduced accuracy in PV-Arch+ mice, with no effects observed in PV-Arch- controls (Fig. 6B). PVN inhibition slightly reduced response latency and did not affect reward collection latency. Response latencies were also higher during incorrect responding during the interference session (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). We were then interested in whether frequency-specific activation of PVNs would affect performance under conditions of increased interference. To test this idea, PV-ChR2 mice were assessed on the interference probe with counterbalanced 30hz and 5hz stimulation sessions. Otherwise, all session parameters were identical to those used for the PVN inhibition experiment. Similar to PVN inhibition, 5 hz stimulation significantly reduced accuracy, while 30 hz stimulation had no effect (Fig. 6C). While there were no effects of PVN activation on response latency, we again observed a general increase in the latency to make an incorrect choice during the interference condition in both sessions (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Additionally, we observed a significant reduction in reward collection latency with 30 hz stimulation in the absence of any effects on task performance (Supplementary Fig. 3C). These data further support the idea that disrupting prefrontal PVN activity, whether by inhibition or low frequency stimulation, disrupts working memory performance under conditions of increased task demands. Discussion

While PVNs have been described as facilitating various PFC-dependent functions, the present study aimed to provide clarification on the role of these cells in working memory. Using a touchscreen-based test of working memory, we confirmed the involvement of the mPFC in working memory maintenance by showing that optogenetic inhibition of prefrontal pyramidal neurons during the delay reduced task performance to chance level. To investigate the involvement of PVNs specifically, we assessed mice on multiple sessions during which task difficulty was adjusted by varying task parameters such as the delay length or the degree of interference. Our data provide the first demonstration that prefrontal PVN activity is modulated by task demands during working memory, and further, that modulation of PVN activity affects working memory in a frequency-specific manner.

The medial prefrontal cortex and working memory in rodents

It has been established that, across multiple rodent working memory paradigms, intact functioning of the rodent mPFC is necessary for task performance, including maze-based6–8,13–15,17,21,75, head-fixed16, and operant chamber-based based methods11,12,18–20,73,76. Here, we also demonstrate that temporally precise inhibition of mPFC pyramidal neurons induced deficits on the TUNL task, specifically when inhibition was provided during the delay period. It is uncertain, however, how the activity of prefrontal excitatory neurons and inhibitory circuits may underlie the processes of working memory in rodents, which is further compounded by a lack of consistency in behavioral paradigms being used, and the possibility that different tasks may differentially recruit PFC circuitry. Recording and imaging studies have demonstrated dynamic and transient increases in mPFC activity across sample, delay, or choice stages, depending on the behavioral paradigm14–16,21,77–80. Similarly, prefrontal PVNs fire across various task epochs, and their activity during the delay has been reported to correspond to action (i.e., go vs. no go)16 or be unassociated with specific task events63. It is important to note, however, that PVNs likely display heterogeneity in their activity across task phases64. Stable working memory would require a balance of activation and inhibition of neural ensembles that convey target and non-target information to appropriately maintain relevant information and suppress distracting or interfering information, respectively81. Thus, distinct groups of PVNs may contribute to either of these processes across temporal phases of a working memory task trial.

Prefrontal parvalbumin neurons and working memory maintenance

We observed that PVN activity was consistently higher during sessions with greater task demands, including increased delay length and interference. When comparing sessions that varied delay length, we observed significantly higher and sustained, relative to baseline, PVN activity immediately prior to making a choice during the more challenging delay session. In primate electrophysiological studies, prefrontal gamma activity has been shown to correlate with the number of items being added to working memory82, with observations of gamma bursts during initial stimulus encoding and at readout41,42. Here, our findings of increased PVN activity at sample presentation aligns with an interpretation that increases in PVN activity may reflect sensory-driven increases in gamma bursting. While a demand-dependent recruitment of prefrontal PVN activity provides support for a dynamic circuit underlying working memory performance, future experiments will aim to elucidate the specific information being carried in these signals. Interestingly, we did not observe associations between PVN activity and choice outcome (correct or incorrect), either at sample presentation or during the delay, during the delay sessions. This finding is in agreement with previous working memory studies16,63, which have not found patterns in PVN signals that correspond to better task performance.

Although we did not observe a relationship between PVN signals and task proficiency during the delay sessions, our optogenetic experiments did demonstrate a causal role of PVNs in task performance. Importantly, these data also suggest that the contribution of these cells to working memory reflects a task demand-dependent recruitment of PFC mechanisms, and offer a possible explanation for the inconsistencies observed in the literature surrounding the role of PVNs in working memory. We observed that inhibiting PVNs impacted task performance only during the maximum delay-length session, indicating that the sustained PVN activity we observed in the calcium signals played a functional role in faciliating task performance. This interpretation is also supported by a recent study using the TUNL paradigm that showed mPFC low oscillatory power increased with delay length, and the degree of coherence between mPFC and the hippocampus was positively correlated with task performance83. Moreover, we show that theta stimulation of PVNs induced similar deficits in task performance, highlighting that synchronous PVN activity at specific frequencies is necessary for optimal task performance. Suboptimal stimulation of PVNs has been shown to induce abberant activity in the local circuit, prevent PVNs from sychronizing to the functionally relevant gamma frequncy, and disrupt other domains of PFC-dependent cognition. Conversely, stimulating prefrontal PVNs at gamma frequencies entrains the local circuit and has been shown to facilitate various PFC functions48,49. Our findings here indicate that the cognitive enhancing effects of gamma stimulation extend to the domain of working memory. These findings, in agreement with our previous report in the domain of attention55, provide more evidence that targeting PVNs may be an effective method for improving cognitive deficits that are present in conditions featuring PVN/gamma abnormalities, such as neuropsychiatric disease54,58,67,84.

In proposed models of the cellular basis of working memory, representations are held through transient synaptic enhancements that result from synchronized bursts of spiking activity45,46. In the primate PFC, gamma bursts are shown to occur at the presentation of the sample and throughout the delay period up until working memory contents are read out, which may facilitate the initial encoding and refreshing of working memory content34,38,40,42,44,85. Given the well-defined role of PVNs in modulating cortical gamma oscillations, it has been somewhat surprising that altering their activity has produced variable results in working memory studies in rodents. We propose that the descrepencies in findings across studies may result from differences in behavioral paradigms and the cognitive demands they confer. In the T-maze, for example, inhibition or general activation of PVNs during the encoding run66 or through short delays (0–10 s)63 had no effect on task performance. Conversely, a pair of studies demonstrated that activating prefrontal PVNs via Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs (DREADDs) reduced T-maze performance at extended delays (60 s)67,86. As excessive activation of PVNs would disrupt the temporal precision necessary for synchronized circuit activity and reduce local gamma activity under normal conditions87, it seems that increased delay lengths may be necessary to recruit PVN involvment in the T-maze (but see ref. 66). Additionally, lengthening the delay or disrupting encoding in the T-maze was shown to enhance prefrontal gamma – hippocampal theta coupling, potentially compensating to preserve working memory in the presence of increased task difficulty88. This interpretation agrees with our observations that PVN involvement in working memory becomes more critical as task demands increase. Thus, an increase in mPFC recruitment in response to increased task demands is likely associated with greater PVN involvement to maintain organized communication during working memory maintenance.

Prefrontal parvalbumin neurons and susceptibility to interference

We have previously shown that lesions or pharmacological inactivations of the mPFC impair TUNL performance under conditions of increased interference even when the delay is kept minimal20,73. As this condition has no delay component, the increased load on the mPFC results from choice and sample phases occurring in rapid succession89. Here, we show that PVN activity was significantly elevated during the interference session, at both the time of sample presentation and choice on correct trials. As modulators of gamma activity, increased PVN activity during the sample phase may represent the encoding of the stimuli into working memory, which has been associated with elevations in gamma bursting40,42,90. Due to the increased presence of interfering information, a greater recruitment of PVN activity could serve multiple roles. Increased inhibitory tone could reflect the suppression of competing representations of past trial configurations that interfere with the current target being committed to working memory. Alternatively, it may be that greater effort is required to accurately encode the sample stimulus amidst lingering non-target representations, and PVN activity is reflecting an increased demand on mPFC resources for this purpose. Importantly, task performance during the interference session did not decrease compared to baseline in the absence of manipulations that disrupt the mPFC. Therefore, elevated PVN activity at sample presentation and prior to choice appears to represent a compensation by the PFC to sustain performance.

We confirmed the necessity of prefrontal PVNs for the protection of task peformance against interference by perturbing their activity with either blanket inhibition or low theta stimulation. Both manipulations reduced accuracy, which is consistent with theories that synchronous PFC activity during working memory maintains the stability of target information against interfering noise. As working memory capacity is reached, the synaptic representations of items can no longer be refreshed at an adequate rate to preserve selectivity, and targets become susceptible to interfering irrelevant information90. While gamma activity is thought to underly the addition of items to working memory, coordinated suppression of gamma by bursts of beta band activity has also been proposed to aid the selectivity of working memory to target information39,91. Thus, suppressing PVN-induced gamma synchrony may disrupt the rhythmic activity proposed to support working memory stability, and is a potential mechanism underlying the impairment observed here.

Summary

These results provide evidence that prefrontal PVNs play an important role in processes associated with working memory and are sensitive to variations in task demands. Moreover, the dynamic modulation of PVN activity by task parameters offers a window into how the PFC flexibly adjusts to changes in cognitive load. These findings underscore the importance of careful consideration of the behavioral methods employed when investigating circuits underlying cognition. Future experiments in this domain should attempt to address how the activity of mPFC mechanisms reflects the encoding and/or maintenance of working memory representations such as the sample stimulus (retrospective working memory), the future choice (prospective working memory), or task-specific features such as the task rules, spatial locations or intended actions.

Methods

Animals

Adult male and female mice were used for behavioral experiments and mostly bred in-house. For optogenetic experiments targeting pyramidal neurons, adult C57BL/6J (000664) mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory. For optogenetic experiments targeting PVNs, mice were generated to selectively express either inhibitory archaerhodopsin (ArchT) or excitatory channelrhodopsin (ChR2) as follows: Homozygous PV-Cre were crossed with Ai40(RCL-ArchT/EGFP)-D (Ai40D) mice to generate offspring selectively expressing ArchT under the PV promoter (termed PV-Arch); Homozygous PV-Cre were crossed with Ai32(RCL-ChR2(H134R)/EYFP (Ai32) mice to generate offspring selectively expressing ChR2 under the PV promoter (termed PV-ChR2). Ai40D (021188) and Ai32 (012569) were originally obtained from Jackson Laboratory prior to on-site breeding. Mice were group housed (two-four animals per cage) and moved to the colony room to be housed under a reverse light cycle (12 h:12 h) a minimum of three weeks prior to behavioral training to acclimate. Two weeks prior to touchscreen training, food was restricted to maintain 85-90% of free feeding body weight with ad libitum access to water. Mice were handled daily for one week prior to training and habituated to strawberry milkshake reward (Neilson Strawberry Shake) for three days prior to training. Following stereotaxic surgery, mice were single housed for the remainder of the experiment. All experiments were conducted in compliance with the standards set by the Canadian Council of Animal Care.

Apparatus

All mice were tested using standard Bussey-Saksida mouse touchscreen chambers (Campden Instruments Ltd., Loughborough, UK) as described in detail elsewhere (Oomen et al. 2013; Horner et al. 2013). The touchscreen was covered with a black plexiglass mask with two rows of six response windows, though only the six windows in the bottom row were used. Strawberry milkshake reward was delivered at the back of the chamber, opposite to the screen, in an open reward tray that faciliated mice attached to light sources via fiber optic patch cables. The task schedules were designed, managed, and events recorded using Whisker Server and ABETTII software (Campden Instruments). A custom analysis script was created using R to analyze separation level specific performance through training.

Trial unique non-match to location (TUNL) task

A trial on the TUNL task consist of 3 phases. Mice nose-poke an original stimulus located in a window on the screen during the sample phase, maintain this information over a delay period, and then use that representation to respond to a novel stimulus location when it is presented along side the originial stimulus68,69 (Fig. 1A). After the sample phase, mice are required to return to the reward tray to initiate the choice phase, which aids to reduce mediating behaviors where mice wait at predicted stimulus locations in front of the screen. Task difficulty can be varied by altering the distance between the original and novel location on the screen, as stimuli presented closer together tax processes of pattern separation in addition to working memory. There are five separation distances, dictated by the number of locations between the original and novel stimulus (eg. Separation distance 4 (S4): Locations 1 and 6; Separation distance 1 (S1): Locations 1 and 3, Locations 2 and 4, Locations 3 and 5, Locations 4 and 6) (Fig. 1B).

We have recently described a novel protocol for running the TUNL task20, which was adapted from the original protocol described by Kim and colleagues. Briefly, touchscreen pre-training stages first trained mice to obtain reward (Strawberry milkshake, Nielson) by touching a light stimulus displayed in one of the six response windows. Next, mice were trained to initiate trials via nose-poking the reward tray. Finally, mice learned to associate an incorrect screen response with a 5 s time-point period where the houselight was turned on. Following pre-training, mice were trained on the “Cued TUNL Task”, which faciliates learning the non-matching rule as the correct novel location during the choice phase is presented with a higher lumenence than the incorrect original location. A response to the novel location was accompanied by a 1 s tone, illumination of the reward tray, and strawberry milkshake reward (800 µL), while an incorrect response to the original location resulted in a 5 s time-out period where the houselight was turned on. Correct responses led to a 15 s intertrial interval (ITI) and presentation of the next sample location. Alternatively, an incorrect response initiated a correction trial loop, where the same sample-choice location configuration was presented to the mouse until a correct response was made. Additionally, correction trial loops contained a reduced ITI period (5 s). A unique feature of this version of TUNL is the inclusion of within-session progression across separation distances. At the beginning of training, mice begin a session with maximum-distance trial configuartions (S4). Once they achieve 3 correct responses in a row, S3 trial configurations are automatically integrated into the trial presentations (Fig. 1C). This continues through all separation levels (S2, S1, S0) until mice reach separation stage S-1, where all trial configurations occur at an equal probability. Importantly, the inclusion of such a vast number of trial configurations reduces the potential development of mediating strategies, where mice may be using behavior strategies outside of working memory to predict where the correct stimulus will be located. Mice progressed to the TUNL task training once they achieved >70% overall accuracy over 48 trials within 60 min, while completing each separation level (S4 to S0).

TUNL task training included the same paramters as Cued TUNL training, while now having the original and novel locations presented at the same brightness during the choice phase. Further, this stage also included an across session progression, where mice would begin subsequent sessions at progressively more challenging separation stages if criteria was met the previous session (Criteria: >70% accuracy on the previous separation stage across two sessions). Importantly, trial configurations were not removed, they just occured at a lower probability until separation stage S-1 is reached (Fig. 1D). For example, a mouse starting on separation stage 2 receives 50% S2 trials, 25% S3 trials, and 25% S4 trials. Training continued until mice passed separation stage S1, where they were then moved onto stage S-1 (Complete TUNL). Criteria for finishing TUNL training was completing >70% accuracy over two sessions on S-1, while completing 48 trials within 60 min each session. All mice completed this training prior to undergoing stereotaxic surgery.

Surgery

Stereotaxic surgery was performed on 4–6 month old male and female mice after intial task training. Mice were anesthetized with isuflorance and mounted in a stereotaxic frame (David Kopf Instruments). A heating pad was placed under the mouse to maintain body temperature and subcutaneous Metacam (5 mg/kg) was administered for pain control. The skull was exposed and bur holes were drilled for virus injection and fiber implants, and two holes for anchoring screws. For calcium imaging experiments, PV-Cre mice were unilaterally injected in the mPFC (AP: +1.90 mm, ML: ±0.30 mm, DV: −2.2 mm) with 0.5 µL of AAV-syn-FLEX-jGCaMP7f-WPRE (Addgene: 10449292) to selectively target cre-expressing PVNs. Virus was injected using a 10 µL Hamilton syringe attached to a microinfusing pump at a rate of 0.1 µL/minute. Following the infusion, the syringe was left in place for 5–10 min to prevent backflow before being slowly removed. A single fiber optic probe (400 µm core, 0.48 NA, 2.3 mm length) was implanted above the injection site and secured to the skull using dental cement (Ketac Cem, 3 M). For optogenetic inhibition of pyramidal neurons, C57Bl6 mice were bilaterally injected with 0.5 µL per hemisphere of CaMKII-dependent AAV-CaMKII-ArchT-GFP (ArchT74) targeted to the mPFC (AP: +1.9 mm, ML: 0.3 mm, DV: −2.2 mm at a 15° angle). Otherwise, the injection procedure was the same. Fiber optic probes (200 µm core, 0.22 NA, 2.4 mm length) were implanted above the injection sites. For PVN optogenetic experiments, PV-Arch and PV-ChR2 were bilaterally implanted with fiber optic probes using the same coordinates. Mice were given three weeks to recover before task re-baselining and optogenetic manipulations.

Patch clamp electrophysiology combined with optogenetics

Adult PV-Arch and PV-ChR2 mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital (100 mg/kg) and transcardially perfused with 30 ml of prechilled slicing solution containing (mM) 92 NMDG, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 HEPES, 25 glucose, 2 thiourea, 5 Na-ascorbate, 3 Na-pyruvate, 0.5 CaCl2, and 10 MgCl2 (pH 7.3–7.4, osmolarity 300–310 mOsm), saturated in 95%O2/5% CO2. Brains were quickly removed and transferred to a prechilled slicing solution. 300 µm coronal sections containing the PFC were cut using a vibratome (VT1200 S, Leica). Thereafter, brain slices were placed in slicing solution maintained at 34 °C for 35 min for recovery. During this time, NaCl were gradually spiked in to increase Na+ concentration as previously described by Ting et al. (Jove, 2018). Slices were then transferred to a slice holding solution containing (in mM) 92 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 HEPES, 25 glucose, 2 thiourea, 5 Na-ascorbate, 3Na-pyruvate, 2 CaCl2, and 2 MgCl2 (pH 7.3–7.4, osmolarity 300–310 mOsm, saturated in 95%O2/5% CO2 and kept at room temperature for the rest of the day. For recording, slices were transferred into a recording chamber superfused with aCSF containing (in mM) 126 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 10 glucose, 2.5 CaCl2, and 1.5 MgCl2 (osmolarity 295–300 mOsm), saturated with 95% O2/5% CO2 (flow rate 1–2 ml/min, 34oC). Slices were visualized using an upright microscope (SliceScope, Scientifica) with a 40× objective, infrared and fluorescent light sources, and prefrontal PVNs expressing ArchT or ChR2 were visually identified by GFP expression. Patch-clamp recording pipettes were pulled from borosilicate glass (Cat#: BF150-86-10, Sutter Instrument) using a Flaming/Brown Micropipette Puller (P-1000, Sutter Instrument) to a tip resistance of 3–5 MΩ. Electrodes were filled with an internal solution containing (in mM): 108 K-gluconate, 2 MgCl2, 8 Na-gluconate, 8 KCl, 1 K2-ethylene glycol-bis(β-aminoethyl ether)-N,N,N’,N’-tetra acetic acid (EGTA), 4 K2-ATP, 0.3 Na3-GTP, 10HEPES were used (Osmolarity: 283–289 mOsm, and pH: 7.2–7.4). Whole cell recordings were obtained using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices), digitized at 20 kHz (Digidata 1440, Molecular Devices) and recorded on a PC using pClamp 10 software (Molecular Devices). Access resistance was monitored every 5 min using the membrane test protocol in pClamp and recordings were discarded if the value exceeded 25 MΩ. To determine the effects of ArchT activation on the excitability of eGFP-positive PV neurons, 500 ms square current pulses were injected in a 40 pA increments starting −80 pA to 400 pA to induce neuronal firing. ArchT was only activated in the middle of each step by turning on green light (550 nm) 200 ms after the start of each current step for a duration of 200 ms. To determine the effects of ChR2 activation on the excitability of eGFP-positive PV neurons, cells were held close to their resting membrane potential (−70 mV) and blue light (450 nm, 2 ms) stimulation with frequencies 5 or 30 Hz was applied to activate ChR2. The number of action potential spikes associated with each light pulse were calculated.

Fiber photometry

Fiber photometry experiments were conducted similarly as previously described93. Fiber implants were constructed from optical fiber (0.48 NA, 400 µm core, cut to 2.4 mm; ThorLabs) and secured to stainless steel ferrules (1.25 mm diameter, 6.4 mm long, 440 µm bore; ThorLabs). A patch cable (Doric Lenses) was connected to the fiber optic implanted in the mPFC and real-time fluorescent signals were collected. The photometry system was equipped with a fluorescent mini-cube (Doric Lenses) to transmit two LED light signals at 465 nm (sinusoidally modulated at 572 hz) and 405 nm (modulated at 209 hz). Excitation light was delivered at 465 nm to stimulate Ca2+-dependent GCaMP7f fluorescence. Isosbestic 405 nm light was delivered to control for tissue bleaching or movement artifacts and removed from the 465 nm channel prior to analysis. LED power was set at ~25 μW. Signals were transmitted via the patch cable to the mini-cube, amplified, and focused via an integrated high sensitivity photoreceiver (Doric Lenses). Transistor-transistor logic (TTL) pulses were delivered at session start and continuously at stimulus presentation from ABETTII (Lafayette Instruments). Signals were sampled at 12 kHz and further decimated to 100 hz via Doric Studio software V5 (Doric Lenses).

TUNL testing with optogenetics

Following surgery, mice were baselined on separation stage S-1 until performance was re-acquired (Criteria: >70% accuracy over two sessions, while completing 48 trials within 60 min). CaMKII-ArchT (n = 7), PV-Arch+, (n = 15; male n = 8, female n = 7) and non-opsin control mice (n = 9; male n = 5, female n = 4) received optogenetic inhibition across multiple working memory probe sessions. Fiber implants were constructed from optical fiber (0.22 NA, 200 µm core, cut to 2.4 mm; ThorLabs) and secured to ceramic ferrules (1.25 mm diameter, 6.4 mm long, 230 µm bore; ThorLabs). Implants attached to two connected optical patch cables (0.66 NA: Plexon) via ceramic mating sleeves, which individually connected to compact LED modules attached to a dual LED commutator above the chamber (PlexBright, Plexon). Light was delivered via a 4-channel optogenetic controller powered by Radiant software (PlexBright, Plexon). Behavioral schedules were coded in ABETTII to synchronize light delivery to the desired task phase (0.5–2 s duration, depending on the session). Light output was measured at the fiber tip via a S140C photodetector connected to a PM100D power meter to confirm adequate light power.

For optogenetic inhibition experiments, green light (550 nm, ~2.5 mW, constant) was delivered bilaterally on half of the trials (30 trials, 60 total on probe sessions) pseudorandomly throughout the session. The first experiment assessed the effects of pyramidal neuron inhibition during working memory, inhibition was delivered either during the sample, delay, or choice phase for each session individually to determine if mPFC contributions were task phase specific. Separation distance S1 was chosen for delay probes based on previous studies94–96. There was a re-baselining day on separation stage S-1 between testing sessions to ensure performance remained stable. For PVN-targeting experiments, inhibition as delivered during the delay phase only based on our fiber photometry recordings and previous studies65,66. Therefore, optogenetic modulation of PVNs was always delivered during the delay period only. Mice received 3 testing sessions with ascending delays (0 s, 1 s, 2 s) at a fixed separation distance of S1. Finally, mice underwent an interference probe session, which consisted of a standard S-1 session with the ITI set to 0 s. While the delay length is set to 0 s for the interference probe, light was delivered during the period following sample touch and prior to choice (duration of 1 s).

For optogenetic activation experiments, PV-ChR2 mice (n = 11; male n = 6, female n = 5) received frequency-specific optogenetic stimulation across probe sessions to assess working memory maintenance and susceptibility to proactive interference. Activation patterns were set to deliver stimulation in either the gamma (30 hz) or theta (5 hz) frequency bands using Radiant software. Blue light (465 nm, 5 ms pulses, ~ 1 mW) was delivered during the delay period at either frequency on half of the trials during testing sessions consisting of S1 trials with a 2 s delay. Additionally, mice were assessed on the interference probe using the same parameters described above. All testing sessions with 30 hz or 5 hz stimulation were counterbalanced across mice to prevent testing order effects.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine and xylazine prior to transcardial perfusion with PBS, followed by 4% paraformaldehye (PFA). Brains were extracted and postfixed overnight in 4% PFA at 4 °C, before being transferred to PBS with 30% sucrose solution for cryoprotection. Brains were coronally sliced in the mPFC region at 40 µm using a vibratome (Leica Biosystems). For immunostaining, slices were mounted to slides, incubated in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) with 1.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with TBS containing 5% normal goat serum (NGS) at room temperature for one hour. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit monoclonal anti-parvalbumin antibody (1:50; Abcam 181086) in TBS with 0.2% Triton X-100 and 2% NGS. Next, sections were incubated in TBS with 0.2% Triton X-100 and 2% NGS with secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (Invitrogen A11012) for one hour before an overnight incubation in primary anti-GFP polyclonal 488-conjugated rabbit (1:1000; Abcam 21311). Lastly, sections were coverslipped with Vectashield with DAPI (MJS BioLynx, VECTH1200) for visualization. Virus expression and fiber optic placement was confirmed using a Thunder imaging microscope (Leica Microsystems) and representative images were processed using ImageJ.

Fiber photometry analysis

Raw trial-by-trial data were analyzed using a custom-written Python script, which used a linear regression function to fit the data to the isobestic prior to the calculation of the dF/F (Palmer, 2022). To assess peak GCaMP7f signals, normalized changes in fluorescence were calculated as: dF/F = [465 nm signal – (fitted 405 nm signal)]/(fitted 405 nm signal). The continuous signals were filtered as a function of response type (Correct choice, incorrect choice), or task event (Display sample, choice period) averaged across the session, and baselined to the ITI period to assess changes in fluorescence (∆F/F). Assessing the changes in calcium signals from the baseline during the delay were calculated as follows: ∆ in signal = (∆F/F – μbaseline)/σbaseline, where μ baseline is the mean of values from baseline period (averaged signal collected 2 s before stimulus onset) and σbaseline is the standard deviation of values from baseline period93. For interference probe sessions (and compared baseline sessions), the lack of an ITI required a custom protocol that baselined signals to the median of the entire trial, allowing for deviations in signal at behavioral events to be assessed. GCaMP7f signals were aligned to either sample presentation or choice and the data output was further organized for area under the curve (AUC) analysis using OriginLab software. AUC analysis was performed in the time windows of 2 s following stimulus presentation and 1 s prior to choice. There were no sex differences observed in behavioral performance or photometry signal, so male and female mice were combined for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Behavioral data was extracted from ABETTII to analyze performance measures across training and during optogenetic testing. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism. During training, data was ran through a custom R script to analyze choice accuracy across separation stages daily to inform training progression. Choice accuracy was calculated as the percentage of correct responses over the number of completed trials (Accuracy %). Latency measures, including correct and incorrect choice latency describe the time in seconds to respond to the screen following the presentation of the original and novel stimuli during the choice phase. Reward collection latency was the time in seconds it took a mouse to return to the reward tray following a correct response. Two-way repeated measures (rm) ANOVAs were used to analyze AUC and Δ signal fiber photometry data, with delay and choice outcome as the factors. Paired Student’s t tests were used to compare differences between conditions. Optogenetic inhibition experiments were analyzed using mixed rm ANOVA to assess effects of delay, group (experimental or control), sex, and optogenetics. Optogenetic activation experiments were analyzed using two-way rm ANOVA to assess effects of sex and optogenetic stimulation frequency. Tukey’s post hoc test was used following signficant ANOVA results when multiple comparisons were analyzed. Paired Student’s t tests were used to examine within-subject effects of optogenetic stimulation. All data were checked by the Shapiro–Wilk test and Leven’s test for normality and homogeneity of variance, respectively. Violation of sphericity assessed by Mauchly’s test was corrected using the Greenhouse–Geisser method. All statistical analyses were conducted using JASP.
