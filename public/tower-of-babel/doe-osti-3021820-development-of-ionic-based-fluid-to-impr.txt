Executive Summary
This research advances the ﬁeld of geothermal energy by developing and evaluating novel
ﬂuid systems designed explicitly for high-temperature subsurface environments to enhance
ﬂow distribution within fractures. The project integrates chemical synthesis, environmental
assessment, rheological testing, microﬂuidic analysis, and reservoir-scale laboratory
testing and analysis to identify optimal ionic and nonionic ﬂuids for Enhanced Geothermal
Systems (EGS).

Some of the project key ﬁndings include,

 (1) Thermal Stability & Safety: Synthesized ILs demonstrated decomposition onset
temperatures between 220 °C and 403 °C, conﬁrming their suitability for high-temperature
geothermal applications. Their non-volatile nature enhances operational safety and
sustainability.

(2) Environmental Impact: Toxicity assessments identiﬁed HPyBr as environmentally
hazardous, while Deep Eutectic Solvents (DES) showed lower but notable impacts. A
mitigation strategy, prevention, containment, and treatment, was developed, and safer
alternatives such as oil-based (OBF) and water-based ﬂuids (WBF) were evaluated and
tested.

(3) Rheological Performance: Under high-pressure, high-temperature conditions, ILs and
alternatives exhibited shear-thinning behavior and thermal resilience. Diluted ILs mimicked
water-like ﬂow while maintaining stability up to 470 °F. Oil-Based Fluids (OBF) formed
protective ﬁlms, and Water-Based Fluids (WBF) offered high thermal conductivity.

(4) Hydraulic Efficiency & Reversibility: Microﬂuidic and fracture-scale tests revealed
increased frictional pressure drops of the tested ﬂuids compared to water, and high ﬂuid
recovery, i.e., less reservoir damage. DES and WBF showed higher reversibility, supporting
potential water use after the proposed intervention.

(5) Reservoir-Scale Evaluation: A dual-fracture ﬂow loop demonstrated DES’s effectiveness
in enhancing ﬂow distribution and mitigating short-circuiting, especially under higher
fracture temperature differences. Performance varied with concentration and temperature,
with DES excelling at elevated conditions.

(6) Operational Feasibility & Cost Analysis: Economic evaluation highlighted OBF and WBF
as cost-effective options, while DES offered a strong balance between performance and
cost. Despite higher upfront costs, advanced ﬂuids promise long-term gains in heat
extraction and system efficiency.

The synthesis and characterization of nineteen ionic liquids (ILs) signiﬁcantly expand the

conﬁrming their thermal resilience. The study also provides new insights into ﬂuid behavior
under extreme conditions, including shear-thinning properties, ﬂow stability, and
reversibility in fractured rock systems, critical parameters for geothermal applications, and
addresses the project objectives.

The research identiﬁes DES, OBF, and WBF as technically viable and economically feasible
alternatives to conventional geothermal ﬂuids. DES emerged as the most effective in
mitigating ﬂow short-circuiting and enhancing fracture coverage, especially under thermal
gradients. OBF and WBF demonstrated strong performance in terms of cost-effectiveness,
thermal conductivity, and environmental safety. Operational evaluations conﬁrmed the
feasibility of implementing these ﬂuids, with DES offering a balanced trade-off between
performance and cost.

The project contributes to public welfare by promoting safer, non-volatile, and
environmentally responsible ﬂuids for geothermal energy extraction. The development of
reversible and recoverable ﬂuid systems supports sustainable energy practices and
minimizes ecological impact. By improving heat extraction efficiency and reducing
environmental risks, the ﬁndings align with clean energy goals and enhance the viability of
geothermal systems as a long-term renewable energy solution.

While the proposed ﬂuids showed promising results in addressing geothermal short-
circuiting, their cost-effectiveness at this stage is questionable given the current market and
the emerging ionic liquids industry. Assuming OBF is used as an interest for 2 2-month
cycles, an initial investment of $24M is needed. Assuming a high-end daily loss rate of 10%,
the intervention will have an additional cost of $144M for the two-month cycle. At this rate,
other interventions, such as workovers or redrills, might be most cost-effective.

1 Introduction
The objective of the project was to develop multiple ionic liquid (IL) ﬂuids that can alter the ﬂuid ﬂow
through fractures in EGS reservoirs. To achieve this goal, the IL ﬂuid must eliminate short circuits and
redistribute the ﬂuid ﬂow in an EGS reservoir, thereby enabling sustained long-term heat exchange
within the system. To verify the hydraulic enhancement technology, multiple variables, such as ﬂow
rate, temperature, pressure drop through the fracture system, and rheology, must be compatible in
EGS conditions. The expected outcome of the proposed research was a non-damaging, solid-free,
shear-thinning ﬂuid that can affect the ﬂow through fracture networks within the reservoir and away
from the wellbore for an indeterminate amount of time, to increase the performance of EGS
reservoirs.

The accomplished work involved a comprehensive evaluation of ILs and alternative ﬂuids for EGS,
focusing on their synthesis, environmental impact, rheological behavior, hydraulic performance, and
operational feasibility. Nineteen ILs were synthesized and characterized for thermal stability, with
decomposition temperatures conﬁrming their suitability for high-temperature geothermal
applications. Environmental assessments identiﬁed risks and proposed mitigation strategies, while
rheological and microﬂuidic tests demonstrated favorable ﬂow properties and reversibility.
Reservoir-scale testing and economic analyses have highlighted Deep Eutectic Solvents (DES) as the
most effective ﬂuid for achieving ﬂow control and cost-performance balance, supporting the
development of safer, more efficient, and sustainable geothermal energy systems.

The project provided substantial academic and research support by funding two master’s students,
two PhD candidates, and two postdoctoral researchers. Their contributions spanned experimental
design, data analysis, and modeling. The project yielded ﬁve conference papers, two published
journal articles, and two additional manuscripts currently under peer review, demonstrating a
signiﬁcant scholarly impact. Furthermore, ﬁve curated datasets were submitted to the Geothermal
Data Repository, ensuring transparency, reproducibility, and public access to key ﬁndings. These
combined efforts have advanced the understanding and application of ionic liquids and alternative
ﬂuids for use in EGS, supporting both academic growth and sustainable energy innovation.

3.2 Hazard identiﬁcation and prevention strategies
The potential impact of IL-based geothermal ﬂuids on operators has been discussed for four ionic
liquids, including HPrBr, HPyBF4, BMIMBr, and ChCl:MgCl hexahydrate (DES), as per their respective
safety data sheets.

The toxicity levels of candidate ILs, including HPyBr and DES, were evaluated by conducting a set of
tests, which included a 48-hour acute toxicity test using Daphnia magna, fathead minnow (ﬁsh), gas
chromatography (GC) analysis, and a soil toxicity study using worms as test organisms. The main
results of this task were published in Atashnezhad et al. (2024), where the hazardous and
environmental implications of the developed IL were discussed.

3.3 Conclusion and environmental impact assessment
In summary, the toxicity evaluation of DES (Deep Eutectic Solvents) and HPyBr (1-Hexylpyridinium
Bromide) on aquatic organisms and earthworms has provided a valuable understanding of their
hazardous and environmental impacts. For Daphnia magna, DES showed a relatively higher 48-hour
LC50 value of 573 ppm, demonstrating that Daphnia magna is less sensitive to DES. In contrast,
HPyBr displayed a signiﬁcantly lower 48-hour LC50 value of 1.6 ppm, suggesting its higher toxicity to
Daphnia magna. These results highlight the requirement for caution in handling HPyBr due to its
potential harm to aquatic ecosystems, even at relatively low concentrations. Comparative toxicity
analysis with other components revealed that DES, despite having relatively lower toxicity, still poses
a threat to aquatic life.

Gas chromatography analysis of DES and HPyBr emissions revealed the presence of several
chemical elements, some of which could have adverse health and environmental effects.

In the case of Eisenia foetida earthworms, the toxicity tests showed concentration-dependent
effects of both DES and HPyBr. At higher concentrations of HPyBr, survival rates dropped to zero,
indicating severe adverse effects on growth. Similarly, DES showed reduced survival rates and growth
at higher concentrations, with an estimated LC50 of 7659 ppm for 7 days and 2892 ppm for 14 days.

These ﬁndings underscore the signiﬁcance of responsible handling and disposal of DES and HPyBr
to minimize their hazardous and environmental impact. Furthermore, supplementary research and
monitoring are necessary to fully understand the long-term effects of exposure to these ionic liquids
on humans, ecosystems, and organisms. Overall, toxicity assessment provides valuable information
for environmental scientists, and industries working with ionic liquids must make informed decisions
regarding their use and regulation based on this.

While these ﬁndings shed light on the toxicity of DES and HPyBr, it's key to note that even seemingly
harmless elements, such as NaCl (table salt), can become dangerous to the natural environment
when they appear in large quantities. Hence, it becomes evident that mitigating these risks requires
the implementation of a robust mitigation strategy to proactively avoid any potential incidents. Thus,
to address these potential hazards successfully, a mitigation strategy was established.

3.4 Mitigation steps to reduce toxicity
Based on the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) deﬁnition, the toxicity of a
substance refers to its capacity to cause harm to living organisms. It is inﬂuenced by factors such as

 12

exposure concentration and period, which can vary based on the species, age, and health status of
the exposed organisms. It's essential to note that components with low toxicity can become harmful
and have toxic impacts on ecosystems when subjected to high concentrations in the natural
environment. Given the toxicity of ILs, implementing an effective remediation plan is key to ensuring
the safety of ecosystems, the environment, and the handling of these chemicals.

In the event of an oil spill, a range of techniques is utilized for cleanup, including the use of oil booms,
skimmers, sorbents, in-situ burning, dispersants, hot water and high-pressure washing, manual
labor, and bioremediation (Agarwal, 2021). However, due to the distinct properties of our ionic liquids
(HPyBr and DES), speciﬁc techniques from the aforementioned list are not appropriate. Both HPyBr
and DES, unlike crude oil, are miscible in water, which renders methods such as oil booms and
skimmers ineffective and burning in-situ practices are not possible because these ILs do not have
ignitable properties. The other methods, such as sorbent application, hot water, high-pressure
washing, and manual labor, are also viewed as appropriate. The following outlines a strategy for
remediation steps to decrease the toxicity of ionic liquids, including HPyBr and DES. This strategy
incorporates the mitigation strategy initially formulated by the EPA in 1993 for oil spills
(Environmental Protection Agency, 1994) into the approach for addressing IL spills.

There are three main steps in the mitigation strategy to reduce the toxicity associated with the use of
ILs in EGS, including prevention, containment, and treatment.

3.4.1 Prevention
Spill and leakage prevention are crucial and immediate steps when dealing with any toxicants. In the
context of EGS systems, this can be achieved by continuously monitoring the entire system and
identifying any deviations that may signal possible leaks within the system. The following points
should be considered to enhance spill prevention.

 • Conduct regular well check-ups and maintenance to ﬁnd and address any structural issues
 promptly.
 • Conduct well integrity tests periodically, such as pressure testing and cement bond logs, to
 ensure the seals and casings are intact in the injection and production wells. This ensures
 that no leakage occurs in the wells.
 • Install pressure and ﬂow monitoring systems on injection and production wells to detect
 deviations from anticipated values, which could imply leaks.
 • Use secondary containment systems around wellheads and ﬂuid transfer points to capture
 any possible leaks or spills.
 • Apply corrosion prevention methods, such as corrosion-resistant coatings and cathodic
 protection, to extend the life of pipes and prevent leaks.

Conduct routine checks of all components in the ﬂuid transfer and heat exchange systems to detect
and resolve possible issues proactively.

3.4.2 Containment
In the event of a spill or leak of ionic liquids, act promptly to contain and isolate the affected area to
prevent the spread of the ionic liquid. Similar to oil spills, the primary tools for responding to IL spills
involve mechanical containment, recovery, and cleanup. This can be achieved by using barriers,

 13

such as absorbent or berms, to stop the spread of the ILs to adjacent areas. This containment step
is crucial in limiting the extent of environmental damage. Also, separating the leaked ILs from close
water bodies, soil, or sensitive environments is required. This can involve physically blocking drains
or constructing temporary dams to prevent the ILs from reaching water resources or sensitive
habitats.

3.4.3 Treatment
After the IL is separated, the contaminated soil requires treatment to ensure it meets environmental
standards. Four methods could be used, including wiping with absorbent materials, pressure
washing, raking or bulldozing, and low-temperature treatment.

Wiping with absorbent materials: Materials with the capacity to absorb multiple times their weight in
ILs can assist as valuable tools for wiping and cleaning up contaminated soil. There are beneﬁts
related to the use of absorbents. The use of absorbents generally does not harm the soil itself or the
organisms inhabiting it, and no residual materials are left behind after the cleanup operation.
Furthermore, some sorbents are reusable, thereby reducing the disposal burden following a spill
(EPA, 1993). Sorbents are materials fabricated to absorb liquids, and they can be effectively applied
to recover ILs through absorption, adsorption, or both mechanisms. These materials are often
crafted into large squares, like mops. These squares or mop-like structures should be used to wipe
down IL-coated rocks, which become saturated during this process. Absorbents allow ILs to
penetrate the pore spaces within the material, while adsorbents attract ILs to their surface without
allowing penetration. Once sorbents have been applied to recover ILs, they must be removed and
appropriately disposed of on land or cleaned for potential reuse. Any ILs extracted from the sorbent
materials should also be subject to proper disposal or recycling measures. This approach ensures
the responsible management of ILs and minimizes their environmental impact in spill situations.

It's worth mentioning that using absorbent supplies requires a substantial quantity of these materials
and the involvement of multiple personnel. Responders must wear proper protective clothing to
minimize immediate contact with the ILs during the elimination activity. IL-saturated absorbents and
protective gear used by the response workforce must be appropriately disposed of after cleanup,
which can entail additional costs.

Power washing: Pressure washing is a technique that requires employing hoses to deliver either low-
or high-pressure water streams for rinsing contaminated soil and rocks. Both hot and cold water can
be utilized to make these streams. The ILs are efficiently cleaned out from the soil and directed into
plastic-lined trenches, where it is afterward collected using sorbent materials and disposed of
appropriately. Furthermore, this action may also push ILs deeper into the soil, creating difficulties in
the cleanup activities. Pressure washing offers the beneﬁt of being a relatively cost-effective and
straightforward method. However, it requires the participation of a signiﬁcant number of staff
members to be executed successfully.

Raking and bulldozing: When ionic liquids (ILs) travel downward into the soil, their removal becomes
progressively challenging. If the ILs have moved only a short distance, methods such as tilling or
raking can be utilized. However, if the ILs have penetrated several inches into the soil, bulldozers may
be needed to dig the upper layers of soil. This procedure exposes the ILs, making it possible for them

 14

to be saved and removed from the site using absorbent materials, or they can be washed away using
a pressure hose.

Low-temperature treatment: After ﬁnishing the preceding steps and separating the infected soil from
the unaffected area, the ﬁnal stage includes soil remediation. When dealing with soil contaminated
by oil, several methods have been discussed in the literature for this purpose. These techniques
include bioremediation, low-temperature treatment, high-temperature treatment, and various other
physical techniques (Meegoda and Rathaweera, 1995). Meegoda and Rathaweera (1995) employed
low-temperature treatment for soil remediation of petroleum-contaminated soils. In this method,
the contaminated soils are subjected to thermal treatment by heating them within a range of 150 to
300 °C using rotary soil dryers. The soil contaminated with ionic liquid, collected during earlier
stages, should be subjected to heating in rotary soil dryers at speciﬁc temperatures of 200 °C and
260 °C. A temperature of 200 °C corresponds to the degradation temperature of DES, while 260 °C
corresponds to the degradation temperature of HPyBr ionic liquids. This dual-temperature approach
guarantees the efficient degradation of the corresponding ionic liquids present in the contaminated
soil, marking the ﬁnal step in mitigating the impact of these substances when they contaminate the
soil.

A complete approach to managing the toxicity of ionic liquids in EGS contains a combination of
prevention, containment, and treatment strategies. By highlighting prevention measures, carefully
monitoring the system for early detection of problems, implementing effective containment
methods in case of unexpected incidents, and having a well-planned action plan in place, the
potential environmental impact of toxicants can be substantially reduced. This thorough procedure
not only protects the sustainability and safety of EGS operations but also helps to produce
geothermal energy in a responsible and eco-friendly manner.

increases near its maximum operating temperature. Thus, the usage of such ﬂuid would require a
robust pressure management system in high-temperature applications. Additionally, salt deposition
from the mixture may necessitate routine maintenance to prevent scale buildup in the circulatory
system. WBF-2 has a corrosion inhibitor in its mixture.

Both OBF-1 and WBF-2 are environmentally safe ﬂuids that can be disposed of in accordance with
state guidelines. Both ﬂuids, OBF-1 with RELATHERM HT-1 and WBF-2 with Dynalene HC-50, are
commercially available, environmentally friendly, and hold substantial promises to exhibit similar

5.5 Microﬂuids reversibility
5.5.1 Microﬂuids reversibility of ILs
While ILs offer promising results for EGS, the cost implications associated with synthesizing most ILs
may pose challenges to economic feasibility and large-scale utilization (Hussain et al., 2023).
Moreover, recycling of the test ﬂuids may not be entirely possible due to ﬂuid losses and
contamination. This challenge highlights the need to explore a hybrid approach, combining the test
ﬂuids with water, to potentially achieve a more cost-effective solution. Thus, the feasibility of
transitioning EGS working ﬂuids from test ﬂuids to water after the initial use of water as the working
ﬂuid was investigated. The concept of reversibility is similar to the near wellbore permeability
alteration caused by the invasion of drilling and completion ﬂuids in oil and gas reservoirs. The
interaction between working ﬂuids and EGS formations inevitably results in reservoir damage
through several mechanisms, including ﬁnes migration, mineral precipitation, and chemical
reactions (Jiang et al., 2024). High reversibility indicates that the IL does not leave a signiﬁcant
footprint in the reservoir and can be displaced with water when the temperature of thermal short-
circuits recovers. The reversibility of the ILs was quantiﬁed by comparing the pre-ﬂow and post-ﬂow
differential pressures as described by Equation 5.1. The reversibility results for the test ﬂuids are
discussed in the following sections.

 ΔPpost - ΔPpre
 ΔPpost
In Equation 5.1 ΔPpre and ΔPpost are, respectively, the pressure drops measured during the initial and
subsequent ﬂow of water through the microchannels. High reversibility indicates that the IL did not
leave a signiﬁcant footprint in the microchannels and can be alternated with H2O without causing
an additional pressure drop.

Single-channel ﬂow tests were conducted using Channels A and B of the Microﬂuidic chip at
constant ﬂow rates of 0.001 mL/min and 0.01 mL/min. The collected data for all three phases, as

6.2 Reversibility experimental procedure
The subject ﬂuid is injected into the fracture core as an intervention. The pressure drop using
deionized water before and after the intervention is measured to determine reversibility.

Fracture ﬂow experiments were conducted according to the following procedure:

 1) The sample was placed in the cell, and the axial and conﬁning stresses of 11.72 MPa (1,700
 psi) and 6.89 MPa (1,000 psi), respectively, were applied.
 2) For high-temperature tests, the cell temperature was gradually raised to the target. The ﬂuid
 was preheated to match the target temperature prior to injection.
 3) To initiate sample saturation, deionized (DI) water was injected at an injection pressure of
 5.52 MPa (800 psi) for approximately 1 to 2 hours. This saturation cycle was repeated until a
 stable ﬂow rate was achieved.
 4) A back-pressure regulator, calibrated to 0.69 MPa (100 psi), was installed on the outlet line.
 5) During the Pre-Flow stage, DI water was injected at 5.52 MPa (800 psi) for 15 minutes, and
 ﬂow rates were recorded utilizing ISCO Data Acquisition Software.
 6) Then, the ionic liquid (IL) or alternate ﬂuids were injected for approximately 10 minutes.
 7) Subsequently, the post-ﬂow cycle was followed immediately and continued until the ﬂow
 rate stabilized and a consistent value was reached.
 8) After the test, the injection pressure was then raised to match the conﬁning stress to ﬂush
 the fracture over 5-10 minutes.
 9) The injection pressure was then adjusted to the next test condition, and the steps were
 repeated from Step 5 through the ﬁnal pressure condition.

6.8 Reversibility comparison
Reversibility refers to the extent to which ﬂow through a rock fracture returns to its original state after
the injection of an intermediate ﬂuid, a non-Newtonian ﬂuid in this study. It serves as an indicator of
the alteration induced by the intermediate ﬂuid on the fracture surface. Speciﬁcally, it quantiﬁes the
additional pressure drop observed during the post-ﬂow cycle that can be attributable to the
preceding ﬂow of an IL or an alternate ﬂuid. It is calculated using the following equation:

The reversibility of each tested fluid is quantified and presented in Figure 6.21 for two temperature
conditions: 20 °C and 50 °C. Among all evaluated fluids, WBF-2 consistently demonstrated the
highest reversibility, with approximately 90% reversibility at 20 °C and 80% reversibility at 50 °C,
despite having the shortest test duration. BMiMBr showed the second-highest reversibility at both
temperatures, followed by OBF-1 at 50 °C, which ranked third overall. In contrast, [HPy][BF4] and
[HPy][Br] demonstrated significantly lower reversibility.
A clear trend emerges from the plots: reversibility decreases with increasing flow rate across all
fluids, indicating that higher flow conditions intensify the resistance caused by non-Newtonian fluid
injection. On the other hand, reversibility generally increases with temperature, highlighting the
thermally responsive behavior of these non-Newtonian fluids. This suggests that elevated
temperatures may help restore fracture condition by reducing fluid viscosity and enhancing flow
recovery.

This behavior is particularly relevant for EGS operations, where such fluids may be used as
temporary flow mitigators to delay or restrict flow through cooled fractures. The increase in
reversibility with temperature implies that as the rock temperature recovers, the resistance to
working fluid flow diminishes, returning to near-initial conditions, especially for fluids exhibiting high
reversibility.
Additionally, reversibility was found to be time-dependent, increasing with the duration of the post-
flow period. Especially, WBF demonstrated rapid recovery even within a fixed test duration of 40
minutes. However, because WBF tests were not extended to the same durations as other fluids, the
observed reversibility at 50 °C appeared slightly lower than at 20 °C. It is likely that, given more time,
WBF would have reached reversibility levels near 100%, as seen at lower temperatures.
Overall, these findings suggest that the influence of fluid viscosity and flow resistance diminishes
both with time and temperature, indicating a potential self-regulating mechanism. This reinforces
the promise of non-Newtonian fluids for thermally dynamic subsurface environments and may
ultimately enhance the long-term efficiency and performance of Enhanced Geothermal Systems
(EGS).

7 Reservoir Scale EGS Flow Loop
A reservoir scale dual fracture ﬂow loop system was designed and manufactured for testing and
evaluation of ILs and alternative EGS ﬂuids under varying temperature and ﬂow conditions, with
performance comparisons made against water. When comparing different ﬂuids to water, a
sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of various potential EGS ﬂuids at different
temperature and ﬂow rate scenarios. Finally, a comprehensive analysis of the ﬂow distribution
performance of each EGS ﬂuid was conducted to evaluate its overall effectiveness in reducing
potential ﬂow short-circuiting.

7.1 Flow loop design and operation
The experimental setup consists of a 10 ft long dual-channel test section with granite rock slabs
inside steel square tubes, forming dual channels with 14 and 5 mm fracture apertures (Figure 7.1).

 Figure 7.1. Dual channel geothermal flow loop experimental setup.
A progressive cavity pump, controlled by a variable frequency device (VFD), injects the working ﬂuid
into a single pipe. A ﬂow meter was installed at the inlet before the ﬂow branches into the two
fractures. Each branch features a valve at the inlet side, enabling the system to be used in either
single or dual fracture conﬁgurations. The 10 ft ﬂow channels consist of a small fracture with an
aperture of 0.16 in (4mm) and a large aperture fracture of 0.55 in (14 mm) (Figure 7.1). Each fracture
is equipped with a differential pressure sensor installed at the inlet and outlet, and Resistance
Temperature Detector (RTD) sensors are installed at the inlet and outlet to collect the ﬂuid inlet and
outlet temperatures. The fractures are heated and controlled independently with two heating cables
on each fracture. A single high-temperature ﬁberglass layer and four aluminum-covered ﬁberglass
layers insulated each fracture. Once the working ﬂuid reaches the end of each fracture, it ﬂows into

 62

the cooling tank, where it is cooled by tap water and/or ice. Following the cooling tank, the ﬂuid is
combined in the mixing tank, which feeds the ﬂuid back into the pump.

A total of 4 different fracture conﬁgurations were analyzed. The ﬁrst two conﬁgurations were single-
fracture conﬁgurations, where either the small or large fracture values were closed, enabling ﬂow
into one fracture individually. These conﬁgurations were tested at three different temperature
scenarios: room temperature, 149 °F, and 230 °F. The two dual-fracture conﬁgurations that were
examined were: (1) the dual constant-temperature conﬁguration and (2) the dual varying-
temperature conﬁguration. In the constant-temperature conﬁguration, both large and small
fractures were heated to uniform temperatures of 149 °F and 230 °F, with additional tests conducted
at room temperature. The small fracture was maintained at 230 °F in the varying-temperature
conﬁguration simulating short-circuiting, while the large fracture was set to 149 °F and room

Water was used as a control working ﬂuid under identical test conditions for comparison with all
tested EGS working ﬂuids. Each temperature scenario was tested at four ﬂow rates: 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5
gallons per minute (GPM) for single-fracture tests and 2.5, 3, 3.5, and 4 GPM for dual-fracture tests.
Fracture temperatures were controlled using a thermostat, while ﬂow regulation was achieved via a
variable frequency drive (VFD) controlled through LabVIEW. Differential pressure and temperature
data were recorded for approximately 60 seconds, initiated manually through the data acquisition
system.

This experimental ﬂow loop aimed to mimic a geothermal short-circuiting environment for all working
ﬂuids, allowing for a comparison with that of water. To determine the ﬂow distribution in the dual
fracture conﬁguration and evaluate short-circuiting, single fracture conﬁgurations were utilized in the
ﬂow distribution calculations.

The pressure drop measurements from the single-fracture conﬁguration were utilized to determine
the ﬂow distribution within the dual-fracture conﬁguration. For each dual-fracture scenario, ﬂow
distribution was assessed by ﬁtting a quadratic equation to the pressure drop versus ﬂow rate data
obtained from the single-fracture conﬁguration for both the large and small fractures. The average
values derived from the raw data, as discussed in the previous section, were used to generate these
ﬁts at each temperature condition for both fracture sizes.

Figure 8.1 shows a conceptual diagram showing the basic working principle of enhanced geothermal

the need for modiﬁcations to the secondary ﬂuid used in the surface power generation system.

8.3 Recommendation
Ultimately, recommendations are made based on the performance of the ﬂuids in conjunction with
their economic implications. The deep eutectic solvent (DES) demonstrated the best overall
performance in mitigating geothermal short-circuiting, maintaining consistent thermal performance
across all applied constant temperatures. From an economic perspective, DES falls within the mid-
range in terms of cost per gallon.

The oil-based ﬂuid emerged as the second most effective option in reducing geothermal short-
circuiting and was identiﬁed as the most cost-effective ﬂuid on a per-gallon basis. Furthermore, if
the oil-based ﬂuid is considered environmentally benign compared to ionic liquids, this factor should
also be taken into account when selecting an appropriate heat transfer ﬂuid.

fracture zone of 100,000 cubic meters with an assumed porosity of 1–2%. These estimates are
derived as rough approximations based on the ﬁndings of Tester et al. (2006), who analyzed various
EGS using microseismic data to estimate reservoir volumes. By combining the effective ﬂuid volume
within the fractures and the stimulated rock volume, the total ﬂuid volume in the fracture network is
estimated to be approximately 264,170 gallons. Altogether, the total estimated ﬂuid volume required
for the EGS system is 322,800 gallons.

Based on these volume estimates, the implementation cost of the oil-based ﬂuid (OBF) for the
reservoir would amount to approximately $ 24.21 million. Given the substantial initial investment,
further analysis is necessary to evaluate the ﬂuid’s long-term performance and its potential to
enhance heat extraction. In comparison, according to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency
(2024), the average cost of water for commercial use is $5.56 per 1,000 gallons, meaning the same
volume of water for the EGS system would cost only $2,214. This contrast highlights the importance
of conducting further research into the use of advanced geothermal heat transfer ﬂuids, particularly
to assess whether the long-term operational beneﬁts and improvements in power generation
efficiency justify the increased initial costs.

9. Milestone 6.1: Manufacture the fracture block test and use water to conduct pressure and
 ﬂow test using water.

A granite fracture core was prepared in accordance with the proposed experimental design using
American Pink and Sierra White core granite samples. The assembled test core was placed within a
high-pressure triaxial cell, and axial and conﬁning stresses were applied to replicate subsurface
stress environments. Deionized water was selected as the baseline ﬂuid for initial pressure and ﬂow
testing. Experiments were conducted at injection pressures of 3.45, 4.14, 4.83, and 5.52 MPa under
a constant outlet pressure of 0.69 MPa. Flow data collected under these conditions were consistent
and repeatable, conﬁrming the integrity and operational readiness of the fracture core system. These
results establish a performance baseline for subsequent evaluation of ionic liquids and alternate
ﬂuids under identical test conditions.

10. Milestone 6.2: Test the reversibility of the IL ﬂuids. For veriﬁcation, compare the measured
  pressure drop of the IL ﬂuids with the water pressure drop.

Reversibility testing was performed using synthesized ionic liquids (ILs), including [HPy][Br],
[HPy][BF₄], and BMiMBr, injected through the fracture core under controlled stress and temperature
conditions. The objective was to assess the ability of each IL to restore pre-ﬂow hydraulic
performance after injection, using deionized water as the reference ﬂuid.

Pressure drop measurements were recorded before and after IL injections at various temperatures
(20 °C and 50 °C) and differential pressures (3.45 to 5.52 MPa). Post-IL water ﬂow measurements
were used to evaluate the degree of reversibility based on changes in ﬂow rate and pressure drop.
Results demonstrated temperature-dependent reversibility, with improved recovery of ﬂow
characteristics at higher temperatures. For example, [HPy][Br] showed an increase in post-ﬂow
recovery from 44% at room temperature to 51% at 50 °C.

Comparison with baseline water ﬂow conﬁrmed that ILs inﬂuence fracture conductivity in a thermo-
responsive manner, and reversibility is measurable via pressure drop differentials. These ﬁndings
validate experimental design and conﬁrm that the reversibility behavior of ILs can be benchmarked
against water-based ﬂow performance.

22. Milestone 11.1: Test the reversibility of alternate ﬂuid at different ﬂow rates.

Reversibility testing was conducted using two alternate ﬂuids, WBF-2 and OBF-1, to assess their
capacity to restore baseline ﬂow conditions after injection through a fractured granite core. The ﬂuids
were tested at differential injection pressures of 3.45, 4.14, 4.83, and 5.52 MPa, corresponding to
varying ﬂow rate conditions. Each test was followed by post-ﬂow injection of deionized water to
evaluate the extent of ﬂow recovery.

Results indicated that WBF-2 exhibited high reversibility across all tested ﬂow rates, with minimal
deviation from baseline water performance. In contrast, OBF-1 showed limited ﬂow at lower
temperatures and pressures, resulting in delayed recovery, but reversibility improved at higher
temperatures and pressures. These outcomes suggest that ﬂow rate signiﬁcantly affects the

reversibility behavior of alternate ﬂuids, with lower-viscosity ﬂuids demonstrating more consistent
recovery across operating conditions.

The experimental ﬁndings conﬁrm that reversibility is ﬂow rate dependent and that certain alternate
ﬂuids, particularly WBF-2, are well-suited for temporary deployment in Enhanced Geothermal
Systems (EGS) without causing long-term ﬂow impairment.

23. Milestone 11.2: Test the reversibility of alternate ﬂuid at different temperatures.

Reversibility testing of alternate ﬂuids—WBF-2 and OBF-1—was conducted across a range of
temperatures (20 °C, 50 °C, and 80 °C) to evaluate the thermal responsiveness of each ﬂuid in
fractured granite core systems. After injecting the test ﬂuids at each temperature, deionized water
was reintroduced to measure the degree of ﬂow recovery, which served as an indicator of
reversibility.

The results showed that reversibility improved with increasing temperature for both ﬂuids. WBF-2
demonstrated consistent and high reversibility at all temperatures, with minimal deviation from the
baseline water ﬂow. OBF-1 exhibited poor reversibility at 20 °C due to its higher viscosity, but
performance signiﬁcantly improved at elevated temperatures, particularly at 80 °C.

These observations conﬁrm that reversibility is temperature-dependent, and that thermal activation
enhances ﬂuid removal from fracture surfaces. The outcomes support the viability of WBF-2 as a
highly reversible ﬂuid and suggest that OBF-1 may be effective in applications where elevated
reservoir temperatures are present.
