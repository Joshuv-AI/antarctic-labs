Tumor-responsive nanoparticles precisely overcome hypoxia-mediated STING dysfunction to unleash potent antitumor immunity
Authors: Linna Hai, Jian Wang, Dan Liu, Kaiqing Yun, Kongshuo Ma, Yuxuan Peng, Mengdi Xu, Xinbo Ma, Shan Gao, Qin Wang, Jiahui Zhou, Shuang Liang, Zhaohui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) pathway plays a central role in anti-tumor immunity by mobilizing both the innate and adaptive immunity. However, hypoxia, a tumor hallmark was shown to severely constrain immune responses and lead to suboptimal therapeutic efficacy. Through bioinformatic analyses, we revealed that hypoxia directly repress STING-pathway-related genes. Furthermore, we developed tumor-targeted pH-responsive nanoparticles (SPF) to overcome hypoxia-induced STING dysfunction for amplifying immune responses. The nanoparticles (NPs) effectively accumulate and reoxygenate the hypoxic tumor, achieving a potent STING activation with enhanced secretion of type-I IFNs and other inflammatory cytokines. Moreover, SPF are capable of decreasing Tregs and polarizing M2 to M1 macrophages, thus creating an immune-promoting microenvironment to unleash cascade immune response. In colon cancer models, SPF NPs strongly inhibited tumor growth with 33% tumor-free over 50 days and long-term immune memory. Importantly, robust STING activation was achieved in human tumor samples with a substantial 782.3-fold of IFN-β and 1683.8-fold CXCL10 over control, highlighting the significance of hypoxia overcoming on STING activation in clinical settings. This study demonstrates a tumor-responsive nanomedicine with precise spatiotemporal control of hypoxia relief to overcome STING resistance, guiding the rational design and clinical translation of therapeutics for next-generation cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has revolutionized oncology by harnessing the host immune system to eradicate malignancies through mechanisms such as T cell-mediated tumor clearance1, while the activation of tumor-specific T cells fundamentally relies on antigen-presenting cells (APCs) (e.g., dendritic cells, DCs) via innate immune pathways2. Among various immune pathways, Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) has emerged as a key bridge between innate and adaptive immunity by inducing type I interferons (IFNs) and cytokines to promote DC maturation and T cell priming3, remodeling of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME)4. However, accumulating evidence indicates that STING signaling is highly context-dependent and can be functionally attenuated in pathological microenvironments, particularly within solid tumors5,6. Consequently, substantial efforts have been devoted over the last decade to develop novel immunotherapeutic strategies that target STING innate pathways7, 8, 9, 10.Scheme 1Illustration of SPF that alleviates tumor hypoxia and remodels TME, amplifying the activation of STING signaling pathway for tumor immunotherapy.

Despite the therapeutic promise of STING activation, the clinical translation of STING agonists has been hindered by several intrinsic limitations11. Systemically administered STING agonists exhibit limited tumor accumulation and can induce off-target inflammatory responses, resulting in a narrow therapeutic window. Furthermore, increasing evidence from both patients and experimental models suggests the STING-mediated immune response is severely repressed in hypoxic environments, leading to suboptimal activation and allowing tumor cells to escape from immune surveillance12. Recent mechanistic studies have further highlighted that STING signaling is tightly regulated by cellular stress and microenvironmental cues, which critically shape the magnitude and quality of downstream immune responses13. Together, these limitations constrain the efficacy of STING monotherapy and underscore the need for strategies that enhance the bioactivity and tumor selectivity of STING agonists while mitigating hypoxia-driven suppression of STING signaling.

As a hallmark of solid tumors14, hypoxia exerts multifaceted immunosuppressive effects that impair antitumor immunity15, while its impact on STING signaling remains to be fully elucidated12. Our previous work revealed an indispensable role of DCs in STING-mediated antitumor immunity—genetic ablation of conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1s) completely abolished the therapeutic efficacy of STING agonists in murine models16. However, while current efforts on hypoxic inhibition of the STING pathway are limited to tumor cells, the corresponding effects on DCs has not yet been well revealed17,18. Given the critical significance of DCs-conditioned STING activation, dissecting how hypoxia disables this axis is therefore essential for STING-mediated cancer immunotherapy. Furthermore, restoring STING signaling by overcoming the hypoxic status inside the TME could be a potent strategy to augment the activating magnitude and duration of STING agonists, amplifying the anti-tumor immune response and advancing our understanding of STING signaling under hypoxic conditions.

Various oxygen-carrying delivery vehicles have been developed to address the severe immunosuppressive effect of tumor hypoxia19,20. However, the premature leakage from current nanoparticles (NPs) challenges the efficiency of hypoxic tumor reoxygenation and thus limits the activation of the STING pathway21. Moreover, the simple mixture of STING agonists and hypoxia-overcoming nanomaterials for in vivo administration resulted in chaotic dissemination, which hinders the efficient modulation of the immune system and clinical usage by transient responses and various adverse effects. Therefore, it remains a challenge to achieve the precise spatiotemporal control of STING stimulation by efficient hypoxia relief and targeted delivery of agonists, which is urgently needed for robust tumor-specific response but seldom explored.

pH-responsive nanomedicine has emerged as a promising platform for targeted delivery and controlled release of drugs for precision cancer treatment22. We herein pioneered a copolymer design strategy (SPF) by integrating oxygen-carrying monomers with ultra-pH-sensitive diisopropylaminoethyl methacrylate (DPA) monomers in a highly controllable manner to alleviate hypoxia and amplify STING activation for enhanced cancer immunotherapy. The pKa of the developed polymer was precisely tuned to 6.1, optimized to match the severely polarized extracellular acidity (as low as 5.3), granting sharp pH responsiveness and precise TME targeting capability23. The NPs remain as intact micelles with minimal leakage at physiological pH (7.4) during blood circulation but disassemble into unimers upon exposure to the tumor acidic milieu, rapidly releasing the incorporated STING agonist and O2. SPF effectively restores STING activity through a synergistic mechanism, thereby inducing the secretion of IFN-I and other inflammatory cytokines to promote DC maturation and activation, and activate NK cells and specific T cell immune response. The SPF led to potent tumor growth inhibition in colorectal cancer models after systemic intravenous administration. Notably, robust STING activation was observed in human colorectal tumor samples, indicating potential for clinical impact. Together, by leveraging hypoxia alleviation as a ubiquitous amplifier of STING signaling with precision nano-engineering, our approach may provide a transformative avenue for overcoming immune resistance and guide the rational design of nanomedicine for cancer immunotherapy (Scheme 1).

Western blot analysis demonstrated the efficient activation of STING pathway of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) in normoxic conditions, while the STING pathway phosphorylation (STING, TBK1, NF-κB) was disrupted under hypoxic environment (5% O2)24, even with the treatment with agonist SR717 (Fig. 1e, Supporting Information Fig. S11). Design and development of SPF NPs

Based on the immunosuppressive effects of tumor hypoxia on the activation of STING pathway, we are motivated to design an innovative polymeric nanoparticle composed of O2-carrying perfluorocarbon, pH-sensitive tertiary amines, and a hydrophilic PEG block (PEG-b-P(DPA-HFMA-AMA), PF) to augment the activating magnitude of STING agonists (Fig. 2a). PF was meticulously synthesized through the copolymerization of hexafluorobutyl methacrylate (HFMA) and 2-(diisopropylamino)ethyl methacrylate (DPA) with fine-tuning ratios using atom transfer radical polymerization techniques (Supporting Information Fig. S2)25. The successful synthesis was confirmed via 1H NMR and 19F NMR spectroscopy (Supporting Information Figs. The copolymer self-assembles into pH-responsive nanomicelles when incorporating STING agonist SR717 (SPF). For comparative purposes, empty micelles prepared without SR717 were designated as PF, and micelles containing SR717 but lacking the perfluoropolymer component were designated as SP22. Morphological analysis by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that SPF is spherical and remains stable at pH 7.4 but disassembles at pH 5.5 (Fig. 2b). This pH-responsive behavior was attributed to the the tertiary amine groups near their pKa, demonstrating precise sensitivity to TME (Supporting Information Fig. S5). To further corroborate the pH-triggered disassembly process, fluorescence dequenching experiments were performed using SPFCy5 nanoparticles. As shown in Supporting Information Fig. S6, a pronounced fluorescence activation occurred around pH 6.2, corresponding to nanomicelle disassembly. The fluorescence intensity exhibited a sharp, threshold-like transition with an approximate 100-fold on/off contrast, confirming the high pH sensitivity of SPF under acidic tumor-relevant conditions. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed an average diameter of 43.8 nm with a positive zeta potential, which may facilitate cell association and further internalization (Fig. 2c and d).

The long-term stability and systemic administration stability of PF, SP, and SPF were monitored by DLS. Measurements were taken at 4 °C in PBS and at 37 °C in both PBS and DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). No significant change in average diameter was observed under any of these conditions (Supporting Information Fig. S7). Collectively, these results demonstrate that all nanoparticle formulations evaluated in this study possess sufficient stability for both storage and subsequent biological applications.

To investigate the O2 carrying and releasing capability of SPF, a portable dissolved oxygen meter was used to measure O2 concentrations in various solutions. An extraordinary increase of O2 was observed after adding the pre-oxygenated PF solution to deoxygenated water, followed by a sustained level for over 2 min, indicating efficient O2 supplementation (Fig. 2e, Supporting Information Fig. S8). Next, the release profile of O2 was further studied in solution with different pH. In a deoxygenated flask at pH 5.5, PF solution resulted in a remarkable increase of O2 in the gas phase, reaching a peak as early as 5 min (Fig. 2f). In contrast, minimal O2 release was shown under physiological pH 7.4. The pH-specific and efficient O2 release may overcome the premature leakage of current NPs, thus preserving O2 during circulation and allowing targeted release in acidic tumor sites.

The loading content of SR717 was quantified at 5.9% using high-performance liquid chromatography (Supporting Information Fig. S9), and the release kinetics from SPF was evaluated by an ultrafiltration method. As shown in Fig. 2g, a slight release of SR717 was observed at pH 7.4 across different time points up to 12 h, while almost complete release was obtained within 1 h at pH 5.5. These results indicated that the SPF can remain as intact micelles at neutral pH during systemic circulation while dissociating into unimers when reaching acidic TME to release SR717 and O2 promptly, allowing the spatiotemporal orchestration of STING innate stimulation with hypoxia alleviation.

Given the pH-responsive release properties of SR717 and O2, we next investigated the STING signaling pathway using THP1-Lucia STING Reporter Cells27. The cells were seeded in 96-well plates and then treated with PBS, PF, SR717, and SPF to detect the induction of IFN-I at different time points. As illustrated by Fig. 3c, PF yielded negligible IFN-I induction over time, while modest STING activation was shown by SR717 treatment. In contrast, SPF treatment resulted in the highest level of STING activation, reaching a peak at 24 h. Importantly, durable activation was also demonstrated, as evidenced by a robust IFN-I induction at 24 h with 2.9- and 12.5-fold over that of SR717 and PF, respectively.

As we rationally hypothesized that SPF would maximize STING activation by overcoming hypoxia-induced suppression (Fig. 3d), the marked drop of HIF-1α protein was clearly observed, which is typically upregulated under hypoxia (Fig. 3e). In the meantime, the activating effect of SPF under hypoxia was further investigated in BMDCs. SPF successfully activated the STING pathway even under hypoxic conditions, leading to elevated phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and NF-κB, which is in line with the result from reporter cells. Specifically, SPF induced higher p-STING protein levels, with 2.0- and 6.0-fold over that of SR717 and PBS under hypoxia, respectively (Fig. 3f). Concurrently, SP was unable to elicit the same response in the STING/TBK1/NF-κB pathway as SPF did under hypoxic conditions, achieving only 57.6% of the p-STING protein level observed in the SPF group, underscoring the pivotal role of oxygen supplementation in STING phosphorylation (Fig. S11).

As a primary IFN-I that is activated by the STING pathway, IFN-β plays a crucial role in enhancing antitumor immune responses by promoting dendritic cell maturation and activating CD8+ T cells and NK cells (Fig. 3g). The potent STING activation by SPF resulted in upregulated secretion of IFN-β and other pro-inflammatory cytokines. Expectedly, the secretion of IFN-β in the SPF group was 3.5-, 2.2-, and 1.7-fold over those with PBS, SR717, and PF, respectively (Fig. 3h). Similar trends were seen in other pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as C-X-C motif chemokine 10 (CXCL10) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which are essential for modulating the immune response against tumors (Fig. 3i and j)28. The secretion of IFN-β by SP is significantly compromised compared to SPF under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 3k), which further confirms the substantial impact of oxygen supplementation on STING phosphorylation (Supporting Information Figs. S11 and S12).

To explore the STING activating effect on the maturation and activation of DCs, we co-cultured BMDCs with SPF for the detection of co-stimulatory molecules using flow cytometry. SPF markedly promoted DC maturation, as evidenced by the heightened expression of co-stimulatory molecules CD40, CD80, and CD86 (Fig. 3l‒o) as well as antigen-presenting molecules MHC-I and MHC-II expression (Fig. 3p and q) on BMDCs compared to PBS and SR717 treatments, allowing for the initiation of immune response29. Collectively, SPF effectively overcomes hypoxia-mediated immune suppression, enhancing the magnitude and duration of STING-mediated immune responses, and promoting the secretion of IFN-β and inflammatory cytokines, thereby achieving robust immune activation.

Hypoxia alleviation and STING activation of SPF in vivo

Having verified the synergistic effect of hypoxia relief on STING activation in vitro, we next evaluated whether SPF could reverse tumor hypoxia and activate STING efficiently in vivo. Photoacoustic (PA) imaging was employed to monitor the levels of deoxygenated hemoglobin (Hb) and oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO2) (Fig. 4a)30. The results showed that tumors in the PBS and SR717 groups were severely hypoxic. Interestingly, both PF and SPF treatments led to a remarkable upregulation of HbO2/Hb ratio within tumor tissues, suggesting effective hypoxia alleviation through successful delivery and release of oxygen into the TME (Fig. 4b and c)31. We thus examined the expression of transcriptional factor HIF-1α, which has emerged as a key regulator of hypoxic response. Consequently, we quantified IFN-β secretion to confirm the activation of STING signaling pathway in the tumor. SR717 and PF induced negligible increases of IFN-β when compared with PBS control as measured by ELISA, while a dramatic increase was observed in the SPF-treated group. The IFN-β level stimulated by SPF was 6.7-, 3.0-, and 4.8-fold over that of PBS, SR717, and PF groups, respectively (Fig. 4e), indicating efficient STING activation. The immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections revealed markedly intensified green fluorescence (CD11c) in the SPF group, indicating the increased DC recruitment to the tumor site, while SR717 and PF failed to induce this effect. Further analysis showed that the SR717-treated group exhibited moderate red fluorescence intensity, indicative of mild STING pathway activation. However, SPF treatment generated red fluorescence intensity to a greater extent, with substantial co-localization of red (p-STING) and green (CD11c), highlighting enhanced STING activation mostly within DCs (Fig. 4f). Taken together, these data demonstrated the capability of SPF in overcoming hypoxia and reinvigorating STING activation in vivo, underscoring the therapeutic potential of SPF in hypoxic solid tumors.

Encouraged by the robust hypoxia-alleviating, STING-activating, and tumor-targeting capabilities of SPF, we next evaluated its anti-tumor efficacy. Mice with tumor volumes of approximately 50 mm3 were treated every 3 days with PBS, SR717, PF, or SPF (Fig. 5c)32. Based on the drug loading content of 5.9%, the administered dose of SR717 in the nanoparticle formulations was 5.9 mg/kg. At this dose, treatment with free SR717 or PF resulted in only modest reductions in tumor volume, indicating limited therapeutic activity. In stark contrast, the SPF group exhibited a superior therapeutic effect with an inhibition rate of 88.9%, resulting in complete regression (CR) in 33% of the tumors (Fig. 5d‒g, Supporting Information Figs. S14 and S15). While all mice in the control group succumbed by day 22, 33% of mice in the SPF treatment group survived to Day 110 (Supporting Information Fig. S16). Furthermore, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of tumor tissues showed that SPF treatment induced higher levels of tumor cell apoptosis and necrosis than other groups (Fig. 5h), confirming its superior anti-tumor efficacy.

Furthermore, a tumor rechallenge assessment was conducted to evaluate the immune memory effect of SPF (Fig. 5j). Mice previously treated with SPF displayed considerable resistance to newly injected tumor cells, while such tumors developed rapidly in naive mice (Fig. 5k). The data suggest that SPF activates memory T cells for a durable antitumor response.

No obvious loss of body weight was observed in each group (Fig. 5i). Histological analyses of the liver, heart, lungs, spleen, and kidneys showed no signs of short-term systemic toxicity, underscoring the excellent biosafety of SPF (Supporting Information Fig. S17). Meanwhile, the regulatory T cell (Treg) ratios in the spleen were not significantly changed, indicating SPF did not trigger a systemic immune storm (Supporting Information Fig. S18)33. Additionally, IFN-γ levels in peripheral blood showed no significant difference among all groups (Supporting Information Fig. S19). Additionally, the biocompatibility of SPF was also supported by no apparent toxicity in DC2.4 cells at concentrations up to 400 μg/mL and no obviously hemolytic effect (Supporting Information Figs. S20 and S21).

To further explore the compatibility of SPF with current clinically approved therapeutics, the SPF NPs was combined with anti-PD-1 antibody therapy. Remarkably, the combination treatment synergistically enhanced tumor growth suppression and increased the complete regression rate from 33% to 50% (Fig. 5l‒n, Supporting Information Fig. S22), demonstrating a synergistic effect with checkpoint blockade immunotherapy, probably attributed to SPF-induced T cell priming and PD-1-mediated reversal of T cell exhaustion.

Mechanistic insights of the anti-tumor immune response

Motivated by the superior anti-tumor efficacy of SPF, we further investigated the underlying mechanisms. To determine the impact of SPF on the activity of immune cells in vivo, we isolated spleens, lymph nodes, and tumors from treated mice and ground them into single-cell suspensions for analysis. As the robust IFN-β secretion was described previously (Fig. 4e), we next examined the expression of STING-related cytokines to investigate the correlation between the anti-tumor effect and STING activation. SPF treatment upregulated CXCL10 levels dramatically , being 8.5 times higher than that of SR717 at the same dose (Fig. 6a). As anticipated, similar trends were observed for other pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), TNF-α, interleukin-2 (IL-2), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) (Fig. 6b‒e), which were 4.2-, 2.7-, 3.9-, and 1.4-fold higher than SR717 treatment, respectively. Previous in vitro studies demonstrated that SPF strongly stimulates DC maturation and activation for initiating immune responses. To explore the DC activation in vivo, we isolated the lymph nodes and measured the percentage of CD80 and CD86 using flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 6f, SPF-treated mice exhibited significantly elevated expression of DC maturation markers CD80 and CD86 compared to those treated with PBS, SR717, or PF NPs, implying SPF effectively induced DC maturation for initiating a subsequent anti-tumor immune response.

Natural killer (NK) cells, as innate cytotoxic lymphocytes, can directly kill tumor cells or reinforce adaptive immunity34. Our results showed a pronounced proliferation and activation of NK cells in the spleen following SPF treatment, while both SR717 and PF failed to generate this effect. SPF markedly promoted the activation of NK cells, which was 2.1-, 1.7-, and 1.9-fold over that of PBS, SR717, and PF treatments, respectively (Fig. 6g and h). Similarly, NK cell activation was also observed in the lymph nodes (Fig. 6i).

To further investigate the effects of SPF on adaptive immunity, we quantified CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the spleen and tumor tissues35. In tumor tissue, SPF treatment increased infiltration of T cells (Supporting Information Fig. S23), and CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations increased by 1.6- and 1.4-fold, respectively, compared to the SR717 group (Fig. 6j and k). Moreover, SPF treatment significantly boosted intratumoral IFN-γ production by CD8+ T cells by 1.8-fold over SR717-treated mice, contributing to cytotoxic effects through the induction of apoptosis or promotion of non-apoptotic cell death (Fig. 6l). However, only mild CD4+ and CD8+ effects were observed in mice treated with PF and SR717. In addition, the robust CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses were also shown in the spleen, suggesting that SPF promotes systemic immune response (Fig. 6m and n). Furthermore, the percentage of CD8+ central memory T cells (TCM, defined as CD44+ CD62L+) was markedly boosted, suggesting that SPF enhances the activation and differentiation of memory cells into effector T cells, thereby inducing long-term immune memory for sustained tumor inhibition (Fig. 6o and p).

The immunosuppressive TME plays a critical role in the resistance of tumors to immunotherapy. To test the potential of SPF in reversing suppression of TME, we analyzed the expression of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and Tregs using flow cytometry. PF treatment significantly increased the M1/M2 macrophage ratio (CD80+/CD206+ cells), with a 1.6-fold increase compared to PBS. Notably, SPF further enhanced this ratio to 2.6-fold over PBS, indicating hypoxia alleviation within the tumor can repolarize TAMs toward a more pro-inflammatory and tumoricidal M1 phenotype (Fig. 6q). Additionally, SPF reduced Treg within CD4+ T cells by 55.8%, while SR717 and PF did not induce considerable Tregs reduction (Fig. 6r). These data indicated that SPF favors an immune-promoting TME for the strengthened killing effect of immune cells.

Altogether, the powerful anti-tumor immune responses induced by SPF were mediated by multiple mechanisms. SPF effectively alleviated tumor hypoxia and amplified STING activation, enhancing the secretion of IFN-I and other pro-inflammatory factors for a cascade immune response. Moreover, SPF significantly decreased the ratio of Tregs, polarized M2 macrophages to M1 type, reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment into a supportive one and thus restoring the killing ability of immune cells.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further corroborated these results, identifying key pathways associated with immune regulation and tumor suppression (Fig. 7e). Enriched pathways included cytokine activity, response to tumor necrosis factor, response to IFN-γ, chemokine-mediated signaling pathway, CXCR chemokine receptor binding, and lymphocyte chemotaxis—all critical for bridging innate immune activation (via STING) to adaptive T cell responses. Additionally, pathways related to microenvironmental regulation, such as oxygen transport and cellular pH homeostasis, were significantly modulated, directly linking SPF’s pH-triggered oxygen release to the transcriptome level. The statistical robustness of these pathways was underscored by their high enrichment scores (‒log10(value) range: 2–8) and gene counts (2–7 genes per pathway), supporting the role of SPF in reprogramming immune and metabolic processes within the TME.

To better understand the impact of SPF treatment on immune-related gene expression, a heatmap of differentially expressed immune-related genes was generated (Fig. 7f). This analysis showed enhanced expression of key immune-related genes following SPF treatment, suggesting a reprogramming of the TME to promote immune activity. In contrast, there was a pronounced downregulation of genes associated with tumor invasion and migration, which corresponds to the remarkable efficacy of SPF. Additionally, protein–protein interaction networks of key proteins involved in STING activation were constructed (Fig. 7g), revealing significant interactions between proteins critical for immune responses. Furthermore, a chord diagram and gene concept network were generated to illustrate the biological processes associated with immune responses, showing the enrichment of these processes in the SPF-treated group. The different colors in the diagram correspond to distinct categories of immune-related processes (Fig. 7h, Supporting Information Fig. S24). Collectively, these results suggest that SPF treatment reprograms the TME by alleviating immunosuppression and enhancing tumor-specific immune responses, thereby contributing to immune activation and tumor suppression.

Chemokines CXCL10 and CXCL9 facilitate the infiltration of effector T cells and NK cells into the TME, which is essential for converting “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors, thereby strengthening the antitumor immune response. Chemokines CCL4 and CCL5 have been reported to regulate the tumor migration of subsets of innate and adaptive immune cells, including DCs, macrophages, and T cells. As expected, free drug exhibited minimal up-regulation in mRNA expression of CCL4 and CCL5 compared to the PBS group, while the encapsulation of SR717 into pH-sensitive NPs substantially improved the cytokine levels. Remarkably, SPF further induced a 4.5- and 4.3-fold enhancement of CCL4 and CCL5 mRNA expressions over that of SP, respectively, demonstrating its superior potency in recruiting immune cells into the TME (Fig. 8e and f).

Collectively, SPF induced potent activation of STING signaling in human tumor tissues and increased intra-tumoral levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, supporting its clinical translational potential. The data also highlight the significance for oxygen on STING activation, facilitating the identification of mechanisms underlying antitumor immune activation and the development of hypoxia-overcoming strategies for cancer immunotherapy.

Discussion

The cGAS-STING pathway plays a central role in mobilizing both innate and adaptive immunity, which is critical for achieving long-term tumor control. However, as a hallmark of tumors, hypoxia has been shown to severely constrain STING activation, thereby limiting the clinical efficacy of STING-targeted therapies. Although immune cells are essential for STING-mediated antitumor responses, the impact of hypoxia on STING signaling remains poorly understood, and effective strategies to modulate this interaction are still needed. In this study, bioinformatic analysis of colorectal cancer cohorts revealed that hypoxia is associated with suppressed STING signaling, characterized by reduced immune cell infiltration and diminished type I interferon responses. These findings suggest that alleviating intratumoral hypoxia may represent a promising strategy to restore the STING pathway and enhance antitumor immunity.

To address this limitation, we developed SPF, an innovative pH-responsive nanoplatform to amplify STING activation by overcoming hypoxia-induced immunosuppression. Oxygen-carrying perfluorocarbon was incorporated into a PEGylated ultra-pH-sensitive copolymer in a highly controllable manner, serving as a reservoir for tunable and stable O2 delivery. The innovative polymer retains the sharp pH-responsiveness to avoid premature leakage during circulation before reaching the targeted tissue of , which is a common limitation of current delivery system. Upon reaching the acidic tumor site, SPF disassembles into unimers and rapidly releases the STING agonist and oxygen to achieve robust immune activation. SPF NPs efficiently alleviate hypoxia and overcome its suppressive effect on immune activation. Consequently, potent and durable STING activation is achieved even in hypoxic conditions, enhancing the secretion of type I IFNs and other inflammatory cytokines to promote DC maturation and activation, and activate NK cells and specific T cell immune response. Notably, SPF also relieves the immunosuppressive TME through decreasing Tregs and polarizing M2 to M1 macrophages, thus creating a “hot” TME for the enhanced effect of immune cells. In murine colon cancer models, systemic administration of SPF strongly inhibited tumor growth and achieved a 33% tumor-free rate. It is noteworthy that therapeutic efficacy was achieved at a relatively modest drug loading content and systemic dose (5.9 mg/kg SR717), which is substantially lower than that typically required for non-carrier-assisted delivery36. This enhancement is likely attributable to improved tumor accumulation, enhanced stability, and controlled release facilitated by the carrier system. Remarkably, ex vivo analysis of patient-derived colorectal cancer samples treated with SPF showed a 782.3-fold increase in IFN-β expression, showing potential for clinical impact and highlighting the significance of oxygen on STING activation.

Beyond colorectal cancer, this approach could be extended to diverse hypoxic solid tumors, including the 4T1 breast carcinoma model (Supporting Information Fig. S25). Future work will explore the combination of SPF with other clinically approved therapeutic modalities, e.g., cancer vaccines or adoptive cell therapies, to further amplify antitumor immunity. Collectively, we demonstrate a tumor-responsive nanomedicine with precise spatiotemporal control of hypoxia relief to overcome STING resistance and amplify immune activation.

Monomer 2-aminoethyl methacrylate (AMA) was purchased from Damas-beta, and recrystallized with isopropanol and ethyl acetate (1:1). Monomer 2-(perfluorohexyl) allyl methacrylate (HFMA) was purchased from Aladin (Shanghai, China). Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound was purchased from SAKURA Finetek Co. BSA and 2-(4-Amidinophenyl)-6-indolecarbamidine dihydrochloride (DAPI) were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). GM-CSF and IL-4 were purchased from Pepro Tech (Cranbury, USA). All antibodies used for flow cytometry were purchased from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, USA). All ELISA kits were purchased from Elabscience (Wuhan, China). Calcein/PI Cytotoxicity Assay Kit and BCA Protein Assay Kit were obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). The antibodies used in flow cytometry were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, America). Most of the antibodies used in Western blot include P-TBK1/NAK (5483S), P-STING (72971S), P-NF-κB (3033S), and HIF-1α (36169T) were obtained from CST (Massachusetts, USA).

Synthesis of PEG-b-P(DPA-HFMA-AMA): The synthesis of copolymers poly (ethylene glycol)-block-[2-(diisopropylamino) ethyl methacrylate]-co-[2-(perfluorohexyl) allyl methacrylate]-co-[(2-aminoethyl methacrylate] [PEG-b-P(DPA-HFMA-AMA)], abbreviated as PF, were obtained via atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP). Briefly, mPEG-Br (250 mg, 0.05 mmol), AMA (45 mg, 0.27 mmol), DPA (700 mg, 3.28 mmol), HFMA (400 mg, 0.93 mmol), and PMDETA (13 μL, 0.06 mmol) were dissolved in 1 mL of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and 1 mL of isopropanol (IPA). After three freeze-pump-thaw cycles, CuBr (8.5 mg, 0.06 mmol) was added to the above solutions and allowed to react at 42 °C overnight. After the reaction, CuBr was removed by a neutral Al2O3 column, and the organic solvent and excess reactants were removed by dialysis. The degree of polymerization was calculated, and the chemical structure was confirmed by 1H-NMR and 19F-NMR.

Synthesis of PEG-b-P(DPA-HFMA-Cy5) (PFCy5): PFCy5 was synthesized by an amide reaction. 2.5 mg of Cy5-NHS were mixed with 50 mg of PF and dissolved in 1 mL of THF. The resulting solution was stirred at 25 °C overnight and purified by dialysis.

Preparation of PF NPs: For the preparation of PF NPs, PF (5 mg) were dissolved in 200 μL methanol (CH3OH) The organic solvent was then dispersed into 4 mL deionized water under ultrasonic conditions with a power of 70 W to form NPs, followed by the removal of residual CH3OH through an ultrafiltration with a molecular weight cutoff of 100 kDa.

Preparation of SPF NPs: The preparation procedure of SPF was as follows: PF (5 mg) and SR717 (0.5 mg) were dissolved in 200 μL of methanol (CH3OH) solution. The organic solvent was then dispersed into 4 mL deionized water under ultrasonic conditions with a power of 70 W to form encapsulated NPs. Unpackaged SR717 was removed by centrifugation at a speed of 10,000 rpm for 5 min, followed by the removal of residual CH3OH through an ultrafiltration tube with a molecular weight cutoff of 100 kDa.

Fluorescence dequenching assay: Fluorescence dequenching experiments were conducted using SPFCy5. Briefly, SPFCy5 were dispersed in buffer solutions with pH values ranging from 4.0 to 7.4 at 0.2-unit intervals. After equilibration, fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence spectrometer (λex = 630 nm, λem = 670 nm). SPFCy5 nanoparticles fully dissolved in DMF were used as the fluorescence reference and defined as 100%. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

In vitro release of SR717: SPF was incubated in PBS solution at pH 7.4 and 5.5 at 37 °C with constant stirring to study the release of SR717. At the time intervals of 0.25, 0.5, 1, 3, and 6 h, aliquots were taken, filtered through an ultrafiltration membrane, and the supernatant was analyzed using a spectrophotometer set at a wavelength of 260 nm to determine the concentration of released SR717.

In vitro release of oxygen: PF was dispersed in PBS solution at pH 7.4 and 5.5. The PF solution (pH 7.4, 5.5) and pure water were slowly filled with oxygen until the saturation point of oxygen solubility was reached. Upon reaching this saturation point, the solution was transferred to an airtight anaerobic round-bottom flask. Subsequently, the oxygen instrument electrode was introduced into the surrounding air above the solution, and the O2 release was calculated until the oxygen solubility reached a balance.

Cell cultures: THP1-Lucia™ STING Reporter Cells, DC2.4 mouse dendritic cell line, and CT26 murine colorectal cancer cell line were incubated with RPMI 1640 medium with 10% FBS and antibiotics (penicillin 100 U/mL and streptomycin 100 μg/mL). All cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cellular uptake of SPF: DC2.4 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in a 24-well plate and allowed to adhere overnight. After that, SPFCy5 NPs (100 μg/mL) were cultured with DC2.4 cells for 1 h, and the nuclei were stained with 2-(4-amidinophenyl)-6-indolecarbamidine dihydrochloride (DAPI). The endocytosis of SPFCy5 NPs by DC2.4 cells was observed using an EbbaBiotech fluorescence microscope. To further investigate the endocytic mechanism, the cells were pretreated with endocytosis inhibitors such as chlorpromazine (CPZ), amiloride (AMR), methyl β-cyclodextrin (Mβ-CD), genistein, cytochalasin D (CytD), and chloroquine (CQ) for 1 h before NP incubation. After PBS washing, flow cytometry was employed to measure the mean fluorescence intensity.

Maturation and activation of BMDCs: Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were obtained to examine the relevant immune indicators in vitro. Specifically, bone marrow cells derived from the femur and tibia of mice were cultured with a RPMI 1640 medium containing GM-CSF (1 × 103 U/mL) and IL-4 (1 × 103 U/mL). The culture medium was changed every other day and cells were collected on the 7th day for the following research. To evaluate the activation of BMDCs stimulated by various NPs, DCs were co-cultured with different treatments on 24-well plates (SR717: 5.96 μg/mL, PF: 100 μg/mL, SPF 100 μg/mL) for 24 h and stained with anti-CD40, anti-CD80, and anti-CD11c antibodies. Then, flow cytometry was performed to detect the expression of co-stimulators. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were employed to measure the secretion level of cytokines in cell supernatants, including IFN-β, CXCL10, and TNF-α.

Western blot: The expression of phosphorylated IRF3 (p-IRF3), phosphorylated STING (p-STING), and phosphorylated TBK1 (p-TBK1) following activation of the STING signaling pathway was assessed through Western blot analysis. BMDC were cultured under different oxygen concentrations (21% O2 and 5% O2) for 3 h before being co-cultured with various formulations (SR717: 5.96 μg/mL, PF: 100 μg/mL, SPF 100 μg/mL) or (SR717 20 μg/mL). Following PBS washing, proteins were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer within 5 min of removal from the 5% O2 environment, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, 0.45 μm) membrane. The membrane was blocked with NcmBlot Blocking Buffer and incubated overnight with primary antibodies, including P-TBK1, P-STING, P-NF-κB, HIF-1α rabbit antibodies, and β-actin mouse antibodies. Goat anti-rabbit IgG and rabbit anti-goat IgG secondary antibodies were added, and the solution was incubated for another 1 h. Finally, ECL substrate western blotting was performed to visualize protein bands using chemiluminescence detection.

Animals: BALB/c mice (female, 18‒20 g) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking Union Medical College.

In vivo tumor targeting: CT26 tumor cells were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal region of mice to establish a colorectal tumor model. Afterwards, a dose of 2 mg of SPFCy5 was intravenously administered to the tumor-bearing mice. The accumulation of SPFCy5 in the tumor was observed using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) at various time intervals (0, 1, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h). The fluorescence intensity was calculated, and the fluorescence intensity curve was plotted with time. Additionally, the mice were sacrificed at 24 h post-administration, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected for ex vivo imaging.

In vivo anti-tumor efficacy: The CT26-bearing mice models were generated by subcutaneously injection of 1.2 × 106 CT26 cells in the right flank of BALB/c mice. All mice were randomly divided into four groups and treated with PBS, SR717 (120 μg/mouse), PF NPs (2 mg/mouse), and SPF NPs (2 mg/mouse) via intravenous injection every three days. The tumor volume and weight of mice were recorded every two days. At the end of the study, all mice were sacrificed, and tumors, spleen, and lymph nodes were harvested for the subsequent analysis of the immune effect.

Re-challenge of CT26 tumor cells: 60 days after the treatment of SPF NPs, the cured mice were inoculated with 1.2 × 106 CT26 tumor cells. The tumor volume of the mice was recorded every two days. After an interval of 18 days, the animals underwent sacrifice, followed by the harvesting of spleens for detecting central memory T cells and effector memory T cells using flow cytometry.

In vivo immune activation analysis: The tumors, spleens, and lymph nodes of the treatment mice were collected after the mice were euthanized. The organs were ground into a single-cell suspension and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. The cells treated with fluorescent-labeled antibodies: mature DCs in lymph nodes (CD11c+CD40+, CD11c+CD80+, CD11c+CD86+), T cells in lymph nodes (CD3+CD8+, CD3+CD4+), T cells in spleen (CD3+CD8+, CD3+CD4+), NK cells in spleen (CD49b+CD107a+), regulatory T cells (Tregs) in spleen (CD3+CD4+Foxp3+) and tumor infiltrating T cells (CD3+CD4+, CD3+CD8+), M1 macrophages (F4/80+CD80+), M2 macrophages (F4/80+CD206+), IFN-γ+ T cells (CD3+CD8+ IFN-γ+), Treg cells (CD3+CD4+Foxp3+). The cells were stained at 4 °C for 30 min. The detailed flow cytometry gating strategy used for all immune-cell subsets is provided in Supporting Information Fig. S26. After washing with PBS, DC maturation in lymph nodes and the effect of immune response of T lymphocytes in lymph nodes and spleens were analyzed by flow cytometry. The supernatant cell suspension was collected after centrifugation, and the content of cytokines (IFN-β, TNF-α, CXCL-10, IL-6, IFN-γ, and IL-2) was measured with ELISA kits to evaluate the immune response mechanism in vivo.

H&E staining: After all mice were sacrificed, hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys, and tumors were collected and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, followed by microscopic observation of the pathology of each tissue.

RNA sequencing analysis: The cDNA library construction, library purification, and transcriptome sequencing were carried out according to the Beijing Glbizzia Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Three samples were used in each group for RNA-sequence assay. Gene-level quantification was performed for each sample using featureCounts, and gene expression matrices were subsequently generated and combined across all samples. Gene expression levels were normalized and represented as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression analysis was conducted based on normalized expression values. P values were adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing to obtain q values, and genes with |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 and q value < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Investigation of STING activation in resected human tumor tissues by RT–qPCR: All patients enrolled in the study provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Cancer Institute & Hospital (protocol number: EK20240248; Ek2023150). Freshly resected human tissues (colorectal cancer tissues) were rinsed and divided into several sections using a scalpel, followed by injection at multiple sites using 5% glucose control, SR717, PF, SP, SPF. Each section was cultured in 1 mL RPMI 1640 medium (supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated human serum, 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium, 1% GlutaMAX, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin) in a 24-well plate for 24 h. Total RNAs were extracted from human tumor tissues by using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen). RNA was then subjected to quantity and quality detection by using the NanoDrop (DeNovix DS-11) system. The RNA purity was further confirmed by removing genomic DNA, and then cDNA was synthesized by using the iScript gDNA clear cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). Universal SYBR green fast qPCR mix and CFX connect real-time system were used for PCR analysis. The following primers were used for qPCR: human IFNB1: forward: GTCTCCTCCAAATTGCTCTC, reverse: ACAGGAGCTTCTGACACTGA; human CXCL10: forward: TGGCATTCAAGGAGTACCTC, reverse: TTGTAGCAATGATCTCAACACG; human ACTIN: forward: GGACTTCGAGCAAGAGATGG, reverse: AGGAAGGAAGGCTGGAAGAG.

Statistical analysis: All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated. Sample size (n) is indicated in each figure legend. using GraphPad software for statistical analysis, and the differences were assessed using ANOVA or the Student’s t-test. P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. For multiple comparisons, data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. When the ANOVA showed a significant main effect, Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was applied as the post-hoc procedure to compare each group with the final formulation group (SPF). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
