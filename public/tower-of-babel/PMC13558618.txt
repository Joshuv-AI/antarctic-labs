Changes in obesity and waist circumference in children and parents during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Yulika Yoshida-Montezuma, Charles D G Keown-Stoneman, Joseph Jamnik, Brendan T Smith, Roman Pabayo, Laura M Kinlin, Leigh M Vanderloo, Jessica Omand, Jonathon L Maguire, Catherine S Birken, Laura N Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 pandemic disrupted daily behaviours. We compared the annual change in obesity prevalence before and during the pandemic in children and parents. Participants were recruited from TARGet Kids! in Canada and followed prospectively. Each participant had at least one body mass index (BMI) measure before and during the pandemic. Marginal logistic models with piecewise functions were used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for the association between year and obesity prevalence before (2015–2020) and during the pandemic (2020–2023). Among children (n = 1,163), in the 5 years pre-pandemic, the average annual increase in the odds of obesity was 18% per year (aOR = 1.18, 95%CI 1.02–1.36).This increased substantially early in the pandemic (aOR = 2.17, 95%CI 1.45–3.25), and during the second year of the pandemic it decreased (aOR = 0.66, 95%CI 0.46–0.93). Among parents (n = 653), pre-pandemic there was an annual increase in the odds of obesity (aOR = 1.27, 95%CI 1.12–1.42) but this plateaued during the early (aOR = 0.79, 95%CI 0.50–1.25) and later pandemic periods (aOR = 1.08, 95% CI:0.81–1.43). Increases in child obesity during the pandemic were greatest among lower income families and younger children. Introduction

Obesity is a chronic complex disease associated with increased health risks across the life course1. Globally, obesity prevalence is approximately 8% in children and 16% in adults2. In Canada, national data prior to 2018 indicated that 13% of children and 27% of adults had obesity3,4. Starting in March 2020, public health restrictions due to the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted daily routines, reducing opportunities for physical activity and altering diets, which may have contributed to weight gain in children and adults5–7. Public health measures led to the closure of workplaces, schools, and recreational facilities, resulting in decreased physical activity, increased screen time, and changes in eating habits and sleep routines8–10. The pandemic also had adverse consequences on mental health, stress, and socioeconomic factors, further exacerbating health inequities and weight gain within families11–13.

A 2022 systematic review of 74 longitudinal studies found modest, but potentially clinically significant, increases in weight, body mass index (BMI), and obesity prevalence during the pandemic for both children and adults10. However, many studies focused on specific patient populations (e.g., patients with diabetes or obesity) or lacked sociodemographic context, which is crucial given the disproportionate impact of the pandemic and obesity on populations with lower socioeconomic status14,15. Most research has only examined short-term effects of the pandemic10 with few studies investigating the longer-term impact beyond the first year of the pandemic. Many existing studies were either cross-sectional or conducted solely during the pandemic, without considering any pre-pandemic temporal trends in obesity prevalence, and relied on parent-reported recall of BMI16,17. Furthermore, most previous studies have primarily assessed obesity defined by BMI, overlooking other important measures of adiposity, such as waist circumference.

Thus, our primary research objective was to compare the annual change in the prevalence of obesity before and during the COVID-19 pandemic in children and parents and to assess whether these associations were modified by sociodemographic factors and child or parent pre-pandemic weight status categories. The secondary objective was to compare the rate of change in waist circumference before and during the pandemic in children and parents.

Methods

Study population and design

We conducted a cohort study using data from children 0–18 years and their parents in The Applied Research Group for Kids (TARGet Kids!) primary care practice-based research network in the Greater Toronto Area, Canada. TARGet Kids! is an open longitudinal cohort study established in 200818. Children younger than 6 years of age at enrollment are recruited from pediatric or family practice clinics where questionnaires and physical measures are collected at regularly scheduled health supervision visits18. TARGet Kids! exclusion criteria at enrollment include severe health conditions affecting growth (e.g., failure to thrive or cystic fibrosis), any acute or chronic conditions (other than asthma and high functioning autism), severe developmental delay, and families unable to communicate in English18. For the primary analytical sample, children and parents were included in the study if they had at least one BMI measure before (February 1, 2015 to March 16, 2020) and one BMI measure during the pandemic (March 17, 2020 to February 28, 2023). Anthropometric measures were not collected among parents that were pregnant.

Ethics

Parents of all children participating in the TARGet Kids! cohort study provided informed written consent at enrollment. Ethics approval was provided by the Research Ethics Boards at both The Hospital for Sick Children (#10,000–12,436) and Unity Health Toronto (#17–335) and all research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Exposure

The COVID-19 “pandemic period” was defined as the period from March 17, 2020 to the most recent data availability which was February 28, 2023. March 17, 2020, was the date that the province of Ontario declared a State of Emergency and schools and workplaces were first closed6. The “pre-pandemic period” was defined as the 5 years preceding the pandemic period, which was between February 1, 2015 to March 16, 2020. Time was measured in years. Although there was no defined washout period in this study, there were only 25 observations recorded from March to October 2020 due to pandemic restrictions and the lack of in-person/research visits.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was obesity defined by BMI. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. For children, weight and height were obtained from measurements taken at health visits by trained research personnel using standardized instruments19, medical record abstraction, or self- or parent-reported measurements. We have previously shown high agreement between the measurement of weight and height by trained research assistants and primary care team members20. For parents, weight and height were obtained only from measurement by research personnel or self-report. For children, BMI z-score (zBMI) was externally standardized to age- and sex- specific scores using the World Health Organization (WHO) Growth Standards21, and categorized as underweight (zBMI < – 2), normal weight (– 2 ≤ zBMI ≤ 2), and zBMI > 2 is classified as overweight for children < 5 years and obesity for children ≥ 5 years22,23. For parents, standard BMI categories were used: underweight (< 18.5), normal weight (18.5 to < 25), overweight (25 to < 30), and obesity (≥ 30)24. Obesity was not further subcategorized due to small sample sizes. Parent BMI, obtained from self-report was corrected using Statistics Canada BMI correction factors and then recategorized into BMI categories25. For children, 83% of BMI values were measured by research staff in clinic or medical record abstraction and 17% were from parent report, whereas for parents 71% were measured in clinic compared to 29% self-report.

The secondary outcome was waist circumference which was defined continuously (in centimetres) and categorically (high/low). Waist circumference was obtained from measurements taken at health visits by trained study personnel. In the absence of established waist circumference standards for young children, internally standardized waist circumference z-scores were calculated within our study population by age and sex. For children, waist circumference was categorized as high (≥ 90th percentile) and low (< 90th percentile)26. For parents, waist circumference was categorized as high (female: ≥ 88 cm, male: ≥ 102 cm) and low (female: < 88 cm, male: < 102 cm)27.

For child and parent BMI as well as child waist circumference, TARGet Kids! anthropometric outlier removal protocols were used to determine biologically implausible measures21,28, which were replaced with prior or subsequent measurements or excluded from the analysis. In the absence of outlier removal protocols for parent waist circumference, observations less than the 1st percentile or greater than 99th percentile of the sample were excluded.

Additional variables

Additional variables were collected via parent-reported questionnaires and included based on their association with child and parent obesity or weight status13,14,27,28. Age (in years) at anthropometric measurements for the child, mother, and father were recorded in years. Parent recall of child birthweight (kg) was collected at baseline and breastfeeding duration (months) was asked at follow-ups until age 6 years. Categorical variables were collected at baseline and included child sex, parent sex, maternal and paternal ethnicity, highest level of maternal education, self-reported household income, dwelling type, and parent history of cardiometabolic risk.

Statistical analysis

Marginal logistic models fit using generalized estimating equations (GEE) were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between time and obesity and high waist circumference separately, accounting for repeated measures within each individual and clustering within families. The primary effect estimate of interest was the annual change in the odds of obesity. Gaussian GEEs were also used to estimate beta coefficients and 95% CIs for the association between time and continuous waist circumference. To differentiate between the pandemic and pre-pandemic period, we employed piecewise-linear functions because there was a non-linear association between time and BMI based on analysis of the residual plot29. Knot locations were visually determined using a smoothed LOESS curve and knots were included in the regression models to generate distinct effect estimates for each segment of the regression and capture non-linear changes in the data. A knot was placed at March 17, 2020 (start of the pandemic) and at 14 months into the pandemic for the plot based on children and 10 months for parents. Since there was no non-linear association observed between time and waist circumference based on the residual plot, a knot was only placed at March 17, 2020 to indicate the start of the pandemic.

Unadjusted and adjusted models were run. For children, we adjusted for child and parent’s age at anthropometric measurements, child and parent’s sex, family income, maternal education, maternal and paternal ethnicity, birthweight, breastfeeding duration and parent history of cardiometabolic conditions. For analyses investigating parent outcomes, we adjusted for all variables except for child age at measurement, child sex, birthweight, and breastfeeding duration. Plots of the predicted probability of obesity for parents and children were estimated using the mode for categorical covariates and the mean for continuous covariates.

The potential multiplicative interactions between the date variables and sociodemographic factors (i.e., age at measurement for children and parents, child sex, parent sex, family income, and maternal education) were examined in association with the primary outcome of obesity. We used a p-value threshold of < 0.1 to indicate evidence of interaction30. Stratified analyses were conducted for all sociodemographic factors, even if the interaction term was not significant, to help identify effect modification and any inequities in health outcomes. Additionally, as parental weight and early childhood weight have been shown to be a predictor for obesity development in children31,32, we included analyses stratified by child and parent baseline BMI to ascertain potential differences in child BMI. Lastly, to address potential biases in self- and parent-reported measurements, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using clinic-measured data only.

Missing data for covariates were imputed using multiple imputation using a total of 19 imputed data sets using the PROC MI procedure in SAS. The maximum amount of missingness was 19% before imputation. All analyses were conducted using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary NC).

Results

The primary analytic sample included 1,163 children with 6,004 observations (median 4.0 per child; IQR: 3.0). Median ages were 2.0 years (IQR: 3.8 years) before and 6.3 years (IQR: 5.2 years) during the pandemic. The sample also included 653 parents with 2,543 observations (median 4.0 per parent; IQR 3.0). Median ages were 37.0 years (IQR: 7.2 years) for mothers and 39.0 years (IQR: 8.4 years) for fathers in the pre-pandemic period, increasing to 38.7 (IQR: 9.6 years) for mothers and 40.5 years (IQR 10.1 years) for fathers during the pandemic. Obesity

Among children, the predicted probability of obesity increased from 3.8% (95% CI 1.0, 6.6%) in 2015 to 7.1% (95% CI 3.5, 10.7%) in 2019 and during the first year of the pandemic this increased substantially to a high of 18.6% (95% CI 10.5, 26.6%) in May 2021 and then decreased to 10.1% (95% CI 4.3, 15.9%) in March 2023 (Fig. 1). As shown in Table 2, in the 5 years prior to the pandemic, the average annual adjusted OR (aOR) for the prevalence of obesity was 1.18 (95% CI 1.02, 1.36). From March 2020 to May 2021, the annual aOR was 2.17 (95% CI 1.45, 3.25). From May 2021 to February 2023, an annual decrease in aOR was observed (0.66, 95% CI 0.46, 0.93).

Statistically significant interactions were observed between time and child age (p = 0.003), maternal education (p = 0.04), and pre-pandemic child BMI (p < 0.0001). Stratified analyses revealed that from March 2020 to May 2021, obesity odds increased more in younger children aged 0–8 years, compared to older children, and in children who were underweight or normal weight at the start of the pandemic, compared to overweight or obesity. In the second period of the pandemic from May 2021 to February 2023, there was evidence that the same population groups had lower obesity odds. Children whose mothers had a high school education or less showed a higher estimate of obesity odds in both periods, but confidence intervals were too wide to draw meaningful conclusions. Results are presented in Table 2.

Among parents, the predicted probability of obesity increased from 12.9% (95% CI 5.2, 20.5%) in 2015 to 27.4% (95% CI 16.2, 38.6%) in 2019 and this remained relatively stable during the pandemic with a prevalence of 29.2% (95% CI 16.0, 42.3%) in 2023 (Fig. 2). The average annual increase in the odds of parent obesity was 27% per year in the five years before the pandemic (aOR 1.27, 95% CI 1.12, 1.42) (Table 3). However, the annual change in obesity odds during the first 10 months of the pandemic (aOR 0.79, 95% CI 0.50, 1.25) or later (aOR 1.08, 95% CI 0.81, 1.43) were closer to null and not statistically significant.

Waist circumference

For children, there was a small 0.09 z-score annual increase in waist circumference in the five years before the pandemic (95% CI 0.04, 0.14) and this was similar during the pandemic (0.07 z-score, 95% CI -0.03, 0.17) (Supplementary Table 1). The annual change in the odds of high waist circumference before (OR 1.14, 95% CI 0.87, 1.50) and during the pandemic (OR 1.33, 95% CI 0.93, 1.89) suggested a possible increase but estimates were not statistically significant.

For parents, there was an annual increase in waist circumference of 2.05 cm (95% CI 1.41, 2.62) and the odds of high waist circumference increased annually before the pandemic (OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.30, 1.76) (Supplementary Table 2). However, from March 2020 to February 2023, the annual changes in waist circumference (-0.64 cm, 95% CI -1.55, 0.27) or odds of high waist circumference (OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.76, 1.14) were not statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis

To address potential biases in self- and parent-reported measurements, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using only clinic-measured data from children (5,005 observations) and parents (1,815 observations). The results were consistent with those in our primary analytic sample and are presented in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4.

Discussion

The findings from this study suggest that the proportion of children with obesity increased during the COVID-19 pandemic beyond the usual pre-pandemic increase. This increase was most pronounced during the first 14 months of the COVID-19 pandemic, then attenuated towards pre-pandemic levels with incomplete recovery. The magnitude of most effect estimates were similar after adjusting for several early life, sociodemographic, and health factors. Additionally, our findings indicate that increased obesity rates disproportionately affected younger children (with some evidence for children with low family income and maternal education), potentially exacerbating existing health inequities which emphasize the importance of addressing social determinants of health. While we did not find evidence of an increase in obesity rates for parents during the pandemic, our study does provide evidence that the prevalence of obesity in parents was increasing prior to 2020. Our results showed no annual change in waist circumference for children or parents during the pandemic.

Our findings are in line with other international studies in children that reported an increase in the prevalence of obesity within the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic33–37, followed by decreases later into the pandemic38,39. A US study of 241,600 patients aged 2–19 years found that the monthly rate of change in obesity prevalence increased from March 2020 to January 2021 (slope: 0.39%, 95% CI 0.39, 0.42) but significantly slowed from January to November 2021 (slope: – 0.02%, 95% CI – 0.04, – 0.001)38. An Australian study of 345,836 individuals ≤ 18 years found a 6.3% increase in obesity prevalence after COVID-19 public health measures were implemented39. Further, BMI percentiles peaked immediately after the restrictions and returned to pre-pandemic levels over 21 months39. Starting in 2021, as public health measures eased and Canadian schools returned to in-person learning5,7, we noted a decrease in obesity rates. While causation cannot be established, school re-openings and resuming extracurricular activities, including sports, may have contributed to stabilizing weight during the pandemic’s later stages due to reduced stress or increased physical activity.

While we did not find evidence of an increase in parent obesity during the pandemic, a stark increase was observed pre-pandemic. This is in contrast with recent Canadian data that suggest a large increase in the prevalence of obesity before and during the pandemic among young women40. In comparison, a large nationally representative sample of US adults found a significant increase of 3% during the pandemic (+ 3%, p < 0.05) compared to 2019–202041. However, the US study did not account for pre-pandemic changes in obesity rates, potentially overestimating the pandemic’s impact. A systematic review that included only repeated cross-sectional and cohort studies, found a statistically non-significant increase of 1% in obesity prevalence (95% CI – 0.01, 0.03)10. It is possible that weight changes in adults may be more subtle than in children, due to differential impacts of the pandemic on sedentary behaviour or stress.

Limited longitudinal research has focused on waist circumference during the COVID-19 pandemic. A systematic review found increases in children’s waist circumference10, while we found limited evidence of such changes. The review also noted no significant differences in waist circumference before and during the pandemic for parents10, aligning with our findings. However, individuals who had in-person waist circumference measurements during the pandemic exhibited higher levels of educational attainment and came from higher-income households compared to the general sample and we can not rule out the possibility of selection bias which may have attenuated the results.

A major strength of this study was its longer-term analysis covering 8 years, including 5 years pre-pandemic and nearly 3 years after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, allowing for a thorough assessment of trends. This allowed us to consider our results in relation to the usual patterns of changing prevalence of obesity prior to the pandemic. We were also able to adjust for confounding variables that are not routinely available in administrative data. This study also had several limitations. Since our sample from TARGet Kids! included children from selected primary care practices in a few urban areas in Canada, we did not have a population-based representative sample. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations, especially as our sample had relatively high income and education compared to the overall population. Additionally, height and weight measurement methods varied throughout the study. However, a sensitivity analysis which excluded self- and parent-reported measurements mirrored our main findings. Further, there were a limited number of observations during the initial stages of the pandemic which impacted the precision of our estimates, especially for our analyses stratified by sociodemographic factors.

Conclusions

In the TARGet Kids! cohort study, a significant increase in obesity prevalence was observed in children during the early stage of the COVID-19 pandemic coupled with incomplete recovery in later stages (May 2021 to February 2023), suggesting a sustained high prevalence of obesity in this cohort. Our findings emphasize the importance of monitoring children’s weight and height as well as prevention and treatment efforts for childhood obesity. In the future, healthcare providers and policymakers should prioritize strategies that promote physical activity, access to healthy foods, and mental health support, especially for vulnerable populations, during and following periods of societal disruption.
