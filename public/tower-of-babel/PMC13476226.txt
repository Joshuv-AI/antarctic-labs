Environmental sustainability in Somalia: the role of clean cooking energy and remittances on ecological footprints
Authors: Hassan Abdikadir Hussein, Abdimalik Ali Warsame
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnvironmental degradation in Somalia, driven primarily by reliance on biomass fuels and limited access to clean energy, poses a significant challenge to sustainability. However, the impact of clean cooking energy and remittances on environmental outcomes remains underexplored in this context. In this regard, this study examines the impact of access to clean cooking energy and remittances on environmental degradation in Somalia over the period 1990–2022. The study employs a novel supremum right-tail Augmented Dickey–Fuller unit root test, time-varying Granger causality analysis, and a machine learning approach using Kernel Regularised Least Squares (KRLS). Date-stamping results reveal episodes of explosive behaviour in the ecological footprint (2006–2009), clean cooking energy (2004–2020), and remittances (2013–2014). The time-varying Granger causality results indicate that clean cooking energy Granger-causes the ecological footprint during two periods (2006–2008 and 2019–2020), with a peak episode in 2019–2020. Additionally, causality from remittances to the ecological footprint is observed in two periods (2004–2005 and 2006–2009), with a peak during 2006–2009. The KRLS results reveal that access to clean cooking energy and remittances improves environmental quality by reducing the ecological footprint in Somalia. Policymakers should prioritise investments in renewable energy technologies, such as solar cookers, biogas systems, and improved cookstoves, particularly in rural areas, to reduce dependence on firewood and charcoal. Additionally, policies should encourage the channelling of remittances into sustainability-focused investments through mechanisms like tax incentives or matching grants.Keywords: Clean cooking energy, Environmental sustainability, Remittance, SomaliaSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

The extensive utilisation of traditional biomass, particularly firewood, charcoal, and agricultural residuals for cooking, is a pressing issue in numerous developing and least developed nations1. Although such fuels have been utilised for ages, their consumption incurs considerable environmental harm. The ineffective burning of biomass fuels leads to deforestation, air pollution, and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, hence exacerbating environmental damage and global warming2,3. Furthermore, dependence on traditional fuels intensifies health problems, especially in rural areas, whereby households experience elevated levels of indoor air pollution4. The growing global awareness of the environmental consequences of biomass utilisation has fostered a burgeoning recognition of the need for a shift to clean, modern cooking fuels5. The utilisation of clean cooking fuels, such as liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), electricity, and biogas, can significantly mitigate the environmental and health impacts associated with traditional biomass consumption6. Clean cooking solutions reduce reliance on firewood and charcoal, thereby lowering strain on forests, while also reducing carbon emissions and improving air quality. However, the shift to clean fuels is often hampered by factors such as cost, infrastructure constraints, and social and cultural preferences7. Unfortunately, more than 2.6 billion households worldwide lack access to clean cooking options8. Over 80% of these people live in Sub-Saharan Africa, predominantly in rural areas9. In low-income developing nations, home cooking accounts for the largest share of energy consumption relative to other domestic services.

Energy scarcity poses a substantial challenge for most Sub-Saharan African countries, including Somalia, even though energy is essential for economic development and poverty alleviation. In Somalia, a nation facing energy constraints, urban and traditional households primarily rely on traditional biomass, particularly charcoal and firewood, to meet their energy needs. This represents around 82% of the overall energy use10. The substantial reliance on traditional biomass hinders socio-economic advancement and results in environmental degradation. The use of traditional biomass for cooking and heating in Somalia has several detrimental effects. The unsustainable use of trees for charcoal production leads to deforestation and environmental degradation, hence altering ecosystems and biodiversity11. The inefficient combustion of biomass fuels releases substantial amounts of hazardous pollutants. This consequently leads to air pollution both indoors and outdoors.

Alongside the challenge of securing reliable energy access, remittances primarily support basic household needs. Remittances can also influence household decisions related to energy use and environmental practices. Families with consistent and reliable remittance income are often better positioned to invest in clean cooking technologies, solar systems, or other environmentally sustainable solutions that help reduce dependence on traditional biomass fuels and alleviate pressure on forests and fragile ecosystems12,13. Remittances play an equally vital role in shaping Somalia’s economic and social development. The Somali diaspora, dispersed across Europe, North America, and the Gulf states, maintains deep socio-economic connections with their homeland. Each year, this transnational community remits an estimated US$1.3–2 billion, representing about 20–30% of Somalia’s GDP14. These remittances serve as a crucial lifeline, sustaining millions of Somali households by supporting food security, education, healthcare, and small-scale entrepreneurial activities.

Somalia faces a multidimensional sustainability challenge shaped by persistent energy poverty, environmental degradation, and strong dependence on remittance inflows. Many households continue to rely on charcoal and firewood for cooking, contributing to deforestation, indoor air pollution, and increasing ecological pressure15. Although clean cooking energy options, such as solar technologies and liquefied petroleum gas, could help reduce these environmental and health burdens, their adoption remains limited due to high costs, weak infrastructure, and insufficient policy support. At the same time, remittances from the Somali diaspora remain a vital source of household welfare and national economic support, yet their environmental implications are still poorly understood. Remittances may enable households to afford cleaner energy technologies, but they may also increase consumption patterns that expand ecological footprints if not directed toward sustainable choices. Existing studies often examine remittances, clean energy access, and environmental sustainability separately, overlooking how financial inflows and clean cooking energy may interact in shaping ecological outcomes in low-income, energy-poor settings16,17. Therefore, this study contributes to the environmental and development economics literature by jointly examining clean cooking energy access and remittances as complementary pathways for reducing ecological pressure and promoting sustainable development in Somalia.

This study contributes to the literature by exploring the simultaneous impact of access to clean cooking energy and remittance on ecological footprint. Previous studies have mainly examined the links among economic growth, energy use, and environmental degradation18,19. However, the combined influence of clean cooking energy and remittances on Somalia’s ecological footprint has received almost no attention. Thus, this undertaking focuses on Somalia, which is the first study of its kind. Second, this undertaking employs the ecological footprint as an indicator of environmental pollution. Ecological footprint offers a broader measure of environmental impact—it goes beyond carbon emissions to capture the strain on land, water, and biological resources20. Yet, very few studies have applied this concept in the Somali context. Finally, existing studies have primarily relied on traditional econometric methods such as PMG, ARDL, FMOLS, and DOLS21. In contrast, this study uses a more advanced approach, incorporating the supremum right-tail Augmented Dickey-Fuller unit root test, time-varying Granger causality, and Kernel Regularized Least Squares (KRLS). The KRLS technique was developed by Hainmueller and Hazlett22 as part of their analytical framework. Unlike conventional econometric approaches that rely on the assumption of constant marginal effects, the KRLS method allows for greater flexibility by estimating both average marginal effects and local derivatives across the data. This makes it particularly effective for capturing nonlinear relationships and generating more reliable, unbiased estimates of the variables under study. In addition, the study employs the supremum right-tail Augmented Dickey-Fuller unit root test that examines for explosive behavior or outliers23. Consequently, the time-varying Granger causality framework is applied to capture potential shifts in causal relationships over time24.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. The second section provides a concise review of the relevant empirical literature, while the third outlines the data sources and methodological approach used in the analysis. The fourth section presents and discusses the empirical findings, and the final section concludes with key insights and policy recommendations drawn from the study.

Literature review

Environmental degradation driven by human activities is an urgent global issue that adversely affects ecosystems, disrupts climate patterns, and jeopardises people’s safety, especially in developing regions. This section provides concise summaries of the overall impact of access to clean energy cooking and remittance on environmental sustainability.

The role of access to clean cooking energy in environmental sustainability

Energy consumption patterns are central to environmental degradation, particularly in low-income countries where biomass fuels, such as firewood and charcoal, dominate. These conventional fuels significantly contribute to deforestation, air pollution, and greenhouse gas emissions25. Several studies highlighted the benefits of transitioning from biomass to clean energy sources like liquefied petroleum gas (LPG). They reported that LPG can effectively reduce deforestation and carbon emissions, making it a viable clean energy option for rural households. Hayford et al.26, Sreeja27. LPG is regarded as one of the most efficient sustainable energy sources for families, especially in rural and semi-urban areas. LPG is a practical and efficient clean energy option for rural and semi-urban households, helping to reduce fuelwood use and emissions when supported by subsidies and awareness efforts28. Moreover, access to LPG is associated with reduced pressure on forest resources, as firewood collection has decreased29.

Rej et al.30 explored the impact of LPG on environmental degradation in India. They revealed that the utilisation of liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) reduces environmental degradation and promotes a cleaner, more sustainable environment. Sreeja et al.27 examined the nexus between clean cooking technology and environmental degradation in BRICS countries. Their results indicate that implementing clean cooking technology can significantly mitigate environmental degradation, thereby promoting a sustainable environment in BRICS countries. Bakehe and Hassan31 offer an important contribution by examining the relationship between clean fuels and deforestation across 92 developing countries. Their findings indicate that access to clean fuels and cooking technologies mitigates deforestation.

Nevertheless, the cost and accessibility of LPG continue to pose major challenges in several low-income areas32,33. Studies indicate that the utilisation of LPG mitigates deforestation by lowering the demand for firewood and charcoal in Sub-Saharan African countries34. This finding highlights the need to expand LPG distribution to support environmental preservation. Barik and Padhi35 demonstrate that socio-economic issues, including poverty and inadequate infrastructure, hinder broad access to LPG. Furthermore, cultural preferences and old fuel consumption practices present major barriers36.

The role of remittances in environmental sustainability

Remittances, funds transferred to the home by migrants, are essential for supporting families in numerous poor nations. These contributions frequently help meet fundamental needs such as food, education, and medical care. However, regarding the environment, the impacts are not as obvious. The allocation of funds can either alleviate environmental problems or, in some cases, exacerbate them. Numerous studies indicate that remittances enhance environmental sustainability by bolstering household financial stability, thereby reducing reliance on environmentally harmful practices21,37,38. Zafar et al.39 analysed the impact of remittances, export diversification, education, and CO2 emissions in 22 major remittance-receiving countries. Using advanced econometric methods, the results show that remittances reduce environmental degradation by lowering CO2 emissions. Dilanchiev et al.40 used PMG-ARDL and CS-ARDL methods to assess the impact of remittances on environmental quality in top remittance-receiving countries (2000–2021). They found that remittances exhibited an inverted U-shaped relationship with emissions, with environmental quality in developing countries improving after reaching a threshold. Arogundade et al.41 investigated the effect of diaspora income on the ecological footprint across 22 African countries. The study employs several econometric techniques, including a robust fixed-effects model, fixed-effects instrumental-variables regression, method-of-moments quantile regression (MMQR), and a heterogeneous Granger causality test. The empirical results indicate that diaspora income exerts a negative and statistically significant influence on the ecological footprint.

Theoretical foundation of the study

This study draws on several key theories to explain the connection between household income, clean energy, and environmental degradation in Somalia. The study primarily draws on the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis, which suggests that while economic growth initially worsens environmental degradation, it eventually leads to improvements once a certain income level is reached42,43. This framework helps explain how remittances, by increasing household income, may initially increase consumption but, over time, can fund investments in clean cooking energy, thereby reducing the ecological footprint. We also use the theory of Technological Leapfrogging, which argues that developing countries can skip polluting technologies and adopt cleaner ones directly44,45. In Somalia, remittances enable households to leap directly to clean cooking energy technologies, bypassing traditional biomass methods. Additionally, the New Economics of Labour Migration (NELM) theory highlights how remittances can either alleviate financial constraints for sustainable investments or lead to higher consumption, which might increase environmental degradation46,47. This study explores how remittances enable households to invest in sustainable technologies, thereby reducing their ecological footprint. Hence, this framework integrates the EKC, Technological Leapfrogging, and NELM to understand how remittances and clean cooking energy impact environmental sustainability in Somalia, offering insights into how these factors can help reduce environmental degradation.

Research model.

This study addresses a significant research gap by integrating access to clean cooking energy and remittances to examine their combined impact on environmental degradation in Somalia. Data and methods

Data

This study assesses the impact of access to clean cooking energy, remittance, economic growth, population density, and globalisation on environmental degradation in Somalia. Unbalanced annual time series spanning 1990–2022 are used. The ecological footprint data were obtained from the Global Footprint Network. Remittance inflows were retrieved from the Federal Reserve Bank, while access to clean cooking energy was obtained from CEIC. Population density data were extracted from the World Bank, economic growth data from the SESRIC database of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, and globalisation data from the KOF Swiss Institute. Economic growth, population density, and globalisation are included in the model as control variables, given their established relevance to environmental sustainability and their expected influence on ecological degradation. These variables are not arbitrarily selected but are driven by Somalia’s specific context and the study’s theoretical underpinnings. Moreover, economic growth is a crucial factor in our model, as it often drives higher energy consumption and resource use, which can significantly affect environmental sustainability48,49. In Somalia, as the economy grows, demand for energy is likely to increase, which could contribute to environmental damage unless it is paired with sustainable energy practices.

Population density is another critical factor in determining the ecological footprint. Higher population density can lead to increased consumption of resources and greater waste production, both of which contribute to environmental degradation50,51. In Somalia, rapid urbanisation is driving up population density, placing additional strain on local resources and exacerbating environmental pressures, including deforestation and air pollution. Globalisation impacts both economic growth and environmental practices52. As Somalia integrates into the global economy, it may drive industrialisation, trade, and foreign investment, which can either mitigate or worsen environmental degradation, depending on the nature of these factors19. This inclusion allows us to explore how external economic forces influence Somalia’s domestic environmental drivers. Method

Political and economic shocks at the global and regional levels can generate abnormal fluctuations in production and consumption, giving rise to explosive dynamics. The term explosive behaviour refers to the presence of atypical time-series patterns in environmental and macroeconomic variables23. To detect such dynamics, we employ the supremum right-tailed Augmented Dickey–Fuller (SADF) unit root test, which is based on the null hypothesis of a unit root; rejection of the null indicates the presence of explosive behaviour or outliers23. Given the evidence of explosiveness, we subsequently apply a time-varying Granger causality framework to capture potential instabilities in causal relationships over time53. Traditional vector autoregression (VAR) models are often inadequate in this context because they fail to account for regime shifts and structural breaks that can induce parameter instability. In contrast, time-varying Granger causality methods effectively address these challenges by allowing causal relationships to evolve over time and by providing robust test statistics across different subsamples53. Specifically, we estimate a reduced-form VAR with up to two lags and a trimming parameter of 0.15, assuming homoskedastic idiosyncratic shocks. We implement four test statistics—the robust exponential Wald (ExpW), robust mean Wald (MeanW), optimal Nyblom (Nyblom), and quasi-likelihood ratio (QLR)—to test the null hypothesis of no Granger causality53.

This study further employs the Kernel Regularised Least Squares (KRLS) approach, a machine learning technique with strong econometric foundations developed by Hainmueller and Hazlett22. The KRLS method is particularly effective for modelling complex relationships in social science research and addressing key challenges in statistical inference. Compared to conventional econometric and machine learning methods, KRLS offers several advantages.

First, it estimates average marginal effects and pointwise derivatives without assuming constant marginal effects, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of relationships among variables. This results in consistent and unbiased estimates. Second, KRLS surpasses many other machine learning algorithms in managing model misspecification, yielding results that are both flexible and interpretable. Importantly, it eliminates the need to pretest the parameters of interest54.

Unit root

One of the prerequisite steps of the time series analysis is to assess the stationarity of the variables. The presence of a unit root in the data can lead to biased estimates. To examine this potential issue, we used the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) and Phillips-Perron (PP) tests. In these tests, the null hypothesis states that the series contains a unit root, while the alternative hypothesis states that the series is stationary. If the calculated t-statistic for a variable exceeds its corresponding critical t-value, the null hypothesis of non-stationarity is rejected in favour of the alternative hypothesis, confirming stationarity. As presented in Table 3, the findings confirm that all variables are stationary at the first-difference level I(1). Both the ADF and PP unit root tests robustly verified the stationarity of the sampled variables, thereby supporting the rejection of the null hypothesis of non-stationarity.

Results of explosive behaviours

Before the formal analysis, we examined the explosive behaviours of the sampled variables. The result is reported in Fig. 3. We examined the explosive behaviour of all our variables of interest, but we only reported those that exhibited outliers. One explosive behaviour (2006–2009) is observed in the ecological footprint, where the statistic sharply exceeds both the 90% and 95% critical thresholds, as shown in Fig. 3a. This indicates a period of explosive growth in environmental pressure, possibly linked to rapid resource exploitation and urban expansion during the mid-2000s. Overall, the ecological footprint shows transient spikes, highlighting unsustainable consumption patterns followed by environmental correction.

Explosive behaviours of (a) Ecological footprint, (b) Clean energy cooking, (c) Remittance.

Figure 3b presents the explosive behaviour of clean energy consumption. Its result shows a gradual and consistent rise after 2004, surpassing both the 90% and 95% critical thresholds. Hence, renewable energy consumption exhibits explosive growth after 2004. This pattern indicates persistent explosive dynamics, implying a structural increase in the adoption of renewable energy. The sustained explosive behaviour suggests policy-driven or donor-supported interventions that expanded access to clean cooking energy sources and renewable technologies in Somalia. The absence of significant downward corrections implies that clean energy development has achieved stable, long-term momentum, which may be crucial for mitigating environmental degradation and achieving sustainability goals.

Figure 3c depicts the presence of explosive behaviour in remittance inflows during 2013–2014. In the remaining years, it reflects non-explosive behaviour in remittance inflows. The absence of any explosive episodes implies that remittances remained relatively stable without bubble-like surges. This stability suggests that remittances play a smoothing role in Somalia’s economy, providing consistent household support rather than driving short-term volatility.

To ensure robust results, we further employed time-varying Granger causality to detect the date-stamping causality between the ecological footprint and the independent variables. Its result is reported in Figs. 4 and 5. In the forward and rolling window results, no evidence of Granger causality from clean cooking energy to ecological footprint is observed, as shown in Fig. 4. However, the recursive expanding window results indicate time-varying predictive episodes during 2006–2008 and 2019–2020, suggesting that past values of clean cooking energy helped predict changes in the ecological footprint during these periods. These results should be interpreted as evidence of predictive content rather than as evidence of a positive or negative effect. The direction and magnitude of the relationship are therefore assessed separately using the KRLS estimates. During 2006–2008, increased use of cleaner cooking alternatives, particularly improved cookstoves and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) in urban and peri-urban areas, may have contributed to reduced dependence on charcoal and firewood. Charcoal production and deforestation are major drivers of environmental degradation in Somalia58,59. This transition likely helped reduce pressure on forest ecosystems and lower biomass combustion-related ecological impacts. Furthermore, we have observed that clean energy cooking had an ecological footprint during 2019–2020. This could be attributed to renewed momentum from humanitarian energy programs, international development interventions, and expanded access to modern energy technologies, alongside growing awareness of environmental health impacts. Overall, these episodes highlight how transitions away from traditional biomass toward cleaner cooking solutions can yield measurable environmental benefits in fragile, resource-constrained contexts such as Somalia.

In these episodes, remittances provided a stable source of income that reduced reliance on environmentally destructive survival strategies such as excessive fuelwood collection, charcoal production, and overexploitation of land.

An average increase in economic growth is associated with a 0.0380% rise in the ecological footprint, signifying that expanding economic activities tend to place more pressure on the environment. This effect varies across the quartiles, with growth influencing ecological degradation by -0.039%, 0.032%, and 0.108% at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, respectively. Economic growth hampers environmental quality by increasing ecological footprints in all percentiles except the 25th percentile. In the same vein, the squared term of economic growth hinders environmental quality by increasing the ecological footprint. An average increase in squared economic growth leads to an increase in the ecological footprint of about 0.0032%. In the 25th percentile, it improves environmental quality by about − 0.0038%. However, increasing economic growth by 1% increases the ecological footprint by 0.0029%, and by 0.0099% if it is squared. Even though squared economic growth has a negligible effect on environmental quality, it exerts heterogeneous dynamic effects, with environmental degradation accelerating as income levels rise. This result contradicts the Environmental Kuznets Curve hypothesis, which posits that environmental damage often worsens during the initial phases of economic expansion before stabilising at higher levels of development.

In contrast, access to clean cooking energy demonstrates a strong and consistent negative effect. On average, an increase in clean energy usage reduces the ecological footprint by 0.042%, with respective reductions of − 0.019%, − 0.044%, and − 0.025% at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, respectively. Clean cooking energy has a decreasing marginal effect on the ecological footprint, indicating that its adoption is a critical driver of environmental quality. Similarly, remittances exert a modest yet significant negative effect by decreasing the ecological footprint by approximately 0.010% on average. Remittance also enhances environmental quality by reducing the ecological footprint by about − 0.0191, − 0.008, and − 0.002 at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, respectively. This implies that remittance inflows may support environmentally sustainable practices, particularly in lower-income regions, possibly by improving household welfare and promoting cleaner consumption choices. Population density emerges as a key determinant of environmental sustainability. An average increase in population density reduces the ecological footprint by roughly 0.21%, with effects of 0.33%, 0.22%, and 0.06% across the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, respectively. The homogeneous adverse effects of remittances on environmental pollution suggest that denser populations benefit from urban efficiency, such as shared infrastructure, reduced transportation needs, and lower per capita resource use.

Discussion of the result

The findings reveal that greater access to clean cooking energy significantly enhances environmental quality in Somalia. This outcome can be attributed to the transition from traditional biomass fuels—such as charcoal, firewood, and kerosene—to cleaner alternatives such as liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), biogas, and solar-powered cooking systems. The adoption of clean energy reduces deforestation, lowers household air pollution, and decreases greenhouse gas emissions, all of which improve both environmental and public health outcomes. Moreover, clean cooking technologies increase energy efficiency and lessen the ecological footprint associated with resource extraction and fuel combustion. From 2020 to 2023, access to clean cooking energy in Somalia improved gradually due to coordinated policy efforts, international support, and the growing availability of affordable technologies. Government commitment through national renewable energy strategies and initiatives, such as Ascent and Hareact, played a key role in promoting modern fuels and efficient cookstoves60. At the same time, recurring droughts and dwindling biomass resources encouraged many households to shift toward cleaner alternatives. Together, these developments fostered a steady rise in clean energy use, marking tangible progress toward achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 7 on affordable and clean energy. This result corroborates previous empirical evidence indicating that improved access to clean cooking energy plays a significant role in enhancing environmental quality, such as Hayford et al.26 in E7 economies, Sreeja et al.27 in BRICS countries, and Suman61 in G20 countries.

The results further revealed that remittances play a positive role in enhancing environmental quality in Somalia. This can be explained by the fact that remittance inflows improve household welfare and enable investment in cleaner and more sustainable practices. Remittances often reduce reliance on environmentally harmful activities, such as charcoal production or unsustainable farming, as families can afford alternative livelihoods and cleaner energy sources. In addition, remittances may support education and awareness about environmental conservation, fostering more sustainable consumption and resource use. In Somalia’s context, where formal employment opportunities and public infrastructure are limited, remittances serve as a crucial private source of green financing, indirectly reducing ecological pressure and improving environmental outcomes. However, between 2007 and 2008, Somalia faced a sharp drop in remittances due to the global financial crisis and worsening domestic insecurity. This disruption reduced household incomes and forced many families, especially in rural areas, to rely on charcoal production, firewood collection, and subsistence farming to survive, leading to increased deforestation and land degradation. The decline in remittance inflows also slowed investments in clean energy technologies such as solar panels and improved cookstoves, prolonging dependence on polluting fuels. This finding aligns with previous empirical evidence indicating that remittance plays a vital role in improving environmental quality, such as Javed et al.38 in Pakistan, Islam et al.37 in the top 20 remittance-earning countries, and Umair et al.21 for the top 50 remittance-receiving countries.

Conclusion and policy implications

Environmental quality plays a vital role in achieving sustainable development because it sustains ecosystems, enhances human well-being, and provides the foundation for economic and social stability. Empirical research on this issue remains limited in the existing literature, particularly within least developed countries such as Somalia. In this regard, this study assessed the impact of access to clean cooking energy, remittance, economic growth, population density, and globalisation on ecological footprint in Somalia by using a novel supremum right-tail Augmented Dickey-Fuller unit root, time-varying Granger causality, and machine learning method of KRLS. The results established one explosive behaviour in the ecological footprint (2006–2009), clean energy cooking (2004–2020), and remittance (2013–2014). In the time-varying Granger result, we have observed that clean cooking energy causes an ecological footprint in two episodes (2006–2008 and 2019–2020), with peak episodes in later years, in the recursive result. Moreover, one episode of Granger causality from remittances to ecological footprint (2006–2008) is observed in the rolling-window results. The recursive result produced two episodes of causality from remittance to ecological footprint (2004–2005 and 2006–2009), with a peak episode in 2006–2009. The empirical results of the KRLS revealed that remittance, economic growth, access to clean cooking energy, and population density are statistically significant, whereas globalisation is statistically insignificant. Furthermore, access to clean energy cooking, remittances, and population density enhance environmental quality, whereas economic growth and squared growth impede it.

In light of the empirical results, the study recommends several policy implications. The findings reveal that access to clean cooking energy has a significant and negative effect on the ecological footprint, implying that expanding clean energy use can substantially mitigate environmental degradation. This outcome emphasises the importance of integrating sustainable energy solutions into Somalia’s broader development agenda. In practical terms, policymakers should prioritise investments in renewable and low-carbon household energy technologies, such as solar cookers, biogas systems, and improved stoves. Expanding access to these technologies, particularly in rural areas, would reduce dependence on firewood and charcoal—two of the main drivers of deforestation and carbon emissions. In addition, financial incentives and targeted subsidies could help make clean energy options more affordable for low-income households, thereby enhancing adoption rates. Beyond infrastructure, the government should pursue a comprehensive clean energy policy framework that aligns with national climate and health goals. Collaboration between the public sector, private investors, and international partners could facilitate technology transfer and the development of efficient distribution systems. Moreover, community-level awareness programs and environmental education initiatives are vital to encouraging long-term behavioural change. By promoting clean cooking energy, Somalia can make tangible progress toward sustainable development while improving public health and reducing ecological strain.

The study also finds that remittances exert a modest but statistically significant negative impact on the ecological footprint, suggesting that remittance inflows from the Somali diaspora can support environmental well-being when effectively utilised. This relationship highlights remittances not merely as household income but as a potential instrument for sustainable financing. Policymakers could build on this by creating channels that link remittance inflows with green investments. For instance, local financial institutions might introduce remittance-linked savings accounts or micro-investment programs that encourage recipients to allocate part of their funds to renewable energy, sustainable agriculture, or environmentally friendly small enterprises. In addition, tax incentives or matching grants could be used to stimulate the use of remittance capital for sustainability-focused ventures. Engaging the diaspora community through “green remittance” programs would also be valuable. Such initiatives could encourage migrants to contribute directly to community-level environmental projects, including reforestation, clean water systems, and energy cooperatives. To enhance the effectiveness of these efforts, financial literacy and entrepreneurship training for remittance recipients should be promoted to enable households to convert external income into long-term, environmentally sound investments.

Based on our study, there are several limitations that future research can address. First, it excludes factors such as institutional quality, conflicts, and ICT, which could provide a more comprehensive understanding of environmental sustainability. Second, the study is limited to Somalia from 1990 to 2022, so future research could expand the temporal scope or include cross-country comparisons. Lastly, globalisation showed no significant impact, suggesting that further research is needed to explore its complex role, particularly in trade, foreign investment, and international partnerships.
