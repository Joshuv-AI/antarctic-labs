ExecuƟve Summary
Geothermal heaƟng and cooling (GHC) systems oﬀer one of the most eﬃcient approaches to provide
space condiƟoning and water heaƟng in buildings. Due to the relaƟvely stable temperature of the
ground year-round, these systems can provide an eﬃcient soluƟon to decarbonize heaƟng systems. Yet
this technology has faced limited market adopƟon due to its high ﬁrst cost. This project seeks to address
this barrier by designing a community GHC system at a public housing development in New York, taking
advantage of load diversity (in terms of the Ɵming and amount of heaƟng / cooling used per person) that
a larger community oﬀers to reduce the size and thus ﬁrst cost of a GHC system. Project success would
result in societal beneﬁt from reduced energy and fossil fuel use, reduced local emissions, and improved
comfort, which would posiƟvely impact the health and well-being this community. This report presents
the ﬁndings from Phase 1 project supported by the Department of Energy’s Geothermal Technologies
Oﬃce and includes the technical design of a GHC at a public housing development in New York. To
support the success of a potenƟal deployment of a GHC system, the team conducted community
engagement and prepared an assessment of the local workforce needs.

The project team, led by EPRI, considered a subset of NYCHA housing developments that were previously
idenƟﬁed by NYCHA as suitable for GHC retroﬁts. This report presents the site selecƟon criteria the team
developed and the overall score assigned to each of the eight developments considered. One site was
selected for focus of the technical design: Stapleton Houses in Staten Island, a mid-size development
with roughly 700 apartments across 6 buildings. The site has an exisƟng hot water hydronic loop served
by natural gas boilers for space heaƟng and water heaƟng. The technical design partner assessed
baseline energy consumpƟon at the site and established thermal models of the apartment buildings to
assist in the design. The team considered several GHC system conﬁguraƟons and selected a hybrid
geothermal design fed from water-source heat pumps with auxiliary gas boilers in a central plant. This
design best leveraged the exisƟng site conﬁguraƟon and minimized costs due to addiƟonal electrical
infrastructure upgrades.

The report also details the feedback received from community engagement eﬀorts and the ﬁndings from
the workforce needs assessment and workforce transiƟon plan. Members of the site maintenance staﬀ
and the resident advisory council were interviewed for their input and feedback about both the site’s
exisƟng heaƟng/cooling infrastructure and how a construcƟon project would impact resident life. This
feedback was taken into consideraƟon when compleƟng the geothermal system design. Research was
conducted into exisƟng workforce development opportuniƟes available to NYCHA residents. A workforce
development plan was established using informaƟon from NYCHA’s resident economic empowerment
and sustainability group, along with training material and curriculum from the InternaƟonal Ground
Source Heat Pump AssociaƟon (IGSHPA).

Technical Assessment
Geothermal or ground-source heat pumps (GHP) represent one of the most eﬃcient technologies for
space condiƟoning buildings. Because ground temperatures remain consistent throughout the year, GHP
operate much more eﬃciently that air-source heat pumps. In addiƟon, they have much lower winter
peak power draw during cold weather, presenƟng much lower peak power demand on the grid. Such
systems typically present much higher ﬁrst cost due to the added cost of installing ground heat
exchangers. Despite lower energy cost, this added ﬁrst cost oŌen pushes system payback beyond what
many building owners can consider, parƟcularly for residenƟal applicaƟons. Yet this equipment is well
suited to community and campus applicaƟons that are interconnect by a thermal network, where GHP
oﬀer several beneﬁts. The diversity of thermal load that exists in a community or campus allows the GHP
system to be (right) sized for the coincident or simultaneous peak demand which is typically lower than
the total load of all buildings if separate due to load diversity. This can reduce the size and cost of the
ground heat exchanger (typically verƟcal boreholes for large systems), reduce payback periods to more
tenable range. In parƟcularly, communiƟes that integrate residenƟal and commercial building spaces
with varying load proﬁles and operaƟng schedules can signiﬁcantly reduce the size / capacity of needed
boreholes, making GHP more aƩracƟve in these applicaƟons.

System SelecƟon Process
During the preliminary design process, the team considered diﬀerent potenƟal conﬁguraƟons of a
community GHC system. The ﬁrst opƟon that was evaluated was an ambient loop design, with
distributed water source heat pumps located within each apartment unit, that are connected through an
ambient loop piping network to the ground heat exchanger. One of the biggest advantages of this design
is that it can provide heaƟng or cooling independently to each unit, as controlled by each resident. This
design distributes electrical loads for the new heaƟng/cooling system to each building and each dwelling
unit, and as such would require signiﬁcant electrical work throughout the enƟre site. There was also a
concern for addiƟonal maintenance and noise from the heat pumps in each apartment causing a
disturbance to the residents. As such, the concept of an ambient loop design was deemed not feasible
for this site. Other system conﬁguraƟons considered include 4-pipe distribuƟon and heat pumps in each
building (i.e., a semi-central system), but the ﬁrst cost of these systems—namely electrical upgrades to
each building and doubling the piping, respecƟvely—made these conﬁguraƟons cost prohibiƟve.

The concept of a central heat pump plant located within an exisƟng mechanical space, with distributed
hot/chilled water to fan-coil units in the apartment alleviated the need for extensive electrical upgrades
and potenƟal noise concerns within the units. By placing the heat pumps in a central plant, electrical

 9

service upgrades can be limited to that one locaƟon, reducing overall electrical cost and complexity of
the project.

System Design NarraƟve
This project includes the design and engineering documents to upgrade the exisƟng heaƟng (including
domesƟc hot water) system serving the New York City Housing Authority’s (NYCHA) Stapleton site,
located in Staten Island, New York City. This technical design includes geothermal, mechanical and
electrical engineering scopes, in the form of pricing drawings, speciﬁcaƟons for major equipment, an
energy modeling summary and energy conservaƟon measures, and a rough order of magnitude (ROM)
cost esƟmate. These documents are intended to provide a low-carbon replacement heaƟng, cooling, and
hot water system at the project site. These ﬁrst phase design includes a verƟcal closed loop geothermal
system, a new centralized heat pump plant (replacing the exisƟng plant), and new electrical equipment
to serve the new plant. In this design, hot and chilled water is distributed from the new plant to each of
the six (6) residenƟal buildings and verƟcally piped into each building tenant unit to fan coil units (for
heaƟng and cooling) and hot water storage tanks (for domesƟc hot water).

Thermal models were created to aid and inform the geothermal, electrical and mechanical designs
described. One of the six (6) idenƟcal apartment buildings was modeled in Integrated Environmental
SoluƟons Virtual Environment (IES-VE) soŌware and replicated for the physical orientaƟon of each of the
six (6) buildings to assess the thermal loads, or demands, of each building throughout the year. Energy
consumpƟon from the last ﬁve (5) years was uƟlized to aid in the calibraƟon of the site energy model.
Building heaƟng and cooling loads were then input into a Ground Loop Design (GLD) soŌware to
determine approximately how much heaƟng and cooling can be accommodated by the geothermal
porƟon of the system. Published United States Geological Survey (USGS) resources were consulted to
idenƟfy the geologic formaƟons beneath the site and esƟmate the thermal capacity of the ground below
the project site, as a geothermal test bore was not within the scope of this project. Due to the large
heaƟng loads for the site, the new plant and geothermal system would handle 100% of the site’s cooling
loads but require several new, high-eﬃciency gas-ﬁred boilers to handle the remainder of the building
heaƟng load as well as the domesƟc water heaƟng load. See Appendix B for the results from energy
modeling.

The mechanical design presented here consists of a central mechanical plant with hybrid geothermal
heat pump and gas boiler backup that generates hot and chilled water to the six (6) original apartment
buildings on the campus. Space condiƟoning is provided using a two-pipe hydronic distribuƟon system
throughout the campus and domesƟc hot water is provided by a separate, parallel hot water distribuƟon
system. HeaƟng and cooling in each unit is provided by a new fan coil unit (FCU) that replaces exisƟng
radiators. As a two-pipe system, the space condiƟoning loop can provide either heaƟng or cooling, but
not both simultaneous, and must undergo a system changeover. The secƟons below provide a more
detailed descripƟon of the system design developed by Salas O’Brien. Technical design documentaƟon is
provided in the Appendix, which are intended to allow submission for local permiƫng and pricing from
project implementers. Note that these designs are not intended to be used directly in construcƟon, and
require compleƟon of detailed plans such as in-unit piping and its implicaƟons with structural. A list of
design exclusions associated with Phase 1 of the project can be found with the design documents in the
Appendix.

Mechanical Design Overview
The design uses an array of modular heat pumps and high-eﬃciency gas-ﬁred condensing boilers to
generate chilled and heaƟng water for the project. The modular heat pumps are coupled with three (3)
geothermal ground loop heat exchangers (GLHX) to handle 100 percent of the cooling demand while
only handling a porƟon of the heaƟng demand, which will be oﬀset with boilers. Available space for the
ground loop heat exchangers is limited throughout site and is a primary driver for heat pump sizing. Heat
pump capaciƟes are intended to maximize uƟlizaƟon of the ground loop heat exchanger capacity while
the remaining heaƟng loads will be supported by the boilers. HeaƟng or chilled water for heaƟng or
cooling, respecƟvely, is generated and distributed to buildings through a primary (source)-secondary
(distribuƟon) pumping arrangement. HeaƟng water for domesƟc water heaƟng will is also distributed to
the building through a separate, primary (source)-secondary (distribuƟon) system.

A new 4000 A, 480 V electrical service will be brought to the central plant/mechanical room to power
the heat pump plant. A new switchboard will be added, along with a new 75 kVA transformer, and 225 A
panelboard. A new 450 kW generator and associated transfer switch will be added to power the
distribuƟon pumps and boilers to circulate heaƟng hot water in the event of a power outage. Note that
the generator is not sized to provide backup power to the enƟre heat pump plant. In the event of an
outage, the system would rely on the boilers for heaƟng, which are not sized to meet peak heaƟng loads
but can provide minimal heat required to prevent pipe freezing.

Building Envelope
The building envelope at the Stapleton Houses is of poor quality which leads to increased thermal
heaƟng and cooling loads. The front doors for most of the apartments have large air gaps at the boƩom
of the doors, which allows for signiﬁcant amounts of inﬁltraƟon, or outside air entering the building.
Also, the exisƟng windows have not been replaced since the original construcƟon and were in poor
condiƟon. Poor windows let in more sunlight and more heat in summer and do not retain heat well in
winter. Energy ConservaƟon Measures (ECMs) could be implemented in the short term, prior to ﬁnal
design, to improve these envelope deﬁciencies: door sweeps could be installed relaƟvely cost-eﬀecƟvely
to signiﬁcantly reduce the inﬁltraƟon, and the windows could be replaced with windows that meet
current energy code. Door sweeps can reduce the annual heaƟng by approximately 17% and cooling by
approximately 16% over the Baseline. New windows can reduce the annual heaƟng by approximately 7%
and cooling by approximately 14% over the Baseline. Together, door sweeps and new windows combined
can reduce the annual heaƟng by approximately 25% and cooling by approximately 31% over the
Baseline. Combined, these ECMs can potenƟally remove one boiler (of 6 proposed) and one WWHP
module (of 7 proposed) from the central plant. In addiƟon to equipment capital cost reducƟons, ECMs
reduce the system's annual operaƟng cost. See Appendix B for graphical results from energy modeling.

Energy ConservaƟon Measures
During this ﬁrst phase of the project, through review of exisƟng documents, several visits to the site, and
interviewing site personnel, it quickly became evident that the exisƟng system is severely ineﬃcient due
to old age, inadequate control systems, and poor building envelopes to name a few. These observaƟons
highlight the importance that energy eﬃciency upgrades can play an important role in any
decarbonizaƟon project in exisƟng buildings, parƟcularly when electrifying space heat in buildings of this
vintage. By directly reducing the thermal load of the building, the heaƟng/cooling equipment can be
reduced in size. This lower ﬁrst cost of the equipment and any potenƟal cost of electrical infrastructure,
if needed. In addiƟon, these updates can have a direct impact on resident / occupant comfort, as
idenƟﬁed in resident interviews. To address this, several energy conservaƟon measures were idenƟﬁed
that could be included in a retroﬁt project: door sweeps at tenant doors, replacement of windows, and
replacement doors. A summary of the energy modeling performed for the project was included early on
to demonstrate the methodology used to determine preliminary design for the building and highlight
how making changes at the building level can have a signiﬁcant impact on the size and quanƟty of the
new heaƟng and cooling equipment at the plant level. Refer to the separate aƩachment for more detail.

Geothermal Heat Pump InstallaƟon
A unique element of geothermal heat pump installaƟon compared to installaƟon of other electriﬁed
heaƟng and cooling technologies is the drilling requirement. Geothermal bore hole drilling requires
specialized drilling rigs, equipment, and experienced drillers. Fortunately, there are similariƟes between
geothermal bore hole drilling and other types of drilling like drilling for water wells and petroleum/oil
and gas drilling techniques. These similariƟes allow for potenƟal cross sector contribuƟon of labor, which
could alleviate any potenƟal drilling shortages due to uptake in geothermal heat pump installaƟons.

Once drilling is complete, installaƟon of the HVAC components of a geothermal heat pump system
commences. The proposed system design includes central water source heat pumps located in the
exisƟng mechanical room for central boilers. The piping from the geothermal bore holes will manifold
and connect to the water source heat pumps in the mechanical room, where distribuƟon pipes will route
to each building in the development, supplying hot water to fan-coil units in the winter, and chilled water
to fan-coil units in the summer. The system is designed as a hybrid system, with boilers providing peak
heaƟng capacity. The system also provides year-round domesƟc hot water to each building in the
development. This installaƟon requires HVAC and plumbing experience, speciﬁcally related to boilers,
refrigerant, controls, water piping connecƟons, pipe rouƟng, underground pipe installaƟon, fan coil unit
installaƟon, and thermostat installaƟon.

Geothermal Heat Pump Maintenance
There are many similariƟes between the development’s exisƟng hydronic heaƟng system and the
proposed geothermal heaƟng and cooling system. The largest diﬀerence between the baseline hydronic
system and the proposed geothermal heat pump is the introducƟon of heat pump equipment. These
require familiarity working with refrigerant-based systems common in HVAC and refrigeraƟon. Regular
maintenance of the heat pump requires measurement of the refrigerant charge levels in the system.
AddiƟon or removal of refrigerant requires EPA cerƟﬁcaƟon to ensure proper capture of the refrigerant,
which is a potent greenhouse gas. These skills are common in faciliƟes management staﬀ but are not
required at NYCHA’s developments today.

Another signiﬁcant diﬀerence is the presence of a fan-coil unit in each apartment, which is equipped

of the space condiƟoning systems. The geothermal heat pump system maintenance will require in-unit

Lessons Learned
The team has learned several lessons through the site selecƟon and design process that can inform
future development of geothermal heaƟng/cooling in public housing and other campus environments.
Besides the preferenƟal site criteria noted in the Site SelecƟon secƟon, one of the largest learnings from
this project was the challenge in reusing exisƟng infrastructure for geothermal heaƟng/cooling. The site
selected oﬀered an exisƟng hydronic loop for distribuƟon of hot water to the buildings for space

 28

condiƟoning and domesƟc hot water. This was idenƟﬁed as a resource that could potenƟally be
leveraged by a geothermal network. Yet once the iniƟal design concept was draŌed it was idenƟﬁed that
the exisƟng hydronic distribuƟon piping and in-unit equipment could not be reused with the geothermal
system. Speciﬁcally, the geothermal system would require increased ﬂow rates to meet the heaƟng
demand since it would deliver lower temperature water than the exisƟng boiler system, which delivered
200°F water. Because of this, the geothermal system could not reuse the exisƟng hydronic infrastructure
and would require replacing the campus distribuƟon, in-building piping (risers), and radiators in the
living units. Moreover, the exisƟng loop was found to have degraded over its life and was recommended
for replacement by facility heaƟng supervisor at the site.

As is common for sites with natural gas, the capacity of exisƟng electrical infrastructure at the site was
not suﬃcient to support electrify space and water heaƟng. This requires signiﬁcant investment to update
electrical service. These costs can be minimized by a central system such that electrical infrastructure
only needs to be addressed at one locaƟon. It was uncovered that the site selected experiences regular
electrical disrupƟons, most frequently as brownouts during hot summer weather. When considering
conversion from gas to electric heaƟng, backup power needs must be considered for emergency outages
during cold weather. With a gas heaƟng system, backup generators can be used with the electrical
equipment (pumps, etc.) needed to keep the hydronic system operaƟonal and to prevent catastrophic
failures from freezing pipes. An all-electric system would pose a signiﬁcant increase in backup power
requirements during these outages and would be much more costly and diﬃcult to procure. The ﬁnal
design for the site incorporated a hybrid gas and geothermal system was found to oﬀer a compromise.
The gas boilers could be operated alone during an electrical outage at the site, yet they were not sized to
saƟsfy the full heaƟng and hot water load during the coldest weather expected at the site. Thus, a hybrid
system may be able to provide some comfort and avoid catastrophic failures during an outage without
full electrical backup.

Several challenges to retroﬁƫng building systems were idenƟﬁed through the design process that can
inform future eﬀorts to decarbonize exisƟng mulƟ-family housing. A major challenge in any occupied
building is the impact that retroﬁt acƟviƟes would have on building occupants. Part of the
implementaƟon and construcƟon plan would have to consider how to provide temporary housing for
displaced residents as equipment in their living unit was replaced to support a geothermal system. In
addiƟon, since the design at this site planned to drill boreholes in the campus parking lot, alternaƟve
parking consideraƟons may be necessary. This can be especially challenging in a dense urban
environment like New York.

The age of the development that was selected also presented challenges that can be common with
retroﬁt projects. First, it was noted that the ﬂoor Ɵles in the apartments were very likely to be asbestos,
which would require signiﬁcant abatement to cut or penetrate. To avoid this added cost, the design
called for new piping risers to be installed on the exterior of the building rather than replacing the
exisƟng piping risers, which passed through the apartment ﬂoors. Such a design is not always feasible
due to the layout of the buildings, e.g. with a large number of interior rooms. External piping adds cost
for architectural design of the exterior and may not be permiƩed by local code, architectural, or
historical protecƟons.

In addiƟon, the energy eﬃciency of the building envelope was found to present a clear deﬁciency that
needed to be addressed as part of any heaƟng / cooling system upgrade. In parƟcular, resident feedback

 29

idenƟﬁed cold draŌs felt in the apartments in winter due to poor air sealing and insulaƟon of exterior
doors and windows. Several energy eﬃciency measures were idenƟﬁed to address this, speciﬁcally new
doors, door sweeps, and new windows. A more involved renovaƟon or “deep energy retroﬁt” could also
improve the insulaƟon of exterior walls—which were uninsulated concreate block—through insulated
over-cladding, for example. These issues with building envelope not only cause resident comfort
complaints, but also increase the thermal load that must be saƟsﬁed by the new heaƟng/cooling
equipment, and in turn increase the heaƟng/cooling equipment costs. It is recommended that these
issues be addressed prior to upgrading the heaƟng/cooling systems, which will reduce the capacity and
cost of heaƟng/cooling equipment. This development was unique in that every apartment had an
exterior door to an outdoor balcony that served as entry hallway, resulƟng in a unique need for
replacement/improvement of one exterior door per apartment, compared to a more typical
arrangement with two or three exterior doors per building. However, it is expected that window and
insulaƟon deﬁciencies are common for buildings of this vintage.

Finally, the grounds and landscaping can present a challenge to new geothermal heaƟng / cooling system
retroﬁts. As noted in the site selecƟon process, a building needs to have suﬃcient land area to support a
ground heat exchange that would saƟsfy the thermal load. In addiƟon, the land must be reasonably
open and accessible for a drilling equipment needed for boreholes. For exisƟng buildings, established
trees can present an obstrucƟon to drilling. Removing large trees can add costs, but in some cases may
not be permiƩed by the building owner or local jurisdicƟon due to the beneﬁts they provide (e.g.,
energy, environment, aestheƟc).

Best PracƟces IdenƟﬁed
Throughout the project the team idenƟﬁed several best pracƟces. Around site selecƟon, the team
idenƟﬁed characterisƟcs that make for a preferred site for geothermal: a substanƟal amount of space
that is free of underground obstrucƟons, relaƟvely ﬂat, accessible to drilling equipment, and with limited
exisƟng tree cover. Through the community engagement eﬀorts, it was idenƟﬁed that building envelope
improvements were needed at the site, as indicated by resident comfort concerns. This reinforced the
strategy that energy conservaƟon measures should always be considered as the ﬁrst step in
decarbonizing exisƟng buildings. Improvements made to the building envelope (e.g., insulaƟon,
windows, doors) directly reduce the thermal load on the heaƟng and cooling system and can have a
direct impact on occupant comfort. Moreover, reducing thermal load can directly reduce the heaƟng /
cooling capacity needed, and may lower ﬁrst costs of the new system. Finally, working with a design
partner that had extensive experience with community / campus geothermal systems allowed the team
to consider several system conﬁguraƟons and the technical and pracƟcal implicaƟons of each that should
be considered. In addiƟon, the design team was able to provide high-level cost informaƟon relaƟvely
early in the design process.

Outcome
At the conclusion of the iniƟal design phase funded by DOE, the team produced the technical design of a
community geothermal heaƟng/cooling system for the site. Technical drawings of the geothermal,
mechanical, and electrical systems were provided such that the system designed could be submiƩed for
permit review and pricing esƟmates for a follow-up construcƟon phase. The rough order-of-magnitude

 30

cost of the new system was esƟmated to be $61M, including geothermal system (drilling piping,

made to minimize the extend and cost of retroﬁt acƟviƟes, the esƟmated cost of the project far

Accomplishments
By the end of the project, the project team had completed all tasks and produced all deliverables
established in the project plan by DOE. In doing so, the project accomplished its objecƟve to design a
GHC system for a public housing provider. However, the project ulƟmately did not lead to the
deployment of this high eﬃciency system, with the associated beneﬁts around improved occupant
comfort and reduced emissions it would produce. The team was successful in idenƟfying the site
selecƟon criteria that make for a good candidate site for GHC and the technical consideraƟons that go
into system design. These ﬁndings will help promote the successful development of this technology in
other future projects. UlƟmately, the cost-eﬀecƟveness of GHC proved to be a challenge without
addiƟonal detailed analysis of separate energy eﬃciency measures, which was not in scope of the
project. Overall, the results of the project highlight the value of energy eﬃciency as the ﬁrst resource in
addressing building decarbonizaƟon, parƟcularly in older buildings in cold climates. Thus, it is
recommended that any future consideraƟons of GHC retroﬁts include (and budget) for the idenƟﬁcaƟon,
design and deployment of energy eﬃciency measures as part of the project to reduce the ﬁrst cost of a
GHC system.

E. Preliminary Case Study

Geothermal heat pumps oﬀer an eﬃcient technology for decarbonizing heaƟng and cooling. Yet this

and ﬁrst cost. Case studies of successful project pilots can serve to address these educaƟonal barriers
and provide technology transfer from research and demonstraƟon projects. This appendix provides the
framework for a case study of geothermal heaƟng and cooling at the NYCHA site selected for feasibility

Site Overview
The Stapleton development is a New York City Housing Authority (NYCHA) owned aﬀordable housing
development located in Staten Island, NY. NYCHA is the largest public housing authority in North
America, and was created in 1935 to provide decent, aﬀordable housing for low- and moderate-income
New Yorkers. NYCHA houses 1 in 17 New Yorkers and has 177,569 apartments in 2,411 buildings across
335 developments. NYCHA oﬀers both public housing and SecƟon 8 housing, and residents must meet
eligibility requirements which include income limits based on the number of people in each family.

Located at 181 Gordon St, the Stapleton development, from now on referred to as “the development”.
consists of 7 buildings comprised of 693 dwelling units that house approximately 1,850 residents. 31% of
the families living at the development operate on a ﬁxed income. The buildings are currently 62 years old
and operate using their original heaƟng system. Space heaƟng is provided by natural gas hydronic boilers
located in a central plant with hot water distribuƟon piping rouƟng to each building. This system also
provides domesƟc hot water for plumbing ﬁxtures throughout the development. There is no central
cooling system, but residents have the opƟon to uƟlize window air condiƟoners if they choose.

Lessons Learned
The team has learned several lessons through the site selecƟon and design process that can inform
future development of geothermal heaƟng/cooling in public housing and other campus environments.
Besides the preferenƟal site criteria noted in the Site SelecƟon secƟon, one of the largest learnings from
this project was the challenge in reusing exisƟng infrastructure for geothermal heaƟng/cooling. The site
selected oﬀered an exisƟng hydronic loop for distribuƟon of hot water to the buildings for space
condiƟoning and domesƟc hot water. This was idenƟﬁed as a resource that could potenƟally be
leveraged by a geothermal network. Yet once the iniƟal design concept was draŌed it was idenƟﬁed that
the exisƟng hydronic distribuƟon piping and in-unit equipment could not be reused with the geothermal
system. Speciﬁcally, the geothermal system would require increased ﬂow rates to meet the heaƟng
demand since it would deliver lower temperature water than the exisƟng boiler system, which delivered
200°F water. Because of this, the geothermal system could not reuse the exisƟng hydronic infrastructure
and would require replacing the campus distribuƟon, in-building piping (risers), and radiators in the
living units. Moreover, the exisƟng loop was found to have degraded over its life and was recommended
for replacement by facility heaƟng supervisor at the site.

As is common for sites with natural gas, the capacity of exisƟng electrical infrastructure at the site was
not suﬃcient to support electrify space and water heaƟng. This requires signiﬁcant investment to update
electrical service. These costs can be minimized by a central system such that electrical infrastructure
only needs to be addressed at one locaƟon. It was uncovered that the site selected experiences regular
electrical disrupƟons, most frequently as brownouts during hot summer weather. When considering
conversion from gas to electric heaƟng, backup power needs must be considered for emergency outages
during cold weather. With a gas heaƟng system, backup generators can be used with the electrical
equipment (pumps, etc.) needed to keep the hydronic system operaƟonal and to prevent catastrophic
failures from freezing pipes. An all-electric system would pose a signiﬁcant increase in backup power
requirements during these outages and would be much more costly and diﬃcult to procure. The ﬁnal
design for the site incorporated a hybrid gas and geothermal system was found to oﬀer a compromise.
The gas boilers could be operated alone during an electrical outage at the site, yet they were not sized to
saƟsfy the full heaƟng and hot water load during the coldest weather expected at the site. Thus, a hybrid
system may be able to provide some comfort and avoid catastrophic failures during an outage without
full electrical backup.

Several challenges to retroﬁƫng building systems were idenƟﬁed through the design process that can
inform future eﬀorts to decarbonize exisƟng mulƟ-family housing. A major challenge in any occupied
building is the impact that retroﬁt acƟviƟes would have on building occupants. Part of the
implementaƟon and construcƟon plan would have to consider how to provide temporary housing for
displaced residents as equipment in their living unit was replaced to support a geothermal system. In
addiƟon, since the design at this site planned to drill boreholes in the campus parking lot, alternaƟve
parking consideraƟons may be necessary. This can be especially challenging in a dense urban
environment like New York.

The age of the development that was selected also presented challenges that can be common with
retroﬁt projects. First, it was noted that the ﬂoor Ɵles in the apartments were very likely to be asbestos,
which would require signiﬁcant abatement to cut or penetrate. To avoid this added cost, the design
called for new piping risers to be installed on the exterior of the building rather than replacing the
exisƟng piping risers, which passed through the apartment ﬂoors. Such a design is not always feasible
due to the layout of the buildings, e.g. with a large number of interior rooms. External piping adds cost
for architectural design of the exterior and may not be permiƩed by local code, architectural, or
historical protecƟons.

In addiƟon, the energy eﬃciency of the building envelope was found to present a clear deﬁciency that
needed to be addressed as part of any heaƟng / cooling system upgrade. In parƟcular, resident feedback
idenƟﬁed cold draŌs felt in the apartments in winter due to poor air sealing and insulaƟon of exterior
doors and windows. Several energy eﬃciency measures were idenƟﬁed to address this, speciﬁcally new
doors, door sweeps, and new windows. A more involved renovaƟon or “deep energy retroﬁt” could also
improve the insulaƟon of exterior walls—which were uninsulated concreate block—through insulated
over-cladding, for example. These issues with building envelope not only cause resident comfort
complaints, but also increase the thermal load that must be saƟsﬁed by the new heaƟng/cooling
equipment. It is recommended that these issues be addressed prior to upgrading the heaƟng/cooling
systems, which will reduce the capacity of heaƟng/cooling equipment and thus ﬁrst cost. This
development was somewhat unique in that every apartment had an exterior door to an outdoor balcony
that served as entry hallway, resulƟng in a unique need for replacement/improvement of one exterior

door per apartment, compared to a more typical arrangement with two or three exterior doors per
building. However, it is expected that window and insulaƟon deﬁciencies are common for buildings of
this vintage.

Finally, the grounds and landscaping can present a challenge to new geothermal heaƟng / cooling system
retroﬁts. As noted in the site selecƟon process, a building needs to have suﬃcient land area to support a
ground heat exchange that would saƟsfy the thermal load. In addiƟon, the land must be reasonably
open and accessible for a drilling equipment needed for boreholes. For exisƟng buildings, established
trees can present an obstrucƟon to drilling. Removing large trees can add costs, but in some cases may
not be permiƩed by the building owner or local jurisdicƟon due to the beneﬁts they provide (e.g.,
energy, environment, aestheƟc).

Best PracƟces IdenƟﬁed
Throughout the project the team idenƟﬁed several best pracƟces. Around site selecƟon, the team
idenƟﬁed characterisƟcs that make for a preferred site for geothermal: a substanƟal amount of space
that is free of underground obstrucƟons, relaƟvely ﬂat, accessible to drilling equipment, and with limited
exisƟng tree cover. Through the community engagement eﬀorts, it was idenƟﬁed that building envelope
improvements were needed at the site, as indicated by resident comfort concerns. This reinforced the
strategy that energy conservaƟon measures should always be considered as the ﬁrst step in
decarbonizing exisƟng buildings. Improvements made to the building envelope (e.g., insulaƟon,
windows, doors) directly reduce the thermal load on the heaƟng and cooling system and can have a
direct impact on occupant comfort. Moreover, reducing thermal load can directly reduce the heaƟng /
cooling capacity needed, and may lower ﬁrst costs of the new system. Finally, working with a design
partner that had extensive experience with community / campus geothermal systems allowed the team
to consider several system conﬁguraƟons and the technical and pracƟcal implicaƟons of each that should
be considered. In addiƟon, the design team was able to provide high-level cost informaƟon relaƟvely
early in the design process.

Outcome
At the conclusion of the iniƟal design phase funded by DOE, the team produced the technical design of a
community geothermal heaƟng/cooling system for the site. Technical drawings of the geothermal,
mechanical, and electrical systems were provided such that the system designed could be submiƩed for
