3. Executive Summary

3.1 Technical Abstract
 Cully Community Solar project evaluated the technical feasibility of integrating
community-responsive deployment strategies into distributed photovoltaic system
development within a complex urban regulatory environment. Funded by the U.S.
Department of Energy Solar Energy Technologies Office (SETO), the project focused
on preconstruction engineering, permitting, and deployment readiness for community
solar infrastructure serving historically underserved populations.
The project resulted in the successful development and commissioning of a 1.25 MW
ground-mounted photovoltaic system while advancing engineering planning for
additional community solar capacity exceeding 11 MW. Engineering methodologies
addressed interconnection infrastructure requirements, aviation-related permitting
constraints, ecological site design integration, and workforce-centered deployment
sequencing.
This work was implemented through a multi-partner collaboration, integrating
engineering, workforce development, and community engagement into a coordinated
deployment framework. Subscriber engagement and workforce development were
embedded directly into technical workflows, aligning community participation milestones
with engineering and construction schedules. Results demonstrate that projects
designed around community-driven priorities can operate within conventional
engineering frameworks while maintaining system performance targets and contributing
to scalable distributed solar deployment.

3.2 Narrative
Cully Community Solar project; unlike conventional solar projects; prioritize rapid
deployment timelines, embedded workforce training, ecological design, and subscriber
education into core engineering workflows. This required adaptive coordination across
permitting agencies, utilities, and community stakeholders while maintaining system
performance objectives.
Major outcomes include:
 • Successful energization of a 1.23 MW community solar system.
 • Development of permitting strategies addressing aviation overlays and zoning
 constraints.
 • Integration of pollinator-friendly ecological design into photovoltaic infrastructure.
 • Engineering planning for multi-site expansion.
 The project shows that projects built around community needs can achieve technical
outcomes comparable to traditional photovoltaic development while expanding access
to renewable energy.

5. Background
Community solar represents a rapidly evolving segment of distributed photovoltaic
deployment, particularly in urban environments where rooftop ownership and structural
limitations restrict individual solar adoption. Over the past decade, research from
national laboratories and industry stakeholders has emphasized the importance of
shared solar models in addressing equity gaps within renewable energy access.
Traditional photovoltaic engineering workflows prioritize system performance
optimization, cost efficiency, and rapid interconnection. However, community solar
introduces additional variables including subscriber management, workforce training
integration, and environmental co-benefits. These factors influence engineering
decisions related to site layout, construction sequencing, and commissioning schedules.
Urban deployments introduce further complexity through aviation overlays, zoning
restrictions, and environmental review processes. Engineering teams must coordinate
with multiple regulatory agencies while maintaining system performance targets. These
challenges require adaptive deployment strategies that extend beyond conventional
solar development methodologies.

This project used a combined approach, supported through partnerships, where
community input helped inform engineering decisions. Cully Community Solar project
builds on this by integrating:
 • Workforce development into construction sequencing.

 • Ecological landscaping is aligned with photovoltaic infrastructure.
 • Subscriber engagement synchronized with engineering milestones.
Rather than treating community initiatives as external programmatic components, the
project incorporated community participation as an operational parameter influencing
engineering design decisions. This approach contributes to emerging research
exploring how distributed energy systems can align technical performance with
community-driven outcomes.

6. Project Objectives
The primary objective of Award DE-EE0010128 was to complete preconstruction and
engineering development activities supporting at least 1.5 MW of community solar
capacity while identifying scalable pathways for future deployment.

7. Project Results and Discussion

7.1 Engineering Development Methodology
Engineering development began with comprehensive site surveys evaluating soil
conditions, groundwater impacts, and potential environmental constraints. These
assessments informed system layout decisions, including racking configuration and
placement of electrical infrastructure.
Design development progressed through iterative phases involving conceptual layouts,
electrical schematics, and final construction drawings. Engineering teams prioritized
minimizing site disturbance by utilizing existing materials where feasible, for example,
existing gravel and aggregate from the site were reused to stabilize the access road and
equipment staging areas, reducing the need for new imported material. This reduced
environmental impact while maintaining structural integrity.

7.2 Permitting and Aviation Coordination
The project site’s proximity to regulated airspace required extensive coordination with
federal agencies. Engineering teams conducted glare analysis and height evaluations to
ensure compliance with aviation safety requirements. These processes introduced
extended review timelines but established a replicable permitting pathway for future
urban solar deployments.

7.3 Interconnection Engineering Analysis
Interconnection planning initially followed standard commissioning assumptions;
however, a late-stage utility requirement for installation of a recloser device, a utility
safety device that automatically interrupts power during faults and restores it once
conditions are safe, necessitated redesigning of portions of the electrical system.
Engineering teams adjusted commissioning workflows and procurement schedules to
integrate the new infrastructure. Despite extended timelines, system performance
following energization met expected benchmarks.

7.4 Construction Sequencing and Workforce Integration
Construction sequence incorporated workforce training objectives alongside
engineering milestones. Tasks suitable for trainee participation were scheduled to align
with training program timelines while ensuring compliance with safety standards.
Engineering supervisors collaborated with workforce coordinators to maintain
construction efficiency while enabling hands-on learning opportunities. For example,

basic assembly activities under supervision, allowing them to gain practical experience

7.5 Ecological Design and Pollinator Habitat Integration
A distinguishing engineering feature of Community Solar Site 1 involved the integration
of pollinator-friendly ecological landscaping within the photovoltaic system footprint.
Unlike conventional solar development approaches that prioritize uniform vegetation
clearance, the project implemented a design strategy that balanced electrical safety
requirements with ecological restoration goals.
Engineering considerations included:
 • Maintaining appropriate vegetation height to avoid shading or interference with
 photovoltaic modules.
 • Designing access pathways that allow for ongoing vegetation management while
 preserving habitat continuity.
 • Selecting native plant species compatible with long-term operations and
 maintenance protocols.
The ecological design required coordination between landscape specialists, electrical
engineers, and site maintenance planners to ensure that vegetation growth patterns
would not compromise system performance. Soil preparation activities incorporated
stormwater management strategies to reduce runoff and improve long-term site stability.
From a technical perspective, the integration of pollinator habitat did not introduce
measurable performance losses. Instead, it demonstrated that environmental co-
benefits can be incorporated into photovoltaic design without compromising system
efficiency.

7.6 Subscriber Integration as a Deployment Engineering Strategy
Subscriber enrollment and education are often treated as external programmatic
activities within community solar projects. However, this project integrated subscriber
engagement directly into deployment planning to ensure alignment between system
commissioning and community readiness.

Engineering teams worked with outreach staff to schedule workshops and enrollment
activities alongside construction milestones. This coordination helped reduce delays
between when the system was turned on and when customers began receiving the
benefits of the electricity produced. Technical benefits of this approach included:

•Reduced delays in paperwork and coordination when the system was turned on.
• Made it easier to estimate how much of the system’s energy people would use.
• Improved communication between the project team and community members.

7.8 Engineering Evolution of Community Solar Site 2
Community Solar Site 2 underwent significant design evolution during the project
lifecycle. Originally conceptualized as a smaller rooftop installation, engineering
feasibility analysis identified opportunities to expand the project into a larger
ground-mounted configuration adjacent to Site 1.
This transition required reassessment of electrical design, structural layout, and
interconnection planning. Engineering teams conducted additional site surveys to
evaluate soil conditions and potential environmental impacts associated with the
expanded footprint.
Despite achieving engineering readiness, construction activities were paused due to
financing uncertainty and evolving tax credit conditions. From a technical standpoint,
however, the project achieved several key milestones:

 • Completion of conceptual and preliminary design phases.
 • Execution of site lease agreements.
 • Development of interconnection strategy aligned with utility requirements.
For organizations planning community solar projects, establishing stable financial
planning early in the project lifecycle is critical. This includes securing committed
financing partners, confirming eligibility and timelines for tax credits and incentives, and
aligning project scale with available funding. Projects should also avoid relying solely
on tax credit incentives, as changes in federal policy or incentive structures can
introduce significant uncertainty for project delivery. Diversifying funding sources
and building contingency plans can help reduce this risk.
This experience highlights an important lesson for distributed solar deployment:
technical readiness must be complemented by stable financial planning and risk
mitigation strategies to ensure successful implementation.

7.9 Scaling the Deployment Model Through Future Site Exploration
Task 3 activities expanded significantly beyond original project targets. Engineering
exploration identified multiple potential sites representing approximately 11 MW of
additional community solar capacity. These opportunities are ground-mounted
configurations across diverse urban environments.
Engineering activities for future sites focused on:

 • Preliminary layout development.
 • Interconnection feasibility analysis.
 • Evaluation of workforce development opportunities
The expansion of the development pipeline demonstrates the scalability of the
engineering workflows established during the project. Key lessons learned from earlier
project phases informed planning for these future opportunities. From a permitting
perspective, early coordination with regulatory agencies and utilities is critical to reduce
delays and clarify requirements. In terms of ecological design, aligning vegetation
strategies with long-term operations and maintenance needs early in the design process
helps avoid rework. For workforce integration, clearly defining tasks that can be safely
performed by trainees and aligning training schedules with construction timelines
improves both efficiency and learning outcomes. These lessons were incorporated into
early-stage planning for outcomes.

7.10 Technical Risk Management and Adaptive Engineering
Throughout the project lifecycle, engineering teams addressed multiple technical risks
requiring adaptive decision-making.

Utility Infrastructure Requirements
Later in the interconnection process, the utility required installation of a recloser, an
automated protection device that detects electrical faults and helps improve grid
reliability. Because this requirement was introduced after the initial design was
completed, portions of the electrical system had to be redesigned, including updates to
protection equipment and coordination with utility specifications. The project team
successfully incorporated the required equipment and obtained interconnection
approval without impacting final system performance.

Regulatory Coordination
The permitting process required coordination with multiple agencies, including the
Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the City of Portland. FAA review included
submission of Form 7460-1, glare analysis, and height assessments to evaluate
potential impacts on nearby airspace. At the local level, land use and construction
permits required multiple review cycles and design revisions to address zoning and site
requirements. These processes extended project timelines and required ongoing
coordination and resubmittals to meet agency requirements.

Equity Integration as Engineering Innovation
A central contribution of the Cully Community Solar project lies in redefining equity
centered deployment as a form of engineering innovation. By embedding workforce
development and community participation into technical workflows, the project
demonstrated that distributed solar systems can achieve both performance objectives
and broader societal benefits.
Engineering teams observed that early engagement with community stakeholders,
including local residents eligible for community solar participation, community-based
organizations like Hacienda CDC and Habitat for Humanity supporting outreach and
enrollment, and workforce training partners; Leaders Become Legends improved project
transparency and facilitated smoother commissioning processes. Workforce integration
enhanced local capacity for future solar development, creating a feedback loop between
technical deployment and community empowerment.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
8.1 Major Technical Accomplishments
Successful Deployment and Operation of a 1.23 MW Community Solar System
The most significant accomplishment of the project was the commissioning and
operation of Community Solar Site1; a 1.23 MW ground-mounted photovoltaic system
developed within a highly regulated urban environment. Engineering teams successfully
navigated complex permitting requirements, interconnection challenges, and workforce-
integrated construction sequencing while maintaining system performance targets.
The operational system demonstrates that community-centered solar deployment
models can achieve technical outcomes comparable to conventional photovoltaic
projects. Early operational data indicates stable generation output consistent with
design projections.

Establishment of Replicable Urban Solar Permitting Pathways
As the first community solar project developed within the City of Portland, the Goodling
project required a learning process to navigate and align multiple permitting
requirements across agencies and jurisdictions. Because the installation is located on
Port of Portland property, the permitting process began with securing site approval and
design alignment with the Port. This initial step was critical to confirm site feasibility,
access, and compatibility with Port operational and development requirements before
advancing into external permitting processes.
Following site approval, early-stage environmental review requirements were addressed
under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), which guided initial site evaluation
and project planning considerations. This process informed design decisions and
ensured that potential environmental impacts were identified and addressed prior to
advancing the project.
Due to the site’s proximity to regulated airspace, the project required Federal Aviation
Administration (FAA) review. This included submission of FAA Form 7460-1, as well as
glare analysis and height assessments to evaluate potential impacts on aviation safety.
The design was refined through this process to confirm that the photovoltaic system
would not create hazardous glare or interfere with flight paths.
At the local level, the project required coordination with the City of Portland for land use,
zoning, and construction-related permits. This included review of site design,
compliance with zoning requirements, and approval of civil components such as
grading, access, and site improvements.
In parallel, the project team coordinated with the serving electric utility to complete the
interconnection process. This included engineering review of system design,
identification of required grid protection equipment, and approval to connect the system
to the distribution network.
In later stages of the project, coordination with the Oregon Department of Environmental
Quality (DEQ) was required to address site-specific environmental conditions and
ensure compliance with applicable state requirements. Additional site-related permitting
and coordination included stormwater management, erosion control, access, fencing,
lighting, and landscaping.
The overall permitting process required multiple rounds of review, design adjustments,
and coordination across agencies and project partners. A key lesson from the

Community Solar 1 experience is that urban community solar projects should begin site

interconnection, and environmental constraints.

Integration of Ecological Site Design Within Photovoltaic Infrastructure
The project successfully demonstrated the compatibility of pollinator-friendly ecological
landscaping with photovoltaic system performance. Engineering teams incorporated
native vegetation and stormwater management strategies into site design without
introducing shading risks or operational challenges.
This accomplishment highlights the potential for distributed solar projects to deliver
environmental co-benefits while maintaining technical efficiency. The ecological design
framework developed during the project provides a replicable model for integrating
habitat restoration into solar deployment workflows.

Expansion of Development Pipeline Beyond Original Scope
Engineering exploration conducted under Task 3 identified multiple future community
solar opportunities totaling approximately 11 MW of potential capacity. This expansion
significantly exceeded the original project goal of supporting 1.5 MW of community solar
development.
The expanded pipeline reflects the scalability of the deployment model established
through Site 1 and demonstrates the potential for continued growth of community-
responsive solar infrastructure in urban environments.

8.2 Challenges Encountered and Lessons Learned
Interconnection Infrastructure Requirements
The late-stage requirement for an additional piece of utility protection equipment
highlighted the importance of early and continuous coordination with grid operators.
Engineering teams adapted quickly to integrate the new infrastructure, but the
experience underscores the need for proactive interconnection planning in future
projects.
Urban Regulatory Complexity
Permitting timelines extended beyond initial projections due to aviation and zoning
considerations. Future projects should incorporate longer planning horizons to account
for regulatory review processes.
Financial and Market Conditions
Although engineering development for Community Solar Site 2 progressed successfully,
external financing conditions influenced project timelines. This outcome emphasizes
that technical readiness alone does not guarantee deployment; alignment with stable
financial structures is essential.

8.3 Broader Impacts and Contributions to the Field
The Cully Community Solar project contributes to national distributed energy research
by demonstrating that projects designed around community-driven needs can be
integrated into standard engineering and construction processes. In this project,
workforce training and community engagement were incorporated into project delivery
by aligning outreach activities and training schedules with construction milestones. For
example, enrollment workshops were conducted during construction to prepare
participants ahead of system completion, and trainees were assigned to defined tasks
such as site preparation and material organization under supervision.

8.4 Conclusions
The project validates the feasibility of integrating workforce development, ecological
design, and community participation into photovoltaic engineering processes without
compromising system performance. The outcomes provide a scalable framework for
advancing distributed solar deployment in urban environments while expanding
equitable access to renewable energy.

9. Path Forward
9.1 Future Engineering and Deployment Activities
Building on the technical foundation established during the project, future work will focus
on advancing engineering development for identified community solar sites. Activities
may include detailed design development, expanded interconnection studies, and
evaluation of cooperative ownership structures.
Engineering teams will continue refining deployment methodologies that integrate
ecological landscaping, workforce participation, and subscriber engagement into
construction workflows.

9.2 Technology Transfer and Commercialization Potential
The deployment model developed through this project offers strong potential for
replication across other urban environments. Key elements with commercialization
relevance include:
 • Workforce-integrated construction sequencing.

 • Equity-centered subscriber enrollment frameworks.
 • Ecological landscaping compatible with photovoltaic infrastructure.
These components provide a blueprint for scaling community solar development while
maintaining technical rigor.

Project Team and Collaborative Structure

Cully Community Solar project was delivered through a structured, multi-partner
collaboration designed to integrate technical execution, regulatory compliance,
workforce development, and community participation. The partnership model ensured
that engineering, interconnection, subscriber management, and community engagement
activities were coordinated in alignment with federal, state, and utility requirements.

Verde served as the lead recipient and project sponsor. Verde is a Portland-based
nonprofit organization with experience delivering community-based climate
infrastructure projects, including distributed solar and building decarbonization
initiatives.

For the Cully Community Solar project, Verde was responsible for:
 • Overall project management and DOE reporting compliance

 • Engineering coordination and contractor oversight

 • Budget administration and financial tracking
