Development and internal validation of a Viremia-Integrated Risk Assessment (VIRA score) for predicting mortality at diagnosis of COVID-19 pneumonia
Authors: Sonsoles Salto-Alejandre, Cristina Rodríguez-Urbistondo, Javier Martin-Escolano, Ana Ruiz-Molina, Carmen Infante-Domínguez, Gabriela Abelenda-Alonso, Mikel Del Álamo, Natalia Maldonado, Francisco Arnaiz-De las Revillas, Jorge Alba, Alexander Rombauts, Regino J Rodríguez-Álvarez, Zaira R Palacios-Baena, Claudia González-Rico, Sonia Santibañez, Marta Carretero-Ledesma, Laura Merino, Belén Gutiérrez-Gutiérrez, Mónica Gozalo-Margüello, María Carmen Fariñas, José Antonio Oteo, Ane J Goikoetxea-Agirre, Jordi Carratalá, Jesús Rodríguez-Baño, Elisa Cordero, Rocío Álvarez-Marín, Manuela Aguilar-Guisado, José Miguel Cisneros, Jerónimo Pachón, Javier Sánchez-Céspedes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCURB-65 score usefulness has been assessed in COVID-19 pneumonia, but not in cohorts including immunocompromised patients. SARS-CoV-2 RNAemia is associated with mortality and unfavorable clinical outcomes in COVID-19. This study aimed to develop the VIRA score (Viremia-Integrated Risk Assessment), incorporating CURB-65 and RNAemia, for predicting 30-day COVID-19 pneumonia mortality.MethodsWe included two multicenter cohorts of COVID-19 pneumonia: 539 immunocompetent and 280 immunocompromised patients, vaccinated against SARS-CoV-2 in 191 (35.4%) and 240 (85.7%), respectively. We employed multivariable logistic regression to identify predictors of 30-day mortality, with model performance assessed through area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and internal validation using calibration plots and bootstrap resampling. CURB-65 was available in 422 immunocompetent and 222 immunocompromised patients, constituting the derivation cohorts for the VIRA score. Integrated discrimination improvement was calculated for the addition of RNAemia, dyspnea, and male sex to CURB-65. Logistic regression coefficients were converted to the VIRA score for clinical application.ResultsMortality rates were 5.2% in immunocompetent and 15.0% in immunocompromised patients. Although CURB-65 ≥ 2 was associated with 30-day mortality in both groups (p < 0.001 and p = 0.001), the score consistently underestimated observed mortality across risk categories, most markedly in immunocompromised patients, where predicted mortality for the high-risk category (3 points: 14.5%) contrasted sharply with the observed 50.0%. At the high-risk threshold (CURB-65 score ≥ 3), sensitivity was poor in both groups (9.1% in immunocompetent and 8.6% in immunocompromised patients), with NPV of 95.2% and 85.3%, respectively. Incorporating RNAemia improved CURB-65’s discriminatory performance, raising AUROCs in immunocompetent (0.806 [95% CI, 0.701–0.910] vs. 0.771 [95% CI, 0.661–0.881]; IDI 0.023) and immunocompromised (0.745 [95% CI, 0.639–0.851] vs. 0.637 [95% CI, 0.532–0.742]; IDI 0.121) cohorts. At the equivalent high-risk cut-off (score ≥ 2), the VIRA score substantially improved sensitivity and NPV over CURB-65 alone: from 9.1% to 72.7% and from 95.2% to 98.2% in immunocompetent patients, and from 8.6% to 60.0% and from 85.3% to 91.9% in immunocompromised patients.ConclusionsRNAemia strengthens CURB-65’s predictive accuracy for 30-day mortality of SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia. The VIRA score, freely accessible at virascore.com, improves risk stratification especially in higher-risk categories and in immunocompromised patients.Clinical trial numberNot applicableSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41479-026-00208-z.Keywords: COVID-19, Pneumonia, Mortality, CURB-65, SARS-CoV-2 RNAemia, VIRA score, Immunocompromised patients

Introduction

SARS-CoV-2 primarily affects the respiratory system, causing a wide range of symptoms, from low-grade fever to severe pneumonia and, in critical cases, death [1]. In this new endemic phase, the virus remains a significant cause of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), alongside other well-known pathogens [2]. Studies reported mortality rates for hospitalized adults with COVID-19 pneumonia of approximately 11% [3–4], though rates varied widely depending on patient characteristics, ranging from 8% in general cohorts [2, 5] to more than 40% in older nursing home residents [6]. Reported COVID-19 pneumonia mortality in immunocompromised patients is substantially higher, approaching 20% in solid organ transplant recipients [7–9], a population largely excluded from prognostic studies.

The CURB-65 score [10] and Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) [11], established prognostic scoring systems designed for immunocompetent patients, have been assessed in COVID-19 CAP cohorts. A multicenter study involving 10,238 COVID-19 pneumonia patients in 2020 reported an area under the receiver operating characteristics (AUROC) curve of 0.84 for PSI and 0.83 for CURB-65, both with high sensitivity and negative predictive values (NPVs) [12]. Similar findings in smaller cohorts showed AUROCs ranging from 0.79 to 0.85 [5, 13, 14]. However, other studies have shown less consistent results, with one reporting that 61.3% of patients had CURB-65 scores of 0–1, yet a substantial number experienced increased mortality [15]. To enhance predictive accuracy, several studies have explored adding biomarkers such as D-dimer (DD) or procalcitonin to CURB-65 [13] and C-reactive protein (CRP) to PSI [5], with limited success. These attempts highlight the challenges of refining existing scoring systems for COVID-19 pneumonia.

Systemic dissemination of SARS-CoV-2 serves as both a marker and a mechanism of disease severity [16]. Large cohort studies have demonstrated a strong, independent association between RNAemia and adverse clinical outcomes, including in SOTRs [17, 18–23]. Despite these findings, no study has yet attempted to incorporate RNAemia into existing pneumonia risk scoring systems [24].

The present study has two primary objectives: to validate, for the first time, the CURB-65 score in immunocompromised patients with COVID-19 pneumonia; and to rigorously assess the potential of RNAemia detection to improve the predictive accuracy of CURB-65 in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised patients. By developing the VIRA score (Viremia-Integrated Risk Assessment), incorporating RNAemia and CURB-65 values, we aim to enhance the ability to predict 30-day mortality in SARS-CoV-2 CAP patients, particularly in vaccinated immunocompromised population.

Methods

The development of the predictive models followed the recommendations stated in the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) Initiative [25].

Source of data and participants

This study is a post-hoc analysis of a prospectively collected multicentre cohort of patients with SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia in Spain, from January 2020 to May 2023. The two developing cohorts — one comprising immunocompetent patients (n = 539) and one comprising immunocompromised patients (n = 280) — included the first consecutive 819 patients with SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia attended at the six participating centers. Immunocompromised status was defined as the presence of solid organ transplant, hematological stem cells transplantation, hematological malignancy, active solid neoplasia, primary immunodeficiency, human immunodeficiency virus infection with < 200 CD4 + lymphocytes/µL, and immunosuppressive therapy for other diseases, regardless of current treatment or disease status. The eligibility criteria required confirmation by RT-PCR assay from nasopharyngeal swabs, chest radiography findings consistent with pneumonia, and RNAemia determination at hospital admission (regardless of the result) [17]. All three criteria had to be met simultaneously for a patient to be included in the study. Patients were followed for 30 days after the diagnosis. Patient management was based on the clinical decisions of the attending physicians at each centre, guided by the periodically updated local protocols in each center, following the scientific soundness of the new available knowledge and the national and international treatment recommendations at each time point throughout the study period. Vaccinated patients were those who have received two or more doses of any authorized SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, or one dose in the case of single-dose vaccines. Otherwise, they were classified as unvaccinated.

Patient Consent Statement

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Virgen Macarena and Virgen del Rocío University Hospitals (CI 0771-N-20, CI 1683-N-21), which waived the requirement for informed consent in the context of the COVID-19 public health emergency, in line with the exceptional criteria applied by Ethics Committees across Spain and other countries during this period. Approval was also obtained from the Institutional Review Boards at each participating center and the study complied with the Helsinki Declaration.

Outcome and predictors

The outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality, measured from the day of diagnosis. Baseline variables recorded at the first hospital evaluation were grouped into the following clinically meaningful domains: (1) demographics: age in years and sex at birth; (2) comorbidities, encompassed by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI); (3) symptoms, including dyspnea and impaired consciousness; (4) signs, including low peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), fever, and hypotension; and (5) laboratory values, such as white blood cell and platelet counts, creatinine, liver enzymes, CRP, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and DD. The association of these variables with 30-day mortality was first explored within each domain using multivariable logistic regression. Variables showing a statistically significant association within their domain, as well as those not reaching statistical significance but considered clinically relevant and biologically plausible, were retained as candidate predictors. The CURB-65 score was also calculated when all values were available, and it was applied to 644 patients.

Statistical analysis methods

We conducted a complete case analysis, as well as a separate analysis including only patients for whom the CURB-65 score was available. CURB-65 could not be calculated in all patients due to missing values for some of its individual components — most notably respiratory rate and level of consciousness — which were not consistently recorded. Variables are presented as frequency and percentages and median with interquartile range (IQR). P values (two-sided) are calculated by χ2, Fisher’s exact test, Student’s t-test, or Mann-Whitney’s U test. Continuous variables were dichotomized for the analysis based on their correlation with mortality: age was divided into < 65 and ≥ 65 years, CCI into < 3 and ≥ 3 points, SpO2 into ≤ 90% and > 90%, CURB-65 into ≥ 2 and < 2 points, lymphocytes into < 1000/µL and ≥ 1000/µL, creatinine into > 1.3 and ≤ 1.3 mg/dL, LDH into > 300 and ≤ 300 U/L, CRP into > 100 and ≤ 100 mg/L, and DD into > 600 and ≤ 600 ng/mL.

Univariable logistic regression was performed on the candidate variables to estimate the magnitude of their individual association with mortality, and potential confounding and interaction effects were explored. The final models were constructed using a sequential, AUROC-based selection strategy: starting with the candidate predictor with the highest individual AUROC, additional variables were introduced sequentially, always selecting the combination yielding the highest joint AUROC. This process was repeated to form models of increasing complexity, stopping when the addition of a new variable resulted in an AUROC increment of less than 0.005 units. The predictive accuracy of our models was also compared with that of CURB-65.

We assessed model performance by examining measures of calibration and discrimination. Calibration plots were developed with estimates of the calibration slope and intercept. Calibration was also assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Discrimination was examined by calculating AUROC with 95% confidence intervals (CI). We conducted internal validation using bootstrap sampling with 1,000 random samples to estimate the optimism and shrinkage factor of the models. To determine the amount of improvement in the CURB-65 score, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) was calculated for the addition of RNAemia, dyspnea, or male sex, as appropriate.

The logistic regression coefficients were converted to the VIRA score (Viremia-Integrated Risk Assessment) for clinical application. The simplified scores were developed by dividing each coefficient by the coefficient with the lowest value and rounding to an integer. Risk groups were created based on the 30-day probability of death according to these scores. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and NPVs were calculated for different scores and the cut-offs indicating poor prognosis in each predictive assessment. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 27.0) and R (Version 4.4.1) packages. Graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism (Version 8.4.2).

Results

Participants

The first developing cohort included 539 immunocompetent patients, of whom 28 (5.2%) died, and 511 (94.8%) were alive within 30 days of diagnosis. The second cohort included 280 immunocompromised patients, with 42 (15%) deaths and 238 (85%) survivors (Fig. 1). Patient characteristics are detailed in Table 1. The median age (64 vs. 66 years) and the proportion of males (64.6% vs. 63.2%) were similar in both cohorts. Vaccination rates were 35.4% (n = 191) for immunocompetent and 85.7% (n = 240) for immunocompromised patients; 9.4% (n = 18) and 67.5% (n = 162) of them received ≥ 3 vaccine doses, respectively. Significant associations with mortality were identified for age ≥ 65 years, dyspnea, SpO2 ≤90%, CURB-65 ≥ 2, and RNAemia. In immunocompetent patients, additional variables associated with mortality were CCI ≥ 3, lymphocytes < 1000/µL, creatinine > 1.3 mg/dL, and CRP > 100 mg/L, while female sex was only associated in immunocompromised patients (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Model development and performance

We first assessed the standalone performance of the CURB-65 score. At the high-risk threshold (score ≥ 3), the CURB-65 score showed poor sensitivity in both patient groups (9.1% in immunocompetent and 8.6% in immunocompromised patients), with NPV of 95.2% and 85.3%, respectively (Supplementary Table S3). The VIRA score (Viremia-Integrated Risk Assessment) substantially improved both metrics at the equivalent high-risk cut-off (score ≥ 2): sensitivity and NPV reached 72.7% and 98.2% in immunocompetent patients, and 60.0% and 91.9% in immunocompromised patients, respectively (Supplementary Table S4).

For immunocompetent patients, the final prediction models are shown in Table 2 for all cases and those with available CURB-65, respectively. The model for all cases started with age ≥ 65 years as the variable with the highest predictive capacity (AUROC 0.735, 95% CI: 0.661–0.809). The final sequence included age ≥ 65, SpO2 ≤90%, and RNAemia, with 30-day mortality probability: P death at day 30 = 1 / (1 + exp (-b)), where: b = 0 (if age < 65 years) + 2.513 (if age ≥ 65 years) + 1.872 (if SpO2 ≤90%) + 1.221 (if RNAemia) − 5.599. The final model was well-calibrated (Hosmer-Lemeshow test: 3.21, p = 0.524 vs. p < 0.050; Supplementary Figure S1A) and showed an AUROC of 0.846 (95% CI: 0.763–0.928), p < 0.001 (Supplementary Table S5). Internal validation using bootstrapping indicated an optimism of 0.003 and a shrinkage factor of 0.929. In 28.9% of samples, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was significant.

The model for immunocompetent patients with available CURB-65 started with CURB-65 ≥ 2, yielding an AUROC of 0.771 (95% CI: 0.661–0.881). The final sequence included CURB-65 ≥ 2 and RNAemia, where 30-day mortality predicted probability was: P death at day 30 = 1 / (1 + exp (-b)), where: b = 0 (if CURB-65 < 2) + 2.331 (if CURB-65 ≥ 2) + 0.979 (if RNAemia) − 4.234. This model also demonstrated good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test: 0.002, p = 0.999 vs. p < 0.050; Supplementary Figure S1B) and an AUROC of 0.806 (95% CI: 0.701–0.910), p < 0.001 (Supplementary Table S5). Using bootstrapping, an optimism of 0.005 and a shrinkage factor of 0.984 were estimated. In 97.5% of samples, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was significant. The addition of RNAemia significantly improved predictive performance compared to CURB-65 alone, with an IDI of 0.023 (95% CI: 0.012–0.090).

For immunocompromised patients, the final prediction models are in Table 3 for all cases and those with available CURB-65, respectively. The model for all cases began with RNAemia, achieving an AUROC of 0.655 (95% CI: 0.571–0.738). The final sequence of variables included RNAemia, age ≥ 65, dyspnea, and male sex, following the equation: P death at day 30 = 1 / (1 + exp (-b)), where: b = 0 (if negative RNAemia) + 1.462 (if RNAemia) + 1.541 (if age ≥ 65 years) + 1.134 (if dyspnea) − 1.004 (if male sex) − 3.773. This model demonstrated good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test: 8.296, p = 0.405 vs. p < 0.050, Supplementary Figure S1C), with an AUROC of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.717–0.869), p < 0.001 (Supplementary Table S5), estimated optimism of 0.033 and a shrinkage factor of 0.931. In 20.7% of samples, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was significant.

The model for immunocompromised patients with available CURB-65 also started with RNAemia. The final sequence included RNAemia, CURB-65 ≥ 2, dyspnea, and male sex. The predicted probability of 30-day mortality was: P death at day 30 = 1 / (1 + exp (-b)), where: b = 0 (if negative RNAemia) + 1.084 (if RNAemia) + 1.272 (if CURB-65 ≥ 2) + 0.934 (if dyspnea) − 0.924 (if male sex) − 2.890. The final model showed good calibration across the range of risk (Supplementary Figure S1D), and the goodness-of-fit Hosmer-Lemeshow test was 12.602, p = 0.082 vs. p < 0.050, confirming the calibration of the model, with an AUROC of 0.745 (95% CI: 0.639–0.851), p < 0.001 (Supplementary Table S5). Using bootstrapping, an optimism of 0.045 and a shrinkage factor of 0.921 were estimated. In 45.9% of samples, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was significant. The IDI for adding these variables to CURB-65 was 0.121 (95% CI: 0.044–0.253).

Discussion

This study is the first to validate the CURB-65 scoring system for predicting 30-day mortality in a population of immunocompromised patients with COVID-19 pneumonia, over 80% of whom had been previously vaccinated against SARS-CoV-2, while also reaffirming its clinical value for immunocompetent patients. However, our findings reveal consistent and clinically relevant discrepancies between CURB-65 predictions and observed mortality, particularly in immunocompromised patients, underscoring the need for an enhanced tool. Incorporating RNAemia into CURB-65 — the basis of the VIRA score (Viremia-Integrated Risk Assessment) — significantly improves AUROC, sensitivity, and NPV, highlighting its critical role as a biomarker for effective risk stratification in both patient groups.

Reported COVID-19 pneumonia mortality varies widely depending on age, comorbidities, and disease severity [2–6]. While immunocompetent individuals typically show mortality rates < 10% [2], immunocompromised patients demonstrate rates approaching 30% [17]. Our findings align with this landscape, with 5.2% and 15% mortality rates in immunocompetent and immunocompromised patients, respectively — the latter despite a high vaccination coverage exceeding 80%.

RNAemia consistently emerged as an independent predictor of mortality across both cohorts, reflecting viral escape into the bloodstream that overwhelms the immune response and accelerates organ damage [18, 19]. Its prevalence in our cohorts (19.5% in immunocompetent and 54.6% in immunocompromised patients) was consistent with rates previously reported by our group [17], reinforcing the biological plausibility and reproducibility of this finding. While prior studies have struggled to improve CURB-65’s predictive performance by incorporating biomarkers such as DD, CRP, procalcitonin, glucose, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, eosinophils, lactate, and LDH [5, 13, 26, 27], our approach prioritizes RNAemia, a variable with strong mechanistic rationale and straightforward clinical acquisition. Previous assessments of CURB-65 and PSI for COVID-19 were moreover primarily conducted early in the pandemic [12–14], limiting their applicability to the current endemic and vaccinated context.

Integrating RNAemia into CURB-65 improved prediction of 30-day mortality in both cohorts. In immunocompetent patients, AUROC increased from 0.771 to 0.806, and at the high-risk threshold the VIRA score (≥ 2) markedly improved sensitivity (9% to 73%) while preserving a high NPV (95% to 98%). The benefit was greater in immunocompromised patients, where baseline discrimination was poor with AUROC 0.637, increasing to 0.745 with the addition of RNAemia, dyspnea, and male sex (IDI 0.121). At the same threshold, sensitivity rose from 9% to 60% and NPV from 85% to 92%.

CURB-65 [10] showed acceptable calibration only at low risk in immunocompetent patients, but substantially underestimated mortality at higher scores (14.5% predicted vs. 25.0% observed for score 3). The VIRA score corrected this miscalibration and improved discrimination, stratifying patients into four clearly distinct risk groups (observed mortalities ranging from 1.4% to 28.6%) and providing a clinically actionable gradient. In immunocompromised patients, CURB-65 consistently and markedly underestimated mortality (14.5% and 37.5% predicted vs. 50.0% and 100.0% observed), limiting its clinical utility. The VIRA score provided better calibration and more realistic risk gradient (8.1% to 77.8%), and critically avoided classifying any patient as low risk — a clinically appropriate reflection of this group’s baseline vulnerability that the original CURB-65, designed for immunocompetent populations, was unable to capture. Overall, CURB-65 alone is insufficient for risk stratification in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia, particularly in the immunocompromised, whereas the VIRA score offers a more accurate and clinically meaningful tool for early prognostication.

The apparently higher AUROCs of the models excluding CURB-65 compared to the VIRA score most likely reflect the statistical advantage of larger sample sizes rather than superior intrinsic discriminative capacity, as both sets of models were evaluated in different patient populations. Structurally, both models are closely related: CURB-65 ≥ 2 replaces age ≥ 65 years in immunocompromised patients, and both age ≥ 65 years and SpO2 ≤ 90% in immunocompetent patients — variables already embedded within CURB-65. In immunocompromised patients, the VIRA score preserves the negative predictive value of the simplified score while improving positive predictive value and specificity. We prioritize the VIRA score as our primary clinical contribution because it enhances an internationally validated and widely adopted prognostic tool rather than proposing an entirely new scoring system, thereby maximizing clinical translatability.

A particularly notable finding in the immunocompromised model was the independent protective effect of male sex (OR 0.37–0.40), which was consistent across both models with and without CURB-65. This observation, while exploratory and requiring prospective confirmation, is biologically plausible and aligned with emerging literature on sex-based differences in COVID-19 immunity. Women generally mount more robust innate and adaptive immune responses than men; however, in the context of pharmacological immunosuppression, this heightened immunoreactivity may paradoxically confer a disadvantage. A more exuberant inflammatory response under conditions of partial immune suppression could promote greater cytokine-mediated tissue damage and worse clinical outcomes, particularly in severe viral infections such as COVID-19 pneumonia [28]. Sex-related differences in immunosuppressant pharmacokinetics — including higher effective drug exposure in women at equivalent doses for agents such as tacrolimus — may further compound this effect by resulting in deeper real-world immunosuppression [29]. These mechanisms warrant dedicated investigation in larger, prospective cohorts of immunocompromised patients.

There are several limitations in our study. First, immunocompromised patients were treated as a single group without differentiating between immunosuppression types, which may overlook meaningful variations in risk profiles across distinct patient subpopulations. Second, we did not distinguish between episodic and persistent RNAemia, a distinction that might offer additional prognostic insights and warrants exploration in future studies. Third, the relatively small cohort size limits generalizability, and the absence of an external validation cohort highlights the need for caution when applying these results to broader populations. The post-hoc nature of this analysis, performed on a prospectively collected cohort, while appropriate for model derivation, may introduce some limitations in the granularity of certain clinical variables, as data collection was not originally designed with this specific predictive objective in mind. Additionally, as patient management followed attending physician decisions guided by evolving national and international recommendations throughout the study period, treatment heterogeneity across time and centres cannot be excluded as a potential source of variability in outcomes. Our cohort was drawn exclusively from hospital settings in a single country, which raises questions about the score’s transportability to primary care environments — where RNAemia determination is not yet routinely available — and to populations with different epidemiological or healthcare contexts; cross-validation with multinational datasets would be valuable for confirming generalizability. A further limitation relates to the temporal heterogeneity of the study cohort, which spans multiple variant eras (wild-type/Alpha, Delta, and Omicron) from January 2020 to May 2023. The progressive consolidation of hybrid immunity — through vaccination and prior infection — and the emergence of new variants with altered pathogenicity have further reduced the risk of severe outcomes beyond the study period, even in immunocompromised patients.

Our study offers several important strengths. It is the first to validate CURB-65 in the post-vaccine era for COVID-19 pneumonia, and the first to do so specifically in immunocompromised patients, over 80% of whom had received prior vaccination against SARS-CoV-2 — providing a more representative evaluation of CURB-65’s performance in the current endemic landscape. By incorporating RNAemia, a biomarker with strong biological plausibility and consistent independent prognostic value, the VIRA score improves on CURB-65 in a way that previous attempts with other biomarkers have failed to achieve. Model development followed TRIPOD recommendations throughout, ensuring rigorous and transparent reporting, and performance estimates were further strengthened by calibration assessment and internal validation through bootstrap resampling. To facilitate implementation, the VIRA score is freely accessible as an interactive online calculator at virascore.com, allowing clinicians to obtain an immediate and individualized mortality risk estimate at the point of care — a feature that distinguishes this tool from conventional scoring systems and supports its adoption in routine clinical practice.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that CURB-65, while associated with 30-day mortality in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised patients with SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia, consistently underestimates true mortality risk — most critically in immunocompromised patients. Incorporating RNAemia into the CURB-65 framework gives rise to the VIRA score, which corrects these deficiencies by improving discrimination, sensitivity, and NPV across a clinically meaningful risk gradient. Designed for immediate use at the point of first hospital evaluation, the VIRA score could meaningfully improve early risk stratification, particularly for immunocompromised patients — a population that has most to gain from more accurate and tailored risk assessment tools. External validation in independent, multinational cohorts will be essential to confirm its generalizability.
