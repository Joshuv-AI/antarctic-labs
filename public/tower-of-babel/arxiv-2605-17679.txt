PULSE: Agentic Investigation with Passive Sensing for Proactive Intervention in Cancer Survivorship Journal: IMWUT Volume: 0 0 0 9 CCS: Human-centered computing Ubiquitous and mobile computing systems and tools CCS: Computing methodologies Natural language processing
Zhiyuan Wang, Ariful Islam, Indrajeet Ghosh, Xinyu Chen, Katharine E. Daniel, Subigya Nepal, Philip Chow, Laura E. Barnes

Abstract
. Cancer survivors face elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and general emotional distress, yet the precise moments they most need support are often the moments when self-report is sparse, a phenomenon we term the diary paradox . Passive smartphone sensing offers a continuous, unobtrusive alternative, but prior sensing-based affect prediction has been limited by an accuracy ceiling, suggesting a bottleneck not only in available data, but in how behavioral signals are interpreted. We present Pulse , a system that shifts from fixed feature pipelines to agentic sensing investigation : LLM agents equipped with eight purpose-built tools autonomously query smartphone sensing data, compare current behavior against personalized baselines, and calibrate inferences through retrieval-augmented population-level comparisons. Rather than receiving pre-formatted feature summaries, agents decide which modalities to inspect, how far back to look, and how deeply to investigate, mirroring hypothesis-driven clinical reasoning. We evaluate Pulse through a 2 × \times 2 factorial design crossing reasoning architecture (structured vs. agentic) with data modality (sensing-only vs. with diary) on 50 cancer survivors from a longitudinal study of cancer survivors. Agentic reasoning is the primary driver of performance: agentic multimodal agent achieves balanced accuracy of 0.743 for emotion regulation desire with diary and sensing data, while agentic agents predict intervention availability at 0.713 with passive sensing data only. These results suggest that agentic investigation may be a cornerstone for unlocking the clinical value of passive sensing, advancing the feasibility of proactive just-in-time mental health support.

1.
Introduction

More than 18 million cancer survivors live in the United States in 2026, and almost a third experience clinically significant depression, anxiety, or emotional distress in the years following treatment
(
59
;
39
)
. Clinical guidelines now recommend routine screening and management of these symptoms
(
6
)
, yet timely and scalable mental health interventions remain underdeveloped. Just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs) aim to use mobile technology to offer support to survivors at precisely the moments when support is most needed and most likely to help
(
42
)
. Effective JITAIs could reduce the mental health burden of cancer survivors at scale. However, an effective JITAI must do more than detect distress—it must infer whether support is currently desired and whether the survivor is available to engage with different types of support were it to be offered. In JITAI terminology, this process is determined by
tailoring variables
,
intervention options
,
decision points
, and
decision rules
, or components that determine when, whether, and how to intervene from real-time signals
(
43
)
.

The dominant approach to estimating if an intervention should be delivered relies on ecological momentary assessment (EMA), which are brief self-report surveys delivered multiple times daily to a survivor through their mobile phone
(
57
)
. While EMA provides invaluable ground truth for research, it introduces a fundamental paradox when used as the input to a proactive support system:
the very moments when intervention is most needed are precisely when self-report data are most likely to be absent

(
20
;
41
)
. We call this the
diary paradox
. For example, one cancer survivor experiencing acute distress may stop responding to surveys because they are too overwhelmed; another survivor caught in a depressive episode may lack the motivation to complete a diary entry. As a result, the data that would be most clinically informative for these hypothetical survivors—those that capture periods of high distress and low functioning—would be missing not at random. In CALLM
(
71
)
, the authors highlighted that diary text is highly predictive of emotional states when available, but it cannot be used to predict emotional states when the diary itself is absent. A truly proactive system must be able to reason about the user’s state from data that requires no active participation.

Passive smartphone sensing provides exactly this: continuous, unobtrusive streams of behavioral data—movement patterns, location traces, screen usage, sleep timing, and social communication—that accumulate regardless of the user’s engagement with diaries or other self-report prompts. A decade of mobile sensing research, from StudentLife
(
68
)
and early digital phenotyping
(
55
;
48
)
to GLOBEM
(
76
)
, has established that these behavioral signals correlate with mental health outcomes. Yet, the translation from correlation to clinically useful prediction has been frustratingly slow. Prior work reveals a persistent prediction ceiling: traditional machine learning models achieve approximately 0.52-0.60 balanced accuracy on passive sensing-based affect prediction, barely above chance for many targets, and this ceiling persists across datasets and feature engineering approaches
(
76
;
2
;
34
)
.

Why does this ceiling exist? While there are several possibilities, we argue that the problem is not in the data itself but in the reasoning that traditional models apply to these data. Fixed feature pipelines extract the same summary statistics from every user on every day, regardless of context. They cannot notice that a particular user’s 3:00 AM screen session is anomalous relative to their personal baseline, or that a sudden drop in GPS mobility following a week of high activity might signal a withdrawal pattern. They cannot synthesize sleep disruption, reduced social communication, and increased late-night phone use into a coherent clinical narrative. The bottleneck lies not in feature extraction but
contextual behavioral signal interpretation

(
44
)
—the kind of integrative reasoning that a clinician performs when interpreting a patient’s behavioral record.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) suggest a different path forward. LLMs can synthesize behavioral evidence in natural language, and prior work has shown that they can reason about both mental health text and wearable sensing summaries
(
26
;
77
;
74
)
. But most systems still hand the model a pre-formatted summary and ask it to reason using whatever the fixed pipeline has already chosen to expose. The model may be eloquent, yet it still cannot decide what to inspect, which anomaly to pursue, or how to compare today’s behavior against the right personal baseline.

In this paper, we ask two questions:
(1) can LLM agents predict intervention opportunities from passive sensing data, and (2) does giving the LLM autonomy to investigate sensor data—dynamically deciding which modalities to query, how far back to look, and which anomalies to pursue—improve prediction over fixed analytical pipelines?

We introduce
Pulse
, a system that shifts the paradigm from feature engineering to
agentic investigation
of passive sensing. Rather than feeding pre-extracted features into a prediction model,
Pulse
equips an LLM agent with eight purpose-built sensing query tools—implemented via the Model Context Protocol (MCP)
(
7
)
—and allows the agent to investigate each user’s behavioral data over multiple turns. The agent decides what sensing data modalities to examine, what temporal windows to query, what baseline comparisons to make, and how to synthesize evidence into a prediction. This investigation strategy is not fully pre-specified by the system designer; it emerges from the interaction between the agent’s self-emerged behavioral reasoning capabilities and the eight tools it is offered, following the ReAct paradigm of interleaving reasoning with action
(
79
)
. In doing so,
Pulse
operationalizes the idea of contextual behavioral signal interpretation as an interactive reasoning process rather than a one-shot feature transform.

To cleanly isolate the contribution of agentic investigation from data modality, we evaluate
Pulse
through a 2
\times
2 factorial design crossing two dimensions: (1)
reasoning architecture
—structured (fixed pipeline, single LLM call) versus agentic (autonomous multi-turn tool use); and (2)
data modality
—sensing-only (passive sensing data alone) versus multimodal (sensing plus diary text). This yields four primary conditions—Struct-Sense, Auto-Sense, Struct-Multi, and Auto-Multi— all evaluated on 50 cancer survivors from the BUCS (Better Understanding Cancer Survivors) observational study, with each system producing predictions for all 4,112 EMA entries (50 users, 74–96 entries per user, mean 82.2).

We focus on
intervention opportunity
as the conjunction of two complementary constructs.
Emotion regulation desire
asks whether the user currently wants support.
Intervention availability
asks whether the user is behaviorally able to engage with support right now. A useful JITAI must estimate both: a willing but unavailable user cannot act on an intervention, while an available but uninterested user is unlikely to benefit from one.

Our contributions are:

(1)

A new paradigm: agentic sensing investigation.
We introduce the concept of LLM agents that autonomously investigate passive sensing data through purpose-built query tools, replacing fixed feature pipelines with adaptive, context-sensitive behavioral reasoning. We provide eight reusable MCP tools and an open architecture that the community can extend to other sensing domains.

(2)

A rigorous 2
\times
2 factorial evaluation.
Our factorial design cleanly isolates the agentic effect: agentic agents outperform structurally identical but non-agentic agents on the same data, with all differences significant at
p<0.0001
(bootstrap test). This is the first controlled comparison demonstrating that agentic reasoning, not merely data modality, is the primary driver of prediction accuracy in mobile sensing.

(3)

State-of-the-art affect and intervention-opportunity prediction in a clinical population.

Pulse
achieves 0.743 balanced accuracy on emotion regulation desire (with diary and sensing data) and 0.713 on intervention availability (from passive sensing alone), substantially exceeding traditional ML baselines (
{\sim}
0.52) and prior diary-dependent approaches on the latter target.

(4)

A dissociation between behavioral and emotional constructs.
We demonstrate that intervention availability is best predicted by passive sensing (0.713), with negligible improvement from adding diary text (0.722), while emotion regulation desire
(
22
)
benefits substantially from diary input (0.743 vs. 0.651). This dissociation has direct implications for JITAI design: systems should model desire and availability as separate constructs leveraging distinct data sources.

2.
Related Work

Pulse
sits at the intersection of four research threads: passive mobile sensing for mental health, large language models for health and affective computing, agentic AI with tool use, and just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs).

2.1.
Passive Sensing for Mental Health

The foundational insight that smartphone sensor data correlates with mental health outcomes dates to StudentLife
(
68
)
, which demonstrated associations between passively sensed behavioral patterns (sleep, sociability, physical activity, phone usage) and depression, stress, and academic performance in college students. Saeb et al.
(
55
)
further showed that GPS features—location variance, circadian movement, and time spent at home—correlated with depressive symptom severity, while Canzian and Musolesi
(
10
)
demonstrated that mobility traces alone could track depressive states. Onnela and Rauch
(
48
)
formalized the concept of “digital phenotyping,” and Torous et al.
(
60
)
connected it to the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework for psychiatry. These early studies launched a productive decade of mobile sensing for mental health research, supported by instrumentation frameworks such as AWARE
(
19
)
, with systems extending to detecting relapse and symptom changes in schizophrenia
(
67
;
8
)
, tracking depression dynamics
(
69
)
, identifying digital biomarkers of mood disorders
(
25
)
, detecting behavioral changes during the COVID-19 pandemic
(
24
;
47
)
, and detecting depression onset from longitudinal passive data
(
11
)
. Recent large-scale efforts include a four-year mobile sensing study of college students
(
45
)
and systematic reviews of digital phenotyping for mental health monitoring
(
35
;
12
)
.

Alongside these advances, however, a persistent challenge has emerged:
predictive models trained in one context rarely generalized to another
. The GLOBEM benchmark
(
76
)
, which systematically evaluated 18 algorithms across four longitudinal sensing datasets, revealed that most models achieve balanced accuracy in the range of 0.50–0.55 on depression and affect prediction, with cross-dataset transfer often performing at chance. Meegahapola et al.
(
34
)
replicated this ceiling across eight countries for mood inference, and Adler et al.
(
2
)
documented the same pattern across longitudinal sensing datasets, showing that models trained on one study population failed to generalize to another. Feng et al.
(
18
)
recently provided a comprehensive comparison of traditional ML, deep learning, and LLM approaches on smartphone sensing data, confirming that standard supervised approaches plateau around the same accuracy levels.

As Adler et al.
(
3
)
argued in “Beyond Detection,” the field must move from passive detection of correlates to actionable clinical support. The problem is not that smartphone sensors lack informative signals—they do contain them—but that fixed feature pipelines cannot perform the contextual interpretation needed to translate raw signals into clinically meaningful predictions. This explanation is intuitive: The same 500 steps of walking means different things for a user who typically walks 5,000 steps versus one who averages 200, and its emotional significance likely depends on time of day, concurrent phone usage, location context, and the user’s longitudinal trajectory.

Pulse
addresses this limitation directly: rather than engineering better features, it gives an LLM agent the tools to investigate behavioral data in context, performing the kind of personalized, integrative reasoning that fixed pipelines cannot.

2.2.
LLMs for Affective Computing and Health

The application of LLMs to health prediction has evolved rapidly, building on the few-shot reasoning capabilities first demonstrated in GPT-3
(
9
)
and the instruction-following capabilities of RLHF-aligned models
(
49
)
. Singhal et al.
(
58
)
showed that LLMs encode substantial clinical knowledge, while Cosentino et al.
(
15
)
demonstrated that LLMs can reason about personal health data including sleep and fitness metrics. Health-LLM
(
26
)
established that LLMs can reason about wearable sensor data across multiple health tasks, evaluating 12 models on 10 prediction targets with various prompting strategies. Mental-LLM
(
77
)
demonstrated LLM-based mental health prediction from online text data, achieving state-of-the-art results on depression and stress detection benchmarks. Zhang et al.
(
81
)
were among the first to combine LLMs with smartphone sensing features for affect prediction, showing that LLMs with structured sensor summaries could outperform traditional ML in some configurations. SensorLLM
(
32
)
further explored aligning LLMs with raw motion sensor data for activity recognition, and a recent survey
(
53
)
documents the rapid growth of LLM applications for wearable health data. Zhang et al.
(
82
)
provide a reality check on LLM sentiment analysis, highlighting both capabilities and limitations relevant to affect prediction.

The system most directly related to our work is CALLM
(
71
)
, which demonstrated that combining diary text with TF-IDF-based cross-user retrieval and LLM reasoning achieves strong performance on emotional state prediction for cancer survivors (the same population and prediction targets we address here). However, CALLM is fundamentally
reactive
: it requires diary text as input and cannot make predictions when diary entries are absent.
Pulse
extends CALLM’s approach from reactive, diary-dependent prediction to proactive, sensing-based investigation, replacing diary dependence with agentic investigation of passive sensing data.

Several systems have explored related uses of LLMs. MindScape
(
46
)
integrates LLMs with behavioral sensing to personalize and contextualize LLM-generated journal prompts. GLOSS
(
13
)
employs multiple LLMs for open-ended sense-making of passive sensing data, using code generation to analyze behavioral patterns. LENS
(
75
)
aligns multimodal sensing data with language models for mental health narrative synthesis. Vital Insight
(
31
)
combines visualization with human-in-the-loop LLM analysis for expert-driven sensemaking of personal tracking data. PHIA
(
36
)
represents the closest conceptual comparison: it is an LLM agent that generates and executes code to analyze wearable data, answering retrospective health questions. IoT-LLM
(
5
)
provides a general framework for LLM reasoning over IoT sensor data. These systems collectively demonstrate that LLMs can reason about health-relevant behavioral data across a range of modalities and populations. However, they differ substantially in the degree of autonomy granted to the model—a distinction we examine next through the lens of agentic AI.

2.3.
Agentic AI and Tool Use

The agentic AI paradigm, in which LLMs autonomously plan, reason, and take actions through tool use, has emerged as a powerful framework for complex problem-solving. A foundational enabler is chain-of-thought prompting
(
73
)
, which unlocks multi-step reasoning in LLMs and underlies the deliberative processes of agentic systems. ReAct
(
79
)
demonstrated that interleaving reasoning traces with tool-use actions produces more reliable and interpretable outcomes than either pure reasoning or pure action. Toolformer
(
56
)
showed that LLMs can learn to use tools in a self-supervised manner, and Gorilla
(
51
)
extended this to massive API function calling. Voyager
(
65
)
introduced the concept of embodied LLM agents with growing skill libraries that explore open-ended environments—an analogy that resonates with
Pulse
’s approach of equipping agents with investigation tools for an open-ended behavioral data space. Lewis et al.
(
30
)
introduced retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), which provides the foundation for
Pulse
’s cross-user calibration mechanism, and Gao et al.
(
4
)
recently surveyed the growing role of RAG in healthcare applications.

More recently, Wang et al.
(
66
)
provided a comprehensive survey of LLM-based autonomous agents, categorizing approaches by their planning, memory, and tool-use capabilities. Huang et al.
(
23
)
offered a complementary analysis focused specifically on the planning capabilities of LLM agents. The Model Context Protocol (MCP)
(
7
)
has emerged as an open standard for connecting LLMs to external tools and data sources, providing a reproducible interface specification. ContextAgent
(
78
)
demonstrated proactive LLM agents with wearable sensing perceptions in an egocentric modality space. In the clinical domain, ReflecTool
(
33
)
introduced reflection-aware tool-augmented agents that build long-term memory from successful problem-solving episodes and verify tool usage through iterative refinement, demonstrating that structured reflection over tool outcomes can improve clinical task performance.

An important caveat for health sensing is that LLMs are unreliable numerical reasoners when asked to directly manipulate tabular data; they can produce fluent but incorrect interpretations.
Pulse
addresses this by assigning counting, aggregation, thresholding, and temporal retrieval to deterministic tool code, while reserving the LLM for contextual interpretation of those results. This division of labor is central to our design and to our claim that agentic value comes from investigation strategy rather than from letting the model “do the math” itself.

Positioning of
Pulse
.
Within this landscape,
Pulse
occupies a distinct position along several dimensions. First, it employs a ReAct-style reasoning loop
(
79
)
for prospective prediction from passive sensing, rather than retrospective analysis or single-pass prompting. Second, it provides eight domain-specific sensing tools that the agent invokes selectively during investigation, in contrast to code generation or fixed prompt templates. Third, it combines peer-calibrated retrieval with persistent session memory to personalize investigation across episodes, drawing on both population-level patterns and individual longitudinal context. Finally, it is evaluated on a clinical cancer survivorship cohort with validated psychological outcome measures, rather than general-population or convenience samples. These differences are not intended to suggest that prior systems are inadequate for their respective goals; rather, they reflect a set of design choices motivated by the specific requirements of proactive intervention prediction from continuous passive sensing in a clinical population.

2.4.
Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions

The JITAI framework
(
42
;
43
)
identifies six key components for adaptive intervention systems: decision points, intervention options, tailoring variables, decision rules, proximal outcomes, and distal outcomes. Tailoring variables—the real-time signals used to adapt intervention delivery—are recognized as the lynchpin of effective JITAI design, yet estimating them in real time from passive data remains the central challenge
(
42
)
. Even still, a recent meta-analysis
(
17
)
found that JITAIs produce small but significant improvements in mental health outcomes across diverse populations, despite finding that JITAIs in mental health are mostly stuck in the conceptual stage and have rarely moved beyond initial piloting. This emphasizes their value while showing that there is room for improvement.

Kunzler et al.
(
29
)
introduced the concept of “state-of-receptivity” for mHealth interventions, decomposing receptivity into separate components including physical availability and psychological openness. Mishra et al.
(
38
)
advanced this with in-the-wild receptivity detection using smartphone and wearable sensors, demonstrating that contextual factors (time, activity, phone usage) predict intervention engagement. Klasnja et al.
(
27
)
developed the microrandomized trial (MRT) methodology for evaluating JITAI components, later applied in HeartSteps
(
28
)
for physical activity promotion. Mohr et al.
(
40
)
introduced the IntelliCare suite of skills-based apps for treating depression and anxiety, illustrating that digital interventions can adapt to user needs. Notification timing in behavior change contexts has also been studied through MRT designs
(
21
)
.

Pulse
’s prediction targets—emotion regulation desire (ER
desire
) and intervention availability (INT
avail
)—map directly to the various JITAI components. ER
desire
, grounded in the emotion regulation framework of Gross
(
22
)
, captures the psychological dimension of receptivity (does the user want support?), while INT
avail
captures the behavioral dimension (is the user in a context where they can engage?). Our finding that these two constructs are best predicted by different data modalities has direct implications for JITAI design, as we discuss in Section
6
.

3.
System Design

Figure
2
presents the
Pulse
system architecture. The system follows a three-stage pipeline:
Sense
(passive smartphone data and optional diary text),
Think
(an LLM agent that investigates behavioral data through a ReAct-style reasoning loop with eight MCP sensing tools and cross-user RAG for calibration), and
Inference
(predicted affect, emotion regulation desire, and intervention availability, accompanied by natural-language reasoning and a confidence score). After each prediction, the real outcome—defined as the conjunction of elevated ground-truth desire to emotionally regulate and self-reported intervention availability—feeds an agent reflection step whose summary is stored in persistent per-user memory for future episodes. Figure
3
illustrates a concrete investigation trace from the evaluation.

3.1.
Problem Formulation

Given a cancer survivor’s available data at a decision point
t
—which may include passive sensing history, an optional diary entry, a user profile, and accumulated session memory—
Pulse
predicts: (1) three continuous affective states (Positive Affect, Negative Affect
(
72
)
, and emotion regulation desire
(
22
)
); and (2) sixteen binary state indicators, including individualized positive and negative affect states, discrete emotions (happy, sad, afraid, miserable, worried, cheerful, pleased, grateful, lonely), interaction quality, physical pain, future outlook, emotion regulation desire state, and intervention availability.

We focus our analysis on four clinically grounded targets that map to JITAI tailoring variables
(
42
;
16
)
:

•

ER_desire
(Emotion Regulation Desire State): Does the user currently desire support for emotion regulation?—an opportunity indicator for intervention delivery.

•

INT_availability
(Intervention Availability): Is the user available to engage with an intervention right now?—a behavioral readiness indicator.

•

PA_State
(Positive Affect State): Is the user’s positive affect high relative to their personal baseline?

•

NA_State
(Negative Affect State): Is the user’s negative affect high relative to their personal baseline?

These targets decompose the intervention opportunity into complementary dimensions: ER_desire captures the psychological need for support, while INT_availability captures the contextual feasibility of delivering it. A JITAI system should ideally intervene when both are present
(
29
;
38
)
.

3.2.
Factorial Design

3.3.
The BUCS Dataset

Pulse
is evaluated on the BUCS dataset
(
16
)
, a longitudinal study of 407 adult cancer survivors who completed 5 weeks of data collection. The dataset was obtained with the original study team and used with institutional review board approval. Data collection included:

•

Ecological Momentary Assessment

(
57
)
: Three daily surveys (randomly delivered each day in the morning, afternoon, evening) capturing positive and negative affect ratings, discrete emotions, emotion regulation desire, intervention availability, and brief diary entries about emotional drivers.

•

Passive Smartphone Sensing
: Continuously collected streams including accelerometer-based motion (stationary, walking, running, automotive activity), GPS location (distance traveled, time at home, location variance), screen usage (session counts, durations, lock/unlock patterns), app usage (category-level durations; Android only), and sleep detection (accelerometer-derived sleep periods). Keyboard activity (typing sessions, character counts) is sparsely available given participants’ willingness and agreement to share this modality.

•

User Profiles
: Baseline demographic information, cancer type and treatment history, and validated psychological trait measures.

Platform differences between iOS and Android affect data availability: app-level usage is available only on Android (approximately 36% of the test sample). The sensing tools are designed to gracefully handle missing modalities, returning explicit “data not available” responses that the agent incorporates into its reasoning. The data collection protocol was approved [anonymous IRB]; all participants provided informed consent

3.4.
Structured Agents

The structured agents (Struct-Sense, Struct-Multi) implement a fixed pipeline in which all available data is pre-assembled and the LLM produces a prediction in a single call. This design mirrors the predominant approach in prior LLM-for-sensing work
(
81
;
26
)
.

Input Assembly.
For each prediction, the system pre-computes a sensing summary covering all available modalities for the current day (sleep metrics, mobility statistics, screen usage patterns, activity breakdowns). This summary, along with the user’s trait profile, a longitudinal memory document, and cross-user calibration examples, is assembled into a structured prompt.

Cross-User Calibration.
To ground the LLM’s predictions in empirical distributions, both structured and agentic agents have access to cross-user calibration data. For structured agents, this takes the form of TF-IDF-based text retrieval (in multimodal mode) or cosine-similarity-based sensing fingerprint matching (in sensing-only mode) against a peer database constructed from training-fold participants. The top-
k
most similar entries, including their
ground truth
EMA outcomes, are included in the prompt as calibration anchors.

Prompting Strategy.
Within a single LLM call, the prompt asks the model to consider the available behavioral modalities (sleep, mobility and activity, and social signals), reference the peer calibration anchors, and produce a final prediction. The natural-language reasoning the model generates inside the prompt is open-ended, but the input context and the elicited output schema are held fixed across all predictions to keep this baseline a faithful representative of the predominant “pre-assemble and prompt” approach in prior LLM-for-sensing work.

3.5.
Agentic Agents

The agentic agents (Auto-Sense, Auto-Multi) implement autonomous behavioral investigation through a multi-turn tool-use loop, leveraging chain-of-thought reasoning
(
73
)
interleaved with tool actions following the ReAct paradigm
(
79
)
. Rather than receiving a pre-formatted data summary, the agent receives a system prompt describing its clinical task, a user profile, an optional diary entry, and access to eight MCP sensing query tools. For example, the sensing-only system prompt begins:

You are an expert behavioral data scientist specializing in affective computing and just-in-time adaptive interventions for cancer survivorship. Your task: predict the emotional state of a cancer survivor at the moment they are about to complete an EMA survey, using ONLY their passive smartphone sensing data. You have access to MCP sensing tools that let you query their behavioral data. Use these tools like a detective—investigate the signals that matter, compare to their personal baseline, look for behavioral anomalies.

The multimodal variant additionally instructs the agent to analyze the current diary entry and use sensing tools to validate and calibrate diary-derived hypotheses. The agent’s investigation strategy
emerges
from the interaction between its clinical reasoning and the available tools.

3.5.1.
MCP Sensing Tools.

A core design principle is the separation of computation from interpretation. All counting, aggregation, thresholding, and temporal retrieval is handled by deterministic tool code; the LLM receives only natural-language results and is responsible solely for contextual interpretation. This division of labor directly addresses the known unreliability of LLMs as numerical reasoners
(
18
;
26
)
while preserving their strength in integrative reasoning. Our tool design is further grounded in two additional principles. First, clinical reasoning in behavioral assessment is hypothesis-driven: practitioners begin with an overview, form provisional explanations, and then seek confirming and disconfirming evidence through targeted investigation. Second, affect prediction from behavioral data is strongly idiographic: what matters is often not an absolute value but whether today’s pattern deviates from this person’s own norm. We therefore provide tools that support broad orientation (
get_daily_summary
,
get_behavioral_timeline
), targeted follow-up (
query_sensing
,
query_raw_events
), within-person contextualization (
compare_to_baseline
,
find_similar_days
), and cross-user calibration (
find_peer_cases
).

We implement eight domain-specific tools via the Model Context Protocol
(
7
)
, each designed to mirror a step in clinical behavioral chart review:

(1)

get_daily_summary
: Returns a natural-language overview of a day’s behavioral patterns across all sensing modalities. Supports multi-day lookback for trend analysis. Serves as the typical starting point for investigation.

(2)

get_behavioral_timeline
: Reconstructs the day as a chronological timeline of behavioral segments (e.g., “6:00–9:00 AM: stationary, low screen use, possible sleep continuation; 9:00–12:00 PM: walking detected, moderate screen activity”). Configurable segment width (1–6 hours).

(3)

query_sensing
: Targeted query for a specific modality within a configurable time window (1–48 hours before the EMA, with 1–24 hour durations). Returns hourly or daily aggregates. Allows the agent to zoom into specific signals of interest.

(4)

query_raw_events
: Accesses the raw event stream for a modality (individual app sessions, screen lock/unlock events, activity transitions, typing sessions). Provides fine-grained temporal detail when the agent needs to resolve specific questions (e.g., “When did she first unlock her phone this morning?”).

(5)

compare_to_baseline
: Computes z-scores comparing a current metric to the user’s personal historical distribution. Enables anomaly detection—the agent can identify when today’s behavior deviates from this individual’s norm, which is more informative than absolute values.

(6)

get_receptivity_history
: Retrieves the user’s past intervention availability patterns and aggregate mood trends over a configurable lookback window (up to 14 days). Provides temporal context for receptivity prediction.

(7)

find_similar_days
: Identifies past days with the most similar behavioral fingerprint (cosine similarity over normalized sensing features) and returns the user’s actual emotional states on those days. Enables analogical reasoning: “On past days with similar behavior, this user felt…”

(8)

find_peer_cases
: Searches across other participants for similar cases via text (TF-IDF similarity of diary entries) or sensing (behavioral fingerprint similarity) and returns their ground truth outcomes. Provides population-level calibration anchors.

All tools enforce a strict temporal boundary: only data collected
before
the current EMA timestamp is accessible. This prevents information leakage and mirrors the prospective deployment scenario where the system must predict before knowing the outcome.

3.5.2.
Investigation Loop.

The PULSE agent operates through a multi-turn conversation with tool-use capabilities. A typical investigation proceeds as follows:

(1)

The agent reads the user profile and (if available) diary entry, forming initial hypotheses about the user’s likely emotional state.

(2)

It calls
get_daily_summary
to orient itself on today’s behavioral patterns.

(3)

It calls
get_behavioral_timeline
to understand how the day unfolded chronologically.

(4)

Based on these overviews, it formulates targeted queries—for example, calling
query_sensing
on screen usage if the summary suggests unusual phone behavior, or
query_raw_events
on app usage to inspect which applications were active.

(5)

It calls
compare_to_baseline
to contextualize observed metrics against the user’s personal history.

(6)

It uses
find_similar_days
and/or
find_peer_cases
to retrieve empirical calibration anchors.

(7)

It synthesizes all evidence into a prediction, outputting a structured JSON object with all 16 binary targets, 3 continuous scores, a reasoning trace, and a confidence estimate.

Critically, the agent is free to deviate from this typical order. It may skip steps it deems uninformative, revisit earlier queries after discovering new information, or pursue investigation paths not anticipated by the system designer. The maximum number of tool-use turns is capped at 16 to bound inference time, though most predictions complete in 6–12 turns (30–90 seconds).

Figure 3
.
Actual Auto-Multi investigation trace from the evaluation (Participant 089). The agent forms a grief hypothesis from the diary, confirms behavioral withdrawal through sensing tools, but encounters conflicting evidence from peer calibration (peers in grief typically want support). Session memory from 39 prior entries resolves the conflict: this person’s stoic coping style (6% receptivity) overrides the population prior. All four binary predictions are correct.

3.5.3.
Per-User Session Memory.

As the agent processes sequential EMA entries for a given user, it accumulates a session memory document containing brief reflections from prior predictions—summaries of the user’s observed patterns, notable behavioral changes, and calibration insights. Importantly, this memory contains only the agent’s own reasoning and behavioral observations,
never
ground truth EMA outcomes, preventing data leakage across entries. The memory document is provided as additional context in the system prompt.

3.6.
Cross-User RAG for Calibration

A distinctive feature of
Pulse
is the use of cross-user retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
(
30
)
not for factual knowledge retrieval—the LLM already possesses clinical knowledge—but for
empirical calibration
. When the agent calls
find_peer_cases
, it receives examples from other cancer survivors with similar behavioral patterns or diary entries, along with their actual EMA outcomes. These serve as calibration anchors: the agent learns what PANAS scores, emotion regulation desire levels, and intervention availability states are
empirically associated
with the behavioral patterns it is observing.

This calibration mechanism operates in three modes across the system:

•

Text-based
(multimodal conditions): TF-IDF similarity over diary text, retrieving participants with similar diary content and their outcomes.

•

Sensing-based
(sensing-only conditions): Cosine similarity over normalized daily sensing feature vectors, retrieving participants with similar behavioral fingerprints.

•

Tool-based
(agentic conditions): Both modes available through
find_peer_cases
, with the agent choosing which to invoke based on available data.

The peer database is constructed from training-fold participants and strictly excludes the current user, enforcing the across-subject evaluation protocol.

4.
Evaluation Methodology

4.1.
Evaluation Setup

We evaluate
Pulse
on a test sample of 50 users randomly selected from the 399 BUCS participants with sufficient data (mean 82.2 entries per user, range 74–96). We chose 50 users as a balance between inference cost and statistical robustness. Each agentic user costs approximately $2–5 in LLM inference (across
{\sim}
80 multi-turn predictions), making full-cohort evaluation of all 399 users across five systems prohibitively expensive. At the same time, 50 users with
{\sim}
80 entries each provide sufficient power for within-subject factorial comparisons while spanning diverse behavioral profiles. The main comparison evaluates the four factorial systems plus CALLM, each on all 4,112 EMA entries for the 50 users.

4.2.
Baselines

4.2.1.
Reference Baselines.

We report three families of reference baselines to contextualize the aggregate scoreboard. First, we include traditional sensing baselines (Random Forest, XGBoost, and Logistic Regression) trained on hourly sensing features across six modalities: motion (stationary, walking, running, automotive minutes), screen (total minutes, session count), GPS (travel distance, time at home, location variance), keyboard (session count, character count), app usage (total and per-category minutes), and light (ambient lux). For each EMA timestamp, features are extracted from hourly windows preceding the prediction point, yielding a flat feature vector (e.g.,
h0_screen_on_min
,
h1_motion_walking_min
, etc.). The lookback horizon (1, 2, 4, 6, 12, or 24 hours), number of selected features, and model hyperparameters are jointly optimized via grid search within 5-fold across-subject cross-validation on the full 399-user dataset. Second, we include text-only baselines: TF-IDF+Logistic Regression, Bag-of-Words+Logistic Regression, and MiniLM sentence embeddings+Logistic Regression. Third, we include combined baselines (Random Forest and Logistic Regression) over the concatenation of hourly sensing features and diary embeddings. All reference predictions are filtered to the same 50-user evaluation subset.

These baselines are informative but not the paper’s cleanest fairness comparison. They differ from
Pulse
in training population, feature representation, and data access. The primary comparative claim therefore remains the within-same-50-users factorial contrast between structured and agentic agents operating on the same modality conditions.

4.2.2.
LLM Baseline.

CALLM
(
71
)
serves as the LLM baseline, using diary text with TF-IDF cross-user retrieval and longitudinal memory. CALLM represents the strongest existing LLM-based approach for affect prediction in cancer survivor populations.

4.3.
Metrics

Balanced Accuracy (BA) is the primary metric for binary targets, defined as the arithmetic mean of sensitivity and specificity. BA is preferred over standard accuracy for health data with class imbalance, as it gives equal weight to both classes regardless of base rates. Macro F1 is reported as a secondary metric.

For all analyses, we report entries-level balanced accuracy computed across all predictions pooled from all 50 users, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 resamples). Statistical significance is assessed via bootstrap resampling of the BA difference between conditions, with
p
-values computed as the proportion of resamples where the observed advantage disappears.

4.4.
Implementation

All LLM inference uses Claude Sonnet (Anthropic), a transformer-based
(
64
)
LLM, via the Claude CLI, with a maximum of 5 concurrent processes and 16 tool-use turns per prediction. Sensing tools are served via an MCP server launched as a subprocess for each prediction, ensuring per-user data isolation. Inference time ranges from 30 to 90 seconds per prediction depending on the number of tool calls. Each of the five systems (four factorial plus CALLM) produces 4,112 predictions on the 50-user sample.

5.2.
Intervention Opportunity Detection

The central clinical question is whether the system can detect
intervention opportunity
: whether support is desired and whether the user is available to engage with it. Table
5
summarizes per-target and aggregate results for all four focus constructs across systems.

5.2.1.
ER_desire.

Emotion regulation desire shows the expected pattern of benefiting from multimodal data. Auto-Multi achieves 0.743 balanced accuracy, substantially exceeding both the matched structured multimodal agent (Struct-Multi: 0.655) and the diary-only LLM baseline CALLM (0.629). Diary text is highly informative for this construct because it directly expresses the psychological state that ER_desire captures. The multimodal agentic gain over Auto-Sense (0.651) confirms that diary content carries signal that passive sensing alone cannot fully replace.

5.2.2.
INT_availability: A Behavioral Construct.

Intervention availability reveals a strikingly different pattern. Auto-Sense achieves 0.713 balanced accuracy from passive sensing alone, and adding diary text provides only marginal improvement (Auto-Multi: 0.722,
\Delta=0.010
). This near-equivalence is in sharp contrast with the substantial diary benefit for ER_desire (
\Delta=0.092
). Moreover, the sensing-only agentic agent (0.713) dramatically outperforms both CALLM (0.545)—which has diary text but no sensing data—and Struct-Multi (0.552)—which has both data modalities but uses a structured pipeline.

This dissociation reveals that INT_availability is fundamentally a
behavioral
construct: it captures whether the user is in a physical and contextual state conducive to intervention engagement (e.g., not driving, not in a meeting, not asleep), and these states are directly reflected in passive sensing streams (motion activity, screen usage, location, time patterns). Diary text, which captures
what the user is feeling
, provides minimal additional information about
what the user is doing
. The agentic architecture is essential because it can synthesize these behavioral signals into a holistic availability assessment—something that neither a fixed feature pipeline nor a diary-only method can achieve.

5.2.3.
Joint Intervention Opportunity Framing.

Taken together, ER_desire and INT_availability define the intervention opportunity problem. We do not introduce a separate conjunction metric here, but the pattern across the two components is clinically revealing: Auto-Multi is the only main system that is simultaneously strong on both desire (0.743) and availability (0.722), while Auto-Sense already recovers most of the availability signal without any diary input. This is precisely the combination needed for proactive JITAI decision-making.

{\sim}
16.7 vs. ground truth
{\sim}
18.0, MAE = 5.42), exhibiting variance compression (predicted SD 4.7 vs. ground truth SD 8.1). ER desire shows the best continuous calibration (MAE = 1.74,
r
= 0.61).

Importantly, these continuous biases do not substantially impair binary classification performance because the
individualized
state thresholds absorb much of the systematic offset. Whether a user’s predicted Negative Affect of 9 is classified as “unusually high” depends on that user’s personal baseline, not on the population mean. The structured agents (CALLM, Struct-Multi) show better-calibrated continuous predictions but worse binary performance, suggesting that the agentic agents trade absolute calibration for more discriminative binary decisions. At the same time, the negativity bias is a genuine failure mode: it indicates that stronger binary performance should not be misread as perfect affect calibration.

5.8.
Agentic Investigation Patterns

5.8.1.
Tool Usage Patterns.

Across all predictions (31,686 total tool calls), agentic agents invoke an average of 7.5 tools per prediction. Three tools form a near-universal core:
get_daily_summary
(99.7% of predictions),
get_behavioral_timeline
(99.7%), and
find_peer_cases
(99.7%). The anomaly detection tool
compare_to_baseline
is used selectively (60.7%), while the remaining tools—
find_similar_days
(14.2%),
query_sensing
(4.9%),
query_raw_events
(4.4%), and
get_ receptivity_history
(4.3%)—are deployed only when the agent’s initial overview suggests they are needed. This emergent three-tool core strategy—orient with overview tools, then calibrate with peers—was not prescribed; the agent converged on it autonomously.

5.8.2.
Confidence Calibration.

Each prediction includes a self-reported confidence score between 0 and 1 (mean 0.76, SD = 0.10). This confidence is well-calibrated with actual accuracy: predictions with confidence below 0.65 achieve 66.9% average accuracy across the four focus targets, predictions with confidence 0.65–0.80 achieve 74.1%, and predictions above 0.80 achieve 82.5%. We examine the relationship between confidence and ER_desire accuracy in more detail in Section
5.10
.

5.8.3.
Computational Footprint.

Each prediction consumes approximately 88,000 input tokens and 4,000 output tokens, with session memory growing to an average of 51,000 characters per user by the end of the evaluation. Each prediction takes 30–90 seconds wall-clock time, including MCP server startup, multi-turn tool use, and response generation.

5.8.4.
Example: Structured vs. Agentic on the Same Entry.

Consider a multimodal prediction for a user whose diary entry reads: “Feeling tired but trying to stay positive after my appointment.” The structured agent (Struct-Multi) receives a pre-formatted summary showing moderate screen usage, low walking, and moderate GPS mobility. It follows the fixed pipeline: notes adequate sleep, observes reduced mobility, notes the appointment mention in the diary, and predicts slightly elevated positive affect based on the “stay positive” language.

The agentic agent (Auto-Multi), given the same underlying data, pursues a different strategy:

(1)

Reads the diary and notes the tension between tiredness and positive coping.

(2)

Calls
get_daily_summary
and observes the day’s overall patterns.

(3)

Notices low walking minutes and calls
compare_to_baseline
on walking, discovering it is 1.8 standard deviations below this user’s personal mean.

(4)

Calls
get_behavioral_timeline
and observes a gap in activity during the morning hours, consistent with a medical appointment.

(5)

Calls
query_sensing
on screen usage and finds a prolonged phone session in the waiting room.

(6)

Calls
find_similar_days
and identifies three past days with similar behavioral patterns (low mobility + medical context), finding that the user’s affect on those days was lower than their diary language suggested.

(7)

Calls
find_peer_cases
with the diary text and finds that peers expressing similar “trying to stay positive” language had moderate-to-low positive affect.

(8)

Synthesizes: the diary expresses coping effort, not genuine positive affect; behavioral evidence confirms reduced activity; past analogs suggest the user’s positive affect is likely below their personal mean.

The agentic agent’s prediction is more calibrated because it was able to (a) detect the anomalous mobility through personal baseline comparison, (b) contextualize the diary language through peer calibration, and (c) use analogical reasoning from the user’s own history. None of these reasoning steps are available to the structured agent, which processes all data in a single pass without the ability to form and test hypotheses.

5.8.5.
Case Study: Personalization Overrides Population Priors.

The previous example illustrated how the agentic agent uses tools to build evidence. The following real case from our evaluation (also depicted in Figure
3
) demonstrates a more subtle capability: the agent’s ability to
override
population-level evidence with accumulated personal knowledge.

Participant 089 is a cancer survivor with a stoic coping style—across 39 prior EMA entries, this person was receptive to intervention in only 6% of cases. On December 22, their diary reads:
“Funeral of close friend today. Worried about mother’s declining health.”
The agent’s investigation proceeds through five steps:

(1)

Diary analysis.
The agent identifies two compounding stressors—grief and caregiving worry—and forms an initial hypothesis: elevated negative affect and likely elevated ER desire.

(2)

Behavioral overview.
Calling
get_behavioral_timeline
and
get_daily_summary
for both the current day and the previous day reveals sustained withdrawal: fully stationary, only 2 minutes of screen use by mid-morning. This multi-day pattern is consistent with deep grief, not a transient bad morning.

(3)

Baseline anomaly detection.

compare_to_baseline
on screen usage confirms extreme disengagement: 2 minutes versus this person’s typical 14 minutes per hour—far below their personal norm.

(4)

Peer calibration.

find_peer_cases
with “funeral friend” returns other cancer survivors who wrote about grief. Most reported elevated ER desire—they wanted emotional support. This creates a
conflict
: the population evidence suggests this person should want help.

(5)

Session memory resolves the conflict.
The agent’s accumulated session memory contains reflections from 39 prior entries, including three prior grief episodes (December 15, 16, and 21). In every case, this person was
not
receptive. The agent’s memory records a consistent pattern: stoic, self-directed coping even under acute emotional distress. The agent overrides the peer prior and predicts low ER desire.

The prediction is correct on all four binary targets: PA_State = low, NA_State = elevated, ER_desire_State = not elevated, INT_availability = yes. The key reasoning step is the last one: the agent uses accumulated personal experience from binary receptivity feedback—never having seen this person’s actual emotion scores—to override a reasonable population-level prior. This is the core advantage of in-context personalization through session memory.

5.8.6.
Case Study: Two Learning Signals Enable Calibration.

A natural question is how the agent produces accurate predictions without access to the current user’s ground truth emotion labels. The following case illustrates the two complementary information sources that make this possible.

Participant 258 is a cancer survivor with high trait negative affect and moderate depression. On an evening EMA, their diary reads:
“Tired and a bit frazzled not sure why.”
The agent draws on two types of evidence:

Signal 1: Personal history from receptivity feedback.
The agent has observed three prior entries for this person. From these, it learned a pattern: when the diary is
internally focused
(describing feelings, e.g., “frazzled”), the person tends to be receptive; when the diary is
externally focused
(describing activities, e.g., “nice weather”), the person is not receptive. The current entry is internally focused, suggesting receptivity. Additionally, passive sensing reveals 35.7 km of travel (z = +1.79 above baseline), indicating that the tiredness is physically driven by an unusually active day rather than depressive withdrawal.

Signal 2: Peer outcomes from cross-user retrieval.
The agent calls
find_peer_cases
and retrieves 15 other cancer survivors who wrote similar “tired” or “frazzled” diary entries. Among these peers, the majority had typical positive affect, elevated negative affect, and elevated ER desire. This empirical distribution provides calibration anchors that the agent cannot derive from reasoning alone. The agent anchors its prediction to the peer base rates, then adjusts for this person’s high trait negative affect.

The resulting prediction is correct on all four binary targets: PA_State = typical, NA_State = elevated, ER_desire_State = elevated, INT_availability = yes. The two signals are complementary: receptivity feedback from this person’s own history teaches the agent
when
this individual is likely to need intervention, while peer outcomes teach
what
predictions are typical for people in similar situations. Together, they enable accurate personalized prediction without direct access to the current user’s emotion labels.

5.9.
Investigation Strategy Evolves Over Time

To understand how the agent’s investigation strategy changes with experience, we tracked tool usage across the first 70 EMA entries per user. Total tool usage decreases from 8.8 calls per prediction in entries 1–5 to 7.4 calls in entries 66–70, a reduction of 16%. Simultaneously, self-reported confidence increases from 0.69 to 0.76 (+10%).

This efficiency gain is not uniform across tools. Two tools decrease substantially:
compare_to_baseline
drops from 2.4 to 1.6 calls per entry (
-
33%), and
get_receptivity_history
drops from 0.4 to 0.1 calls per entry (
-
75%). Both provide information that the agent
internalizes
into session memory over time—personal baselines and receptivity patterns become part of the agent’s accumulated context, making explicit tool queries redundant. In contrast, the three core tools (
get_daily_summary
,
get_behavioral_timeline
,
find_peer_cases
) remain perfectly stable, each at approximately 1.0–2.0 calls per entry throughout. These tools provide real-time information that changes with each new day and cannot be memorized.

The pattern suggests that the agent learns to distinguish between
memorizable
information (personal norms, coping patterns) and
ephemeral
information (today’s behavioral signals, current peer comparisons), allocating its investigation budget accordingly. Figure

5.10.
Confidence Grows with Experience and Predicts Accuracy

The agent’s self-reported confidence increases over time as session memory accumulates, rising from 0.69 (entries 1–10) to 0.76 (entries 61–70). This growth coincides with the reduction in investigation effort described above: as the agent internalizes personal baselines and receptivity patterns, it becomes both more efficient and more confident.

Crucially, this confidence is correlated with actual prediction accuracy (Figure
4
b). For ER_desire_State, binning predictions by confidence reveals a monotonically increasing accuracy curve: predictions with confidence around 0.40 achieve 65.5% balanced accuracy (
n=55
), those around 0.55 achieve 70.8% (
n=312
), those around 0.70 achieve 81.6% (
n=435
), and those at 0.85 or above achieve 92.2% (
n=348
). The high-confidence band achieves accuracy comparable to inter-rater agreement levels in clinical affect assessment.

This calibration property has practical significance for JITAI deployment: a system can adopt a
selective prediction
strategy, acting on high-confidence predictions (correct more than 90% of the time for ER desire) and deferring low-confidence ones, thereby maintaining high precision in intervention delivery.

6.
Discussion

6.1.
Why Agentic Investigation Works

The 2
\times
2 factorial results suggest that the agentic architecture—not merely the presence of an LLM or the addition of more data—is the primary driver of prediction performance. Why might autonomous investigation outperform a structured pipeline that receives the same underlying information?

We hypothesize three contributing mechanisms, while noting that we do not yet have per-tool ablations to establish them causally. First,
selective attention
: the agentic agent can focus its reasoning on the most informative signals for a specific user and time point, rather than applying the same analytical pipeline to every example. A user with disrupted sleep may trigger deeper sleep analysis; a user with unusual social behavior may trigger communication-oriented investigation. The agent appears to allocate its limited reasoning budget adaptively.

Second,
anomaly detection through personal baselines
: the
compare_to_baseline
tool transforms absolute sensor values into deviations relative to the individual’s history, enabling the agent to detect what is unusual for
this
person. The structured agent receives the same raw values, but it does not have an equally explicit mechanism for iteratively testing those values against personal norms within a single-pass prompt.

Third,
analogical reasoning
: through
find_similar_days
and
find_peer_cases
, the agent grounds its predictions in retrieved examples rather than relying only on parametric prior knowledge. When the agent observes a behavioral pattern, it can look for what actually happened on matching days—both for the same user and for similar users. This may function as a practical calibration mechanism that a fixed pipeline cannot easily emulate.

These mechanisms connect to the Behavioral Signal Processing (BSP) framework proposed by Narayanan and Georgiou
(
44
)
and to Xu et al.’s Penetrative AI framing
(
74
)
: the agentic agent appears to perform
contextual behavioral signal interpretation
, extracting meaning from sensor streams through dynamic, context-sensitive reasoning rather than through fixed feature engineering alone.

A reasonable concern is whether the agentic advantage simply reflects making more LLM calls rather than reflecting the investigation paradigm itself. We cannot fully disentangle those factors here. What we can say is that the structured agents already receive the same underlying sensing content in summarized form, so the observed gains are not explained by a simple information asymmetry. The difference lies in
how
the information is processed: selectively, iteratively, and in response to emerging hypotheses rather than in a single forward pass over a preformatted summary.

6.2.
The Diary Paradox and Graceful Degradation

The diary paradox—the systematic absence of self-report data during periods of greatest clinical need, a well-documented challenge in EMA research
(
41
;
20
)
—motivates a fundamental design requirement for proactive mental health systems: they must function, at least partially, without active user participation.
Pulse
’s sensing-only capabilities directly address this requirement.

The degradation from multimodal to sensing-only is real: mean balanced accuracy across the four focus targets drops from 0.725 (Auto-Multi) to 0.637 (Auto-Sense), a decline of 0.088. Yet the drop is strongly target-dependent. INT_availability changes by only 0.010 (0.722 to 0.713), whereas ER_desire falls by 0.092 (0.743 to 0.651). This pattern suggests that passive sensing may already be sufficient for behavioral availability estimation, while emotional desire still benefits materially from diary input.

At the same time, we are cautious about calling this
graceful degradation
in a deployment sense. Our evidence is retrospective, and the 9-point mean gap may still matter clinically. Moreover, the same crisis states that suppress diary completion may also degrade passive sensing coverage if the phone is turned off, left behind, or used differently. A practical system may therefore be able to
fall back
to sensing-only reasoning, but whether that fallback is adequate in real-time care settings remains unvalidated.

6.3.
INT_availability as a Behavioral Construct

Perhaps our most conceptually important finding is the clean dissociation between ER_desire and INT_availability. ER_desire benefits substantially from diary text (
\Delta=+0.092
), while INT_availability shows negligible benefit (
\Delta=+0.010
). This dissociation is not an artifact of prediction difficulty—both constructs achieve high balanced accuracy with the right data source—but reflects a fundamental difference in their nature.

ER_desire captures a
psychological state
: the user’s subjective desire for emotion regulation support. This state is directly expressed in diary text (“I feel overwhelmed,” “I need to talk to someone”) and only indirectly reflected in behavioral signals. INT_availability captures a
behavioral state
: whether the user is in a context where they can engage with an intervention (not driving, not in a meeting, phone accessible). This state is directly captured by sensing streams (motion activity, screen usage, location) and only tangentially related to diary content.

This dissociation has direct design implications for JITAI systems
(
42
;
29
;
38
)
. Rather than treating receptivity as a unitary construct, intervention delivery may benefit from modeling desire and availability as separate tailoring variables estimated from different data sources: passive sensing for behavioral availability and brief check-ins or diary text for emotional desire when available.

Two caveats matter here. First, our availability target is a self-reported binary item; it reflects
stated availability
, not necessarily true
receptivity
or eventual intervention engagement. Second, some of the availability signal may be recoverable from simpler heuristics such as time of day or motion state. We therefore interpret INT_availability as a promising behavioral construct, not as a solved deployment variable.

6.4.
Cross-User RAG as Calibration

The cross-user RAG mechanism in
Pulse
serves a different purpose than typical RAG in question-answering systems
(
30
;
4
)
. Rather than retrieving factual knowledge, it provides
empirical calibration
: grounding predictions in the outcomes observed for similar cases. When the agent retrieves peer cases with similar behavior or diary content, it receives a small empirical reference set for interpreting what it sees.

We hypothesize that this mechanism may help with the cross-dataset generalization challenge documented in GLOBEM
(
76
)
: instead of learning only a fixed population-specific decision surface, the system can combine general reasoning with local empirical anchors. Whether this truly improves transfer remains an open question, but the architecture is at least compatible with population-adaptive calibration.

6.5.
Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations that contextualize our findings.

Retrospective evaluation.

Pulse
was evaluated in a retrospective replay of existing BUCS data, not in a prospective deployment. While the temporal boundary enforcement ensures the agent cannot access future data, real-time deployment introduces additional challenges: latency constraints (30–90 seconds per prediction), network reliability, model availability, and user interface design. Our results demonstrate proof of concept, not deployment readiness.

Sample size.
The evaluation includes 50 randomly selected users. While the representativeness analysis (Section
4.1.1
) shows that these users are broadly similar to the full BUCS sample on most measures, 50 users may not capture the full diversity of behavioral patterns in the cancer survivor population. The within-subject design and 4,112 predictions per system provide substantial statistical power for the comparative analyses, but absolute performance levels should be interpreted in the context of this sample.

Baseline comparability.
The broader baseline scoreboard is useful for reader orientation, but it is not a perfectly fair head-to-head comparison. Several baselines differ from
Pulse
in training population, feature access, temporal resolution, or modality richness. The stronger claim is the controlled within-family comparison between structured and agentic systems on the same 50 users.

Model dependency.
All experiments used a single LLM (Claude Sonnet). While the agentic investigation paradigm is model-agnostic in principle—any LLM capable of multi-turn tool use could instantiate it—the specific performance levels may vary across models. Open-source alternatives such as LLaMA
(
61
)
could improve reproducibility and enable on-device deployment. The MCP tool interface is an open standard, facilitating cross-model evaluation in future work.

Inference cost.
Each prediction requires 30–90 seconds and approximately $2–5 per user in LLM inference, making full-cohort evaluation expensive and real-time deployment cost-sensitive. This cost constrained our evaluation to 50 of 399 available users. For deployment, model distillation, cheaper frontier models, or on-device inference could reduce costs while preserving the agentic reasoning advantage.

No memory ablation.
We did not ablate the per-user session memory component. While memory provides longitudinal context that plausibly improves predictions, we cannot quantify its contribution without a controlled ablation study.

Continuous calibration and negativity bias.
The agentic agents show systematic biases in continuous predictions (over-predicting negative affect, under-predicting positive affect) despite strong binary classification. One plausible concern is that the investigation process itself may amplify negativity bias by rewarding anomaly-seeking in a clinical context. We have not yet tested post-hoc calibration or alternative prompting strategies to mitigate this.

6.6.
Implications for JITAI Design

Our findings suggest four tentative design principles for next-generation JITAI systems, informed by the growing evidence base for JITAI efficacy
(
17
)
and the specific needs of cancer survivor populations
(
59
;
6
)
:

(1)

Separate desire from availability.
Intervention receptivity is not a unitary construct. Systems should independently estimate emotional desire for support (diary-aided, when available) and behavioral availability for engagement (sensing-based, continuous). Intervention delivery may benefit from including evidence for both.

(2)

Use agentic reasoning for state detection.
Fixed rule-based triggers and traditional ML models appear to operate near the generalization ceiling documented by GLOBEM. Agentic investigation offers a plausible path beyond that ceiling by enabling dynamic, personalized, context-sensitive analysis.

(3)

Design for fallback rather than assuming graceful degradation.
A JITAI system should provide its best estimates when all data sources are available, but it should also retain a sensing-only pathway when diary entries are absent. Whether that fallback is clinically sufficient still requires prospective validation.

(4)

Leverage population data for calibration.
Cross-user retrieval provides empirical grounding that pure within-user models lack. Future JITAI systems should maintain growing databases of behavioral patterns linked to outcomes, enabling agents to calibrate their predictions against empirical evidence from similar cases.

Traditional approaches to constructing JITAIs typically involve a multi-step process: conducting observational studies to identify candidate tailoring variables, decision points, and decision rules, followed by separate micro-randomized trials to evaluate and refine these components. While rigorous, this fixed-rule framework may limit the scalability and broader adoption of JITAIs in mental health, as it places a substantial burden on researchers to predefine intervention components that may not generalize well across populations or contexts. In contrast, PULSE introduces a more flexible paradigm by enabling agents to query, dynamically interpret data, and make context-sensitive decisions in real time. Rather than relying solely on pre-specified rules, this approach supports a more adaptive interplay between the system and incoming data, potentially improving responsiveness and personalization across diverse users and settings.

6.7.
Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted under IRB oversight, and all participants provided informed consent for passive smartphone sensing and EMA data collection. Data was de-identified before analysis, with participant IDs replaced by numeric study identifiers.

The combination of passive sensing data and LLM-based inference raises privacy considerations. Sensing data, while not containing explicit personal content, can reveal sensitive behavioral patterns (location history, communication patterns, sleep disruption). As Coravos et al.
(
14
)
emphasize, digital biomarkers must be developed with safety and effectiveness frameworks from the outset. In this study, data was processed in a research context with appropriate safeguards. Future deployment would require careful attention to data handling practices
(
50
)
, user control over data sharing, transparency about how predictions are generated
(
37
)
, and compliance with applicable privacy regulations.

We emphasize that
Pulse
is a prediction layer, not a clinical intervention or diagnostic system
(
63
;
1
)
. Its predictions are intended to inform intervention delivery decisions within a broader JITAI framework, not to replace clinical assessment. The system should not be the sole determinant of clinical action. Given the asymmetric cost structure of intervention delivery—an unnecessary check-in has lower cost than missing acute distress—a modest bias toward caution may be acceptable, but the negativity bias we observe also creates a risk of over-triggering and should be treated as a safety concern rather than as an automatic virtue.

The use of LLM APIs for processing behavioral health data raises questions about data transmission and storage. In the current implementation, data sent to the LLM API is de-identified. Future on-device LLM deployment
(
70
;
80
;
54
)
would eliminate the need for external data transmission entirely.

7.
Future Work

Several directions extend this work. A first priority is
prospective validation
: embedding
Pulse
within a live JITAI or microrandomized trial
(
27
)
to test whether these predictions improve intervention timing and outcomes outside retrospective replay. A second is
cross-model and on-device evaluation
: testing whether the agentic advantage persists across other LLMs and whether lighter-weight or edge models
(
70
;
54
;
80
)
can preserve enough of the benefit to support privacy-preserving deployment. A third is
better calibration and ablation
: isolating the contributions of session memory, peer retrieval, and individual tools while explicitly addressing negativity bias in continuous affect prediction. A fourth is
generalization beyond cancer survivors
: evaluating whether the same agentic investigation pattern transfers to other sensing domains, populations, and intervention settings.

8.
Conclusion

This paper introduces
Pulse
, a system that shifts passive sensing-based affect prediction from fixed feature pipelines toward agentic investigation. By equipping LLM agents with eight purpose-built sensing query tools,
Pulse
enables hypothesis-driven investigation of behavioral data rather than one-shot reasoning over precomputed summaries. The evaluation provides evidence that reasoning architecture matters: agentic investigation outperforms structured prompting in both sensing-only and multimodal settings, with all main contrasts highly significant (
p<0.0001
, bootstrap test). The most clinically consequential result is the dissociation between two intervention-opportunity constructs. Emotion regulation desire benefits substantially from diary text, whereas intervention availability is already predicted strongly from passive sensing alone, suggesting that desire and availability should not be treated as a single tailoring variable. The results suggest a useful direction for the field. Mobile sensing has long established that behavioral signals correlate with mental health.
Pulse
begins to show how an agent can investigate those signals in context.
