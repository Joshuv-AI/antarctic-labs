Building self-efficacy in medical trainees working in high stakes environment
Authors: Ser Hon Puah, Muhammad Amin Shaik, Mathew Sachin Philips, Lynn Xuan Lin Lee, Winnie Li-Lian Teo, Herma Roebertsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to influence outcomes through their actions. Its application to ventilator management (VM) is a critical component of a clinician’s competence in the intensive care unit (ICU). It remains unclear how the training of the ICU facilitates self-efficacy inputs in VM. This study examined final-year internal medicine residents’ perceptions of the conditions that facilitated these inputs during their three-month ICU rotations.MethodsThis convergent parallel mixed method study recruited 20 participants who completed their rotations at Tan Tock Seng Hospital, Singapore. Quantitative data were collected using the General Self-efficacy Scale and a six-item proxy measure developed to assess the perceived availability of self-efficacy inputs in VM. Qualitative data were collected through eight focus group discussions exploring experiences that shaped self-efficacy inputs in VM. Both strands were analysed independently and integrated at the point of interpretation. Transcripts were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsParticipants demonstrated moderate to high perceived general self-efficacy. Item-level frequencies indicated that self-efficacy inputs of mastery experiences, vicarious learning, and positive affective states were perceived as available, although social persuasion in the form of timely feedback was limited. Qualitative analysis revealed seven themes organised into three conceptual categories. Fear of the Vent captured initial apprehensions towards VM. Despite this, Reassuring Mentorship and Work-Place Based Pedagogy were perceived as facilitating self-efficacy inputs. Joint display integration revealed convergence across three of the four self-efficacy inputs and divergence in the social persuasion domain, where limited timeliness of feedback contrasted qualitative accounts of feedback that was formative, consistently available, and situationally responsive.ConclusionMentorship and workplace affordances were perceived as providing the necessary self-efficacy inputs in VM despite initial apprehension. Background

Intensive care units (ICUs) are high-stakes settings where patient safety and clinical decisions depend on a delicate balance between clinical competence, confidence and self-efficacy [1]. Ventilator management (VM) is an important demand of the ICU which requires ongoing clinical decisions to initiate, adjust, and discontinue mechanical ventilatory support in response to a patient’s evolving respiratory needs [2]. Trainee physicians in the ICU must develop competency in adjusting pressures, volumes and oxygenation in response to patient’s physiology, demanding both technical skills and sufficient self-efficacy to carefully consider the risks and benefits of positive pressure ventilation [3, 4].

Self-efficacy is defined as the belief in one’s own capability to successfully perform specific tasks to achieve a defined goal or handle a situation [3]. The concept is distinguished from confidence, which is defined as a broad sense of certainty in one’s abilities, knowledge or judgement [5]. A lack of confidence is characterised by hesitation to make decisions when needed or by spending excessive time on information already known [5]. Low self-efficacy, unlike low confidence, is characterised by heightened levels of anxiety when trainees encounter unfamiliar tasks [6]. This consequently reduces engagement with learning opportunities, manifesting as a lack of motivation to practise VM skills or persist in the face of difficulties [4]. Given that self-reported comfort with VM, a proxy indicator of self-efficacy, is the strongest predictor of ventilator knowledge scores among emergency physicians [7–9], and that belief in one’s own capability directly influences clinical decision-making and outcomes in high-stakes ICU settings [10], it is critical for ICU training environments to embed the conditions for self-efficacy development in VM.

The development of self-efficacy requires inputs from successful performance of a task (mastery experiences), observing others (vicarious learning), receiving timely feedback (social persuasion), and how one interprets stress or emotions in challenging situations pertaining to the task (affective states) [3, 4]. In clinical education, these inputs are embedded in the learning that arises through active workplace participation. Active workplace participation refers to the process by which learners build knowledge and competence through engagement in tasks, routines, and social practices of their workplace environment, securing guidance and developing relationships with colleagues [11]. In the ICU, active workplace participation is facilitated through diverse patient cases, repeated clinical exposures, and interdisciplinary support [12, 13]. However, it remains unclear how medical residents completing a three-month ICU rotation perceive these training elements as providing the necessary conditions for self-efficacy inputs in VM. To address this gap in the literature, a mixed-methods approach was used to answer the following research question: What ICU-based training experiences do internal medicine residents perceive as contributing to their self-efficacy in VM?

Methods

Study design

This is a descriptive mixed method study that uses a convergent parallel design in which quantitative and qualitative data were analysed independently and integrated at the point of interpretation [14]. The design was selected to obtain a comprehensive understanding of availability of self-efficacy inputs in the ICU training environment and the training conditions perceived as facilitating them. Quantitative data characterised the degree to which self-efficacy inputs were present, while qualitative data described how and why those inputs were experienced as meaningful.

Participants

44 eligible final-year Internal Medicine Residency Program trainees who completed their three-month ICU postings in Tan Tock Seng Hospital, Singapore, were invited to participate in the study. Of the eligible pool, 20 consented to participate. The Internal Medicine Residency Programme lasts for three years and has three months of ICU rotation embedded in its curriculum. All trainees of this programme undergo preparatory courses consisting of didactic lectures and hands-on simulations of ventilator manipulation prior to their rotation. During the ICU rotation, they continued to receive structured didactics and workplace-based assessments. Trainees managed patients as part of the ICU team under the direct supervision of an Intensivist. They participated in clinical rounds, made ventilator changes, formulated preliminary management plans, and responded to patients’ needs during both day and night shifts. Tan Tock Seng Hospital has 30 critical care beds for both mechanically ventilated (invasive or non-invasive) and non-ventilated patients. There were a total of 2776 admissions during the recruitment period of this study. The ICU team consisted of critical care trained nurses, respiratory therapists (RTs), trainees, and an Intensivist.

Study procedures

Ethics approval was granted by NHG Health’s Domain Specific ReviewBoard (2022/00565). Eligible participants were invited via email by an independent researcher with no teaching or assessment role in the ICU. Those who were interested received study information and provided written informed consent prior to their participation. Due to scheduling limitations of both the participants and the investigators, the recruitment period lasted 12 months from January 2023 to December 2023. Data collection was conducted in an office that was outside of the ICU to minimise contextual influence and provide participants with privacy. All participants completed quantitative measures first and then participated in focus group discussions (FGDs).

Quantitative study variables

General perceived self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their capacity to perform a task across demanding situations [4]. This was measured using the 10-item General Self-efficacy Scale (GSES) [15] which has been validated in multiple cultural contexts [16] and shown to be highly reliable among healthcare professionals [6]. Items were scored on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true to 4 = exactly true). Summed scores ranged from 10 to 40 with higher scores indicating greater perceived self-efficacy.

Self-efficacy inputs refer to the four primary sources through which self-efficacy beliefs are developed and strengthened [4]. In the absence of validated instruments measuring self-efficacy inputs in the critical care context, six proxy survey items were developed to assess the perceived availability of these inputs in VM. Item development was informed by Bandura’s four input domains and aligned with the demands of VM in the ICU:

Mastery Experiences: Items “I had the opportunity to manage the ventilator independently” and “I can manage the ventilator on a critically ill patient independently” were consistent with Bandura’s definition of successful task performance being the most powerful source of self-efficacy [3].

Items were co-developed by SHP and a senior clinical educator with intensive care experience and reviewed through expert consensus with HR. Items were scored on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true to 4 = exactly true). Summed scores ranged from 6 to 24 with higher scores indicating greater perceived availability of self-efficacy inputs in VM. Internal consistency was high for both the GSES (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) and the 6-item measure of self-efficacy inputs (α = 0.80). Both these measures are available as supplemental material.

Qualitative interviews

Eight FGDs were conducted during the recruitment period of this study. Convenience sampling was utilized to allocate participants to FGDs based on their availability [17], provided they had completed their three-month ICU rotation. Each FGD comprised two to four participants. A semi-structured interview guide was used to explore participants’ perceptions of the training conditions and experiences that facilitated self-efficacy inputs in VM during their ICU rotation. Initial questions were open-ended and exploratory. Subsequent questions examined self-efficacy inputs, for example: (1) How much supervision did you receive when managing the ventilators? (2) How often did you observe ventilator manipulation by seniors or respiratory therapists? (3) What feelings did you experience when managing patients on mechanical ventilation? (4) How frequently did you receive feedback throughout your postings? (5) Was this feedback helpful? The guide was used flexibly to allow deeper exploration while ensuring key areas of interest were consistently addressed. FGDs were facilitated by LXLL and MSP, who were not involved in residency training or in the direct line of reporting of participants. This arrangement was adopted to minimise hierarchy and power influences. FGD durations ranged between 30 and 40 min. Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis

Median and interquartile range (IQR) values were calculated for all scale scores. Item-level responses for self-efficacy inputs were summarised using frequencies and percentages. Qualitative data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis (RTA). RTA is an inductive, interpretive approach that prioritises the researcher’s active role in generating themes from the data rather than applying a predetermined coding framework [18]. RTA was selected over other qualitative approaches because it foregrounds the researcher’s insights and theoretical background in interpreting how participants make sense of their experiences [18, 19]. Following independent analysis of the quantitative and qualitative data, findings were integrated through a joint display approach to illustrate the ways in which they relate to each other [20, 21]. Quantitative scores for each self-efficacy input domain were placed alongside corresponding qualitative themes to identify concordance between the results [21].

RTA was conducted in accordance with the six phases described by Braun and Clarke [18]. Phase 1, familiarisation with the data, involved repeated reading of the transcripts and making note of emergent concepts. For example, initial notes captured recurring expressions of apprehension regarding VM, such as participants describing feeling “anxious” or “overwhelmed” when first encountering the ventilator. Phase 2, generating initial codes, focused on coding interesting features of the data that presented what participants perceived as facilitators or challenges to self-efficacy in VM. Coding was data-driven, meaning codes were inductively generated rather than derived from an established self-efficacy framework. For example, a code from R14 that read “I challenge myself to make my own assessment of the ventilator and formulate my own plan before calling a respiratory therapist or a senior” was coded as ‘confidence over time’. Phase 3, generating themes, involved clustering codes based on conceptual similarities to generate themes that reflected interpretive patterns of meaning in the data. For example, codes such as ‘confidence over time’, and ‘confident to try independently’ were clustered to construct the theme ‘Foundational Confidence’. In phases 4–6, candidate themes were reviewed against the coded extracts and the full dataset to ensure coherence and clear distinction from one another. Final themes were defined and named to clearly articulate the specific aspect of participants’ experiences they represented. For example, the theme ‘Fear of the Vent’ was named to capture the emotional burden of ventilator unfamiliarity described across 18 transcripts. Each theme was developed into an analytic narrative that moved beyond description of what participants said to offer an interpretation of the meaning of their experiences. Illustrative quotes were selected to support and evidence these interpretations.

Analytic rigor and reflexivity

SHP, a respiratory physician at TTSH with 14 years of experience in medical education and intensive care medicine, performed the initial analysis. MAS a doctoral researcher in Psychology at NHG Health, utilized his background in narrative and interpretive methodologies to independently code the data and support the generation of interpretive themes. Credibility, reflecting confidence in the truthfulness of qualitative findings [22] was strengthened through prolonged engagement with the data, investigator triangulation between SHP and MAS, and consensus building with the wider team (SHP, MAS, WLLT, and HR). Reflexivity was maintained through team discussions that considered how researcher backgrounds might shape theme interpretation. The research team also acknowledged that informing participants of the study’s evaluative purpose during informed consent framing may have shaped how participants described their experiences. This potential bias was considered during analysis where the team remained attentive to instances where participants’ accounts may have reflected perceived expectations rather than authentic experiences. Final themes reflected the lead analysts’ interpretations of participants’ accounts, interrogated through team discussions to identify and address potential researcher bias or assumptions [16].

Results

Of the 20 participants, 12 (60%) were female. The median GSES score was 27.0 (IQR = 3.2) out of a maximum of 40, indicating the participants generally reported moderate to high levels of perceived self-efficacy with low variability in scores across the sample. Qualitative analysis generated seven themes organised into three conceptual categories. Fear of the Vent captured the negative affective states associated with VM unfamiliarity. Reassuring Mentorship, characterized by readily available help, a nurturing culture, and accessible guidance, described the interpersonal conditions facilitating vicarious learning and social persuasion. Workplace-based Pedagogy that emphasized self-directed learning, foundational confidence, and situational training, reflected the workplace conditions through which mastery experiences were consolidated and affective states reinterpreted positively. The themes are described in greater detail below with illustrative quotes to support these interpretations.

Fear of the vent (number of transcripts theme has occurred in; N = 18)

Ventilator unfamiliarity carried a heavy emotional burden, often expressed as fear, stress or panic. These feelings intensified during troubleshooting or when managing patients who were difficult to ventilate.

“We do not learn much about ventilators until we step into the ICU. For me, if it is something that I have not seen very much of, or I never had the opportunity to trial, it can be quite anxiety inducing.” (R18).

Reassuring mentorship

Participants described interpersonal facilitators of self-efficacy inputs in VM, specifically social persuasion and vicarious learning. This included guidance from a nurturing culture and practitioners who provided a safety net and accessible support.

Safety net (N = 17)

Participants described that learning was made possible by a safe environment, where decisions could be openly discussed, tested, and refined through formative feedback.

“Sometimes I get feedback after I adjust the ventilator. For example, when I change the settings during pre-rounds, the RTs would check and share their thought processes with me. I get to see where I went wrong and what I did correctly. Similarly, when I try certain ventilator manoeuvres during ward rounds with a consultant, they would tell me whether it was appropriate or not.” (R3).

The consistent presence of experienced practitioners provided assurance and opportunities for participants to focus on learning when making ventilator decisions.

“Those complex cases would typically cause me a lot of stress, but when the consultants or RTs are around, it’s more about learning. I am not that concerned about the case.” (R17).

Nurturing culture (N = 14)

Participants described respiratory consultants who actively explained their clinical decisions, contextualised learning within medical principles, and demonstrated VM techniques as instrumental to their learning.

“One consultant talked us through his decisions on the ventilator for a patient with asthma exacerbation. It was kind of a guided tutorial that helped me understand how the ventilator was supporting the patient. When he demonstrated how to titrate the ventilator for this patient, it was revolutionary. I was truly wowed.” (R2).

Apart from consultants, participants highlighted how the practice of explaining one’s reasoning and the provision of guidance was embedded within the wider ICU culture.

“Other members of the ICU team, especially nurses and RTs give us feedback as well. They talk to us and tell us what they think is going on and what needs to be done.” (R15).

Accessible support (N = 13)

Participants described opportunities for supervised learning and interprofessional support as central to their learning in VM.

“The supervision in this hospital is great. We are constantly supervised by the seniors, consultants and registrars. The RTs are always just a phone call away if we ever need help.” (R4).

They appreciated the availability of teachings covering foundational concepts and protocols, which gave them the knowledge and skills to handle ventilators and manage both routine and new events in the ICU environment.

“When the consultants teach from their side, they tend to go straight to the physiological basics and principles and then build from there.” (R2).

Workplace-based pedagogy

Participants described workplace conditions that consolidated mastery experiences through self-directed learning and situational training and supported positive reinterpretation of affective states through the development of foundational confidence.

Self-directed learning (N = 13)

Participants learned through independent study and hands-on engagement where they tested different approaches and refined clinical reasoning.

“I learned the most about ventilator management when I play around and try to adjust the ventilator settings on my own and see how it turns out under supervision” (R4).

Learning was enhanced through active discussions and collaboration with the ICU team.

“It’s not just the RT taking me through the case, but rather, us figuring out the ventilator together. I would say “Why don’t we try this?” and the RT would respond “Yeah, we can try” Then we would make the changes and observe whether they worked.” (R12).

Foundational confidence (N = 13)

Participants’ confidence was shaped by accumulated experiences from constant exposure to ventilators and routine work.

“I think this knowledge over time, from daily experiences of managing patients in the ICU and developing approaches to high peak pressures or patient desaturation, has made me more confident in dealing with ventilator issues.” (R15).

Confidence grew through routine ventilator adjustments that resulted in positive patient outcomes.

“After a while, you gain enough confidence to handle initial titration. Once that works and the patient improves, you tend to do it more often thereafter.” (R8).

Situational training (N = 12)

Participants learned through real-time situations arising from patient outcomes and the team debriefs that followed.

“I remember the things I was taught during a critical situation. In the debrief, we discussed what happened, what should have been done, what went well, and what to look out for the next time. I found this feedback very useful, especially since I was the first to manage the ventilator and realised, I didn’t know how to troubleshoot.” (R11).

Participants described learning independently during on-call duties and periods of high patient load.

“The difficult part is when a patient with a very complicated physiology suddenly desaturates or turns hypotensive while I’m on call. In those moments, I have to adjust the ventilator settings on my own. I try to think through the physiology behind pressure and volume control, and then adjust the settings based on how I think the ventilation will respond” (R1).

Discussion

This study shows that ICU trainees experience a baseline fear of the ventilator largely due to unfamiliarity with the technology and minimal training. Reassuring mentorship and workplace-based pedagogy, as expressions of active workplace participation, were perceived by trainees as conditions through which self-efficacy inputs were experienced in VM. Prior studies examining self-efficacy and comfort in VM have focused predominantly on emergency medicine physicians in Western settings [8, 9]. Findings of this study are the first to describe the self-efficacy inputs in VM among final-year internal medicine residents completing a three-month ICU rotation in an Asian tertiary hospital. These findings extend existing self-efficacy theory by contextualising how Bandura’s four input domains were experienced within a high-stakes ICU training environment, with implications for the design of critical care training programmes.

The apprehension associated with VM unfamiliarity is a contextually specific finding of this study. It extends Bandura’s original framework [3] by identifying a previously unidentified negative affective barrier that precedes engagement with self-efficacy inputs in high-stakes environments. Mentorship and workplace support functioned as necessary conditions for navigating this barrier, suggesting that access to these training conditions shape whether self-efficacy inputs can be meaningfully experienced.

The mentorship culture identified in this study facilitated the self-efficacy inputs of vicarious learning and social persuasion. Majority of participants perceived opportunities for vicarious learning to be available, describing learning through observation of Intensivists and respiratory therapists who were present, engaged and accessible. Mentors serve as role models and trusted sources of guidance and support, enabling learners to reflect and build their understanding of clinical situations [23, 24]. In this study, the mentorship provided was consistent with Billet’s [11] conceptualisation of guided participation as a key mechanism of workplace learning.

Social persuasion was facilitated through feedback that was formative, consistently available, and enriched through post-event debriefs after critical clinical situations. These practices, embedded in the ICU culture described in this study, align with guideline recommendations and stand in contrast to common practices where summative feedback is the only type provided after the completion of the rotation [25]. The divergence found in this study between the cultural availability of feedback and its timeliness is consistent with previous reports that ICU trainees desire feedback to improve clinical skills but frequently either do not receive it or find it insufficient [25, 26]. To maximise the contribution of feedback to social persuasion as a self-efficacy input in VM, future training programmes should ensure that feedback on VM decisions is prompt in its delivery [27].

Mastery experiences and positive reinterpretation of affective states in VM were fostered through several deliberate educational and workplace mechanisms embedded within the ICU rotation curriculum of the Internal Medicine Residency Programme. Preparatory teaching provided foundational knowledge, whilst clinical experience offered real-time experiential learning and collaborative troubleshooting. Together, these conditions formed workplace affordances, defined as the support and guidance provided by the workplace that enable learners to engage in activities, build on existing knowledge, and generate new questions [28, 29]. Workplace affordances in the ICU setting included daily activities that developed routine expertise, independent management of the ventilator, and collaborative discussions for shared problem-solving. These conditions encouraged trainees to develop confidently (positive affective state) and explore their clinical decisions in VM (mastery experiences), which was perhaps reflected in their ability to remain calm and avoid decision paralysis. This finding adds to existing research demonstrating that self-efficacy allows medical practitioners to remain calm during non-routine events, make rational decisions, and adapt to the outcomes of their decision [5] Importantly, it extends this body of work by identifying the workplace conditions perceived as facilitating self-efficacy inputs in ICU training. Notably, the ability of trainees to manage the ventilator calmly and decisively despite initial apprehension suggests that adaptive capacities begin to develop within the ICU training environment. Future research could examine the relationship between these conditions and adaptive expertise development, given that adaptive expertise is fundamentally linked to self-efficacy beliefs about one’s capacity to handle unfamiliar challenges [30].

This study has several limitations. With respect to the quantitative component, perceived self-efficacy and the availability of self-efficacy inputs were assessed through self-report measures, which are inherently susceptible to social desirability bias, defined as the tendency of participants to respond in ways they perceive as favourable. This bias may have inflated the reported perceived levels of general self-efficacy and availability of self-efficacy inputs given that data were collected within the institutional setting in which participants trained. This risk was partially mitigated by ensuring anonymity and emphasising that participation had no evaluative or training consequences. The six-item proxy measure was developed for descriptive purposes and was not formally validated as a psychometric scale. Findings derived from this instrument reflect perceived availability of self-efficacy inputs rather than objective measures of self-efficacy domains and should be interpreted accordingly. The GSES measures general perceived self-efficacy rather than VM-specific self-efficacy, which limits the interpretability of its scores in relation to trainees’ beliefs about their capability in VM. The absence of an objective measure of clinical competence in VM limits understanding of the relationship between participants’ self-efficacy beliefs and actual performance.

In relation to the qualitative findings, as a single-centre study focusing on one ICU in Singapore, transferability of findings to other clinical contexts, specialties, or cultural settings may be constrained. Informing participants of the study’s evaluative purpose prior to FGDs, may have shaped how participants characterised the availability and quality of self-efficacy inputs in the ICU. Reflexivity was exercised during data analysis to manage this potential bias and ensure participant accounts were grounded in authentic experiences. Participants of this study may also have described their learning experiences more positively knowing they were being recorded. This is known as the Hawthorne effect, where individuals modify their behaviour due to an awareness of being observed. These risks were partially mitigated by conducting FGDs with facilitators outside participants’ direct line of reporting, using open-ended exploratory questions before directing discussion toward self-efficacy inputs, and ensuring confidentiality.

Taken together, these limitations affect the conclusions of this study in specific ways. Quantitative findings should be interpreted with caution given the potential for social desirability bias to have inflated participants’ responses. Qualitative findings are bounded by the single-centre context and should be read with awareness that study framing prior to data collection and observation biases may have shaped participants’ accounts. Despite these limitations, the convergence between quantitative and qualitative findings across most self-efficacy input domains strengthens confidence in the overall interpretation. The availability of a multidisciplinary research team provided a degree of analytical rigour specific to the qualitative component through investigator triangulation and ongoing reflexivity.

Conclusions

This study describes how final-year internal medicine residents completing a three-month ICU rotation in a tertiary hospital in Singapore experienced self-efficacy inputs in VM through their training conditions. Despite initial fear of the ventilator, reassuring mentorship and workplace-based pedagogy, as expressions of active workplace participation, were perceived as the primary conditions through which these inputs were facilitated. These findings highlight guided participation, timely and specific feedback and workplace affordances that progressively expand opportunities for independent VM as important considerations for educators designing ICU training programmes.
