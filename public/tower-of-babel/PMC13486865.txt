The ABCD Study at 10: The NIH perspective on progress, impact, and the road ahead☆
Authors: Nora D. Volkow, George F. Koob, William M. Klein, Jennifer A. Hobin, Eric M. Wargo, Diana Alkire, Elizabeth A. Hoffman, Susan R.B. Weiss, Gayathri J. Dowling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Adolescent Brain Cognitive DevelopmentSM (ABCD) Study is a landmark study of nearly 12,000 U.S. youth from ages 9–10 through their second decade of life. It aims to unravel the complex interplay of biological, environmental, and social factors on adolescent development. The study collects neuroimaging; cognitive assessments; genetic and other biological information; surveys of substance use, mental health, and other health-related factors; and detailed environmental measures. A cornerstone of the ABCD Study® is its open science model, which facilitates broad data sharing and has spurred over 1600 publications, significantly democratizing scientific inquiry. Findings from the first 10 years of the study have provided critical insights. Sociodemographic and contextual factors have emerged as stronger predictors of early substance use initiation than brain, neurocognitive, or genetic measures alone. Research has also highlighted the significant impact of lifestyle factors and multilayered environmental exposures on youth mental health. The study's diverse cohort has enabled robust examination of health disparities, revealing that while socioeconomic deprivation impacts neurodevelopment, these effects can be moderated by positive supports. Furthermore, the ABCD Study is reshaping developmental neuroscience by establishing new standards for large-scale research, advancing analytical techniques, and underscoring the need for large, diverse samples to ensure reproducibility while also exploring individual variability. As the cohort enters early adulthood, the study is poised to provide deeper understanding of the trajectories of substance use disorders, mental illness, and chronic conditions; identify early risk and protective factors; and reveal developmental windows for early prevention interventions to improve long-term health and well-being.

Introduction

The Adolescent Brain Cognitive DevelopmentSM (ABCD) Study is the largest longitudinal study of adolescent brain development ever conducted. Launched in 2015 and supported by multiple National Institutes of Health (NIH) Institutes, Centers, and Offices (ICOs), and other federal partners, it follows nearly 12,000 youth recruited at ages 9–10 over the second decade of their lives. The ABCD Study® was conceived by the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA), the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, and the National Cancer Institute in response to a rapidly shifting substance-use landscape driven by new cannabis policies, rising rates of opioid misuse, persistently high levels of alcohol use among teenagers, and the emergence of novel tobacco/nicotine products. In addition, there were unprecedented advances in neuroimaging, genetics, and computational modeling that made it possible to measure brain and behavioral development with far greater precision than ever before; and a strong federal commitment to building comprehensive, population-based evidence to guide prevention and treatment and advance public health. Together, these factors motivated NIH to invest in a national cohort that could illuminate how biological, psychological, social, and environmental forces shape the well-being of young people over time (Volkow et al., 2018). This review highlights how the ABCD Study has addressed NIH and other federal partner priorities, how it has exceeded what NIH envisioned, and what NIH is looking forward to accomplishing with this study in coming years.

The ABCD Study collects an exceptionally rich set of longitudinal data from participants and their families across 21 U.S. sites. These include structural and functional MRI scans; extensive cognitive, behavioral, and mental-health assessments; repeated surveys of substance use and other health behaviors; detailed measures of family, neighborhood, and environments; digital health data from wearable devices; and biospecimens for genetic and other biological analyses. This multimodal design allows researchers to model developmental trajectories, identify early predictors of later health outcomes, and understand how multiple domains—neurodevelopment, behavior, socioeconomic context among other environmental factors, and genetics—interact dynamically across adolescence. ABCD’s population-based sampling strategy ensures demographic, geographic, and socioeconomic heterogeneity, enabling investigations into health disparities and broad generalizability of findings. Its large sample facilitates exploration of both population and individual developmental trajectories enabling the investigation of factors that underlie intersubject variability.

A defining feature of ABCD is its open science model. In contrast to traditional cohort studies that release data after long embargoes, ABCD shares curated, de-identified datasets annually with approved researchers worldwide, accompanied by tools, documentation, and training resources to support responsible and reproducible use of these data. This open model accelerates discovery, fosters methodological innovation, and promotes transparency by allowing independent validation and reanalysis. The study emphasizes ethical data stewardship, providing guidance on sensitive variables, requiring data-use training, and encouraging community-engaged, contextually informed interpretation to avoid data misuse. Consequently, ABCD exemplifies the reach and potential of modern clinical neuroscience—advancing discovery not only through its scale and rigor, but also through its commitment to accessibility and responsible research practices.

ABCD by the numbers

ABCD has demonstrated that such an open science model can indeed democratize science. Since its first data release in 2018, more than 200 grants have been funded by NIH ICOs to use ABCD data as of November 20, 2025 (Fig. 1), and over 1600 peer-reviewed papers using ABCD data have been published as of January 24, 2026 (Fig. 2). More than 60% of these papers were written by researchers unaffiliated with the study (see methods in Laird et al., 2026 this issue). Progress on initial objectives

The ABCD Study recruited nearly 12,000 demographically diverse youth between 2016 and 2018 and has retained about 95% of the cohort 10 years into the study. Its large sample size, heterogeneous population, and high retention rates have been critical to the rigor and potential generalizability of many of the findings reviewed below. However, even with its nimble, open science model, scientific discovery necessarily lags data collection, due to data curation and quality control processes critical for broad data sharing. As of this writing, data through the first half of the sixth follow-up year (participant ages 15–16) have been released to the scientific community, and we are seeing publications of analyses using data from the first 4–5 years of the study (participant ages 14 or at most, 15). Achieving many of the study’s initial objectives will depend on data from late adolescence (see Looking Forward, below), but we are already gathering insight on a number of important topics including substance use initiation; early adolescent psychopathology; variability of pubertal timing; the effects of adverse environments, protective factors, and gene-by-environment interactions on health outcomes; and brain development trajectories.

Substance use

Central to the initial conception of the ABCD Study was an urgent need to understand how alcohol, tobacco, and other substance use interacts with the developing brain during a period of profound neurobiological change, including: how early brain differences shape later vulnerability to substance use; how use of different substances in turn influences brain development and mental health; the impact of substance use on academic achievement, social, emotional, and physical development, among others; whether those effects persist or reverse; and how polysubstance patterns can be disentangled. As expected, there was minimal substance use in the early data releases, when participants were 9–15 years old, consistent with estimates that 91 and 82 % of 8th and 10th graders, respectively, reported abstaining from alcohol, nicotine, or cannabis use in the past month in 2025 (Miech et al., 2026). Rates of substance use are increasing as the ABCD participants age into young adulthood, and the prospective design and richness of information from the ABCD Study will allow researchers to investigate what factors predict early substance use initiation.

An analysis of ABCD data through year four of the study found that brain functional connectivity patterns linked to accelerated maturation predicted youth substance use initiation through age 14 (Moawad, 2024). Substance use initiation by age 15 was shown in another analysis to be associated with structural brain differences as well—thinner cortex in prefrontal areas and thicker cortex in all other lobes of the brain as well as larger volumes of the globus pallidus and hippocampus; most of these associations preceded substance initiation (Miller et al., 2024). However, these studies did not include other variables that may contribute to risk. An analysis of a large set of variables (420) in the first three years of the study found that the most robust predictors of early substance initiation beyond alcohol sipping for religious observances (by age 12) were sociodemographic variables like religion and income; however, neuroimaging, neurocognitive assessments, and other biological measures did not improve prediction (Green et al., 2024). Similar results were found in a study focused on alcohol initiation prior to age 16, where contextual risk factors were more predictive than behavioral and structural neural correlates of reward and inhibition (Moore et al., 2024). In a study of risk for cannabis use initiation, an association was found with prenatal cannabis exposure even when controlling for alcohol and tobacco use initiation, family or parent alcohol or drug problems, and prenatal alcohol or tobacco exposure (Miller et al., 2023).

The ABCD Study has also led to advances in understanding how best to detect substance use in youth, particularly for cannabis, which is one of the drugs they most commonly use. The ABCD Study collects hair samples for toxicology analyses. Such analyses on a subset of participants showed low concordance with self-report data, with each method identifying participants that were not identified by the other, underscoring the need for multiple methods in characterizing substance use history by youth (for more information see Wade et al., 2023). A study that combined these methods found that those who used cannabis had poorer episodic memory than matched controls and the amount of cannabis use was negatively correlated with performance on verbal, inhibitory, working memory, and episodic memory tasks (Wade et al., 2024).

Psychopathology

ABCD was also structured to investigate the emergence and progression of mental health problems, many of which first appear in adolescence. By collecting multidimensional data before symptoms arise, the study allows researchers to identify early brain biomarkers, psychosocial precursors, and environmental triggers of psychopathology, as well as protective factors that foster resilience and the influence of genotypes on these trajectories. Data so far are showing that lifestyle factors such as exercise, sleep, and screen use are highly influential in children’s mental health, as are factors like prenatal substance exposure, whereas early substance use initiation is associated with development of psychopathology. Meanwhile, analyses show that the association of genetics, as computed with polygenic risk scores (PGS), with externalizing and internalizing dimensions of psychopathology is mediated by its indirect effects on reward sensitivity and cognitive control. By comparison, for addiction-related PGS, the association is mediated predominantly by reward sensitivity (Howard et al., 2026).

Understanding premorbid signs of mental illness and identifying potentially sensitive periods for early intervention, particularly for low-incidence behaviors (e.g., suicide attempts), could have enormous clinical value. To identify early risk factors, however, it is important to understand trajectories to mental illness. A study using data from the first three years of ABCD identified multiple distinct developmental trajectories of internalizing, externalizing, and jointly evolving symptoms in youth and showed that these patterns were differentially shaped by factors such as negative life events, neighborhood safety, and parental acceptance (Brieant et al., 2025). Other studies have looked at symptoms of specific disorders. One identified 4 trajectories of distress-related psychotic-like experiences and found that participants with persistently high distress from psychotic like experiences tended to have certain lifestyle patterns at baseline—including more screen time, higher caffeine use, greater sleep disturbance, and less physical activity and recreation—suggesting modifiable behaviors that may help reduce risk (Cooper and Jalbrzikowski, 2025). A study using data from ABCD and two other youth cohorts found that a single exposomic score—capturing dozens of environmental and lifestyle risk and protective factors—robustly predicted youth suicide attempts, explaining substantial variance (17.6–22.6%) beyond demographics and family history and offering a generalizable tool for suicide-risk classification (Visoki et al., 2024).

Studies using ABCD data are also examining the neural bases of mental health conditions. In some cases, these studies support the hypothesis of shared neural substrates underlying transdiagnostic mental health symptoms. For example, a study using predictive modeling showed that functional connectivity patterns predicted externalizing behaviors in both sexes but internalizing behaviors only in females, yet the underlying brain networks were largely shared, indicating that similar neurobiological patterns can manifest as different psychiatric symptoms across sexes and should be considered in clinical and research contexts (Dhamala et al., 2023). Similarly, a study of the relationship between cortico-limbic connectivity and both environmental adversity in childhood and genetic liability for mental health symptoms found a neural signature that was shared between the latter two factors, suggesting an overlap in how genetic and environmental risk factors influence neural connections involved in mental illness (Vedechkina et al., 2025). Other investigators have used the large neuroimaging dataset in ABCD to facilitate the identification of brain biomarkers for psychiatric disorder risk (Brainwide Risk Score—BRS), which differentiated participants at low-risk of psychiatric disorders from those at high risk but also showed that among high-risk participants, there was variability in BRS between disorders. Such a score could potentially be used for screening and for monitoring of psychiatric interventions (Yan et al., 2023).

With relatively few substance use and mental health diagnoses in the early teen years other than for Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), it will take time to gain a fuller understanding of the relationship between psychopathology and substance use or exposure in adolescents, but there are already some interesting findings. There are 3 primary hypotheses about this relationship: self-medication, substance exposure causing mental illness, and shared vulnerability to both conditions. In support of the self-medication hypothesis, more psychologic and somatic symptoms emerging in late childhood were found in one analysis to predict earlier onset of alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, and other drug use (Voepel-Lewis et al., 2024). In support of a causative role from drug use, prenatal cannabis exposure was associated with attentional problems and with neuroimaging differences in diffusion weighted measures in frontal and parietal cortices and the white-matter tracts associated with them and with striatal-auditory cortex functional connectivity that partly mediated the associations between prenatal cannabis and ADHD (Baranger et al., 2024). Similarly, prenatal cannabis exposure was associated with persistent psychotic-like experiences, and this association was mediated by blunted neural activation during reward anticipation (Amir et al., 2026). Support for the shared vulnerability and self-medication hypotheses was given by a study that showed that the relationships between adolescent cannabis use and psychosis spectrum conditions reflected shared vulnerability and use of cannabis to self-treat symptoms, with only mixed evidence for cannabis initiation preceding psychosis symptoms (Osborne et al., 2024). At this age, participants had low levels of cannabis use, and the emergence of psychotic spectrum conditions peaks in the late teens or early twenties, so re-analyses of upcoming data releases will be needed to corroborate these early findings.

Pubertal timing

Pubertal hormones affect structural and functional brain development, markedly influencing adolescent behavior and contributing to mental health. A non-linear method combining hormonal and physical features of pubertal timing was found in one analysis to outperform traditional linear methods and revealed that earlier physical—more than hormonal—pubertal maturation was most strongly associated with elevated mental health symptoms in early adolescence. Adolescents who reported more advanced pubertal development at ages 9–10 were significantly more likely to exhibit or newly develop self-injurious thoughts and behaviors over the next two years, suggesting that early pubertal timing is an independent risk indicator warranting targeted screening (Luking et al., 2025). However, combining ABCD puberty data with other environmental data revealed that earlier pubertal timing predicted greater emotional and behavioral problems, primarily when adolescents experienced unfavorable proximal social environments, (Vijayakumar et al., 2024) highlighting the powerful buffering—or exacerbating—role of parents, peers, and schools.

Environmental and behavioral factors (exposome)

Large longitudinal cohort studies with deep phenotyping permit analyses of complex interdependent influences of biological, psychological, and environmental factors over time. For the ABCD Study, in addition to a comprehensive battery of brain imaging and laboratory measures, questions about sleep, physical activity, nutrition, digital media use, extracurricular engagement, traumatic brain injury, stress exposure, and socioeconomics were incorporated from its inception, reflecting the growing interest in how environmental and behavioral factors interact in shaping brain function and development.

A wide array of methodological approaches have been used in the ABCD dataset, including multivariate analyses, artificial intelligence, and machine learning, among other tools. This has enabled more accurate modeling of developmental pathways and a more robust identification of meaningful predictors than studies focused on any single variable or domain. For example, computational modeling of ABCD data from the baseline and first two follow-up years showed that a comprehensive exposome measure capturing hundreds of environmental factors robustly predicted children’s current and future cognitive performance and was a stronger predictor than individualized brain network features—underscoring the powerful, multidimensional influence of childhood environments on neurocognitive development (Keller et al., 2024). Similarly, data-mining approaches revealed that social conflicts—especially family fighting and peer reputational harm—were the strongest predictors of current and future psychopathology, whereas neuroimaging measures added little further predictive value, underscoring both the primacy of social factors in youth mental health and the substantial unexplained variance that remains (Jirsaraie et al., 2025).

An area of particular concern has been the impact that the fast rise and widespread use of digital media has had on youth mental health. Studies using ABCD data have shown that merely examining screen time was insufficient to understand these relationships. By using a data-driven approach to digital risk exposures, investigators found that heavy social media use, cyberbullying, and problematic use patterns strongly predicted adolescent psychopathology and suicide attempts beyond traditional risk factors. Similarly, early adolescents who followed high or increasing trajectories of addictive social media, mobile phone, or video game use—rather than simply high total screen time—were at substantially elevated risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors and poorer mental health outcomes (Xiao et al., 2025). In contrast, video gaming was associated with increases in problem-solving, flexible thinking, and recalling facts and skills, highlighting the complexity of the role of the digital environment in youth lives.

The benefits and risks of sports participation on brain function and mental and physical health is another area where ABCD is poised to yield valuable insight. For example, participation in any organized sport was linked to better cognitive performance, greater subcortical gray matter volume, and whole brain white matter integrity compared with nonparticipation, underscoring a broadly positive association between sports involvement and youth neurocognitive development (Tan et al., 2024). The ABCD study will also allow us to understand the mediators of the relationship between sports participation and cognitive and mental health outcomes. For example, although greater sports participation was linked to lower depression in boys and modestly related to white-matter integrity in one tract, these brain measures did not mediate the exercise–depression relationship, suggesting that exercise benefits mental health in preadolescent boys through other pathways (Gorham and Barch, 2020a). ABCD has also provided data with which to study the risk of sport-related mild traumatic brain injury, and its analyses has revealed sex differences such that boys showed higher concussion risk than girls in tennis but lower risk in swimming or water polo (Gorham and Barch, 2020b). The longitudinal design of the ABCD Study will allow researchers to assess the factors that contribute to better recovery from mild brain trauma and help inform rehabilitation interventions.

SES and health disparities

The ABCD cohort’s racial, ethnic, socioeconomic, and geographic heterogeneity has enabled researchers to explore how social and environmental factors such as economic hardship and unequal educational opportunities contribute to disparate developmental outcomes. A study using a deep-learning framework across thousands of ABCD phenotypes found the dominant sources of population variation to be multidimensional socioeconomic and social–environmental stratifications, ranging from concentrated deprivation to affluence. Several studies have shown that children growing up in socioeconomic deprivation exhibited poorer mental, cognitive, and physical health outcomes as well as divergent brain structural and functional trajectories, underscoring the powerful influence of neighborhood-level factors on child neurodevelopment (Tomasi and Volkow, 2021, Xiao et al., 2023).

But these findings do not suggest an immutable predetermined fate for these youth. Although low socioeconomic status has been linked to lower cognitive performance and smaller cortical surface area, several positive ecological factors—especially social support, perinatal well-being, and greater access to resources—independently promoted healthier brain and cognitive development even in economically disadvantaged children (Gonzalez et al., 2020). Moreover, children do not necessarily spend their entire childhood in one environment. Investigators have identified four ecological risk/resilience profiles—distinguished by combinations of SES, family risk, and protective supports—and showed that both early profile membership and transitions between profiles over time predicted later psychopathology (Sripada et al., 2022). The ABCD Study’s geographic distribution also presents an opportunity to examine the impact of macroeconomic features. For example, a study found that although lower family income was linked to smaller hippocampal volume and greater internalizing symptoms, especially in high-cost-of-living states, more generous state anti-poverty benefits substantially attenuated these disparities (Weissman et al., 2023). These findings demonstrate that resilience and risk arise from multilayered environmental factors that can shift across development and highlight actionable targets for more precise, context-informed interventions to promote youth mental health (Yang et al., 2024).

Gene X environment

The ABCD Study aimed to disentangle the contributions of genetics, environment, and their interactions using its large sample size, twin subsample, and rich longitudinal design, enabling rigorous tests of causal hypotheses that have been impossible in smaller studies. Highlighting the complexity of these relationships, a genome-wide association study (GWAS) showed that early alcohol use in the cohort was influenced by a broad set of genetic, neural, personality, and environmental factors, with environmental and genetic influences shaping the timing of a first sip, but genetic and trait characteristics playing a stronger role in progression to a full drink—clarifying distinct etiological pathways for different stages of early alcohol initiation (Choi et al., 2025). ABCD data have also been used to challenge the notion that more heritable phenotypes are less sensitive to environment by showing that even brain, cognitive, and psychopathology traits with higher genetic influence remain strongly associated with socioeconomic conditions and are similarly shaped by state-level socioeconomic and other factors (e.g., cost of living, social safety-net programs) (Williams et al., 2024). This finding underscores how genetic and socioeconomic influences on child development are deeply intertwined.

Normal brain development

One core objective of the ABCD Study was to define healthy trajectories of brain maturation—structural, functional, cognitive, hormonal, and behavioral—during the transition from late childhood into young adulthood. Though we are still too early in the data release to capture the full timeline of this developmental period, studies are being published from the first 5 years of longitudinal data. One study used dynamic functional connectivity measures in more than 5000 resting-state fMRI scans from ABCD participants between the ages of roughly 9 and 14, to disentangle the effects of age, pubertal development, and sex on time-varying brain network organization (Duffy et al., 2025). Youth farther along in puberty, particularly females, showed decreased variability in functional connectivity and increased network segregation —patterns consistent with more advanced brain maturation—particularly in networks implicated in emotion regulation and psychopathology. Other research taking advantage of the longitudinal design of the ABCD Study has also found deleterious effects of obesity, poor sleep, and environmental stress factors on brain development (Adise et al., 2025, Kiss et al., 2024).

Expanding the scope of the ABCD study

Over the course of data collection, various measures were added, in some cases to address new questions or when scientific or technical opportunities arose. Areas of expansion included cardiovascular and respiratory measurements, surveys of participant experiences during the COVID−19 pandemic, as well as residential-history-derived geocodes and school ID linkages to external datasets to help investigate environmental impacts.

Given the relationships between cardiovascular and brain health, in 2018, measures related to blood pressure, cholesterol, hemaglobin A1C (HbA1c), and nutrition were added to the protocol. Although cardiovascular disease does not generally manifest until later in life, we are already seeing higher cholesterol and HbA1c among some participants (Nagata et al., 2025). Studies are also highlighting the relationship between measures of cardiovascular health and brain structure and cognitive functions. Physical activity and overall cardiovascular health were directly associated with higher executive cognitive function; sleep duration, normal body weight, and good cardiovascular health were also associated with measures of brain volume (higher total cortical area and mean cortical thickness); (De Moraes et al., 2024) and cellular density in the nucleus accumbens (NAcc) was tied to waist circumference over time, suggesting a neuroinflammatory mechanism in reward-related brain circuits that may contribute to pediatric obesity (Rapuano et al., 2020, Rapuano et al., 2022).

Respiratory health can have an impact on brain and cognition as well. In a study of youth who snore, their smaller airway volumes were associated not only with poor sleep quality but also with poorer cognition and with smaller frontal and temporal gray matter volumes (Kanhere et al., 2025). Similarly, asthma in ABCD participants has been associated with difficulties with memory and possibly other executive function abilities; with associations being stronger with earlier onset of asthma (Christopher-Hayes et al., 2024).

When the COVID−19 pandemic hit the United States, it was clear that to accurately interpret ABCD longitudinal data, ABCD needed to collect information about participant experiences during this period. Through the height of the COVID−19 pandemic, ABCD sent out surveys to participants every 6–8 weeks to gather data about participant experiences. From these surveys it was possible to determine shifts in risky behaviors and predictors of adverse outcomes. For example, early in the pandemic, there was a decline in early adolescent alcohol use and modest increases in nicotine, prescription drug, and inhalant misuse, which shrank over time (Pelham et al., 2023).

Because ABCD has pre-pandemic, during pandemic, and post-pandemic brain imaging, cognitive testing, and mental health assessments, it is in a unique position to identify pre-pandemic predictors of adverse outcomes. For example, pre-pandemic internalizing symptoms and female sex predicted psychological distress during the pandemic (Kiss et al., 2022 Mar). The study will also allow us to understand post-acute sequelae of COVID infection (PASC) or Long COVID. ABCD’s participation in the NIH RECOVER-Pediatrics cohort study allowed collection of COVID antibody and Long COVID symptom data, which will help disentangle infection and pandemic effects as well as understand the long-term effects of COVID−19 infection. Symptom combinations most associated with a history of COVID infection among school-age children and adolescents from the ABCD cohort were used to construct PASC research indices for both age groups. In adolescents, the index highlighted changes or loss of smell or taste, pain, and symptoms related to fatigue/malaise (Gross et al., 2024).

Self-reported data from the ABCD study have been valuable to help characterize the role of the environment in development, but they are constrained by reliance on an individual’s subjective perception of their environment (Cardenas-Iniguez et al., 2024). Beginning in 2021, ABCD began linking residential-history-derived geocodes to external datasets to provide objective environmental context for primary data collection. For example, estimates of air pollution (particulate matter exposure) were found to be associated with differences in brain structure including subcortical volume, cortical thickness, and cortical surface area in 9–11-year-old participants (Sukumaran et al., 2025). Similarly, researchers found associations between neuroimaging and mental health data with other environmental indicators such as percentages of built-up land versus forest area, night-light radiance, and population density using satellite data (Goldblatt et al., 2024). Built-up land appeared to negatively affect cognitive ability even when controlling for SES. Other researchers using ABCD data, found that participants from lower-income families who are at risk of lead exposure exhibited lower cognitive performance and reduced cortical volume and surface area than those from higher-income families residing in similar high lead-risk areas, highlighting how neurotoxic environmental exposures can compound socioeconomic disadvantage in shaping brain development (Marshall et al., 2020).

ABCD has also added greater nuance to our understanding of well-known associations between the quality of children’s educational environment and academic outcomes by looking specifically at brain development. In 2023, ABCD released data linking participant school IDs to the Stanford Education Data Archive to provide information about school context. One study of baseline and 2-year-followup data found associations between educational opportunity and accelerated white matter development, even when other socioeconomic factors were accounted for, demonstrating that educational environment is not simply a proxy for SES (Roy et al., 2024).

Changing how science is done

The ABCD Study has also helped reshape the norms, tools, and values that govern how science is conducted, interpreted, and applied. It has demonstrated how large, deeply characterized, and openly shared datasets can overcome historical limitations in developmental neuroscience and mental health research, and its unprecedented sample size allows researchers to detect and estimate small effects with precision, improving replicability, and enabling rigorous tests of complex, multilevel hypotheses that previously could only be approached indirectly. All these efforts hinge on retaining the study’s large and diverse cohort. The ABCD retention workgroup monitors visit completion by participant, visit, site, and study related factors (e.g., parental education, participant feedback, in person/remote visit, distance to study site) to understand factors that may influence missed visits so that additional efforts can be made to retain participants most vulnerable to withdrawal. Ultimately, the study’s success has been a result of the dedication and creativity of the research staff at all the sites. Strategies used include providing transportation and childcare, conducting house visits, hosting ABCD participant events (e.g., baseball games, ice skating, college tours), providing bonus payments, being responsive to participant feedback, and maintaining detailed documentation of all interactions with participants that can help tailor future communications. These measures aim to engender a sense of community and commitment to advancing science and lessen barriers to participation among participants.

The study’s harmonized imaging protocols, standardized behavioral and environmental measures, and longitudinal design have also set new methodological benchmarks for population neuroscience. Just as importantly, ABCD has catalyzed the adoption of more robust analytic practices—such as study preregistration, principled covariate selection, and interpretation centered on effect sizes—helping shift the field away from overreliance on p-values toward more quantitative estimates of the magnitude of the differences. With its commitment to open science, annual data releases, and detailed documentation, the study empowers thousands of researchers worldwide to access the same high-quality dataset—promoting transparency, replicability, broad participation, and rapid methodological innovation.

Beyond traditional epidemiologic approaches, ABCD has become a proving ground for predictive modeling and machine-learning methods that leverage the study’s high-dimensional data. Researchers now routinely build multivariate models that integrate genetics, neuroimaging, psychosocial variables, environmental exposures, and digital phenotypes to forecast outcomes such as emerging psychopathology, cognitive development, and risk behaviors. For example, the power of continuous behavioral measures to link real-world behavior with underlying genetics was demonstrated by a study that showed digital phenotypes derived from wearable sensors (FitBit biosensors) could accurately classify adolescent psychiatric disorders and enhance genetic discovery, identifying multiple significant loci and genes (Liu et al., 2024). These approaches also allow for potentially better classification of psychiatric conditions, such as ADHD, where three distinct cognitive profiles were identified but only one subtype exhibited specific structural brain differences, illustrating how data-driven clustering can reveal neuroanatomical heterogeneity obscured in traditional case–control analyses (Yamashita et al., 2024). Follow-up of these participants will enable researchers to assess if these subgroups predict different long term outcomes.

These approaches are also demonstrating ways to predict the onset of psychiatric conditions. An AI-optimized deep learning framework enabled individualized prediction across more than 5700 multimodal variables, resulting in highly accurate prospective prediction of ADHD, Oppositional Defiant Disorder, and Conduct Disorder onset within the same cohort, as well as demonstrating that distinct psychosocial and neural predictors differentiated those disorders (de Lacy and Ramshaw, 2023). Similarly, machine-learning models of extensive multilevel psychosocial data and multimodal neuroimaging prospectively identified functional connectivity, structural brain features, and contextual factors that distinguish adolescents who escalate alcohol sipping over time from those who decrease or abstain, demonstrating a predictive, longitudinal approach to early alcohol-use risk profiling (Ferariu et al., 2025).

ABCD is also reshaping how the field approaches brain-wide association studies (BWAS) by stimulating discussions about the sample sizes, study designs, and analytic strategies needed to produce reproducible analyses and replicable brain–behavior associations across samples. A highly cited study demonstrated that one of the reasons for replication failure in previous BWAS is the sample sizes used, showing that thousands of participants may be needed for identifying replicable brain-behavior associations (Marek et al., 2022). This finding raised serious concerns in the neuroimaging field about the value of smaller BWAS. Since then, others have shown that as few as 40–100 participants, a study size of more typical research programs, should be sufficient to identify replicable brain-behavior associations if they include the right choice of multivariate methods, the most appropriate brain measures, and a more intentional focus on specific behavioral phenotypes (Makowski et al., 2024).

Using resting-state and three task fMRI paradigms from the ABCD Study, another study decomposed task data into model-fit and residual components and showed that functional connectivity (FC) tied specifically to the task design—along with task beta estimates themselves—predicts behavioral performance far better than resting-state or residual connectivity, demonstrating through systematic predictive modeling that task-evoked activity patterns drive the superiority of task-based FC for behavior prediction (Zhao et al., 2023). Similarly, researchers have conducted meta-analyses across many large MRI datasets, including ABCD, to show that optimizing study design—particularly sampling schemes and longitudinal modeling that separates within- and between-subject effects—substantially increases standardized effect sizes and replicability in brain-wide association studies (Kang et al., 2024). By systematically quantifying how variation in covariates and model structure influences replicability, it provides methodological guidance for designing more robust BWAS.

To ensure that brain–behavior predictive models accurately account for differences across racial and ethnic groups and are therefore generalizable, it is also necessary to address the issue of algorithmic bias. One analysis of data from ABCD and the Human Connectome Project showed that machine-learning models trained on brain fMRI data disproportionately composed of white American participants systematically produced larger prediction errors for African American youth and adults. Although models trained exclusively on African American participants demonstrated improved within-group accuracy, performance gaps relative to white participants persist (Li et al., 2022). Together, these findings underscore the need to increase the representation of historically underrepresented populations in neuroimaging research and to rigorously evaluate the cross-group validity of preprocessing pipelines and psychometric measures to ensure fair, generalizable, and accurate scientific outcomes.

Challenges and lessons learned

While the ABCD Study was built on the foundations laid by the National Consortium on Alcohol and Neurodevelopment in Adolescence (NCANDA) and the Pediatric Imaging Neurocognition, and Genetics (PING) study, it broke new ground based on its size, duration, and breadth of data collection. Such a study necessarily confronts challenges and must adapt along the way to succeed.

Real time granular metrics for recruitment and retention

The ABCD Study sought to enroll a participant sample that reflects the U.S. population of 9–10-year-olds by constructing a probability sample of schools for each site based on sex, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and urbanicity. To achieve this goal, the ABCD Study monitored recruitment numbers and racial/ethnic diversity by site in real-time to make necessary adjustments along the way. However, in the beginning the study did not have real-time monitoring of income or education, or more importantly, race/ethnicity stratified by income/education. When the study was more than half-way through recruitment, we identified discrepancies (e.g., under-enrollment of white participants from low-income families) that required targeted recruitment efforts. Because this issue wasn’t identified earlier in the process, the ABCD cohort, while racially/ethnically diverse, skews toward higher income and education levels, similar to other neuroimaging studies. To address this issue, the consortium provides propensity score weights to balance discrepancies across groups (Li et al., 2025).

Expect the unexpected: be nimble

The COVID pandemic presented another challenge to the study. Lockdowns across the country could have derailed the study since it was primarily conducted in person. Instead, ABCD adapted quickly to a new reality that varied across sites. Decisions were made about delaying vs. skipping neuroimaging visits, which measures could be collected remotely vs. not (and the implications of changing methodology midstream), and what data types could not reliably be collected at all during this time period (e.g., saliva for pubertal hormones). While there was an increase in missed visits and reduction in data collection of some measures (e.g., imaging, blood draws), As of April 30, 2026, more than 7000 participants have never missed a visit and more than 6000 have at least 4 imaging visits, demonstrating resilience of both participants and the study design in conducting a study of this magnitude.

Risks and responsibilities of open science

While open science and large population neuroscience datasets such as ABCD have accelerated discovery and advanced population health research, they also come with risks and responsibilities. One concern is the quality of the publications and/or conflicting results that are difficult to reconcile. However, open science also increases transparency, allowing the broader scientific ecosystem to debate the merits of the science and identify flaws in research design and interpretation that may arise. ABCD’s large sample size and prospective design also allows researchers to divide the sample into discovery and replication subsamples to assess reproducibility and increase rigor, as well as facilitate the use of prediction modeling that can then be tested with future outcomes data. In our view, these benefits outweigh these risks. Of greater concern are potential risks to participant privacy, which is why the NIH has implemented strict security controls, including requiring researchers accessing the data to adhere to terms of access that include not identifying participants or sharing data, or they risk losing access to the data.

These data also demand scientifically rigorous use to avoid misinterpreting findings. Responsible analysis requires contextualizing variables, engaging communities, applying theory-driven and reproducible methods, unbiased data interpretation, and transparently articulating limitations. For instance, when researchers have failed to account for relevant environmental and contextual factors in their analyses, it has led to the misattribution of observed brain structural differences to demographic categories, when in fact variations in early life experiences and access to resources likely played a substantial role in neurodevelopmental outcomes. An analysis of adversity-related measures, neighborhood data, and MRI data from 9- and 10-year-old ABCD participants found that accounting for exposure to adversity attenuated the magnitude of race-related differences in gray matter volume in amygdala, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex (Dumornay et al., 2023).

While the peer review process should identify and stop poorly conducted science from being published, this is not always the case. To guard against inadvertent misuse or misinterpretation of ABCD variables, the ABCD consortium has included in the data documentation issues that should be considered when including variables that have the potential for misuse. However, this will not prevent intentional, malicious data analysis or interpretation. Furthermore, it is easy to disseminate research that bypasses the scientific process by journal shopping, creating journals with less rigorous peer-review, and posting findings online and sharing on social media. Everyone— from funders to researchers to journal reviewers and editors to the science-consuming public— has a role to play in ensuring responsible use, interpretation, and dissemination of data.

Looking forward

Over the next several years, as data from late adolescence and early adulthood are released, analyzed, and interpreted by researchers, we can expect to gain much more insight into many of the questions that drove the initial conception of the ABCD study. We hope to learn more not only about the factors influencing substance use initiation but also about those that influence the transition to substance use disorders and the resilience factors that prevent people from transitioning. We will also learn more about the relationships between substance use and mental health. The 7.0 data release includes whole genome sequencing data, and once analyzed, this will allow for a more comprehensive understanding of genetic influences on a variety of health outcomes. And as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and other physical conditions become more prevalent in the cohort, we will be able to understand how early exposures and conditions relate to long-term physical outcomes to identify key mediators not available in other cohort studies.

An early goal of the ABCD study was to use the comprehensive neuroimaging data to produce reference charts for brain development analogous to anthropometric charts (e.g., height and weight) used in pediatric primary care. No such reference standards currently exist, (Bethlehem et al., 2022) nor do we even understand the variability in healthy trajectories of adolescent neurodevelopment. Such charts could allow researchers and clinicians to identify atypical developmental patterns, understand sensitive periods, and improve early detection of emerging mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, ADHD, and psychosis.

ABCD began when youth were 9–10 years old so that they would be largely drug naïve at baseline and substance use and mental illness would begin to emerge within the first 10 years of the study. However, we clearly recognized that critical developmental windows would be missed with this age focus. By demonstrating that a large longitudinal multi-site neuroimaging study could be successful, ABCD laid the foundation for a more complex longitudinal study of the first decade of life, beginning in pregnancy. In 2018, NIH launched the HEALthy Brain and Child Development Study (HBCD), led by NIDA with support from other NIH Institutes, Centers, and Offices and with partial funding from the NIH Helping to End Addiction Long-term (HEAL) Initiative. HBCD is also a prospective, longitudinal project, which is enrolling pregnant women and following them and their children into early childhood. Like ABCD, a range of neuroimaging and other assessments, including biospecimens, will be collected and made available to the wider research community. As of this writing, more than 5000 pregnant women and their children have been enrolled in the study and initial data from the study have already been released to the scientific community.

Conclusion

The ABCD Study has already yielded critical insights into adolescent development; however, many outcomes of interest (e.g., mental illness, substance use disorders, chronic health conditions) have yet to manifest. As they emerge, data collected during the adolescent years will help to identify predictors of later adverse outcomes to inform the development and timing of early interventions. Finally, recent findings suggest that the brain continues to change across the lifespan, with the adolescent period potentially extending into the early 30s (Mousley et al., 2025). The transition from adolescence to adulthood brings new life events (e.g., entering college/workforce, romantic relationships, parenthood), financial independence, and changing social networks, among many other experiences, which will likely impact brain, cognitive, social-emotional, and other health outcomes, highlighting the need to continue to follow this well-characterized cohort into the future. To do so, NIH is in the process of planning for ABCD-E or ABCD-Extended, which will extend the study for at least 5 more years and continue to collect multimodal data from these participants as they enter the third decade of life.
