Scalp salvage with acellular dermal matrix assistance for recurrent exposure of deep brain stimulation hardware: a case report
Authors: Min Ho Lee, Da Yoon Kwon, Junho Lee, Sung-No Jung, Bommie Florence Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecurrent scalp compromise following deep brain stimulation (DBS) implantation remains a challenging reconstructive problem, particularly in patients with chronic infection and repeated revision procedures. We present an acellular dermal matrix (ADM)-assisted salvage strategy using rotational scalp flap coverage with adjunctive calvarial recession for preservation of exposed DBS hardware. After debridement and subperiosteal flap elevation, ADM was applied over the exposed hardware as a biologic reinforcement layer prior to tension-free closure. Adjunctive calvarial recession was performed to reduce hardware prominence. Postoperative computed tomography demonstrated decreased hardware projection, and stable wound healing without recurrent dehiscence or infection was maintained during follow-up. This combined approach may represent an effective salvage option for recurrent DBS scalp compromise while preserving functional neuromodulation hardware.Keywords: Acellular dermis, Case reports, Deep brain stimulation, Scalp, Surgical flaps

INTRODUCTION

Scalp wound complications following deep brain stimulation (DBS) implantation are difficult reconstructive problems, particularly in patients with repeated revision procedures and chronic infection. Progressive scalp thinning, recurrent wound dehiscence, and tissue necrosis may eventually result in hardware exposure. Reported rates of DBS-related infection vary from 0.4% to 15.2%, while scalp erosion remains a clinically significant late complication associated with repeated revision procedures and compromised soft tissue quality [1,2].

In hostile scalp wounds with severely compromised soft tissue quality, conventional local flap coverage alone may not provide durable reconstruction. Acellular dermal matrix (ADM) has been increasingly utilized in complex reconstructive wounds because it provides biologic scaffolding, facilitates neovascularization, increases soft tissue thickness, and reduces shear stress over exposed hardware [3-5]. We report a case of recurrent DBS scalp compromise successfully salvaged using ADM-assisted rotational scalp flap reconstruction combined with adjunctive calvarial recession.

CASE REPORT

A 36-year-old woman with medically refractory epilepsy developed recurrent scalp compromise with left DBS hardware exposure despite multiple revision procedures. Initial aspiration culture at 3.5 years prior to presentation identified Staphylococcus epidermidis, which was managed by wound dressings and intravenous antibiotics. The patient subsequently experienced recurrent wound dehiscence, infection, scalp thinning, and progressive necrosis over several years. Repeated wound management and scalp flap coverage was performed. The reconstructive challenge was further complicated by leukopenia and decreased absolute neutrophil count during the treatment course.

After 3 years of revisional procedures, the patient presented to the plastic surgery department with erosion of the left scalp area and device exposure (Fig. 1). Intravenous antibiotics were prescribed although wound cultures revealed no growth upon this visit. The neurosurgery department highly recommended maintaining the preexisting DBS device as it is difficult to remove or replace, and interruption of DBS therapy can significantly affect seizure control. The patient also suffered financially and desired salvage of the device. Device rescue by lead relocation in a deeper recess of the calvarium was planned as a cooperation between the neurosurgery and plastic surgery departments. A curvilinear incision through previous scars was performed, and all devitalized tissue surrounding the exposed hardware was meticulously debrided. Scar tissue tightly adherent to multiple loops of the extension wires was carefully dissected under loupe magnification. To mitigate the effect of device irritation on the overlying scalp, a calvarial recess with a depth of 6–7 mm was constructed adjacent to the previous location by the neurosurgery team using a high-speed bone burr, within the thickness of the calvarium (9.7–14.3 mm) as calculated on sagittal section of brain computed tomography (CT). The lead was gently moved into the new bony well, with the electrical coils loosely fitting over, providing a decrease in the prominence of the hardware. In order to prevent constant friction between the hardware and the scalp flap tissue, a 3–4 mm thick layer of ADM (MegaDerm; L&C BIO) was trimmed and placed in a tent-like fashion to cover the device. The margins of the ADM tent were fixated to surrounding soft tissue or periosteum with 4-0 Vicryl (Ethicon) sutures to provide additional soft tissue buffering and biologic reinforcement (Figs. 2, 3). A rotational scalp flap based on the left occipital artery was elevated in the subperiosteal plane extending from the surgical field, and thinned unhealthy margins of the flap located near the previous device were excised (Fig. 4). The flap was rotated to ensure that thick, healthy scalp tissue was placed over the device. At the 12-month outpatient follow-up, the patient showed stable scalp coverage without recurrent dehiscence, infection, or hardware exposure (Fig. 5).

The postoperative course was uneventful for 12 months without recurrent dehiscence, infection, intracranial complication, or hardware migration. This represented the longest period of uneventful follow-up for this patient during the preceding 5 years. Immediate postoperative CT demonstrated substantial reduction in hardware prominence following calvarial recession. The calvarium thickness beneath the lead and wires decreased from 8.67 mm preoperatively to 3.76 mm postoperatively (Fig. 6). Upon measuring the distance between the outer table and the maximum point of projection of the wires on sagittal section, the distance decreased from 13.58 mm to 10.06 mm, representing a 25.9% decrease in projection (Fig. 7). The patient underwent a battery replacement (located in the subcutaneous tissue of the chest) around 3 months after flap coverage, and DBS function was otherwise preserved without interruption.

DISCUSSION

DBS is a life-changing treatment modality that is widely applied for a multitude of neurological entities, including Parkinson’s disease, dystonia, obsessive-compulsive disorder, status dystonicus, malignant Tourette syndrome, and refractory epilepsy [6]. The device consists of an external lead placed beneath the scalp that is directly connected to an electrode that protrudes from the lead through a burr hole of the cranium into the targeted area of the cerebral tissue, transmitting electrical stimulation. The external lead is connected via extension wires wrapped with silicone lining to the generator or battery which is usually located in the subcutaneous layer of the upper chest or abdomen (Fig. 8). As with any calvarial device, scalp compromise following DBS implantation is a debilitating complication for both physicians and patients. Patient, hardware and surgical factors such as poor tissue vascularity, repeated surgical scarring, chronic infection, and persistent pressure from underlying hardware may be associated with tissue complications. The reported rate of infection or erosion is wide, ranging from 1% to 24.7% [7]. In a single-center analysis of 525 patients, infection rate was found to be 8.0% per patient, and erosion rate 3.78% per placed DBS electrode [6]. Although local scalp flaps may temporarily restore coverage, hostile scalp wounds with compromised perfusion may cause recurrent device exposure. This may lead to late infection episodes frequently associated with preceding scalp erosion and long-term soft tissue breakdown [1,2]. These patients will require multiple revision procedures, partial hardware removal, or complete system removal, resulting in substantial surgical and economic burden [2,6].

In scalp wounds overlying devices with repeated surgical scarring, reconstruction requires not only vascularized tissue coverage but also reduction of mechanical stress over the implanted hardware. With increasing experience in ADM application in medical device wrapping, especially in breast reconstruction, there have been several reports of ADM being used as a biological lining between the DBS lead and scalp [7,8]. In the present case, ADM served as a biologic reinforcement layer between the rotated scalp flap and underlying hardware, providing additional tissue thickness and mechanical buffering in a chronically scarred wound environment [9-11]. In addition to functioning as a scaffold for neovascularization and tissue integration, ADM may reduce shear stress and hardware palpability by augmenting the soft tissue envelope [12]. Early surgical intervention before frank infection or extensive tissue necrosis may improve the likelihood of successful hardware preservation and durable wound reconstruction [6,8]. Adjunctive calvarial deepening was performed to decrease hardware prominence and minimize mechanical irritation over the reconstructed scalp. Mechanical tension and chronic pressure from protruding devices are considered important contributing factors in progressive scalp thinning and eventual skin erosion following DBS implantation [9,11]. Postoperative CT demonstrated substantial reduction in hardware projection following recession. Rotational or regional scalp flaps have been reported as effective salvage options for a variety of scalp device-related problems, including DBS, cochlear implants, ventriculoperitoneal shunts, or reconstructive plates [13].

Complete hardware explantation may interrupt therapeutic neuromodulation and require additional staged procedures for re-implantation. This would be financially and functionally detrimental to the patient, especially in this case as the patient was still financially active. In selected cases, preservation-oriented reconstruction may allow us to avoid explantation and re-implantation procedures, thereby minimizing disruption of ongoing neuromodulation therapy. The present report is limited by its single-case design and relatively short follow-up duration. Nevertheless, this ADM-assisted reconstructive strategy may represent a useful salvage option for recurrent DBS scalp compromise when hardware preservation is desirable. In conclusion, ADM-assisted rotational scalp flap coverage with adjunctive calvarial recession may represent an effective salvage option for exposed DBS hardware while reducing the risk of recurrent wound complications.
