α-Synuclein overexpression increases the tropism of pre-formed fibrils and MSA-patient derived seeds towards oligodendroglia
Authors: Anne-Line Strange Laursen, Frida Lind-Holm Mogensen, Jonas Folke, Marie Frederikke Garnæs, Luisa Harriet Knecht, Tomasz Brudek, Mikkel Vestergaard Olesen, Marlene Bidstrup, Sanne S. Kaalund, Susanne Holst-Williams, Katarina Willén, Michelle Pinho, Sandra Pritzkow, Alexander K. Buell, Azad Farzadfard, Claudio Soto, Karina Fog, Louise Torp Dalgaard, Florence Sotty, Susana Aznar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare, fatal neurodegenerative disease, pathologically characterized by glial cytoplasmic inclusions (GCIs) containing α-synuclein (αSyn). The causes leading to aberrant αSyn accumulation in oligodendroglia remain unknown. Recent evidence of increased αSyn mRNA in GCI-bearing oligodendrocytes suggests that oligodendroglia αSyn expression may be a key determinant of GCI formation. To ascertain this, we generated a new transgenic (TG) mouse line overexpressing human (h-) αSyn through the myelin basic protein promoter (MBP-hSNCA mice). Immunofluorescence combined with machine learning-based classification confirmed age-independent oligodendroglial αSyn localization, with no evidence of aggregated and pathological αSyn, as assessed by a FRET-based assay, biochemical fractionation, and the seed amplification assay (SAA). Upon intrastriatal inoculation of hαSyn preformed fibrils (hPFFs), the fraction of oligodendroglial αSyn positive insoluble aggregates increased over time in TG compared to wildtype (WT) mice, reaching significance six to nine months after inoculation. This was accompanied by an increase in brain αSyn seeding potency. As proof of concept, we investigated whether preferential oligodendroglia vulnerability is influenced by intrinsic fibril potency by intrastriatally injecting TG and WT mice with αSyn fibrils amplified from 2 MSA or 1 PD patient’s brains. Six months post inoculation, MSA-inoculated brains retained the MSA-characteristic seeding profile and exhibited higher seeding potency compared to PD-inoculated mice. TG mice showed significantly more widespread phosphorylated αSyn (pSyn) pathology than WT, independent of whether they were seeded with MSA- or PD-fibrils. However, oligodendroglial inclusions were significantly more abundant in TG mice injected with MSA-derived αSyn. In conclusion, our results indicate that intracellular αSyn expression facilitates the development of oligodendroglial pathology, supporting the hypothesis that increased αSyn expression may represent a rate-limiting step in GCI formation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02346-3.

Introduction

Synucleinopathies are a group of neurodegenerative disorders characterized by the abnormal accumulation of α-synuclein (αSyn) aggregates in the nervous system. This group includes Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Multiple System Atrophy (MSA), which differ in both their cellular pathology and clinical progression. While PD is marked by αSyn-rich Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites within neurons, the pathological hallmark of MSA is the presence of αSyn inclusions in oligodendroglia, known as glial cytoplasmic inclusions (GCIs) [22, 36]. Despite sharing αSyn pathology, MSA progresses more rapidly than PD and typically leads to death within a decade of symptom onset [36].

Although the multifactorial causes of MSA are still not disentangled, the spread of αSyn pathology is widely acknowledged as central to disease progression [17, 33]. One of the more puzzling features of MSA is the selective aggregation of αSyn in oligodendrocytes, in which physiological expression of αSyn remains debated [3, 31, 40, 46]. Recent findings, however, show that GCI-bearing oligodendrocytes in MSA brains exhibit elevated mRNA levels of SNCA, the gene that encodes αSyn [23], suggesting that αSyn levels in these cells may be upregulated prior or during disease progression. According to the prevailing hypothesis of pathology propagation, the intercellular transfer of specific fibrillar αSyn assemblies, often referred to as “seeds,” induces the aggregation of native monomeric αSyn [8]. Elevated αSyn levels could therefore render oligodendroglia more susceptible to seeding [20]. Moreover, GCI formation may occur secondary to neuronal αSyn aggregation, with αSyn seeds transferring from neurons to oligodendrocytes as the disease progresses [10, 16, 39]. This interpretation is consistent with growing evidence that endogenous αSyn expression levels significantly influence seed-induced pathology [19, 34, 47].

Efforts to model αSyn pathology in vivo in oligodendroglia have employed several approaches. Wildtype rodent models injected with preformed αSyn fibrils (PFFs) have primarily shown αSyn accumulation in neurons, with little or no oligodendroglial involvement unless the inoculation period exceeds 24 months [50]. To specifically investigate the implications of increased αSyn levels in oligodendrocytes, mouse models with postnatal injections of adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) [28] or genetically engineered models with αSyn transgenes under cell-type-specific promoters have been employed [57]. Several overexpression models display motor dysfunction and striatonigral degeneration, with αSyn accumulation in oligodendroglia described as GCI-like pathology [6, 28, 29, 38, 45, 55, 57], whereas multiple studies indicate that overexpression only induces accumulated αSyn lacking select GCI-like features [19, 24, 47]. Despite these significant advances, the factors governing the onset and temporal progression of αSyn aggregation into insoluble GCIs within oligodendroglia remains poorly understood.

To characterize how aggregation kinetics in oligodendrocytes are shaped by seeding and intracellular αSyn levels, we established a novel transgenic (TG) mouse line that overexpresses human αSyn under the control of the myelin basic protein (MBP)-promoter. Six-month old mice were inoculated with recombinant human preformed fibrils (hPFFs) and αSyn pathology was assessed at 2 to 9 months both biochemically, using aggregation-specific assays and fractionation, and immunohistochemically, using quantitative automated image analysis. After determining the post-inoculation time point at which oligodendroglia αSyn pathology increased, we next investigated whether differences in the seeding properties of the inoculated αSyn assemblies could further account for pathology propagation. Based on evidence that αSyn seeds derived from PD and MSA brains exhibit distinct structural, biochemical, and pathological properties, including different seeding properties [2, 34, 35, 56], we amplified αSyn seeds from MSA and PD brains using the standard seed amplification assays (SAA), developed by Soto and colleagues [42]Seeds amplified with this SAA retain their specific structural and biological properties [52]. To quantitatively assess seeding potency in the inoculated mouse brains, we then used the recently developed low-pH SAA, which promotes secondary nucleation at mildly acidic pH values resembling those of intracellular compartments such as endosomes and lysosomes [9]. Because this assay does not rely on cyclic fibril fragmentation, it provides a more quantitative readout of seeding activity [12]. The two assays were therefore used as complementary, orthogonal readouts rather than a basis for direct quantitative comparison across assays. Our results underscore the importance of the cellular milieu in the emergence of disease-relevant pathology and suggest that interaction with αSyn seeds of higher seeding potency may further drive pathology propagation.

Methods

Generation of the MBP-hSNCA mice

The transgenic mouse strain was generated with the pronuclear microinjection method on the C57BL/6N background (Taconic). The full human SNCA gene (423 bp) was fused with the mouse MBP promoter in a pLV.Des2d.P/Neo vector backbone by Cyagen Biosciences Inc (Supplementary file 1, Supplementary Fig. 1a). The plasmid was linearized by digestion with the restriction enzymes KflI and PvuI, purified, and injected into fertilized oocytes, which were transplanted into a pseudopregnant surrogate mother. The hαSyn expression of the resulting expression lines were compared with qPCR and Western blot, and the highest expressor was chosen for breeding and generation of the TgN(MBPSNCA140)RLfSN strain (shortened MBP-hSNCA or transgenic mice (TG) from now). Heterozygous ( ±) mice were generated by ( ±) × WT breeding. Transgenic ± mice were identified by PCR genotyping using the following primer sets: Forward: 5’-ACACTTAGAGAAAATGCATCCC-3’, reverse: 5’-CACTTTGTACAAGAAAGCTGGG-3’. The mice were housed and bred for 8–11 generations at the vivarium at Bispebjerg Hospital, 1–8 animals per cage under a 12:12 h reversed light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food (Altromin 1324) and water. The breeding colony and experiments conducted were approved by the Danish Animal Experimentation Council (license no. 2020–15-0202–00071 and 2023–15-0201–01422).

Registration No. p-2023–15234). Diagnosis was verified by neuropathological examination of brain sections immunohistochemically stained for αSyn. Motor skill function testing and assessment of dopamine neuron integrity

Motor skills were tested in a separate mice cohort (Supplementary file 2) at 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 and 21 months of age using the accelerating rotarod, balance beam, inverted grid test and gait topography analysis. Protocols are presented in Supplementary file 3. For assessing dopaminergic changes, 3–5 sections from 22 to 25 months TG and WT old mouse tissue embedded in paraffin blocks were cut in 20 µm sections on a microtome and deparaffinized, permeabilized and incubated with anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (Ab152 Millipore 1:1000) for 2 nights at 4˚C. EnVision + System -HRP Labelled Polymer Anti Rabbit and DAB (Dako K4003) was used for visualization and counterstained with CresylViolet. After dehydration slides were mounted, imaged and manually counted in Fiji by a blinded investigator.

Inoculating TG mice with hPFFs or MSA and PD fibrils

Mice were anesthetized with sevoflurane (Baxter©, #KDG9117SV), placed in a stereotaxic frame (Kopf® Instruments, CA, USA) and injected with 2 μL of hPFFs or αSyn material amplified from the human brains by use of the standard SAA, as described further down. In preparation for surgery, the mice were subcutaneously injected with Marcain® (Aspen Holdings©, #123,918) on the scalp for local analgesics. In experimental group 2 (Fig. 2a), 4 μg of PFFs was injected in the right striatum (AP + 0.5, ML -2.1, DV -2.6), in experimental group 3 (Fig. 3a), 1 µg of SAA material or monomeric SAA substrate was injected bilaterally using the same coordinates. Injections lasted approximately 6 min, and to avoid backflow, the needle was left for 3 min after injection before removal.

As post-surgical pain relief, mice were injected subcutaneously with 3.3 mL/kg Bupaq® Multidose vet (Salfarm Danmark©, #472,318) immediately post-surgery, in the evening, and the next morning.

Tissue collection

At the time of sacrifice, animals received an intraperitoneal injection of 9 mg/mL pentobarbital, followed by transcardial perfusion with 40 mL heparinized K-PBS (0.01 M phosphate buffer, 0.89% NaCl, 0.02% KCl). The brain was removed and split into the two hemispheres; One hemisphere was fixated in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for a minimum of 24 h followed by transfer to K-PBS + 0.01% sodium azide at 4 °C and the other hemisphere was snap-frozen in powdered dry-ice and stored at -80 °C. For the non-inoculated (experimental group 1) and bilaterally SAA-inoculated mice (experimental group 3), the right or left hemisphere was randomly chosen for fixation or snap-freezing. For the hPFF-inoculated mice (experimental group 2), the hemisphere ipsilateral to the injection was PFA fixated, and the contralateral hemisphere was snap frozen.

Generation of human pre-formed fibrils (hPFFs)

Full length, wild type human αSyn (UniProt ID: P37840) was produced in-house at Lundbeck A/S and used for generation of human PFFs (hPFFs) as described in [15] Briefly, human αSyn was expressed in HEK293 cells, and monomeric αSyn captured using an anion-exchange column and subsequently purified. To prepare PFFs, the monomeric αSyn was diluted to 5 mg/mL in PBS, transferred to round-bottom tubes and placed on a shaker for 5 days at 37 °C. Finally, the PFFs were sonicated for 2 min and stored at -80 °C until further use.

Seeding amplification assay

Standard SAA for the generation of patient derived αSyn fibrils

Putamen tissue samples from the 2 MSA and 1 PD brains were homogenized in PBS without Ca2 + and Mg2 + with cOmplete® protease inhibitor (Roche, #11,697,498,001) (10%w/v) with a tissue grinder pestle. The standard SAA was performed as described previously [7, 52]. Brain homogenates were diluted 100,000-fold and loaded (40 µL/well) onto a clear bottom 96-well plate (Greiner Microplate 96 Well, PS F-Bottom (Chimney Well) U Clear Black, Non-Binding Cat # 655,906) with a mixture of 0.3 mg/mL recombinant 6xHis-tagged α-Syn (Amprion, #S2020), 100 mM PIPES pH 6.5 (Sigma-Aldrich, #80,635), 500 mM NaCl (Lonza, #51,202), 10 μM Thioflavin T (Sigma-Aldrich, #T3516), and two 1/8-inch Si3N4 beads (Tsubaki Nakashima). Each sample was run in triplicates. Amplification was performed using 15-min shaking/incubation (14 min rest and 1 min shaking at 800 rpm) cycles at 42 °C in a BMG FLUOstar Omega instrument (BMG Labtech). The contents of the triplicate wells were pooled prior to purification. The SAA output was purified by the following ultracentrifugation procedure: The raw SAA output material was transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes (Beckman Coulter©, #357,448) and sterile PBS was added to a total weight of 2500 mg. The tubes were ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g at 4 °C for 1 h with the Optima MAX-TL Ultracentrifuge and the TLA-55 rotor (Beckman Coulter©). Supernatant was removed and the pellet was resuspended in 20 µL PBS and sonicated in a water bath for 30 s. The protein concentration was measured with the bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) (Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay, Thermo Scientific™, #23,225), as described in the microplate protocol from the manufacturer. Briefly, 10 µL of X5 diluted sample were mixed with 200 µL working reagent and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After equilibration to RT, the absorbance was measured at 620 nm. The concentrations ranged from 1.5–5.2 µg/µL and samples were diluted to obtain a final concentration of 0.5 µg/µL.

Additionally, the SAA was performed on brain tissue from mice inoculated with MSA1, MSA2 and PD1 derived fibrils (Fig. 3e). The following adaptations were incorporated: 0.3 mg/mL His-tagged aSyn was produced as previously described [52] in 100 mM PIPES pH 6.5 (Thermo Scientific, #J60659.AP), 500 mM NaCl (Thermo Scientific, #AM9760G), 10 µM Thioflavin T (Thermo Scientific, #211,760,250), and two 1/8-inch Si3N4 beads (CarboTech, DK). Samples were added to clear bottom 96-well plate (Greiner Microplate 96 Well, PS F-Bottom (Chimney Well) U Clear Black, Non-Binding Cat # 655,906). Brain samples was diluted 1:100 in buffer mix (without ThT) with the addition of 7.5 µM Human Serum Albumin (HSA, Fujifilm©, #9988) as used in the low-pH αSyn-SAA. Samples were measured on CLARIOstar® Plus instrument (BMG Labtech). The signal (448 nm excitation and 482 nm emission) was read every 15 min for 48 h at 42 °C with shaking 1 min at 700 rpm and rest for 14 min. The appropriate gain setting was obtained by enhanced dynamic range setting.

Low-pH SAA for assessing seeding potential in the mouse brains

Seeding activity in the mouse brain homogenates was assessed by the low-pH SAA described by Farzadfard et al. [12]. Mouse brains samples were homogenized as follows: For experimental group 1 and 2, samples were immersed in PBS without Ca2+ and Mg2+ with PhosSTOP™ (1 tablet pr. 10 mL; Roche©, #04 906 845 001) and cOmplete™ (1 tablet pr. 50 mL; Roche©, #04 693 132 001) phosphatase and protease inhibitors in 2 mL Precellys® Tubes (Precellys®, #KT03961-1–003.2) to obtain 10 w/v% homogenates. The tissues were homogenized for 2 × 30 s at 5000 rpm in the Precellys24 homogenizer (Bertin Technologies). For experimental group 3, samples were homogenized 2 mL MagNA Lyser Green Beads (Roche, 03358941001) in the MagNA Lyser Instrument (Roche) at 6000 rpm for 2 × 25 s, followed by cooling for 1 min and 30 s. The homogenates were centrifuged at 16,000 × g at 4 °C for 1 min, transferred to fresh Eppendorf© tubes, and centrifuged again at 16,000 × g at 4 °C for 20 min. Protein concentration for all samples was measured using the BCA assay. Briefly, 5 µL of X4 diluted protein extracts sample were mixed with 50 µL BCA working reagent and incubated in duplicates on 96-well plates at 37 °C for 30 min before reading the absorbance at 620 nm.5 µL of 4 × diluted were loaded. Absolute protein concentrations were obtained using a standard curve consisting of a serial dilution of BSA (1.4 mg/mL to 0.04 mg/mL).

For the SAA, brain tissue samples were diluted 1:100 in PBS (w/o Ca2+ and Mg2+) (Gibco™, #14,190,144) with human serum albumin (HSA) (0.5 mg/mL at 7.5 µM HSA) (Fujifilm©, #9988). For positive controls a pool of MSA brain protein extracts (Supplementary file 10) and hPFFs were used. A buffer mix consisting of 200 mM citric acid monohydrate (Apoteket, RegionH), 2.5 M sodium sulphate (Apoteket, RegionH), 1 mM Thioflavin T (ThT, Thermo Scientific™, #211,760,250-25G) and 5% W/V sodium azide (Apoteket, RegionH) was mixed in a 14:7:3.5:1 ratio and filtered with a sterile 40 µm syringe filter. Monomeric wild type hαSyn (produced as described previously [12]) was thawed on ice and filtered with a sodium phosphate-washed 100 kDa spin filter (Amicon®, #FC510096) by 5 min of centrifugation at 3000 × g, RT and diluted in 10 mM sodium phosphate to a final concentration of 20 µM. The buffer mix, monomeric hαSyn, and diluted samples were mixed in a 1:1.4:0.4 ratio and the final sample mix distributed on a 384-well plate (Corning®, #CLS3544) with 15 µL pr well. Samples were run in quadruplicates. Buffer mix, monomeric hαSyn and pH buffer without brains samples or hPFFs was used as negative control. All wells were covered with a thin layer of fluorinated oil (4 µL) (Fluo-Oil40, Emulseo, #037) containing 1% drop-stabilizing surfactant (Fluo-Surf C, Emulseo, #222). This surfactant-containing thin layer of oil effectively suppressed undesired de novo aggregation of αSyn [12]. The plate was tightly sealed with MicroAmp Plate sealing tapes (ThermoFisherScientific©, #4,311,971) and inserted into the CLARIOstar® Plus instrument (BMG Labtech). The signal (448 nm excitation and 482 nm emission) was read every 10 min for 100 to 130 h at 37 °C without shaking. The appropriate gain setting was determined using the enhanced dynamic range setting. To minimize intra-assay variation, the same gain setting was used for all measurements. Whenever possible, all data were generated on a single plate, when this was not feasible, samples were distributed randomly across two plates, with equal representation of the groups being compared.

Quantifying low-pH SAA aggregation kinetics

Seeded aggregation kinetics from the mouse brains were quantified based on ThT fluorescence data generated by the low-pH SAA analyzed in R (version 4.3.2). Briefly, data from all replicates were processed individually by fitting a four-parameter sigmoidal function and applying predefined inclusion criteria to classify replicates as positive or negative. Details on the fitting procedure as well as inclusion criteria can be found in Supplementary file 4. A sample was considered negative if two or more out of the four replicates within a sample were classified as negative. The threshold was defined a priori to minimize post hoc classification bias. For illustration, Fig. 1g shows examples of raw data where hPFF was positive and the remaining were negative.

For positive samples, fits to the different replicates constituted the basis for subsequent computation of a series of kinetic measures quantifying seeding potency. These are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 4 a. and specified as: (1) IC50, the time point where the fitted curve intersects the midpoint between the upper and lower asymptote; (2) Lag time, the time point where a tangent to the fitted curve at IC50 intersects the lower asymptote. For group comparisons, these kinetic measures were averaged across replicates within each positive sample.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from frozen cerebellum tissue (experimental group 1) using QIAzol lysis reagent (Qiagen; ~ 1 mL per 100 mg tissue) and a bead homogenizer (BeadBug 6 with 1.0 mm zirconium beads). Chloroform (0.2 mL per 1 mL QIAzol) was added to the homogenate, followed by phase separation via centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C). The aqueous phase was mixed with 100% ethanol (0.5 mL per 1 mL QIAzol) to precipitate RNA. The RNA was pelleted (12,000 × g, 10 min), washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and re-dissolved in 50 µL RNase-free water. RNA yield and purity were assessed by NanoDrop spectrophotometry. Purified RNA was treated with RQ1 DNase (Promega) for 30 min at 37 °C to remove genomic DNA, followed by heat inactivation at 65 °C. Approximately 750 ng of DNase-treated RNA was reverse-transcribed using random hexamer primers and SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen) at 50 °C for 45 min (10 µL reaction). Parallel no-RT controls (no enzyme) were prepared to test for genomic DNA contamination. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed on a ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Five target mRNAs were quantified using gene-specific primer pairs (TAG Copenhagen; sequences in Supplementary file 5). Each 10 µL reaction contained 2 µL of 1:100 diluted cDNA and primers at 0.63 µM each. All samples were run in duplicate, and no-template (water) and no-RT controls were included. Relative mRNA expression was normalised to the geometric mean of two reference genes (Supplementary file 5) and calculated as 2^(-ΔΔCt).

Immunofluorescent staining and quantification

PFA immersed hemispheres were cryoprotected in 20% glycerol and 2% dimethylsulfoxide overnight. Hereafter, they were processed by the MultiBrain® technology developed by Neuroscience Associates (NSAlabs, Knoxville, TN, USA); briefly blocks of multiple hemipheres are embedded in a gelatin matrix, followed by freezing and exhaustive sectioning at 35 μm thickness. The sections were stored in Antigen Preserve solution (50% PBS pH7.0, 50% Ethylene Glycol, 1% Polyvinyl Pyrrolidone) and every 12th slide was processed for double immunofluorescent (IF) staining for the visualization of Olig2 and human αSyn (hαSyn) or phosphorylated αSyn (pSyn) using the antibodies listed in Supplementary file 6. Hoechst was used as counterstain.

Automated digital analysis of double immunofluorescent stained sections

Sections were scanned at 20 × magnification, for experimental group 1 and 3, with an Olympus VS200 scanner and, for experimental group 2, with a Leica Mica scanner (Leica Microsystems©). The software QuPath© vs.0.4.3 [5] was used for automated analysis of colocalization in experimental group 1 and 2. Regions of interest (ROIs) were annotated manually guided by Allens Mouse Brain Atlas [59], with the primary and secondary motor cortex (MO) delineated from the level where the frontal cortex exceeds the size of the olfactory bulb and nucleus until the anterior forceps of the corpus callosum appears, resulting in 3–5 sections per brain. Striatum (STR) was delineated from the beginning of the caudoputamen until the hippocampus appears, resulting in 5–7 sections per brain. Cell detection was performed based on Hoechst staining using the cell detection plugin (see scripts in Supplementary file 7). hαSyn + and Olig2 + cells were quantified by supervised machine learning random trees classifiers (Supplementary Fig. 2a-b) and pSyn was analyzed by threshold-based classifiers. A thorough description of classifiers for Olig2 and hαSyn, can be found in Supplementary file 8.

Manual scoring of pSyn pathology

For the SAA-seeded mice in experimental group 3, pSyn pathology was manually scored on the digital sections by two blinded investigators. Every 12th brain section of each mouse was assessed using fixed settings across all sections. pSyn pathology was defined as the presence of pSyn+ string-like structures or inclusions associated with cell nuclei (Fig. 4a), while small dots or round structures not associated with cell nuclei were dismissed as staining artefacts. A score of 0 meant no pSyn pathology, a score of 1 the presence of pSyn but only occasionally, a score of 2 corresponds to pSyn pathology that was present but not abundantly, and a score of 3 corresponds to the abundant presence of pSyn pathology (Supplementary Fig. 4e).

HTRF™ α-synuclein aggregation assay

For assessing α-Syn aggregation levels in the human brains from which the patient derived αSyn fibrils were generated additional putamen samples were extracted. Samples were homogenized (10% w/v) in Tissue Extraction Reagent II with 1% Halt™ Phosphatase Inhibitor Single-Use Cocktail and 1% Halt™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail in MagNA Lyser Green Beads tubes. Samples were homogenized in the MagNA Lyser instrument (Roche©) at 6000 rpm for 2 × 25 s, followed by cooling in a cooling block for 1 min and 30 s. The homogenates were centrifuged at 16,000 × g at 4 °C for 1 min, transferred to fresh Eppendorf© tubes, and centrifuged again at 16,000 × g at 4 °C for 20 min. Protein concentration was measured using the BCA assay and a serial dilution of BSA as a standard curve, following the same procedure as described above. For assessing α-Syn aggregation levels in the mouse brains, the same protein extracts as used for the low-pH SAA were used.

The HTRF™ α-Syn aggregation assay (Revvity©, #6FASYPEG) was performed as follows, in accordance with the manufacturer protocol: Protein extracts diluted fourfold were added to a mixture of 25% lysis buffer, 74% MilliQ-purified water and 1% Blocking reagent. Initial optimization experiments were conducted to ensure that the signals were in the linear range of the assay with the concentration used. 5 µL of each antibody (d2 and Tb-Cryptate) diluted 100-fold was added to 10 µL lysis buffer-diluted sample in HTRF™ 96-well low volume plates (Revvity©, #66PL96005). Lysis buffer mix and antibody solution or detection buffer were used as negative controls. In addition to the positive control from the kit, a pool of MSA brain extracts (Supplementary file 10) was added. A dilution series of human αSyn PFFs (rPeptide, #ASF-1001–1) was included for generating a standard curve. Samples were run in duplicates or triplicates. The plate was incubated in darkness at RT for 20 h before reading at excitation λ 337 nm and emission λ 620 nm and 665 nm on the CLARIOstar® Plus instrument (BMG Labtech). Replicate values were averaged. The relative aggregation signal was calculated by dividing the 665 nm signal intensity by the 620 nm signal intensity and multiplying by 10,000. For plate comparison, the relative signals were normalized to the negative control by dividing the difference between the relative signal from each sample and the negative control with the relative signal from the negative control and multiplication by 100. The absolute concentration of αSyn aggregates was calculated based on the PFF standard curve.

Fractionation and western blot

For assessing the fraction of soluble α-Syn in the mouse brains from experiment group 1 and 2, 10 µL 10% Triton-X (Sigma-Aldrich®, #T9284) was added to 90 µL of the brain homogenates in ultracentrifuge tubes (Beckman Coulter©, #357,448) and left for 15 min on ice. The Triton-lysate was sonicated for 20 s at 50% amplitude on a Q800R3 Sonicator (QSonica), followed by ultracentrifugation at 100.000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C (Optima™ MAX-XP Ultracentrifuge, Beckman Coulter©; rotor head TLA-55). The supernatant was kept as the Triton-fraction, and the pellet was resuspended in 100 µL 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (Sigma-Aldrich®, #74,255) in buffer (50 nM Tris [Sigma-Aldrich®, #T6066] and 150 nM NaCl [Sigma-Aldrich®, #S5886] in ELGA-purified water). The SDS-lysate was then sonicated for 20 s at 50% amplitude, followed by ultracentrifugation at 100.000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was kept as the SDS-fraction. Protein concentration was measured using the BCA assay and samples stored at -80 °C.

For performing the western blot assay, samples from the triton- and SDS-fractions were mixed with 25% loading buffer, 8% 1 M DTT and MilliQ water to a final protein concentration of 0.67 g/L. The sample mix was heated to 70 °C for 10 min, and 15 µg protein was loaded on 4–12% NuPAGE™ Bis–Tris Gels. The gels were run in 1X NuPAGE™ MES SDS Running Buffer at 200 V for approximately 20 min. Protein was blotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane with the iBlot™ 2 system (Invitrogen™) using 20 V for 7 min. The membrane was rinsed in de-ionized water and fixed in 0.4% PFA for 30 min. Revert™ 700 Total protein stain was used for loading normalization. The total protein stain was removed with Revert™ Destaining Solution, and the membrane rinsed in de-ionized water. Blocking was accomplished by incubation in 5% BSA in 0.05% TBS-T for 1 h at RT, gently rocking. Next, the membrane was incubated at 4 °C overnight in X1000 rabbit anti-Ser129-p-αSyn diluted in the blocking buffer. To remove excess primary antibody, the membrane was washed 3 × 5 min at RT in 0.05% TBS-T. Anti-rabbit horse radish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody was diluted 10,000-fold in blocking buffer, and the membrane was incubated in the solution for 45 min at RT. Excess antibody was removed by washing 3 × 15 min in 0.05% TBS-T, whereafter the membrane was dipped in SuperSignal™ West Atto Ultimate Sensitivity Substrate and immediately imaged at a wavelength of 700 nm on the LI-COR® Odyssey Reader with 0.5 min exposure time. The membranes were stripped using NewBlot™ IR Stripping Buffers for NIR Western Blots, following the manufacturer instructions, and washed 2 × 5 min in 0.1% TBS-T. The membranes were blocked in 5% BSA in 0.1% TBS-T and incubated over night at 4 °C in X1000 mouse anti-hαSyn 4B12 diluted in blocking buffer. The membranes were washed 3 × 5 min in 0.1% TBS-T, and incubated in goat anti-mouse antibody for 1 h at RT. Finally, the membranes were washed 3 × 15 min in TBS and imaged on the LI-COR® Odyssey Reader with 2 min exposure time using ImageStudio software. EmipiraStudio software was used for quantification of band signals and normalization to total protein stain. Samples were further normalized to a positive control included in each blot to increase blot-to-blot comparison.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in Rstudio (v4.5.0) and GraphPad Prism (v10.4.1). Two-Way ANOVAs were conducted using the lm() and anova() functions to assess main effects and interactions. Detailed descriptions of ANOVA test comparisons can be found in supplementary file 11. In case of non-normal residuals, tested by the Shapiro–Wilk test (shapiro.test()), the data was either log transformed or square root transformed to achieve normal-distribution, or a Kruskal–Wallis test (kruskal.test()) was applied. Significant ANOVA effects were followed by post-hoc pairwise testing using estimated marginal means (emmeans package) with Šidák correction. Post-hoc testing after Kruskal–Wallis was performed using pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (pairwise.wilcox.test()). Two-group comparisons were conducted using Welch’s t-test (t.test()). Categorical data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Outliers were identified using Grubbs’ test at a significance level of α = 0.05. The number of observations per group and effect size is stated in all figure legends and confidence intervals are always set at 95% unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results section

The transgenic (TG) MBP-hSNCA mouse as a model of oligodendroglial human αSyn overexpression

We wanted first to characterize our TG mouse model. After generation of the TG mice and breeding for several generations, we confirmed stable expression of human SNCA exclusively in the TG mice across aging (Kruskal–Wallis, H = 4.3, df = 4, p = 0.357) (Fig. 1b). Expression of the transgene construct (Supplementary Fig. 1a) did not affect endogenous murine αSyn (Snca) and myelin basic protein (Mbp) mRNA expression (Fig. 1b) (statistics in Supplementary file 11). The novel transgenic line was viable and healthy, with no significant differences in body weight between the WT and TG line at 6 and 12 months of age (data not shown). To establish whether the TG mice develop a motoric phenotype, several motoric tests were performed at 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, and 21 months of age, observing a significant effect in the balance beam test, with increased number of falls in TG mice at 18 and 21 months of age (ANOVA, 18: Odds Ratio (OR): 3.07[1.11–9.31], 21: OR: 3.03[1.30–7.43]). In addition, we observed a decreased walk time in the TG mice compared to WT littermates (Two-Way ANOVA, Fage: 6.06, df = 5, p < 0.001, Fgenotype: 12.51, df = 1, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1b). We observed no effect of genotype in the grid and accelerating rotarod tests (Supplementary Fig. 1c-d). In the gait topography analysis, there was a significant genotype effect, both when measuring the length between the forepaws and hind paws (Two-Way ANOVA, forepaws: Fgenotype: 9.33, df = 1, p < 0.01, hindpaws: Fgenotype: 9.42, df = 1, p < 0.01), with the TG mice showing increased length (Supplementary Fig. 1e), indicating motor dysfunction. There was a trend toward fewer tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) positive cells in the TG mice at 22–24 months of age, although this did not reach significance (Supplementary Fig. 1f-g). hαSyn positive cells overlap with Olig2 positive oligodendrocytes

Next, we wanted to assess expression of hαSyn in oligodendrocytes. Protein expression of αSyn in oligodendrocytes was confirmed by immunofluorescent (IF) double staining for Olig2, a universal marker of oligodendrocytes [51], and hαSyn. Total number of cells positive for both Olig2 and hαSyn were quantified by applying machine learning-based classifiers to the digitalized brain sections on brain slices of animals sacrificed at different timepoints ranging from 3 to 12 months (Fig. 1a; experimental group 1). We selected the motor cortex (MO) and striatum (STR) for quantification (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Representative images of Olig2+ and hαSyn+ cells can be seen in Fig. 1c. We observed a stable fraction of hαSyn-expressing cells across aging (Fig. 1d, Supplementary file 9). More than 90% of the motor cortical and more than 80% of the striatal, hαSyn+ classified cells, were also classified as Olig2+, confirming that almost all hαSyn+ cells are Olig2+ oligodendrocytes. However, not all Olig2+ oligodendrocytes expressed hαSyn+ (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 3d). We found no sex-related patterns (Supplementary Fig. 3e, supplementary file 9). Furthermore, sections stained for pSyn revealed diffuse pSyn+ staining in Olig2+ cells in TG mice (Supplementary Fig. 3b-c).

hαSyn is mainly soluble without seeding competency

We next assessed whether hαSyn expression is accompanied with increased aggregation of αSyn. For this we used the homologous time resolved fluorescence (HTRF™) immunoassay which uses fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) technology. The aggregation signal is based on proximity among αSyn molecules. A pool of frontal cortex homogenates from three MSA patients was used as a positive control. We observed substantially lower signal in the TG mice compared to the samples from MSA patients, with levels comparable to WT mice, for all the age-groups studied, indicating that hαSyn expression by itself does not lead to the formation of aggregates (Fig. 1e).

We also separated brain homogenates of TG mice and WT littermates into triton-X-soluble and SDS-soluble fractions, denoted as soluble and insoluble fractions, respectively. pS129-αSyn levels were assessed with Western blotting, and no difference was found between TG and WT mice in the levels of insoluble pSyn relative to the total pSyn levels at any age group investigated (Two-Way ANOVA, Fage: 0.21, df = 3, p < 0.05, Fgenotype: 0.26, df = 1, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1f).

To further ascertain that the hαSyn present in the TG does not present pathological activity, we assessed the seeding potential of brain homogenates from 9 and 12 months-old TG mice. After 100 h of incubation, no seed amplification was observed for any of the TG mice (Fig. 1g).

Increased hαSyn aggregation in 6 and 9 months hPFF-seeded TG mice

We then assessed αSyn solubility, αSyn aggregation and seeding potential. The HTRF™ assay confirmed substantial αSyn aggregation signal in inoculated TG and WT mice, comparable to the pool of MSA brains (Fig. 2d). There was a significant main effect of genotype (Two-Way ANOVA Fgenotype: 9.44, df = 1, p < 0.01, Fgenotype x seeding time: 3.8, df = 2, p > 0.05), with post-hoc test revealing a significant increase in the six-months old TG mice inoculated for 9 months (6 + 9 group) (multiple comparisons with Šidák correction, p < 0.01), with no difference in mice examined 2 and 6 months after inoculation (6 + 2 and 6 + 6 groups). No significant difference between genotypes was observed for the older, 12 months old mice, inoculated for 3 months (12 + 3 group) (Welch Two Sample t-test, p > 0.05) (Fig. 2d). To ascertain whether the overexpressed hαSyn in oligodendroglia contributed to the aggregation, we looked at the insoluble hαSyn fraction, and found a significant general effect of time after inoculation (ANOVA, Fseeding_time: 7.67, df = 1, p < 0.01), with post-hoc testing indicating significant higher levels of insoluble hαSyn in the six-months old mice examined 6 and 9 months after inoculation (6 + 6 and 6 + 9 groups) (multiple comparisons with Šidák correction, p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 2e). Assessment of the potential effect of inoculation on SNCA expression revealed a significant effect in the 6 + 9 group (Supplementary Fig. 3f).

When testing seeding potential, there was a positive seed amplification signal across groups comparable to the pool of MSA frontal cortices, highest for the six-months old mice examined 9 months after inoculation (6 + 9 group) (Fig. 2f; Supplementary Fig. 4a–d.). We fitted the amplified signal data using a four-parameter sigmoid function and extracted empirical IC50 and lag time parameters. Genotype differences in these kinetic measures were then assessed in the 6 + 9 group. A hierarchical mixed-effects model with technical replicates nested within mouse showed negligible residual between-mouse variance after accounting for genotype (random-effect variance estimated at approximately 0; singular fit). IC50 and lag time were therefore averaged within each mouse, and genotype effects were tested at the mouse level. The median within-mouse standard deviation across technical replicates was 7.4 in WT and 3.1 in TG for lag time, and 7.5 in WT and 2.7 in TG for IC50. Compared with WT mice, TG mice showed significantly lower IC50 and lag time values (Welch’s t-test, p < 0.01; Fig. 2g), consistent with earlier Thioflavin-T signal emergence and more efficient seeding.

Higher seeding efficiency in MSA-seeded TG mice

We wanted to assess whether the inoculated seeds retained disease-specific SAA characteristics after 6 months inoculation. Using the standard SAA in one randomly chosen mouse brain from each group, we could verify that the disease-specific SAA profiles observed in the original brain extracts were retained (Fig. 3e). For the quantitative assessment of seeding potential, we subsequently analysed all mouse brain samples using the low-pH SAA. When fitting the four-parameter sigmoid function to the SAA data and comparing IC50 and lag time, we found a significant fibril effect (IC50, Two-way ANOVA Fgenotype = 0.339, df = 1, p < 0.05; Lag time, Two-way ANOVA Fgenotype = 0.2866, df = 1, p < 0.05), with post-hoc analysis showing significantly lower IC50 (p < 0.05) and lag time (p < 0.05) for the TG group in the MSA seeded mice (Fig. 3f,g).

Enhanced pSyn pathology burden in seeded TG mice

Mean scores per group and per mouse for each region are shown in Supplementary Fig. 4f. Consistent with the above findings, inoculated TG mice generally exhibited higher pathology scores than inoculated WT mice, most prominent in the MSA2-inoculated group (Fig. 4c). In PD-inoculated mice, pathology scores were comparable between TG and WT mice, although TG mice still showed higher scores in the MO and STR regions. When comparing the composite pathology score per mouse, a significant genotype effect was observed, with the TG mice displaying a higher overall pathology score (Two-Way ANOVA, Fgenotype = 6.114, df = 1, p < 0.05; Fstrain = 0.01, df = 1, p > 0.05) (Fig. 4d).

Predominant oligodendroglial inclusion pathology in MSA-seeded TG mice

Lastly, we assessed the distribution of pSyn-positive cellular inclusions, with particular emphasis on oligodendroglial pathology. TG mice inoculated with MSA- or PD-derived αSyn showed a 100% (16 out of 16) incidence of cellular inclusions (Fig. 4e). In WT mice, cellular inclusions were observed in all MSA-inoculated animals (4 out of 4), whereas only 62% (5 out of 8) of PD-inoculated mice exhibited such pathology. Rare cellular inclusions were detected in a single monomer-inoculated TG mouse, but these were confined to the injection site and were therefore considered negligible. Oligodendroglial pSyn-positive inclusions were largely restricted to TG mice and occurred most frequently following MSA inoculation. Within the TG group, their incidence was significantly higher in MSA- than in PD-seeded mice (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the presence of αSyn within oligodendroglia enhances the propensity of pre-formed αSyn fibrils to induce glial αSyn inclusions and increases their seeding potential in mice. Moreover, when fibrils amplified from MSA or PD brains are used as seeds, intracellular αSyn in oligodendroglia promotes a broader dissemination of pathology throughout the murine brain, including within oligodendroglia themselves. This effect is especially pronounced for the MSA brain-derived fibrils, which exhibit particularly strong seeding potential. Together, these findings underscore a critical role of the oligodendroglial intracellular environment in shaping the response to αSyn seeds in facilitating MSA-like pathological progression.

Seeding with hPFFs demonstrated that the presence of hαSyn in oligodendroglia induces an increased pool of insoluble αSyn aggregates in oligodendrocytes. This agrees with observations from a seeded CNP-hαSyn-GFP model demonstrating time-dependent αSyn aggregation [19]. In that model, however, aggregation coincided with an age-related increase in oligodendroglial αSyn expression [19]. As our model shows no age-dependent changes in hαSyn expression at the mRNA or protein level, oligodendroglia αSyn aggregation appears to be driven predominantly as a function of seeding time.

One limitation is that, because unilateral injections were used to reduce animal use, the histological and biochemical analyses could not be directly aligned across time points. As αSyn pathology spread more slowly to the contralateral hemisphere [25], biochemical aggregation signals emerged later, supporting propagation to oligodendroglia by the increase in insoluble hαSyn. Nevertheless, because the biochemical and kinetic assays were performed in the same non-injected hemisphere at corresponding time points, they allowed for integrated interpretation within a common anatomical and temporal context.

The delayed onset of oligodendroglial pathology may reflect the fact that PFFs are more efficiently internalized by oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) than by mature oligodendrocytes [39] and the requirement for OPC maturation could contribute to the lag in aggregate formation. Another possibility is that oligodendroglial seeding requires a threshold accumulation of smaller, seeding-competent human αSyn species. PFFs preferentially induce neuronal αSyn aggregation [26, 33] and neuron-to-oligodendrocyte transfer of αSyn has been demonstrated both in vitro and in vivo [39]. Consistent with this, oligodendroglial pruning of Lewy neurite-containing axons precedes the emergence of oligodendroglial inclusions [32]. Moreover, while mature oligodendrocytes exhibit limited uptake of PFFs, they readily internalize monomeric and oligomeric αSyn [39]. Whether the increased seeding activity observed in the PFF-seeded TG mice is directly related to αSyn-overexpressing oligodendroglia enhancing the seeding competency of internalized αSyn species cannot be determined from our observations, as these effects cannot be distinguished from those of neuron-internalized seeds. However, previous studies have shown that the oligodendroglial cellular environment can modulate the biochemical properties of PFFs and may be associated with a more potent seeding profile [13, 34]. Together, these findings are compatible with a model in which oligodendroglial αSyn aggregation in MSA may arise, at least in part, through time-dependent secondary uptake and seeding of neuron-derived αSyn species, with possible implications for the amplification of oligodendroglial pathology. This interpretation is also consistent with recent human neuropathology suggesting that MSA may begin as an early, aggressive neuronal α-synucleinopathy that transitions into a dominant oligodendrogliopathy during disease progression [53].

An outstanding question is whether the extensive pathology characteristic of MSA [27, 41] is driven primarily by the intrinsically higher seeding potency of MSA-derived αSyn fibril strains [18, 35, 58], or instead by the accumulation of αSyn within oligodendroglia, the concentration of which may represent a rate-limiting factor for efficient propagation. Our observations of more widespread pathology in seeded TG mice compared to WT mice, regardless of whether they received patient-derived MSA or PD αSyn fibrils favor the latter explanation. The origin of the accumulated oligodendroglial αSyn in MSA remains debated. Although early work suggested that oligodendroglia lack endogenous expression [44], subsequent studies have demonstrated that these cells express αSyn [11], although at substantially lower levels than neurons [21]. Even so, modest endogenous αSyn expression is sufficient to support GCI formation when provided adequate time frames [50], and αSyn expression may further increase during disease progression [23]. Increased αSyn oligodendroglia expression thus appears to be a pathological hallmark of MSA [23]. We observed pathological αSyn accumulation in oligodendrocytes foremost for the TG mice inoculated with MSA-patient-derived fibrils, in line with the superior ability of patient-derived fibrils to model disease-specific features [49] These findings further support that the level of oligodendroglial αSyn expression is a critical determinant of the extend of pathological αSyn deposition.

Using the standard αSyn SAA at neutral pH and with mechanical agitation [42], we demonstrated how the amplified patient-derived fibrils induced and preserved the distinct seeding profiles characteristic of their brain tissue of origin after six months of incubation in the TG mice. The structural signatures distinguishing MSA- from PD-derived αSyn fibrils are tightly linked to their respective seeding potentials [14], and recent work shows that, at least for MSA, the biological and structural properties of brain-derived αSyn are maintained following SAA amplification [52]. Thus, the amplified αSyn fibrils injected into our mice can reasonably be assumed to mirror the disease-specific MSA and PD strains present in the original brain tissue. Previous studies have likewise demonstrated that strain specificity is preserved when mice are inoculated with αSyn derived from different synucleinopathies, with MSA consistently leading to the most severe neuropathology and the most aggressive phenotype [18, 44, 58].

We evaluated seeding potential after inoculation using a low-pH assay that promotes secondary nucleation without requiring fibril fragmentation [9], thereby providing a more quantifiable readout of seeding activity. By using two complementary SAAs—the standard SAA for seed generation and profile verification, and the low-pH SAA for post-inoculation seeding readout—we minimized assay-internal circularity, since their distinct physicochemical conditions reduce the likelihood of favoring the same amplified fibril twice. Aggregation kinetics were summarized using two empirical descriptors, IC50 and lag time, derived from sigmoidal curve fits following concepts applied in prior work [30, 54]. Because analytical approaches differ across studies, no universally standardized pipeline exists for extracting kinetic measures from raw SAA data limiting cross-study comparability [1]. We therefore focused on within-study comparisons, applying identical preprocessing, fitting procedures and inclusion criteria to all samples to support stable parameter estimation. Within this framework, mice receiving MSA-derived fibrils showed increased seeding potency.

Taken together, our data are consistent with a model in which the spread of MSA pathology reflects the convergence of highly potent αSyn seeds and an oligodendroglia cellular environment that favors its propagation. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the observed genotype- and fibril-dependent effects are consistent with previous studies, experiments including larger cohorts will be important to further substantiate these conclusions. Moreover, our use of SAA-amplified αSyn from only two MSA and one PD case limits the generalizability of the disease-specific observations, given the marked heterogeneity of synucleinopathies. As such, these findings should be considered preliminary and viewed foremost as a proof of concept, warranting broader validation across additional patient samples and brain regions. Finally, future studies should combine normalization by active seeding units with direct particle characterization to determine whether the broader pathology spreading induced by MSA seeds reflects greater seeding potency, a higher number of seeding particles, or both.

It should also be noted that all in vivo seeding experiments were performed in a single MBP-driven αSyn transgenic line. As founder lines generated by pronuclear injection may differ because of random genomic integration, validation in an independent line or complementary model will be important to confirm that the observed effects reflect oligodendroglial αSyn expression rather than line-specific features. Despite these limitations, the consistency of our results with independent reports reinforces the robustness of our overall conclusions.

In summary, our work identifies intracellular αSyn expression within oligodendroglia as a critical determinant of αSyn propagation in this specific animal model. By establishing a transgenic model that isolates the contribution of oligodendroglial αSyn expression, we show that increased αSyn levels do not trigger aggregation on their own but markedly enhance susceptibility to pathological seeding, including from patient-derived strains. The enhanced effect of MSA-derived αSyn suggest a likely interplay between fibril-specific pathogenic properties and cell-type–specific vulnerabilities. Together, these findings provide mechanistic support for the concept that elevated oligodendroglial αSyn represents a rate-limiting step in GCI formation and help explain the uniquely aggressive biology of MSA.
