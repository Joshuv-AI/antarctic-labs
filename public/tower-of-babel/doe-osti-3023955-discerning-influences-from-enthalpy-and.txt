complexity, however, creates an opportunity: if entropy of solvation has multiple dependencies, and the
ones that dominate depend on the system, then systems can be designed to harness control over entropies
and hence free energies. Hence, if the true descriptors of entropy of solvation were known, liquid/solid
interfaces could be designed in a way to control the free energies of catalytic processes. This would be
significantly transformative.
 To learn the descriptors of entropy requires probing several more research questions. For example,
are these effects specific to polar solvents, or solvents that comprise hydrogen bond donors? What about
mixed solvents? We have illustrated that interfacial properties such as hydrophilicity, Lewis acidity, and
isothermal compressibility influence entropies of solvation. Can the link between these properties and
solvation entropy be quantified? We have illustrated that confined systems exhibit different behavior than
unconfined systems. To what extent is this due to confinement and to what extent is it due to interfacial and
solvent properties? What are the influences of pore size, topology, and shape? How can fields, either
spontaneous or external, be used to manipulate entropy of solvation? Our models have examined rigid

in the hydrophobic pore are more dramatic than in the hydrophilic pore, which is the opposite of what was
found in experiments; however, experiments were performed in a different zeolite (Ti-MFI) and different
solvent mixture (methanol with acetonitrile cosolvent). Our prior results1, 4 indicate that solvation
thermodynamics depend on a variety of adsorbate, solvent, and interface properties so we cannot expect
exact matching for different systems.
 In prior work, we investigated the influence of a mixed (1 mol:1 mol) water/methanol solvent on
the entropies and enthalpies of solvation of NH*, NH2*, CO*, COH*, CH2OH*, and C3H7O3* adsorbates
on Pt(111).16 Interestingly, we found that the mixed solvent had no influence on the entropies of solvation
of these species, whereas it increased the enthalpies of solvation of CH2OH* and C3H7O3* by 0.2 eV and
0.4 eV, respectively. We did not explore the possible reasons for this in depth; however, we did find that the
first solvation shells of the adsorbates remained consistent regardless of whether the solvent was pure water
or the mixed water/methanol solvent.16 Taken together, these results suggest that the effect of solvent
composition depends on the interface (as with other properties, as discussed throughout this narrative).
 To investigate the potential role that distruption of water-water hydrogen bonds has on entropy of
solvation, we computed the number of water-water and water-methanol hydrogen bonds in the various
solvents. The number of water-water hydrogen bonds per molecule decreases from approximately 2.0 in
pure water to ~0.1 in methanol/water mixtures, suggesting a significant disruption of the hydrogen bonding
network. Additionally, the number of water-methanol hydrogen bonds in the hydrophobic pore is 14% more
than in the hydrohilic pore, suggesting that more water-water hydrogen bonds are broken, while more water-
methanol hydrogen bonds remain intact in the hydrophobic pore, which is in line with disruption of the
hydrogen bonding network due to a cosolvent. Efforts toward understanding this more fully are ongoing.

 We are additionally working on a machine learning (ML) analysis to more precisely clarify the
descriptors of enthalpies and entropies of solvation in general. We have begun this study using our dataset
of adsorbates on a Pt(111) surface,2 as this dataset is large enough that machine learning can produce useful
results in a computationally tractable way without overfitting. We have additionally begun efforts on the
Ti-FAU and Ti-FAU-OH systems (see below). Our Pt(111) model consists of water (H2O), methane (CH4),
methanol (CH3OH), ethane (CH3CH3), formic acid (HCOOH), ethanol (CH3CH2OH), ethylene glycol
(HOCH2CH2OH), and glycerol (CH2OHCHOHCH2OH) adsorbates and their decomposition products.4, 16,
17
 These adsorbates were hydrated using explicit liquid H2O using the MSS method. 501 configurations of
water molecules around each adsorbate were generated in MSS. ML models were created for water-
adsorbate interaction energies (Δ𝐸𝑖𝑛𝑡 ), adsorbate energies of solvation (Δ𝐸𝑠𝑜𝑙 ), and adsorbate Helmholtz
free energies of solvation (Δ𝐹𝑠𝑜𝑙 ). Briefly, Δ𝐸𝑖𝑛𝑡 are the water-adsorbate interaction energies for individual
configurations of water, Δ𝐸𝑠𝑜𝑙 are the ensemble averages of Δ𝐸𝑖𝑛𝑡 and are approximately equal to the
adsorbate enthalpies of solvation,16 and Δ𝐹𝑠𝑜𝑙 = Δ𝐸𝑠𝑜𝑙 − 𝑇Δ𝑆𝑠𝑜𝑙 , where Δ𝑆𝑠𝑜𝑙 are the entropies of solvation.
ML analysis combining molecular descriptors and fingerprints was used to predict Δ𝐸𝑖𝑛𝑡 , Δ𝐸𝑠𝑜𝑙 , and Δ𝐹𝑠𝑜𝑙 .
Features included adsorbate properties, solvent properties, and hydrogen bonding information, while
descriptors brought in adsorbate/interfacial electronic structure information from DFT. ML regression
algorithms (e.g., random forest regressor) and 3D convolutional neural networks (3D-CNNs) were used for
predictive tasks. The models were trained using nested cross-validation and evaluated based on their root
mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) when compared with DFT/MSS-calculated
values.

 Analysis of Ti-FAU and Ti-FAU-OH systems is in progress; preliminary results are as follows. We
used a deep learning framework that integrates multiscale sampling (MSS), molecular dynamics (MD), and
voxel-based 3D convolutional neural networks (3D-CNNs) with attention mechanisms to predict the
solvation thermodynamics of C1-C3 oxygenates confined in hydrophilic and hydrophobic Ti-FAU zeolite
pores. We chose this strategy since solvent structures in porous materials can take many forms. 18 The 3D
CNN model predicts ∆𝐸𝑖𝑛𝑡 based on the solvent and adsorbate structure. It achieves an R2 of 0.82 and MAE
of 0.083 eV for ∆𝐸𝑖𝑛𝑡 , and of 0.95 and 0.04 eV for ∆𝐸𝑠𝑜𝑙 . Cross-validation demonstrates strong
generalization across adsorbates and solvent compositions in the test sets, with performance remaining
robust even when extrapolating to new solvent mixtures, suggesting the model is promising for predicting
solvation thermodynamics for systems of catalytic relevance. Attention mechanisms indicate that the model
focuses on the adsorbate and its immediate surroundings (i.e., the first solvation shell; see Figure 6). This
is promising since it should be descriptive of a variety of solvent structures. Further, feature importance
analysis shows that atom identity and hydrogen bond properties dominate predictive capabilities, while
features such as partial charges and Lennard-Jones parameters are secondary. This information is currently
available in Jiexin Shi’s PhD thesis (Ohio State University, 2025), and a manuscript is in preparation. Our
