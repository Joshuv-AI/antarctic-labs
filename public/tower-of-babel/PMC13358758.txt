The role of radiologic assessment in evaluating and monitoring respiratory function in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) patients: a narrative review
Authors: Bimaje Akpa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveRespiratory failure is the primary cause of mortality in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), usually caused by progressive neuromuscular respiratory weakness. Standard pulmonary function tests (PFTs) such as maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), maximal expiratory pressure (MEP), and both supine and upright forced vital capacity (FVC) are crucial for objective measurements of diaphragmatic weakness but have limitations, including dependence on the patient’s performance and the inability to detect early, subclinical diaphragmatic impairment or be used effectively in patients with bulbar symptoms. Radiological assessments, particularly dynamic imaging, have emerged as potential objective tools for evaluating respiratory function. This review comprehensively summarizes findings on the use of diaphragmatic ultrasound (DUS), dynamic chest magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and deep learning (DL)-based chest computed tomography (CT) for assessing lung function in ALS patients.MethodsKey radiological metrics include diaphragm thickness (DT), thickening fraction during inspiration, real-time diaphragmatic excursion, lung diameter changes and changes in pulmonary length and area. These measures have been compared with conventional PFTs in various studies to validate their use for diagnostic accuracy, particularly in early stages of disease.Key Content and FindingsDUS is a non-invasive, widely available tool that strongly correlates with PFT measurements, especially FVC, MIP, and sniff nasal inspiratory pressure (SNIP). Dynamic measures, such as excursion and velocity, appear more sensitive to early dysfunction than thickness alone. Chest dynamic MRI has also shown significant correlations with spirometric parameters. Small cohort studies indicate that dynamic chest MRI is a superior, sensitive tool for detecting early respiratory impairment in asymptomatic patients with normal spirometry.ConclusionsRadiological assessments, primarily DUS, DL-based chest CT and dynamic MRI, offer valuable, objective, and non-invasive methods for monitoring respiratory muscle strength in ALS. These techniques serve as complementary tools to traditional PFTs, particularly in selected clinical scenarios such ALS patients with early disease, bulbar involvement and unable to perform PFTs. Further longitudinal research with larger cohorts is needed to standardize protocols and validate their role as early parameters to guide the timely initiation of supportive interventions like non-invasive ventilation (NIV).Keywords: Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), neuromuscular respiratory weakness, radiologic assessments

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by the loss of upper and lower motor neurons, leading to neuromuscular respiratory weakness (1,2). The most common cause of death in ALS patients is respiratory failure, resulting from the progressive weakness of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles (3-5). Dyspnea at rest (a cardinal symptom of respiratory failure) is not usually obvious in the early stages of respiratory muscle weakness, often due to the insidious onset (6). Appropriate diagnostic tests are paramount for prognosis, patient counseling, and the timely initiation of non-invasive ventilation (NIV), which has been shown to improve survival and quality of life. Early initiation of NIV has also been shown to preserve respiratory muscle strength (5,7-10).

Traditional methods for assessing respiratory function in ALS primarily rely on pulmonary function tests (PFTs) such as forced vital capacity (FVC), maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), and maximal expiratory pressure (MEP) (3,11,12). These tests are essential components of routine clinical care and provide valuable insights into the decline of respiratory muscle strength but they have limitations (13-16). FVC, while widely used, often remains within the normal range despite significant early inspiratory muscle weakness, particularly diaphragmatic involvement (15,16). Furthermore, PFTs can be effort-dependent and difficult to perform in patients with bulbar impairment (17).

The advent of recent imaging techniques offers novel, non-invasive, and objective methods to directly visualize and quantify respiratory muscle function and structural changes in real time, potentially addressing the limitations of traditional PFTs. This article provides a detailed review of the current and emerging radiologic assessments of respiratory function in ALS patients, focusing on ultrasonography (US), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and includes three tables summarizing key techniques, findings, and clinical implications. This article is presented in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0763/rc).

Methods

Literature for this narrative was identified using the following terms in Medline and PubMed: “Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis”, “neuromuscular respiratory weakness”, “radiologic assessments”, “respiratory failure” (see Table 1). The search was limited to articles published in English between January 1, 1990 and January 31, 2026, ensuring the inclusion of recent advancements in imaging technology. For the initial search, high-quality articles were prioritized, including original articles, clinical trials, case series, and reviews. Titles and abstracts were screened, and relevant full-text articles were reviewed for eligibility based on the predefined criteria.

Respiratory manifestation of ALS

Neuromuscular respiratory weakness caused by ALS can be categorized into 3 groups (see Figure 1) (3): (I) airway control and swallowing controlled by bulbar and glottic muscular function; (II) effective cough, controlled by the coordinated inspiratory, expiratory, and glottic function; and (III) ventilation controlled by inspiratory muscles. There is some heterogeneity in the respiratory manifestation of ALS and how the functional parts of the respiratory system are affected over the course of disease across several types and subtypes (2-4,18).

The overall consequence is a restrictive ventilatory pattern - reduced lung volumes and shallow breathing. The inability to clear secretions effectively leads to an increased risk of recurrent respiratory infections, aspiration pneumonia and atelectasis, common causes of acute respiratory failure. As the disease progresses, patients develop nocturnal hypoventilation [usually in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep], followed by daytime hypoxemia and hypercapnea (13,19-21).

Traditional PFTs and their limitations

PFTs remain the first-line assessment method in clinical practice due to their ease of use and widespread availability. Standard PFTs measure the functional consequences of neuromuscular respiratory weakness in ALS, but subtle changes may go unnoticed until significant muscle weakness has occurred (see Table 2). Imaging techniques offer the ability to directly visualize the structural and dynamic changes in the respiratory muscles themselves, providing potentially earlier and more objective insights into the disease process.

Radiologic assessment of respiratory function

Imaging techniques offer objective and effort-independent ways to evaluate respiratory muscles and lung function. Table 1 summarizes the primary radiologic modalities.

Chest X-ray (CXR)

A standard CXR offers a static view of the lungs, pleura, and other contents of the thoracic cavity. It is effective at detecting structural abnormalities like infiltrates (in aspiration pneumonia) and atelectasis, which serve as indirect markers of neuromuscular respiratory weakness. It does not directly measure respiratory muscle strength or lung capacity, which are dynamic physiological functions; therefore it is a very limited tool. Respiratory failure in ALS is a dynamic, functional process. A static, two-dimensional (2D) X-ray cannot capture diaphragmatic movement or the efficacy of the respiratory muscles during breathing, unlike dynamic imaging techniques. CXR may reveal low lung volumes often characterized by limited inspiration (visualizing fewer than 8–9 posterior ribs) and basal atelectasis, which is suggestive of respiratory muscle weakness (22). CXR may appear normal even in the setting of terminal respiratory failure in ALS (23).

Fluoroscopy

Fluoroscopy provides an advantage over CXR since it provides a dynamic assessment of diaphragmatic movement. This study helps clinicians assess the extent of respiratory muscle weakness, particularly in the diaphragm, the main muscle of breathing by evaluation of the extent of respiratory muscle weakness, particularly in the diaphragm, the main muscle of breathing (24). Fluoroscopy is a real-time X-ray visualization to check how well the diaphragm moves, diagnosing problems like paralysis or weakness by observing its upward/downward motion during tidal breathing and sniffing, with normal movement being smooth and synchronized, while abnormal motion (like slow reduced movement or no movement) indicates dysfunction (see Figure 2). Diaphragmatic dysfunction can be classified as paralysis, weakness, or eventration (26). Diaphragmatic paralysis is suggested by absence of excursion on quiet and deep breathing, with paradoxical motion on sniffing (27). Diaphragmatic weakness is suggested by reduced or delayed orthograde excursion on deep breathing, with or without paradoxical motion on sniffing (27). Eventration is thinning of a portion of the diaphragm and manifests as focal dysfunction. A “positive sniff test” is a unilateral paradoxical movement (movement of the hemidiaphragm upwards during sniffing), implying paralysis on the affected side. This can be misleading in cases of ALS since both hemidiaphragms are usually affected, which may lead to a negative test (28). The use of fluoroscopy was validated to be a reliable method for the evaluation of diaphragmatic mobility in an inter-rater reliability study (25). This has largely been replaced by more accessible, non-invasive, and radiation-free methods such as bedside US in many clinical settings. Its role is primarily diagnostic for identifying diaphragmatic paralysis rather than evaluating for qualitative functional decline (25).

Another promising area in the assessment of respiratory function in ALS using fluoroscopy in evaluation of pharyngeal patency (29). Recent studies utilized videofluoroscopy to evaluate how different NIV positive pressure delivery methods impact the pharyngeal cross-sectional area, helping to optimize air management (29,30). Pharyngeal cross-sectional area assessment during NIV use is critical in determining its effectiveness and tolerance, particularly in bulbar ALS, where pharyngeal weakness may lead to upper airway obstruction. Dorca et al compared pharyngeal cross-sectional area during spontaneous breathing and NIV using videofluoroscopy in a cohort of ALS patients. NIV was delivered using different interfaces—nasal masks, oronasal masks, high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), and helmet. The greatest increase in pharyngeal patency was seen in nasal masks compared to the others (29). Videofluoroscopy confirms that individuals with ALS often lack the protective, expiratory-swallow-expiratory (E-E) pattern, leading to higher aspiration risks (31). Real-time imaging reveals reduced peak cough airflow rates and longer compression phases, correlating with lower PFTs (31).

Diaphragmatic ultrasound (DUS)

DUS has emerged as a valuable, non-invasive, and objective tool for assessing diaphragmatic function in ALS patients (32-37). It is operator-dependent and allows for the real-time, dynamic assessment of the diaphragm, providing both structural and functional information without radiation exposure. DUS parameters include thickness, thickening fraction/ratio, excursion and velocity (37-39). Diaphragm thickness (DT) is measured in B-mode at the zone of apposition, typically at end-expiration (DTex) and end-inspiration (DTin) (33). Diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF) is also evaluated in B-mode and is a measure of muscle shortening, calculated as the ratio [(DTin − DTex)/DTex] (see Figure 3). A reduced thickness or thickening fraction during inspiration indicates atrophy and diaphragmatic weakness (33-37,41). Diaphragm Excursion is evaluated in M-mode and is the measure of the craniocaudal movement of the diaphragm during respiration, often assessed during tidal and forced breathing. Diaphragm Velocity is also measured in M-mode and is the speed of diaphragmatic movement during inspiration (32). Ultrasound is particularly useful for rapid respiratory muscle assessment and follow-up in a clinical setting without the hassles and challenges of performing PFTs (see Table 2). Visualization of the left hemidiaphragm can be challenging due to the juxtaposition of gastric air. Diaphragm ultrasound is particularly useful for serial monitoring due to its safety and portability (see Table 3) (34).

Studies have shown moderate to strong correlations between diaphragmatic excursion and PFT values like FVC and MIP (42-44). Multiple studies have investigated the relationship between DUS parameters and conventional PFTs in ALS patients with compelling findings, particularly with diaphragmatic thickness and thickening fraction (33,38,39,45). These studies have shown a significant positive correlation between FVC and DTin. Other studies have demonstrated significant associations between DTF and spirometric parameters (33,38). A more recent study did not show a significant relationship between diaphragmatic thickness or thickening fraction and PFTs (FVC, MIP, or MEP) (37). The negative finding was attributed to relatively preserved lung function and early NIV initiation in their cohorts. Other DUS parameters such as excursion and velocity have not been extensively studied in ALS patients. While diaphragm thickening ratio has been shown to correlate with PFTs in some studies, others have found that diaphragmatic excursion is a better predictor of clinical deterioration, such as the need for NIV (37). Boussuges et al. (46) and Iglesias et al. (37) showed similar positive correlation findings. This suggests that DUS can provide valuable, complementary, or even alternative tools for monitoring respiratory function in ALS. Variations in BMI and thoracic anatomy can affect the quality of ultrasound images, adding another layer of variability to longitudinal monitoring. While DUS shows promise in ALS, there are limitations in clinical use due to significant heterogeneity in study methodologies, including variations in ultrasound parameter correlations, inconsistent timing of assessments, and frequently small sample sizes. These inconsistencies, combined with a lack of standardized protocols, limit the ability to establish definitive diagnostic thresholds for clinical practice (see Table 3).

Deep learning (DL) chest CT

DL applied to chest CT is an emerging promising concept that uses applied DL-based chest CT-derived respiratory parameters to predict pulmonary function (47,48). Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI), specifically DL chest CT, have emerged as a promising, objective alternative for assessing pulmonary function, overcoming the limitations of conventional spirometry (see Table 2). DL software can automatically segment and measure specific lung and muscle volumes from chest CT, creating specialized indices—lung volume index (LVI) and respiratory muscle index (RMI). LVI measures total lung volume, adjusted for height and RMI measures the volume of key respiratory muscles (47).

In a recent single-center retrospective study, DL chest CT-derived parameters were comparable to FVC for predicting survival in patients with ALS and were particularly valuable for those with bulbar impairment. The study showed that both LVI and RMI show a significant decline as the disease moves through different stages of ALS (see Figure 4) (47). The King’s Staging system for ALS was used to stage and track disease progression. This indicates that these DL-based metrics closely mirror the clinical progression of the disease. Using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, the study demonstrated that both LVI and RMI decreased with increasing King stages (see Figure 4). The prognostic discrimination performance of LVI and RMI, was comparable to FVC for tracheostomy-free survival [LVI: hazard ratio (HR) =0.998; RMI: HR =0.992; FVC: HR =0.982] (see Figure 4). Furthermore, in the subgroup analysis for patients with bulbar involvement, the prognostic performance of both LVI and RMI was maintained (LVI: HR =0.998; RMI: HR =0.991), suggesting that these metrics could be superior to traditional PFTs for assessing pulmonary function given the unreliability of FVC and MIP (see Table 3) (47). Another study on participants who underwent health screening with same-day low-dose CT and spirometry revealed reasonable accuracy in predicting lung function parameters [FVC and forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1)] using a DL-based image screening tool for low-dose chest CT (48). The integration of deep-learning technology in chest CT analysis presents an exciting and promising frontier in monitoring ALS. While AI algorithms show high potential, there is a need for standardization and validation of these findings in larger, prospective multi-center studies before it becomes routine clinical practice.

Chest dynamic magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI)

Chest dMRI is arguably the most advanced modality for comprehensive respiratory assessment in ALS and neuromuscular disease (49,50). It is non-invasive, radiation-free, and can be used as an early biomarker of respiratory impairment in ALS patients by providing detailed structural and functional information about all respiratory muscles, simultaneously with pulmonary function data (51,52). It provides detailed, real-time visualization of diaphragmatic movement and thoracic volumes. It utilizes fast sequences like true fast imaging with steady-state precession (True FISP), to visualize and quantify diaphragmatic and chest wall motion in multiple planes (51,52). Quantitative data related to respiratory function derived from dMRI include anterior-posterior lung diameters delta (ΔAP) which is the difference between the maximum lung diameters in anteroposterior (A-P) directions in inspiration and expiration; cranio-caudal lung diameter delta (ΔCC) which is the difference between the maximum lung diameters in cranio-caudal (C-C) directions in inspiration and expiration (see Figure 5). Two clinical indices—area pulmonary index (ΔPIarea), length pulmonary index (ΔPIlength) are derivatives of ΔAP and ΔCC. Several studies have shown strong correlation between spirometric parameters [%FVC, %FEV1, and peak cough flow (PCF)] and these derived chest dynamic MRI measurements in ALS patients. Barbato et al. revealed a significant positive correlation between MRI pulmonary measurements and spirometric parameters in a cohort of 15 ALS patients [comparing %FVC with ΔPIarea (r=0.835; P<0.001) and ΔPIlength (r=0.894; P<0.001)] (51). Sub-group analysis in the same study including ALS patients without significant pulmonary symptoms and with normal spirometric evaluation revealed the superiority of chest dynamic MRI measurements in detecting signs of early respiratory impairment. This study suggests dMRI can detect subclinical respiratory impairment in ALS patients who show no symptoms and have normal spirometry results. Another study by Harlaar et al. also showed similar findings highlighting the superiority of dMRI in differentiating specific involvement in neuromuscular respiratory weakness between diaphragmatic and intercostal muscles (52). Utility of dMRI derived data was also proven in duchene muscular dystrophy (DMD) and Pompe’s disease (49,50). This also proves very useful for differentiation of respiratory muscle involvement in ALS (see Table 3). This capability is crucial for timely intervention and initiation of NIV.

Optoelectronic plethysmography (OEP)

Ventilatory mechanics can be measured in a non-invasive, effort-independent method in patients with ALS through an external measurement of chest wall surface motion using optoelectronic plethysmography. This can be used to analyze modifications in breathing patterns and chest wall mechanics related to ALS. Lung volume variations of the chest wall with its different compartments (pulmonary rib cage, abdominal rib cage and abdomen) during tidal breathing and while using NIV can also be assessed using OEP. It considers the differential inspiratory pressure exposure on the chest wall surface of the lung and diaphragm-apposed parts of the rib cage. Using software analysis, chest wall volume measurements, including total chest wall volume (Vcw), pulmonary rib cage volume (Vrcp), abdominal rib cage volume (Vrca) and abdomen volume (Vab). The level of thoraco-abdominal asynchrony (TAA) can also be calculated. Layton et al. (53) showed that OEP could reveal ventilatory dysfunction not detected by standard clinical measures (PFTs and ALSFRS measures). In this study, ALS patients had lower FVC (55.2±22.0 vs. 104.7±16.2 L, P<0.001) and higher ventilatory inefficiency (49.2±9.0 vs. 40.0±3.5, P<0.001) compared to matched controls. There was better correlation between the diaphragmatic lower tidal volumes and the ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS) dyspnea score (P=0.03). Another study compared the chest wall motion and volume changes in ALS patients with and without NIV in the supine position. There was improvement in tidal, end-inspiratory and end-expiratory volumes without a significant contribution from the chest wall compartments. This method has been also applied to assess the effectiveness of cough assist devices on cough peak flow, chest wall volumes, and breathing pattern in ALS patients. Overall, OEP is very valuable in detecting early-stage respiratory muscular weakness, degree of diaphragmatic involvement and efficacy of respiratory care in ALS patients (see Table 3).

Limitation

As a narrative review, this work is subject to selection bias and reflects the heterogeneity of available studies, which largely consist of a mixture of prospective cohort, small-scale and retrospective studies.

Conclusions

Radiologic assessments offer a crucial, effort-independent complement to traditional PFTs for monitoring ALS respiratory decline. These tools provide early, objective measures of muscle atrophy and dysfunction, enabling timely intervention with NIV. Integrating imaging into standard care improves prognostic accuracy and personalized management.
