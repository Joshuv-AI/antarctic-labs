Isoform-specific steric zippers drive aberrant assembly and mislocalization of shortened TDP-43
Authors: Katie E. Copley, Megan M. Dykstra, Morgan R. Miller, Miriam Linsenmeier, Longsheng Lai, Yuanhang Wang, Yi-Wei Chang, Sami J. Barmada, James Shorter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prion-like domain (PrLD)–mediated aggregation and concomitant dysfunction of the essential RNA-binding protein transactive response (TAR) DNA-binding protein of 43 kilodaltons (TDP-43) is a common feature of multiple debilitating neurodegenerative disorders, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). However, shortened TDP-43 (sTDP-43) splice isoforms where the PrLD is largely replaced by an 18-residue carboxyl-terminal tail also contribute to ALS pathophysiology and are enriched in motor neurons. Curiously, despite lacking most of the PrLD, sTDP-43 exhibits pronounced insolubility in cells and tissue of patients with ALS. Here, we establish that the short, isoform-specific carboxyl-terminal tail of sTDP-43 confers high aggregation propensity, which is encoded by two clusters of steric zippers, and can be mitigated by short RNA chaperones. Disrupting these zippers enhances sTDP-43 solubility at the pure protein level and in neurons. Notably, these steric zippers, rather than a predicted nuclear export signal in the carboxyl-terminal tail, drive cytoplasmic mislocalization and aggregation of sTDP-43 in neurons. Thus, we define the sequence-encoded determinants of aberrant sTDP-43 assembly and provide mechanistic insights into sTDP-43 disease pathology.

INTRODUCTION

Transactive response (TAR) DNA-binding protein of 43 kilodaltons (TDP-43) is an essential RNA-binding protein, with a multitude of RNA-processing functions, including splicing regulation and 3′ end processing (1–7). In healthy cells, TDP-43 is predominantly nuclear, whereas in ∼97% of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) cases and ∼50% of frontotemporal dementia (FTD) cases, TDP-43 aggregates in the cytoplasm (8–10). TDP-43 pathology also occurs in limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE) (11–14). LATE can occur in isolation, but is often concomitant with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (15). Patients with both LATE and AD comprise ∼55% of patients with AD (16). Thus, TDP-43 dysfunction is a hallmark of multiple neurodegenerative diseases and represents an important therapeutic target.

The canonical TDP-43 isoform, or full-length TDP-43 (flTDP-43), is 414 amino acids in length, containing an N-terminal domain, two RNA-recognition motifs (RRMs), and a C-terminal prion-like domain (PrLD) (17–26). Several shortened TDP-43 (sTDP-43) splice isoforms also exist in human cells in which most or all of the PrLD is replaced by short C-terminal sequences ranging from 13 to 20 amino acids in length (27–31). The most abundant of these sTDP-43 isoforms is 295 amino acids in length, composed of the same first 277 amino acids as flTDP-43, as well as a specific C-terminal tail of 18 amino acids (Fig. 1A), which is also found in several other sTDP-43 isoforms and is predicted to contain a nuclear export signal (NES) (27–32). This major shortened isoform has been referred to as sTDP43-1, which we will refer to as sTDP-43 (30). In cellular models, sTDP-43 forms cytoplasmic aggregates, and its expression is up-regulated by arsenite stress, pharmacological neuronal stimulation, and autoregulation wherein flTDP-43 protein binds to the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of the TARDBP transcript (27, 29, 30, 33). Detergent-based solubility fractionation indicates that sTDP-43 is even more insoluble than flTDP-43 in cells (27, 30). While the aggregation-prone nature of flTDP-43 is conferred by the PrLD (34–39), sTDP-43 lacks almost the entire PrLD (27, 30). Thus, the mechanistic basis for the aggregation-prone nature of sTDP-43 is unclear.

Although sTARDBP-1 transcripts are present at low levels in cellular models (27, 30), ∼30% of TDP-43 transcripts in multiple central nervous system tissue homogenates correspond to sTARDBP-1 (30). sTARDBP-1 constitutes the majority of TDP-43 transcripts in human lumbar motor neurons (30). As ALS is characterized by motor neuron degeneration, sTDP-43 may be a highly relevant isoform for ALS (40). Immunohistochemistry with an antibody specific for sTDP-43 detects higher levels of cytoplasmic sTDP-43 protein in the spinal cord tissue of patients with ALS compared to control spinal cord tissue (28, 30). Overexpression of flTDP-43 or sTDP-43 in rodent primary cortical neurons or human neurons induces toxicity (30, 33).

Here, we uncover the mechanism driving sTDP-43 aggregation and mislocalization. We show that the isoform-specific 18–amino acid C-terminal tail is both necessary and sufficient to confer high aggregation propensity at the pure protein level. This behavior maps to two distinct clusters of residues within the 18–amino acid C-terminal tail that form steric zippers. Disrupting these motifs, or chaperoning sTDP-43 with short RNAs, effectively suppresses aggregation. Notably, these steric zippers, rather than a predicted NES, drive the cytoplasmic mislocalization and aggregation of sTDP-43 in neurons. Collectively, our findings define a PrLD-independent mechanism for aberrant sTDP-43 assembly and provide a foundation for targeting sTDP-43 pathology in ALS.

RESULTS

The isoform-specific C-terminal tail of sTDP-43 imparts aggregation propensity

The aggregation-prone nature of sTDP-43 has been demonstrated in cellular models (27–30). We set out to determine whether sTDP-43 is intrinsically aggregation prone at the pure protein level and to define the contribution of the isoform-specific C-terminal tail. To this end, we purified canonical flTDP-43, sTDP-43, and sTDP-43 lacking its isoform-specific 18–amino acid carboxy-terminal tail (sTDP-43ΔC-terminal tail) (Fig. 1A). Maltose binding protein (MBP) and 6×-histidine (His) solubility and affinity tags maintain solubility for each of these proteins (fig. S1, A to G). Tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease was added to cleave the MBP-His solubility tags, and aggregation was tracked using thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence. Both flTDP-43 and sTDP-43 aggregated over time, with higher ThT signal for flTDP-43 than for sTDP-43 (Fig. 1, B and C). Notably, untagged sTDP-43ΔC-terminal tail did not aggregate (Fig. 1, B and C). In agreement with ThT data, flTDP-43 and sTDP-43 showed increased turbidity signal over the assay time course, whereas sTDP-43ΔC-terminal tail showed no increase in turbidity signal (Fig. 1D). Under these conditions, flTDP-43 displayed faster assembly kinetics and reached higher ThT signal than sTDP-43, indicating a higher aggregation rate (Fig. 1B). This difference likely reflects a higher aggregation propensity of the extended PrLD in flTDP-43 compared to the shorter, isoform-specific 18–amino acid C-terminal tail in sTDP-43.

Although ThT and turbidity measurements enabled us to determine that sTDP-43 but not sTDP-43ΔC-terminal tail is aggregation prone in vitro, numerous factors affect the magnitude of ThT and turbidity signals, including the length of the ThT-binding region and aggregate morphology (41). To assess differences in the extent of aggregation between the proteins, we performed fluorescence microscopy and sedimentation on the samples at the end time point of aggregation assays. As visualized by fluorescence microscopy, both flTDP-43 and sTDP-43 formed large, ThT-positive aggregates (Fig. 1, E and F). The percentage of image area occupied by aggregates was similar for flTDP-43 and sTDP-43 (Fig. 1, E and F). However, sTDP-43ΔC-terminal tail did not form visible aggregates (Fig. 1, E and F). According to sedimentation, both flTDP-43 and sTDP-43 were present to a similar extent and almost completely in the pellet fraction, whereas sTDP-43ΔC-terminal tail resided nearly entirely in the supernatant fraction (Fig. 1, G and H).

Together, these data demonstrate that, similar to flTDP-43 (36), sTDP-43 is intrinsically aggregation prone at the pure protein level. We ascribe the intrinsic aggregation propensity of sTDP-43 to the isoform-specific 18–amino acid C-terminal tail, as removal of this region abolishes detectable aggregation in all assays used.

A short RNA chaperone potently prevents aggregation of sTDP-43

Previous work has established the capacity of RNA to solubilize flTDP-43 in vitro (42–47). We wondered whether RNA can also antagonize the aberrant aggregation of sTDP-43. To assess the chaperoning capacity of RNA for sTDP-43, we conducted in vitro aggregation prevention assays, in which Clip34 RNA is added before the initiation of aggregation. Clip34 is a 34-nucleotide RNA derived from the 3′UTR of the TDP-43 transcript, TARDBP, within the region where TDP-43 binds to autoregulate its expression (48). Clip34 is capable of solubilizing flTDP-43 (42, 45, 47, 49).

Consistent with previous work (42, 49), Clip34 prevented the aggregation of flTDP-43 in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2A and fig. S2A). Clip34 also prevented the formation of sTDP-43 aggregates in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2B and fig. S2B). Because of the preference of TDP-43 for binding UG-rich sequences (1–3, 50, 51), we also tested the ability of the UG-deficient (AC)17 RNA to prevent aggregation. Despite its identical length to Clip34, (AC)17 did not prevent the aggregation of either flTDP-43 or sTDP-43 (Fig. 2C) (42, 49). Thus, the ability of RNA to prevent aggregation is sequence dependent for both flTDP-43 and sTDP-43.

The percentage of image area occupied by aggregates revealed the dose-dependent nature of Clip34-mediated prevention of aggregation for both flTDP-43 and sTDP-43 (Fig. 2, D and E). Clip34 prevented sTDP-43 aggregation more potently than flTDP-43 aggregation, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value nearly sixfold lower for sTDP-43 than for flTDP-43 (Fig. 2, F to H). The enhanced potency of Clip34 at preventing aggregation of sTDP-43 compared to flTDP-43 was also observed via sedimentation at the end time point of the assay and via turbidity measurements over the assay time course (fig. S3, A to G). Our results indicate that analogous to flTDP-43, short, specific RNAs serve as molecular chaperones for sTDP-43.

Steric zippers in the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail confer fibrillization propensity

Next, we aimed to dissect the molecular features underlying the aggregation-promoting nature of the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail. Thus, we assessed whether the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail might harbor steric zippers. Steric zippers are self-complementary β sheet structures that form the spine of diverse amyloid fibrils generated in vitro (52–55) or isolated from patient brains (37, 39, 55, 56). The ZipperDB database catalogs a structure-based approach to predict whether each hexapeptide within a protein sequence has a high propensity to fibrillize and form steric zippers (52). ZipperDB has successfully predicted steric zippers that increase the aggregation propensity of protein variants that underlie human disease (23, 57–60). ZipperDB predicted that the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail contains multiple hexapeptides with high fibrillization propensity (Fig. 3A). The residues contributing to the predicted zippers reside in two clusters, from amino acid 277 to 285, which contains three overlapping steric zippers: 277GVHLIS282, 279HLISNV284, and 280LISNVY285, and from amino acid 288 to 295, which also contains three overlapping steric zippers: 288STSLKV293, 289TSLKVV294, and 290SLKVVL295 (Fig. 3A). Intriguingly, the predicted NES in the C-terminal tail (30), 289TSLKV293, resides within this C-terminal cluster of steric zippers (Fig. 3A).

Previous work in rodent cortical neurons found that mutation of five residues (amino acids 289 to 293) to glycine, which is predicted to ablate the NES, strongly relocalized sTDP-43 to the nucleus, with a diffuse appearance (30). As these mutated residues reside in the second cluster of zipper-forming hexapeptides (Fig. 3A), and glycine residues disrupt steric zippers (52), we wondered whether disruption of zippers may contribute to the previously observed cellular phenotype (30). We used ZipperDB to predict the fibrillization propensity of this mutant form of sTDP-43, herein referred to as sTDP-435G (52). These glycine mutations eliminated the predicted second cluster of zippers (Fig. 3B). We next aimed to design an sTDP-43 variant with targeted mutations that disrupt both clusters of zippers. Taking advantage of the disruptive nature of proline for steric zippers (52), we substituted proline at residues Ile281 (I281) and L291, which occur at the middle of the first and second clusters of zipper-forming residues, respectively. ZipperDB predicted that the C-terminal tail of sTDP-43I281P/L291P is unable to form steric zippers (Fig. 3C). The I281P mutation selectively disrupts the N-terminal cluster of steric zippers (Fig. 3D), whereas the L291P mutation selectively disrupts the C-terminal cluster of zippers (Fig. 3E).

To experimentally test these predictions, we designed 18–amino acid peptides, corresponding to either the natural sTDP-43 C-terminal tail (sTDP-43_18aa) or the C-terminal tail with mutation of the residues corresponding to I281 and L291 in sTDP-43 to proline (sTDP-432P_mut_18aa) (Fig. 3F). sTDP-43_18aa formed fibrils in a concentration-dependent manner, as assessed by ThT fluorescence (Fig. 3, G and H). Thus, the C-terminal tail of sTDP-43 is sufficient to form amyloid fibrils. At the same concentrations, sTDP-432P_mut_18aa did not undergo fibrillization (Fig. 3, G and H). Together, these data indicate that steric zippers impart aggregation propensity to the 18-aa sTDP-43 C-terminal tail.

After determining that the 18–amino acid sTDP-43 C-terminal tail aggregates in vitro, we next performed electron microscopy to assess aggregate structure. Electron micrographs of sTDP-43_18aa display bundles of short fibrillar structures, confirming that this 18–amino acid sequence forms fibrils in vitro (Fig. 3I and fig. S4, A to D). Both flTDP-43 and sTDP-43 form large, dense networks of granulo-filamentous aggregates (Fig. 3I and fig. S4, A to D) (36, 61). These findings further confirm the aggregation propensity of sTDP-43 and reveal that the 18–amino acid C-terminal tail sequence is sufficient to form fibrils in vitro.

Breaking zippers in the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail eliminates aggregation

Having established that steric zippers are necessary for aggregation of the 18–amino acid sTDP-43 C-terminal tail, we next determined whether these zippers are necessary for aggregation of the 295–amino acid sTDP-43 protein. We purified both sTDP-435G and sTDP-43I281P/L291P, which are predicted to disrupt the C-terminal cluster of zippers or both clusters of zippers, respectively (Figs. 3, B and C, and 4A). As with sTDP-43, both sTDP-435G and sTDP-43I281P/L291P did not aggregate without cleavage of the MBP-His solubility tags (fig. S5, A to G). Consistent with our initial experiments, sTDP-43 aggregated after TEV cleavage, as reported by increases in ThT signal and turbidity (Fig. 4, B to D). Although sTDP-435G also displayed small increases in ThT and turbidity signal, the significantly lower magnitude of these increases compared to sTDP-43 indicates that sTDP-435G aggregates to a lesser extent than sTDP-43 (Fig. 4, B to D). sTDP-43I281P/L291P did not aggregate, showing no increase in either ThT signal or turbidity (Fig. 4, B to D).

We also assessed aggregation by performing microscopy and sedimentation of these reactions at the end time point of the assays. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed that sTDP-435G forms aggregates to a reduced extent compared to sTDP-43 (Fig. 4, E and F). sTDP-43I281P/L291P formed extremely few aggregates (Fig. 4, E and F). According to sedimentation, sTDP-43 was present almost completely in the pellet fraction (Fig. 4, G and H). However, sTDP-435G and sTDP-43I281P/L291P were both predominantly in the supernatant fraction, with a significantly higher percentage of protein in the supernatant fraction for sTDP-43I281P/L291P compared to sTDP-435G (Fig. 4, G and H).

We conclude that the prediction of zipper-forming regions within the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail indeed translates to the sTDP-43 protein. We found that a mutant with disruption of the C-terminal cluster of zippers (i.e., 288STSLKV293, 289TSLKVV294, and 290SLKVVL295), sTDP-435G, exhibited reduced aggregation compared to sTDP-43. For the mutant that strongly disrupted both clusters of zippers, sTDP-43I281P/L291P, aggregation is effectively eliminated. These findings provide clear molecular evidence that the zipper sequences in the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail drive the aggregation of sTDP-43 at the pure protein level.

Two clusters of steric zippers both strongly contribute to sTDP-43 aggregation

With the knowledge that disruption of both sets of zippers eliminates sTDP-43 aggregation, we next wondered whether one zipper cluster is the dominant contributor to the aggregation propensity of sTDP-43. To investigate this question, we purified sTDP-43I281P, which breaks only the N-terminal cluster of zippers (i.e., 277GVHLIS282, 279HLISNV284, and 280LISNVY285) (Figs. 3D and 5A). We also purified sTDP-43L291P, which breaks only the C-terminal cluster of zippers (i.e., 288STSLKV293, 289TSLKVV294, and 290SLKVVL295) (Figs. 3E and 5A). Both sTDP-43I281P and sTDP-43L291P remained soluble without cleavage of the MBP-His solubility tags (fig. S6, A to H). After TEV cleavage of the solubility tags, both sTDP-43I281P and sTDP-43L291P displayed small increases in ThT and turbidity signal (Fig. 5, B to D). Although the extent of aggregation was strongly reduced for both sTDP-43I281P and sTDP-43L291P compared to wild-type sTDP-43, neither protein showed a reduction in aggregation to the extent of sTDP-43I281P/L291P (Fig. 5, B to D). These findings indicate that each cluster of zippers makes an important contribution to the aggregation propensity of sTDP-43, but disruption of both clusters of zippers is required to fully eliminate sTDP-43 aggregation.

We performed fluorescence microscopy at the end time point of aggregation assays as an additional assessment of aggregation. In accordance with the ThT signal and turbidity data, we determined that although both sTDP-43I281P and sTDP-43L291P displayed reductions in the image area occupied by aggregates compared to wild-type sTDP-43, neither displayed the near-complete elimination of aggregates observed for sTDP-43I281P/L291P (Fig. 5, E and F). Sedimentation analysis further confirmed that although sTDP-43I281P and sTDP-43L291P exhibited markedly increased solubility compared to wild-type sTDP-43, neither reached the level of solubility of sTDP-43I281P/L291P (Fig. 5, G and H). We therefore conclude that each set of zippers within the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail prominently contributes to sTDP-43 aggregation.

Mutations that disrupt sTDP-43 aggregation promote neurotoxicity, RNA binding, and nuclear localization

We have now comprehensively dissected the molecular basis for sTDP-43 aggregation in vitro. To gain insight into how these molecular features may be relevant to disease, we next aimed to translate these findings into a neuronal model. We overexpressed enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP)–tagged wild-type sTDP-43 or sTDP-43 variants in rodent primary mixed cortical neurons, then tracked neuronal survival using live imaging over the course of 10 days (Fig. 6A). Consistent with previous work, sTDP-43 exhibited enhanced toxicity compared to the EGFP control construct (Fig. 6A) (30). Moreover, expression of sTDP-435G resulted in enhanced toxicity compared to wild-type sTDP-43 (Fig. 6A) (33). sTDP-43I281P/L291P overexpression also enhanced toxicity compared to wild-type sTDP-43 (Fig. 6A). The observed differences in toxicity are not due to differences in expression levels of the sTDP-43 constructs (fig. S7, A to C). Thus, disruption of the C-terminal steric zippers of sTDP-43 can promote toxicity under conditions of sTDP-43 overexpression in primary neurons.

On the basis of recent findings that sTDP-43–induced neuronal death partially stems from a gain-of-function toxicity dependent on its binding to RNA (33), we wondered whether this enhanced toxicity is due to sTDP-435G and sTDP-43I281P/L291P binding to RNA more effectively than wild-type sTDP-43 binds to RNA. To determine the relative binding capacities of each of these sTDP-43 variants for RNA, we performed electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) with a fluorophore-labeled version of Clip34. We found that sTDP-435G and sTDP-43I281P/L291P indeed both showed enhanced binding to Clip34 versus wild-type sTDP-43, and to a comparable extent as flTDP-43 binding to Clip34 (Fig. 6B and fig. S8, A to G). flTDP-43, sTDP-435G, and sTDP-43I281P/L291P each had ∼1.2-fold higher Bmax values and ∼2.0- to 2.5-fold higher hill slopes than wild-type sTDP-43 (Fig. 6B and fig. S8G). Therefore, we conclude that disruption of steric zippers within the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail enhances binding to Clip34 RNA.

Both sTDP-435G and sTDP-43I281P/L291P mutate one or more residues within the C-terminal cluster of steric zippers. However, overlapping with this second cluster of zippers is the predicted NES (30, 32). To disentangle whether the enhanced toxicity stems from disruption of the zippers or the predicted NES motif, we also tested the impact of EGFP-tagged sTDP-43I281P in rodent primary mixed cortical neurons. sTDP-43I281P also enhanced toxicity compared to wild-type sTDP-43 (Fig. 6A). As sTDP-43 I281P selectively disrupts the first, N-terminal cluster of steric zippers without perturbing the predicted NES motif (Fig. 3D), this finding indicates that the enhanced toxicity stems from the breaking of zippers rather than the disruption of the predicted NES motif.

We also wondered how disruption of the steric zippers in the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail affects the aggregation of EGFP-tagged sTDP-43 in rodent primary mixed cortical neurons. Consistent with our in vitro findings, sTDP-43I281P, sTDP-435G, and sTDP-43I281P/L291P each displayed reduced aggregation in neurons (Fig. 6C). Whereas wild-type sTDP-43 formed large puncta, each of these zipper-disrupting sTDP-43 variants appeared more diffuse upon overexpression (Fig. 6C). Therefore, we conclude that disruption of the steric zippers within the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail is sufficient to solubilize sTDP-43 in neurons. As these zipper-disrupting variants still elicit toxicity, cytoplasmic aggregation of sTDP-43 does not appear to be the sole contributor to toxicity under conditions of sTDP-43 overexpression in primary neurons.

Next, we assessed the localization of the EGFP-tagged sTDP-43 constructs. Unexpectedly, whereas wild-type sTDP-43 exhibited cytoplasmic mislocalization, sTDP-43I281P, sTDP-435G, and sTDP-43I281P/L291P were each predominantly nuclear (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, while wild-type sTDP-43 expression induced endogenous TDP-43 cytoplasmic mislocalization, endogenous TDP-43 was predominantly nuclear in neurons with expression of sTDP-43I281P, sTDP-435G, or sTDP-43I281P/L291P (Fig. 6C). Whereas sTDP-435G and sTDP-43I281P/L291P each disrupt both steric zippers and the predicted NES motif, sTDP-43I281P disrupts only the N-terminal cluster of steric zippers (i.e., 277GVHLIS282, 279HLISNV284, and 280LISNVY285) and not the region predicted to harbor a putative NES motif. Therefore, our data indicate that the aggregation-promoting nature of steric zippers results in cytoplasmic mislocalization of sTDP-43, rather than being driven via active nuclear export by a cargo receptor.

To confirm whether sTDP-43 cytoplasmic mislocalization is independent of active nuclear export by a cargo receptor, we assessed the localization of EGFP- or yellow fluorescent protein (YFP)–tagged constructs in human cells (HEK293T) with and without inhibition of the nuclear export receptor, Exportin 1 (XPO1), by leptomycin B (LMB) (62, 63). EGFP localization was unaffected by LMB treatment, consistent with the absence of an NES in EGFP (fig. S9, A and B). As anticipated, a construct containing an established NES and NLS, YFP-NES(PKI)-NLS(SV40), displayed both cytoplasmic and nuclear localization after vehicle treatment, whereas its localization was predominantly nuclear upon XPO1 inhibition by LMB (fig. S9, A and B) (64). Conversely, wild-type sTDP-43 displayed cytoplasmic mislocalization upon treatment with either vehicle or LMB, indicating that sTDP-43 localization to the cytoplasm is independent of XPO1 (fig. S9, A and B). Furthermore, an EGFP construct fused to only the 18–amino acid C-terminal tail of sTDP-43 also displayed cytoplasmic mislocalization upon treatment with either vehicle or LMB (fig. S9, A and B). Thus, the 18–amino acid C-terminal tail of sTDP-43 is sufficient to drive the cytoplasmic aggregation of EGFP (fig. S9A). Together, these data indicate that sTDP-43 cytoplasmic localization is independent of XPO1-mediated nuclear export and dependent on steric zipper sequences within the 18–amino acid C-terminal tail. These findings suggest that cytoplasmic aggregation of sTDP-43 driven by steric zippers effectively traps sTDP-43 in the cytoplasm. We thus provide a clear mechanistic explanation for the cytoplasmic mislocalization and aggregation of sTDP-43 observed in neurons and spinal cord tissue of patients with ALS.

WT sTDP-43 elicits loss-of-function whereas sTDP-43 zipper-disrupting variants cause gain-of-function toxicity

We next asked how disruption of steric zipper sequences alters the effect of sTDP-43 on physiological nuclear splicing. TDP-43 loss of function induces cryptic exon inclusion in transcripts such as EPB41L4A (7). As expected, TARDBP knockdown or overexpression of the RNA-binding–deficient flTDP-432FL (F147L/F149L) in HEK293T cells induced EPB41L4A cryptic exon inclusion (Fig. 7A). Consistent with prior work showing that sTDP-43 impairs TDP-43 function (33), overexpression of WT sTDP-43 also induced EPB41L4A cryptic splicing, whereas none of the zipper-disrupting sTDP-43 variants (sTDP-435G, sTDP-43I281P/L291P, or sTDP-43I281P) induced detectable EPB41L4A cryptic exons (Fig. 7A). These results indicate that WT sTDP-43, but not zipper-disrupting sTDP-43 variants, elicits a TDP-43 loss-of-function phenotype.

WT sTDP-43 elicits loss-of-function whereas sTDP-43 zipper-disrupting variants cause gain-of-function toxicity.

(A to C) RT-PCR gels assessing aberrant splicing of TDP-43 targets in HEK293T with overexpression or knockdown conditions as indicated by labels at the bottom of (C). Transcripts assessed are established to exhibit a cryptic exon upon TDP-43 loss of function: EPB41L4A (A), or skiptic exons upon TDP-43 gain of function: MYBBP1A (B) and HYOU1 (C).

Conversely, excessive TDP-43 activity can result in gain of function, evidenced by extraphysiological splice repression or exon skipping (65, 66). Supporting this possibility, flTDP-43 overexpression in HEK293T cells leads to skipped or “skiptic” exons in both MYBBP1A and HYOU1 transcripts, whereas WT sTDP-43 did not (Fig. 7, B and C). In contrast, each zipper-disrupting sTDP-43 variant (sTDP-435G, sTDP-43I281P/L291P, or sTDP-43I281P) enhanced exon skipping in MYBBP1A and HYOU1 (Fig. 7, B and C). Together, these data support a model in which WT sTDP-43 primarily drives TDP-43 loss of function via cryptic exon inclusion, whereas zipper-disrupting sTDP-43 variants convert to a gain-of-function state that promotes exon skipping.

DISCUSSION

In this work, we delineate the molecular basis for the PrLD-independent aggregation of sTDP-43. We establish that the isoform-specific 18–amino acid sTDP-43 C-terminal tail, which replaces much of the PrLD, is both necessary and sufficient to drive aggregation. The aggregation propensity can be mapped to two clusters of steric zipper-forming residues in the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail. These findings provide critical insight into the highly insoluble nature of sTDP-43 in cellular models (27–30) and inform the development of potential therapeutic approaches targeting sTDP-43.

Our findings demonstrate that the endogenous steric zipper clusters within the isoform-specific tail of sTDP-43 are necessary and sufficient to confer aggregation propensity to both the isolated tail peptide and full-length sTDP-43 in our assays. These clusters therefore act as central sequence-encoded determinants that couple tail structure to aggregation behavior and subcellular localization. In future work, replacing the native zipper clusters with heterologous steric zipper motifs could provide a useful strategy to test the potential generality of this zipper-driven mechanism and to define how different zipper chemistries might tune sTDP-43 aggregation and localization.

flTDP-43 aggregates more rapidly than sTDP-43 in our in vitro assays, as reflected by a shorter lag phase and earlier plateau in ThT signal for flTDP-43 at matched concentrations. This kinetic difference likely arises from the presence of the full PrLD in flTDP-43, which contains multiple interaction motifs that promote efficient nucleation and growth of aggregates, whereas sTDP-43 aggregation is driven primarily by the isoform-specific 18–amino acid C-terminal tail, providing fewer interaction sites and supporting slower nucleation and less extensive aggregate formation under identical conditions. The pathological prominence of sTDP-43 aggregation in ALS/FTD does not require sTDP-43 to be intrinsically more aggregation prone than flTDP-43. Rather, disease relevance likely arises from the combination of intrinsic aggregation driven by the C-terminal tail, enrichment of sTDP-43 in vulnerable motor neurons, and accumulation and mislocalization in the relatively RNA-poor cytoplasm, where reduced RNA buffering favors rapid aggregation (44, 45).

We identify Clip34 RNA as a potent chaperone for sTDP-43, with enhanced efficacy in preventing sTDP-43 aggregation compared to flTDP-43 aggregation. Clip34 suppresses flTDP-43 aggregation by engaging and stabilizing the RRMs, which allosterically destabilizes a conserved helical region in the PrLD and thereby prevents assembly (49). Because sTDP-43 lacks this helical region, we propose that Clip34 stabilizes the RRMs in a manner that may allosterically disfavor the intermolecular steric-zipper contacts in the sTDP-43 C-terminal tail that promote fibrillization.

The increased efficacy of Clip34 against sTDP-43 suggests that short RNA chaperones may preferentially target sTDP-43 over flTDP-43. This observation opens the possibility that RNA-based strategies could be tailored to selectively mitigate sTDP-43–driven toxicity. Although further studies are needed to determine when selective targeting of sTDP-43 would be beneficial, several contexts are plausible. Cytoplasmic sTDP-43 aggregation may cause gain-of-function toxicity and simultaneously contribute to loss-of-function toxicity by recruiting and mislocalizing flTDP-43 to the cytoplasm (30). sTDP-43 overexpression in human induced pluripotent stem cell–derived neurons induces aberrant inclusion of a cryptic exon in the STMN2 transcript, an event regulated by flTDP-43, thus determining that sTDP-43 elicits loss of function of flTDP-43 (33). We further confirm here that sTDP-43 induces loss of function of flTDP-43, as it similarly leads to the inclusion of a cryptic exon in the EPB41L4A transcript. In degenerating neurons in patients with ALS, sTDP-43 appears to be largely depleted from the nucleus and sequestered in cytoplasmic inclusions that colocalize with flTDP-43 (30). Thus, a short RNA chaperone that solubilizes sTDP-43 could potentially resolve both gain- and loss-of-function toxicity imposed by cytoplasmic sTDP-43 aggregation.

However, it is important to note that elevated levels of soluble sTDP-43 in the nucleus may also pose a threat (33). Soluble sTDP-43 appears to interfere with flTDP-43 in the nucleus via dominant-negative mechanisms, including competition for RNA substrates and hetero-oligomerization with flTDP-43 (33). Therefore, strategies to solubilize sTDP-43 may need to be coupled to mechanisms that promote sTDP-43 degradation. Notably, sTDP-43 is more rapidly degraded than flTDP-43 via both the proteasome and autophagy (33), suggesting that therapeutic approaches to enhance these degradation pathways could be effective (67, 68). Moreover, because sTDP-43 lacks known essential functions, a proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC)-based strategy targeting the unique C-terminal tail of sTDP-43 could be an efficient and specific therapeutic intervention (69, 70).

The basis for the selective vulnerability of certain subtypes of neurons in neurodegenerative diseases is a pressing unanswered question. ALS is characterized by the degeneration of motor neurons, yet the reason for this preferential loss of motor neurons relative to other neuronal subtypes is unknown (40). Transcripts encoding sTDP-43 are strongly enriched in lumbar motor neurons compared to other cell types, and sTDP-43 protein is detected at higher levels in spinal cord tissue of patients with ALS compared to healthy control spinal cord tissue (30). These observations highlight the need to investigate the relationship between sTDP-43 expression and ALS etiology.

The increased expression of sTDP-43 in disease-relevant neurons raises the possibility that targeting sTDP-43 could minimize off-target effects on healthy cells. Achieving tissue-specific delivery remains a major challenge for many therapeutic approaches, so exploiting opportunities to bypass this hurdle may accelerate the development of effective treatments. Directing therapeutics to cells that express sTDP-43 could provide a means of selectively targeting disease-relevant populations.

sTDP-43 drives toxicity through both loss- and gain-of-function mechanisms, including interference with flTDP-43, oligomerization, and RNA binding (33). Though toxic when overexpressed in rodent neurons (30, 33), it remains unclear whether sTDP-43 reaches similar levels in the degenerating motor neurons of patients with ALS. Disrupting steric zippers in the C-terminal tail solubilizes sTDP-43, reduces endogenous flTDP-43 aggregation, and abolishes cryptic exon inclusion due to flTDP-43 loss of function. However, disruption of the steric zippers in sTDP-43 enhances toxicity in neurons, presumably mediated in part by enhancing the exon repression activity of TDP-43 (i.e., gain of function). Both enhanced RNA binding and nuclear localization of the zipper-disrupted sTDP-43 variants likely contribute to these gain-of-function effects on TDP-43 splicing. Unlike wild-type sTDP-43, which drives cytoplasmic flTDP-43 aggregation, zipper-disrupted sTDP-43 mutants keep flTDP-43 diffuse in the nucleus. These findings reveal a shift in toxicity mechanism from cytoplasmic aggregation and sequestration of flTDP-43 to nuclear interference with TDP-43 targets. Thus, effective therapies should go beyond merely solubilizing sTDP-43 to also inhibit its nuclear RNA binding and dimerization or promote its degradation. It will be important to assess the impact of zipper-disrupted sTDP-43 variants on toxicity in a cellular model of disease rather than in healthy cells with well-functioning endogenous flTDP-43, as the gain-of-function effects on exon skipping may be attenuated in cells with existing TDP-43 dysfunction. Targeting steric zippers may offer a dual strategy: rescuing flTDP-43 localization while avoiding toxic aggregation.

Although a putative NES within the 18–amino acid sTDP-43 tail (289TSLKV293) has been proposed to explain cytoplasmic mislocalization (30, 32), our findings do not support a classical NES-dependent export mechanism. The predicted NES does not conform to canonical NES motifs, which are typically at least 10 residues long and have defined sequence patterns (71). Moreover, LMB, a specific inhibitor of the nuclear export factor XPO1, does not reduce cytoplasmic mislocalization of sTDP-43. Instead, we find that cytoplasmic accumulation of sTDP-43 is driven by aggregation mediated by the steric zipper sequences. sTDP-43I281P, a zipper-disrupted mutant that retains the predicted NES, is diffuse and nuclear in neurons. These results refine our understanding of sTDP-43 localization and further reinforce that steric zippers are central drivers of sTDP-43 pathobiology. We suggest that the RNA-rich environment of the nucleus prevents nuclear condensation of sTDP-43 (44). However, physiological shuttling to the RNA-depleted cytoplasmic environment may promote sTDP-43 insolubility, rendering it trapped in cytoplasmic aggregates (44, 72). In addition, nuclear import receptors such as KPBN1 can solubilize TDP-43 (73–79), but exhibit reduced activity against sTDP-43 (74, 75), which may further exacerbate the cytoplasmic aggregation of sTDP-43. Disruption of steric zipper sequences in the C-terminal tail of sTDP-43 may promote nuclear localization via markedly enhanced solubility that renders the protein recalcitrant to aggregation and readily chaperoned by nuclear import receptors even in the relatively RNA-depleted cytoplasm.

In conclusion, we identify the isoform-specific 18–amino acid C-terminal tail as the driver of sTDP-43 aggregation. This region contains steric zipper-forming sequences that promote fibrillization and cytoplasmic mislocalization in neurons. We define the molecular basis of this aggregation and demonstrate multiple strategies to solubilize sTDP-43, including targeted disruption of the zipper motifs and RNA chaperoning by Clip34. These findings establish steric zippers as key determinants of sTDP-43 pathology and provide a framework for therapeutic intervention. By targeting these aggregation-prone motifs, it may be possible to neutralize toxic sTDP-43 and open avenues for ALS treatment.

In vitro aggregation assays

Protein was thawed on ice, then clarified by centrifugation at 21,300 rcf at 4°C for 10 min. Protein was then buffer exchanged in assay buffer (aggregation assay: 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.0, and 1 mM DTT; RNA aggregation prevention assay: 166.66 mM NaCl, 22.22 mM Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.0, and 1.11 mM DTT) with Bio-Rad Micro Bio-Spin Chromatography Columns, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein concentration was determined via NanoDrop. Protein was then combined with assay buffer (and RNA/water for RNA prevention assays; this mixture was incubated at RT for 15 min). ThT (2 mM) was added to samples, which were then transferred into a 96-well nonbinding plate (Greiner Bio-One) containing 2.5 μg of TEV protease (or an equivalent volume of TEV protease storage buffer for No-TEV controls). Final reaction concentrations achieved were 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.0, 1 mM DTT, 5 μM TDP-43, and 20 μM ThT (and indicated RNA concentrations for RNA aggregation prevention assays). The plate was sealed with optical adhesive film and monitored for 16 hours in a CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG Labtech) at 27.5°C.

For aggregation assays, an initial turbidity measurement at absorbance 395 nm was performed at t = 0, followed by fluorescence intensity measurements (excitation: 450-15; dichroic: 468.8; emission: 490-20; gain: 1000) every 1 min for 16 hours, and a final turbidity measurement at absorbance 395 nm was performed at t = 16 hours. For RNA aggregation prevention assays, an initial fluorescence intensity measurement was performed at t = 0, followed by turbidity measurements every 1 min for 16 hours, and a final fluorescence intensity measurement was performed at t = 16 hours. At the end time point of the assay, samples were further processed by microscopy and sedimentation. Kinetic data were standardized to set the value to 0 at t = 0 for each condition. ΔThT and Δturbidity were calculated as the difference between the respective values at t = 16 hours and t = 0. GraphPad prism was used to calculate area under the curve of standardized kinetic data.

Microscopy and CellProfiler aggregate quantification

Samples at the end time point of aggregation assays (t = 16 hours) were imaged by microscopy with a 100× objective (EVOS M5000). Bright-field microscopy was used to visualize aggregates, alongside the GFP channel to detect ThT fluorescence. CellProfiler was used to quantify the percentage of total area occupied by aggregates for GFP images. In brief, CellProfiler converted images to gray, then identified aggregates based on typical diameters of 10 to 1000 pixel units, using global thresholding via the robust background method, with the lower outlier fraction set to 0.05. For each experimental replicate, the average percentage of area occupied by aggregates was calculated as the average value for all images for a condition within that experiment (three to six images were taken per condition for each experiment). Each data point shown within graphs represents this average value for one experiment.

SDS-PAGE

Samples were diluted in 3× sample buffer (187.5 mM tris-HCl, 6% SDS, 30% glycerol, 0.05% bromophenol blue, pH 6.8, and 1.42 M β-mercaptoethanol) and boiled for 5 min at 95°C. Precision Plus Protein Dual Color Standard (Bio-Rad 1610374) was used as a molecular weight marker. Samples were run on 4 to 20% tris-HCl gels (Bio-Rad 3450033) at 175 V for 1.25 hours. Gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue, incubated with Destain I (40% methanol and 7% acetic acid), then incubated with Destain II (5% methanol and 7% acetic acid) overnight before imaging.

Sedimentation analysis

At the end time point of aggregation assays (t = 16 hours), sedimentation was performed on samples. Sample was directly transferred to a tube for the input sample. An equal volume of sample was transferred to an additional “pellet” tube and centrifuged at 21,300 rcf at RT for 10 min. The supernatant of this tube was transferred to a new tube as the supernatant sample. An equal volume of resuspension buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.0, 1 mM DTT, and 20 μM ThT) was added to the “pellet” tube to prepare the pellet sample. All samples were then processed for SDS-PAGE.

In vitro peptide fibrillization assays

Peptides were synthesized by DGpeptides. Lyophilized peptides were resuspended in 15% acetonitrile to prepare 1600 μM stocks, which were aliquoted, flash frozen, and stored at −80°C. For fibrillization assays, peptide aliquots were thawed on ice. Working stocks (100×) of peptides were prepared by dilution in 15% acetonitrile. Peptide working stock, 2 mM ThT, and assay buffer (153.06 mM NaCl, 20.41 mM Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.0, and 1.02 mM DTT) were combined in a 96-well nonbinding plate (Greiner Bio-One) to achieve final concentrations of 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.0, 1 mM DTT, 20 μM ThT, 0.15% acetonitrile, and the indicated peptide concentration (5, 10, or 16 μM). The plate was sealed with optical adhesive film and monitored for 44 hours in a CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG Labtech) at 27.5°C, by fluorescence intensity measurements (excitation: 450-15; dichroic: 468.8; emission: 490-20; gain: 1000) every 2 min, with orbital shaking at 200 rpm for 1 min between measurements. Data were standardized to set the ThT value to 0 at t = 0 for each condition. ΔThT was calculated as the difference between ThT values at t = 44 hours and t = 0.

Transmission electron microscopy

For micrographs indicated as “low salt,” samples were taken from the end time point of aggregation/fibrillization assays performed as described above. For micrographs indicated as “high salt,” assays were performed as described above with the following adjustments to enable clearer visualization of fibrillar structure: for flTDP-43 and sTDP-43 aggregation assays, the protein concentration was 50 μM; for the peptide fibrillization assay, at the end time point, the sample was centrifuged at RT at 14,500 rpm for 10 min, and two-thirds of the supernatant was discarded; and for all samples, at the end time point, NaCl was added to achieve a final concentration of 500 to 600 mM NaCl.

Five microliters of sample was added to a glow-discharged 200-mesh carbon support film copper grid (Electron Microscopy Sciences), incubated for 2 min at RT, and blotted dry with filter paper. Subsequently, 5 μl of 1% uranyl acetate was added to the grid and immediately blotted dry with filter paper. Grids were imaged on a Talos L20C electron microscope at RT using a defocus value of −2 μm.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

Protein was thawed on ice and centrifuged at 21,300 rcf for 10 min at 4°C. Protein was buffer exchanged into 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.0, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT using Bio-Rad Micro Bio-Spin Chromatography Columns, following the manufacturer’s instructions. After buffer exchange, NanoDrop measurements were performed to calculate protein concentration. Protein was diluted in buffer to achieve a working concentration of 50 μM in EMSA assay buffer [150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.0, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, bovine serum albumin (BSA, 20 ng/μl; Thermo Fisher Scientific 23209), yeast tRNA (2.5 ng/μl; Thermo Fisher Scientific AM7119), and RNasin (0.4 U/μl; Promega N2511)]. Then, protein was serially diluted in EMSA assay buffer to achieve a range of protein concentrations. Twenty micromolar 5′ 6-FAM Clip34 RNA resuspended in RNase-free water was diluted to 1 μM in EMSA assay buffer (10× working concentration). RNA (10×) was then added to protein samples to achieve 100 nM (1×) RNA and a range of protein concentrations in EMSA assay buffer. Samples were incubated at RT for 30 min. During this incubation, 6% DNA Retardation gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific EC63655BOX) were prerun in 0.5× TBE buffer at 150 V for ∼20 min. Dye (1×) was prepared by dilution of 5× dye (20 mM EDTA, 50% sucrose, and 0.25% bromophenol blue) in EMSA assay buffer. After incubation, heparin (MilliporeSigma H3393) was added to each sample to achieve a final concentration of heparin (0.5 mg/ml). Fifteen microliters of 1× dye was loaded in the first lane to monitor sample progression, while 15 μl of undyed sample was loaded in remaining lanes. Gels were run at 150 V for 40 min. Gels were imaged on a Typhoon Scanner using FAM fluorescence measurement. The signal of bound TDP-43 in each lane was quantified using Image Studio Lite.

RNA oligonucleotides

Unmodified RNA oligonucleotides were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT), with standard desalting. RNAs were resuspended in RNase-free water, and concentration was determined via NanoDrop. RNAs were aliquoted and stored at −80°C. Full RNA sequences are as follows: Clip34: GAGAGAGCGCGUGCAGAGACUUGGUGGUGCAUAA. (AC)17: ACACACACACACACACACACACACACACACACAC.

Primary neuron culture

Cortices from embryonic day (E)19 to 20 Long-Evans rat embryos were dissected and disassociated, and primary neurons were plated at a density of 6 × 105 cells/ml in 96-well plates or on coverslips. On in vitro day 4, neurons were transfected with 100 ng of a plasmid expressing a diffusely localized red fluorescent protein (mApple), in addition to 100 ng of an experimental construct (EGFP-tagged sTDP-43 variants) using Lipofectamine 2000 as previously described (82, 85). Following transfection, cells were placed in Neurobasal Complete Media [Neurobasal (Gibco 21103-049), 1× B27 Supplement (Gibco, 17504-044), 1× Glutamax, and Penicillin-Streptomycin (100 U/ml)] and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2.

Automated fluorescence microscopy

Neurons were imaged as described previously (67, 86, 87), using a Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted microscope with PerfectFocus3a 20× objective lens and either an Andor iXon3 897 EMCCD camera or an Andor Zyla 4.2 (+) sCMOS camera. A Lambda CL Xenon lamp (Sutter) with 5-mm liquid light guide (Sutter) was used to illuminate samples, and custom scripts written in Beanshell for use in micromanager controlled all stage movements, shutters, and filters. For automated analyses of primary neuron survival, custom ImageJ/FIJI macros and Python scripts were used to identify neurons and draw cellular regions of interest (ROIs) based on size, morphology, and mApple fluorescence intensity. Custom Python scripts were used to track ROIs over time, and cell death marked a set of criteria that include rounding of the soma, loss of mApple fluorescence, and degeneration of neuritic processes (82, 87, 88).

Immunocytochemistry

Following 48 hours of transfection, rodent primary neurons were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Sigma-Aldrich, P6148) for 10 min, rinsed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Bio-Rad, 161-0407) for 20 min. Neurons were then treated with 10 mM glycine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, BP381-1) in PBS and then blocked in 3% BSA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, BP9703-100) in PBS at RT for 1 hour before incubation overnight at 4°C in primary antibody diluted in 3% BSA. Cells were then washed three times in PBS and incubated at RT with secondary antibodies diluted 1:250 in 3% BSA for 1 hour. Following three washes in PBS containing 1:10,000 Hoechst 33258 dye (Invitrogen, H3569), cells were mounted on Superfrost Plus Microscope Slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1255015) with Prolong Gold Antifade Mounting Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P10144) and confocal images were taken on a Nikon AXR NSPARC confocal system with a 60× NA 1.42 Oil/DIS PLan-Apochromat Lambda D objective with a working distance of 1.5 mm.

Nuclear export inhibition in HEK293T cells

HEK293T cells were transfected overnight with plasmids of interest (described in the “Cloning” section) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. 11668027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Once green fluorescence was confirmed, cells were treated with 10 nM LMB (Cayman Chemical, no. 87081-35-4) and/or vehicle (ethanol) plus Hoechst (Invitrogen, no. H3569) for 5 hours, followed by fixation in 4% PFA for 10 min. Cells were then washed with and incubated in PBS. Transfected cells were imaged with a Nikon AXR NSPARC confocal system with a 40× objective. To quantify localization of green fluorescence, cytoplasmic and nuclear regions of interest were defined manually based on green fluorescence and Hoechst signal, and green fluorescence was measured within each ROI. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism.

RT-PCR

HEK293T cells were transfected with the following plasmids: pGW1-EGFP, pGW1-TDP43(2FL)-EGFP, pGW1-TDP43-EGFP, pGW1-sTDP43-EGFP, pGW1-sTDP43(5G)-EGFP, pGW1-sTDP43(I281P)-EGFP, or pGW1-sTDP43(I281P/L291P)-EGFP using Lipofectamine 2000 or were transfected with NT-siRNA or TDP43-siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX. Following 24 hours of transfection, cells were collected, and RNA was isolated using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo, R2052). cDNA was reverse transcribed from 2 μg of RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, 4368814). One microliter of cDNA was used as a template for PCR reactions with Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen 10966018) and the following primers: EPB41L4A F: GGACCTCCATATACTTTGTATTTTGGT, EPB41L4A R: AGCTGAGCAGCAGTGTTGAC; HYOU1 F: TTCTATGACATGGGCTCAGGC, HYOU1 R: ACTGCATCTCGGACGACAAA; MYBBP1A F: AAAGTCTGGGAGAGAAGCCC, MYBBP1A R: CGCAGAGCCTTCTCCTTCTG. PCR products were resolved on 2% agarose gels.
