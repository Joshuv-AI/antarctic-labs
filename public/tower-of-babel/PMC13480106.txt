Specialized, integrative treatment for long-standing eating disorders: early insights and implications

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEating disorders (EDs) are severe and multifaceted psychiatric conditions. Despite advancements in treatment modalities, a substantial gap persists in evidence-based interventions specifically designed for individuals with enduring EDs.ObjectiveTo address this critical clinical need, a specialized residential unit dedicated exclusively to the care of adults with long-standing EDs was launched within the Catalan Public Health System in January 2024. This study introduces the unit’s innovative therapeutic model and presents preliminary outcomes related to patient satisfaction, body mass index (BMI), and quality of life.MethodThis preliminary investigation included 18 participants diagnosed with anorexia nervosa (AN) or other specified feeding or eating disorders (OSFED/AN), according to DSM-5 criteria. Each participant underwent a structured clinical interview and completed a comprehensive battery of quantitative and qualitative assessments. These measures evaluated clinical status, psychopathology, personality traits, quality of life, and satisfaction with the treatment received over an average stay of 3–4 months.ResultsParticipants had a mean illness duration exceeding 12 years and had previously undergone at least three treatment attempts that did not result in sustained clinical or functional improvement. Despite having 22% high educational attainment, 94% were unemployed. High rates of psychiatric comorbidity were observed. Post-treatment analyses revealed significant improvements in quality of life (p < 0.001) and BMI (p < 0.001). The mean satisfaction score was 85.3 out of 100, aligning with the positive qualitative feedback provided by participants.DiscussionPreliminary findings suggest that this novel residential approach yields meaningful improvements in quality of life and psychopathological symptoms, while fostering greater autonomy among individuals with enduring EDs. These results underscore the potential of specialized, long-term care models within public health systems for this underserved population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01674-y.

Introduction

Over the last decade, the study of eating disorders (ED)—particularly long-standing anorexia nervosa (AN) and its treatment—has received growing attention within the scientific community [1]. Despite this increased focus, there remains no universally accepted nosological definition for this subgroup of patients. Ongoing debate persists regarding the criteria that should be met, such as illness duration and the number of prior treatment attempts that did not result in sustained clinical or functional improvement [1–5], and the terminology to be used [6, 7]. Nonetheless, there is broad consensus among clinicians and researchers that individuals with long-standing ED, typically exhibit repeated treatment failures, more severe medical complications, greater cognitive impairment, and reduced quality of life [4, 8–13]. In addition, these patients often present with more pronounced general psychopathology, and complex comorbid profiles, including frequent suicidality and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), personality disorders, mood and anxiety disorders, substance use disorders (SUD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [9, 14–17]. Despite the frequent clinical encounters of these patients, there remains a significant gap between clinical practice and empirical research, with limited evidence to guide decision-making or the development of specialized interventions.

Several systematic reviews examining the medium- to long-term efficacy of standard treatments for EDs, have reported that approximately 40% of cases result in poor treatment outcomes [18]. Despite these findings, there is currently no consensus or specific clinical guidelines for managing this subgroup of patients [19]. The scarcity of robust empirical evidence and the methodological limitations of existing studies, significantly hinder the development of personalized treatment approaches for individuals with long-standing EDs. Usually, therapeutic strategies rely on the repetition or intensification of previously implemented interventions, which have already demonstrated limited results [20–22], rather than the adoption of innovative approaches. This highlights the need for alternative models that address the complex and chronic nature of the disorder.

Research has identified several predictors of poor outcome and chronicity, including early traumatic experiences, younger age of onset, and specific temperamental traits such as high impulsivity, extremely high rigidity, low self-directedness, and elevated harm avoidance [22–24]. Additional contributing factors include persistent interpersonal difficulties, limited social support, and co-occurring neurodevelopmental disorders [9, 25]. These factors may partially explain why long-standing EDs often require more individualized, integrative, and sustained therapeutic approaches.

In recent years, there has been growing recognition of the clinical complexity associated with long-standing EDs, documented treatment efforts remain limited and largely inconclusive [8, 19]. Interventions such as cognitive rehabilitation, dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) or scheme therapy, brain stimulation techniques, and the use of pharmacological or psychedelic-assisted approaches have shown modest and still inconclusive results, frequently based on small case series [1, 26–31]. To date, the only standardized randomized controlled trial evaluating psychological treatment in this population, was conducted by Touyz et al. [3], who compared a modified cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) with a supportive therapy, in individuals with severe and enduring anorexia nervosa (AN) receiving community-based care. Both treatment arms yielded significant improvements in quality of life, associated clinical symptoms, motivation for change, social functioning, and body mass index (BMI).

Contemporary approaches have highlighted the importance of shared decision-making, flexible treatment planning, and recovery outcomes that prioritize functioning and quality of life. For instance, Shut et al. [20] and van den Eijnde-Damen et al. [18] propose collaborative care frameworks for severe and enduring AN (SE-AN), promoting collaboration among patient and clinician goals. Earlier conceptual work similarly advocated for a paradigm shift toward recovery-oriented approaches that prioritize quality of life and harm reduction strategies alongside symptom change [32].

In alignment with emerging perspectives on the treatment of long-standing ED, Wonderlich et al. [5] proposed an individualized therapeutic model that emphasizes the formulation of flexible, patient-centered goals. This approach prioritizes improvements in quality of life, medical stabilization, and nutritional rehabilitation, rather than rigid symptom-based targets. Similarly, other authors have advocated for a paradigm shift in the treatment of long-standing AN, suggesting that therapeutic objectives should focus on enhancing functional capacity and subjective well-being, rather than exclusively pursuing weight restoration as measured by BMI [32–36].

Further supporting this perspective, Calugi et al. [37] conducted an inpatient study utilizing a recovery-oriented model to compare outcomes between individuals with AN and those with SE-AN. The study demonstrated comparable improvements in both psychopathological symptoms and BMI across the two groups following treatment with enhanced cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT-E). These findings were subsequently corroborated [21], reinforcing the potential applicability and clinical value of enhanced CBT in addressing the complex needs of this challenging patient population.

In summary, the current literature highlights a significant gap in the understanding and management of long-standing EDs, which are typically characterized by prolonged illness duration, patients with complex care needs, high comorbidity, and persistently low levels of functioning and quality of life. Prior research has highlighted specific treatment needs for this population, including extended and flexible care, individualized therapeutic plans, integration of medical stabilization with psychosocial support, collaborative goal setting, promotion of autonomy, and harm-reduction strategies (. Incorporating these elements into treatment models addresses gaps identified in conventional approaches, which often focus narrowly on symptom reduction and weight restoration, and reflects insights from patients’ lived experiences regarding unmet needs. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to describe a specific therapeutic approach tailored to this patient population and to evaluate it from both quantitative and qualitative perspectives. Although several conceptual frameworks have been proposed, relatively few studies have described and evaluated specialized integrative therapeutic programs combining extended admissions with systematic quantitative and qualitative outcome assessment. Therefore, the present study aims to describe the implementation of a specialized integrative treatment program for individuals with long-standing EDs and to provide early quantitative and qualitative insights into patient-reported outcomes and experiences.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a quasi-experimental, single-group pre-post design to assess the effectiveness of a specialized integrative treatment for patients with long-standing ED. To obtain a comprehensive understanding of treatment outcomes, we implemented a convergent mixed methods design, integrating quantitative and qualitative data.

Qualitative data were collected through semi-structured interviews conducted with seven participants. The interviews explored participants’ perceptions of treatment, changes in autonomy, family dynamics, identity, group belonging, acquisition of therapeutic skills, and ongoing coexistence with the disorder. Thematic analysis was conducted following standard procedures, including independent coding by two researchers, iterative theme development, and triangulation with quantitative outcomes. This approach allowed us to enrich and contextualize the quantitative findings, providing a more nuanced understanding of the effects of the intervention (Creswell, 2014). Themes were developed through an inductive iterative process, with disagreements resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Participants

A total sample of 20 women with ED were consecutively admitted at specialized long-standing ED residential Unit at Fundación Hospitalarias-University Hospital Bellvitge, located in Martorell (Barcelona, Spain), a public supra-sectoral facility operating within the Catalan universal healthcare system (Figure S2). Of these, 18 participants completed the intervention and were assessed pre- and post-treatment. During the study period, two patients discontinued treatment: one due to a physical complication requiring transfer to an acute medical setting and the other due to a serious family situation.

The participants included in this study were diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) (APA, 2013) [38], by means of a semi-structured face-to-face interview. Participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years at the time of assessment; (2) history of at least three previous comprehensive treatment attempts during adulthood without sustained clinical or functional improvement; and (3) long-standing ED with a minimum duration of 10 years.

The sample in this study was mainly represented by AN (restrictive subtype, 50%; binge-purging subtype (44%)] and OSFED-AN (6%), all of them with previous history of AN. Patients with BN or BED were not included due to program criteria focusing on low-weight and severe chronicity; however, these groups are considered in the broader service planning.

All patients were consecutively referred to by specialized ED Units in Catalonia. This inclusion pattern reflects the clinical focus of the unit and may limit generalizability to other ED populations. However, it ensures a clinically homogeneous sample of individuals with severe and long-standing illness requiring intensive residential care.

The social position index was obtained using the Hollingshead’s algorithm [40]. Socio-demographic information including sex, civil status, education, employment, socioeconomic status, as well as clinical data regarding ED, and psychopathological symptoms were assessed by experienced psychologists in the field, by semi-structured face-to-face clinical interview based on the DSM-5 criteria [38].

Assessment

We developed a comprehensive battery of assessments to evaluate ED symptoms, general psychopathology, core symptomatology, and personality traits. The battery comprised two main components: (1) ED-related clinical and psychopathological variables, and (2) core non-ED related symptomatology, as well as qualitative and satisfaction measures. Instruments were selected for prior validation in ED populations, sensitivity to clinical change, and alignment with functional and recovery-oriented outcomes. They are also commonly used in specialist ED services, including our own unit, and are routinely applied in clinical practice to assess multidimensional presentation and outcomes in long-standing EDs.

ED-related clinical and psychopathological variables

Anthropometric parameters and body composition

Both height and weight were measured by trained personnel and used to determine the BMI, calculated as weight (Kg)/[height(m)]2. Body composition was determined using the bioimpedance analysis, a practical, inexpensive, and non-invasive method, utilizing a Multifrequency TANITA MC-80 body impedance device (TANITA, Japan). Values of total fat, non-fat, muscle and bone mass, as body water percentage was analyzed.

Eating Disorder Inventory − 2 (EDI-2) [39]: It consists of 91 self-report questions, looking at psychological and behavioral characteristics on a 6-point Likert scale in relation to eating disorders. The inventory has 11 subscales: drive for thinness, body dissatisfaction, bulimia, ineffectiveness, perfectionism, interpersonal distrust, interoceptive awareness, maturity fears, asceticism, impulse regulation, and social insecurity. It has been validated in the Spanish population [40].

Symptom Checklist-90 Items-Revised (SCL-90R) [41]: This is a self-report questionnaire consisting of 90 items which examine distress and psychopathology. This checklist has 9 primary dimensions: somatization, obsessive-compulsive, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety, paranoid ideation, and psychoticism. Additionally, it has three global indices: Global Severity Index (GSI), Positive Symptom Total (PST), and Positive Symptom Distress Index (PSDI). Moreover, it has been validated in the general adult Spanish population [42].

Lifetime suicidal ideation, suicide attempts and non-suicidal self-injury behaviours

The prevalence of lifetime suicidal ideation, the history of suicide attempts and NSSI were assessed during a face-to-face structured clinical interview, in which participants were asked about past, recent, and present situation, as described previously [43];

Evaluation of ICD and substance abuse/dependence

Lifetime ICDs and alcohol and drug abuse/dependence were assessed with face-to-face interview, according to DSM-5 criteria, by clinical psychologist and psychiatrist of the Unit [38].

Core non-ED related symptomatology and other variables

Temperament and Character Inventory-Revised (TCI-R) [44]: This questionnaire consists of 240 self-report items where there is a Likert scale from 1 to 5. It has 4 temperament scales (novelty seeking, harm avoidance, reward dependence, persistence) and three-character scales (cooperativeness, self-directedness and self-transcendence) and has been validated in the adult Spanish general population) [45].

The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS)

The DERS was developed to assess difficulties in ER [46], through 36 items rated in a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never applies to me) to 5 (almost always applies to me). This self-report questionnaire considers six dimensions of ER – lack of adaptive ER strategies (strategies); nonacceptance of emotional states (nonacceptance); lack of awareness of emotional states (awareness); impulsivity (impulse); absence of goal‐directed behaviors (goals); and lack of clarity of emotional states (clarity). In this study, the Spanish validated version was used [47].

The UPPS-P Impulsivity Scale [48] is a 59-item scale that assesses impulsive behavior on 5 different scales: sensation seeking, lack of premeditation, lack of perseverance, negative and positive urgency. Positive urgency has been included more recently. All items are rated on a 4-point scale from 1 (strongly agree) to 4 (strongly disagree). The UPPS-P has satisfactory psychometric properties in terms of both convergent and discriminative validity. The Spanish version of the UPPS-P scale was obtained by a back-translation process, and its Spanish adaptation shows adequate psychometric properties [49].

The 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) is a widely validated, generic instrument for assessing health-related quality of life (HRQoL), across eight domains of interest: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, bodily pain, general health perceptions, vitality, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health [50]. The SF-36 has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.80) and construct validity. The Spanish version has been extensively validated and shows excellent reliability (α > 0.70 in 96% of evaluations), good discriminative capacity, and responsiveness to clinical change [51]. In populations with ED, the SF-36 has proven to be effective in capturing the multidimensional impact of these conditions on physical and psychological well-being [52].

Quantitative Treatment Satisfaction Scale (CRES-4)

The Consumer Reports Effectiveness Scale (CRES-4) is a concise, four-item instrument designed to assess patients’ satisfaction with psychological treatment and their perceived therapeutic benefit. The Spanish adaptation, developed and validated by Feixas et al. [53], demonstrated robust psychometric properties, including high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.80) and strong construct validity through correlations with established satisfaction and outcome measures. The scale supports a unidimensional structure, capturing a coherent construct of perceived treatment effectiveness. It captures patients’ global satisfaction with treatment, perceived therapeutic alliance, and subjective benefit derived from the intervention.

Qualitative patient’s treatment feedback measure

As a result of focus group meetings with patients during the therapeutic accompaniment process, and before their discharge, recurring themes and experiences began to emerge spontaneously. These conversations revealed particularly significant aspects related to the impact of the disorder, the treatment process, the support received, and the personal transformations experienced during their stay at the center.

Subsequently, a preliminary coding of the emerging content was carried out, identifying expressions, emotions, and key concepts shared by several patients. These elements were grouped into major thematic axes, prioritizing those that the patients themselves expressed as most relevant to their process of change. Based on this analysis, nine open-ended questions were formulated with the aim of exploring their overall experience, the insights gained, and their evaluation of the support received. Subjective evolution of illness and treatment (items 1–2); Insight and individual/family responsibility and awareness (items 3–4–5); Useful techniques used (items 6–7); and the usefulness of the therapeutic setting (items 8–9) (Table 1).

Treatment model

Established in January 2024 by the Catalan Department of Health, this supra-sectoral tertiary residential care unit, serves as a specialized resource, encompassing a total of eight ED Units, across Catalonia (population approximately 8 million inhabitants). Integrated into the universal public healthcare system, access to the unit is regulated, but free of charge.

The residential program was developed using co-design principles, incorporating input from clinicians, patients with long-standing EDs, and family members to ensure the program reflects patient-centered priorities. The program was informed by a review of existing literature on long-standing EDs, including research on recovery-oriented models, harm-reduction approaches, and enhanced CBT interventions (3,8,19,32–37). Lessons from international residential and rehabilitation programs for severe and enduring EDs were incorporated, considering program length, step-care integration, and multi-disciplinary collaboration [29, 36]. The unit operates within a public health system framework and is connected to the broader ED care network in Catalonia, including seven adult ED units, which facilitates referrals, continuity of care, and communication with step-down and community-based services, consistent with recommendations for integrated step-care models in long-standing EDs proposed by Wonderlich et al. [5].

Prior to admission, cases are coordinated with the referring territorial units, which provide clinical reports and referral information. Eligible patients attend an assessment visit with the unit coordinator before admission. Discharge is planned in advance to ensure coordination with the reference ED Units and to agree on the most appropriate follow-up resource. All group interventions are delivered exclusively within the long-standing ED residential program and are not shared with other inpatient populations. It was designed to address patients with complex care needs and is characterized by the following key features:

Coordinated therapeutic process: The unit ensures a comprehensive and coordinated therapeutic process, encompassing pre-admission assessment, residential treatment, and discharge planning. Clinical decisions are made collaboratively between the care team and the patient, with a flexible approach that accommodates individual needs and clinical progress. Key areas of collaboration include the scheduling of therapeutic outings, advancement through nutritional rehabilitation phases, initiation of autonomous eating behaviors, and the implementation of other personalized interventions aligned with the patient’s evolving clinical status. The unit adopts a non-coercive, holistic treatment philosophy, wherein nutritional and weight-related goals are integrated into a broader biopsychosocial recovery framework. The multidisciplinary team comprises a director, clinical coordinator, psychologists, psychiatrists, nursing staff, auxiliary nursing, a social worker, occupational therapist, internal medicine physician, physiotherapists, and a dietitian. It helps in the context of integration of knowledge, methods, and perspectives from multiple disciplines to better understand, prevent, and treat these complex conditions. Psychotherapeutic interventions integrate evidence-based cognitive-behavioral and systemic approaches, complemented by third-wave CBT frameworks. Standard CBT strategies are used to address ED cognitions, behavioral patterns, and relapse prevention, while systemic family therapy supports interpersonal functioning and family dynamics. Third-wave interventions are primarily informed by Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)-based skills training, mindfulness-based practices, and compassion-focused strategies. These components are operationalized through individual sessions and structured group modules aimed at enhancing psychological flexibility, reducing experiential avoidance, improving emotion regulation and distress tolerance, and fostering values-based behavioral change. Mindfulness and compassion-based exercises are used to address chronic shame, self-criticism, and demoralization frequently observed in long-standing ED presentations. These modalities are complemented by psychoeducational workshops, therapeutic leisure activities, and functional skills training led by various team members. These relational processes are embedded across the treatment pathway, including group work, and multidisciplinary coordination, ensuring continuity of therapeutic alliance throughout the program.

Although the unit is described as a residential program, it differs from traditional short-term inpatient models. Residential care refers here to medium-term, structured, home-like accommodations where patients live on-site with individualized schedules, therapeutic engagement throughout the day, and a focus on autonomy and skill development, rather than solely acute symptom management. The program integrates medical stabilization, nutritional support, psychotherapy, group activities, and family involvement, emphasizing flexibility, patient choice, and recovery-oriented goals [29, 36].

The intervention program is structured into three distinct phases, as shown in Table 2. Progression through nutritional rehabilitation is guided by a structured protocol, informed by clinical assessments of medical stability, nutritional status, and patient readiness. The protocol is flexible, allowing individualized pacing from supervised meals to autonomous eating behaviors, and is coordinated with therapeutic and psychosocial goals. Group-based interventions include specialized psychotherapeutic groups focusing on body image, self-esteem, social skills, cognitive restructuring, thematic development, systemic relational therapy, art therapy, and emotional regulation, but also social functioning and normalizing healthy life-styles and well-being.

Compared with standard residential care programs, this specialized integrative unit for long-standing EDs offers longer admissions when clinically indicated, collaborative goal setting, individualized care plans, and integration of both medical stabilization and psychological rehabilitation. It adopts a non-coercive, patient-centered approach, emphasizing voluntary participation, shared decision-making, and harm-reduction principles, which inform all aspects of treatment planning and therapeutic engagement. While typical residential programs often focus on short-term symptom reduction and weight restoration, our approach prioritizes quality of life, functional improvement, and patient-centered outcomes [18, 20, 32].

Phases of the residential treatment

Admission criteria

Voluntary engagement

Admission is contingent upon the patient’s voluntary participation in treatment, in line with a patient-centered and non-coercive care model.

Somatic and nutritional stability

Patients must present with sufficient somatic stability to participate safely in a residential (non-acute) treatment setting. As part of this criterion, individuals with a BMI below 14 kg/m² are initially managed in an acute care setting until medical stabilisation is achieved prior to transfer. Eligibility is determined based on overall clinical stability rather than BMI alone, and involves coordination with the referring ED service to ensure continuity of care.

Age requirement

Patients must be adults (≥ 18 years).

Treatment history

A minimum of three prior completed treatment attempts in an adult ED services without sustained clinical or functional improvement is generally is required.

Duration of ED

A duration of illness of at least 10 years is typically expected, however, some flexibility may be applied based on clinical judgment.

Admission procedure

Referral network

Patients must be linked to a reference ED Unit within Catalonia. If not already connected, we will facilitate contact with one of the seven designated adult ED Units in the region. This ensures continuity of care and a smooth transition post-discharge;

Motivational assessment

Patients lacking clear motivation may be invited for an initial visit to familiarize themselves with the program structure and therapeutic milestones;

Individualized treatment planning

A personalized therapeutic plan will be developed collaboratively with the referring team and the patient, ensuring alignment with long-term recovery goals.

Discharge criteria

Discharge decisions are based not solely on physical parameters such as eating patterns and weight restoration, but on broader clinical and psychosocial improvements. These are evaluated in relation to the individualized therapeutic goals established at admission, including functional, psychological, relational, and community reintegration targets. Discharge is determined through a collaborative process involving the patient, the multidisciplinary team, and the referring service, considering achievement of goals, clinical stability, and readiness for transition to step-down care.

Ethics

According to the Declaration of Helsinki, the present study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee (CEIC) of the Fundación Hospitalarias-Martorell (Ref. PR-2024-06). Furthermore, written and informed consent was obtained from all participants in this study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using R Statistical Software (v4.5.1; R Core Team 2025) [54]. First, descriptive indexes were obtained for sociodemographic and clinical variables, represented as the mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, and as the count and percentage for categorical variables. Next, paired Wilcoxon signed rank exact test was used to calculate the median difference between pre-treatment and post-treatment psychometric variables and BMI, alongside Cohen’s d statistic for mean differences. To infer deeper in the change of quality of life of the patients, Pearson’s correlation between pre-post difference of the SF-36 total score and other psychometric scales was calculated. Lastly, the mean scores of the CRES-4 questionnaire were calculated and rescaled to a common scale of 0-100.

Results

Descriptive baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the sample

Baseline information can be consulted in Table 3, in summary patients with long-standing ED treated in our Unit consisted exclusively of females, with a mean age of 43 years (SD = 9). Most participants were either single or married, had attained secondary or higher education, and were unemployed. The majority belonged to a medium-high social position index.

Clinically, patients had experienced an average illness duration of 27 years (SD = 10), with a mean of 8 previous failed treatment attempts (SD = 6.7). The average BMI was 16.52 (SD = 2.14), indicating a generally underweight profile.

Psychopathological features were notably severe, with high prevalence of self-harm and suicidal behaviors: 67.2% reported NSSI, 50% had attempted suicide, and almost all patients endorsed suicidal ideation (83%). Among impulsive comorbid behaviors, compulsive buying (17%) and kleptomania (17%) were the most frequently reported. Dual pathology involving substance use disorders (SUDs) was present in 28% of cases for alcohol use/dependence and 17% for drug use/dependence. Psychopathology and personality traits

Table 3 and Table S2 (supplementary information) shows the baseline and post-treatment psychopathology traits of patients with long-lasting EDs. Patients exhibited high levels of general psychopathology and ED-specific psychopathology, even scoring above clinical normative values for some SCL-90-R, DERS, UPPS-P and EDI-2 dimensions.

Table S3 (supplementary information) summarizes descriptive personality and impulsivity traits in patients. In general, patients present pronounced personality profiles, characterized specifically by high harm avoidance, low reward dependence, high persistence and low self-directedness. This personality scoring was of clinical significance.

Change after the treatment in BMI and quality of life

At the beginning of treatment, the average BMI of participants was 16.52 kg/m² (SD 2.14). By the end of the intervention, this increased to 17.38 kg/m² (SD 1.90), representing a statistically significant improvement in body weight following therapy (p = 0.00647).

Concerning Quality of Life (see Fig. 1) when comparing pre-treatment and post-treatment scores across the SF-36 subscales, the following domains showed statistically significant improvements (p < 0.05): Physical Function, Role-Physical, Role-Emotional, Vitality, Mental Health, Social Function, General Health and Total Score. These results suggest that the intervention had a meaningful impact on participants’ perceived energy levels, psychological well-being, and social engagement. The overall improvement in the total SF-36 score further supports the positive effect of the treatment on quality of life.

Quantitative patient’s satisfaction with the therapy

For the quantitative patients’ satisfaction analysis, participants reported high levels across several domains. On a scale from 0 to 100, the average satisfaction with the treatment process was 85, perceived resolution of the problem was rated 91.2, and emotional change received a slightly lower score of 79.7. The overall general satisfaction score was 85.3.

Quality of life correlations with other variables

Correlation analyses reveal an inversely proportional relationship between the change in total SF-36 scores and variations in the clinical scales evaluated (GSI, DERS, and EDI-2). As shown by the regression lines, a greater reduction in general symptomatology (GSI) (Fig. 2), emotion regulation difficulties (Fig. 1S), and ED traits (Fig. 1S) is significantly associated with an increase in health-related quality of life (SF-36). These results suggest that improvements in patients’ clinical well-being have a direct and positive impact on their global quality of life.

Qualitative analysis of patient’s feedback

The qualitative analysis included seven participants. Their mean age was 39.9 years (range: 24–51). Diagnoses included AN-R (n = 3), AN-BP (n = 2), AN-P (n = 1), and OSFED-AN (n = 1). Mean BMI at admission was 17.2 kg/m² (range: 13.2–19.1), and mean BMI at discharge was 17.8 kg/m² (range: 15.5–19.6). The mean length of treatment was 121.9 days (range: 98–140). The mean duration of illness was 24.0 years (range: 13–36), and participants reported a mean of 10.0 previous failed treatments (range: 3–17). Regarding comorbidity, three participants presented major depressive disorder, one borderline personality disorder, one impulse control disorder, and two reported no comorbidity.

The analysis of the interviews of seven patients out of the 18 participants, enabled the identification of several thematic axes that provide a transversal view of the participants’ experiences throughout their therapeutic process. These themes illustrate the functional role that the ED played in their lives, as well as the changes achieved during treatment in areas such as: autonomy and self-efficacy, family dynamics, identity, group belonging, the acquisition of therapeutic tools, and the ongoing coexistence with the illness (see Table S4 Supplementary information).

Discussion

This preliminary pilot study aimed to introduce and evaluate a novel residential treatment model specifically tailored for individuals with long-standing EDs, as implemented within a specialized clinical unit. The program was designed to address the complex and enduring nature of these disorders by integrating multidisciplinary therapeutic approaches in a structured residential setting. In addition to detailing the treatment framework, we characterized the psychopathological profiles, clinical features, and personality traits of patients admitted to the program, thereby providing a comprehensive understanding of the target population. Finally, we assessed treatment outcomes and patient satisfaction using a combination of quantitative measures and qualitative feedback, offering valuable insights into the effectiveness and acceptability of this innovative intervention.

Clinical variables and descriptives

The demographic and clinical characteristics of our sample underscore the psychopathological complexity and chronicity inherent in long-standing ED [55]. Consistent with prior research, all participants were female, with a mean age of 43 years, reflecting the tendency for chronic EDs to persist into middle adulthood. The average illness duration of 27 years and the high number of previous treatment attempts (mean = 8), as described previously in the literature [1], highlight the enduring nature of this disorder and the frequent inadequacy of conventional treatment approaches for this population.

The predominance of unemployment (94%) and medium-high social status (90%) suggests that while socioeconomic disadvantage may not be a universal feature, functional impairment is profound, as suggested in previous studies [9, 33].

Eating and general psychopathology

Clinically, the sample presented with severe psychopathology, as evidenced by a low mean BMI (16.52), high rates of non-suicidal self-injury (67.2%), suicide attempts (50%), and universal suicidal ideation. These findings align with existing literature indicating that individuals with long-standing EDs are at significantly elevated risk for suicidality, confirming AN having the highest mortality rate among psychiatric disorders, largely due to suicide [56]. The presence of impulsive behaviors such as compulsive buying and kleptomania, along with dual diagnoses involving substance use disorders (28% alcohol, 17% drugs), further complicates the clinical picture [57, 58]. Comorbid substance use is known to exacerbate the severity of ED and worsen both prognosis and therapeutic outcomes [10].

The baseline psychopathological and personality profiles observed in our sample of patients with long-standing ED reflect a clinically severe and complex presentation. Consistent with previous findings in long-standing ED populations [31], patients scored within clinically significant ranges across all dimensions of the SCL-90-R, DERS, and EDI-2, indicating pervasive emotional dysregulation, elevated general psychopathology, and entrenched ED-specific symptomatology [59], and high comorbidity specially with PD, MDD, ASD, PTSD and SUD [9, 15, 16]. These results underscore the multifaceted nature of long-standing EDs, where affective instability and interpersonal difficulties often coexist with rigid cognitive patterns and maladaptive coping strategies.

Interestingly, post-treatment results revealed a significant increase in BMI, despite therapeutic goals being primarily oriented toward improving functional capacity and subjective well-being rather than solely targeting weight restoration. Comparable outcomes have been reported in other studies involving individuals with SE-AN including the only randomized controlled trial evaluating psychological treatment in this population (3) and evidence summarized in the Cochrane review (19). Other observational and review studies have similarly highlighted the benefits of flexible, non-symptom-centric therapeutic approaches, based on eating-weight recovery, were employed [8, 25, 36].

Personality traits

The personality, and impulsivity traits further delineate a distinctive psychological profile. High harm avoidance and low self-directedness are hallmark features of individuals with anorexia nervosa and other chronic EDs, often associated with heightened anxiety sensitivity, perfectionism, and diminished autonomy [60, 61]. Low reward dependence and high persistence may reflect a tendency toward social withdrawal and compulsive goal pursuit, respectively—traits that can reinforce the persistence of disordered behaviors despite negative consequences. These findings align with the temperament-based models of EDs, which suggest that certain personality configurations may predispose individuals to chronicity and patients with complex care needs [62].

Taken together, the elevated psychopathology and pronounced personality traits observed in our cohort highlight the necessity of individualized, long-term therapeutic approaches that integrate emotion regulation training, personality-informed interventions, quality of life and functionality, and motivational enhancement strategies. Such approaches may be particularly beneficial in residential settings, where the intensity and continuity of care can be tailored to address these enduring psychological patterns, more only centering in weight and eating patterns.

Patient’s satisfaction and quality of life

At the end of the therapeutic intervention, patient satisfaction was assessed using the CRES-4. The results revealed high satisfaction levels across all domains These findings suggest that participants perceived the therapy as highly effective, particularly in addressing core issues related to their ED and functionality. This aligns with previous findings [53, 63], who emphasized the scale’s sensitivity to perceived therapeutic effectiveness and its utility in routine outcome monitoring. Moreover, several studies [31, 64] have similarly highlighted the importance of patient-reported satisfaction and social support in predicting long-term engagement and recovery. The elevated score in the “Solution to the Problem” domain further underscores the intervention’s relevance and impact, while the overall positive ratings support its acceptability and potential to foster therapeutic engagement in this population. Improvements in social functioning and quality of life may have been facilitated, at least in part, by the structured and socially enriched residential environment (i.e., increased daily interpersonal contact and group participation). Future studies should include post-discharge follow-up to determine whether these gains are sustained in community settings.

The present study examined changes in health-related quality of life among individuals with long-standing EDs, following a residential therapeutic intervention. Statistically significant improvements were observed across several key domains of the SF-36, including Vitality, Mental Health, Social Functioning, and the Total Score. Notably, the Social Functioning domain demonstrated the most substantial post-intervention gains reflecting enhanced interpersonal engagement and a marked reduction in social withdrawal, as reported previously [65].

This finding is particularly salient given the inherently isolating nature of chronic EDs and the central role of social reintegration in sustained recovery. Previous literature has underscored the importance of fostering social connectedness as a means to enhance self-worth and self-esteem in this population. The observed improvements in social functioning thus reinforce the therapeutic value of interventions that prioritize relational and psychosocial dimensions of care.

Qualitative analysis of the patient’s feedback

The qualitative section of our study explored the lived experiences of seven individuals, out of the 18 undergoing treatment for long-standing ED, revealing nuanced insights into the illness’s functional role and the therapeutic process. Participants retrospectively described their ED as adaptive mechanisms—serving as survival strategies, internal alert systems, and non-verbal expressions of psychological distress. Treatment facilitated meaningful shifts in autonomy, identity reconstruction, and family dynamics, with patients reporting increased self-awareness, emotional regulation, and engagement in personally fulfilling activities. The acquisition of therapeutic tools, such as emotional regulation strategies and internal coping strategies, was perceived as instrumental in fostering resilience and personal growth. Notably, the therapeutic setting’s emphasis on person-centered care, co-responsible goal setting and therapeutic engagement, group support, and coexistence with the illness—rather than symptom-focused interventions—was identified as a key strength. These findings underscore the importance of integrative, individualized approaches that prioritize functional recovery and psychosocial well-being in the treatment of long-standing ED, as suggested by other authors [1, 5, 17], without coercive measures [66].

Clinical implications

These data reinforce the need for specialized, integrative treatment models that address both the core ED pathology and the associated psychiatric comorbidities. Importantly, our findings highlight the relevance of combining skills-based interventions with relational and trauma-informed therapeutic processes, with particular emphasis on therapeutic alliance in this complex clinical population. The high prevalence of self-harm and suicidality necessitates vigilant risk assessment and crisis management protocols within residential settings. Moreover, the co-occurrence of impulsive and addictive behaviors supports the need for integrated approaches drawing on both ED and addiction frameworks. Our findings support the growing consensus that long-standing EDs require a shift from symptom eradication to a broader focus on harm reduction, quality of life, and sustained engagement in care [1, 5, 36].

Recent literature has also highlighted that the repetition of previously ineffective treatment approaches in long-standing ED may not be neutral, and in some cases may contribute to clinical stagnation or prolonged illness trajectories [33]. This underscores the importance of re-defining treatment approaches from the outset rather than sequentially extending non-effective interventions. Consistent with recovery-oriented principles across ED (RANZCP, 2014), our model seeks to avoid repetition of standard approaches and instead emphasizes individualized, flexible and function-oriented care.

Future research should explore the sustainability of these improvements over time and investigate which specific components of the intervention are most strongly associated with quality-of-life gains. Additionally, integrating qualitative assessments could provide deeper insight into the lived experiences of individuals undergoing treatment for SE-AN. Longitudinal follow-up studies and replication in other clinical settings should also be conducted to confirm the durability and generalizability of these findings.

Limitations and strengths

Although this observational study has significant limitations due to the small sample size analyzed, it is the first to evaluate a specific treatment exclusively for patients with long-standing EDs. As a preliminary pilot study, it offers new insights and considerations for future programs, stemming from a mixed-methods design (qualitative and quantitative). While the sample may introduce notable selection bias, it is unique due to its high clinical and therapeutic complexity. The evaluation approach provides a comprehensive understanding of these patients, focusing not only on eating patterns and BMI, but also on a thorough assessment of core ED symptomatology. As feedback was collected through interviews, responses may have been influenced by social desirability or the therapeutic relationship. This underscores the importance of understanding long-standing EDs as a crucial step toward developing effective future treatments. Also, further research could include formal qualitative content analysis of patient goals to better capture shared and heterogeneous priorities within this population. In addition, certain sociodemographic and contextual variables (e.g., parental status and living situation) were not systematically collected, which limits the interpretation of social functioning and quality-of-life findings. Future longitudinal studies with larger samples will be needed to examine patient retention and medium-term outcomes and to further evaluate the durability of treatment effects over time.

Conclusions

This study underscores the profound clinical severity, chronicity, and functional impairment associated with long-standing EDs, particularly SE-AN. The sample demonstrated extensive psychopathology, high psychiatric comorbidity, and maladaptive personality traits, highlighting the limitations of conventional treatment approaches. Comprehensive assessment of core ED symptomatology is essential for understanding the complexity of long-standing EDs and for informing the development of more effective interventions. One of the most striking findings was the high rate of unemployment among participants, despite their high levels of education and qualifications, reflecting the pervasive impact of the disorder on functional outcomes. Notably, the residential intervention yielded significant improvements in BMI, quality of life, and patient-reported satisfaction, despite its primary focus on functionality and well-being rather than symptom-centric goals. These results support the value of individualized, integrative therapeutic models that emphasize emotional regulation, personality-informed care, and psychosocial rehabilitation. Such approaches offer a promising pathway for addressing the complex and unmet needs of this underserved population. It should be noted that this study represents a preliminary pilot assessment of a newly implemented residential program, and findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.
