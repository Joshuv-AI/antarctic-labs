Preoperative hyperchloraemia and intraoperative metabolic acidosis in infants undergoing anorectal pull-through for Hirschsprung’s disease: A retrospective observational study
Authors: Esther E. H. Chan, Usha Shenoy, Satish K. R. Challa, Rambha Rai
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Infants are particularly prone to fluid and electrolyte imbalance due to their limited physiological reserve. Bowel preparation, including rectal washouts using saline-based solutions, is often performed before elective intestinal surgery. This may predispose children to hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis.[1] Unrecognised and therefore uncorrected preoperative metabolic derangements at induction may influence the perioperative course of the child. It can negatively impact haemodynamic stability, intraoperative fluid requirements, ventilation strategies, transfusion thresholds, and postoperative disposition.[2] We observed episodes of intraoperative metabolic acidosis and unanticipated postoperative children’s intensive care unit (CICU) admission in children undergoing anorectal pull-through (ARP) for Hirschsprung’s disease. This was noted even in children with a reassuring preoperative assessment. This prompted a clinical study to explore the prevalence of preoperative electrolyte derangements and evaluate associations with perioperative outcomes.

METHODS

This retrospective observational study was conducted as a part of broad-based longitudinal analyses of childhood surgical conditions at a tertiary paediatric hospital after institutional ethics committee approval (ECOS Ref: 2019-2040/08 July 2025). Patients undergoing elective ARP for histologically confirmed Hirschsprung’s disease between August 2019 and August 2024 were included. Data were collected from August 2025 to October 2025. Patients undergoing pull-through procedures for non-Hirschsprung pathology were excluded. As this was a retrospective clinical study, the sample size was determined by the total number of eligible patients undergoing elective ARP over the last 5 years (2019 to 2025).[3]

The following operational definitions were used: hyperchloraemia, serum chloride ≥109 mmol/L[4] abnormal bicarbonate, <14 mmol/L or >22 mmol/L,[5] intraoperative metabolic acidosis, base excess (BE) <−6 intraoperatively,[6] and high intraoperative crystalloid requirement, >8 ml/kg/h.[7] Venous blood results obtained after bowel preparation and before anaesthetic induction were reviewed. Intraoperative anaesthetic records were examined for acid–base status, fluid administration, transfusion, vasopressor use, and duration of surgery. The consulting anaesthesiologist determined the type/volume of fluid and sampling frequency. Postoperative disposition, including CICU admission, was noted.

Data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics. Continuous variables were summarised as means ± standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges. Independent-Samples T-Test or Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons. Categorical variables were analysed using Pearson’s Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 38 patients underwent ARP for Hirschsprung’s disease during the study period. Two patients were excluded as they did not undergo bowel washout before definitive surgery (one had oral bowel preparation with polyethylene glycol, and one had a pre-existing diverting ileostomy). Thirty-six patients were included [Supplementary File: Figure 1]. The median age was 4.52 months, and the mean age was 8.8 months (range 1.4–51.9 months, SD 10.61); the mean weight was 6.55 kg, with 71% males and 92% born at term. All underwent elective ARP [Supplementary File: Table 1].

Preoperative dyselectrolytaemia was identified in 56.1% (n = 22) of patients. The most common electrolyte abnormality was hyperchloraemia. It was observed in 33% (n = 13) of the cohort. We also noted that an inverse correlation existed between preoperative chloride and bicarbonate (Pearson’s r = -0.584, P < 0.001). 10.5% of cases had hyperkalaemia (>5.5 mmol/L). This often coexisted with hyperchloraemia.

Rectal washouts using saline-based solutions were performed in all patients. Of the 36 cases analysed, 47.3% (n = 18) received 0.45% saline and 47.3% (n = 18) received 0.9% normal saline. The washout count was equal in both groups, with a median of 4 [interquartile range (IQR) 2–6 vs 3–7; P = 0.690]. The mean washout volumes were 144 mL (range 69–394 mL) versus 165 mL (range 65–523 mL) (P = 0.501).

Intraoperative metabolic acidosis (BE <−6) occurred in 43.6% (n = 17). Crystalloid administration >8 ml/kg/h occurred in >50%, blood transfusion in 51.3%, vasopressor use in 12.8%, and unplanned CICU admission in 15.4%; 2/6 children required overnight ventilation. Plasma-Lyte was the primary fluid. A positive correlation was detected between postoperative chloride (Cl) and bicarbonate (HCO3) (r = 0.537, P = 0.017).

Children with longer surgical durations [5.82 h (IQR 5.03–7.50) vs 4.67 h (IQR 4.25–5.82), P = 0.043] were more likely to have intraoperative acidosis. Higher transfusion rates (66.7% vs 20%, P = 0.007) were also detected in acidotic children. Preoperative bicarbonate showed a trend towards significance (P = 0.052). Preoperative chloride (P = 0.476), excess intravenous fluids (P = 0.202), and vasopressor use (P = 0.308) were not significantly associated with acidosis. Surgical duration (P = 0.028) and transfusion (P = 0.026) were associated with CICU admission. Lactate and blood loss were inconsistently recorded. The temperature remained between 36 and 37°C, multimodal analgesia was used, and no neuraxial techniques were performed [Tables 1 and 2].

DISCUSSION

This study explores perioperative biochemical aberrations in children undergoing elective ARP. It reveals a high prevalence of preoperative dyselectrolytaemia (56.1%) and intraoperative metabolic acidosis (43.6%). Hyperchloraemia was the most frequent preoperative derangement (33%). The inverse correlation between preoperative chloride and bicarbonate (r = -0.584, P < 0.001) [Figure 1, Supplementary File] is characteristic of compensated hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis.[18] The acidaemia is likely to have caused the hyperkalaemia (10.5%) due to transcellular shift.[5]

Preoperative bowel preparation may provide a mechanism for this “biochemical priming”. In our cohort, 94.6% of infants received large-volume saline-based rectal washouts. The absence of statistical differences between saline groups suggests cumulative chloride load may be the trigger. Rectal and colonic mucosa act as semipermeable membranes. They passively absorb chloride while secreting bicarbonate. Repeated high-chloride irrigation may overwhelm immature renal clearance. This induces non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis (NAGMA).[38] Consequently, these children may have depleted buffering reserve at the beginning of the surgery. This will increase their vulnerability to intraoperative stressors.[9]

Intraoperative metabolic decompensation (BE <-6) was multifactorial in the patients included in the present study. It was associated with surgical duration (P = 0.043) and blood transfusion (P = 0.007). Nevertheless, longer procedures exacerbate insensible losses, cold stress, and occult tissue hypoperfusion.[6] Laparoscopic insufflation introduces a respiratory acid load through peritoneal carbon dioxide absorption. In infants, this is an added stress, particularly if the bicarbonate buffer reserve is depleted. Pneumoperitoneum reduces the splanchnic perfusion and renal filtration. If prolonged, this can further impair metabolic compensation. The citrate anticoagulant and acidic nature of stored blood products introduce an additional acid load. These mechanisms, when combined, may shift a compensated state into decompensated intraoperative acidosis.[81011]

The primary intraoperative fluid used in the patients in our study was Plasma-Lyte. Over half the children required >8 ml/kg/h of balanced crystalloids. Although balanced fluids reduce the risk of hyperchloraemia compared with 0.9% saline, high-volume administration during prolonged surgery still requires acid–base monitoring.[10] Infants with longer procedures received significantly less fluid per hour (P = 0.012). This probably reflects a restrictive strategy to avoid tissue oedema and fluid creep.[10] Lack of association between vasopressor use and surgical duration suggests that haemodynamic compensation may mask occult hypoperfusion in prolonged cases.[59]

Longer surgical duration was also associated with unplanned CICU admission in the current study (P = 0.028). Paediatric literature shows that procedures exceeding 5 h increase postoperative respiratory fatigue and intensive care requirements.[1011] Although intraoperative acidosis itself did not significantly correlate with CICU admission (P = 0.186), the near-significant trend for preoperative bicarbonate (P = 0.052) suggests depleted physiological reserve may influence postoperative outcomes.[12]

Although mechanical bowel preparation remains debated within enhanced recovery frameworks,[8] balanced salt solutions should be preferred during bowel preparation. To avoid preoperative metabolic buffer depletion, practice should shift from high-chloride saline to balanced bowel washouts.[137] Hyperventilation during laparoscopy to manage the carbon dioxide load will help mechanically maintain respiratory compensation without precipitating severe uncompensated acidaemia.[3] These children may have a baseline compensated partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) of 25–30 mmHg. Hyperventilation must be balanced against the risk of hypothermia and cerebral vasoconstriction.

The present study addresses a blind spot in paediatric anaesthesia where clinically stable infants may harbour significant biochemical risks.[12] Use of predefined thresholds (BE <-6) supports reproducibility. The limitations include those inherent to a retrospective study. Other drawbacks are the single-centre design, small sample size (n = 36), and inability to control for confounders. The proposed dual-hit mechanism lacks prospective validation. Future multicentre randomised controlled trials comparing buffered washout solutions with standard saline are required to determine whether preoperative hyperchloraemia can be mitigated.

CONCLUSION

This retrospective study highlights under-recognised preoperative biochemical abnormalities in infants undergoing surgery for Hirschsprung’s disease. Preoperative hyperchloraemia may act as a physiological “first hit”, compromising buffering capacity before surgical stress, pneumoperitoneum, and haemorrhage. These findings suggest clinicians maintain a high index of suspicion following bowel preparation and consider balanced salt solutions to preserve intraoperative homeostasis.
