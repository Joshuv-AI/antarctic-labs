The longitudinal relationships between parent–child alienation and depression in adolescents: insights of network analysis
Authors: Qin Dai, Ying He, Xiaoxiao Sun, Zongpei Dai, Wenxiang Jian, Xiaomei Ren, Qiaoning Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent depression is a major health issue requiring precise interventions. While Network theory guides symptom-targeted interventions, longitudinal studies in community adolescents remain scarce. Ecological Systems Theory indicates parent–child alienation (PA) as a depression disruptor, but how PA influences depressive networks is unknown. This study aims to bridge this gap by examining symptom-level interactions between PA and depression in community adolescents through two-wave network analysis.Methods1503 Chinese adolescents (mean age = 13.95 ± 2.05 years; 49.63% female) were surveyed at two time points one year apart [baseline (T1), 12-months later (T2)]. Analyses included: T1 cross-sectional depression network (n = 1503), T1/T2 bridge networks (n = 1148 each), and T1 → T2 cross-lagged panel network (CLPN; n = 1148).Results(1) Anhedonia had the highest expected influence (EI = 1.03) within the T1 depression network. (2) Anhedonia showed the highest bridge EI (BEIT1 = 0.19; BEIT2 = 0.13) linking PA and depression. (3) CLPN revealed bidirectional PA-depression prediction, with stronger prediction effect of depression on subsequent PA than vice versa. Compared with alienation towards father, alienation towards mother predicted subsequent depressive symptoms more strongly. Key paths: “alienation towards mother” predicting subsequent “anhedonia” and “negative mood” (both weight = 0.04); “negative self-esteem” predicting subsequent “alienation towards father (weight = 0.57) /mother (weight = 0.42)”. (4) In CLPN, negative self-esteem was the central symptom (out-EI = 1.52), while anhedonia was the most affected depressive symptom (in-EI = 0.39).ConclusionsThis study confirms anhedonia as central symptom within depression network and a core bridge linking PA and depressive symptoms cross-sectionally. Longitudinally, PA and depression bidirectionally predict each other, with depression exerting stronger forward prediction. Furthermore, alienation towards mothers shows stronger prediction on subsequent depressive symptoms. Negative self-esteem functions as an activator and anhedonia as maintainer in CLPN. These findings integrate Network Theory and Ecological Systems Theory to elucidate how PA prompts depression network dynamic and depression self-maintenance pathways. This calls for school-based anhedonia screening for alienated youth and family-focused interventions targeting negative self-esteem and anhedonia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13034-026-01120-6.

Introduction

Adolescent depression represents a major global health concern, affecting approximately 3.7% of adolescents worldwide [1]. Characterized by anhedonia, persistent sadness, fatigue, and impaired concentration, it significantly impairs psychosocial functioning and increases suicide risk [2, 3]. Critically, the effectiveness of current treatments is limited: antidepressants (excepting fluoxetine) lack superiority over placebo in youth [4], whereas psychotherapeutic interventions exhibit non-response rates exceeding 40% [5]. These challenges underscore the urgent need to identify modifiable risk factors underlying the onset and persistence of adolescent depression. However, most researches have focused on distal (e.g., genetics) or static proximal risk factors (e.g., peer bullying) [6, 7], neglecting the proximal, dynamic, and modifiable relational processes, particularly within the context of the parent–child relationship.

Parent–child alienation (PA) represents a proximal, dynamic risk factor for adolescent depression. Drawing on Ecological Systems Theory [8], which focuses on proximal, dynamic relational processes, adolescent development is shaped by reciprocal interactions between individuals and their immediate environment, particularly within the family microsystem. Within this microsystem, the parent–child relationship functions as the foundation for children’s emotional development and stress regulation [9]. When this relationship deteriorates into parent–child alienation (PA), defined as negative feelings such as emotional distancing, possessiveness, and coercive control during parent–child interactions [10], it disrupts the core proximal process. Importantly, PA refers to a sustained negative relational pattern. Previous longitudinal data from rural left-behind children showed that PA predicted depression one-year later [11]. Increasing alienation towards parents at Grade 6 was associated with higher levels of anxiety at Grade 12, and increasing alienation with mothers in high school was associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms at Grade 12 [12]. Parent–child conflict positively related to a sense of alienation towards parents, which then related to more depressive symptoms in adolescents, which persisted from high school to emerging adulthood [13]. Overall, PA fundamentally undermines the adolescents’ ability to perceive parental love and express love toward parents and co-occurring vulnerabilities like low self-esteem, subsequently elevates depression risk [14]. Despite its clinical importance, quantitative studies on PA in adolescent samples remain scarce, and its symptom-level impact on depressive trajectories is rarely examined.

To understand the influential mechanism linking PA to depression, we draw on diathesis–stress models of depression [15], wherein PA functions as an interpersonal stressor that amplifies one’s vulnerability to depression. When PA persists [11], this chronic relational stressor may cumulatively erode the adolescent’s emotional development and socialization [16], and form maladaptive cognitive and affective patterns gradually, thereby increasing risk for depression. Adolescent depression comprises multiple dimensions, as negative self-esteem and anhedonia play core roles in adolescent depression network dynamics [17], we propose that PA may contribute to depression through two critical developmental domains: self-esteem and anhedonia mechanisms.

First, PA may intensify negative self-esteem. Bowlby posits that insecure infant-caregiver bonds establish maladaptive internal working models featuring negative self-perceptions (e.g., “I am not worthy of love”, linking to negative self-esteem) and mistrust of others (e.g., “others are untrustworthy”) [18]. These early attachment experiences establish vulnerabilities to difficulties in self-esteem and relational expectations. Although insecure attachment reflects suboptimal parent–child bond quality, PA represents a more severe relational deficit characterized by impairment in the capacity to love within the parent–child relationship. When alienated children experience rejection, threat of abandonment, or insult from a parent, they internalize these behaviors as evidence confirming beliefs like “I am unworthy of love” [19]. This relational deficit acts as a stressor that reinforces negative self-esteem. This reinforced negative self-esteem persists into adolescence, biasing emotion regulation and social information processing and increasing risk of depression [20].

Beyond self-esteem, PA may be linked to disruptions in reward function (predisposing to anhedonia). Theoretically, based on previous evidence, the formation of parent–child bonds is linked to cerebral reward system [21]. Positive parent–child interactions such as smiles are considered as social reward cues that have been found to activate neural reward pathways, facilitating emotion, cognition, and reward functions in children [9, 22]. Conversely, dysfunctional parent–child relationships such as insecure attachment have been shown to perturb reward processing and increase the risk of depression [23, 24]. Thus, persistent PA may limit adolescents’ perception of these social rewards, diminish their motivation to seek parental love or reduce their happiness from experiencing love. This behavioral pattern aligns with anhedonia, which is characterized by diminished pleasure or impaired ability to pursue rewards [25]. Furthermore, anhedonia is a manifestation of reward dysfunction and a core symptom of depression [26]. Therefore, PA may impair adolescent reward processing (predisposing to anhedonia) and trigger depressive cascades.

However, the centrality of negative self-esteem versus anhedonia in depression varies across populations. Network analysis identified self-hatred as central among Brazilian adolescents [27], while studies in Chinese clinical and community adolescents identified anhedonia as central [28, 29]. The differences may stem from cultural context, methodology (network analysis vs. latent variable model), or sample characteristics (clinical vs. non-clinical). Critically, in Chinese community adolescents, traditional values emphasizing academic performance and collective harmony may compel teenagers to sacrifice personal pleasure to meet parental and societal expectations [30], intensifying the effect of anhedonia. Thus, clarifying whether anhedonia/self-esteem is the central depressive symptom in community adolescents is crucial for identifying PA-targeted intervention priorities (Hypothesis 1).

Although evidence supports a connection between PA and depressive symptoms, however, what symptom connects PA and depression remains unrevealed. According to the Interpersonal Theory of depression [31, 32], these social withdrawal responses increase emotional distance and thereby exacerbate PA. Unlike anhedonia, other depressive symptoms such as negative self-esteem do not directly undermine motivation to establish positive connections [33]. Therefore, anhedonia may uniquely serve as a “bridge” linking PA and depression. However, existing methodologies have not empirically tested the bridging role of anhedonia at the symptom level in adolescents (Hypothesis 2).

Emerging evidence suggests bidirectional links between PA and depression though quantitative research, even remains limited. Existing studies have primarily used cross-sectional designs or retrospective adulthood reports in Western contexts. However, retrospective data cannot capture adolescent dynamics, and Western definition of PA narrowly focuses on one parent coercively alienating the child from the other with divorce background [34], limiting its generalizability. In China, traditional filial piety norms emphasizing parental authority and child obedience force children to suppress their emotions and needs during conflict [35], potentially normalizing PA. Notably, research on PA within China is particularly scarce. Nevertheless, two longitudinal studies suggest a bidirectional relationship between PA and depression. Ebbert et al. found that increasing PA during high-school predicted depression by 12th grade in an affluent White sample of New England [12], while Sun et al. observed a reciprocal PA-depression effects in rural Chinese left-behind children [11]. However, existing research has predominantly examined the correlation between overall PA and total depression scores, offering limited insight into the longitudinal bidirectional dynamics between PA and depression at symptom level (Hypothesis 3).

Prior research mainly relies on latent variable models that conceptualize depression and PA as underlying entities causing observed symptoms [36], obscuring symptoms interactions. While a traditional cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) could test bidirectional relationships between overall PA and total depression scores, it cannot identify which specific depressive symptom drive these longitudinal dynamics or serve as bridge linking PA to depression. Network Theory offers a framework to address symptom-level interaction by conceptualizing mental disorders as dynamic systems of interrelated symptoms[37]. First, network analysis can elucidate depression’s internal structure and identify central symptoms-those with highest centrality indices pivotal for network activation and maintenance. This approach can determine whether anhedonia or negative self-esteem is the central depressive symptom in Chinese non-clinic adolescents (Hypothesis 1). Second, network analysis can detect bridge symptoms connecting symptom communities, potentially generating feedback loops in comorbidities [38]. This can determine whether anhedonia or other depressive symptoms serve as bridges linking PA and depressive symptoms (Hypothesis 2). Third, cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) [39] can infer directional associations using metrics like Out-expected influence (out-EI, a symptom’s tendency to activate others), In-expected influence (in-EI, the degree to which a symptom is activated by others), and Bridge expected influence (BEI). Symptoms with high out-EI are intervention priorities, as modifying them can disrupt network activation [40]. Thus, CLPN helps detect the predictive directionality between PA and depressive symptoms (Hypothesis 3) and identify intervention targets (Hypothesis 4). Despite its potential, network research on adolescent depression remains limited due to inconsistent findings on central symptoms across populations [27, 29] and lack of longitudinal network studies at symptom-level in the Chinese cultural context.

To fill these gaps, the present study integrated Network Theory and Ecological Systems Theory and employed a two-wave longitudinal design (baseline/T1; 12-month follow-up/T2) to investigate the following four research questions at symptom level (1) Is anhedonia the most central symptom within the depression network in this population? (2) Does anhedonia function as a key bridge symptom linking PA and depressive symptoms in cross-sectional network analysis? (3) Is there a bidirectional relationship between PA and depressive symptoms in CLPN? (4) What symptom (anhedonia or negative self-esteem) is the high-impact intervention targets based on network roles (e.g., out-EI)? This study conducted four network structures, including a T1 cross-sectional depression network, T1 and T2 bridge networks linking PA and depressive symptoms, and a T1 → T2 cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) modeling their temporal dynamics. PA was operationalized as “alienation towards mother/father”, and depressive symptoms of adolescent were assessed across five dimensions: negative mood, interpersonal problems, ineffectiveness, anhedonia, and negative self-esteem (Additional file 1).

Methods

Participants

This study randomly selected three middle schools from Chongqing, China, using convenience sampling of students in grades 7, 8, 10, and 11. At baseline (T1, September 2022), 1727 questionnaires were distributed with 1503 valid responses (87.03% completion). The 12-month follow-up (T2, October 2023) yielded 1148 valid responses from 1212 questionnaires (94.72% completion). The final matched sample comprised 1148 adolescents (boys/girls: 589/559; mean age: 13.78 ± 2.13 years; junior/senior high school: 771/377). Attrition analysis indicated no significant differences in PA and depression scores between participants who completed both assessments and those who dropped out (both p > 0.05; see Additional file 2: Table S1). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 12–18 years, (3) severe physical illnesses.

Measures

Parent–child alienation (PA)

PA was assessed using the Chinese version of the Inventory of Alienation towards Parents [10], comprising nine items each for maternal and paternal forms. Example items include: “I feel that I cannot communicate with my mother/father” “Even when with my mother/father, I can not feel warmth” and “I am reluctant to ask for money from mother/father”. Item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, where higher scores denote higher levels of alienation. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients in the original validation were 0.887 (mother) and 0.821 (father) [10]. In this study, the internal consistencywas good at T1 (α = 0.90) and T2 (α = 0.92).

Adolescents’depression

Depression was assessed using the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI), comprising 27 items across five subscales: anhedonia, negative mood, negative self-esteem, ineffectiveness, and interpersonal problems [41]. Each item is rated on a 3-point scale (“0 = nottrue” to “2 = entirely true”), yielding a maximum total score of 54. Higher scores represent greater symptom severity. Validation of this inventory was excellent in Chinese adolescent (α = 0.84) [42]. The internal consistency in this study was high at T1 (α = 0.89) and T2 (α = 0.90).

Procedures

This study received ethical approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University (Approval Number: 2021-28-03). Researchers explained the study’s purpose, benefits, risks, confidentiality, and procedures to adolescents and guardians through online video conference. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their guardians. After that, adolescents were surveyed in classroom with questionnaires. Data was collected at two time points: baseline (T1) and 12-month follow-up (T2). As compensation for participation, researchers provided interpretations of their results, along with availability of free psychological lectures and counseling services.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics showed means and standard deviations of PA and depression at T1 and T2. Paired-sample t-tests compared longitudinal differences in PA and depression between T1 and T2. Attrition analysis compared PA and depression between participants who dropped out and those retained using Mann–Whitney U tests. Node normality assumptions were evaluated through the thresholds (|skewness|< 3, |kurtosis|< 10), as recommended by Kline (2016) [43]. These analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0. Network and power analysis were conducted in R 4.4.2.

Network estimation

Cross-sectional networks: Three Gaussian graphical models (GGM) were estimated using the qgraph package [44].T1 depression network (n = 1503) and T1/T2 bridge networks (linking PA and depressive symptoms, n = 1148). Graphical lasso regularization with the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) was applied to estimate sparse networks. Nodes represent the symptom dimensions. Edge thickness indicates connection strength, with blue/red color denoting positive/negative correlations [36]. Node centrality was evaluated through expected influence (EI), which quantifies node significance by summing the connected edge weights [40]. Bridge centrality was assessed using bridge expected influence (BEI), which assesses cross-community bridging roles by summing the edge weights connected to other communities [45]. The T1 and T2 bridge networks were compared using the Network Comparison Test (NCT) package [46].

Longitudinal network: A T1 → T2 cross-lagged panel network (CLPN, n = 1148) was estimated using glmnet package with LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) regularization with tenfold cross-validation[39]. Auto-regressive effects (T1 → T2 self-predictions) were constrained to zero to isolate cross-lagged effects (T1 → T2 cross-variable predictions)[47]. Edges represent prediction strength (thickness) and direction (blue = positive, red = negative). Centrality indices include in-expected influence (in-EI: a node’s total predictive reception from T1 variables), out-expected influence (out-EI: a node’s total predictive output to T2 variables), and bridge expected influence (BEI: sum of a node’s cross-community predictive edges). Network was visualized using the qgraph package [44].

Network accuracy and stability

Network robustness was assessed using the bootnet package [48]. Centrality stability (CS) was tested via case-dropping bootstraps (1000samples), with CS ≥ 0.50 indicating good stability, CS between 0.25 and 0.50 suggests moderate stability requiring cautious interpretation, and CS < 0.25 indicates instability [48]. Edge weight accuracy was tested through nonparametric bootstraps (1000 samples; 95% confidence intervals), with narrower intervals showing higher accuracy [49]. Bootstrap difference tests (1000 samples, α = 0.05) were conducted to compare edge weights and centrality indices.

Power analysis

Statistical power was evaluated via netSimulator function [50], assessing sensitivity (true-positive edge detection rate), specificity (true-negative edge detection rate), and structural correlation (consistency between estimated and true edge weights)[51]. Sample sizes ≥ 1000 achieved acceptable performance across all metrics (sensitivity > 0.65, specificity > 0.65, correlation > 0.65; see Additional file 2: Fig. S1).

Discussions

By integrating Ecological Systems Theory and Network Theory, this study employed network analysis to identify central depressive symptoms and elucidate the symptom-level associations between PA and depression in Chinese community adolescents. Key findings revealed anhedonia as the central depressive symptom and the key bridge symptom linking PA and depression. The CLPN revealed bidirectional predictive relationships between PA and depression, with depression exerting a stronger influence on subsequent PA than vice versa. Alienation towards mothers predicted subsequent depressive symptoms more strongly than alienation towards fathers. Furthermore, negative self-esteem was the central symptom, and anhedonia was the most affected depressive symptom in CLPN. These findings offer trans-theoretical mechanistic insights into the dynamic interactions between PA and depression at symptom-level.

Hypothesis 1

Anhedonia as the central cross-sectional depressive symptom.

In our study, anhedonia emerged as the most central symptom within the depression network among Chinese community adolescents. This finding is consistent with previous research on Chinese community adolescents [29]. However, it contrasts with study on Western adolescents, which have identified self-hatred—a core manifestation of negative self-esteem—as the most central symptom [52]. This discrepancy reflects the influence of the cross-cultural macrosystem (cultural context, Ecological Systems Theory) on symptom centrality (Network Theory). Specifically, Chinese intense academic environment, characterized by Gaokao, educational resource disparities, and parental anxiety, fosters chronic stress in adolescents [53, 54]. Prior studies have reported that chronic academic stress impairs reward processing and increases vulnerability to anhedonia in adolescents [55]. Furthermore, Confucian collectivism promotes emotional suppression to avoid disappointing parents [56], potentially amplifying stress-induced anhedonia and PA. Subsequently, anhedonia functions as a central hub, triggering other symptoms such as appetite loss, social withdrawal, and low self-esteem, thereby contributing to the persistence of the depression network [57–59]. Critically, this highlights a culturally specific intervention target. Implementing routine school-based screening for anhedonia, followed by targeted positive affect training [60], can disrupt this culture-driven vicious spiral before it consolidates. This approach offers tailored depressionprevention strategies for contexts of high academic stress.

Hypothesis 2

Anhedonia as a bridge symptom connecting PA and depression.

Anhedonia functioned as a key bridge symptom linking PA and depression, revealing a potential mechanism for their co-occurrence. Previous evidence has identified anhedonia as both a core depressive symptom and a marker of reward system dysfunction [61]. Crucially, secure parent–child bonding activates these reward pathways [9]. However, PA disrupts positive parent–child interactions (e.g., lack of parental warmth), potentially impairing the reward processing. Drawing on this prior evidence, shared neurobiological substrate may explain why anhedonia plays a role of bridge in triggering the co-occurrence of depression and PA. Building on Hypothesis 1, this discovery further integrates Ecological Systems Theory and Network Theory by: PA (a microsystem stressor) disrupts brain reward pathways (anhedonia), which functions as a bridge that transforms the initial ecosystem stressor (PA) into a broader self-sustaining depressive network. Understanding this mechanism helps to provide practical guidance. Screening measures in high-risk environments (e.g., migrant schools) should concurrently evaluate PA and anhedonia to identify adolescents requiring family focused interventions. For these adolescents, family therapists and social workers could design reward-based parent-adolescent interactions, such as parent–child group counseling incorporates games.

Hypothesis 3

PA and depression exhibit a bidirectional predictive relationship, with the influence of depression on PA being greater than the reverse; alienation towards mothers predicted subsequent depressive symptoms more strongly than alienation towards fathers.

CLPN analysis revealed a reciprocal relationship between PA and depressive symptoms, addressing a key literature gap. This suggests that depressive symptoms do not merely function as passive outcomes but as active drivers exacerbating PA, thereby creating a vicious circle. That is, PA (relationship disconnection) and depression (emotional status) mutually reinforce each other at symptom level, consistent with Network Theory’s symptom-to-symptom causal dynamics. These dynamics are in accord with Chinese cultural micro-systems, where filial piety norms amplify network activation and modulate connections.

Two key findings can be explained by this integration. First, depression exerted a stronger predictive effect on subsequent PA than vice versa. This asymmetry reveals a critical mechanism: acute depressive symptoms (e.g., irritability and social withdrawal) directly provoke interpersonal conflict and emotional distance (alienation nodes) via rapid emotional contagion [62, 63]. Concurrently, within Chinese cultural contexts (microsystems) emphasizing filial piety, parents may interpret adolescent depression as “filial failure”, further amplifying PA [64]. In contrast, PA’s impact on depression operates more indirectly and cumulatively (e.g., through attachment injuries, negative schemas) [65, 66], potentially explaining its weaker effect. Second, alienation towards mothers (primary emotional caregivers) prospectively predicted depressive symptoms more strongly than alienation towards fathers. This difference likely stems from the culturally normalized authority distance with fathers [67]. It underscores culture-bound relational pathways to depression and highlights maternal alienation as a specific intervention target.

Therefore, for adolescents experiencing clinical depression, pharmacotherapy may be prioritized during depression episodes to rapidly reduce symptoms impacting PA. Family therapy can be delivered during the recovery phase. Prevention programs can train mothers as the primary source of support for children’s mental health.

Hypothesis 4

Negative self-esteem as a network “activator” and anhedonia as a “maintainer”.

CLPN analysis revealed different pathological roles: negative self-esteem functioned as the primary network activator (highest out-EI), dynamically influencing other depressive symptoms and PA. Anhedonia functioned as a core network maintainer (higher in-EI), sustaining the depressive cluster through reward processing, a core mechanism in depression persistence [25]. The dual-role distinction further clarifies mechanisms of depression network self-perpetuation. Critically, longitudinal results diverge from cross-sectional networks where anhedonia exhibited stronger centrality, reflecting a symptomatic amplifier linking PA and other depressive symptoms. However, longitudinal analysis revealed temporal precedenceof negative self-esteem as a primary activator, which was obscured in static cross-sectional network possibly due to adolescent neuro-developmental sensitivities amplifying reward-system reactivity [68, 69]. This reward hyper-sensitivity partially elucidates why anhedonia is easily influenced by other symptoms and difficult to remit, thereby maintaining the chronic process of depression [70].

These findings necessitate precision interventions targeting distinct pathways. For negative self-esteem (activator): implementing self-esteem enhancement techniques, such as solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT) to impede symptom interactions by building self-efficacy [71]. Meanwhile, for anhedonia (maintainer): behavioral activation techniques can be applied to modify reward processing and restore positive emotional responsiveness [61]. To implement this dual-focus strategy, schools should establish family-school collaboration, including school-based self-esteem curricula alongside parent workshops focused on enhancing parental understanding, communication skills, and supportive capacities (i.e., love capacity training).

Implications

This study proposes a Chinese culturally grounded family-school-society model to help adolescents. First, brief assessments for anhedonia and PA should be integrated into routine school health protocols without increasing the administrative burden. Second, shifting the focus of parental training from problem-solving to cultivating the capacity to love (e.g., using Chapman’s five love languages [72]. Third, counteract strict cultural tropes suppressing emotional expression (e.g., “men shed no tears”) and promote societal acceptance of academic vulnerability through public reframing of success/failure and fostering healthier learning mindsets.

Limitations and strengths

First, self-reported measures may have introduced response bias. Second, unaccounted confounders, including social support, adverse life-events, and coping styles, may affect the observed variables. Third, the design of two time-point survey with a one-year gap may not precisely capture the progressive development of PA and depression. Notwithstanding these limitations, this study pioneers key advances: longitudinal symptom-network approaches, large community adolescent sample, dynamic alienation-depression co-occurrence, quantification of depression’s stronger prediction, and symptom-targeted intervention frameworks.

Conclusions

By integrating Ecological Systems Theory and Network Theory, this study is among the first to employ network analysis to investigate the dynamic association between PA and depression in Chinese community adolescents. Cross-sectionally, anhedonia is central to the depression network and a key bridge linking PA and depression. Longitudinally, PA and depression bidirectionally predict each other, with depression exerting a stronger influence on later PA. Alienation towards mothers predicted subsequent depressive symptoms more strongly than alienation towards fathers. Anhedonia and negative self-esteem emerge as key nodes with distinct roles (maintainers vs. activators). These findings offer an intervention framework for school-based anhedonia screening for alienated youth and the development of family-focused interventions targeting anhedonia (particularly enhancing mother–child relationships) and negative self-esteem to prevent and treat depression in clinical and school settings.
