Sampling-based Continuous Optimization for Messenger RNA Design
Feipeng Yue, Ning Dai, Wei Yu Tang, Tianshuo Zhou, David Mathews, Liang Huang

Abstract
Designing messenger RNA (mRNA) sequences for a fixed target protein requires searching an exponentially large synonymous space while optimizing properties that affect stability and downstream performance. This is challenging because practical mRNA design involves multiple coupled objectives beyond classical folding criteria, and different applications prefer different trade-offs. We propose a general sampling-based continuous optimization framework, inspired by SamplingDesign, that iteratively samples candidate synonymous sequences, evaluates them with black-box metrics, and updates a parameterized sampling distribution. Across a diverse UniProt protein set and the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, our method consistently improves the chosen objective, with particularly strong gains on average unpaired probability and accessible uridine percentage compared to LinearDesign and EnsembleDesign. Moreover, our multi-objective COMBO formulation enables weight-controlled exploration of the design space and naturally extends to incorporate additional computable metrics.

Introduction

Designing optimized messenger RNA (mRNA) sequences has received increasing attention in recent years, driven in part by the success of mRNA vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic
1
;
10
. Given a target protein sequence, mRNA design seeks a synonymous coding sequence that preserves translation while optimizing desired mRNA properties. Because codon degeneracy creates an exponentially large set of synonymous sequences, exhaustive search is infeasible
14
; effective methods must therefore navigate this space under explicitly defined objectives.

Several methods have demonstrated that large-scale optimization over synonymous spaces is feasible when objectives are clearly defined. LinearDesign optimized synonymous mRNAs under a minimum free energy (
\mathrm{MFE}
) objective using dynamic programming, enabling efficient search at scale
14
. More recently, EnsembleDesign
4

and
JAX-RNAfold
8

optimized a distribution-level objective, ensemble free energy (
\mathrm{EFE}
),
using continuous optimization,
but with very different approaches
(e.g., the former extended LinearDesign’s lattice parsing framework
while the latter did not).
These approaches highlight the value of objective-driven optimization for navigating the vast synonymous coding space. However, practical mRNA design involves many objectives beyond
\mathrm{MFE}
and
\mathrm{EFE}
, and different applications often require different trade-offs. One example is the average unpaired probability (
\mathrm{AUP}
), which summarizes expected unpairedness across the transcript and is often used as a proxy related to degradation
9
. Another example is accessible uridine percentage (accessible U% /
\mathrm{AccessU}
), which we introduce in this work as a user-defined objective that measures the fraction of uridines that are structurally accessible (i.e., more likely to be unpaired). These considerations motivate a more general optimization framework that can directly optimize diverse objectives, rather than focusing on a small set of classical targets.

Motivated by this gap, we propose a general-purpose framework for mRNA design based on sampling-based continuous optimization. Our sampling-based optimization perspective is inspired by SamplingDesign, which demonstrates that continuous optimization coupled with Monte-Carlo sampling can be an effective paradigm for RNA design
12
. Our method maintains a continuously parameterized sampling distribution over amino-acid–preserving nucleotide sequences and improves the distribution iteratively using samples scored by external evaluators. Concretely, each iteration draws candidate sequences from the current distribution, evaluates them under a chosen objective (for example,
\mathrm{MFE}
,
\mathrm{EFE}
,
\mathrm{CAI}
,
\mathrm{AUP}
,
\mathrm{AccessU}
, or weighted combinations of multiple metrics such as
\mathrm{COMBO}
), and updates the distribution parameters using sampling-based gradient estimates computed from the sampled sequences and their objective scores. This formulation supports optimizing both established objectives and additional potential mRNA evaluation objectives.

We evaluate on a diverse set of proteins from UniProt spanning a wide length range and on the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein as a representative long target, using a consistent scoring pipeline. Under single-metric optimization, our method consistently improves the targeted objective; most notably, when optimizing
\mathrm{AUP}
or
\mathrm{AccessU}
, our designed sequences achieve lower values than both LinearDesign and EnsembleDesign. Under
\mathrm{COMBO}
optimization, our method enables weight-controlled navigation of the design space to obtain sequences that satisfy different optimization preferences. Importantly, because the optimization loop treats metrics as black-box evaluators of sampled sequences, the framework naturally extends to incorporate additional computable metrics beyond the current set, enabling richer objective combinations for future mRNA design tasks.

Preliminaries: mRNA Design for Protein

2.1
Protein Encoding in mRNA

Let
\bm{{p}}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m})
be a target protein sequence of length
m
,
where each
p_{i}
is an amino acid.
Let
\mathcal{N}\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\{\texttt{A},\texttt{C},\texttt{G},\texttt{U}\}
denote the RNA nucleotide alphabet.
An mRNA coding sequence is a nucleotide string

\bm{{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathcal{N}^{N}
,
where
x_{i}\in\mathcal{N}
and
N=3m
.

Partition
\bm{{x}}
into
m
consecutive codons (triplets) in
\mathcal{N}^{3}
.
Under the standard genetic code, each codon maps to an amino acid (or a stop signal);
concatenating the resulting amino acids yields a protein sequence.
We say that
\bm{{x}}
encodes
\bm{{p}}
if this translation yields
\bm{{p}}
.
Since multiple codons can map to the same amino acid, there are typically many distinct
mRNA sequences that encode the same protein.
Accordingly, our design space consists of synonymous coding sequences for
\bm{{p}}
:
all candidates encode
\bm{{p}}
but may differ at the nucleotide level.

2.2
mRNA Design Problem

Given a fixed target protein sequence
\bm{{p}}
, mRNA design seeks a coding sequence
\bm{{x}}

that encodes
\bm{{p}}
while optimizing sequence-level properties measured by computable metrics.

Design space.

We define the synonymous design space for
\bm{{p}}
as

\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\left\{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{N}^{N}\;:\;\mathrm{translate}(\bm{{x}})=\bm{{p}}\right\},

(1)

i.e., the set of all mRNA coding sequences that translate to
\bm{{p}}
under the standard genetic code.

Objective.

Although all
\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})
encode the same protein, different synonymous choices
can yield different properties relevant to downstream use.
We assess a candidate by different computable metrics

g_{1}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}}),\ldots,g_{k}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})
, where
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.
Because some metrics are to be maximized and others minimized, we map each raw metric to a
direction-aligned term
f_{k}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})
via a monotone transformation

f_{k}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\phi_{k}\!\big(g_{k}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})\big),

(2)

so that smaller
f_{k}
always indicates a better score, i.e., better performance according to metric
k
.

We then optimize a scalar objective over
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})
using a normalized weighted sum of objective terms:

F(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\,f_{k}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}}),\qquad w_{k}\geq 0,\ \ \sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}=1.

(3)

When
K=1
, this reduces to optimizing a single metric.
The design problem is

\bm{{x}}^{\star}\;\in\;\argmin_{\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})}F(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}}).

(4)

2.3
Sequence Optimization Metrics

For a fixed target protein
\bm{{p}}
, each candidate coding sequence

\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})
encodes the same protein but can exhibit different
sequence-level and structure-level behaviors relevant to downstream use.
We therefore evaluate
\bm{{x}}
with several standard computable metrics.

Folding model and notation.

RNA secondary-structure metrics are based on the standard thermodynamic view
that a sequence
\bm{{x}}
can adopt many feasible secondary structures, each
associated with an energy.
Let
\mathcal{Y}(\bm{{x}})
be the set of feasible secondary structures for
\bm{{x}}

under a chosen energy model, and let
\Delta G^{\circ}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}})
denote the Gibbs
free energy of structure
\bm{{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(\bm{{x}})
.
We use thermodynamic parameters
(R,T)
, where
R
is the universal gas constant
and
T
is temperature.
Let
p_{i,j}(\bm{{x}})
denote the marginal probability that positions
(i,j)
form
a base pair in the Boltzmann ensemble, and define the unpaired probability of position
i

q_{i}(\bm{{x}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;1-\sum_{j\neq i}p_{i,j}(\bm{{x}}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.

(5)

Minimum free energy (
\mathrm{MFE}
).

The minimum free energy summarizes the most stable (lowest-energy) structure:

\mathrm{MFE}(\bm{{x}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\min_{\bm{{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(\bm{{x}})}\Delta G^{\circ}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}}).

(6)

We compute
\mathrm{MFE}
using LinearFold
6
in this work.

Boltzmann ensemble and ensemble free energy (
\mathrm{EFE}
).

Rather than focusing on a single structure, the partition function aggregates
contributions from all feasible structures, weighting each by its Boltzmann
factor:

Q(\bm{{x}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\sum_{\bm{{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(\bm{{x}})}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta G^{\circ}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{y}})}{RT}\right).

(7)

The corresponding ensemble free energy is

\mathrm{EFE}(\bm{{x}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;-RT\,\log Q(\bm{{x}}),

(8)

which we compute using LinearPartition
15
in this work.

Average unpaired probability (
\mathrm{AUP}
).

The average unpaired probability is

\mathrm{AUP}(\bm{{x}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}(\bm{{x}}).

(9)

Accessible U% (
\mathrm{AccessU}
).

Accessible U% weights unpaired probabilities by whether the nucleotide is U:

\mathrm{AccessU}(\bm{{x}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[x_{i}=\texttt{U}]\;q_{i}(\bm{{x}}).

(10)

Codon Adaptation Index (
\mathrm{CAI}
).

\mathrm{CAI}
measures synonymous codon-usage bias relative to a reference
set of highly expressed genes, and is commonly used as a metric of codon
optimality
11
.

Combined objective (
\mathrm{COMBO}
).

To express trade-offs among multiple criteria, we combine direction-aligned
terms in a normalized weighted sum.
Since
\mathrm{CAI}(\bm{{x}})
,
\mathrm{AUP}(\bm{{x}})
, and
\mathrm{AccessU}(\bm{{x}})
lie in
[0,1]
, they are
directly comparable after aligning directions (maximized terms are converted to
minimization by
1-\cdot
).
In contrast,
\mathrm{MFE}(\bm{{x}})
is length-dependent; we therefore normalize it using
a fixed baseline sequence for the same target protein.
Let
\mathrm{MFE}_{\mathrm{LD}}(\bm{{p}})
denote the
\mathrm{MFE}
of the LinearDesign baseline
sequence for
\bm{{p}}
.
We use the normalized MFE score

\mathrm{MFE}_{\mathrm{rel}}(\bm{{x}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\frac{\mathrm{MFE}(\bm{{x}})}{\mathrm{MFE}_{\mathrm{LD}}(\bm{{p}})},

(11)

so that larger
\mathrm{MFE}_{\mathrm{rel}}(\bm{{x}})
indicates a better (more stable) design relative to
the baseline.

Putting these together, the combined objective is

\displaystyle\mathrm{COMBO}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}

\displaystyle\alpha\bigl(1-\mathrm{CAI}(\bm{{x}})\bigr)+\beta\,\mathrm{AUP}(\bm{{x}})

(12)

\displaystyle+\gamma\,\mathrm{AccessU}(\bm{{x}})+\delta\bigl(1-\mathrm{MFE}_{\mathrm{rel}}(\bm{{x}})\bigr)

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\geq 0
and
\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta=1
.

(a–b) parameterized sampling lattice.
To avoid enumerating the exponentially large synonymous space for a fixed protein
\bm{{p}}
, we represent all valid coding sequences as a DFA-based lattice, where each complete path corresponds to a synonymous mRNA
\bm{{x}}
. We then equip the lattice with probabilistic parameters, forming a pDFA in which each state defines a locally normalized distribution over its outgoing edges (Eq.
13
). Edge labels are nucleotides, and sampling generates an mRNA by traversing the lattice and concatenating the emitted labels.

Illustrative workflow.
Starting from an initialized lattice (a), we sample candidate mRNAs and Evaluate them under the chosen objective (here, minimizing accessible U%). Using these scores, we perform a gradient update on the lattice parameters, yielding an updated probabilistic lattice (b), from which we sample and evaluate again. After the update, the sampled sequences exhibit a clear reduction in accessible U% (as highlighted by the cyan regions in the table), and the corresponding decision region shifts probability mass away from U-emitting branches (as highlighted by the cyan regions in the pDFA), consistent with reducing U-rich choices as one effective route to lowering accessible U%. This update–sample–evaluate loop is repeated until the optimization metrics converge.

The synonymous design space
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})
is exponentially large and
is defined by a hard amino-acid constraint.
To avoid enumerating
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})
while preserving feasibility, we
represent it as a lattice and parameterize a sampling distribution directly on
this structure (Fig.
1
).

DFA lattice representation.

Following LinearDesign, the set of coding sequences that encode a fixed target
protein
\bm{{p}}
is compactly represented by a deterministic finite-state
automaton (DFA)
(
14
)
.
In this lattice, each edge is labeled by a nucleotide, and any complete path
generates an mRNA sequence
\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{N}^{N}
by concatenating edge labels.
By construction, every complete path translates to
\bm{{p}}
, and conversely,
every
\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})
is represented by at least one complete
path in the lattice.
This DFA therefore provides a constraint-preserving representation of the
synonymous space.

From a lattice to a sampling distribution.

To enable continuous optimization in an otherwise discrete space, we follow the
continuous relaxation perspective of EnsembleDesign and equip the same lattice
with probabilistic parameters, yielding a probabilistic DFA (pDFA) that defines
a distribution over synonymous sequences
(
4
)
.
Crucially, probability mass is assigned only to valid DFA paths, so sampling
from the pDFA produces sequences in
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})
automatically.

Formally, let
s
denote a lattice state and let
\mathcal{A}(s)
be its set of
outgoing edges.
We associate each state with a categorical distribution over outgoing edges,

p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(a\,\middle|\,s\right)\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}(s)}p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(a\,\middle|\,s\right)=1,

(13)

where
\bm{\theta}
collects all trainable parameters.
Sampling corresponds to traversing the lattice by repeatedly selecting an
outgoing edge
a\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(\cdot\,\middle|\,s\right)
and advancing to the next state.
The resulting concatenation of edge labels yields a sampled

\bm{{x}}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
supported on
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})
.

Parameterized local transitions.

Rather than optimizing probabilities directly on the simplex, we introduce
unconstrained logits
\theta_{s,a}\in\mathbb{R}
for each
a\in\mathcal{A}(s)
.
Local transition probabilities are obtained via the softmax,

p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(a\,\middle|\,s\right)=\frac{\exp(\theta_{s,a})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}(s)}\exp(\theta_{s,a^{\prime}})},\qquad a\in\mathcal{A}(s),

(14)

which automatically enforces nonnegativity and normalization while allowing
updates to be performed in an unconstrained Euclidean space.

Sequence probability and path factorization.

A sampled sequence
\bm{{x}}
corresponds to a complete path

\tau=(s_{0},a_{0},\ldots,s_{N-1},a_{N-1},s_{N})

on the lattice, where
a_{n}
denotes the chosen outgoing edge at state
s_{n}
.
Under (
14
), the probability of sampling the path (and thus the
sequence) factorizes along the path:

p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{{x}})=p_{\bm{\theta}}(\tau)=\prod_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(a_{n}\,\middle|\,s_{n}\right).

(15)

Equivalently,

\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{{x}})=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(a_{n}\,\middle|\,s_{n}\right).

(16)

This factorization is central to our approach.
Our objective
\mathrm{COMBO}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})
is evaluated on discrete sequences using
folding-based metrics and is treated as a black-box function of
\bm{{x}}
.
Although
\mathrm{COMBO}
is not differentiable with respect to nucleotide identities,
the pDFA provides a differentiable handle through
\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{{x}})
.
In the next section, we leverage (
16
) to derive
sampling-based gradient estimates and implement a sample–evaluate–update
procedure to optimize
\bm{\theta}
.

4
Sampling-Based Continuous Optimization on the Lattice

In this section, we present our sampling-based continuous optimization
algorithm for synonymous mRNA design.
The method iterates a sample–evaluate–update loop on the parameterized
sampling lattice, using a score-function gradient estimator in logit space and
terminating via an early-stopping rule.

4.1
Sampling

Starting from the initial state, a candidate sequence
\bm{{x}}
is generated by
traversing the lattice and repeatedly sampling an outgoing edge

a_{n}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(\cdot\,\middle|\,s_{n}\right)
.
Concatenating the nucleotide labels along the sampled path yields

\bm{{x}}\in\mathcal{N}^{N}
.
For each sampled
\bm{{x}}
, we record the corresponding lattice path

\tau=(s_{0},a_{0},\ldots,s_{N-1},a_{N-1},s_{N})
,
where
a_{n}
is the chosen outgoing edge at

s_{n}
.

4.2
Evaluation

Each sampled sequence
\bm{{x}}
is assigned a scalar objective value

F(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})
, where
F(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})
is either a single metric or the
weighted combination
\mathrm{COMBO}(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})
defined in
Section
2.3
.
These objective values provide the learning signal for updating
\bm{\theta}
.

4.3
Gradient-Based Update

The update step adjusts the lattice logits (and hence the induced sampling
distribution) to reduce the expected objective under the current lattice
distribution.
We index optimization iterations by
t\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
, and let

\bm{\theta}_{t}
denote the lattice parameters used to sample the batch
at iteration
t
(i.e., after
t-1
updates).
At iteration
t
, we perform a gradient-based update

\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}_{t}-\eta\,\widehat{\nabla J}_{t},

(17)

where
\widehat{\nabla J}_{t}
is a stochastic estimate of

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}J(\bm{\theta}_{t};\bm{{p}})
derived below and

\eta
is the learning rate.
In practice, we use Adam to compute an adaptive update from
\widehat{\nabla J}_{t}

(Paragraph
(vii)
).

(i) Expected objective under the lattice distribution.

Fix a target protein
\bm{{p}}
.
Sampling on the lattice induces a distribution

p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{{x}})
over synonymous coding sequences
\bm{{x}}
.
We minimize the expected objective

J(\bm{\theta};\bm{{p}})\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\E_{\bm{{x}}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)}\!\left[F(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})\right].

(18)

(ii) Score-function (log-derivative) gradient.

Since
\bm{{x}}
is discrete, we differentiate (
18
) using the
log-derivative trick:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}J(\bm{\theta};\bm{{p}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\sum_{\bm{{x}}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{{x}})\,F(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{{x}}}p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{{x}})\,F(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{{x}})

\displaystyle=\E_{\bm{{x}}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)}\!\left[F(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{{x}})\right].

(19)

With
M
i.i.d. samples

\bm{{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{{x}}^{(M)}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
,
a Monte Carlo estimate of (
19
) is

\widehat{\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}J}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}F(\bm{{x}}^{(i)},\bm{{p}})\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\big(\bm{{x}}^{(i)}\big).

(20)

The remaining task is to express

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\big(\bm{{x}}^{(i)}\big)

in terms of local lattice decisions.

(iii) Path decomposition of the score term.

Each sampled sequence
\bm{{x}}
corresponds to a complete lattice path

\tau=(s_{0},a_{0},\ldots,s_{N-1},a_{N-1},s_{N})

of length
N
.
By the path factorization in Section
3

(Eqs. (
15
)–(
16
)),

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{{x}})=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(a_{n}\,\middle|\,s_{n}\right).

(21)

Substituting (
21
) into (
20
) reduces the global
score term to a sum of local contributions along the sampled path.

(iv) Local softmax derivative.

At each visited state
s
, the outgoing distribution over

\mathcal{A}(s)
is parameterized by logits via the softmax in
Eq. (
14
).
Let
a\in\mathcal{A}(s)
be the chosen edge on the sampled path,
and let
u\in\mathcal{A}(s)
index an arbitrary outgoing edge.
Then

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{s,u}}\log p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(a\,\middle|\,s\right)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[u=a\right]-p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(u\,\middle|\,s\right).

(22)

This provides an explicit per-logit local score term.

(v) Mean–variance normalization.

For the batch scores

\{F(\bm{{x}}^{(i)},\bm{{p}})\}_{i=1}^{M}
,
we compute the batch mean and standard deviation

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}F(\bm{{x}}^{(i)},\bm{{p}}),

(23)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bigl(F(\bm{{x}}^{(i)},\bm{{p}})-b\bigr)^{2}}+\varepsilon.

and define the normalized weight

A^{(i)}\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\frac{F(\bm{{x}}^{(i)},\bm{{p}})-b}{\sigma}.

(24)

Subtracting a baseline is a standard variance-reduction device for score-function
gradient estimators
(
13
;
5
)
.
Here we set
b
to the batch mean so that each sample is weighted by
its performance relative to the current batch rather than by the absolute scale
of
F
.

(vi) Fully expanded per-logit gradient estimator.

Let the
i
-th sampled sequence have path

\tau^{(i)}=(s^{(i)}_{0},a^{(i)}_{0},\ldots,s^{(i)}_{N-1},a^{(i)}_{N-1},s^{(i)}_{N})
.
Combining (
20
), (
21
), and
(
22
), for any state
s
and any

u\in\mathcal{A}(s)
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\frac{\partial J(\bm{\theta};\bm{{p}})}{\partial\theta_{s,u}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}A^{(i)}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbf{1}\!\left[s^{(i)}_{n}=s\right]\,

(25)

\displaystyle\cdot\Bigl(\mathbf{1}\!\left[u=a^{(i)}_{n}\right]-p_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(u\,\middle|\,s\right)\Bigr).

The indicator
\mathbf{1}\!\left[s^{(i)}_{n}=s\right]

ensures that only states visited by the sampled paths contribute to the
corresponding logits.

(vii) Adam updates in logit space.

Let
\widehat{\nabla J}_{t}
denote the collection of all per-logit gradient
estimates in (
25
) at iteration
t
.
We use the Adam optimizer, which maintains exponential moving averages of past
gradients and squared gradients to form a momentum term and an adaptive,
per-parameter step size
(
7
)
.
This is convenient for stochastic sampling-based gradient estimates and
typically yields more stable updates than a fixed learning rate.

We update logits with Adam:

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}\,m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{1})\,\widehat{\nabla J}_{t},

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=\beta_{2}\,v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{2})\,\widehat{\nabla J}_{t}^{2},

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{t}}{1-\beta_{1}^{t}},\qquad\hat{v}_{t}=\frac{v_{t}}{1-\beta_{2}^{t}},

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}_{t}-\eta\frac{\hat{m}_{t}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t}}+\epsilon}.

(26)

4.4
Early Stopping and Termination Criteria

The sample–evaluate–update loop is stochastic and can be computationally
expensive, as each iteration requires evaluating folding-based objectives on a
batch of sequences.
In practice, the optimization often converges to a stable region of the search
space, after which additional iterations produce little or no improvement in
the observed objective values while incurring substantial runtime.
We therefore adopt an early stopping criterion that terminates the procedure
once progress on the target objective has plateaued.

Best-so-far tracking.

At iteration
t
, we draw a batch

\{\bm{{x}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot)

and compute their objective values

\{F(\bm{{x}}^{(i)},\bm{{p}})\}_{i=1}^{M}
.
Let the current best objective value in the batch be

\bigl(\widehat{F}_{\min}\bigr)_{t}\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}}F(\bm{{x}}^{(i)},\bm{{p}}),

(27)

and let the best value observed up to iteration
t
be updated by

F^{*}_{t}\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\min\!\bigl(F^{*}_{t-1},\,\bigl(\widehat{F}_{\min}\bigr)_{t}\bigr),\qquad F^{*}_{0}\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;+\infty.

(28)

Patience-based early stopping.

We declare an iteration to be improving if it achieves a sufficient
decrease relative to the previous best:

F^{*}_{t}\leq F^{*}_{t-1}-\xi_{\mathrm{stop}}.

(29)

Let
P
be a patience window (in iterations). We stop if no improving
iteration occurs for
P
consecutive updates, i.e., if the number of
iterations since the last improvement exceeds
P
.

Maximum-iteration safeguard.

To bound runtime, we also impose a hard cap of
T_{\max}
iterations and stop
once
t=T_{\max}
.

5.5
Initialization of the Sampling Distribution.

The initial lattice distribution is constructed by mixing a
LinearDesign-based distribution with a uniform distribution.
Concretely, for each lattice state
s
, we initialize the local
transition distribution as

p_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\!\left(\cdot\,\middle|\,s\right)=\epsilon\;p_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{LD}}}\!\left(\cdot\,\middle|\,s\right)+(1-\epsilon)\;p_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{unif}}}\!\left(\cdot\,\middle|\,s\right),

(30)

where
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{LD}}}\!\left(\cdot\,\middle|\,s\right)
corresponds to
the LinearDesign solution projected onto the lattice, and

p_{\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{unif}}}\!\left(\cdot\,\middle|\,s\right)
assigns equal
probability to all outgoing edges in
\mathcal{A}(s)
.
The mixing coefficient
\epsilon\in[0,1]
controls the trade-off between
initializing near the LinearDesign solution and maintaining exploration.
Unless otherwise stated, we set
\epsilon=0.5
in all experiments.
We then set logits
\bm{\theta}_{1}
such that the softmax in Eq. (
14
)
matches the mixture in Eq. (
30
) at every state.

5.6
Stopping Criterion and Output Selection.

Our method includes a patience-based early stopping rule
(Section
4.4
) that terminates optimization when the best
objective value has not improved for
P
consecutive iterations.
In principle, this criterion reduces runtime by avoiding unnecessary iterations
once the optimization has converged.

In the main experiments reported in this section, however, we adopt a fixed
iteration budget
T_{\max}
for each target and objective.
This choice is motivated by the stochasticity of sampling-based optimization:
because each iteration is driven by a finite batch, the best-so-far value
can exhibit occasional minute improvements even after the optimization has
effectively stabilized, which may lead to run-to-run variability in the stopping
time under purely automatic early stopping.
To obtain comparable compute budgets across targets and baselines and to reduce
the sensitivity of termination to stochastic fluctuations, we select
T_{\max}

based on preliminary convergence curves (objective value versus iteration),
choosing a conservative cutoff after which further iterations yield negligible
improvement on average.
We still track the best-so-far sequence throughout the run and report the final
output as

\bm{{x}}^{\star}\;\mathrel{{\mathop{=}\limits}}\;\argmin_{\bm{{x}}\ \text{sampled during the run}}F(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}}),

(31)

i.e., the best-scoring sampled sequence under the optimized objective.

7
Conclusions

We introduced sampling-based continuous optimization framework for synonymous mRNA design that leverages a parameterized lattice representation of the synonymous space
\mathcal{X}(\bm{{p}})
. By placing trainable logits
\bm{\theta}
on local lattice transitions, the method induces a distribution
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{{x}})
supported only on feasible coding sequences, and optimizes the expected objective via an iterative sample–evaluate–update procedure.

In single-metric experiments, our method reliably drives down the targeted objective across the UniProt set and the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein under a shared scoring pipeline. Most notably, when optimizing
\mathrm{AUP}
or
\mathrm{AccessU}
, our designed sequences achieve lower values than both LinearDesign and EnsembleDesign, demonstrating a consistent advantage in reducing global unpairedness and U-accessibility.

In
\mathrm{COMBO}
optimization, the explicit weight
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)
enables weight-controlled navigation of the multi-objective design space: by tuning these coefficients, we can select design sequences that satisfy different optimization preferences and occupy different regions of the design space (e.g., trading off stability and codon optimality). Importantly, the framework is extensible: additional computable metrics can be incorporated into
F(\bm{{x}},\bm{{p}})
, allowing our approach to optimize richer objective combinations beyond the current metric set.
