Monochromatic Paths and a Topological Approach to Norine’s Conjecture
Adam Džavoronok

Abstract
. Motivated by Norine’s conjecture, this paper investigates monochromatic antipodal paths in 2 2 -edge-coloured hypercubes. Our main method relies on a topological criterion applied to triangulated 2 2 -skeleta. We show that any antipodal colouring of a centrally symmetric, simply connected 2 2 -complex yields a monochromatic path linking an antipodal pair of vertices. By symmetrically triangulating opposite square faces in certain classes of colourings, we obtain a topological verification of Norine’s conjecture for these classes. We also establish quantitative bounds for cases in which only a limited number of square faces present structural obstructions.



1.
Introduction

Norine’s conjecture concerns edge-colourings of the hypercube that are compatible with the antipodal symmetry. Let
Q_{n}
be the graph on
\{0,1\}^{n}
, where two vertices are adjacent if they differ in exactly one coordinate, and let

\tau(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=(1-u_{1},\dots,1-u_{n})

be the antipodal involution. We call
\tau(v)
the

of

2
-

and
\tau(u)\tau(v)
are different for every edge

is a shortest path between two vertices.

Norine conjectured that every antipodal

contains a monochromatic path from some vertex to its antipode. This conjecture is closely related to several stronger or more flexible formulations. We shall consider the following family of conjectures.

(Norine
[

(Feder–Subi
[

(4)

(Leader–Long
[

Our Contributions

The main contribution of this paper is a topological approach to Norine’s conjecture. The guiding idea is that the square faces of the hypercube encode the obstruction to finding monochromatic antipodal paths. Every induced
C_{4}
in
Q_{n}
is a square face, and the union of all vertices, edges, and square faces will be called the
2
-
skeleton
of the hypercube, or of an induced subgraph of the hypercube. We use these square faces in two ways. First, they provide the
2
-dimensional cells needed for simple connectivity. Second, when a square contains a suitable monochromatic connection between a pair of opposite vertices, it can be triangulated by adding the corresponding diagonal.

We now recall the relevant terminology. A finite
simplicial complex

\Delta
is a collection of finite sets, called
simplices
, which is
closed under taking subsets. Its vertices are the one-element simplices, and
its edges are the two-element simplices. To avoid self-intersection, we realise
\Delta
geometrically
in
\mathbb{R}^{V(\Delta)}
by sending each vertex
v
to the corresponding
standard basis vector
e_{v}
; the
geometric realisation

\|\Delta\|

is the union of the convex hulls of the sets
\{e_{v}:v\in\sigma\}
, where

\sigma\in\Delta
. Equivalently,
\|\Delta\|
is obtained by realising each
simplex as a geometric simplex and gluing these simplices along common faces.
The graph formed by the vertices and edges of
\Delta
is called its

1
-skeleton.

For our purposes, central symmetry is most conveniently formulated
combinatorially. We say that
\Delta
is
centrally symmetric
if it is
equipped with a fixed-point-free involution

\tau\colon V(\Delta)\to V(\Delta)
such that

\tau(\sigma)\in\Delta
for every simplex
\sigma\in\Delta
, and no simplex
of
\Delta
contains both a vertex
v
and its image
\tau(v)
. Thus

\tau
is a simplicial automorphism of order two. It extends affinely over
each simplex to a fixed-point-free involution of
\|\Delta\|
in
\mathbb{R}^{V(\Delta)}
. We call

v
and
\tau(v)
antipodal vertices. For the
1
-skeleton of such a
complex, an antipodal
2
-edge-colouring is defined as follows: the edges

uv
and
\tau(u)\tau(v)
receive opposite colours. We say that
\|\Delta\|
is
simply connected
if it is path-connected
and every continuous loop in
\|\Delta\|
can be continuously contracted to a
point within
\|\Delta\|
.

We will also need the notion of equivariance. If
(X,\tau)
and
(Y,\nu)
are topological spaces with fixed-point-free involutions, then a continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
is called
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant if
f\circ\tau=\nu\circ f.
Our first main result says that simple connectivity rules out antipodal colourings with no monochromatic antipodal path.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a centrally symmetric simplicial complex such that
\|\Delta\|
is simply connected. Then every antipodal
2
-edge-colouring of the edges of the
1
-skeleton of
\Delta
contains a monochromatic path connecting some antipodal pair of vertices.

The proof is based on equivariant topology. Suppose, for a contradiction, that no monochromatic antipodal path exists. The red and blue connected components then allow us to construct a continuous
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant map from
\|\Delta\|
to a unit circle
S^{1}
, where
S^{1}
is equipped with the antipodal involution. This contradicts the standard Borsuk-Ulam type fact that no such equivariant map exists from a simply connected centrally symmetric complex to
S^{1}
.

The criterion gives a short topological proof of a theorem of Feder and Subi. The relevant local obstruction is the colouring of a square.

Definition 1.3
.

In a given
2
-edge-colouring, we call a square, that is, an induced
Q_{2}
,
simple
if its edges are coloured alternately.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
2
]
)
.

Given an antipodal
2
-edge-colouring of
Q_{n}
for
n\geq 3
that does not contain a simple square, there is a monochromatic path connecting an antipodal pair.

The idea is that if a square is not simple, then one of its two diagonals can be represented by a monochromatic
2
-edge path inside the square. By choosing such diagonals antipodally, we obtain a centrally symmetric triangulation of the full
2
-skeleton of
Q_{n}
. Since this
2
-skeleton is simply connected,
Theorem

1.2
gives a monochromatic antipodal path in the triangulated complex. Replacing each added diagonal by the corresponding monochromatic
2
-edge path gives the desired monochromatic path in
Q_{n}
. This motivates a more flexible notion.

Definition 1.5
.

In a given
2
-edge-colouring, we call a square, that is, an induced
Q_{2}
,
unresolved
if no pair of opposite vertices of the square is connected by a monochromatic path in
Q_{n}
.

Thus, unresolved squares are precisely the square faces that cannot be resolved by adding a diagonal corresponding to an already existing monochromatic connection. Extending the preceding argument gives a useful consequence of
Theorem

1.2
, proved as
Section

2
at the end of
Section

2
: if an antipodally symmetric induced
2
-skeleton is simply connected and contains no unresolved square, then the original colouring of the hypercube already contains a monochromatic antipodal path.

We further build upon this observation and obtain some quantitative results. In order to do so, we describe some simply-connected and centrally symmetric
2
-skeleta of the hypercube. In
Section

3
, we prove simple-connectivity results for induced
2
-skeleta of consecutive layers of the cube. In particular, the
2
-skeleton induced by any three consecutive layers is simply connected, and the same holds for any interval of at least three consecutive layers.
By combining
Section

2
with the simple connectivity of middle-layer subcomplexes and a counting argument over their translates, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
n\geq 3
. There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that, if an antipodal
2
-edge-colouring of

contains fewer than
C\sqrt{n}

Although unresolved squares are conceptually useful, they are global objects: deciding whether a square is unresolved may be essentially as difficult as finding a monochromatic antipodal path directly. Still, the framework gives a quantitative sufficient condition. Note that the unresolved property in the following statement may also be replaced by the simple property, which provides a quantitative version of the result by Feder and Subi
[
2
]
.

2.
Monochromatic paths in simplicial complexes

The goal of this section is to prove
Theorem

1.2
. We then derive the hypercube consequence,
Section

2
, which is the form used in the later applications. We first recall the following result about simply-connected simplicial complexes.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
[
8
]
(Proposition 5.3.2)]
There is no continuous
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant map from a centrally symmetric, simply connected complex
\|\Delta\|
to the circle
S^{1}
equipped with the antipodal involution.

Intuition behind the lemma.

The statement may be viewed as a generalisation of the familiar consequence of the Borsuk–Ulam theorem that there is no antipodal-equivariant map
S^{2}\to S^{1}
. The essential point is that
\|\Delta\|
is simply connected, so any circle-valued map on
\|\Delta\|
can be “unwrapped”: instead of recording only an angle modulo
2\pi
, one can choose a continuous real-valued angle. Thus, if an equivariant map
f:\|\Delta\|\to S^{1}
existed, we could write

f(x)=e^{i\theta(x)}

for some continuous function
\theta:\|\Delta\|\to\mathbb{R}
. Equivariance would then imply that passing from (x) to its antipodal point changes this lifted angle by an odd multiple of
\pi
. Since this change is continuous and takes values in a discrete set, it must be constant on
\|\Delta\|
. But applying the involution twice returns to the original point, while the lifted angle would have changed by twice an odd multiple of
\pi
, a nonzero real number. This shows why no such equivariant map can exist.
∎

Next, we use an obstruction formulated in terms of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant maps. The next proposition extracts such a map from any colouring with no monochromatic antipodal path.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\Delta
be as described in
Theorem

1.2
and let
\Delta_{2}
be its
2
-skeleton. If there is no monochromatic path connecting a vertex to its antipode, then there exists a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant continuous map
g\colon\|\Delta_{2}\|\to S^{1}
, where
S^{1}
is equipped with the antipodal involution.

Proof.

First, note that
\|\Delta\|
is simply connected if and only if
\|\Delta_{2}\|
is as well. Let us denote the red components (including singleton vertices) by
R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{m}
and the blue components canonically by
B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{m}
, so that
\tau(R_{i})=B_{i}
. Since each vertex belongs to exactly one red and one blue component, we assign to every vertex
v
a pair
(i(v),j(v))
such that
v\in R_{i(v)}\cap B_{j(v)}
.

We now construct a vertex map
F\colon V(\Delta_{2})\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. Choose
m
distinct points
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
on the upper open semicircle of the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; for instance,
p_{k}=(\cos\theta_{k},\sin\theta_{k})
with
0<\theta_{k}<\pi
. Define

F(v)=p_{i(v)}-p_{j(v)}.

Because
i(v)\neq j(v)
for all vertices, we have
F(v)\neq 0
. Moreover,
F
is equivariant since
(i(\tau(v)),j(\tau(v)))=(j(v),i(v))
.

Next, we show that
F
extends linearly over each simplex without hitting the origin. Let
\sigma=\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
be a
2
-simplex. Among its three edges, at least two have the same colour, and those two edges already involve all three vertices. Hence either all three vertices lie in the same red component or all three lie in the same blue component. The two cases are symmetric, so assume that all three vertices lie in the same red component
R_{c}
. Then
i(v_{1})=i(v_{2})=i(v_{3})=c
, and the image points are

F(v_{k})=p_{c}-p_{j(v_{k})}\qquad(k\in\{1,2,3\}).

Taking the dot product with
p_{c}
gives

(p_{c}-p_{j(v_{k})})\cdot p_{c}=1-p_{j(v_{k})}\cdot p_{c}>0,

because the points
p_{i}
are distinct unit vectors on the semicircle. Thus all three image points lie in the open half-plane
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x\cdot p_{c}>0\}
, so their convex hull avoids the origin. Consequently,
F
extends affinely over each
2
-simplex to a continuous map

f\colon\|\Delta_{2}\|\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}.

Moreover, this affine extension is equivariant: if
x=\sum_{r}\lambda_{r}v_{r}
in a simplex, then
\tau(x)=\sum_{r}\lambda_{r}\tau(v_{r})
and hence
f(\tau(x))=-f(x)
. Finally, normalising gives a continuous map

g(x)=\frac{f(x)}{\|f(x)\|}\colon\|\Delta_{2}\|\to S^{1}.

It follows that
g(\tau(x))=-g(x)
for all
x\in\|\Delta_{2}\|
. Hence
g
is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Assume, for a contradiction, that no monochromatic path connects a vertex to its antipode. By
Section

2
, there exists a continuous

-equivariant map

∎

We now translate
Theorem

1.2
back to the original hypercube problem. This is the point at which the global definition of an unresolved square is used: every resolved square can be triangulated by a diagonal whose colour is witnessed by an existing monochromatic path in the cube.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X\subseteq V(Q_{n})
be a set of vertices such that
\tau(v)\in X
whenever
v\in X
, and suppose that the induced
2
-skeleton on
X
is simply connected. If an antipodal
2
-edge-colouring of
Q_{n}
contains no unresolved square inside this induced
2
-skeleton, then there exists

such that

and
\tau(v)
are connected by a monochromatic path in

.

Proof.

Let
K
be the induced
2
-skeleton on
X
. Since no square of

is unresolved, every square
S\subseteq K
has a pair of opposite vertices

which are connected by a monochromatic path in
Q_{n}
.

For each antipodal pair of squares

, choose such a pair

in

, add the diagonal

, and colour it by the colour of a chosen monochromatic path from

to

. In the opposite square

, add the diagonal
\tau(a)\tau(b)
and colour it with the opposite colour. This is valid because the antipodal image of a monochromatic path is a monochromatic path of the opposite colour. Thus, we obtain an antipodal colouring of the
1
-skeleton of a centrally symmetric triangulation
\Delta
of

.

The underlying space of
\Delta
is the same as that of

, so it is simply connected. Hence, by

, there is a monochromatic path in
\Delta
connecting some

\tau(v)
. Finally, replace every added diagonal used by this path with the corresponding monochromatic path in

. Since each replacement has the same colour as the diagonal it replaces, we obtain a monochromatic walk in
Q_{n}
from
v
to
\tau(v)
. Removing repeated vertices if necessary gives a monochromatic path.
∎

3.
Simply connected subsets of
Q_{n}

In this section, we describe some simply connected subsets of
Q_{n}
and prove
Theorem

1.6
. As described in the Introduction and proved in the previous section, this provides us with structural information about which antipodal colourings contain a monochromatic path connecting antipodal vertices. We start by examining consecutive layers of the hypercube. We will be particularly interested in the middle-layer cases obtained by taking
n=2k
and the layers
(k-1),k,(k+1)
, or by taking
n=2k+1
and the layers
(k-1),k,(k+1),(k+2)
. In both cases these layers form centrally symmetric complexes. We first prove that the
2
-skeletons of three consecutive layers are simply connected.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
0\leq r\leq n-2
, and let
X
be the set of vertices of
Q_{n}
whose Hamming distance from
[0]^{n}
belongs to
\{r,r+1,r+2\}
. Then the
2
-skeleton induced by
X
is simply connected.

To prove
Section

3
, it suffices to consider only combinatorial edge paths in the
1
-skeleton of
X
using a standard cellular approximation argument (see Hatcher
[
4
]
Thm. 4.8 and Cor. 4.12). This allows us to represent an oriented loop
\gamma
as a cyclic word
w
over the alphabet
e_{i}
for
i\in[n]
, which denotes that we have moved in the
i
-th direction. Two words
w
and
u
are equivalent, written
w\approx u
, if the corresponding edge paths are homotopic. We observe that each
e_{i}
appears in
w
an even number of times and that
e_{i}e_{i}\approx 1
is essentially a backtrack. The following lemma describes the relevant commutation relation for these words.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\gamma
be a closed edge-path in
X
, and let
e_{i},e_{j}
be two coordinates appearing
consecutively in the cyclic word of
\gamma
.

•

If
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
meet at a vertex
v
in the
(r+1)
-st layer, then

e_{i}e_{j}\approx e_{j}e_{i}
if and only if the two edges do not both go from
v
to the same

•

If
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
meet at a vertex
v
in the
r

(r+2)
-nd layer, then

e_{i}e_{j}\approx e_{j}e_{i}
.

Proof.

The two letters commute exactly when the square spanned by the corresponding coordinate
directions lies entirely in the induced
2
-skeleton on
X
.

First suppose that
v
lies in the middle layer
r+1
. The relevant square is

C=\{v,\ v+e_{i},\ v+e_{j},\ v+e_{i}+e_{j}\}.

This square is contained in
X
if and only if

and

lie in different boundary layers, in which case

lies again
in the middle layer. Then the path

can be homotoped inside

v+e_{i}\to v+e_{i}+e_{j}\to v+e_{j}
, which is exactly the relation
e_{i}e_{j}\approx e_{j}e_{i}
.

Now suppose that
v
lies in the
r
-th layer; the
(r+2)
-nd layer is analogous.
Then the same square

has

and

in the

(r+1)
-st layer and

in the
(r+2)
-nd layer, so

. Therefore the

and

commute.
∎

We now proceed with the proof of
Section

3
.

Proof of
Section

3
.

It suffices to show that every closed combinatorial edge-loop in the
1
-skeleton of
X
is
null-homotopic in the induced
2
-skeleton.

Encode a closed oriented edge-loop
\gamma
by the cyclic word

w=e_{i_{1}}e_{i_{2}}\cdots e_{i_{t}}
, where
e_{i_{s}}
records the coordinate toggled by the

s

\gamma
is closed, each letter
e_{i}
occurs an even number of times. Choose a letter
e_{q}
whose two consecutive occurrences in the cyclic word are at minimal cyclic distance, and rotate the word so that

, where

is that minimal distance.
We prove, by induction on the pair

ordered lexicographically, that every such word is
equivalent to the empty word under the relations
e_{i}^{2}\approx 1
and

e_{i}e_{j}\approx e_{j}e_{i}
whenever the corresponding square lies in

.

By minimality of

, the letters

are pairwise distinct, and none of them equals

. If

, then

, so the word length drops by

, and the induction
closes. Assume now that

. Consider the first pair

. If
e_{q}
and

commute, then

, so the chosen two occurrences of

are now at

, and the induction applies.

Suppose instead that
e_{q}
and

do not commute. By

, their common vertex lies
in the middle layer
r+1
, and both edges go from that vertex to the same boundary layer. The two boundary cases are identical; suppose first that they both go to the lower layer
r
. Hence

, because after traversing

the

and

meet at a boundary-layer vertex, so

. Also, since

, the coordinate

is

and

and

meet at a middle-layer

and

both point to the upper layer, the same argument shows that

and

point to different boundary layers. Therefore, in either case,

In every case we eventually reduce

, and shorten the word.
Repeating this process reduces the loop word to the empty word. Hence every closed edge-loop is

2
-skeleton on

is simply connected.
∎

We next extend this inductively to any number of consecutive layers.

Proposition 3.3

Let
0\leq a\leq b\leq n

Proof.

For

, that is,

Write

The case

is connected for every
0\leq r<n
: given two vertices in the same layer, one can replace their differing coordinates one at a time by passing through the adjacent layer, and vertices in adjacent layers are connected by an edge whenever one is obtained from the other by changing a single coordinate. Thus
K[a,b]
is the union of two simply connected complexes whose intersection is non-empty and connected. By the Seifert-van Kampen theorem,
K[a,b]
is simply connected. This completes the induction.
∎

We now have everything ready to prove
Theorem

1.6
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.6
.

We prove it first for
n
even. Write
n=2k
and let

X=\{x\in V(Q_{2k}):|x|\in\{k-1,k,k+1\}\}.

By
Section

3
, the induced
2
-skeleton on
X
is simply connected, and hence so is the induced
2
-skeleton on every translate
X+v
.

Let
P
be the number of unresolved squares. Choose
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2k}
uniformly at random, and let
Y_{v}
be the number of unresolved squares contained in
X+v
. Fix a square
S
of
Q_{2k}
. Then
S\subseteq X+v
if and only if
S-v\subseteq X
. If
i,j
are the varying coordinates of
S
, this happens exactly when among the remaining
2k-2
fixed coordinates of
S-v
there are exactly
k-1
ones, since then the four vertices of
S-v
have weights
(k-1),k,k,(k+1)
. There are
\binom{2k-2}{k-1}
choices for these fixed coordinates, and the two varying coordinates are arbitrary, so
S
is contained in exactly
4\binom{2k-2}{k-1}
translates of
X
. Therefore

\Pr(S\subseteq X+v)=\frac{4\binom{2k-2}{k-1}}{2^{2k}}=\frac{\binom{2k-2}{k-1}}{2^{2k-2}}.

By linearity of expectation,
\mathbb{E}[Y_{v}]=P\binom{2k-2}{k-1}/2^{2k-2}
. Thus if

P<\frac{2^{2k-2}}{\binom{2k-2}{k-1}}=\Theta(\sqrt{k})=\Theta(\sqrt{n}),

then
\mathbb{E}[Y_{v}]<1
, so for some
v
we have
Y_{v}=0
. Hence
X+v
contains no unresolved square.

Now
Section

2
applies to
X+v
: it is antipodally symmetric, its induced
2
-skeleton is simply connected, and it contains no unresolved square. Therefore there exists
x\in X+v
such that
x
and
\tau(x)
are connected by a monochromatic path in
Q_{2k}
.

The odd case is analogous, but we include the counting detail. Write
n=2k+1
and let

X=\{x\in V(Q_{2k+1}):|x|\in\{k-1,k,k+1,k+2\}\}.

Fix a square
S
of
Q_{2k+1}
, and let its two varying coordinates be fixed. If, after translating by
v
, the remaining
2k-1
coordinates contain exactly
t
ones, then the four vertex weights of
S-v
are

t,(t+1),(t+1),(t+2).

Thus
S\subseteq X+v
if and only if
t=k-1
or
t=k
. Hence the number of choices for the fixed coordinates is

\binom{2k-1}{k-1}+\binom{2k-1}{k}=2\binom{2k-1}{k-1}.

The two varying coordinates of
v
are arbitrary, so
S
is contained in
8\binom{2k-1}{k-1}
translates of
X
. The rest is the same as for the even case.
∎
