Incidence of malignant transformation in giant cell tumor of bone following denosumab therapy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ibrahim S Alshaygy, Mishari Alanezi, Omar Aldosari, Abdulrahman AlOmar, Mohammad A. Arafah, Abdulrahman Bobseit, Motaz Alaqeel, Abdulrahman Alaseem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDenosumab has transformed the management of giant cell tumor of bone (GCTB), particularly in cases that are unresectable or surgically morbid. However, accumulating reports of malignant transformation (MT) during or after therapy have raised concern about a potential causal association. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate the incidence of MT in denosumab-treated GCTB and to characterize the clinical, radiological, and pathological features of affected patients.MethodsWe searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science to October 2025 for studies reporting denosumab-treated GCTB with MT. Clinical trials, cohort studies, case series, and case reports with original patient data were eligible. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment. A random-effects meta-analysis was used to pool MT incidence.ResultsTwenty-four studies were included in the qualitative analysis, and 11 (800 denosumab-treated patients) contributed to the meta-analysis. MT was reported in 18 patients. The pooled incidence of MT was 3% (95% CI 2–7%; I2 = 49.2%). Patients who transformed typically had high-risk disease, characterized by axial or otherwise surgically challenging sites, multiple prior local procedures, recurrent disease, or previous radiotherapy. Among cases with available timing data, MT was diagnosed from 3 months to 6 years after denosumab initiation, although most transformations occurred during active treatment or within 1–3 years of treatment initiation. MT most often manifested as high-grade osteosarcoma or undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, characterized by biphasic histology, a markedly increased Ki-67 index, and variable H3F3A status. The prognosis was poor despite aggressive multimodal treatment. Cohort studies were generally rated as fair to good quality, whereas non-randomized interventional studies had a serious to critical risk of bias.ConclusionsMT in denosumab-treated GCTB is rare and does not appear more frequent than historical baseline rates, but it predominantly affects a biologically and clinically high-risk subgroup. Vigilant clinical and radiological surveillance, prompt re-biopsy of atypical lesions, and referral to expert sarcoma centers are essential, and prospective registries with integrated molecular profiling are urgently needed.

Introduction

Giant cell tumor of bone (GCTB) is a benign, locally aggressive primary bone neoplasm, characterized by its osteolytic nature and high recurrence rates [1], [2]. It predominantly affects young adults and is most commonly found in the epiphyses of long bones [3]. Histologically, GCTB is composed of osteoclast-like giant cells, mononuclear stromal cells, and monocytic pre-osteoclasts [4]. The neoplastic component is the stromal cells, which harbor a characteristic mutation in the H3F3A gene in over 90% of cases [5]. While GCTB is benign, it can undergo malignant transformation (MT) into a high-grade sarcoma, a rare event with a historically reported incidence of 1–4% [6]. This transformation is often associated with predisposing factors such as multiple local recurrences or prior radiotherapy, a well-established risk factor for secondary malignancy in GCTB [7].

The introduction of denosumab has substantially changed the management of GCTB. Denosumab is a human monoclonal antibody that targets the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) [8], [9]. By inhibiting RANKL, denosumab suppresses the formation and function of the osteoclast-like giant cells, leading to decreased bone resorption, tumor control, and subsequent ossification of the lesion [10]. Its efficacy has been demonstrated in large-scale phase 2 trials, establishing its role as a standard of care for patients with surgically unsalvageable GCTB or those where surgery would result in severe morbidity [11], [12]. Furthermore, denosumab is widely used in the neoadjuvant setting to downstage tumors, facilitating less morbid, joint-preserving surgical procedures [13].

Despite its clinical benefits, the long-term use of denosumab has been accompanied by emerging reports of MT occurring during or after therapy. This has triggered a critical debate regarding a potential causal relationship. Some authors express significant concern, highlighting the temporal association and proposing that RANKL inhibition might alter the tumor microenvironment in a way that favors malignant progression [14], [15], [16]. One hypothesis suggests that denosumab may suppress the dominant H3F3A-mutated clone, thereby allowing a pre-existing, more aggressive H3F3A-negative subclone to proliferate [17], [18]. Conversely, other analyses, including a large expert panel review, concluded that most malignancy cases were likely misdiagnosed at baseline or coincidental, given that the patient population receiving denosumab is often enriched with high-risk features like recurrent or unresectable disease [19]. This uncertainty highlights a significant knowledge gap regarding the true incidence and nature of MT in the context of denosumab treatment. A comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence is needed to guide clinical practice, inform patient counseling, and direct future research. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to systematically evaluate the literature and address three primary objectives: 1) to determine the pooled incidence of MT in patients with GCTB treated with denosumab; 2) to characterize the demographic, clinical, radiological, and histopathological features of patients who develop MT; and 3) to summarize the clinical management and outcomes of these cases.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420261282189.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they (1) were clinical trials, prospective or retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, case-matched analyses, case series, or case reports; (2) included patients of any age with histologically confirmed GCTB; (3) involved treatment with denosumab; (4) reported data on MT of GCTB, including at least one of the following outcomes: incidence, risk factors, latency period, histological or molecular characteristics, survival, or recurrence; and (5) were published in English. Studies were excluded if they did not include patients with GCTB treated with denosumab; did not report extractable data on MT or relevant outcomes; or were reviews, editorials, commentaries, non-human, or in vitro studies.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science (WOS). The search was performed to identify all relevant articles published in the database from their inception until October 2025. The search terms were used, focusing on giant cell tumor of bone and malignant transformation, including related terms such as sarcoma and secondary malignancy. The full search strategy is presented in Supplementary File 1. Additionally, the reference lists of included articles were manually screened to identify any additional studies.

Study selection

All records identified through the database search were imported into EndNote for duplicate removal, followed by a further deduplication step using the Rayyan web application. Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining unique records to assess their potential eligibility. The full texts of all potentially relevant articles were then retrieved and assessed against the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria by the two reviewers. Any disagreements at either the screening or full-text review stage were resolved through discussion and consensus or, if necessary, by consulting a third senior reviewer.

Data collection and abstraction

Two reviewers extracted data from the included studies using a standardized Microsoft Excel sheet. The extracted information included: 1) study characteristics (first author, year, study design, sample size); 2) patient characteristics (age, sex, primary tumor location); 3) GCTB history (recurrent disease, prior treatments including surgery and radiotherapy); 4) denosumab treatment details (indication, regimen, duration); 5) characteristics of MT (time to MT, histology); and 6) post-MT management and clinical outcomes.

Assessment of study quality

Single-patient case reports and multi-patient descriptive case series were appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists for Case Reports and Case Series, which evaluate internal validity and reporting across domains such as patient characteristics, diagnostic methods, intervention details, follow-up, selection processes, and outcome reporting [20], [21]. Observational cohort studies were assessed with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies, which uses a star-based system to rate study quality in three domains: selection of groups, comparability of groups, and outcome assessment [22]. Non-randomized interventional denosumab studies (including single-arm phase II trials and non-randomized comparative clinical studies) were evaluated using the ROBINS-I (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions), a tool that judges the risk of bias across seven domains (confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results) to generate an overall risk-of-bias judgement for each study [23]. Two reviewers independently performed the quality assessments, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

A qualitative synthesis was performed to narratively summarize the findings from all included studies. Key data on study characteristics, patient demographics, treatment details, and the features of MT were tabulated. For the quantitative synthesis, a meta-analysis was conducted to calculate the pooled incidence of MT in patients treated with denosumab. The analysis was performed on studies that provided the number of MT events and the total number of patients treated with denosumab. A random-effects model was used to account for anticipated heterogeneity between studies. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic and the p-value from the chi-squared test. Potential publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots and formally tested using Egger's linear regression test of funnel plot asymmetry. We also conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, repeating the random-effects meta-analysis while omitting each study in turn and comparing the resulting pooled estimates and heterogeneity statistics with the primary model to identify any study exerting undue influence on the overall results. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 2025.50.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the ‘meta’ package.

Results

Literature search results

A total of 9556 records were identified through systematic database searches and manual screening. After the removal of 4652 duplicates, 4904 records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 4676 were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria. Full-text review was conducted for 228 articles, resulting in the exclusion of 204 studies. Ultimately, 24 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis (systematic review), and of these, 11 were included in the quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) (14,16,17,19,24–30). Study characteristics

The sample sizes ranged from small case series of six patients to large multicenter trials involving over 500 patients [12], [31]. The patient populations primarily consisted of adults and skeletally mature adolescents with histologically confirmed GCTB. Indications for denosumab therapy varied, including surgically unsalvageable disease, salvageable disease with anticipated high surgical morbidity, and neoadjuvant use to downstage tumors for less morbid procedures [11], [13], [14]. Denosumab regimens were largely consistent across studies, typically involving a 120 mg subcutaneous injection every four weeks, with loading doses on days 8 and 15 of the first cycle [12], [28]. The duration of exposure varied substantially, from short-term neoadjuvant courses of 3–6 months to long-term maintenance therapy lasting several years [16], [32]. The primary endpoints of the included studies ranged from safety and efficacy assessments to specific outcomes like surgical downstaging and local recurrence rates. Quality assessment and risk of bias

Across the included observational and descriptive studies, overall reporting quality was generally acceptable but with important limitations. Case reports and case series were usually well described, with clear presentation of patient demographics, clinical history, diagnostic work-up, denosumab exposure, and subsequent outcomes. Most case reports fulfilled nearly all JBI domains, and the majority of case series met key criteria for reliable ascertainment of GCTB, reporting of demographics and clinical information, and clear outcome description; only a minority had unclear reporting of outcomes. However, several case series did not clearly state whether patients were consecutively or completely included, and statistical methods were often minimal or poorly described, so representativeness and analytic rigour were sometimes uncertain. Cohort studies scored in the fair-to-good range on the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, with strengths in representativeness of the exposed cohort, reliable exposure ascertainment, and adequate follow-up for most studies. Where comparator groups existed, they were usually drawn from the same source population and, in some cases, matched or adjusted for key prognostic factors, but single-arm cohorts could not score on comparability, and one cohort had relatively short or incomplete follow-up. In contrast, the non-randomized interventional denosumab studies, particularly the single-arm phase II trials, were judged to be at serious to critical risk of bias on ROBINS-I. The dominant concerns were unaddressed confounding and selection into treatment, reflecting the lack of concurrent non-denosumab control groups and highly selected trial populations determined by eligibility and clinical judgement. Classification of the intervention itself was robust, as denosumab regimens were protocol-defined and well documented, but open-label designs meant that deviations from intended treatment, decisions about surgery, and outcome assessment could all be influenced by knowledge of response. Missing data, variable follow-up, and limited transparency around pre-specified analyses contributed to a moderate risk of bias in the domains of missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting, as shown in Supplementary file 2.

Incidence of malignant transformation

A total of 800 denosumab-treated patients with GCTB from 11 studies were included in the meta-analysis to estimate the pooled incidence of MT. Overall, MT was reported in 18 patients. The study-specific proportion of patients treated with MT ranged from 0% to 18% [24], [27]. The random-effects model yielded a pooled incidence of 3% (95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 2% to 7%). The heterogeneity level among the studies was moderate (I2 = 49.2%, τ2 = 0.526, p = 0.0322), as shown in Fig. 2a. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the pooled estimate was robust. The omission of any single study yielded a pooled estimate ranging from 3% to 4%, with overlapping 95% confidence intervals. However, the exclusion of the largest trial (Palmerini et al.) marginally increased the pooled estimate to 5% (95% CI, 3–8%) and eliminated heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), as shown in Fig. 2b. Demographic and clinical presentation

Patients who developed MT after denosumab treatment presented across a wide age range, from 14 to 59 years [33], [34]. While GCTB has a slight female predilection, the cases of MT were observed in both males and females without a clear gender dominance in the compiled case reports [34], [35], [36]. The primary GCTB lesions were located in various skeletal sites, including the pelvis, sacrum, femur, tibia, and skull base, which are often considered challenging or high-risk locations for surgical management [18], [33], [37].

History of GCTB and prior treatments

A significant proportion of patients who developed MT had a history of recurrent GCTB and had undergone multiple prior treatments. These treatments often included one or more surgical procedures, such as intralesional curettage or resection, before the initiation of denosumab [34], [35], [36]. For instance, one patient had undergone a laminectomy and two subsequent resections for a recurrent vertebral GCTB before starting denosumab [35]. Another patient had a history of curettage and two en bloc resections for a proximal tibia GCTB [36]. Some patients also had a history of prior radiotherapy, a well-established risk factor for secondary malignancy in GCTB [26], [33]. In one case, a patient with a skull base GCTB received 50.4Gy of local radiotherapy, which was followed by disease progression, leading to the initiation of denosumab [33]. The indication for denosumab was frequently unresectable or recurrent disease, or for neoadjuvant downstaging in surgically difficult cases [13], [25].

Denosumab treatment details

The denosumab regimen was generally consistent with the standard protocol of 120 mg administered subcutaneously every four weeks, often with loading doses on days 8 and 15 of the first month [12], [28].

Among cases with extractable exposure information, denosumab duration prior to the diagnosis of MT varied, from as short as 3 months to long-term maintenance therapy lasting several years [16], [32]. In one case series, the mean lag time from the start of denosumab to MT was 7 months [31]. Another case of MT occurred in a patient on a de-escalated 12-weekly dosing schedule, 2.3 years after the first dose [32]. Additionally, MT was reported after 4 years of continuous denosumab treatment in a patient with metastatic GCTB [35].

Histopathological and molecular features of malignancy

The most common histologies of the malignant component were high-grade osteosarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS) [36], [37], [38]. Other reported histologies included fibrosarcoma [30]. Histological examination of the transformed lesions often revealed a biphasic pattern, with areas of residual, denosumab-treated GCTB (characterized by fibrosis, new bone formation, and depletion of giant cells) juxtaposed with a high-grade sarcomatous component [37], [38]. The sarcomatous areas were defined by marked cellular pleomorphism, high mitotic activity with atypical mitoses, and necrosis [24].

Immunohistochemical and molecular analyses provided further insights. The Ki-67 proliferation index was consistently and dramatically elevated in the malignant component compared to the benign GCTB areas [17], [35]. Arndt et al. noted an increase from 5% in the GCTB to 50–60% in the sarcoma [17]. The status of the H3F3A G34W mutation, a hallmark of GCTB, was variable in the malignant component. Some studies reported persistence of the H3F3A mutation in the osteosarcoma, suggesting a clonal evolution from the original GCTB neoplastic cells [18], [28]. Conversely, other reports documented a loss of the H3F3A mutation in the sarcoma, which was present in the preceding GCTB. This finding supports the hypothesis that the sarcoma may arise from a pre-existing, H3F3A-negative subclone within the tumor microenvironment [17]. Hasenfratz et al. described one such case where the sarcoma was H3F3A-negative but shared an FGFR1 mutation with the preceding GCTB recurrence, providing a clonal link [18].

Alterations in other genes have also been reported. Nakata et al. identified a homozygous loss of the tumor suppressor genes CDKN2A and CDKN2B in a case of MT that occurred during denosumab therapy, whereas p53 expression was not significantly altered in this specific case [30].

Radiological features of malignant transformation

The expected radiological response of GCTB to denosumab includes the formation of a sclerotic rim and progressive intra-lesional ossification, leading to increased density on CT scans and stabilization or reduction in tumor size [13], [26]. Several reports have highlighted radiological signs that should raise suspicion for MT. These include a lack of the expected mineralization, the development of a new or rapidly enlarging soft-tissue mass, new areas of cortical destruction, and an aggressive periosteal reaction [19], [31]. In one case, a CT scan revealed a mass with two distinct components: a high-density, calcified area consistent with denosumab-treated GCTB, and an adjacent low-density component that was histologically confirmed to be high-grade sarcoma [36]. Another case showed enlargement of a poorly defined blastic mass after an initial period of osteosclerosis [38].

Outcomes of malignant transformation

The prognosis for patients who develop MT is generally poor. Despite aggressive multimodal treatment, including chemotherapy, radical surgery (amputation or hemipelvectomy), and radiotherapy, many patients experience disease progression [35], [36], [37]. In the large phase 2 trial, two deaths were considered treatment-related, both from sarcoma that developed during the study [12]. However, some patients have achieved disease-free survival after aggressive management [19], [36]. One patient with sacral MT and subsequent lung and heart metastases was reported to be alive at 3-year follow-up after treatment with carbon-ion radiotherapy and pazopanib [30].

Relationship to denosumab

The reported attribution of MT to denosumab varied across studies. Several reports classified events as unrelated, radiation-associated, or likely baseline misdiagnosis, particularly in patients with prior radiotherapy or atypical baseline pathology [12], [26]. The expert panel review by Palmerini et al. (2021) concluded that in 15 of 20 malignancy cases, the malignancy was definitively or likely present before denosumab was initiated, suggesting misdiagnosis at baseline rather than treatment-induced transformation [19]. Ultimately, the consensus is that while the incidence of MT does not appear to be higher than historical rates, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for MT in denosumab-treated patients, particularly those who show an atypical clinical or radiological response [19], [31].

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize the evidence on MT in patients with GCTB treated with denosumab. Across 11 studies including 800 patients, we observed a pooled incidence of MT of 3% (95% CI: 2%–7%). When combined with a detailed qualitative synthesis of 24 studies, these findings provide a comprehensive estimate of the frequency and clinical context of this rare but catastrophic complication. The central unresolved question is whether denosumab acts as a direct causative agent, facilitates the expansion of pre-existing malignant clones, or merely coincides with MT in a subgroup of intrinsically high-risk patients.

The pooled MT incidence of 3% (95% CI, 2%–7%) as broadly consistent with pre-denosumab estimates, which have generally ranged from approximately 1% to 4% in large pooled analyses, with higher rates reported in older single-center series in which radiotherapy was frequently used [19], [39]. Palmerini et al. reported malignant GCTB in 4.0% of 2315 patients (1.6% primary and 2.4% secondary malignant GCTB), whereas Dahlin et al. reported MT in 8.7% (17/195) of recurrent tumors, often after radiotherapy [19]. The present pooled estimate, therefore, does not suggest a marked increase in overall MT frequency beyond the established historical background rate. This interpretation is supported by the Palmerini et al. expert review, in which 15 of 20 malignancy reports from the phase 2 denosumab program were considered to represent misclassified primary malignant GCTB or coincidental malignancy rather than treatment-induced transformation [19]. However, our findings also provide support for the concerns raised by several smaller cohort studies and case series. Murphy et al., for example, reported a MT rate of 9.5% (2/21 patients) in their denosumab-treated cohort compared with 0% in the surgery-alone group, raising legitimate concern regarding a potential causal association [16]. Similarly, authors such as Agarwal et al. and Kumar et al. have urged caution, hypothesizing that RANKL inhibition may modify the tumor microenvironment in a manner that favors malignant progression [14], [27]. Although our pooled incidence is lower than that observed in these selected high-risk cohorts, the presence of such reports, together with the frequent temporal proximity between denosumab initiation and MT diagnosis (typically within 1–3 years), provides a compelling rationale for continued heightened clinical vigilance [33], [36], [37].

A recent systematic review by Barreto et al., which primarily evaluated the efficacy of different denosumab regimens, identified only a single case of MT among 1742 patients; however, its principal outcomes were clinical benefit and recurrence, rather than a targeted assessment of MT [40]. In contrast, the present study, with its specific focus on MT and dedicated search strategy, provides a more precise and robust estimate of this particular risk. Overall, our findings help to reconcile evidence from large clinical trial datasets and smaller observational reports showing that although the overall incidence of MT in denosumab-treated GCTB does not appear substantially elevated, the clinical and treatment characteristics of those patients who do undergo MT warrant careful scrutiny and ongoing surveillance.

The qualitative synthesis of case reports allows for the construction of a distinct clinical profile for patients with GCTB who experience MT during or after denosumab therapy. The journey typically begins with a diagnosis of GCTB in a challenging anatomical location, such as the sacrum, pelvis, or spine, where surgical resection is associated with high morbidity [18], [33], [37]. This subset is therefore already enriched for high-risk disease. A crucial feature of this profile is a history of therapeutic failure. Many patients had undergone multiple prior interventions, including repeated intralesional curettage and resections for recurrent disease, before denosumab was initiated [34], [35], [36]. In some cases, prior radiotherapy, a well-established independent risk factor for secondary sarcoma, was also part of the treatment history [38]. Consequently, denosumab is frequently employed not as a first-line modality, but rather in the context of unresectable, recurrent, or surgically complex disease, with the intention of downstaging the tumor or achieving longer-term disease control [13].

The MT event itself is frequently preceded by atypical clinical or radiological warning signs. After an initial phase of stabilization or apparent improvement, patients may develop a paradoxical escalation of pain or new worrisome imaging findings. Rather than the expected progressive ossification, radiological assessment may demonstrate a new or rapidly enlarging soft-tissue mass, new areas of cortical destruction, or a failure of the lesion to mineralize as anticipated [19], [31]. Biopsy of these suspicious areas typically reveals a high-grade sarcoma, most commonly osteosarcoma or UPS, often juxtaposed with areas of denosumab-treated GCTB [36], [37]. The subsequent clinical course is generally unfavorable. Despite aggressive multimodal management, including radical surgery and systemic chemotherapy, outcomes are poor, with many patients ultimately dying from metastatic disease, in keeping with the prognosis observed in other high-grade primary bone sarcomas [19], [35], [37].

Clinical insights and management implications

Our findings have several important implications for clinicians managing patients with GCTB. First, although a 3% incidence of MT is not markedly high, it nevertheless mandates a sustained high index of suspicion throughout the course of denosumab therapy. Lack of response or paradoxical lesion progression should not be dismissed as simple treatment failure; rather, such changes must be regarded as potential warning signs of MT. This consideration is particularly critical for patients who conform to the high-risk profile identified in our synthesis, especially those with recurrent disease, axial tumors, and a history of multiple prior interventions [18], [34], [35].

Radiological and clinical surveillance

Radiological and clinical surveillance are central to the early detection of MT. Atypical imaging findings should be regarded as key warning signs: the emergence of a new soft-tissue mass, development of lytic areas within a previously sclerotic lesion, or aggressive periosteal reactions ought to trigger prompt re-evaluation and further investigation [19], [31]. As demonstrated by Riva et al., a volumetric increase in pathological tissue during denosumab therapy represents a clear indication for repeat imaging and biopsy [41]. Any clinical deterioration, particularly renewed or worsening pain after an initial period of symptomatic improvement, should be taken seriously, carefully correlated with imaging, and not attributed to “treatment failure” without exclusion of MT.

The role of biopsy and molecular pathology

When MT is suspected, repeat biopsy is important, and histopathological assessment should specifically interrogate for high-grade sarcoma morphology and biphasic patterns. Immunohistochemical assessment of Ki-67 is particularly informative; a marked rise in the proliferation index (for example, from <10% to >50%) supports MT [17]. In parallel, molecular testing for H3F3A mutations has become an essential diagnostic adjunct. Persistence of the H3F3A mutation within the sarcomatous component supports clonal evolution from the original GCTB stromal population, whereas its loss is equally informative. The absence of H3F3A in the malignant component, as reported by Arndt et al., is consistent with a model in which denosumab suppresses the dominant H3F3A-mutant clone, permitting outgrowth of a pre-existing, H3F3A-negative (potentially more aggressive) subclone [17]. These observations emphasize the importance of baseline tumor heterogeneity and highlight that initial biopsies may not always capture the full genomic and phenotypic spectrum of the lesion. Accordingly, suspected cases of MT should be referred to expert sarcoma centers, where multidisciplinary teams can integrate clinical, radiological, and advanced pathological data to reach an accurate diagnosis and optimize management [41].

Patient counseling

Patients, particularly those with high-risk GCTB, should be counselled about the rare but serious risk of MT before initiating long-term denosumab therapy. This discussion should frame the risk in the context of the historical incidence and the significant benefits of denosumab for unresectable or morbid disease, allowing for a truly informed consent process.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the quality of the primary evidence is a significant constraint. The meta-analysis included data from non-randomized phase II trials, retrospective cohorts, and case series. As our risk of bias assessment revealed, the single-arm interventional studies were judged to be at a serious to critical risk of bias, primarily due to confounding by indication and selection bias. Patients receiving denosumab are inherently a higher-risk group, making it difficult to disentangle the effects of the drug from the natural history of aggressive GCTB. The lack of randomized controlled trials comparing denosumab to no denosumab for this specific outcome is a fundamental gap in the literature. Second, there is significant heterogeneity in reporting across the included studies. The duration of denosumab exposure, follow-up times, and the details provided on molecular and pathological features varied widely. This limited our ability to perform more granular subgroup analyses, for example, to determine if the risk of MT correlates with the duration of therapy. Third, the issue of baseline misdiagnosis, as highlighted by Palmerini et al. (2021), represents a major potential confounder [19]. It is possible that some cases reported as MT were, in fact, primary giant cell-rich sarcomas that were initially misdiagnosed as benign GCTB and were inherently unresponsive to denosumab. Finally, publication bias may be at play, with dramatic cases of MT being more likely to be published than cases with uneventful long-term follow-up, potentially inflating the perceived risk.

Future directions

Addressing the knowledge gaps identified in this review requires a multi-pronged research effort. Large, international registries are needed to prospectively collect long-term data on GCTB patients treated with denosumab. Such registries should mandate the collection of standardized clinical, radiological, and pathological data, including baseline and follow-up tissue samples for molecular analysis. This would allow for a more accurate determination of the true incidence of MT and the identification of robust predictive biomarkers.

For patients who develop MT, novel therapeutic strategies are urgently needed, as outcomes with standard sarcoma chemotherapy are poor [33], [34]. The case report by Nakata et al. of a patient responding to the multi-kinase inhibitor pazopanib is interesting [30]. Further investigation into targeted therapies based on the molecular profile of the sarcoma may offer new hope.

Conclusions

MT of GCTB in patients treated with denosumab was uncommon, with 18 events among 800 denosumab-treated patients in the meta-analysis and a pooled incidence of 3% (95% CI, 2%–7%). The available evidence does not establish a definitive causal relationship with denosumab, and many cases occurred in patients with pre-existing high-risk features, including challenging tumor location, recurrent disease, multiple local procedures, or prior radiotherapy. However, a contributory role of denosumab in selected biologically susceptible tumors cannot be excluded. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for MT in high-risk patients with atypical clinical or radiological progression. Prompt repeat biopsy with integrated histopathological and molecular assessment should be performed when MT is suspected. Prospective registries with standardized imaging, pathology review, and molecular profiling are required to define the true risk, mechanisms, and optimal surveillance strategy.
