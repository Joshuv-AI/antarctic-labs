Monads and Distributive Laws in Substructural Contexts (Extended Version)
Soichiro Fujii, Yun Chen Tsai, Yoàv Montacute, Ichiro Hasuo

Abstract
We present a categorical theory of monads and distributive laws in substructural contexts . In the study of distributive laws, the roles of (the absence of) structural rules for variable contexts have been recognized; our theory formalizes these substructural situations using Tronin’s verbal categories 𝐖 \mathbf{W} , in a uniform and presentation-independent manner. We introduce the classes of 𝐖 \mathbf{W} -operadic monads (those defined via the structural rules in 𝐖 \mathbf{W} ) and of 𝐖 \mathbf{W} -commutative monads (those invariant under the structural rules in 𝐖 \mathbf{W} ). We give a canonical construction of a distributive law S ​ T → T ​ S ST\to TS of monads on 𝐒𝐞𝐭 \mathbf{Set} ; it is applicable when S S is 𝐖 \mathbf{W} -operadic and T T is 𝐖 \mathbf{W} -commutative (under mild conditions). This accounts for many known and new distributive laws. Even when S S fails to be 𝐖 \mathbf{W} -operadic, we can refine S S and force 𝐖 \mathbf{W} -operadicity; this captures Varacca and Winskel’s construction of indexed valuations.

Introduction

Monads

In program semantics and, more generally, in system behavior modeling, the categorical notion of
monad
has been successfully used as a uniform interface to various computational
effects
. Examples of effects include non-functional
side effects
in functional programming
[
49
]
, additional behavioral layers for reactive systems such as (possibilistic or probabilistic) nondeterminism
[
2
,
21
]
, and so on. The mathematical theory of monads—together with a closely related theory of
universal algebra
—not only offers unified foundations for effects, but also enables novel programming principles such as
effect handlers

[
54
]
.

Distributive laws

In this context,
distributive laws
between monads have attracted attention. Originally a mathematical construct
[
3
]
, distributive laws enable us to
combine effects
. Specifically, given monads
S
and
T
on the category
\mathbf{Set}
of sets (in this paper, we focus on monads on
\mathbf{Set}
), a distributive law
\delta\colon ST\to TS
of
S
over
T
equips the composite
TS
with a monad structure, with the consequence that unified monadic frameworks for programming, static analysis, verification, etc. accommodate the coexistence of two different layers of effects modeled by
S
and
T
.

The question of obtaining a distributive law has intrigued many theoreticians, too. This was initiated by Plotkin’s observation that there is no distributive law
DP\to PD
of the (finitely supported) probability distribution monad
D
over the powerset monad
P
(see
[
68
]
).
This observation sparked much work: both on the positive side (obtaining distributive laws
[
11
,
52
,
17
]
) and the negative side (proving “no-go” theorems
[
29
,
71
]
).

Two approaches: weak distributive laws and refinement

On the positive side, towards obtaining a distributive law, there have mainly been two technical approaches. The first “weak distributive law” approach,
when a distributive law
ST\to TS
does not exist, finds it sufficient to have a
weak distributive law

\delta\colon ST\to TS
. Here “weak” means
\delta
’s compatibility with the unit
\eta^{S}
of
S
is dropped. A weak distributive law
\delta
induces a combination of
S
and
T
, but this time it is not given simply by the composite
TS
. Roughly speaking, this approach modifies
T
: concretely, for
S=D
and
T=P
, the combination essentially replaces
T=P
with the convex powerset monad over the Eilenberg–Moore category
\mathbf{Set}^{D}

[
15
,
17
,
4
,
1
,
62
]
.

The second “refinement” approach modifies
S
instead. It finds a reason for the failure of a distributive law
\delta\colon ST\to TS
in
equations
in (an equational presentation of)
S
, and thus proposes to use a
refinement

\widehat{S}
of
S
—obtained by dropping the problematic equations—in place of
S
. This approach is initiated
in
[
68
]
for specific monads (variations of
D
and
P
—see
\cref
subsec:IV-revisited).
Generalization of this approach is sought in
[
11
,
52
]
.
We follow this line of work.

Categorical theory of substructural contexts for monads and distributive laws

In this paper, we introduce a general framework for the aforementioned “refinement” approach to distributive laws, centered around the idea of
substructural contexts
. Our categorical framework uses
verbal categories

[
64
]
for modeling substructural contexts.

The relevance of structural rules (exchange, weakening, and contraction) to algebraic theories (and thus monads) is widely acknowledged, explicitly or implicitly
[
10
,
20
,
37
]
. In particular,
[
11
,
52
,
44
]
study the roles of structural rules in distributive laws. Our framework here offers a unifying categorical picture parametrized by a verbal category
\mathbf{W}
.

We sketch our theory in the coming paragraphs.

Substructural contexts and verbal categories (
\cref
sec:notions-of-operad)

Restricting structural rules—
exchange
(E),
weakening
(W), and
contraction
(C)—in variable contexts is a common tool in logic.
In the categorical study of algebraic theories, operads, and binding syntax, it is common to model the application of structural rules with 1) morphisms of
\mathbf{F}
, the category of finite ordinals and functions between them
[
13
]
, and 2) their string diagram presentation
[
10
,
37
]
. For example, the context transformation
from
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}\vdash p(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
to

x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\vdash p(x_{1},x_{3},x_{1})
—obtained with E, W, and C for a term
p
—is represented by
the function
\alpha\colon 3\to 4

with
\alpha(0)=\alpha(2)=1
and
\alpha(1)=3
;
the corresponding string diagram (in which we have
\alpha(i)=j
iff
i
on the right is connected to
j
on the left) is depicted on the left of (
1
) below.

The structural rules E, W, and C correspond to the three “parts” of the string diagram as on the right of (

), respectively (see also
[

for a related discussion).

Conveniently, considering a
substructural context
—i.e. prohibiting the use of certain structural rules—corresponds to singling out a wide subcategory of
\mathbf{F}
. Concretely, allowing the rules designated below on the left corresponds to the subcategories of
\mathbf{F}
on the right.

\emptyset

\{\mathrm{E},\mathrm{C}\}

Here,

\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{id}}
has only identity functions as morphisms;

\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{m,inj}}
has monotone injections (with respect to the total order
0<1<\dots<n-1
);

\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{bij}}
has bijections;

\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{inj}}
has injections;

\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{surj}}
has surjections.
(The settings
\{\mathrm{C}\}
and

are ill-behaved and thus omitted; see

The notion of
verbal category

[

]
axiomatizes (wide) subcategories of
\mathbf{F}
suited for modeling substructural contexts. Examples include the six categories in (
2
) (see also
\cref
ex:verbal-cats).

\mathbf{W}
-operads and their monads (
\cref
sec:notions-of-operad)

The notion of
\mathbf{W}
-operad
in
[
64
]
embodies the idea of “algebraic theories in the
\mathbf{W}
-substructural context”. Its definition follows the same format—with
\mathsf{id}
and
\mathsf{subst}
—as for (symmetric or non-symmetric) operads.

For our theory, we introduce the notion of
\mathbf{W}
-operadic monad
, as those which are induced by a
\mathbf{W}
-operad via a left Kan extension. This formalizes the idea of “a monad in the
\mathbf{W}
-substructural context”. (For example,
\mathbf{F}
-operadic monads coincide with finitary monads.)

\mathbf{W}
-commutative monads (
\cref
sec:notions-of-commutative-monad)

Our construction of a distributive law
ST\to TS
(
\cref
thm:main-detailed) requires, very roughly speaking, that

•

S
is a monad defined using the structural rules in
\mathbf{W}
, and

•

T
’s effect is invariant under the structural rules in
\mathbf{W}
.

We just formalized the former as
\mathbf{W}
-operadic monads; for the latter, we introduce the notion of
\mathbf{W}
-commutative monad
.
The definition of
\mathbf{W}
-commutativity is by a suitable diagram, one similar to those used in
[
52
,
11
,
51
]
. It unifies some known notions such as
(ordinary)
commutativity

[
30
]
,
affinity

[
32
]
, and
relevance

[
20
]
.

Canonical distributive laws and application (
\cref
sec:dist-law,sec:monad-to-operad,sec:applications)

One of our main results in this paper is
\cref
thm:main-detailed where, as announced above, we present a general construction of a distributive law
\delta\colon ST\to TS
,
assuming that

•

S
is a
\mathbf{W}
-operadic monad, and

•

T
is a
\mathbf{W}
-commutative monad.

The construction is categorical and canonical, along the coend description of (the left Kan extension used in) a
\mathbf{W}
-operadic monad.

The construction of this canonical
\delta
is uniform for any verbal category
\mathbf{W}
. An additional condition is required for
\delta
to be compatible with the multiplication
\mu^{T}
of
T
: it is that
\mathbf{W}
accommodates the exchange rule (E)—unless
S
is so lean that it is generated by operations of arity
\leq 1
. See
\cref
thm:main-detailed for a precise statement.

Given the last condition (of accommodating (E)), it is natural for us to focus on the following four verbal categories.

\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{bij}}
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{surj}}
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{inj}}
\mathbf{F}

(3)

We also show the following
monotonicity
results:

•

\mathbf{W}
-operadicity is up-closed (i.e. if
S
is
\mathbf{W}
-operadic and
\mathbf{W}\subseteq\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
, then
S
is
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
-operadic,
\cref
extension-of-operad(2)); and

•

\mathbf{W}
-commutativity is down-closed (
\cref
rmk:commutativity-monotonicity).

Therefore, for the application of the general construction in
\cref
thm:main-detailed, we can look for
\mathbf{W}
in the intersection of the upper set of
S
’s
\mathbf{W}
-operadicity and the lower set of
T
’s
\mathbf{W}
-commutativity.

This “find an intersection” strategy has two usages. The first usage is negative: given a pair
(S,T)
, the empty intersection of the above two sets shows that the canonical distributive law in
\cref
thm:main-detailed does not work. While this does not prove a no-go result (other distributive laws may exist), it is often easy to check and thus a useful red flag. Of course, the intersection is empty for the combinations with known no-go results.

The second use is a positive one: when the intersection is empty, we can modify
S
or
T
for a nonempty intersection, so that the canonical distributive law works. While modifying
T
is possible—techniques for forcing
\mathbf{W}
-commutativity on a monad are studied for some
\mathbf{W}
, see e.g.
[
20
,
6
,
42
]
—we are interested in modifying
S
in this paper. This modification of
S
must extend the upper set of
\mathbf{W}
-operadicity downwards, so that a new monad relies on fewer structural rules.

To do so, we introduce a categorical construction, namely the
\mathbf{W}
-operadic refinement

\mathrm{Rf}_{\mathbf{W}}(S)
of a monad
S
(
\cref
sec:monad-to-operad). It is the monad induced, in the Kan extension-way described in
\cref
sec:notions-of-operad, by the
\mathbf{W}
-operad given by
restricting

S
. The refinement
\mathrm{Rf}_{\mathbf{W}}(S)
is
\mathbf{W}
-operadic by construction. We show that the
indexed valuation monad

IV
in
[
68
]
—the first example of the “refinement” approach for distributive laws—is indeed an instance of
\mathbf{W}
-operadic refinement.

Preliminaries

The set of all natural numbers (or finite ordinals) is denoted by
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
.
Each
n\in\mathbb{N}
is identified with the
n
-element set
\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
.
The category of sets and functions between them is denoted by
\mathbf{Set}
.
It has a symmetric monoidal structure
(1,\times)
via products.
For any set
X
, we denote by
!_{X}\colon X\to 1
the unique function from
X
to
1
.

Since our focus in this paper is on monads on
\mathbf{Set}
, by a
monad
we shall always mean a
monad on
\mathbf{Set}
.
Specifically, a
monad

{\mathbb{T}}
(on
\mathbf{Set}
) is a triple
{\mathbb{T}}=(T,\eta,\mu)
where
T\colon\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
is a functor (called the
functor part
of
{\mathbb{T}}
) and
\eta\colon\mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{Set}}\to T
and
\mu\colon TT\to T
are natural transformations (called the
unit
and
multiplication
of
{\mathbb{T}}
, respectively), such that
\mu\circ T\eta=\mathrm{id}_{T}=\mu\circ\eta T
and
\mu\circ T\mu=\mu\circ\mu T
hold.
Here are some examples of monads.

Example 1

(
C
-exception monad
{\mathbb{E}}^{C}
)
.

Any set
C
has a unique monoid structure
(C,e_{C}\colon 0\to C,\nabla_{C}\colon C+C\to C)
with respect to coproducts.
This induces a monad
{\mathbb{E}}^{C}
called the
C
-exception monad
. Its functor part is
C+(-)\colon\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
, and its unit and multiplication are
\eta_{X}=e_{C}+X\colon X\to C+X
and

\mu_{X}=\nabla_{C}+X\colon C+C+X\to C+X
for each set
X
.
\lipicsEnd

Example 2

(
C
-reader monad
{\mathbb{R}}^{C}
)
.

Any set
C
also admits a unique comonoid structure
(C,!_{C}\colon C\to 1,\Delta_{C}\colon C\to C\times C)
with respect to products.
It induces the
C
-reader monad

{\mathbb{R}}^{C}
in the same way as in
\cref
ex:exception-monad: its functor part
is
(-)^{C}\colon\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
, and its unit and multiplication
are
\eta_{X}=X^{!_{C}}\colon X\to X^{C}
and
\mu_{X}=X^{\Delta_{C}}\colon(X^{C})^{C}\cong X^{C\times C}\to X^{C}
.

\lipicsEnd

Example 3

(
{\mathsf{M}}
-writer monad
{\mathbb{W}}^{{\mathsf{M}}}
)
.

Let
{\mathsf{M}}=(M,1\in M,\cdot\colon M\times M\to M)
be a monoid (with respect to products).
It induces the monad
{\mathbb{W}}^{{\mathsf{M}}}
called the
{\mathsf{M}}
-writer monad
, whose functor part is
M\times(-)\colon\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
, and whose unit and multiplication are defined by
\eta_{X}(x)=(1,x)
and
\mu_{X}(m,n,x)=(mn,x)
for each set
X
,
x\in X
, and
m,n\in M
.

\lipicsEnd

Recall that a
semiring
is a tuple
{\mathsf{S}}=(S,0\in S,+\colon S\times S\to S,1\in S,\cdot\colon S\times S\to S)
where
S
is a set,
(S,0,+)
is a commutative monoid,
(S,1,\cdot)
is a monoid, and
such that
\cdot\colon(S,0,+)\times(S,0,+)\to(S,0,+)
is a bihomomorphism of monoids, i.e., for all
x,y,z\in S
,

x\cdot 0=0
,
x\cdot(y+z)=x\cdot y+x\cdot z
,

0\cdot z=0
, and
(x+y)\cdot z=x\cdot z+y\cdot z
hold.
The semiring
{\mathsf{S}}
is
commutative
if
(S,1,\cdot)
is a commutative monoid.

Example 4

(
{{\mathsf{S}}}
-multiset monad
{\mathbb{M}}^{{\mathsf{S}}}
)
.

Let
{\mathsf{S}}=(S,0,+,1,\cdot)
be a semiring. We define the monad
{\mathbb{M}}^{{\mathsf{S}}}
called the
{\mathsf{S}}
-multiset monad
. The functor part
M^{{\mathsf{S}}}
of
{\mathbb{M}}^{{\mathsf{S}}}
maps each set
X
to the set
M^{{\mathsf{S}}}X
of all functions
p\colon X\to S
such that the set
\{\,x\in X\mid p(x)\neq 0\,\}

(called the
support

\supp p
of
p
)
is finite, and maps each function
f\colon X\to Y
to the function
M^{{\mathsf{S}}}f\colon M^{{\mathsf{S}}}X\to M^{{\mathsf{S}}}Y
defined for each
p\in M^{{\mathsf{S}}}X
by

\bigl((M^{{\mathsf{S}}}f)(p)\bigr)(y)=\textstyle\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(y)\,\cap\,\supp p}p(x).

We can also regard
p\in M^{{\mathsf{S}}}X
as a formal linear combination

\textstyle\sum_{x\in\supp p}p(x)\cdot x

(4)

of elements of
X
with coefficients from
{\mathsf{S}}
; from this viewpoint, the function
M^{{\mathsf{S}}}f
maps (
4
) to

\textstyle\sum_{x\in\supp p}p(x)\cdot f(x)
.

The unit
\eta_{X}
and multiplication
\mu_{X}
of
{\mathbb{M}}^{{\mathsf{S}}}
at
X\in\mathbf{Set}
are given as follows.
For each
x\in X
,
\eta_{X}(x)\in M^{{\mathsf{S}}}X
is defined by

\bigl(\eta_{X}(x)\bigr)(x^{\prime})=1

if
x^{\prime}=x
, and

=0
otherwise.
That is,
\eta_{X}(x)
is
x
regarded as a trivial linear combination.
For each
\varphi\in M^{{\mathsf{S}}}M^{{\mathsf{S}}}X
,
\mu_{X}(\varphi)\in M^{{\mathsf{S}}}X
is defined by

\bigl(\mu_{X}(\varphi)\bigr)(x)=\textstyle\sum_{p\in\supp\varphi}\varphi(p)\cdot p(x)
.
This flattens a “linear combination of linear combinations”
\varphi
into a linear combination.

Here are some instances of
{\mathbb{M}}^{{\mathsf{S}}}
.

{bracketenumerate}

When
{\mathsf{S}}

{\mathsf{N}}=(\mathbb{N},0,+,1,\cdot)
of natural numbers,
{\mathbb{M}}^{{\mathsf{N}}}
is called the
(finite) multiset monad

{\mathbb{M}}=(M,\eta,\mu)
. For a set
X
,
MX

X
.

When
{\mathsf{S}}
is the semiring
{\mathsf{2}}=(\{0,1\},0,\vee,1,\wedge)
,
{\mathbb{M}}^{{\mathsf{2}}}
is called the

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathrm{fin}}=(P_{\mathrm{fin}},\eta,\mu)
.
For each set
X
,
P_{\mathrm{fin}}X
is the set of all finite subsets of
X
.

When
{\mathsf{S}}
is the semiring
{\mathsf{R}}_{\geq 0}=(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},0,+,1,\cdot)

{\mathbb{M}}^{{\mathsf{R}}_{\geq 0}}
is called the

3.3
The Monad Induced by a
\mathbf{W}
-Operad

In this section, we show that every
\mathbf{W}
-operad

{\mathbb{O}}
induces a monad
\mathrm{Mnd}({\mathbb{O}})
on
\mathbf{Set}
.
Intuitively,
\mathrm{Mnd}({\mathbb{O}})
is the (finitary) monad corresponding to the algebraic theory expressed by
{\mathbb{O}}
.
One way of making this precise is to proceed as follows.
Define the notion of
{\mathbb{O}}
-algebra
in
\mathbf{Set}
(as in
[
65
, Def. 1.1]
), show that the forgetful functor
\mathbf{Alg}({\mathbb{O}})\to\mathbf{Set}
from the category
\mathbf{Alg}({\mathbb{O}})
of
{\mathbb{O}}
-algebras is monadic, and then let
\mathrm{Mnd}({\mathbb{O}})
be the induced monad on
\mathbf{Set}
.

Instead, here we use left Kan extensions
to define
\mathrm{Mnd}({\mathbb{O}})
. This is useful for describing the canonical distributive laws later.

Definition 13

(
\mathrm{Mnd}({\mathbb{O}})
)
.

Let
\mathbf{W}
be a verbal category and
{\mathbb{O}}=(O,\mathsf{id},\mathsf{subst})
be a
\mathbf{W}
-operad. The monad
\mathrm{Mnd}({\mathbb{O}})

induced by

{\mathbb{O}}
is

\mathrm{Mnd}({\mathbb{O}})=(\mathrm{Lan}_{J}O,\eta,\mu),

(9)

using the unit and multiplication described below.
\lipicsEnd

Here,
\mathrm{Lan}_{J}O
in (
9
) is
the left Kan extension of

O
along the inclusion functor
J
:

\mathbf{W}
\mathbf{Set}
\mathbf{Set}
O
J
\mathrm{Lan}_{J}O
\Rightarrow

(10)

Concretely, for each

\textstyle\int^{n\in\mathbf{W}}O_{n}\times X^{n}

(cf. e.g.
[

\textstyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}O_{n}\times X^{n},

(12)

whose element we write as
\bigl(p,(x_{i})_{i\in n}\bigr)
where
p\in O_{n}
and
x_{i}\in X
(with

), or sometimes as

The set

eqn:LanJO-coproduct

\sim

\bigl(p,(x_{\alpha(i)})_{i\in m}\bigr)\sim\bigl(\alpha p,(x_{j})_{j\in n}\bigr)

Given a function

The multiplication

3.4
Change of Base

When one verbal category
\mathbf{W}

\mathbf{W}^{\prime}

\mathbf{W}
-operads and
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
-operads—this is much like the one in

-operads and monads. We describe these constructions, together with their consequence on the relationship with monads.

Let
\mathbf{W}

\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
be

\mathbf{W}
is a subcategory of
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}

K\colon\mathbf{W}\to\mathbf{W}^{\prime}

\mathbf{W}^{\prime}

of
{{\mathbb{O}}}^{\prime}
along

is a
\mathbf{W}

6.3
The Indexed Valuation Monad Revisited

As mentioned in
\cref
sec:intro, in order to model the combination of probability and nondeterminism, Varacca and Winskel
[
68
]
consider the existence of a distributive law
VP_{\mathrm{fin}}^{+}\to P_{\mathrm{fin}}^{+}V
of the valuation monad
{\mathbb{V}}
(
\cref
ex:MS(3)) over the nonempty finite powerset monad
{\mathbb{P_{\mathrm{fin}}^{+}}}
(
\cref
ex:AS(1)).
Such a distributive law does not exist, but they obtain a distributive law
IVP_{\mathrm{fin}}^{+}\to P_{\mathrm{fin}}^{+}IV
of the
indexed valuation monad

\mathbb{IV}
over
{\mathbb{P_{\mathrm{fin}}^{+}}}
.
They construct
\mathbb{IV}
by removing “problematic” equational axioms from an equational presentation of
\mathbb{V}
.

Since
{\mathbb{P_{\mathrm{fin}}^{+}}}
is
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{inj}}
-commutative (
\cref
ex:aff-mnd), from our point of view, a natural approach is to replace
{\mathbb{V}}
with its
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{inj}}
-refinement
\mathrm{Rf}_{\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{inj}}}({\mathbb{V}})
.

In this case, these two approaches lead to the same result:

Proposition 61
.

{bracketenumerate}

The
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{inj}}
-operadic refinement
\mathrm{Rf}_{\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{inj}}}({\mathbb{V}})
of the valuation monad
{\mathbb{V}}
is the indexed valuation monad
\mathbb{IV}
.

The distributive law of
\mathbb{IV}
over
{\mathbb{P_{\mathrm{fin}}^{+}}}
obtained in
[
68
, § 4.3]
coincides with the one obtained from
\cref
thm:main-detailed. ∎

The key to the proof of
\cref
IV-as-Finj-operadic-refinement(1) is to observe that the set

IV(X)

of finite indexed valuations
[
68
, Def. 4.3]

can be written as the coend

IV(X)=\textstyle\int^{n\in\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{inj}}}M^{{\mathsf{R}}_{\geq 0}}n\times X^{n}
.
Props
61
(1) and
59
(2) yield the following novel characterization of
\mathbb{IV}
.

Corollary 62
.

The indexed valuation monad
\mathbb{IV}
is isomorphic to the
{\mathsf{N}}_{0}[({\mathsf{R}}_{\geq 0})_{\mathrm{mult,0}}]
-multiset monad
{\mathbb{M}}^{{\mathsf{N}}_{0}[({\mathsf{R}}_{\geq 0})_{\mathrm{mult,0}}]}

Remark 63
.

In general, simply removing “problematic” axioms from an equational presentation of an algebraic theory does not give the same result as the
\mathbf{W}
-operadic refinement.
(An easy way to see this is to notice that the
\mathbf{W}
-operadic refinement construction is not idempotent in general.)
In fact, the former construction is presentation-dependent, whereas our
\mathbf{W}
-operadic refinement construction is a universal construction independent of presentations (see
\cref
Mnd-W-Opd-adjunction).
We leave it for future work to see whether the similar modification of the theory done in
[
11
, § 5]
can be captured by a suitable
\mathbf{W}
-operadic refinement.

We follow the storyline sketched in
\cref

•

•

[

.

{bracketenumerate}

Similarly, one can show that
