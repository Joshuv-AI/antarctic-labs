Plasma LC-HRMS metabolomics in parkinsonian syndromes: comparative evaluation of sample preparation protocols for exploratory metabolic signatures
Authors: Erika Esposito, Alessandro Perrone, Giovanna Lopane, Rosalba Vitagliano, Nicolò Interino, Alice Caravelli, Chiara Cancellerini, Giovanna Calandra-Buonaura, Jessica Fiori, Manuela Contin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Untargeted plasma metabolomics by LC-HRMS is increasingly used to explore candidate biomarkers in neurodegenerative disease, yet the analytical output and downstream biological interpretability remain highly dependent on end-to-end workflow choices. This limits reproducibility and cross-study comparability in parkinsonian syndromes where objective fluid biomarkers for differential diagnosis are still lacking. Here, we implemented a QC-anchored, untargeted LC-HRMS plasma platform to compare sample preparation strategies and to explore disease-associated metabolic patterns across Parkinson’s disease (PD), multiple system atrophy–parkinsonian subtype (MSA-P), and progressive supranuclear palsy–parkinsonism (PSP-P) in a prospective single-center cohort (n = 102; 57 PD, 19 MSA-P, 26 PSP-P). Three protein precipitation-based workflows were compared within a harmonized analytical framework: tube precipitation, 96-well precipitation-filtration, and 96-well phospholipid removal. Integrated preparation QCs, system QCs, and blanks supported performance monitoring, while MS2 annotation enabled interpretation of disease-associated signals. Phospholipid removal provided the most favorable trade-off for discovery and quantitation, delivering reduced phospholipid-related chromatographic background, improved quantitative behavior, and a higher density of MS2-supported annotations despite a modest reduction in raw feature counts. Differential abundance was assessed using empirical Bayes moderated linear models implemented in limma. Pairwise contrasts were defined for PD vs. MSA-P, PD vs. PSP-P, and MSA-P vs. PSP-P, and P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate (FDR). Across workflows, the most robust exploratory disease-associated pattern was observed for PD versus PSP-P, whereas MSA-P comparisons yielded limited FDR-significant features. Because of the absence of MSI Level 1 annotation, the hypothesis-generating pathway-level was based on exploratory metabolomics interpretation, requiring targeted confirmation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00216-026-06669-x.

Introduction

The identification of reliable and clinically actionable biomarkers remains a significant unmet need in neurodegenerative diseases, with the potential to improve diagnostic stratification, refine prognostic assessment, and support personalized interventions [1, 2]. Untargeted metabolomics based on high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) has been integrated into clinical research and translational studies to characterize disease-associated metabolic alterations and to prioritize candidate biomarkers [3–5]. However, in complex biofluids, the analytical output of untargeted workflows—feature yield, chemical-class coverage, precision, and downstream interpretability—remains strongly dependent on pre-analytical variables and on the end-to-end workflow, from sample preparation to data processing and annotation [6–8]. This dependency represents a key limitation for biomarker discovery efforts and can limit cross-study comparability, underlining the need for fit-for-purpose benchmarking and standardization of the pipeline [9–21].

These challenges are particularly relevant in parkinsonian syndromes, where objective fluid biomarkers are still lacking and differential diagnosis, especially at earlier stages, remains clinically challenging. Parkinson’s disease (PD) is pathologically characterized by dopaminergic neuron loss in the substantia nigra pars compacta and Lewy bodies enriched in misfolded α-synuclein, and clinically by motor and non-motor manifestations such as autonomic dysfunction [22–25]. The core motor triad—bradykinesia, tremor, and rigidity—is not pathognomonic and overlaps with atypical parkinsonism (AP), including multiple system atrophy-parkinsonian subtype (MSA-P) and progressive supranuclear palsy-parkinsonian phenotype (PSP-P), which frequently mimic early Parkinson’s disease but differ in disease course [26–28]. MSA-P is an α-synucleinopathy with oligodendroglial cytoplasmic inclusions and demyelination, whereas PSP-P is a tauopathy dominated by four-repeat tau; both typically progress more rapidly than PD [26, 29–32]. Therapeutic responses also differ across these disorders [22]. In routine practice, available imaging tools have limited discriminatory power at early stages, and no validated fluid biomarker is currently available to support confident stratification across these disorders [33]. These considerations motivate the search for robust disease-associated molecular features, ideally in minimally invasive, scalable biospecimens such as plasma.

Plasma is attractive for large clinical studies because it is readily accessible, but it is analytically challenging due to high protein and lipid content, a wide dynamic range, and susceptibility to matrix effects and exogenous influences. Phospholipids can drive ion suppression and compromise coverage and repeatability, making sample preparation and QC-driven data processing key determinants of analytical performance in plasma untargeted LC-HRMS [6–8].

The aims of this study are (i) to evaluate how sample preparation and data processing choices shape data quality and downstream biological interpretation in plasma untargeted metabolomics, and (ii) to explore disease-associated metabolic patterns across PD, MSA-P, and PSP-P and prioritize robust candidate features for follow-up studies. We performed a head-to-head comparison of three protein-precipitation-based workflows and integrated workflow-specific sample preparation QCs, LC–MS system QCs, and blanks to support performance monitoring and MS2-informed annotation. Data were acquired by LC-HRMS and processed through a robust workflow comprising peak detection, alignment, normalization, MS2-informed annotation across the feature set, and statistical analysis to explore multi-metabolite signatures.

Material and methods

Subjects and selection criteria

We conducted a prospective, single-center, non-pharmacological interventional study of adults (age ≥ 18 years) at the Istituto delle Scienze Neurologiche di Bologna, IRCCS, Bologna, Italy. The study was designed to compare plasma metabolomic profiles across PD, MSA-P, and PSP-P (for brevity indicated as MSA and PSP, respectively); healthy controls were not enrolled. Inpatients and outpatients were screened and enrolled from November 2021 to April 2022. Participants were eligible if they had a diagnosis of PD, MSA, or PSP, established according to the clinical and instrumental criteria of the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society [34–36]. Exclusion criteria included refusal to participate, withdrawal of consent, and any condition that precluded the informed consent, such as inability to read, write, understand study procedures, or communicate effectively.

A total of 102 subjects, divided into 57 PD, 19 MSA, and 26 PSP, were enrolled. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Comitato Etico Indipendente dell’Area Vasta Emilia Centro (No. 843–2021-SPER-AUSLBO). All participants provided written informed consent prior to any study procedure. At enrolment, patients underwent a comprehensive neurological examination, and a Case Report Form (CRF) designed for this study was completed, including demographic data, age at symptom onset, severity of parkinsonian symptoms, treatments for parkinsonian syndrome since symptom onset, and therapies for comorbidities. Sample collection, pre-processing, and storage

Venous blood was collected in the morning into lithium-heparin tubes (5 mL) under fasting conditions and before morning medications. Tubes were kept on ice packs until pre-processing. Plasma was obtained by centrifugation at 1700 × g for 10 min (min) at 4 °C, aliquoted and immediately stored at −80 °C to arrest metabolic activity for subsequent untargeted metabolomic profiling. Aliquots were stored without freeze–thaw cycles.

Materials

LC–MS grade acetonitrile, LC–MS grade water, and LC–MS grade formic acid were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany, EU). Regenerated Cellulose (RC) Membrane 0.22 µm 4 mm Syringe Filters were purchased from Phenomenex (Torrance, CA, USA). Impact™ Protein Precipitation Plates (PP-Plate) and Phree™ Phospholipid Removal Plates (PLR-Plate) were also from Phenomenex (Torrance, CA, USA).

Sample preparation workflow

For untargeted plasma metabolomics, plasma proteins were precipitated with cold acetonitrile containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (extraction solvent) at a plasma-to-solvent ratio of 1:4. Three workflows were used.

In a tube-based protocol (PP-Tube), 100 µL plasma was mixed with 400 µL extraction solvent, vortex-mixed for 30 s, incubated at −20 °C for 15 min (min), and centrifuged at 4700 × g for 10 min at 4 °C; the supernatant was collected, dried by vacuum centrifugation at room temperature, reconstituted in 100 µL H2O/0.1% formic acid, vortex-mixed, sonicated for 5 min, centrifuged, and filtered through 0.22 µm regenerated-cellulose membranes before LC-HRMS.

In a 96-well protein-precipitation filtration plate workflow (PP-Plate), 400 µL extraction solvent was dispensed into each well, 100 µL plasma was added, vortex-mixed for 10 min, and processed under vacuum; the eluate was dried, reconstituted, sonicated, centrifuged, and filtered as above.

In a 96-well protein- and phospholipid-removal plate (PLR-Plate), 100 µL of plasma was combined with 400 µL of extraction solvent, vortex-mixed for 10 min, and processed under vacuum. The eluate was then handled identically to the tube-based protocol, including filtration through 0.22 µm membranes prior to injection.

Sample preparation quality controls (spQCs) were generated by pooling equal volumes from all individual samples before protein precipitation; for each preparative workflow, spQCs were processed in parallel with study samples to monitor the protocol performance and for compound identification via AcquireX intelligent data acquisition workflow. Mass-spectrometry quality controls (msQCs) were generated by pooling equal volumes of the sample processed within each workflow and injected every five injections to monitor LC–MS analytical stability across the run.

Ultrapure water blanks were interleaved at predefined intervals to monitor background and carryover.

Metabolite profiling

The instrumental setup for the metabolomic analysis featured an Orbitrap Exploris 240 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) mass spectrometer coupled to a Vanquish Flex ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) system equipped with a Thermo Scientific™ Accucore™ aQ C18 Polar Endcapped (150 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 µm particle size) column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The column oven was maintained at 40 °C, while the autosampler was kept at 4 °C to ensure sample stability. The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B). Gradient time program was set as follows: 0–2 min, 0.5% B; 2–18 min, 0.5–98% B; 18–21 min, 98% B; 21–23 min, 98–0.5% B. Flow rate was set at 0.350 mL/min. A volume of 5 µL of the filtered sample was injected. Ionization was achieved using a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source, with a spray voltage of 3.5 kV in both positive and negative modes, an ion transfer tube temperature of 325 °C, and a vaporizer temperature of 350 °C. Sheath gas was set at 50 arbitrary units, auxiliary gas at 10 arbitrary units, and sweep gas at 1 arbitrary unit. Samples were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, including the AcquireX workflow, to facilitate comprehensive compound identification. Furthermore, polarity switching was employed to expand compound coverage, allowing the detection of both positively and negatively charged ions within a single analytical run. Full MS scans were acquired at 180,000 resolution (at m/z 200) over a scan range of m/z 100–1200. MS2 spectra were acquired at 60,000 resolution (at m/z 200) with a stepped normalized collision energy of 20, 40, and 80%. Dynamic exclusion was applied for 3 s to prevent the repeated fragmentation of the same precursor ions. Injections were randomized across biological classes, and pooled QC samples were regularly interleaved throughout the analytical sequence to monitor instrument stability and enable potential correction of analytical drift during data processing.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Raw LC-HRMS files were processed in Thermo Scientific Compound Discoverer v3.4. Spectra were filtered (Fourier-transform peak signal-to-noise, S/N ≥ 1.5), and retention times were aligned using an adaptive-curve model with linear fallback (max shift 1.0 min; m/z tolerance 5 ppm; outlier removal enabled). Unknown compounds were detected at 5 ppm mass tolerance with baseline removal, minimum peak intensity 1 × 105, a minimum of five data points across each peak and chromatographic S/N ≥ 1.5; isotope grouping with “use most intense isotope only” enabled, an extended adduct list, and a precursor mass tolerance of 0.025 Da were applied. Compounds were grouped across samples (m/z tolerance 5 ppm; 0.2 min window) with peak-rating ≥ 5 present in ≥ 2 files, and areas were integrated on the most common ion. Gaps were filled using restrictive real-peak detection (m/z tolerance 5 ppm; S/N ≥ 1.5; minimum of three MS1 scans). To mitigate analytical drift, QC-based SERRF correction was performed, after which chemical background was hidden using blanks (sample/blank threshold set to 5). MS2-based identification queried mzCloud (Autoprocessed and Reference libraries; cosine identity search; precursor and FT fragment tolerances 10 ppm; ion-trap fragment tolerance 0.4 Da; activation-type matching; match-factor threshold 50; fragment annotation enabled) and was complemented by mzVault/LipidBlast, ChemSpider (BioCyc, HMDB, KEGG; m/z tolerance 2 ppm), targeted mass-list searches (Endogenous Metabolites; HMDB Serum), elemental-composition prediction (m/z tolerance 2 ppm with isotope/fragment matching), spectral-distance scoring, neutral-loss and compound-class scoring, molecular networking, and pathway mapping (Metabolika). Signals flagged as background were excluded from interpretation.

The resulting data matrix was exported for multivariate and univariate data analyses in R 4.3.0. Data matrix was pre-processed to exclude technical outliers (detailed description in supplementary material “Comparison of sample-preparation workflows and QC-derived acquisition metrics: data-set cleaning”). Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess QC clustering and potential run drift; no supervised modelling was performed. Differential abundance was assessed using empirical Bayes moderated linear models implemented in limma. Pairwise contrasts were defined for PD vs. MSA, PD vs. PSP, and MSA vs. PSP. Resulting P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate (FDR). Volcano plots summarized the results, and boxplots were used to visualize selected annotated metabolites across groups. To assess whether the observed disease-associated metabolomic signals were influenced by demographic or treatment-related imbalances, we performed covariate-adjusted sensitivity analyses using limma. These models included diagnosis together with age and sex, and, in a second model, diagnosis together with age, sex, and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD). These analyses were used to assess the covariate sensitivity of the diagnosis-associated features and to guide biological interpretation.

Results and discussion

Analysis of study participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics

We enrolled 102 participants between November 2021 and April 2022 from inpatient and outpatient clinics at ISNB (PD, n = 57; MSA, n = 19; PSP, n = 26). Following diagnostic classification against International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society criteria, nine individuals were excluded because their revised diagnoses no longer met the PD, MSA, or PSP definitions. The final analysis set comprised 93 patients (PD, n = 53; MSA, n = 18; PSP, n = 22). As the second study’s objective was to investigate metabolomic differences among PD, MSA, and PSP rather than to compare patients with healthy individuals, and since such patient-control comparisons are extensively documented, healthy controls were not included [28, 37–39].

Sex distribution, calculated as female percentage, differed across diagnoses (PD 32%, MSA 67%, PSP 55%). Age was lower in PD than in both MSA and PSP, whereas BMI was broadly comparable across groups (Fig. 2). Regarding disease course, symptom duration was longer in PD, whereas clinical severity—assessed by the Hoehn and Yahr stage and the MDS–UPDRS total score (Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale)—was higher in MSA and PSP than in PD. Exposure to dopaminergic therapy also differed: levodopa treatment duration was longest in PD, and LEDD (levodopa equivalent daily dose) was higher in PD than in PSP. Consistent with clinical expectations, levodopa responsiveness was frequent in PD and uncommon in MSA/PSP, and dyskinesia occurred predominantly in PD (Fig. 3). For lifestyle variables, alcohol and coffee consumption were frequent across groups, current smoking was uncommon, with former smoking more prevalent, and the proportion reporting regular physical activity varied by diagnosis.

As demonstrated by group-wise statistical comparisons (Figs. 2 and 3), several variables differ significantly between groups. Given the demographic and clinical imbalance across diagnostic groups, we performed covariate-adjusted sensitivity analyses using limma linear models adjusted for age and sex. In a second model, LEDD was also included to account for treatment-related imbalance. These models were used to assess the extent to which the diagnosis-associated metabolic signature was preserved or attenuated after covariate adjustment.

Comorbid conditions were heterogeneous at the individual level, which is expected given the clinical setting and the rarity of MSA and PSP. Profiles are summarized in supplementary material (Figure S1). The most represented categories included cardiovascular, metabolic-endocrine, and gastrointestinal conditions, but there were no systematic differences across diagnostic groups. Accordingly, we did not include comorbidity terms as covariates in downstream metabolomic analyses and profiles are reported for transparency.

Effect of sample preparation workflows on LC-HRMS acquisition metrics

Three precipitation workflows were compared: tube-based protein precipitation, 96-well protein-precipitation filtration plate, and 96-well protein- and phospholipid-removal plate. Under routine laboratory conditions, the plate-based workflows reduced hands-on pipetting and enabled parallel processing of larger batches relative to the tube protocol and are compatible with future automation on liquid-handling/positive-pressure systems. In all cases, however, the manual 0.22 µm membrane-filtration step remained the rate-limiting operation. All 93 specimens were processed with PP-Tube; both plate-based workflows were performed on 91 specimens each, as two PD samples lacked sufficient plasma volume (PP-Tube, n = 93; PP-Plate, n = 91; PLR-Plate, n = 91).

Representative total-ion chromatograms (TICs) from the same plasma specimen are shown in Fig. 4. Across sample preparation workflows, early eluting small polar metabolites (0–10 min) displayed broadly similar retention profiles, indicating that all procedures efficiently recover the high-abundance polar fraction. In the mid-retention window (10–16 min), profiles for PP-Tube and PP-Plate remained similar, whereas in PLR-Plate, the broad phospholipid features began to attenuate from 14 min, resulting in improved peak-to-baseline profile. In the late-eluting window, characteristic of phospholipids and co-eluting amphiphiles (16–26 min), both plate-based protocols exhibited a cleaner baseline than the tube protocol, with the phospholipid-removal plate showing the most evident elimination of broad unresolved peaks. PP-Plate showed the lowest counts, likely because a cleaner late-retention baseline reduces spurious/borderline peaks, thereby lowering the raw feature tally without implying poorer chemical coverage (see Fig. 4 and Figure S2).

As a proxy for sample cleanliness, we quantified the proportion of total MS1 ion current eluting in the amphiphile-rich late window (16–26 min). The PLR-Plate showed a markedly lower late-window TIC fraction (median 0.199, IQR 0.025) than both PP-Tube (median 0.364, IQR 0.048) and PP-Plate (median 0.363, IQR 0.078). This corresponds to a 45% reduction compared to PP-Tube/PP-Plate, indicating effective depletion of late-eluting phospholipids/co-extractives.

We evaluated repeatability using the areas of sentinel MS1 features. PLR-Plate showed the tightest repeatability (median coefficient of variation (CV) 9.7%, IQR 10.2), followed by PP-Tube (12.0%, IQR 9.2) and PP-Plate (14.3%, IQR 13.8). All medians were < 15%, indicating generally good repeatability (Table S2).

We then assessed missingness using an spQC-derived feature panel. Per-injection missingness was low and broadly comparable across sample preparation protocols: PLR-Plate 1.56% (IQR 0.22%), PP-Plate 1.62% (IQR 0.43%), and PP-Tube 1.95% (IQR 0.35%).

Carry-over was assessed using a blank-sample-blank design, based on TIC area over the entire run and the late window (16–26 min). Across protocols and polarities, carry-over remained below 5% (Figure S3 and Table S3).

Taken together, these data indicate that the phospholipid-removal workflow trades a small reduction in total feature count for improved chromatographic cleanliness, enhanced signal-to-noise profile, highest repeatability, reduced late-eluting background, and slightly lower missingness/CVs. Tube and protein-precipitation plate workflows maximize raw feature counts, including lipid-associated ions, but at the cost of a greater late-eluting baseline. These complementary characteristics justify carrying forward all three workflows to maximize chemical-class coverage in subsequent annotation and pathway analyses, while leveraging phospholipid-removal extracts for the most robust quantitative behavior.

Metabolite profiling and data analysis

The analytical run was monitored with interleaved instrument QCs (msQCs; n = 16 per workflow), and per-feature variability within the top N QC panel was low in each workflow: median CVs were 8.7% (IQR 3.2%) for PLR-Plate, 9.95% (IQR 6.89%) for PP-Plate, and 11.7% (IQR 11.4%) for PP-Tube, indicating stable performance across the run with slightly tighter precision for the plate-based workflows.

Untargeted LC-HRMS profiling (both polarities; AcquireX MS2) generated a high-dimensional feature matrix across three workflows. The analysis comprised 93 PP-Tube, 91 PP-Plate, and 89 PLR-Plate plasma samples. After preprocessing in Compound Discoverer, the curated wide tables contained 18,970 MS1 features for PP-Tube (10,784 in positive mode and 8186 in negative mode), 10,412 for PP-Plate (6258 positive and 4154 negative), and 20,996 for PLR-Plate (13,106 positive and 7890 negative). Figure S4 illustrates typical Compound Discoverer outputs.

Each detected feature was assigned a Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) level: MSI 2 was assigned to putatively annotated compounds supported by high-quality spectral-library matches (high-quality mzCloud and/or mzVault/LipidBlast matches), MSI 3 was assigned to putatively characterized compound classes or structurally related annotations supported by multiple orthogonal MS criteria (e.g., exact mass and isotope pattern, Fragment Ion Search coverage ≥ 10%, neutral-loss evidence in MS2, mass-list or ChemSpider hits), and MSI 4 for accurate-mass features lacking sufficient orthogonal evidence for compound or class-level annotation; MSI 1 was not applied because no authentic standards were analyzed. Processed and curated matrices were filtered to retain features detected in at least 80% of injections within each workflow and polarity. They served as the basis for subsequent statistics. We first conducted exploratory unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) separately by workflow and ionization mode, using Pareto scaling, to assess sample structure, batch effects, and QC behavior.

Unsupervised PCA (Figure S5) showed QC injections clustered tightly in all six PCA score plots, confirming the reproducibility of the analytical platform and the effectiveness of signal correction. No systematic drift or clustering by injection order was observed, indicating that batch effects were minimal. In contrast, biological samples showed greater dispersion, consistent with underlying biological variability rather than technical noise. No spontaneous separation of diagnostic groups was evident in the unsupervised setting, as expected in complex metabolomic datasets and, specifically, among groups represented by closely related diseases rather than markedly different groups, such as healthy and diseased subjects.

Thereafter, univariate differential testing was performed using limma (empirical Bayes moderated t-tests with trend and robust settings) separately by polarity within each workflow. We assessed PD-MSA, PD-PSP, and PSP-MSA contrasts, reporting effects as log2 fold changes (FC). Benjamini–Hochberg FDR controlled multiplicity (FDR < 0.05 primary); nominal P value thresholds (0.1/0.05/0.01/0.001) are reported for completeness (Table S4).

Results are summarized by contrast below.

PD vs. PSP

Across sample preparation workflows, PD-PSP separation is driven primarily by positive ionization. The PLR-Plate Positive panel exhibits the broadest range of discoveries, with many FDR-significant features and several also exceeding |log2FC|≥ 1. PP-Plate Positive follows with a substantial, but smaller, band of FDR hits, while PP-Tube Positive shows a modest yet clear set of signals. In negative mode, discoveries are fewer: PP-Plate yields the highest number of FDR hits, PLR-Plate shows a smaller cluster, and PP-Tube contributes mainly nominal effects, with only a handful surviving FDR. Overall, these patterns are consistent with preparative chemistry. PLR-Plate, by depleting phospholipids, likely reduces ion suppression and clarifies co-elution, which amplifies positive-mode signals and yields the broadest set of PD and PSP differences. Between the two protein-precipitation protocols, despite PP-Tube producing more raw features, PP-Plate delivers more FDR-significant hits. This is plausibly explained by better precision (lower within-group variance resulting from plate processing and filtration, as well as reduced handling variability), which increases statistical power even when effect sizes are comparable. In summary, PLR-Plate improves chemical visibility, while PP-Plate appears to optimize reproducibility, translating into a higher yield of reproducible, FDR-level differences.

For study significance compounds, we adopted a tiered scheme: Tier A (FDR ≤ 0.05 with |log2FC|≥ 1) as primary discoveries, Tier B (FDR ≤ 0.05 without an effect-size constraint) as statistically robust but small-effect signals, and Tier C (|log2FC|≥ 1 without FDR control) as hypothesis-generating. In PLR-Plate, Tier A was small (n = 6) and largely unsupported by libraries, with a single MSI 2 annotation; all six Tier A features occurred in positive mode. The weight of evidence is concentrated in Tier B: by polarity this corresponds to 236 features in positive mode and 29 in negative. Tier C contributed a single feature overall observed in negative mode.

In PP-Plate, there were no Tier-A discoveries. Tier B totals 154 features, corresponding to 118 features in positive and 36 in negative. Tier C added one overall feature in negative mode.

In PP-Tube, Tier A is minimal overall (n = 2) and entirely MSI 4, confined to positive mode. Tier B contains 87 hits: 78 in positive mode and 9 in negative mode. Tier C contributed one overall feature in negative mode.

Taken together, these results show that Tier A is sparse and towards MSI 4, while Tier B concentrates the actionable chemistry. The positive/negative counts clarify that most of these Tier B signals arise from positive ionization (especially in PLR-Plate and PP-Plate), with the negative mode contributing less, resulting in more MSI 4 and occasional MSI 3 confirmations.

Having mapped the discovery space, we focused on MSI level 2 annotations to provide a biologically interpretable view of the diagnosis-associated features. Before interpretation, obvious exogenous signals, including therapeutics, laboratory contaminants, and diet-linked compounds, were curated out, not providing any information about disease-related biology. Aware of the exploratory value of the annotations, subsequent validation of the metabolites will require identification with a reference standard (MSI level 1).

The most interpretable annotations involved purine metabolism, mitochondrial lipid handling, oxidized lipid species and nitrogen/niacin-related metabolism. Within the purine axis, hypoxanthine was higher in PD (log2FC = + 0.27; positive mode; PLR- and PP-Plate), while adenosine was lower (log2FC = −0.31/−0.32; positive mode; PLR- and PP-Plate) (Fig. 7). These opposite changes suggest an alteration in ATP/AMP turnover and purine salvage in the PD-PSP contrast [40, 41]. Consistently, the modified nucleoside N6-methyladenosine trended lower in PD (log2FC = −0.06; positive mode; PLR- and PP-Plate), and 8-hydroxy-7-methylguanine was also lower (log2FC = −0.18; positive mode; PP-Plate), supporting a possible contribution of altered nucleic-acid turnover and oxidative base chemistry (Fig. 7). A small polar negative-ion signal annotated as a pentose-phosphate-like species (putatively a pentose monophosphate; log2FC = + 0.27; negative mode; PP-Plate) was higher in PD, suggesting a possible link between carbohydrate/pentose-phosphate metabolism and purine balance.Fig. 7PD-PSP boxplots for selected metabolites: hypoxanthine, adenosine, N6-methyladenosine, decanoylcarnitine, an oxidized linoleate derivative tri-HODE, Citrulline and nicotinuric acid. The lipid-mitochondrial axis included decanoylcarnitine, which was lower in PD (log2FC = −0.32; positive mode; PP-Plate), consistent with differences in acyl-group trafficking and β-oxidation (Fig. 7). An oxidized linoleate derivative, tri-HODE (9,10,11-trihydroxyoctadec-12-enoic acid), was also lower in PD (log2FC = −0.32; positive mode; PP-Tube), pointing to lipid-peroxidation-related variation (Fig. 7). These lipid-mitochondrial readouts are consistent with previous plasma studies reporting alterations in acylcarnitines in PD and with the central role of mitochondrial dysfunction in PD pathobiology [42, 43].

Additional small-molecule readouts mapped to nitrogen/sulfur handling: Citrulline rose in PD (log2FC = + 0.73; positive mode; PLR-Plate), aligning with flux through the arginine/NO-urea cycle (Fig. 7); nicotinuric acid fell (log2FC = −0.21; positive mode; PLR-Plate), pointing to differences in niacin/NAD(H) handling (Fig. 7), and N-acetyl-methionine was modestly higher (log2FC = + 0.12; negative mode; PP-Tube). A putative tryptophan feature (log2FC = + 0.25; positive mode; PP-Plate) suggests involvement of indole/kynurenine pathways. Although the attribution is putative, all these signals map onto immune-metabolic axes where kynurenine pathway activation and B6-dependent transamination are well-described in neurodegeneration [44].

MSI level 3 annotations (Table S5) corroborated pathway-level perturbation hypothesis discussed for MSI 2, consistent with purine-related features, mitochondrial acyl-group trafficking, steroidogenesis and membrane lipid remodeling, tryptophan-pathway involvement.

PSP is a clinically relevant comparator because the diagnostic question at initial presentation is not PD versus health, but rather a synucleinopathy (PD) versus a primary 4R-tauopathy (PSP), with substantial early clinical overlap. In this context, a recent metabolomics study of PSP brain tissue revealed widespread metabolic reprogramming with disease-specific traits relative to other dementias (e.g., AD), involving energy metabolism, membrane lipid remodeling and oxidative stress pathways, which are conceptually aligned with the plasma pathways observed in the present PD-PSP comparison [45].

Because PD and PSP differed in age and treatment exposure in our cohort (see Section “Analysis of study participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics”), some metabolite classes may partly reflect demographic or clinical heterogeneity. This is particularly relevant for acylcarnitines, oxidized lipid species and niacin/NAD(H)-related metabolites, which have been associated with ageing, mitochondrial function and oxidative stress [46–48].

Therefore, these features were not interpreted as covariate-independent disease markers. Instead, their relevance was evaluated in the context of the covariate-adjusted sensitivity analyses described below (Table S5).

After adjustment for age and sex, all listed features retained the same direction of effect as in the primary diagnosis-only analysis, but as expected, FDR-level significance was markedly reduced. Several features nevertheless remained nominally associated with the PD-PSP contrast. In the second model, which additionally included LEDD, all features again retained the same direction of effect, but none remained significant after FDR correction, although a subset retained nominal significance. These findings indicate that the PD-PSP-associated metabolic pattern is directionally preserved but statistically sensitive to demographic and treatment-related covariates.

Overall, the PD-PSP comparison captured a biologically coherent but covariate-sensitive metabolic pattern consistent with recognized bioenergetic and inflammatory components of parkinsonian neurodegeneration. Therefore, the biological interpretation is based on MSI levels 2–3 annotations and should be considered hypothesis-generating. These findings should therefore be interpreted as chemically coherent pathway-level candidates requiring targeted confirmation and validation in larger, clinically balanced cohorts, rather than as validated biochemical mechanisms or covariate-independent diagnostic biomarkers.

MSA comparisons (PD vs. MSA and PSP vs. MSA)

Across workflows and polarities, contrasts involving MSA yielded one compound discovery after multiple-testing correction (Figure S6). In PP-Plate/positive, PD vs. MSA produced a single Tier B hit (indol-2-one, MSI level 3; log2FC = −0.38; P = 4.48 × 10–6; q = 0.028). All other workflow-polarity combinations generated only nominal signals (e.g., P < 0.01), which did not survive FDR control at q ≤ 0.05, despite thousands of features tested per contrast. The indol-2-one signal in PD vs. MSA is based on MS2 evidence but remains chemically non-unique at MSI Level 3 and may be influenced by dietary/microbiome or exogenous sources; it is not cross-replicated across workflows and thus should not be overinterpreted.

This pattern can be explained by statistical power and multiplicity, as well as clinical/biological proximity. In addition, the smaller and imbalanced MSA group may have been more vulnerable to within-group clinical heterogeneity, a recognized feature of MSA, which can present with variable combinations of parkinsonism, autonomic dysfunction, and cerebellar involvement [30].

Unlike PD vs. PSP, PD and MSA are both α-synucleinopathies, making greater systemic metabolomic overlap biologically plausible, especially in plasma, where brain-region-specific differences are diluted by peripheral inputs [49].

The PSP-MSA contrast, despite representing different proteinopathies (4R-tau vs. α-synuclein), can likewise appear signal-sparse in plasma when effect sizes are small, cohorts are imbalanced, and biological heterogeneity is high. Conversely, studies that report MSA metabolomic separation often rely on supervised multivariate modelling and/or patient-versus-control designs, which are not directly comparable to strict univariate FDR discovery in patient-versus-patient contrasts [39, 50]. Taken together, our data do not support widespread plasma metabolomic differences between MSA and either PD or PSP at the pre-specified discovery thresholds.

Conclusion

We established a fully untargeted, multi-workflow LC-HRMS plasma platform to explore the metabolite profile difference among Parkinson’s disease, multiple system atrophy-parkinsonian phenotype, and progressive supranuclear palsy-parkinsonism. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare all three disorders within an analytical framework that benchmarks three sample preparation workflows: tube-based protein precipitation, 96-well protein-precipitation filtration, and 96-well phospholipid-removal.

Among the three workflows, PLR-Plate provided the most favorable trade-off for routine discovery and quantitation. Mechanistically, selective phospholipid depletion mitigates ESI ion suppression and co-elution, yielding reduced spectral congestion and more reliable MS2 acquisition. Accordingly, if a single workflow must be adopted, PLR-Plate is preferred because it provides reduced phospholipid-related chromatographic background, improved quantitative repeatability, low missingness, and higher MSI level 2 annotation yield.

Although cohort size and group imbalance remain important limitations, across workflows, the most robust exploratory disease-associated pattern was observed for PD versus PSP, driven predominantly by positive-ion mode and reproduced across complementary preparations. This delineates a coherent but covariate-sensitive plasma metabolic pattern including purine-related, mitochondrial lipid-handling, and redox-inflammatory features.

By contrast, comparisons involving MSA yielded at most a single feature after multiple testing correction. This limited discovery is plausibly explained by statistical power constraints, clinical heterogeneity, and biological proximity, rather than analytical instability.

This work also highlights the limitations inherent in untargeted plasma metabolomics. A substantial fraction of discriminant signals remains unassigned, reflecting the current bottleneck in metabolite identification. The biological interpretation should be considered hypothesis-generating. Immediate priorities include external replication in larger, demographically balanced cohorts, targeted MSI level 1 confirmation of the highlighted annotations, pathway-focused validation, and multivariate modeling that incorporates clinical covariates before clinical deployment.
