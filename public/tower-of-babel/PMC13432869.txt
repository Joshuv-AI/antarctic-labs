Decoding strain level interactions between Vibrio cholerae and gut microbiota
Authors: Guozhong Chen, Shucheng Li, Qinghua Zhao, Yanfei Han, Zhiyuan Zhang, Zhi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVibrio cholerae is a highly motile, comma-shaped, Gram-negative bacterium, which can cause severe diarrhea, dehydration, and even human death. Accumulating evidence suggests that gut microbiota and their culture supernatants affect the fitness and colonization of V. cholerae in the host intestine. However, the effects of the most abundant gut bacterial strains on the physiological activities of V. cholerae have not been systematically investigated at the strain level. Here, our systematic screening revealed that gut bacterial culture supernatants exert profound and diverse effects on the physiology of V. cholerae. Specifically, the culture supernatants from Clostridium butyricum L65, Ruminococcus bromii CNGBCC 1800554, and Bacteroides clarus CNGBCC 1801343 strongly inhibited the growth of V. cholerae. In contrast, the culture supernatants of Akkermansia muciniphila L86 and Roseburia inulinivorans DSM 108,070 enhanced the biofilm formation of V. cholerae. Additionally, ROS resistance in Eubacterium rectale CNGBCC 1801156 and R. inulinivorans 108,070 was linked to their KatE activity, while the culture supernatants of Bifidobacterium animalis L73 and R. inulinivorans DSM 108070 significantly suppressed motility. Furthermore, gut microbial culture supernatants modulated the expression of major regulatory genes in V. cholerae. Integration of our data with human cholera cohort data validated Ruminococcus bromii CNGBCC 1800554 as a promising probiotic candidate. This work provides a theoretical foundation and valuable strain resources for further elucidating the interactions between the gut microbiota and V. cholerae at the strain level, and supports the development of next-generation probiotics for cholera prevention and treatment.

Introduction

Cholera is an acute intestinal infectious disease caused by Vibrio cholerae, characterized by severe watery diarrhea and rapid dehydration, which can be fatal if untreated [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has reported an increase in both the number of people who fell sick and died from cholera and is responding with urgency to reduce deaths and contain outbreaks in countries around the world [2].

Infection primarily occurs through the ingestion of water or food contaminated with V. cholerae. After surviving the acidic environment of the stomach, the bacteria reach the intestines, where they must withstand bile salts and fluctuating pH levels [3]. V. cholerae penetrates the intestinal mucus layer and adheres to small intestinal epithelial cells via the toxin-coregulated pili (TCP), colonizing the crypts of the small intestine [4]. This pathogen can also produce cholera toxin (CT), which activates host cell signaling pathways, leading to massive electrolyte and fluid loss. However, the outcome of infection is not solely determined by the virulence of V. cholerae but is also profoundly influenced by the host’s intestinal microbiota [4,5].

Recent studies suggest that the composition and functional state of the gut microbiota significantly affect susceptibility to V. cholerae infection and disease severity [6]. Commensal strains from gut microbiota interact with V. cholerae through numerous mechanisms, which include a single gene called KatE from E.coli favoring the proliferation of ROS-sensitive V. cholerae [7], AI-2 produced from Ruminococcus obeum [8] or tryptophol acetate from Kluyveromyces marxianus reducing V. cholerae colonization by quorum sensing [9], growth inhibition in the presence of Paracoccus aminovorans cell-free spent culture supernatant [6], anti-biofilm properties of the fecal probiotic Lactobacilli against Vibrio spp. [10], the BSH enzyme of Blautia obeum reduced colonization of V. cholerae by degrading the bile salt taurocholate that activates the expression of virulence genes [11], and short-chain fatty acids derived from Bacteroides vulgatus for host resistance against V. cholerae infection [12]. In addition, bioactive small molecules produced by the human gut microbiome and Escherichia coli-produced colibactin modulate the feeling of V. cholerae in the sessile and planktonic lifestyles or host gut [13,14]. Therefore, gut bacteria may influence V. cholerae through various mechanisms, including nutrient competition, alterations in the gut environment (e.g. bile acids, pH), production of antimicrobial substances, and interference with quorum sensing. However, systematic research on strain-level interactions between gut microbes and V. cholerae remains to be further explored, as current investigations are hindered by uncultured gut microorganisms.

This study aims to systematically elucidate the interaction network between V. cholerae and the most abundant gut bacterial strains, analyzing their effects on the ROS resistance, acid tolerance, bile resistance, motility, growth, biofilm formation, and master regulatory gene expression profiles in V. cholerae. Furthermore, by analyzing cohort samples from metagenomic sequencing data of cholera patients and healthy controls, several potential probiotic strains were identified and validated (Figure 1). Notably, Clostridium butyricum L65, Ruminococcus bromii CNGBCC 1800554, and Bacteroides clarus CNGBCC 1801343, Akkermansia muciniphila L86 and Roseburia inulinivorans DSM 108070 which exhibits multi-dimensional effects on the physiological activities on V. cholerae. Integration of our data with human cholera cohort data from 3 countries, 5 cohorts, and 801 samples validated Ruminococcus bromii CNGBCC 1800554 as a promising probiotic candidate. This work will provide a theoretical foundation and strain resources for further elucidating the interactions between the gut microbiota and V. cholerae at the strain-level, as well as for developing next-generation probiotics for the prevention and treatment of cholera. Materials and methods

Microbial strains selection

The bacterial strains used in this experiment were selected based on previously published studies [15]. Our previous work conducted a large-scale analysis of 593 fecal metagenomes from healthy individuals across six countries, identifying multiple core species of the human gut microbiome. Additionally, we incorporated many probiotic strains, excluding Streptococcus thermophilus BNCC 335885 and Saccharomyces cerevisiae L87. A total of 76 strains representing 69 species were obtained and assigned sequential identifiers from GMHUST1 to GMHUST76, including newly added isolates such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus 20300, Clostridium perfringens CNGBCC 1800374, Bacteroides dorei DSM17855, Sporosarcina koreensis PC9, and Bacillus pumilus red1.

These strains were isolated from fecal samples of healthy individuals [15,16] or obtained from probiotic products. No additional ethical approval or informed consent procedures were repeated for this study.

Microbial cultivation

Detailed information and culture conditions are provided in Data S1. In brief, all strains were cultivated under medium-specific conditions as follows: 1) GMHUST1 was grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (Qingdao Hope Bio-technology, Cat# HB9305). 2)GMHUST2-29 and GMHUST72-74 were cultured in MRS broth (Qingdao Hope Bio-technology, Cat# HB0384-1). 3) GMHUST31-63 were grown in BGI104 medium (BGI Research, Cat# BGICC_Medium001). 4) GMHUST64, GMHUST66, and GMHUST69 were cultured in ATCC Medium 260, ATCC Medium 1102, and JCM Medium 1130, respectively. 5) GMHUST71, GMHUST65, and GMHUST67-68/GMHUST70 were cultivated in DSMZ Medium 78, DSMZ Medium 1611, and DSMZ Medium 104c, respectively. 6) GMHUST75-76 were grown in Pr medium (containing 10.0 g/L peptone, 3.0 g/L beef extract, and 5.0 g/L NaCl; pH adjusted to 7.3 ± 0.1 at 25°C). 7) GMHUST30 was cultured in BHIM medium (Qingdao Hope Bio-technology, Cat# HB8478) supplemented with 1% (w/v) mucin (Merck, Cat# M1778). For anaerobic cultivation, media were deoxygenated by boiling under a stream of nitrogen gas and supplemented with 1.0 g/L L-cysteine hydrochloride. Solid media were prepared by adding 15.0 g/L agar.

The V. cholerae was derived from strain E1 Tor C6706 and cultured in LB medium, M9 minimal medium containing 2 mM MgSO4, 0.1 mM CaCl2 (Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Cat# A507024) and 0.2% casein acid hydrolyzate (Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Cat# A603060), or defined medium containing appropriate antibiotics (streptomycin 100 μg mL−1, chloramphenicol 25 μg mL−1, ampicillin 100 μg mL−1 for V. cholerae) or 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 37°C unless otherwise noted. Antibiotic and IPTG powders were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. They were reconstituted in sterile water, filter-sterilized through a 0.22 μm membrane (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Cat# FF372), aliquoted, and stored at −20°C. All anaerobic strains were cultured in the anaerobic workstation under a gas mixture of 85% N2, 5% CO2, and 10% H2.

In subsequent experiments, the 1:1 inoculation ratio between gut bacteria and V. cholerae was initially adopted to ensure equal initial competitive pressure and enable straightforward comparisons among different strains under standardized experimental conditions.

Phylogenetic tree analysis

The single colonies of all the bacterial strains were picked into an anaerobic tube containing defined medium. After 48 h, 50 μL of the culture were collected and centrifuged for bacterial pellets, lysing with 3 μL of NaOH/SDS lysis buffer (0.2 M NaOH, 1% SDS) and diluted with 100 μL deionized water at 100 °C for 30 min. Three microliters of dilution were used as a template for PCR-based amplification of 16S rRNA gene sequences with DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# K1081) (primers: 27 F: 5′-AGAGTTT GATCCTGGCTCAG-3′; 1492 R: 5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). The PCR products were sequenced using Sanger sequencing for the 16S rRNA gene sequences by Sangon Biotech. For each new species, the phylogenetic tree was constructed with the 16S rRNA gene sequences using MEGA7 [17] under the neighbor-joining method. The resulting file was uploaded and visually enhanced on the iTOL v7 website (https://itol.embl.de/), such as labeled content, color matching, and branch settings.

Bacterial growth curves

Colonies of V. cholerae grown on LB agar plates were resuspended in LB broth at an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of ~1.0. The bacterial culture supernatants were filtered for sterilization through 0.22 µm membrane (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Cat# FF372). V. cholerae was diluted 1:100 into these bacterial supernatants containing 10 mM HEPES (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Cat# C0215) with pH = 7.4. Subsequently, 200 µL of the mixture was transferred to a 96-well plate (Corning, Cat# 3599) to measure the growth curve using a microplate reader (Tecan, Spark), readings every 30 min for 12 h at an optical density of 600 nm. The bacterial growth curves were performed under aerobic conditions (rotation speed: 360 rpm; incubation temperature: 37°C).

Biofilm formation assays

The V. cholerae and the supernatants from collected intestinal bacteria with adjusted pH = 7.4 were then inoculated into 10 × 75 mm borosilicate glass tubes containing LB for 24 h under aerobic conditions. After adjusting to OD600 = 1.0, V. cholerae and intestinal bacteria were inoculated (1:10) into their respective media, and 100 µL of each culture was added to a 96-well plate for a 96 h incubation. When biofilm formation occurs, the cell suspension was removed, and the biofilms adhering to the tube walls were rinsed three times with sterile PBS (Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Cat# E607008). Subsequently, the biofilms were stained with crystal violet for 5 min, resolved by DMSO (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Cat# 30072418) and their density was quantified using a microplate reader at an absorbance wavelength of 570 nm.

RT-PCR

The intestinal bacteria and V. cholerae were co-cultured at a 1:1 inoculation ratio and a 1:100 dilution in a trans-well system (Jet Biofil, Cat# TCS000024, a membrane with a pore size of 0.1 μm), with intestinal bacteria in the upper chamber with their respective media (150 μL) and V. cholerae in the lower chamber with 2 mM MgSO4, 0.1 mM CaCl2 and 0.2% casein acid hydrolyzate (0.9 mL). Monocultures with their respective media of each bacterial strain were used as controls. All experiments were performed under anaerobic conditions. After 24 h of cultivation, the V. cholerae in the lower chamber were collected by centrifugation under the condition (12,000 rpm, 2 min, 4 °C). Then, RNA for real-time PCR (RT-PCR) was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Cat# RC101). The RT-PCR reaction was performed using SYBR green fluorescent dye (Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Cat# 11201ES50) and CFX connect real-time detection system (Bio-Rad). The gene recA was used for normalization. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method. For reverse transcription, approximately 400 ng of RNA was extracted using the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Cat# 11119ES60). Notably, the relative expression levels of target genes were measured under specific conditions: virulence genes were assessed under AKI induction with microaerobic conditions, QS-related hapR expression was evaluated in the ΔluxO strain as positive control, and ROS-related genes were examined after AKI induction (HiMedia Laboratories, Cat# M1879) combined with H2O2 (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Cat# FP17050002) treatment for 0.5 h. The RT-qPCR primers used for detection of mRNA of specific genes are listed in Data S2. All primers were tested for PCR specificity and efficiency.

Motility assays

V. cholerae strains were incubated overnight at 37°C for 12 h. Motility assays were carried out in LB medium containing 0.3% selective agar (Invitrogen, Cat# 30391023) with or without sterile metabolic medium. To eliminate the effect of pH on the growth of V. cholerae, the pH of metabolic medium was adjusted to approximately 7.4. Single colonies were picked using sterile toothpicks and inoculated at 37°C. The diameters of the motility were measured after 18 h [13]. For statistical analysis, the diameter of motility in the treatment group was subtracted from that of its respective control.

Bile salt tolerance experiments in vitro

The V. cholerae with plasmid Plac-Lux was incubated overnight at 37°C for 12 h. The culture from intestinal bacteria was collected according to the report above, then mixed with 2× LB broth at a 1:1 ratio. V. cholerae was then inoculated at a 1:100 dilution with 10 mM HEPES and challenged with 1% bile salt (MERCK, Cat# T4009). Subsequently, 200 µL was transferred to a 96-well plate to measure the growth curve using a microplate reader combined with luminescence detection. Readouts were performed every 30 min for 3 h. Serial dilutions of the samples were plated on LB agar for an enumeration of viable cells. Survival rate was calculated for each strain by comparing the CFU in the bile salt-challenged and the unchallenged samples.

Acid tolerance experiments in vitro

V. cholerae carrying the Plac-Lux plasmid, in which the luxCDABE operon is driven by the lac promoter, was incubated at 37°C for 12 h. The culture from intestinal bacteria was collected, then mixed with 2× LB broth at a 1:1 ratio. V. cholerae was then inoculated at a 1:100 dilution with 10 mM HEPES and adjusted pH = 2. Subsequently, 200 µL was transferred to a 96-well plate to measure the growth curve using a microplate reader combined with luminescence detection. Readouts were performed every 30 min for 1 h.

H2O2 killing experiments in vitro

The V. cholerae with plasmid Plac-Lux was incubated overnight at 37°C for 12 h. The culture from intestinal bacteria was collected according to the report above, then mixed with 2× M9 minimal medium containing 2 mM MgSO4, 0.1 mM CaCl2 and 0.2% casein acid hydrolyzate at a 1:1 ratio. V. cholerae was then inoculated at a 1:100 dilution with 10 mM HEPES and challenged with 800 μm H2O2. Subsequently, 200 µL was transferred to a 96-well plate to measure the growth curve using a microplate reader combined with luminescence detection. Readouts were performed every 30 min for 20 h.

During the functional verification of KatE, overnight cultures with plasmids were 1:1,000 sub-cultured into M9 minimal medium with 2 mM MgSO4, 0.1 mM CaCl2 and 0.2% casein acid hydrolyzate, and grown at 37°C, 200 rpm until mid-log phase. For KatE overexpression, V. cholerae harboring the plasmid was induced with 1.0 mM IPTG. Briefly, 800 μm H2O2 were directly added into exponential-phase cultures, followed by stationary incubation at 37°C for 30 min. Serial dilutions of the samples were plated on LB agar for an enumeration of viable cells. Survival rate was calculated for each strain by comparing the CFU in the H2O2-challenged and the unchallenged samples.

Plasmid construction and electroporation

The plasmid pMal-c2x with IPTG-inducible elements, ampicillin resistance, and a pBBR322 origin of replication (Addgene, Cat#75286) was linearized using restriction enzymes NdeI (New England Biolabs (Beijing) Ltd., Cat#R0111S) and BamHI (New England Biolabs (Beijing) Ltd., Cat# R3136S). The katE-like gene sequence was amplified by PCR using FastPfu DNA Polymerase (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Cat# AP221). The purified PCR products were recovered using the GeneJET gel extraction kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat# 0692), and the linearized vector was assembled using Gibson Assembly® Cloning Kit (New England Biolabs (Beijing) Ltd., Cat# E5510S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting construct was transformed into E. coli DH5α (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Cat# CD201). Positive clones were screened by PCR and verified by sequencing. The confirmed plasmids were then introduced into V. cholerae via electroporation. To prepare electrocompetent cells, V. cholerae was washed twice with ice-cold 0.02 mM CaCl2 and once with 10% glycerol.

Electroporation parameters: voltage 1.8 kV, capacitance 25μF, resistance 200 Ω, cuvette gap 0.2 cm. Immediately after electroporation, add 1 mL LB medium, and incubate with shaking at 37°C and 200 rpm for 1 h for recovery. Spread the recovered bacterial suspension onto LB agar plates supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin, incubate at 37°C for 18 h, then pick single colonies for PCR screening verification.

In silico analysis of cholera cohort

We accessed five publicly available gut metagenomic datasets from cohort-based studies on cholera, with accession numbers PRJNA352220 [18], PRJNA668607 [19], PRJNA1035800, PRJEB11419, and PRJNA976726 [20] in the NCBI database. Raw sequencing data were downloaded from the SRA database using the prefetch command with the corresponding accession numbers (Data S3). The downloaded data were converted to FASTQ format and quality-controlled using fastp (version 0.23.2) [21]. Subsequently, the analysis was performed with metaWRAP (version 1.3.2) [22]. Briefly, the FASTQ datasets were first processed with the read_qc module to perform quality trimming and removal of host-derived sequences by aligning to the human reference genome (hg38) with Bowtie2. The cleaned reads were then assembled and binned using the assembly and binning modules within metaWRAP, respectively, to reconstruct Metagenome-Assembled Genomes (MAGs). The binning thresholds were ≥90% completeness and ≤5% contamination. Then, all MAGs were annotated by GTDB-tk version 2.4.0 [23] and analyzed their relative abundances LEfSe [24], using the Kruskal–Wallis test with a threshold of p <0.05, followed by the Wilcoxon test (p <0.05) for subsequent pairwise comparisons. An LDA score threshold of 3.0 was applied to identify biologically significant biomarkers. By aligning with 16S rDNA data from 76 bacterial strains, their relative frequency (reads mean/total reads) was calculated using CoverM [25] with the following parameters: a minimum of 80% of the read aligned, a minimum read percent identity of 99%, and a minimum of 80% of the read length aligned. Cohort-related information is presented in Data S4–S5.

Statistical analysis

The results are reported as means or means with their respective standard errors. All experiments were performed at least three times, with the exception of certain preliminary screening assays. The results were analyzed using two-tailed Student´s t-test and one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Data were graphed using either GraphPad Prism (v.9 for Windows) or the CNSknowall platform (https://www.cnsknowall.com/#/HomePage), or the OE Biotech Cloud Analysis Platform (https://cloud.oebiotech.com). All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism (v.9), and p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The performed statistical tests are described in the figure legends.

Results

Phylogenetic tree analysis

The host gut harbors a microbial community that is approximately ten times more abundant than the host’s own cells. This complex assemblage, comprising probiotics, pathogens, and opportunistic pathogens, collectively influences host health and disease. The overall composition of the gut microbiota is primarily dominated by five major bacterial phyla: Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia [26]. The synthetic microbial community used in this study was designed based on our previous work, where we identified core members by selecting for bacterial species that were highly prevalent and abundant [15]. In the current study, we refined this consortium by focusing exclusively on gut bacteria. Consequently, the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae L87 (which is not a bacterial species) was excluded. Furthermore, the composition was updated by incorporating five new bacterial isolates, including Lactobacillus rhamnosus 20300, Clostridium perfringens CNGBCC 1800374, Bacteroides dorei DSM17855, Sporosarcina koreensis PC9, and Bacillus pumilus red1, which replaced Streptococcus thermophilus BNCC 335885. Analysis of the collected strains revealed that 14 strains belonged to the genus Bacteroides, accounting for the majority, followed by the genera Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, each with 8 strains (Figure 2(a)). In addition, the obtained 16S rRNA gene sequences were used for phylogenetic analysis. The taxonomic cladogram displays the taxonomic diversity of the 76 strains. Nodes representing 69 characterized species and 31 genera are indicated in different colors (Figure 2(b)). The culture supernatants from gut microbes regulate the growth of V. cholerae

Growth is a fundamental and essential physiological process for bacteria. To assess the strain-level impact of the 76 gut bacterial strains on the growth of V. cholerae, we first cultured each strain for 48 h, collected and filtered the bacterial culture supernatants to obtain the sterile culture supernatants. These sterile supernatants were adjusted to pH 7.4 by 1 M NaOH, mixed with 2× LB broth at a 1:1 ratio, and inoculated with V. cholerae cultures to evaluate its growth response. The vast majority of the bacterial culture supernatants significantly inhibited the growth of V. cholerae (Figure 3(a), Data S6). Notably, specifically GMHUST2 (Lactobacillus rhamnosus 20300), GMHUST5 (Lacticaseibacillus paracasei L39), GMHUST17 (Clostridium butyricum L65), GMHUST41 (Ruminococcus bromii CNGBCC 1800554), and GMHUST62 (Bacteroides clarus CNGBCC 1801343), exhibited potent inhibitory activity against V. cholerae. In previous reports, this inhibition is likely mediated by the acidification of the medium through the production of short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate and acetate [27–30]. However, the inhibitory effect persisted even after the initial pH was adjusted, indicating the presence of non-pH-dependent mechanisms. Since the genomic information of these bacterial strains is currently unavailable, future work should focus on genome sequencing to identify the key genetic determinants underlying this inhibitory effect. These findings highlight the complexity of growth interactions between V. cholerae. Also, these strains thus represent promising candidates for the biocontrol of V. cholerae.

The culture supernatants from gut bacteria modulate V. cholerae biofilm formation

Biofilm formation represents an adaptive strategy employed by V. cholerae to resist external stress conditions [26]. To eliminate the potential influence of the pH of metabolite supernatant on biofilm formation via altering bacterial biomass, all supernatants and blank culture media were adjusted to pH 7.4. The results indicated that GMHUST56 (Roseburia intestinalis CNGBCC 1850050) and GMHUST72 (Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis BNCC 341709) enhanced biofilm formation by 5.5-fold and 4.0-fold (Figure 3(b), Data S6), respectively, even though their supernatants slightly inhibited the growth of V. cholerae. GMHUST65 (Roseburia inulinivorans DSM 108070) increased biofilm formation by 3.6-fold, an effect presumably attributable to the promotion of V. cholerae biomass by its culture supernatants (Figure 3(a)). In addition to the culture supernatants promoting V. cholerae biofilm formation, when GMHUST30 (Akkermansia muciniphila L86) and GMHUST65 were co-cultured with V. cholerae in a 96-well plate, the formation of dual-species biofilms was also significantly enhanced (Figure 3(b), Data S7, Figure S1). Previous studies have reported that primary bile salts and indole can modulate biofilm dynamics or enhance intestinal colonization [31,32]. Meanwhile, exogenous para-aminobenzoic acid (pABA) supports folate biosynthesis, alleviating bacterial stress, and upregulating adhesins to promote colonization, despite concurrently suppressing exopolysaccharide production and attenuating virulence in vivo [33]. However, the precise culture supernatants from R. intestinalis, B. longum subsp. infantis, and R. inulinivorans that promote V. cholerae biofilm formation remain to be experimentally confirmed, even though comparative genomic and metabolic profiling studies have revealed their considerable biosynthetic potential [34–37].

The culture supernatants from gut microbes modulate life cycle events of V. cholerae

V. cholerae survives gastric acid (pH = 2) and enters the host intestine via contaminated water or food to initiate colonization. When the pH of metabolite media supplemented with 76 bacterial strains was adjusted to 2, V. cholerae did not exhibit enhanced acid tolerance in the presence of these culture supernatants (Figure 4(a), Data S8). This suggests that these culture supernatants do not promote the survival of V. cholerae in highly acidic environments. Upon invading the intestinal environment, the V. cholerae primarily faces challenges from intestinal innate immunity, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) and bile salts [38]. In response to complex signals in the gut environment, V. cholerae induces the expression of the toxin-co-regulated pilus (TCP) and secretes cholera toxin (CT), thereby promoting its colonization (e.g. motility) on the intestinal epithelium [39]. Accordingly, we also investigated the effects of gut microbial culture supernatants on V. cholerae motility, bile salt tolerance, and ROS resistance (specifically to H2O2). The results showed that the culture supernatants from GMHUST25 (Bifidobacterium breve BNCC 185972) and GMHUST75 (Sporosarcina koreensis PC9) significantly enhanced bacterial motility, whereas those from GMHUST20 (Bifidobacterium animalis L73) and GMHUST65 markedly suppressed it (Figure 4(b), Data S8). These findings suggest that certain gut bacteria may modulate the motility of V. cholerae, thereby influencing its intestinal fitness through their culture supernatants.

Furthermore, the culture supernatants from GMHUST1 (Escherichia coli L15), GMHUST75, and GMHUST76 (Bacillius pumilus red1) significantly increased tolerance to bile salts (Figure 4(c), Figure S2). This may have triggered V. cholerae‘s ToxRS bile-sensing system to adapt to the changing environment [40].

In assays evaluating ROS resistance (H2O2), the culture supernatants from GMHUST56 (Eubacterium rectale CNGBCC 1801156), GMHUST64, and GMHUST71 (Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis BNCC 341709) significantly enhanced bacterial survival under oxidative stress (Figure 4(d), Figure S3). The previous studies have shown that the katE gene from E. coli was one of the key mechanisms mediating in vivo interactions between commensal gut bacteria and can enhance ROS resistance in V. cholerae [7]. Through sequence analysis, we identified katE homologs gene in the genomes of GMHUST56, GMHUST64, and GMHUST71 (Data S2). Upon IPTG-induced overexpression of these genes, we observed that the katE genes from GMHUST56 and GMHUST64 conferred enhanced H2O2 resistance to V. cholerae, whereas the katE from GMHUST71 did not (Figure S4). These results confirmed that GMHUST56 and GMHUST64 help V. cholerae combat ROS through their katE gene, suggesting their presence in the gut may exacerbate the severity of the infection.

In conclusion, these findings demonstrate that the culture supernatants derived from gut microbes modulate key life cycle events of V. cholerae, modulating its adaptability to the host intestinal environment.

Gut microbiota-derived signals reprogram the gene expression in V. cholerae

The aforementioned experiments screened the effects of 76 bacterial strains on various phenotypes of V. cholerae. In addition, the influence of metabolite exchange between 76 gut bacterial strains and V. cholerae on the expression of genes associated with virulence, quorum sensing, ROS stress response, natural competence, and biofilm formation was investigated. The major regulatory systems in V. cholerae include the ToxT-dependent virulence regulatory network, which controls downstream genes such as tcpA and ctxA, the OhrR and OxyR1-OxyR2-mediated oxidative stress response pathways [41,42], genes involved in the transfer of the virulence island VPI (e.g. VC0817 and VC0847) [43], regulatory genes related to natural competence for foreign DNA uptake (e.g. tfox) [44], vpsT expression during biofilm formation [39], and the master quorum sensing regulator gene hapR [45].

Using a trans-well co-culture system under anaerobic conditions, we examined how the culture supernatants from 76 bacterial strains influence the transcriptional regulation of V. cholerae (Figure 5, Data S9). The results demonstrated that GMHUST67 (Bacteroides dorei DSM17855) markedly upregulated the expression of tcpA and ctxA by 18.7-fold and 9.8-fold, respectively. In strains GMHUST15 (Lactobacillus helveticus L62), GMHUST19 (Lactobacillus crispatus L68), and GMHUST30, overexpression of toxT upregulated the expression of ctxA, tcpA, or both. In contrast, although toxT overexpression was observed in strains GMHUST3 (Lactobacillus rhamnosus L30), GMHUST11 (Lactobacillus acidophilus L54), and GMHUST12 (Lactobacillus gasseri L57), it did not lead to a significant increase in the expression of ctxA, tcpA, or both. Notably, GMHUST19 significantly enhanced the expression of toxT, vc0817, and oxyR2 by approximately 12.1-fold, 5.5-fold, and 8.6-fold, respectively. GMHUST64 induced a 10.5-fold upregulation of hapR. GMHUST30 (Akkermansia muciniphila L86) promoted the expression of tfox by 6.0-fold. Additionally, GMHUST15 strongly stimulated the expression of ohrR and vc0847 by about 18.2-fold and 27.2-fold, respectively, while suppressing hapR expression (an effect similarly observed with GMHUST31). The culture supernatants from strains such as GMHUST65, GMHUST31, GMHUST39, and GMHUST66 promoted the expression of V. cholerae‘s vpsT gene (Figure 5, Data S9), correlating with enhanced biofilm formation (Figure 2(b)). A second group of strains (e.g. GMHUST9, GMHUST10, GMHUST26, GMHUST41, GMHUST67, GMHUST76, GMHUST55, GMHUST59, GMHUST61, GMHUST62, GMHUST68, GMHUST69) also upregulated vpsT but, unexpectedly, co-upregulated the gene hapR. In contrast, GMHUST72 suppressed the expression of both vpsT and hapR (Figure 5, Data S9). The regulatory relationship between hapR and vpsT constitutes a classic “bidirectional negative regulation,” forming a molecular switch that controls the lifestyle transition of V. cholerae between the planktonic/motile state and the biofilm/colonizing state [46]. The differential expression of hapR and vpsT in response to gut bacterial culture supernatants may reflect a dynamic balance in the physiology of V. cholerae that varies across different growth phases.

In summary, these findings confirm that gut microbial culture supernatants can engage in multi-dimensional chemical communication with V. cholerae, significantly influencing its gene expression and adaptive fitness.

Evaluation of strains under real-world conditions

Beyond phenotypic screening such as experimental infection and biofilm formation assays, to establish the relevance of our in vitro findings on the 76 bacterial strains to V. cholerae infection in vivo, their roles need to be further investigated in real-world clinical or environmental samples. This supports the development of next-generation probiotics for the prevention and treatment of cholera. The five publicly available gut metagenomic datasets from cohort-based studies on cholera obtained from the NCBI database were analyzed. Using bioinformatics tools including metaWRAP, GTDB-tk, and CoverM, we obtained strain-level taxonomic abundance profiles (Data S3). Based on grouping information and the species abundance table, LEFSe analysis revealed significant disparities in the gut microbiota composition between the healthy control and cholera patient groups (Data S4). The bacterial taxa significantly enriched in the cholera patient group included V. cholerae at the species level. In contrast, the healthy control group exhibited a higher abundance of Phocaeicola vuiguatus at the species level. The results of the LEFSe analysis are visually summarized in the cladogram (Figure 6(a), Data S4), which depict the phylogenetic distribution and effect size of the differentially abundant species. Discussion

With the advancement of culturomics technologies, researchers have gained access to enormous and culturable gut microbial resources, with approximately 1,500–2,000 species of gut microorganisms successfully isolated, characterized, and utilized. However, incorporating high-throughput sequencing data conservatively estimates that 50%–70% of gut microbial species remain uncultured [47–49]. Moreover, significant genomic and functional diversity exists among different strains within the same species, highlighting the need for more precise investigations into microbial interactions at the strain-level. In this study, we systematically screened cultivable bacterial strains to evaluate their interactions with V. cholerae across multiple phenotypic and molecular dimensions, including growth, virulence, biofilm formation, bile salt tolerance, pH resistance, motility, ROS tolerance, and transcriptional responses. Through multi-cohort analyses, we identified distinct gut microbial profiles in cholera patients compared to healthy individuals. Our results revealed that the culture supernatants from Clostridium butyricum L65, Ruminococcus bromii CNGBCC 1800554, and Bacteroides clarus CNGBCC 1801343 strongly inhibited the growth of V. cholerae. In contrast, Akkermansia muciniphila L86 and Roseburia inulinivorans DSM 108070 enhanced its biofilm formation. Additionally, ROS resistance in Eubacterium rectale CNGBCC 1801156 and R. inulinivorans DSM 108070 was linked to their catalase (KatE) activity, while Bifidobacterium animalis L73 and R. inulinivorans DSM 108070 significantly suppressed bacterial motility. In addition, we demonstrated that the culture supernatants derived from gut microbiota significantly modulate the expression of key regulatory genes in V. cholerae. Furthermore, their roles were validated through multi-cohort analyses to confirm its clinical relevance in real-world clinical or environmental samples. These results significantly expand the understanding of interactions between V. cholerae and gut bacteria, establishing a systematic phenotypic and mechanistic foundation for further investigation of these relationships in animal hosts.

Gut bacteria and pathogens communicate via chemical signaling within the intestinal environment [5]. The culture supernatants derived from gut microbiota can directly influence pathogen physiology and profoundly modulate the host immune system to facilitate pathogen clearance [50]. In this study, GMHUST30 (Akkermansia muciniphila L86), which is enriched in healthy individuals but reduced in cholera patients (Figure 6(b)), promoted the growth of V. cholerae (Figure 3(a)), upregulated tfox expression by sixfold, and significantly enhanced virulence-related phenotypes (Figure 5, Figure S1). These findings suggest that although GMHUST30 is generally considered a probiotic, it may pose potential risks in the context of cholera prevention and treatment.

Although LEfSe analysis of multiple cholera cohorts revealed numerous differentially abundant gut microbes, most of the strains contributing to the gut microbiota differences between cholera patients and healthy individuals did not overlap with the dominant intestinal strains prioritized in our study, such as Blautia wexlerae, Dorea formicigenerans, Blautia massiliensis, and Alistipes putredinis. Resource limitations prevented the evaluation of all candidate strains. Future work will focus on optimizing culture methods and medium formulations to obtain additional microbial resources for further investigating strain-specific interactions with V. cholerae, which will allow us to more comprehensively and thoroughly evaluate the regulatory roles of commensal gut bacteria during cholera infection. Future work will also validate these findings in germ-free and streptomycin-treated adult mice to identify potential probiotic strains that can effectively regulate the colonization of V. cholerae, providing novel candidate strains for the clinical treatment and prevention of cholera.

The rational design of synthetic microbial communities is also limited by the availability of bacterial strains [51]. Defined microbial communities represent a strategic tool for developing anti-infective interventions against V. cholerae. By enabling the precise deconvolution of multi-species interactions, this approach moves beyond correlation to establish causality, identifying specific gut microbes that modulate pathogen virulence and fitness [52]. The controlled platform allows systematic interrogation of inhibitory mechanisms, including nutrient competition, metabolite production, and direct interference with virulence pathways. Consequently, the interaction profiles identified in this study provide a foundation for the rational design of synergistic live biotherapeutics or metabolite-based treatments to prevent or ameliorate cholera.

In addition, host innate and adaptive immune factors regulate the intestinal colonization of V. cholerae through multilayered defense mechanisms [53]. As the first-line barrier, intestinal epithelial cells recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) of V. cholerae, including LPS and flagellin, via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), thereby activating pathways such as NF-κB and secreting proinflammatory cytokines including IL-8 and IL-6 to recruit immune cells including neutrophils and macrophages [54,55]. Meanwhile, epithelial cells and Paneth cells release antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) such as human β-defensins (hBD) and HD-5, which directly kill or inhibit V. cholerae and may upregulate the expression of virulence genes including CT and TCP [56]. Reactive oxygen and nitrogen species including ROS and NO produced by DUOX2 and iNOS exert bactericidal effects by damaging bacterial structural components, whereas V. cholerae can resist such stress through catalase systems such as KatG and KatB. As a key effector molecule of adaptive immunity, mucosal secretory IgA (sIgA) agglutinates bacteria and blocks the binding of adhesion factors including TCP to epithelial receptors, thereby inhibiting colonization and toxin activity [57]. Furthermore, neutrophil infiltration and phagocytosis, the physical barrier of the intestinal mucus layer, and microbiome-mediated immune enhancement (e.g. induction of mucus and AMP secretion) collectively contribute to colonization resistance, whereas V. cholerae achieves immune evasion and establishes colonization by suppressing inflammation and immune cell recruitment and downregulating host defenses via cholera toxin (CT) and MARTX toxin [58]. However, the present study did not investigate the immunomodulatory effects of bacterial culture supernatants on intestinal epithelial cells or other cell lines using in vitro cellular models.

In summary, this study provides a systematic screen of bacterial metabolite effects on multiple virulence- and fitness-related traits of V. cholerae. By aligning with 16S rDNA data from 76 bacterial strains and LEfSe analysis from human cohorts, we further validated the communication network between gut microbes and V. cholerae at the strain-level. These findings establish a theoretical foundation for the prevention and treatment of cholera and offer a rich repository of gut microbial strains with potential.
