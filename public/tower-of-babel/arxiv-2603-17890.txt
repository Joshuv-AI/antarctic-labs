Mysterious points in keys but not trees
Scott Neville, José Simental

Abstract
. The deep locus of a cluster variety is defined to be the set of its points that do not belong to any cluster torus. We show that, if the cluster variety has a seed whose mutable part is a tree without multiple edges, then the deep locus can be characterized as the set of points whose stabilizer under a certain group action is nontrivial. Deep points without a stabilizer are called mysterious. We establish that many other classes of acyclic quivers (including keys) often have mysterious points. This refutes [ 6 , Conjecture 1.1] , but establishes it in many important cases.

1.
Introduction

An affine cluster variety
\mathcal{A}
is the affine scheme
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Spec}(A)
associated to a cluster algebra
A
in the sense of Fomin–Zelevinsky
[
10
]
. Recall that the algebra
A
has a distinguished set of generators which can be grouped into finite sets
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma
runs over the (typically infinite) set of
seeds
of
A
. Each of the finite sets
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(\Sigma)
has the same cardinality
r
and, by the Laurent phenomenon
[
11
]
, induces an open embedding

(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{r}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{A}.

We call the image of this embedding a
cluster torus
, denoted by
T(\Sigma)
. It is tempting to hope that
\mathcal{A}
can be covered by the cluster tori. However, this is not always true. The
deep locus
of
\mathcal{A}
is precisely the set of points that are not covered by the cluster tori.

Note that
p\in\mathcal{A}
is deep if and only if for every cluster
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(\Sigma)
there exists some element
x\in\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(\Sigma)
such that
x(p)=0
. If the set of seeds is infinite, verfying whether a point is deep can be a hard problem. A complete description of the deep points of a cluster variety exists in the following settings.

(1)

Cluster algebras of simply-laced finite cluster type,
[
6
]
(see also
[
4
]
for type
A
)

(2)

Cluster algebras associated to unpunctured surfaces,
[
4
]
.

(3)

Rank
2
cluster algebras
[
4
]
.

(4)

The Markov cluster algebra
[
4
,
6
]
.

(5)

The cluster algebra associated to a family of positroid cells in
\mathrm{Gr}(3,n)
, including the top-dimensional one
[
6
]
.

All of these are obtained by
ad-hoc
methods, special to the cluster algebras in question.

There is an easy condition that guarantees a point is deep. The
cluster dilation group

\mathrm{Dil}(\mathcal{A})
is a group that acts by dilations, hence freely, in every cluster torus.
1
1

1

This is usually called the
cluster automorphism group
in the literature. See Remark
2.8
for our reasons to not use this name.
So a sufficient condition for a point to be deep is that it has a nontrivial stabilizer under the action of
\mathrm{Dil}(\mathcal{A})
. This condition is not always necessary (see
[
6
, Proposition 3.22]
). Nevertheless, in
[
6
, Conjecture 1.1]
we conjecture that it is necessary for a large class of cluster algebras, namely, the locally acyclic ones. In this note we will establish this conjecture when
\mathcal{A}
has a seed whose mutable part is a tree. However, we will also see that it fails to hold in general, even for cluster algebras that have an acyclic seed.

More precisely, following
[
6
]
let us
say that a
p\in\mathcal{A}
is
mysterious
if
p
is deep but
\mathrm{Dil}(\mathcal{A})
acts freely on
p
. The first main result of this paper reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume
\mathcal{A}
has a seed
\Sigma=(\widetilde{B}(Q),\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})
such that the mutable part of
Q
is a tree. Then,
\mathcal{A}
has no mysterious points.

Remark 1.2
.

Following convention, trees do not have parallel arrows. So if there is more than one arrow between a pair of mutable vertices then
Q
does
not
satisfy the conditions in Theorem
1.1
.

Note that Theorem
1.1
implies that cluster algebras of (simply-laced) finite cluster type have no mysterious points, thus recovering
[
6
, Theorem 5.19]
using more elementary methods.

Let us explain our proof strategy. Assume first that
\mathcal{A}
is any cluster variety of skew-symmetric type. Assume
p\in\mathcal{A}
has a trivial stabilizer under the action of the cluster dilation group
\mathrm{Dil}(\mathcal{A})
. We need to find a cluster torus containing
p
. Fix a seed
\Sigma=(\widetilde{B}(Q),\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})
, and assume that there exists a mutable vertex
i
such that
x_{i}(p)\neq 0
. Consider the seed
\widetilde{\Sigma}
obtained from
\Sigma
by freezing the vertex
i
, and assume that the cluster variety
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
coincides with the locus
\{x_{i}\neq 0\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
. Then, we may consider
p\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
and work inductively on the number of mutable vertices. However, we have a natural embedding
\mathrm{Dil}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathrm{Dil}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}})
, and it may happen that
\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{Dil}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}})}(p)
is nontrivial even if
\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{Dil}(\mathcal{A})}(p)
is (see Example
1.5
below for an instance of this). One sufficient condition that guarantees that
\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{Dil}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}})}(p)
is trivial is that
x_{j}(p)\neq 0
for every vertex
j
neighboring
i
in
Q
.

This suggests we should work with
acyclic
seeds, and use the stratification of
\mathcal{A}
studied by Lam and Speyer in
[
15
]
.
This stratification is indexed by the independent sets of vertices of the mutable part of
Q
.
Using the strategy outlined above, we may reduce to the case where
p
is in a stratum indexed by a maximal independent set.
Up to mutation equivalence, we may assume that every vertex is either a source or a sink, in particular, one-step mutations of
Q
are still trees and we can perform a well-chosen sequence of mutations to take care of the case of maximally independent sets.

Outside of trees, the inductive strategy outlined above may fail. Analyzing where it may fail we are led to a family of mysterious points.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider the quiver

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}

Assume that
\gcd(a,b)=1
,
\min(a,b)\geq 2
. Then, the corresponding cluster algebra has mysterious points.

Remark 1.4
.

While the quiver
Q
in Theorem

does not feature frozen variables, we will see below in Proposition

that Theorem
1.3
implies that we can add arbitrary frozen variables to

Example 1.5
.

As an example, consider the quiver

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 3}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 3}

We claim that any point

in the locus

is mysterious. An example of such a point

is taking

Thus we have that

is in general false. However, as shown by the results of this paper, it seems to be true in many cases. It’d be interesting to find conditions guaranteeing the non-existence of mysterious points.

Remark 1.6
.

The quiver in Theorem
1.3
is an example of a

, a class of quivers studied in
[

]
. In fact, keys tend to have mysterious points. See Section

below.

Remark 1.7
.

The cluster algebras considered in
[
6
]
(that do not have mysterious points) all come from positroid varieties in the Grassmannian
[
12
]
(which in turn come from Postnikov’s plabic graphs
[

]
). It is shown in
[

]

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we give preliminary results on cluster algebras/varieties and set up notations. In particular, we define the cluster dilation group and present it as a subgroup of a torus. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
1.1
. In Section
4
we discuss a method to decide whether a point is deep based on restriction to rank
2
subalgebras. This has the advantage of not referring to the stabilizer of a point and will ultimately lead us to produce a family of mysterious points in Section
5
, in particular proving Theorem
1.3
. Moreover, in this section we exhibit several families of quivers with mysterious points, the simplest ones being those considered by Theorem
1.3
. These include quivers of arbitrarily large rank, as well as locally-acyclic quivers which are not mutation acyclic.

2.
Cluster varieties

We give preliminary results on cluster algebras and cluster varieties.

2.1.
Cluster algebras

Let
n,m
be non-negative integers, and let
\mathcal{F}
be the field of rational functions in
n+m
variables with complex coefficients.

Let
Q
be an ice quiver with
n
mutable vertices, labeled
\{1,\dots,n\}
, as well as
m
frozen ones, labeled
\{n+1,\dots,n+m\}
. We will always assume that
Q
has no loops or oriented
2
-cycles, and that there are no arrows between two frozen vertices.

We define an
(m+n)\times n
matrix
\widetilde{B}(Q)=(b_{ij})
defined as follows:

b_{ij}:=\#\{\text{arrows $i\to j$}\}-\#\{\text{arrows $j\to i$}\}.

We call
\widetilde{B}(Q)
the
extended exchange matrix
of the quiver
Q
. Since
Q
has no loops or oriented
2
-cycles and there are no arrows between frozen vertices, the data of
Q
and of
\widetilde{B}(Q)
are equivalent.
The number of mutable vertices
n
is called the
rank
of
Q
. We denote by
Q^{\mathrm{mut}}
the quiver obtained after deleting all the frozen vertices and arrows incident to them, and call it the mutable part of
Q
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
seed
in
\mathcal{F}
is the data
\Sigma=(\widetilde{B},\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})
where

(1)

\widetilde{B}=\widetilde{B}(Q)
is the extended exchange matrix of an ice quiver
Q
.

(2)

\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m+n}\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
is such that
\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{C}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m+n})
.

We call
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}
the
extended cluster
of the seed
\Sigma
, and its elements are called
cluster variables
. The cluster variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are called
mutable
while the rest
x_{n+1},\dots,x_{n+m}
are called
frozen
.

If
k
is a mutable vertex of
Q
, the mutation
\mu_{k}(Q)
of
Q
at
k
is defined to be the quiver obtained from
Q
by the following three-step procedure: (1) for each pair of arrows
i\to k\to j
in
Q
where
i
and
j
are not both frozen, add an arrow
i\to j
; (2) reverse all arrows incident to
k
; (3) delete a maximal collection of oriented
2
-cycles that may have been created in step (1).
Two quivers are
mutation-equivalent
if they are related by a sequence of mutations.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\Sigma=(\widetilde{B},\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})
be a seed, and let
1\leq k\leq n
. The mutation
\mu_{k}(\Sigma)
is the seed
\mu_{k}(\Sigma)=(\mu_{k}(\widetilde{B}),\mu_{k}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}))
where

(1)

\mu_{k}(\widetilde{B})
is the extended exchange matrix of the quiver
\mu_{k}(Q)
.

(2)

\mu_{k}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})=(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n+m})
, where
x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{i}
for
i\neq k
, and
x^{\prime}_{k}=x_{k}^{-1}\left(\prod_{b_{ik}>0}x_{i}^{b_{ik}}+\prod_{b_{ik}<0}x_{i}^{-b_{ik}}\right)
.

We remark that mutation is involutive, that is,
\mu_{k}(\mu_{k}(\Sigma))=\Sigma
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\Sigma=(\widetilde{B},\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})
be a seed in
\mathcal{F}
. The
cluster algebra

A(\Sigma)
is defined to be the
\mathbb{C}[x_{n+1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n+m}^{\pm 1}]
-subalgeba of
\mathcal{F}
generated by all the cluster variables of
\Sigma
and all its iterated mutations. We denote by
\mathsf{Seeds}(\Sigma)
the set of all seeds obtained from
\Sigma
by iterated mutations.

The following result is essential in the theory of cluster algebras.

Theorem 2.4

(The Laurent phenomenon
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Seeds}(\Sigma)
. Then, any cluster variable of
\Sigma^{\prime}
can be written as a Laurent polynomial in the cluster variables of
\Sigma
.

2.2.
Cluster varieties and the deep locus

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\Sigma
be a seed and
A(\Sigma)
its associated cluster algebra. The affine variety

\mathcal{A}(\Sigma):=\mathrm{Spec}(A(\Sigma))

is called the
cluster variety
associated to
\Sigma
.

Now let
\Sigma^{\prime}=\big(\widetilde{B^{\prime}},\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n},z_{n+1},\dots,z_{n+m})\big)\in\mathsf{Seeds}(\Sigma)
be a seed that can be obtained from
\Sigma
by mutations. By the Laurent phenomenon, every cluster variable of
A(\Sigma)
can be written as a Laurent polynomial in the cluster variables of
\Sigma^{\prime}
, so that
A(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathbb{C}[z_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,z_{m+n}^{\pm 1}]
. Since, by definition,
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{m+n}]\subseteq A(\Sigma)
we obtain

(2.1)

A(\Sigma)[z_{1}^{-1},\dots,z_{n+m}^{-1}]=\mathbb{C}[z_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,z_{n+m}^{\pm 1}].

Thus,
\Sigma^{\prime}
determines an open subset
T(\Sigma^{\prime})
of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
, defined by the non-vanishing of the variables
z_{1},\dots,z_{m+n}
and by (
2.1
) we have

T(\Sigma^{\prime})\cong(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+m}.

Definition 2.6
.

The open tori
T(\Sigma^{\prime})
where
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Seeds}(\Sigma)
are called the
cluster tori
of the variety
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
. The
cluster manifold
is

\mathcal{M}(\Sigma):=\bigcup_{\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Seeds}(\Sigma)}T(\Sigma^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{A}(\Sigma).

The
deep locus
of
\Sigma
is

\mathcal{D}(\Sigma):=\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\setminus\mathcal{M}(\Sigma).

In other words, an element
p\in\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
belongs to the deep locus if, for any seed
\Sigma^{\prime}=(\widetilde{B^{\prime}},\widetilde{\mathbf{z}})\in\mathsf{Seeds}(\Sigma)
, there exists
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
z_{i}(p)=0
.

2.3.
The cluster dilation group

In
[
6
]
the authors observe a relationship between the deep locus of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
and the action of a certain group on
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\Sigma
be a seed and
A(\Sigma)
its corresponding cluster algebra. An algebra automorphism
\varphi:A(\Sigma)\to A(\Sigma)
is said to be a
cluster dilation
if, for every cluster variable (in every seed)
z
of
A(\Sigma)
, there exists
\varphi_{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
such that
\varphi(z)=\varphi_{z}z
. The set of all cluster dilations of
A(\Sigma)
forms a group, called the
cluster dilation group

\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)
.

Remark 2.8
.

The cluster dilation group
\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)
is called
cluster automorphism group
in
[
14
,
15
,
6
,
13
]
among others. Since the term
cluster automorphism group
is also used to name a different object (see
[
1
,
2
,
16
,
5
]
among others) we prefer the term
cluster dilation group.

By the Laurent phenomenon a cluster dilation is entirely determined by its values in a single extended cluster, so that in fact every seed
\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Seeds}(\Sigma)
determines an embedding
\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+m}
. Following
[
13
]
, we can determine the image of this embedding precisely, as follows. Let
\widetilde{B^{\prime}}=(b^{\prime}_{ij})
be the extended exchange matrix of the seed
\Sigma^{\prime}
.

Lemma 2.9

(Lemma 2.3,
[
13
]
)
.

For any seed
\Sigma^{\prime}=(\widetilde{B^{\prime}},\widetilde{\mathbf{z}})\in\mathsf{Seeds}(\Sigma)
, the cluster dilation group
\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)
can be identified with the subgroup of
(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+m}
defined by the equations

(2.2)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
b^{\prime}_{ik}>0\end{subarray}}^{m+n}t_{i}^{b^{\prime}_{ik}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
b^{\prime}_{ik}<0\end{subarray}}^{m+n}t_{i}^{-b^{\prime}_{ik}},\qquad k=1,\dots,n.

Note that the equations (
2.2
) are obtained simply by requiring that the mutated variable

x_{k}^{-1}\left(\prod_{b^{\prime}_{ik}>0}x_{i}^{b^{\prime}_{i_{k}}}+\prod_{b^{\prime}_{ik}<0}x_{i}^{-b^{\prime}_{ik}}\right)

is homogeneous.

Example 2.10
.

Consider the quiver (without frozen variables)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 3}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 3}

so that we have the following equations

\begin{array}[]{ccc}t_{1}^{2}=1,&t_{2}^{3}t_{3}^{2}=1,&t_{1}^{3}=1,\end{array}

corresponding to the vertices
3,1
and
2
, respectively. Note that the first and third equations imply that
t_{1}=1
, so the cluster dilation group is simply
\{(t_{2},t_{3})\in(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2}\mid t_{2}^{3}t_{3}^{2}=1\}
.

By definition,
\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)
acts on
A(\Sigma)
by algebra automorphisms so it also acts on
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
. Also by definition,
\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)
acts by dilations, hence freely, on any cluster torus
T(\Sigma^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
. In other words, any element
p\in\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
with non-trivial stabilizer under the action of
\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)
must be deep.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\Sigma
be a seed. We define the stabilizer locus

\mathcal{S}(\Sigma):=\{p\in\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\mid\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)}(p)\neq\{1\}\}.

From the discussion above,

\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\Sigma).

This inclusion need not be an equality, and we call a point
p\in\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)

mysterious
if
p\in\mathcal{D}(\Sigma)\setminus\mathcal{S}(\Sigma)
. Thus,
p
is mysterious if it is deep, but its deepness cannot be explained by looking at the cluster dilation group.

2.4.
Acyclic case

Let
\Sigma=(\widetilde{B},\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})
be a seed with associated quiver
Q
and assume that the mutable part
Q^{\mathrm{mut}}
is
acyclic
, that is, it does not have directed cycles.
In this case, the cluster algebra
A(\Sigma)
admits an explicit description by generators and relations. Namely, by
[
3
, Corollary 1.21]
we have that

(2.3)

A(\Sigma)\cong\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{n}^{\prime},x_{n+1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n+m}^{\pm 1}]\Big/\left(x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime}-\prod_{j=1}^{n+m}x_{j}^{[b_{ji}]_{+}}-\prod_{j=1}^{n+m}x_{j}^{[-b_{ji}]_{+}},i=1,\dots,n\right)

and consequently we have the following description for the cluster variety

(2.4)

\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\!\cong\!\left\{\!(p_{1},p^{\prime}_{1},\dots,p_{n},p^{\prime}_{n},p_{n+1},\dots,p_{n+m})\!\in\!\mathbb{C}^{2n}\!\times\!(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{m}\middle|p_{i}p^{\prime}_{i}\!=\!\prod_{j=1}^{n+m}p_{j}^{[b_{ji}]_{+}}\!\!+\!\!\prod_{j=1}^{n+m}p_{j}^{[-b_{ji}]_{+}}\!,i=1,\dots,n\!\right\}.

In (
2.3
), the element
x^{\prime}_{i}
is the unique element in
\mu_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})\setminus\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}
, and the relations are the exchange relations defining
x^{\prime}_{i}
.

In
[
15
, Section 3]
, Lam and Speyer obtain a stratification of
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
whose strata are particularly nice when
\widetilde{B}
has
really full rank
, i.e., when the rows of
\widetilde{B}
span
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module. Recall that a subset
I
of the vertices of
Q
is an
independent set
if there are no arrows between any two of its members.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\Sigma
be a seed such that
Q^{\mathrm{mut}}
is acyclic. For each independent set
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
, define

\mathcal{O}_{I}:=\{(p_{1},p^{\prime}_{1},\dots,p_{n},p^{\prime}_{n},p_{n+1},\dots,p_{n+m})\in\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)\mid p_{i}=0\forall i\in I,\,p_{j}\neq 0\forall j\notin I\}.

Then

\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,\ldots n\}\\
\text{is independent}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{O}_{I}.

If, moreover,
\widetilde{B}
has really full rank then

\mathcal{O}_{I}\cong\mathbb{C}^{|I|}\times(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{n+m-2|I|}

The coordinates on the
\mathbb{C}^{|I|}
factor are
p^{\prime}_{i}
for
i\in I
.

Note that when
I=\emptyset
the stratum
\mathcal{O}_{I}
is precisely the cluster torus
T(\Sigma)
corresponding to the acyclic seed
\Sigma
.

We can use the explicit description (
2.4
) to prove the following result.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\Sigma
be a seed such that
Q^{\mathrm{mut}}
is acyclic, and let
\widetilde{\Sigma}
be obtained from
\Sigma
by adding
\ell
arbitrary frozen rows to the exchange matrix. Let

p=(p_{1},p^{\prime}_{1},\dots,p_{n},p^{\prime}_{n},p_{n+1},\dots,p_{n+m})\in\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)

and define the point

\widetilde{p}=(p_{1},p^{\prime}_{1},\dots,p_{n},p^{\prime}_{n},p_{n+1},\dots,p_{n+m},1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}\times(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{m+\ell}.

Then

(1)

\widetilde{p}\in\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\Sigma})
.

(2)

p
belongs to a cluster torus in
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma)
if and only if
\widetilde{p}
belongs to a cluster torus in
\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\Sigma})
.

(3)

The groups
\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)}(p)
and
\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{Dil}(\widetilde{\Sigma})}(\widetilde{p})
are isomorphic.

Proof.

An exchange relation in
\Sigma
is of the form
x_{k}x_{k}^{\prime}=M_{1}+M_{2}
and an exchange relation in
\widetilde{\Sigma}
is of the form
x_{k}x_{k}^{\prime}=M^{\prime}_{1}+M^{\prime}_{2}
, where
M_{i}
differs from
M^{\prime}_{i}
by a monomial in the extra frozen variables in
\widetilde{\Sigma}
. From this observation, (1) is clear. Statement (2) follows from
[
9
, Lemma 3.3.2]
. Finally, let

Remark 4.2
.

Note that, in Lemma

, the cluster algebra

is of infinite cluster type if

. This phenomenon is

, where we need all five tori to cover the cluster manifold. Let us briefly show this. Since the quiver

where

. For each of the five cluster tori, we exhibit a point that belongs to this cluster torus but does not belong to any of the other four cluster tori.

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\text{Seed}&\text{Cluster}&(p_{1},p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2},p_{2}^{\prime})\\
\hline\cr\Sigma&\{x_{1},x_{2}\}&(-1,0,-1,0)\\
\hline\cr\mu_{1}(\Sigma)&\{x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}\}&(0,-1,-1,-1)\\
\hline\cr\mu_{2}(\Sigma)&\{x_{1},x^{\prime}_{2}\}&(-1,-1,0,-1)\\
\hline\cr\mu_{2}\mu_{1}(\Sigma)&\{x_{1}^{\prime},x_{1}^{\prime}x_{2}^{\prime}-1\}&(-1,-1,0,0)\\
\hline\cr\mu_{2}\mu_{1}(\Sigma)&\{x^{\prime}_{1}x^{\prime}_{2}-1,x^{\prime}_{2}\}&(0,0,-1,-1)\\

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Sigma_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Sigma_{d-1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Sigma_{d}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Sigma_{d+1}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{1}}

Assume

. Then, we have to show that

. But this follows from the same argument given for

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Sigma_{1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Sigma_{d}}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{1}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{d-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}

Remark 4.5
.

Proposition
4.4
can be generalized to algebras where the

constraint is violated on a small (or well controlled) set.
The proof shows that

is not within the set of cluster tori connected to
\Sigma
by mutations where

remains connected to all other mutable vertices by at least
2
arrows.

Remark 4.6
.

While the proof of Proposition
4.4
uses the existence of a stabilizer to establish that

is deep in certain rank

algebras, this is not necessary.
It is possible (but more tedious) to show

is deep directly from the exchange relations and our assumptions on
\Sigma
.

5.
Mysterious points

Proposition
4.4
allows us to show that many points are deep without any reference to their stabilizer under the action of the cluster dilation group. In this section, we take advantage of this in order to construct several examples of mysterious points in locally acyclic cluster algebras. In particular, this implies that
[
6
, Conjecture 1.1]
is false in general.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Recall that our quiver is

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 3}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}

With
\gcd(a,b)=1
and
\min(a,b)\geq 2
.

The gcd vector
\vec{d}(\widetilde{B})
is the vector with
\vec{d}_{i}=\gcd(b_{i1},b_{i2},\ldots,b_{in})
. This vector is a mutation invariant
[
21
]
. Because
\vec{d}=(1,b,a)
, we must have that
b^{\prime}_{13}\in a\mathbb{Z}
and
b^{\prime}_{12}\in b\mathbb{Z}
in every seed.
But because
\gcd(b^{\prime}_{12},b^{\prime}_{13})=1
neither can be
0
.
Thus
\Sigma
satisfies the hypothesis of Proposition
4.4
.

We note that the locus
\{x_{1}=x_{1}^{\prime}=0\}
is nonempty, given by the equation
1+x_{2}^{b}x_{3}^{a}=0
.
We claim that any point in this locus is mysterious.
Any such point is deep by Proposition
4.4
. As observed in Example
2.10
, any element
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\in\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)
already satisfies
t_{1}=1
, and this implies that any point in the locus
\{x_{1}=x_{1}^{\prime}=0\}
has trivial stabilizer. Thus, any such point is mysterious.
∎

5.1.
Keys

The class of quivers described in Theorem
1.3
are examples of a more general class of quivers with well understood mutation classes (Definition
5.1
below).
They were described precisely in
[
8
]
.
We will not use the full description, so we state only the necessary definitions and a few corollaries, before giving examples.

Definition 5.1

(
[
8
, Definition 4.1]
)
.

A
key
is a quiver
Q
with two distinguished vertices
k,k^{\prime}
such that:

•

|b_{kk^{\prime}}|<2
, and otherwise (for
i\neq j
and
\{i,j\}\neq\{k,k^{\prime}\}
)
|b_{ij}|\geq 2
,

•

Q
is acyclic,

•

b_{ki}>0\iff b_{k^{\prime}i}>0
for all
i\not\in\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.

Example 5.2
.

In Theorem
1.3
, we have a family of keys all with distinguished vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}=\{1,3\}
.

Keys have the smallest number of arrows of any quiver they can be mutated into.
In fact, this is essentially true for each
|b_{ij}|
(with
i,j
fixed).

Theorem 5.3

(
[
8
, Lemmas 4.21 & 4.23, cf. Corollary 4.24]
)
.

Let
Q
be a key with distinguished vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
and
\Sigma=(\widetilde{B},\widetilde{x})
a seed whose associated quiver has mutable part
Q
.
If
b_{kk^{\prime}}(Q)=0
then
b^{\prime}_{ij}\geq b_{ij}
for every seed
(B^{\prime},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\in\mathsf{Seeds}(\Sigma)
.
(If
b_{kk^{\prime}}(Q)=\pm 1
then instead
b^{\prime}_{ij}\geq\min(b_{ij},b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)})
where
\sigma
is the transposition
(kk^{\prime})
.)

From Theorem
5.3
and Proposition
4.4
, we have the following.

Corollary 5.4
.

Suppose the mutable part of
Q
is a key with
1\not\in\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
Then the locus
\{x_{1}=x_{1}^{\prime}=0\}
is nonempty and deep.

Proof.

To see that this locus is nonempty, fix
x_{2},\ldots x_{n-1}\neq 0
and take a solution to the exchange relation

0=x_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}=\prod_{b_{i1}>0}x_{i}^{b_{i1}}+\prod_{b_{i1}<0}x_{i}^{-b_{i1}}

(as a polynomial in
x_{n}
).
In order to apply Proposition
4.4
, we need to know that every
x_{i}\neq 0
for
i>1
on this locus.
This follows from the fact that
b_{i1}\neq 0
and
Q
is acyclic (see
[
17
, Corollary 5.4]
).
∎

Example 5.5
.

Consider the key

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}

with

. We consider the locus

, which is given by

,

(the nonzero conditions on

come from the exchange relations

and

Any point in this locus is deep by Corollary

, and it is easy to see that it has a trivial stabilizer. Therefore every point in this locus is mysterious.

.

Consider the key

5.2.
Forks

The quivers from Definition
5.1
are closely related to a family of quivers considered by Warkentin in
[
22
]
. These give additional families of quivers with deep (and often mysterious) points. We again summarize the relevant results and give further examples.

Definition 5.7
.

A quiver is
abundant
if
|b_{ij}|\geq 2
for all
i\neq j
.

Definition 5.8

(
[
22
, Definition 2.1]
)
.

A
fork

Q
is an abundant quiver which is not acyclic, with a distinguished vertex
r
(called the
point of return
) such that:

(1)

the full subquiver of
Q
with vertices
j
with
r\rightarrow j
(resp.
j\rightarrow r
) forms an acyclic subquiver,

(2)

for each oriented cycle through
r
,
i\rightarrow r\rightarrow j\rightarrow i
, we have
b_{ij}>\max(b_{ir},b_{rj}).

Definition 5.9
.

The
fork-less part
of a mutation class is the subset of quivers which are not forks.

Proposition 5.10

(
[
22
, Lemma 2.8, Corollary 4.5]
)
.

If
Q
is a fork with point of return
r
, then
\mu_{j}(Q)
is a fork with point of return
j
for
j\neq r
.
Thus the fork-less part of a mutation class is connected.

Because forks are abundant, any mutation sequence that goes through forks will automatically satisfy the
|b_{1i}|\geq 2
condition in Proposition
4.4
.
When the fork-less part is finite, then checking the conditions of Proposition
4.4
becomes a finite computation.
This is especially easy for abundant acyclic quivers.

Proposition 5.11

(
[
22
, Lemmas 2.5 and 2.8]
)
.

Suppose
Q
is an abundant acyclic quiver. Then every mutation equivalent quiver is abundant.

We thus have the following corollary (whose proof is essentially identical to Corollary
5.4
).

Corollary 5.12
.

If the mutable part of
Q
is an abundant acyclic quiver,
then the locus
\{x_{1}=x_{1}^{\prime}=0\}
is nonempty and deep.

.

5.3.
Locally acyclic

We have seen several examples of acyclic quivers which have mysterious points.
So it is unsurprising that there are quivers which are not acyclic, but are locally-acyclic, which have mysterious points.

Example 5.16

(
[
22
, Example 12.30]
)
.

Consider a quiver
Q
of the form:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 4}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 3\par}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c+ab}

with
\min(a,b,c,d,e,f)\geq 2
.
This quiver is locally acyclic (and in fact Banff).
The subquiver supported by the vertices
\{1,2,3\}
is mutation acyclic; indeed it is acyclic in
\mu_{1}(Q)
.
Thus we can specify a point in the cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}(\Sigma(Q))
by specifying a nonzero value for
x_{4}
and the cluster variables in each of
\Sigma(Q),\mu_{1}(\Sigma(Q)),\mu_{2}(\mu_{1}(\Sigma(Q))),
and
\mu_{3}(\mu_{1}(\Sigma(Q)))
(satisfying the exchange relations).
If we choose
x_{1}=0
in both
\Sigma(Q)
and
\mu_{1}(\Sigma(Q))
, then the other cluster variables will necessarily be nonzero, and by Proposition
4.4
these points will be deep.
The same argument shows that all the points in

\{x_{1}=x^{\prime}_{1}=0\}\bigsqcup\{x_{2}=x^{\prime}_{2}=0\}\bigsqcup\{x_{3}=x^{\prime}_{3}=0\}

are deep (where
x_{i}
is the cluster variable in
\mu_{1}(\Sigma(Q))
and
x_{i}^{\prime}
is the cluster variable in
\mu_{i}(\mu_{1}(\Sigma(Q)))
).

The quiver
Q
can be shown locally acyclic in multiple ways. In particular,
\mu_{1}(Q)
has a sink vertex at
2
and the subquiver supported by the vertices
\{1,3,4\}
is acyclic in
Q
.
So the points in the cluster variety with
x_{4}=0
in both
\Sigma(Q)
and
\mu_{4}(\Sigma(Q))
are also deep.

Often these points have no stabilizer. For example, when the values
a,b,c,d,e,f
are all pairwise coprime.
So this gives many examples of mysterious points.

Further examples of locally-acyclic quivers with finite fork-less part (which may consist of only abundant quivers) can be constructed via
[
7
, Theorem 4.27]
.

We also remind the reader of
[
6
, Propositions 3.15-16]
, which state that having a mysterious point is a quasi-cluster isomorphism invariant.

Proposition 5.17
.

If
Q
is a quiver so that its cluster algebra has mysterious points, then adding frozen variables to
Q
will still produce a cluster algebra with mysterious points.

Proof.

This is the contrapositive of
[
6
, Proposition 3.18]

∎

Thus, while some of the examples of cluster algebras with mysterious points we have produced may not have really full rank, we can always add frozen variables to produce example of cluster algebras with really full rank and mysterious points.

5.4.
Abundant without mysterious points

Based on the previous examples, it seems that the property of having mysterious points is subtle and depends on number-theoretic properties of the multiplicities of arrows. In fact, we have the following result.

Proposition 5.18
.

Consider the cluster algebra
A
with exchange matrix associated to the quiver

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\boxed{\overline{3}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\boxed{\overline{1}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\boxed{\overline{2}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 3}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}

(1)

If either
\min(a,b)=1
or
\gcd(a,b)>1
, then
A
has no mysterious points.

(2)

Else,
A
has mysterious points.

Proof.

We assume that
a
and
b
are not both equal to
1
; otherwise the result follows from Theorem
3.1
. The cluster dilation group is

\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)=\{t=(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{\overline{1}},t_{\overline{2}},t_{\overline{3}})\in(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{6}\mid t_{1}^{a}t_{\overline{3}}=t_{3}^{a}t_{2}^{b}t_{\overline{1}}=t_{1}^{b}t_{\overline{2}}=1\}.

Let
p\in\mathcal{A}
have trivial stabilizer in
\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)
. If
p\in\mathcal{O}_{\emptyset}=T(\Sigma)
we are done. If
p\in\mathcal{O}_{\{3\}}
then
x_{1}(p)x_{2}(p)\neq 0
, so we may freeze vertex
2
in order to reduce to the case of Lemma
4.1
. Similarly if
p\in\mathcal{O}_{\{1\}}
.

It remains to treat the case of the maximal independent sets
\{2,3\}
and
\{1\}
. If
p\in\mathcal{O}_{\{2,3\}}
but either
x^{\prime}_{2}(p)
or
x_{3}^{\prime}(p)\neq 0
, we mutate at the corresponding vertex to reduce to the case of the previous paragraph. So we assume
x_{2}(p)=x^{\prime}_{2}(p)=x_{3}(p)=x_{3}^{\prime}(p)=0
. If, say,
a>1
, this implies that
(\zeta,1,1,1,1,1)\in\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)}(p)
, where
\zeta
is a primitive
a
-th root of unity. So this case cannot happen.

Now assume that
p\in\mathcal{O}_{\{1\}}
. We may also assume that
x^{\prime}_{1}(p)=0
. If
\gcd(a,b)>1
, we have
(1,\xi,1,1,1,1)\in\mathrm{Stab}_{\mathrm{Dil}(\Sigma)}(p)
, where
\xi
is a primitive
\gcd(a,b)
-root of unity, a contradiction. If
\min\{a,b\}=1
, say
a=1
, then we apply a strategy similar to that of the proof of Case 2.1 of Theorem
3.1
, and we see that
p\in T(\mu_{1}\mu_{3}(\Sigma))
, indeed

x^{\prime\prime}_{1}=\frac{x^{\prime}_{3}+x_{2}^{b}x_{\overline{1}}}{x_{1}}=\frac{\frac{1+x_{\overline{3}}x_{1}}{x_{3}}+x_{2}^{b}x_{\overline{1}}}{x_{1}}=\frac{1+x_{\overline{3}}x_{1}+x_{3}x_{2}^{b}x_{\overline{1}}}{x_{1}x_{3}}=\frac{x^{\prime}_{1}+x_{\overline{3}}}{x_{3}}

so
x^{\prime\prime}_{1}(p)\neq 0
and thus
p
is not mysterious. We are done with (1). Statement (2) is Theorem
1.3
. ∎
