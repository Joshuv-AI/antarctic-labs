Abstract
. Let Q Q be a set of primes with relative density δ \delta . We count integers in [ 1 , x ] [1,x] with prime factors all in Q Q that also have a divisor in ( y , 2 ​ y ] (y,2y] . We establish the order of magnitude for all δ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \delta\in(0,1] . This generalizes the case δ = 1 \delta=1 from the 2008 work of Ford. We also show that there is a phase transition at the critical point δ = 1 / log ⁡ 4 \delta=1/\log 4 , for which we explicitly determine the behaviour. Along the way, we prove estimates for uniform order statistics with strong barrier conditions which are of independent interest.

Introduction

In his seminal 2008 work
[
ford2008distribution
]

Suppose

(1.1)

H(x,y,2y)\asymp\frac{x}{(\log y)^{\eta}(\log\log y)^{3/2}},

where

\eta:=1-\frac{1+\log\log 2}{\log 2}.

The number
\eta

[

Using

1.1

A(N)\asymp\frac{N^{2}}{(\log N)^{\eta}(\log\log N)^{3/2}}.

The aim of the present paper is to study multiplication tables where the row and column numbers are restricted to a special set which is of arithmetic interest. To be more precise, we start with an infinite set

\Big|\#\big(Q\cap[1,x]\big)-\frac{\delta x}{\log x}\Big|\leq\frac{\kappa x}{(\log x)^{2}},\quad(x\geq 2).

We will frequently write

In the present paper, we will focus on the important case

\delta=1

\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{f(n)}{n}\geq\bigg(\sum_{a\leq x}\frac{\mu^{2}(a)\tau_{k}(a)}{a}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{m\leq x}\frac{\mu^{2}(m)g(m)}{m}\bigg)^{-1}.

Since
g(p)=\tau_{k}(p)-f(p)=k-f(p),
we can can use the inequality
1+t\leq e^{t}
to write

(2.6)

\sum_{m\leq x}\frac{\mu^{2}(m)g(m)}{m}\leq\exp\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x}\log\Big(1+\frac{g(p)}{p}\Big)\bigg)\leq\exp\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{k-f(p)}{p}\bigg).

On the other hand, by standard analytic estimates, we have

(2.7)

\sum_{a\leq x}\frac{\mu^{2}(m)\tau_{k}(a)}{a}\gg_{k}(\log x)^{k}\asymp_{k}\exp\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{k}{p}\bigg).

By substituting (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) into (
2.5
), we find that

(2.8)

\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{f(n)}{n}\gg_{k}\exp\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{f(p)}{p}\bigg).

The equations (
2.8
) and (
2.3
) together show that

(2.9)

\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{f(n)}{n}\asymp_{k}\exp\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{f(p)}{p}\bigg).

To finish the proof, we Note that all products
mp
with
m\leq x^{1/3}
and
p>x^{1/3}
are distinct. Hence, we can bound the sum from below by isolating these terms:

(2.10)

\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\geq\sum_{m\leq x^{1/3}}f(m)\sum_{x^{1/3}<p\leq x/m}f(p).

Since
m\leq x^{1/3}
, the upper limit of the inner sum satisfies
x/m\geq x^{2/3}>\sqrt{x}
. This places
x/m
inside the domain where our hypothesis
\sum_{p\leq y}f(p)\geq cy/\log y
holds. We can therefore estimate the inner sum by writing:

\sum_{x^{1/3}<p\leq x/m}f(p)=\sum_{p\leq x/m}f(p)-\sum_{p\leq x^{1/3}}f(p)\geq c\frac{x/m}{\log(x/m)}-O_{k}\bigg(\frac{x^{1/3}}{\log x}\bigg).

Because
\log(x/m)\asymp\log x
and the error term is negligible relative to the main term when
x/m\geq x^{2/3}
, the inner sum is
\gg_{c,k}\frac{x}{m\log x}
for sufficiently large
x
. Substituting this back into (
2.10
) yields

\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\gg_{c,k}\frac{x}{\log x}\sum_{m\leq x^{1/3}}\frac{f(m)}{m}.

By our result in (
2.9
), the sum over
m
is

\asymp_{k}\exp\Big(\sum_{p\leq x^{1/3}}\frac{f(p)}{p}\Big).

We can extend this prime sum up to
x
at the cost of a constant factor depending on
k
, since

\sum_{x^{1/3}<p\leq x}\frac{f(p)}{p}\leq k\sum_{x^{1/3}<p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}\ll_{k}1.

Using Mertens’ theorem,
1/\log x\asymp\exp\big(-\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}\big)
, we arrive at the lower bound:

(2.11)

\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\gg_{c,k}x\cdot\exp\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{f(p)-1}{p}\bigg).

For the upper bound, we use estimates on sums of multiplicative functions which appear in the literature. By
[
koukoulopoulos2019distribution
, Theorem 14.2]
, we have

\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\ll_{k}x\exp\bigg(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{f(p)-1}{p}\bigg).

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Let

Lemma 3.2

For any given

\sum_{a\in\mathscr{A}}\frac{L(a)}{a}\gg\frac{\left(\sum_{a\in\mathscr{A}}\frac{\tau(a)}{a}\right)^{2}}{\left(\sum_{a\in\mathscr{A}}\frac{W(a)}{a}\right)},

Lemma 3.3

Let

1.2

Proof.

By the definition of
\lambda_{j}

\leq\delta\log 2

1/q\ll 1/\lambda_{j}\leq 1/\log\lambda_{j-1}

(3.5)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{j-1}<p\leq\lambda_{j}\\

By

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{j-1}<p\leq\lambda_{j}\\

Exponentiating both sides yields

Applying this recursively down to

2^{j}(\log\lambda_{0})\exp\bigg({-r\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\frac{1}{\log{\lambda_{i}}}}\bigg)\leq\log\lambda_{j}\leq 2^{j}(\log\lambda_{0})\exp\bigg({r\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\frac{1}{\log{\lambda_{i}}}}\bigg).

Lemma 3.6

Remark
.

The condition (iii) represents a conceptual refinement of the methods found in the works of Ford
[
ford2008distribution

In contrast, we impose a

By defining the region

For the lower range

For the upper range

gives an upper bound of the form

(4.1)

H_{Q}(x,y,2y)\ll_{c,\kappa}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\delta}(\log y)^{1+\delta}}\sum_{a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}}\frac{L(a)}{a}.

We will cut up the above sum according to the number of prime factors of
a
. Hence, we desire an upper bound for

T_{Q}(k,2y):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}\\
\omega(a)=k\end{subarray}}\frac{L(a)}{a},\quad(k\in\mathbb{N}).

The next lemma is the first step in this direction.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
c,\kappa
be as in
Theorem

1.2
. Suppose
y
is sufficiently large in terms of
c,\kappa
. Let
v=\lfloor\frac{1}{\log 2}\log\log y\rfloor
, and suppose that
1\leq k\leq 10v
. One has

T_{Q}(k,2y)\ll(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{k}U_{k}(v),

where

(4.2)

U_{k}(v)=\underset{{0\leq\xi_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\xi_{k}\leq 1}}{\idotsint}\min_{0\leq j\leq k}2^{-j}(2^{v\xi_{1}}+\cdots+2^{v\xi_{j}}+1)d\bm{\xi}.

Lemma

4.4
is proven in
Section

8
. The last step is to provide an estimate for
U_{k}(v)
. This is the content of the next lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
k,v
be integers such that
1\leq k\leq 10v
. One has

U_{k}(v)\ll\frac{1+|v-k|}{(k+1)!(2^{(k-v)/2}+1)}.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma 3.6
[
ford2006integers
]
combined with the refined Lemma 3.3.3 from
[
koukoulopoulos2010generalized
]
. We give a sketch of the proof here, citing the relevant results. Let
\gamma\geq 0
be given, and define

\mathcal{T}(k,v,\gamma):=\{\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:0\leq\xi_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\xi_{k}\leq 1,2^{v\xi_{1}}+\cdots+2^{v\xi_{j}}\geq 2^{j-\gamma}\;(1\leq j\leq k)\}.

Lemma 3.3.3 from
[
koukoulopoulos2010generalized
]
states that for
\gamma\geq 0,v\geq 1,
and
\gamma+v-k\geq-C,
where
C\geq 0
is a constant, one has

\Vol\big(\mathcal{T}(k,v,\gamma)\big)\ll_{C}\frac{(\gamma+2)(v-k+\gamma+C+1)}{(k+1)!}.

If we assume
1\leq k\leq v,
then we can take
C=0
above, and the bound becomes

(4.3)

\Vol\big(\mathcal{T}(k,v,\gamma)\big)\ll\frac{(\gamma+2)(v-k+\gamma+1)}{(k+1)!},\quad(1\leq k\leq v).

As is noted in the proof of Lemma 3.6 in
[
ford2006integers
]
, we have

U_{k}(v)\leq\sum_{m\geq 0}2^{1-m}\Vol\big(\mathcal{T}(k,v,m+1)\big).

By substituting (
4.3
) into the above, we find that for
1\leq k\leq v
, one has

\displaystyle U_{k}(v)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{(k+1)!}\sum_{m\geq 0}2^{-m}(m+3)(v-k+m+2)

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{v-k+1}{(k+1)!}+\frac{1}{(k+1)!}\sum_{m\geq 1}2^{-m}(m+3)(v-k+m+2).

In the
m
sum on the right hand side of the above equation, the ratio of the
(m+1)^{\text{st}}
term with the
m^{\text{th}}
term is

\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(v-k+m+3)(m+4)}{(v-k+m+2)(m+3)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(m+4)}{(m+2)}<1,

since
v-k\geq 0
. As such, the
m
sum above is dominated by the first term
m=1
, and we conclude that one has

(4.4)

\displaystyle U_{k}(v)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{v-k+1}{(k+1)!},\quad(1\leq k\leq v).

On the other hand, it is shown in Lemma 3.6 of
[
ford2006integers
]
that

\displaystyle U_{k}(v)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{|v-k|^{2}+1}{(k+1)!2^{k-v}},\quad(v\leq k\leq 10v).

Since
t+1\ll 2^{t/2}
for
t\geq 0
, the above bound can be rewritten as

(4.5)

\displaystyle U_{k}(v)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{|v-k|+1}{(k+1)!2^{(k-v)/2}},\quad(v\leq k\leq 10v).

Since
2^{(k-v)/2}\ll 1
when
k\leq v
, we can combine (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) into a single estimate:

U_{k}(v)\ll\frac{|v-k|+1}{(k+1)!(2^{(k-v)/2}+1)},\quad(1\leq k\leq 10v).

This completes the proof of the lemma.∎

We are now ready to complete the upper bound proof. We distinguish three ranges of
k
. The first one is
k>10v
. Using bound (i) from
Lemma

4.1
, we have
L(a)\leq\tau(a)=2^{k}
for every integer
a
in the summation range of
T_{Q}(k,2y)
(recall all these integers are square-free). Thus,

\sum_{k>10v}T_{Q}(k,2y)\leq\sum_{k>10v}2^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}\\
\omega(a)=k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{a}.

Note that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}\\
\omega(a)=k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{a}\leq\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\in[1,2y]\cap\mathcal{S}\\
p_{i}\neq p_{j}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{k!}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in[1,2y]\cap\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{k}.

Using
Lemma

2.1
, we thus have

\displaystyle\sum_{k>10v}T_{Q}(k,2y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k>10v}\frac{2^{k}}{k!}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in[1,2y]\cap\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>10v}\frac{\big(2\delta\log\log y+O(\kappa)\big)^{k}}{k!}.

Since
10v>10\log_{2}y>2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa)
for sufficiently large
y
, then the above Poisson sum is dominated by the single term
k=10v+1
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k>10v}T_{Q}(k,2y)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\left(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa)\right)^{10v+1}}{(10v+1)!}

\displaystyle\ll_{\kappa}\frac{\left(2\delta\log_{2}y\right)^{v}}{(v+1)!}.

Our second range is
v<k\leq 10v
. Here, we use
Lemma

4.4
and
Lemma

4.5
:

\displaystyle\sum_{v<k\leq 10v}T_{Q}(k,2y)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{v<k\leq 10v}\frac{k-v+1}{2^{(k-v)/2}}\cdot\frac{(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{k}}{(k+1)!}.

By re-indexing the sum with
j=k-v
, we can factor out the
v
-th term. Using the fact that
\frac{2\delta\log_{2}y}{v}=\delta\log 4+O(1/v)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{v<k\leq 10v}T_{Q}(k,2y)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{v}}{(v+1)!}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{j+1}{2^{j/2}}\bigg(\frac{2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa)}{v+2}\bigg)^{j}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{v}}{(v+1)!}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(j+1)\bigg(\frac{\delta\log 4}{\sqrt{2}}+o(1)\bigg)^{j}.

Because
\delta\leq 1
and
\frac{\log 4}{\sqrt{2}}\approx 0.98<1
, the infinite series can be bounded by an absolute constant. We conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{v<k\leq 10v}T_{Q}(k,2y)\ll\frac{(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{v}}{(v+1)!}\ll_{\kappa}\frac{(2\delta\log_{2}y)^{v}}{(v+1)!}.

The final range is
0\leq k\leq v
. We again apply
Lemma

4.4
and
Lemma

4.5
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq v}T_{Q}(k,2y)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{v-k+1}{(k+1)!(2^{(k-v)/2}+1)}\cdot(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{k}

\displaystyle\asymp\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{v-k+1}{(k+1)!}\cdot(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{k}.

We wish to replace
(k+1)!
with
v\cdot k!
in the denominator of the above sum. This can be done by noting that the contribution of
k\leq v/100
is negligible. Indeed, the sum

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v/100-1}\frac{v-k+1}{(k+1)!}\cdot(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{k}

is, by the ratio test, dominated by the single term
k=v/100-1
. As such, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k\leq v}T_{Q}(k,2y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v/100\leq k\leq v}\frac{v-k+1}{v}\cdot\frac{(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{k}}{k!}+O\Big(\frac{(99v+2)\cdot(2\delta\log\log y)^{v/100-1}}{100(v/100)!}\Big)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{v/100\leq k\leq v}\frac{v-k+1}{v}\cdot\frac{(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{v-k+1}{v}\cdot\frac{(2\delta\log_{2}y+O(\kappa))^{k}}{k!},

since the error term in the first line is exponentially smaller than the term
k=v
. Putting together the bounds from our three cases gives

H_{Q}(x,y,2y)\ll_{c,\kappa}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\delta}(\log y)^{1+\delta}}\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{v-k+1}{v}\cdot\frac{(2\delta\log_{2}y)^{k}}{k!}.

This establishes the upper bound for
Proposition

1.4
. Combining this with the lower bound (
3.10
) established in
Section

3
yields the asymptotic order of magnitude claimed in (
1.7
). This completes the proof of
Proposition

1.4
.

5.
Proof of
Proposition

1.5
: Phase Transitions for a poisson-type sum

In this section, we study the behavior of the sum
\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{(v-k+1)}{v}
for varying
\lambda.
In particular, we demonstrate a change of behaviour for the sum with the critical point being
\lambda=v
. Before we begin, we state one useful lemma on the partial sums of the Poisson distribution. The proof follows from the saddle-point expansion for the Poisson distribution; see
[
jensen1995saddlepoint
, Example 2.4.5]
for the general asymptotic expansion from which this leading-order term is derived.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\lambda
be a given number, and let
z=z(\lambda)
be such that
z=o(\lambda^{2/3}).
Then

\sum_{k\leq\lambda+z}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\sim\frac{e^{\lambda}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{z/\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt\quad(\lambda\to\infty).

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of
Proposition

1.5
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. For convenience throughout the proof, we define the parameter
\theta:=\lambda-v
. Since
\lambda=2\delta\log\log y
and
v=\lfloor\frac{1}{\log 2}\log\log y\rfloor
, we note the relation
\theta\asymp(\delta-\frac{1}{\log 4})\log\log y
. Furthermore, we will write

a_{k}:=\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{v-k+1}{v}.

To establish the bounds in the proposition, it is convenient to divide the range of
\theta
into five distinct regimes. We will prove the following five estimates, which collectively imply the proposition:

(i)

If
\delta\in\big(\epsilon,\frac{1}{\log 4}-\epsilon\big)
(so that
\theta\leq 0
and
|\theta|\gg_{\epsilon}\lambda
), then

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\asymp_{\epsilon}e^{\lambda}.

(ii)

If
\delta\in\big(\frac{1}{\log 4}+\epsilon,1\big]
(so that
\theta>0
and
\theta\gg_{\epsilon}\lambda
), then

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\asymp_{\epsilon}\frac{\lambda^{v}}{(v+1)!}.

(iii)

If
|\theta|\leq\sqrt{\lambda},
then

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\asymp\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}\asymp\frac{e^{\lambda}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}.

(iv)

If
\theta\leq-\sqrt{\lambda}
and
\theta=o(\lambda),
then

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\asymp\frac{|\theta|e^{\lambda}}{\lambda}.

(v)

If
\theta\geq\sqrt{\lambda}
and
\theta=o(\lambda),
then

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\asymp\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}\cdot\frac{v}{\theta^{2}}.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}

\displaystyle=\bigg(1+\frac{1}{v}\bigg)\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}-\frac{1}{v}\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{k\cdot\lambda^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=\bigg(1+\frac{1}{v}\bigg)\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}-\frac{\lambda}{v}\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{v-\lambda+1}{v}\bigg)\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}+\frac{\lambda}{v}\bigg(\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}-1\bigg).

Since
v-\lambda=-\theta
and
\lambda/v\asymp 1,
the above calculation implies that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1-\theta}{v}\bigg)\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}+\frac{\lambda^{v+1}}{v\cdot v!}+O(1).

Equation

5.1
is a key identity which will be used throughout the proof. We now begin proving the different parts of the Lemma.

Proof of (i)
. Here, we have
-\theta\geq 0
and
-\theta\asymp_{\epsilon}v.
Thus, from (
5.1
), we have

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\asymp\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}+\frac{\lambda^{v+1}}{(v+1)!}+O(1)=\sum_{k=1}^{v+1}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}+O(1).

On the one hand, we have

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v+1}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\leq\sum_{0\leq j\leq\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}=e^{\lambda}.

This proves the upper bound for (i). On the other hand, since
\delta<1/\log 4
we have
\lambda<v
. Thus,

(5.3)

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v+1}\lambda^{k}/k!\geq\sum_{1\leq k\leq\lambda}\lambda^{k}/k!.

By
Lemma

5.1
, we have

(5.4)

\sum_{1\leq k\leq\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\sim\frac{e^{\lambda}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt=\frac{e^{\lambda}}{2}.

This proves the lower bound in (i) and hence concludes the proof of this part.

Proof of (ii)
. In the case when
\delta>\frac{1}{\log 4}+\epsilon
, we have
(\delta\log 4)^{-1}\leq 1-\epsilon/2
. Furthermore, since
v=\frac{1}{\log 2}\log\log y+O(1)
, then for sufficiently large
y
, we have

(5.5)

\frac{v}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\delta\log 4}+\frac{\epsilon}{4}\leq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{4}.

By making the change of index
j=v-k,
we see that

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}=\sum_{0\leq j\leq v-1}a_{v-j}=\sum_{0\leq j\leq v-1}\frac{j+1}{v}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{v-j}}{(v-j)!}.

As such,

\frac{1}{a_{v}}\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}=\sum_{0\leq j\leq v-1}(j+1)\lambda^{-j}\frac{v!}{(v-j)!}\leq\sum_{0\leq j\leq v-1}(j+1)\Big(\frac{v}{\lambda}\Big)^{j},

having used the inequality
v!/(v-j)!\leq v^{j}
. Substituting (
5.5
) into the above, we see that

(5.6)

\frac{1}{a_{v}}\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\leq\sum_{0\leq j\leq v-1}(j+1)\cdot\big(1-\epsilon/4\big)^{j}\ll_{\epsilon}1,

where we’ve handled the
j
-sum above using a classical summation identity for geometric series:

\sum_{0\leq j\leq v+1}(j+1)r^{j}\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq\infty}jr^{j-1}=\frac{1}{(1-r)^{2}},\quad(|r|<1).

Multiplying through by
a_{v}
on both sides of (
5.6
) shows that

(5.7)

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\ll_{\epsilon}a_{v}.

On the other hand, since
a_{k}>0
for
1\leq k\leq v
, we have

(5.8)

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\geq a_{v}\asymp\frac{\lambda^{v}}{(v+1)!}.

Equation

5.6
together with (
5.8
) completes the proof of (ii).

Proof of (iii).
It is helpful to treat the cases
\theta\leq 1/2
and
\theta>1/2
separately. Let us first suppose
\theta\leq 1/2
. In this case,
(1-\theta)/v\geq 0
, and so (
5.1
) is a sum of two non-negative terms (and a
O(1)
error term). The first of these two terms is

(5.9)

\bigg(\frac{1-\theta}{v}\bigg)\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\asymp\bigg(\frac{1+|\theta|}{v}\bigg)\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}.

Since
|\theta|\ll\sqrt{\lambda}
, we have by
Lemma

5.1
that

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq\lambda-\theta}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\sim\frac{e^{\lambda}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{-\theta/\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt.

We see that

\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{-\theta/\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt=1,

and so

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\asymp e^{\lambda}.

Substituting this back into (
5.9
), and using the fact that
|\theta|\ll\lambda
proves that

(5.10)

\bigg(\frac{1-\theta}{v}\bigg)\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\asymp\bigg(\frac{1+|\theta|}{v}\bigg)\cdot e^{\lambda}.

The second term in (
5.1
) is

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{v+1}}{(v+1)!}\asymp\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}=\frac{v^{v}}{v!}\bigg(\frac{\lambda}{v}\bigg)^{v}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{e^{v}}{\sqrt{v}}\bigg(1-\frac{\theta}{\lambda}\bigg)^{-\lambda\frac{v}{\lambda}},

By Stirling’s formula. The assumption
|\theta|\leq\sqrt{\lambda}
implies that
(1-\theta/\lambda)^{-\lambda}\asymp e^{\theta}
. In addition,
v/\lambda=(1-\theta/\lambda),
hence

(5.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{v+1}}{(v+1)!}\asymp\frac{e^{v}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}e^{\theta(1-\frac{\theta}{\lambda})}\asymp\frac{e^{\lambda-\frac{\theta^{2}}{\lambda}}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\asymp\frac{e^{\lambda}}{\sqrt{\lambda}},

again using the assumption
|\theta|\leq\sqrt{\lambda}
. Substituting (
5.10
) and (
5.11
) into (
5.1
) implies that

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{(v-k+1)}{v}\asymp\bigg(\frac{1+|\theta|}{v}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\bigg)\cdot e^{\lambda}\asymp\frac{e^{\lambda}}{\sqrt{\lambda}},

since
|\theta|\ll\sqrt{\lambda}
. This concludes the proof of (iii) in the case when
\theta\leq 1/2
.

Now suppose
\theta>1/2
. In this case, the right-hand side of (
5.1
) is a sum of one positive and one possibly negative term which are potentially of the same size. Thus, we must be careful of potential cancellations. By the triangle inequality and (
5.1
), we have

(5.12)

\bigg|\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\bigg|\leq\bigg(\frac{1+|\theta|}{v}\bigg)\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}+\frac{\lambda^{v+1}}{v\cdot v!}+O(1)\ll\frac{e^{\lambda}}{\sqrt{\lambda}},

with the last inequality following by the same argument we used in the case when
\theta\leq 1/2
above.
It remains to prove a matching lower bound. Note that

a_{k}=\frac{v-k+1}{v}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\geq\frac{1}{v}>0.

Furthermore, since
\theta\leq\sqrt{\lambda}
we have

(5.13)

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq\lambda-\theta}a_{k}\geq\sum_{1\leq k\leq\lambda-\sqrt{\lambda}}a_{k}.

By part (v) of our proof (which we prove below),
3
3

3

The proof of (v) appears after that of (iii) for aesthetic reasons. The reader can verify that there is no circular reasoning, as the proof of (v) does not use the claim (iii) in any way.
we have

(5.14)

\sum_{1\leq k\leq\lambda-\sqrt{\lambda}}a_{k}\gg\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}\cdot\frac{v}{\lambda}.

By (
5.13
) and (
5.14
) we have

(5.15)

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}a_{k}\gg\frac{e^{\lambda}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}.

Putting together the matching upper bound of (
5.12
) and the lower bound of (
5.15
) proves (iii) in this second case.

Proof of (iv).
If
\theta<0
, then
v\geq\lambda
. Arguing as in (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), we have

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\asymp e^{\lambda}.

Substituting this into (
5.1
) and using the fact that
(1-\theta)/v>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{(v-k+1)}{v}

\displaystyle\asymp\bigg(\frac{1-\theta}{v}\bigg)\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}+\frac{\lambda^{v+1}}{v\cdot v!}+O(1)

(5.16)

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{\lambda^{v+1}}{v\cdot v!}+\frac{e^{\lambda}(1+|\theta|)}{v}+O(1).

Since
\lambda/v\asymp 1,
we have

(5.17)

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{v+1}}{v\cdot v!}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}.

By Stirling’s approximation,

\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}\asymp\frac{e^{v}\lambda^{v}}{v^{v}\sqrt{v}}=\frac{e^{v}}{\sqrt{v}}\cdot\exp\big(v\log(\lambda/v)\big)=\frac{e^{v}}{\sqrt{v}}\cdot\exp\big(v\log(1+\theta/v)\big),

having written
\lambda=v+\theta.
Using the inequality
\log(1+u)\leq u-u^{2}/4,
which is valid for
u=\theta/v=o(1)
, the above becomes

\frac{\lambda^{v+1}}{v\cdot v!}\ll\frac{e^{v}}{\sqrt{v}}\cdot\exp\bigg(\theta-\frac{\theta^{2}}{4v}\bigg)=\frac{e^{v+\theta}}{\sqrt{v}}\cdot\exp\bigg(-\frac{\theta^{2}}{4v}\bigg)=\frac{e^{\lambda}}{\sqrt{v}}\cdot\exp\bigg(-\frac{\theta^{2}}{4v}\bigg).

Since we’ve assumed that
|\theta|\geq\sqrt{\lambda}
, then
e^{-\frac{\theta^{2}}{4v}}\ll|\theta|/\sqrt{\lambda},
and so we conclude that

\frac{\lambda^{v+1}}{v\cdot v!}\ll\frac{e^{\lambda}}{\sqrt{v}}\cdot\frac{|\theta|}{\sqrt{v}}\asymp\frac{|\theta|e^{\lambda}}{v}.

Substituting this into (
5.16
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{(v-k+1)}{v}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{e^{\lambda}|\theta|}{v}.

This completes the proof of (iv).

Proof of (v).
First, note the trivial bound

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v/100}\frac{\lambda^{k}(v-k+1)}{k!\cdot v}\ll\exp[v/10],

which is considerably smaller than the claimed size of the full sum over
k
. Let us now consider the range
k\in(v/100,v]
. For each
k
, define
h_{k}
to be the smallest non-negative integer such that

\frac{\lambda^{k-h_{k}}}{(k-h_{k})!}\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}.

The number
h_{k}
exists for each
k\in[v/100,v]
since it is the minimum of a nonempty finite set. Indeed,
\lambda^{0}/0!<(1/2)\lambda^{k}/k!
for each
k\in[v/100,v]
, and

h_{k}=\min\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:\frac{\lambda^{k-n}}{(k-n)!}\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\bigg\}.

Having proven the existence of
h_{k}
, our next goal is to identify its size.

Claim
.

For
k\in[v/100,v]
, we have

(5.18)

h_{k}\asymp\frac{1}{\log(\lambda/k)}.

Proof of claim.

Let us first show that
h_{k}\ll 1/\log(\lambda/k)
. Let
\beta>0
be a parameter to be chosen later. We note that
h_{k}\leq\lfloor\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor
is equivalent to

(5.19)

\lambda^{-\lfloor\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor}\bigg(\frac{k!}{(k-\lfloor\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor)!}\bigg)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Since
k!/(k-j!)\leq k^{j}
, we have

\lambda^{-\lfloor\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor}\bigg(\frac{k!}{(k-\lfloor\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor)!}\bigg)\leq(k/\lambda)^{\lfloor\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor}=e^{-\beta}\big(\lambda/k\big)^{f},

where
f=\{\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\}
, and
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part. Since
f\in[0,1)
and
k\leq\lambda
by the assumption of (v), we have
\big(\lambda/k\big)^{f}\leq\lambda/k
. Thus,

\lambda^{-\lfloor\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor}\bigg(\frac{k!}{(k-\lfloor\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor)!}\bigg)\leq e^{-\beta}\frac{\lambda}{k}.

By the assumption
k\geq v/100
, we have

e^{-\beta}\frac{\lambda}{k}\leq\exp\big[\log 100-\beta-\log(v/\lambda)\big]=\exp\big[\log 100-\beta-\log(1-\theta/\lambda)\big].

Since
\theta=o(\lambda)
, then for sufficiently large
\lambda
we have
-\log(1-\theta/\lambda)\leq 1
. The conclusion is that

\lambda^{-\lfloor\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor}\bigg(\frac{k!}{(k-\lfloor\beta/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor)!}\bigg)\leq\exp\big[1+\log 100-\beta\big].

By choosing
\beta
sufficiently large, say
\beta=10
, we see that the above implies (
5.19
), and hence
h_{k}\ll 1/\log(\lambda/k)
.

Let us now show that
h_{k}\gg 1/\log(\lambda/k)
. Let
\gamma\in(0,1]
be a parameter to be chosen later. Because of the minimality condition in the definition of
h_{k}
, we note that the claim
h_{k}>\lfloor\gamma/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor
is equivalent to

(5.20)

\lambda^{-\lfloor\gamma/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor}\bigg(\frac{k!}{(k-\lfloor\gamma/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor)!}\bigg)>\frac{1}{2}.

Write
x=\lfloor\gamma/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor
, and note that
0<x\ll\gamma\sqrt{\lambda}
by our assumption that
\theta\geq\sqrt{\lambda}
. Indeed, since
\log(\lambda/k)
is minimized on our interval at
k=v
, and
\log(\lambda/v)=\log(1+\theta/v)\geq\theta/(2v)
, we have

x\leq\frac{\gamma}{\log(\lambda/v)}\leq\frac{2\gamma v}{\theta}\ll\gamma\sqrt{\lambda}.

Using our assumption
k\geq v/100
, we therefore have

\displaystyle\lambda^{x}\bigg(\frac{(k-x)!}{k!}\bigg)\leq\bigg(\frac{\lambda}{k}\bigg)^{x}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{x}{k}\Big)^{-x}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\lambda}{k}\bigg)^{x}\cdot\exp\bigg(\frac{x^{2}}{k}+O\Big(\frac{x^{3}}{k^{2}}\Big)\bigg)

(5.21)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\gamma+O(\gamma^{2})\Big).

Choosing
\gamma
to be a sufficiently small positive real number, we can make the right hand side of (
5.21
) to be
<2
, i.e.,

\lambda^{x}\bigg(\frac{(k-x)!}{k!}\bigg)<2.

Hence, (
5.20
) is satisfied with such a
\gamma
, and so
h_{k}\geq\lfloor\gamma/\log(\lambda/k)\rfloor\gg 1/\log(\lambda/k)
. This completes the proof of the claim.
∎

Now, write
v=v_{0}
, and define recursively
v_{j+1}=v_{j}-h_{v_{j}}
for
j\geq 0
. We stop this sequence at step
J
, where
v_{J+1}\leq v/100
.

Claim
.

For
j\geq 0
and
k\in(v_{j+1},v_{j}]
, we have

\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\leq\frac{1}{2^{j}}\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}.

Proof of claim.

We proceed by induction on
j
to show the bounds at the sequence endpoints:
\lambda^{v_{j}}/v_{j}!
. The base case
j=0
is trivial since
v_{0}=v
.

Assume the bounds hold for some
j\geq 0
. By the definition of
h_{v_{j}}
and the inductive hypothesis, we have

(5.22)

\frac{\lambda^{v_{j+1}}}{v_{j+1}!}=\frac{\lambda^{v_{j}-h_{v_{j}}}}{(v_{j}-h_{v_{j}})!}\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda^{v_{j}}}{v_{j}!}\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{2^{j}}\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}\bigg)=\frac{1}{2^{j+1}}\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!},

completing the induction. Finally, for any
k\in(v_{j+1},v_{j}]
, the sequence
\lambda^{k}/k!
is monotonically increasing in
k
(since
k\leq v<\lambda
). Therefore, its value is bounded above by the value at the right endpoint (
v_{j}
). This proves the claim.
∎

We can evaluate the sum of
(v-k+1)
over a single interval
(v_{j+1},v_{j}]
exactly by recognizing it as an arithmetic progression. The sequence has
v_{j}-v_{j+1}=h_{v_{j}}
terms. The first term (when
k=v_{j+1}+1
) is
v-v_{j+1}
, and the last term (when
k=v_{j}
) is
v-v_{j}+1
. Using the standard formula for the sum of an arithmetic progression, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in(v_{j+1},v_{j}]}(v-k+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{v_{j}}}{2}\bigg[(v-v_{j}+1)+(v-v_{j+1})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{v_{j}}}{2}\bigg((v-v_{j})+(v-v_{j}+h_{v_{j}})+1\bigg)

(5.23)

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{v_{j}}}{2}\bigg(2\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}h_{v_{i}}+h_{v_{j}}+1\bigg),

for each
j\geq 0
.

By partitioning the range
(v/100,v]
into these intervals
(v_{j+1},v_{j}]
for
0\leq j\leq J
, and applying the bound from our second claim, we can establish an upper bound for the total sum.

To upper bound the interval sums, we note from (
5.18
) that
h_{k}\asymp 1/\log(\lambda/k)
is an increasing function of
k
. Therefore,
h_{v_{i}}\leq h_{v}
for all
i\leq J
. Applying this to (
5.23
) yields

\sum_{k\in(v_{j+1},v_{j}]}(v-k+1)\ll h_{v_{j}}\sum_{i=0}^{j}h_{v_{i}}\leq(j+1)h_{v}^{2}.

Dividing by
v
and applying the second claim gives

\sum_{v/100<k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{v-k+1}{v}\ll\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v\cdot v!}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\frac{j+1}{2^{j}}h_{v}^{2}\ll\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v\cdot v!}\cdot h_{v}^{2}.

For the lower bound, since all terms are positive, we restrict our attention to the interval
j=0
(i.e.,
k\in(v_{1},v_{0}]
) and use the fact that
\lambda^{k}/k!
is monotonically increasing in this range:

(5.24)

\sum_{v/100<k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{v-k+1}{v}\geq\frac{\lambda^{v_{1}}}{v_{1}!}\sum_{k\in(v_{1},v_{0}]}\frac{v-k+1}{v}.

To bound
\lambda^{v_{1}}/v_{1}!
from below, we use the minimality of
h_{v_{0}}
. By definition, the term immediately preceding
v_{1}
strictly fails the halving condition:

\frac{\lambda^{v_{1}+1}}{(v_{1}+1)!}>\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda^{v_{0}}}{v_{0}!}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}.

Multiplying by the ratio between consecutive terms gives

\frac{\lambda^{v_{1}}}{v_{1}!}=\frac{\lambda^{v_{1}+1}}{(v_{1}+1)!}\cdot\frac{v_{1}+1}{\lambda}>\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}\cdot\frac{v_{1}+1}{\lambda}.

Since
v_{1}=v-h_{v_{0}}
and
h_{v_{0}}\ll\sqrt{v}
, we have
v_{1}\sim v\sim\lambda
. Thus,
(v_{1}+1)/\lambda\gg 1
, which implies

\frac{\lambda^{v_{1}}}{v_{1}!}\gg\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}.

Substituting this into (
5.24
) gives

(5.25)

\sum_{v/100<k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{v-k+1}{v}\gg\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v\cdot v!}\sum_{k\in(v_{1},v_{0}]}(v-k+1).

By using the closed form evaluated at
j=0
in (
5.23
), we see that

\sum_{k\in(v_{1},v_{0}]}(v-k+1)=\frac{h_{v_{0}}}{2}(h_{v_{0}}+1)\asymp h_{v}^{2},

since
v_{0}=v
. Substituting this into (
5.25
) gives

\sum_{v/100<k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{v-k+1}{v}\gg\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v\cdot v!}h_{v}^{2}.

We have proven matching upper and lower bounds for the sum of
k\in(v/100,v]
. Adding the negligible contribution from
k\leq\lambda/100
, and noting that
h_{v}\asymp 1/\log(\lambda/v)\asymp v/\theta
, we conclude

\sum_{1\leq k\leq v}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{v-k+1}{v}\asymp\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}\frac{h_{v}^{2}}{v}\asymp\frac{\lambda^{v}}{v!}\cdot\frac{v}{\theta^{2}}.

This completes the proof of (v), and hence the proof of the Lemma.
∎

6.
Strong barriers for Generalized Smirnov Statistics

The proof of
Lemma

3.7
uses estimates related to uniform order statistics with barrier conditions. In particular, we need upper and lower bounds for
Q_{k}(u,v)
, which is defined in the following way: If
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}
are the order statistics for
k
random samples from the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
, then

(6.1)

Q_{k}(u,v):=\mathbb{P}\Big(\xi_{i}\geq\frac{i-u}{v},\quad 1\leq i\leq k\Big).

The quantity
Q_{k}(u,v)
has been referred to as a
generalized Smirnov statistic
in
[
ford2007generalized
]
and
[
Ford2008
]
. The next lemma is our ‘go-to’ for estimates related to these statistics. Part (i) is due to Daniels
[
daniels1945statistical
]
, and part (ii) appears explicitly as Lemma 3.3.1 in
[
koukoulopoulos2010generalized
]
, though it essentially follows from Lemma 11.1 of
[
ford2008distribution
]
and Theorem 1 of
[
Ford2008
]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
Q_{k}(u,v)
be defined as in (
6.1
), assume
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and write
w=u+v-k
.

(i)

If
k-v<u\leq 1
, then

Q_{k}(u,v)=\frac{w}{v}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{v}\bigg)^{k-1};

(ii)

Uniformly in
u>0
,
w>0
and
k
,

Q_{k}(u,v)\ll\frac{(u+1)(w+1)}{k}.

We now state and prove a lemma on generalized Smirnov statistics with strong barrier conditions. This lemma also simplifies part of the lower bound proof of the main theorem in
[
ford2008distribution
]
in the important
z=2y
case.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}
be uniform order statistics. Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1/12)
, and
C>10
. Suppose that
1\leq k\leq v
. Define the events

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\Big\{\xi_{i}\geq\frac{i-1}{v}\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}

\displaystyle:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\Bigg\{\xi_{i}\geq\max\bigg\{\frac{i-1}{v},\frac{i+\min\{i,(k-i)\}^{1/6-\epsilon}-C}{v}\bigg\}\Bigg\}.

Then,

\mathbb{P}[\mathscr{B}]=\mathbb{P}[B]\big(1+O\big(C^{-\epsilon}\big)\big).

Remark
.

One can replace the exponent
1/6-\epsilon
with the optimal
1/2-\epsilon
by a more complicated proof using first incidence conditions instead of a union bound. The exponent
1/6-\epsilon
is sufficient for our purposes of proving
Theorem

1.2
, and so we’ve decided to keep our simple non-optimal argument. Although there seems to be no literature proving the
1/2-\epsilon
exponent for the specific case of uniform order statistics, there has been recent work proving an analogous theorem for random walks with Gaussian increments in
[
arguin2020fyodorov
, Appendix B]
.

Proof of
Lemma

6.2
.

For ease of notation, write
\mu=\frac{1}{6}-\epsilon,
and define

M_{i}:=\min\{i,(k-i)\}^{\mu}\quad(1\leq i\leq k).

Note that the event
\mathscr{B}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}

\displaystyle:=B\cap\bigg(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\Big\{\xi_{i}\geq\frac{i+M_{i}-C}{v}\Big\}\bigg).

We define, for any given
u>0
,

S_{k}(u,v):=\Big\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:0\leq\xi_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\xi_{k}\leq 1;\;\xi_{i}\geq\frac{i-u}{v}\Big\}.

Note that
\mathbb{P}[B]=k!\Vol(S_{k}(1,v))
. By a simple union bound, we have

(6.2)

\mathbb{P}[\mathscr{B}|B]\geq 1-\sum_{1\leq j\leq k}\mathbb{P}\big[v\xi_{j}\leq j+M_{j}-C\big|B\big].

For fixed
j\in[1,k]
, we want to bound the probability of the event

B_{j}=B\cap\{v\xi_{j}\leq j+M_{j}-C\}.

Let
y=y_{j}=v\xi_{j}-j.
When
B_{j}
occurs, we have that
y_{j}\in[-1,M_{j}-C]
. We also note that
y_{j}\leq v-j
, since
\xi\leq 1
. We make the change of variables

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=\xi_{i}\cdot\frac{v}{y+j}\quad(1\leq i\leq j-1),

\displaystyle\beta_{i}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\xi_{j+i}-\frac{y+j}{v}\bigg)\cdot\frac{v}{v-y-j}\quad(1\leq i\leq k-j).

We see that

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{j-1})\in S_{j-1}(1,y+j),

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k-j})\in S_{k-j}(y+1,v-y-j),

and
d\bm{\xi}=d\bm{\alpha}\cdot d\bm{\beta}\cdot(v-y-j)^{k-j}(y+j)^{j-1}v^{-k}
. As such,
\mathbb{P}[B_{j}]
is bounded above by

\frac{k!}{v^{k}}\int_{-1}^{Y_{j}}\frac{(v-y-j)^{k-j}(y+j)^{j-1}}{(k-j)!(j-1)!}Q_{j-1}(1,y+j)Q_{k-j}(y+1,v-y-j)dy,

where
Y_{j}:=\min\{M_{j}-C,v-j\}.

To bound the
Q
probabilities, we can use
Lemma

6.1
(ii). By directly applying this to the above integral,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[B_{j}]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{k!(v-k+1)}{v^{k}}\int_{-1}^{Y_{j}}\frac{(v-y-j)^{k-j}(y+j)^{j-1}}{(k+1-j)!j!}(y+2)^{2}dy

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{(v-k+1)}{k\cdot v^{k}}\int_{-1}^{Y_{j}}\binom{k+1}{j}(v-y-j)^{k-j}(y+j)^{j-1}(y+2)^{2}dy.

From
Lemma

6.1
(i), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[B]=Q_{k}(1,v)\asymp\frac{v-k+1}{v}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{v}\bigg)^{k-1}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{v-k+1}{v}\cdot e^{(k-1)\log(1+1/v)}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{v-k+1}{v},

since
(1+1/v)^{k-1}\asymp 1
uniformly for all
1\leq k\leq v
. Together with the definition of
\mathbb{P}[B_{j}]
, this implies that

\mathbb{P}\big[B_{j}|B\big]=\frac{\mathbb{P}[B_{j}]}{\mathbb{P}[B]}\asymp\frac{v}{v-k+1}\mathbb{P}[B_{j}].

Using the above estimate for
\mathbb{P}[B_{j}]
, we thus have

(6.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\big[B_{j}|B\big]

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{v^{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{-1}^{Y_{j}}\binom{k+1}{j}(v-y-j)^{k-j}(y+j)^{j-1}(y+2)^{2}dy.

We require a bound on the terms inside the above integral. Note that we have

(v-y-j)^{k-j}(y+j)^{j-1}\leq\sup_{a\in[0,v]}a^{k-j}(v-a)^{j-1}=v^{k-1}\frac{(k-j)^{k-j}(j-1)^{j-1}}{(k-1)^{k-1}}.

Therefore, Stirling’s approximation gives

\binom{k+1}{j}(v-y-j)^{k-j}(y+j)^{j-1}\ll\frac{v^{k}\cdot k^{3/2}}{j^{3/2}(k-j+1)^{3/2}}.

We can interchange the sum with the integral in (
6.3
) and then apply this bound. Note that the condition
y\leq M_{j}-C
can also be written as
(y+C)^{1/\mu}\leq j\leq k-(y+C)^{1/\mu}
. As such,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}[B_{j}|B]\ll\int_{-1}^{Y}(y+2)^{2}\sum_{j=(y+C)^{1/\mu}}^{k-(y+C)^{1/\mu}}\frac{k^{3/2}}{j^{3/2}(k-j+1)^{3/2}}dy

\displaystyle\ll\int_{-1}^{Y}\frac{(y+2)^{2}}{(y+C)^{\frac{1}{2\mu}}}dy,

where
Y=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}Y_{j}.
When
y\leq C
, note that
(y+C)^{1/(2\mu)}\asymp C^{1/(2\mu)},
so

\int_{-1}^{C}\frac{(y+2)^{2}}{(y+C)^{\frac{1}{2\mu}}}dy\ll C^{-\frac{1}{2\mu}}\int_{-1}^{C}(y+2)^{2}dy\ll C^{3-\frac{1}{2\mu}}.

On the other hand, when
y>C,
we can use the bounds
(y+C)\asymp y\asymp y+2
to get

\int_{C}^{Y}\frac{(y+2)^{2}}{(y+C)^{\frac{1}{2\mu}}}dy\ll\int_{C}^{Y}y^{2-\frac{1}{2\mu}}dy\ll C^{3-\frac{1}{2\mu}}.

Using the definition of
\mu
, our conclusion is that

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}[B_{j}|B]\ll C^{3-\frac{1}{2\mu}}=C^{-18\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2})}\ll C^{-\epsilon}.

Substituting this bound in (
6.2
), we infer that
\mathbb{P}[\mathscr{B}|B]=1+O\big(C^{-\epsilon}\big).
From this and the fact that
\mathscr{B}\subseteq B
, we deduce that

\mathbb{P}[\mathscr{B}]=\mathbb{P}[B]\big(1+O\big(C^{-\epsilon}\big)\big).

This concludes the proof.
∎

7.
Proof of Lower Bound Lemmas

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
.

We restrict our attention to squarefree integers
n\leq x
which can be written as
n=apb
, with
p
prime, and
a,p,b
satisfying:

(i)

a\leq y^{1/8}
;

(ii)

For all primes
q|b,

q\in(y^{1/4},y^{3/4}]\cup(2y,x]
;

(iii)

\log(y/p)\in\mathscr{L}(a)
.

Remark
.

These three conditions ensure that the representation
n=apb
is unique: Condition (iii) implies the existence of a
d|a
for which
y/p<d
. Hence,
y/p\leq a\leq y^{1/8}
by condition (i). Similarly, from condition (iii) one can conclude that
p\leq 2y/d
for some
d|a
, and so
p\leq 2y
. We end up with
y^{7/8}\leq p\leq 2y.
The uniqueness of the representation follows easily by examining the prime factors of
n
that lie in each of the sets
[2,y^{1/8}]
,
(y^{1/4},y^{3/4}]\cup(2y,x]
, and
(y^{7/8},2y]
.

The condition (iii) ensures that each
apb
is an integer counted by
H_{Q}(x,y,2y)
, because if
\log(y/p)\in\mathscr{L}(a),
then there exists
d|a
such that
y<dp\leq 2y
and hence
apb
has a divisor in the desired interval for any given integer
b\leq x/ap
.

For a given
a,p
, we give a lower bound on the number of
b
such that
apb\leq x
and conditions (i)-(iii) are satisfied. When
y^{1/2}<\frac{x}{ap}\leq 4y
, then count only those
b
’s for which
P^{-}(b)>y^{1/4}
. By
Lemma

2.3
, the number of such
b
is

\gg_{c,\kappa}\frac{x}{ap(\log x/ap)^{1-\delta}(\log y)^{\delta}}\gg\frac{x}{ap(\log x)^{1-\delta}(\log y)^{\delta}}.

When
\frac{x}{ap}>4y,
count only those
b
’s for which
P^{-}(b)>2y
. By
Lemma

2.3
, the number of such
b
is

\gg_{c,\kappa}\frac{x}{ap(\log x/ap)^{1-\delta}(\log y)^{\delta}}\gg\frac{x}{ap(\log x)^{1-\delta}(\log y)^{\delta}}.

As such,

(7.1)

H_{Q}(x,y,2y)\gg_{c,\kappa}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\delta}(\log y)^{\delta}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leq y^{1/8}\\
a\in\mathcal{S}\\
\mu^{2}(a)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{a}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathcal{S}\\
\log(y/p)\in\mathscr{L}(a)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}.

We now deal with the sum over
p
. If we write
\mathscr{L}(a)
as a finite disjoint union of intervals:

\mathscr{L}(a)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}(u_{i},v_{i}],

then within each interval, the condition
\log(y/p)\in(u_{i},v_{i}]
is equivalent to
p\in[ye^{-v_{i}},ye^{-u_{i}}).
Using
Lemma

2.1
, one has

(7.2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathcal{S}\\
\log(y/p)\in\mathscr{L}(a)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}\asymp_{\kappa}\delta\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(v_{i}-u_{i})}{\log y}\geq c\frac{L(a)}{\log y}.

Combining (
7.1
) and (
7.2
) completes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

3.4
.

For each
a\in\mathscr{A}(\bm{b})
, we have
\omega(a)=k:=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{h}
and
\mu^{2}(a)=1
. As such,
\tau(a)=2^{k}
. We are left to show that

\sum_{a\in\mathscr{A}(\bm{b})}\frac{1}{a}\gg\frac{\delta\log 2}{b_{M}!\cdots b_{h}!}.

By definition, each interval
D_{j}
contains
b_{j}
primes, and so it is plain to see that

(7.3)

\sum_{a\in\mathscr{A}(\bm{b})\cap\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{a}=\prod_{j=M}^{h}\frac{1}{b_{j}!}\bigg(\sum_{p_{1}\in D_{j}}\frac{1}{p_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{2}\in D_{j}\\
p_{2}\neq p_{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{2}}\cdots\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{b_{j}}\in D_{j}\\
p_{b_{j}}\neq p_{i}\\
(1\leq i\leq b_{j}-1)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{b_{j}}}\bigg).

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{r}\in D_{j}\\
p_{r}\neq p_{i}\\
(1\leq i\leq r-1)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{r}}

(0,x/(n^{\prime\prime}y^{1/10})]
we include all integers in our
H_{Q}^{*}
count. On each of the dyadic intervals
(x/(2^{r+1}n^{\prime\prime}),(x/(2^{r}n^{\prime\prime})]
, we apply (
8.3
). This leads to the upper bound

\displaystyle H^{*}_{Q}\bigg(\frac{x}{n^{\prime\prime}},\frac{y}{g},\frac{2y}{g}\bigg)\ll

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{n^{\prime\prime}y^{1/10}}+\sum_{r\leq\frac{\log y}{10\log 2}}\frac{x}{2^{r}n^{\prime\prime}(\log(x/2^{r}n^{\prime\prime})^{1-\delta}}\sum_{a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}}\frac{L(a)}{a\log((y/g)^{1/6}/a+P^{+}(a))^{1+\delta}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{n^{\prime\prime}y^{1/10}}+\frac{x}{n^{\prime\prime}(\log x)^{1-\delta}}\sum_{a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}}\frac{L(a)}{a\log((y/g)^{1/6}/a+P^{+}(a))^{1+\delta}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{n^{\prime\prime}y^{1/10}}+\frac{x}{n^{\prime\prime}(\log x)^{1-\delta}}\sum_{a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}}\frac{L(a)}{a\log(y^{3/20}/a+P^{+}(a))^{1+\delta}}.

We always have
1\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}
, and so the above
a
sum is

\gg xL(1)/n^{\prime\prime}=x(\log 2)/n^{\prime\prime}\gg\frac{x}{n^{\prime\prime}\log y}\gg\frac{x}{n^{\prime\prime}y^{1/10}}.

As such, we are simply left with the bound

H^{*}_{Q}\bigg(\frac{x}{n^{\prime\prime}(\log x)^{1-\delta}},\frac{y}{g},\frac{z}{g}\bigg)\ll\frac{x}{n^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}}\frac{L(a)}{a\log(y^{3/20}/a+P^{+}(a))^{1+\delta}}.

Substituting this bound into (
8.2
) gives

\displaystyle H_{Q}(x,y,2y)\ll

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\delta}}\sum_{n^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\cap[1,y^{1/10}]}\frac{1}{n^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g|n^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}}\frac{L(a)}{a\log(y^{3/20}/a+P^{+}(a))^{1+\delta}}+O\left(\frac{x}{y^{1/20}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\delta}}\sum_{a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}}\frac{L(a)}{a\log(y^{3/20}/a+P^{+}(a))^{1+\delta}}\sum_{n^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\cap[1,y^{1/10}]}\frac{\tau(n^{\prime\prime})}{n^{\prime\prime}}+O\left(\frac{x}{y^{1/20}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\delta}}\sum_{a\in\mathscr{P}(2y)\cap\mathcal{S}}\frac{L(a)}{a\log(y^{3/20}/a+P^{+}(a))^{1+\delta}}.

This completes the proof of
Lemma

4.2
.∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.4
.

The proof is largely similar to the proof of Lemma 3.5 in
[
ford2006integers
]
. Let us write
a=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
. Recalling the definition of
D_{j}
and
\lambda_{j}
from Section 3, let us define
j_{i}
to be the unique integer for which
p_{i}\in D_{j_{i}}
. Note that
2\leq p_{i}\leq 2y
for each
i
, and so
0\leq j_{i}\leq v+O(1).
Let
\tilde{p_{1}}\leq\tilde{p_{2}}\leq\cdots\leq\tilde{p_{k}}
denote the rearrangement of the
p_{i}^{\prime}s
into increasing order, so that
\tilde{p_{i}}\in D_{\tilde{j_{i}}}
and
\tilde{j_{1}}\leq\tilde{j_{2}}\leq\cdots\leq\tilde{j_{k}}
. By
Lemma

4.1
, we have

L(a)\leq 2^{k}\min_{0\leq g\leq k}2^{-g}(\log(2\tilde{p_{1}}\cdots\tilde{p_{g}}))\leq 2^{k+K}F(\bm{j}),

with

F(\bm{j})=\min_{0\leq g\leq k}2^{-g}(2^{\tilde{j_{1}}}+\cdots+2^{\tilde{j_{g}}}+1).

Thus,

\displaystyle T_{Q}(k,2y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{k+K}}{k!}\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}=1}^{v+O(1)}F(\bm{j})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1},\dots p_{k}\\
p_{i}\in D_{j_{i}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p_{1}\cdots p_{k}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{k!}(2\delta\log 2)^{k}\sum_{\bm{j}}F(\bm{j}).

It is shown in
[
ford2006integers
]
, p.11 that

\sum_{\bm{j}}F(\bm{j})\ll(v+O(1))^{k}k!U_{k}(v).

Since
k\leq 10v
, then
(v+O(1))^{k}\ll v^{k},
and this completes the proof of
Lemma

4.4
. ∎
