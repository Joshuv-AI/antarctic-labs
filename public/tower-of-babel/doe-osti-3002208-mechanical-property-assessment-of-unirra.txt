 ABSTRACT

Light water reactors in the United States are operated within inherent safety limits designed to account for
anticipated operational occurrences (AOOs). Unlike more severe reactor transients, these events permit
the potential reuse of reactor cladding, highlighting the nuanced role of thresholds in influencing reactor
efficiency compared to design-basis or beyond-design-basis transient conditions. The threshold
designated for AOO peak cladding and fuel temperatures are currently grounded around thermal
hydraulic limits such as surpassing the critical heat flux (CHF), rather than in fuel-cladding material
performance. This CHF criterion therefore represents a conservative approach to AOO transients during
the reactor lifetime. Extending this threshold to higher temperatures than CHF can result in additional
operation efficiency gains. Here, a material performance-based approach is assessed to better understand
the physical material limits of short time scale exposures to elevated temperatures. This approach, termed
time at temperature (t@T), references fuel cladding microstructural and mechanical response to rapid
temperature transients in absence of radiation damage. The results of this work are expected to help
identify margin for the United States reactor fleet, which could be utilized to increase operational
efficiencies through decreased reactor outages.

 BACKGROUND

 The United States’ demand for energy is rapidly increasing and the current reactor fleet has
potential to offer near term solutions by performing extended power uprates (EPUs). Coupling EPUs to
burnup extension enables light water reactors (LWRs) to operate more economically and to generate more
power to meet the needs of the nation. One pathway to achieve power uprates is to increase the
operational window through a reassessment of fuel safety limits around anticipated transients. LWRs
utilize a targeted operational window with well-defined operational efficiencies, yet this window is also
often bound by conservative safety limits that result in sub-optimal operational performance. One way the
existing nuclear reactor fleet can better understand true safety limits while improving economic
efficiencies is by enhancing understanding of fuel materials during anticipated transients to increase
competitiveness of the current fleet.

 Transient temperature excursions in reactors can be categorized by frequency and severity: (1)
severe accidents like Fukushima occurring <10-6 to 10-4 events per reactor-year [1], (2) design basis
accidents, which plants must withstand (10-4 to 10-2 per reactor-year), and (3) anticipated operational
occurrences (AOOs), which are moderate-frequency transients (>10-2 per reactor-year) leading to rapid
temperature spikes. Current regulatory criteria set limits for AOO temperature excursions to prevent
boiling transitions where the CHF is exceeded. Surpassing CHF results in “failed fuel,” requiring
shutdown and removal of fuel rods. The CHF safety criterion represents a cautionary approach that
restricts core design and reduces fuel cycle efficiency.

 AOO instances where CHF could potentially be exceeded include events such as a reactor turbine
trip in boiling water reactors (BWRs) or a locked rotor event in pressurized water reactors (PWRs). Fuel
is considered failed upon exceedance of the CHF, represented as departure from nuclear boiling (DNB) in
PWRs and Dryout (DO) in BWRs. CHF is inherently defined by thermal hydraulics criteria and therefore
does not provide insight on the fuel or fuel cladding integrity. Thus, there remains potential to increase
the operational margin through defining safety limits bound by fuel and fuel cladding performance [2]. A
material-based safety threshold also has promise to provide bounds for reactivity initiated accidents,
which consist of even more rapid temperature transients for short durations of time [3]. Such a strategy
has been developed for cladding reuse post boiling transition by the Atomic Energy Society of Japan,
where upper safety limits are placed on peak cladding temperature and the exposure time at peak
temperature [4].

 5

 Limited events are present in the literature for detailed assessment of fuel cladding performance
after exceeding CHF. BWR Forsmark Unit 2 in Sweden experienced a dryout event due to trips of the
recirculation pumps related to instabilities in the electrical grid. Analysis of the event suggests that select
fuel assemblies were exposed to 450°C for approximately 1 second [5]. The assemblies were removed,
inspected, and eventually reloaded to continue planned operation. The Oskarshamn 2 BWR also had an
instance of local Dryout due to bowing of re-used channels, which changed coolant flow conditions and
local power [6]. Dryout has been experimentally studied at Halden via in pile simulation of a pump trip to
achieve short time scale exposure of fueled cladding at elevated temperatures [7,8]. Some cladding
continued one month in service without failure, while others were characterized in terms of
microstructure and mechanical performance. Clear evidence of cladding collapse onto fuel, grain growth,
and a decrease in the ultimate tensile strength are notable highlights from post irradiation examination, yet
the fuel returned to service for one month did not experience failures.

 Transitioning to a thermomechanical limit based on t@T could yield substantial benefits in the U.S.
with estimates up to ~$1.5 million savings per cycle per plant for BWRs [9]. The potential benefits for
PWRs are more complex, as PWR AOOs are generally more severe than those in BWRs, and the
effective use of additional margin may depend on a variety of factors. Yet, PWRs could see a potential
~5% increase in power output or peaking factors [3], which, when combined with successful burnup
extension and higher enrichment levels, can further enhance performance.

 Here, a methodology and baseline performance threshold were developed for assessment of fuel
cladding performance after exposure to simulated post CHF environments, termed time at temperature
(t@T) throughout this report. Both stress relief annealed and fully recrystallized and annealed cladding
types were exposed to rapid temperature transients, followed by microstructural evaluation and
mechanical testing.

 MATERIALS AND TESTING METHODOLOGY

 Zircaloy-4 (Zry-4) and Zircaloy-2 (Zry-2) were chosen for assessment as representative PWR and
BWR cladding baselines, respectively. Zry-4 was stress relief annealed (SRA) material with an outer
diameter of 9.5 mm and a wall thickness of 0.575 mm. Zry-2 was fully recrystallized and annealed
(RXA), with a liner on the inner diameter of the cladding. The Zry-2 was supplied from a vendor for
assessment of relevant BWR cladding material, and therefore most information related to the Zry-2 used
in this work will remain intentionally normalized and without scale bars.
 Specimens were conditioned within the Severe Accident Test Station (SATS) at Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL). A 12-lamp infrared heating furnace was used to rapidly heat specimens,
anneal at temperature, and then rapidly cool specimens back to room temperature. Two sample conditions
were explored. First, 76 mm or 152 mm cladding segments were suspended into the furnace and exposed
to the temperature transient in a flowing steam environment. After exposure, the specimens were
sectioned to produce segments for further testing and microstructure analysis. The second condition
performed testing on pre-machined double-sided axial tensile test (ATT) specimens. More information on
the ATT specimen geometry can be found elsewhere [10].
 The general furnace profile for t@T conditioning is as follows. First, the cladding segment was
affixed with two thermocouples; one thermocouple controlled the power supply while the other was a
furnace safety limit thermocouple. Thermocouples were attached to either the middle of the 76 mm
cladding tube or to the top head of an ATT specimen. The sample would then be placed inside of a quartz
reaction tube and heated to 300°C for at least five minutes. Steam flow was established at 300°C. The
transient was then performed, followed by rapid cooling with insertion of high flow rate room
temperature compressed air into the reaction tube.

 6

 The cladding heating rate was maintained above 5°C/s but was not constant for all specimens
explored in this work. A guiding hypothesis was that heating rate effects would be generally captured
within the calculated exposure time and temperatures throughout each test, i.e. a slow heating rate would
result in an increased time at elevated temperatures. Two approaches were used for conducting t@T tests.
One approach used the existing proportional-integral-derivative (PID) power supply settings calibrated
for loss of coolant accident tests within SATS. A second approach utilized manual power control with
time-based power output limits. The second approach overrides the internal PID settings, allowing for
higher heating rates. Multiple surrogate specimens were run to determine the desired power output and
hold times to achieve cladding profiles similar to the target test conditions, provided in Table 1. The
average heating rate for all tests conducted was approximately 50°C/s. The cladding t@T properties were
determined with a 20°C threshold from the targeted annealing temperature, such that all actual anneal
times and annealing temperatures could be quantified.
 After cladding conditioning, tensile testing was performed on an Instron 5966 with a 10kN load
cell. Testing was performed at 300°C with a 1x10-3 /s strain rate to calculate yield stress (YS) and
ultimate tensile strength (UTS). Select microscopy was also performed to assess microstructural response
to the transients. Cladding segments were mounted in resin, polished with diamond suspension and
colloidal silica, and chemically etched to remove a damaged surface layer from mechanical polishing.
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was performed with a
Tescan Mira3 and Oxford instruments EDAX detector at a 25 keV accelerating voltage.

 Representative stress strain curves for both Zry-4 and Zry-2 performed at 300 °C are shown in
Figure 2. Datasets were chosen to highlight distinct differences in performance for both material classes,
where the times and temperatures provided on the figures represent the conditioning temperature prior to
tensile testing. SRA Zry-4 showed continual loss of mechanical integrity at intermediate temperatures of
550°C as the conditioning time was increased. Rapid decreases in YS and UTS, coinciding with increases
in ductility, were witnessed within 6s of exposure. In contrast, RXA Zry-2 retained as-fabricated strength
up to 750°C for 10s. While oxidation of Zr alloys is expected at 750°C, the short exposure times suggest
that oxidation has a minor effect on the cladding performance. Instead, variations in the stress-strain
curves for Zry-2 are attributed to general scatter between tests.

Little change in the cladding microstructure was found after 500 and 550°C for nominally 3s of exposure

 ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION

 The Zry-4 yield stress was found to decrease by almost 50% of the as-received value after
material conditioning at the highest temperature and longest times (Figure 3a). This threshold appeared to
be saturated for multiple conditions, and as such likely represents the minimum yield stress achievable for
the fully annealed and recrystallized Zry-4 used in this work. EBSD showed that Zry-4 microstructure
evolution was limited to the initially highly deformed cladding regions which showcased ultrafine grains
and high dislocation densities. The recrystallization and gain growth of these highly deformed regions
was rapid and in agreement that the microstructure changes were related to the mechanical performance.
Therefore, the Johnson-Mehl-Avrami (JMA) relationship for recrystallization [11–13] was fit to the
experimental mechanical data as a proxy for the proposed recrystallization and grain growth processes
that occurred during the temperature transients.

 The Avrami exponent is defined by the 1+D, where D is the dimensionality of the transformation
taking place (i.e. 1D, 2D, or 3D recrystallization behavior). In this work, an exponent value of 4 was
chosen due to the assumption that homogeneous nucleation occurred in all three dimensions. The
activation energy for recrystallization has been reported for Zr alloys and will be used here [14]. k0 was
determined by fitting Equation 2 to the measured yield stress data using a least squares method.
 The resulting recrystallization model is compared against measured yield stress data in Figure
7a. General agreement was found between the model and the change in cladding yield strength after t@T
conditioning. A rapid decrease in YS was witnessed beyond 550°C exposures for all conditions tested.
Due to the inability to experimentally decouple 1. recrystallization of defect-free grains and 2. rapid grain
growth of ultrafine grains, the present recrystallization model must also be encompassing Hall-Petch
effects on the cladding yield stress [15]. It is possible that modifications to metallurgical cumulative
annealing parameter equations could allow for alternative data interpretations [16], and future work will
investigate such relationships.
 The UTS data for Zry-4 was also compiled and compared against the same JMA relationship,
Figure 7b. Similar trends were witnessed for both UTS and YS after exposure to t@T conditioning.
Figure 7c shows total elongation compared against Equation 2, which again was originally fitted to the
YS experimental data. Since TE generally increases with increasing conditioning temperature, the TE
proportional factor was subtracted from 1 to allow for comparison against Equation2. TE trends after t@T
agreed well with Equation 2 for all test data below 650°C, although clear discrepancies can be noticed for
all 750°C t@T conditions. Additional efforts are needed to identify the mechanism for the deviation in
trends at 750°C with TE data, although the authors speculate that a possible cause would be dissolution
and reprecipitation of secondary phases at grain boundaries during the 750°C transient condition. The
Zry-2 data was not fitted to Equation 2, as the initial cladding state was already fully recrystallized and
negligible changes in mechanical properties were measured after t@T exposures.

 Segments exposed for over 600 seconds were separated from the remainder of the data in Figure
7 to highlight possible deviation in trends. While more data is needed for the Zry-4 material tested in this
work, longer anneal times resulted in trends that were not fully captured by the simple recrystallization
model. Multiple mechanisms are proposed to explain this behavior. First, long hold times may need to be
modelled differently due to a continuously decreasing driving force for grain growth after complete
recrystallization has occurred. Other potential impacts such as heating and cooling rates or effects of
steam oxidation during the transients were also not factored into this analysis, which will be investigated
in the future. It should be noted that anneal times around 600s are not realistic time scales for AOO’s such
as a locked rotor or turbine trip. Additionally, it should be noted that cladding YS after t@T conditioning
were used to establish Equation 2. Similar analyses could be performed with UTS data, as was shown
with similar trends in Figure 7b. Thus, UTS also represents a functional material property for assessment
of t@T. The cladding elongation during tensile testing could possibly be used as a metric for t@T
assessment, although clear deviations in trends above 750°C first need to be better understood from a
microstructural level. More advanced characterizations are ongoing to identify differences that would
result in decreased elongation after exposure to 750°C.
 Zry-4 annealing studies from the literature were compiled for comparison, as shown in Figure 8
[17–19]. Here, the proportional factor was calculated based on indentation hardness measurements on the
cladding. For comparison, all data were separated by the estimated annealing time. A clear time
dependence can be visualized from the compiled data, where longer annealing times result in a shift to
lower temperatures for the loss of cladding integrity. Yet, there limited information was available
regarding the initial cladding state, making direct comparisons to the SRA Zry-4 material used in this

work qualitative. The “shoulder” associated with the initial and rapid loss of cladding integrity from
literature sourced Zry-4 data does not fully align with the temperature transition measured in this study.
Hunt et al. assessed heating and cooling rate dependences for a variety of cladding annealing conditions
and incorporated such effects into a first order reaction model [18]. Differences in recrystallization onset
were found with between heating rates of 0.5 and 25°C/s, although the differences were attributed to the
total exposed time at elevated temperature rather than the inherent heating rate. This finding supports a
primary assumption of this work that the heating and cooling rates do not dramatically impact
recrystallization rates when heating rates were high, as long as the average peak temperature range and
total time at temperature were accurately quantified. Likely, such temperature transitions are proportional
to the cladding heat treatments and the as-fabricated microstructure variation. It should be noted that
similar performance trends after short time scale annealing were found in the literature for RXA Zry-2
with 50-80% coldwork [20,21].

 Clear connections between recrystallization, defect annihilation, and grain growth were made to
the mechanical response of cladding after exposure to rapid temperature transients. The Avrami-type
relationship between mechanical properties and cladding recrystallization imply that a fully recrystallized
and annealed cladding type, such as the Zry-2 used in this work, would not show changes in cladding
strength after t@T. Only the Zry-2 specimen exposed to 750°C for 600s showed a loss of ductility and an
increase in the yield strength compared to the baseline cladding. This behavior is likely indicative of
cladding oxidation in the steam environment and the results are in agreement with the BWR cladding
failure criteria established by the Atomic Energy Society of Japan [4].

 From the t@T cladding performance assessed in this work and compiled from the literature, the
following guidance is presented for future methodology development regarding a material property-based
failure threshold at temperatures beyond CHF.

 - Recrystallization is the primary driving force for changes in mechanical properties of the Zry-4
 cladding used in this work. Initial rapid grain growth is concurrent with recrystallization
 phenomena, and grain growth is expected to impact mechanical performance at short time scales
 as the Hall Petch effect is active. Therefore, factors such as initial grain size, degree of cold work,

 16

 and texture of the as-fabricated state must be included in an equation to appropriately capture
 t@T fuel cladding integrity across a wide variety of Zr cladding fabrication processes.

 - The majority of the literature data available is associated with Vicker’s microhardness
 measurements as a proxy for retention of cladding mechanical integrity. Further, indentation
 techniques are often more suitable for irradiated materials testing throughput. Future work should
 incorporate hardness measurements to qualify materials performance alongside tensile test data.
 Fuel cladding are optimized for robust performance in the hoop direction, while this work
 associated axial mechanical properties to recrystallization phenomena. Future work should also
 validate the mechanical performance trends after t@T in the cladding hoop direction.

 - Medium-occurrence events where CHF is briefly exceeded are most often expected to occur
 during the first irradiation cycle. While pellet-cladding interactions are expected to be minimal,
 irradiation damage recovery must also be appropriately modeled from experimental data. Defect
 annihilation is expected to follow similar mathematical relationships, although activation energies
 and rate constants are subject to change the threshold for irradiation recovery compared to
 recrystallization.

 - For both a fuel cladding reuse case and from a cladding handling perspective post-CHF, a
 detailed relationships between transient oxidation kinetics, cladding embrittlement, and
 microstructure evolution should be evaluated. Such data is available for a majority of Zr alloys at
 higher temperatures related to accident scenarios and at longer hold times more closely associated
 with steady state oxidation kinetics [22–25]. A similar approach to defining post quench ductility
 through a criterion of equivalent cladding reacted during high-temperature steam oxidation
 [26,27] can be applied to intermediate temperature transient oxidation during t@T transient
 conditions.

 - Secondary phase particles, engineered for improved waterside corrosion resistance, may be
 altered during t@T, particularly with higher temperature exposures. Detailed characterization of
 SPP size and distribution should be coupled with PWR and BWR corrosion testing to assess the
 long-term impacts of t@T.

 - Fully recrystallized and annealed claddings are not limited by the recrystallization phenomena
 which impacts mechanical performance. Therefore, failure thresholds for RXA cladding types
 should focus on irradiation recovery, medium- and high-temperature oxidation limits, and post-
 CHF corrosion resistance as a means to justify return to service.

 CONCLUSION

 Two differing fuel cladding microstructures were exposed to rapid thermal transients in a steam
environment to provide an initial assessment of the mechanical property response to simulated t@T
transient conditions. One cladding type, Zry-4, was stress relief annealed with an initially bimodal grain
structure. Recrystallization was found to rapidly degrade mechanical performance of this alloy above
550°C. A material integrity-based failure criterion for similarly fabricated alloy classes would thus be
bound by the recrystallization temperature. Standard mechanical properties were found to be acceptable
for development of a correlation factor between cladding mechanical integrity and the degree of cladding
recrystallization. The second cladding type, Zry-2, was fully recrystallized and annealed in the as-
fabricated state. Zry-2 was not susceptible to mechanical property degradation up to 750°C for 10s. Signs
of oxidation embrittlement were apparent after exposure to 750°C for 600s. Cladding in the fully
recrystallized and annealed state are prime candidate materials for considering cladding reuse after

 17

exceedance of CHF, where failure criterion would be bound by transient oxidation rates and temperature
limits for major microstructure evolution.
