Targeting injury-induced transglutaminase-2 activity with a cementoin–SLPI fusion protein: a novel therapeutic strategy for alkali-induced corneal injury
Authors: Juan Pablo Salica, Constanza Potilinski, Gustavo Ortiz, Diego Guerrieri, Paulo C Maffia, Eduardo Chuluyan, Juan E Gallo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChemical injury of the cornea, particularly alkali-induced corneal injuries, triggers a self-amplifying cascade of inflammation, extracellular matrix disruption, and pathological neovascularization that often resists conventional therapy. Transglutaminase-2 (TG2) is a calcium-dependent enzyme whose activation in injured tissues orchestrates crosslinking of matrix proteins and modulation of inflammatory signaling, positioning it as a mechanistic hub in tissue damage.Main textHere we review the molecular and cellular mechanisms underpinning TG2’s role in corneal injury, focusing on its compartment-specific functions in intracellular stress responses, cell–matrix adhesion dynamics, and extracellular matrix stabilization. We also synthesize evidence from preclinical models indicating that injury-induced TG2 activity generates a microenvironment permissive to targeted therapeutic anchoring. Building on this concept, we describe the design and biological rationale of a genetically engineered fusion protein composed of a TG2 substrate domain (cementoin) linked to secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI), enabling covalent anchoring to TG2-rich injured tissue. Our preclinical studies demonstrate that this fusion protein improves local tissue retention, modulates inflammatory signaling, reduces protease activity, and attenuates pathological neovascularization in alkali-induced corneal damage.ConclusionsExploiting injury-generated enzymatic activity, rather than inhibiting isolated downstream pathways, represents a novel therapeutic paradigm. The cementoin–SLPI fusion protein exemplifies a context-dependent strategy to achieve spatially restricted modulation of inflammation and tissue remodeling. These mechanistic insights and translational perspectives lay the groundwork for clinical evaluation of TG2-targeted biologics in severe ocular surface disease.Keywords: Transglutaminase-2, Cementoin–SLPI fusion protein, Tissue anchoring, Alkali-induced corneal injury, Corneal neovascularization, Extracellular matrix remodeling, Targeted biologics

Introduction

Chemical injuries of the cornea, particularly alkali-induced corneal injuries, constitute true ophthalmic emergencies and are frequently associated with severe and often irreversible visual impairment [1]. Population-based studies have shown that severe ocular chemical injuries are rare but require prolonged follow-up and frequently result in multiple surgical interventions and long-term visual morbidity [2]. Severe cases are frequently associated with limbal stem cell deficiency, chronic ocular surface instability, and stromal scarring, often leading to long-term visual impairment. Importantly, corneal neovascularization significantly compromises corneal immune privilege and represents a major risk factor for graft rejection, limiting the success of subsequent corneal transplantation [3].

Alkali agents penetrate rapidly into ocular tissues through saponification of cell membrane lipids, resulting in extensive epithelial, stromal, and limbal damage within minutes of exposure [4, 5]. Beyond the initial chemical insult, alkali injury initiates a complex cascade of inflammatory, angiogenic, and remodeling processes that may persist long after the acute phase, ultimately compromising corneal transparency and visual function [6, 7].

Despite advances in acute management, long-term outcomes following severe alkali injury remain suboptimal in a significant proportion of patients [8]. Current therapeutic strategies are largely centered on topical corticosteroids to suppress inflammation, anti-angiogenic agents to limit corneal neovascularization, and adjunctive measures such as vitamin C supplementation and doxycycline to reduce stromal degradation [9–12]. However, these approaches are supported by heterogeneous levels of evidence and are frequently limited by incomplete efficacy or adverse effects. In particular, prolonged corticosteroid use is associated with delayed epithelial healing, increased susceptibility to infection, cataract formation, and steroid-induced glaucoma, restricting their long-term applicability in many cases [13].

Alkali-induced corneal injury is characterized by sustained activation of innate immune pathways, excessive protease activity, oxidative stress, and profound alterations of the extracellular matrix [7, 14]. Therapeutic interventions targeting isolated downstream mediators, such as individual cytokines or vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), have demonstrated only partial clinical benefit, suggesting that upstream regulatory nodes governing inflammation, tissue remodeling, and angiogenesis may represent more effective therapeutic targets [15, 16]. These limitations highlight a clear therapeutic gap and underscore the need for novel strategies capable of modulating multiple pathogenic pathways simultaneously.

In this context, there is a growing need for therapeutic approaches that can modulate the underlying biological processes driving injury progression rather than targeting isolated downstream pathways. Transglutaminase-2 (TG2) has emerged as a multifunctional regulator involved in inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and angiogenesis, positioning it as a potential upstream therapeutic target in ocular surface disease.

While our recent primary research study (see conclusion section) provides experimental validation of a TG2-targeted, tissue-anchored therapeutic strategy, the present review expands this framework by integrating TG2 biology, secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI) function, and translational considerations. We propose that leveraging injury-induced TG2 activity as a biological anchoring mechanism may enable localized and sustained modulation of inflammation and tissue remodeling, offering a novel approach for the treatment of severe ocular surface disease.

Importantly, given the central role of TG2 in regulating inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and angiogenesis across multiple ocular compartments, this strategy may have broader therapeutic implications beyond alkali-induced corneal injury. Conditions characterized by chronic inflammation and aberrant tissue remodeling, such as dry eye disease, pterygium, and other fibrovascular ocular surface disorders, may also benefit from TG2-targeted, tissue-anchored approaches, supporting the concept of a context-dependent therapeutic platform rather than a single disease-specific intervention.

Review methodology and scope

This narrative review was developed through a structured literature search focused on transglutaminase-2 biology, alkali-induced corneal injury, ocular surface inflammation, corneal neovascularization, secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor, cementoin, and tissue-anchored therapeutic strategies. Searches were conducted in PubMed and Scopus using combinations of relevant keywords.

Articles published in English up to 2026 were considered. Experimental, translational, clinical, and review studies were included when relevant to TG2 biology, ocular surface disease, inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, and SLPI-based strategies. Additional references were identified through manual screening of reference lists of key publications.

Pathophysiology of alkali-induced corneal injury

Alkali-induced corneal injury represents one of the most aggressive forms of ocular surface damage. In contrast to acidic agents, alkalis penetrate deeply and rapidly into the cornea, producing widespread epithelial necrosis, stromal disruption, and endothelial dysfunction [4, 17]. The immediate physicochemical injury is followed by a self-perpetuating cascade of inflammatory, angiogenic, neurogenic, and fibrotic responses that collectively determine long-term clinical outcomes [6, 18].

Early tissue damage and inflammatory activation

The earliest phase of alkali injury is dominated by massive epithelial cell death and keratocyte apoptosis, accompanied by disruption of stromal collagen architecture and loss of corneal transparency [6, 19]. Cellular necrosis leads to the release of danger-associated molecular patterns, reactive oxygen species, and intracellular proteases, which serve as potent activators of innate immune signaling pathways [20].

Resident corneal macrophages become rapidly activated, followed by intense recruitment of neutrophils from the limbal vasculature [21]. This leukocyte influx is mediated by early upregulation of chemokines, adhesion molecules, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, which amplify tissue injury and perpetuate inflammation [22]. Oxidative stress further exacerbates damage by activating redox-sensitive transcription factors, particularly nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), which orchestrates the expression of multiple inflammatory and proteolytic mediators [23, 24].

Angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling

As inflammation progresses, the corneal microenvironment becomes strongly pro-angiogenic. Infiltrating macrophages and activated stromal cells produce VEGF and other angiogenic factors, disrupting the normally avascular state of the cornea [25]. Although anti-VEGF therapies can partially suppress neovascular growth, experimental and clinical studies indicate that VEGF-independent mechanisms also contribute significantly to pathological angiogenesis in alkali injury [16, 26].

Concurrently, matrix metalloproteinases are markedly upregulated, degrading the epithelial basement membrane and stromal extracellular matrix, releasing sequestered growth factors, and facilitating vascular invasion [27]. Excessive protease activity predisposes the cornea to stromal melting and perforation, underscoring the destructive potential of dysregulated remodeling processes [26, 28].

Epithelial repair, limbal dysfunction, neuroinflammation and fibrosis

Successful re-epithelialization is a critical determinant of prognosis following alkali injury [29]. Severe injuries frequently result in limbal stem cell deficiency, leading to conjunctivalization of the corneal surface and chronic inflammation [30, 31]. Corneal sensory nerve damage further contributes to neurogenic inflammation and impaired epithelial healing [32]. Over time, activated keratocytes differentiate into myofibroblasts under profibrotic signaling, resulting in stromal scarring and permanent loss of transparency [33, 34].

Inflammation and angiogenesis as central drivers of corneal damage

Inflammation and angiogenesis constitute tightly interconnected and self-amplifying processes that play a central role in the progression of corneal damage following alkali-induced injury. While the initial chemical insult causes rapid tissue destruction, it is the persistence of inflammatory signaling and pathological neovascularization that largely determines long-term structural and functional outcomes of the cornea [31, 32].

Innate immune activation and chronic inflammation

Alkali injury triggers a robust innate immune response characterized by early activation of resident corneal macrophages and massive recruitment of neutrophils from the limbal vasculature [32]. These cells release high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and proteolytic enzymes that perpetuate tissue damage beyond the acute phase. Neutrophil-derived elastase, matrix metalloproteinases, and reactive oxygen species contribute directly to epithelial breakdown, stromal degradation, and amplification of inflammatory signaling [20, 35].

Importantly, macrophages persist within the corneal stroma long after the initial injury, adopting activated phenotypes that sustain inflammation and promote tissue remodeling [36]. Experimental models have demonstrated that depletion or functional modulation of macrophages significantly attenuates corneal neovascularization and stromal scarring, underscoring their central role in disease progression [37].

At the molecular level, inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinases remain chronically activated following alkali injury, driving prolonged expression of cytokines, adhesion molecules, and angiogenic mediators [38]. This sustained inflammatory milieu creates a permissive environment for pathological angiogenesis and fibrosis.

Breakdown of corneal angiogenic privilege

The healthy cornea is actively maintained in an avascular state through a finely balanced network of anti-angiogenic factors and immune-regulatory mechanisms. Alkali injury disrupts this angiogenic privilege by tipping the balance toward pro-angiogenic signaling [11]. Macrophage-derived vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) plays a dominant role in this process, promoting endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and vessel sprouting from the limbal vasculature [39].

However, angiogenesis in alkali-injured corneas is not solely VEGF-dependent. Studies have shown that inflammatory cytokines, extracellular matrix degradation products, and mechanical alterations of the stromal microenvironment contribute to neovascular growth through VEGF-independent pathways [14]. This complexity likely explains the incomplete and transient efficacy of anti-VEGF therapies in severe chemical injuries.

Pathological consequences of corneal neovascularization

Corneal neovascularization has profound structural and immunological consequences. Newly formed vessels facilitate ongoing leukocyte trafficking into the corneal stroma, reinforcing chronic inflammation and promoting further tissue damage [39, 40]. In addition, vascular invasion disrupts stromal architecture, increases corneal opacity, and compromises visual acuity.

Beyond its direct effects on corneal transparency, neovascularization profoundly alters the immune microenvironment of the cornea. Newly formed vessels facilitate sustained leukocyte trafficking, amplify local immune activation, and disrupt the mechanisms that normally preserve corneal immune privilege [41]. Clinically, these changes are highly relevant, as severe alkali injuries frequently progress to stromal scarring and opacity requiring corneal transplantation. Pre-existing neovascularization is a well-established risk factor for graft rejection and poor surgical outcomes. The use of limbal corneal stem transplantation before doing conventional corneal graft has moderately improved the outcomes. Thus, inflammation-driven angiogenesis represents not merely a secondary phenomenon, but a central pathogenic mechanism linking acute chemical injury to long-term structural failure and surgical complexity.

Implications for therapeutic targeting

The central role of inflammation-driven angiogenesis in alkali-induced corneal injury highlights important limitations of current therapeutic strategies. Topical corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of clinical management due to their potent anti-inflammatory effects, particularly during the acute phase of injury [42]. By suppressing cytokine production, leukocyte recruitment, and vascular permeability, corticosteroids can effectively reduce early tissue damage and pain [13, 43].

However, their broad and non-selective mechanism of action, together with well-documented adverse effects such as delayed epithelial healing, increased susceptibility to infection, steroid-induced ocular hypertension, and cataract formation, significantly restricts their prolonged use in severe or chronic cases [44–46]. These limitations are especially relevant in alkali injuries, where persistent inflammation often necessitates extended treatment beyond the safe therapeutic window of corticosteroids.

Anti-angiogenic therapies, particularly those targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), have provided additional tools to control corneal neovascularization [47]. Nevertheless, these agents primarily act on downstream vascular pathways and fail to adequately address the upstream inflammatory, proteolytic, and matrix-remodeling processes that sustain tissue damage following chemical injury [48]. As a result, their efficacy is often partial and transient, especially in the context of persistent inflammation.

Collectively, these limitations underscore the need for therapeutic approaches capable of modulating central regulatory mechanisms that integrate inflammation, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling, rather than targeting isolated downstream effectors. Such strategies may offer more durable control of disease progression while minimizing the drawbacks associated with long-term corticosteroid dependence.

Transglutaminase-2 in ocular surface physiology and disease

Transglutaminases constitute a family of calcium-dependent enzymes that catalyze post-translational modifications through the formation of covalent ε-(γ-glutamyl)-lysine bonds between proteins, thereby contributing to protein stabilization and extracellular matrix organization [49]. In humans, nine transglutaminase isoforms have been identified, of which eight exhibit enzymatic activity. These enzymes participate in a wide range of physiological processes, including epithelial differentiation, wound healing, and tissue repair, as well as in pathological conditions such as fibrosis, cancer, and inflammatory diseases [50, 51].

Among them, transglutaminase-2 (TG2) is unique due to its remarkable functional versatility. In addition to its canonical Ca2 + -dependent transamidation activity, TG2 can function as a GTP-binding protein, a scaffold for protein–protein interactions, and a regulator of intracellular and extracellular signaling pathways. TG2 is dynamically distributed across cellular compartments, including the cytosol, cell surface, and extracellular matrix, where it exerts context-dependent effects on inflammation, cell adhesion, and tissue remodeling [50, 52]. This multifunctionality distinguishes TG2 from other members of the transglutaminase family and underlies its emerging role as a central integrator of injury-induced responses in complex biological systems [52].

Dysregulated TG2 activity has also been implicated in multiple non-ocular conditions, including cancer progression, acute kidney injury, pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, where it contributes to extracellular matrix stabilization and fibrotic remodeling [53].

Notably, TG2 has been reported to exert context-dependent and sometimes seemingly opposing functions across different biological systems. While numerous studies support its role in promoting fibrosis, inflammation, and extracellular matrix stabilization, others have shown neutral or even protective effects, particularly in certain models of tissue injury. These discrepancies may be explained by differences in TG2 localization, conformational state, redox regulation, and the specific microenvironment in which it is activated, highlighting the importance of contextual factors in determining its biological impact [54].

Importantly, not all studies support a central role for TG2 in tissue repair processes. For instance, Minella et al. demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of TG2 using cysteamine hydrochloride does not significantly affect corneal epithelial or stromal wound healing in both in vitro and in vivo models. These findings suggest that TG2 may not be universally required for wound healing and further support the concept that its role is highly context-dependent [55].

TG2 expression and regulation in ocular tissues

Early histological and biochemical studies demonstrated constitutive TG activity in multiple ocular compartments, including the corneal epithelium, stroma, endothelium, conjunctiva, trabecular meshwork, retina, and lens [56–58]. In the cornea, TG2 expression is tightly regulated under physiological conditions and contributes to epithelial differentiation, barrier integrity, and controlled extracellular matrix organization [59, 60].

Importantly, TG2 expression is markedly upregulated in response to tissue injury, oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines, and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), all of which are hallmarks of alkali-induced corneal damage [61–63]. Experimental corneal injury models have shown rapid induction of TG2 at the epithelial-stromal interface and within activated stromal cells, suggesting a role in early wound responses as well as in later remodeling phases [64].

In addition to corneal findings, emerging clinical evidence supports the involvement of TG2 in other ocular tissues. Elevated levels of TG2 have been detected in the aqueous humor of patients with congenital cataract and axial elongation, suggesting a potential role in ocular growth and structural remodeling processes [65].

A key aspect of TG2 biology is that, TG2 activity is tightly regulated at multiple levels and cannot be inferred solely from its expression. TG2 exists in distinct conformational states: in its GTP-bound “closed” conformation, the enzyme is catalytically inactive, whereas elevated intracellular calcium levels promote an “open” conformation that enables transamidation activity [66, 67]. In addition to calcium-dependent regulation, TG2 is highly sensitive to the redox environment. Oxidation of key cysteine residues (notably Cys370–Cys371) inhibits its enzymatic activity, while reducing conditions -mediated in part by thioredoxin-1- can restore catalytic function [68, 69]. These mechanisms highlight that TG2 activation is a dynamic and context-dependent process, governed by local biochemical conditions rather than expression levels alone. This distinction is particularly relevant in the setting of tissue injury, where changes in calcium flux, oxidative stress, and extracellular microenvironment may collectively determine the spatial and temporal activity of TG2.

TG2 as a mediator of inflammation and immune regulation

Beyond its structural functions, TG2 plays a pivotal role in the regulation of innate and adaptive immune responses. TG2 has been shown to modulate macrophage activation, dendritic cell differentiation, and cytokine production, influencing the balance between pro-inflammatory and resolution pathways [70]. Notably, TG2 can interact with NF-κB signaling, either directly or indirectly, sustaining transcriptional programs that promote chronic inflammation [71]. In line with these findings, recent studies have identified TG2 as a key regulator and biomarker of systemic inflammatory responses, including sepsis, further supporting its role as an active mediator of inflammation rather than a passive structural enzyme [72].

In ocular tissues, sustained TG2 activation has been linked to prolonged inflammatory states in several pathological conditions, including ocular surface disease, uveitis, and glaucoma-associated trabecular meshwork dysfunction [56, 61, 62]. Beyond these associations, TG2 has been shown to modulate key upstream inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB signaling, as well as immune cell activation and cytokine production [52, 53]. In addition, its ability to simultaneously regulate extracellular matrix organization and cell-matrix interactions further supports its role as a multi-level regulator. Together, these findings position TG2 as an upstream integrator of inflammatory signaling rather than a passive downstream effector.

TG2 in extracellular matrix remodeling, adhesion, and angiogenesis

TG2 exerts profound effects on the extracellular matrix (ECM) through its ability to catalyze irreversible ε-(γ-glutamyl)-lysine cross-links between structural and regulatory proteins. This cross-linking activity promotes the stabilization of extracellular matrix components such as collagen and fibronectin, making them more resistant to proteolytic degradation, increasing matrix stiffness, and altering cell–matrix interactions [57, 73]. In addition, TG2 has been shown to enhance the production and maturation of fibrillar fibronectin and to influence extracellular matrix architecture beyond its crosslinking activity. In the cornea, excessive or dysregulated TG2 activity may therefore contribute to stromal fibrosis, loss of transparency, and aberrant wound healing.

In addition to its enzymatic activity, cell-surface TG2 functions as a co-receptor for integrins and fibronectin, promoting cell adhesion, migration, and survival through mechanisms that are partially independent of its transamidation activity [74, 75]. These properties are particularly relevant in the context of corneal neovascularization, where endothelial cell migration and stabilization of newly formed vessels depend on coordinated interactions between inflammatory cells, ECM components, and angiogenic signals [52].

Experimental studies in non-ocular tissues, particularly in pulmonary fibrosis models, have demonstrated that TG2 directly participates in angiogenesis and tissue remodeling by regulating endothelial cell behavior, fibroblast activation, and matrix organization [66]. Emerging ocular data suggest that similar mechanisms operate in the cornea, linking inflammation-driven TG2 activation to pathological vascular ingrowth [76].

TG2 as a pathogenic amplifier in ocular surface disease

These observations support a model in which TG2 may function as a pathogenic amplifier during ocular surface injury. This integrative role is consistent with its multifunctional biochemical properties and compartment-specific distribution. Following alkali exposure, inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress, and mechanical disruption converge to induce both TG2 expression and activation. In turn, TG2 has been shown to reinforce inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-κB activation, promote sustained immune cell activation, stabilize a pro-angiogenic extracellular matrix through crosslinking activity, and facilitate pathological tissue remodeling [77–80].

This feed-forward model may help explain why inflammation and neovascularization often persist despite suppression of individual downstream mediators. Positioned at the intersection of inflammation, extracellular matrix dynamics, and angiogenic signaling, TG2 can be viewed as a multi-compartment molecular integrator of ocular surface injury (Fig. 2). Its central regulatory role suggests that it may represent a promising node for tissue-targeted therapeutic strategies in severe corneal damage. Secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor: beyond antiprotease activity

Secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI) is a low-molecular-weight, non-glycosylated protein initially characterized as a potent inhibitor of serine proteases, particularly neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, and trypsin. SLPI belongs to the family of endogenous protease inhibitors that play a central role in maintaining tissue homeostasis at mucosal and epithelial surfaces exposed to inflammatory and microbial challenges [83, 84].

Beyond its classical antiprotease function, SLPI has emerged as a multifunctional molecule with relevant immunomodulatory, antimicrobial, and tissue-protective properties. These activities position SLPI as an important regulator of inflammation rather than a passive protease scavenger [85, 86].

SLPI as a modulator of inflammation and innate immunity

Experimental evidence has demonstrated that SLPI can attenuate excessive innate immune responses by modulating inflammatory signaling pathways. In monocytes and macrophages, SLPI inhibits lipopolysaccharide-induced cytokine release and interferes with Toll-like receptor–dependent signaling cascades [87]. Mechanistically, SLPI has been shown to translocate intracellularly and inhibit NF-κB–dependent transcription by preventing p65 binding to κB consensus sequences, resulting in reduced expression of pro-inflammatory mediators [88].

Through these mechanisms, SLPI contributes to the resolution of inflammation and limits tissue damage caused by sustained innate immune activation, a feature particularly relevant in severe inflammatory conditions.

SLPI in tissue protection and wound control

In inflamed tissues, neutrophil-derived serine proteases play a critical role in extracellular matrix degradation, epithelial injury, and amplification of inflammatory responses. By inhibiting these proteases, SLPI protects structural components of tissues and preserves epithelial barrier integrity [84]. Loss or insufficiency of antiprotease activity has been associated with uncontrolled tissue damage in several inflammatory diseases [89].

Importantly, SLPI can exert its protective effects locally at sites of inflammation, where protease activity is highest, thereby limiting collateral tissue destruction while allowing physiological defense mechanisms to proceed.

This injury-restricted mode of action suggests that SLPI functions as a conserved regulator of epithelial homeostasis in inflamed barrier tissues. In line with this concept, accumulating clinical and experimental evidence reinforces the view of SLPI as an endogenous “damage-limiting” molecule across injured epithelial organs. In acute kidney injury, early increases in circulating SLPI have been associated with ischemia–reperfusion–related renal damage, and exogenous SLPI administration attenuates tissue injury by modulating innate immune signaling while directly protecting epithelial cells from apoptosis and promoting repair [90, 91]. In inflammatory lung disease, aerosolized recombinant SLPI reduced active neutrophil elastase and downstream inflammatory mediators, providing proof-of-concept for topical delivery in self-amplifying inflammatory environments [92]. SLPI upregulation has also been documented in acute ischemic stroke and in migrating keratinocytes during human skin wound healing, where it participates in barrier restoration and tissue remodeling [93–95]. Collectively, these findings support the view of SLPI as a context-dependent regulator of inflammation and repair, strengthening its biological plausibility as a therapeutic candidate in ocular surface injury.

SLPI and the ocular environment: inducible expression in disease

In contrast to some mucosal tissues, SLPI is not constitutively expressed at high levels in healthy ocular tissues. However, compelling evidence indicates that SLPI is strongly inducible in the eye under pathological conditions, particularly during infection and severe inflammation.

Reviglio and colleagues demonstrated marked upregulation of SLPI in experimental models of Staphylococcus aureus keratitis and endophthalmitis, with detectable expression in corneal, vitreous, and retinal tissues during active infection [96, 97]. In these models, SLPI expression was absent or minimal in normal control eyes, supporting the concept that SLPI acts as an inducible component of the ocular innate immune response rather than a constitutive barrier molecule.

These findings are consistent with broader analyses of protease–protease inhibitor balance in ocular diseases, which highlight SLPI as a relevant player in inflammatory, infectious, and degenerative eye conditions characterized by excessive proteolytic activity [98].

Therapeutic implications of SLPI biology in ocular disease

The inducible nature of SLPI in ocular tissues, combined with its antiprotease, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties, makes it an attractive candidate for therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring endogenous protective mechanisms during severe ocular injury.

However, the therapeutic use of SLPI as a soluble protein is limited by rapid clearance, susceptibility to proteolytic degradation, and insufficient tissue retention in highly dynamic inflammatory environments [99, 100]. These limitations suggest that strategies designed to enhance SLPI localization, stability, and persistence at sites of ocular injury may be required to fully exploit its therapeutic potential.

Cementoin and transglutaminase-dependent tissue anchoring

Cementoin is a short N-terminal peptide sequence originally identified in trappin-2 (pre-elafin) that confers high-affinity substrate recognition by transglutaminases, enabling efficient covalent crosslinking to extracellular matrix proteins. This domain was first described by Nara et al., who demonstrated that cementoin mediates transglutaminase-dependent anchoring of elafin to elastin-rich tissues, thereby prolonging its local antiprotease activity at sites of inflammation [101].

Subsequent studies confirmed that cementoin-containing proteins are preferentially crosslinked by tissue transglutaminases, particularly TG2, under conditions of tissue injury and inflammation [102, 103]. This anchoring mechanism results in stable retention of bioactive molecules within damaged tissues, preventing rapid diffusion or clearance while preserving functional domains exposed to the extracellular milieu.

Importantly, secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI), although structurally related to elafin, lacks the cementoin domain and therefore exhibits limited capacity for transglutaminase-mediated tissue anchoring. While SLPI can serve as a transglutaminase substrate specificity under certain experimental conditions, its crosslinking efficiency is significantly lower and lacks spatial specificity and efficient matrix anchoring capacity [104]. This distinction underscores the biological rationale for engineering fusion proteins that combine the anti-inflammatory properties of SLPI with the anchoring capacity conferred by cementoin.

Within injured tissues, including the ocular surface, transglutaminase-mediated anchoring represents a mechanism by which endogenous protective proteins are retained at sites of damage. Exploiting this mechanism therapeutically offers the possibility of achieving localized and sustained bioactivity without systemic exposure.

Engineering cementoin–SLPI fusion proteins for targeted ocular therapy

Building on the transglutaminase-dependent anchoring properties of cementoin and the anti-inflammatory profile of SLPI, engineering a fusion protein represents a logical therapeutic convergence of both biological mechanisms.

The concept of cementoin–SLPI fusion proteins arises from the need to overcome the limited tissue retention of SLPI while preserving its broad anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory functions. By genetically linking the cementoin domain of elafin to full-length SLPI, it is possible to generate a chimeric molecule capable of transglutaminase-dependent anchoring within inflamed tissues while maintaining SLPI’s protease inhibitory and signaling properties.

This strategy has been experimentally validated in immune and epithelial systems, where cementoin–SLPI fusion proteins demonstrated enhanced binding to monocytes and epithelial cells, prolonged tissue retention, and superior biological activity compared to native SLPI [105]. These effects were shown to depend on transglutaminase activity, confirming that the cementoin domain effectively mediates covalent anchoring under inflammatory conditions.

From a molecular perspective, the fusion protein is generated using recombinant DNA techniques that preserve the functional domains of both components. The cementoin sequence provides a transglutaminase substrate site, while SLPI retains its capacity to inhibit serine proteases, modulate cytokine production, and interfere with NF-κB–dependent inflammatory signaling [106, 107].

Importantly, this engineered approach does not require pharmacological inhibition of TG2. Instead, it leverages injury-induced TG2 activity as a biological ally, enabling selective localization of the therapeutic molecule to diseased tissue. In the context of ocular surface inflammation, such localization is particularly advantageous given the need for sustained activity in a compartment characterized by rapid tear turnover and limited drug residence time. Transglutaminase-2 as a contextual enabler in alkali-induced corneal injury

Alkali-induced corneal injury generates a distinctive microenvironment characterized by epithelial disruption, stromal exposure, persistent inflammation, and active extracellular matrix remodeling. Within this context, transglutaminase-2 (TG2) functions not simply as a downstream marker of tissue damage but as a context-dependent enzymatic facilitator that may enable spatially and temporally restricted therapeutic interventions.

Experimental models have demonstrated that TG2 expression and presumed activity increase significantly in the cornea following chemical injury, particularly within epithelial and anterior stromal compartments undergoing active wound healing and neovascularization [58, 108]. This upregulation is spatially restricted to regions of matrix disruption and inflammatory cell infiltration, where calcium influx and oxidative stress favor TG2 activation.

A key feature of alkali-induced injury is the extracellular redistribution of TG2. Under homeostatic conditions, TG2 is predominantly intracellular; however, tissue damage promotes its translocation to the cell surface and extracellular matrix, where it associates with fibronectin, integrins, and provisional matrix components [81, 109]. This extracellular localization is essential for transglutaminase-mediated crosslinking and renders TG2 accessible for anchoring strategies based on cementoin-containing fusion proteins.

Beyond its structural role, TG2 actively organizes the inflammatory and reparative niche. By stabilizing provisional extracellular matrices and modulating cell–matrix interactions, TG2 influences leukocyte adhesion, macrophage persistence, endothelial migration, and stromal remodeling [66, 70]. These functions position TG2 upstream of multiple inflammatory and angiogenic pathways, acting as a molecular scaffold that integrates cellular signaling with matrix dynamics.

From a therapeutic standpoint, this injury-associated, TG2-enriched microenvironment may create a permissive window for tissue-anchored interventions. Available experimental evidence suggests increased TG2 expression and activity during active inflammation, wound healing, and neovascularization; however, the precise temporal profile of catalytic TG2 activity in alkali-induced corneal injury remains incompletely defined [108, 110]. Therefore, therapies exploiting TG2-dependent anchoring may preferentially act within injured tissue, but their duration of activity and degree of self-limitation will require further experimental validation.

In alkali-induced corneal injury, TG2 may therefore be viewed as a contextual enabler rather than solely as a conventional pharmacological target. Its localized expression, potential activation, and extracellular redistribution may convert injury-associated enzymatic activity into a therapeutic opportunity, providing a biologically rational framework for cementoin-based fusion proteins to achieve selective tissue anchoring and sustained modulation of inflammation at the ocular surface.

Limitations

Despite the strong biological rationale supporting a transglutaminase-dependent, tissue-anchored therapeutic strategy for alkali-induced corneal injury, several limitations and translational challenges warrant careful consideration.

First, much of the current understanding of TG2 biology is derived from non-ocular systems, including fibrosis, cancer, and systemic inflammatory conditions. While these studies provide valuable mechanistic insights, extrapolation to corneal and ocular surface pathology must be made with caution, as tissue-specific differences in microenvironment, cellular composition, and regulatory pathways may significantly influence TG2 function.

Second, an important distinction between TG2 expression and enzymatic activity remains insufficiently characterized in ocular tissues. TG2 function is highly dependent on its conformational state, calcium availability, and redox conditions, which regulate its transamidation activity. Therefore, increased expression does not necessarily imply increased catalytic activity, and the temporal dynamics of TG2 activation following corneal injury remain incompletely understood.

As previously mentioned, not all studies support a central role for TG2 in tissue repair processes. Minella et al. demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of TG2 using cysteamine hydrochloride does not significantly affect corneal epithelial or stromal wound healing in both in vitro and in vivo models. These findings suggest that TG2 may not be universally required for wound healing and highlight the need to better define the specific conditions under which TG2 contributes to pathological versus adaptive responses.[55]

From a translational perspective, the use of protein-based therapeutics entails formulation and delivery challenges, including stability, storage conditions, and preservation of biological activity. However, such constraints are well established in ophthalmology and have been successfully addressed for multiple biologic agents currently used in clinical practice. In the context of ocular surface disease, transglutaminase-dependent anchoring may partially overcome rapid tear clearance and limited tissue residence, potentially reducing the need for high dosing frequency.

Another consideration relates to the heterogeneity of alkali-induced corneal injury. The extent of tissue damage, inflammatory burden, and remodeling response may vary among patients, potentially influencing the spatial distribution and enzymatic activity of TG2 within the injured cornea. Nevertheless, experimental models consistently demonstrate increased TG2 expression and activity in injured corneal tissue, supporting the robustness of this enzymatic context as a therapeutic platform.

Finally, growing evidence indicates that TG2 is involved in chronic ocular surface disorders characterized by sustained inflammation and aberrant extracellular matrix remodeling, such as pterygium and related fibrovascular conditions. Increased TG2 expression has been reported in these settings, where long-term tissue remodeling rather than acute injury predominates. These observations suggest that TG2-dependent anchoring strategies may extend beyond acute chemical injury, potentially offering sustained modulation of inflammation and matrix remodeling in chronic ocular surface disorders [111–113].

Translational considerations and therapeutic development roadmap

The clinical translation of TG2-targeted, tissue-anchored therapeutic strategies requires a stepwise development pathway that balances biological rationale, safety, feasibility, and clinical applicability.

Although alkali-induced corneal injury represents the mechanistic model in which this strategy was developed, its direct evaluation in early clinical trials presents important practical challenges. Chemical injuries are relatively infrequent, heterogeneous, and often occur in emergency settings, where rapid intervention is required and prospective recruitment with adequate informed consent may be difficult. In addition, injury severity, timing of presentation, prior irrigation, and concomitant treatments can vary widely, complicating trial design and endpoint interpretation.

For these reasons, an initial development pathway may be more feasible in chronic inflammatory ocular surface diseases, such as dry eye disease or related non-infectious inflammatory conditions. Preclinical studies in these models would allow evaluation of topical safety, dosing frequency, tissue retention, and anti-inflammatory activity in a controlled setting, while also helping to determine whether TG2-dependent anchoring remains biologically relevant outside acute alkali injury.

In parallel, comparative studies against current first-line therapies, including topical corticosteroids, anti-inflammatory agents, and anti-angiogenic approaches where appropriate, will be essential. These studies should clarify whether TG2-targeted, tissue-anchored therapy provides superior efficacy, improved safety, or additive benefits when combined with existing treatments. This distinction will be critical to define its clinical role, whether as a replacement for current therapies, an adjunctive strategy, or a means to enhance therapeutic durability in inflammatory ocular surface disease.

A first-in-human study could therefore be designed as a topical safety and tolerability trial in patients with mild-to-moderate inflammatory ocular surface disease. Relevant endpoints would include adverse events, ocular surface staining, tear film parameters, symptom scores, inflammatory biomarkers, and structural changes of the corneal and conjunctival surface. If safety and biological activity are demonstrated, subsequent studies could progressively address more severe inflammatory conditions and, ultimately, acute corneal chemical injury.

Parallel mechanistic studies should continue to refine the understanding of TG2 activation in the injured corneal microenvironment, including its temporal dynamics, spatial distribution, and regulation by redox state and calcium availability. These insights will be essential to define the therapeutic window and optimize patient selection for future clinical studies.

This staged translational approach may offer a more feasible and clinically meaningful pathway, enabling early demonstration of safety and biological activity in controlled inflammatory settings before extending the strategy to rare, heterogeneous, and time-sensitive conditions such as alkali-induced corneal injury.

Conclusions

Alkali-induced corneal injury remains a major unmet clinical challenge, frequently resulting in chronic inflammation, pathological neovascularization, and irreversible visual impairment despite aggressive conventional therapy. Current treatment paradigms largely rely on corticosteroids and anti-angiogenic agents that target downstream mediators and are limited by adverse effects and incomplete long-term efficacy.

In this context, a cementoin–SLPI fusion protein anchored through injury-induced TG2 activity represents a mechanistically grounded therapeutic strategy that shifts the paradigm from downstream inhibition to contextual modulation. Importantly, TG2-dependent tissue anchoring introduces a novel therapeutic concept, in which a disease-associated enzymatic microenvironment is harnessed to achieve localized and sustained control of inflammation and tissue remodeling, rather than broadly suppressing isolated signaling pathways.

Experimental evidence has already demonstrated the biological efficacy of transglutaminase-dependent fusion proteins in preclinical models, supporting the feasibility of this approach and its impact on inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and angiogenesis [82, 105, 114]. These findings position TG2 not only as a structural enzyme, but as a biologically relevant therapeutic node capable of integrating multiple pathogenic pathways.

While further experimental and clinical validation is required, converting injury-associated TG2 activation into a therapeutic ally represents a promising step toward more precise and durable interventions. This strategy may open new avenues for the management of severe ocular surface disease and, more broadly, for inflammation-driven pathologies in which TG2 plays a regulatory role. Future studies will determine whether this context-driven strategy can be translated into clinically meaningful outcomes across both acute and chronic ocular surface diseases.
