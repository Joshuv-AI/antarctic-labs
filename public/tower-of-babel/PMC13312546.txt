Magnetic field-assisted immersion freezing preserves starch structure and quality of fresh waxy corn puree
Authors: Jie Fan, Hongsong Ren, Yaxuan Liao, Wanting Yang, Hongting Shi, Fan Gong, Peng Wang, Li Zhang, Shaobo Cheng, Guogang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To improve the frozen quality of fresh waxy corn puree, this study evaluated magnetic field-assisted immersion freezing (MIF) at 5, 10, and 20 mT, compared with slow freezing (AF), air blast freezing (CF), and immersion freezing (IF). The effects on quality attributes and starch multi-scale structure were assessed, and the underlying mechanism was elucidated. Results showed that MIF at 5 mT (MIF-5) improved freezing kinetics, reducing phase transition time by 40.32% compared with IF, while enhancing the retention of total phenolics and flavonoids and minimizing color and texture degradation. Starch structure analysis revealed that MIF-5 increased relative crystallinity and short-range molecular order while better preserving granule integrity. This preservation inhibited water migration and molecular rearrangement upon thawing, maintaining macroscopic quality. In conclusion, MIF-5 is identified as an effective technology for improving frozen quality of waxy corn puree, providing a theoretical basis for applying MIF in high-moisture native starch-based foods.

Introduction

Fresh waxy corn refers to corn ears harvested at the milk-ripe stage and is characterized by its distinctive waxy texture. It can be consumed directly or used as a raw material for further processing. Fresh waxy corn is rich in carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, carotenoids, and other nutrients (Sheng et al., 2018), and provides multiple health benefits, including antioxidative activity and regulation of blood glucose and lipid metabolism, while serving as an important energy source. Consequently, it is highly favored by consumers. However, fresh waxy corn exhibits a high respiration rate and is highly susceptible to postharvest deterioration. Even under refrigerated storage at 4 °C, its shelf life is limited to approximately 7 days, which severely restricts its edible and commercial value (Huang et al., 2021). Additionally, the substantial quantity of inferior ears of fresh waxy corn after harvesting further leads to a waste of raw materials. Therefore, processing fresh waxy corn into semi-finished products represents an effective strategy to extend shelf life and improve raw material utilization. Among these products, fresh waxy corn puree (FCP) has attracted increasing interest from the food industry as a key intermediate, owing to its broad application potential in food fermentation, catering, and the production of biodegradable materials.

The application of freezing technology plays a vital role in food preservation, contributing to greater safety, consistent quality, and a markedly longer shelf life. Its effectiveness stems from the ability to drastically slow down both microbial proliferation and biochemical deterioration (Jia et al., 2024). Nevertheless, during traditional slow freezing, the coarse and uneven ice crystals formed will seriously damage the food tissue structure, leading to quality deterioration such as color changes, nutrient loss, and flavor degradation. In contrast, rapid freezing promotes the generation of ice crystals that are both fine and evenly dispersed, which effectively preserve food microstructure and are therefore critical for maintaining high-quality frozen products. Fan et al. (2024) demonstrated that rapid freezing better preserved the color, hardness, amino acid composition, moisture state, and tissue structure of bamboo shoots compared with slow freezing (AF).

As an advanced rapid-freezing technique, immersion freezing (IF) has been widely applied in the frozen food industry. By using a liquid medium for heat transfer, IF achieves a substantially higher freezing rate than air freezing, thereby better preserving the texture, color, flavor, taste, and appearance of foods (Cao et al., 2024). To further enhance frozen food quality, novel physical field-assisted freezing technologies have been developed, including ultrasonic freezing of potatoes (Cui et al., 2025), electric field-assisted freezing of rice flour (Zhang et al., 2022), and magnetic field-assisted freezing of apples (Leng et al., 2023). Compared with ultrasonic- and electric-field-assisted freezing, magnetic field-assisted freezing avoids cavitation-induced damage and does not rely on high-voltage electricity, thereby preventing ion migration and protein denaturation and enabling more effective nutrient retention (Cui et al., 2025; Ruan et al., 2024; Shams et al., 2024).

Magnetic field-assisted immersion freezing (MIF) integrates IF with a static magnetic field, where the intervention disrupts water's hydrogen-bond network and promotes smaller molecular cluster development, thereby suppressing the uncontrolled growth of large ice crystals to yield a fine, homogeneous ice crystal structure (Jiang et al., 2023). Leng et al. (2023) demonstrated that static magnetic field-assisted freezing at appropriate intensities reduced ice crystal size and shortened phase transition time, thereby improving the texture quality of apples, peaches, cucumbers, and Indian dates, although the optimal magnetic field intensity varied among different food matrices.

The quality of frozen products is primarily determined by two key factors: the freezing technology employed and the intrinsic composition of the product. As the major constituent of FCP, starch plays a critical role in determining texture, structural stability, and overall product quality. Consequently, evaluating the effects of freezing processes on starch structure and functionality is essential for a comprehensive understanding of quality changes in FCP.

According to Li et al. (2023), the texture of frozen zongzi was shown to be enhanced by static magnetic field-assisted freezing, a benefit attributed to the production of finer, homogeneous dispersion, and less aggregated ice crystals. This improvement was attributed to an increased freezing rate, a shortened phase transition period, and the inhibition of bound water conversion, which collectively reduced mechanical damage to gelatinized starch.

Although magnetic field-assisted freezing has been applied to fruits, vegetables, and other foods, most related studies have focused on intact plant tissues, where quality preservation is mainly attributed to the alleviation of ice-crystal-induced damage to cellular structures (Sun et al., 2024). Existing studies on starch-based foods have primarily focused on gelatinized starch systems, in which frozen quality changes are mainly associated with starch retrogradation and water redistribution (Li et al., 2023). In contrast, FCP is a high-moisture native starch-based puree system containing abundant ungelatinized starch granules, and its frozen quality depends more strongly on the preservation of native starch granule integrity and water-holding capacity. Therefore, the mechanism by which MIF affects FCP cannot be directly explained by previous findings from fruits, vegetables, or gelatinized starch systems.

Considering the unique characteristics of FCP as a high-moisture native starch-based puree system, this study subjected FCP to AF, air blast freezing (CF), IF, and MIF at different magnetic field intensities (5, 10, and 20 mT). The objectives were to: (a) Compare and evaluate the efficacy of MIF in maintaining the nutrition and texture quality of FCP, and determine its applicability in FCP frozen processing; (b) investigate the effect of magnetic field intensity on freezing quality; and (c) elucidate the relationship between textural deterioration and starch structural evolution, as well as the underlying mechanisms, through multi-scale structural characterization of starch. The findings of this study provide a theoretical basis for the industrial application of MIF in the freezing processing of FCP, and offer new insights for quality regulation of similar high-moisture native starch-based foods.

Materials and methods

Preparation of fresh corn puree

Fresh waxy corn (variety: Kennuoyu 4008) was harvested from the experimental field of the Crop Research Institute of the Xinjiang Agricultural Reclamation Academy (Xinjiang, China) in August 2025. Its starch content was 52.1% (with an amylose content of 3.5%), and its non-starch material content was 47.9% (comprising 9.9% protein, 29.3% moisture, and 8.7% other components). The samples were transported to the cold storage facility of the College of Food Science, Shihezi University (Shihezi, China) on the day of harvest and temporarily stored at 4 °C prior to processing. The husks and corn silk were manually removed, after which the ears were washed, air-dried, and threshed. Prior to sample preparation, all utensils, containers, homogenizer cups, and quick-freezing bottles were thoroughly cleaned and disinfected. The collected kernels were subsequently homogenized using a high-speed beater at 18,750 rpm for 6.5 min to obtain a uniform fresh waxy corn puree. The resulting raw puree was immediately transferred into 100 mL quick-freezing bottles (40 mm × 40 mm × 100 mm) for subsequent experiments. Fresh, unfrozen puree was used as the control group (CK).

Sample freezing

As shown in Fig. S1, samples in the MIF and IF groups were frozen using a quick-freezing cabinet equipped with stable static magnetic field maintenance and immersion freezing capability (XODCW-2030, Nanjing Xianou Instrument Manufacturing Co., Ltd., China). The MIF system generated a cylindrical and spatially uniform static magnetic field, with an adjustable magnetic field intensity ranging from 1 to 100 mT. During MIF treatment, the quick-freezing bottles containing FCP were placed vertically at the center of the cylindrical uniform magnetic field region formed by the Helmholtz coils, ensuring that the samples were exposed to a stable magnetic field environment. The magnetic field was oriented upward from the bottom of the coils and was parallel to the longitudinal axis of the samples. The equipment was equipped with an integrated magnetic field measurement module, which allowed real-time monitoring of magnetic field intensity during operation. The calibration records showed that the deviation between the preset and measured magnetic field intensities remained within ±0.05 mT throughout the freezing process.

Prior to freezing, anhydrous ethanol was added to the freezing tank as a heat transfer medium, and the equipment was pre-cooled to −20 ± 0.5 °C. During the freezing process, the central temperature of the samples was monitored in real-time using a T-type thermocouple (TT-T-36, Kaipusen (Taizhou) Measurement and Control Technology Co., Ltd., China) with a measurement range of −50 to 230 °C and an accuracy of ±0.2 °C. To measure the central temperature of the sample, the thermocouple was fixed at the center of the quick-freezing bottle mouth and inserted to a depth of 5 cm into the bottle to ensure consistent positioning for each test. Temperature data were collected using a handheld temperature tester (JK808, Changzhou JinAilian Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China) at 1-s intervals until the central temperature of the samples dropped to −18 ± 0.5 °C. Given the randomness of ice crystal nucleation during freezing, each group of samples was independently tested 8 times to ensure the reproducibility and statistical significance of the experimental results. Prior to freezing, all FCP samples were placed in a refrigerator at 4 °C for 6 h to equilibrate their temperature and ensure that the initial sample temperature was consistent across all treatment groups. The FCP samples were subjected to four different freezing treatments, all of which were terminated when the core temperature of the samples reached −18 ± 0.5 °C:(1)AF group: Fresh waxy corn puree was frozen in a conventional refrigerator (SD-739; Haier Smart Home Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China) at −20 ± 0.5 °C.(2)CF group: Samples were frozen in an air-blast freezing chamber (ZT-CTH-80S; Dongguan Zhengtai Testing Instrument Co., Ltd., Dongguan, China) at an air temperature of −20 ± 0.5 °C.(3)IF group: Samples were frozen in the IF tank of the magnetic field-assisted freezing system with the magnetic field intensity set to 0 mT and a freezing temperature of −20 ± 0.5 °C.(4)MIF group: Prior to the formal experiment, preliminary tests at 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 mT showed that 5–20 mT was more favorable for increasing the freezing rate of FCP. Therefore, samples were frozen under static magnetic fields of 5, 10, or 20 mT using the same IF system.

After freezing, all FCP samples were stored at −20 °C for 7 days and subsequently thawed at 4 °C for 12 h prior to further analyses.

Freezing curve

Following the procedure outlined by Fan et al. (2024), freezing curve analysis was performed, incorporating slight modifications. Temperature–time data recorded between 4 °C and − 12 °C were used to construct the freezing curves. The freezing process was characterized using three parameters: freezing time (Tf), phase change time (Tp), and freezing rate (FR). Tf was defined as the time required for the core temperature of the sample to decrease from 4 °C to −10 °C. Tp was defined as the time interval during which the core temperature passed through the maximum ice crystal formation zone, from −1 °C to −5 °C. FR was calculated as the average cooling rate of the sample as the core temperature decreased from 4 °C to −10 °C. The parameters were calculated using the following equations:(1)Tf=T−10°C−T4°Cwhere T-10°C is the time at which the core temperature reached −10 °C, and T4°C is the time at which freezing commenced.(2)TP=T−5°C−T−1°Cwhere T-5°C and T-1°C represent the times at which the core temperature reached −5 °C and − 1 °C, respectively.(3)FR=4°C−−10°C/Tf

Evaluation of FCP quality

TPC, TFC and antioxidation capacity

Following the Folin–phenol colorimetric method (Kumar et al., 2022), total phenolic content (TPC) was assessed. The absorbance reading at 760 nm was used to derive the final values, which are presented in milligrams per gram on a dry weight basis (mg/g).

Total flavonoid content (TFC) was quantified using a previously established method by Liu et al. (2008). In brief, 2 mL of extract was pipetted into a 10 mL tube, to which 0.3 mL of a 5% sodium nitrite (NaNO₂) solution was subsequently added. After standing for 6 min, 0.3 mL of 10% AlCl3, 4 mL of 10% NaOH, and 3.4 mL of distilled water were added sequentially. The mixture was thoroughly mixed, and absorbance was recorded at 510 nm. Results were expressed as mg/g on a dry weight basis.

The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity, and 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical scavenging activity of thawed FCP were determined using commercial FRAP, DPPH, and ABTS assay kits (Suzhou Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China). FRAP results were calculated using FeSO4 standard curves and expressed as μmol FeSO4/g. DPPH and ABTS activities were quantified using Trolox standard curves and expressed as μg Trolox/g. Each measurements was conducted with three replicates.

Color

Color parameters were measured using a portable colorimeter (YS3060; Hangzhou Caipu Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). The color parameters, including lightness (L⁎), red–green value (a⁎), and yellow–blue value (b⁎), were measured. Subsequently, the ΔE (total color difference) was determined using the following formula:(4)ΔE=L∗−L02+a∗−a02+b∗−b02where L0, a0 and b0 represent the corresponding values of the control sample (CK), and L⁎, a⁎, and b⁎ represent those of the tested samples.

Texture characteristics

Texture profile analysis was conducted using a texture analyzer (TA.GEL; Suzhou Baoman Precision Instrument Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) equipped with an A/BE-d35 probe. Instrument settings included a 1.0 mm/s pre-test speed, 0.5 N trigger force, 0.5 mm/s test speed, and 6 mm compression distance. Following a 5-s pause between two compressions at a 1.0 mm/s return speed, the TPA parameters (hardness, cohesiveness, springiness, and adhesiveness) were obtained. Each sample underwent three repeated measurements.

Moisture state and water distribution

The water state and distribution of FCP were analyzed using low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (MacroMR12–60; Suzhou Newmax Analytical Instruments Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). Approximately 2 mL of the FCP was loaded into a glass tube for assessment. The transverse relaxation time (T2) was subsequently determined by applying the Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) sequence. Key acquisition parameters included a 250 kHz sampling frequency, 0.2 ms echo time, 12,000 echoes, a 1500 ms waiting time, and 4 scan repetitions.

MRI measurements were performed using a multi-spin echo (MSE) sequence to acquire proton density images. The repetition time (TR) was set to 1500 ms and the echo time (TE) to 8.44 ms. Samples were scanned along the axial direction in three slices, Each has an interlayer gap of 0.5 mm and a thickness of 1.5 mm. The resulting grayscale images were processed using Niumag NMR imaging software to visualize hydrogen proton density and water distribution. In addition, pseudo-color visualization was applied to the MRI images using the same processing criteria to enhance their visual clarity and presentation quality.

Extraction and quality determination of starch

Extraction of starch

Starch was extracted according to the method described by Hu et al. (2023) with minor modifications. The FCP samples from the CK group and all frozen treatment groups were sequentially filtered through 100-mesh and 200-mesh sieves, and the filtrate was collected. The filtrate was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. Yellow impurities were carefully removed from the precipitate, and the starch fraction was repeatedly washed with deionized water. The cycles of centrifugation and washing were performed iteratively until the yellow coloration in the starch pellet had completely disappeared. The purified starch was then dried in an oven at 40 °C for 48 h, ground into powder, and stored in a desiccator for subsequent analyses.

Determination of short-range order of starch

Starch samples were thoroughly mixed with potassium bromide at a mass ratio of 1:100, finely ground, and pressed into pellets. Optical spectra were collected using a Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Thermo Nicolet IS5; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) over the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 64 scans per sample. Baseline correction and spectral deconvolution were performed using OMNIC 9 software, and applied to analysis the short-range molecular order within the starch samples.

Determination of long-range order of starch

To analyze the long-range crystalline order of starch, X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed on an X-ray diffractometer (Ultima IVX; Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with Cu–Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). The scanning parameters were set at 40 kV and 40 mA, covering a 2θ range from 10° to 50° at a speed of 2°/min. The acquired data were subsequently processed with MDI Jade 6 software (Materials Data Inc., Livermore, CA, USA) to determine the relative crystallinity.

Determination of thermodynamic properties of starch

The thermal properties of starch were determined using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (DSC214; NETZSCH-Gerätebau GmbH, Selb, Germany). Starch samples (3.0 mg) were mixed with 9.0 μL of distilled water in aluminum crucibles, hermetically sealed, and equilibrated at 4 °C for 24 h prior to analysis. Thermal scans were performed from 30 °C to 95 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen flow rate of 20 mL/min, with an empty sealed crucible used as the reference.

Characterization of starch microstructure

The microstructural morphology of starch granules was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (SU8010; Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Starch samples were evenly dispersed onto conductive adhesive tape mounted on aluminum stubs and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold prior to observation. Micrographs were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV, and surface morphology was observed at a magnification of 25,000 × .

The porosity and pore size of the samples were quantified from SEM images using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). After scale calibration, the images were converted to 8-bit grayscale images, thresholded, and binarized to distinguish pores from the solid matrix. The pore regions were then analyzed using the “Analyze Particles” function. Porosity was calculated as follows:(5)Porosity%=AporeAtotal×100where Apore is the total pore area and Atotal is the total image area.

Pore size was expressed as the equivalent circular diameter, calculated as:(6)Pore sizeμm=Aπwhere A is the area of an individual pore.

Data statistics and analysis

The analysis of all experimental data was conducted with SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance and Pearson correlation analysis were performed to evaluate differences among treatments and relationships between variables. Figures were generated using Origin 2024 software (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Influence of different freezing methods on freezing kinetics

Fig. 1A illustrates the freezing curve of FCP under AF conditions, which can be divided into three typical stages: the precooling stage, the phase transition stage, and the tempering stage (Jiang et al., 2023). During the precooling stage, the core temperature of the sample decreases rapidly as sensible heat is removed. When the core temperature reaches the theoretical freezing point, phase transition does not occur immediately; instead, the temperature continues to decrease below the freezing point, resulting in a supercooling phenomenon. Supercooling provides the necessary thermodynamic driving force for water crystallization by overcoming the Gibbs free energy barrier associated with the formation of critical ice nuclei (Jia et al., 2022). Once nucleation occurs at a specific degree of supercooling, latent heat is released, causing the core temperature to rise rapidly to near the freezing point. During the phase transition stage, continuous latent heat release maintains the temperature near the freezing point. In the tempering stage, residual latent heat is removed and the core temperature further decreases to the target final temperature.Fig. 1Freezing curves of different freezing methods. FR is a critical parameter for evaluating freezing quality (Fan et al., 2024). A higher FR significantly shortens Tf, thereby suppressing adverse effects associated with slow freezing, such as oxidative reactions, enzymatic activity, and excessive ice crystal growth. The phase transition stage (−1 °C to −5 °C), also known as the maximum ice crystal formation zone, is particularly critical, as approximately 80% of water undergoes phase transition during this period (Zhang et al., 2022). Shortening the residence time within this temperature range effectively reduces ice crystal size and number while promoting uniform distribution, thereby minimizing freezing-induced tissue damage (Wei et al., 2025).

As summarized in Table 1, CF, IF, and MIF treatments significantly shortened Tf and Tp and increased FR compared with AF (p < 0.05), with overall freezing efficiency following the order MIF > IF > CF > AF. These differences are primarily attributed to variations in heat transfer efficiency. AF relies on natural air convection and exhibits the lowest efficiency, whereas CF enhances heat transfer through forced convection. IF further improves heat transfer due to the use of a low-temperature liquid medium. The observed increase in FR under MIF conditions may be associated with the application of a magnetic field, and could be tentatively explained by two possible mechanisms: (i) disruption and weakening of hydrogen bonding among water clusters, leading to the rearrangement of large clusters into smaller ones (Gou et al., 2024; Ruan et al., 2024), and (ii) reduction of the nucleation free energy barrier, which reduces the duration of supercooling state and accelerates nucleation (Jing et al., 2025). These effects shorten the Tp, increase the FR, and inhibit excessive ice crystal growth, resulting in a finer and more uniform size distribution (Otero et al., 2020).

Interestingly, FR was significantly higher (p < 0.05) at a magnetic field intensity of 5 mT than at 10 or 20 mT, indicating that excessively high magnetic field strength adversely affects freezing efficiency. This may be due to excessive energy input altering the Gibbs free energy of water molecules, thereby reducing the degree of supercooling and lowering FR. In addition, overly strong magnetic fields may excessively constrain molecular motion, interfering with the hydrogen bond network alignment required for effective nucleation. Consistent with this interpretation, Ruan et al. (2025) found that among magnetic field intensities of 2, 4, and 6 mT, 2 mT was most effective in reducing Tf and Tp in bamboo shoots. However, Jiang et al. (2022) reported that magnetic fields of 2 or 5 mT did not significantly affect Tp and FR in broccoli, whereas an intensity of 8 mT markedly shortened Tp and increased FR. The variability among studies highlights the complexity of food systems, which are multiphase and multicomponent in nature, and suggests that the optimal magnetic field intensity for freezing is highly dependent on food composition and structure.

Effects of different freezing methods on TPC, TFC, and antioxidant capacity in FCP

As naturally occurring bioactive compounds in plants, TPC and TFC contribute positively to antioxidant activity, blood lipid regulation, and glycemic control (Zeng et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 2A, all freezing treatments resulted in a significant reduction (p < 0.05) in TPC and TFC contents compared with the CK group. Among the frozen samples, the MIF treatments exhibited the highest retention of TPC and TFC, indicating that magnetic field application effectively inhibited their degradation. This protective effect may be attributed to the higher FR, which suppresses excessive ice crystal growth and facilitates the formation of fine, uniformly distributed ice crystals. This process reduces the extrusion of solutes by ice crystals, thereby mitigating the freezing concentration effect of phenolic compounds during freezing (Min et al., 2019). Lower local concentrations can suppress enzymatic reactions and oxidative degradation. FRAP, ABTS, and DPPH assays are widely used to assess antioxidant capacity by reflecting the capability of bioactive compounds to scavenge free radicals or reduce oxidants (Golmakani et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 2B, the FRAP, DPPH, and ABTS values of the AF and CF groups were significantly lower (p < 0.05) than those of the other treatments, indicating that slow freezing had a more pronounced negative impact on the antioxidant capacity of FCP. Notably, no significant differences in DPPH and ABTS values were observed between the MIF-5 group and the CK group, suggesting that magnetic field-assisted freezing effectively preserved antioxidant activity. However, as magnetic field intensity increased, TPC, TFC, FRAP, DPPH, and ABTS values exhibited a decreasing trend, indicating that excessively high magnetic field intensities did not confer additional benefits for the retention of phenolic compounds or the maintenance of antioxidant capacity.

Texture is another key indicator of food quality (Cui et al., 2025). Although freezing effectively prolongs the shelf life of FCP, ice crystal formation during freezing can disrupt its original organization structure and compromise quality stability during subsequent processing, thereby affecting consumer acceptance. Texture profile analysis showed that, compared with the CK group, frozen samples exhibited increased hardness and decreased cohesiveness, springiness, and adhesiveness (Table 2), indicating overall texture deterioration. This may be explained by ice-crystal-induced damage to starch crystallization regions. Upon thawing, the amylopectin double-helix structure of crystallization regions rearranges into a denser and more rigid starch network, resulting in increased hardness and altered textural attributes (Yang et al., 2022). In addition, water loss during freezing further contributes to increased hardness in starch-based foods (Li et al., 2025). Compared with the AF group, all MIF treatments significantly improved textural properties. Notably, the MIF-5 group exhibited texture parameters most comparable to those of the CK group, with no significant differences (p > 0.05) in cohesiveness, springiness, or adhesiveness. This suggests that magnetic field-assisted freezing effectively preserves texture quality, with 5 mT representing the optimal magnetic field intensity. The enhanced texture retention is attributed to the higher FR, which promotes the generation of finer and more uniformly distributed ice crystals, thereby reducing structural damage and inhibiting amylopectin rearrangement.

Water state and water distribution

LF-NMR is widely used to characterize bound water (T21, 0–10 ms), immobilized water (T22, 10–100 ms), and free water (T23, 100–1000 ms) in foods, providing direct insight into internal moisture states (Cui et al., 2025). The moisture states and water distribution of FCP subjected to different freezing methods are shown in Fig. 2C–D. Compared with the CK group, the T2 relaxation curves of all frozen samples shifted toward longer relaxation times, indicating that ice-crystal-induced structural damage caused the release of water originally bound to macromolecules, resulting in its transition to less constrained states (Li et al., 2015). After freezing, the T21 fraction decreased significantly (p < 0.05), while T22 and T23 fractions increased, confirming pronounced water migration. This behavior can be attributed to mechanical stress exerted by ice crystal growth, which damages both the amorphous and crystalline regions of starch. Such damage disrupts hydrogen bonds between hydroxyl groups of amylose chains and water molecules (Lu et al., 2025), thereby reducing starch water-holding capacity. In addition, freezing-induced densification of the starch network reduces the contact area between amylose and water molecules, further weakening hydrogen bonding. Collectively, these changes promote the conversion of bound water into immobilized and free water.

Compared with AF and CF treatments, IF and MIF samples exhibited significantly higher T21 contents and lower T23 contents (p < 0.05), indicating improved water retention. This suggests that faster FR and the resulting formation of smaller, more uniform ice crystals effectively mitigate damage to starch structure and hydrogen bonding, thereby enhancing water-holding capacity. Cui et al. (2025) reported similar findings, also showing that increased FR reduced tissue damage and inhibited water migration in potatoes. Notably, no significant differences in water state were observed between the MIF-5 and CK groups, indicating that an appropriate magnetic field intensity can effectively preserve the native water state of FCP. However, as magnetic field intensity increased, water migration gradually intensified, which may be attributed to the reduction in FR at higher field strengths, leading to aggravated structural damage.

Pseudo-color MRI images visualization further confirmed these observations (Fig. 2E). Regions with higher water content exhibited stronger hydrogen proton signals and appeared red, whereas regions with lower water content appeared blue (Jiang et al., 2025; You et al., 2024). The CK group showed a uniformly blue distribution, indicating homogeneous water distribution and strong water–matrix interactions. In contrast, frozen samples displayed localized red or yellow regions, reflecting increased free water accumulation in structurally damaged areas following thawing (Cui et al., 2025). Compared with AF and CF, the IF and MIF treatments exhibited fewer high-signal regions, which implies diminished free water content and improved water distribution homogeneity. This is consistent with previous reports by Zhou et al. (2022). Among all treatments, the MIF-5 group showed MRI images most similar to the CK group, whereas higher magnetic field intensities resulted in increased high-signal regions, further confirming that an appropriate magnetic field intensity effectively inhibits water migration by enhancing FR.

Changes in starch structure

Analysis of long-range and short-range ordered structures

Relative crystallinity (RC) reflects the long-range molecular order of starch chains, and different crystalline forms exhibit distinct characteristic diffraction peaks in XRD patterns (Yao et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 3B, characteristic diffraction peaks for starch from all frozen treatments and the CK group appeared at approximately 15°, 17°, 18°, 20°, and 23°, with no new peaks observed. This indicates that starch in all samples predominantly retained an A-type crystalline structure and that freezing did not alter the crystalline polymorph, consistent with the findings of Tao et al. (2020). However, notable differences in diffraction peak intensity were observed among treatments, indicating that freezing methods markedly influenced starch RC. The AF and CF groups exhibited significantly lower peak intensities and smoother peak profiles than other groups, suggesting that lower FR caused substantial damage to the starch crystalline structure, resulting in reduced crystalline regions and expanded amorphous domains. This effect is primarily attributed to the formation of large and unevenly distributed ice crystals under slow freezing conditions, which exert strong mechanical stress on starch granules and disrupt hydrogen bonds stabilizing the amylopectin double-helix structure, thereby severely damaging internal crystalline regions and sharply reducing RC (Almeida et al., 2025) (Table 3). Consistent with its lowest FR, the AF group exhibited the most pronounced crystalline damage. In contrast, among all frozen treatments, the MIF-5 group showed diffraction characteristics most similar to the CK group and the highest RC, indicating that the application of a magnetic field effectively alleviated ice-crystal-induced damage and helped preserve starch crystalline integrity. FTIR was used to characterize the short-range ordered structure of starch (Lu et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 3C, no new absorption peaks appeared and no characteristic peaks disappeared in frozen starch samples, indicating that freezing did not induce the formation or cleavage of covalent bonds. All samples exhibited a broad –OH stretching vibration band between 3000 and 3700 cm−1. Compared with the CK group, frozen starch samples showed a shift of the –OH stretching band toward lower wavenumbers, indicating enhanced hydroxyl-related hydrogen-bond interactions or changes in the hydrogen-bonding environment within the starch matrix. This may be related to ice-crystal-induced mechanical damage during freezing, which disrupted the original starch network structure and exposed more hydroxyl groups. During the subsequent thawing and drying processes, starch molecular chains may rearrange and tend to aggregate, thereby promoting the reformation and strengthening of intermolecular hydrogen bonds between hydroxyl groups (Liu et al., 2026). This shift reflects disruption and reorganization of the original hydrogen-bond network caused by ice crystal formation during freezing.

In the FTIR spectra, absorbance bands near 1047 cm−1 and 1022 cm−1 correspond to ordered and amorphous starch structures, respectively, and their ratio (R1047/1022) represents the degree of short-range order (DO). The band at 995 cm−1 is associated with amylopectin double-helical structures, and the ratio R995/1022 reflects the degree of double helicity (DD). As shown in Table 3, all frozen samples exhibited decreased DO and DD compared with the CK group, indicating a reduction in the short-range ordered structure and partial disintegration of the double-helical structure in the frozen starch. This reduction is attributed to the growth of ice crystals during the freezing process disrupting the short-range ordered structure of starch, specifically by breaking the intermolecular hydrogen bonds within the double-helical structure of amylopectin, thereby leading to the reduction in both DO and DD (Long et al., 2024). The DO and DD values of the MIF-5 group did not differ significantly from those of the CK group (p > 0.05) and were significantly higher (p < 0.05) than those of the AF, CF, and IF groups, suggesting that magnetic field-assisted freezing effectively suppressed excessive ice crystal growth thereby reducing the damage to starch structure and preserving short-range molecular order (Gong et al., 2020). However, both DO and DD gradually decreased with increasing magnetic field intensity, and no significant differences were observed between the MIF-20 and IF groups (p > 0.05), likely due to reduced FR at higher magnetic field strengths.

Thermodynamic characteristics analysis

Thermodynamic properties of starch are characterized by the onset temperature (T₁), peak temperature (T2), conclusion temperature (T3), and gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH). As shown in Table 3, T1 decreased significantly (p < 0.05) and T2 decreased to varying extents in all frozen groups, indicating that freezing disrupted the starch crystalline structure and reduced molecular orderliness (Zhao et al., 2018). The treatments exhibited no significant differences in T3. ΔH represents the energy required for starch gelatinization and is closely associated with crystalline integrity, short-range molecular order, and amylopectin double-helical content (Zhang et al., 2024). Compared with the CK group, all frozen samples exhibited significantly lower ΔH values (p < 0.05), indicating reduced gelatinization energy due to ice-crystal-induced destruction of both crystalline and amorphous regions, which weakened starch structural integrity. As ΔH is positively correlated with RC (Luo et al., 2017), the lower ΔH values observed in frozen samples are consistent with the reduced RC discussed above. Notably, the ΔH value of the MIF-5 group was significantly higher (p < 0.05) than those of other frozen treatments, which indicates that the thermal stability was enhanced. This improvement is attributed to the higher FR achieved under the 5 mT magnetic field, which better preserved the starch crystalline structure and minimized changes in RC.

Microstructure

SEM observations provide direct visual evidence of ice-crystal-induced damage to starch microstructure during freezing (Liu et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 3D, starch granules in the CK group exhibited intact morphology with smooth surfaces and well-defined edges. In contrast, starch granules from frozen samples displayed varying degrees of mechanical damage. The AF and CF groups showed the most severe damage, characterized by surface collapse, cracks, and numerous pores, along with reduced surface smoothness, consistent with previous observations by Zhao et al. (2018). This damage can be attributed to prolonged phase transition times under slow freezing conditions, which allow water molecules sufficient time to reorganize and form large ice crystals that exert stronger mechanical stress on starch granules, thereby increasing pore number and size (Yang et al., 2021).

The size and distribution of ice crystals formed during food freezing can be indirectly characterized to some extent by analyzing the pore size and porosity on the surface of starch granules (Zhu et al., 2019). Generally, the larger the ice crystals formed during freezing, or the more unevenly they are distributed, the more severe the mechanical damage to starch granules, as reflected by an increased number of surface pores and enlarged pore size. As shown in Fig. 3E, quantitative analysis of the SEM images showed that the porosity and pore size of the MIF-5 group were significantly lower than those of the AF, CF, and IF groups (P < 0.05), indicating that an appropriate magnetic field intensity effectively reduced pore formation and decreased pore size during freezing, thereby maintaining a relatively smooth and intact surface structure of starch granules. This may be attributed to the ability of MIF treatment to increase the freezing rate and inhibit water migration, promoting the formation of smaller and more uniformly distributed ice crystals and consequently reducing ice-crystal-induced mechanical damage to starch granules (Tanveer et al., 2026). Furthermore, as the magnetic field strength further increased, both the porosity and pore size on the surface of starch granules showed an upward trend, suggesting that excessively high magnetic field strength may weaken the inhibitory effect of MIF on ice crystal growth, thereby leading to more pronounced structural damage to starch granules. These results indicate that magnetic field strength may be an important factor influencing ice crystal morphology and the extent of ice crystal-induced damage to starch granules.

As a result, relative crystallinity, short-range order, double-helical order, and gelatinization enthalpy could be better retained. The preservation of these ordered structures contributes to maintaining the stability of the starch network and slowing the rearrangement of amylopectin double-helical structures and network densification during thawing, thereby suppressing the increase in FCP hardness and alleviating the decreases in cohesiveness, springiness, and adhesiveness. In addition, a relatively intact starch structure and hydrogen-bonding network facilitate the maintenance of interactions between starch and water molecules, reducing the conversion of bound water into immobilized and free water and thus inhibiting water migration during thawing. Therefore, the reduction in ice crystal size not only mitigates multi-scale structural damage to starch but also helps maintain starch molecular order and water stability, ultimately improving the textural quality of FCP. Overall, by increasing FR and regulating ice crystal formation, MIF effectively mitigates freezing-induced quality deterioration in starch-based frozen foods, highlighting its potential as an advanced freezing technology for maintaining product quality.

Although the present study mainly attributed the texture changes of FCP to freezing-induced alterations in starch structure, FCP is a complex multi-component system, and the possible contributions of other constituents should also be considered. In addition to starch, proteins, lipids, and non-starch polysaccharides may influence texture and structural stability by affecting water retention, matrix continuity, and molecular interactions during freezing and thawing. Therefore, starch structural damage is considered a major factor contributing to texture deterioration in this study, but it may not be the only mechanism involved. Further investigations on the roles of non-starch components and their interactions with starch and water are needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of freezing-induced quality changes in FCP.

Conclusion

This study focused on the applicability of MIF for improving the freezing quality of FCP. The results demonstrated that, compared with AF, CF, and IF, MIF significantly increased the freezing rate of FCP and better preserved its nutritional quality, color, and texture. MIF also exhibited advantages in inhibiting thaw-induced water migration and effectively maintaining uniform water distribution. Among all treatments, the protective effect of MIF on FCP quality was most pronounced at a magnetic field intensity of 5 mT, and this effect gradually diminished as the intensity increased. Starch structural analysis revealed that MIF-5 treatment better maintained the integrity of starch granules, crystalline structure, and molecular order. This effective preservation of starch structure not only inhibited the rearrangement of the starch network upon thawing but also reduced the disruption of hydrogen bonds between amylose and water molecules, thereby better preserving the textural properties and water distribution of FCP. These results indicate that reducing starch structural damage through increasing FR is a key mechanism for maintaining the textural stability of FCP during freezing. This study systematically evaluated the effectiveness of MIF in preserving FCP quality under laboratory conditions and demonstrated its potential to mitigate quality deterioration associated with conventional slow freezing. Although this study demonstrated the potential of MIF in preserving FCP quality under laboratory conditions, only one thawing condition was used, and microbial safety and economic feasibility were not evaluated. Therefore, future studies should further investigate improved thawing strategies, microbiological safety and shelf-life stability after thawing, as well as process optimization, equipment design, energy consumption, and operating costs for large-scale application.
