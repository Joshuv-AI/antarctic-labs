Melatonin rhythm-mimicking smart hydrogel for circadian clock regulation and promotion of intervertebral disc regeneration
Authors: Jie Hu, Fang Zhou, Yisi Liu, Yu Qian, Yan Su, Fengyu Ma, Qiang Wei, Haobo Guo, Hui He, Qianglong Chen, Shibin Sun, Hao Jiang, Bin Meng, Houfeng Zheng, Xiaodong Wang, Ting Liang, Bin Li, Fengxuan Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a leading cause of low back pain, with growing evidence suggesting a connection between its pathogenesis and circadian rhythm disruption. This study investigated the link between circadian regulation and IVDD by integrating epidemiological evidence from the UK Biobank cohort (n = 287,150), Mendelian randomization analysis, and multi-species validation using Bmal1 knockout mice and human and rat disc samples. Core circadian clock proteins were significantly downregulated in degenerated intervertebral disc tissues across species. To counteract this, a composite hydrogel was developed for the cyclic delivery of melatonin, which effectively upregulated BMAL1 and CLOCK protein expression, restored circadian rhythm, and enhanced extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis. When implanted into degenerated rat discs, the hydrogel alleviated IVDD by reestablishing circadian homeostasis. These findings highlight cyclic melatonin delivery as a promising therapeutic approach for IVDD.Keywords: Intervertebral disc degeneration, Circadian clock, Melatonin, Repair and regeneration

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a leading cause of low back pain and represents a major global public health issue affecting more than 600 million people [[1], [2], [3]]. Although discectomy effectively alleviates symptoms from nerve root compression, it fails to restore the nucleus pulposus (NP) lost due to herniation and often leaves a significant defect, which may predispose to further herniation [4,5]. Of the estimated 480,000 lumbar discectomy procedures performed annually [6], reoperation rates have been reported at approximately 10.3%, with the cumulative risk reaching 14.4% within five years [7,8]. Even in the absence of clinical reherniation, discectomized intervertebral disc (IVD) exhibit a high prevalence of advanced degeneration, severely impacting long-term clinical outcomes [9].

The IVD is influenced by the circadian clock. Its height and composition change in response to daily loading cycles. During the day, higher compressive forces on the disc cause fluid to flow out, whereas at night, reduced loading and osmotic pressure facilitate fluid reabsorption into the tissue [10,11]. Epidemiological studies indicate a connection between circadian rhythm disruptions and an increased risk of IVDD [12]. Recent research has identified an intrinsic molecular circadian clock within IVD tissues, regulated by age and inflammatory cytokines. Notably, targeted deletion of BMAL1 in mice leads to severe IVDD, highlighting the critical role of BMAL1 expression in maintaining the disc's internal environment [13,14]. BMAL1 (brain and muscle ARNT-like 1) and CLOCK, basic helix-loop-helix-PAS (bHLH-PAS) transcription factors, are core components of the molecular circadian oscillator [15]. BMAL1/CLOCK heterodimers bind to E-box elements and activate the transcription of PER and CRY. In turn, PER/CRY complexes suppress BMAL1/CLOCK activity. This feedback loop generates a 24-h rhythm and regulates downstream clock-controlled genes [16,17]. As with many regulatory processes, circadian clock function declines with age, losing its precise temporal control. In both animal models and humans, the amplitude and phase of circadian rhythms diminish with age [18,19]. Such a decline is considered a risk factor for numerous pathologies, ranging from metabolic syndrome and malignancies to musculoskeletal conditions [20]. Consequently, elucidating the role of circadian disruption in disease progression and developing strategies to modulate the circadian clock may provide a valuable direction for future studies.

Melatonin (MT), first isolated in 1958 and structurally characterized in 1959, is an amphiphilic indoleamine derived from tryptophan and is well known for its potent free radical scavenging and antioxidant properties. This antioxidant activity is widely considered one of the most ancient and likely primary functions of MT [21,22]. In mammals, pineal MT synthesis is regulated by the master clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus (SCN). The SCN receives photoperiodic information from intraretinal photoreceptor ganglion cells and synchronizes MT production with the light/dark cycle. This ensures MT is produced exclusively during the night for cyclic secretion [23]. The duration of the nocturnal period corresponds with the length of the nocturnal peak in MT secretion, making the circadian rhythm of MT essential for maintaining normal physiological functions [24,25]. In addition to its antioxidant activity, MT also acts as a chronobiological regulator. It does so by binding to MT receptors, which modulate the expression of core clock proteins like BMAL1 and CLOCK, influencing the transcription of downstream genes. As a result, MT is recognized not only as a key circadian regulator but also as a therapeutic agent commonly used in clinical settings to restore disrupted circadian rhythms.

However, current pharmacological strategies for regulating the cellular circadian clock face several challenges, including the short half-life of drugs and the complexity of the in vivo environment [17,26]. To address these limitations, the development of tailored drug delivery systems for circadian-modulating agents offers significant advantages. Such systems can improve therapeutic efficacy and enhance local delivery. They may also enable more precise regulation of cellular circadian rhythms and help restore physiological function. Drug delivery has employed a variety of carriers, including hydrogels, liposomes, dendrimers, and inorganic nanoparticles. Among them, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) are particularly attractive because of their biodegradability, high physicochemical stability, adjustable pore structure, large surface area, and facile surface modification [27,28]. Significant efforts are underway to develop multifunctional MSNs for targeted drug delivery. To achieve controlled release, various stimuli-responsive strategies have been explored, including light, temperature, enzymes, pH, and magnetic fields [29,30]. Among these, light activation is particularly promising due to its remote controllability and minimally invasive nature [31]. Given the potential tissue damage caused by ultraviolet and X-ray radiation, near-infrared (NIR) light has gained increasing attention for its excellent biocompatibility and superior tissue penetration capabilities, offering new opportunities to enhance drug delivery efficiency [31,32]. However, the low energy of NIR light limits its ability to directly induce photoresponsive reactions, thus restricting its broader application. Lanthanide ions (e.g., Tm3+, Er3+, and Ho3+) possess a unique ladder-like energy level structure, allowing lanthanide-based UCNPs to convert absorbed NIR light into higher-energy UV emission. This property holds significant potential for photocontrolled drug delivery applications [33].

Based on this background, the present study first investigated the relationship between circadian disruption and IVDD by integrating multidimensional evidence from a large-scale human cohort, Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis, and Bmal1 knockout mouse models. These epidemiological, genetic, and pathological findings collectively suggested that circadian clock dysregulation, particularly impaired Bmal1-related signaling, is closely associated with disc degeneration and may represent a potential therapeutic target. Since melatonin (MT) is an endogenous circadian regulator capable of modulating core clock-related signaling, we hypothesized that rhythm-mimicking MT delivery could help restore local circadian clock homeostasis in degenerated intervertebral discs. To test this hypothesis, we developed a near-infrared (NIR)-triggered cyclic MT release system for IVDD repair. Specifically, azobenzene (AZO)-modified upconverting mesoporous silica nanoparticles (UPMSN-AZO) were used as drug carriers and capped with β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) to enable light-responsive MT release. These nanoparticles were subsequently incorporated into a methacryloyl-modified decellularized annulus fibrosus matrix (DAFMMA) to construct an injectable multifunctional composite hydrogel. After confirming its optical responsiveness and cyclic release behavior, we systematically evaluated its ability to regulate circadian clock-related signaling, rescue extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolism, and modulate autophagy-related changes in vitro. Finally, we assessed its therapeutic efficacy in promoting disc repair in a rat IVDD model (Scheme 1). Compared with previous melatonin- or hydrogel-based strategies, this study establishes a mechanism-guided, time-regulated therapeutic approach that links circadian disruption with IVDD pathogenesis and applies cyclic MT delivery for local disc regeneration.

Materials and methods

Study population

Data for this investigation were sourced from the UK Biobank (UKB), an extensive prospective repository comprising approximately 500,000 middle-aged individuals recruited across 22 sites in the UK between 2006 and 2010 (Application No. 41376) [34].

To explore the link between prolonged night shift employment and LDH, we analyzed a subset of the UK Biobank comprising 251,407 individuals. The selection process originated from 287,150 workers (either employed or self-employed). We sequentially excluded 954 participants with prevalent LDH at baseline to focus on incident cases, and a further 35,743 individuals due to incomplete data regarding primary covariates, such as ethnicity, BMI, and lifestyle factors.

Outcome assessment

The outcome, incident LDH (field ID: 131922), was extracted from the “first occurrence of health outcomes” using the corresponding ICD-10 code(s) for lumbar intervertebral disc disorders. The diagnosis of LDH was identified through linkage to primary care data, death register records, and hospital admission data. In this study, incident LDH refers to newly diagnosed cases.

Statistical methods

To evaluate the link between long-term night shift work and LDH risk, we employed multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression. Two adjustment levels were implemented: Model 1 accounted for age (continuous) and sex (categorical), while Model 2 (fully adjusted) further incorporated ethnicity (European vs. non-European), educational background, BMI (continuous), and lifestyle factors, including smoking and alcohol habits. Gender-stratified analyses were also performed to explore potential sex-specific effects. To ensure the reliability of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses by omitting incident cases diagnosed within the initial year of follow-up.

Human genetic evidence for the association between Bmal1 gene expression and LDH

To replicate our experimental finding, we conducted a series of MR analyses to explore the association between Bmal1 gene expression and LDH. Specifically, to select the instrumental variables for the expression of the Bmal1 gene, we only included genetic variants associated with the expression of Bmal1 gene (P-value<5 × 10−8), based on the expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data from whole blood in the eQTLGen consortium [35]. Linkage clumping was conducted using PLINK software with the parameters --clump-r2 0.001 and --clump-kb 250 to ensure the independence of the selected instrumental variables. We then extracted the genetic association estimates of these instrumental variables with LDH from the previously published GWAS summary statistics of LDH, which encompassed 829,699 individuals (80,724 cases and 748,975 controls) [36]. Inverse-variance weighted fixed effect estimate was used as the main analysis to assess the association between genetically predicted Bmal1 gene expression and LDH [37], and weighted median method [38], weighted mode method [39], and MR-Egger regression [40] were utilized as sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of MR findings.

Micro-CT and histological analyses of mouse IVD

Six-month-old WT and Bmal1 KO mice were first subjected to micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) scanning using a Bruker micro-CT system (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) to evaluate structural alterations in the lumbar IVDs. Following imaging, the L4-L5 disc segments were carefully isolated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. The specimens were subsequently decalcified in 14% EDTA for 1 month, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 6 μm.

For histological assessment, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to examine the overall morphology of the intervertebral discs. Safranin O-fast green (S-O) staining was used to evaluate changes in disc matrix composition, particularly proteoglycan content. Sirius Red staining was further carried out to assess collagen organization and structural alterations within the disc tissue.

Human and rat sample study

Human IVD samples were collected during spinal surgery with approval from the institutional ethics committee (Approval No. MR-32-24-045436). Based on preoperative MRI evaluation, the samples were classified according to the severity of disc degeneration. Annulus fibrosus tissues from discs of different degeneration grades were isolated and analyzed by immunohistochemical staining to assess the expression of the circadian clock-related proteins BMAL1 and PER2 (n = 3).

Rat intervertebral disc tissues were subjected to the same experimental procedure (n = 3). Briefly, Healthy rat disc samples and degeneration samples were collected and processed for immunohistochemical staining of BMAL1 and PER2, allowing comparison of clock protein expression patterns across species and degeneration stages.

Synthesis of UPMSN-AZO

To prepare the UPMSN-AZO nanoparticles, firstly, N-(3-triethoxysilyl)propyl-4-phenylazobenzamide (AZO) was prepared according to reference [41]. Briefly, 0.20 g of 4-aminoazobenzene and 0.27 g of 3-(triethoxysilyl) propyl isocyanate were dissolved in 60 mL of tetrahydrofuran and heated at 73 °C under nitrogen atmosphere. After being stirred for 9 h, the solvent was evaporated to dryness and the residue was recrystallized in hexane to get the AZO. Secondly, 55.0 mg of upconverting mesoporous silica nanoparticles (UPMSN, NaYREF4 (RE:Yb, Er), Xi'an Qiyue biological technology Co. Ltd) and 13.75 mg of AZO were dispersed in 50 mL of ethanol, and then the mixture was kept refluxed at 75 °C and stirred for 3 h. The product was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min. After centrifugation, washing with ethanol, and drying under vacuum oven, UPMSN-AZO was obtained and stored at 4 °C.

Preparation of methacryloyl-modified decellularized annulus fibrosus matrix hydrogel (DAFMMA)

The preparation of DAFM utilized a specialized decellularization procedure. Initially, AF tissues harvested from fresh porcine spines were rinsed in PBS and minced into small fragments. These pieces were then subjected to enzymatic digestion in a 0.25% trypsin solution (Yuanye, China) with agitation at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the tissue was washed with PBS and incubated in a nuclease solution (Yuanye, China) at 37 °C overnight. The tissue was further decellularized in 1% Triton X-100 with agitation for at least 24 h. After six 8-h PBS rinses, the obtained DAFM was lyophilized, milled into powder, and stored at −80 °C. The powder was then digested with pepsin acetate for 48 h to prepare a 1% (w/v) DAFM solution, followed by centrifugation to remove undigested material. The digested solution was salted out, dialyzed for 48 h, freeze-dried, and stored at −20 °C until further use. Finally, the lyophilized product was formulated into a 10 mg/mL substrate solution with 0.5 M acetic acid solution. The pH was adjusted to 8.5 with sodium hydroxide solution, and the MA solution was slowly added dropwise while stirring vigorously and avoiding light, and the pH was adjusted to 8.5 with sodium hydroxide, and the reaction was continued for 2 days. The above reaction solution was placed in pre-cooled acetone, centrifuged to obtain a white precipitate dialyzed at 4 °C for 7 days, and the precipitate was lyophilized to obtain DAFMMA [42].

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR was performed to verify the successful grafting of AZO onto the surface of UPMSN. Three samples, including AZO, UPMSN, and UPMSN-AZO, were analyzed. Prior to measurement, all samples were thoroughly dried to minimize interference from adsorbed moisture. The dried powders were mixed with spectroscopic-grade KBr, finely ground, and compressed into pellets for analysis. FTIR spectra were recorded using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1, with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans per sample. Successful grafting of AZO onto UPMSN was determined by comparing the characteristic absorption bands of AZO, UPMSN, and UPMSN-AZO, with particular attention to the appearance of AZO-related peaks in the spectrum of UPMSN-AZO together with the typical silica framework bands.

Morphology of UPMSN-AZO and the composite hydrogels

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; FEI, USA) was employed to characterize the morphology of UPMSN-AZO at a 120 kV accelerating voltage. For the composite hydrogels, surface and structural insights were captured using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Quanta 250, America).

In vitro drug release experiment

Melatonin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was first dissolved in PBS solution. Then, UPMSN-AZO was added to the MT solution and stirred for 24 h away from light, and then appropriate amount of β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) was added to continue stirring for 24 h. The collected supernatant was washed three times with deionized water and centrifuged at 14,800 rpm for 5 min each time, after which the supernatant was discarded. UPMSN-AZO-β-CD was then dispersed in 10% DAFMMA solution, and the pregel was exposed to UV light to form the composite hydrogel. For the cyclic melatonin release assay, the composite hydrogel was exposed to repeated 10-min light and 10-min dark periods for six cycles, with a total release duration of 120 min. During each light period, NIR irradiation was applied for the first 5 min, followed by 5 min without irradiation before sample collection. During each dark period, the hydrogel was maintained in a dark environment at room temperature for 10 min before sample collection. The release medium was collected at each time point and replaced with fresh medium. The concentration of released melatonin was quantified using a melatonin ELISA kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and western blot analysis

To explore the role of cyclic release of melatonin, five groups were established as follows: Control, H2O2, H2O2+MT/UPMSN-AZO/DAFMMA, H2O2+MT/UPMSN-AZO/β-CD/DAFMMA, H2O2+MT/UPMSN-AZO/β-CD/DAFMMA + NIR. The concentration of H2O2 is 100 μM. After culture for 7 days, protein and RNA were extracted for qPCR and western blot, respectively. Total protein from AFCs was isolated using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with PMSF, protease, and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China). Following concentration measurement via a BCA kit (Beyotime, China), proteins were denatured, separated via 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Signals were captured using an Odyssey infrared imaging system and analyzed with ImageJ. Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA), measured by NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and reverse-transcribed with 5×All-in-One RT MasterMix (ABM, Canada). qRT-PCR was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and gene expression was normalized to Gapdh by the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

To further exclude the influence of photothermal effects and to demonstrate the unique role of cyclic melatonin release, four groups were established as follows: Control, Control + H2O2, H2O2+MT/UPMSN-AZO/DAFMMA + NIR, H2O2+MT/UPMSN-AZO/β-CD/DAFMMA + NIR. After cultured for 7 days, proteins were extracted for Western Blot.

Cell proliferation activity assay

For cell proliferation assessment, AFCs were incubated with EdU solution for 1 h following specific treatments. Post-incubation, the cells were fixed in 4% PFA (15 min) and permeabilized. The EdU-positive cells were then identified using a Click reaction cocktail for 30 min under light-shielded conditions. After three PBS rinses and DAPI nuclear counterstaining, fluorescent images were captured. Triple PBS washes were performed between each major step to ensure clarity.

Animal study

Animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Soochow University (SUDA20240927A04) in adherence to NIH guidelines. To induce IVDD, the Co7/8 discs of adult SD rats (8–10 weeks) were percutaneously punctured using a 21G needle under 2% phenobarbital sodium anesthesia. The needle reached the NP region, where it was rotated and maintained for 30 s. Seven days after needle puncture, the composite hydrogel was implanted into the AF defect site. For the NIR treatment groups, irradiation was applied at a fixed time for 5 min daily for two consecutive weeks. Target IVD samples were collected at 4 and 8 weeks after treatment (n = 3).

Statistical analysis

All data are shown as mean ± SD from at least three replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple-comparison test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Associations between night shift work and lumbar disc herniation (LDH) in human, and the essential role of the Bmal1 gene in maintaining IVD health

UK Biobank data were used to examine the association between long-term night shift work and LDH. Over a median follow-up period of 13.7 years, 1410 participants developed LDH (Supplementary Table 1). Compared to controls, those with LDH were older, predominantly male, more likely to smoke, consume alcohol, have a lower education level, and present with a higher BMI (Supplementary Table 1).

The associations between shift work and LDH risk are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Shift workers exhibited a higher likelihood of experiencing LDH events (model 1: HR = 1.56, 95% CI = 1.37-1.77, P = 5.79 × 10−12; model 2: HR = 1.38, 95% CI = 1.22-1.58, P = 8.18 × 10−7). Additionally, increasing night shift categories correlated with a higher risk of incident LDH in age- and sex-adjusted models (P for trend = 9.36 × 10−16), with rotating shifts and permanent night shifts presenting the highest risk (HR = 2.12, 95% CI = 1.72-2.63, P = 3.85 × 10−12) (Supplementary Table 2). This association remained significant after adjusting for demographic and LDH-related risk factors (model 2) (P for trend = 2.17 × 10−9). Sensitivity analyses, which excluded participants with incident LDH <1 year from baseline, did not alter the results materially (Supplementary Table 2).

Given the central role of the Bmal1 gene in circadian homeostasis, MR analyses were conducted to investigate the association between genetically predicted Bmal1 expression and the risk of LDH, integrating genome-wide association study (GWAS) and expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data from whole blood. As shown in Fig. 1A and B, a total of 68 genetic variants within ±250 kb of the Bmal1 gene coding region demonstrated genome-wide significant associations with LDH, among which rs12295734 showed the strongest association (P = 1.81 × 10−8). Notably, all these genetic variants also showed genome-wide significant associations with Bmal1 gene expression in whole blood (all P < 5 × 10−8). Such co-localization of GWAS and eQTL signals in this region suggests Bmal1 as a potential functional candidate gene underlying the genetic risk for LDH. Supporting our hypothesis, MR analyses consistently revealed that genetically predicted lower expression of Bmal1 in whole blood is associated with an increased risk of LDH (OR: 1.100, 95% CI = 1.053-1.148, P = 1.64 × 10−5 from inverse-variance weighted method). Sensitivity MR analysis replicated this finding (weighted median: OR: 1.112, 95% CI = 1.071-1.155, P = 4.26 × 10−8; weighted mode: OR: 1.117, 95% CI = 1.074-1.163, P = 4.76 × 10−8) (Fig. 1C). No directional pleiotropy was observed in the MR-Egger regression (P-valueintercept = 0.515). These findings consistently support the association between Bmal1 gene expression and LDH risk.

To further investigate the role of Bmal1 in IVD homeostasis, Bmal1 knockout (KO) mice were used to evaluate how Bmal1 deficiency affects the structural and functional integrity of the lumbar and caudal spine. Micro-CT and histological analyses were then performed on caudal IVDs from 6-month-old wild-type (WT) and Bmal1 KO mice. The results revealed significantly narrowed intervertebral spaces in the Bmal1 KO mice, with signs of potential intervertebral fusion (Fig. 1D). H&E staining confirmed a marked reduction in the NP area, likely due to compression by bone-like tissue, along with disruption of the annulus fibrosus (AF)(Fig. 1E). Additionally, Safranin O (S-O) staining demonstrated intense red staining within the AF, indicating abnormal proteoglycan deposition, suggestive of disc remodeling or degenerative changes (Fig. 1F). Sirius Red (S-red) staining was used to assess collagen fiber distribution, density, and organization. In WT mice, collagen fibers in the AF were well organized and layered, whereas in Bmal1 KO mice, the fibers appeared disordered, fragmented, and structurally compromised (Fig. 1G and H). After examining the caudal IVD, a similar analysis was conducted on the lumbar IVD to identify the presence and characteristics of the pathological changes observed in the caudal discs. Micro-CT revealed significantly reduced lumbar IVD height in Bmal1 KO mice, although fusion signs were scarcely observed (Fig. 1I). H&E staining also showed a reduction in NP area (Fig. 1J). S-O and S-red staining results were consistent with those seen in the caudal discs, indicating degenerative changes in the lumbar IVD (Fig. 1K–M). These findings support a critical role for Bmal1, a core circadian clock component, in preserving IVD homeostasis.

Associations between IVDD and circadian clock in both human and rat

Given the critical role of the Bmal1 gene in maintaining IVD health, this study investigated the reciprocal relationship—specifically, how circadian clock-related proteins are affected in the context of IVDD. IVD samples from patients undergoing spinal surgery were collected, representing varying degrees of degeneration (Fig. 2A), with all procedures approved by the institutional ethics committee. To explore the potential involvement of circadian clock components in disc degeneration, immunohistochemical staining was performed to examine the expression patterns of two key circadian regulators—BMAL1 and PER2 proteins—in the human disc tissues. The results showed a gradual decline in the expression of both BMAL1 and PER2 as degeneration progressed (Fig. 2B–D), suggesting a correlation between circadian disruption and IVDD progression. To validate this association under controlled conditions, a needle puncture-induced rat model of IVDD was established. Disc degeneration and its progression were then evaluated by MRI at 4 and 8 weeks (Fig. 2E and F). Histological and immunohistochemical analyses were then performed on both normal and degenerated rat discs to assess BMAL1 and Per2 expression. Consistent with the human tissue findings, degenerated rat discs showed significantly reduced expression of BMAL1 and PER2 compared to the control group (Fig. 2G, H, and Fig. S1). These results suggest a conserved relationship between IVDD and circadian clock dysregulation, highlighting the circadian pathway as a potential therapeutic target for IVDD.

As IVDD progresses, oxidative stress plays a critical role in microenvironmental deterioration [43,44]. To simulate oxidative stress in a degenerated annulus fibrosus cells (AFCs) model, cultured AFCs were treated with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Following H2O2 exposure, alterations in both circadian clock components and ECM-related proteins were systematically evaluated. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a marked decrease in BMAL1 and collagen type I (COL1) fluorescence intensity (Fig. S2A and B), indicating disruptions in both circadian regulation and matrix production. Western blot analysis further confirmed a significant reduction in the expression of BMAL1 and PER2 proteins, alongside a parallel downregulation of matrix proteins COL1 and collagen type II (COL2) (Fig. S2C). Consistently, quantitative real-time PCR analysis demonstrated that oxidative stress led to the transcriptional suppression of key circadian genes (Bmal1, Clock, and Per2) and ECM anabolic genes (Col1a1, Col2a1, and Acan) (Fig. S2D–I).

These results emphasize the importance of circadian clock homeostasis in preserving AFC function and ECM integrity. More importantly, they highlight the detrimental impact of oxidative stress on circadian rhythm regulation, which may contribute to the pathological mechanisms underlying IVDD.

Characterization of the composite hydrogel

Building on these findings, an NIR-responsive circadian clock-regulating nanovalve was designed to restore disrupted circadian rhythms in the IVD and promote disc regeneration (Fig. 3A). UPMSN-AZO (UPZ) nanoparticles were synthesized as previously described [45]. TEM revealed that both UPMSN and UPMSN-AZO nanoparticles exhibited relatively uniform spherical morphology. A slight increase in particle size was observed after AZO modification, with average diameters of 57.2 ± 1.8 nm for UPMSN and 62.0 ± 1.4 nm for UPMSN-AZO (Fig. 3B and C). FTIR confirmed the successful synthesis of UPMSN-AZO, with characteristic stretching vibrations of the benzene ring in AZO appearing at 1557–1657 cm−1. After grafting AZO onto UPMSN, the composite UPMSN-AZO displayed a benzene ring peak at 1547 cm−1, confirming successful conjugation (Fig. 3D). Brunauer–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) analysis of pore volume curves indicated that the majority of the pores in UPMSN nanoparticles were distributed in the 2.4–3.7 nm range, with a smaller fraction in the 1.5–2.0 nm range. The average pore size was calculated to be approximately 3.2 nm (Fig. 3E). Additionally, fluorescence spectra demonstrated that upon excitation with 980 nm light, UPMSN exhibited strong emission peaks at 450 nm and 476 nm, with weaker peaks at 345 nm and 360 nm. After AZO conjugation, a notable attenuation in fluorescence intensity at these wavelengths was observed, suggesting that AZO effectively absorbed the emitted light, confirming its successful surface grafting and optical responsiveness (Fig. 3F). The methacryloyl-modified decellularized AF matrix hydrogel (DAFMMA) was obtained by decellularizing porcine AF tissue and grafting it with methacrylic anhydride (Fig. S3A–E). MT was loaded onto UPZ (Fig. S3F), and the mixture was uniformly blended with DAFMMA before being photo-crosslinked to form the composite hydrogel (Fig. S3G). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed that DAFMMA possessed a distinct, loose, and porous architecture, with the incorporation of 0.25% nanoparticles not significantly altering the microstructure (Fig. 3G–and S3H). The composite hydrogel was further evaluated for swelling and mechanical performance. The pure DAFMMA hydrogel exhibited rapid swelling within the first 4 h, reaching equilibrium at approximately 650% of its dry weight, with a swelling ratio of 648.14 ± 25.20%. The incorporation of UPZ slightly reduced the swelling ratio to 608.01 ± 26.78%, likely due to increased crosslinking density (Fig. 3H). The Young's modulus of the pure hydrogel was 13.20 ± 0.20 kPa, with no significant change after adding 0.25% UPZ (Fig. 3I and J). Biocompatibility, a key factor for tissue regeneration, was assessed next. AFCs adhered well to the composite hydrogel at a nanoparticle concentration of 0.25%, with no significant impact on cell proliferation. Furthermore, short-term NIR irradiation did not affect cell viability (Fig. 3K, and Fig. S4). Finally, the drug release curve of the composite hydrogel was investigated. The hydrogel exhibited an NIR-dependent release profile, supporting the potential for cyclic MT release (Fig. 3L, and Fig. S3I). The degradation profiles of DAFMMA hydrogel and UPZ/DAFMMA-0.25% hydrogels were investigated in PBS and collagenase solution. Both hydrogels degraded slowly in PBS, whereas collagenase significantly accelerated degradation. UPZ/DAFMMA-0.25% consistently showed higher residual mass than DAFMMA, indicating enhanced structural stability and improved resistance to enzymatic degradation (Fig. S3J and K).

Promotion of AFCs proliferation and inhibition of apoptosis by the composite hydrogel

To evaluate the protective effects of the composite hydrogel on AFCs under oxidative stress, cellular injury in rat primary AFCs was induced using H2O2 to simulate the oxidative microenvironment associated with IVDD. Treatment groups were as follows: PBS, MT/UPMSN-AZO/DAFMMA (MT/UPZ/DAM), MT/UPMSN-AZO-β-CD/DAFMMA (MT/UPZD/DAM), and MT/UPMSN-AZO-β-CD/DAFMMA combined with NIR irradiation (MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR). As shown in Fig. 4A and Fig. S5A, B, intracellular ROS levels were significantly increased after H2O2 stimulation, confirming successful induction of oxidative stress. All hydrogel treatments reduced ROS accumulation, with the most notable decrease observed in the MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR group, suggesting that the NIR-responsive hydrogel system enhances antioxidative properties.

Cell proliferation, assessed by EdU staining, was markedly improved in the MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR group compared to the MT/UPZ/DAM group (Fig. 4B, and Fig. S5C, D), indicating that the NIR-responsive behavior of the hydrogel enables cyclic melatonin release, thereby creating a more favorable microenvironment for tissue regeneration. Western blot analysis further confirmed these results, showing a significant downregulation of the pro-apoptotic protein Caspase3 in the MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR group, while the anti-apoptotic marker Bcl2 was upregulated, suggesting that the composite hydrogel modulates apoptosis-related signaling pathways (Fig. 4C–E). Consistent with these findings, flow cytometry analysis of Annexin V/PI-stained cells (Fig. 4F) demonstrated a marked reduction in the percentage of apoptotic cells after MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR treatment. These results suggest that the NIR-responsive composite hydrogel (MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR) not only mitigates oxidative damage through efficient ROS scavenging but also promotes AFC survival and proliferation by inhibiting apoptosis. This dual functionality highlights the potential of this smart hydrogel system for IVD regeneration under pathological conditions characterized by oxidative stress.

Regulation of circadian clock and recovery of AFCs matrix secretory function by the composite hydrogel

Western blot analysis was performed to determine the expression of the core clock proteins PER2, BMAL1, and CLOCK in AFCs following treatment with the composite hydrogel. Exposure to H2O2-induced oxidative stress significantly downregulated the expression of all three proteins, indicating circadian rhythm disruption. Treatment with MT/UPZ/DAM partially reversed this downregulation, suggesting a protective role. In contrast, treatment with the composite hydrogel alone combined with NIR irradiation had negligible effects on circadian rhythm regulation and matrix secretion under normal conditions, indicating the biosafety of the photothermal intervention. Notably, the MT/UPZ/DAM + NIR group exhibited a more pronounced restorative effect, with significant upregulation of BMAL1 and PER2 expression (Fig. 5A and B, and Fig. S6, S7). These findings demonstrate that MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR treatment not only mitigates oxidative damage but also restores the expression of core circadian clock components, highlighting its potential to regulate circadian rhythm homeostasis in degenerated AFCs.

Regeneration of IVDs critically depends on the maintenance of normal physiological functions in AFCs, particularly their capacity to synthesize and maintain the ECM. Key ECM components, including COL1, COL2, and Aggrecan, provide mechanical strength and hydration to the AF. To evaluate the impact of different treatments on ECM metabolism under oxidative stress, western blot analysis was conducted to quantify the expression of these matrix proteins (Fig. 5C and D). H2O2-induced oxidative stress significantly reduced COL1 expression in AFCs, indicating impaired matrix production. Interestingly, MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR treatment restored COL1 expression to a level slightly higher than the control group, with the difference being statistically significant. Similar trends were observed for COL2 and Aggrecan: both were substantially downregulated after H2O2 exposure and significantly upregulated following MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR treatment, suggesting that the composite hydrogel effectively rescues ECM synthesis under oxidative stress. In contrast, matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP13), a key enzyme involved in ECM degradation, was significantly elevated after H2O2 treatment, indicating increased catabolic activity. However, MMP13 expression was markedly reduced following MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR treatment, approaching levels comparable to the control group (Fig. 5E). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that MMP13 expression, elevated after H2O2 stimulation, decreased following MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR treatment, highlighting the efficacy of the composite hydrogel in preserving AFC function and enhancing physiological homeostasis (Fig. S8A). These results suggest that the hydrogel not only promotes ECM synthesis but also suppresses abnormal matrix degradation, maintaining tissue homeostasis.

To further validate these observations at the transcriptional level, quantitative PCR analysis was conducted for circadian clock-related genes (Bmal1, Clock, and Per2), matrix synthesis-related genes (Col1a1, Col2a1, and Acan), and matrix degradation-related genes (Adamts4, Adamts5, and Mmp13). The qPCR results were consistent with the protein expression data. Specifically, H2O2 exposure significantly downregulated circadian rhythm-related and ECM synthesis-related genes, while upregulating matrix degradation-related genes. Treatment with MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR effectively reversed these effects (Fig. 5F–H, and S8B, C). These findings provide strong evidence that MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR can effectively ameliorate oxidative stress-associated circadian dysregulation and ECM metabolic disorder in AFCs, highlighting its therapeutic potential for IVD regeneration.

To further explore the link between ECM synthesis and circadian rhythm restoration in AFCs, immunofluorescence staining was performed for the core circadian protein BMAL1 and the primary collagen synthesized by AFCs, COL1. The staining results revealed a consistent pattern, with the MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR group showing the highest fluorescence intensity and staining area for both BMAL1 and COL1, closely resembling the control group (Fig. 5I, J, and Fig. S9). In summary, the physiological function of AFCs is essential for IVD regeneration. Deterioration of the microenvironment disrupts the circadian rhythm of AFCs, impairing their functional capacity. Our findings indicate that cyclic MT release can restore circadian rhythms and enhance AFC physiological functions, presenting a promising strategy for disc regeneration.

Restoration of circadian clock homeostasis enhances AFC function through modulation of autophagy-related pathways

In the IVD, autophagy supports cellular homeostasis under stress through the removal of damaged organelles and misfolded proteins, thereby helping limit apoptosis and preserve tissue integrity [46]. However, the effects of autophagy appear to be context-dependent. Although basal autophagic activity is generally protective, excessive or dysregulated autophagy may contribute to pathological progression. For example, increased autophagy has been reported in nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) from diabetic rats with disc degeneration [47], suggesting that autophagy overactivation may aggravate disc pathology under certain conditions. Chen et al. further showed that H2O2-induced oxidative stress increased both apoptosis and autophagy in NPCs [46]. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of autophagy reduced cell death in this setting, indicating that excessive autophagic activity may exacerbate cellular injury and that its controlled modulation may help delay disc degeneration. Growing evidence also suggests that the circadian clock machinery, particularly core clock genes such as Bmal1 and Clock, is involved in the regulation of autophagy through rhythmic control of autophagy-related genes, including ULK1, LC3, and Beclin-1. Circadian disruption has been associated with autophagy-related abnormalities and impaired cellular homeostasis in multiple tissues [48,49]. In turn, altered autophagy may also influence circadian gene expression, suggesting a reciprocal interaction between these two systems [50].

To explore whether circadian regulation affects autophagic homeostasis in AFCs, this study assessed the expression of autophagy markers LC3, Beclin-1, and P62 by western blot, along with ECM-related markers, under oxidative stress induced by H2O2. A significant increase in LC3 and Beclin-1 expression was observed, indicating autophagosome accumulation and impaired autophagic degradation. This was accompanied by a marked downregulation of PER2, COL1, and COL2, suggesting disrupted circadian regulation and ECM synthesis under oxidative stress. Treatment with CLK-8 (a CLOCK inhibitor) further exacerbated autophagy, as evidenced by increased LC3 levels, supporting the idea that CLOCK inhibits excessive autophagy. In contrast, treatment with the NIR-responsive nanovalve-controlled MT delivery system (MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR) significantly reversed these pathological changes. LC3, Beclin-1, and P62 levels were restored to near-normal levels, while COL1 and COL2 expression was notably upregulated. Notably, this restorative effect was blocked by CLK-8 (Fig. 6B–H). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that after H2O2 exposure, LC3 fluorescence intensity increased, while P62 intensity decreased. This phenomenon was reversed after treatment with MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR, but could be inhibited by CLK-8 (Fig. 6I and J), confirming the involvement of circadian signaling in mediating the therapeutic benefits of MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR.

These findings suggest that restoring circadian clock function modulates autophagy-related changes in AFCs under oxidative stress. By alleviating dysregulated autophagy and promoting ECM synthesis, circadian modulation—particularly through MT-based nanodelivery—may enhance AFCs viability and facilitate IVD regeneration.

Rat IVD repair by regulating circadian clock using the composite hydrogel

IVDD was induced in rat caudal discs by needle puncture. According to the in vitro screening results, six groups were established: Sham, Defect, UPZ/DAM (DMP), UPZ/DAM + NIR (DMP + N), MT/UPZ/DAM + NIR (DMPT + N), and MT/UPZD/DAM + NIR (DMPTD + N). Seven days after puncture, the composite hydrogel was implanted into the AF defect site. For the NIR treatment groups, irradiation was then applied at a fixed time for 5 min daily for two consecutive weeks (Fig. 7A, and Fig. S10A). X-ray and MRI were performed at 4 and 8 weeks after treatment to evaluate IVD height and NP signal intensity, respectively. As recovery of the disc height index (DHI) typically correlates with increased water content, MRI signal intensity served as an indicator of disc hydration, with lower signal intensity reflecting reduced water content and more severe degeneration. At 4 weeks, the Defect and DMP groups showed significantly lower IVD height than the Sham group. The DMP + N group exhibited a mild improvement, but no significant difference was found relative to the Defect and DMP groups. Notably, the DMPT + N and DMPTD + N groups, which received co-treatment of MT and NIR, exhibited only minimal reductions in IVD height and DHI (Fig. S10B–E). At 8 weeks, the DMPTD + N group showed significantly better recovery compared to the DMPT + N group. In terms of MRI signal intensity, the DMP + N group demonstrated higher intensity than the Defect and DMP groups. Although the DMPT group showed further improvement, the DMPTD + N group exhibited the strongest signal across all treatment groups, closely resembling the Sham group. These findings suggest that cyclic MT release may facilitate circadian rhythm restoration and enhance disc repair (Fig. 7B–E).

Histological analysis of IVDs was performed at both the 4- and 8-week post-surgery intervals. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining was used to assess structural integrity, while Safranin O/Fast Green (S-O) staining evaluated collagen and proteoglycan content. The Sham group exhibited normal disc morphology with an intact NP and well-organized AF. In contrast, the Defect group demonstrated complete loss of the NP and disorganized AF. Similar structural degradation was observed in the DMP and DMP + N groups, with no significant differences from the Defect group. However, the DMPTD + N group maintained a more intact NP and exhibited a disc microarchitecture comparable to the Sham group (Fig. 7F, and Fig. S10F). S-O staining further showed that, among all treatment groups, the DMPTD + N group had the highest collagen and aggregated proteoglycan levels, second only to the Sham group (Fig. 7G, and Fig. S10G).

To assess the restoration of disc biomechanical function, axial cyclic tension-compression tests were conducted 8 weeks post-treatment (Fig. 7H–J). Compared with the Sham group, the Defect group showed significantly reduced axial range of motion (ROM) and neutral zone (NZ) length, confirming biomechanical degeneration of the disc. No obvious improvement was observed in the DMP or DMP + N groups relative to the Defect group. By contrast, the DMPTD + N group showed the smallest decreases in ROM and NZ length, and its compressive and tensile stiffness values were closer to those of the Sham group (Fig. 7K–N), indicating partial recovery of biomechanical function.

To further elucidate the mechanism by which the composite hydrogel promotes IVD regeneration in vivo, immunohistochemical analysis was performed on IVD tissue sections. The status of the IVD circadian clock was evaluated by assessing the expression of clock proteins PER2 and BMAL1. Collagen regeneration was examined through COL1 expression, and the autophagy-related changes associated with circadian clock regulation were assessed by detecting LC3 expression. Quantitative analysis, which included calculating mean signal intensity and the percentage of positive cells, reflected the relative protein expression levels. In the Defect group, BMAL1, PER2, and COL1 expression were significantly decreased. However, in the DMPTD + N group—treated with cyclic MT delivery and NIR—expression levels of these proteins were significantly restored (Fig. 8A–C, E-G, and S11A-C, E-G). Conversely, LC3 expression, elevated in the Defect group, was reduced to near-normal levels in the DMPTD + N group (Fig. 8D–H, and S11D, H). These findings suggest that the composite hydrogel regulates excessive autophagic activity through modulation of the IVD circadian clock, thereby facilitating disc regeneration and repair.

Discussion

Evolutionarily conserved circadian rhythms are integral to biology, influencing nearly every aspect of physiology and behavior in alignment with the Earth's rotation. Endogenous circadian rhythms regulate sleep, eating, and exercise patterns, modulate immune responses, affect drug responsiveness, and govern daily metabolic cycles. This temporal coordination between behavior, physiology, and the external environment ensures optimized metabolism and energy allocation to meet daily physiological demands [51,52]. The IVD is also subject to circadian regulation, with disruptions potentially contributing to the onset of IVDD [53]. IVDD is characterized by structural degeneration and functional decline, including reduced cellular function and number in both NP and AF. This leads to imbalances in ECM synthesis, increased inflammation, and elevated apoptosis, which accelerate disc degeneration.

Recent studies indicate that the circadian clock plays a pivotal role in the metabolic regulation of IVD cells, influencing matrix metabolism, inflammation, and apoptosis. This makes the circadian clock a potential therapeutic target for IVDD. Cells within the IVD, including AFCs and NPCs, exhibit distinct circadian rhythms, regulated primarily by circadian clock genes such as Bmal1 and Clock, which form the molecular basis for circadian oscillations [13,54]. BMAL1 and CLOCK form heterodimers and activate the transcription of CRY and PER family members through E-box motifs. The resulting feedback loop inhibits BMAL1/CLOCK activity and sustains a 24-h rhythm in many clock-controlled genes [55]. These genes govern a variety of cellular processes, including the cell cycle, metabolic pathways, and protein synthesis and degradation. The rhythmic expression of these genes is critical for maintaining a balance between the synthesis and degradation of matrix proteins, such as collagen and proteoglycans. Disruption of this circadian rhythm can lead to unbalanced matrix metabolism, driving degenerative changes in the disc [56,57]. Histochemical staining of degenerative AF tissues from both human patients and rat models revealed a consistent decline in circadian clock-related and matrix-related protein expression as disc degeneration progressed.

Given the circadian clock's role in regulating disc cell metabolism, modulating this clock could be a promising strategy for repairing discs and delaying degeneration. This modulation can be achieved through behavioral interventions, such as improving sleep quality and optimizing light exposure, which can help restore the circadian rhythm of IVD cells [58]. Pharmacological interventions, including MT receptor agonists or other compounds capable of regulating the circadian clock, are also viable options. MT has been shown to enhance matrix metabolism and improve resistance to oxidative stress through its effects on the circadian clock [59]. However, the cyclic release pattern of MT is critical for maintaining circadian rhythms. In this study, an NIR-triggered, AZO-modified UPMSN system was designed for cyclic MT release, which was encapsulated in a DAFMMA hydrogel. Our results suggest that this composite hydrogel provides an effective platform for NIR-responsive MT release. It improved clock-related signaling and promoted the expression of matrix proteins, including COL1, COL2, and Aggrecan, during IVD repair.

During the progression of IVDD, the mechanical properties of the IVD, including load-bearing capacity, compression resistance, and elastic modulus, significantly decline, impairing disc function [60,61]. Consequently, restoring these mechanical properties is a key criterion for evaluating the success of IVD repair. Effective therapeutic strategies must address not only biological repair but also the restoration of mechanical function to ensure the long-term maintenance of IVD performance. Our biomechanical testing confirmed that the composite hydrogel improved IVD mobility and partially restored its mechanical properties. These findings suggest that the composite hydrogel may regulate clock-related signaling in AFCs, promote matrix protein expression, and slow IVDD progression.

Additionally, this study explored the mechanisms by which the composite hydrogel modulates circadian rhythms, impacting matrix metabolism and AFC function. Cyclic MT release from the hydrogel increased PER2 expression and was associated with changes in matrix metabolism and autophagy. Autophagy, a vital process for IVD homeostasis, is intricately regulated by the circadian clock. This process involves the degradation of cellular macromolecules and damaged organelles under normal conditions or in response to stressors such as oxidative stress, nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, radiation, and anticancer treatments [62,63]. The circadian clock not only regulates matrix metabolism in IVD cells but also maintains intracellular homeostasis by modulating autophagic processes. While autophagy serves as a protective mechanism, aiding in the removal of damaged components and reducing apoptosis, excessive autophagy may be detrimental in the context of IVD. In this study, the composite hydrogel alleviated dysregulated autophagy-related changes in degenerated discs and AFCs, thereby helping restore AFC function and promote IVD repair. These findings suggest that the hydrogel can regulate the circadian clock within the IVD, mitigating the harmful effects of excessive autophagy on healthy cells.

The novel approach of regulating the circadian clock in the IVD through cyclic MT release represents a promising strategy for IVDD treatment. Although significant repair was achieved, This study has several limitations. First, the in vivo repair experiments were performed using a rat caudal disc degeneration model, which may not fully reproduce the biomechanical and pathological features of human lumbar disc degeneration. Second, although the current observation period demonstrated promising repair effects, longer-term studies are needed to evaluate the durability and safety of this hydrogel system. Third, circadian regulation may differ between humans and animal models, and further studies are required to clarify the translational relevance of local circadian clock modulation in human IVD tissues. Finally, although the NIR-responsive system enabled cyclic melatonin release, more precise control of the timing, dose, and duration of in vivo melatonin release remains an important direction for future optimization. Additionally, exploring gene therapy to precisely regulate the expression of circadian clock-related genes, such as Bmal1 and Clock, could offer another avenue for enhancing IVDD treatment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study supports a close association between disc degeneration and circadian clock dysregulation, with core clock genes contributing to the maintenance of matrix homeostasis in disc cells. Circadian disruption was associated with impaired matrix metabolism and accelerated disc degeneration. We developed a photo-responsive, ROS-scavenging composite hydrogel for cyclic melatonin delivery to modulate clock-related homeostasis in IVD cells. In vitro, this hydrogel restored the expression of core circadian clock components and promoted ECM secretion. In vivo, it improved disc repair and partially restored biomechanical function. These findings suggest that cyclic melatonin delivery is a promising strategy for IVDD treatment.
