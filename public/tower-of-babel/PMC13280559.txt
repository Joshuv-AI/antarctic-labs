Recurrent Pneumothorax Following Multilevel Posterior Spinal Fixation in a Patient With Pre‐Existing Pulmonary Contusion: A Case Report
Authors: Parisa Mirzaei, Zahra Baghani, Mohammad Javad Mousavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecurrent pneumothorax following posterior spinal fixation is a rare but life‐threatening complication, particularly in polytrauma patients with underlying pulmonary contusion. We report a 19‐year‐old male involved in a high‐speed maritime accident who sustained a T12 fracture and right‐sided pulmonary contusion. Initial pneumothorax was managed with a chest tube, which was removed after radiographic resolution. Subsequently, the patient underwent posterior T10–L2 fixation. Immediately postoperatively, he developed acute dyspnea and massive recurrent pneumothorax requiring urgent re‐intervention. Despite limited initial imaging data due to his foreign national status, the patient recovered fully following tertiary care referral. This case illustrates that radiographic lung re‐expansion is an insufficient criterion for chest tube removal before spinal surgery. The physiological stress of prone positioning and positive pressure ventilation can reactivate occult air leaks in contused lungs. We recommend retaining chest tubes until after spinal fixation to prevent catastrophic recurrence.Keywords: alveolar‐pleural fistula, chest tube management, maritime accident, polytrauma, positive pressure ventilation, posterior spinal fixation, pulmonary contusion, recurrent pneumothorax

Introduction

Blunt trauma is one of the most common causes of severe injuries worldwide, accounting for a substantial proportion of trauma‐related mortality and disability [1]. In recent years, increased road and maritime activities have led to a rise in high‐energy injuries, particularly among young individuals [2]. These patterns impose a significant burden on healthcare systems and highlight the need for optimized diagnostic and treatment strategies [1].

WHO guidelines highlight the need for improved trauma care systems, including standardized management and prevention of complications such as pneumothorax [3]. This is particularly relevant in patients undergoing major surgical procedures, including spinal fixation [4]. Although spinal surgery is generally safe, it may be associated with pulmonary complications such as pneumothorax, which in recurrent cases can lead to prolonged hospitalization and increased morbidity [5, 6]. In patients with pulmonary contusion, the risk of pneumothorax is significantly higher due to parenchymal injury and impaired healing [7, 8, 9]. The combination of blunt trauma, lung injury and multilevel spinal fixation represents a high‐risk scenario for recurrent pneumothorax [5, 7, 10].

However, data addressing this complication remain limited, particularly in developing settings. This report aims to highlight this underrecognized clinical scenario and provide a mechanism‐based approach to its management.

Case Report

A 19‐year‐old male with no significant medical history was admitted to the emergency department following a high‐velocity maritime accident (speedboat collision). The mechanism of injury involved a direct high‐energy thoracoabdominal impact. Upon admission, the patient was hemodynamically stable but reported acute dyspnea and presented with an extensive abdominal laceration.

Primary survey and initial imaging revealed a right‐sided pulmonary contusion and an associated pneumothorax. Laboratory findings showed an initial inflammatory response with an elevated White Blood Cell (WBC) count of 16,100/mL and a mild decrease in haemoglobin (11.1 g/dL). A right‐sided tube thoracostomy was performed in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU). After 4 days of drainage, the lung showed complete re‐expansion on Chest X‐ray (CXR), and the chest tube was subsequently removed (Figure 1). A formal water‐seal trial was not documented, and cessation of air leak was assessed clinically rather than via a digital drainage system. This represents a potential contributing factor to recurrence.

Chest radiograph after initial chest tube removal (4 days post‐trauma). The image shows complete expansion of the right lung without residual pneumothorax. Unfortunately, due to the patient's foreign national status and lack of national ID access in the electronic record system, the initial radiographic image (pre‐chest tube insertion) was unavailable and could not be retrieved.

Following pulmonary stabilization, the focus shifted to a T12 vertebral fracture identified via MRI. The patient underwent a multilevel posterior spinal fixation (T10–L2) to stabilize the thoracolumbar junction. The procedure lasted approximately 120 min under general anaesthesia with no immediate intraoperative complications. Detailed intraoperative ventilator parameters were not available due to incomplete anaesthesia records; however, it is likely that standard PPV with moderate PEEP was applied. The absence of precise ventilatory data limits the ability to fully quantify the contribution of barotrauma.

In the immediate postoperative period, the patient experienced a sudden recurrence of dyspnea. A STAT chest radiograph demonstrated a massive recurrent right‐sided pneumothorax with significant lung collapse and a leftward mediastinal shift (Figure 2). A second chest tube was urgently inserted, resulting in immediate clinical improvement. Due to the complexity of the case and the patient's status as a foreign national with limited primary imaging records, he was referred to a tertiary trauma center for specialized surveillance.

Chest radiograph immediately after posterior T10–L2 fixation surgery (performed for T12 vertebral fracture). The image demonstrates complete right lung collapse with leftward mediastinal shift, indicative of severe recurrent pneumothorax. Following this finding, a second chest tube was immediately inserted, and the patient was referred to a tertiary center for specialized management.

The second chest tube remained in situ for 5 days. Serial CXRs confirmed resolution of the pneumothorax and gradual healing of the pulmonary contusion (Figure 3). The patient was discharged after a 10‐day uneventful follow‐up period. At the 20‐day outpatient visit, he remained asymptomatic with SpO2 of 98% on room air and no limitation in daily physical activity, consistent with functional respiratory recovery. Discussion

Recurrent pneumothorax after spinal fixation, particularly in patients with initial lung injury, is relatively rare but significant and can lead to hypoxia, prolonged hospitalization, re‐insertion of chest tubes, referral to tertiary centers and increased risk of respiratory complications [6]. In this case, initial pulmonary contusion was the primary factor for pneumothorax recurrence post‐surgery, as parenchymal injury increases lung tissue fragility and impairs ventilation [9]. Early chest tube removal in the presence of contusion increases recurrence risk, as observed after 4 days in this patient [8]. The restrictive effect of thoracolumbar fixation reduces lung compliance and heightens recurrence risk, especially in multilevel surgeries like T10–L2 [5]. Lack of initial CT led to underestimated injury severity, more common in foreign nationals [11]. Prolonged surgery (though 2 h here), bleeding, and transfusion needs exacerbate pulmonary inflammation and predispose to pneumothorax recurrence [7]. This report can improve protocols for chest tube removal and post‐operative monitoring in Iran's trauma centers, focusing on a proposed algorithm for chest tube removal (minimum 5–7 days with contusion), repeated radiological assessments and oxygenation monitoring [3]. The clinical importance of this report lies in emphasizing precise monitoring of trauma patients with initial lung injury before and after spinal surgery, potentially reducing recurrence rates [12]. Comparison with similar reports shows higher recurrence rates in adolescents, with factors like young age, multilevel surgery and initial lung injury increasing risk [13]. Differential diagnosis included other causes of postoperative respiratory distress such as pulmonary embolism, aspiration pneumonitis, or iatrogenic injury. However, the timing of symptom onset, imaging findings and response to repeat chest tube insertion support recurrent pneumothorax as the primary diagnosis.

The recurrence of pneumothorax in this patient can be attributed to the physiological stressors of spinal surgery on an already compromised pulmonary parenchyma. While the initial chest tube removal was guided by an apparently normal chest radiograph, it is critical to distinguish radiographic resolution of pneumothorax from true biological closure of an alveolar‐pleural fistula (APF) [14, 15]. Radiographic resolution primarily reflects lung re‐expansion and redistribution of intrapleural air, whereas definitive closure of the fistulous tract requires inflammatory healing and fibrotic sealing, a process that may be significantly delayed in contused and inflamed lung tissue [14, 15].

During general anaesthesia, the application of positive pressure ventilation (PPV)—particularly with positive end‐expiratory pressure (PEEP)—can result in regional overdistension of relatively compliant alveoli adjacent to contused, non‐compliant regions [15, 16]. This heterogeneity in lung mechanics generates localized shear stress and increases transpulmonary pressure gradients, predisposing to reopening of previously sealed or subclinical alveolar‐pleural communications [15, 16].

Furthermore, prone positioning redistributes ventilation and perfusion, altering regional transpulmonary pressures. In the presence of unilateral lung injury, this redistribution may exacerbate stress at the interface between injured and healthy lung segments, further increasing the risk of fistula reactivation and recurrent pneumothorax [17].

In conclusion, chest radiography alone is insufficient to guide chest tube removal in patients with pulmonary contusion undergoing major spinal fixation. A more conservative, physiology‐based approach is required to reduce the risk of recurrence. Furthermore, we strongly recommend preoperative thoracic CT for all polytrauma patients with radiographic evidence of parenchymal lung injury who are scheduled for major surgery under general anaesthesia and PPV, regardless of documentation status or nationality.
