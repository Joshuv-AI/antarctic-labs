Determinants of BCG vaccine coverage among 0–2 months aged Somali children: Promoting equality in vaccination
Authors: Abdirashid M. Yousuf, Mohamed A. Hussein, Hassan Sh Abdirahman Elmi, Md. Moyazzem Hossain, Abdisalam Hassan Muse, Harun I. Warsame, Hodo Abdikarim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSomalia reports low overall immunization rates; the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (SDHS) indicated that overall BCG vaccination coverage for children under five years of age was only 37.0%. However, coverage specifically for newborns and early infants remains underexplored. This study assessed the determinants of BCG vaccination among infants aged 0–2 months. Secondary data from the 2020 SDHS were analyzed for 457 mother – infant pairs. Descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression were used to identify factors associated with BCG uptake. BCG coverage was 79.7% (95% CI: 75.8%–83.2%). Infants in nomadic communities (OR = 0.18, p < .01) were significantly less likely to be vaccinated than urban infants. Mothers with primary education (OR = 2.62, p < .05) and the lack of maternal healthcare utilization – specifically antenatal care and health-facility delivery in the past 12 months (OR = 0.41, p < .01) – were key determinants of vaccination status. While early infant BCG coverage is significantly higher than the national average for all children under five, severe inequities still exist in nomadic and rural populations. Improving maternal education and increasing contact with healthcare facilities are essential steps to closing the immunization gap.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a communicable disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, predominantly affecting the lungs but also capable of affecting other organs.1 Over the past decade, TB has had a significant global impact, with an estimated 9.6 million cases reported worldwide and 1.5 million deaths.1,2 The World Health Organization (WHO) has set ambitious targets for ending the tuberculosis (TB) epidemic by 2030, including treating 40 million people with TB by 2022.3 However, the global incidence of TB is higher than previously estimated, with 10.6 million cases reported in 2021.4 India has the highest number of TB cases, with 2.84 million reported in 2015.5 Despite some progress, including a 1.8% decline in TB cases in 2017, there is still a need for intensified efforts to improve TB diagnosis, treatment, and prevention.6

Moreover, the estimated number of TB cases in Africa is a significant concern, with the region accounting for a large proportion of global cases.7 The burden of TB in children aged 0–2 months in Sub-Saharan Africa is significant, with a high prevalence of acute symptoms and comorbidities.8 Recent global analyses of 34 sub-Saharan African countries highlight that while regional vaccination coverage is improving, fragile states like Somalia continue to lag behind due to systemic healthcare barriers and geographical inequities.9 Immunization, a key tool in achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), has been found to have a significant impact on health and other SDGs, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.10 The Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine, introduced in 1921, administered at birth or soon after, has shown efficacy in preventing tuberculosis (TB) and other mycobacterial infections.11 This vaccine is administered in countries with a high prevalence and risk of TB exposure to protect individuals, particularly infants and young children, from the devastating effects of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB) and meningeal tuberculosis.

Furthermore, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends BCG vaccination for infants in areas with high TB prevalence and exposure risk.1 Vaccinations, which boost the immune system to prevent future infections, are fundamental to public health and serve as essential tools in protecting individuals from infectious diseases. The effectiveness of immunization protocols in reducing mortality rates associated with infectious diseases cannot be overstated. Vaccination efforts have significantly reduced pneumonia-related deaths by 59% and diarrheal-related deaths by 29%.12 This highlights the crucial role of vaccination initiatives in preventing fatalities. Administering a single dose of the BCG vaccine to newborns shortly after birth is recommended to reduce the risk of tuberculosis transmission and its associated complications.

Despite its importance, access to the BCG vaccine remains unequal worldwide, with disparities evident in countries more specifically in sub-Sahara Africa. Countries that have implemented national BCG vaccination policies aimed at covering all children have varying levels of vaccine coverage, ranging from 50% to 80%. For example, Ethiopia reports a coverage rate of 82% and Papua New Guinea reports 52%.1 In contrast, in Somalia, not all children have access to this crucial immunization. The 2020 Somalia Demographic Health Survey report indicated that only 37% of all Somali children under five years old received the BCG vaccine. However, coverage rates during the immediate early infancy period (0–2 months) require specific evaluation. These statistics highlight the need for targeted efforts to improve access to vaccines, ensuring equitable distribution and maximum protection for children against TB.13

Moreover, studies investigating the determinants of BCG vaccine coverage have identified several factors, including place of residence, attendance at antenatal care, location of birth, maternal education level, economic status, maternal occupation, and maternal age.14 Furthermore, research in East Africa has also identified various factors influencing BCG vaccine coverage. Canavan et al.,15 found that delivery location and region play a significant role, with public or private institutions associated with higher coverage. Kaboré et al.,16 highlighted the impact of geographical distribution, transport logistics, and vaccine shortages on vaccination services in Burkina Faso. Tsehay et al.,17 identified residence, mother’s age, place of delivery, antenatal care, wealth index, and occupation as determinants of BCG vaccination coverage in Ethiopia. These studies collectively underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve BCG vaccine coverage in the region.

Therefore, the determinants of BCG vaccine coverage in the Somali context are not scientifically known, despite reports from the World Health Organization and the United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund. Additionally, from the existing literature, there is a dearth of highlighting this low coverage of the BCG vaccine in Somalia and potentially key associated factors. Thus, this study aims to model BCG coverage and identify potentially associated determinants in Somalia using 2020 Somalia Demographic Health Survey (SDHS) data, and later identifying these determinant characteristics will help improvements of immunization service coverage in Somalia. Also, make a contribution of evidence-based knowledge about immunization in general and more specifically to the BCG vaccine.

Material and methods

Study area

The study focused on examining factors influencing BCG vaccination coverage among Somali children aged 0–2 months. Somalia, located in the Horn of Africa, faces diverse socio-economic and healthcare challenges that shape child health outcomes. Geographically, the country is bordered by Kenya to the southwest, Ethiopia to the west, the Gulf of Aden to the north, and the Somali Sea and Guardafui Channel to the east. Covering an area of approximately 637,657 square kilometers, Somalia’s terrain consists mainly of plateaus, plains, and highlands. It also possesses the longest coastline on mainland Africa, extending over 3,333 kilometers. The country lies at approximately 5.1521° N latitude and 46.1996° E longitude, giving it a shape often compared to a slanted number seven. In 2023, Somalia’s population was estimated at 18,143,378, reflecting a 3.1% increase from 2022.13

Study design & setting

This study employed a cross-sectional design to examine the determinants of BCG vaccine coverage among Somali children aged 0–2 months. Secondary data were utilized from the 2020 SDHS, obtained from the Somalia National Bureau of Statistics (SNBS). The SDHS is a nationally representative survey that collects comprehensive information on reproductive health, demographic trends, and socio-economic indicators. It was conducted by the SNBS in collaboration with the Federal Government Ministry of Health of Somalia (FGMOH) and funded by the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA). The survey covered selected districts across both urban and rural areas, with data gathered from public health facilities and community settlements. These sources provided reliable and regionally comparable information on BCG vaccination coverage and its associated factors across Somalia.

Study population

The target population consisted of mothers or primary caregivers of infants aged 0–2 months residing in the selected areas during the data collection period. Caregivers were eligible if they lived in the district for at least six months and provided informed consent. Infants with severe illness at the time of the visit or households unavailable after repeated attempts were excluded.

Data source and sampling procedures

This study utilized secondary data from the 2020 SDHS. The SDHS employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design. In the first stage, primary sampling units (PSUs) were selected. In the second stage, households were systematically selected from these clusters. Our analysis was restricted to a sub-sample of 457 mother-infant dyads where the infant was aged 0–2 months.

Variables of the study

The dependent variable was BCG Vaccination Status, coded as a binary outcome: 1 if the child received the BCG vaccine and 0 if not. Additionally, the explanatory factors for the study were chosen retroactively from the body of existing literature and the variables that were present in the dataset. These include the mother’s age group, wealth quantile, mother’s highest education level, place of residence, usage, and intention of contraceptives, utilization of antenatal care and health-facility delivery within the last year, distance to the health facility, distance to the health facility, frequency of radio listening, and ever had vaccinations were therefore determined to be the most frequently used.18

Data analysis method

Using the 2020 SDHS dataset, the study employed descriptive and inferential statistics to assess BCG vaccination coverage among Somali children aged 0–2 months, analyzed in STATA 17. Descriptive statistics summarized vaccination status using frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests (p < .05) examined associations at the bivariate level, while multivariate logistic regression, using odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals, identified factors independently linked to BCG vaccination. This analysis aimed to enhance understanding of maternal knowledge and determinants influencing BCG uptake.

Ethical statement of the study

The data used in this study were obtained through formal request with the SNBS. Permission was granted by SNBS to access and utilize the relevant SDHS data on BCG vaccine coverage. Throughout the data acquisition and analysis process, strict measures were followed to ensure privacy and confidentiality, with all personal identifiers removed and no individually identifiable information disclosed.

Maternal education emerged as a strong positive predictor; mothers with a primary education had significantly higher odds of vaccinating their children (OR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.25–5.49) compared to those with no formal education. Although mothers with secondary education showed a high odds ratio (OR = 3.77), the wide confidence interval (95% CI: 0.83–17.03) indicates this association is not statistically significant at the 5% level.

Wealth quintiles and contraceptive intentions did not show statistically significant associations in the multivariable model, as all of their 95% confidence intervals crossed 1.0. However, healthcare utilization proved crucial: mothers who did not visit a health facility in the last 12 months had significantly lower odds of their infant receiving the BCG vaccine (OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.21–0.80). Lastly, self-reported lack of distance barriers to health facilities suggested slightly higher odds of vaccination, but this association remained statistically non-significant (OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 0.84–2.52).

Discussion

This study aimed to identify the determinants of BCG vaccination among Somali infants aged 0–2 months using national survey data. We found that approximately 79.7% of infants in this age group had received the BCG vaccine. While this is higher than the 37.0% national “full immunization” rate reported for older children, it still indicates that one in five infants misses this critical birth dose. Key determinants included place of residence, maternal education, and recent health facility utilization.9 Place of residence was found to be a significant determinant of BCG vaccination coverage.

However, the stark disparity observed among nomadic populations (OR = 0.18) reflects unique socio-cultural and structural challenges in the Somali context. Nomadic communities are frequently on the move, making them difficult to reach via fixed-site immunization programs designed for sedentary populations. Furthermore, socio-cultural reliance on traditional healing practices and a documented lack of trust in formal healthcare systems during transit often result in missed opportunities for neonatal vaccination.

Previous research in Somalia suggested that residence was not always significantly associated with immunization,19 but our findings emphasize that for the BCG birth dose, the nomadic lifestyle presents a profound barrier to early-life healthcare access. The low coverage among nomadic populations (OR = 0.18) reflects the unique socio-cultural and structural challenges in Somalia. Nomadic communities are frequently on the move, making them difficult to reach via fixed-site immunization programs. Furthermore, cultural reliance on traditional healing and a lack of trust in formal healthcare systems during transit often result in missed opportunities for neonatal vaccination.

Furthermore, geographical location within Somalia plays a role; studies have noted that East and North West Somalia often report poorer BCG vaccination rates.20 This may be attributed to the fact that these areas are often border territories with limited infrastructure, making the distribution of temperature-sensitive vaccines difficult. Residents in these remote zones are frequently less educated and live far from medical facilities, exacerbating variations in service utilization.21

Maternal education significantly boosted the likelihood of child immunization. In this study, mothers with a secondary level of education were 3.766 times more inclined to administer the BCG vaccine to their children compared to those with no formal education. This aligns with extensive literature identifying maternal education as a primary predictor of immunization completion.20,22–24 Literate mothers typically possess a greater awareness of vaccine-preventable diseases and a better understanding of the long-term benefits of preventive care, allowing them to navigate the healthcare system more effectively.

The study also identified a link between reproductive health choices and infant immunization. Non-users of contraceptives and those with no intention to use them had lower BCG coverage, suggesting that families disengaged from maternal health services (like family planning) are also less likely to access neonatal services. Finally, utilizing antenatal care and health-facility delivery in the previous 12 months was a strong positive predictor of vaccination. This highlights that any contact with the formal health system serves as a gateway for health education and service delivery.25

Overall, the multivariate analysis underscores that BCG coverage in Somalia is not merely a matter of vaccine availability, but is deeply tied to sociodemographic and socio-cultural factors. To close the 20.35% gap, Somalia needs targeted, culturally sensitive initiatives – particularly mobile outreach for nomadic communities – that address mistrust and integrate immunization with other maternal health services. Improving access to education and encouraging regular health facility engagement are critical measures for protecting Somali children from the high burden of tuberculosis.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strength lies in the use of a nationally representative dataset (SDHS), providing a broad overview of immunization trends across Somalia. However, several limitations must be noted. First, as a cross-sectional study using secondary data, we cannot establish direct causality. Second, vaccination status was partly based on maternal recall when vaccination cards were unavailable, which may introduce recall bias. Finally, the analysis did not account for seasonal migrations of nomadic groups, which may impact healthcare access at different times of the year.

Conclusion

The analysis of determinants of BCG vaccine coverage among Somali children aged 0–2 months in Somalia revealed several influential factors. The multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that type of place of residence, maternal education level, contraceptive use, and visiting a health facility in the last 12 months were significant determinants of vaccine coverage. Specifically, rural and nomadic areas had lower coverage, highlighting the need for targeted interventions in these communities. Mothers with primary education had higher coverage compared to those with no education, emphasizing the role of education in promoting vaccination.

In developing nations, tuberculosis (TB) is a highly contagious disease, and immunization against it using BCG is a critical strategy in mitigating its high burden. Interventions aimed at lowering the prevalence of tuberculosis in Somalia should prioritize and focus on high-risk areas with low BCG vaccination rates. These findings provide valuable insights for policymakers and public health professionals to design interventions that address the specific barriers and improve BCG vaccine coverage among Somali children aged 0–2 months.
