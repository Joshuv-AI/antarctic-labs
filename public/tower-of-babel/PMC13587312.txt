The SHH/GLI target Netrin-1 supports self-renewal in medulloblastoma tumor cells
Authors: P. Falcón-Urrutia, E. Fernández-Caniulén, J. M. Zúñiga-Hernández, B. Gaete-Ramírez, C.P. Prieto, S. M. Pfister, M. Kool, U. Schüller, B. Wainwright, M. Remke, M.D. Taylor, F. Alvarez-Astudillo, R. Armisén, M. Varas-Godoy, V. Palma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deregulation of the Sonic Hedgehog (SHH)/GLI signaling pathway has been strongly implicated in carcinogenesis, particularly in brain and skin tumors, through mutations affecting various pathway components. Given its central role in tumor development, the SHH/GLI axis has emerged as a key target for therapeutic intervention, underscoring the need to identify GLI-specific targets in cancer cells. Netrin-1, a multifunctional signaling molecule with pro-oncogenic properties, is overexpressed in diverse tumor types. While its tumor-promoting functions are well documented, the regulatory mechanisms governing its expression remain poorly understood. Here, we provide through gene expression profiling and in vitro and ex vivo analyses evidence demonstrating that Netrin-1 (NTN1) is a direct transcriptional target of canonical SHH signaling. We further show that in conditions of SHH pathway hyperactivation—such as in SHH-driven medulloblastoma (MB) NTN1 is significantly upregulated. This overexpression promotes the self-renewal of MB stem-like cells through the modulation of ERK signaling, thereby contributing to enhanced cancer stemness. Our findings identify NTN1 as a novel downstream effector of the SHH/GLI pathway and demonstrate that quantifying NTN1 levels can refine molecular classification of SHH-subtype medulloblastomas—distinguishing tumors with high SHH pathway activation and enhanced stem-like properties, with potential implications for prognosis and targeted therapy selection. Introduction

Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) signaling is one of the key pathways governing vertebrate development, regulating a wide range of processes, including stem cell niche establishment and organogenesis [1–4]. In the Central Nervous System (CNS), SHH is the only Hedgehog member expressed, acting as a potent morphogen during neural tube patterning, and playing a mitogenic role in dorsal brain expansion [5–8]. Specifically, SHH serves as the main mitogenic signal during postnatal cerebellum growth, targeting granular neural progenitors (GNPs). These GNPs actively proliferate in the external granular layer (EGL), a temporary germinative neuroepithelium located at the subpial surface of the developing cerebellum [9, 10]. Once GNPs become unresponsive to SHH, they exit the cell cycle and migrate inward, guided by Bergman glial filaments, to form the internal granule layer, their final destination [2].

Growth factors that regulate the proliferation and maintenance of stem cells and progenitors are critical for maintaining a homeostatic proliferative balance. When this balance is disrupted, it can lead to malignancy. Aberrant activation of the SHH pathway has been linked to the induction of a cancer stem/progenitor cell phenotype. For example, persistent activation of the canonical SHH pathway in GNPs results in the development of SHH-driven medulloblastoma (MB), the most common pediatric primary CNS tumor [11–13]. SHH-subtype MB accounts for around 30% of all MB cases and has a bimodal incidence, predominantly affecting infants (< 3 years) and adults (> 16 years), while contributing less to childhood tumors (4–15 years) [14]. Clinically, SHH MB exhibits several histological phenotypes, with the desmoplastic/nodular subtype being the most representative. While most of the patients with SHH MB have relatively good outcomes, the toxicity of post-surgical treatments for MB, especially conventional radiotherapy, can lead to severe side effects, such as an IQ decrease when 36 Gy of craniospinal irradiation (CSI) is administered [15]. Therefore, there is a pressing need for new, targeted therapies that not only improve the survival rate but also enhance the life quality for affected patients [16].

Activation of the canonical SHH pathway begins when the SHH ligand binds to its transmembrane receptor PATCHED1 (PTCH1), relieving its inhibitory effect on the co-receptor SMOOTHENED (SMO). This triggers an intracellular cascade that culminates in the transcriptional regulation of target genes via the glioma-associated oncogene (GLI) downstream effectors. The GLI family (GLI1-3) comprises zinc-finger transcriptional factors that can either activate or repress target genes depending on the signaling context, with GLI1 uniquely acting as a transcriptional activator, amplifying pathway activity [17]. GLI proteins bind to the consensus 9-mer DNA sequence –GACCACCCA- in the regulatory regions of their target genes, though variants with lower affinity but significant transcriptional activation have also been reported [18]. SHH/GLI signaling involves feed-forward and feedback loops, such as GLI1 regulating PTCH1 and auto-regulating its own expression. Activator forms GLI2 and GLI3 drive GLI1 transcription, initiating a feed-forward loop in which GLI1 amplifies its own expression. The importance of the so-called GLI code- the combinatorial action of GLI1-3 - is underscored by genetic or epigenetic changes that lead to aberrant activation of GLI, driving oncogenesis [19]. A genome-wide survey of GLI1 GLI-binding sites (GBS) revealed numerous locations in both normal and malignant tissues. However, the expression patterns differed significantly between normal and tumor cells [20].

Given the broad biological significance of the SHH pathway in development and disease, identifying novel GLI targets is crucial for understanding how this pathway integrates its effects through fine-tuned mechanisms. This is especially relevant in cancer, where targeting specific signaling pathways for drug therapy is taking center stage, making the identification of GLI-specific targets in tumor cells essential.

Netrin-1 (encoded by NTN1) is the most well-characterized member of the laminin-related glycoprotein Netrin family, which includes Netrins 1–4 [21]. First discovered in a C. elegans screening (ortholog Unc-6) [22, 23], Netrin-1 was initially identified as an axonal guidance protein [24, 25]. However, it has been implicated in a wide range of cellular processes, including acting as a survival factor via interactions with the DCC/NEOGENIN1 (NEO-1) and UNC5s dependence receptor families, which trigger apoptosis in the absence of their ligands [26]. Netrin-1 also plays roles in cell survival [27], angiogenesis [28], cell adhesion [29], migration [30], and immune modulation [31–33].

Beyond its physiological roles, Netrin-1 is over-expressed in several cancers, including glioblastoma [34], neuroblastoma [26], MB [35], breast cancer [36], and gastric cancer [37]. In these contexts, Netrin-1 acts as a survival factor, inhibiting the apoptotic signaling generated by its receptors via autocrine secretion [38], and promoting cell migration [39] and invasion [40]. Delloye-Bourgeois et al. reported a truncated intranuclear form of Netrin-1(ΔN-Netrin-1), lacking the first part of the N-terminal domain IV, which stimulates cell proliferation by interacting with nucleolar proteins and ribosomal DNA promoters [41]. The ΔN-Netrin-1 truncated isoform promoter was found to be NF-κB dependent. Consequently, in cancers like pancreatic adenocarcinoma, gastric cancer, ovarian cancer, and brain metastases, the overexpression of Netrin-1 and ΔN-Netrin-1 has been associated with a poor prognosis [42–47].

Recent research highlights a new role for Netrin-1 in maintaining stemness in both development and cancer contexts [48–52]. Netrin-1 modulates cancer cell plasticity [49] and promotes stemness characteristics [34]. In glioblastoma, where cancer stem cells are well-characterized [53], Netrin-1 induces stemness via Notch pathway activation [34]. Blocking Netrin-1 in glioblastoma stem cells inhibits tumor growth, invasion, and angiogenesis [51].

Given the diverse biological roles of Netrin-1 across contexts, it is important to understand the mechanisms that regulate its expression. Although Netrin-1 is known as a transcriptional target of the NF-κB pathway [54], the developmental signaling that controls Netrin-1 expression remains unclear. However, there is consistent evidence that Netrin-1 is a direct transcriptional target of the SHH pathway. Netrin-1 is expressed in areas under strong SHH morphogenetic signaling, such as the neural tube floor plate [55]. Furthermore, Netrin-1 in the ventral most of the spinal cord is lost in the Gli2 mutant mice [56]. Netrin-1, like SHH, has also been reported in neurogenic niches in vivo in both embryonic and adult neural stem and progenitor cells [57]. In the postnatal cerebellum, Netrin-1 is strongly expressed in the EGL from P0 until P10, during murine development [58]. By performing global profiling of gene expression in different SHH/Gli gain- and loss-of-function scenarios and others, we established the Netrin-1 homolog ntn1a as a potential new SHH target in zebrafish [59, 60]. Also, Podjaski et al. demonstrated that modulation of the canonical SHH pathway induces NTN1 up-regulation in human brain-derived endothelial cells [61].

Here, we demonstrate through gene expression profiling and in vitro and ex vivo functional analyses that Netrin-1 is a direct transcriptional target of the SHH/GLI pathway. Furthermore, we provide evidence that Netrin-1 supports the self-renewal of MB stem-like cells in DAOY, a widely used in vitro model of SHH-driven medulloblastoma, supporting its potential role in desmoplastic SHH-MB. Since MB consists mainly of undifferentiated stem- or progenitor-like cells, our findings offer new insights into therapeutic targeting for SHH-driven desmoplastic MB by interfering with Netrin-1.

Materials and methods

Gene expression profiling studies

MB and healthy brain expression data correspond to a compilation of 23 transcriptomic datasets reported under the GEO accession number GSE124814 and published in [62]. The data were analyzed and obtained through the genomics analysis and visualization platform R2 (https://hgserver1.amc.nl/).

In silico analysis

GLI1 binding sites in Netrin-1 gene elements: GLI1, GLI2 and GLI3- DNA binding site consensus sequences were retrieved from HOCOMOCO v11 database. To scan TF binding sites, the NTN1 gene promoter sequence elements were recovered from Ensembl (Human genome version GRCh38). The scanning was performed using FIMO (Find Individual Motif Occurrences) tool using the default background model [63].

Human medulloblastoma samples

The samples were obtained through collaborations with the German Cancer Research Center (Heidelberg, Germany), the Center for Neuropathology, Ludwig-Maximilians University (Munich, Germany), and the Hospital for Sick Children and the Brain Tumor Research Centre (Toronto, Canada). These samples had been molecularly characterized by neuropathologists in the respective histopathology core laboratories, following standard histology and Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining procedures (Supplementary Table 1). In compliance with internal regulations and national guidelines, all protocols were approved by the Bioethics Committees of the respective institutions (Certificate #1764-FCS-UCH), including the Faculty of Sciences and the Faculty of Medicine of the University of Chile and the Bioethics Committee of the National Fund for Science and Technology (FONDECYT, ANID).

Mouse models

All the experimental procedures involving the use of mice were approved and followed the guidelines of the University of Chile Ethics Committee (Certificate #1764-FCS-UCH).

GNP cell isolation

Primary GNP cultures were prepared from the postnatal cerebellum (P6) of mice according to the procedures described in [9].

Immunostaining

Samples were deparaffinated by incubation at 60 °C for one hour, followed by washing in xylol, and rehydration through a graded series of ethanol solutions. After rehydration, immunohistochemistry was performed on tissue sections according to established protocols [64]. The following antibodies were used: anti-Netrin-1 1/400 (#H-104, Santa Cruz), anti-β-III tubulin 1/1000 (#T8660, Sigma), anti-PCNA 1/100 (#13-3900, Invitrogen), anti-Sox2 1/200 (#ab97959, ABCAM) and TOPRO 1/200 (#T3605, Invitrogen). Standard optimized procedures were applied to enhance antigen retrieval, primary antibody dilution, and other relevant factors. Stained sections were dehydrated in xylol and mounted with Entellan. Confocal imaging was conducted using a Zeiss LSM 510 META, and fluorescence microscopy was performed with an Olympus BX-51 microscope.

Cell culture

The DAOY human medulloblastoma cell line (#HTB-186, ATCC), which is predicted to be the SHH subtype, was cultured in high glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone), penicillin 10,000 U/mL and 10,000 µg/mL streptomycin. HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS, 10,000 U/mL penicillin, and 10,000 µg/mL streptomycin, following standard procedures [35].

ChIP, chromatin immunoprecipitation

The ChIP assay was performed as described by Soutoglou & Talianidis [71], with minor modifications. Nuclear extracts were obtained by sonication of DAOY cells, producing chromatin fragments between 200 and 500 bp. For each immunoprecipitation, 25 µg of sheared chromatin was incubated with 5 µg of antibody against GLI1 (# NB600-600, Novus Biologicals), GLI2 (# ab26056, ABCAM) or GLI3 (# H-280, Santa Cruz). A histone H3 antibody (#ab1791, ABCAM) served as a positive control, whereas normal Rabbit IgG (#sc-2027, Santa Cruz) was used as the negative immunoprecipitation control. DNA recovered from the immune complexes was quantified by SYBR Green qPCR, using 1µL of the INPUT and 5µL of each IP sample. The reaction parameters were set using an INPUT concentration curve. An independent positive-control ChIP targeting the human PTCH1 promoter verified assay performance.

Luciferase assay

The transcriptional contribution of the proximal NTN1 promoter (–2 kb upstream of the transcription-start site) was analyzed in DAOY medulloblastoma cells using a dual-luciferase system. The − 2 kb fragment containing the wild-type GLI-binding site 1 (GBS1) was PCR-amplified and inserted upstream of the Firefly Luciferase gene in pGL3-Basic. A derivative construct in which the core GBS1 motif was mutated (hereafter Mut GBS1) was generated by site-directed mutagenesis to abolish GLI binding. DAOY cells (1 × 10^5 cells per well, 12-well plates) were transfected with Lipofectamine Plus (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For transactivation assays with the wild-type reporter, cells were co-transfected with 500 ng of NTN1-Luc reporter, 50 ng of the pRL-TK Renilla plasmid (internal control), and either 150 or 300 ng of GLI1 or GLI3A expression plasmids, or an empty vector (control). For mutation analysis, only GLI1 (300 ng) was co-transfected with either the wild-type or Mut GBS1 reporter.

After 3–5 h in serum-free medium, cells were returned to complete growth medium, incubated for 48 h and harvested. Lysates were prepared with Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega), and Firefly and Renilla activities were quantified sequentially using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (E1960, Promega) on an Infinite® M1000 Pro plate reader (Tecan). Relative luciferase units (RLU) were calculated as the Firefly/Renilla ratio and normalized to the empty-vector control.

As a positive control for GLI-mediated transcription, cells were co-transfected with GLI1 and GLI3A along with a GLI-responsive luciferase plasmid containing eight tandem GLI-binding sites (8xGBS-Luc; GBS, specific GLI Binding Site) [65].

RNA extraction and reverse transcription

Total RNA was extracted in a single step using the TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen) and stored at -80 °C until further processing. Total RNA (1 µg) was reverse transcribed using the iScript ™ cDNA Synthesis kit (BioRad), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

SYBR green real-time qPCR assays

Real-Time qPCR was performed using the Brilliant II SYBR® Green QPCR Master Mix kit and the Stratagene Mx3000P real-time PCR system (Agilent Technologies). Data analysis was conducted using MxPro Software, with relative quantification determined by the comparative Ct method using β-actin as the internal parameter. Genes and primers are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Recombinant SHH treatment

DAOY cells or GNPs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells per well. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with EMEM containing 0.5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and treated with either 0.1% BSA (vehicle control) or 20 nM recombinant SHH (R&D). After the treatment period (24–48 h), the cells were washed with PBS 1X, trypsinized, and trypsinization was halted by adding two volumes of complete medium. The cells were then centrifuged to obtain a pellet, which was either stored at -20 °C or processed for protein or RNA extraction.

Quantitative PCR based on Taqman probes

The reaction was carried out with specific primers in a LightCycler 480 apparatus (Roche) using the LightCycler TaqMan Master Kit (Roche) following the manufacturer''s instructions. In the case of human samples such as DAOY cells, the normalization control was β-actin, whereas for murine samples such as GNP cells, the normalization control was Hprt. Fluorescence was measured using LightCycler 4.05 software. The Ct values for each point were obtained both for the normalizing gene and the genes evaluated in each experiment. The amount of target transcripts, normalized against the housekeeping gene, was calculated using the comparative Ct method [61]. The genes and Taqman primers are detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

Western blot

For Western blot analysis, 40–70 µg of total proteins was loaded and separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE system. Proteins were transferred to 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes using the Transblot semi-dry transfer system (Biorad). Blocking was performed with 5% skim milk in TBS for 1 h, followed by incubation with the primary antibodies at 4 ° C for 1 h. After three washes with TBS-Tween, the membrane was incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody for 3 h. The membrane was washed three times for 10 min and revealed with the chemiluminescent reagent (Pierce) using the SYNGENE G: BOX equipment (Synoptics Ltd). Primary antibodies used were anti-NTN-1 1/400 (# AF6419, R&D), anti-SOX2 1/200 (#ab97959, ABCAM), anti-β-actin 1/1000 (#A5316, Sigma-Aldrich), anti-ERK1/2 1/1000 (#4695, Cell Signaling), anti-p-ERK1/2 1/1000 (#4370, Cell Signaling). The secondary antibodies used were anti-sheep HRP 1/1000 (#HAF016, R&D), anti-mouse HRP 1/2000 (#7076, Cell Signaling), and anti-rabbit HRP 1/1000 (#7074, Cell Signaling). Quantifications were performed using integrated density analysis with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Lentiviral transduction and stable Netrin-1 knockdown cell line generation

To generate a stable Netrin-1 knockdown line, the CRISPRi plasmid pLVhU6-sgRNAhUbC-dCas9-KRAB-T2a-Puro (#71236, Addgene) was used. Guide RNAs targeting the NTN1 promoter (sg1NTN1, sg2NTN1) and control guide (sgCTL) were designed using CHOPCHOP and cloned into the plasmid. HEK293T cells were triple-transfected with psPAX2 (#12260, Addgene), pCMV-VSV-g (#8454, Addgene), and pLVhU6-sgRNAhUbC-dCas9-KRAB-T2a-Puro using the CaCl2 transfection method [66]. Viral supernatants were collected 60 h post-transfection, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, filtered through a 0.45 μm filter and applied in a 1:1 ratio of culture media to DAOY cells. Stable lines were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL), and knockdown efficiency was evaluated by RT-qPCR, Western blot, and immunofluorescence.

Tumorsphere formation assay

DAOY sgCTL and sg2NTN1 cells, were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with epidermal growth factor (EGF; Invitrogen; 20 ng/mL), basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; Gibco; 20 ng/mL), insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; 5 µg/mL) and B-27 Supplement (Gibco; 1X). Cells were seeded at 1 × 103 cells/mL in ultra-low attachment 96-well plates and cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cultured medium was added every other day. After 7 days, tumorsphere formation was imaged using a Zeiss Axiovert S100 phase-contrast microscope. Sphere number and size were quantified using Zen 3.2 and ImageJ software.

Tumorsphere formation was also assessed in sgCTL and sg2NTN1 DAOY cells supplemented with human recombinant Netrin-1 (6419-N1, R&D), added every other day at 200 ng/mL. Similarly, ERK inhibition was performed with the selective ATP-competitive ERK1/2 inhibitor LY3214996, MedChemExpress) at 1µM [67].

Flow cytometry analysis

DAOY cells (1 × 105) were cultured in 6-well plates and harvested 24 h later by trypsinization with Trypsin-EDTA (0.25%; Gibco). Cells were resuspended in PBS with 1% BSA and 1 mM EDTA and incubated with 5 µL of CD24-PE antibody (# 130-098-861, Miltenyi Biotec) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After washing, cells were analyzed on a FACS CantoII (Becton Dickinson). Doublets and dead cells were excluded by gating using FSC and SSC.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Prism 5 software. A Shapiro-Wilk normality test was performed to assess the data distribution. For non-parametric data, a Kruskal-Wallis test was used for groups larger than 8, and the Mann-Whitney test for smaller groups. Parametric data were analyzed by ANOVA.

Results

NTN1 overexpression in SHH-driven MB

MB has been classified into four molecular groups: WNT, SHH, Group 3, and Group 4, based on DNA methylation and/or transcriptional profiling, as established by an international panel of experts [68, 69]. In our study, we conducted extensive transcriptomic meta-analyses using a cohort of 291 normal cerebellum samples and 1213 human MB samples distributed across these four molecular groups: WNT (112), SHH (390), Group 3 (222), and Group 4 (489). Our analyses revealed elevated levels of transcriptional expression of NTN1 in SHH-driven MBs compared to the normal cerebellum and MB Groups 3 and 4 (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 4). Interestingly, NTN1 also showed high transcriptional levels in the WNT-MB subgroup. Additionally, we examined the expression profiles of the known SHH target genes, GLI1 and PTCH1, both of which were overexpressed in the SHH-MB group (Fig. 1B and C, Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). These findings suggest that Netrin-1 is a potential SHH signaling target, with the possibility of defining a distinct NTN1-high subgroup within SHH-driven MB. Notably, this may intersect with SHH subtypes as defined by the WHO classification (e.g., TP53-mutant vs. TP53-wildtype), warranting further investigation.

Netrin-1 expression in MB tumor cells

Histologically, MB manifests in four variants: classic, anaplastic/ large cell, nodular /desmoplastic and MB with extensive nodularity [70]. The latter two are found exclusively in the SHH-driven MB, with nodular/desmoplastic being the most common within this group. This histological variant is characterized by neural differentiation within nodules (Fig. 1D, black asterisk), and active proliferation in the internodular zones (Fig. 1D, black arrow). Consequently, we analyzed the expression and distribution of Netrin-1 protein across medulloblastoma molecular groups, comparing SHH with non-SHH tumors (WNT, Group 3, and Group 4). Immunostaining revealed that most Netrin-1-positive cells are present in these internodular zones (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table 7), colocalizing with the proliferation marker PCNA in proliferating tumor cells (Fig. 1D). Conversely, no significant correlation with the neural differentiation marker TUJ1 (β-III tubulin) was observed (Fig. 1D). As a positive control of active SHH/GLI signaling, we analyzed the presence of SOX-2 [71], a cancer stem cell marker [72, 73]. Accordingly, SOX-2 distribution was predominantly localized in the internodular regions (Fig. 1D). This pattern further supports the expression of Netrin-1 in SHH-driven MB tumor cells, with no detectable Netrin-1 expression in non-SHH MB, as illustrated by a representative Group 4 MB sample (Fig. 1D).

Two intracellular Netrin-1 distribution patterns were noted in SHH- driven MB: nuclear-cytoplasmic and cytoplasmic (Supplementary Fig. S2). Interestingly, the nuclear-cytoplasmic pattern was also observed in the human MB cell line DAOY (Supplementary Fig. S3, see also Supplementary Fig. S4C), consistent with the presence of ΔN-Netrin-1. This isoform localizes to the nucleolus, where it interacts with nucleolar proteins and ribosomal DNA promoter sequences, promoting cell proliferation [41].

Taken together, our results indicate that Netrin-1 expression is predominantly associated with SHH-driven MB tumor cells, though low levels of expression were also observed in some WNT-MB tumors (data not shown).

GLI effectors bind to NTN1 regulatory regions

To determine whether NTN1 is a direct transcriptional target of the SHH/GLI pathway, we first performed an in-silico analysis using position weight matrices (PWMS) from the HOCOMOCO v11 database to identify putative GBSs within the NTN1 promoter (Fig. 2A). This analysis revealed three candidate GBSs (GBS 1–3), located within a 2-kb proximal region upstream of the NTN1 transcription start site (Fig. 2B). Interestingly, an E-box motif was also found in proximity to GBS1, suggesting potential regulatory crosstalk. Notably, the NTN1 gene also contains putative GBSs in intronic regulatory element 2 (GBS 4 and 5), suggesting that SHH/GLI signaling may regulate NTN1 transcription through this element (Fig. 2B Supplementary Table 8). To assess the statistical strength of these predicted GBSs, we generated a heatmap of –log₁₀(p-values) corresponding to PWM matches across the promoter region. Among the three sites, GBS1 displayed the highest PWM score across GLI1, GLI2, and GLI3 matrices, highlighting it as a strong candidate for functional GLI-mediated regulation (Fig. 2C). To experimentally validate GLI protein binding to GBS1, we conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays in DAOY medulloblastoma cells using antibodies against GLI1, GLI2, and GLI3. The canonical SHH target gene PTCH1 was used as a positive control to confirm GLI2 and GLI3 binding (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Additional ChIP controls included IgG and histone H3 precipitations for both PTCH1 and NTN1 promoter regions (Supplementary Fig. S5B–C). At the NTN1 promoter, significant enrichment of GLI3 was detected at the GBS1 site compared to the IgG control (Fig. 2D). On the other hand, immunoprecipitation with antibodies against either GLI1 or GLI2 did not show significant differences. Notably, the lack of endogenous GLI1 enrichment at GBS1 does not rule out this site''s responsiveness to GLI1, as shown by the luciferase assays described below. Finally, given the intronic position of GBS5, we evaluated the possible role of this sequence as a transcriptional enhancer. For this purpose, we performed ChIP analysis using antibodies against post-translational modifications of histones associated with transcriptionally activated enhancers, such as H3K4Met1 and H3K27Ac. Interestingly, GBS5 was amplified when immunoprecipitating with both antibodies, revealing statistical significance compared to the IgG control (Fig. 2E).

GLI1 activates NTN1 transcription through GBS1

To assess the functional significance of the identified GBS1 within the NTN1 promoter, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays in DAOY cells. A 2-kb fragment of the NTN1 proximal promoter containing GBS1 was cloned upstream of the Firefly luciferase gene in the pGL3-Basic vector. A mutated version of GBS1 (Mut GBS1), in which the core GLI-binding motif was disrupted, was generated by site-directed mutagenesis. Each construct was co-transfected with a Renilla luciferase plasmid to control for transfection efficiency. Reporter assays were carried out in the presence of either GLI1, a potent transcriptional activator, or a constitutively active form of GLI3 (GLI3A). As a positive control for GLI-mediated transcription, a synthetic reporter containing eight tandem GLI-binding sites (8xGBS-Luc) was co-transfected with GLI1 or GLI3A, resulting in robust luciferase activation (Supplementary Fig. S5D), confirming the activity of the GLI constructs. Co-transfection of the wild-type NTN1 reporter with either GLI1 or GLI3A resulted in dose-dependent increases in luciferase activity, with GLI1 eliciting a significantly stronger transactivation compared to GLI3A (Fig. 2F). Importantly, mutation of GBS1 completely abolished GLI1-mediated transcriptional activation, demonstrating that this site is necessary for NTN1 promoter responsiveness to GLI1 (Fig. 2G).

Interestingly, sequence analysis revealed the presence of an E-box motif located at − 935 bp relative to the transcription start site, in close proximity to GBS1 (Fig. 2B). E-box motifs are typically recognized by basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors and have been shown to cooperate with SHH/GLI signaling components during oncogenic transformation of GNPs [20].

These results, in conjunction with our ChIP-qPCR data, provide functional evidence that GLI1 directly engages GBS1 to activate NTN1 transcription, and suggest a context-dependent interplay between GLI1 and nearby E-box-binding factors in regulating NTN1 expression.

NTN1 responds to SHH/GLI signaling modulation

We next assessed the impact of SHH/GLI pathway modulation on NTN1 expression in DAOY cells. Treatment with recombinant SHH for 24 h induces a significant increase in GLI1 and NTN1 levels, compared to the control condition (Fig. 3A and B). These confirm that NTN1 is a SHH/GLI transcriptional target, with implications for tumorigenesis when its signaling is dysregulated. Given that medulloblastoma is a neoplasia that originates from genetic abnormalities of the SHH pathway acting on cerebellar progenitors, we also sought to evaluate the SHH signaling regulatory role on mNetrin-1 expression in an ex vivo approach. In the extension of our study, 24 and 48-hour activation of the Shh/Gli pathway on wildtype (WT) mice GNP led to a significant increase in transcriptional levels of mNetrin-1 and mGli1 (Supplementary Fig. S6A and S6B).

Taken together in vitro and ex vivo data reported here support that Netrin-1 is a direct transcriptional target of SHH/GLI signaling in the context of cerebellar development as well as in cancer, suggesting that Netrin-1 signaling function and control play a pivotal role during the development, while altered might contribute to cell-death signaling dysregulation, consequently giving rise to neoplasia. Likewise, these pieces of evidence, both in mouse models and in human samples, suggest that signaling regulation is conserved between mammals.

Netrin-1 supports the self-renewal of MB stem-like cells

Given that Netrin-1 is expressed in proliferative, poorly differentiated areas of desmoplastic MB, we investigated its role in cancer stem cell maintenance. Using CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) to knockdown NTN1 in DAOY cells, we verified the efficient downregulation of Netrin-1 (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. S4). Even though two different sequences (sgRNAs) downregulated the Netrin-1 transcript (Supplementary Fig. S4A), only sg2NTN1 substantially diminishes the Netrin-1 protein expression (Supplementary Fig. S4B-C), and hence this cell line was selected for subsequent experiments. Under tumorsphere inducing culture conditions, we found a significant reduction in tumorsphere formation in the sg2NTN1 cell line (Fig. 4B). However, the diameter of the spheres generated from the Netrin-1 knockdown cells was similar to the spheres generated from the control. Through FACS, we observed a decrease in CD24-positive MB progenitor cells [74] in Netrin-1 knockdown cells relative to the control cell line (Fig. 4C).

Supplementation with exogenous recombinant Netrin-1 (200 ng/ml) was insufficient to rescue the phenotype (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that Netrin-1 supports self-renewal of MB stem-like cells, without affecting their proliferation. Because an exclusively extracellular ligand failed to recover the knockdown phenotype, these data also strongly imply that MB stem-like self-renewal relies on the intracellular or intracrine functions of the Netrin-1 axis.

ERK signaling is involved in the effects of Netrin-1 on medulloblastoma stem-like cells

Interestingly, we verified that sg2NTN1 cells exhibit higher levels of ERK1/2 phosphorylation in comparison to the control (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S7). Netrin-1 was previously shown to maintain mESC self-renewal by activating the WNT pathway and inhibiting the MAPK pathway through ERK1/2 dephosphorylation/inactivation [48]. We therefore evaluated whether ERK1/2 activation was mechanistically involved in the reduced cancer-stem-cell population observed after Netrin-1 knockdown, treating both Netrin-1-knockdown and control DAOY cell lines with the MEK/ERK inhibitor LY3214996 (1 μM) every other day for one week. Inhibition of ERK recovered the significant reduction of tumorsphere formation observed in Netrin-1-knockdown DAOY cells (Fig. 4F), suggesting that Netrin-1 supports self-renewal of MB stem-like cells by modulating the MAPK pathway.

Taken together, our results demonstrate that Netrin-1 is a critical SHH/GLI target that promotes MB tumorigenesis by supporting the self-renewal of MB stem-like cells and modulating key developmental pathways. Thereby reinforcing the stem-like state and contributing to tumor persistence.

Discussion

Netrin-1 was originally identified as a key axonal guidance molecule [23]. However, the discovery that its receptor families also play roles in apoptosis modulation sparked a new research direction, focusing on Netrin-1’s involvement in cell survival. This property has garnered significant interest in cancer research, given its potential to modulate pro-apoptotic receptor signaling [75]. Consequently, investigations have expanded to explore the pleiotropic functions of Netrin-1, driven by its diverse receptor repertoire.

In the context of cancer, NF-κB has been implicated as a regulator of Netrin-1 gene expression, with evidence also suggesting involvement of the tumor suppressor p53 [38, 54]. Nonetheless, the developmental signaling pathways controlling Netrin-1 expression have remained elusive. Here, we demonstrate that the canonical SHH/GLI signaling pathway directly regulates Netrin-1 expression at the transcriptional level, particularly in SHH-driven MB. Furthermore, these findings raise the possibility of a distinct NTN1-defined subgroup within SHH-driven MB. Future studies should investigate whether this subgroup aligns with SHH subtypes delineated by the WHO classification, such as TP53-wildtype and TP53-mutant variants.

A recent study by Podjaski et al. revealed that canonical SHH signaling regulates Netrin-1 expression in human brain-derived endothelial cells, with implications for autoimmune CNS diseases due to its anti-inflammatory effects [61]. Given the well-established connection between chronic inflammation and cancer, we sought to determine whether Netrin-1 is a downstream effector of SHH signaling in MB. Our data show that activation of the canonical SHH pathway directly regulates Netrin-1 acting through a functional GBS1 in the NTN1 promoter region.

MB is the most common malignant pediatric brain tumor, yet despite extensive research into its molecular drivers, effective therapies remain elusive due to the tumor’s heterogeneous nature. MB consists of distinct molecular groups, each with unique clinical and molecular characteristics, which lead to varying clinical outcomes [70, 76]. Over the past decade, research has focused on functionally validating genes and molecular processes that have been implicated in human genomic studies. Identifying molecular biomarkers for early diagnosis and treatment stratification across MB groups is crucial for improving therapeutic outcomes [77].

SHH-driven MB represents an intermediate prognosis group. Germline mutations in genes encoding negative regulators of the SHH pathway, such as PTCH1 or suppressor of fused homologue (SUFU), lead to the development of SHH MB, as do somatic mutations and copy number alterations in pathway components, including in GLI2 [77–79]. We and other groups have identified transcriptional targets active during embryonic development and in cancer [79, 80]. In the cerebellum, these SHH targets include PTCH1, PTCH2, GLI1, N-MYC, CyclinD1, Bmi, Gpr153, Foxo6, YAP1, Neuropilin1 and NCOR2 [81]. Recently, we identified NEO1, a death receptor for Netrin-1, as a gene controlled by the SHH/GLI pathway in SHH-driven MB [82].

To further investigate NTN1 expression across MB groups, we performed a transcriptomic meta-analysis comparing expression levels in SHH-MB, WNT-MB, and normal cerebellum samples. This analysis revealed elevated NTN1 expression in both SHH-MB and WNT-MB compared to other MB groups and normal tissue. We propose that NTN1 overexpression in SHH-MB results from constitutive activation of the canonical SHH pathway, while overexpression of NTN1 in the WNT-MB group likely involves other regulatory mechanisms [38, 54]. Recent studies have linked NTN1 expression with WNT pathway activation, as demonstrated in various cellular contexts. Netrin-1 binding to UNC5B induces FAK-mediated inactivation of GSK3α/β in mouse embryonic stem cells, thereby stabilizing β-catenin [48]. Additionally, Boyé et al. showed that Netrin-1 interaction with UNC5B can activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling through LRP6 phosphorylation in endothelial cells, regulating blood-brain barrier integrity [83]. Subsequently, Furtado et al. demonstrated that Netrin-1 can regulate hematoretinal integrity through a similar mechanism, where Netrin-1 binding to UNC5B activates LRP5 phosphorylation and stabilizes β-catenin in endothelial cells [84]. Moreover, Xia et al. have recently shown that a Netrin-1/UNC5B-neogenin receptor axis can potentiate β-catenin expression, thereby amplifying Wnt signaling activity, in colorectal cancer [96]. These studies underscore the importance of studying Netrin-1 regulation and its diverse roles in both physiological and pathological contexts. Taken together, our results highlight prominent Netrin-1 expression in SHH-driven MB, with some expression also observed in WNT-subtype tumors, suggesting that its role outside the SHH context deserves future study.

It is important to note that all transcriptional and chromatin-based assays in this study were performed using DAOY cells. While acknowledging recent debates regarding the ontogeny of the DAOY line, we utilized it here as a well-characterized, SHH-responsive pediatric CNS model. Furthermore, its TP53-mutant status aligns with the SHH-activated TP53-mutant subtype defined by the WHO classification. While this genetic background may influence GLI1-mediated transcription and limit the generalizability of our findings to all SHH subtypes, it provides a relevant model for this high-risk category. Future studies should validate these results in additional models, including those representing SHH-TP53-wildtype tumors.

On the other hand, while in vitro studies often identify transcription factor binding to consensus sites, not all sequences exhibit the same activity in vivo [85]. Our analysis revealed a functional GBS in an NTN1 regulatory region, capable of transcriptional activation upon GLI induction, which aligns with in vivo data on NTN1 expression during development and tumor progression. GBS elements in both promoters and enhancers can act in a tissue-specific manner, depending on local GLI effector concentrations [86]. This explains how SHH-dependent Netrin-1 expression can be dynamically regulated by the same genetic elements in different contexts. Interestingly, both in vitro and in vivo evidence links the SHH/GLI signaling pathway with Netrin-1 modulation in a wide variety of species. Hence, the conservation of SHH/GLI-Netrin-1 signaling mechanisms across species suggests a conserved regulatory framework.

Sequencing studies in MB cohorts have revealed differential distribution of chromatin state markers—such as H3K4me—across molecular groups, particularly in genes with key biological functions [87]. The dynamic interplay between repressive (H3K27me) and activating (H3K4me) histone modifications at regulatory elements of neural lineage genes is critical for maintaining the progenitor state [87, 88]. Disruption of this chromatin balance can trigger cell differentiation through activation of pro-differentiation transcriptional programs. In this context, the NTN1 gene harbors putative intronic regulatory elements that are recognized by GLI transcription factors, suggesting a potential mechanism by which SHH/GLI signaling may regulate NTN1 expression. Furthermore, these intronic GBSs exhibit transcriptionally active enhancer marks—such as H3K4me1 and H3K27ac [89]—which would support their role as enhancer elements responsive to SHH/GLI signaling.

In addition to GBSs in the regulatory regions of NTN1, we identified an E-box sequence located 171 bp downstream of the GLI1-responding GBS (Fig. 2B). E-box sequences are recognized by bHLH factors and recent studies suggest that the E-box/bHLH transcriptional factors cooperate with GLI1 to regulate downstream SHH target genes during the transformation of GNPs to tumor cells (MB) [90–92]. One bHLH transcriptional factor strongly involved with cerebellum development is Atoh1, which controls cell-specification in the first rhombomere, the origin of the cerebellar neuronal population [93]. Notably, Atoh1 is essential for maintaining GNPs in an SHH-responsive state, inhibiting their differentiation [90–92]. Overexpression of Atoh1 in GNPs results in a significant increase in Netrin-1 expression (+ 2.16-fold change over the control) [90]. Furthermore, Atoh1 function is regulated downstream of Shh/Gli signaling [94], contributing to the up-regulation of Netrin-1 expression level in a Shh/GLI overactivated context. Atoh1 and Gli1 cooperate in the transformation of GNPs to MB-initiating precursor cells, promoting aggressive tumor growth in murine models [90, 91]. Clinical data also correlate Netrin-1 and ATOH1 expression levels in human MB [90]. This evidence suggests that GLI1 and ATOH1 may jointly regulate Netrin-1 transcription in MB, warranting further investigation.

The precise cell of origin for SHH MB remains debated. Given the heterogeneity within and between MB groups, several studies suggest the presence of subpopulations in SHH subtype MB, including CD15/SSEA1, stem cell-like CD133, and neural stem cell marker SOX2-enriched cell populations, pointing to a potential cancer stem/progenitor cell [72, 73]. Our data suggest the existence of a Netrin-1 positive tumor stem cell.

Netrin-1 has been established as a modulator of pluripotency in embryonic stem cells (ESCs), where it maintains cells in an undifferentiated, naïve state via NEO1 and UNC5B receptor activation [48]. Consistent with this role in promoting stem-like phenotypes, Netrin-1 has been implicated in the stemness, relapse, and drug resistance of breast and ovarian cancers [52] and most recently, in driving sphere formation in colorectal cancer [95, 96]. Based on this landscape, we explored the role of Netrin-1 in MB. Using CRISPRi, we demonstrated that Netrin-1 is required for tumorsphere formation in the SHH-MB DAOY cell line. Furthermore, Netrin-1 knockdown significantly reduced expression of CD24, a validated marker of MB-initiating neural progenitor cells [74], confirming that Netrin-1 is critical for maintaining the progenitor pool in MB. Importantly, the persistence of Netrin-1 expression in these tumorsphere assays without exogenous SHH supplementation highlights that NTN1 regulation in cancer is not strictly or exclusively dependent on the SHH pathway. While SHH is a known regulator, transcription in tumors is heavily influenced by alternative, SHH-independent mechanisms. For instance, NF-κB acts as a direct transcriptional activator of NTN1 in various tumor and inflammatory contexts [54]. Additionally, stress-response pathways involving p53 and its isoform Δ40p53 directly bind the NTN1 promoter to upregulate its expression and promote cell survival [97]. Furthermore, basal NTN1 expression levels are strongly governed by chromatin context and epigenetic regulation, such as DNA methylation [98]. Together, these intersecting inflammatory, stress-responsive, and epigenetic pathways likely maintain the robust Netrin-1 expression required to support cancer stem cell self-renewal even in the absence of active SHH supplementation [99].

While our data demonstrate a robust functional impairment of CSC traits following Netrin-1 knockdown, we noted that supplementation with exogenous recombinant Netrin-1 was insufficient to rescue the phenotype. We postulate that this is due to the complex autocrine and intracrine dynamics of the Netrin-1 signaling axis. First, Netrin-1 and its dependence receptors (e.g., NEO1, UNC5B) are tightly co-regulated via transcriptional positive feedback loops [97]. Prolonged knockdown of endogenous Netrin-1 likely leads to the secondary downregulation of its surface receptors, rendering the cells deaf to extracellular recombinant ligands. Second, Netrin-1 exhibits critical intracrine activity; specifically, truncated intracellular isoforms (such as ΔN-Netrin-1) localize to the nucleolus to drive cell proliferation and stemness independent of surface receptor engagement [41, 42]. Because recombinant Netrin-1 cannot cross the cell membrane to restore these depleted intracellular pools, our findings suggest that the intracellular/nuclear localizations of Netrin-1 are functionally indispensable for maintaining the CSC phenotype in this model.

Mechanistically, our data suggest that Netrin-1 could regulate this stemness by suppressing downstream ERK signaling. This finding stands in sharp contrast to the recently published mechanism in adult colorectal cancer, where Netrin-1 was shown to activate ERK to drive proliferation [96]. However, our observation strongly resonates with the embryonic nature of pediatric malignancies, which frequently co-opt developmental programs to maintain a primitive phenotype. Our results mirror the mechanism described by Huyghe et al. [48] in ESCs, where Netrin-1 prevents differentiation by recruiting PP2A to dephosphorylate and inhibit ERK1/2. By dampening ERK activity, which acts as a pro-differentiation signal in this developmental context, Netrin-1 effectively enforces a ‘differentiation block,’ locking these pediatric cancer cells in a high-potency, stem-like state.

Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of Netrin-1 via NP137, a monoclonal antibody that blocks Netrin-1, prevents cancer progression by selectively targeting cancer stem cells. NP137 is currently in phase I clinical trials for various solid tumors [100].

These findings underscore the central role of SHH/GLI signaling in sustaining stemness-like phenotypes across different cancer contexts, with Netrin-1 acting as a key mediator. Depending on the receptor or combination of receptors activated downstream, Netrin-1 promotes both cell survival and stemness features in MB. Our previous work demonstrated that NEO1 was highly expressed in SHH-driven MB [82]. However, the precise interaction between Netrin-1 and NEO1 in SHH-driven MB requires further investigation.

In the clinical context, survival rates for MB patients have reached approximately 70–80% due to conventional treatments, including surgery, craniospinal radiotherapy (for children over 3 years old), and chemotherapy. While these advancements represent significant progress, many survivors experience long-term adverse effects, including developmental, neurological, neuroendocrine, and psychosocial complications [101]. One of the most detrimental side effects of radiotherapy is the profound impact on patients’ cognitive development. This underscores the urgent need for molecular characterization of MB subgroups to facilitate the implementation of stratified, precision-guided therapies. Supporting this notion, a recent study showed that combining SMO inhibitors with anti–Netrin-1 produces a synergistic effect, effectively suppressing MB cell survival and tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo [102]. Our findings establish a mechanistic link between Netrin-1 and SHH-driven MB progression, GLI-dependent transcriptional activation of Netrin-1 sustains tumor cell self-renewal by engaging survival signaling pathways, thereby reinforcing the stem-like state and contributing to tumor persistence, which are important as a target in the context of personalized medicine.
