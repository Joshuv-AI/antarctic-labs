Factors influencing adolescents’ participation in school recess activities in urban and rural schools: A qualitative study
Authors: Kang Zhu, Yi Huang, Jiaxiang Zhang, Yangyang Shen, Zhenwen Xie, Guoyang Qin, Bei Liu, Yiping Yan, Xiuyu Li, Rui Luo, Dai Li, Wenyu Xu, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesSchool recess activities provide opportunities to enhance physical activity levels among adolescents. This study aims to explore factors influencing Chinese adolescents’ participation in recess activities in urban and rural schools.MethodsA total of 49 participants (33 students, 16 teachers) were interviewed. Semi-structured interviews were used to collect primary data, including 2 focus group interviews and 36 one-on-one interviews. Data analysis was conducted using a grounded theory approach comprising three steps: open coding, axial coding, and selective coding.ResultsThe framework derived from the data analysis aligned closely with the social ecological model (SEM), with identified factors spanning four levels of the SEM. Fifteen factors are distributed across the individual level (motivation to participation, body condition, academic pressure, and gender), interpersonal level (peer influence, teacher influence, and social interaction), physical environment level (equipment and facilities, accessibility of activity spaces, weather, and safety), and organizational and policy level (organized activities, physical activity design, recess duration, and school rules and regulations) of the SEM. Beyond these shared factors, we further identified 1 unique factor influencing urban schools (space size) and 2 unique factors influencing rural schools (motor skills and policy).ConclusionsThe SEM provides a reference framework for understanding the factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities in urban and rural schools. Building on these identified factors, schools can design targeted recess activities and interventions to promote adolescents’ participation. Notably, no relevant factors were found at the community level. Therefore, future research should examine potential factors at the community level.

Introduction

Physical activity (PA) plays a crucial role in promoting adolescents' physical and mental health.1 Regular participation in PA can improve overall health, enhance mental well-being, and reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease.2 Therefore, the World Health Organization recommends that children and adolescents accumulate at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous PA per day.3 However, research by Guthold et al. indicates that more than 81% of adolescents worldwide do not meet these recommendations.4 In China, only 13.1% of adolescents meet the recommended target of at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous PA per day.5 Insufficient PA increases the risk of various health problems, which might lead to a heavy economic burden on society.6 Consequently, enhancing adolescents’ PA levels is a global public health priority.

Adolescents spend most of their day at school, making it a key setting for implementing interventions aimed at enhancing their PA levels.7 Recess, an integral part of the school day, provides opportunities to increase adolescents' daily PA.8 Research indicates that school recess contributes 17-40% to adolescents' total daily PA.9 Consequently, many schools have designed and implemented interventions to promote adolescents' participation in recess activities. However, developing more targeted and effective interventions requires a thorough understanding of the factors influencing adolescents’ participation in school recess activities.

Qualitative methods have proven highly effective in revealing individuals' unique experiences and phenomena within specific contexts, whereas quantitative approaches often struggle to fully capture these deep insights and contextual variations.10 Although previous qualitative studies have examined factors influencing adolescents' participation in school recess activities, existing research was published over a decade ago and primarily originates from European countries, lacking studies grounded in the Chinese context.11 Compared with Western countries, Chinese schools differ markedly in the frequency and duration of recess. In Chinese schools, fixed recess periods follow each class and typically occur four to five times per day. In terms of duration, recess is commonly categorized as short (10-15 min) or long (20-30 min). Additionally, differences exist in school policies and sociocultural contexts, which may lead to varying factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities. Against this backdrop, a systematic examination of factors influencing Chinese adolescents’ participation in school recess activities is needed to address existing research gaps.

The social ecological model (SEM) posits that an individual's development does not occur in isolation but is shaped by the continuous interaction between individual characteristics and the external environment.12 Previous studies have shown that environmental factors influence adolescents' propensity to participate in PA; however, there is currently a lack of research on how the activity environment influences adolescents' participation in recess activities, particularly regarding differences between urban and rural school environments.13 Given the potential disparities between urban and rural schools in resource allocation, facilities, and support for extracurricular activities, these environmental inequalities may further contribute to differences in adolescents' opportunities to participate in PA during recess.14 Therefore, it is necessary to conduct an in-depth exploration of the factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities in urban and rural schools from a multi-level perspective, to provide a basis for developing more targeted and practical intervention strategies, thereby promoting an increase in PA levels among adolescents in both urban and rural areas.

As the primary participants in recess activities, adolescents can provide firsthand information regarding their participation experiences, as well as the factors that promote or hinder their involvement. At the same time, teachers, as the organizers, supervisors, and supporters of school recess activities, play a crucial role in shaping opportunities for participation, the atmosphere of the activities, and the environmental conditions. Incorporating these two groups helps to identify the factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities in both urban and rural schools more comprehensively, from the dual perspectives of participants and administrative supporters. Therefore, this study employs a grounded theory approach, following an analytical pathway of open coding, axial coding, and selective coding, to systematically analyze the factors influencing adolescents’ participation in recess activities in urban and rural schools in China, and to derive a contextualized theoretical framework from the interview data.

Methods

Research methodology

This study employs grounded theory to explore factors influencing adolescents’ participation in recess activities in urban and rural schools. Grounded theory, originally developed by Glaser and Strauss in 1967, centers on researchers refraining from preconceived assumptions. Instead, they directly analyze data through continuous comparison and inductive reasoning to progressively abstract concepts, construct categories, and form theoretical explanatory frameworks.15

Research participants

This study employed convenience sampling, primarily based on considerations of participant accessibility, access to school premises, and the feasibility of conducting interviews. Although convenience sampling may introduce some selection bias, efforts were made during recruitment to balance sample heterogeneity and informational richness, thereby enhancing sample diversity. From March to December 2025, we recruited 33 adolescents (18 males) and 16 teachers (11 males) from urban and rural schools in six regions of China: Jiangxi, Zhejiang, Shanghai, Sichuan, Anhui, and Shandong. Among them, 16 adolescents and 8 teachers were from rural schools, and 17 adolescents and 8 teachers were from urban schools. The average age of adolescents was 15.72 years, and the teachers averaged 38.31 years. Inclusion criteria for adolescents were: (1) aged 12-18 years; (2) Participants must be able to understand the interview questions and communicate effectively to ensure they can participate fully in the interview; (3) Informed consent signed by the adolescent's parent or legal guardian. The inclusion criterion for teachers was that they were currently employed as teachers and taught adolescents. The research was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (ref: 102772023RT112) before participant recruitment, and all procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

This study employed semi-structured interviews to collect primary data. Before the formal interviews, the research team developed a semi-structured interview guide based on the study's objectives. The team then conducted preliminary discussions and revised the guide before data collection began to ensure that the questions effectively addressed the factors influencing adolescents' recess activities and were appropriate for the respondents' levels of understanding and communication styles (Table 1). All interviews were conducted by members of the research team who had received standardized training, including 1 doctoral student and 4 master's students. The training covered the research objectives, interview procedures, questioning techniques, and guidelines for data recording and organization, in order to ensure the consistency and standardization of the interview process to the greatest extent possible. This study conducted a total of 36 one-on-one interviews and 2 focus group sessions. The 36 one-on-one interviews involved 20 students and 16 teachers. Both focus groups consisted of students, with 6 students in one group and 7 in the other. Before each interview, researchers explained the study objectives, interview procedures, audio-recording arrangements, and confidentiality protections to participants and proceeded only after obtaining informed consent. Before the formal interview, to ensure that all participants had a consistent understanding of the core concepts, the interviewer explained the term “recess activities” and provided examples to help participants accurately understand that the study focused on recess activities related to PA. All interviews were conducted in a relatively quiet setting with minimal distractions and were recorded in their entirety. After data collection, 2 interviewers independently transcribed the same recordings verbatim. Data analysis

This study employed NVivo 15 (QSR International, Australia) to code and analyze the interview transcripts. The first author coded the data following the grounded theory procedure, sequentially applying open, axial, and selective coding.16 Subsequently, the research team conducted a comprehensive review of the resulting coding framework. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through collaborative review of the original data and discussion of analytical findings. Finally, theoretical saturation testing was performed to ensure data validity and completeness. After completing the coding analysis and establishing a preliminary theoretical framework, the research team further compared and categorized the various subcategories and their relationships. They found that the resulting framework showed a high degree of consistency with the SEM and that the identified subcategories corresponded well with its different levels. It should be noted that this study did not use the SEM as a predefined theoretical framework to guide coding or analysis at the outset.

Open coding

Open coding aims to conduct a detailed line-by-line and sentence-by-sentence analysis of the raw interview data and to generate concepts as closely grounded in the data as possible. The research team read the interview transcripts line by line, annotating statements related to adolescents’ participation in recess activities in light of the research questions and assigning conceptual labels to meaningful statements to generate initial codes. On this basis, the team continuously compared similar and contrasting statements across different respondents and school contexts, inductively grouping semantically similar initial codes to derive preliminary concepts and categories that captured the core meanings of the data.

Axial coding

Building on the open coding phase, axial coding further systematized and analyzed the relationships among the initial concepts and categories. Through ongoing comparative analysis, the research team consolidated initial categories with similar meanings and logical connections into higher-level subcategories and further explored the relationships among these subcategories. Through this process, the scattered initial codes were gradually integrated into a more general category structure, resulting in several major categories that reflect the factors influencing adolescents’ participation in recess activities.

Selective coding

During the selective coding phase, the research team focused on the research questions to further consolidate and refine the major categories, identify core categories capable of synthesizing the overall analytical findings, and clarify the intrinsic logical relationships among these categories. On this basis, the team integrated categories at different levels to ultimately develop a theoretical framework for the factors influencing adolescents’ participation in recess activities in urban and rural schools. This theoretical framework was not predetermined but emerged through continuous comparison and gradual integration of the data.

Theoretical saturation test

After completing open coding, axial coding, and selective coding, this study subjected the remaining one-third of the original interview data to a theoretical saturation test. During this process, the research team continued to follow the same coding procedures used in the earlier stages, conducting a step-by-step analysis of the reserved data and focusing on the following aspects: whether any new concepts emerged, whether any new categories were formed, whether the attributes and connotations of existing categories were further expanded, and whether any new relationships among categories were established. The results indicated that all relevant statements in the reserved data could be incorporated into the established category system, and no new concepts, categories, attributes, or intercategory relationships were identified. At the same time, the logical relationships among the existing categories remained stable and were sufficient to explain the factors influencing recess activities among adolescents in urban and rural schools. On this basis, this study concluded that data collection had reached theoretical saturation.

Results

After analyzing interview data from adolescents and teachers in urban and rural schools and completing concept extraction, selective coding, and theoretical saturation testing, we found that the resulting coding system closely aligned with the SEM framework. The SEM emphasizes that individual development is shaped by the combined influences of multi-level environmental systems.12 In applying the SEM to health behavior research, McLeroy et al. (1988) identified five levels of factors influencing health behaviors: the individual level (e.g., knowledge, attitudes, and competencies), the interpersonal level (e.g., family, peer, and teacher support), the organizational level (e.g., school rules and regulations), the community level (e.g., community environment and cultural atmosphere), and the policy level (e.g., institutional arrangements and national policies).17 In recent years, this model has been widely applied in qualitative research on recess activities to analyze the factors influencing children's and adolescents' participation in these activities 18, 19, 20. Based on the coding results, factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities span four levels of the model: individual level, interpersonal level, physical environment level, and organizational and policy level (Fig. 1). This study identified 15 shared factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities across urban and rural schools (Table 2), as well as 3 unique factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities in urban and rural schools (Table 3). Shared factors influencing adolescents’ participation in recess activities in urban and rural schools

Individual level

Motivation for participation

Respondents generally noted that adolescents participate in recess activities primarily to relax both mentally and physically, relieve classroom fatigue, obtain physical exercise, and improve athletic skills; however, a lack of interest may also reduce their willingness to participate (quote 1-13).

Body condition

Both adolescents and teachers noted that body condition influences students’ willingness to participate in recess activities. When students feel unwell or fatigued, their willingness to participate decreases accordingly (quote 14-19).

Academic pressure

Both adolescents and teachers noted that when facing exam pressure or a heavy academic workload, adolescents tend to use recess time for studying, completing homework, or resting, rather than participating in recess activities (quotes 20-23). To some extent, this finding reflects the reality of high academic expectations in Chinese schools.

Gender

Gender differences can influence adolescents’ participation in recess activities. Participants noted that boys tend to participate in activities, while girls prefer staying in the classroom and chatting with classmates (quote 24-25).

Interpersonal level

Peer influence

Peers are a key factor in encouraging adolescents to participate in recess activities. Invitations from friends, the company of peers, and the atmosphere fostered by group participation all increase students’ enthusiasm for participating in recess activities (quote 26-32). This suggests that recess activities not only promote PA among adolescents but also provide important opportunities for the development of their social relationships.

Teacher influence

Teachers play a distinctly dual role. On the one hand, their encouragement, support, and involvement help create a lively atmosphere during recess and boost students' motivation to participate; on the other hand, when teachers run over time, it directly reduces the amount of recess time available to students, thereby diminishing their opportunities to participate in recess activities (quote 33-40). This finding also reflects a structural feature of daily instructional management in Chinese schools: namely, that classroom scheduling directly influences students’ opportunities to participate in recess activities.

Social interaction

Adolescents indicated that social needs motivate their participation in recess activities. Some noted that recess provides opportunities to interact with classmates, helping to strengthen friendships and thus increasing their willingness to participate (quote 41-42).

Physical environment level

Equipment and facilities

The availability of equipment and facilities directly influences whether students “can participate” and “are willing to participate”. If equipment is insufficient, facilities are inadequate, or they do not align with students’ interests, both their willingness to participate and their overall experience will be negatively impacted (quote 43-49).

Accessibility of activity spaces

Accessibility of activity spaces is also a key factor influencing adolescents’ participation in recess activities. When classrooms are located far from activity areas or on higher floors, the time students spend travelling back and forth further reduces the already limited recess period, thereby diminishing the practical feasibility of participating in recess activities (quote 50-53).

Weather

Both adolescents and teachers noted that weather conditions significantly influence participation in recess activities. They are less inclined to participate during rainy weather or when temperatures are too cold or too hot, whereas favourable weather encourages participation (quote 54-60).

Safety

The school teacher expressed concerns about safety during recess activities. Teachers also noted potential safety hazards associated with conducting activities in hallways. As a result, the school often restricted adolescents’ participation in recess activities (quote 61-62).

Organizational and policy level

Organized activities

Both adolescents and teachers emphasized that organizing group and competitive activities can encourage adolescents’ participation in recess activities, with structured recess programs attracting more adolescents (quote 63-66).

Physical activity design

Some adolescents suggested increasing the diversity of recess activity options, proposing that offering varied programs could draw greater adolescent participation (quote 67). Teachers also noted that designing activities based on adolescents’ interests could draw them into recess participation (quote 68).

Recess duration

The duration of recess is a key factor influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities. Respondents generally believed that current recess periods were relatively short and that it was particularly difficult to support effective recess activities when students needed to change classrooms or travel to and from activity venues (quote 69-73). In addition, teachers noted that organizing recess time effectively and appropriately extending recess periods could help increase students’ willingness to participate in recess activities (quote 74-75).

School rules and regulations

School regulations have a dual impact on participation in recess activities: on the one hand, restrictions imposed for reasons of safety, discipline, and routine management may limit students' opportunities for activity; on the other hand, institutional arrangements that explicitly encourage or require participation in recess activities may facilitate such participation (quote 76-78). This finding also reflects the strong emphasis on organizational management and rule-based approaches in Chinese schools, characteristics that influence adolescents’ opportunities for recess activities, modes of participation, and use of space to a certain extent.

Unique factors influencing adolescents’ participation in recess activities in urban and rural schools

Individual level

Motor skills

Teachers in rural schools noted that students’ motor skills influence their participation in recess activities. The more diverse the motor skills adolescents possess, the more likely they are to independently organize and participate in activities during recess. This suggests that, in the context of rural schools, motor skills may serve as a key factor linking willingness to engage with actual behavior (quote 1-2).

Physical environment level

Space size

A particularly significant constraint in urban schools is the lack of space for activities. Due to limited space and high student numbers, overcrowding not only hinders the smooth conduct of recess activities but also diminishes students’ enjoyment of these activities, thereby dampening their enthusiasm for participation (quote 3-4).

Organizational and policy level

Policy

Rural school teachers noted that PA policies promote adolescents’ participation in recess activities. To implement these requirements, schools need to provide more opportunities for adolescents to participate in recess activities (quote 5).

Discussion

This study aims to examine factors influencing adolescents’ participation in recess activities in urban and rural schools in China. It identifies 18 influencing factors, which span 4 levels of the SEM. 15 shared factors include: individual level (motivation to participation, body condition, academic pressure, and gender); interpersonal level (peer influence, teacher influence, and social interaction); physical environment level (equipment and facilities, accessibility of activity spaces, weather, and safety); and organizational and policy level (organized activities, PA design, recess duration, and school rules and regulations). Beyond these shared factors, the study further identified 1 unique factor influencing urban schools (space size) and 2 unique factors influencing rural schools (motor skills and policy).

Individual level

At the individual level, factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities include intrinsic motivation, academic pressure, body condition, gender, and motor skills. Motivation typically arises from needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, and individuals' satisfaction in these domains largely determines their level of motivation.21 Previous research indicates that intrinsic motivation promotes adolescents' participation in PA.22 This finding is corroborated in the present study, where participants indicated that adolescents with needs for exercise, relaxation, improvement, and interest in participation were more willing to participate in recess activities. In addition, this study found that academic pressure hinders adolescents' participation in recess activities. Previous research indicates a negative correlation between academic pressure and PA levels among Chinese adolescents.23 Driven by high expectations from parents and teachers, as well as peer competition, academic pressure is prevalent among Chinese adolescents and occupies a significant place in their daily lives.24 The findings of Koka et al. (2025) further corroborate these results, suggesting that creating a supportive environment and reducing controlling influences during recess may enhance adolescents' motivation to participate and alleviate external pressures, thereby encouraging them to participate in recess activities.25 Regarding body condition, illness, or physical disorders directly hinder adolescents' participation in recess activities. Simultaneously, adolescents' willingness to participate in PA declined when they felt physically fatigued. At the same time, gender is an important factor influencing adolescents’ participation in school recess activities. During recess, boys are generally more active than girls and more eager to participate in activities. The primary reason for this difference is that boys typically perceive recess as an opportunity for PA and team competitions, whereas girls tend to view it as a time for social interaction.26

This study found that differences in motor skills influenced rural adolescents’ participation in recess activities, whereas no such effect was observed among adolescents in urban schools. Compared with adolescents in urban schools, those in rural schools have fewer opportunities to access extracurricular sports training and professional guidance, resulting in more limited mastery of motor skills.27 Existing research indicates that adolescents with stronger motor skills may have a wider range of activity options, thereby enhancing their participation in recess activities.28 Conversely, adolescents with weaker motor skills may lack the skills needed to participate in activities, face a risk of exclusion, and consequently show reduced participation in recess activities.29

Interpersonal level

At the interpersonal level, factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities include peer influence, teacher influence, and social interaction. Previous research indicates that peer influence can promote adolescents' participation in recess PA.18 This study also found similar results, with most adolescents being more willing to participate in recess activities when invited and supported by peers. Peer invitations and support lower participation barriers, enhance activity accessibility, and reduce fears of exclusion, thereby increasing adolescents' willingness to participate.30 Furthermore, peer attraction also promotes adolescents' participation in recess activities. Related studies indicate that active peers serve as behavioral role models for adolescents’ participation in PA, and over time, their own PA participation gradually increases.31 Therefore, fostering a positive peer support system and recess activity atmosphere is crucial for promoting adolescent participation in recess activities.

As key figures in school daily life, teachers can both facilitate and hinder adolescent participation in recess activities. In Chinese school settings, teachers typically participate in recess activities as organizers or facilitators, for example, by taking turns leading activities, providing safety guidance, or joining group games. In this way, they not only serve as role models but also offer practical organizational support, which can further encourage students to participate in PA during recess.32 In line with the framework for classifying teacher motivational behaviors proposed by Ahmadi et al. on the basis of Self-Determination Theory, such practices can be understood as “need-supportive” teacher behaviors. That is, by providing supportive guidance, positive feedback, and interactive assurance, they enhance students' autonomy, competence, and relatedness, thereby increasing their intrinsic motivation to participate in recess activities.33 In contrast, the practice of teachers extending class time beyond the scheduled period, as mentioned in this study, can be viewed as a “need-thwarting” behavior. This is because it prioritizes fulfilling instructional schedules at the expense of students' autonomous time, thereby reducing their opportunities for spontaneous participation in recess activities. In the context of Chinese schools, it is relatively common for teachers to use recess time for instruction due to factors such as curriculum schedules and the pressure of teaching tasks. This not only directly reduces the time students have available for activities but also, to some extent, undermines their autonomy and willingness to participate. Therefore, from a practical perspective, school interventions should not only encourage teachers to foster an environment conducive to participation through support, guidance, and active interaction but also strive to avoid behaviors, such as extending class time, that may stifle students' autonomy and motivation to participate, thereby more effectively promoting adolescents’ participation in recess activities.

Social interaction is another crucial factor promoting adolescent participation in recess activities. School recess serves as a vital venue for adolescents to socialize, helping them establish and maintain peer relationships. Most adolescents express a desire to strengthen friendships through recess activities.34

Physical environment level

At the physical environment level, factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities include equipment and facilities, space size, accessibility of activity spaces, weather conditions, and safety. Equipment and facilities largely determine the feasibility of adolescents participating in recess activities. Previous research has also indicated that increasing recess activity facilities can promote adolescents' participation in recess activities.35 When equipment and facilities are adequate and diverse, adolescents are more likely to find activity options suited to their interests and abilities, thereby enhancing their willingness to participate.36 This study also found that inaccessibility to activity venues hinders adolescent participation. Thus, utilizing classrooms as activity spaces and providing flexible, moveable equipment warrants consideration as viable solutions to boost participation.18 Insufficient space is a significant hindrance to adolescents' participation in recess activities in urban schools. Compared with rural schools, urban schools often face inadequate per capita activity space, which can lead adolescents to experience crowding during recess activities and consequently reduce their willingness to participate.37 Furthermore, limited space restricts the diversity of recess activity content and formats, further diminishing adolescents’ participation motivation.20

Weather is recognized as a significant factor influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities. Existing research indicates that rain or other inclement weather reduces students' opportunities to participate in recess activities.19 This study found that weather can serve as both a hindrance and a facilitator. Rain, poor air quality, and extreme weather conditions hinder adolescents' participation in recess activities, whereas sunny weather can stimulate their willingness to participate. Furthermore, concerns about school safety also influence adolescents' participation in recess activities. Schools may restrict activity areas or equipment use for safety reasons. Although such restrictions can reduce risk, they may also limit students’ opportunities to participate in recess activities, thereby influencing their participation.23

Organizational and policy level

At the organizational and policy level, factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities include organized activities, PA design, recess duration, school rules and regulations, and policy. Organized activities lower participants' skill requirements, enabling more adolescents to join with lower barriers to entry and thereby promoting their participation.35 At the same time, organized competitive events can stimulate adolescents' competitive spirit and interest in participation, thereby enhancing their motivation to participate in activities.38 In addition, PA design also influences adolescents' participation in recess activities. Arranging recess activities based on adolescents’ interests and needs, while designing diverse content and organizational formats, helps promote their participation.37

Regarding recess duration, respondents noted that insufficient time can be a significant hindrance to participation in recess activities. Shorter recess periods not only make it difficult for students to reach activity venues in time and participate in related activities, but also limit their actual opportunities for participation.18 Existing research shows that recess duration is positively associated with students' PA levels; extending recess time not only increases participation opportunities but also enhances students' willingness to participate.32 Against this backdrop, regulations regarding recess duration have a significant impact on adolescents' participation in recess activities. In addition, Chinese schools typically rely on rules and regulations to ensure safety, maintain discipline, and facilitate management; these regulations can influence the opportunities and ways in which adolescents participate in recess activities. When schools prioritize recess activities, they generally encourage student participation through institutional measures and provide facilities, equipment, and organizational support; conversely, for safety or disciplinary reasons, schools may implement restrictive measures (such as limiting activity areas or the use of equipment), thereby dampening students’ enthusiasm for participation.

Furthermore, PA policies can also enhance adolescents' participation in recess activities at rural schools. Previous research indicates that the participation of rural school adolescents in recess activities is frequently limited by environmental factors, including insufficient equipment and facilities, as well as limited activity diversity.39 Simultaneously, compared with urban schools, rural schools generally possess relatively scarce environmental resources for extracurricular PA.40 Against this backdrop, PA policy can strengthen environmental support in rural schools through institutional safeguards and resource allocation, thereby increasing opportunities for participation and promoting adolescents’ participation in recess activities.41 In contrast, urban schools typically have more abundant facilities and equipment, along with a wider range of activity options, which provide adolescents with greater opportunities to participate. Consequently, the scope for further improvement through policy interventions may be relatively limited in these settings.

However, this study did not identify any influencing factors at the community level. One possible explanation for this finding is that the study focused on adolescents’ participation in recess activities, which primarily take place within the school setting. Consequently, when describing relevant influencing factors, participants tended to focus on factors directly related to the school context, such as individuals, peers, teachers, the school environment, and school policies, while the direct influence of community spaces or the neighborhood environment on recess activities was relatively limited. Furthermore, this finding may also be related to the structure of the interview guide. Because the interview guide was primarily organized around school recess activities, it may, to some extent, have constrained the emergence of community-related themes.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study is the first qualitative investigation to examine factors influencing Chinese adolescents' participation in recess activities. Its findings provide valuable insights for designing recess activities content, developing intervention strategies, and informing relevant school policy. Second, by recruiting adolescents and teachers from both urban and rural schools and examining factors within an urban-rural context, this study enhances its generalizability. Although this study followed rigorous procedures during data collection and analysis, several limitations remain. First, the study employed convenience sampling, and the rural school sample included a high proportion of high school students, which may, to some extent, limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups or school settings. Second, respondents may have been influenced by social expectations during the interviews and thus may have tended to provide answers that conformed to school norms or societal expectations, thereby affecting the presentation of the findings. Furthermore, although this study included both teachers and adolescents as participants, it did not systematically compare differences in perspectives between the 2 groups. Future research could expand the age range and types of schools examined and employ mixed-methods approaches or triangulation of multiple data sources to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing adolescents’ participation in recess activities. At the same time, further exploration of the similarities and differences in how teachers and adolescents perceive these influencing factors may yield richer insights.

Conclusion

We found that the SEM provides a useful framework for deepening understanding of the factors influencing adolescents' participation in recess activities in both urban and rural schools. Overall, adolescents' participation in recess activities is shaped by a range of factors, including individual, interpersonal, and physical environmental factors, as well as organizational and policy factors, and shows certain differences between urban and rural school settings. Based on these findings, schools can design and implement targeted interventions by motivating student participation, strengthening peer and teacher support, optimizing the provision of facilities and equipment as well as activity design, appropriately scheduling recess periods, and improving management and institutional support. These measures may help promote adolescents’ participation in recess activities and thereby increase their PA levels.
