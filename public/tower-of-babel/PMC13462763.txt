Phylogenetic Diversity of Fusarium Species Complexes Causing Wilt in Watermelon and Melon in Korea
Authors: A-Ram Jeong, Oh-Kyu Kwon, Gunn Yeom, Seung-Jun Yu, Ji-Hun Kim, Ryoung Shin, Gung Pyo Lee, Chang-Jin Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWatermelon (Citrullus lanatus Thunb.) and melon (Cucumis melo L.) are globally important horticultural crops, cultivated widely for their economic and dietary value. However, their productivity is increasingly threatened by Fusarium wilt, which causes substantial yield losses. The genus Fusarium comprises over 400 phylogenetically distinct species, classified into multiple species complexes (SCs). Many of these species are soilborne and resilient in agroecosystems, complicating accurate identification and disease management. While Fusarium oxysporum formae speciales–f. sp. niveum (FON) and f. sp. melonis (FOM)–have long been considered the main causal agents of Fusarium wilt in watermelon and melon, an increasing number of regional studies suggest that diverse Fusarium spp. may also be involved. In this study, we examined the diversity and pathogenicity of Fusarium spp. causing wilt in watermelon and melon across key cultivation regions in Korea. Thirty-three Fusarium isolates were initially identified using internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequencing and further characterized through multi-locus phylogenetic analysis of the translation elongation factor 1-alpha (EF-1α) and the RNA polymerase second largest subunit (RPB2) genes. The isolates were classified into five distinct SCs: F. oxysporum (FOSC), F. nisikadoi (FNSC), F. fujikuroi (FFSC), F. incarnatum–equiseti (FIESC), and F. solani (FSSC). Morphological and microscopic features were examined to support SC-level classification. Pathogenicity assays confirmed that representative isolates from each SC induced wilt symptoms in both watermelon and melon. These results broaden the understanding of Fusarium diversity in cucurbits and underscore the needs for phylogenetically informed diagnostics and disease management strategies.Keywords: cucurbits, Fusarium species complex, Fusarium wilt

Watermelon (Citrullus lanatus Thunb.) and melon (Cucumis melo L.), both members of the Cucurbitaceae family, are among the most widely cultivated horticultural crops worldwide (Wang et al., 2024). In Korea alone, these crops were grown on 11,092 hectares for watermelon and 4,569 hectares for melon in 2023, yielding 41,429 and 42,491 tons, respectively (FAOSTAT, 2023). Their high nutritional value and water content make them important contributors to dietary health (Rolnik and Olas, 2020). However, their productivity is increasingly constrained by Fusarium wilt–a devastating vascular disease that can result in severe yield reductions and threaten the profitability of commercial production (Lee et al., 2015a; Sabahi et al., 2021; Zhong et al., 2022).

The genus Fusarium comprises over 400 phylogenetically distinct species, which are classified into at least 23 species complexes (SCs) (Geiser et al., 2021; O’Donnell et al., 2022; Torres-Cruz et al., 2022), including the F. oxysporum SC (FOSC), F. nisikadoi SC (FNSC), F. fujikuroi SC (FFSC), F. incarnatum–equiseti SC (FIESC), and F. solani SC (FSSC). Although FSSC is sometimes treated as the genus Neocosmospora, we retain it within Fusarium to maintain nomenclatural stability (Geiser et al., 2021; O’Donnell et al., 2022). Many of these species are both seedborne and soilborne, persisting under diverse environmental conditions. Such characteristics complicate crop rotation and render conventional control strategies only partially effective (Paret et al., 2019). Accurate identification of the causal species is therefore crucial for effective disease diagnosis, surveillance, and integrated management.

Fusarium wilt in cucurbits has traditionally been attributed to host-specific formae speciales of Fusarium oxysporum–notably f. sp. niveum (FON) in watermelon (Zhong et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2010) and f. sp. melonis (FOM) in melon (Chai et al., 2025, Oumouloud et al., 2012; Schmidt et al., 2016).

However, the formae speciales concept is based on host specificity and does not reflect genetic relatedness (Lievens et al., 2008; O’Donnell et al., 2022; van Dam et al., 2018). Multiple studies have shown that many f. sp. designations are polyphyletic, having evolved independently in genetically distinct clades. Likewise, species-level classification has also proven inadequate, as it often fails to reflect the extensive cryptic diversity, polyphyly, and host-range variability within Fusarium taxa (Geiser et al., 2021; O’Donnell et al., 2004; Summerell et al., 2010). Furthermore, increasing evidence suggests that Fusarium wilt in watermelon and melon involves not only F. oxysporum formae speciales (FON and FOM) but also phylogenetically distinct Fusarium taxa (Jeong et al., 2024; Khuna et al., 2022; Medeiros Araújo et al., 2021; Sabahi et al., 2023; Subramanian et al, 2025). These observations underscore the need for SC-level classification in Fusarium research.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive survey of Fusarium isolates associated with wilt in watermelon and melon across major cultivation regions in Korea. Using multi-locus phylogenetic analysis, morphological characterization, and pathogenicity assays, we assessed the diversity and pathogenicity of local Fusarium populations. These findings provide an updated framework for understanding the complex etiology of Fusarium wilt in cucurbits and support the development of more accurate diagnostic tools and targeted disease management strategies.

Materials and Methods

Field survey, sample collection and fungal isolation

Between 2019 and 2023, field surveys were conducted in 68 fields across 11 cities–Changwon, Cheonan, Cheongyang, Chuncheon, Chungju, Gochang, Goryeong, Gwangju, Yanggu, Yangpyeong, and Yongin–representing major watermelon and melon production regions in Korea. Leaves, stems, and fruits exhibiting typical wilt symptoms were collected. Small segments were excised from the margins of healthy and diseased tissues, surface-sterilized with 70% ethanol for 1 min, rinsed three times in sterile water, and placed on potato dextrose agar (PDA; MBcell, Seoul, Korea). Plates were incubated at 28°C for 7–10 days.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 7-day-old fungal mycelia grown on PDA using the CTAB method (Jeong et al., 2022). Briefly, mycelia were incubated in CTAB extraction buffer at 65°C for 10 min, followed by chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (24:1) extraction and isopropanol precipitation. The resulting gDNA was washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in distilled water.

For molecular identification, the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was amplified using ITS1/ITS4 primers to confirm genus-level identity (Table 1). For phylogenetic analysis at the SC level, the translation elongation factor 1-alpha (EF-1α) and the RNA polymerase second largest subunit (RPB2) regions were amplified using EF-1/EF-2 and RPB2-5F2/RPB2-7cR primers, respectively. To test for the presence of FON and FOM, formae speciales–specific primers targeting effector and hypothetical protein-encoding genes were used (van Dam et al., 2018) (Table 1).

Each 20 μL reaction contained 0.5 U of FIREPol® DNA polymerase (Solis BioDyne, Tartu, Estonia), 1 μL each of forward and reverse primer (10 μM), 0.2 μL dNTPs (200 μM each), 2 μL 10× buffer, 4 μL 5× Q-solution, 1 μL gDNA (~100 ng), and distilled water to volume. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 35 cycles of 94°C for 15 s, 53°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 40 s (ITS and EF-1α); 94°C for 15 s, 55°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 90 s (RPB2); and 94°C for 15 s, 54°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 30 s (formae speciales-associated primers).

PCR products were separated on 0.8% agarose gels, stained with RedSafe™ (iNtRON Bio, Seongnam, Korea), and visualized under UV light. Target bands were excised and purified with the FavorPrep™ GEL/PCR Purification Mini Kit (Favorgen, Ping-Tung, Taiwan). Purified amplicons were submitted to Macrogen (Macrogen Inc., Seoul, Korea) for Sanger sequencing.

Phylogenetic analysis

For accurate SC-level classification and precise phylogenetic placement, EF-1α sequences were compared against the FUSARIUM-ID v.3.0 database (Torres-Cruz et al., 2022). The reference EF-1α dataset was obtained from the official GitHub repository (https://github.com/fusariumid/fusariumid) to perform local BLAST queries. Additionally, the RPB2 sequences were compared against well-characterized reference and type strain sequences available in the NCBI GenBank database (O’Donnell et al., 2022). EF-1α and RPB2 sequences were aligned with MUSCLE and curated in MEGA 11 (Kumar et al., 2018). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the maximum likelihood method with the Kimura 2-parameter model, which was selected based on the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) score in MEGA 11. Branch support was evaluated with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. All sequences were deposited in GenBank, and accession numbers are listed in Table 2.

Morphological characterization

Representative isolates were selected to include at least one from each of the five identified species complexes. Morphological traits of Fusarium isolates were evaluated using a previously described method with minor modifications (Han et al., 2022). For colony morphology–including type, color, and margin–isolates were incubated on PDA at 28°C in the dark for 7–14 days. To induce conidial formation, mycelial plugs from PDA-grown colonies were inoculated into 100 mL of V-8 broth (with CaCO3). Cultures were shaken at 150 rpm at 28°C for 7 days. The culture was filtered to remove mycelia, centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 10 min, and the conidia were resuspended in distilled water. Conidial morphology was observed under a light microscope at 400× magnification.

Pathogenicity test

Pathogenicity of five representative Fusarium isolates was assessed on watermelon (Seotaja) and melon (Earls Top One) using the root-dipping method (Cohen et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2015b). Seeds were germinated in a growth chamber and inoculated 14 days after sowing, when cotyledons were fully expanded. Conidial suspensions were prepared by culturing isolates in V-8 broth and adjusting the concentration to 1.0 × 106 conidia/mL using a hemocytometer. Seedlings were uprooted, rinsed, and immersed in the conidial suspension for 30 min. Treated plants were transplanted into pots and maintained overnight in a dark, humid chamber before being transferred to a growth chamber set at 24°C with a 16 h light/8 h dark.

Symptom severity was monitored for 4 weeks in watermelon and melon. The disease index was recorded 21 days post-inoculation based on a scale of 1 to 5 (Supplementary Fig. 1): 1 = no symptoms; 2 = slightly retarded growth; 3 = mild chlorosis, necrosis, and stunted growth; 4 = severe chlorosis, necrosis, and defoliation; and 5 = plant death. The mean disease index (±SD) was calculated from eight replicates per isolate (Supplementary Table 1). Finally, the Fusarium isolates were consistently re-isolated from the infected hypocotyl tissues of the symptomatic plants, thereby fulfilling Koch’s postulates.

Results and Discussion

Survey and initial identification of Fusarium spp

From 2019 to 2023, field surveys were conducted in major watermelon and melon production areas in Korea to investigate the species diversity and pathogenicity of Fusarium spp. associated with wilt symptoms. A total of 68 fields were examined across 11 cities, including Changwon, Cheonan, Cheongyang, Chuncheon, Chungju, Gochang, Goryeong, Gwangju, Yanggu, Yangpyeong, and Yongin (Fig. 1A). Plants exhibiting typical symptoms–including leaf yellowing, stem and root browning, fruit and stem cracking, growth retardation, wilting, and death–were sampled for pathogen isolation (Fig. 1B). Fungal isolates exhibiting Fusarium-like morphology–such as white to pale pink colonies with cottony or felt-like aerial mycelium–were selected for molecular identification using the ITS region amplified with ITS1 and ITS4 primers (White et al., 1990) (Table 1). BLASTn analysis of the resulting sequences against the NCBI database confirmed that 33 isolates belonged to the genus Fusarium (Table 2).

Panels (a–f) show symptoms caused by isolates classified into distinct species complexes (see Fig. 2). The ITS region, often referred to as the universal fungal barcode, is widely employed in fungal diagnostics due to its amplification efficiency and extensive sequence availability (O’Donnell et al., 2022; Schoch et al., 2012). However, it lacks the variability required to resolve closely related species within Fusarium (Geiser et al., 2004), limiting its utility to reliable genus-level classification in most cases (O’Donnell et al., 2015, 2022; Sanna et al., 2023).

Phylogenetic analysis of Fusarium species complexes

To overcome the limited resolution of the ITS region and enable more accurate classification at the SC-level, we proceeded with multi-locus phylogenetic analysis using EF-1α and the second largest subunit of RPB2, which are widely used for higher taxonomic resolution in Fusarium systematics (Ha et al., 2023; Medeiros Araújo et al., 2021; Mirghasempour et al., 2022). Gene-specific primers amplified fragments of 600–700 bp (EF-1α) and 1,100–1,800 bp (RPB2) (O’Donnell et al., 1998, 2010) (Table 1). A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was generated using concatenated EF-1α and RPB2 sequences from 33 isolates. Detailed information on host origin and GenBank accession numbers for all isolates is provided in Table 2. Reference sequences from 44 well-characterized Fusarium strains (Geiser et al., 2021; Ha et al., 2023; Lombard et al., 2019; Xia et al., 2019; Supplementary Table 1) were included for SC-level placement, with Trichoderma brevicompactum (strain 2IG2102) serving as the outgroup (Fig. 2). Our isolates clustered into five distinct SCs: FOSC (16 isolates), FNSC (2), FFSC (5), FIESC (8) and FSSC (2). The grouping patterns were consistent with previous reports describing the phylogenetic boundaries of each SC (Geiser et al., 2021; Lombard et al., 2019), supporting the robustness of our phylogenetic inference.

Notably, isolates belonging to the FNSC, FFSC, FIESC, and FSSC were recovered from symptomatic watermelon and melon plants. Although some of these SCs have been previously reported in cucurbit crops such as oriental melon (C. melo var. makuwa) in Korea (Kim and Kim 2004; Seo and Kim 2017), their association with commercial melon and watermelon production has not previously been documented. These findings suggest that the host range and distribution of certain SCs may be broader and more complex than previously recognized.

Although the current approach enabled confident SC-level classification, species-level delimitation within each complex remains challenging due to cryptic diversity and unresolved taxonomic boundaries. Finer-scale resolution–particularly for species-level identification–will likely require more extensive multi-locus sequence typing or whole-genome sequencing (Achari et al., 2020; Brankovics et al., 2020). Despite these limitations, our results establish a reliable phylogenetic framework for understanding the diversity of Fusarium spp. associated with wilt in cucurbits and provide a foundation for future taxonomic and epidemiological studies.

Fusarium diversity in watermelon and melon

Historically, F. oxysporum, particularly its formae speciales FON and FOM, has been regarded as the primary causal agent of wilt disease in watermelon and melon, respectively (Oumouloud et al., 2012; Schmidt et al., 2016; Zhong et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2010). To determine whether any of the FOSC isolates recovered in this study corresponded to FON or FOM, we applied five diagnostic primer sets developed through comparative genomics (van Dam et al., 2018). This marker system enables reliable differentiation among F. oxysporum formae speciales infecting cucurbits (Fraser-Smith et al., 2014; van Dam et al., 2016, 2018) (Supplementary Fig. 2). When evaluated on reference strains from the Korean Agricultural Culture Collection (KACC)–FON (KACC 40902, 40905) and FOM (KACC 43205, 43206)–the primers produced the expected amplification patterns, confirming their diagnostic reliability. In contrast, weak, non-specific amplification was occasionally observed, but none of the field isolates could be reliably identified as FON or FOM.

The absence of FON and FOM in our field samples may be attributed to the widespread use of grafted seedlings in Korean commercial cucurbit production (Jo et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2015a). Notably, grafting with resistant rootstocks has been reported as a useful strategy for controlling soil-borne diseases (An et al., 2021) and may contribute to reducing FON abundance in the rhizoplane and endosphere by significantly altering the composition of the root-associated microbiome (Ge et al., 2022). Consequently, the long-term use of resistant rootstocks might have been a contributing factor to the low prevalence of F. oxysporum formae speciales (FON and FOM) in production fields (Keinath et al., 2019; Ling et al., 2013; Nisini et al., 2002). Additionally, spatial and temporal variation in pathogen populations, as well as potential bias from symptom-based sampling, may also have contributed to their absence in this survey.

Notably, phylogenetic analysis using EF-1α and RPB2 loci revealed that the reference strains FON (KACC 40905) and FOM (KACC 43206), though classified into different formae speciales, were more closely related to each other than to any of the field isolates, within the FOSC (Fig. 2), suggesting a potential disconnect between host-based classification and genetic relatedness at these specific loci. While this observation is consistent with previous reports on the polyphyletic nature of certain formae speciales (Achari et al., 2020; Brankovics et al., 2017), further phylogenomic analyses with a broader set of reference strains are required to draw definitive conclusions about the evolutionary relationships within the FOSC. Collectively, our results further illustrate the inherent taxonomic complexity of Fusarium wilt pathogens in cucurbits. The identification of multiple SCs from diverse geographical locations underscores the importance of SC-level surveillance for precise pathogen identification and the development of effective disease management strategies.

Morphology and pathogenicity of representative Fusarium SCs

To complement SC-level identification, eight representative isolates spanning five Fusarium SCs were selected for morphological characterization: GJ192-3 (FOSC Clade 3), GC192-28 (FOSC Clade 5), GR211-8 (FOSC Clade 7), CA211-18 (FNSC), CW216-2 (FFSC), GC192-29 (FIESC Equiseti Clade), YP223-4 (FIESC Incarnatum Clade), and GR212-11 (FSSC).

Colony morphology on PDA varied among these isolates (Fig. 3A). FOSC Clades 3, 5 and 7 exhibited radial growth with white to pinkish pigmentation. FNSC formed pink-centered colonies with abundant aerial mycelium, while FFSC formed reddish colonies. FIESC isolates produced cottony colonies with slow growth and orange or white pigmentation. FSSC formed dense white colonies. Microscopic examination revealed that conidial morphology also varied among SCs (Fig. 3B). Most SCs (FOSC, FNSC, FFSC, and FSSC) produced hyaline, slightly curved, oval- to elliptical-shaped, mostly aseptate conidia. In contrast, conidia from FIESC were slender, septate, and tapered at the apex.

Pathogenicity tests were performed using the watermelon cultivar ‘Seotaja’ and the melon cultivar ‘Earls Top One’ via a root-dipping method. Five isolates each representing a distinct Fusarium SCs, induced typical wilt symptoms in both hosts within 28 days after inoculation (Fig. 4). Infected seedlings showed wilting, growth retardation, and dark brown lesions at the stem base, occasionally progressing to plant death. No symptoms developed in mock-inoculated controls. All tested isolates were pathogenic to the specific watermelon and melon cultivars tested, regardless of their original host. While these representative isolates did not exhibit strict host specificity under our experimental conditions, further studies involving a broader range of isolates and diverse host cultivars are necessary to determine if this broader host range is a general characteristic of these species complexes. The severity of the symptoms was further quantified using a disease index scale (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2), with all representative isolates inducing wilt and growth retardation. To confirm the causal agents and fulfill Koch’s postulates, the respective Fusarium strains were consistently re-isolated from the infected hypocotyl tissues of the symptomatic plants.

These results demonstrate that representative isolates from multiple Fusarium SCs identified in this study, beyond the traditionally recognized FON and FOM (O’Donnell et al., 2022; van Dam et al., 2018), can be pathogenic on both watermelon and melon. The development of typical wilt symptoms suggests that a broad range of Fusarium taxa must be considered in resistance screening and diagnostic workflows.

This study reveals that Fusarium wilt in Korea watermelon and melon is caused by phylogenetically diverse SCs, extending beyond the traditionally recognized FON and FOM. The detection of phylogenetically distinct and pathogenic isolates highlights the urgent need for updated diagnostics and resistance breeding strategies that reflect this expanded pathogen spectrum. These findings lay the groundwork for more accurate pathogen monitoring and sustainable disease management in cucurbit production systems.
