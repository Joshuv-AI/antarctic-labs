The World Health Organization Antenatal CorTicosteroids for Improving Outcomes in preterm Newborns (ACTION-III) trial—rationale for the selected lower steroid dose
Authors: The WHO ACTION Trials Collaborators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntenatal corticosteroids (ACS), most commonly administered as betamethasone or dexamethasone, remain a cornerstone of care for women at risk of preterm birth. However, the standard 24-mg regimen introduced more than five decades ago has not undergone formal dose-finding evaluation. Emerging concerns regarding possible dose-related adverse effects, particularly among late preterm infants subsequently born at term, have renewed interest in optimizing corticosteroid exposure. Experimental data across species, supported by human pharmacokinetic analyses, indicate that fetal lung maturation is driven by sustained low corticosteroid concentrations rather than high peak levels. This commentary summarizes key experimental, pharmacokinetic, and modelling evidence that informed the selection of the lower-dose betamethasone phosphate regimen evaluated in the WHO ACTION-III trial and explains the scientific rationale for this dosing strategy. Pharmacokinetic modeling indicates that 2 mg betamethasone phosphate administered intramuscularly every 12 h for four doses achieves fetal concentrations within the 1 to 4 ng/mL range identified in experimental studies, while avoiding the supratherapeutic peaks observed with conventional regimens. Introduction

Antenatal corticosteroids (ACS) are a cornerstone of modern preterm birth care, as they have been shown to accelerate fetal lung maturation and improve survival among preterm newborns. The concept of enhancing fetal maturity through ACS exposure originated from pivotal work by Liggins in the 1960s [1]. Using a sheep model to study parturition, he observed that lambs born prematurely after steroid infusion demonstrated partial lung inflation, which he attributed to accelerated surfactant production. This seminal observation led to the first clinical trial of ACS in pregnant women in 1972, which showed a significant reduction in neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) [2], a finding that has since been validated in numerous subsequent trials [3].

Liggins’ empiric selection of betamethasone, administered as two doses of 12 mg betamethasone, each comprising a 1:1 mixture, i.e., 6 mg of betamethasone phosphate (Beta-P) and 6 mg of betamethasone acetate (Beta-Ac), given by intramuscular injection (IM) 24 h apart, has become one of the most widely studied and used ACS regimens [4, 5]. Both betamethasone and dexamethasone demonstrate similar efficacy in improving preterm birth outcomes [4, 6], despite differences in their pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends either IM dexamethasone or betamethasone, given in divided doses totaling 24 mg, as the ACS of choice [7]. The indication remains off-label though injectable dexamethasone and betamethasone are readily available, inexpensive and widely used.

Despite nearly five decades of use, the optimal corticosteroid salt and dose remain uncertain. Although the guiding principle of therapeutics is to use the lowest effective dose, an optimal dose for ACS is yet to be determined. This principle is particularly important for glucocorticoids, which have effects on multiple organ systems, and in particular, the preterm fetus, which normally has a low cortisol concentration [8]. Dose-ranging trials for ACS were never performed to evaluate efficacy or safety. The generally established regimen (24 mg total in divided doses) has not been compared to alternative regimens in randomized trials until recently [9]. The need to explore alternative regimens is particularly important given an emerging body of evidence from observational cohorts linking ACS exposure to long-term outcomes in high income settings suggesting ACS may have an impact on neurodevelopment [10–12] in children who were exposed in the late preterm period, particularly those born at term. Optimizing dosing is therefore increasingly important to maximize benefit while minimizing harm.

The WHO ACTION-III trial, protocol published in this journal [13] presents the design of a multi-country, multi-center, three-arm, parallel group, double-blind, placebo controlled, individually randomized trial to assess the benefits and possible harms of two regimens of antenatal corticosteroids, i.e., dexamethasone phosphate (Dex-P) 4 × 6 mg IM q12h (total dose 24 mg) and Beta-P 4 × 2 mg IM q12h (total dose 8 mg), compared to placebo, when given to pregnant women at risk of preterm birth in the late preterm period (gestation age of 34 + 0 to 36 + 5 weeks). The dose of Dex-P 4 × 6 mg IM q12h is the conventional ACS regimen used in practice. The lower dose Beta-P 4 × 2 mg IM q12h has been selected based on a detailed consideration of animal studies across species, human data from pregnant and non-pregnant women and pharmacokinetic modeling, prior to commencing the trial. This article summarizes key experimental, pharmacokinetic, and modelling evidence that informed the selection of the lower dose regimen evaluated in the WHO ACTION-III trial and explains the scientific rationale for this dosing strategy.

Scientific rationale for the lower-dose betamethasone regimen

Pharmacokinetics of the conventional betamethasone regimen used in the Liggins 1972 trial

Liggins and Howie [2] chose two IM doses of 12 mg betamethasone given 24 h apart, each comprising 6 mg Beta-P and 6 mg Beta-Ac. Subsequently, Ballard et al. [14] described the betamethasone level in maternal and cord blood in 1975, after maternal administration of the dose used in the Liggins trial. Their measurements indicated that betamethasone in maternal blood reached peak levels of 75 mcg cortisol equivalents per 100 mL in 1 h, falling by half 6 h after the first dose and to very low levels at 48 h. In cord blood, peak levels were lower, at 14.3 and 4.7 mcg cortisol equivalents per 100 mL at 1 h and 20 h, respectively.

Below is a schematic diagram (Fig. 1) based on the original level observed by Ballard and Liggins [5] demonstrating fetal blood levels with repeat doses (2 doses of 12 mg, 24 h apart) of Beta-P and Beta-Ac mixture (still used today). The regimen produces a sharp initial peak as the phosphate salt undergoes rapid hydrolysis, followed by prolonged lower levels as the acetate component is slowly deacetylated.

Graph of fetal blood levels of betamethasone or dexamethasone after treatment with 2 doses of 12 mg Beta-P + Beta-Ac (Beta-PA regimen) or 4 doses of 6 mg Dex-P. The shaded area shows the required concentrations (1 to 4 ng/mL) for lung maturity. (Figure reproduced with permission) [5]

The other widely used ACS regimen is four doses of 6 mg Dex-P given IM at 12 h intervals (dotted line in Fig. 1) to achieve continuous fetal exposure for about 48 h, based on estimates of maternal and fetal drug levels. Dexamethasone 6 mg needs to be given more frequently (q12h) compared to Beta-P regimen (12 mg doses q24h), as dexamethasone has a shorter half-life and is cleared more quickly.

What matters more for fetal lung maturity: peak concentrations or prolonged exposure to ACS?

The key consideration in optimizing ACS regimens is whether fetal lung maturation is driven primarily by peak corticosteroid concentrations or by sustained lower-level exposure. Evidence from preclinical studies provides important insights into this question.

Findings from animal studies

Studies in preterm lambs have shown that high maternal–fetal peak betamethasone concentrations do not correlate with greater improvements in lung maturation. The largest and most consistent improvements in lung maturation were observed with:

ii.Continuous low-dose Beta-P infusion that produced fetal concentrations of approximately 2 ng/mL for 26 h.

These findings indicate that lung maturation is associated with prolonged lower concentrations rather than high peak exposure. [15].

A separate study in pregnant ewes reported that a single IM dose of Beta-Ac (0.125 mg/kg) improved lung compliance, gas exchange, and ventilation efficiency to a similar degree as the standard two dose Beta-P + Beta-Ac regimen (0.25 mg/kg) [16]. This again suggested that prolonged low fetal betamethasone concentrations (1 to 4 ng/mL for at least 24 h) provided by Beta-Ac rather than the initial high peak levels (10 to 20 ng/mL) provided by Beta-P are responsible for lung maturity.

These and other sheep studies have indicated that prolonged duration of exposure with Beta-Ac or repeated Beta-P injections are important for lung maturity. A consistent finding is the threshold of 1 to 4 ng/mL in fetal circulation to achieve fetal lung maturity in sheep; higher fetal steroid levels are not necessary (Fig. 1).

Findings from pregnant rhesus macaque monkeys mirror these observations. A single 0.125 mg/kg dose of Beta-Ac alone improved fetal lung pressure–volume curves equivalent to two doses of 0.25 mg/kg of Beta-P + Beta-Ac, with fetal betamethasone concentrations of approx. 5 ng/mL at 8 h and 4 ng/mL at 24 h post-injection [17]. Thus, results are consistent between sheep and monkeys, demonstrating that a low continuous fetal exposure to betamethasone, at similar levels across species, can mediate fetal lung maturation with avoidance of the high peak drug exposures from Beta-P [5]. However, it is of note that prolonged steroid exposure may be undesirable for both mother and fetus.

Taken together, these experimental findings indicate that sustained fetal corticosteroid exposure within the range of approximately 1 to 4 ng/mL is sufficient to promote lung maturation, whereas high peak concentrations appear unlikely to be required for this effect. These findings suggest that ACS regimens designed to limit excessive peak levels and maintain more prolonged exposure within the effective window may be more physiologically appropriate.

Human studies supporting target ACS concentration in the human fetus

Pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic studies in sheep and non-human primate models, as described above, suggest that a low prolonged fetal steroid concentration in the range of 1 to 4 ng/mL can result in fetal lung maturation in these species. Because similar fetal steroid concentration ranges have been observed across species, it is plausible that comparable exposure levels may be effective in humans.

Indirect evidence from human studies supports this assumption. A study published in 2010 from the USA reported fetal cord blood steroid concentrations following maternal administration of Beta-P + Beta-Ac at different intervals after the last dose [18]. Concentrations measured 2 to 3 days after the last dose, reflecting the Beta-Ac component, ranged between 1 and 4 ng/mL, consistent with the effective levels identified in animal models (Fig. 2). As the initial peak is unlikely to mediate the therapeutic effect (as discussed above), this prolonged low concentration of 1 to 4 ng/mL is likely the pharmacologically relevant exposure contributing to fetal lung maturation.

Betamethasone concentration in cord blood (ng/mL) by days since last steroid dose. The shaded area shows the required concentrations (1 to 4 ng/mL) for lung maturity (reproduced with permission) [18]

A second more recent study measured the levels of ACS in maternal and cord blood [19] in women from the b-Mhyalines study (Betamethasone and Severity of Hyaline Membrane Disease) [20]. After adjustment for gestational age and twin pregnancy, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome was found to be associated with the duration of in-utero exposure below quantifiable steroid concentrations (i.e., < 1 ng/mL) per day. This finding suggests that maintaining fetal steroid concentrations above this threshold is necessary for effective lung maturation. Cord blood steroid levels reported in the b-Mhyalines study closely correspond to those observed in the USA study described earlier. As shown in Fig. 3, following the initial peak concentration produced by Beta-P, cord blood levels fall below 5 ng/mL for the next 2 to 3 days. A second dose extends the duration for a few more days. It is likely again that this prolonged concentration in the range of 1 to 4 ng/mL is the pharmacologically active exposure responsible for promoting fetal lung maturation.

Dose selection for the WHO ACTION III trial

According to the current WHO recommendations, women at risk of preterm birth should be offered either a single course of betamethasone in two 12-mg IM doses at a 24-h interval or dexamethasone in four 6-mg IM doses at 12-h intervals [8]. Although dexamethasone may have a greater affinity for glucocorticoid receptors, betamethasone has a longer half-life [21]. The ASTEROID trial [6] reported that the efficacy and safety (in terms of neonatal survival and neurosensory disability at age two) did not differ when maternal IM dexamethasone or Celestone® (a 1:1 Beta-P and Beta-Ac formulation) was used prior to preterm birth.

Given emerging evidence that sustained lower corticosteroid exposure, rather than brief high peak concentrations, is more important for fetal lung maturation, we simulated steroid concentrations under a range of dosing regimens to inform selection of the lower-dose regimen to be evaluated in the WHO ACTION-III trial. PK models were developed using data from human studies to guide both steroid salt and dose selection. These models incorporated variation in physiologic parameters, pregnancy-related changes in drug metabolism, including increased activity of cytochrome enzymes involved in steroid metabolism, as well as published estimates of the fetal-to-maternal concentration ratio of approximately 0.4 derived from human cord blood studies [19]. Animal pharmacokinetic data were not used directly in the modeling process; instead, experimental animal studies informed the target fetal exposure range and the duration required for lung maturation. The modeling framework was then used to identify dosage regimens predicted to maintain fetal corticosteroid concentrations within the proposed therapeutic range of 1 to 4 ng/mL while minimizing unnecessary peak exposures.

Preliminary population PK models developed during early exposure prediction were simple models and have since been superseded by more advanced modeling approaches described in the literature [22–26]. Over the past several years, both population-based and physiologically based PK models have been applied to characterize ACS disposition and maternal–fetal transfer, demonstrating broadly consistent exposure predictions across modeling approaches [22–26]. Recent modeling work by Van Der Heijden et al. using placenta perfusion data combined with physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling similarly supports reduced-dose regimens of betamethasone and dexamethasone that are broadly comparable to those evaluated in the ACTION-III trial [26].

Maternal–fetal ratios of ACS

In the USA study described above [18], the ratio of fetal to maternal betamethasone concentrations varied from 1:3 to 1:4. The b-Myhalines trial in France reported a median fetal to maternal ratio of 0.35, i.e., 1:3 [19]. Another report studying ACS levels in women with singleton versus twin pregnancies also reported fetal to maternal ratios varying between 1:2 and 1:5, depending on dose to birth interval [21].

Pharmacokinetic studies in non-pregnant women

A 2019 study evaluated the pharmacokinetics of a single dose (6 mg) of IM Dex-P, Beta-P, or a 1:1 injectable mixture of Beta-P + Beta-Ac in 48 non-pregnant, healthy South Asian women [22]. Results (Fig. 4) showed that 6 mg IM betamethasone had a half-life of approximately 11 h, twice as long as the 5.5 h for 6 mg IM dexamethasone. The 1:1 mixture of Beta-P and Beta-Ac showed an immediate release of betamethasone (from the phosphate) followed by a slow release of betamethasone (from a depot formed by the acetate) which could be measured up to 14 days after the single dose administration.

Concentration time profiles (PK) of steroids (6 mg dose) in South Asian non-pregnant women of reproductive age [22]. Dexamethasone IM refers to dexamethasone phosphate and betamethasone IM refers to betamethasone phosphate

Physiological changes during pregnancy may influence corticosteroid pharmacokinetics, including potential effects on distribution and clearance. However, direct comparative data between pregnant and non-pregnant women appear to be limited, as most antenatal corticosteroid pharmacokinetic studies have been conducted exclusively in pregnant populations without matched non-pregnant controls. As a result, quantitative estimates of pregnancy-related differences remain uncertain and would benefit from further dedicated comparative and population pharmacokinetic investigations.

Maternal levels with the lower dose ACS regimen

Figure 6 shows the predicted maternal plasma concentrations following administration of a lower dose regimen of Beta-P alone (2 mg, repeated every 12 h for 4 doses). Beta-P alone was of interest because (a) Beta-Ac was not prioritized because its depot formulation produces prolonged systemic exposure lasting upto 14 days (Fig. 4); and (b) Beta-Ac is not available as a stand-alone formulation for clinical use.

Predicted maternal plasma concentrations following Beta-P 2 mg every 12 h for a total of four doses and Dex-P 1.5 mg every 6 h for a total of 8 doses

For comparison, a corresponding lower-dose regimen of Dex-P (1.5 mg given every 6 h for 8 doses) was modeled, to serve as an alternative in settings where Beta-P is unavailable. As shown in Fig. 6, both regimens provide comparable maternal exposure.

The simulations predict that both lower-dose steroid regimens produce maternal peak-to-trough plasma concentrations ranging from 25 ng/mL to 5 ng/mL, corresponding to estimated fetal concentrations ranging between 1 and 4 ng/mL. Because Beta-P releases betamethasone rapidly, compared to Beta-Ac which releases the drug slowly, dosing Beta-P every 12 h is required to maintain adequate levels. Similarly, Dex-P is given every 6 h instead of every 12 h as for betamethasone, as dexamethasone has a shorter half-life compared to betamethasone, and more frequent dosing is required to sustain therapeutic levels.

Rationale for lower dose regimen choice in ACTION-III trial

Drug selection: Dexamethasone is more widely available in resource constrained settings and is less expensive. However, in a reduced dose regimen, it needs to be injected IM every 6 h because of its shorter half-life (Fig. 6). Twelve hourly injections of 1.5 mg dexamethasone also result in trough levels close to zero, thereby preventing the continuous low concentration exposure desired. Beta-P was therefore selected as its longer half-life allows for 12 hourly dosing, at a low dose of 2 mg.

Steroid salt selection: Beta-P monotherapy was selected to achieve a controlled and time-limited exposure profile. In the conventional 1:1 Beta-P + Beta-Ac formulation, the phosphate component produces a rapid peak concentration, while the acetate component results in prolonged systemic exposure that may persist for up to 14 days. Experimental data indicate that high peak concentrations do not enhance lung maturation beyond that achieved with sustained low exposure [27]. In contrast, prolonged systemic exposure may be associated with greater fetal hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis suppression and reduced birthweight in preclinical models [28].

The Beta-P + Beta-Ac combination (e.g., Celestone) produces an early peak from the phosphate component followed by prolonged exposure from the acetate component, whereas Beta-P alone allows repeated low dosing to maintain concentrations within the target therapeutic range while avoiding extended exposure

Dosing schedule: The evidence from animal and available human studies suggest that a prolonged fetal exposure of 1 to 4 ng/mL is adequate for lung maturity. Therefore, an appropriate regimen would be a maternal dosing schedule that would ensure these levels in the fetus. Based on population pharmacokinetic modeling, the Beta-P regimen that would provide fetal exposure of 1 to 4 ng/mL would be 2 mg every 12 h for four doses.

Route of administration: Intramuscular (IM) administration was selected because all proven clinical benefits of antenatal corticosteroids derive from randomized trials using the IM route. While studies in non-pregnant women suggest similar pharmacokinetics of oral and injected steroids [19], women at high probability of imminent preterm birth are frequently managed nil per os, limiting oral feasibility. Until alternative routes are validated in appropriately powered trials, IM administration remains the evidence-based standard of care.

Comparison with other low dose ACS trials

The BETADOSE trial [29] in France was a randomized, double-blind, controlled, non-inferiority trial comparing a standard dose regimen (11.4 mg of Beta P + Beta Ac, every 24 h for 2 doses) versus a reduced half-dose regimen (11.4 mg of Beta P + Beta Ac, single dose) in pregnant women at risk of preterm delivery at < 32 weeks’ gestation. The primary outcome was severe respiratory distress syndrome in preterm babies born < 32 weeks gestation. The choice of the half-dose regimen (one single dose of 11.4 mg) was perhaps pragmatic, based on formulation available and the possibility to build in a double-blind design with placebo. The trial was unable to demonstrate non-inferiority of the half-dose regimen.

The pharmacokinetics of the half-dose regimen used in the BETADOSE trial and the lower dose being used in the WHO ACTION-III trial (2 mg q12hrly for 4 doses) are different (Fig. 7). The half-dose regimen in the BETADOSE trial has a high peak concentration but a decreased duration of adequate exposure of 2 to 4 ng/mL. The dosing regimen in the WHO ACTION III trial, on the other hand, has a lower peak concentration and a longer duration of adequate exposure between 2 and 4 ng/mL. Physiological changes across gestation, including increased plasma volume, altered protein binding, enhanced CYP3A activity, and progressive changes in placental surface area and transporter expression, may influence corticosteroid pharmacokinetics. However, gestational age–specific human pharmacokinetic data for antenatal corticosteroids remain limited. Available cord blood studies across the studied preterm range suggest broadly similar fetal exposure levels, but later gestations have been less extensively characterized. The current modelling approach therefore applies gestational age–independent average parameters anchored to available maternal and cord blood data. Importantly, the reduced-dose strategy evaluated in ACTION-III is not intended for extrapolation to earlier gestational ages where benefit from standard dosing is well established.

Conclusion

The lower-dose betamethasone phosphate regimen selected for evaluation in the WHO ACTION-III trial was derived through integration of translational experimental data, human pharmacokinetic studies, and model-informed simulations. Converging evidence indicates that sustained fetal corticosteroid concentrations of 1 to 4 ng/mL are sufficient to induce lung maturation, whereas higher peaks confer no additional benefit and increase systemic exposure.

The proposed regimen of 2 mg betamethasone phosphate administered intramuscularly every 12 h for four doses is therefore expected to achieve therapeutic fetal levels required for lung maturation.

ACTION-III will provide critical evidence on whether reduced corticosteroid exposure can maintain clinical efficacy while potentially minimizing unnecessary fetal and maternal exposure in the late preterm population.
