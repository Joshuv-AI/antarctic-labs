 1. INTRODUCTION

The accurate accounting of nuclear materials is a cornerstone of international nuclear safeguards. One
emerging challenge in this domain is the fabrication of TRIstructural ISOtropic (TRISO) particle fuels.
Although these innovative fuel forms are critical for advanced reactor applications, their robust refractory
ceramics and coating compositions present significant obstacles to destructive analysis (DA) methods.
Ensuring full and quantitative recovery from these particles is essential for accurate mass accountancy. The
current study was initiated to address these challenges, first by validating a previously established
destructive method developed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) for the quantitative recovery of
uranium from TRISO particles and then following that process with uranium content determination through
isotope dilution mass spectrometry (IDMS) and Davies-Gray titration. This study expands on the scope of
a digestive method that was developed under the Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development and
Qualification program and is currently implemented in both the Coated Particle Fuel Development
Laboratory and Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory at ORNL. The success of the previous Advanced
Gas Reactor work relied on developing a DA method to evaluate the fabrication process and reactor
experiments [1-3].

The methodology described in this report was designed to rigorously investigate the efficacy of the
crush/burn/leach sample preparation of TRISO particles; it aims to quantify uranium recovery while also
assessing the effects of TRISO constituents (e.g., silicon and zirconium) on analytical precision and
accuracy. By comparing the results from the titration method and IDMS, we sought to determine whether
existing analytical procedures accepted by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) could be
effectively translated to TRISO fuel forms.

The team employed an approach that involved processing replicate TRISO samples, optimizing the milling
(i.e., crushing) step and performing serial leaches. The elemental composition of the analytical samples was
examined to prepare for interference studies in the second year of this project. The integration of gamma
spectrometry to verify residual uranium activity further strengthened the validation. Statistical methods
were applied to the collected data to evaluate the uncertainties arising from sampling, sample preparation
and uranium quantification. These uncertainties were then compared to the IAEA’s international target
values (ITVs). Additional data collected in upcoming project work will strengthen the uncertainty
estimates. Ultimately, it is hoped that this project will contribute materially to the body of work related to
characterization of TRISO based fuels for the purpose of material accountancy and its applications to
international safeguards.

 2. METHODS

2.1 DESTRUCTIVE ANALYSIS

TRISO fuel particles are heated at 900°C in air to burn off the outer pyrolytic carbon (OPyC) layer. The
residual SiC-coated particles are milled to a fine powder, exposing the uranium within the kernels. The
powder is heated in air to 750°C to burn off the remaining pyrocarbon from the inner pyrolytic carbon
(IPyC) and buffer coating layers and to oxidize the uranium to U3O8. The residue is leached twice with
concentrated nitric acid to dissolve uranium oxide. The acid leachates are then sent to the analytical
laboratory for measurement of total uranium content—by Davies-Gray titration and Isotope Dilution Mass
Spectrometry. The first leach is confirmed by ICPMS-IDMS (quadrapole) while the second leach is
confirmed by weighted external uranium calibration using ICPMS. The accuracy of the uranium-content
measurement is dependent on the completeness of the dissolution of the uranium contained in the residue
of the burned-back particles.

2.2 DAVIES-GRAY TITRATION

Davies-Gray titration [4] is a well-established analytical method for assaying uranium that relies on a redox
titration procedure to quantify the uranium content in a sample. The method’s robustness and versatility
enable it to be applied to a wide array of uranium forms, including pellets, metals, alloys, oxides, and mixed
oxide fuels. At ORNL, the method is accredited to the ISO/IEC-17025 standard by A2LA [5].
Approximately 20 mg of uranium is required for the analysis; at ORNL, 30 mg of uranium is typically
analyzed. Calibrated or standard reference material (SRM) potassium dichromate serves as the titrant (e.g.,
National Institute of Standards and Technology [NIST] SRM 136f), and a titrant factor, T (mg U/g titrant),
is calculated or standardized. In this case, independent preparations of National Nuclear Security
Administration (NNSA) Nuclear Material Reference Program (NMRP) certified reference material (CRM)
112-A are used to standardize the titrant and act as the method quality control. Before titration, chemical
treatment including iron (II) reduces all uranium in the sample to its tetravalent state; the potassium
dichromate then oxidizes the uranium from (IV) to (VI) until the potentiometric endpoint is reached. A pH
meter equipped with a platinum wire (16 gauge, 99.99%) and saturated calomel reference electrode is used
to measure the endpoint. Before it is used, the pH meter is verified for linearity using an Oakton pH/mV
simulator, and the electrodes are checked for accuracy using an oxidation-reduction potential standard
called Light’s solution.

In this work, the platinum wire was cleaned by flame before use each day. All chemicals were reagent grade
and were prepared immediately before titration. Phosphoric acid was treated with potassium dichromate
because of the presence of antimony.

2.3 ISOTOPE DILUTION MASS SPECTROMETRY

2.4 GAMMA SPECTROMETRY

At ORNL, high-purity germanium gamma spectrometry is used to detect and quantify isotopes in bulk
environmental samples for the IAEA Network of Analytical Laboratories. These analyses are then used to
inform subsequent, more sensitive analyses for uranium and plutonium. In general, 238U emits very weak
gamma rays, whereas 235U has a sharp and distinctive peak at 186 keV. Quantification is typically carried
out using calibration with standards or efficiency calibration curves. The detection limits depend on detector
efficiency and shielding, counting time, sample geometry, and self-attenuation of the sample. The filter
samples encountered in this work were at concentrations approaching the detection limits for 235U (tens of
micrograms) and 238U (hundreds of micrograms).

Four broad energy germanium detectors were employed for the counting. Each model 3830 detector was
mechanically cooled using Mirion Cryo-Pulse 5 Plus chillers, which kept the cold finger at −185°C. Signals
were processed digitally using Mirion Lynx spectroscopy systems driven by Mirion Genie 2000
multichannel analyzer software version 3.4.1. Counting occurred near the carbon fiber end cap of each
detector, and x-ray summing effects were mitigated by using 0.062 in. thick aluminum disk absorber.
Spectra were acquired in loss-free add back acquisition mode for count times between 72 and 91 h, and
backgrounds were acquired under identical conditions for 117 h. All spectra were reduced using PeakEasy
5.21 with peak search sensitivity set to 0.30. Regions of interest were uniformly applied to all spectra for
each detector, and count rates in each region were compared to calibration materials. Uranium nitrate
calibration solutions were prepared from purchased materials and pipetted gravimetrically onto cotton cloth
folded to exhibit counting geometries similar to those of the samples.

Uranium nitrate calibration solutions (natural isotopic abundances) were prepared from NNSA NRMP
CRM-112A and NIST SRM 3164 in the range of 5 to 210 µg total uranium. Each calibration material was
counted on each detector over the course of several days.

 3. EXPERIMENTAL

3.1 SOURCE MATERIALS

Zirconia-based surrogate TRISO particle samples were used for preliminary trials and served to
(1) visualize the logistics of iterative microscopic inspections during milling and (2) resolve safety concerns
related to conducting such inspections using pulverized uranium-bearing samples. Once this methodology
proved viable, it could be repeated with uranium-based TRISO.

Duplicate 10 g samples (Sample A and Sample B) of TRISO particles were obtained in a previous campaign
for analytical measurements. Samples A and B were sourced from the same production batch and comprise
particles containing a kernel composed of a UO2–UC2 mixture coated with a carbon buffer and then with
an IPyC layer, a SiC layer, and an OPyC coating. Although Sample A was studied in a previous
crush/burn/leach campaign, ORNL retained Sample B for this study, and the analytical results of both
samples are included herein to provide a larger dataset for statistical analysis.

A SPEX SamplePrep mixer/mill ball mill (model 8000M) was used to mill (i.e., crush) the TRISO particles
(Figure 1a); particle sublots were loaded into 30 mL HDPE screw cap bottles (Figure 1b) with Ø3 mm
zirconia milling media (Figure 1c) and housed in an aluminum milling canister (Figure 1b). Figure 1d
displays the canister-securing clamp assembly and shaker apparatus within the mixer/mill housing.

A Leica M205 C stereomicroscope was used to image samples of crushed particles for the mill time

residues were achieved with the aid of an Eppendorf 5804 centrifuge. The radiological activity of post-
leach residues was assessed using a Bicron M portable radiation survey meter. Other assorted consumables
used included 50 mL polyethylene Falcon centrifuge tubes, Whatman Grade 42 ashless filter paper, NERL
reagent grade water, and polyethylene press-to-close bags.

3.3 MATERIAL PREPARATION FOR SAMPLING

The particle samples were separated into sublots via rotary riffling to ensure random sampling. Separation
in this manner ensured that each sublot retained the same proportion of variation in physical properties from
particle to particle present in the original as-supplied sample—and, thus, that each sublot was representative
of the original sample. Figure 2 shows the source material and illustrates the sample division process.

Sample B (10 g) was riffled into 10 sublots of approximately 1 g each. Three 1 g sublots were designated
as B1, B2, and B3 and set aside as replicates for final testing, and the remaining seven were available to
serve as trial sublots for mill time optimization.

3.4 MILL TIME OPTIMIZATION

Trial particle sublots (1 g each) were poured into clean quartz flasks and then placed in the air-fed muffle
furnace and burned for 3 h at 900°C to remove the OPyC layer, leaving SiC-coated particles for milling.
Two zirconia-based surrogate TRISO sublots (1 g each) were prepared in this manner, as were two of the
1 g trial sublots from Sample B.

New HDPE bottles were loaded with approximately 3 g each of the zirconia milling media; one particle
trial sublot (1 g) was added to each bottle, and the caps were sealed with vinyl tape. Each bottle was then,
in turn, placed inside the aluminum milling canister; the cap was sealed with vinyl tape, and the canister
was secured in the clamp assembly of the ball mill and milled for the determined interval.

The time required to completely mill all particles (hereafter referred to as fully milled) in each sublot was
determined by iterative visual inspection; at each of the predetermined interval time stamps of 1, 3, 5, 7,
10, 15, and 20 min, milling was interrupted, and the particle debris was poured from the current trial bottle
into an aluminum weigh pan. A sifting motion was used to spread the partially pulverized samples within
the pan, and the powder-form debris was examined beneath the stereomicroscope. Broad-tipped tweezers
were used to scour the debris and expose any intact particles in time stamps beyond 7 min. Representative
photomicrographs were collected at each time stamp to document the presence or absence of intact particles
among the debris, thereby establishing the total milling time after which no intact particles were observed.

This overall optimization process was performed on two zirconia-based surrogate TRISO sublots and
repeated on two trial sublots riffled from UCO TRISO Sample B. Note that this optimization was performed

 9

only for 1 g sublots, and repeating these experiments with larger sublots will be required in future work,
with the minimum and maximum sample size indicated in the method.

3.5 CRUSH/BURN/LEACH PROCESSING [6]

3.5.1    Milling

Once an optimized 1 g mill time was selected, Sample B sublots B1, B2, and B3 were burned at 900°C as
described previously. They were then pulverized according to the milling method for the optimized milling
time established by the results of the iterative inspection trials. Unlike the trial sublots, these test sublots
remained sealed in their bottles throughout milling and no debris were inspected.

3.5.2 Burn Processing

The unopened bottles of as-milled material were returned to the same quartz flasks used previously for the
OPyC burn-back of bare particles. The flasks were placed in the muffle furnace and heated using a program
to first melt and burn the polyethylene bottles and then burn the pyrocarbon from the powder-form sublots
at 750°C for 5 h. Burning in this manner ensured that all milled material was preserved throughout the
burning and leaching phases such that the resulting leachate would accurately reflect the total uranium
content leached from the particle residue.

3.5.3 Leach Processing

Concentrated (67%–70%) nitric acid was added to each flask to cover the powder sublots by approximately
1 cm of liquid depth. The flasks were heated and held at a moderate boil condition for approximately 4 h
in the power-controlled heating mantles (Figure 3). The liquid from each flask was decanted into new 50
mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuged for 15 min at 4,250 rpm. These centrifuged samples were further
decanted into new 50 mL sample tubes. The condenser and quartz vessel were rinsed with 5 mL
concentrated nitric acid into the centrifuge tube, shaken, centrifuged again, and then decanted. This step
was then repeated once more. The final leachate samples were submitted for analysis via Davies-Gray
titration and IDMS. These samples are referred to in the remainder of the document as Leach 1.

The centrifuge tube residues were returned to their flasks; new nitric acid was added to each tube, and the
samples were leached for an additional 4 h in the same manner as in the first iteration. A second set of liquid
samples was produced in the same manner as in Leach 1, and the final leachate samples were likewise
collected and submitted for analysis for uranium content. These samples are referred to as Leach 2.

 10

 Figure 3. Equipment setup for the leaching and dissolution of uranium in nitric acid.

3.5.4 Residue Preparation

At this stage, the sublot residues consisted of both the milling media and the leached TRISO pulp, which
was assumed to consist primarily of SiC fragments. The residues from each flask and corresponding
centrifuge tube from Leach 2 were rinsed with ASTM Type I water into separate funnels formed from
folded circles of ashless filter paper. The rinse water was permitted to fully drain, and then the funnel-bound
residue was rinsed with water a second time. The filtrate water was discarded after it fully drained, and the
rinsed residues were left to dry in air in a fume hood. The water used to rinse the residue will be analyzed
for uranium recovery in the upcoming year’s work. The dry residue funnels were flattened and sealed in
new press-to-close bags for further characterization.

A particle sample processed by this crush/burn/leach method is deemed complete when the activity of the
residue is less than or equal to the activity of an exemplar of 0.1% of the whole. In this instance, in which
1 g sublots were processed, each exemplar consisted of a single untreated TRISO particle.

Six exemplars were prepared, comprising three single unprocessed Sample B particles placed in new filter
paper funnels and three single previously unprocessed Sample B particles crushed with tweezers while
contained within filter paper funnels. A blank filter paper funnel was prepared as a control baseline.

3.5.5 Activity Check

A cursory activity check of each dry residue was performed using the portable radiation survey meter set
to 10× sensitivity. Activity was recorded in kilovolts and counts per minute.

The activity of the three post-leach B1, B2, and B3 residues, the six untreated exemplars, and the filter
paper blank was ascertained and recorded. A total of 10 filter paper samples (Figure 4) were loaded into
acrylic petri dishes and submitted for gamma spectroscopy of uranium traces.

 4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

4.1 MILL TIME OPTIMIZATION

The surrogate trials proved valuable in both the framing of details of microscopic inspection during milling
and in demonstrating the process to radiological control technicians so they could deem the operation safe.
Regarding the former point, it quickly became evident that because the zirconia kernels were not being
pulverized by the zirconia media during milling, accurate identification of the time stamp at which no intact
particles could be observed could be skewed in the surrogate sublots. Regarding the latter point, work with
powder-form uranium is typically permitted only within the confines of a fume hood. However, by
loading/unloading the sample debris in/out of the milling bottle inside the hood and using multiple layers
of containment when transferring the debris between the hood and the stereomicroscope, the new work
method required by these experiments could be approved—even with the repetitive action of doing so after
each milling time interval.

Although the surrogate kernels remained intact after milling, they were distinctly smaller than the intact
particles observed in these trials and could easily be identified as such. Bare zirconia kernels were
considered crushed when crushed versus uncrushed specimens were tabulated. Particles that were only
partially crushed—that is, nonintact particles whose kernel retained some degree of layer attachment—were
categorized as uncrushed.

Figure 5 depicts photomicrographs captured at six key interval time stamps of those utilized in the mill time
optimization trials. The images taken at 0, 3, and 5 min are from the surrogate trials; they highlight the
zirconia kernels against the carbon and SiC debris and demonstrate the size discrepancy between the
zirconia kernels and the intact particles and milling media around them. Images taken at 7, 10, and 15 min
are from the uranium-based trial sublots, and intact specimens are circled to aid in differentiating them from
the surrounding debris types.

To account for the possibility of attaining only incomplete milling at 15 min and because the additional
time is not outside reasonable laboratory practice, 20 min was selected as the optimized milling time for
the crushing of 1 g UCO TRISO sublots. This milling time was deemed to be a balance between adequately
economizing process time and more confidently ensuring complete milling of the TRISO particles before
the burn and leach phases.

It should be emphasized that the optimized milling time of 20 min applies only to 1 g sublots of UCO
TRISO particles. To broaden the scope of such optimization, trials are planned with 2 and 3 g sublots in
future extensions of the present work.

4.3 GAMMA ANALYSIS OF FILTER RESIDUES

After extensive destructive preparation of the TRISO particles, including mechanical processing and acid
refluxing, DA techniques were deemed to have been exhausted for recovering further uranium. Therefore,

 5. SAMPLING AND METHOD UNCERTAINTIES

The purpose of this section is to assess the quality of the bulk measurements of TRISO fuel via DA and
compare these to ITVs issued by the IAEA [7]. In part, the IAEA performs statistical analysis to calculate
random and systematic measurement uncertainty estimates to reach the ITVs. The ITVs represent
“measurement uncertainties that should be achievable under nominal conditions by typical nuclear fuel
cycle (NFC) facility laboratories or during safeguards inspections [7].” In the IAEA’s most recent
reevaluation of the ITVs, significant effort was made to reconcile the IAEA statistical error model-based
uncertainty quantification approach with the Guide to the expression of uncertainty in measurement
(GUM).

“ITVs are values of uncertainties, expressed as relative random and systematic error standard deviations
associated with a single determination, which should be achievable under nominal conditions by a typical
NFC facility laboratory on one sample analysis or as the result of an NDA measurement performed on a
single item. It is important to note that ITVs should not be interpreted as uncertainties that can only be
achieved under ideal laboratory conditions. Likewise, they do not represent regulatory requirements, but
rather a motivating goal—as their name ‘target values’ suggests—and a reference for laboratories and
safeguards inspectors and evaluators” [7].

The IAEA has many years’ worth of operator–inspector paired measurements, but this quantity of data does
not exist for novel TRISO fuel forms; thus, we propose an approximation of the ITV based on a limited
dataset. Historical data are insufficient or not available for all nondestructive analysis (NDA) and DA
methods listed in the ITVs; in such instances, technically based uncertainties can be provided by subject
matter experts and listed as prospective ITVs.

5.1 DAVIES-GRAY TITRATION/IDMS METHOD COMPARISON

In this section, the results of the Davies-Gray measurements are compared statistically to the results of the
IDMS measurements for Sample B only. Sample A is not considered here because this is a measurement
method comparison; thus, we compare measurements taken on the same sample. The ITV for Davies-Gray

5.2 SAMPLING UNCERTAINTIES

The following annotation from De Bièvre et al. [8] gives an excellent summary of the determination and
proper treatment of a sampling uncertainty for a specific material: “Random as well as systematic
uncertainties in sampling depend on numerous factors such as the nature of the material, its homogeneity
and stability, the size of the batch or item, the technique of sampling, the size of the sample, the mode of
handling the samples, etc. The stability and homogeneity of the material are themselves a function of the
fabrication process, the conditions and time of storage.”

Here, a top-down approach to giving a prospective ITV sampling uncertainty based on laboratory
measurements is described. This set of measurements is a limited set, and more data will be collected in the

 24

coming year for this project. This statistical analysis also bears the caveat that the sample range is 1 to 3 g
of material. Importantly, the method of sampling, which included riffling (as mentioned in previous
sections), is applied to this statistical analysis and is considered requisite whenever this uncertainty is
applied. Here, the riffling sampling method was applied at the sample level but not in the preceding step
when Sample A and Sample B were pulled from the parent batch. Finally, because these data include results
for Sample A, which was prepared before milling time was optimized, the proposed prospective sampling
uncertainty may change significantly as new data are collected.

The sampling uncertainty can be taken as the combination of the random and systematic components of
uncertainty. In this dataset, systematic uncertainty is considered to be the issue of quantitative recovery of
the crush/burn/leach sample preparation; this uncertainty was determined to be 0.1% relative, as confirmed
by gamma spectroscopy and a simple activity check. This uncertainty is considered to be systematic because
by its nature, a negative bias from the true value for the material is always inherently possible because not
all uranium is recovered. Conservatively, for this initial dataset, we consider this value to represent a
coverage level of k = 1. No reference material currently exists for TRISO particles; thus, comparison to a
certified result was not possible in considering systematic bias. Confusion still remains with regard to how
to apply this systematic bias because, technically, it is not part of sampling but rather sample preparation.
Conversely, this systematic bias is also not part of the method of measurement. Considering these things,
it is applied here in the sampling uncertainty due to the nature of the sample and the difficulty of recovering
uranium from TRISO particles.

The random component of the uncertainty is taken considering the Davies-Gray measurements for Sample
A and the mean of the Davies-Gray and IDMS results for Sample B. Using both Davies-Gray and IDMS
data independently for Sample B would skew the ANOVA by using three groups. Sample A and Sample B
give their own analytical results, and for each sample two nested random sources of variation are present—
between-sample variance (between Sample A and Sample B) and within-sample variance (analytical
repeatability). For random sampling uncertainty, we isolate the uncertainty from sampling the material—
that is, between-sample variance, not analytical repeatability. These data should consider the inhomogeneity
of the material, because they were sampled from the same batch, and random uncertainty of sample
processing, because these samples went through a total of six sample preparations. ANOVA was used to
calculate the between-sample standard deviation using the following equation and values determined by

 6. CONCLUSION

This study presents initial data collected to validate a DA methodology for quantitative uranium recovery
from TRISO fuel particles, demonstrating both the practicality and precision of the crush/burn/leach
approach coupled with complementary analytical techniques. Optimizing milling time and evaluating leach
completeness ensured near-total uranium recovery (~99.9%). Gamma spectroscopy provided further
verification of the residue’s uranium content consistent with the method’s 0.1% recovery threshold. The
agreement of uranium content measurements using IDMS and Davies-Gray titration when considering the
GUM uncertainty and application of each method’s ITV indicate each measurement method should yield a
sample that falls under previously determined ITV values. In contrast, the prospective sampling uncertainty
(0.21% relative uncertainty, k = 1) for a uranium mass fraction measurement is greater than existing ITVs
for materials such as UF6, uranium oxide pellets, and uranium nitrate solutions but is approximately
equivalent to existing ITVs for uranium oxide powder, uranium oxides with Gd and uranium metals [7].

Overall, this work provides a critical foundation for method validation of DA procedures for advanced fuel
forms. The demonstrated agreement between independent analytical techniques, consistency with ITV
criteria, and uncertainty assessments establish confidence in uranium recovery and measurement accuracy
in the absence of a reference material. In future efforts, we will extend this foundation by conducting
targeted interference studies, refining uncertainties—particularly in sampling—and collecting more data on
sample sizes varying from 1 to 3 g.
