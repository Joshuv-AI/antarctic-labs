A total-Lagrangian vectorial lattice Boltzmann method for finite-strain hyperelastic dynamics
Jingsen Feng, Xu Chu

Abstract
Inspired by the vectorial lattice Boltzmann method for linear elastodynamics [ 1 ] , we construct a total-Lagrangian vectorial lattice Boltzmann formulation for two-dimensional finite-strain hyperelastic dynamics. The governing equations are first written as a conservative first-order system for the material velocity and the full deformation gradient. This representation separates the kinematic part of the dynamics from the constitutive closure: the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress is evaluated locally from the current deformation gradient and enters the lattice only through nonlinear flux moments. A D2Q4 stencil with six-component vector populations is then used to match the state and the two material-coordinate fluxes. The formulation includes a second-order population initialization, trapezoidally centered body forcing, displacement reconstruction by velocity quadrature, and half-way reconstructions for velocity Dirichlet and Neumann traction boundaries on grid-aligned domains. The resulting method preserves the local collide–stream structure of standard lattice Boltzmann schemes while adapting the vectorial first-order strategy from linear elastodynamics to hyperelastic finite-strain dynamics.

Introduction

The lattice Boltzmann method (LBM)
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
is designed to approximate solutions to a simplified kinetic equation by evolving a finite set of populations associated with discrete microscopic velocities. The gas-kinetic origin of these populations gives the method a local collide–stream structure
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. This structure is attractive for explicit time integration and parallel implementation. In its native form, the LBM recovers the nearly incompressible Navier–Stokes equations through statistical moments of the populations and has become a mature tool in fluid dynamics and transport
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. These algorithmic features have also motivated attempts to use LBM in solid mechanics.

The extension of LBM to solids is motivated by the possibility of retaining locality, explicitness, and favorable parallel scaling for continuum-mechanics problems. A natural starting point is linear elasticity. For linear elastostatics, Yin et al.
[
13
]
proposed an LBM based on displacement distribution functions for the linear elastic Lamé equation; this line of work was later characterized as a diffusion-type pseudo-time formulation whose steady state solves the elliptic equations of linear elastostatics
[
1
]
. Building on this idea, Boolakee et al. developed second-order accurate lattice Boltzmann schemes for quasi-static linear elasticity and corresponding second-order Dirichlet and Neumann boundary formulations on arbitrary curved two-dimensional domains
[
14
,
15
]
. These works demonstrated that LBM can be constructed systematically for linear elastostatic boundary-value problems. At the same time, as noted in subsequent work
[
1
]
, the need to march in pseudo-time until a steady state is reached limits the competitiveness of such approaches for static problems when compared with established finite element methods.

For solid dynamics, especially problems involving elastic-wave propagation, the algorithmic structure of LBM is more naturally aligned with the target equations. Early attempts include the lattice Boltzmann model for solid-body dynamics of Marconi and Chopard
[
16
]
and the elastic-wave formulation of O’Brien et al. for Poisson solids
[
17
]
. Murthy et al.
[
18
]
later introduced a lattice Boltzmann formulation for elastic wave propagation with a tunable Poisson ratio, based on a moment-chain construction. Escande et al.
[
19
]
provided a theoretical and stability analysis of a regular-lattice version of this approach and validated it for bulk and surface waves, while related developments addressed crack loading and boundary conditions for elastodynamics
[
20
,
21
]
. These contributions showed that LBM can reproduce transient elastic responses and wave propagation in solids. Nevertheless, formulations based on scalar populations and standard velocity sets have limited freedom to represent the coupled flux structure of elasticity. In practice, moment-chain schemes have relied on additional moment equations, finite-difference corrections, or artificial dissipation to supply missing quantities and improve stability
[
1
]
. As a consequence, second-order consistency, stability for arbitrary material parameters, and accurate boundary treatment remain delicate issues
[
21
,
1
]
.

Within this scalar-population moment-chain framework, a recent development has extended the approach to geometrically and constitutively nonlinear elastodynamics
[
22
]
. In that formulation, stresses and deformation measures are evaluated in the reference configuration, and nonlinear constitutive behavior is incorporated through a forcing term. The method demonstrates the feasibility of LBM for finite-strain solid dynamics through benchmark problems such as uniaxial tension, simple shear, and bending waves. However, it still relies on finite-difference evaluations of gradients and divergences, and the nonlinear Piola stress does not enter the lattice through a direct moment representation of the physical fluxes. This leaves open the question of whether finite-strain hyperelastic dynamics can be formulated in a way that remains closer to the basic lattice Boltzmann algorithm while treating the solid-mechanics fluxes as primary moment-matched quantities.

In this paper, we pursue this direction using vectorial LBM. The key modification of a vectorial formulation is the use of vector-valued populations instead of scalar ones, while preserving the local collision and streaming steps. Vectorial kinetic formulations have been used in several contexts, including magnetohydrodynamics
[
23
]
, hyperbolic conservation laws and relaxation schemes
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
, and incompressible-flow discretizations with boundary and stability analysis
[
27
]
. Most relevant to the present work, Boolakee et al.
[
1
]
recently introduced a vectorial LBM for linear elastodynamics by first rewriting the displacement equation as an equivalent first-order hyperbolic system. They established second-order consistency, stability estimates under a CFL-like condition, second-order population initialization, and Dirichlet boundary conditions on rectangular domains. This result indicates that vector-valued populations provide the additional degrees of freedom needed to represent the state and fluxes of coupled solid-dynamics systems.

We construct here a total-Lagrangian vectorial lattice Boltzmann formulation for two-dimensional finite-strain hyperelastic dynamics. The governing equations are first written as a conservative first-order system for the material velocity and the full deformation gradient. In this representation the lattice remains fixed in the reference configuration, while the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress is evaluated locally from the current deformation gradient and enters the method through nonlinear flux moments. A D2Q4 stencil with six-component vector populations is then used to match the macroscopic state and the two material-coordinate fluxes. The formulation includes trapezoidally centered body forcing, a second-order population initialization, displacement reconstruction by velocity quadrature, and half-way reconstructions for velocity Dirichlet and nominal-traction Neumann boundaries on grid-aligned domains. Numerical experiments verify the construction for manufactured solutions, finite-strain benchmark tests, several hyperelastic constitutive laws, acoustic-tensor wave speeds, finite-amplitude periodic waves, and a bounded-domain cantilever bending wave.

2
Total-Lagrangian finite-strain elastodynamics

This section prepares the total-Lagrangian equations for the vectorial lattice Boltzmann discretization. We work on a fixed reference domain
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with material coordinate
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})^{T}=(X,Y)^{T}
. The current position is
\bm{x}(\bm{X},t)
, the displacement is
\bm{u}(\bm{X},t)=\bm{x}(\bm{X},t)-\bm{X}
, and the material velocity is
\bm{v}=\partial_{t}\bm{u}
. We write
\partial_{A}=\partial/\partial X_{A}
, use
\nabla_{\!X}\bm{u}
for the material displacement gradient, and denote the identity tensor by
\bm{I}
. Latin indices
i,k=1,2
denote spatial components, capital indices
A,B=1,2
denote material-coordinate directions, and repeated indices are summed.

Let
W(\bm{F})
be the strain-energy density per unit reference volume,
\bm{P}(\bm{F})=\partial W/\partial\bm{F}
the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress, and
\bm{b}=(b_{1},b_{2})^{T}
the prescribed body acceleration. In the nondimensional displacement variables used below, the hyperelastic equations can be written as
[
29
,
30
,
31
]

\partial_{t}^{2}u_{i}=\partial_{A}P_{iA}(\bm{F})+b_{i},\qquad\bm{F}=\bm{I}+\nabla_{\!X}\bm{u},\qquad P_{iA}(\bm{F})=\frac{\partial W}{\partial F_{iA}}(\bm{F}).

(1)

Equation (
1
) combines material momentum balance with the hyperelastic constitutive map. The stress divergence depends on displacement gradients through
\bm{F}=\bm{I}+\nabla_{\!X}\bm{u}
, and the equation is second order in time. Classical LBM is naturally suited to first-order conservation and balance systems, since its local collision and streaming update is closed through moments representing macroscopic states and fluxes. For hyperelasticity, the corresponding step is to lower the order of Eq. (
1
): we introduce
\bm{v}=\partial_{t}\bm{u}
, evolve
\bm{F}
directly, and obtain a first-order system. The vectorial LBM used below then carries this first-order system with vector-valued populations whose moments recover the state and the two material-coordinate fluxes.

2.1
Scaling and reference description

The total-Lagrangian description is the natural setting for a fixed Cartesian lattice: lattice nodes remain stationary, material points are indexed by their reference coordinates, and the stress divergence is written in terms of the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress. We nondimensionalize by a reference length
L_{0}
, density
\rho_{0}
, and velocity
V_{0}
. Thus
\bm{X}=\bm{X}^{\rm dim}/L_{0}
,
\bm{x}=\bm{x}^{\rm dim}/L_{0}
,
\bm{u}=\bm{u}^{\rm dim}/L_{0}
,
t=V_{0}t^{\rm dim}/L_{0}
, and
\bm{v}=\bm{v}^{\rm dim}/V_{0}
. The stress, body acceleration, and Lame parameters are scaled by

\bm{P}=\frac{\bm{P}^{\rm dim}}{\rho_{0}V_{0}^{2}},\qquad\bm{b}=\frac{L_{0}\bm{b}^{\rm dim}}{V_{0}^{2}},\qquad\lambda=\frac{\lambda^{\rm dim}}{\rho_{0}V_{0}^{2}},\qquad\mu=\frac{\mu^{\rm dim}}{\rho_{0}V_{0}^{2}}.

The deformation gradient
\bm{F}=\partial\bm{x}/\partial\bm{X}
is dimensionless because it is a ratio of two lengths. If
V_{0}
is chosen as the infinitesimal longitudinal wave speed
c_{L,0}^{\rm dim}=\sqrt{(\lambda^{\rm dim}+2\mu^{\rm dim})/\rho_{0}}
, then
\lambda+2\mu=1
. Other choices of
V_{0}
simply rescale the nondimensional moduli.

The lattice spacing and time step used in Section
3
are
\Delta x=\Delta X^{\rm dim}/L_{0}
and
\Delta t=V_{0}\Delta t^{\rm dim}/L_{0}
, so the nondimensional lattice speed is
c=\Delta x/\Delta t=\Delta X^{\rm dim}/(V_{0}\Delta t^{\rm dim})
. All variables below are nondimensional.

2.2
Kinematics, balance laws, and boundary conditions

Finite-strain dynamics are carried by the deformation gradient
\bm{F}=\bm{I}+\nabla_{\!X}\bm{u}
and its determinant
J=\det\bm{F}
. The admissible range is
J>0
, since loss of orientation makes the hyperelastic stress and the Piola transformation ill-defined. In the present two-dimensional formulation
\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
. Plane-strain or plane-stress reductions of a three-dimensional material require separate constitutive reductions, which are outside the scope of this formulation.

Introducing
\bm{v}=\partial_{t}\bm{u}
in Eq. (
1
) and evolving
\bm{F}=\bm{I}+\nabla_{\!X}\bm{u}
directly gives the nondimensional first-order system

\partial_{t}v_{i}=\partial_{A}P_{iA}(\bm{F})+b_{i},\qquad\partial_{t}F_{iA}=\partial_{A}v_{i}.

(2)

The first equation is the momentum balance from Eq. (
1
) written for velocity. The second is the kinematic identity obtained by differentiating
F_{iA}=\delta_{iA}+\partial_{A}u_{i}
in time. Thus Eq. (
2
) is the order-reduced form of the displacement equation. Once
\bm{F}
is known at a lattice node, a hyperelastic stress can be evaluated locally; no finite-difference reconstruction of strain from displacement is needed in the bulk.

The initial conditions are prescribed as
\bm{u}(\bm{X},0)=\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{X})
and
\bm{v}(\bm{X},0)=\bm{v}_{0}(\bm{X})
, which imply
\bm{F}(\bm{X},0)=\bm{I}+\nabla_{\!X}\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{X})
. This initialization automatically satisfies the compatibility relation
\partial_{X}F_{i2}-\partial_{Y}F_{i1}=0
at
t=0
. For smooth solutions, Eq. (
2
) preserves this compatibility at the continuum level.

Let the boundary be split into a displacement part
\Gamma_{D}
and a traction part
\Gamma_{N}
. On
\Gamma_{D}
, the physical condition is
\bm{u}=\bm{u}_{D}
. The first-order state evolves velocity, so the lattice boundary condition uses the corresponding boundary velocity
\bm{v}_{D}=\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{D}
. On
\Gamma_{N}
, the Neumann condition is written in total-Lagrangian form as

\bm{P}(\bm{F})\bm{N}=\bar{\bm{T}}\qquad\text{on }\Gamma_{N},

(3)

where
\bm{N}
is the outward unit normal to the reference boundary and
\bar{\bm{T}}
is the prescribed nominal traction per unit reference length/area. This is the Neumann condition expressed in reference variables. The spatial traction
\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}=\bar{\bm{t}}
and the nominal traction are related by Nanson’s formula,
\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}\,\mathrm{d}a=\bm{P}\bm{N}\,\mathrm{d}A_{0}
with
\bm{P}=J\bm{\sigma}\bm{F}^{-T}

[
30
,
31
]
. Thus the total-Lagrangian prescribed traction is
\bm{P}\bm{N}
; a spatial traction
\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}
must first be converted to the reference configuration.

The boundary reconstructions in Section
3.6
are written for grid-aligned rectangular boundaries, so
\bm{N}\in\{(1,0),(-1,0),(0,1),(0,-1)\}
. The half-way D2Q4 boundary construction imposes this algorithmic restriction.

2.3
Hyperelastic closure

The lattice method requires only a pointwise map from deformation gradient to stress and, for initialization, wave-speed estimates, and traction-boundary Newton iterations, its tangent. For a hyperelastic material with strain-energy density
W(\bm{F})
, these quantities are

\bm{P}(\bm{F})=\frac{\partial W}{\partial\bm{F}},\qquad\mathbb{C}_{iAkB}(\bm{F})=\frac{\partial P_{iA}}{\partial F_{kB}},\qquad\delta P_{iA}=\mathbb{C}_{iAkB}\,\delta F_{kB}.

(4)

These two quantities play different roles in the first-order system below. The stress
\bm{P}(\bm{F})
supplies the stress components of the material-coordinate flux vectors
\bm{\Phi}_{X}
and
\bm{\Phi}_{Y}
in Eq. (
7
). The tangent
\mathbb{C}
supplies the corresponding flux derivatives with respect to
\bm{F}
, which enter the Jacobian actions in Eq. (
8
).

The numerical method does not depend on a particular strain-energy form. The implementation used in the examples includes St. Venant–Kirchhoff, compressible neo-Hookean, logarithmic neo-Hookean, Mooney–Rivlin, Yeoh, and Gent closures. Their explicit
W(\bm{F})
and
\bm{P}(\bm{F})
are collected in Appendix
A
. In the main algorithm, any admissible hyperelastic law can be used as long as
\bm{P}(\bm{F})
and the tangent actions in Eq. (
4
) are available for
J>0
. The local wave-speed estimates used below also assume that the tangent remains strongly elliptic over the simulated states.

2.4
First-order system used by the lattice method

The vectorial LBM will approximate Eq. (
2
) through moments of six-component populations. The macroscopic state is

\bm{U}=(v_{1},v_{2},F_{11},F_{12},F_{21},F_{22})^{T}.

(5)

With this ordering, the coupled velocity–deformation-gradient equations become

\partial_{t}\bm{U}+\partial_{X}\bm{\Phi}_{X}(\bm{U})+\partial_{Y}\bm{\Phi}_{Y}(\bm{U})=\bm{B},\qquad\bm{B}=(b_{1},b_{2},0,0,0,0)^{T},

(6)

where the two material-coordinate fluxes are

\bm{\Phi}_{X}(\bm{U})=(-P_{11},-P_{21},-v_{1},0,-v_{2},0)^{T},\qquad\bm{\Phi}_{Y}(\bm{U})=(-P_{12},-P_{22},0,-v_{1},0,-v_{2})^{T}.

(7)

The signs follow from writing the equations in the conservative form
\partial_{t}\bm{U}+\partial_{A}\bm{\Phi}_{A}=\bm{B}
. Taking the divergence of
\bm{\Phi}_{X}
and
\bm{\Phi}_{Y}
gives
-\partial_{A}P_{iA}
in the velocity rows and
-\partial_{A}v_{i}
in the deformation-gradient rows, so Eq. (
6
) is exactly Eq. (
2
).

This representation is the finite-strain analogue of the first-order linear elastodynamic system used in vectorial LBM. The essential change is that the stress entries in
\bm{\Phi}_{X}
and
\bm{\Phi}_{Y}
are nonlinear functions of the last four components of
\bm{U}
. Consequently, the flux Jacobians
\bm{A}_{X}=\partial\bm{\Phi}_{X}/\partial\bm{U}
and
\bm{A}_{Y}=\partial\bm{\Phi}_{Y}/\partial\bm{U}
are state dependent. Their action on an increment
\delta\bm{U}=(\delta v_{1},\delta v_{2},\delta F_{11},\delta F_{12},\delta F_{21},\delta F_{22})^{T}
is

\bm{A}_{X}\delta\bm{U}=(-\delta P_{11},-\delta P_{21},-\delta v_{1},0,-\delta v_{2},0)^{T},\quad\bm{A}_{Y}\delta\bm{U}=(-\delta P_{12},-\delta P_{22},0,-\delta v_{1},0,-\delta v_{2})^{T},

(8)

with
\delta P_{iA}=\mathbb{C}_{iAkB}\delta F_{kB}
. These Jacobian–vector products are sufficient for initialization and local wave-speed estimates, allowing the implementation to avoid dense
6\times 6
matrices.

For a unit material direction
\bm{n}
, define the total-Lagrangian acoustic tensor
[
29
,
30
]

Q_{ik}(\bm{n};\bm{F})=n_{A}\mathbb{C}_{iAkB}(\bm{F})n_{B}.

(9)

When the material is strongly elliptic,
\bm{a}\cdot Q(\bm{n};\bm{F})\bm{a}>0
for all nonzero
\bm{a}
and all unit
\bm{n}
. The eigenvalues of
Q
are the squared local tangent wave speeds in direction
\bm{n}
, after the reference density has been absorbed by nondimensionalization. This quantity is used in Section
4
to audit the lattice speed against the largest tangent wave speed encountered in the validation cases.

3
D2Q4
\times
6 vectorial lattice Boltzmann discretization

The previous section reduced finite-strain hyperelastic dynamics to a first-order system with one state vector and two physical fluxes. The vectorial LBM now has a clear task: store enough population information at each node to reconstruct
\bm{U}
,
\bm{\Phi}_{X}(\bm{U})
, and
\bm{\Phi}_{Y}(\bm{U})
by moments. This is the same design principle used in the linear vectorial elastodynamic scheme, where vector-valued populations enable a first-order hyperbolic system to be approximated while preserving the local collide–stream structure of LBM
[
1
]
. The finite-strain extension keeps the algebraic moment structure and replaces constant elastic fluxes by the nonlinear Piola fluxes.

3.1
Lattice populations and what their moments represent

The material domain is discretized by a uniform Cartesian lattice with spacing
\Delta x=\Delta y
and time step
\Delta t
. The four D2Q4 directions are
\mathcal{V}=\{(1,0),(0,1),(-1,0),(0,-1)\}
, and the velocity associated with
q=(i,j)
is
\bm{c}_{q}=c(i,j)
, where
c=\Delta x/\Delta t
. For each direction, the method stores a six-component population
\bm{f}_{ij}(\bm{X},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
. Here “population” denotes a numerical carrier of moments, with no gas-kinetic molecular interpretation for the solid model.

Each D2Q4 population is a six-component vector, so the four lattice directions provide four independent vector combinations. The continuum system requires three of them: the zeroth moment gives the state, and the two first moments give the
X
- and
Y
-fluxes. The remaining vector degree of freedom has no continuum counterpart; we fix it by setting the symmetric difference between the horizontal and vertical population pairs to zero. This gives a unique nearest-neighbour equilibrium. With body acceleration, the state is recovered as

\bm{U}^{\rm num}(\bm{X},t)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{V}}\bm{f}_{ij}(\bm{X},t)+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\bm{B}(\bm{X},t).

(10)

The half-step source shift is the discrete analogue of trapezoidal source centering. It makes the body force contribute to the recovered state without contaminating the flux moments.

3.2
Equilibrium distribution by moment matching

The equilibrium populations are defined by requiring their moments to reproduce the continuum quantities:

\sum_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}=\bm{U},\qquad\sum_{q}ci\,\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}=\bm{\Phi}_{X}(\bm{U}),\qquad\sum_{q}cj\,\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}=\bm{\Phi}_{Y}(\bm{U}),\qquad\sum_{q}c^{2}(i^{2}-j^{2})\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}=\bm{0}.

(11)

Here and below
q=(i,j)
, and
\sum_{q}
means summation over
\mathcal{V}
. Solving these four vector equations gives the compact expression

\bm{f}_{ij}^{eq}(\bm{U})=\frac{1}{4}\left[\bm{U}+\frac{2}{c}\{i\bm{\Phi}_{X}(\bm{U})+j\bm{\Phi}_{Y}(\bm{U})\}\right],\qquad(i,j)\in\mathcal{V}.

(12)

This equation is the central algebraic object of the method. It says that the equilibrium distribution is the lowest-order D2Q4 interpolation of one state and two fluxes. The finite-strain mechanics enter only through the evaluation of
\bm{\Phi}_{X}
and
\bm{\Phi}_{Y}
, i.e. through
\bm{P}(\bm{F})
.

Because the fluxes are nonlinear, the derivative of the equilibrium is needed whenever the method is expanded or initialized to second order. For any increment
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
,

(\bm{f}_{ij}^{eq})^{\prime}(\bm{U})\bm{Z}=\frac{1}{4}\left[\bm{Z}+\frac{2}{c}\{i\bm{A}_{X}(\bm{U})\bm{Z}+j\bm{A}_{Y}(\bm{U})\bm{Z}\}\right].

(13)

Taking moments of this identity recovers
\bm{Z}
,
\bm{A}_{X}\bm{Z}
, and
\bm{A}_{Y}\bm{Z}
. Thus the equilibrium derivative carries exactly the linearized continuum flux information needed for a Taylor expansion of the lattice update.

3.3
Collision, streaming, and source centering

A time step begins by evaluating
\bm{U}^{\rm num}
from Eq. (
10
). The last four entries define
\bm{F}
; the selected hyperelastic model then gives
\bm{P}(\bm{F})
, and hence the fluxes in Eq. (
7
). The BGK collision step
[
28
,
3
,
4
]
relaxes each population toward the local equilibrium:

\bm{f}_{ij}^{*}=\bm{f}_{ij}+\omega(\bm{f}_{ij}^{eq}(\bm{U}^{\rm num})-\bm{f}_{ij})+\Delta t(2-\omega)w_{ij}\bm{B},\qquad w_{ij}=\frac{1}{8}.

(14)

The post-collision population is then streamed to the neighbouring node in direction
(i,j)
:

\bm{f}_{ij}\bigl(\bm{X}+c(i,j)\Delta t,t+\Delta t\bigr)=\bm{f}_{ij}^{*}(\bm{X},t).

(15)

The symmetric weights satisfy
\sum_{q}w_{q}=1/2
,
\sum_{q}ciw_{q}=0
, and
\sum_{q}cjw_{q}=0
. Thus the source enters the zeroth moment consistently with Eq. (
10
), leaving the stress and kinematic flux moments unchanged.

The nondissipative second-order setting is
\omega=2
, for which the explicit forcing term in Eq. (
14
) vanishes because the source has already been time-centred in the state recovery. Values
1<\omega<2
introduce kinetic dissipation, which can be useful in strongly nonlinear computations. The formal second-order nondissipative expansion is tied to
\omega=2
; the consistency argument is collected in Appendix
B
.

3.4
Second-order population initialization

The prescribed initial fields determine
\bm{U}_{0}
and leave the four populations underdetermined. An equilibrium initialization,
\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{X},0)=\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}(\bm{U}_{0})
, recovers the leading macroscopic state. The non-equilibrium part expected by the lattice dynamics is then absent, and the resulting initial kinetic layer reduces short-time accuracy. For smooth initial fields, following the asymptotic initialization used in vectorial LBM
[
1
,
33
]
, the leading correction follows by expanding the distribution one half time step backward along its lattice characteristic:

\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{X},0)=\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}(\bm{U}_{0})-\frac{\Delta t}{2}D_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}(\bm{U}_{0}),\qquad D_{q}=\partial_{t}+ci\partial_{X}+cj\partial_{Y}.

(16)

No additional time derivative is prescribed. The continuum equation gives
\partial_{t}\bm{U}
, so the derivative in Eq. (
16
) can be evaluated from spatial derivatives of the initial fields. For
q=(i,j)
, set

\bm{Z}_{q}=D_{q}\bm{U}_{0}=\bigl(Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3},Z_{4},Z_{5},Z_{6}\bigr)^{T},

(17)

where all quantities on the right are evaluated at
t=0
, and

\displaystyle Z_{1}

\displaystyle=b_{1}+\partial_{X}P_{11}+\partial_{Y}P_{12}+ci\,\partial_{X}v_{1}+cj\,\partial_{Y}v_{1},

\displaystyle Z_{2}

\displaystyle=b_{2}+\partial_{X}P_{21}+\partial_{Y}P_{22}+ci\,\partial_{X}v_{2}+cj\,\partial_{Y}v_{2},

\displaystyle Z_{3}

\displaystyle=\partial_{X}v_{1}+ci\,\partial_{X}F_{11}+cj\,\partial_{Y}F_{11},

\displaystyle Z_{4}

\displaystyle=\partial_{Y}v_{1}+ci\,\partial_{X}F_{12}+cj\,\partial_{Y}F_{12},

\displaystyle Z_{5}

\displaystyle=\partial_{X}v_{2}+ci\,\partial_{X}F_{21}+cj\,\partial_{Y}F_{21},

\displaystyle Z_{6}

\displaystyle=\partial_{Y}v_{2}+ci\,\partial_{X}F_{22}+cj\,\partial_{Y}F_{22}.

(18)

The stress derivatives in Eq. (
18
) are computed from the material tangent, for example
\partial_{\alpha}P_{iA}=\mathbb{C}_{iAkB}\partial_{\alpha}F_{kB}
for
\alpha=X,Y
. Define

\bm{Z}_{F}=\begin{pmatrix}Z_{3}&Z_{4}\\
Z_{5}&Z_{6}\end{pmatrix}.

(19)

The stress increment induced by
\bm{Z}_{F}
is
\delta P^{q}_{iA}=\mathbb{C}_{iAkB}Z_{F,kB}
, or componentwise

\displaystyle\delta P^{q}_{11}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}_{1111}Z_{3}+\mathbb{C}_{1112}Z_{4}+\mathbb{C}_{1121}Z_{5}+\mathbb{C}_{1122}Z_{6},

\displaystyle\delta P^{q}_{12}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}_{1211}Z_{3}+\mathbb{C}_{1212}Z_{4}+\mathbb{C}_{1221}Z_{5}+\mathbb{C}_{1222}Z_{6},

\displaystyle\delta P^{q}_{21}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}_{2111}Z_{3}+\mathbb{C}_{2112}Z_{4}+\mathbb{C}_{2121}Z_{5}+\mathbb{C}_{2122}Z_{6},

\displaystyle\delta P^{q}_{22}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}_{2211}Z_{3}+\mathbb{C}_{2212}Z_{4}+\mathbb{C}_{2221}Z_{5}+\mathbb{C}_{2222}Z_{6}.

(20)

Using Eq. (
13
), the correction is then the explicit vector

D_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}(\bm{U}_{0})=\frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}Z_{1}-\frac{2}{c}\bigl(i\,\delta P^{q}_{11}+j\,\delta P^{q}_{12}\bigr)\\
Z_{2}-\frac{2}{c}\bigl(i\,\delta P^{q}_{21}+j\,\delta P^{q}_{22}\bigr)\\
Z_{3}-\frac{2i}{c}Z_{1}\\
Z_{4}-\frac{2j}{c}Z_{1}\\
Z_{5}-\frac{2i}{c}Z_{2}\\
Z_{6}-\frac{2j}{c}Z_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(21)

For manufactured solutions the derivatives are obtained analytically. For benchmark initial conditions, the same formula is evaluated using second-order finite differences, with one-sided stencils next to nonperiodic boundaries.

3.5
Displacement and stress recovery

The lattice evolves velocity and deformation gradient. Displacement is reconstructed only for output and for displacement error norms. A trapezoidal update is used: with
\bm{u}^{*,0}=\bm{u}_{0}-(\Delta t/2)\bm{v}_{0}
, set

\bm{u}^{n}=\bm{u}^{*,n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\bm{v}^{n},\qquad\bm{u}^{*,n+1}=\bm{u}^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\bm{v}^{n}.

(22)

The stress used by the dynamics is always the first Piola stress
\bm{P}(\bm{F})
. When Cauchy stress is needed for diagnostics or comparison with spatial benchmark fields, it is recovered by
\bm{\sigma}=J^{-1}\bm{P}\bm{F}^{T}
.

3.6
Half-way boundary reconstruction

The boundary treatment should be read from the viewpoint of the lattice. At a boundary node, the macroscopic equation remains available; after streaming, one or more incoming populations are unknown. The role of the boundary condition is therefore to reconstruct each missing incoming population from the known outgoing population and the imposed physical boundary condition.

For a grid-aligned boundary, let
\bm{N}
be the outward reference normal. The missing incoming direction is
d=(i,j)=-\bm{N}
, and
-d=\bm{N}
is the opposite outgoing direction. Following link-wise boundary reconstructions used in elastic-solid LBM
[
15
,
21
,
1
]
, we use a half-way reconstruction of the form

\bm{f}_{d}(t+\Delta t)=\bm{D}\bm{f}_{-d}^{*}(t)+\bm{S}_{d}(t+\Delta t/2).

(23)

The signs in
\bm{D}
are fixed by the pair identities obtained from Eq. (
12
):
\bm{f}_{d}^{eq}+\bm{f}_{-d}^{eq}=\bm{U}/2
and
\bm{f}_{d}^{eq}-\bm{f}_{-d}^{eq}=d_{A}\bm{\Phi}_{A}(\bm{U})/c
. Thus anti-bounce-back imposes a state component, and bounce-back with a correction imposes a normal flux component.

3.6.1
Velocity Dirichlet boundary condition

For a prescribed displacement
\bm{u}_{D}
, the first-order state sees
\bm{v}_{D}=\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{D}
. Anti-bounce-back reconstructs the velocity entries of the missing population. Bounce-back reconstructs the deformation-gradient entries with the compatible kinematic flux inserted. The rule is

\bm{D}_{D}=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1,1,1,1),\qquad\bm{S}_{d}^{D}=\left(\frac{v_{D1}}{2},\frac{v_{D2}}{2},\frac{N_{1}v_{D1}}{c},\frac{N_{2}v_{D1}}{c},\frac{N_{1}v_{D2}}{c},\frac{N_{2}v_{D2}}{c}\right)^{T}.

(24)

The first two entries impose the boundary velocity. The last four entries express the normal kinematic fluxes of
F_{11},F_{12},F_{21},F_{22}
. Hence a displacement boundary enters through
\partial_{t}\bm{u}_{D}
, because the evolved lattice variables are velocity and deformation gradient.

3.6.2
Neumann boundary condition

For a total-Lagrangian Neumann boundary, the prescribed datum is the nominal traction
\bar{\bm{T}}=\bm{P}\bm{N}
. Since
d=-\bm{N}
, the normal flux of the velocity rows satisfies
d_{A}\bm{\Phi}_{A}=(\bm{P}\bm{N},0,0,0,0)^{T}
in its first two components. Bounce-back plus the traction correction reconstructs the velocity entries. Anti-bounce-back reconstructs the deformation-gradient entries using a boundary deformation gradient
\bm{F}^{b}
:

\bm{D}_{N}=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1,-1,-1,-1),\qquad\bm{S}_{d}^{N}=\left(\frac{\bar{T}_{1}}{c},\frac{\bar{T}_{2}}{c},\frac{F_{11}^{b}}{2},\frac{F_{12}^{b}}{2},\frac{F_{21}^{b}}{2},\frac{F_{22}^{b}}{2}\right)^{T}.

(25)

The unresolved part is
\bm{F}^{b}
. At a boundary whose normal is aligned with material direction
A
, the tangential column of
\bm{F}^{b}
is extrapolated from interior nodes; the normal column is determined from the two scalar traction equations
P_{iA}(\bm{F}^{b})N_{A}=\bar{T}_{i}
,
i=1,2
. We solve this local nonlinear system by Newton iteration with line search and reject trial states with
\det\bm{F}^{b}\leq 0
. The procedure introduces no global boundary solve; the nonlinearity is confined to a two-unknown constitutive inversion at each boundary link.

The traction
\bar{\bm{T}}
is measured per reference area. Loads prescribed per current area, such as a spatial traction
\bm{\sigma}\bm{n}
, are pulled back to the reference boundary before they enter Eq. (
25
).

3.7
One step of the method

At the
n
-th time step, the update consists of the following local operations:

1.

Recover the macroscopic state
\bm{U}^{{\rm num},n}=\sum_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{n}+(\Delta t/2)\bm{B}^{n}
. Its first two components give
\bm{v}^{n}
, and the last four define
\bm{F}^{n}=(F_{11}^{n},F_{12}^{n},F_{21}^{n},F_{22}^{n})
.

2.

Evaluate the constitutive response
\bm{P}^{n}=\bm{P}(\bm{F}^{n})
, then assemble the material-coordinate fluxes
\bm{\Phi}_{X}^{n}=\bm{\Phi}_{X}(\bm{U}^{{\rm num},n})
and
\bm{\Phi}_{Y}^{n}=\bm{\Phi}_{Y}(\bm{U}^{{\rm num},n})
.

3.

Form the equilibrium populations
\bm{f}_{q}^{{\rm eq},n}=\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}(\bm{U}^{{\rm num},n})
and apply the collision rule to obtain
\bm{f}_{q}^{*,n}
.

4.

Stream each post-collision population along
\bm{c}_{q}=c(i,j)
, i.e.
\bm{f}_{q}^{n+1}(\bm{X}+\bm{c}_{q}\Delta t)=\bm{f}_{q}^{*,n}(\bm{X})
.

5.

Complete boundary nodes by reconstructing each missing incoming population,
\bm{f}_{d}^{n+1}=\bm{D}\bm{f}_{-d}^{*,n}+\bm{S}_{d}^{n+1/2}
, using the periodic, Dirichlet, or Neumann rule.

6.

Update output variables with
\bm{u}^{n}=\bm{u}^{*,n}+(\Delta t/2)\bm{v}^{n}
and
\bm{u}^{*,n+1}=\bm{u}^{n}+(\Delta t/2)\bm{v}^{n}
; when needed, recover
\bm{\sigma}^{n}=(J^{n})^{-1}\bm{P}^{n}(\bm{F}^{n})^{T}
.

The first moments of the equilibrium populations are constrained to equal
\bm{\Phi}_{X}
and
\bm{\Phi}_{Y}
, so the streaming step applies the lattice stencil directly to these fluxes. In the velocity rows this gives the stress-divergence term
\partial_{A}P_{iA}
.

5
Constitutive response and finite-strain wave dynamics

The preceding validation cases establish the accuracy of the nonlinear flux discretization and the grid-aligned boundary closures. We now examine the same formulation in settings chosen to isolate material response, tangent dynamics about finite deformations, finite-amplitude wave propagation, and a bounded-domain bending wave. The first three cases use analytical or semi-analytical references. The final case uses an independent finite-element reference and provides a longer-time dynamic assessment on a cantilever geometry.

5.1
Affine finite-strain material response

A homogeneous deformation gives a direct view of the constitutive interface used by the lattice method. Consider an affine motion

\bm{x}(\bm{X},t)=\bm{F}(t)\bm{X}+\bm{\beta}(t),\qquad\bm{v}(\bm{X},t)=\dot{\bm{F}}(t)\bm{X}+\dot{\bm{\beta}}(t),

(36)

where
\bm{\beta}(t)
is a time-dependent rigid translation and overdots denote time derivatives. The deformation gradient is spatially constant. Since
\bm{P}(\bm{F}(t))
is also spatially constant,
\nabla_{\!X}\!\cdot\bm{P}=\bm{0}
. In the numerical calculations
\dot{\bm{F}}
is constant during the loading interval, so the affine velocity boundary data generate an exact bulk solution without body forcing. This construction leaves the constitutive law free: any admissible
W(\bm{F})
in Appendix
A
supplies an exact stress path.

The affine-patch calculations set
\bm{\beta}(t)=\bm{0}
, because rigid translation does not affect
\bm{F}
,
\bm{P}
, or
\bm{\sigma}
. Over a loading interval
0\leq t\leq T
, the exact reference fields are

\bm{F}(t)=\bm{I}+\frac{t}{T}(\bm{F}_{*}-\bm{I}),\qquad\bm{u}(\bm{X},t)=\bigl[\bm{F}(t)-\bm{I}\bigr]\bm{X},\qquad\bm{v}(\bm{X},t)=\frac{\bm{F}_{*}-\bm{I}}{T}\,\bm{X},

(37)

with stresses obtained pointwise as
\bm{P}(t)=\bm{P}(\bm{F}(t))
and
\bm{\sigma}(t)=J^{-1}\bm{P}(t)\bm{F}(t)^{T}
. The stress curves in Fig.
6
use the terminal states
\bm{F}_{*}
from the following four families:

The next case probes the tangent dynamics about finite homogeneous states. Let

6
Conclusions and outlook

This work has introduced a total-Lagrangian vectorial lattice Boltzmann formulation for two-dimensional finite-strain hyperelastic dynamics. The governing equations were written as a conservative first-order system for the material velocity and the full deformation gradient, allowing the lattice to remain fixed in the reference configuration while the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress is evaluated locally from
\bm{F}
. A D2Q4 lattice with six-component vector populations was then used to match the macroscopic state and the two material-coordinate fluxes. In this way, finite-strain material nonlinearity enters only through nonlinear flux moments, whereas the method retains the local collide–stream structure of LBM. The formulation further includes trapezoidally centered forcing, second-order population initialization, displacement recovery by velocity quadrature, and half-way reconstructions for velocity Dirichlet and nominal-traction Neumann boundaries on grid-aligned domains.

Figure 11:
Cantilever response compared with a finite-element reference. The present D2Q4
\times
6 errors are measured against an independent Q1 reference using the benchmark definitions of
E_{\infty}
and
E_{2}
, and are shown together with the
\tau=0.55
results of Müller et al.
[
22
]
.

The numerical results support the accuracy and applicability of the construction. Periodic manufactured solutions show second-order convergence for displacement, first Piola stress, and Cauchy stress when the second-order initialization is used. Boundary manufactured solutions confirm the expected behaviour for Dirichlet data and show that mixed traction boundaries mainly introduce localized stress errors, while the interior remains governed by the second-order bulk discretization. The uniaxial-tension and simple-shear benchmarks further demonstrate that the same flux and boundary reconstructions carry over to finite-strain benchmark configurations. The method also reproduces homogeneous affine stress responses for several hyperelastic laws, acoustic-tensor wave speeds about predeformed states, finite-amplitude periodic shear waves, and a bounded-domain cantilever bending wave.

These results indicate that the proposed total-Lagrangian vectorial LBM can represent both nonlinear constitutive response and elastodynamic wave propagation within one explicit local framework. The formal consistency argument in Appendix
B
supports this observation for smooth solutions: under acoustic scaling,
\omega=2
, second-order initialization, and sufficiently accurate boundary data, the leading macroscopic field satisfies the target first-order hyperelastic system. Compared with the linear vectorial formulation, the essential change is the use of state-dependent Piola fluxes and tangent moduli; the moment-matching mechanism itself remains unchanged.

Several limitations remain and define natural directions for future work. The present study is restricted to two dimensions, uniform Cartesian lattices, and grid-aligned boundaries. A natural next step is to carry the same construction to three dimensions, where the state would contain three velocity components and the full
3\times 3
deformation gradient. The treatment of boundaries will also need to move beyond the present grid-aligned setting. For practical solid-mechanics applications, the method should be able to handle curved surfaces, cut cells, corners, and mixed displacement–traction data on arbitrary geometries, while preserving as much as possible the locality that makes LBM attractive. Another promising extension is anisotropic hyperelasticity. In a total-Lagrangian formulation, material directions are fixed in the reference configuration and can therefore be passed directly to the local constitutive update, making orthotropic and fiber-reinforced solids a natural target.

Appendix A
Constitutive laws used in the implementation

Let

\bm{C}=\bm{F}^{T}\bm{F},\qquad\bm{E}=\frac{\bm{C}-\bm{I}}{2},\qquad J=\det\bm{F},\qquad\bm{H}=\bm{F}^{-T}.

(A.1)

For the invariant-based Mooney–Rivlin, Yeoh, and Gent closures, define

I_{1}=\operatorname{tr}(\bm{C})+1,\qquad I_{2}=J^{2}+\operatorname{tr}(\bm{C}),\qquad\bar{I}_{1}=J^{-2/3}I_{1},\qquad\bar{I}_{2}=J^{-4/3}I_{2},\qquad A=\bar{I}_{1}-3,

(A.2)

and

\bm{G}_{1}=\frac{\partial\bar{I}_{1}}{\partial\bm{F}}=J^{-2/3}\left(2\bm{F}-\frac{2}{3}I_{1}\bm{H}\right),\qquad\bm{G}_{2}=\frac{\partial\bar{I}_{2}}{\partial\bm{F}}=J^{-4/3}\left[2\bm{F}+\left(2J^{2}-\frac{4}{3}I_{2}\right)\bm{H}\right].

(A.3)

The strain energies and first Piola stresses are

\displaystyle W_{\rm SVK}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2}[\operatorname{tr}(\bm{E})]^{2}+\mu\,\bm{E}:\bm{E},

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{\rm SVK}

\displaystyle=\bm{F}\bigl[\lambda\operatorname{tr}(\bm{E})\bm{I}+2\mu\bm{E}\bigr],

(A.4)

\displaystyle W_{\rm NH}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{2}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}(\bm{C})-2-2\log J\bigr)+\frac{\lambda}{4}\bigl(J^{2}-1-2\log J\bigr),

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{\rm NH}

\displaystyle=\mu(\bm{F}-\bm{H})+\frac{\lambda}{2}(J^{2}-1)\bm{H},

(A.5)

\displaystyle W_{\log{\rm NH}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{2}\bigl(\operatorname{tr}(\bm{C})-2-2\log J\bigr)+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\log J)^{2},

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{\log{\rm NH}}

\displaystyle=\mu(\bm{F}-\bm{H})+\lambda\log J\,\bm{H},

(A.6)

\displaystyle W_{\rm MR}

\displaystyle=c_{10}(\bar{I}_{1}-3)+c_{01}(\bar{I}_{2}-3)+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\log J)^{2},

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{\rm MR}

\displaystyle=c_{10}\bm{G}_{1}+c_{01}\bm{G}_{2}+\lambda\log J\,\bm{H},

(A.7)

\displaystyle W_{\rm Yeoh}

\displaystyle=c_{1}A+c_{2}A^{2}+c_{3}A^{3}+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\log J)^{2},

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{\rm Yeoh}

\displaystyle=(c_{1}+2c_{2}A+3c_{3}A^{2})\bm{G}_{1}+\lambda\log J\,\bm{H},

(A.8)

\displaystyle W_{\rm Gent}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu J_{m}}{2}\log\left(1-\frac{A}{J_{m}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\log J)^{2},

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{\rm Gent}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{2(1-A/J_{m})}\bm{G}_{1}+\lambda\log J\,\bm{H}.

(A.9)

When material parameters are specified through
\mu
and Poisson’s ratio
\nu
, the implementation uses
\lambda=2\mu\nu/(1-2\nu)
. It sets
c_{10}+c_{01}=\mu/2
for Mooney–Rivlin and
c_{1}=\mu/2
for Yeoh unless these parameters are supplied explicitly. Admissible states satisfy
J>0
, with the additional Gent restriction
A<J_{m}
.

Appendix B
Formal second-order consistency

This appendix gives the Chapman–Enskog/Taylor expansion behind the second-order statement used in Section
3
. All lattice moments, fluxes, source terms, and boundary reconstructions are those already defined in the main text. The expansion assumes smooth fields, acoustic scaling with fixed
c=\Delta x/\Delta t
, the nondissipative choice
\omega=2
, the initialization in Eq. (
16
), and half-way boundary data that are consistent to the order stated below.

B.1
Bulk Chapman–Enskog expansion

Use
\Delta t
as the small parameter and write

\bm{f}_{q}=\sum_{m\geq 0}\Delta t^{m}\bm{f}_{q}^{(m)},\qquad\bm{U}^{\rm num}=\sum_{m\geq 0}\Delta t^{m}\bm{U}^{(m)},\qquad\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}(\bm{U}^{\rm num})=\sum_{m\geq 0}\Delta t^{m}\bm{e}_{q}^{(m)}.

(B.1)

The equilibrium expansion is

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{q}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}(\bm{U}^{(0)}),

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{q}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}\right)^{\prime}(\bm{U}^{(0)})\bm{U}^{(1)},

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{q}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}\right)^{\prime}(\bm{U}^{(0)})\bm{U}^{(2)}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}\right)^{\prime\prime}(\bm{U}^{(0)})[\bm{U}^{(1)},\bm{U}^{(1)}].

(B.2)

The Hessian term is present because the stress fluxes are nonlinear. It does not produce an additional macroscopic consistency term: the zeroth moment identity in Eq. (
11
) implies
\sum_{q}\bm{e}_{q}^{(m)}=\bm{U}^{(m)}
order by order.

From the source-shifted state recovery in Eq. (
10
),

\sum_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{(0)}=\bm{U}^{(0)},\qquad\sum_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{(1)}=\bm{U}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{B},\qquad\sum_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{(m)}=\bm{U}^{(m)}\quad(m\geq 2).

(B.3)

For
\omega=2
, the explicit source term in Eq. (
14
) vanishes and the combined collide–stream equation becomes

\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{X}+\bm{c}_{q}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)=2\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}(\bm{U}^{\rm num})-\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{X},t).

(B.4)

Expanding the left-hand side along the lattice characteristic gives

\bm{f}_{q}+\Delta tD_{q}\bm{f}_{q}+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}D_{q}^{2}\bm{f}_{q}+O(\Delta t^{3})=2\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}(\bm{U}^{\rm num})-\bm{f}_{q},

(B.5)

with
D_{q}
defined in Eq. (
16
). Substitution of Eq. (
B.1
) into Eq. (
B.5
) gives the following balances.

At order
\Delta t^{0}
,

\bm{f}_{q}^{(0)}=\bm{e}_{q}^{(0)}=\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}(\bm{U}^{(0)}).

(B.6)

At order
\Delta t^{1}
,

D_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{(0)}=2(\bm{e}_{q}^{(1)}-\bm{f}_{q}^{(1)}),\qquad\bm{f}_{q}^{(1)}=\bm{e}_{q}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{2}D_{q}\bm{e}_{q}^{(0)}.

(B.7)

Taking the zeroth moment of Eq. (
B.7
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{q}D_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\sum_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{(0)}+\partial_{X}\sum_{q}ci\,\bm{f}_{q}^{(0)}+\partial_{Y}\sum_{q}cj\,\bm{f}_{q}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\bm{U}^{(0)}+\partial_{X}\bm{\Phi}_{X}(\bm{U}^{(0)})+\partial_{Y}\bm{\Phi}_{Y}(\bm{U}^{(0)}),

(B.8)

where Eq. (
11
) has been used with Eq. (
B.6
). The right-hand side is

2\left(\sum_{q}\bm{e}_{q}^{(1)}-\sum_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{(1)}\right)=2\left(\bm{U}^{(1)}-\bm{U}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{B}\right)=\bm{B}.

(B.9)

Thus the leading field satisfies

\partial_{t}\bm{U}^{(0)}+\partial_{X}\bm{\Phi}_{X}(\bm{U}^{(0)})+\partial_{Y}\bm{\Phi}_{Y}(\bm{U}^{(0)})=\bm{B}.

(B.10)

At order
\Delta t^{2}
,

D_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2}D_{q}^{2}\bm{f}_{q}^{(0)}=2(\bm{e}_{q}^{(2)}-\bm{f}_{q}^{(2)}).

(B.11)

Using Eqs. (
B.6
) and (
B.7
), the left-hand side reduces exactly:

D_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2}D_{q}^{2}\bm{f}_{q}^{(0)}=D_{q}\left(\bm{e}_{q}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{2}D_{q}\bm{e}_{q}^{(0)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}D_{q}^{2}\bm{e}_{q}^{(0)}=D_{q}\bm{e}_{q}^{(1)}.

(B.12)

Taking the zeroth moment of Eq. (
B.11
) therefore gives

\sum_{q}D_{q}\bm{e}_{q}^{(1)}=2\left(\sum_{q}\bm{e}_{q}^{(2)}-\sum_{q}\bm{f}_{q}^{(2)}\right)=\bm{0}.

(B.13)

The differentiated moment identities following from Eq. (
13
) give

\sum_{q}\bm{e}_{q}^{(1)}=\bm{U}^{(1)},\qquad\sum_{q}ci\,\bm{e}_{q}^{(1)}=\bm{A}_{X}(\bm{U}^{(0)})\bm{U}^{(1)},\qquad\sum_{q}cj\,\bm{e}_{q}^{(1)}=\bm{A}_{Y}(\bm{U}^{(0)})\bm{U}^{(1)}.

(B.14)

Consequently,

\partial_{t}\bm{U}^{(1)}+\partial_{X}\!\left(\bm{A}_{X}(\bm{U}^{(0)})\bm{U}^{(1)}\right)+\partial_{Y}\!\left(\bm{A}_{Y}(\bm{U}^{(0)})\bm{U}^{(1)}\right)=\bm{0}.

(B.15)

This is the homogeneous equation for the first correction. The only finite-strain change relative to the linear vectorial calculation is that the Jacobians in Eq. (
B.15
) are evaluated along
\bm{U}^{(0)}
; the nonlinear second derivative in Eq. (
B.2
) cancels from the zeroth-moment equation through the exact equilibrium moment identity.

B.2
Initialization and half-way boundaries

The initialization in Eq. (
16
) removes the first-order initial error. Comparing it with Eqs. (
B.6
)–(
B.7
) at
t=0

\left(\bm{f}_{q}^{eq}\right)^{\prime}(\bm{U}_{0})\bm{U}^{(1)}(\bm{X},0)=\bm{0}.

(B.16)

Summation over
q
, together with the zeroth differentiated moment in Eq. (
13
), yields

\bm{U}^{(1)}(\bm{X},0)=\bm{0}.

(B.17)

For a missing direction
d=-\bm{N}
at a half-way boundary, Eq. (
12
) gives the pair identities

\bm{f}_{d}^{eq}+\bm{f}_{-d}^{eq}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{U},\qquad\bm{f}_{d}^{eq}-\bm{f}_{-d}^{eq}=\frac{1}{c}\,d_{A}\bm{\Phi}_{A}(\bm{U}).

(B.18)

Thus anti-bounce-back imposes a state component at leading order, whereas bounce-back with a correction imposes the corresponding normal flux component.

For the velocity Dirichlet rule in Eq. (
24
), the anti-bounce-back velocity entries give

\frac{1}{2}\bm{v}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{v}_{D},\qquad\bm{v}^{(1)}=\bm{0}

(B.19)

at the boundary. The bounce-back deformation-gradient entries impose the kinematic normal flux because

\frac{1}{c}d_{A}\Phi_{A}^{F_{iB}}(\bm{U}^{(0)})=\frac{N_{B}v_{i}^{(0)}}{c},

(B.20)

which is exactly the correction used in Eq. (
24

For the Neumann rule in Eq. (
25

\frac{1}{c}d_{A}\Phi_{A}^{v_{i}}(\bm{U}^{(0)})=\frac{1}{c}P_{iA}(\bm{F}^{(0)})N_{A}=\frac{\bar{T}_{i}}{c}.

(B.21)

The anti-bounce-back deformation-gradient entries impose the boundary value
\bm{F}^{b}
. Hence this boundary is second-order consistent provided

\bm{F}^{b}=\bm{F}(\bm{X}_{b},t+\Delta t/2)+O(\Delta t^{2})

(B.22)

and the local traction inversion defining
\bm{F}^{b}
is solved to the same order. Under this condition the
O(\Delta t)
Neumann error data are homogeneous, namely the linearized traction condition
\delta\bm{P}\,\bm{N}=\bm{0}
together with the corresponding state condition on the reconstructed deformation-gradient entries. If the tangential column extrapolation or local Newton solve is only first order, this boundary part no longer supplies the zero first-order data required by the formal second-order boundary argument, although the interior expansion above is unchanged.

With Eq. (
B.17
) and homogeneous first-order boundary data, uniqueness of the linearized homogeneous problem in Eq. (

\bm{U}^{(1)}=\bm{0}
. The leading field
\bm{U}^{(0)}
satisfies the target first-order system by Eq. (
