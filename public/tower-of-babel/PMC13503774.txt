Colour polymorphism in the model aphid pest Myzus persicae as a survival strategy in tritrophic interactions
Authors: Jamin Ali, Adil Tonğa, Sohail Abbas, Asim Munawar, Apostolos Pekas, Qiyun Li, Rizhao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe peach-potato aphid, Myzus persicae Sulzer, is one of the most destructive agricultural pests worldwide, exhibiting remarkable colour polymorphism, with stable green and red morphs. Previous research has primarily examined isolated traits, leaving a critical gap in understanding how colour variation shapes integrated ecological performance across life history, behaviour, and trophic interactions. Our study addresses this through comprehensive bioassays under controlled conditions, systematically evaluating development, fecundity, population growth, settlement patterns, orientation, mobility, and migration capabilities while assessing predation by Harmonia axyridis Pallas larvae and parasitism by Aphidius gifuensis Ashmead. We demonstrate that green morphs exhibit higher fecundity on mature plants with slower maturation, while red morphs develop faster and perform better on younger plants. Green morphs showed reduced host odour responsiveness, slower movement, with a more limited migration range compared to red morphs. Alate green morphs exhibited significantly higher settlement, and apterous green morphs showed a significantly larger population increase. Crucially, red morphs were significantly more vulnerable to predation, while parasitism showed no morph preference. These integrated findings suggest a trade-off that may reflect a ‘fast-slow’ life-history continuum within the studied clone, indicating that morph-specific advantages are context-dependent. However, as our study examined a single clone under controlled conditions, further research across multiple clones and field settings is needed to establish the generality of these patterns. Our work provides a novel ecological framework for understanding colour polymorphism maintenance and may inform the development of morph-specific monitoring and biological control for Brassica crops.Keywords: Myzus persicae, Colour polymorphism, Ecology, Avoidance, Prey-predator interactionSubject terms: Evolution, Genetics, Plant sciences, Zoology

Introduction

Myzus persicae Sulzer (Hemiptera: Aphididae), also known as the peach-potato aphid, is one of the most threatening sucking pests for agricultural production1–3. This insect pest feeds on over 400 host plants belonging to 40 plant families, causing direct damage by sucking the sap from plants and reducing plant quality through honeydew deposition, which can impair photosynthesis, suppress plant defences, and promote microbial growth1,3–5. Additionally, this pest is highly efficient in transmitting over 100 plant pathogenic viruses3. M. persicae causes billions of dollars of global losses annually2. This pest has developed resistance to several major classes of insecticides, limiting available control options6,7. Furthermore, ecological factors such as high reproductive capacity, short maturation time (rapid attainment of reproductive maturity), telescoping generation (a phenomenon where embryos develop within mother aphids even before their birth), and the ability to adapt to new plants as hosts make this pest a serious threat and a model for ecological and evolutionary studies8,9. Therefore, studying its ecology is crucial for developing effective management strategies.

Polymorphism is a common survival phenomenon in aphids, with colour variation influenced by a complex interaction of genetic and environmental factors, including temperature, nutrition, host plants, light intensity, and pigmentation (e.g., carotenoids, melanin, and aphins) and endosymbiont infections1,10–13. This polymorphism is maintained by multiple, context-dependent selective pressures rather than a single dominant function, leading to differences in behaviour, susceptibility to natural enemies, and environmental adaptations14. In M. persicae, colour morphs are correlated with distinct biological and ecological traits, though these associations may vary among host-adapted populations. For instance, red morphs are often associated with greater tolerance to low temperatures4,15 and are frequently found on specific hosts such as tobacco (where they have been described as the subspecies M. persicae nicotianae), although green forms persist there as well, highlighting the complex and population-specific determinants of this polymorphism16. Abiotic and biotic factors can further modulate colour expression in aphids; for example, Acyrthosiphon pisum exhibits temperature-dependent colour variation, with cooler conditions favouring green morphs17, and aphids on nitrogen-fertilized plants tend to be larger and darker18,19. The primary biological functions of colour morphs in M. persicae thus include differences in reproductive performance, temperature tolerance, with some biotypes (e.g., tobacco-adapted form) also potentially exhibiting morph-linked differences in insecticide resistance29.

The ecological relevance of this colour polymorphism is underscored by the variable frequencies of red and green morphs observed in field populations. On major crops such as oilseed rape (Brassica napus) and other brassicas, green morphs typically predominate, likely reflecting their cryptic advantage on green foliage. However, red morph frequencies can increase under certain conditions, including cooler temperatures, on specific host plants like tobacco, or in response to insecticide pressure in some populations4,16,20. This temporal and spatial variation in morph frequencies suggests that the relative advantages of each colour morph shift with environmental context, crop phenology, and natural enemy pressure, a complexity that our integrated experimental approach seeks to address.

One important ecological function of polymorphism is avoidance of natural enemies. Colour matching with the background can make aphids less visible to predators21–23, a strategy often employed by green morphs on plant leaves. Furthermore, recent evidence shows that colour polymorphisms in M. persicae result in variations in salivary enzymes (CarD763) that enhance the feeding and survival on infested plants24. Aphid morphs also show different behavioural defences; red forms show higher instance of behaviours like dropping from hosts25, and the production of alate aphids is itself considered an avoidance response to crowding or tactile stimuli26. These colour-based strategies significantly influence tritrophic interactions. Aphid colour influences interactions with natural enemies, which are crucial for biological control27,28. For instance, some predators are more likely to detect aphids when their colour contrasts with the background29, and red morphs may escape predators more quickly30. Similarly colour morphs also show variation in susceptibility to parasitoids31.

Despite extensive research, most studies on M. persicae have examined isolated aspects such as host plant performance, insecticide resistance, or single behavioural traits4,20,24. This fragmented approach has limited the development of a unified ecological framework, and the performance differences between colour morphs across multiple ecological contexts remain poorly understood11. In particular, little is known about how red and green morphs respond to variation in host plant condition or how they interact with different natural enemies. Thus, the ecological basis of colour polymorphism, especially its potential trade-offs between reproduction, dispersal, and susceptibility to predators or parasitoids, remains unclear. To address these gaps, this study presents an integrative assessment of M. persicae colour morphs under standardised conditions, simultaneously quantifying development, fecundity, dispersal capacity, and interactions with natural enemies. By incorporating both young and mature host plants and two representative natural enemies, Harmonia axyridis Pallas (Coleoptera: Coccinellidae) and Aphidius gifuensis Ashmead (Hymenoptera: Braconidae), this work moves beyond single-factor comparisons to provide a comprehensive ecological perspective on colour polymorphism. This framework enables a systematic evaluation of morph-specific trade-offs within life history, behavioural, and trophic contexts, thereby addressing a key gap in understanding how ecological and biotic factors jointly shape colour polymorphism in this major pest species.

Materials and methods

Insect

The initial colony of both Myzus persicae Sulzer (Hemiptera; Aphididae) green and red morphs were purchased from Qu Shou Natural, Nanyang, China, and the culture was established in February 2024 at the insect rearing lab, College of Plant Protection, Jilin Agricultural University, Jilin, China, to avoid potential geographic variations. Both colour morphs were obtained from the same source population and reared under identical conditions, with each morph maintained as a separate clonal line. Visual screening of morphological traits and reproductive consistency was conducted prior to the start of the study to confirm stable morph expression within each line. The aphids were reared on Brassica rapa (Brassicales: Brassicaceae) in insect-rearing cages (60 × 60 × 60 cm) under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L : 8D). Depending on the experiment, adult apterous aphids, alate aphids, or nymphs were used.

Plants

Brassica rapa plants were grown from seeds purchased from Seminis, Monsanto Holdings Private Limited, India. The plants were grown in a controlled environment room (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L: 8D). Each B. rapa plant was individually cultivated in 7.5 cm pots filled with compost (Jiangsu Siweibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. China). Experiments were conducted using two- and four week old plants depending upon the experiments.

Natural enemies

Initial colony of predator Harmonia axyridis Pallas (Coleoptera: Coccinellidae) was obtained from Keyun company, Henan, China and reared on M. persicae (mixed green and red morphs) under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L: 8D h). Fourth instar larvae were used in larval feeding bioassay. The colony of parasitoid Aphidius gifuensis Ashmead (Hymenoptera: Braconidae) was established from aphid mummies provided by Better Life Co. Ltd., Yunnan, China. (A) gifuensis were reared on (B) rapa plants infested with M. persicae (mixed green and red morphs) under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L : 8D h). After emergence, parasitoid adults were provided with honey + water solution (1:1) as diet. Female parasitoids (48–72 h old) were used in experiments.

Life history and demographic parameters

An adult aphid was transferred to a two-week old plant. After 24 h, the adult aphid and all produced nymphs were removed, leaving a single new-born nymph on the plant. The number of days from birth to the adult reproductive stage was recorded, defined by the appearance of the first nymph. Plants were checked daily at the same time each day, and the day the first nymph was observed was considered the onset of reproductive maturity. The total number of days to reach this stage was recorded.

To assess total fecundity for life table analysis, aphid age synchronization was performed by transferring a newly developed adult aphid to a two-week-old plant. The number of nymphs produced was recorded daily and removed from the plant. This process continued until the death of the adult aphid, with newly produced nymphs being recorded and removed daily. The bioassays were conducted in a controlled environment (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L: 8D), with fifteen replicates for each morph. Each replicate consisted of a single aphid tracked from birth to death, resulting in 15 individual aphids being studied per colour morph.

Aphid performance

To assess whether reproductive capability is growth stage dependent, performance bioassays were performed. This experiment was designed to test the hypothesis that morph performance is dependent on host plant quality, which changes with ontogeny. Young plants (two-week-old) typically represent rapidly growing tissues with high-nutrient availability, while mature plants (four-week-old) represent older vegetative tissues that may differ in nutritional quality. This comparison allows assessment of how host plant ontogeny influences morph performance. In this experiment, we assessed the increase of population of both morphs of M. persicae. Three newly born nymphs were transferred to the plant. After that, plant was enclosed in a vented bag (30 cm × 40 cm). Plants were left undisturbed for 14 days and irrigated as per requirement. After 14 days, the total number of aphids, including adults and nymphs, were recorded for each morph. Additionally, plants in different developmental stages (two and four week old plants) were used to examine whether the developmental stage of host plants has any effects on aphid performance. Experiments were conducted in two series; in series 1, two week old plants were used as a host plants, while in series 2, four week old plants were used. Experiments were conducted in a greenhouse with controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L: 8D) to investigate the aphid performance in more natural conditions. Fifteen replicates were conducted for each morph and each plant age (two-week-old and four-week-old plants), with each replicate consisting of two plants. Therefore, the total number of plants used for each morph was 30, and for both morphs combined, the total was 60 plants for each plant age.

Settlement

This experiment was designed to investigate the settlement preference of alate red and green aphid morphs, quantifying both their preference for plants versus non-plant surfaces and their distribution among plants of different ages. Settlement behaviour, the decision to terminate flight and remain on a surface, is a key determinant of host plant selection and colonization success for winged aphids. By recording aphids present on plants after 24 h, this assay captures the net outcome of multiple behavioural processes including orientation toward plants, landing decisions, and the propensity to remain on plant versus non-plant surfaces (cage walls, vial). This provides an integrated measure of host acceptance under standardised conditions, complementing more dynamic assays that track individual movement decisions. Four plants were placed in an alternate position in a BugDorm (60 × 60 × 60 cm), and a vial containing 50 alate aphids was positioned at the centre of the BugDorm. After 24 h, the numbers of aphids settled on the plants were recorded. The experiment was conducted separately for red and green morphs, with each morph kept in different cages to avoid any potential bias, as the alate forms do not exhibit colour polymorphism as apterous forms. The experiment was conducted in two series: series 1 used two week old plants, while series 2 used four week old plants. Experiments were conducted in a controlled environment room (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L: 8D), with fifteen replicates for each morph.

Speed and mobility

To compare the responsiveness of red and green morphs to host plant volatiles under controlled conditions, we measured the time required for apterous aphids to traverse a straight glass tube connected to a plant-derived odour source. This assay quantifies movement toward the odour source within a linear apparatus but does not distinguish between olfactory sensitivity, general locomotor activity, hunger level, stress state, or responses to airflow. The results therefore reflect odour-guided movement under controlled conditions rather than natural host-seeking behaviour in complex environments. The use of continuous standardised airflow ensures consistent odour delivery. A four week old plant was placed in a container (25 × 25 × 20 cm) with two openings: one for connecting a supply of charcoal-filtered air and another for attaching a labelled (5 cm, 10 cm and 15 cm with small signs for 1 cm) glass tube (20 cm in length and 1.6 cm in diameter) (Fig. 4d). An adult apterous aphid was gently released into the glass tube using a camel brush, and air was supplied at a pressure of 200 ml s−1. Once the aphid crossed the start point at 0 cm, a timer was started to record the time taken to reach each predetermined distance. Each aphid was given 10 min to reach the final distance (15 cm). Observations were recorded alternately, with data for one green morph followed by one red morph. The glass tube was replaced with a fresh, cleaned glass tube after every three replicates of respective aphid morph to minimize any potential contamination, including possible residues of alarm pheromones released during handling, tubes were later cleaned with 70% ethanol and air dried. Different glass tubes were employed for each morph. Aphids that did not cross the start point ‘0’ within the first 2 min were discarded to ensure that measured times reflected active orientation rather than generalised inactivity. All observations were conducted under uniform illumination in a controlled environment room (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L: 8D). Thirty replicates were performed for each aphid morph.

To assess whether red and green morphs differ in baseline locomotor activity independent of host plant cues, we measured aphid movement in a host-free arena. This assay provides a standardised reference for comparing inherent activity levels between morphs, which may help interpret differences observed in other behavioural assays (e.g., orientation, migration). While movement on bare substrate does not directly reflect natural host-seeking behaviour, it allows controlled comparison of an intrinsic trait under identical conditions. Aphids that did not move within the first minute were excluded to ensure that we measured active locomotor behaviour rather than individuals that were quiescent or unresponsive due to handling. An adult apterous aphid was placed in a Petri dish (10 × 5 cm). After placing the aphid in the Petri dish, the movement (walking) of aphids was recorded using a stopwatch (smartphone). The stopwatch was paused whenever the aphid stopped moving. Each replicate was run for five minutes. Aphids were collected 2 h prior to the start of the experiment to standardise hunger level across individuals, and all observations were recorded in a controlled environment room (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L: 8D). Fifteen replicates were recorded for each aphid morph.

Migration bioassay

To assess migration and colonization success of alate aphids over varying distances, we recorded their distribution among plants positioned at increasing distances from a central release point over 24 h. Unlike the settlement experiment (“Settlement” section), which used a smaller cage (60 × 60 × 60 cm) with plants at equal distances to assess initial host acceptance, this larger arena (200 cm diameter) with graded distances (20–80 cm) specifically evaluates how distance affects flight dispersal and subsequent settlement—the primary mechanism for longer-range population spread within and between fields. The 24 h time point captures the net outcome of flight orientation, landing decisions, and settlement, providing an integrated measure of colonization potential at each distance under standardised conditions.

For this bioassay, an umbrella-shaped insect cage (200 cm in diameter and 60 cm in height at the centre) was used. Inside the cage, a vial containing 50 alate adults was positioned in the centre, and four plants (four week old) were arranged at distances of 20 cm, 40 cm, 60 cm, and 80 cm from the centre in different directions. To avoid directional bias, the arrangement of plants was changed clockwise, altering the positions of plant distances directionally. For instance, if replicate 1 was performed with the pattern shown in Fig. 5a, in the next replicate, the positions were changed such as plant D replaced plant A, plant A replaced plant B, plant B replaced plant C, and plant C replaced plant D, along with their associated distances. After 24 h, the numbers of settled aphids on the plants were recorded. Plant positions were rotated between replicates to control for any directional bias, and the use of four distances allows comparison of colonization success across a gradient rather than relying on a single proximity measure. The experiment was conducted in a greenhouse under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 10% RH, 16 L: 8D). Ten replicates were conducted for each aphid morph.

Performance of natural enemies

Predation

To dissect the mechanisms of predator-prey interaction, we used two complementary setups. In Petri dish: To evaluate the predation behaviour of H. axyridis larvae on the two colour morphs of adult M. persicae, bioassays were conducted using both Petri dish and on-plant setups. The Petri dish assays isolated the inherent visual preference of the predator for each morph in a simplified environment devoid of host plant complexity. Prior to the experiment, H. axyridis larvae were starved for 1 h. In the Petri dish assays, two designs were used: a no-choice and a choice experiment. In the no-choice setup, twenty adult aphids of each colour morph were placed in separate Petri dishes (8 cm diameter), whereas in the choice setup, twenty aphids from each morph were released together in larger Petri dishes (14 cm diameter). A 4th instar H. axyridis larva was introduced into each dish and allowed to feed for 2 h under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L: 8D photoperiod). After the feeding period, the remaining live aphids were recorded, and the number of aphids consumed was determined by subtraction from the initial count. Fifteen replicates were conducted for each aphid colour morph in each experiment.

On plant: The procedure for the on-plant experiment was similar to that of the Petri dish experiments, with the addition of a host plant. This setup introduced the ecological context of the host plant, allowing aphids to employ potential defensive behaviours (e.g., crypsis, dropping) and testing whether the green morph’s colouration confers a survival advantage in a more natural setting. Aphids were released on a two-week-old host plant, and the same choice and no-choice experimental designs were used to assess predation. This experiment aimed to determine whether the presence of a host plant would affect the predation capacity of H. axyridis larvae on the red and green aphid morphs. Aphids may exhibit a tendency for enemy avoidance when on host plants, while in an open arena such as a Petri dish, they may not have such an opportunity32. Fifteen replicates were conducted for each aphid morph in each experiment. All bioassays were conducted in a controlled environment room (22 ± 2 °C, 45 ± 5% RH, 16 L: 8D).

Parasitism

To see whether the colour variation in aphid affects the behaviour of parasitoids, a parasitism bioassay was conducted in a BugDorm (60 × 60 × 60 cm). Unlike predators, parasitoids like A. gifuensis rely heavily on olfactory and tactile cues rather than vision. This choice-test experiment was therefore designed to determine if the colour morphs differ in susceptibility when chemical cues are the primary mechanism for host location. In a BugDorm, plants infested with green and red aphids were placed opposite to each other (alternate position), with four plants total (two plant for green morph and two for red morph). Each plant was infested with 50 adult M. persicae 2 h prior to the release of A. gifuensis. Eight female parasitoids were released into the BugDorm33. After 24 h, parasitoids were collected from the cages and plants were kept contained in bread bags (40 × 25 cm) for mummy formation. After 12 days, the number of mummified aphids for both colour aphids were recorded. All bioassays were conducted in a controlled environment room (22 ± 2 °C, 40 ± 5% RH, 16 L : 8D), and replicated 10 times.

Statistical analysis

The life table data of aphids in both morphs were analysed based on the age-stage, two-sex life table theory34,35 using TWOSEX-MSChart software36. Parameters associated with longevity, survival and population growth were calculated for both morphs. To ensure more precise estimates, all standard errors were bootstrapped (100,000). Parameters of red and green aphid morphs were then compared with the paired-bootstrap procedure41 using TWOSEX-MSChart software36.

The remaining statistical analysis were performed in R statistical environment42. Count data such as settlement, population increase and predation studies were subjected to normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homoscedasticity (Levene test). Since the data did not show consistent normality and variance homogeneity, statistical analyses were performed using generalised linear models (GLMs) with different models such as Poisson, gaussian, negative binomial distributions. The glm.nb function of MASS package and glm function of stats package were used for data modelling processes. The best GLM model fit was processed based on lowest AIC and BIC values followed by checking model residuals and overdispersion43. The models were also checked using standard overdispersion, residual diagnostics, including tests for residual normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homoscedasticity (Studentized Breusch-Pagan test via lmtest package) (P < 0.05). ANOVA() function from car package generated likelihood ratio test results. The models with multiple factors (migration data) were subjected to Tukey multiple comparison tests, using emmeans package.

In host odour orientation behaviour tests, we pre-determined three arrival points (5, 10 and 15 cm) to measure the time it takes to reach each arrival point. The median arrival times at each point and confidence intervals were generated using Kaplan-Meier estimators. Log-rank test derived the overall significance level for both morphs to arrive the predetermined points. The survival and survminer packages implemented survival analysis42,44,45. All figures were generated using ggplot2 package while other components were prompted from the packages dplyr, ggpubr, tidyr and ggforce.

However, the adult preoviposition period (APOP) did not differ between morphs (P > 0.05). The demographic parameters GRR, R0, r, λ, and T of green aphids were higher when compared with red aphids in a consistent manner (P < 0.05, Table 1).

Orientation and mobility

Movement toward host plant odour

Survival analysis of the time-to-event (i.e., arrival to pre-determined destinations toward host plant odours) indicated a clear difference between red and green aphid morphs in the time taken to move toward the odour source at the 5 cm, 10 cm and 15 cm points along the glass tube (Log-rank test; χ2= 3.9, P = 0.048 for 5 cm; χ2= 4.0, P = 0.046 for 10 cm; χ2= 10.8, P = 0.001 for 15 cm) (Fig. 4A–D). These results indicate faster movement by red morphs toward host plant odours under these simplified linear conditions.

Mobility in host free arena

The green morph aphids were significantly slower than red morph aphids in a host free arena (χ2 = 14.48, df = 1, P < 0.001), (Fig. 4E).

Migration capability

The GLM with Poisson distribution for migration experiment (Fig. 5A) revealed that the migration capability differed significantly between morphs. The effects of morph, distance and their interaction were statistically significant (χ2morph = 16.16, df = 1, 112, P < 0.001; χ2distance = 84.50, df = 3, 112, P < 0.001; χ2morph × distance = 76.03, df = 3, 112, P < 0.001). Multiple comparison tests for nested factors confirmed the differences between morphs and distances. Migration capability increased with the distance between release point and the host plant. Green aphids showed an increased tendency to migrate over distances up to 60 cm, but plants positioned at 80 cm were less preferred, suggesting that the green morph has a more limited migration capability than the red morph (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

Our integrated study of a single M. persicae clone reveals distinct patterns between red and green colour morphs across life history, behaviour, and enemy susceptibility under controlled conditions. These patterns suggest that the green morph may be better suited for exploiting stable habitats on mature plants, while the red morph exhibits traits associated with more rapid colonization of young plants. However, given that our experiments were conducted with a single clone on one host plant species under controlled laboratory conditions, the extent to which these patterns represent general strategies across the species’ diverse populations requires further investigation. Specifically, the green morph’s higher fecundity on mature plants, greater settlement by alates, and larger population increase in the stable greenhouse environment point to a strategy favouring persistence and growth in established, resource-rich patches. In contrast, the red morph’s faster maturation, superior performance on young plants, and enhanced mobility and orientation suggest a superior capacity for rapid colonization and exploitation of ephemeral, high-quality resources. The critical trade-off for the red morph’s “fast” strategy is its significantly higher susceptibility to visually-hunting predators like H. axyridis, whereas the green morph’s “slow” strategy is bolstered by its cryptic colouration. This pattern of context-dependent morph advantages, observed in our experimental system, offers a potential explanation for the maintenance of colour polymorphism. We emphasize that this framework was observed in our experimental system and requires testing across multiple clones, host plants, and field conditions before it can be considered a general mechanism. However, confirming this as a general framework for M. persicae populations in agricultural settings would require studies incorporating multiple clones, host plants, and field conditions. The variable frequency of red and green morphs observed in field populations, and their association with different host plants—further underscores the need for such investigations.

Field surveys on oilseed rape and other brassicas typically report a predominance of green morphs, with red morph frequencies varying considerably across seasons and regions4,16,20. This predominance likely reflects the cryptic advantage of green morphs on green foliage, as well as potential fitness costs associated with the red morph’s higher susceptibility to visually hunting predators, as observed in our study. However, red morph frequencies can increase under certain conditions, for instance, on tobacco where they are often referred to as the subspecies M. persicae nicotianae16, during cooler temperatures4, or in populations under insecticide pressure20. Whether the trait differences we observed (faster development, enhanced mobility, greater predation risk in red morphs; higher fecundity on mature plants, crypsis advantage in green morphs) are consistent across these diverse contexts remains unknown. The tobacco-adapted form, for example, exhibits additional specialized traits such as altered insecticide resistance profiles20, suggesting that morph-associated characteristics may interact with host-adaptation and require separate investigation. Future studies examining multiple clones from different host plants and geographic regions will be essential to determine whether the “fast-slow” framework we propose operates consistently across the species’ extensive ecological range.

Our findings regarding development and fecundity both align with and contradict specific previous studies4,46, highlighting the context-dependency of morph performance. The key novel insight from our integrated approach is not merely confirming that differences exist, but in quantifying the suite of traits that co-vary with colour. While a previous study might find shorter maturation in red morphs46 and another might find greater longevity on a specific host like tobacco4, our study, conducted under standardised conditions, systematically demonstrates that the red morph consistently has a shorter preimaginal stage, adult longevity, and total lifespan. This pattern, when viewed alongside their behavioural traits, strengthens the hypothesis of a “fast-slow” life-history continuum limited to the clone and controlled conditions of this study. The novelty lies in revealing this coordinated set of traits, which allows for a more predictive understanding of how morph success will shift across different agricultural environments (e.g., young vs. mature crop stands).

The observed variation in population increase between the red and green morphs of M. persicae across different plant growth stages suggests that the red morph performs better on younger plants, while the green morph has a reproductive advantage on older plants. Younger leaves typically contain higher sugar content and amino acid-to-sugar molar ratios in phloem sap, which may enhance aphid growth despite elevated glucosinolate levels47–49. This nutritional advantage likely explains the red morphs’ better performance on younger plants. In contrast, older leaves may present lower nutrient levels but fewer defences, favoring the green morphs. These findings underscore the role of leaf ontogeny and associated changes in nutritional quality in influencing aphid feeding preferences and reproductive performance. This shift might be attributed to changes in plant quality, defensive responses, or resource availability with plant age or may be related to the ability of aphids to suppress the host defence system by detoxifying the toxic chemicals associated with plant growth stage50–53. Furthermore, the potential for differential insecticide resistance between morphs, as suggested by studies on the tobacco-adapted form of M. persicae (subspecies M. persicae nicotianae) where the red morph is linked to higher resistance20, could represent another layer of adaptation in this polymorphism, though whether this extends to other host-adapted populations remains to be investigated. If the red morph’s “fast” strategy is genetically or physiologically linked to enhanced detoxification mechanisms, this would create a powerful, multi-tactic adaptive advantage in intensively managed crops. Investigating the linkage between colour, life-history strategy, and resistance mechanisms is a critical avenue for future research.

The differences in speed and mobility between the red and green morphs of M. persicae reveal significant behavioural variations that impact their ecological interactions. Red morphs reached predetermined points more quickly than green morphs in a glass tube containing host plant odours, suggesting more rapid movement toward the odour source under these simplified, linear conditions. This could reflect differences in odour perception, motivation to move toward host cues, or general locomotor activity, but does not directly measure orientation toward plants in a natural context. We note that the linear tube assay measured movement toward a single odour source without choice, and the continuous airflow (200 ml s−1) may have assisted movement. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as odour-guided movement under simplified conditions rather than as direct evidence of natural host-seeking or orientation capacity. Such behavioural differences between colour morphs have been reported in other arthropods54,55. Differences in response to the same stimulus (host plant volatiles) depend upon various factors such as previous experience, age, morph and mating status, which are modulated by various biotic and abiotic factors56. The variation in responding to host plant odour also indicates the possibility of intraspecific variation in perception or recognition of host volatiles related to olfactory responses57.

Building on the observed differences in orientation and mobility, the spatial distribution of the red and green morphs of M. persicae highlights further behavioural distinctions. The number of aphids recorded on plants increased with distance from the release point, however green morphs exhibited a more limited range compared to red morphs. After 24 h, green morphs were found in lower numbers on plants at 80 cm compared to red morphs, while distributions were similar at shorter distances. This single time-point observation could indicate that green morphs have a more limited dispersal range, or alternatively, that they explore distant plants but return to closer hosts within the observation period. Without tracking individual movement over time, we cannot distinguish between these possibilities. Variability in dispersal tendency has been recorded within populations of pea aphid A. pisum58 and some studies have reported high migratory tendency of red morph as an avoidance or escape behaviour25,59.

Several aspects of our experimental design warrant consideration when interpreting the behavioural assays. The orientation experiment measured time to traverse a straight glass tube toward host plant odours—a simplified environment that does not replicate the complex, multimodal cues present during natural host-seeking. Unlike four-arm olfactometers where insects have freedom of choice between multiple odour fields60, our linear tube design may have constrained movement options and directed aphids toward a single odour source. The continuous airflow (200 ml s−1), while standard in olfactometer studies, may have assisted movement to an extent that cannot be separated from active orientation. Additionally, the exclusion of aphids that did not initiate movement within the first two minutes may have selected for more active individuals, potentially overestimating population-level responsiveness. Similarly, the mobility assay in host-free Petri dishes likely measures locomotion under stress (starvation, novel environment) rather than ecologically relevant host-seeking behaviour, as wingless aphids in nature are largely sedentary once settled on a host plant. These caveats suggest that while our assays reveal consistent differences between morphs under standardised conditions, the extent to which these translate to field-relevant behavioural differences requires further investigation using more naturalistic approaches.

The differential susceptibility of the colour morphs to different natural enemies can be explained by their distinct foraging strategies. Predators like H. axyridis larvae are primarily visual foragers. A plausible explanation for the higher predation rates on red morphs is that they contrast more sharply with green foliage, making them more easily detected by visually hunting predators. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies on colour polymorphism in pea aphids, which have demonstrated visually mediated predation risk29,30. However, we did not directly quantify colour contrast between morphs and host leaves, nor test predator visual recognition under different background conditions, so this mechanism remains to be confirmed in future studies. This is consistent with the ‘search image’ theory, where predators develop a heightened sensitivity to more conspicuous prey. Ladybirds primarily search along plant structures, and visual cues become particularly relevant once prey are encountered during this search. The fact that this preference persisted, though was less pronounced, on the host plant suggests that visual cues remain critical even in a more complex environment. In contrast, parasitoids such as A. gifuensis rely less on vision and more on olfactory and tactile cues to locate hosts, using volatile chemical signals from the infested plant or contact chemicals (cuticular hydrocarbons) on the aphid’s body31,61,62. Our results suggest that these critical chemical cues do not differ significantly between the red and green morphs, leading to the observed lack of preference. This dichotomy highlights a fundamental trade-off in the aphids’ defence strategy: while the red morph may benefit from certain physiological advantages (e.g., on young plants), it pays a cost through increased apparency to visually hunting predators, whereas the green morph’s crypsis provides a clear survival advantage against this guild of natural enemies.

In summary, this study provides a detailed analysis of the colour morphs of M. persicae, revealing substantial differences in performance and behaviour between the red and green morphs. The red morph exhibited faster maturation and higher susceptibility to predation by H. axyridis larvae, while the green morph showed increased and prolonged fecundity (period) on mature plants and a more restricted migration range. These differences can be attributed to various factors. The red morph’s quicker maturation may reflect underlying physiological differences, but our data do not directly link this to predator detection. On plants, wingless aphids are largely sedentary once settled, so any contribution of activity levels to predation risk would likely be limited to brief periods of movement. The observed higher predation on red morphs is more parsimoniously explained by their greater visual contrast against green foliage, as supported by our choice experiments where predator preference persisted even on host plants. Additionally, red morphs may have less effective chemical defences, making them more vulnerable to predation63,64. In contrast, the green morph’s prolonged maturation and greater fecundity on mature plants could result from an evolutionary strategy favouring stable, resource-rich environments. The green morph’s slower mobility and limited migration range might be due to lower energy reserves or a more conservative dispersal strategy, ensuring population stability. The uniform parasitism rates by A. gifuensis suggest that visual traits do not influence parasitism, implying that other factors like aphid behaviour or chemical cues are more significant; parasitoids primarily rely on chemical and tactile cues for host location, and aphid behaviour such as dispersal can influence encounter rates61. While this study did not investigate the presence of common endosymbionts such as Regiella and Hamiltonella, these symbionts could potentially influence the observed colour polymorphism and associated behavioural traits. Further research is warranted to determine if and how endosymbionts contribute to the observed differences in performance and behaviour between the morphs. Understanding these variations provides valuable insights for developing targeted pest management strategies and informs future research directions. We note that these management implications are derived from our controlled laboratory study using a single clone and host plant; their applicability to diverse field conditions requires validation through further research. For example, the results shown here, that the red morph is significantly more susceptible to predation by H. axyridis larvae, even on host plants, suggests that conservation biological control programs can be optimised by conserving this predator in fields where red morph populations are dominant or escalating, with augmentative release more applicable to greenhouse systems. Conversely, the superior performance of the green morph on mature plants and in greenhouse conditions highlights these as key risk environments requiring intensified monitoring. Scouting programs could use colour ratios as an early indicator to predict population dynamics and deploy targeted insecticides or biological controls pre-emptively, thereby increasing intervention efficiency and reducing unnecessary pesticide use. These findings also inform future research directions, particularly in understanding the molecular and symbiotic mechanisms underpinning these morph-specific trade-offs. While our results reveal consistent differences between red and green morphs in this clone under controlled conditions, these findings should be viewed as hypotheses about morph-specific ecology that require testing across the species’ broader genetic and environmental diversity.
