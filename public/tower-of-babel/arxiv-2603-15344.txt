Unsupervised Cross-Protocol Anomaly Analysis in Mobile Core Networks via Multi-Embedding Models Consensus
Aayush Garg, Orlando Amaral Cejas

Abstract
Mobile core networks rely on several signalling protocols in parallel, such as SS7, Diameter, and GTP, hence many security-relevant problems only become visible when their interactions are analyzed jointly. Moreover, labeled examples of real attacks and cross-protocol misconfigurations are scarce, which complicates the deployment of supervised detection methods. Motivated by these constraints, we study unsupervised cross-protocol anomaly analysis on fused representations that combine SS7, Diameter, and GTP signalling. We first combine SS7, Diameter, and GTP messages for each subscriber into per-minute fused records. We then serialize each fused record as text, obtain vector representations from several embedding models, and finally apply unsupervised anomaly detection. A consensus score is then assigned to each record as the number of embedding models that flag it as anomalous. For evaluation, we construct cross-protocol-plausible synthetic anomalies by swapping one chosen field group at a time between pairs of records, so that each signalling message remains valid in isolation while the fused cross-protocol view becomes contradictory. From our experiments, we observe that the fraction of flagged records decreases sharply as the required level of model agreement increases. On a corpus of 219,294 fused records, 44.15% are flagged by at least one model, yet only 0.97% reach full agreement of all six models. Across consensus levels, we find that high agreement is tightly coupled to the synthetic records that encode cross-protocol inconsistencies. For k = 1 − 4 k=1-4 , the odds that a flagged record is synthetic are hundreds of times larger than for original records, and for k ≥ 5 k\geq 5 all flagged records are synthetic, with extremely small p p -values indicating statistical significance. To further analyze how the models structure their decisions, we measure cosine distances in embedding space between synthetic and original records. We observe that these distances increase with the consensus level, suggesting that records on which the models agree more strongly tend to be more clearly separated from original traffic in embedding space. Taken together, these results support the use of multi-embedding models consensus to enable prioritization of a much smaller candidate set of cross-protocol inconsistencies for further inspection.

II
Related Work

The research on signalling security in mobile core networks has evolved along three converging areas: (i) protocol-specific defenses aimed at mitigating spoofing, tunnelling abuse, and signalling fraud, (ii) rule-based and supervised learning detectors that encode known message sequences or rely on labelled attack data, and (iii) unsupervised models that learn latent representations of normal behaviour to expose novel anomalies. Below, we present an overview of these three research lines applied to SS7, Diameter, and GTP protocols.

In general, prior work has achieved high precision within isolated protocols, it rarely addresses inconsistencies that emerge across signalling layers, where attacks often traverse all SS7, Diameter, and GTP in coordinated chains. The dependence on protocol-specific features and scarce labelled datasets limits generalization and adaptability in live networks. Rule-based systems excel at deterministic enforcement of signalling logic but fail to detect coordinated or previously unseen behaviours. More recent unsupervised and representation-learning approaches alleviate this dependence by modelling normal traffic distributions, yet they are typically confined to a single protocol and overlook causal dependencies across the control and user planes. Consequently, security analytics remain fragmented, with detectors tuned to protocol syntax rather than the semantics of end-to-end subscriber sessions. These identified gaps motivate our design of a cross-protocol anomaly analysis framework that employs joint representations of multi-protocol signalling flows, enabling a unified view and candidate ranking without requiring exhaustive labelling or per-protocol tuning. Our work addresses this fragmentation by correlating SS7, Diameter, and GTP features within a shared embedding space through an unsupervised, multi-model consensus ensemble. This method enables a holistic analysis of multi-protocol inconsistencies that become visible only when records are viewed jointly. Our approach builds upon complementary advances by combining multi-protocol signalling data within a shared embedding space and enabling unsupervised analysis and prioritization of cross-protocol inconsistencies.

III
Problem Statement

We study cross-protocol inconsistencies that arise when SS7, Diameter, and GTP signalling messages are viewed jointly, for the same subscriber and minute. For each fused record (i.e., all messages for one subscriber in a one-minute window), the goal is to decide whether the information carried across protocols is mutually consistent under normal operation. We consider core consistency expectations that operators enforce implicitly (or explicitly), including stable subscriber identity across protocols, compatible visited-network and location indicators, unique and non-conflicting session or tunnel references, coherent routing addresses or numbers, and agreement between the access point in use and the subscribed profile. Below, we provide the details of expectations that guide the decision for a fused record.

•

Identity coherence.
The subscriber identifier (e.g., IMSI) is the same across SS7, Diameter, and GTP for the window.

•

Visited-network and location compatibility.
Network identifiers and location indicators (e.g., PLMN and related location codes) are compatible across protocols.

•

Unique session and tunnel references.
Session identifiers in Diameter and tunnel endpoint identifiers in GTP do not collide across subscribers or concurrent sessions.

•

Address allocation coherence.
Packet Data Network (PDN) addresses assigned in GTP are unique across subscribers and consistent with the subscriber’s active context.

•

Routing plausibility.
Routing addresses or numbers in SS7 (e.g., Global Titles) and host or realm information in Diameter are plausible together and with the subscriber’s context.

•

Protocol semantic consistency.
The application context in SS7 is compatible with the invoked operation, without opcode or context mismatches.

•

Access-profile agreement.
The access point in use (APN) matches the subscribed or provisioned profile implied by control-plane signalling.

A fused record is anomalous when any expectation above fails in a way that remains plausible within each protocol but becomes contradictory in the combined (fused) view.

IV
Methodology

We analyze and surface candidate cross-protocol inconsistencies by combining per-subscriber, per-minute fusion with learned representations and per-embedding model unsupervised detectors. We build a fused record that aggregates SS7, Diameter, and GTP signalling messages and preserves their raw fields. We introduce protocol-plausible synthetic anomalies that create cross-protocol contradictions by swapping consistency-critical field groups (
one at a time
) across fused records. We pass the fused record’s JSON text to several embedding models and fit an unsupervised per-model detector on the resulting vectors. The final decision uses a consensus score equal to the number of models that flag the record as anomalous. Below, we detail on every step.

IV-A

Per-Subscriber, Per-Minute Fusion

We construct a fused record for each pair (subscriber, minute) by grouping decoded SS7, Diameter, and GTP messages on a canonical subscriber identifier and a UTC minute timestamp. Messages are sorted by their event time and appended to protocol-specific arrays (ss7, diameter, gtp) while preserving their raw JSON fields. We retain for downstream analysis only those fused windows that contain messages from at least two protocol families so that cross-protocol checks are possible. Within each window, we keep all messages in time order and preserve their native raw fields in protocol-specific arrays (
ss7[ ]
,
diameter[ ]
,
gtp[ ]
). The fusion step is deterministic and does not alter message contents.

IV-B

Synthetic Anomaly Construction

We create protocol-plausible inconsistencies through single field-group swaps applied to pairs of fused records. A field group is a coherent set of paths that refer to the same concept across messages. For each mutation, we leverage two given source records and exchange all paths in the chosen group so that each swap preserves per-protocol validity yet induces a contradiction in the joint view. For both mutated outputs we attach minimal metadata, indicating the mutation family and field group. Each synthetic record includes exactly one swap group. Pairs where paths are absent or values are identical are skipped, and all other fields remain untouched. We implement the thirteen field groups listed below. Methodologically related studies have used mutation-based transformations and controlled perturbations to examine model behaviour, evaluation sensitivity, and vulnerability-relevant artefacts
[
17
]
. In the present setting, a similar evaluation logic is applied to fused signalling records, where the goal is not to mimic a single known attack trace but to introduce industrial partner-approved protocol-plausible contradictions in the joint cross-protocol view.

In the GPRS Tunnelling Protocol control plane (GTPv2-C), the PDN Address Allocation (PAA) information element carries the IP address (IPv4 and/or IPv6) assigned to the user equipment and is included by the Packet Data Network Gateway (P-GW) in procedures such as
Create Session Response

[
11
]
. The allocation and binding of this PDN address to the subscriber’s EPS bearer context are specified in the Evolved Packet System mobility architecture, which requires consistent association of the address with the corresponding PDN connection and user identity
[
46
]
. Our mutation swaps the assigned PDN IPv4 address between two fused records while leaving GTPv2-C message structure valid, so the control-plane signalling remains syntactically correct although the address-to-subscriber binding is altered. In the fused, minute-level view, this change can make the same IP address appear on multiple subscribers or can decouple a subscriber’s Diameter and GTP contexts, which is inconsistent with the EPS requirement that a PDN address be uniquely and correctly associated with a single active PDN connection
[
43
]

IV-C

Text Serialization

We use the fused JSON text itself as input for the embedding models. Each record is a deterministic JSON line containing the protocol arrays and their raw fields. Instead of truncating fused records for length, for each model, we include only those records whose tokenization length fits its context window. This selection is performed during dataset preparation.

Next, we normalize each fused record by removing newline characters and collapsing consecutive whitespace into single ones. Each record becomes a single-line, space-separated string of tokens
[
15
,
16
]
. Then, we obtain vector representations of the normalized text, using multiple embedding models. Embeddings are obtained only for those records fitting the context window of a given model, as selected during dataset preparation. The resulting embeddings are cached for reuse.

IV-D

Per-Model Detection and Consensus

For each embedding model, we employ an unsupervised detector that operates on that model’s embedding vectors and produces a binary decision (i.e., anomalous or not) for each record. Given a fused record, the embedding model first encodes its serialized representation into a fixed-dimensional vector. The detector then assigns the label based solely on this embedding vector. When a given record exceeds an embedding model’s context window, its embedding vector is not obtained, and the unsupervised detector makes no decision for the record. For each fused record, we aggregate the available per-model decisions into a consensus count, defined as the number of embedding models whose detector flags the record as anomalous. This consensus count serves as a ranking score summarizing the level of agreement across models, with higher consensus count indicating stronger agreement. We retain the per-model binary decisions alongside the consensus count for downstream analysis.

V-C

Evaluation

To validate our results without human labels, we treat the unlabeled fused records as
background
and generate synthetic anomalies by swapping one field group at a time (mutation), as described in Section
IV
. For each model, we form an unlabeled training pool that contains background embeddings and a small proportion of synthetic embeddings (
5%
of background embeddings). Including a small proportion of synthetic embeddings in the pool lets the detector learn a density landscape where the synthetic rows are rare, while the method remains label-free as the training does not use any labels. We fit an unsupervised Isolation Forest on this pool with a fixed contamination rate of
0.01
. To avoid circular evaluation, we hold out a disjoint subset of synthetic records for testing, and we report all agreement and precision results on this held-out subset. We split the synthetic set within each mutation family so that both the training pool and the evaluation set contain examples of every family.

Next, we estimate precision at thresholds
S\geq k
using the held-out synthetic set as the positive control and the unlabeled original fused corpus as background. We apply the statistical tests to quantify the association between consensus and the held-out synthetic anomalies. Finally, we quantify how much the anomaly decisions change between a synthetic fused record and its original counterpart. For a subset of synthetic records linked to their original fused records, we compute the cosine distance between them. This provides a similarity score illustrating how differently the ensemble behaves on the two records. In the remainder of this section, we describe these evaluation steps in more detail.

V-C
2
Precision Estimation at Consensus Thresholds

We estimate precision as a function of the consensus threshold using the held-out synthetic set as the positive control. Let
\mathcal{U}
be the unlabeled fused records and let
\mathcal{S}
be the held-out synthetic records. For any threshold
k
, we consider the set

\mathcal{A}_{k}=\{\,r\in\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{S}\;:\;N(r)\geq 1\text{ and }S(r)\geq k\,\}.

The precision at threshold
k
is

\operatorname{Prec}(k)=\frac{|\mathcal{A}_{k}\cap\mathcal{S}|}{|\mathcal{A}_{k}|}.

This metric is employed to define, among the records that reach the threshold
S\geq k
, the fraction that are synthetically crafted. Records with no model decision are excluded because they cannot reach any consensus level.

We compute
\operatorname{Prec}(k)
for
k=1,\dots,6
and, for each threshold, summarize how many records satisfy
S\geq k
, how many of these belong to the original set, and what fraction of the full corpus this predicted-anomalous set represents. This enables us to examine how the synthetic share among flagged records (and thus precision on synthetic anomalies under our construction) varies with the consensus threshold. All computations use only the held-out synthetic set together with the original (unlabeled) set.

V-C
3
Statistical Association

We quantify the relationship between consensus and the held-out synthetic anomalies with tests that do not rely on distributional assumptions. Throughout this section, held-out synthetic anomalies refer to the subset of mutated fused records (field-swap) reserved for evaluation and excluded from detector fitting. We employ the
Threshold association
for this evaluation, where for a chosen threshold
k
we form a
2\times 2
table over eligible records with
N(r)\geq 1
, as shown here:

\begin{array}[]{c|cc}&\text{Synthetic}&\text{Original}\\
\hline\cr S(r)\geq k&a&b\\
S(r)<k&c&d\end{array}

We test the association between the row and the column with
Fisher
’s exact test when any cell count is small and with the
chi-square
test otherwise. We report the
p
-value and an effect size. The effect size is the odds ratio
\mathrm{OR}=\frac{a\,d}{b\,c}
with a confidence interval. We repeat this procedure for
k=1,\dots,6
. When we examine many thresholds or many mutation families we control the false discovery rate with the
Benjamini-Hochberg
procedure.

V-C
4
Cosine Similarity

For a subset of synthetic fused records, we additionally quantify how much the anomaly decisions differ from those on their original counterparts. For every synthetic–original pair, we form two six-dimensional vectors that contain the binary anomaly decisions of the six models, one vector for the original record and one for the synthetic record. We then compute the cosine similarity between these two decision vectors,

\mathrm{cos}(u,v)=\frac{u^{\top}v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|},

and use the corresponding cosine distance
1-\mathrm{cos}(u,v)
as a measure of disagreement between the ensemble decisions on the pair. Smaller distances indicate that the ensemble behaves similarly on the original and synthetic records, while larger distances indicate greater changes in the anomaly pattern across models.

VII
Threats to Validity

This section outlines potential threats to the validity of our findings, given the lack of ground-truth incident labels and the use of synthetic anomalies.

Construct validity.

Our evaluation relies on protocol-plausible synthetic anomalies created by swapping a single field-group between pairs of fused records, targeting a subset of SS7, Diameter, and GTP fields that telecom protocol security reports identify as security-relevant. This design introduces cross-protocol inconsistencies while keeping individual messages valid. However, it may only partially overlap with behaviours that matter in practice. For instance, real attacks or severe misconfigurations might touch other fields, involve multiple coordinated changes, or unfold over longer time scales. In addition, we serialize each fused record as a single JSON-like string, which can under-represent temporal structure, cross-session correlations, and rare protocol edge cases. Thus, the observed relationship between consensus and anomalousness (or irregularity) is specific to this perturbation scheme and representation, and should not be viewed as a direct calibration against real-world security incidents.

External validity.

Our dataset consists of SS7, Diameter, and GTP signalling traffic, however, traffic mixes, protocol flavours, and configuration practices can differ substantially across operators and over time. As a result, the observed relationship between consensus and anomalies may not transfer directly to other networks or environments with additional protocols or interfaces (e.g., 5G service-based or user-plane telemetry), or to different threat models. Additionally, the ensemble is instantiated with six specific embedding models and one family of unsupervised detectors, whereas alternative model choices, vector dimensions, distance metrics, or aggregation rules could alter both the distribution of consensus scores and their association with anomalies. This means that our conclusions remain valid for the selected configuration.

Internal validity.

We separate the training pool for the unsupervised detector from the evaluation corpus and exclude held-out synthetic records from fitting to limit optimistic bias and leakage, but the resulting anomalies may still be influenced by implementation artifacts, pre-processing choices, or correlations between the synthetic generation process and the detector rather than by properties that would align with real-world incidents. Statistical associations between consensus and synthetic anomalies are estimated from per-threshold contingency tables and significance tests. Although we control the false discovery rate, repeated testing and dependence between fused records mean that the resulting p-values should be viewed as approximate indicators rather than exact measures of evidence.

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper, we conducted an unsupervised cross-protocol anomaly analysis in mobile core networks by fusing SS7, Diameter, and GTP signalling data into per-subscriber, per-minute records and analyzing them with a multi-embedding unsupervised ensemble. The fused representation keeps the original signalling messages intact while exposing inconsistencies that only become visible via cross-protocols approaches. Taken together, the obtained results suggest that, under the specified synthetic anomaly design, higher consensus behaves as a useful ranking value for concentrating anomalies into a smaller candidate set, while keeping the number of background records in that set very low.

In more detail, a substantial fraction of fused records across the evaluation corpus are flagged as anomalous at the lowest consensus threshold, but high consensus remains comparatively rare. At

Additional analyses in the embedding space and over token and field co-occurrences provide a consistent picture. High-consensus records tend to lie further from typical fused records under distance-based measures and form clusters aligned with specific mutation families, whereas low-consensus records appear more dispersed. The distribution of key identifiers, locations, and routing attributes also shifts between groups of high consensus and low consensus. This is aligned with the intended cross-protocol conflicts introduced by the mutations (field swaps). While these observations are specific to the data and mutation scheme at hand, they indicate that agreement across diverse models can capture structured inconsistencies rather than arbitrary variations.

The limitations analyzed in Section
VII
point at several directions for future work. A next step could be to extend the fusion and anomaly-generation pipeline to additional protocols and network functions, including 5G service-based interfaces. This would allow to explore synthetic anomalies that affect temporal patterns as well as single snapshots. A complementary direction could be to incorporate longer-range temporal structure over sequences of fused records, for instance, by combining per-record consensus with time-series models. Furthermore, once sparse labels or analyst judgments become available, it would be natural to calibrate and stress-test the consensus scores against real-world cases, and to investigate how robust the observed patterns are across deployments, protocol mixes, and model ensembles.
