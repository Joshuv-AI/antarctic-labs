 SUMMARY
Thermophysical properties of salt systems relevant to molten salt reactors (MSRs) are experimentally mea-
sured in support of the US Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE) MSR campaign
within the Advanced Reactor Technology (ART) Program. These property measurements also support
the development of the thermophysical arm of the Molten Salt Database (MSD) within DOE-NE’s Nu-
clear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) Program. Several US Department of En-
ergy (DOE) laboratories, including Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), have been capitalizing on
modern methodologies and sample characterization techniques to conduct thermophysical property mea-
surements to support these programs and to provide MSR developers and modelers with more recent and
higher-quality data.
Researchers at ORNL have recognized that previous studies of fuel salt transport properties only con-
sider fresh fuel mixtures and do not account for property changes throughout the operational lifetime of
an MSR. Exposure to neutron fluence in an operating MSR will cause fission and transmutation of the fuel
salt, leading to fuel depletion and the augmentation of transmutation and fission products. This alteration
in salt chemistry may affect the thermophysical properties by an appreciable amount, which needs to be
quantified to properly reflect this change in transport calculations and simulation codes. To satisfy this re-
search need, viscosity and thermal conductivity measurements were performed on identical actinide chlo-
ride salt mixtures (NaCl-UCl3 , 63-37 mol %) with and without the addition of surrogate fission products.
The quantity of fission products expected to build into the fuel salt mixture was determined using the Stan-
dardized Computer Analyses for Licensing Evaluation (SCALE) code package and based on a flux spec-
trum representative of the Molten Chloride Fast Reactor (MCFR). A conservatively high estimate of ∼2.75%
for the total mass fraction of a surrogate fission product mixture was chosen to simulate end-of-life condi-
tions of a fuel salt in a fast spectrum MSR to represent bounding conditions on the altered thermophysical
properties and magnify any differences for statistical significance. Ultimately, a small but statistically sig-
nificant increase in viscosity was identified due to the addition of fission products, and thermal conductiv-
ity was marginally increased under the same conditions but remained within the uncertainty margin of the
fresh base mixture results.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Accurate knowledge of the thermophysical properties of the fuel salt in a molten salt reactor (MSR) is cru-
cial for design, modeling, licensing, and safe operation. In terms of steady-state heat transfer, these ther-
mophysical properties determine how effectively heat can be removed from the reactor core and transferred
from the fuel salt to the coolant salt by the heat exchanger. Furthermore, in transient conditions, reliable
thermophysical data help predict the system’s response to potential off-normal events, and these predic-
tions can inform design decisions to mitigate the potentially negative consequences of such events. Fi-
nally, thermophysical properties have an impact on the reactor kinetics, such that the thermophysical state
of the salt affects the temperature-dependent feedback mechanisms associated with the reactivity of the
core. Given all these ways in which the performance of an MSR is impacted by thermophysical properties,
there is a necessity for accurate, well-validated, and strategic measurements of thermophysical properties
of molten salts. These measurements can be used to inform the design and modeling efforts of MSR devel-
opers, of which there are numerous at various stages in the pathway to deployment (Jenet et al. 2025). No-
tably, three domestic test MSRs are planned to come online within the decade (Rykhlevskii 2024; Jenet et
al. 2025; Krepel and Kramer 2024), and these represent stepping stones to the deployment of commercial-
scale MSRs.
In support of the developing MSR industry, multiple national laboratories have endeavored to measure
the thermophysical properties of key molten salt mixtures (Birri et al. 2023; Karlsson et al. 2023; Rose et
al. 2023; Parker et al. 2022; Lonergan et al. 2023; Makovsky et al. 2024; Gardner, Chamberlain, and Rose
2024; Nguyen et al. 2025; Strzelecki et al. 2025). This effort is funded by the MSR Program under the US
Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE). Under this program, there have been efforts
to measure the thermophysical properties (e.g., density, viscosity, heat capacity, and thermal conductivity)
of key molten salt systems. These efforts may be either to fill gaps that exist among the plethora of exist-
ing data or to generate duplicate (and typically higher-quality) data of previously characterized molten salt
systems that require validating measurements. A primary driver for the selection of measurements that
are performed is the Molten Salt Database (MSD). This database has both a thermophysical arm, Molten
Salt Database–Thermophysical (MSD-TP) (Chesser et al. 2025), and a thermochemical arm, Molten Salt
Database–Thermochemical (MSD-TC) (Ard et al. 2022). MSD-TP consists of tabulated thermophysical
properties and relations for computing properties as a function of temperature or composition, whereas
MSD-TC contains Gibbs energy models and values for molten salt components. From MSD-TP specifi-
cally, it can be understood where the data gaps exist for various chloride and fluoride mixtures of key inter-
est, and their approximate uncertainties can be determined as well.
In general, there has been a tremendous effort (as is clear based on the breadth of the MSD) to characterize
what can be considered “fresh” salt systems. That is, property measurements have generally been con-
ducted on candidate representative fuel salts (or salt systems that are relevant to fuel salts) that represent
the beginning-of-life (BOL) condition of the fuel, where none of the fissile content has burned up. These
measurements therefore ignore the fission products (FPs), which will build up over time in an MSR as a
consequence of the fissioning of the fissile and fissionable atoms in the fuel. Some of these FPs are no-
ble gases and metals, which will not solubilize within the fuel salt and will leave the salt into the off-gas
stream or plate out on the salt-wetted components containing the fuel as it loops through the core. The FPs
that are retained within the salt can be considered “salt seekers.” These FPs are halide-forming and include
rare-earth metals as well as Zr, Cs, Ba, and Sr (McMurray et al. 2021). To some degree, these salt seek-
ers have already been thermophysically characterized, generally as pure fluoride and chloride compounds,
or for various compositions for select pseudo-binary mixtures which represent subsystems of MSR fuels.

 2

However, this does not directly address the need to understand the thermophysical state of the highly mul-
ticomponent fuel as the fuel experiences burnup, and the FPs buildup, at generally low molar percentages.
Based on the need to understand the realistic implications of FP buildup on thermophysical properties, this
report focuses on the thermophysical characterization of the NaCl-UCl3 (63-37 mol %) system as a rep-
resentative fuel salt, with the addition of surrogate FPs. Specifically, viscosity and thermal conductivity
are experimentally assessed, as these are two properties that have limited data for U-bearing salt systems,
and the uncertainties for these properties tend to be high in legacy literature. In this work, the viscosity
and thermal conductivity were measured for the BOL condition and the end-of-life (EOL) condition of
the salt. The EOL condition of the salt was determined based on burnup simulations using the Oak Ridge
Isotope GENeration (ORIGEN) code within the Standardized Computer Analyses for Licensing Evalua-
tion (SCALE) package. The viscosities of the BOL and EOL conditions of the salt were measured using
a rolling ball viscometer, and the thermal conductivities were measured using a variable gap apparatus.
Note that the NaCl-UCl3 (63-37 mol %) was assessed in previous work (for the BOL condition) for both
properties (Termini et al. 2024), so this report also acts as a source of duplicate data for that system, giving
insight into systematic errors that may arise in these types of measurements.
Understanding how a realistic addition of surrogate FPs into this representative fuel salt impacts these
transport properties is important for a few key reasons. Firstly, potential changes to the thermal conductiv-
ity will ultimately impact the spatially dependent temperature distribution in the core, both at steady-state
and transient conditions; this would have implications on core neutronics as well as temperature-dependent
thermochemical phenomena. Secondly, changes to viscosity will have impacts on the fluid flow rates,
which will have implications on conditions within the primary heat exchanger and the fuel salt pump.
Finally, changes to both of these properties will have implications on the heat removal capability from
the fuel salt to the coolant salt because conductive and convective heat transfer processes are affected by
both properties. If a realistic addition of surrogate FPs into the fuel salt results in a minimal change to both
properties, this signifies that these aforementioned impacts on temperature distribution, fluid flow, and heat
transfer may be minimal and that it may not be necessary to place a high importance on the effect of surro-
gate FPs on transport properties. As such, the results of this report can be contextualized more adequately
based on thermophysical property sensitivity studies on MSRs, wherein reactor performance metrics have
been evaluated over a range of possible thermophysical property values (Creasman 2023; Abou-Jaoude et
al. 2021).

 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
This section details the theory and governing equations for the rolling ball viscometer and the variable
gap thermal conductivity systems. Both systems are calibrated before the respective experiments. Vis-
cosity calibration uses standard oils of known viscosity and accounts for inherent variability among sys-
tem components, such as crucible inner diameter and ball bearing diameter. Calibration measurements
are performed at several crucible angles and ball diameters with a range of standard oil viscosities, result-
ing in a calibration factor that is a function of Reynolds number and the ratio of ball diameter to crucible
inner diameter. This factor is then applied to the data collected from molten salt experiments. Similarly,
thermal conductivity calibration uses helium gas as a fluid of known thermal conductivity to quantify ax-
ial and radial heat losses (heat shunting) due to furnace temperature, insulation, cooling air pressure, and
main/guard power heating. Calibration measurements are performed at several temperatures and gap sizes
that cover the conditions experienced during molten salt experiments.
During molten salt viscosity testing, only one crucible angle and ball diameter are used. However, due to
the difference in thermal expansion between the stainless steel ball and the glass crucible, the diameter ra-
tio is a function of testing temperature. Experiments are performed at four temperatures with three trials
at each temperature. This is done to generate a temperature trend in viscosity and quantify experimental
uncertainty. In molten salt thermal conductivity tests, a range of gap sizes are used at each temperature to
vary the thermal resistance offered by the salt sample and reduce the experimental uncertainty. The thermal
conductivity is then determined as a function of temperature based on a calculation involving the calibra-
tion and experimental data.

2.1 VISCOSITY
The rolling ball viscometer measures the terminal velocity of a ball rolling through a tubular crucible filled
with the molten salt system of interest. The viscosity, µ, is realized by considering a balance of forces act-
ing on the ball while it rolls. Horizontal components of the buoyancy force and gravitational driving force
are opposed by viscous forces of the fluid. Considering the contribution of each force along the axis of mo-
tion and isolating the viscous force, it can be shown that viscosity is described by Eq. (1):

where d is the ball diameter, θ is the inclination angle of the crucible measurement section, g is gravita-
tional acceleration, ρb is the density of the rolling ball, ρ f is the density of the fluid through which the ball
is rolling, t is the time required for the ball to roll a length L, K is a calibration constant based on standard

trial.
Each crucible is calibrated using a variety of known viscosity oils standardized by National Institute of
Standards and Technology (NIST). The calibration process accounts for experimental factors inherent to
each crucible (e.g., inner diameter and resulting wall effects) and for fluid flow forces (e.g., flow regime
effects) consistent from calibration to experiment. These effects are captured by the calibration constant,
K. During calibration, the velocity of the rolling ball is measured automatically through a series of gates
separated by precise distances. Each gate employs a constant laser signal. When the rolling ball passes the
laser and interrupts the signal, the time is automatically recorded with high precision. Several gates are
incorporated to provide better statistics and verify that the ball reaches a constant, terminal velocity. An
image of the viscosity calibration setup is shown in Figure 1.

Prior to loading the salt sample and assembling the experiment, all materials are thoroughly cleaned in a
multistep process. The crucible and ball bearings are rinsed with water in an ultrasonic bath for 1 h. This
process is repeated with ethanol. For the stainless steel ball bearings, an additional rinse is performed with
acetone. The glass crucible is not exposed to acetone due to the possibility of etching. Finally, the crucible
and ball bearings are heated to 200◦ C in a vacuum furnace to drive off any residual moisture.
Once dried, the crucible is transferred to a glove box for salt loading, minimizing the salt exposure to
moisture and oxygen, which may contaminate the salt sample. An appropriate amount of salt is weighed
such that the ball will be fully immersed in the molten salt while the crucible is in the “hold” position (see
Figure 2). After the dry salt is loaded into the crucible, a fitting is attached to the open end of the crucible
to seal the crucible with inert atmosphere. This fitting, which allows the crucible to be secured within the
furnace, contains three inlets to allow ball insertion, followed by vacuum purge and argon gas refill, be-
fore the ball is introduced to the molten salt. A secondary steel containment is installed around the glass
crucible and filled with argon gas. This secondary containment is meant to contain the salt in an inert at-
mosphere in the event that the glass crucible breaks during or after testing. Before melting, the loaded cru-
cible is vacuum dried at 200◦ C for several hours to drive off any residual moisture in the salt sample. Addi-
tional details regarding the secondary containment are provided in a previous report (Termini et al. 2025).
Finally, the crucible is repressurized with argon atmosphere and slowly heated to the testing temperature
overnight. An air-pressure-operated vibrational tool is loosely attached to the crucible fitting. This tool
drives off any gas bubbles that form during melting.
To perform an experiment, a ball is loaded into the molten salt while the crucible is in the “hold” position.
The ball is given 5 min to reach thermal equilibrium with the molten salt. Next, the crucible is rotated to
the “drop” position, transferring the ball to the measurement section of the crucible. Simultaneously, the
x-ray begins firing every 0.5 s. X-ray imaging is required to track the rolling ball due to the steel outer con-
tainment. One data file contains all the recorded x-ray images for postprocessing. This process is repeated
for triplicate trials at a given temperature and at four different temperatures.

2.2 THERMAL CONDUCTIVITY
This section provides a brief summary of the thermal conductivity apparatus and measurement procedure.
A complete explanation of the methodology in the thermal conductivity experiment is available in the ref-
erenced literature (Birri et al. 2023; Termini et al. 2025).
The variable gap apparatus drives a known heat flux across a fluid specimen gap and measures the rela-
tive temperature difference across a measured gap. The biggest advantage of using a variable gap system
is that the relative temperature difference between varying gaps allows for the measurement of conductiv-
ity without explicitly knowing the thermal resistance of other elements in the system. A heat flux can be
driven across the sample by heating one side of the salt sample and cooling the other, as shown in Figure 4.
The difference in temperature between the heated side, T upper , and the cooled side, T lower , is defined as the
∆T spec and can be used to calculate the thermal conductivity.

 7

Figure 4. Schematic of variable gap thermal conductivity system illustrating heating from above and
 cooling from below introducing a temperature differential across the salt specimen (adapted from
 Gallagher et al. 2022).

There are five primary thermal resistances in the system. The first two are fixed resistances, defined by
the thermal resistance from the material difference between the temperature probes and the specimen. The
total fixed resistance is defined in Eq. (5):

 Rth, f ixed = Rth,upper + Rth,lower (5)

where Rth, f ixed is the total fixed resistance, Rth,upper is the fixed resistance at the top of the sample, and
Rth,lower is the fixed resistance at the bottom of the sample. The other three resistances are dependent on
the salt specimen. These are the resistance due to radiation (Rth,rad ), conduction (Rth,cond ), and convection
(Rth,conv ). For a sufficiently small gap size, the contribution from both convection and radiation are negligi-
ble. Therefore, using Fourier’s law and the electrical circuit analog, the thermal conductivity (κcond ) can be
determined from the heat flux (q"), fixed thermal resistance (Rth, f ixed ), and ∆T spec :

                                        ∆T spec (x)                   1
                                                    = Rth, f ixed +       x                                  (6)
 q” κcond

The largest uncertainty in the variable gap apparatus comes from determining the q" across the salt spec-
imen. While the power to the guard heaters and cooling pressure is known, heat shunting is extremely
sensitive to furnace temperature, cooling pressure, insulation, and other factors. The heat shunting effects
can be determined as a function of ∆T across the sample. To determine the heat flux driven by the guard
heaters and convective cooling, a calibration is performed with helium with a known convection. The heat
flux is determined using Eq. (6). Figure 5 shows a plot of calculated true power, Q, as a function of ∆T
measured at 0.150 mm from the helium calibration. A linear equation is fitted to this data, which can be
used to determine q" in the salt measurements from the ∆T at 0.150 mm, along with the assumption that
q" = Qtrue /A, where A is the area of the specimen.

 8

 Figure 5. True power (W) as a function of ∆T spec (0.150 mm) across salt sample, as determined
 through calibration with helium.

To measure thermal conductivity, the salt is loaded in the containment and sealed. The apparatus is placed
into an insulated furnace and brought to the measurement temperature. The internal guard heaters are
turned on, the cooling air is set to a constant pressure, and the gap is set to ∼0.500 mm. Once the tem-
perature in the system has stabilized, the temperature measurement begins. The gap size is decreased at
∼0.100 mm increments until it reaches ∼0.100 mm with time allotted at each gap size such that the tem-
perature is stable for a long enough period to be measured. Figure 6 shows the ∆T over time as the gap
size is changed and held. The resistance is then calculated from q"/∆T and plotted as a function of gap
size (Figure 7). The inverse of this slope is taken to be the thermal conductivity.

2.3 JUSTIFICATION FOR FISSION PRODUCT ADDITIONS
The quantity of FPs expected to build into the representative NaCl-UCl3 (63-37 mol %) fuel salt was deter-
mined using the SCALE code package for determination of the isotopic changes to the fuel salt. The flux
spectrum used in the model was determined based on a representative core model of the Molten Chloride
Fast Reactor (MCFR) (Cisneros, Wargon, and Latkowski 2023), as this flux spectrum is going to impact
the quantity of FPs and activation products associated with the use of the representative fuel salt that has
been studied herein. This normalized flux spectrum is shown in Figure 8(a). As would be expected, this
flux predominately consists of fast neutrons (with energies greater than 0.1 MeV). For determination of the
FPs buildup for the fuel salt, it was assumed that the reactor would be operating for 20 effective full power
years (EFPY) at 1,000 MWth and that the starting fuel enrichment was that of high-assay low-enriched
uranium (HALEU) (i.e., 19.5%). This total power was used to calculate total neutron fluence based on the
spectrum in Figure 8(a) and the core volume. These data were used as inputs into an ORIGEN calculation
to determine isotopic changes to the BOL fuel as a function of time over the 20 EFPY.
The isotopic data for the mass percents of the salt-seeking FPs, as a function of EFPY, are shown in Fig-
ure 8(b). The total mass percent of the salt-seeking FPs stays relatively low, at 1.68% (1.32 mol %) after
20 EFPY. The predominant FPs are Zr and Cs, which would be retained in the salt as ZrCl4 and CsCl.

herein is that the total FP inventory of the salt-seeking elements was approximately 1.5 mol % based on

for the levels of FPs calculated for the NaCl-UCl3 (63-37 mol %) salt considered in this work.
However, it is important to recognize that there are several factors that could impact these results and cause
higher levels of burnup over a 20 EFPY time period, leading to the generation of more FPs. For exam-
ple, different enrichment levels would lead to a different FP distribution curve, potentially resulting in the
generation of a higher concentration of certain FPs. Also, the volume of the core may ultimately be dif-
ferent than the one considered in this work, which may result in an increase in the needed flux magnitude
required to achieve a certain power level, resulting in more rapid burnup of the fuel. Finally, these calcu-
lations were performed without consideration of additions of fuel throughout the fuel cycle, which would
provide more fissile and fissionable material to produce a higher yield of FPs. As such, it has been cho-
sen to consider an even higher concentration of FPs in the representative fuel salt samples measured in this
work; ultimately, a mass fraction of ∼2.75% was considered as a conservatively high estimate of the EOL
condition of the fuel. It is anticipated that this value should provide a reasonable bounding condition for a
wide array of core configurations, enrichment levels, and fuel cycle lengths. Also, given the uncertainties
associated with performing viscosity and thermal conductivity measurements, it is sensible to increase the
FP content to increase the likelihood that an observable difference is possible between the BOL and EOL
conditions of the fuel salt. The composition of the EOL condition of the NaCl-UCl3 (63–37 mol %) is de-
scribed in Table 1.

3.1.1 Viscosity of NaCl-UCl3 (63-37 mol %)
As described previously, extensive calibration is performed with NIST standard oils and different ball sizes
to determine a calibration coefficient, K. This coefficient is a function of Reynolds number (Re) and the

tablished for K as a function of Reynolds number and d/D ratio, based on the calibration data given in

For the NaCl-UCl3 system, viscosity testing was performed at four temperatures, including 825, 900, 950,

the GUI described previously. Associated uncertainties from the calibration coefficient, ball and fluid den-

value in the experimental viscosity calculated for each trial (methodology described in (Birri et al. 2023)).

 4. CONCLUSION
Viscosity and thermal conductivity measurements were performed on NaCl-UCl3 (63-37 mol %) with and
without 2.21 mol % surrogate FP additions to determine the impact of burnup on these thermophysical
properties during the lifetime of an operating MSR. The selected quantity of surrogate FPs was determined
by using ORIGEN to determine the extent to which the uranium would burn up and quantify the content of
salt-seeking FPs. Results indicated that the addition of approximately 2.21 mol % surrogate FPs caused a
consistent and statistically significant increase in viscosity of about 10% across the measured temperature
range. In contrast, while thermal conductivity values were marginally higher with the addition of surrogate
FPs, the change remained within the experimental uncertainty margin and was not considered statistically
significant. These results will ideally provide a bounded understanding of how these properties will evolve
over the course of operation in an MSR and may justify simplifications in the modeling and licensing pro-
cesses. Namely, these results suggest that thermal conductivity of the fuel will not have any statistically
significant changes over a significant operational period; this may allow for the assumption of temporal
independence of this property, making thermal hydraulic modeling less cumbersome.
