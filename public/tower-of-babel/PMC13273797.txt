Mechanisms associated with tDCS as an adjuvant to shortened phonomotor treatment in chronic post-stroke aphasia
Authors: Venkatagiri Krishnamurthy, Amy D Rodriguez, Bruce A Crosson, Maryanne Weatherill, Elizabeth O Tibus, Rachael M Harrington, Josseirys Valentin Rivera, Gabriell S Champion, Lisa C Krishnamurthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdjuvant therapies in aphasia rehabilitation may help reduce the cost and clinical resources required for intensive speech-language interventions. We conducted a proof-of-principle pilot study to evaluate whether transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) paired with a shortened course of phonomotor treatment (sPMT)—targeting sounds and nonwords but not real words—enhances phonological production in chronic post-stroke aphasia, and to examine the neural mechanisms underlying this approach. Using a double-blind, parallel-group design, participants received 30 h of sPMT combined with 1 mA tDCS (anode/cathode over left/right inferior frontal gyrus) delivered at the start of each intervention session. Here, we report data from six older male participants matched on aphasia severity (active: n = 3; sham: n = 3). Phonological production and confrontation naming were assessed at baseline, immediately post-intervention, and at 3-month follow-up. Structural and resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) were acquired at baseline and post-intervention.Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant group-by-time interaction for phonological production, with the active tDCS + sPMT group showing gains from baseline to post-intervention that were maintained at 3 months, whereas the sham group showed no significant improvement. Confrontation naming showed no significant effects of time or group. MRI-based estimates of current density (J) indicated that J varied systematically with lesion volume and inter-electrode distance, underscoring the importance of individualized electrode placement. rs-fMRI analyses demonstrated significant group-by-time interactions, with greater connectivity increases in the active versus sham group across domain-specific and domain-general networks.These preliminary findings suggest that active tDCS may enhance the effects of sPMT on phonological production and provide mechanistic support for individualized, network-informed neuromodulatory approaches in post-stroke aphasia rehabilitation.Keywords: tDCS, PMT, Aphasia, rs-fMRI, Current modeling, Phonological production, Domain-general, Domain-specific, Language, Speech

Introduction

The burden of post-stroke aphasia and the need for scalable intervention

Chronic post-stroke aphasia is a language disorder resulting from damage to language-related brain regions and their supporting networks. It is associated with substantial healthcare costs and markedly reduced quality of life compared to both stroke survivors without aphasia and age-matched peers (Ellis et al., 2012; Hilari and Byng, 2009; Hilari et al., 2012; Ross and Wertz, 2002). Among a wide range of medical conditions, aphasia has been shown to have one of the most profound negative impacts on health-related quality of life (Lam and Wodchis, 2010). These findings underscore the devastating personal, social, and economic consequences of communication impairment and highlight the urgent need for effective, accessible, and scalable rehabilitation approaches.

One promising intervention is Phonomotor Treatment (PMT), a multimodal therapy designed to strengthen phonological sequence knowledge through the integration of visual, auditory, kinesthetic, tactile, and orthographic cues (Silkes et al., 2019). Prior work by Kendall and colleagues has demonstrated that PMT improves naming of trained words and, importantly, promotes generalization to untrained words—a key challenge in aphasia rehabilitation (Kendall et al., 2003, 2006; Raymer et al., 2002; Bislick, 2020). These findings suggest that targeting phonological processing can support broader language recovery.

Despite its demonstrated efficacy, the standard PMT protocol requires approximately 60 h of therapy, which presents a significant barrier to implementation in most clinical settings (Dignam et al., 2024). This limitation raises an important clinical and mechanistic question: whether the duration of PMT can be reduced without compromising its effectiveness. Addressing this question requires not only testing shorter treatment protocols but also identifying adjunctive approaches that may enhance treatment efficiency and support language recovery.

Phonological processing, inferior frontal gyrus dysfunction, and the mechanistic basis of PMT

Phonological impairments are a core feature of post-stroke aphasia and are commonly associated with damage to the left inferior frontal gyrus (L-IFG) and its connected language networks. The L-IFG plays a critical role in phonological encoding, sequencing, and articulatory planning, enabling the transformation of sound-based representations into spoken output (Indefrey and Levelt, 2004; Hickok and Poeppel, 2007; Hagoort, 2005). Following a stroke, disruption of this region and its associated networks can impair the ability to accurately assemble and produce phonological units, leading to errors in speech production (Dell et al., 2013; Martínez-Molina et al., 2022).PMT is designed to directly target these phonological deficits by strengthening phonological representations and the processes that support speech production (Kendall and Nadeau, 2016; Kendall et al., 2015). Through repeated engagement with phonemes and phoneme sequences across multiple sensory modalities, PMT aims to reinforce the mapping between phonological representations and motor output (Kendall and Nadeau, 2016). This approach is thought to promote more stable and accessible phonological representations, thereby improving both trained and untrained word production (Kendall and Nadeau, 2016; Kendall et al., 2015).

From a mechanistic perspective, PMT may facilitate recovery through activity-dependent recruitment of spared perilesional language tissue and residual fronto-temporo-parietal connections during repeated phonological practice (Wilson and Schneck, 2020; Fridriksson et al., 2012; Hartwigsen and Saur, 2019). Functional imaging studies of post-stroke aphasia suggest that better recovery is often associated with re-engagement of residual left-hemisphere language regions, including inferior frontal areas, whereas right-hemisphere recruitment may emerge when left-hemisphere language networks are extensively damaged or insufficiently available (Wilson and Schneck, 2020; Mattioli et al., 2014; Crosson et al., 2009; Turkeltaub et al., 2011). In this context, PMT may engage phonological encoding and articulatory planning processes supported by residual L-IFG networks, while bilateral frontal regions, including the R-IFG, may provide compensatory support for phonological selection, sequencing, and speech-motor control when left-hemisphere resources are limited. However, the extent to which these regions support recovery, and how they can be optimally engaged during therapy, remains an open question (Crosson et al., 2019).

Given this reliance on repeated activation of phonological and articulatory systems, PMT provides a strong candidate for augmentation with neuromodulatory approaches that may enhance cortical excitability and network-level plasticity (Kendall and Nadeau, 2016; Kendall et al., 2015; Nitsche and Paulus, 2000; Stagg and Nitsche, 2011). This raises the possibility that pairing PMT with targeted brain stimulation could amplify therapy-related activity within bilateral IFG networks (Baker et al., 2010; Marangolo et al., 2016; Elsner et al., 2020; Zettin et al., 2021).

Neuromodulation as an adjuvant to enhance phonological rehabilitation

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a non-invasive neuromodulatory technique that delivers low-intensity electrical currents to modulate cortical excitability (Nitsche and Paulus, 2000, 2001). Anodal stimulation is generally associated with increased neuronal excitability, whereas cathodal stimulation is thought to reduce excitability, thereby influencing ongoing neural activity during task performance (Nitsche and Paulus, 2000, 2001; Stagg and Nitsche, 2011). In the context of aphasia rehabilitation, tDCS has been explored as a means of enhancing the effects of behavioral language therapy, by facilitating activity in residual language networks (Baker et al., 2010; Elsner et al., 2020; Zettin et al., 2021; Monti et al., 2008).

Meta-analyses of tDCS in aphasia suggest that stimulation of frontal language regions, particularly the L-IFG, is associated with improved language outcomes (Elsner et al., 2020). However, most prior work has focused on lexical-semantic processing, with comparatively limited investigation of phonological interventions such as PMT (Baker et al., 2010; Marangolo et al., 2016; Elsner et al., 2020; Zettin et al., 2021; Monti et al., 2008). One notable exception is Marangolo et al. (2016), which demonstrated that bilateral frontal stimulation improved articulatory accuracy, suggesting that neuromodulation may be particularly well-suited to enhancing phonological training (Marangolo et al., 2016).

Building on this evidence and the central role of the IFG in phonological encoding and articulatory planning, bilateral IFG stimulation (anode over L-IFG and cathode over R-IFG) represents a mechanistically motivated approach to augment PMT. Specifically, anodal stimulation over the L-IFG may increase excitability in residual perilesional phonological processing regions, thereby strengthening the left-hemisphere networks repeatedly engaged during PMT (Marangolo et al., 2013). In parallel, cathodal stimulation over the R-IFG may modulate contralesional frontal activity, potentially reducing inefficient right-hemisphere over-recruitment while still allowing compensatory bilateral network engagement when needed (Baker et al., 2010; Otal et al., 2015). When paired with PMT, which repeatedly engages phonological representations and speech-motor mappings, this stimulation approach may amplify treatment-related neural activity and promote more robust behavioral gains.

In addition to local effects on cortical excitability, tDCS may also influence distributed language networks by modulating connectivity between domain-specific (language) and domain-general (cognitive control) systems. Given that recovery from aphasia is thought to depend on the dynamic interaction of these networks (Hartwigsen and Saur, 2019; Grefkes and Fink, 2011; Carter et al., 2012; Corbetta and Shulman, 2011; Vossel et al., 2014), combining tDCS with PMT provides a promising framework for enhancing both localized and network-level mechanisms of recovery.

Mechanistic considerations for combining sPMT and tDCS in post-stroke aphasia

While tDCS offers a promising approach to augment phonological rehabilitation, its effectiveness in stroke populations depends on both the physical distribution of current within the lesioned brain and its interaction with functional brain networks that support recovery. Accordingly, two key mechanistic considerations are critical for understanding how tDCS may enhance the effects of PMT.

First, the distribution of electrical current in the presence of a stroke lesion differs substantially from that in the healthy brain. Prior studies optimizing electric field (E) and current density (J) have largely been conducted in anatomically intact brains (Caulfield and George, 2022), limiting their direct applicability to stroke populations. In lesioned brains, current flow is altered as it passes through tissues with varying electrical properties, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and is further distorted by the presence of a lesion. These factors can reduce or redirect the amount of current reaching target cortical regions, thereby influencing the effectiveness of stimulation. Indeed, simulation studies have demonstrated that electric field magnitude can differ by up to 42% relative to non-lesioned brains, depending on lesion size, lesion-to-target distance, and tissue conductivity (Evans et al., 2023). Moreover, empirical studies suggest that lesion location can significantly influence behavioral responses to stimulation (Baker et al., 2010; Meinzer et al., 2016). Despite these insights, there remains no standardized approach for optimizing electrode placement in stroke populations, contributing to variability in outcomes across studies (Fridriksson et al., 2018, 2019). These challenges highlight the need to better characterize how current density is modulated in the presence of a lesion, particularly in relation to its distance from the stimulation target.

Second, beyond its effects on current distribution, tDCS may influence recovery by modulating large-scale brain networks involved in language processing. Contemporary models of post-stroke recovery emphasize that improvements in language function depend on dynamic interactions between domain-specific language regions and domain-general cognitive control networks (Hartwigsen and Saur, 2019; Grefkes and Fink, 2011; Carter et al., 2012; Corbetta and Shulman, 2011; Vossel et al., 2014). Rather than directly inducing synaptic plasticity, tDCS is thought to alter the resting membrane potential of neurons, thereby increasing (anodal) or decreasing (cathodal) the likelihood of neuronal firing during task engagement (Nitsche and Paulus, 2000; Weber et al., 2014). When paired with behavioral therapy such as PMT, which actively engages phonological and articulatory systems, this modulation may facilitate more efficient communication between surviving nodes of the language network (e.g., left inferior frontal and temporal regions) and supporting domain-general regions (e.g., frontal and parietal control networks). In turn, these network-level changes may support neuroplastic reorganization and contribute to improvements in language performance.

Together, these mechanistic considerations suggest that the effects of tDCS as an adjuvant to PMT are shaped by both individual anatomical factors, such as lesion characteristics, and system-level processes related to functional connectivity. Understanding these interacting mechanisms is essential for optimizing stimulation protocols and improving the precision of combined behavioral and neuromodulatory interventions in aphasia rehabilitation. Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) provides a non-invasive approach for examining treatment-related changes in functional connectivity across distributed brain networks, including domain-specific language regions and domain-general cognitive control systems (Duncan and Small, 2016).

To date, however, there has been limited neuroimaging work examining PMT, with emerging studies primarily focused on study protocols rather than reporting empirical neuroimaging findings. For example, Boukrina et al. recently proposed integrating fMRI with PMT in a randomized clinical trial targeting reading recovery in post-stroke aphasia (Boukrina et al., 2025); however, to our knowledge, no published studies have reported functional neuroimaging results examining the neural mechanisms underlying PMT specifically in relation to phonological production. Accordingly, it remains unclear which neural networks are modulated during PMT, particularly when combined with neuromodulation.

Study rationale and hypotheses

Building on the need to enhance the efficiency of phonological rehabilitation, the present study investigates whether combining shortened Phonomotor Treatment (sPMT) with targeted neuromodulation can improve treatment outcomes in individuals with chronic post-stroke aphasia. Specifically, we implemented a condensed version of PMT (30 h over 3 weeks) designed to focus on strengthening phonological representations through training on phonemes and non-word sequences. Given prior evidence that training on non-words can generalize to real-word production (Castro et al., 2022; Mayer et al., 2024), this approach provides a controlled framework for targeting core phonological processes while reducing treatment duration.

To augment the effects of sPMT, we applied bilateral IFG tDCS, with anodal stimulation over the L-IFG and cathodal stimulation over the R-IFG. This montage was selected based on its role in phonological processing and the potential for neuromodulation to enhance activity in residual language regions while modulating contralesional contributions (Indefrey and Levelt, 2004; Hickok and Poeppel, 2007; Hagoort, 2005; Baker et al., 2010; Marangolo et al., 2016; Otal et al., 2015; Nixon et al., 2004). In line with the Rehabilitation Treatment Specification System (RTSS) framework (Van Stan et al., 2019; Fridriksson et al., 2022), this combined intervention can be conceptualized as applying a treatment ingredient (tDCS + sPMT) to influence a specific target (phonological processing) and produce measurable outcomes (improved phonological production and related language functions).

Guided by these mechanistic considerations, this study was designed to evaluate both behavioral outcomes and underlying neural mechanisms associated with sPMT combined with tDCS. We hypothesized that participants receiving active tDCS paired with sPMT would demonstrate greater improvements in phonological production compared to those receiving sham stimulation (Hypothesis 1). We further examined whether individual anatomical factors influence stimulation effects, hypothesizing that the estimated current density (J) in the lesioned brain would be related to the distance between the stroke lesion and the stimulation target (anode) (Hypothesis 2). Finally, we investigated network-level changes in functional connectivity. We hypothesized that both groups would exhibit changes over time in domain-specific (language) and domain-general (cognitive) networks, and that these changes would be greater in the active tDCS condition compared to sham (Hypothesis 3). To further explore the potential generalization of treatment effects, we conducted an exploratory analysis examining whether improvements in phonological processing extend to confrontation naming performance.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was approved by the joint Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Emory University and the Joseph Maxwell Cleland Atlanta Veterans Affairs Medical Center (Atlanta VA). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Study procedures were conducted at the Atlanta VA in dedicated research spaces, with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed at Emory University.

Using a double-blind, parallel-group design, twelve participants with chronic post-stroke aphasia were initially enrolled. Participants were recruited via approved flyers posted in Atlanta VA clinics and through word of mouth.

Inclusion criteria were: age 18–89 years, English as the primary language, and ≥6 months post-onset left-hemispheric stroke resulting in aphasia and phonological impairment, assessed using the Western Aphasia Battery (WAB) (Kertesz, 2007) and the Standardized Assessment of Phonology in Aphasia (SAPA) (Kendall et al., 2010), respectively, with impairment determined through consensus among licensed speech-language pathologists on the study team (ADR, MW, EOT). Exclusion criteria included (1) severe apraxia; (2) neurological disease other than stroke affecting language; (3) severe hearing or vision impairment interfering with study procedures; (4) MRI contraindications, including implanted medical devices, metal in the body, and claustrophobia; and (5) tDCS contraindications, including uncontrolled seizures, use of medications that lower the seizure threshold, and sensitive scalp by self-report.

Of the twelve enrolled participants, one withdrew due to the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, two withdrew due to time constraints, one did not complete longitudinal imaging, and one was unable to safely undergo MRI. Of the remaining seven participants, one additional participant was excluded following unblinding to match groups based on Western Aphasia Battery Aphasia Quotient (WAB AQ). The final cohort included six participants, whose demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Participants received sPMT paired with either active or sham tDCS. Group assignment was determined via coin toss by a study team member (VK). Stimulation parameters are described in detail in Section 2.2.

All participants received 30 h of sPMT (2 h/day, 5 days/week for 3 weeks) delivered by a licensed speech-language pathologist (EOT) who was blinded to group assignment. The intervention focused on phoneme-level training (Stage 1) and phoneme sequencing in nonwords (Stage 2); consistent with the shortened protocol, real-word training was not included.

Intermittent short breaks were permitted during each session as requested by participants; break duration and frequency were kept consistent across sessions to minimize variability in performance.

Behavioral assessments of phonological production and confrontation naming were administered at baseline (≤1 week prior to intervention), immediately post-intervention (≤1 week after completion), and at 3-month follow-up. The WAB was administered at baseline only. All assessments were conducted by blinded licensed speech-language pathologists (ADR, MW, RMH).

Anatomical MRI was acquired at baseline to identify lesion location and estimate tDCS-induced electric field (E) and current density (J). Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) was collected at baseline and post-intervention to assess changes in functional connectivity.

No formal power analysis was conducted due to the pilot nature of this study. The primary aim was to assess feasibility and generate preliminary effect size estimates to inform future, adequately powered studies (Eldridge et al., 2016).

tDCS setup and stimulation

tDCS was delivered using a single-channel, battery-operated device designed for double-blind administration (Soterix 1×1 tES Clinical Trials platform). Prior to each session, the device was powered on for at least 15 min to allow for system stabilization.

At the start of each intervention session, stimulation was delivered for 20 min at 1 mA. Stimulation was applied using 5 × 7 cm sponge electrodes, each saturated with 12 mL of saline solution to ensure adequate scalp conductivity. For the active condition, stimulation consisted of a 15-s ramp-up, followed by 19.5 min of constant 1 mA current, and a 15-s ramp-down. For the sham condition, stimulation consisted of a 15-s ramp-up to 1 mA followed immediately by a 15-s ramp-down to 0 mA, resulting in 19.5 min of no stimulation (Gandiga et al., 2006). Both participants and study personnel involved in treatment delivery remained blinded to the stimulation condition.

Before initiating stimulation, electrode contact quality was verified using the device indicator, which was required to display a “green” signal. During stimulation, contact quality was continuously monitored to ensure adequate current delivery. This indicator was visible regardless of stimulation condition, allowing monitoring without compromising blinding.

At the conclusion of the 20-min stimulation period, study staff (not involved in treatment delivery) entered the room to turn off the device and remove electrodes and associated equipment. To preserve blinding, the clinician administering therapy turned away during equipment removal and did not participate in this process. Following removal, participants wore a hat covering electrode sites (F7 and F8) to prevent the clinician from observing any skin changes that could reveal the stimulation condition. The clinician then resumed administration of sPMT for the remainder of the session.

Behavioral outcomes

The primary outcome measure of sPMT paired with tDCS was phonological production, operationalized using the SAPA Subtest 3 (Repetition, Parsing, Blending). SAPA Subtest 1 (reading) and Subtest 2 (auditory comprehension) were not included as primary outcomes, as the study specifically aimed to assess changes in phonological production.

The secondary outcome measure was confrontation naming, assessed using 41 untrained real-world noun stimuli presented as photographic color images from prior PMT studies (see Table 2 in (Kendall et al., 2015)). These stimuli were selected to ensure comparability with existing PMT literature, and all participants were administered the same set of items.

MRI data collection

MRI data were collected at baseline and immediately post-intervention to capture non-invasive intervention-related brain changes. The MR protocol included a T1w and T2w high-resolution anatomical scan and an rs-fMRI scan with an associated TopUp pair of scans to remove distortions. All scans were collected on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner (Erlangen, Germany) using the body coil for radio frequency (RF) transmission and a 32-channel phased-array head coil for RF receiving. Participants were asked to remain still and relaxed with their eyes open during the scan while gazing at a fixated cross at the center of the screen. Foam pads were used to minimize head movement both during and between scans. Anatomical scans were used to identify lesion location and to estimate electric field (E) and current density (J) (see Section 2.6) and were collected with the following parameters: (1) T1-MPRAGE (TR = 2530ms, TE = 2.96ms, TI = 1100ms, FA = 7°, isotropic resolution = 1x1x1mm (Hilari et al., 2012), acquisition bandwidth = 130Hz), and (2) T2-SPACE (TR = 3200ms, TE = 285ms, FA = 120°, isotropic resolution = 1x1x1mm (Hilari et al., 2012), acquisition bandwidth = 700Hz). The resting state fMRI timecourse was acquired with the CMRR suite of Multi-Band Accelerated EPI Pulse Sequences (Moeller et al., 2010; Setsompop et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2013) using a BOLD-weighted single-shot 2D gradient recalled echo planar imaging (EPI) multi-band sequence with a field of view of 220 × 220 mm2, multi-band acceleration factor of 3 and in-plane GRAPPA acceleration of 2, 72 axial slices with a slice thickness of 2 mm, TR of 1500 ms, TE of 25 ms, acquisition bandwidth of 2165 Hz/px, FA of 50°, and voxel size = 2 x 2 x 2 mm3. The rs-fMRI acquisition lasted 7 min and 30 s (300 vol), consistent with prior work demonstrating that functional connectivity estimates achieve reasonable stability at approximately 5 min, with diminishing gains beyond 9–12 min (Birn et al., 2013).

Lesion demarcation

The T1w and T2w anatomical images underwent denoising, bias field correction, and then skull-stripped, co-registered, and spatially normalized to MNI-152 standard space using linear (FSL, FLIRT) and non-linear (FSL, FNIRT) algorithms via enantiomorphic normalization (Nachev et al., 2008). Precise lesion demarcation was carried out using the Tissue Integrity Gradation via T2w T1w Ratio (TIGR)(Krishnamurthy et al., 2021a) technique developed by our group. The TIGR map in native space was thresholded at TIGR≥0.8 to define the core lesion, also referred to as the cavitation of the lesion. The core lesion volume was extracted in 1 mm isotropic native space and further used in analyses for E and J modeling and resting state connectivity.

Modeling of tDCS electric field and current density

During each sPMT + tDCS intervention session, 1 mA stimulation generated an electric field (E) between the anode (F7) and cathode (F8), along with an associated current density (J). To estimate these parameters for each participant, individualized head models were generated using the CHARM pipeline in SimNIBS (Puonti et al., 2020).

High-resolution T1w and T2w anatomical MRI images were segmented into tissue classes, including white matter, gray matter, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), bone, scalp, eyeball, compact bone, spongy bone, blood, and muscle. Lesion cavitation identified via TIGR maps was labeled as CSF. Standard tissue conductivity values implemented in SimNIBS were applied (Saturnino et al., 2019).

Finite element meshes were constructed using tetrahedral and triangular elements, with each element assigned a conductivity value based on tissue classification. Electric field (E) and current density (J) distributions were then modeled based on tissue conductivity and stimulation parameters.

The resulting E and J distributions were resampled into volumetric NIFTI space. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined as 30 mm spheres centered at F7 and F8 in MNI space and transformed to native space to estimate average E and J beneath each electrode.

rs-fMRI preprocessing and connectivity analysis

Preprocessing was performed using AFNI and FSL. Functional images were corrected for slice timing (AFNI, 3dTshift), head motion (AFNI, 3dvolreg), and susceptibility-induced EPI distortions (FSL, topup). Denoising was performed using independent component analysis (FSL, melodic), with physiological and MRI-related artifacts manually identified and removed (fsl_regfilt) as described previously (Krishnamurthy et al., 2021b). This approach was selected to reduce structured non-neuronal variance in the rs-fMRI signal, including physiologic and scanner-related artifacts, consistent with prior work from our group demonstrating the importance of physiologic-noise correction for improving resting-state signal quality and interpretability (Krishnamurthy et al., 2018).

Denoised functional images were co-registered to T1-weighted anatomical images using boundary-based registration (FSL, bbregister; FreeSurfer), and subsequently normalized to MNI space (2 × 2 × 2 mm3 resolution; FSL, applywarp). Lateral ventricles were excluded to minimize pulsatile-motion artifacts in adjacent brain regions.

Following preprocessing, time series were low-pass filtered (cutoff frequency = 0.1 Hz) using a Chebyshev Type II filter implemented in MATLAB, consistent with prior work (Krishnamurthy et al., 2018). Spatial smoothing was applied using a 4 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel (AFNI, 3dBlurInMask), constrained by a mask excluding CSF-filled ventricles and lesion cavitation.

Seed-based connectivity analysis was used to quantify functional brain connections at baseline and post-intervention. Seed regions were selected to represent domain-specific (F7, left supramarginal gyrus [L-SMG]) and domain-general (left middle frontal gyrus [L-MFG]) networks. All seeds were defined in MNI space and adapted for each participant by excluding voxels corresponding to lesion cavitation.

The F7 seed was selected due to its proximity to the stimulation site and the role of the L-IFG in phonological production and executive control (Nixon et al., 2004). The L-SMG was included due to its involvement in phonological processing and working memory (Oberhuber et al., 2016), and the L-MFG was selected as a domain-general region implicated in language recovery and cognitive control (Cherney et al., 2021; Song et al., 2023).

For each seed, the mean time course was extracted and correlated with the time courses of all other voxels. Correlation coefficients were transformed using the Fisher z-transformation to normalize the distribution.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using JMP Pro 17 and AFNI. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 unless otherwise specified.

Behavioral analysis

To evaluate the effects of sPMT + tDCS on phonological production and confrontation naming, repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed with group (active vs. sham) as a between-subjects factor and time as a within-subjects factor. Group-by-time interaction effects were assessed, and results are reported using F-statistics and associated p-values. Effect sizes comparing change scores between groups were computed using G*Power 3.1.

Electric field and current density analysis

Paired t-tests were used to compare current density (J) between hemispheres (F7 vs. F8) to assess differences between lesioned and contralesional regions.

To investigate sources of variability in J under the anode (F7), multiple linear regression was performed using three predictors: (1) distance between electrode centers, (2) distance between the lesion center of mass and the F7 ROI, and (3) lesion volume. Results are reported using t-statistics and associated p-values.

Associations between current density and behavioral outcomes were evaluated using repeated-measures ANOVA including group-by-J interaction terms.

Functional connectivity analysis

Seed-based functional connectivity analyses were conducted using AFNI (3dMVM) with fixed effects of group (active vs. sham) and time (baseline vs. post-intervention), and lesion volume included as a covariate (Chen et al., 2014). Analyses were performed separately for each seed region (F7, L-SMG, L-MFG).

Whole-brain statistical maps were thresholded at p < 0.05 and cluster-corrected at a minimum of 100 voxels. A whole-brain mask excluding lesion regions was applied to minimize bias due to lesion heterogeneity.

Results

Group-level demographics after unblinding

Following unblinding and matching for WAB AQ, we assessed whether demographic and clinical variables differed between the active and sham groups. Baseline WAB AQ was matched across groups (IP2_02 with IP2_03, IP2_05 with IP2_06, and IP2_09 with IP2_11; Table 1) and did not differ significantly (t (4) = 0.06, p = 0.95; active = 76.3 ± 10.6, sham = 75.3 ± 10.6).

There were no significant group differences in age (t (4) = 2.08, p = 0.11), core lesion volume (t (4) = 0.95, p = 0.42), or time post-stroke (t (4) = -0.41, p = 0.70).

No significant relationships were observed between age and baseline WAB AQ (F (1,4) = 2.15, p = 0.22), baseline SAPA score (F (1,4) = 0.33, p = 0.59), or confrontation naming performance (F (1,4) = 3.01, p = 0.16). Therefore, age was not included as a covariate in subsequent analyses.

Behavioral response to sPMT + tDCS

To assess the behavioral response of sPMT + tDCS, repeated-measures ANOVA of SAPA Subtest 3 revealed a significant time-by-group interaction (F (2,2) = 17.16, p = 0.05), indicating differential changes in phonological production between groups (Fig. 1, left panel).

Across both groups, phonological production improved significantly from baseline to post-intervention (t (5) = 3.42, p = 0.02). This effect was driven by the active tDCS group (t (2) = 4.80, p = 0.04), whereas the sham group did not show a significant change (t (2) = 1.56, p = 0.26). No significant change was observed between post-intervention and 3-month follow-up (t (5) = 0.62, p = 0.57), indicating maintenance of gains over time.

In contrast, repeated-measures ANOVA of confrontation naming revealed no significant effect of time (F (2,2) = 3.92, p = 0.20) or time-by-group interaction (F (2,2) = 0.41, p = 0.71), indicating no improvement in naming performance (Fig. 1, right panel).

The mean change in phonological production from baseline to post-intervention was greater in the active group (4.56 ± 1.64) compared to the sham group (1.67 ± 1.86) (effect size, Cohen's d = 1.65). From baseline to 3-month follow-up, mean change scores were 4.17 ± 0.24 (active) and 3.33 ± 1.86 (sham) (d = 0.63).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate brain mechanisms associated with adding tDCS as an adjuvant to a shortened course of phonomotor treatment in persons with chronic post-stroke aphasia. Data from six older male participants, who were matched on aphasia severity (n = 3 active, n = 3 sham), were selected from a larger data set. Both behavioral assessments and mechanistic resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) indicated that active tDCS combined with sPMT yielded immediate improvement in phonological production that was maintained three months later. In contrast, the sham group, which is equivalent to just receiving sPMT, exhibited connectivity changes in some domain-general and domain-specific language areas but did not demonstrate significant improvements in phonological production. Neither group showed generalization to confrontation naming. The following sections will discuss our findings in relation to the proposed hypotheses.

Active tDCS may enhance sPMT outcomes

We hypothesized that combining sPMT with active bilateral IFG tDCS would result in greater improvements in phonological production compared to sham stimulation (Hypothesis 1). Our findings support this hypothesis. Participants receiving active tDCS demonstrated improvements in phonological production at post-intervention that were maintained at 3-month follow-up, whereas the sham group did not exhibit significant change despite receiving the same behavioral intervention.

These findings suggest that active tDCS may enhance the efficacy of sPMT, potentially by modulating cortical excitability and facilitating engagement of residual phonological networks during therapy in older males. This interpretation is consistent with prior work demonstrating that tDCS can augment behavioral language interventions by increasing the likelihood of neuronal firing in task-relevant regions and supporting activity-dependent plasticity (Nitsche and Paulus, 2000; Weber et al., 2014). In particular, stimulation of frontal language regions, including the L-IFG, has been associated with improved language outcomes in aphasia rehabilitation (Elsner et al., 2020).

Importantly, the observed between-group effect size (d = 1.65) suggests a substantial benefit of active stimulation in this pilot cohort. This effect is unlikely to be explained by baseline differences, as groups were matched on aphasia severity (WAB-AQ), and no significant differences were observed in age or time post-stroke. Together, these findings suggest that tDCS provided an additive benefit beyond sPMT alone in this sample.

In contrast to prior work demonstrating that PMT can generalize to untrained words (Kendall et al., 2015), we did not observe significant improvements in confrontation naming in either group. One possible explanation is that the shortened sPMT protocol did not include training on real words, which may limit the extent to which phonological gains transfer to lexical retrieval. This interpretation aligns with evidence suggesting that generalization depends, in part, on overlap between trained and untrained representations, as well as the integrity of underlying semantic networks (Schwen Blackett et al., 2025; Holland et al., 2018; van Hees et al., 2013), and differences in the neural pathways supporting real-word versus pseudo-word processing (Krishnamurthy et al., 2019).

Alternatively, the absence of generalization may reflect the complexity of mapping phonological improvements onto higher-level language functions such as naming, which require coordinated engagement of phonological, lexical, and semantic systems. Prior studies have shown that tDCS applied to frontal regions can support language improvements across domains, including work demonstrating cross-domain generalization from motor to language recovery (Hesse et al., 2007). However, such effects may depend on task demands, stimulation targets, and the extent to which the trained system overlaps with the desired outcome.

Taken together, our findings suggest that while active tDCS may enhance phonological gains when paired with sPMT, generalization to untrained lexical-semantic outcomes may require either direct training of real words or additional targeting of semantic processing systems. These results highlight the importance of aligning treatment targets, stimulation parameters, and outcome measures when designing combined behavioral and neuromodulatory interventions.

Current modeling informs tDCS optimization for stroke rehabilitation

Based on our previous work, we hypothesized that the estimated current density (J) in the stroke brain is related to how far the lesion is from the tDCS anode (Hypothesis 2). As a first step, we showed that the J estimates were higher in the lesioned hemisphere compared to the non-lesioned hemisphere. We attribute this difference to the presence of the lesion. When a stroke occurs, the tissue is damaged, becomes necrotic, and then partially replaced by CSF (Krishnamurthy et al., 2021a), causing an up to six-fold increase in the conductance and a significant modification to the region's geometry. Therefore, it is crucial to consider the anatomical brain changes caused by stroke when deciding on the tDCS electrode montage.

From Fig. 2, we observe that across the brain, E and J have opposite trends. Theoretically, based on Ohm's law, E and J will tend in opposite directions to maintain optimal net conductivity (Evans et al., 2023; Puonti et al., 2020; Saturnino et al., 2019). Accordingly, in IP2_03, where the anode was immediately over the lesion, we observed a meaningful reduction in E to maintain optimal J. In Fig. 3, we note that our results did not support our hypothesis that J underneath the anode (F7) is dependent on the distance between the lesion and the core lesion volume. Instead, we observed that J underneath F7 is dependent on the distance between the two electrodes as well as the volume of the lesion, but it is not dependent on the distance between the anode and the center of the lesion. Our previous work has shown that aligning the lesion within the path of the current flow is critical for the priming of the network of interest (Krishnamurthy et al., 2025). The current results add additional critical information that for distal lesions (i.e., distal from the anode), the distance between the anode and lesion does not impact J, but the core lesion volume does. These findings specific to older males suggest that variability in current distribution driven by lesion characteristics may contribute to variability in behavioral response to tDCS, reinforcing the need for individualized stimulation approaches.

rs-fMRI connectivity describes the brain mechanisms underlying tDCS as an adjuvant to sPMT

We hypothesized that both active and sham groups would show connectivity changes over time within domain-specific and domain-general networks, with greater group-by-time effects in the active tDCS condition (Hypothesis 3). As summarized in Table 2, Table 3 and illustrated in Fig. 4, Fig. 5, both groups demonstrated connectivity changes over time, but the active tDCS group exhibited a greater number and extent of changes across both domain-general and domain-specific networks.

These findings are consistent with prior work demonstrating that tDCS can modulate functional connectivity at the network level, including effects on regions distal to the stimulation site (Krishnamurthy et al., 2015, 2025). In the present study, this network-level modulation was particularly evident in the active group, suggesting that tDCS may enhance the engagement of distributed language and cognitive control systems during sPMT. Importantly, the observed strengthening of connectivity within left-hemisphere regions—particularly involving the L-IFG and its associated networks—is consistent with models of post-stroke recovery emphasizing re-engagement of perilesional language networks rather than reliance on contralesional compensation (Hartwigsen and Saur, 2019; Grefkes and Fink, 2011).

Our domain-general findings (Table 2) highlight the contribution of cognitive control networks to phonological recovery in older males. While both groups showed connectivity changes involving domain-general regions, group-by-time analyses revealed a greater number of active > sham effects in regions associated with attentional allocation (mCC) (Xiao et al., 2005), internal processing (PCC) (Smirnov et al., 2014), and executive control (preSMA) (Tourville and Guenther, 2011). These results suggest that active tDCS may enhance phonological outcomes not only by targeting language-specific regions, but also by strengthening domain-general processes that support task engagement, monitoring, and control during speech production. This interpretation aligns with the behavioral findings, in which only the active group demonstrated significant improvements and maintenance of phonological gains.

Our domain-specific findings in older males (Table 3) further demonstrate that active tDCS was associated with enhanced connectivity within speech-language networks. In particular, increased connectivity between the L-IFG and left angular gyrus (L-AG)(Yu et al., 2018) in the active group supports improved integration of phonological and semantic representations, a process critical for efficient language production. Additional active > sham effects in regions such as the right pars triangularis (Oberhuber et al., 2016) and right angular gyrus (Coslett et al., 2018) suggest that tDCS may also facilitate cross-hemispheric interactions that support phonological processing. We also observed greater active > sham connectivity between the L-SMG and bilateral lingual gyri, which may reflect enhanced integration of visual/orthographic pattern recognition with phonological processing (Twomey et al., 2015). This finding is relevant because PMT incorporates orthographic cues alongside auditory, visual, tactile, and articulatory information, potentially engaging posterior visual-language regions even though the treatment primarily targeted phonological production (Kendall et al., 2015). Notably, although these connectivity changes indicate strengthening of networks relevant to lexical access, they were not accompanied by measurable improvements in confrontation naming in this small cohort. This dissociation suggests that while network-level changes may precede or support recovery, behavioral generalization to higher-level language functions may require additional training or larger sample sizes to detect.

Taken together, these findings provide mechanistic insight into how tDCS may act as an adjuvant to sPMT by enhancing both domain-general and domain-specific network interactions. By strengthening connectivity within perilesional left-hemisphere networks and associated control systems, tDCS may facilitate more efficient engagement of phonological processes during therapy. These results support a network-based framework for understanding combined behavioral and neuromodulatory interventions and highlight the importance of targeting both language-specific and domain-general systems in aphasia rehabilitation.

Significance

These findings have practical implications for the development and implementation of tDCS in stroke rehabilitation across clinical, scientific, and engineering domains. From a clinical perspective, our results highlight the importance of precise electrode placement and careful consideration of stimulation geometry to ensure that target regions receive adequate current density. Given the variability introduced by stroke lesions, standard electrode montages may not reliably produce consistent stimulation effects, underscoring the need for individualized treatment approaches in clinical practice.

From a mechanistic and translational standpoint, these findings emphasize the value of incorporating lesion characteristics and subject-specific current modeling into stimulation planning. Rather than relying on protocols derived from anatomically intact brains, integrating neuroimaging-based modeling may allow for more accurate prediction of current distribution and improved targeting of residual functional networks. This approach has the potential to enhance the consistency and efficacy of neuromodulatory interventions when combined with behavioral therapies such as PMT.

More broadly, this work supports a shift toward precision neurorehabilitation, in which treatment parameters are adapted to an individual's neuroanatomy, lesion profile, and functional network organization (Caulfield and George, 2022; Evans et al., 2023; Cherney et al., 2021; Song et al., 2023; Krishnamurthy et al., 2025). Advancing such approaches will require continued collaboration between clinicians, neuroscientists, and engineers to develop scalable tools for individualized stimulation planning and to translate these methods into routine clinical care.

Limitations and future directions

This was a proof-of-principle pilot study with only six older male participants (three per group). Although the observed effects were consistent and promising, the extremely small and demographically homogeneous sample limits statistical power, interpretability, and generalizability. Accordingly, the present results should be viewed as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, rather than confirmatory. Future studies with larger and more demographically diverse cohorts, including younger participants and women, are needed to confirm these patterns and evaluate the reproducibility of both the behavioral and connectivity effects.

We acknowledge that group matching was based primarily on aphasia severity, and we did not achieve full balance for other potentially influential factors such as age, time post-stroke, and lesion characteristics. While this imbalance did not appear to account for the observed effects, future definitive randomized trials should incorporate stratified designs or covariate-adjusted analyses to ensure better group equivalence. Furthermore, while our approach to reveal the tDCS code before final participant selection for analysis was practical, a more ideal approach would be to pre-define all analysis sets prior to unblinding to minimize potential selection bias.

Our primary outcome was evaluated using SAPA subtest-3. This particular subtest measures the participant's ability to repeat, parse, and blend sounds, which are essential for assessing phonological outcomes. However, it does not behaviorally differentiate between phonological knowledge, production, and processes. We recognize that the selected behavioral outcome (i.e., SAPA) does not capture the subtle nuances between various phonological phenomena. Therefore, we emphasize that future research should include more sensitive assessments to distinguish nuances in phonological abilities.

From the perspective of brain mechanisms, distinguishing between linguistic and cognitive linguistic functioning can be enhanced by examining both domain-general and domain-specific networks, as conducted in this study using rs-fMRI. However, concepts such as phonological knowledge and associated processes are complex, involving a detailed interplay between language and various cognitive domains. These complexities are more effectively explored through task-based fMRI experiments that are designed to disentangle such nuances, rather than rs-fMRI alone (Song et al., 2023). Future studies should aim to integrate both rs-fMRI and task-based fMRI approaches within the same cohort to more comprehensively characterize the neural mechanisms underlying treatment response, allowing for the simultaneous investigation of intrinsic network organization and task-evoked functional dynamics.

From our exploratory investigation of sPMT + tDCS to induce generalization to confrontation naming, we expected generalization to a certain degree (as observed via the rs-fMRI findings), but did not observe behavioral naming improvements. From a pragmatic standpoint, while we aspire for PMT to be accessible and clinically translatable, this pilot study demonstrates that treatment specification is complex. Invoking the RTSS framework, future research should delve deeper into how the various treatment ingredients (e.g., including training of real words in sPMT, tDCS parameters, dosage, and dosing frequency) can enhance the accessibility and translation of PMT using tDCS as an adjuvant in clinical settings. This approach may help achieve significant progress across multiple treatment targets, including phonological processing, naming, discourse, and reading. Additionally, by employing individual neurobiological markers, we hope that future treatment plans can be personalized to better reflect individual demographic, neurobiological, and medical conditions.

Conclusion

This study is among the first to investigate brain mechanisms associated with tDCS as an adjuvant to intensive aphasia treatment, such as PMT. Our study demonstrates that a shortened course of PMT paired with active tDCS using a bifrontal electrode montage generated substantial connectivity in both domain-general and domain-specific networks, which were accompanied by significant improvements in phonological production immediately after treatment and three months later. Our brain imaging-based current modeling work highlights the importance of considering core lesion volume and electrode distance, laying the foundation for achieving optimal effects from tDCS and thereby enhancing treatment outcomes. Overall, tDCS combined with sPMT appears promising for translation into clinical settings, with the hope of tailoring interventions and adapting strategies within a personalized framework.
