An integrated patient-derived colon organoids platform as a functional model for nutraceutical and stress response
Authors: Angelita Costantino, Paolo Giuseppe Bonacci, Giulia Zerbo, Tonia Luca, Oleksandr Popov, Morena Terrana, Azzurra Sargenti, Domenico Andrea Cristaldi, Carmela Bonaccorso, Cosimo Gianluca Fortuna, Stefano Puleo, Ornella Coco, Gaetano Magro, Luca Lanzanò, Sergio Castorina, Nicolò Musso, Massimo Gulisano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNutraceuticals are increasingly investigated for their capacity to modulate oxidative and inflammatory stress, yet preclinical testing still relies largely on immortalized cell lines or animal models that poorly recapitulate human epithelial complexity. To address this gap, we developed an integrated platform based on patient-derived colon organoids generated from non-tumoral mucosa and maintained under proliferative or differentiation conditions to model distinct epithelial states. The system combines millifluidic measurement of individual organoid mass, density, and diameter with bulk RNA sequencing and digital PCR profiling to enable multiparametric characterization. Transcriptional analysis revealed state-specific gene programs and shifts in epithelial and immune-related pathways, while biophysical measurements captured structural remodeling. In this pilot validation, a defined oxidative insult followed by nutraceutical treatment elicited coordinated transcriptional and phenotypic responses. This integrated approach provides a scalable and physiologically relevant framework for functional nutraceutical profiling and mechanistic studies of epithelial stress responses.Subject areas: molecular Genetics, bioengineering, nutraceuticals

Introduction

Advances in three-dimensional epithelial culture systems have transformed the ability to model human intestinal physiology in vitro. Among these, patient-derived organoids (PDOs) faithfully recapitulate the architecture, cellular composition, and genetic background of the native mucosa, preserving stem cell dynamics and epithelial differentiation hierarchies.1,2 PDOs have been widely adopted in cancer research,3,4 inflammatory bowel disease modeling,5,6 and host-pathogen interaction studies,7,8 establishing them as physiologically relevant platforms for translational investigation.

Despite their extensive biomedical application, PDOs remain underutilized for nutraceutical and toxicological assessment in a patient-specific context.9,10,11,12,13 Preclinical evaluation of bioactive dietary compounds still relies predominantly on immortalized cell lines or animal models, which inadequately reproduce the structural complexity, barrier properties, and metabolic specialization of the human intestinal epithelium, the primary interface for nutrient absorption and xenobiotic sensing.14,15,16,17,18 This limitation underscores the need for scalable human-based systems capable of capturing both beneficial and adverse epithelial responses.

Here, we establish and technically validate a dual-state, multi-layered colon PDO platform that integrates epithelial differentiation modeling with single-organoid millifluidic phenotyping and bulk transcriptomic profiling. Unlike conventional PDO-based profiling approaches that rely primarily on viability or endpoint molecular assays, this system enables coordinated assessment of epithelial state, physical remodeling, and transcriptional adaptation within the same patient-derived background. This integrative design provides a sensitive and physiologically grounded framework for functional evaluation of epithelial stress and nutraceutical responses.

Results

Establishment and multi-layer validation of a dual-state healthy colon PDO platform

To develop a patient-specific in vitro system for functional nutraceutical and toxicological studies, we established a biobank of colon PDOs generated from histologically normal mucosal tissue collected during surgical resections for colorectal cancer (CRC). Tissue samples were obtained from macroscopically healthy areas distant from the tumor margin and were confirmed as non-neoplastic by pathological evaluation. Collected tissues included samples from various anatomical segments of the colon, with the following distribution: left colon (41.6%), right colon (37.5%), and sigmoid colon (20.8%). These segments, routinely removed to ensure complete oncologic resection, provided a reliable source of healthy epithelial tissue while preserving patient-specific genetic background (Figure 1A). For platform validation, PDOs derived from six independent patients were used (three males and three females; age range 56–78 years). Clinical and organoid-specific information is summarized in Table S1. Isolated colonic crypts were embedded in extracellular matrix and cultured under defined proliferative conditions to generate long-term expandable organoids (growth PDOs). These cultures supported sustained epithelial expansion, passaging, and cryopreservation, forming the basis of a living biobank for downstream applications (Figure 1A).

Workflow of the patient-derived intestinal organoid platform

(A) Generation of PDOs from endoscopic biopsies of macroscopically normal colonic mucosa. Following tissue processing, intestinal crypts were isolated, embedded in Matrigel, and cultured to establish proliferative organoids under growth conditions. Growth PDOs represent the expandable and self-renewing compartment of the platform and constitute the long-term storage source (biobank) for downstream applications.

(B) Induction of epithelial differentiation. Growth PDOs were transitioned to differentiation conditions to generate differentiated (Diff) organoids, modeling the mature and functionally specialized intestinal epithelium. This step enables the generation of two complementary epithelial states—proliferative (growth) and differentiated (Diff)—derived from the same patient.

(C) Integrated application of growth and Diff PDOs within the multiparametric platform. Both organoid models were subjected to phenotypic, biophysical, and molecular characterization, exposed to toxic or nutraceutical stimuli, and subsequently re-evaluated to assess epithelial stress responses and rescue effects. This workflow enables state-dependent functional profiling of human intestinal epithelium using matched patient-derived models.

To model epithelial maturation, growth PDOs were subsequently subjected to a differentiation protocol based on the modulation of Wnt and Notch signaling pathways (Figure 1B). Under these conditions, organoids acquired morphological features consistent with epithelial maturation and gave rise to differentiated PDOs (Diff PDOs), representing a complementary epithelial state enriched in mature intestinal cell types.

Both growth and Diff PDOs were then integrated into a multiparametric characterization platform (Figure 1C). This workflow included: (1) histological and immunophenotypic validation; (2) genomic fidelity assessment through targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) to evaluate mutational concordance with the tissue of origin; (3) bulk RNA sequencing to map global transcriptional differences between proliferative and differentiated states; (4) millifluidic single-organoid biophysical analysis (W8 system) for the measurement of density, mass, and diameter; and (5) functional exposure assays followed by high-sensitivity gene expression quantification using digital PCR (dPCR). Importantly, the platform is designed to enable state-matched comparisons between growth and Diff PDOs derived from the same patient, while the W8-based millifluidic analysis allows non-destructive biophysical profiling, preserving organoid integrity for downstream molecular and functional assays.

This structured pipeline establishes a dual-state, PDO platform that integrates genomic stability, transcriptional profiling, biophysical characterization, and functional readouts within the same experimental framework.

Phenotypic and transcriptional characterization of growth and differentiated PDOs

To model distinct epithelial states within the same patient-derived background, growth PDOs were subjected to a differentiation protocol based on Wnt withdrawal and Notch inhibition (Figures 1B and S1A). Time-course differentiation experiments further documented progressive architectural remodeling (Figure S1A). By day 7 of differentiation, organoids displayed increased structural organization and epithelial polarization. Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining confirmed the presence of mucus-producing goblet cells (Figure S1B), reinforcing the acquisition of secretory lineage features. Histological comparison between native colon tissue and matched PDOs confirmed the preservation of epithelial architecture in both culture conditions. Hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E) showed glandular organization comparable to the tissue of origin (Figure 2A). Immunohistochemical analyses further clarified the biological identity of the two organoid states. Growth PDOs displayed strong Ki-67 nuclear positivity, consistent with an actively cycling epithelial population, and limited expression of differentiation markers. In contrast, Diff PDOs exhibited a marked reduction in Ki-67–positive cells and increased expression of epithelial maturation markers, including CK20. Importantly, only Diff PDOs showed robust expression of MUC2 and Chromogranin A, indicating the presence of goblet and enteroendocrine cells, respectively (Figure 2A).

Finally, Vimentin staining was absent, excluding mesenchymal contamination, and the Villin-1 staining confirmed epithelial maturation and apical-basal polarity (Figures 2A and S1C).

These features demonstrate that Diff PDOs more closely recapitulate the cellular composition of the adult, functionally specialized colonic epithelium, whereas growth PDOs reflect a regenerative crypt-like state.

Morphologically, growth PDOs exhibited compact, cystic structures with limited luminal complexity, whereas Diff PDOs displayed larger organoids with more elaborate architecture, including budding and luminal expansion, as observed by bright-field (BF) microscopy (Figure 2B). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in organoid diameter in Diff PDOs compared to growth conditions (Figure 2C). Consistently, proliferative status was assessed by Ki-67 immunostaining. Growth PDOs showed widespread nuclear positivity, consistent with active cycling epithelium, whereas Diff PDOs exhibited a marked reduction in Ki-67–positive cells, indicating transition toward a less proliferative, more mature epithelial state (Figures 2A–2C).

To evaluate epithelial plasticity, we performed a differentiation-reversion experiment. Organoids initially exposed to differentiation medium (early-Diff) were subsequently returned to growth conditions (post-growth). Histological analysis revealed morphological regression toward a more compact structure (Figure S2A). Functionally, MUC2-positive goblet cells were significantly reduced following reversion to growth conditions (Figures S2B and S2C), indicating that differentiated lineage features are dependent on niche cues and that PDOs retain reversible state plasticity.

As a proof-of-principle application for non-destructive subcellular imaging, Diff PDOs were analyzed using live vital-dye-based confocal microscopy (Figure S3). Multiparametric staining allowed the visualization of nuclei, plasma membrane, and lysosomes within intact organoids. Lysosomal labeling showed regionally enriched distribution, frequently polarized toward luminal domains, consistent with epithelial organization and supporting their value for phenotypic and morpho-functional studies (Figure S3A). Nile Red staining highlighted lipid and membrane compartmentalization (Figure S3B), providing a functional readout compatible with dynamic monitoring of cell integrity and metabolic status. Beyond its classical role as a lipid droplet marker, Nile Red fluorescence intensity and distribution reflect changes in membrane lipid composition and polarity parameters that can shift under oxidative stress, cytotoxic insult, or nutraceutical protection. Thus, this assay provides a rapid, label-free means to detect subtle alterations in epithelial physiology in response to defined stimuli and demonstrates that PDOs support high-resolution live imaging without architectural disruption.

Following phenotypic validation, we performed bulk amplicon RNA sequencing to characterize global transcriptional differences between growth and Diff PDOs (Figure 2E). Differential expression analysis was conducted using growth PDOs as the reference condition, and gene expression changes are reported as log2 fold change (differentiated vs. growth). Heatmap visualization revealed coordinated transcriptional remodeling associated with epithelial maturation. Differentiated PDOs showed the upregulation of gene programs involved in epithelial differentiation, lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, stress sensing, and innate immune signaling, whereas growth PDOs were enriched for transcripts related to proliferative activity and inflammatory signaling (Figure 2E). This coordinated transcriptional shift is further demonstrated by differential expression analysis (Figure S4A), highlighting significantly upregulated genes in Diff PDOs (adjusted p < 0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction), including regulators of epithelial differentiation and innate immune sensing (e.g., ACVR2A, BMP2, IL1B, JAK1, HLA-DRA, TLR1, TLR3, TLR4, and PPARG) and genes involved in metabolic adaptation (AKR1C1, PLIN2). Conversely, downregulated transcripts included multiple inflammatory mediators and chemokines (e.g., TNF, IL18, CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL8, and CCL5). Notably, among the top differentially expressed genes, several upregulated transcripts belong to innate immunity and epithelial sensing pathways, while many downregulated genes are associated with chemokine signaling, cytokine production, and inflammatory responses (Figure S4B). This pattern further reinforces the transition from a proliferative, epithelial state toward a structurally organized and functionally specialized adult epithelium.

Genomic fidelity of growth PDOs recapitulates the mutational profile of native tissue

To validate the genomic stability of the expandable organoid lines maintained in the biobank, we performed targeted NGS on growth PDOs and their matched healthy colon tissue (Figure 3). Because growth PDOs represent the long-term expandable population used for downstream applications, mutational analysis was conducted at this stage. Differentiated PDOs derive directly from growth cultures and therefore share the same genetic background. The sequencing panel included genes representing major signaling pathways governing intestinal epithelial homeostasis, proliferation, stress adaptation, and metabolic regulation. Comparative analysis revealed strong concordance between tissue and matched PDO samples across multiple signaling axes. Variants in APC, a key regulator of Wnt/β-catenin signaling and epithelial renewal, were consistently preserved between tissue and organoids (Figure 3A). Similarly, components of the MAPK/ERK pathway (EGFR, BRAF, KDR, PDGFRA, FGFR3, and ERBB4) displayed comparable variant allele frequencies (VAFs), indicating maintenance of growth-factor-associated signaling networks (Figure 3B). Concordant variants were also observed in genes of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway (PIK3CA and PTPN11), as well as in tumor suppressor and cell-cycle-associated genes (TP53, STK11, and HRAS), and in genes linked to metabolic and epigenetic regulation (MET, IDH1, HMGXB3, and CSF1R) (Figures 3C–3E). Detailed allelic frequencies (%) for all detected variants in both tissue and PDO samples are reported in Table S3.

To quantitatively assess mutational overlap, a weighted concordance analysis based on VAF comparison was performed (Figure 3F). Variants were classified as full, partial, or discordant matches between tissue and PDO samples. Weighted concordance scores were calculated by assigning values of 1 to full matches, 0.5 to partial matches, and 0 to discordant variants. This analysis yielded overall concordance values of 75% for P(F65) and 80% for P(F78). The predominance of full and partial matches indicates high mutational consistency between PDOs and their tissue of origin, whereas limited discrepancies may reflect minor clonal selection during ex vivo expansion.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that growth PDOs retain the patient-specific mutational landscape of the native healthy epithelium, supporting their use as genetically faithful models for downstream functional and nutraceutical studies.

Millifluidic W8 profiling enables quantitative physical characterization of growth and differentiated PDOs

To quantitatively monitor structural properties of PDOs, we employed a flow-based millifluidic system (W8) capable of measuring density, mass (weight), and diameter of intact individual organoids. This platform enables rapid, non-destructive, and high-throughput biophysical profiling without disrupting organoid architecture. To evaluate whether long-term in vitro expansion affects the intrinsic physical properties of growth PDOs, we first performed millifluidic W8 analysis at passages 4, 6, and 9 in organoids derived from three independent patients (P(M69), P(F72), P(M66)) (Figure 4A). Density measurements revealed overall stability across passages, although modest but statistically significant differences were observed in specific cases (Figure 4B). In P(M69) and P(F72), density at passage 9 differed significantly compared to earlier passages (∗∗p < 0.01), whereas no significant variation was detected in P(M66). Despite these differences, density values remained within a narrow range across passages, indicating the preservation of global structural compactness during expansion. Weight measurements showed no statistically significant differences across passages in any of the three patients (Figure 4C), supporting maintenance of organoid mass during prolonged culture. Diameter analysis similarly demonstrated overall stability, except for P(F72), where a significant reduction was observed at passage 9 compared to passage 6 (∗∗p < 0.01) (Figure 4D). No significant changes were detected in P(M69) or P(M66). Collectively, these data indicate that growth PDOs maintain stable biophysical properties during early and mid-passages (p4–p6), while subtle structural variations may emerge at later passages, particularly at p9.

To determine whether epithelial differentiation is associated with measurable structural heterogeneity, Diff PDOs were analyzed using the W8 millifluidic system (Figure 5A). During real-time visualization within the microfluidic channel, we consistently observed three recurrent morphological configurations: (1) cystic, characterized by round, thin-walled structures; (2) mild, displaying intermediate epithelial folding; (3) budding, exhibiting complex crypt-like protrusions (Figure 5B). These morphotypes were not pre-defined a priori but emerged during instrumental observation, prompting us to investigate whether this visual heterogeneity corresponded to quantifiable biophysical differences. Density measurements revealed significant differences among morphological classes across patients (Figure 5C).

In P(M69), cystic organoids displayed the highest density, followed by mild and budding forms (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001). A similar pattern was observed in P(F72), where budding organoids exhibited significantly lower density compared to cystic and mild forms (∗p < 0.05). In P(M66), cystic organoids were significantly denser than mild forms (∗∗∗p < 0.001), while no difference was observed between mild and budding structures. Overall, density inversely tracked architectural complexity, with more structurally elaborate (budding) organoids tending toward lower compactness. Weight analysis showed patient-dependent variability (Figure 5D). In P(M69), mild organoids displayed significantly higher mass compared to cystic and budding forms (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001), while budding organoids were lighter than mild structures (∗∗p < 0.01).

In P(F72), cystic organoids were significantly lighter than mild forms (∗∗p < 0.01), whereas no significant difference was observed between mild and budding. In P(M66), no significant differences in weight were detected across morphologies. Thus, while weight contributes to morphological discrimination, it appears less consistent than density across individuals. Diameter measurements showed robust and consistent separation among morphotypes (Figure 5E). In P(M69), budding organoids exhibited significantly larger diameters compared to both cystic and mild forms (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001). In P(F72), budding organoids were significantly larger than cystic and mild structures (∗p < 0.05–0.01). In P(M66), budding organoids were significantly larger than cystic forms (∗∗p < 0.01). These results indicate that epithelial architectural complexity is accompanied by volumetric expansion. Correlation analysis further revealed a strong inverse relationship between density and diameter across morphologies (Figure 5F). In all three patients, increased diameter was associated with reduced density, supporting the concept that epithelial remodeling during differentiation involves coordinated expansion and decreased structural compactness. Collectively, these data demonstrate that morphological heterogeneity in Diff PDOs is not merely visual but corresponds to quantifiable biophysical states. The ability of the W8 system to resolve these states in a non-destructive manner establishes a framework for integrating structural phenotyping into organoid-based platforms. Importantly, while the present study focuses on structural discrimination, future investigations will determine whether these biophysical subclasses also correspond to distinct functional profiles, including differential metabolic activity, stress responsiveness, or nutraceutical sensitivity.

Because a potential concern in millifluidic analysis is the impact of fixation on physical measurements, we performed a validation experiment comparing live and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA)-fixed organoids from the same patient (P(F65)) (Figures S5A–S5D). Growth PDOs at passage 6 were split into two fractions and analyzed either live or after fixation. No statistically significant differences were detected in density, mass, or diameter between live and fixed samples (Figure S5B). The same validation was performed on Diff PDOs derived from the same patient, again revealing no significant differences between live and fixed conditions for any measured parameter (Figures S5C and S5D). These results confirm that W8-derived biophysical measurements are fixation-independent and reflect intrinsic organoid properties rather than artifacts introduced by sample processing.

Together, these data demonstrate that millifluidic profiling provides a robust, reproducible, and state-sensitive quantitative readout of PDO architecture. To ensure robust and reproducible measurements, strict quality control criteria were applied during W8 acquisition, including size thresholds and exclusion of debris or irregular events. Representative examples of accepted and excluded events, as well as selection parameters, are provided in Figure S6.

The stability across passaging, sensitivity to differentiation-associated structural remodeling, and independence from fixation conditions support the integration of W8-based physical phenotyping into standardized organoid profiling workflows.

Nutraceutical rescue reveals epithelial state-dependent stress responsiveness in growth and differentiated PDOs

To functionally validate the platform under controlled stress conditions, growth PDOs derived from patient P(M56) were exposed to increasing concentrations of PB4, a heteroaryl-ethylene compound previously characterized for its cytotoxic and oxidative stress-inducing properties.19,20,21,22 Organoid viability was assessed at 24 h and 48 h using the CellTiter-Glo 3D luminescence assay (Figures 6A and 6B). PB4 induced a clear dose- and time-dependent reduction in viability. At 1 μM, organoids showed marked loss of viability, particularly at 48 h, whereas 0.5 μM induced a measurable stress response while maintaining overall structural integrity and survival. This concentration was therefore selected as a sublethal oxidative insult, enabling molecular interrogation without overt cytotoxicity (Figures 6B and 6C). In this context, “sublethal” was operationally defined as a condition that induces a measurable reduction in viability and activation of stress-response pathways while preserving overall organoid architecture and metabolic activity, as assessed by CellTiter-Glo readouts and morphological inspection. Notably, although formal dose-response modeling was performed, the data did not support a robust IC50 determination within the tested concentration range. Therefore, concentration selection was based on empirical viability profiles rather than extrapolated pharmacological thresholds, identifying 0.5 μM as the highest non-cytotoxic dose suitable for functional interrogation. BF imaging confirmed intracellular accumulation of PB4-associated signal while preserving organoid architecture (Figure 6C).

To evaluate rescue potential, growth PDOs were treated for 6 h with PB4 (0.5 μM) alone or in combination with B2 Classic (400 ng/μL), a pistachio-derived nutraceutical extract (Figure 6D).23,24,25,26,27 RNA was extracted and analyzed using dPCR, allowing highly sensitive and absolute quantification of transcript levels. PB4 exposure significantly increased the expression of oxidative stress and apoptosis-related genes NRF2, SOD1, and BAX (Figure 6E), consistent with the activation of redox-adaptive and pro-apoptotic pathways. Co-treatment with B2 classic significantly attenuated this transcriptional activation, restoring expression levels toward baseline. These findings demonstrate a clear cytoprotective effect of the nutraceutical under controlled oxidative insult. Importantly, epithelial identity and transcriptional control markers (TGFB1 and KRT8) remained stable across conditions (Figure 6F), indicating that sublethal stress selectively activated oxidative pathways without compromising epithelial lineage identity in the proliferative state.

To determine whether epithelial maturity influences stress responsiveness, the same experimental design was applied to Diff PDOs following 5–7 days of lineage maturation (Figure 6G). As observed in growth PDOs, PB4 significantly upregulated NRF2, SOD1, and BAX, and this effect was attenuated by B2 Classic co-treatment (Figure 6H), confirming the reproducibility of the oxidative stress response across epithelial states. However, a notable difference emerged in epithelial homeostasis markers. In Diff PDOs, PB4 significantly reduced TGFB1 expression, an effect that was restored upon nutraceutical treatment (Figure 6I). In contrast, KRT8 expression remained stable. This selective modulation suggests that mature epithelial structures are more sensitive to oxidative perturbation at the level of transcriptional homeostasis and repair signaling, and that this vulnerability can be functionally rescued by nutraceutical intervention. Collectively, these results demonstrate that: 1) The PDO platform supports controlled modeling of sublethal oxidative stress; 2) dPCR provides sensitive detection of transcriptional shifts under stress and rescue conditions; and 3) epithelial state influences response dynamics, with Diff PDOs exhibiting additional modulation of homeostatic pathways beyond classical oxidative stress genes, validating the platform as a multiparametric, state-aware system for functional nutraceutical evaluation and for dissecting epithelial stress biology in a patient-derived context.

These stress-responsive pathways are consistent with the transcriptional reprogramming observed during differentiation, further supporting the biological coherence of the dual-state platform.

Discussion

A distinguishing aspect of this work is the integration of single-organoid millifluidic biophysical profiling with genomic, transcriptomic, and high-sensitivity molecular analyses within a unified patient-derived platform. Measurements of density, mass, and diameter revealed overall physical stability across passaging in growth organoids, supporting long-term reproducibility of the expandable biobank state, while differentiated cultures displayed morphology-dependent physical heterogeneity consistent with structural maturation. These data indicate that epithelial differentiation is accompanied not only by transcriptional remodeling but also by measurable architectural reorganization. By combining physical and molecular layers of analysis, the platform enables parallel interrogation of tissue architecture and gene expression adaptation dimensions that are typically investigated independently in conventional organoid workflows. Importantly, the ability to resolve differentiation-associated morphological subclasses through non-destructive millifluidic phenotyping represents a methodological advance beyond conventional endpoint imaging approaches. While the current implementation integrates multiple analytical layers at the platform level, biophysical characterization and downstream molecular analyses were not performed on the same individual organoid due to current technical constraints. Future developments could enable fully coupled workflows in which single organoids are tracked in real time during millifluidic analysis and subsequently recovered for downstream molecular profiling, for example, through micro-manipulation approaches or integration with single-organoid sequencing strategies. Such advances would allow true single-organoid multi-parametric analysis, further strengthening the integrative potential of the platform.

Transcriptomic profiling further demonstrated that the transition from growth to Diff PDOs is characterized by coordinated remodeling of epithelial identity, innate immune sensing, and inflammatory signaling pathways. Upregulation of genes associated with epithelial maturation and stress sensing, together with the attenuation of chemokine- and cytokine-related transcripts, suggests a shift from a proliferative, regenerative program toward a functionally specialized epithelial state. This integrated molecular and physical reprogramming underscores the biological relevance of modeling epithelial maturity when evaluating stress responses or compound efficacy.

Functionally, oxidative stress was induced using a defined sublethal concentration of PB4, enabling the interrogation of adaptive responses without overt cytotoxicity. Molecular readouts were quantified using dPCR, a highly sensitive approach for absolute transcript quantification. The use of dPCR strengthens the analytical resolution of the system, allowing the detection of subtle but biologically meaningful modulation of oxidative stress, apoptotic, and epithelial integrity markers. In this context, the selected PB4 concentration was operationally defined as sublethal based on quantitative viability criteria (limited reduction in viability while preserving organoid architecture and metabolic activity), allowing the study of adaptive stress responses rather than terminal cytotoxic effects. Within this controlled framework, nutraceutical co-treatment attenuated stress-associated transcriptional changes, demonstrating the platform’s capacity to capture coordinated injury and rescue responses. Notably, differentiated organoids exhibited dynamic regulation of epithelial homeostasis genes under stress conditions, suggesting that epithelial maturation enhances the discriminatory power of functional profiling.

Collectively, these findings position this dual-state PDO system as a flexible, multiparametric framework for studying epithelial state-dependent responses in a patient-specific context. By integrating mutational fidelity assessment, global transcriptomic mapping, biophysical phenotyping, and high-sensitivity molecular quantification, the platform extends beyond conventional viability-based evaluation and establishes a structured strategy for the precision evaluation of epithelial stress adaptation and nutraceutical modulation. Throughout the manuscript, we therefore refer to this approach as a functional profiling platform rather than high-throughput screening, as single-organoid analysis using the W8 system is inherently sequential and currently requires dedicated handling time per organoid. While this limits throughput at present, it provides high-resolution, label-free measurements, and future improvements in automation and parallelization may enable increased scalability. The conceptual novelty of the present study does not lie in organoid generation per se, but in the structured integration of orthogonal readouts-biophysical, genomic, transcriptomic, and functional within a single patient-derived experimental framework. This integration transforms PDOs from descriptive 3D cultures into a quantitatively stratified profiling system. Importantly, the present work should be interpreted as a platform validation study, in which PB4 and the nutraceutical compound are used as proof-of-concept perturbations to demonstrate system sensitivity, rather than as a comprehensive evaluation of compound efficacy.

In this perspective, future studies may further extend the platform by incorporating additional functional layers, including protein-level validation, live-cell functional assays (e.g., ROS monitoring), and benchmarking against established viability and physiological readouts, thereby enhancing its translational applicability.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although mutational profiling, bulk transcriptomics, biophysical characterization, and functional assays collectively validate the platform, not all analytical layers were performed within a fully integrated single-patient multi-omics design. Future studies implementing longitudinal, patient-matched, multi-layer analyses will allow for deeper correlations between physical and transcriptional phenotypes. Under current experimental conditions, acquisition time ranges from approximately 30–60 s for simple cystoid organoids to several minutes for more complex budding structures, resulting in an average throughput of approximately 10–20 organoids per hour. While this limits large-scale applications, it enables high-resolution, label-free characterization at the single-organoid level. Future developments in automation and parallelization are expected to substantially increase throughput and scalability. Second, the cohort size was limited and not powered to capture the full extent of inter-individual variability. The present work was designed as a technical validation study rather than a population-level assessment. Expansion to larger and clinically diverse cohorts will be essential to evaluate robustness and predictive potential across genetic backgrounds.

Third, while bulk amplicon RNA sequencing provides a comprehensive overview of transcriptional states, it does not resolve cell-type-specific contributions within heterogeneous organoid structures. Integration of single-cell or spatial transcriptomic approaches would further refine the interpretation of lineage-specific adaptations.

Finally, the nutraceutical experiments were conceived as proof-of-concept validation of platform responsiveness. Although the use of dPCR provides high analytical sensitivity, the findings should be interpreted as a demonstration of functional detectability rather than definitive evidence of therapeutic efficacy.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects and patient-derived organoid cultures

Colonic tissue specimens were obtained from n = 6 adult patients undergoing surgical resection for primary colorectal carcinoma at Fondazione G.B. Morgagni, Catania, Italy. For organoid generation, tissue samples were collected from macroscopically normal, non-neoplastic colonic mucosa, spatially separated from the primary tumor site, as assessed by the attending surgical and pathology teams.

Approval of studies involving humans and patient informed consent Human samples were collected under study protocol conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinkiand approved by the Ethics Committee “Comitato Etico Catania 2” (Azienda Ospedaliera Garibaldi, Catania, prot. 601/C.E.) and informed consent was obtained from patients undergoing surgery for suspected colon adenocarcinoma. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Clinical and demographic information, including patient age, sex, tumor localization, and relevant clinicopathological features, are summarized in Table S1. The study included both male and female adult patients ranging from 56 to 78 years.

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) were established from isolated colonic crypts and maintained as three-dimensional epithelial cultures embedded in growth factor-reduced Matrigel under defined culture conditions, as described below.

Because this study was designed as a technical platform validation study, patient samples were not prospectively allocated to experimental groups based on biological variables but rather assigned to downstream analytical workflows according to organoid establishment success, culture performance, sample availability, and technical suitability for specific assays (including mutational profiling, transcriptomics, millifluidic biophysical characterization, and functional molecular analyses).

The influence of sex and/or gender on experimental outcomes was not formally assessed in this study. Due to the limited cohort size and the proof-of-concept nature of the work, the study was not statistically powered to evaluate sex-associated biological differences. This represents a limitation of the current study and will be addressed in future investigations involving larger patient cohorts.

Method details

CC tissue processing and establishment of primary human colon organoids

Healthy colon organoids were produced from patient-biopsy as described for the first time by Sato et al. (2011) with specific modification in order to set up the platform. Here, the procedure will be described step by step. In general, the operating room provided a fullthickness section of healthy resection tissue measuring approximately 2.5 × 2.5 cm. Through close collaboration with the surgical team, we optimized the procedure for obtaining the biopsy specimen. Specifically, the surgeon opened the resection channel and exposed the mucosal layer outward, which facilitated its isolation from the underlying tissue layers and subsequent processing. The isolated mucosa was dissociated and washed in cold Phosphate Buffer Saline without Ca++ and Mg++ (Euroclone) supplemented with 50 mg/mL gentamicin (Life Technologies), P/S 100U (Euroclone) and 2.5 mg/mL amphotericin (Life Technologies) in order to prevent common contaminations. On a rocking platform, colon fragments were treated with Gentle Dissociation Reagent (STEMCELL Technologies, Inc. (STI)) on ice for 30 min. Then, the fragments were allowed to settle by gravity, resuspended in DMEM F12 with 15 mM HEPES (STEMCELL Technologies Inc. (STI) supplemented with BSA (Sigma) at 1% in Phosphate Buffer Saline without Ca++ and Mg++ (Euroclone) and passed through 70 μm cell strainers. The crypts obtained were resuspended in a 1:1 mixture of DMEM F12 with 15 mM HEPES ((STEMCELL Technologies Inc. (STI)) +1% BSA and Matrigel (Corning) and 50 μL/well of the suspension was pipetted into pre-warmed 24-well TC plates (Euroclone) to form domes that were solidified at 37 °C for 15 min. At the end of this time, 600 μL/well of complete IntestiCult Organoid Growth Medium (OGM) (STEMCELL Technologies Inc. (STI) was added, and organoids were maintained in an incubator at controlled temperature (37 °C) and CO2 (5%). In order to perform organoids maintenance, the medium was changed 2–3 times per week and organoids were passed every 4–7 days. For primary culture (organoids have not yet been passaged), 10 μM of Y-27632 (Rock inhibitor) was added to each well for 2 days. All steps for colon crypts insolation, as well as organoid culture establishment were performed under sterile conditions (biosafety hood - Biosafety Level 2 (BSL2)). The biopsies were processed within 3–4 h from the collection. All codes and detailed descriptions of reagents used in this study are provided in the key resources table.

Passaging and maintenance of PDOs (growth condition)

Organoid cultures were maintained by replacing the OGM medium two to three times per week. Primary cultures were passaged after 7–21 days, whereas previously established organoids were passaged every 4–7 days (split 1:6). All codes and detailed descriptions of reagents used in this study are provided in the key resources table.

Cryopreservation of PDOs

Organoids were checked under an inverted microscope and pooled as needed to obtain ∼100 organoids per cryovial. OGM medium was removed and replaced with 1 mL of cold (2°C–8 °C) GCDR, and Matrigel was disrupted by pipetting 2–3 times with a pre-wetted tip. Suspensions were transferred to a 15 mL conical tube, combined with an additional 1 mL wash of cold GCDR or PBS, and pelleted by centrifugation at 290 × g for 5 min at 2°C–8 °C. Pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of cold DMEM/F-12 and fragmented by pipetting. Fragmentation efficiency was assessed microscopically to ensure organoid disruption without excessive debris. After the addition of 9 mL cold DMEM/F-12 with 15 mM HEPES, organoids were pelleted at 200 × g for 5 min at 2°C–8 °C and the supernatant removed. Organoids were resuspended in 1 mL of cold CryoStor CS10 freezing medium per cryovial (∼200 fragmented organoids) and transferred to labeled cryovials. Samples were placed at −80 °C and then transferred to liquid nitrogen (≤−135 °C) for long-term storage.

Establishment and maintenance of differentiated PDOs

To reproduce the cellular diversity of the intestinal crypt–villus axis, patient-derived organoids (PDOs) maintained under growth conditions were subjected to a differentiation protocol. After at least three passages, organoid growth medium (OGM) was replaced with organoid differentiation medium (ODM). ODM was prepared using the IntestiCult Organoid Differentiation Medium (Human) kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. #100–0214). The basal medium and supplement were mixed in equal proportions and supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin (1×) and 5 μM DAPT (γ-secretase/Notch pathway inhibitor; STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. #72082) to promote differentiation and enhance secretory lineage expression. For differentiation, OGM was removed and replaced with an equal volume of ODM. PDOs were cultured at 37 °C for 5–7 days, with complete medium changes every 2 days. Differentiation efficiency was confirmed morphologically by the presence of organoids displaying pronounced budding, a darkened lumen, and a thickened epithelium. During culture in ODM, PDOs entered a low-proliferative state and were not passed further. At the end of the differentiation protocol, PDOs were directly processed for downstream analyses.

Sample fixation and embedding

Colon resections were opened along the taenia coli, washed with saline to remove fecal material, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h. For patient-derived organoids (PDOs), culture medium was removed, and domes were gently disrupted in cold Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (GCDR; StemCell Technologies) using pre-rinsed wide-bore pipette tips to prevent shear stress. Organoids were collected in 15 mL conical tubes pre-coated with anti-adherence rinsing solution (StemCell Technologies) and subjected to alternating incubation on ice with gentle shaking and pipetting to maximize Matrigel removal. Organoids were then fixed in 4% PFA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) overnight at room temperature in the dark. Following fixation, samples were washed with DPBS, centrifuged, and resuspended in molten bio-agar, which was allowed to solidify at −20°C before downstream processing.

Fixed tissues and organoids embedded in bio-agar were transferred into histology cassettes and processed using a Donatello Series 3 tissue processor (Diapath) for sequential dehydration, xylene clearing, and paraffin infiltration. Paraffin embedding was performed using a Dante embedding station (Diapath), and blocks were cooled on a −20°C plate until fully solidified. Sections were cut at 1 μm (for histological staining) on Dia-Print slides (Diapath) and at 2 μm (for immunohistochemistry) on adhesive glass slides (TOMO). Slides were dried at 57°C for 30 min and stored until staining.

Histology and imaging of FFPE tissue and PDOs sections

Tissue and organoid slides were subjected to histological (hematoxylin and eosin [H&E], Periodic Acid–Schiff [PAS]) and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining using identical protocols. Depending on the assay, staining was performed either automatically or manually. Specifically, H&E and IHC staining were processed automatically, whereas PAS was performed manually following the manufacturer’s instructions. For IHC, slides were processed on the fully automated BenchMark ULTRA system (Roche Tissue Diagnostics), which enables independent slide handling and optimized staining reproducibility. The list of monoclonal antibodies used, along with their concentrations, is provided in the key resources table, and all antibodies were applied according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. After staining, slides were mounted automatically using the CTM6 cover-slipping instrument (Fisher Scientific), which dispenses mounting medium (Bio-Optica, cod. 05-BMH508) and applies 24 × 50 mm coverslips (Diapath, cod. 061051). Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections of normal human colon tissue and PDOs were mounted on glass slides and imaged using a Leica DM5500 microscope equipped with the Leica Application Suite (LAS X, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Images were acquired at 20× magnification in brightfield mode with the following acquisition parameters: exposure time 50 ms, gain 1, illumination intensity 20, aperture 20, and TL-Fld 33.

Confocal live engineering-free imaging

To prepare organoids for live-confocal imaging, the medium was removed, and organoids were recovered from the Matrigel incubated on ice on a rocking platform for 20 min with GCDR as previously described.28,29 Once the organoids are settled by gravity, the supernatant was aspirated, and organoids were ready for the labeling step. Labeling was performed at 37 °C with Nile Red (Thermofisher N1142) at a final concentration of 1 μM and Hoechst 33,342 (Thermofisher 62249) at a final concentration of 4 μM for 90 min or with ORGANELLE-ID-RGB reagent I (Enzo, ENZ- 53007) at a dilution 1:500 for 90 min or with CellMask Orange Plasma Membrane stain (Termofisher C10045) at a dilution 1:1000 for 60 min at 37 °C. We used an excitation wavelength of 488 nm for Nile Red and the following 4 emission detection bands: 525–575, 575–625, 625–675, 675–725 nm. The signal of Nile Red was spectrally unmixed in 2 components (plasma membrane vs. internal membranes; lipid droplets vs. membranes) using the method described in Longo et al. (2025). Before imaging, organoids were transferred into on 8-well chambered coverslips (μ-Slide 8 Well Glass Bottom, ibidi 80827, Germany).

Imaging data acquisition

Measurements were performed on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope, using a 1.40 NA 63× oil immersion objective (HCX PL APO CS2 63/1.40 Oil Leica Microsystems). We used an excitation wavelength of 488 nm for Nile Red and the following 4 emission detection bands: 525–575, 575–625, 625–675, 675–725 nm. CellMask Orange was excited at 561 nm and its fluorescence emission was detected at 565–650 nm using a hybrid detector (Leica Microsystems). Hoechst 33,342 was excited in line sequential mode at 405 nm, and its emission was detected in the band 410–482 nm using a single detector.

Genomic analysis

DNA extraction and quantification

Genomic DNA was extracted from both tissue samples and organoid cultures derived from P(F78) and P(F65). DNA from tissue samples was purified following the Protocol: DNA Purification from Tissues of the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany; Cat# 51304). For organoid samples, previously frozen pellets were processed following the Protocol for Cultured Cells of the same kit. DNA from all samples was eluted in 30 μL of nuclease-free water. Quantification was performed using both a NanoDrop One/OneC Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and the Qubit 4 Fluorometer with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat# Q32851); this one is the elected quantification method for library preparation. Results of quantification are reported in the Supplementary information, Table S2.

Library preparation

All DNA samples were diluted to a concentration of 5 ng/μL, and 20 ng (4 μL) of each were used as input for library preparation. Libraries were generated using the AmpliSeq Cancer HotSpot Panel v2 for Illumina. (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA; Cat# 20019161), which targets 2,800 COSMIC mutations across 50 oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. Sample indexing was performed using the AmpliSeq CD Indexes Set A for Illumina. (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA; Cat# 20019105). All samples, including patient P(F78) healthy tissue, patient P(F78) organoid, patient P(F65) healthy tissue, and patient P(F65) organoid, were sequenced in triplicate. The concentration and quality of the prepared libraries (Supplementary information, Table S3) were assessed using the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA; Cat# 5067-4626) on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Sequencing run and bioinformatic analysis

Libraries were denatured and diluted following the Illumina “Denature and Dilute Libraries Guide” (Document #15039740 v10), with a final loading concentration of 9 pM. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) using the MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA; Cat# 15043895) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. FASTQ files were uploaded to the Illumina BaseSpace Sequence Hub and analyzed with the “DNA Amplicon” application (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) for mutation detection within the targeted genes. Variant annotation included assessment of clinical significance, population frequency (minor allele frequency, MAF), and genomic context using the NCBI dbSNP (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/) and ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) databases. Detected variants were then compared between tissue and organoid samples from each patient to evaluate mutational similarities and differences.

cDNA synthesis

For cDNA synthesis, 0.4 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat. No. 205311, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This kit includes an integrated genomic DNA wipeout step to prevent contamination and uses a mix of oligo-dT and random primers to ensure efficient and unbiased reverse transcription. The resulting cDNA was stored at −20°C until further use.

Digital PCR

Gene expression was quantified using the QIAcuity Digital PCR System five-plex device (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) with Nanoplate 8.5K configurations. Reactions were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol, loading 10 ng of cDNA per well. The expression of the following target genes was analyzed: TGFB, SOD, BAX, NRF2, IL6, IL10, IL1B, and KRT8. RPLP0 was included as the reference gene. Quantitative results were generated using the QIAcuity Software Suite, version 2.2.0.26 (Qiagen). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.

RNA extraction

Organoids cultured under both Growth and Differentiation conditions were subjected to RNA extraction following nutraceutical treatment. After exposure to nutraceutical and toxic compounds, organoids were first washed twice with PBS and then incubated for 2 min with Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent ((STEMCELL Technologies Inc. (STI), Cat. #100–0485) at room temperature. The organoids were carefully pipetted up and down to disrupt the Matrigel and release intact structures. After a 10-min incubation on ice with gentle shaking, the organoids were precipitated by gravity and the dissociation reagent was carefully removed without disturbing the pellet. Then, the pellet was washed twice in ice-cold PBS. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (ref. 74104, Qiagen, 40724 Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were measured using the NanoDrop One/OneC Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), while accurate quantification of RNA yield the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (ref. 32850, Invitrogen, 92008 Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used on Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Bulk RNA-seq amplicon analysis

RNA extraction, quantification and integrity assessment

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen 217604) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was determined using the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (Ref. Q32852, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Ref. Q33238, ThermoFisher Scientific). RNA integrity was assessed by determining the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) using the Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Kit (ref. 5067-1511, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) on a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Ref. G2939BA, Agilent Technologies). Quantification values, RNA Integrity Number (RIN), and related quality control parameters are reported in Table S4.

Library preparation and targeted RNA sequencing

Targeted RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the AmpliSeq for Illumina Custom Panel workflow, following the manufacturer’s instructions. For each sample, 100 ng of total RNA (based on Qubit quantification) were used as input. All biological samples were processed in triplicate. Reverse transcription was performed using the AmpliSeq cDNA Synthesis for Illumina Kit (ref. 20022654, Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Library preparation was carried out using the AmpliSeq Library PLUS for Illumina Kit (ref. 20019102, Illumina Inc.), followed by dual indexing with the AmpliSeq CD Indexes Set A (96 indexes; ref. 20019105, Illumina Inc.). The custom panel was designed using the Illumina DesignStudio Assay Design Tool and comprised 272 target genes, selected based on pathways related to inflammation, oxidative stress, metabolic regulation and cellular stress responses (see Table S5 for the complete gene list). The biological rationale underlying panel design has been previously described in detail (https://doi.org/10.1002/adhm.202303692).

Library quality control

Library size distribution and concentration were assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the High Sensitivity DNA Kit (Agilent Technologies), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All libraries showed a fragment size distribution centered around ∼260–290 bp, consistent with the expected amplicon profile (see electropherogram summary in Supplementary Data). Library denaturation and dilution were performed according to Illumina guidelines for sequencing-ready libraries. Sequencing was performed on a MiSeq i100 Plus System (Illumina Inc.) using a solid-state cartridge-based sequencing chemistry, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The MiSeq i100 Plus platform integrates onboard clustering and sequencing with a fully integrated cartridge system, ensuring reduced hands-on time and optimized run reproducibility. Paired-end sequencing was conducted following standard Illumina run parameters.

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw sequencing reads were processed using CLC Genomics Workbench v24 (Qiagen). Reads were quality-trimmed and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38), and gene-level read counts were generated for each sample. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis was performed using the built-in statistical framework of CLC Genomics Workbench. Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed when reaching an FDR-adjusted p-value <0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction).

PB4 toxicity assay and subtoxic concentration selection

To determine the highest subtoxic concentration of PB4, clusters of colon organoids in growth condition were homogenized in an appropriate volume of Matrigel and 5 μL of this mix was seeded in 96-well plates. After 24h organoids were treated with escalating concentrations of the toxic compound PB4 (0.125 μM, 0.25 μM, 0.5 μM and 1 μM). Viability measurements were performed in five replicates. Cell viability was assessed after treatment using the CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The luminescence was acquired with BioTek Synergy H1. To correct for variability in organoid seeding, raw CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay luminescence readings were normalized to organoid count. For each assay time point (0, 24, and 48 h; T0, T24, T48), luminescence measurements from parallel untreated control wells were correlated with their manually counted organoid numbers by linear regression to generate a calibration curve. In particular, a linear regression was performed to correlate the manually counted number of organoids per well (X) with the corresponding CellTiter-Glo luminescence signal (Y) in untreated control wells. All the information about calculation and quantification are described in the quantification and statistical analysis.

Nutraceutical treatment

Nutraceutical treatment was performed on organoids maintained under both Growth and Differentiation conditions. Organoids from patient P(M56) were thawed at passage 6 and maintained in IntestiCult Organoid Growth Medium (Human) (StemCell Technologies Inc. (STI), Cat. #06010) supplemented with 100 I.U./mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. For growth conditions, clusters of passage 8 colonic organoids were homogenized in an appropriate volume of a 1:1 mixture of DMEM/F12 with 15 mM HEPES (STEMCELL Technologies Inc.) supplemented with 1% BSA and Matrigel. Three 50-μL domes of this suspension were seeded into a single well of a preheated 12-well plate for each experimental condition and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 15 min. For the differentiation condition, the culture medium was changed from OGM to IntestiCult Organoid Differentiation Medium (Human) (StemCell Technologies, Cat. #100–0214) at passage 7 to promote lineage commitment and epithelial maturation. Organoids were cultured in ODM for 7 days with medium changes every 2 days. The colonic organoids were then treated under both conditions for 6 h with either PB4 (0.5 μM) alone or in combination with the pistachio extract. The extract was dissolved in PBS: ethanol (70%) in a 1:1 ratio and added to the respective culture medium (OGM or ODM) at a final concentration of 400 ng/μL.

Biophysical organoids analysis (W8 system)

Organoid biophysical properties were assessed using the W8 system (CellDynamics iSRL), which measures terminal velocity by tracking sample motion during free fall within a dedicated flow channel under static conditions.30 Analyses were performed on fresh and fixed patient-derived organoids (PDOs) cultured under growth conditions for 5 days or differentiation conditions for 5–7 days. For sample preparation, organoids were collected, resuspended in 5 mL of GCDR, and incubated on ice on a shaking platform for 10 min, followed by rapid but gentle pipetting. A second 10-min incubation on ice under shaking was performed to maximize removal of residual Matrigel, which may otherwise interfere with flow during analysis. For fixed organoids: organoids were subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature overnight. The next day, samples were washed twice with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and transferred into 15 mL tubes. For analysis, organoids were resuspended at low density (∼50 organoids/ml) in 7 mL of W8 Analysis Solution (WAS, CellDynamics). For each condition, 10–20 organoids were evaluated for mass density (fg/μm3), diameter (μm), and weight (ng), as previously described.31

W8 gate selection criteria

Organoids were recovered from Matrigel by mechanical dissociation followed by repeated PBS washes to minimize residual extracellular matrix contamination. Only intact organoids displaying well-defined borders and preserved three-dimensional architecture were selected under brightfield microscopy prior to loading into the W8 system. Visible debris, fragmented structures, and aggregates were excluded. To ensure measurement consistency, only organoids within a predefined size range (60–800 μm in diameter) were included in the analysis. This range was selected to exclude small debris or epithelial fragments (<60 μm) and large aggregates or fused organoids (>800 μm). At the instrument level, events were filtered based on signal quality and transit profile. Only single-organoid events displaying a consistent buoyant mass trace and a regular transit-time profile were retained. Irregular events suggestive of aggregates, debris, incomplete organoids, or unstable flow dynamics were excluded from analysis. Quality control filtering was applied to exclude debris and irregular events based on size and morphological criteria. Detailed selection parameters and representative examples are shown in Figure S6.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Mathematical approach for dPCR

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. In the digital PCR gene expression experiment, differences among multiple experimental groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A one-way ANOVA was conducted for each target gene (normalised to the reference gene RPLP0) to determine whether expression levels differed significantly between treatment conditions (control, PB4-treated, and PB4 plus pistachio extract). When the overall ANOVA indicated a significant effect, pairwise group comparisons were further examined with adjustment for multiple testing. Specifically, the two-stage linear Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR) for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 after FDR correction. Symbols denoting significance levels were as follows: ∗ for p < 0.05, ∗∗ for p < 0.01, ∗∗∗ for p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗ for p < 0.0001. Error bars represent the standard deviation (SD).

Mathematical approach for toxicity assay

In the CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), differences among multiple experimental groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Toxicity assay data from multiple PB4 concentrations and control were analyzed by one-way ANOVA to evaluate treatment effects on organoid viability. When the overall ANOVA indicated a significant effect, pairwise group comparisons were further examined with adjustment for multiple testing. Specifically, the two-stage linear Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR) for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 after FDR correction. Symbols denoting significance levels were as follows: ∗ for p < 0.05, ∗∗ for p < 0.01, ∗∗∗ for p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗ for p < 0.0001. Error bars represent the standard deviation (SD). This analysis was performed at each time point (T0, T24, T48) and resulted in a regression equation of the form Y = mX + b for each time point.
