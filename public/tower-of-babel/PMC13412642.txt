Implementation of the Baxter Animated Retching Faces Nausea Assessment Scale in Pediatric Oncology: A Quality Improvement Project
Authors: Carina Braeutigam, Jennifer Young, Jessica Shaw, Kristen Laswell, Kathleen Demmel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Chemotherapy-induced nausea in the pediatric oncology population can be uniquely challenging to assess and manage. Adequate assessment and documentation of a patient’s nausea can be used to evaluate the efficacy of antiemetic interventions and to optimize patient management. This project aimed to introduce a pediatric nausea assessment tool and develop a standardized process to improve the frequency of nursing documentation of nausea.Methods:This single-institution quality improvement project utilized a stepwise process to introduce the Baxter Animated Retching Faces (BARF) scale as a new tool to the pediatric oncology unit, educated staff on the use of the tool, and ultimately facilitated documentation of BARF scales within the patient’s electronic medical record. Plan-do-study-act methodology was used to evaluate the effectiveness of each incremental change.Results:The percentage of oncology patients with at least three documented nausea assessments on active chemotherapy days increased from 5% at baseline to 75% within the first month following implementation into the electronic medical record, peaked at 94% in September 2022, and remained above the project goal of 75% for two years after completion of the active project.Conclusions:The BARF scale was readily integrated into routine care in a pediatric oncology service. This documentation helped bridge the gap between bedside evaluations and treatment decisions, improving patient care.

INTRODUCTION

Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) is a prevalent and distressing side effect of chemotherapy. Although vomiting is an objective measure that is easy to quantify and document, the subjective nature of nausea can be more challenging. In the pediatric population, there are added challenges as nausea may be a difficult concept for children to understand and communicate. Adequate assessment and documentation of a patient’s nausea can be used to evaluate the efficacy of antiemetic interventions and optimize a patient’s CINV management.1 Improving CINV control improves patient and family satisfaction and decreases the risk of chemotherapy-related complications such as dehydration, acute kidney injury, and anorexia.2,3

In 2020, Cincinnati Children’s developed an institutional guideline for standardized use of empiric antiemetics based on the emetogenic risk of a chemotherapy regimen. This provided an evidence-based approach for empiric antiemetic selection. Building upon this framework, in 2022 a multidisciplinary team undertook a quality improvement project to facilitate nausea evaluation and documentation. The project goal was to implement a standardized tool for nursing staff to evaluate nausea and develop a standardized process for nursing documentation. The project aimed to increase the daily percentage among all oncology patients receiving chemotherapy with at least three nausea assessments recorded in the electronic medical record (EMR).

METHODS

Cincinnati Children’s is a 670-bed academic medical center with a 44-bed inpatient oncology unit providing comprehensive care to pediatric, adolescent, and young adult patients with a wide range of malignant conditions. Cincinnati Children’s serves 400–450 oncology patients each year.

In 2022, we formed a multidisciplinary team to improve documentation of nausea in the EMR. The team consisted of key stakeholders, including physicians, nurses, and pharmacists. To identify and address potential areas for improvement, both process and outcome improvement opportunities were systematically explored. These opportunities were evaluated using appropriate performance measures, with data collection and analysis in alignment with the Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s quality improvement methodology.

Aim and Operational Definitions and Key Driver Diagram Development

The primary project outcome was the project’s specific aim. After initial data analysis, we established the project’s SMART aim as: increase the percentage of oncology patients actively receiving chemotherapy on a particular day and who had at least 3 documented nausea assessments in the medical record, from a baseline of 5% to 75%. Active chemotherapy treatment days were defined as days with chemotherapy active administration. The numerator for the outcome metric was the number of oncology patients actively receiving chemotherapy on a particular day who also had at least 3 documented nausea assessments. The denominator is the number of oncology patients receiving chemotherapy on the same day. Theories of improvement were developed and noted as drivers on the key driver diagram (Fig. 1). Successful interventions were adopted after plan-do-study-act (PDSA) testing cycles were applied. Through iterative testing and refinement, successful strategies were identified and adopted for broader implementation to enhance the accuracy and consistency of nausea assessment and documentation within the EMR.

Key drivers are outlined with associated interventions geared toward the SMART aim of increasing nausea assessments for patients receiving chemotherapy.

Institutional review board approval was not required for this quality improvement project.

Baseline Staff Survey

Before interventions began, we surveyed bedside nurses and oncology providers including oncology faculty, staff physicians, and nurse practitioners to identify the scope of the current problem. The survey aimed to identify gaps in both understanding and practice and to gather staff insights into current practices and challenges in assessing and documenting nausea (Figure 2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A772). Bedside nurses were asked about their understanding of nausea and vomiting, their perception of patients’ ability to differentiate pain from nausea and report each independently, and their perception of the current assessments. We queried providers about the ease of finding nausea-specific data in the EMR, ease of evaluating the efficacy of current antiemetic regimens, and their perception of nursing practice for assessing and communicating patient nausea. Providers were also asked if they felt they needed to speak directly with a patient to assess the severity of nausea, and whether this contributed to a delay in care. We received 38 nursing responses (31% response rate) and 38 provider responses (65% response rate).

Survey results suggested that the bedside nursing staff was confident in their understanding of nausea versus vomiting and felt that they were regularly and adequately assessing patients. The majority of provider responses (79%), however, reflected a difficulty in finding data regarding a patient’s nausea in the EMR, and difficulty interpreting if an antiemetic regimen or as-needed antiemetic was helpful for a patient despite the nursing assessments of nausea; this information was not readily available to the providers, which hindered their ability to make informed decisions. The providers desired more consistent and objective data within the patient’s chart. Providers felt they needed to speak directly with a patient to understand their nausea, which resulted in a potential delay in care for the patient. In total, the survey responses revealed a clear disconnect between nursing assessments and provider access to important data, underscoring the need for a more consistent and objective approach to nausea documentation and communication within the EMR. These survey results illustrated a gap that could be rectified with a standard process for nursing to document nausea within the patient’s chart.

Baxter Animated Retching Faces Scale

The Baxter Animated Retching Faces (BARF) scale is a validated tool for nausea assessment in children six years and older (Figure 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A771).4 The BARF scale was selected over other available options5 due to its alignment with the existing 10-point pain scales already in use at our institution. We preferred consistency with the 0–10 approach already embedded with pain assessments and this similarity facilitates a more intuitive and consistent approach for both patients and healthcare providers in assessing symptoms, ensuring ease of integration into current clinical practices.

The BARF scale provides a visual representation of varying levels of discomfort, helping children communicate their subjective experience of nausea in a way that is both accessible and easy to understand. The BARF scale includes 6 animated faces, beginning with a neutral or content face and progressing through a series of increasingly distressed faces to a final actively vomiting face. Each face on the scale is associated with a numerical value from 0 to 10. This tool helps the patient translate their subjective feeling of nausea into a quantifiable value.4,6–8

With the introduction of the BARF scale at Cincinnati Children’s, we implemented a comprehensive educational initiative for bedside nurses and oncology providers to ensure effective integration and utilization of the tool. To ensure widespread use and accessibility of the BARF scale, laminated versions of the tool were posted on the walls of each patient’s room. This strategic placement allowed both patients and healthcare providers to have immediate access to the scale during nursing assessments, making it easier for pediatric patients to visually communicate their level of nausea consistently. Bedside nurses were also provided with a detailed script for introducing the BARF scale to patients and families. The script was designed to provide clear, age-appropriate instructions for using the scale and to encourage open communication about the patient’s experience with nausea.

Recognizing the diversity of the patient population and the importance of ensuring all families could fully engage with the tool, the BARF scale was also translated into multiple languages, including Arabic, French, and Spanish. This multilingual approach was essential to increase accessibility for non-English-speaking families.

Integration into Nursing Workflow

In accordance with the standards set forth by the Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations, bedside nurses are required to assess and document pain scores for every patient every four hours, as well as within one hour following the administration of any as-needed pain medication. This standardized practice has been reliably incorporated into the daily workflow for nursing staff at our institution. Given this established routine, we identified a unique opportunity to integrate a nausea assessment alongside the existing pain assessments, leveraging the structure and familiarity already in place. This approach aimed to streamline the integration of the BARF scale into the nursing staff’s daily clinical workflows, thereby minimizing disruptions and enhancing the accuracy of symptom documentation.

To facilitate this integration, the PDSA approach was utilized and is illustrated in Figure 2. The initial test of change was conducted by a single nurse using a paper form for BARF scale assessment and documentation. This pilot phase enabled feedback and observations to identify potential challenges in the process and assess feasibility. As the process proved manageable, the scope of the test gradually expanded to include additional nurses and more patients. Once BARF charting was integrated into the EMR, the paper process was phased out. The transition to electronic charting offered significant advantages over the paper-based process, addressing concerns about workflow inefficiencies and data loss associated with manual entry.

The current flowsheet for electronic documentation is shown in Figure 3. This approach to documentation was designed to optimize workflow, ensuring that the assessment of both pain and nausea was seamlessly incorporated into the daily routine of nursing staff, supporting improved communication and care coordination among the multidisciplinary team.

“Nausea Assessment” flowsheet built within the EMR.

Although the study team focused its analysis on patients who were actively receiving chemotherapy, workflow considerations guided broader implementation. To simplify processes and avoid adding additional decision-making tasks for nursing staff, a blanket approach was adopted after the initial PDSA cycles. Once the electronic flowsheet was available, BARF assessments were obtained alongside each pain assessment for all patients on our oncology units, regardless of age or treatment status. This approach eliminated the need for nurses to decide whether to ask a patient about nausea, thereby supporting consistency and streamlining workflow. Additionally, this allowed the capture of nausea outside of active chemotherapy days, which provides value in capturing delayed nausea and nausea related to enteral feeds, gastrointestinal infections, or chronic nausea.

Nausea Dashboard

Following the successful implementation of the BARF scale documentation within the EMR, a dedicated nausea dashboard was developed (Fig. 4). The purpose of this dashboard was to centralize all patient-specific information relevant to CINV management into a single, easily accessible location within the EMR. Although the addition of a nausea flowsheet improved documentation and visibility of symptom scores, provider feedback indicated that providers were still required to navigate multiple flowsheets or summary tabs to obtain a complete clinical picture. For example, correlating nausea severity with chemotherapy timing, medication administration, or changes in feeding rates required moving between separate documentation areas, and determining which as-needed antiemetic interventions were most effective required comparison across multiple sources.

“Antiemetic and Chemotherapy” dashboard built within the EMR.

The dashboard was designed to address these workflow challenges by consolidating key clinical information into a unified visual timeline. It integrates nausea scores and interventions, episodes of emesis, chemotherapy administrations, antiemetic timing, use of aromatherapy, oral intake, enteral feeding status, intake and output data, weight, and intravenous fluid rates, all with graphical displays to visualize trends over time. The intent of this centralized view was to provide clinicians with a more comprehensive overview of each patient’s CINV-related data within a single interface and to reduce the need to manually gather information from multiple locations in the EMR.

RESULTS

Before the integration of the BARF scale assessments into the EMR, data regarding nausea and emesis were documented in the nursing body assessment, intake and output measurements, and the patient’s care plan. Although descriptions of emesis or general nausea were occasionally present in narrative documentation, the documentation was inconsistent in both content and location across the medical record. After reviewing multiple notes and flowsheets, it was difficult to accurately assess how a patient had clinically progressed over the course of a given day, hence the need for a standardized documentation tool. Despite these data-gathering challenges, we determined the mean baseline rate of nausea documentation at least three times daily in patients actively receiving chemotherapy was 5% (Fig. 5). This data was derived from reviewing 255 charts—an average of seven charts per day over a 6-week period before PDSA cycle implementation.

Statistical process control P chart displaying the percentage of patients with 3 or more documented nausea assessments that increased from a baseline of 5% to a peak of 94% in September 2022. Multiple centerline shifts occurred between June and October 2022. The figure represents 3,381 total patient days. The green arrow represents the desired change in direction.

Following the integration of BARF scale assessments into the EMR (PDSA cycle 4), the mean percentage of patients with at least three documented nausea assessments increased to 75% within the first month (June 2022) and 94% within 4 months of implementation (September 2022; Figure 5).

Following completion of the active project in October 2022, tracking compliance continued for an additional 2 years through 2024. During that 2-year follow-up, the goal of 75% of patients receiving active chemotherapy, with at least 3 documented nausea assessments, was maintained. The mean percentage improved to 90% during this time, ranging from 79% to 97% (data not shown). Results from a follow-up survey of providers revealed that 86% of survey respondents thought that finding relevant information regarding their patient’s nausea status in the EMR was easier.

DISCUSSION

The oncology service at our institution undertook a multidisciplinary quality improvement initiative with the goal of developing a process for reliable assessment and documentation of a patient’s nausea in real time within the EMR. This project aimed to achieve 75% compliance with nausea documentation during chemotherapy days. Within just one month of implementation of a BARF score flowsheet within the EMR, the mean percentage of documented nausea assessments rose to 84%.

The success of this project stemmed from 2 key points: (1) coupling nausea documentation within an already existing nursing workflow and (2) the creation of a nausea flowsheet within the EMR. Before the availability of the electronic flowsheet, compliance with nursing documentation was low. The initial paper process was unreliable and unsuccessful. Previous reports in the literature on documenting nausea outside of the EMR faced similar challenges and poor uptake.9,10 Once charting was available within the EMR, compliance improved drastically. This was successful not only because it was readily available but also because the process for nausea documentation mimicked the established process for nursing pain assessment and documentation. By designing a parallel process with identical assessment frequency and a mirrored process for electronic documentation, this new process had minimal impact on nursing workflow, as reported by nursing colleagues. Providers were then able to see nausea scores and make treatment decisions in real time. BARF scores were discussed during daily rounds, providing positive feedback to nurses regarding the utility of these new data.

The subsequent introduction of the nausea dashboard further solidified improvements in nausea communication, ensuring that all healthcare providers had immediate access to essential data related to CINV management in a single central place within the EMR. This centralized system empowered clinical teams to make more informed decisions about antiemetic strategies and overall treatment plans, thereby improving patient care. The nausea dashboard created within this project is unique from previously published dashboards3,11 in that our dashboard provides a deep view into the CINV control for a single patient, with the greatest utility in determining not only control of CINV but also efficacy of individual as-needed antiemetics.

Following initial success, improvements were sustained over a two-year follow-up period. This marked achievement reflects the successful integration of nausea documentation into the nursing workflow and underscores the value of standardized assessment tools, such as the BARF scale, in improving patient care.

Utilization of the BARF scale and routine nausea documentation is currently standard practice at our hospital within the hematology and oncology services, with planned expansion to the gastrointestinal and bone marrow transplant services. Future directions for the use of nausea scores may include combining nausea scores with emesis episodes to define targets for successful CINV management and collecting outpatient nausea scores during periods of delayed nausea risk. Although always needing patient-specific tailoring, these data points in concert may facilitate better management of a patient’s antiemetic regimen, better selection of breakthrough agents, and better management of nutrition support during periods of CINV. Areas of future study should evaluate BARF scores and episodes of emesis together to investigate the impact of CINV on readmission rates, nutritional outcomes, and patient satisfaction.
