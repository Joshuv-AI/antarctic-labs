 1. PROJECT OBJECTIVES

The purpose of this Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA) was to develop and
demonstrate new synthetic strategies for fabricating lithium-selective polymeric adsorbents by covalently
incorporating crown ether functionalities into durable, processable support materials. These advanced
sorbents are designed to selectively extract lithium ions (Li⁺) from dilute aqueous sources such as brine,
seawater, industrial wastewater, and battery recycling streams.

This effort directly addresses a critical national challenge—the growing supply-demand imbalance for
lithium driven by rapid expansion of the electric vehicle and grid storage markets. The project aimed to
combine Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s expertise in surface-initiated atom transfer radical
polymerization (SI-ATRP) and functional nanocomposite synthesis with the partner’s interest in scalable
lithium separation technologies.

The overarching objectives included:
 • Designing crown ether-containing monomers tailored for Li⁺ complexation.
 • Developing SI-ATRP routes to graft these polymers onto solid supports.
 • Evaluating structural, chemical, and adsorption characteristics of the resulting hybrid materials.
 • Establishing foundational knowledge for transitioning this chemistry toward scalable,
 deployable sorbent technologies.

1.1 TECHNICAL APPROACH

The project was based on a SI-ATRP process (Scheme 1) that enables precise control over polymer chain
length, grafting density, and chemical functionality. The key innovation was the synthesis of a vinyl-
functionalized 12-crown-4 monomer, 2-propenoic acid, 1,4,7,10-tetraoxacyclododec-2-ylmethyl ester,
derived from the esterification of 2-hydroxymethyl-12-crown-4 with acryloyl chloride. This monomer
allowed the direct introduction of lithium-selective crown ether moieties into a polymer backbone via
controlled radical polymerization.

Two distinct substrate systems were used:
 1. Silica nanoparticles (Si) silicon dioxide/silica (SiO2)~15 nm) – providing high surface area for
 dense polymer grafting and enhanced Li⁺ accessibility.

 1

 2. Poly(vinyl chloride)-co-chlorinated poly(vinyl chloride) fibers – offering a mechanically robust,
 flexible, and scalable platform suitable for deployment in column or flow-through adsorption
 systems.

The polymerization reactions were catalyzed by CuBr₂/Me₆TREN under mild conditions in methanol.
Post-synthesis, the hybrid materials were purified by dialysis and characterized by:

  Fourier Transform Infrared and Nuclear Magnetic Resonance spectroscopy for functional group
 verification.

  Thermogravimetric Analysis and Differential Scanning Calorimetry for polymer content and
 thermal stability.

  Scanning Electron Microscopy and Transmission Electron Microscopy for morphology and
 coating uniformity.

  Brunauer–Emmett–Teller surface area analysis.

  Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) to quantify Li⁺ uptake
 capacity from aqueous LiTfSI solutions.

1.1.1 Major Accomplishments

 1. Monomer and Polymer Synthesis

  Successfully synthesized a novel vinyl-functionalized 12-crown-4 monomer that retains
 lithium-binding selectivity and is compatible with ATRP polymerization chemistry.
  Produced linear homopolymers with controlled molecular weights (Mn ≈ 57,000) and narrow
 polydispersity (Mw/Mn ≈ 1.47).

 2. Fabrication of Hybrid Sorbents

  Developed SiO₂-g-poly(crown ether) particle brushes and fiber-g-poly(crown ether)
 composites with polymer loadings of 44% and 40% by weight, respectively.
  Microscopic and spectroscopic characterization confirmed the formation of uniform polymer
 shells on nanoparticle and fiber surfaces.

 3. Adsorption Performance

  The SiO₂-based sorbent achieved a lithium adsorption capacity of 0.229 mmol Li⁺/g, while
 the fiber-based sorbent reached 0.148 mmol Li⁺/g, consistent with the crown ether content.
 These extraction efficiencies are at the high end of values reported for polymer-based lithium
 sorbents, demonstrating strong Li⁺ selectivity and binding strength. Although further
 optimization and scale-up are needed for economic deployment, the results indicate a
 technically promising and potentially viable pathway for lithium recovery from dilute
 aqueous resources.
  The adsorption experiments confirmed strong Li⁺ selectivity due to the cavity size of the 12-
 crown-4 ether, which matches the ionic radius of Li⁺ and forms a stable host–guest complex
 (see Table 1).

 2

 4. Process Advantages

  The SI-ATRP approach eliminates the need for mechanical blending or post-functionalization
 steps, enabling precise molecular control and reproducibility.
  The hybrid sorbents exhibit robust chemical and thermal stability, as well as potential for
 regeneration and reuse, aligning with sustainability goals.

 5. Knowledge Advancement

  The project demonstrated a broadly applicable methodology for immobilizing macrocyclic
 ligands on solid supports.
  The findings open opportunities to design other selective sorbents for strategic materials such
 as cobalt, nickel, and rare earth elements.

1.1.1.1 Impact and Benefits

The developed technology represents a transformational approach to lithium-ion capture from
unconventional resources. The combination of size-selective crown ethers, robust polymer frameworks,
and scalable solid supports offers a new class of high-performance sorbents.
Key benefits include:
  Enhanced Selectivity and Efficiency: Covalent bonding prevents ligand leaching, ensuring long-
 term stability and selectivity.

  Compatibility with Pyro- and Hydrometallurgical Systems: These materials can integrate into
 existing flowsheets for lithium recovery or critical material recycling.

  Environmental and Economic Gains: Reduced reliance on solvent-intensive or high-temperature
 processes currently used in lithium recovery.

  Scientific Value: The project bridges the gap between molecular recognition chemistry and
 scalable materials engineering, creating a platform for multifunctional adsorbents.

 3

The technology aligns with Department of Energy’s mission to ensure secure and sustainable supply
chains for critical materials and contributes to national goals in energy storage, recycling, and resource
recovery.

Technical Barriers and Future Work

While the laboratory-scale demonstrations were successful, several challenges remain:

  Optimization of Adsorption Kinetics: Future efforts will explore copolymerization with
 hydrophilic monomers to improve lithium ion accessibility and diffusion.

  Regeneration and Longevity Testing: Long-term cycling stability and regeneration efficiency
 under real brine or seawater conditions must be evaluated.

  Scale-Up and Manufacturing: Translating SI-ATRP synthesis to pilot-scale processes requires
 further engineering optimization to ensure cost-effectiveness and reproducibility.

  Selective Ion Competition: Performance testing in multicomponent systems containing sodium,
 magnesium, and calcium ions will be pursued to assess selectivity under realistic conditions.

These efforts will advance the technology readiness level (TRL) from the current TRL 3–4 proof of
concept and laboratory validation toward TRL 5–6 integrated prototype demonstration.

Summary

This CRADA successfully demonstrated a new synthetic platform for lithium-selective polymeric
sorbents through the integration of crown ether chemistry and surface-initiated controlled radical
polymerization. The outcomes establish a foundation for developing robust, regenerable, and highly
selective sorbents for lithium recovery and potentially other critical elements. The collaboration
strengthened the partner’s understanding of functional polymer synthesis and positioned the technology
for transition into applied Research & Development and pilot-scale testing.
