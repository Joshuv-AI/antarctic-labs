A Benchmark for Multi-Party Negotiation Games from Real Negotiation Data
Leo Benac, Jonas Raedler, Zilin Ma, Finale Doshi-Velez

Abstract
Many real-world multi-party negotiations unfold as sequences of binding, action-level commitments rather than a single final outcome. We introduce a benchmark for this under-studied regime featuring a configurable game generator that sweeps key structural properties such as incentive alignment, goal complexity, and payoff distribution. To evaluate decision-making, we test three value-function approximations—myopic reward, an optimistic upper bound, and a pessimistic lower bound—that act as biased lenses on deal evaluation. Through exact evaluation on small games and comparative evaluation on large, document-grounded instances derived from the Harvard Negotiation Challenge, we map the strategic regimes where each approximation succeeds or fails. We observe that different game structures demand different valuation strategies, motivating agents that learn robust state values and plan effectively over long horizons under binding commitments and terminal only rewards. Code and data for the benchmark are available at: https://github.com/dtak/negotiation_benchmark_public .

Introduction

Computational models of negotiation have long been studied in game theory and multi-agent reinforcement learning
(
Rubinstein 1982
;
Baarslag et al. 2010
;
Abdelnabi et al. 2024
;
Mak et al. 2023
;
Meta Fundamental AI Research Diplomacy Team et al.(2022)Meta Fundamental AI Research Diplomacy Team (FAIR), Bakhtin, Brown, Dinan, Farina, Flaherty, Fried, Goff, Gray, Hu, Jacob, Komeili, Konath, Kwon, Lerer, Lewis, Miller, Mitts, Renduchintala, Roller, Rowe, Shi, Spisak, Wei, Wu, Zhang, and Zijlstra FAIR
)
, with the promise of helping bounded decision-makers identify effective strategies in mixed-motive settings. Much of this work operationalizes negotiation as bargaining over a specific outcome, and offers are typically non-binding until acceptance
(
Lewis et al. 2017
;
Baarslag et al. 2010
)
.

However, many high-stakes, real-world multi-party negotiations cannot be reduced to a single agreement over a final outcome. In our collaborations with professional frontline negotiators, we find that agreements are reached through sequences of bilateral and small-group deals in which parties make binding commitments over concrete actions. Each deal constrains future options, reshaping future bargaining opportunities. Here, the strategy includes not just on the final agreement, but partner order, commitment sequencing, and how intermediate commitments interact. While existing MARL algorithms could be applied to these scenarios, the regime is under-benchmarked, and it remains unclear when current methods can provide reliable guidance (e.g., which parties to engage first).

Drawing from our experience with real frontline negotiators, we introduce a benchmark for this underrepresented regime: multi-party, sequential, action-level negotiation games with binding commitments and terminal-only rewards. Specifically, our contributions are:

Contribution 1: A controllable game generator.

We provide a configurable generator that produces diverse instances of sequential action-level negotiation games with binding commitments. The generator supports controlled sweeps over structural properties that shape strategic difficulty, including:
(i)
scale
(number of players, goals, and commitments);
(ii)
incentive alignment
(more cooperative vs. more adversarial preference structure);
(iii)
payoff structure
(balanced vs. positive-/negative-dominated utilities);
(iv)
goal complexity
(how many commitments goals require);
(v)
goal non-linearity
(the fraction of all-or-nothing vs. linear goals); and
(vi)
preference structure across goals
(controlled via latent-factor dimensionality that sets how correlated utilities are across issues).
Together, these controls make it possible to systematically construct and study qualitatively different negotiation landscapes – from incremental, locally-improvable regimes to settings with complementarity, delayed credit assignment, and traps.

Contribution 2: A reference gameplay procedure and evaluation suite.

We provide a simple
reference
decision procedure – turn-taking with partner selection, proposal construction, and acceptance – that instantiates one natural way to interact with our games. We use this fixed procedure to isolate instance difficulty and compare different value-function approximations under controlled conditions. We support exact evaluation on small instances via dynamic programming over the induced negotiation tree, and comparative evaluation on large instances where the exact solution is intractable. Importantly, the benchmark itself is protocol-agnostic: the same game instances can be paired with alternative protocols, information structures, or action spaces.

Contribution 3: Exposing challenging regimes via “value-function lenses.”

A central challenge in sequential action-level negotiation is estimating the downstream value of partial commitments. We study the benchmark through a small set of simple value-function approximations, each encoding a different assumption about how the remaining (uncommitted) part of the game may unfold. These approximations act as intentionally biased lenses: when instantiated within a simple reference gameplay procedure (used for controlled comparison), they induce distinct strategic behaviors (e.g., avoidance of negative exposure versus willingness to accept short-term sacrifices to unlock future gains). We use large-scale parameter sweeps to map where each lens succeeds or fails, showing that no single approximation dominates. The resulting regime-level characterization highlights which structural properties (e.g., payoff structure, non-linear goal ratio, and incentive alignment) drive negotiation difficulty, providing concrete targets for future algorithm design.

Contribution 4: Realistic large instances from negotiation settings.

We complement synthetic instances with large negotiation games derived from converting position papers from the Harvard Negotiation Challenge climate exercise into machine-readable game instances. This bridges controlled synthetic analysis with realistic, document-grounded scenarios, and allows us to test whether qualitative patterns discovered in regime sweeps transfer to large, structured negotiation problems.

2
Related Works

Negotiations span a wide range of settings, some of which have well-established benchmarks and some that do not. We organize this space around five factors that shape strategic behavior, helping us surface both well-studied settings and under-benchmarked regimes:

•

Object of negotiation (outcome vs actions/commitments):
Do agents negotiate directly over a
final outcome
(e.g., a complete allocation or agreement), or over
individual actions/commitments
that accumulate over time and jointly determine the eventual outcome?

•

Number of parties (bilateral vs multilateral):
Bilateral negotiation vs. multilateral settings, where coalition dynamics and strategic externalities can arise.

•

Temporal structure (simultaneous vs sequential):
Simultaneous choice vs. sequential offer/response dynamics (often with horizons or deadlines).

•

Information structure (perfect vs imperfect):
Common knowledge utilities and state vs. imperfect information, often with private values or hidden constraints.

•

Commitment structure (non-binding vs. binding):
Non-binding proposals until agreement vs. binding, persistent commitments that directly constrain future feasible decisions.

Benchmarks for Outcome-level Bargaining Games.

A large fraction of negotiation research evaluates agents in
outcome-level
bargaining settings: typically bilateral (though sometimes multi-party) interactions with
private preferences
, where agents exchange proposals – most commonly via
sequential alternating offers
– over a
final allocation
(e.g., a price or division of items), and proposals remain
non-binding until acceptance
.
Rubinstein 1982
’s alternating-offers model formalized this protocol and served as a reference point for many subsequent benchmark designs.

This setting has motivated several widely used benchmarks. The Automated Negotiating Agents Competition (ANAC) established a standard suite for bilateral multi-issue negotiation under private preference profiles, which primarily uses this alternating-offers model
(
Baarslag et al. 2010
)
. Outcome-level bargaining has also been instantiated in language-based benchmarks: Deal or No Deal operationalizes bargaining over item divisions with private values through dialogue
(
Lewis et al. 2017
)
, and the “CraigslistBargain” benchmark enables training and evaluation for buyer-seller price negotiation with alternating offers
(
He et al. 2018
)
. More recent work extends outcome-level bargaining to multilateral settings with LLM agents
(
Abdelnabi et al. 2024
)
.

While these benchmarks are well-suited for bargaining over a final allocation, they generally do not model binding intermediate commitments that persist and constrain future feasible agreements – an important feature of frontline crisis negotiation and other complex multi-party settings.

Coalition formation and coalitional bargaining games.

Multi-party negotiation is often studied through coalition formation and coalitional bargaining: in
outcome-level
settings with
3+
agents, parties negotiate over a coalition structure (how agents partition into coalitions) and a payoff allocation (how coalition value is divided), typically via
sequential
proposal/accept dynamics with offers remaining
non-binding until agreement
; information may be either perfect or imperfect.

This literature is rich in theoretical results on solution concepts and computational complexity
(
Rahwan et al. 2015
;
Aziz & de Keijzer 2011
)
, but – to the best of our knowledge – lacks a dominant benchmark suite for systematic empirical evaluations. Empirical studies therefore often rely on small, paper-specific synthetic instances (e.g.,
Chalkiadakis & Boutilier 2004
), alongside a smaller number of domain-specific benchmarks such as collaborative vehicle routing, where firms form coalitions and negotiate over gains under uncertainty
(
Mak et al. 2023
)
.

In this setting, negotiation is still framed around coalition/value outcomes and payoff allocation, rather than negotiation over action commitments that directly modify an evolving environment state.

Sequential action-level negotiation games with commitments.

Closest to our focus are
action-level
negotiation settings, where two or more agents interact
sequentially
(under either perfect or imperfect information) by proposing
binding commitments
whose acceptance constrains future feasible behavior and changes downstream dynamics. For example,
Zhu et al. 2025
introduce Markov Commitment Games, in which agents propose joint commitments that – if mutually accepted – become enforceable constraints on future actions. However, their contribution is primarily algorithmic rather than a benchmark, and their evaluation is limited to small mixed-motive matrix games that do not capture long-horizon strategic interaction.

Several existing benchmarks incorporate commitment-like, sequential action negotiation, but within domain-specific environments. Diplomacy, for instance, is a multi-party, long-horizon game in which agents coordinate and compete via committed actions, often mediated by natural language, and recent work demonstrates strong language-driven play in this setting
(
Meta Fundamental AI Research Diplomacy Team et al.(2022)Meta Fundamental AI Research Diplomacy Team (FAIR), Bakhtin, Brown, Dinan, Farina, Flaherty, Fried, Goff, Gray, Hu, Jacob, Komeili, Konath, Kwon, Lerer, Lewis, Miller, Mitts, Renduchintala, Roller, Rowe, Shi, Spisak, Wei, Wu, Zhang, and Zijlstra FAIR
)
. Welfare Diplomacy extends this interaction with an explicitly welfare-oriented general-sum variant to benchmark cooperative behavior among language models
(
Mukobi et al. 2023
)
. Apart from games, RICE-N, an integrated assessment model, augments a climate-economy simulator with a multilateral negotiation stage over policy commitments (e.g., mitigation rates) that can be enforced as constraints on future actions, with agents optimizing discounted long-run utility
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
.

While these environments capture sequential commitment dynamics, they either (i) entangle negotiation with domain-specific mechanics that make it difficult to isolate which game features (e.g., action complementarities or utility alignment) drive success, or (ii) study commitment mechanisms only in narrow, small game families. In contrast, we provide a configurable benchmark for multi-party, sequential action-level negotiation with binding commitments, that is grounded in frontline crisis negotiation scenarios.

3
Negotiation Game Benchmark

In this section, we describe the design and instantiation of our benchmark. We first summarize key insights from interviews with frontline negotiators that motivate our modeling choices (Sec.
3.1
). We then formalize the underlying negotiation game (Sec.
3.1.1
) and introduce a configurable random game generator that allows controlled sweeps over structural properties such as goal complexity and incentive alignment (Sec.
3.1.2
). Next, we specify our default gameplay assumptions and negotiation protocol –including the information structure, turn-taking, partner selection, and offer/acceptance rules – while noting viable alternatives (Sec.
3.2
). Finally, we present baselines and evaluation approaches for both small negotiation games with exact solutions and large games where only comparative evaluation is tractable (Sec.
3.3
).

3.1
Insights from Domain Experts and Real-World Practice

Our benchmark design is driven by prior studies of frontline humanitarian negotiators
(
Ma et al. 2025
)
and the structural realities of global climate conferences like COP30
(
Depledge 2005
;
Yamin & Depledge 2004
)
. These sources highlight that real-world agreements rarely emerge from single-step bargaining; rather, they are built incrementally through repeated bilateral and small-group consultations. We provide full details of our interviews with domain experts in Appendix
A
.

This directly motivates our core environment formalization: a multi-party game featuring sequential deal-making, binding commitments, and path-dependent state updates. In this setting, partner selection, commitment ordering, and multi-goal risk analysis become the primary strategic drivers. Because this complex regime remains under-studied, we introduce a configurable game generator to support controlled evaluations across key structural dimensions. Furthermore, we supplement these synthetic sweeps with large-scale “real-life negotiation games” computationally derived from Harvard Negotiation Challenge (HNC) climate position papers. Extended context on these domain insights and the HNC dataset translation process is provided in Appendix
B
.

3.1.1
Game Formalization

Players and Commitments.

In our game, several players negotiate over a finite number of turns
T
to reach a joint commitment that satisfies various goals, with each player valuing the resulting multi-goal satisfaction differently.

Specifically, there are
N_{P}
players
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{N_{P}}\}
. Each player
p_{n}
has a binary commitment vector
C_{n}
, where
C_{nj}=1
indicates that
p_{n}
commits to doing
j
. The joint commitment state at time
t
is
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}=(C_{1}^{(t)},\dots,C_{N_{P}}^{(t)})
. Commitments are binding, meaning that entries may flip
0\to 1
but never
1\to 0
. At
t=0
,
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
is all zeros.

Goal Satisfaction and Reward.

Players negotiate to satisfy various goals according to how much they care about each goal. In a game, there are
N_{G}
goals
g_{1},\dots,g_{N_{G}}
. Player-goal utilities are given by
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{G}\times N_{P}}
, where
\mathbf{G}_{g,n}
is the utility player
p_{n}
assigns to goal
g
(positive, negative, or zero).

Each goal
g
is associated with a set of required commitments
\mathcal{C}_{g}
, consisting of indices
(n,j)
into the joint commitment state
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
. Thus,
\mathcal{C}_{g}
specifies which entries
\mathbf{C}_{nj}^{(t)}
determine goal
g
’s satisfaction.

Goal Types (Linear vs All-or-Nothing).
We consider linear and all-or-nothing goals. For a linear goal, satisfaction is the fraction of required commitments that have been made:
S_{g}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{g}|}\sum_{(n,j)\in\mathcal{C}_{g}}\mathbb{I}\left[\mathbf{C}_{nj}^{(t)}=1\right]
. For an all-or-nothing, satisfaction is binary:
S_{g}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})=1
iff
\mathbf{C}_{nj}^{(t)}=1
for all
(n,j)\in\mathcal{C}_{g}
. Otherwise,
S_{g}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})=0
.

S(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})
denotes the vector that contains the satisfaction value for every goal
g
.

While a utility vector
R(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})=\mathbf{G}^{\top}S(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})
can conceptually be computed at any step
t
, rewards are only realized at the terminal step
T
. Players receive
R(\mathbf{C}^{(T)})
with no intermediary payoffs.

3.1.2
Random Game Generation

Building on this formalization, we introduce a configurable generator to produce diverse negotiation games. By controlling structural properties, we can create games across multiple axes of difficulty, providing a challenging benchmark to evaluate solving algorithms.

Controllable Game Properties.

The controllable axes include (i) the game’s scale (number of players, commitments per player, and goals), and (ii) incentive alignment (whether a game is more cooperative – players’ utilities are often similar – or more adversarial – players’ utilities are often opposing each other; generally, though, every game is mixed-motive.). We further control (iii) the payoff structure (balanced vs. positive- or negative-dominated utilities), (iv) goal complexity (commitments required per goal), and (v) goal non-linearity (the fraction of all-or-nothing vs. linear goals). Finally, we specify (vi) the preference structure, dictating how utilities correlate across goals. While scale is directly specifiable, we detail the remaining axes below.

Property: Correlation of Goal Utilities.

The goal-utility matrix is generated with dependence on the hyperparameter
d
, which represents the number of hidden latent factors driving preferences. We sample a
d
-dimensional latent vector for every goal
g
,
v_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for every player
p
,
u_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, from a Gaussian distribution and calculate goal-utility values like this:
{G}_{g,p}=v_{g}^{\top}u_{p}\;+\;\epsilon_{g,p}\;
, where
\epsilon_{g,p}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
adds random noise. We rescale the matrix
{G}
to a target integer range
[g_{\min},g_{\max}]
and round to integers; in our main experiments we use
g_{\min}=-30
and
g_{\max}=30
.

The degree of utility correlation across goals is directly controlled by the number of latent factors
d
: a smaller
d
induces stronger shared structure across goals (utilities lie in a lower-dimensional subspace, increasing correlations), while a larger
d
allows preferences to depend on many independent factors, yielding more uncorrelated goal utilities.

Property: More Cooperative vs More Adversarial Games.

To control how aligned player’s preferences are, we vary the distribution of player vectors
u_{p}
. If the game is specified to be more cooperative, we sample
u_{p}
from a Gaussian with positive mean (biasing utilities toward similar signs across players). In more adversarial games, we sample with mean zero, producing more mixed signs and hence more frequent preference conflicts.

Property: Payoff Structure.

We control whether a game’s utilities are balanced, positive-dominated, or negative-dominated by shifting the bounds of the target utility range
[g_{\min},g_{\max}]
(we use
[-30,30]
for balanced,
[-8,30]
for positive-dominated, and
[-30,8]
for negative-dominated).

Property: Linear vs All-or-Nothing Goals.

The generator specifies the fraction of all-or-nothing goals, which are assigned uniformly at random. This is a key difficulty axis: all-or-nothing goals demand long-horizon planning, which may be forcing agents to accept short-term tradeoffs for delayed payoffs. Conversely, linear goals offer more straightforward, incremental reward signals with each commitment.

Property: Complexity of Goals.

One can also specify the hyperparameter
\alpha
, which specifies how many commitments are generally required for a goal. We use a Zeta distribution to sample the number of commitments per goal,
c_{g}\sim\text{Zeta}(\alpha)
, where
\alpha
controls the heaviness of the tails, leading to more commitments needed per goal. We upper-bound
c_{g}
so that it makes sense given the other parameters of the game. We also ensure that
c_{g}\geq 1
for linear goals and
c_{g}\geq 2
for all-or-nothing goals (as they would otherwise be trivial). We then sample
c_{g}
distinct
(n,j)
player-commitment indices uniformly (without replacement) from the set of all possible indices to form the set
\mathcal{C}_{g}
of required player-commitments for goal
g
.

Summary.

Overall, our generator produces diverse sequential negotiation games by sampling player-goal utilities and goal structure under a small set of interpretable hyperparameters yielding benchmark instances that span a wide range of strategic difficulty.

3.2
Gameplay Assumptions and Dynamics

Information Structure (Our Approach).

We assume perfect information: all players know everyone’s goal preferences (with full access to
\mathbf{G}
). Players also observe the entire game state
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
at all times
t
and are aware of all goal requirements
\mathcal{C}_{g}
. Furthermore, we assume that negotiation is sequential and bilateral: at each turn, only a single pair of players negotiates and updates
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
, so the next state
\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}
is fully determined by the current pair’s decisions.

Information Structure (Alternative Approaches).

Because the underlying game is separable from the negotiation protocol, our game generator can also be used to study partial observability. Two examples include: (i) Players do not have access to the utilities of others; they have to estimate them throughout the negotiation. (ii) Simultaneous negotiation: multiple pairs negotiate in parallel, making
\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}
dependent on concurrent agreements and introducing strategic uncertainty about others’ simultaneous decisions. Other uncertainty mechanisms are also possible.

Negotiation Protocol (Our Approach).

In our approach, players are purely self-interested: at each decision point, player
p_{n}
aims to maximize their own terminal payoff
r_{n}=R_{p_{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{(T)})
. To enable this, we define a value function
V
that maps the current commitment state
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
to the vector of expected terminal payoffs for all players.
V
can be viewed as an oracle continuation value under our protocol, in practice we instantiate it with an approximation
\hat{V}
. Finding an effective
\hat{V}
is a core challenge that we discuss further in Sec.
4
.

Game Dynamics.
As mentioned, the game is turn-based. Each turn has a proposer
p_{n}
, decided via a round-robin order, and we ensure that each player proposes equally often, with the number of proposer turns per player set as a hyperparameter. Each turn proceeds in three phases: (i) the partner selection phase, where
p_{n}
selects a partner
p_{n^{\prime}}
; (ii) the offer selection phase, where
p_{n}
formulates their best offer to
p_{n^{\prime}}
; and (iii) the decision phase, where
p_{n^{\prime}}
either accepts or rejects
p_{n}
’s offer.

Partner Selection.
The proposer
p_{n}
seeks to select the partner
p_{n^{\prime}}
that maximizes their expected terminal payoff given the current state
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
. We use Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) with
\hat{V}
as a leaf evaluator for this selection, reducing the brittleness of greedy
\arg\max\hat{V}
choices (More details are available in the Appendix
C
). We treat offer construction and acceptance decisions as a downstream subroutine scored by
\hat{V}
.

Offer Selection and Decision Phase.

After selecting partner
p_{n^{\prime}}
, proposer
p_{n}
searches for a feasible joint commitment update
\Delta C_{n,n^{\prime}}^{(t)}=(\Delta C_{n}^{(t)},\Delta C_{n^{\prime}}^{(t)})
– where
\Delta C_{n}^{(t)}
indicates the new commitments of player
p_{n}
at turn
t
. Offers must be: (i)
binding
(flipping 0s to 1s only) and (ii) add at most
k
new commitments per player per turn, reflecting that real-world interactions typically only lead to a small number of agreements (according to domain experts). More formally, the proposer
p_{n}
and their partner
p_{n^{\prime}}
enter an ultimatum bargaining game where
p_{n}
solves:

\Delta C_{n,n^{\prime}}^{(t),\;\star}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\Delta C_{n,n^{\prime}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})}\;\;V_{p_{n}}\left(\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\oplus\Delta C_{n,n^{\prime}}^{(t)}\right)\quad\quad\text{s.t.}\quad V_{p_{n^{\prime}}}\left(\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\oplus\Delta C_{n,n^{\prime}}^{(t)}\right)\geq V_{p_{n^{\prime}}}\left(\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\right)

(1)

where
V
maps the commitment state
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
to the expected terminal payoff vector, assuming all players act optimally to maximize their individual payoffs in all subsequent turns,
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})
denotes the set of monotone (flipping 0s to 1s only) feasible updates satisfying
\lVert\Delta C_{n}^{(t)}\rVert_{0}\leq k
and
\lVert\Delta C_{n^{\prime}}^{(t)}\rVert_{0}\leq k
, where
\oplus
denotes
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
updated with the commitments specified by
\Delta C_{n,n^{\prime}}^{(t)}
. If a solution exists,
p_{n^{\prime}}
accepts (as their value weakly improves), yielding
\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{C}^{(t)}\oplus\Delta C_{n,n^{\prime}}^{(t),\;\star}
. If there is no solution – meaning that there is no offer that would be acceptable for the partner – the partner rejects and the game proceeds to the next turn:
\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
.

Negotiation Protocol (Alternative Approaches).

While our instantiation assumes self-interested play, the game formalization readily supports alternative dynamics. For example, players could be welfare-oriented (maximizing social welfare) or fairness-oriented (maximizing minimum payoff). In such variants, partner-selection, offer-construction, and acceptance rules would optimize the chosen criterion rather than individual rationality (
V_{p_{n}}
). For readers interested in these outcomes, Appendix
D
provides a solver that explicitly optimizes social welfare.

Summary.

Our benchmark is intentionally modular: the core game formalization easily pairs with varied information structures (perfect vs. partial observability; sequential vs. simultaneous) and negotiation dynamics (self-interested vs. welfare-/fairness-oriented). Combined with the generator’s control over structural properties, this yields a broad family of negotiation environments spanning diverse strategic regimes, enabling systematic algorithmic evaluation under varying behavioral and informational assumptions.

3.3
Baselines and Evaluations

Gameplay performance depends on both the negotiation protocol and the value approximations used to make decisions. As this work focuses on the game instances—rather than protocol design—we fix the protocol from Sec.
3.2
and evaluate different approximations
\hat{V}
within the same decision procedure (partner choice via MCTS; offer/acceptance via Eq.
1
). This controlled setup isolates the performance of each
\hat{V}
across diverse game regimes. We provide a “No Negotiation” reference baseline, evaluating methods using exact optimal play on small instances and pairwise comparisons relative to that baseline on large instances.

Baseline: No Negotiation.

From our conversations with domain experts, it has emerged that in real-world scenarios parties often fail to reach an agreement. To capture this, we include a "No Negotiation" baseline in which players do not exchange offers; instead, each player selects commitments unilaterally. Mathematically, starting from an initial empty state
\mathbf{C}^{(0)}
, the final agreement matrix
\mathbf{C}^{(T)}
is formed such that for every player
p_{n}
:
(\mathbf{C}_{n}^{(T)})=\arg\max_{C_{n}}R_{p_{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{(0)}\oplus C_{n})
. This baseline provides a simple reference point: effective negotiation should typically improve outcomes relative to unilateral action selection.

Evaluation: Exact Solution (Small Games).

We evaluate agents using terminal payoff vectors
r\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{P}}
, where
r_{n}
is the final payoff for player
p_{n}
. Let
\hat{r}
denote the payoff vector produced by a negotiation protocol dependent on some value function approximation
\hat{V}
. When the game is sufficiently small, we compute the optimal self-interested payoff vector
r^{\star}
under our fixed protocol via dynamic programming (Algorithm
2
) over the negotiation tree. We then quantify payoff deviation from optimal play using the
\ell_{1}
error:
\text{ L1 Error}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{P}}|r^{\star}_{n}-\hat{r}_{n}|.

Evaluation: Pairwise Comparison (Large Games).

For larger games where computing
r^{\star}
is computationally infeasible, we compare methods directly. For methods
A
and
B
with terminal payoff vectors
\hat{r}^{A}
and
\hat{r}^{B}
, we define per-player improvement as
\Delta_{n}^{A,B}=\hat{r}_{n}^{A}-\hat{r}_{n}^{B}
. We summarize comparisons by reporting the aggregate improvement
\sum_{n}\Delta_{n}^{A,B}
, which measures the change in total payoff under
A
relative to
B
.

Depending on the desiderata, one could also consider alternative summaries of
\Delta^{A,B}
: e.g., the minimum improvement
\min_{n}\Delta_{n}^{A,B}
to emphasize worst-case effects; inequality-sensitive summaries such as the variance of
\{\Delta_{n}^{A,B}\}_{n=1}^{N_{P}}
to emphasize how evenly gains are distributed; or the fraction of players improved
\frac{1}{N_{P}}\sum_{n}\mathbb{I}[\Delta_{n}^{A,B}>0]
.

4
Benchmark-Produced Insights on Negotiation Game Regimes

Having defined our benchmark – game formalization, possible protocols, and evaluation procedures – we now examine how game difficulty varies across the benchmark’s instance space, and how key structural properties shape these outcomes. Because calculating the exact value function is only tractable for small instances, we study the benchmark through a set of simple value-function approximations that instantiate different assumptions about how uncommitted parts of the game may evolve. These approximations act as intentionally biased “lenses” on the game: they induce distinct partner-selection, offer-selection, and acceptance behavior. We then use the generator to sweep key properties that induce qualitatively different negotiation regimes. We empirically map where each lens succeeds or fails, showing that no single approximation dominates across the benchmark and yielding a regime-level characterization that can guide future algorithm design.

4.1
Value Function Approximations

As described in Sec.
3.2
, our negotiation protocol relies on a value function
V
mapping the current commitment state
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
to the expected terminal payoff vector. While
V
can be computed via dynamic programming in small games (Algorithm
2
), this quickly becomes intractable as the game size grows. We therefore consider three simple approximations
\hat{V}
, each encoding a different assumption about how the rest of the game may unfold. In the following sections, we use these approximations both as decision heuristics and as diagnostic lenses in our regime sweeps.

Value Approximation 1: Myopic Reward.

This approximation ignores future negotiations, it evaluates a joint commitment only by its immediate impact on the payoff vector:
\hat{V}^{\text{reward}}(C^{t})\;=\;R(C^{t})
.
Intuitively, this means
\hat{V}^{\text{reward}}
tends to work best when progress is incremental – e.g., in games with mostly linear goals.

Value Approximation 2: Upper-bound (Optimistic Completion).

Given a player
p_{n}
, this approximation assumes that player
p_{n}
will secure all remaining rewards from
positively valued goals
:

\displaystyle\hat{V}^{\text{upper}}_{p_{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})=R_{p_{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})+\sum_{g:\mathbf{G}_{g,n}>0}\bigl(1-S_{g}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})\bigr)\mathbf{G}_{g,n}=\sum_{g:\mathbf{G}_{g,n}>0}\mathbf{G}_{g,n}+\sum_{g:\mathbf{G}_{g,n}<0}S_{g}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})\mathbf{G}_{g,n}

Accordingly, new commitments are not judged by how much more value they add – as they all feed into the constant
\sum_{g:\mathbf{G}_{g,n}>0}\mathbf{G}_{g,n}
– but by how much value they could take away:
\sum_{g:\mathbf{G}_{g,n}<0}S_{g}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})\mathbf{G}_{g,n}
. This results in the player being highly cautious about deals that increase the satisfaction of goals with negative effects.

Value Approximation 3: Lower-Bound with Credible Threats (Worst-Case Completion).

Given a player
p_{n}
, it assumes future players will complete goals that are negative for
p_{n}
. However, we restrict attention to credible threats
\mathcal{G}_{\textsc{CT}}(p_{n})
. These are goals that
p_{n}
values negatively but remain non-negative for every other required contributor. This filters out self-harming threats aimed at hurting
p_{n}
:

\hat{V}^{\text{lower}}_{p_{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})\;=\;R_{p_{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})\;+\;\sum\limits_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\textsc{CT}}(p_{n})}\bigl(1-S_{g}(\mathbf{C}^{(t)})\bigr)\,\mathbf{G}_{g,n}

where
\mathcal{G}_{\textsc{CT}}
is formally defined as:

\mathcal{G}_{\textsc{CT}}(p_{n})=\left\{g\in\mathcal{G}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{G}_{g,n}<0\text{ and }\mathbf{G}_{g,m}\geq 0\text{ for all }p_{m}\neq p_{n}\text{ with pending actions for }g\right\}

State valuations become driven primarily by positive progress and more tolerant of immediate negative exposure which can be useful for “investment” scenarios where short-term sacrifices are required to unlock long-term payoffs.

The Upper and Lower bounds account for future play by shifting baseline expectations to adjust evaluation strictness:
\hat{V}^{\text{upper}}
strictly penalizes negative exposure, whereas
\hat{V}^{\text{lower}}
is more tolerant of it and focuses instead on pursuing positive goals.

Poison pill example.
To illustrate why strictness matters, consider a
Poison-Pill
scenario between proposer
p_{n}
and partner
p_{n^{\prime}}
. There are two goals: a mutually beneficial “bait” requiring joint action from both players, and a “poison pill” benefiting
p_{n}
but hurting
p_{n^{\prime}}
requiring action from
p_{n^{\prime}}
only:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{g_{\text{bait}}}

\displaystyle=\{(p_{n},0),(p_{n^{\prime}},0)\},

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{G}_{g_{\text{bait}},p_{n}}

\displaystyle=30,\;\mathbf{G}_{g_{\text{bait}},p_{n^{\prime}}}=30

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{g_{\text{poison}}}

\displaystyle=\{(p_{n^{\prime}},1)\},

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{G}_{g_{\text{poison}},p_{n}}

\displaystyle=10,\;\mathbf{G}_{g_{\text{poison}},p_{n^{\prime}}}=-25.

If
p_{n}
proposes completing both goals simultaneously, the myopic reward heuristic accepts since the immediate net payoff is positive (
30-25=5>0
). This is suboptimal, as
p_{n^{\prime}}
could refuse now and secure the bait later without taking the poison since its beneficial for both player to make
g_{\text{bait}}
happens anyway (Both players get
+30
). The upper bound correctly avoids this trap: by assuming the
+30
from
g_{\text{bait}}
is already secured,
p_{n^{\prime}}
evaluates the deal solely on the
-25
penalty and rejects it. To capture this dynamic – which mirrors real negotiations where parties may accept an immediate concession to avert a larger downstream harm (e.g., p.60 in
Centre of Competence on Humanitarian Negotiation (2019) CCHN
) – we augment our generator with an additional parameter that deliberately injects these bilateral poison-pill scenarios. This addition allows us to systematically create environments that demand such strict deal evaluation commonly seen in real-world negotiations.

4.3
Understanding Why Different Approximations Perform Better Across Regimes

The regime-specific winners follow directly from the inductive bias each approximation imposes on deal evaluation. The Myopic Reward lens evaluates a commitment state by its immediate impact on the payoff vector. The Upper lens effectively assumes that all positively valued goals will be realized anyway, so additional commitments are judged mainly by the extra negative exposure they create. The Lower lens assumes that certain harmful outcomes are unavoidable, so it favors states that are robust to others completing negative goals. Consequently, it emphasizes securing positive progress wherever possible.

In
balanced
games, players face mixed-sign utilities across many goals, yielding the highest degree of strategic conflict; consequently, whether a commitment is “good” depends heavily on how future trades unfold. In this regime, being consistently optimistic (Upper) or consistently threat-focused (Lower) can be brittle: Upper may screen out incremental deals that are necessary stepping stones, while Lower may tolerate sacrifices that are not reliably compensated later. In contrast, Myopic Reward makes the fewest assumptions about how the remainder of the game will resolve; it therefore relies least on speculative completions, which is often safest when both upside and downside are plausible and neither clearly dominates. This intrinsic conflict is also consistent with the higher L1 errors we observe in small balanced instances, and these errors tend to increase further as all-or-nothing goals become more common, since non-linearity makes partial progress harder to evaluate.

In
negative-dominated
games, where avoiding penalties dominates outcomes, the Upper approximation lens performs well by behaving conservatively with respect to negative exposure. This advantage becomes most pronounced when we inject poison pill structures: offers that look locally attractive but trigger large downstream penalties. Upper’s strict filtering helps avoid these traps, whereas the myopic baseline is more likely to accept them based on immediate payoff signals.

In
positive-dominated games
, most goals yield positive utility to players, so negotiation is less about avoiding penalties and more about reliably completing beneficial goals under uncertainty about follow-through. The Lower lens performs well here, as it assumes that opponents will realize credible threats, and thus prioritizes states that remain safe under unfavorable continuations. It therefore emphasizes locking in positive progress wherever possible. This regime also shows lower L1 error on small games, consistent with reduced strategic conflict when most goals are positively valued.

4.4
Opportunities for Future Work

Our benchmark generates diverse, non-trivial games and reveals regime-dependent advantages of different value-approximation lenses, with no single lens dominating across the instance space. This suggests two research directions for robust negotiation agents: (i) learning substantially more accurate state-value estimates that generalize across regimes, and/or (ii) developing adaptive methods that infer the regime online and select evaluation strategies accordingly.

A second direction concerns the drivers of difficulty. The small-game sweeps indicate that the hardest instances are those with stronger mixed-motive conflict, and that difficulty increases as all-or-nothing goals become more prevalent. Both factors increase the strategic weight of intermediate commitments: partial progress is hard to value reliably under all-or-nothing goals, and stronger conflict means many commitments are not jointly beneficial, increasing path dependence because commitments can sharply constrain which future agreements remain feasible. Promising future work is therefore to design methods that reason strategically over long horizons under binding commitments with only terminal rewards.

Finally, our current protocol intentionally adopts simplifying assumptions – perfect information and sequential bilateral negotiation within a multi-party game – to isolate the effect of state valuation. A natural next step is to make the environment more challenging by introducing partial observability (e.g., unknown opponent utilities requiring inference during play) and richer multi-party dynamics (e.g., multiple negotiations occurring concurrently), where state evolution depends on several parallel commitment processes and agents must anticipate others’ likely agreements.

5
Conclusion

We introduce a benchmark for multi-party, sequential action-level negotiation with binding commitments and terminal-only rewards, motivated by real negotiation practice. The benchmark includes (i) a controllable game generator that sweeps key structural properties (scale, incentive alignment, payoff structure, goal complexity, and non-linearity), (ii) a reference turn-taking protocol with partner selection and offer/acceptance dynamics, and (iii) an evaluation suite that supports exact dynamic-programming evaluation on small games and comparative evaluation for larger games.

Using this setup, we studied three intentionally biased value-function “lenses” – myopic reward, optimistic upper bound, and pessimistic lower bound – and mapped where each succeeds or fails. Across both synthetic sweeps and document-grounded climate negotiation instances, performance is strongly regime-dependent: no single lens dominates, difficulty rises with mixed-motive conflict and with all-or-nothing goals, and different payoff structures favor different valuation strategies. Together, these results make the case for future negotiation agents that learn more accurate, robust state values and reason strategically over long horizons under binding commitments with terminal rewards only. Finally, the benchmark is intentionally modular, enabling straightforward extensions to alternative negotiation protocols and information structures (e.g., partial observability).

Nash Social Welfare:
Maximizes the product of all players’ rewards (
\prod y_{i}
, or equivalently
\sum\log y_{i}
), which encourages fairness and prevents one player from getting nothing.

We can find these global optimums by solving a Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP). We optimize over the decision variables
P_{i,a}\in\{0,1\}
(actions),
S_{j}\in[0,1]
(goal satisfaction), and
y_{i}\geq\delta
(player payoffs, bounded slightly above zero for the Nash log calculation). The formulation is:

Maximize:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\quad\textbf{(Utilitarian)}\quad\text{or}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log(y_{i})\quad\textbf{(Nash)}

Subject to:

\displaystyle y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{G}\mathbf{G}_{j,i}S_{j}\hskip 122.34692pt\text{(Player payoff sum)}

\displaystyle S_{j}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{j}|}\sum_{(i,a)\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}P_{i,a}\hskip 82.51282pt\text{(If }j\text{ is a linear goal)}

\displaystyle S_{j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\sum_{(i,a)\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}P_{i,a}=|\mathcal{C}_{j}|\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{(If }j\text{ is a all-or-nothing goal)}

Appendix E
Zero-Shot LLM Negotiatior

Baseline: LLM Zero-Shot Negotiation.

To test whether general-purpose language models can reason about these structured games without training, we include an LLM-based (
gpt-5-mini
) zero-shot negotiator. At each turn, the LLM is prompted via a structured API call that enforces a strict JSON output schema. To optimize for context caching and token efficiency, the input is partitioned into three distinct components:

•

System Prompt:
Instructs the LLM to act as an expert strategic negotiator with two explicit objectives: maximize the proposer’s expected terminal reward, and formulate a rational deal that the partner will accept (i.e., an offer at least as good as rejecting). It also defines the data schema and hard constraints (proposals must be binding
0\rightarrow 1
flips, and bounded to a maximum budget of
k=3
new commitments per player).

•

Static Context:
The immutable game properties, serialized consistently to leverage API prompt caching. This includes the game topology (number of players, round-robin order, available action counts) and the core matrices (the goal-value matrix
\mathbf{G}
and satisfaction masks
\mathcal{C}_{g}
).

•

Dynamic Context:
The turn-specific variables, including the current timestep
t
, the identity of the current proposer
p_{t}
, and the current commitment state
\mathbf{C}^{(t)}
.

For every turn, the LLM outputs a JSON object containing the partner choice
p_{n^{\prime}}
and the proposed joint action update
\Delta C_{n,n^{\prime}}^{(t)}
. To ensure evaluation stability during automated gameplay, the baseline incorporates an exponential backoff retry mechanism. Furthermore, a strict post-generation validation step intercepts structural hallucinations: if the model outputs an invalid partner ID, misaligned action vector lengths, or violates the commitment budget constraint, the system gracefully overrides the invalid components with zero-vectors (proposing no new commitments) and falls back to a random partner selection.
