Health status and lifestyle behaviours of older adults in a Hilly Municipality of Dhankuta, Nepal
Authors: Bhagawaty Kalikotay, D. Rajeswari, Kamala Uprety, Muna Shrestha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAgeing is a global concern, and the growing geriatric population in developing countries such as Nepal faces significant health and social challenges. This study aimed to assess the health status and lifestyle behaviours of older adults.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in 2023 with 275 older adults in Mahalaxmi Municipality, Dhankuta, Nepal, using non-probability convenience sampling. Structured interview schedules, based on WHO recommendations and the Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (ADLs), were used to collect data. Descriptive and Inferential statistical analyses were performed, followed by a binary multiple logistic regression model to assess statistically significant factors associated with chronic health problems and functional status in ADLs among older adults.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 73.12 ± 5.29 years; 50.9% were male, and 96.7% were unable to read and write. Nearly half of the older adults reported at least one chronic health problem (48.4%), and 45.0% mentioned moderate to severe functional impairment in ADL. The fitted model revealed that unmarried or widowed older adults had higher odds of chronic health problems (AOR:2.83, 95% CI:1.42–5.66, p = 0.003). Smoking was also associated with increased odds (AOR: 2.10, 95% CI: 1.03–4.30, p = 0.042), whereas engagement in light-to-moderate physical activity was associated with significantly lower odds of chronic health problems (AOR = 0.15, 95%CI:0.08–0.29, p < 0.001). Functional impairment in ADL was more likely among older adults aged ≥ 75 years (AOR = 2.09, 95% CI:1.07–4.08, p = 0.03) and was strongly associated with the presence of chronic health problems (AOR = 18.43, 95% CI: 9.64–35.24, p < 0.001).ConclusionThe findings indicate a high burden of self-reported chronic health problems and functional dependency among older adults in a hilly municipality of Nepal. Marital status, smoking, and physical activity were significantly associated with chronic illness, while age and chronic illness were significantly associated with ADL dependency. These results highlight the need for community-based geriatric screening and targeted behavioural interventions to improve health status among older adults.

Background

Globally, the population aged 60 and above is rising faster than ever, projected to double from 11% (605 million) in 2000 to 22% (2 billion) by 2050 [1]. This rapid demographic change raises the risk of deteriorating functional ability, loss of social roles and independence, and increased health and social care demands unless proactive, multisectoral schemes are implemented [2].

According to the 2021 national census, Nepal had approximately 3 million older adults aged 60 or older, representing 10.2% of the total population [3]. As people age, they may experience a decline in physical strength due to changes such as poor mobility, vision and hearing issues, digestive problems, memory loss, difficulties controlling certain bodily functions, and chronic conditions [4]. This process is influenced by environmental factors, lifestyle, diet, mindset, and genetics [5]. Studies show that older adults commonly face hearing and vision loss, joint pain, chronic diseases, depression, and dementia, often experiencing multiple conditions simultaneously [6].

The results of earlier research indicated that approximately 48.3% of older adults reported having chronic morbidities (hypertension, diabetes, circulatory problems, digestive problems, eye problems, musculoskeletal conditions). Despite that, 68.1% reported having trouble getting care, and only 51.1% had been to a hospital in the previous six months [7].In eastern Nepal, it was reported that 66.5% of the older adults had at least one of the five non-communicable chronic diseases (hypertension, osteoarthritis, chronic respiratory disease, diabetes, heart disease) [8].

In a study assessing Activities of daily living, 8.3% of older adults in Nepal had poor functional status, with the greatest dependence on activities such as climbing stairs (17.3%) and dressing (21.9%) [9]. However, only 28.9% of older adults in Kathmandu fulfilled the requirements for “successful ageing” (no significant illness or disability, high functioning, active engagement, and spiritual well-being [10]. Older adults in Nepal often struggle to access proper healthcare because government support and community services are limited, leaving them largely dependent on their families and facing financial difficulties [11].

Existing research in Nepal has mostly focused on urban centres, leaving a critical gap in understanding the health status and lifestyle behaviours of older adults in hilly semi-urban and rural municipalities, where approximately 83% of the population resides [11]. This geographic bias limits the generalizability of findings, as older adults in these underserved regions face distinct challenges related to accessibility, socioeconomic conditions, and lifestyle factors [8, 9].

Addressing this gap is crucial for informing local public health strategies and national health policies. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of chronic health conditions, functional status in ADL, and lifestyle behaviours among older adults in a hilly municipality of Nepal. It also examined the sociodemographic and lifestyle factors associated with both health status and functional status. This study further provides context-specific evidence from an under-researched setting, contributing to geographically representative evidence on older adults in Nepal.

Methods

Study design and setting

A community-based cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in Mahalaxmi Municipality, Dhankuta, Koshi Province, Nepal. The municipality is a hilly, semi-urban area with diverse ethnicities and religions, limited health facilities, and infrastructure, which may influence the health status and lifestyle behaviours of older adults. The municipality consists of nine wards, with a total population of 24,800, of whom 2,375 were aged 60 years and above [12]. Due to logistical feasibility, resource constraints, and accessibility challenges typical of hilly terrains, the study was confined to this municipality rather than covering all ecological regions of the province. This setting was selected using a convenience sampling approach to generate context-specific evidence in the under-researched hilly municipalities of Nepal.

Study population and sampling

The study population comprised older adults (aged ≥ 60 years) residing in Mahalaxmi Municipality during the data collection period. Age categories were further grouped during analysis (Young-old adult: 60–74 years, Middle-old adult: 75–84 years, oldest-old adult: ≥85years) to allow meaningful interpretation of age-related variations in health status and lifestyle behaviours, which are reported in the Results section.

Based on a previous study by Chalise and Rosenberg (2019) [13], which reported a 76.5% prevalence of physical health problems among older adults in Nepal, the sample size for this study was calculated uing the single-population proportion formula (Cochran’s formula, n = (Z² × p × q) / L² [14], , with a 95% confidence level (z = 1.96), to ensure adequate statistical power and precision. With an estimated prevalence (p) of 0.765, its complement (q = 1–p = 0.235), and a 5% margin of error (L = 0.05), the initial sample size was 276 older adults. Because the target population of older adults in the municipality was finite (N = 2,375), finite population correction (FPC) was applied: The adjusted sample size was 247 participants (n = n0 / [1 + (n0 – 1) / N]). After accounting for a 10% nonresponse rate, the final sample size was 275 older adults.

The inclusion criteria for the study were: all older adults, irrespective of sex, aged 60 years or older, residing in the Mahalaxmi municipality, able to communicate verbally during the interview, willing to participate, and available at the time of data collection. Older adults who were cognitively unable to respond and those unavailable during visits were excluded. A non- probability convenience sampling technique was adopted for the study. Three of the nine wards were selected using convenience sampling, based on geographic accessibility and the feasibility of data collection in hilly terrain (Fig. 1). Although the wards were selected for accessibility, efforts were made to include households from different geographic segments within each ward to improve variability and representation of older adults residing in the municipality. Convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of findings. Still, it is frequently employed in community-based research where populations are widely dispersed, sampling frames are unavailable, and resource and cost constraints necessitate a feasible approach [15, 16]. The study employed clear inclusion and exclusion criteria, ensured an adequate sample size, and transparently reported its limitations.

Data collection tools

Socio-demographic information

Data were collected using a set of self-developed Structured interview schedules. This section contained structured questions related to socio-demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, marital status, religion, ethnicity, education, and number of children), family and social characteristics (living with, separate sleeping room), management of expenses, involvement in family decision making, and satisfaction with family relations and involvement in social work.

Lifestyle behaviours of older adults

A structured interview schedule was developed to identify participants’ lifestyle behaviours, based on WHO recommendations for healthy ageing, including diet, physical activity, and sleep practices among older adults [17–19]. The interview schedule consisted of questions related to the dietary patterns of older adults (9 items), including the consumption of junk food, which was operationally defined as packaged snacks, fried foods, and sweets. Physical activity (4 items) was assessed based on engagement in light-to-moderate-intensity activities, such as walking, cleaning, farming, and gardening, for at least 150 min per week. Self-reporting current smoking status was assessed with a yes/no question about whether the participants currently smoke. Similarly, the alcohol consumption was measured as self-reporting current drinking alcohol status with yes/ no, over the past three months, respondents had at least one alcoholic drink. Sleeping habits (3 items) included sleep duration and patterns.

Health status of older adults

In this study, the health status of older adults was assessed based on participants’ self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions or perceived presence of chronic health problems without clinical verification, as well as their ability to perform Activities of Daily Living (ADLs).

Chronic health problems

Chronic health problems were assessed by using the researcher–developed structured interview schedule. This tool was specifically designed to gather self-reported chronic physical health conditions commonly affecting older adults. The tool comprised key components: type of chronic health problems (specific chronic physical health conditions they were diagnosed with, e.g., diabetes, hypertension, arthritis, cardiovascular diseases, etc.), medication use, and follow-up practices. The content validity of this tool was ensured through a literature review and expert consultation. However, formal psychometric testing was not conducted. In addition, chronic health conditions were based on self-report without clinical verification, which may have introduced recall error and misclassification bias, particularly for undiagnosed or asymptomatic conditions. These limitations were acknowledged.

Functional status in activities of daily living

The study used the Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (ADL), also known as the Katz ADL, to assess how well older adults performed basic daily tasks independently [20]. This tool focuses on six fundamental self-care tasks: showering, clothing, using the toilet, getting up and down from a bed or chair, staying clean, and feeding themselves. For each task, participants get 1 point for being independent and 0 points for being dependent. The score might range from 0 to 6. A score of 6 signifies that the older adults can do everything on their own, which is high function (independent). A score of 3 to 5 suggests they have moderate impairment in daily living activities, and a score of 2 or less means they have severe functional impairment(dependent). Cronbach’s alpha values for this instrument range from 0.82 to 0.94, test-retest reliability is 0.99, and content validity is 0.96 [21]. During pretesting, an additional question on washing clothes was explored to reflect local functional expectations. However, this item was analysed descriptively and not included in the Katz ADL score, thereby preserving the validity of the standardised six-item scale.

The questionnaire was prepared in English, translated into Nepali, and back-translated to ensure linguistic accuracy. Pretesting was conducted among 30 older adults (approximately 10%) in a community setting outside the study area. Necessary wording modifications were made in response to participant feedback to improve clarity and cultural appropriateness.

Ethical considerations

This research complied with the ethical standards set out in the Declaration of Helsinki. Before data collection, the Ethical Review Committee of the Institute of Medicine at Tribhuvan University in Nepal (Ref. no. 446(6–11) t2) granted ethical approval for the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits were clearly explained. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time without consequences. No financial incentive was provided. Participants were assured of the confidentiality of their responses throughout the study. The authors confirmed that no academic misconduct, such as plagiarism, data fabrication, falsification, or duplicate publication, occurred during this research.

Data collection procedures

After obtaining ethical approval, the researcher obtained formal permission from the municipal office. Data were collected by the researchers themselves, who were trained in research methodology (interview techniques, ethical considerations, and tool administration to ensure consistency) using face-to-face interviews conducted through door-to-door visits over 12 weeks (1/06/2023 to 31/08/2023). During door-to-door visits within selected wards, households were selected sequentially. If more than one older adult was present in a household, only one participant was selected to avoid intrahousehold clustering. In such cases, the eligible and available older adults at the time of data collection were recruited. They were informed that data collection would take approximately 15–20 min and that they could withdraw from participation at any time.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 20. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, mean and standard deviation) summarised sociodemographic characteristics, health status, and lifestyle behaviours. The chi-square test assessed associations between health status and functional status in activities of daily living (ADLs), as well as between selected sociodemographic variables and lifestyle behaviours, after confirming the assumption of expected cell counts. Selected variables were recategorised a priori (e.g., age < 75/≥75 years; marital status as married vs. unmarried/widowed) to improve analytical validity. Before performing multivariable logistic regression, multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance statistics. A tolerance value greater than 0.1 and a VIF value less than 10 were considered indicative of the absence of problematic multicollinearity. Influential outliers were assessed using Cook’s distance, with values less than 1 indicating no highly influential observations [22]. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with chronic health conditions and functional status in ADLs among older adults. Variables significant at p < 0.05 in bivariate analysis, along with theoretically relevant variables, were entered into multivariable logistic regression models. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Model adequacy and predictive performance were evaluated using the Omnibus test of model coefficients for overall model significance, the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, Nagelkerke R² to estimate the proportion of variance explained by the model, and overall classification accuracy. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Model adequacy and predictive performance were evaluated using the recommended logistic regression procedures [23].

Results

Most older adults were unable to read or write, reflecting limited access to formal education in rural Nepal in the early decades. Nearly half reported having six or more children (Table 1).

Before the multivariable logistic regression analysis, assumptions of multicollinearity and influential outliers were assessed, and no violations were observed. Binary logistic regression analysis revealed that marital status, smoking, and involvement in light to moderate intensity work were significantly associated with chronic health problems. Older adults who were unmarried or widowed and those who smoked had higher odds of chronic health problems. Discussion

This study assesses the health status, lifestyle behaviours, and functional ability of older adults residing in a semi-urban, hilly municipality in Nepal. The findings indicate a considerable burden of self-reported chronic health conditions and impairments in daily living activities among older adults. The results align with prior community-based research from Nepal and other South Asian nations, indicating elevated rates of chronic morbidity and functional impairments among older adults [8, 24–27]. The predominance of sensory impairments, musculoskeletal problems, and chronic diseases observed in this study reflects common ageing-related conditions influenced by physiological decline, lifelong physical labour, and limited access to specialised healthcare services in resource-constrained settings [13, 28].

The binary multivariable logistic regression analysis identified factors associated with these outcomes. Marital status, smoking, and physical activity were significantly associated with chronic health conditions. Older adults who were unmarried or widowed were more likely to report chronic illness, which may be explained by reduced social and emotional support, as well as differences in health-seeking behaviour [29]. Similarly, smoking was associated with a higher odds of chronic illness, consistent with established evidence linking tobacco use to non-communicable diseases [30]. Engagement in light-to-moderate physical activity was associated with lower odds of chronic illness, supporting existing literature highlighting the protective role of regular physical activity in promoting healthy ageing and preventing chronic disease [30, 31].

Advancing age and chronic health conditions were significantly associated with ADL impairment. These findings align with previous studies showing that increasing age and multimorbidity contribute to functional decline and reduced independence among older adults [26, 32]. The association between chronic illness and ADL dependency underscores the cumulative impact of long-term health conditions on functional capacity. However, a considerable proportion of older adults remained independent in basic daily activities, particularly in routine self-care, consistent with evidence that functional autonomy is often maintained in the early stages of ageing [33].

Physical activity, marital status, educational status, and the presence of chronic health conditions were associated with impairment in activities of daily living. Previous research has also emphasised the importance of physical activity in maintaining functional ability and delaying functional decline in later life [26, 27]. However, given the cross-sectional design, reverse causation cannot be excluded, as older adults with chronic illness may have lower physical activity levels. Residual confounding by unmeasured variables may also have influenced the observed associations.

Lifestyle behaviours observed in this study provide additional context for these findings. Most older adults remained physically active through daily household and agricultural activities, a pattern commonly reported in community-based studies of ageing populations [19, 33]. Adequate sleep at night was also commonly reported, suggesting that active daily routines may support better sleep. However, this differs from some global findings, in which sleep disturbances are more prevalent among older adults [24]. Despite these positive behaviours, the prevalence of risk factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption remains a concern, as these behaviours are widely documented in South Asian populations and are linked to adverse health outcomes [34].

Family and social support systems are central to the lives of older adults in this setting. Joint family living arrangements were common, with most participants residing with their children, reflecting traditional and cultural caregiving practices observed in many Asian contexts [35, 36]. Economic support was largely family-based, with government allowances serving a supplementary role. While these arrangements provide essential support, limited engagement in social activities suggests potential gaps in broader social participation, which may affect overall well-being. The availability of separate living spaces within shared households may help maintain privacy and dignity among older adults, as highlighted in previous studies [37, 38].

Overall, this study’s findings align with regional and global evidence on ageing and offer context-specific insights from a hilly, semi-urban area in Nepal. However, because of its cross-sectional design, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution, as causality cannot be established and reverse causation may be present.

Limitations

The study generated considerable findings; however, it had some limitations. The study was restricted to selected communities of the Mahalaxmi municipality, and a convenience sampling method may have introduced selection bias and limited the external validity of the findings. Survivor bias may also be present, as only older adults who were alive and available at the time of data collection could participate. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and reverse causation cannot be ruled out. Data were collected through a structured interview schedule, which may be subject to recall bias, self-reporting bias, and social desirability bias, particularly for behaviours such as smoking and alcohol use. Chronic health conditions were not clinically verified, which may have resulted in misclassification or underreporting, particularly for early or subclinical conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and cancer. Psychological health conditions (Dementia and depression) were not included due to concerns regarding the reliability of self-reported data and the need for specialised assessment tools. Although multivariable analysis was conducted, the depth of analysis may be limited by sample size and the number of available variables.

Recommendations

Based on these results, Preventive health strategies for older adults are essential to reduce impairment in activities of daily living. Integration of geriatric screening into primary healthcare services could be prioritised to enable early identification and management of chronic conditions and to detect early functional decline. Nutrition awareness programs may promote fruit intake, and community awareness on smoking and alcohol cessation can reduce long-term risks. Municipal and provincial health authorities could introduce targeted health programs for older adults, such as healthy-lifestyle awareness, including smoking and alcohol cessation, and health checkups through geriatric health camps and mobile clinics.

Future research may use probability-based sampling and larger, more diverse populations to enhance generalizability. Incorporating clinical assessments, validated mental health measures, and broader social and environmental factors would provide a more comprehensive understanding of ageing and its determinants. Future research may include clinical assessments and longitudinal studies to capture morbidity (physical and psychological) and ageing transitions. Intervention-based and comparative studies are needed to identify effective strategies for nutrition, physical activity, and psychosocial support in elderly care.

Conclusion

This study found a high prevalence of self-reported chronic health conditions and functional impairments among older adults. Multivariable analysis indicated that marital status, smoking, and physical activity were significantly associated with chronic health conditions. Age and the presence of chronic illness were associated with dependency of functional status in activities of daily living. These findings suggest the importance of addressing both behavioural and health-related factors in older populations. Strengthening community-based screening, promoting age-appropriate physical activity, and integrating functional assessment into primary healthcare services are essential. Context-specific interventions focusing on healthy lifestyle behaviours, including smoking and alcohol cessation, along with improved access to geriatric services, may support healthy ageing among older adults.
