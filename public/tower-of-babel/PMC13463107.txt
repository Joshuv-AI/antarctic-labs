Reimagining School Meals From a Systems Thinking Perspective: A Case Study of School Meal Supply Chain Collaboration in California
Authors: Amy DeLisio, Shannan Young, Alyssa McClelland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

To help ensure nutrition equity and food safety, federal and state laws and policies regulate child nutrition programs. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans inform daily school meal preparation with healthy eating patterns that include milk and dairy foods [1]. In 2024, the U.S. Department of Agriculture's (USDA) Food and Nutrition Administration released new school meal nutrition requirements for added sugars, sodium, grains and dairy that started in the 2025–2026 school year [2]. Also in 2024, the California School Food Safety Act authorized the ban of harmful food dyes in school meals [3]; numerous other requirements govern procurement, locally sourced foods and waste reduction.

While regulatory oversight helps advance nutrition security and standards, challenges remain for effectively meeting these guidelines across the supply chain. To objectively understand some challenges faced by stakeholders, including policy leaders, producers, processors, dietitians, foodservice professionals and student consumers, a systems thinking approach was employed to cooperatively identify problems and solutions beyond regulatory action. The aim was to catalyze system‐level improvements in school meals by examining the role of dairy as representative of broader food system challenges in California schools.

Reviewing a complex system like school meals requires a new way of looking at the problem. To fully assess ideas and pain points across the system, an alternative approach to capture different perspectives and assess solutions that support all interest holders was employed. Systemic thinking makes sense of a complex world by organizing it into concepts and components that allow for a better understanding of what is happening. Systematic reflection is a core practice within systems thinking because it provides the structured inquiry needed to test assumptions, surface mental models and examine how different actors experience the system. By using structured tools to interrogate beliefs, assumptions and stakeholder perspectives, systematic reflection enables practitioners to apply systems thinking in practice [4, 5].

The “What? So What? Now What?” framework [6, 7] is a practical reflective tool that emphasizes a cycle of understanding and action. The “What? So What? Now What?” framework was first introduced as a streamlined model of critical reflection by distilling reflective practice into three progressive questions that guide practitioners from descriptive awareness to interpretive meaning making to actionable change [7]. Each phase in the framework can be used in systematic thinking. During the “what” phase, a better understanding of the issue is gathered through facts and data. The “so what” phase is a time to make sense of that data. The “now what” phase focuses on making concrete steps for improvement or action [6, 8]. This framework was used with a diverse group of stakeholders across the school food system to guide current understanding and inspire actionable changes.

School meal stakeholders were systematically engaged throughout the project in advisory roles and by interview, survey and in‐person reflection to explore shared values and gain a better understanding for meaningful change to meet new school meal standards, support nutrition security, address food preferences and implement sustainability measures for local food purchasing and waste reduction. The complexity of the school meals system requires collaborative and innovative solutions and long‐term focus.

Methods

A multiphase descriptive case study (Figure 1) called Well‐Nourished, Brighter Futures: Dairy Dialogue was designed to examine challenges and opportunities for reimagining school meals using the “What? So What? Now What?” framework. This three‐stage framework for compiling insights and strategies and developing actionable steps provided a structure for stakeholder interviews and discussions on shaping inclusive and sustainable school meals with nutrient‐dense dairy foods to efficiently meet new regulatory standards and support scholar success. The case study draws on three qualitative data sources collected sequentially across the framework's phases: key informant interviews (the “what” phase, facilitated dialogues at in‐person stakeholder convening (the “so what” and “now what” phases) and pre‐post event surveys. The case is analyzing the collaborative process itself and how structured systems thinking supported diverse supply chain stakeholders in identifying problems and actionable opportunities. Reporting follows the Standard for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR). The complete SRQR is provided as a Supporting File 1.

Advisory Council

In July 2024, an advisory council of five members was established to provide different perspectives on the changing environment of school nutrition and help advise and guide the “what” phase—information gathering and advising. Two members were school foodservice directors and representatives of California School Nutrition Association, two were dairy processors and one was a California Fluid Milk Processors Board representative. The council met five times with additional opportunities for guidance through email regarding the development of the convening content, through January 2025.

Key Informant Interviews

Between August 20 and September 23, 2024, Dairy Council of California (DCC) worked with an independent research firm to conduct 19 key informant phone interviews to better understand the needs of school foodservice and school dairy processors considering new school meal standards.

Key informants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure inclusion of individuals with direct experience and expertise in dairy and school nutrition. Eligibility criteria included professional role, experience in either dairy processing or school nutrition and geographic location. Potential participants were identified through organizational networks and professional contacts and invited via email. Recruitment continued until the target sample size was achieved. Eleven informants were California school nutrition directors representing districts with a range in student population from 2,000 to 390,000 with free and reduced meal eligibility ranging from 30% to 90%. The sample included a mix of urban and rural districts across multiple regions of the state. The remaining eight informants were dairy industry stakeholders, including dairy processors serving schools, representatives from trade associations, members of DCC board and other sector thought leaders. Two semi‐structured interview guides were developed by DCC and Datalink Partners and reviewed by the advisory council (Supporting File 2). Semi‐structured interviews included 14 questions for school nutrition directors organized in six sections: general background, current practices, challenges and opportunities, additional insights, external support and closing. Dairy community interviews included 13 questions in six sections: general background, product supply, challenges, understanding federal school meal regulations, external support and closing. Interviews were conducted via an audio platform (i.e., Teams) by a trained interviewer from Datalink Partners that lasted approximately 30–45 min and were audio recorded and transcribed for analysis.

Stakeholder Convening

A 1‐day convening was organized that reflected emerging themes from the interviews, input by strategic designer and recommendations from the advisory council. The convening included three presentations, four themed dialogues and a strategic prioritization session.

In addition, the advisory council and strategic designer identified student voice as a key contributing factor in strengthening school nutrition efforts. To ensure this perspective was fully represented, three National Youth Council members from California were invited to actively participate and contribute alongside school nutrition professionals.

Forty California school meal supply chain stakeholders from school meal programs, the dairy community, state nutrition and agriculture leadership and youth leaders were invited to the convening, held in Sacramento, California, on January 6, 2025. To add context from the interviews, content experts delivered three presentations on key topics at the convening. A university nutrition faculty member presented Food is Medicine: The Dairy Matrix and Key Life Stages. Members of a National Youth Council led a session titled Uplifting and Integrating Youth Voice. A panel discussion addressed challenges facing both schools and the dairy industry, featuring perspectives from a dairy farmer, a school nutrition director, a trade association representative and a dairy sales representative.

An online pre‐event survey was completed as part of the registration process. All participants answered three short questions prior to the event to assess what they hoped to learn or gain, what they expected to contribute or share and the key questions they wanted answered during the day. A post‐event reflection was shared at the conclusion of the convening via QR code, included in the immediate follow‐up email and re‐sent in a personal email reminder 1 week post event. During the convening, facilitators captured dialogue and strategic planning outputs on structured worksheets and flip charts, which were photographed and transcribed after the event.

Data Analysis

Interview data was analyzed using a framework matrix approach. One analyst at Datalink Partners charted each informant's responses, including verbatim quotes, into a structured matrix organized by the interview guide. A DCC team member reviewed the charted matrix data against the source data for accuracy and discussed changes in interpretation with the first analyst. Domain summaries were then compared across informants and between the two stakeholder groups to identify convergent and divergent perspectives and cross‐cutting, systems level challenges. The synthesized findings were reviewed with the advisory council and informed the design of the four themes and the convening dialogue. Outputs of the convening dialogues and strategic planning session were synthesized by the facilitation team (CustomEd and DCC team members) and shared back with participants in plenary session for confirmation before being consolidated into three priority action areas reported in the results. Open‐ended survey responses were reviewed by DCC team and CustomEd.

Rigor and Trustworthiness

Several strategies supported the trustworthiness of findings. Credibility was supported by triangulation across data sources (interviews, facilitated dialogue and surveys) and across stakeholder groups. Convening participants heard and confirmed group syntheses in plenary sessions. Post‐event surveys provided a further check on whether the priorities reflected the participants' experiences. Dependability was supported using consistent semi‐structured interview guides, a documented analysis matrix, facilitator worksheets and exported electronic survey data. Confirmability was supported by independent data collection and external facilitation. Transferability is supported in two ways. First, description of the California policy and supply chain context allows readers to judge how specific findings apply to their own settings. Secondly, the engagement process is documented in sufficient detail to allow others to adapt the dialogue framework.

Reflexivity

This project was created, funded and coordinated by DCC, a nutrition education organization funded by California producers and processors, with grant funding from the California Cattle Council. The authors are registered dietitians and program leaders at the organization; we recognize that our organizational position is why we focused on the dairy food group as a component of school meals and could predispose the project towards more favorable interpretations of dairy foods. To mitigate this, interviews were conducted and charted by an independent researcher, the convening was facilitated by an external design team and the advisory council included school foodservice representatives. Critical perspectives were reported alongside favorable ones, including informants who questioned if dairy required a larger place in school meals.

Ethical Considerations

Institutional Review Board approval was not sought because the project constituted program quality improvement and stakeholder engagement rather than generalizable human subjects research, which we acknowledge as a limitation. All interview participants received a written project description and provided informed verbal consent, including consent to use de‐identified quotations; convening participants consented at registration to participation and to reporting of aggregated, de‐identified outputs. The three youth participants provided consent with parental permission and participated only in convening sessions. Data was de‐identified, stored on password‐protected organizational systems accessible only to the project team and contracted partners, and recordings were destroyed after analysis.

Results

What: Gathering the Facts

Key informant interviews mapped challenges across procurement, nutrition and policy compliance. Interviews provided the following summary of insights, presented by interview section. The first one was economics. School nutrition directors describe reimbursement rates that no longer cover high labor and food costs. While processors describe thin margins on school milk and substantially higher costs for organic, lactose‐free and shelf stable products, showing financial strain on both ends of the supply chain. Secondly, infrastructure and logistics constraints include aging kitchens, limited cold storage and staffing shortages on the school side mirrored by truck, driver and equipment constraints including difficulty scheduling deliveries before the bell and during school breaks on the processor side. Thirdly, regulatory complexity including strict nutrition standards, meal accommodation requirements, packaging and recycling legislation, fleet emission compliance and burdensome district bidding processes experienced as fragmented demands without coordination across the system creating confusion and added responsibility on both sides. The fourth theme identified was students' preference and waste. Informants in both groups linked milk waste directly to product palatability and choice, with divergent views on flavored milk and describing growing student demand for lactose‐free, plant‐based and culturally relevant options. Lastly, education and workforce theme was identified. Both groups recognized gaps in culinary skills training, nutrition education and food literacy and understanding where food on the tray comes from. Perspectives were not uniform, some directors questioned whether dairy requires a larger place in school meals at all. Detailed insights are summarized below by stakeholder group and interview section.

School Nutrition Director Interviews

Shifts with school meal programs include movement toward more scratch‐cooked, fresh and organic meals. However, there are challenges due to high costs, outdated facilities and labor shortages. School districts employ diverse strategies to source and incorporate dairy into school meals. About half of the districts use central kitchens to prepare meals. Dairy procurement involves both direct purchases from dairies and third‐party suppliers, with some districts focusing on sustainable and community‐supportive purchasing policies. These efforts underline a commitment to quality, community engagement and sustainable practices in school meal programs.

School districts experience a range of challenges related to incorporating dairy in their meal programs, from dietary requirements and costs to student preferences, dietary restrictions and regulatory hurdles. Ensuring that sodium maximums are not exceeded is a challenge for districts. Despite challenges, districts find opportunities and success in reducing milk waste and enhancing consumption by offering flavored milk options. Students favor cheese‐based meals like nachos, tacos and pizza, as well as yogurt parfaits, overnight oats, smoothies and handheld items.

Schools are focused on different goals in the 2025–2026 academic year, including increased participation and engagement with the school meal program and shifting to scratch cooking and reducing additives. To achieve these goals, districts would like to see external supports such as higher reimbursement rates to cover rising labor and food costs, unrestricted infrastructure funds for kitchen updates, professional development for staff and support for sustainability initiatives like local sourcing, farm‐to‐school connections and food waste management programs.

There is also a desire for educational programs that connect students with farms to show where milk comes from. Districts seek less bureaucracy, with advocacy needed to simplify regulations. Respondents emphasized the need for education targeting both school foodservice professionals and students to support informed decision‐making and improve dairy integration in school meals. They also recommend prioritizing education on current dairy science and dairy's role in nutrition at professional conferences to strengthen understanding among stakeholders.

Dairy Community Interviews

The new requirement for reducing waste lacks infrastructure and investment; however, opportunities include sustainability and product innovation.

Packaging concerns have shifted from shortages to ensuring milk cartons are recyclable. Cost is a major hurdle, particularly for organic, bulk and shelf‐stable milk products. Logistical challenges include limited storage space, delivery schedules with schools and a lack of education about dairy's nutritional role. Regulatory challenges include adapting to sugar and sodium reduction rules and complying with California's recycling laws. Nutritional regulations, especially restrictions on fats and sugars, limit options like whole milk, which some believe would boost student consumption.

Most of the dairy community interviewees are familiar with USDA's new regulations, with many already exceeding standards. Few adjustments were needed, though it was noted that some districts have been phasing out chocolate milk due to costs, and other interviewees advocated for whole milk. Processors stay updated through industry groups and regulatory websites. In the next 5 years, trends like shelf‐stable and lactose‐free milk, cleaner labels and organic options are expected to grow.

Suggestions for improving partnership between schools and dairy industry include better public education about school nutrition challenges, simplifying the bidding process, promoting dairy through storytelling, expanding lactose‐free and other dairy options and fostering greater collaboration to ensure dairy remains a key part of school meals. Most processors find value in dialogues to advocate for dairy, learn from other stakeholders and collaborate on solutions. However, there is a call for these discussions to be inclusive and forward‐looking, ensuring that all voices are heard.

So What: Making Meaning Together

Looking again at the reflective framework, the “so what” phase, which took place at the convening, asked for new understandings from the stakeholders resulting from the facts and insights gathered. The convening shifted to the task of identifying opportunities for school meal system improvements through interactive activities and focused dialogues. Activities included systems thinking facilitated dialogue, broken into four themes (Figure 1). With these activities as the foundation, participants generated the following insights within each dialogue theme.

Dairy Innovation Dialogue: Collaborate with school nutrition operators to offer more culturally responsive meals. Support scholar success by increasing youth access to nutrient‐dense dairy through more diverse offerings such as lactose‐free or flavored milk.

Future of School Meals Dialogue: Center evidence‐based nutrition in all discussions around school meal policy. Activate local communities and partnerships to tailor school meals to community‐specific needs. Support students' desire to help market the school cafeteria by giving them co‐ownership in what foods are offered and inviting them to raise awareness with their peers. Increase sustainability and reduce waste in all aspects of school meals—from packaging to leftover food.

Nutrition Education Dialogue: Develop strategies to increase students' food literacy, including dairy nutrition for growth and development. Ensure students are learning about where and how their food is being grown, raised and processed. Identify and expand educational opportunities inside and outside of the cafeteria setting.

Professional Development and Training Dialogue: Increase opportunities for evidence‐based staff training of school meal personnel and educators. Support school meal services with best practices to involve students in school meals. Provide educators with informative, evidence‐based materials to teach nutrition, including dairy nutrition, to their students.

Now What: Strategic Plan for Action

The reflective framework's “now what” phase explored the effects of collaborative problem‐solving in identifying ways to meet new school meal standards, support nutrition security, address food preferences and implement sustainable options. Participants broke into curated groups representing students, school nutrition professionals and dairy‐sector partners to synthesize their learning and formulate actionable next steps. Each group then shared its insights with the larger convening to support broader collective alignment. Through this process, participants shaped their learnings into three next steps:

Redefine what nutrition education means in schools by prioritizing evidence‐based nutrition, engaging youth in their own learning and emphasizing farm‐to‐cafeteria supply chains.

Nineteen post‐event surveys were completed, reflecting 48% of participants. Post‐event surveys captured participant feedback and specifically highlighted learning new strategies to incorporate dairy foods into school meals. Survey results consistently emphasized the need for child nutrition advocates statewide to champion student success through increased access to nutrient‐dense school meals, specifically highlighting the inclusion of milk and dairy products.

Discussion

While regulatory oversight provides structural boundaries for school meals, and nutritional standards elevate food quality [9], this dairy supply chain collaboration case study suggests that the school meal system can also benefit from systems thinking dialogues for better understanding of facts, needs and opportunities across the supply chain.

Other studies have looked at benefits of school meal collaboration from different perspectives. A Focus Midwest project from 2012 to 2015 determined procurement benefits when school food professionals from different school districts collaborated to improve purchasing power for chicken, turkey, legumes and produce [10]. A review of articles published prior to February 2017 looked at chef collaboration and other interventions to improve food palatability in school meal studies [11].

The Well‐Nourished, Brighter Futures: Dairy Dialogue is the first case study that engages California dairy processors, school nutrition professionals, students and other thought leaders in structured systems thinking to discuss the complexities of providing milk and dairy foods as part of the school meal system. Findings have implications for prioritizing youth involvement, farm‐to‐cafeteria nutrition education and local partnerships.

Interviews and facilitated discussions used in this project on collaboration offer a possible blueprint for examining other food groups and institutional food systems in the future. Reflections on the convening made clear the value in bringing together thought leaders from a wide variety of backgrounds, with 100% of post‐event survey respondents expressing their confidence that the information they learned and the connections they made would continue to benefit them.

Strengths and Limitations

This case study has several strengths. It is, to our knowledge, the first structured systems thinking process to convene the California school dairy supply chain to discuss school meal improvement. Additional strengths include triangulation across interviews, facilitated dialogues and surveys, the deliberate inclusion of youth voice and the use of an accessible reflective framework that participants could carry into their own organizations. This makes it a practical, replicable design of engagement framework that others can use.

Several limitations also impact interpretations of the results. First, the project was funded and convened by DCC, an organization funded by the dairy sector, which created a risk of sponsorship bias toward dairy favorability. Safeguards included independent third‐party interviewing and charting, external professional facilitation, an advisory council that included school foodservice leaders, and the reporting of critical perspectives. Second, the case is situated in California, which has a unique policy environment. California has universal school meals, comparatively stringent state nutrition, food‐additive, packaging and organic‐waste legislation and unusually large and diverse districts. Third, the purposive sample was small, convening participants were invited rather than randomly selected, and the post‐event survey response was 48%, so the views reported may not represent all supply chain partners. Fourth, analysis used framework matrix charting rather than full transcript coding with multiple independent coders, and the What, So What, Now What framework, while accessible, is less comprehensive than formal systems mapping methods. Finally, outcomes reported here are agreed intentions rather than implemented changes; a follow‐up cycle of implementation, evaluation and reflection is needed to understand impacts on school meals.

Conclusion

This case study demonstrates how structured systems thinking can move a fragmented school food supply chain from isolated frustrations toward shared understanding and coordinated action. The What, So What, Now What framework assisted this process in three concrete ways [12]. First, it gave stakeholders with very different points of views a common structure for examining the same system, revealing interdependencies such as the chain linking student taste preferences, food waste, nutrition standards, processor margins and packaging legislation that no single partner could see or solve alone. Second, the facilitated reflection surfaced and tested participants' assumptions and mental models, allowing divergent views (e.g., on flavored milk and bulk dispensing) to be examined openly rather than negotiated away. Third, the framework's action orientation converted shared understanding into three cross‐sector commitments redefining school nutrition education, broadening culturally relevant dairy offerings and strengthening food system partnerships. Each commitment had identified roles for schools, industry, students, educators and policymakers. Everyone in the school meal supply chain has a role in shaping more relevant, nourishing and sustainable school meals; this case suggests that inclusive, structured reflection offers a replicable blueprint for reimaging school meals and institutional food systems.
