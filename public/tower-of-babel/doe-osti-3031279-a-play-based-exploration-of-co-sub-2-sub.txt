EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report documents the workflow developed and applied for a play-based exploration (PBE)
of carbon capture and storage (CCS) potential in the Illinois Basin, conducted under U.S.
Department of Energy Cooperative Agreement DE-FE0032366. The project was led by the
Illinois State Geological Survey (ISGS) at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign in
collaboration with Visage Energy.
The goal of this workflow is to identify locations where subsurface, surface, and societal
conditions align to support safe and effective geologic CO₂ storage. To achieve this, the project
team developed a systematic, seven-step workflow for conducting play-based exploration: (1)
defining play elements, (2) collecting data, (3) constructing a geodatabase, (4) defining
suitability criteria, (5) conducting suitability mapping, (6) generating Common Risk Segment
(CRS) maps, and (7) creating composite play maps. The workflow is designed to be repeatable
and adaptable for CCS assessments in other regions or for alternative subsurface energy
applications such as hydrogen or natural gas storage.
The workflow was applied to Illinois as a case study, evaluating stacked Cambro-Ordovician
reservoir formations including the Mt. Simon Sandstone and St. Peter Sandstone. The project
team assembled an integrated geodatabase containing more than 20 play elements organized into
three categories: subsurface factors (depth, thickness, salinity, seal geometry, faults, seismic
hazard, legacy well penetrations, and saline springs), surface factors (land cover, wetlands, karst
terrain, mines, transportation infrastructure, and CO₂ point sources), and societal factors (city
limits, parks, historic places, federal and tribal lands, population density, and community water
supply wells). Each play element was evaluated against suitability criteria informed by
regulatory guidance, scientific literature, and expert-led discussion, and reclassified on a
standardized scale ranging from Less Suitable to Most Suitable, with a fifth category of More
Information Needed assigned to areas with incomplete data coverage.
Reclassified play elements were combined into CRS maps using priority logic implemented
through a custom Python script (see Appendix) wrapped in an ArcGIS toolbox, ensuring that
areas marked as unsuitable in any individual layer remained unsuitable in the composite output.
CRS maps were then integrated across categories to produce composite play maps that illustrate
the relative suitability for CCS project development across the state. A blue-to-yellow color
palette was adopted for all suitability maps to promote clarity, neutrality, and accessibility.
This report presents the generalized PBE workflow and details its application to the Illinois
Basin. It describes each step of the process from raw data to final composite suitability maps,
providing sufficient documentation for the methodology to be reproduced or adapted. The
flexible framework established through this project offers a scalable tool for regional CCS site
screening and broader subsurface resource management.

INTRODUCTION
A Play-Based Exploration of CCS Potential of the Illinois Basin is characterized as data analysis
to build a database using existing subsurface, surface, and societal data for entities screening
areas of Illinois for commercial geologic CO2 storage. Each play represents a combination of
geological settings, land characteristics, and community factors that influence the viability of
underground CO₂ storage. This framework allows different types of data to be evaluated in a
consistent manner so regions can be compared objectively. By identifying where favorable
geologic properties align with accessible land and supportive societal conditions, play-based
exploration helps guide early-stage CCS planning and reduces uncertainty before site
characterization or permitting.
This report represents a generalized workflow for conducting a play-based exploration exercise.
The workflow describes how to build an integrated geodatabase, define play elements, establish
suitability criteria, and generate composite maps such as Common Risk Segment (CRS) maps
(Figure 1). It outlines the steps required to process raw data, reclassify, and evaluate play
elements, and combine them into maps that show areas of greater or less suitability. The
workflow is designed to be repeatable and adaptable so it can be used for CCS assessments in
different regions or extended to other subsurface energy applications.
Illinois is used as a case study to demonstrate how the play-based exploration workflow
functions when applied to an actual region. The Illinois Basin provides a suitable setting because
it contains multiple potential storage formations and a range of surface and societal conditions.
By applying the workflow to Illinois, the report shows how the database, suitability criteria, and
mapping steps translate into real-world results.

PLAY-BASED EXPLORATION WORKFLOW FOR CCS SITE
SCREENING
1. Define Play Elements
This step identifies the geospatial variables used in the analysis and specifies how each will be
evaluated.
 • Determine which subsurface, surface, and societal variables influence carbon storage
 feasibility.
 • Group related variables into play elements, so each factor is evaluated in a consistent
 framework.
 • Document the rationale for including each play element to ensure repeatability.
 • Specify how each variable will be represented spatially to prepare for later processing.

2. Collect Data
This step gathers all datasets needed to represent the defined play elements, so the analysis has
complete and credible inputs.
 • Identify data sources for geological, environmental, land-use, and societal information.
 • Acquire raw datasets in formats suitable for GIS and record their metadata.

 • Screen datasets for completeness and quality to ensure they meet project needs.
 • Store raw inputs in a structured workspace for controlled processing.

3. Construct Geodatabase
This step organizes the collected datasets into a centralized geodatabase that supports efficient
processing and ensures consistency across analyses.
 • Create a geodatabase structure with feature datasets for subsurface, surface, and societal
 categories.
 • Import datasets and standardize naming conventions and coordinate systems
 • Maintain metadata documenting sources, preprocessing steps, and version control.
 • Validate geometry, check topology, and ensure all datasets are aligned for analysis.

4. Define Suitability Criteria
This step establishes the rules for how each play element will be evaluated so raw datasets can be
converted into comparable suitability classes.
 • Develop thresholds or classification rules supported by scientific literature and regulatory
 guidance.
 • Set suitability scales that represent favorability for CCS (for example, 0 to 3).
 • Document criteria so evaluations are consistent and defensible.

5. Conduct Suitability Mapping
This step reclassifies each dataset according to the defined criteria to create standardized
suitability layers for all play elements.
 • Within ArcGIS Pro, reclassify values into numerical suitability categories.
 • Project, align, and resample rasters to the shared extent, projection, and resolution.
 • Apply consistent environment settings (snap raster, cell size, processing extent).
 • Output a standardized raster for each play element that can be used in composite
 mapping.

6. Generate CRS Maps
This step combines the reclassified suitability layers within each play element category to
produce Common Risk Segment maps that show the integrated influence of multiple related
factors.
 • Overlay reclassified rasters within a category using priority logic.
 • Ensure lowest values in a cell override other classes where required.
 • Inspect outputs for gaps, inconsistencies, or areas where data is missing.
 • Produce CRS maps that reflect the combined influence of multiple related play elements.

7. Create Composite Play Maps
This step integrates CRS maps across subsurface, surface, and societal categories to develop a
composite view of CCS suitability.
 • Reclassify the output into interpretive categories (for example, Less Suitable, Suitable,
 More Suitable, Most Suitable, and More Information Needed).
 • Apply standardized symbology so support comparisons across the region and play
 elements.
 • Prepare composite maps for communication, decision-making, and further site
 evaluation.

This step established Illinois-specific thresholds, buffers, and rules for converting raw datasets

DATA PREPARATION
All datasets were first downloaded from their original sources, saved to the project Box
directory, and then imported into the geodatabase. Each layer was renamed using a standardized
convention so that the play element, category, and data type were clear. Datasets that extended
beyond Illinois were clipped to the Illinois state boundary. Datasets in a different spatial
reference were reprojected to NAD 1983 UTM Zone 16N. Attribute fields were cleaned by
removing null or erroneous records and standardizing field names and units so layers could be
compared and combined. For datasets that did not fully cover Illinois but contained sufficient
surrounding data, we used kriging to interpolate continuous rasters for analysis. For geologic
rasters such as seal geometry, thickness, and depth where no additional data existed outside the
mapped area, we did not interpolate and assigned NODATA to areas beyond the available
coverage. The only extrapolation applied in this workflow was distance accumulation from point
or line features, which was calculated using the Illinois state boundary as the processing extent.

GEODATABASE
The geodatabase serves as the central framework for organizing, storing, and managing the
spatial data used in the suitability workflow. It ensures that all datasets are consistently
formatted, easily accessible, and ready for spatial analysis. To support efficient process and
interpretation, the geodatabase is structured around the three primary categories of play
elements: subsurface, surface, and societal.
Each dataset is stored as a feature class or raster layer with standardized attributes and coordinate
systems. Metadata is maintained in a Box folder for each layer to document its source, data, and
any preprocessing steps performed. Before use in the suitability model, each dataset within the
geodatabase is reviewed and if needed processed to:
 • Clip to the Illinois area boundary.
 • Remove null or erroneous values.
 • Normalize attributes for comparison across layers by converting raw values into
 consistent units. For distance-based datasets, this included converting all distances to
 miles before applying suitability criteria. This ensured that layers using different original
 units could be evaluated on the same scale during CRS generation and composite
 mapping.
 • Reclassify values based on suitability criteria.

SUITABILITY MAPPING
A Python script was developed to automate the process of creating composite suitability maps by
combining multiple reclassified raster layers, each representing a different suitability factor such
as faults, proximity to wells, or seal thickness. Each input raster is reclassified to a common scale
(e.g., 0 to 3) to reflect varying levels of suitability, with higher values indicating more favorable
conditions for carbon storage (Table 2). The script then merges these layers using priority logic
to generate a final composite raster that highlights the most suitable areas for CCS site selection.

The script merges these standardized rasters using the required priority logic so pixels with a
value of 0 always override other suitability classes. This priority system ensures that areas
marked unsuitable in any input layer remain unsuitable in the final composite. The script handles
these rules automatically, so composite CRS maps remain consistent across subsurface, surface,
and societal play elements.
The script is developed to read directly from the project geodatabase. Analysts can select all
reclassified rasters associated with a play element at once because the tool accepts multi-value
inputs. This reduces manual work and lowers the risk of errors that can occur when processing
layers individually. The tool is wrapped in a custom ArcGIS toolbox so users can run it through a
simple interface without editing the code.

RECLASSIFICATION PROCESS
Each play element was reclassified using the Reclassify tool in ArcGIS Pro based on criteria
defined by subject matter experts and reviewed literature. The classification thresholds reflect
geologic, engineering, or social conditions that influence the feasibility of CCS:
 • Less suitable: Locations that fail to meet minimum requirements or have major
 constraints, such as shallow depth or incompatible land use.
 • Suitable: Areas that meet minimal thresholds but have notable limitations.
 • More Suitable: Areas that meet most conditions and present fewer risks.
 • Most Suitable: Areas that meet optimal conditions for storage, infrastructure, or
 community acceptance.
These categories replace the numeric 0–3 scale and help communicate results to nontechnical
audiences. Areas labeled More Information Needed represent locations where input data
coverage is incomplete or uncertain. These areas are not treated as unsuitable. Instead, they
indicate where additional subsurface, surface, or societal information is required before making a
screening decision. By converting the composite output into these five classes, the final maps
present suitability in a form that is consistent, comparable across play elements, and easier to
interpret in public-facing materials.
Continuous datasets such as injection zone depth, salinity, and distance to infrastructure were
reclassified using numeric breakpoints. Categorical datasets such as land cover, protected areas,
or population density were reclassified by assigning values to each category. All rasters were
clipped to the Illinois play boundary and resampled to a uniform cell size, projection, and extent
to ensure alignment during map creation operation.

OUTPUTS AND INTERPRETATION
Each reclassified raster was symbolized with a four-class color ramp to show areas of higher and
lower suitability (Figure 2). The resulting maps reveal patterns of favorability across Illinois and
show how different criteria influence potential site locations. For example, subsurface maps
highlight formations with adequate depth and distances from faults for CO₂ storage.

Composite play maps were produced for the Cambro-Ordovician storage system, including maps
for the Mt. Simon Sandstone (Figure 3) and the St. Peter Sandstone (Figure 4). Each composite
map integrates the reclassified play elements relevant to that formation’s storage potential,
containment characteristics, and associated surface and societal conditions. The subsurface
composite for the Mt. Simon Sandstone, for example, combines storage formation thickness and
depth, Eau Claire Formation caprock thickness, formation water salinity, legacy well density,
mapped faults, seismic hazard, and proximity to saline springs. Separate surface and societal
composites were generated and can be viewed alongside or combined with the subsurface
composites to provide a comprehensive screening perspective.
A blue-to-yellow color gradient was applied to all suitability maps in place of a traditional red-
to-green scale. While a red-to-green scheme is often seen as intuitive, evoking a stoplight
metaphor, it can unintentionally introduce cognitive bias, with red perceived as dangerous or
negative. This framing is inappropriate for suitability assessments, where lower suitability does
not imply risk or negativity but simply lower favorability under the applied criteria. The blue-to-
yellow palette also serves as a perceptually uniform and colorblind-accessible alternative,
ensuring the maps are readable by the widest possible audience.
Areas classified as More Information Needed appear on the maps where input data coverage is
incomplete or absent. These areas are distinct from areas classified as Less Suitable: they are not
judged to be unfavorable, but rather cannot be evaluated with confidence given current data.
Identifying these zones is one of the practical benefits of the workflow, as it highlights where
targeted data collection could meaningfully improve screening resolution.

SUMMARY
This report documented a generalized, seven-step workflow for conducting play-based
exploration of CCS suitability and demonstrated its application to the Illinois Basin. The
workflow provides a structured, repeatable process for defining play elements, assembling an
integrated geodatabase, establishing suitability criteria, and generating composite maps that
convey the spatial distribution of storage potential and risk. By presenting the methodology first
in generalized form and then as an Illinois case study, this report is intended to serve both as a
record of the analytical approach used in this project and as a practical guide for teams
conducting similar assessments in other basins or for other subsurface applications.
Applied to Illinois, the workflow integrated more than 20 play elements across subsurface,
surface, and societal categories into Common Risk Segment and composite play maps for the
Cambro-Ordovician storage system. The resulting maps identify areas of varying suitability for
CO₂ storage development, from locations where current data strongly support favorable
conditions to areas where additional subsurface, surface, or societal information is needed before
a screening determination can be made. Priority logic embedded in the composite mapping
process ensures that any single factor identified as unsuitable is preserved through to the final
map output, maintaining a conservative screening standard throughout.
This workflow report is one component of a broader final report for the project. Companion

APPENDIX
The Composite CRS Map tool (Composite_CRS.pyt) is a custom ArcGIS Python Toolbox that
combines multiple reclassified suitability rasters into a single Common Risk Segment composite
raster. The tool accepts any number of reclassified input rasters and applies priority logic on a
cell-by-cell basis, assigning each cell the lowest (least suitable) value present among the input
layers. Specifically, a cell is classified as 0 (Least Suitable) if any input layer assigns it a value of
0; otherwise it is classified as 1 (Less Suitable) if any input assigns a 1; then 2 (More Suitable) if
any input assigns a 2; and 3 (Most Suitable) only if all inputs assign a 3. This conservative,
worst-case logic ensures that unfavorable conditions identified by any single play element are
preserved in the composite output. The tool is wrapped in a standard ArcGIS toolbox interface,
allowing analysts to select multiple reclassified rasters from the project geodatabase through a
graphical dialog without modifying the underlying code. It requires the ArcGIS Spatial Analyst
extension.
