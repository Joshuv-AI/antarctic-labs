Deep soil layers show the most pronounced genetic variation in wheat root length
Authors: Bjarne Nielsen, Marta Malinowska, Pernille Sarup, Winnie S. Füchtbauer, Dario Fè, Arnesta Odone, Kristian Thorup‑Kristensen, Just Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wheat is one of the most important cereals worldwide, yet significant gaps remain in our understanding of genetic variability in root traits, especially those associated with deeper rooting that support resource acquisition in challenging environments. Root traits are typically controlled by many genes with small effects and often display low heritability. Our aim was to develop a statistical approach to analyse root variation across soil depth and to determine where genetic differences in root intensity are most detectable. An experiment was conducted at the RadiMax semi-field facility, which is designed to measure deep root systems. Five years of phenotypic data recorded each June produced observations from 1500 rows. Each row captured root intensity across the soil profile from 0.6 m to 2.6 m, enabling detailed analysis of vertical root distribution. Across the five years, 513 winter wheat cultivars were grown in the facility, and among those 409 were genotyped with SNP chips. Depth-resolved regression models with random coefficients were used to quantify genetic and non-genetic variation in root intensity across soil depths, while accounting for spatial variation between rows. Random variation within rows was found to be constant across depths. The models showed that genetic variance for cumulative root intensity increased substantially below 1.1 m, with the deepest layers exhibiting the largest differences between wheat lines. Introduction

Wheat provides 19% of daily calories and 21% of protein requirements for global human consumption, and it is cultivated across diverse climates and regions [1]. Maintaining stable wheat production under climatic and resource constraints remains a major challenge, as erratic rainfall, rising temperatures, and limitations in water and nutrient availability increasingly affect crop performance. However, wheat yields have stagnated since the 1990s [2–4], despite ongoing genetic improvements [5]. Significant yield gaps between commercial production and breeding trial performance have been observed in several wheat growing areas around the world (http://www.yieldgap.org accessed on 28.01.2025). This may be further compounded by intensifying abiotic and biotic stressors, as well as the effects of climate change, including erratic rainfall and rising temperatures, which generally destabilize wheat production and limit progress in yield gains [6, 7]. Projections indicate that wheat yields could decline by up to 6% for every 1°C increase above pre-industrial temperatures, underscoring the urgency of addressing these challenges [7–10].

The root system could offer part of the solution, particularly in enhancing water and nutrient uptake under drought conditions [11–13]. Root systems are central to wheat’s ability to access water and nutrients, especially in deeper soil layers, and thus play a key role in drought tolerance, nutrient use efficiency, and yield stability under stress [14–17].

While roots are less critical for plant performance under well-watered conditions [18–20], they take on a central role during periods of drought or nutrient scarcity [21–24]. Despite their importance, breeding programs have historically neglected root traits, focusing on above-ground characteristics like grain yield and plant architecture instead [25–27]. Over the past century, breeding for high-input, high-density systems may have unintentionally favoured cultivars with smaller root systems and more conservative water uptake strategies, likely due to selection for resource efficiency under high nutrient availability, where large root systems were less advantageous [28]. Recently, however, root traits have gained recognition as essential for breeding climate-resilient crops that can maintain productivity under stress conditions [29, 30]. However, significant gaps remain in our understanding of genetic variability in root traits, particularly those linked to deeper rooting, which enable plants to access resources in challenging environments. The importance of such traits for water and nutrient acquisition has been demonstrated using isotopic tracers in controlled field facilities such as RadiMax [31, 32].

Root measurements are notoriously problematic, as roots and their uptake activity are hidden in the soil and difficult to observe. Traditional phenotyping methods are often destructive, low-throughput, and run in well controlled environments, such as pots or rhizotrons, which fail to replicate field conditions like soil compaction, the presence of bio-pores, and spatial heterogeneity [33, 34]. Root traits are typically controlled by many genes with small effects, have low heritability, and are strongly influenced by genotype × environment interactions, making them difficult to select for in breeding programs [30, 35–37]. The lack of cost-effective, high-throughput field phenotyping and standardized protocols is also a major obstacle to progress in breeding for root development [35, 38–40]. The complex, dynamic, and context-specific nature of root-soil interactions, along with trade-offs between root and shoot growth, make it challenging to link specific root traits to agronomic outcomes, with root plasticity adding another layer of complexity [12, 30, 35, 37, 41, 42].

Despite these challenges, recent advances in technology have opened new possibilities for studying root traits [29]. Semi-field facilities and other scalable phenotyping platforms now allow for non-destructive, high-throughput evaluation of root traits under more realistic conditions [43, 44]. Techniques such as electromagnetic induction surveys, and drone imaging combined with machine learning models allow root systems to be analysed at much larger scale [45–47]. At the same time, low-cost genotyping and advanced statistical models make it easier to integrate complex datasets, providing a cleared picture of root variability and its role in agronomic performance [37, 48, 49].

Still, identifying where along the soil profile genetic variation in root traits can be reliably detected remains a challenge. Environmental variation and measurement noise often complicate heritability estimates [50]. In this study, we used five-years dataset of deep root phenotyping in winter wheat to analyse how genetic and non-genetic variation in root intensity changes with soil depth. Our aim was to develop and apply a statistical approach to partition this variation across the soil profile and determine where genetic differences in root intensity are most detectable.

Materials and methods

To investigate variation in root length across soil depth, an experiment was conducted at the RadiMax semi-field facility at the University of Copenhagen, Taastrup. The facility was constructed in 2015 to allow for large scale phenotyping of deep root system under semi-field conditions [44, 50]. A semi-field system is defined as a controlled environment that supports the full crop growth cycle while maintaining conditions close to those in field cropping systems [51].

The RadiMax facility consists of four beds; however, no more than two identical beds were used in any given year. Each bed contains 150 single-row plots spaced 25 cm apart. Below each row, a transparent minirhizotron tube (inner diameter 60 mm) is installed along the sloping bed bottom, allowing root images to be recorded from a depth of 0.6 m to 2.6 m. The facility contains local topsoil over a constructed subsoil profile; a detailed technical description and illustration of the V-shaped bed structure, row orientation, soil profile, and minirhizotron tube configuration is provided by Svane et al. [44].

Root length recordings were collected over five consecutive years (2018–2022). Each year, two beds were used, resulting in 300 rows per year and a total of 1500 row observations across years. Although the two beds were identically constructed, they were treated as two separate environments, and spatial variation across rows was observed within and between beds.

Different winter wheat cultivars were grown each year. Cultivars were selected by two Danish breeding companies based on breeding priorities, including interest in variation for root traits. The material therefore represents winter wheat cultivars relevant to the participating breeding programmes rather than a globally representative diversity panel. The experimental unit was a single row. The number of cultivars and the number of rows allocated to each cultivar varied among years, reflecting annual design decisions such as prioritising replication of selected material, testing larger genotype sets, or evaluating specific family groups. Some cultivars were included in multiple years, but the dataset was not balanced for repeated evaluation of the same genotypes across years. In total, 513 winter wheat cultivars were tested over the five years. Crops were sown each year in late September, additional crop management details are provided in Wacker et al. [32].

Images of the roots were recorded once per year in mid-June, corresponding approximately to the period of maximum rooting depth and flowering. As only a single recording time was used each year, the data reflect differences between years rather than within-season dynamics.

Under normal conditions, recording depth ranged from 600 to 2600 mm below the soil surface (Fig. 1). In 2018, recordings were limited to 1200 mm and 2200 mm, and in 2022 to 600–2200 mm (Fig. 1). Images were collected using four cameras operating in parallel. For each row a camera was lowered to the bottom of a tube (2600 mm) and recorded images while moving upward, resulting in up to 143 images per row. Missing images occured in some rows due to tube damage or water accumulation, particularly in deeper section. Over all years, rows between positions 120 and 150 showed consistent limitations in recording depths below 1300 mm. This was likely due to localized soil compaction during construction, affecting tube integrity. While this did not affect most rows, it introduced additional spatial heterogeneity that was taken into account in the subsequent statistical analysis. The RadiMax facility is subject to spatial and temporal environmental variation, including differences in soil structure, compaction, and local conditions within beds, as well as variation between years (e.g. weather conditions and shelter effects). These sources of variation were not fully controlled experimentally but were taken into account in the statistical models through fixed year–bed effects, spatial terms, and random row effects.

All images were recorded using a custom automated multispectral camera with integrated illumination systems (Videometer, Denmark) for recordings in visible light wavelengths [44]. Each image had resolution of 2448 × 2048 pixels, covering an area of 52 × 38 mm on the tube surface. Images were analysed using RootPainter to segment root from soil and estimate root length [52]. Images with no detected roots were assigned a value of zero.

Root lengths along each row were accumulated from images recorded in the corresponding minirhizotron-tube, starting from the top at 600 mm depth to the bottom, using Eq. 1. Figure 2 shows cumulative root length patterns for 2020. In the upper part of the soil (starting at 600 mm), the cumulative root length generally increases with depth as roots are recorded progressively deeper. This trend continued until a certain depth, where the increase vanishes, and the curves flatten. Some curves flattened at high cumulative values (up to 390 mm), while others flattened near zero, indicating shallow rooting (down to 1100 mm). The different level of flattening for the different rows shows the variation in total root length between rows. The depth at which a root curve was flattening out represents the actual maximal root depth for that row. Below this actual root depth of flattening point, the variance in root length between images within rows vanished, since most root lengths were close to zero. However, between rows, the variance in cumulative root length continued to increase until all curves from all rows reached their flattening depths. Evaluation of residuals

Residual diagnostics were used to evaluate the fit of the random regression model (Eq. 2). Residuals plotted against fitted cumulative root intensity showed no strong increase in residual spread across the prediction range from 0 to 800 mm (Fig. 4), suggesting that the model captured the main variation across low and high cumulative root intensities. Discussion

In this study, we developed and applied a statistical approach to partition genetic and non-genetic variation in root intensity across soil depth in winter wheat. Although not all lines were genotyped, the model allowed additive genetic effects to be estimated for genotyped lines while while accountinc separately for non-genotyped line effects. Across five years of RadiMax phenotyping, genetic variation in cumulative root intensity became most detectable below 1100 mm, while shallow layers showed comparativelt little genetic differentiation among lines. This indicates that depth-resolved modelling can help identify where along the soil profile genetic signal is most informative.

Genetic variances and heritability

Our results show that additive genetic variance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{a}^{2}$$\end{document}) increased significantly below 1100 mm, with deeper layers showing larger differences between wheat lines. Narrow-sense heritability based on additive genetic effects was low to moderate and reached approximately 0.13 around 1500 mm (Fig. 8). Including measurements from upper layers also contributed to the cumulative trait, with the highest heritability obtained when all images down to approximately 1500 mm were included. The increasing importance of genetic variation in deeper layers aligns with previous findings [37] which reported higher heritability (albeit measured at the line level) for the deeper root traits. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that soil physical constraints become more important with depth. Below compacted layers, roots may increasingly depend on bio-pores or other preferential pathways, creating conditions under which genotypic differences in penetration and resource acquisition become more apparent [33, 34, 56]. The increase in non-genetic variance at depth also indicates that deeper layers are environmentally heterogeneous, which may reduce precision but also reflects the physical constraints roots encounter [57].

In contrast, genetic variance and heritability in shallow soil layers (above 1100 mm) were minimal. This may reflect environmental heterogeneity, including fluctuating moisture and nutrient availability, which can mask genetic effects [33, 34, 37, 58], or lower inherent variability among the genotypes in shallow soil layers. It should also be noted that RadiMax measures visible root intensity at the minirhizotron tube surface rather than complete root architecture in the surrounding soil matrix, which may limit its detection of finer genetic differences in these layers. In addition, root distribution may partly reflect above-ground vigour, tillering, phenology, or growth dynamics. These traits were not recorded consistently across all years and genotypes, so the variance components reported here describe variation in the observed root intensity phenotype rather than separating root-specific effects from plant size or developmental stage. Nevertheless, shallow root traits remain important for total biomass estimation, understanding early-stage plant establishment, and overall yield stability [33, 59].

Statistical methods

Statistical models in plant breeding (e.g., repeatability and random regression models as used in this study) aim to partition variance into genetic and non-genetic components. Acuña and Wade [60] highlighted the importance of separating these components in root phenotyping studies, where environmental and neighbour effects can confound genetic estimates. In our study we used two statistical model approaches to achieve that. Both, random regression and repeatability models provide complementary insights into genetic and non-genetic variance partitioning across soil depths, but they differ in their methodologies, assumptions, and contributions to understanding variance partitioning across soil depths.

The repeatability model allowed for depth-specific variance decomposition, and independent estimation of variance components at each soil layer, including additive genetic effects (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{a}^{2}$$\end{document}), non-additive genetic effects (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{pe}^{2}$$\end{document}), effects from non-genotyped lines (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{l}^{2}$$\end{document}), neighbour effects (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{\eta }^{2}$$\end{document}), and between-row effects (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{\tau }^{2}$$\end{document}). This provided a detailed understanding of variation within specific depths. The model showed a significant increase in between-row variance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{\tau }^{2}=5117, Table 2$$\end{document}) in the deepest soil layers, suggesting increased environmental and spatial variability with depth. On the other hand, within-row variance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{\varepsilon }^{2}$$\end{document}) decreased sharply in deeper layers, likely reflecting the predominance of zero root recordings in these regions, as also shown by the flattening of curves in the deeper layers (Fig. 2). The repeatability model’s ability to decompose variance at each soil depth, even with limited data makes it well suited for analysing depth-specific root traits.

In contrast, the random regression model allowed variance in root intensity to be decomposed continuously across the soil profile, identifying depth dependent patterns such as the increase in additive genetic variance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{a}^{2}$$\end{document}) below 1100 mm. This is particularly valuable for root studies, as prior RadiMax experiments typically evaluated only total observed root length [49, 50, 61, 62]. The random regression model assumes constant residual variance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{\varepsilon }^{2}$$\end{document}) throughout the profile (see Figs. 4, 5), which simplifies analysis. This assumption is a simplification, as residual spread varied with depth and many deeper images contained no detected roots, as reflected by the flattening of cumulative root curves in Fig. 2.

A useful feature of the model is that it accommodates both genotyped and non-genotyped lines. Additive genetic effects were estimated for genotyped lines using the genomic relationship matrix, while non-genotyped lines were represented by a separate line-effect variance component. This allowed phenotypic information from non-genotyped material to be retained in the analysis without treating those lines as part of the genomic relationship structure. This is relevant for breeding and field phenotyping datasets, where incomplete genotyping is common and pedigree information may not always be available [63]. However, in our case no pedigree data was available, so we included extra variance components for the non-genotyped lines to capture their genetic variance. Such models allowed us to estimate genetic effects in the absence of a complete genotypic data proving the efficiency and scalability of such method for breeding programs with the limited genetic data.

Together, both models offer a comprehensive framework for analysing genetic and non-genetic variance in root traits, addressing both continuous trends and layer-specific dynamics.

The value of the approach is not limited to the RadiMax facility. Many root phenotyping methods generate observations ordered along a soil profile, whether from minirhizotron images, soil cores, profile-wall measurements, or related depth-resolved systems. In such datasets, individual measurements at a given depth can be highly variable, whereas cumulative or integrated traits can provide a more stable description of vertical root distribution. Previous studies have therefore analysed root data as depth profiles or used integrated traits rather than relying only on total root length or single-depth measurements [32, 34, 37, 64–66]. The random regression model used here builds on this idea by estimating how genetic and non-genetic variance change continuously with soil depth. This modelling principle may be applied to other depth-resolved root datasets, provided that major sources of non-genetic variation, such as spatial heterogeneity, measurement error, soil conditions, and environmental differences, are accounted for in the design or analysis.

Spatial corrections

Both models included spatial corrections to address systematic variability across the experimental layout, an issue identified in earlier RadiMax work [49, 50, 62]. The random regression model used sinusoidal terms to capture global spatial trends, as recurring variation across rows and beds, over the five years of recorded data. This was effective in accounting for a broader pattern that could confound variance estimates, particularly for cumulative root intensity. The repeatability model, in turn isolated localised spatial effects by independently estimating within-row and between-row variances at specific soil depths. This approach captured row-specific variations, such as localised soil compaction, and highlighted depth-independent variability, like substantial increase in between-row variance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma}_{\tau }^{2}=5117$$\end{document}) in the deepest soil layers. By addressing both global and localised spatial trends, the two models improved the accuracy of variance component estimates and provided a clearer understanding of genetic and non-genetic effects, highlighting the importance of precise spatial adjustments in setups like RadiMax.

Breeding perspective of cumulative root intensity

Root system architecture traits (including root depth) have been highlighted as an important target for breeding programs to improve drought tolerance, nutrient use efficiency, and yield stability under stress [29, 33, 37, 58]. Beyond drought resilience, deep rooting may also contribute to carbon sequestration and improving crop performance in low-input systems [24, 32, 67]. However, uncertainty remains about which specific root traits are most relevant for breeding and the optimal approaches and environments for their phenotypic evaluation [12, 16, 29, 42].

Our study addresses this issue by using a semi-field facility to evaluate cumulative root intensity with fine depths resolution (600–2600 mm) over five years, providing insight into genetic and non-genetic variations in root traits that field studies often cannot capture. Unlike pot or above-ground rhizotron studies (i.e. root towers or pipes) which often fail to replicate the physical constraints like compaction and bio-pore reliance [33], our setup mimics field conditions while maintaining experimental control aiding in the identification of deep root genetic variation.

Although early root traits, like seminal root angle, are important for establishment, drought resistance and biomass accumulation [68–71], deeper root traits are equally important for resource acquisition under stress [21, 40]. We observed that, in mature winter wheat, narrow-sense heritability and genetic variance are highest in deeper soil layers (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}^{2}=0.13, Fig.8$$\end{document}), suggesting that root intensity in deeper soil layers may be a useful trait for a further evaluation in breeding programmes. This highlights the complementary roles of early development and deep root traits and suggests integrating both into breeding strategies could maximise crop performance across diverse environments.

By combining depth-resolved, high-resolution root phenotyping with statistical modelling, we identified the sub-layer of soil below 1100 mm where genetic variance in root length is most pronounced. This provides insight that may help guide future breeding-oriented studies targeting deeper root traits. However, practical integration remains challenging due to phenotyping bottlenecks, particularly for capturing root data on a large scale [59, 72–74]. This highlights the need for scalable, high-throughput methods that can link root traits to agronomic performance. Future research should validate these findings across different environments, assess their effect on yield stability under realistic field conditions, and integrate these traits into genomic selection and yield prediction under stress conditions.
