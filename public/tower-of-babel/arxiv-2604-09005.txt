Abstract
While textiles have existed throughout much of human history as complex mechanical metamaterials, textile science has largely been overlooked by the physics community until recently. In this review, we consider the symmetry, topology, and mechanics of woven and knitted materials, showing that they represent a unique, if under-explored, regime of condensed matter. We start with the basic construction and mechanics of spun yarn, reviewing recent developments twisted bundle structures. We then introduce woven and knitted fabrics as materials with layer symmetries that can be topologically characterized as knots and links in the thickened torus. We finally discuss fabric mechanics and geometry in terms of yarn-level geometry, dissipation mechanisms, and defect structures.

INTRODUCTION

Humans have used textiles in some form for much of our collective history.
From spinning grasses and animal hairs to make stronger threads, yarns, and ropes, to industrial-scale weaving of bespoke patterns with the Jacquard loom, and finally to the technical textiles and smart wearables found today, the methods and materials used for the creation of textiles have undergone a dramatic evolution.
Nonetheless, the themes of twisting, knotting, and entangling as a way of combining fiber-like constituents remains unchanged.
While we will not attempt a historical introduction to textiles, we refer the interested reader to refs.

In recent years, physicists have taken an interest in textiles.
Textile science and engineering is a mature field that has developed standard measures, metrology methods, and models that have enabled the industry to grow to the staggering scale it has today.
Similarly, fiber artists and the crafter community ensure that traditional methods of weaving, knitting, crocheting, and other techniques persist, while finding new ways of building complex patterns and mechanical behaviors into fabrics.
There are at least two reasons for physicists to pick up this thread: one, through the exploration of mechanical metamaterials and inverse design; two, through an interest in mathematical modeling towards developing structure-function relationships across scales.

In this Review, we will consider textiles through the lens of condensed matter physics, focusing on topology, symmetry, and non-linear mechanics.
In doing so, we offer an invitation for others to explore this fascinating topic.

In order for physicists to contribute to the study and tradition of textiles, it is helpful to learn some of the basic language.
Textile terminology has a complex history and even has differences amongst traditional fiber artists and industrial-scale textile engineers, as well as across regions, reflecting the global and culturally interwoven nature of textile practices; a minimal dictionary is provided in the margin.
A classical example of this is the use of “woven” to describe a textile whose structure consists of yarn that crosses the fabric in a well-prescribed manner based on a specific pattern.
By contrast, a “non-woven” can be defined as any other textile involving some degree of randomness to the microstructure, including felt—a material made by pressing fibers together and held together by inter-fiber friction.
However, while this definition would include knitted and crocheted fabrics as wovens, these, in fact, have structures distinct from those created through a weaving process.
Notably, even within the textile field, terminology can remain unsettled.
For example, the definition of “nonwoven fabric” in the Textile Institute’s Textile Terms and Definitions begins with the phrase “Opinions vary,” reflecting on several open disagreements in the field, including whether “nonwoven fabric” refers broadly to any fabric that is

YARN: A BASIC STRUCTURAL ELEMENT

Yarn, threads, and strings all serve to describe the basic structural element of woven and knitted fabrics; for brevity, we will describe all such filamentous materials used for fabric creation as “yarn.”
In the textile industry, yarn is a context-dependent term, generally referring to the effectively 1-dimensional material intended for fabric formation, rather than intermediate assemblies used in stages of yarn processing which have specific terms
[
22
]
. Throughout history, yarn has been made from a variety of materials, both natural — sourced from animals and plants — and synthetic.
Traditionally created from fibers consisting of either animal products (e.g. wool and silk) or plant material (e.g. cotton, hemp, linen), modern synthetic yarns are formed from fibers formed when a polymer melt (e.g. polyacrylate, polyester, rayon, nylon) is extruded through a narrow pore.
Semi-synthetic “regenerated fibers” harvest and reconstruct cellulose (e.g. from bamboo) to create rayon and associated fibers.
Finally, there is increasing interest in the design of shape-actuating fabrics whose yarn is based on shape-changing materials
[
24
]
such as shape memory alloys
[
25
,
26
]
, liquid crystal elastomers
[
27
]
, as well as yarn constructed from human cells for tissue engineering
[
28
]
.

The structure of the yarn itself can be varied and is often dependent on the constituent material.
Since natural fibers tend to be too short (“filament silk” is an exception) for fabric creation, yarn is traditionally a hierarchical bundle of filaments that are
spun
together under tension.
In contrast, polymer extrusion can create long
filaments
that extend the full length of the yarn.
Filament materials, which can range from polymers, including elastomers, to metallic wires, can be used as the yarn itself (“monofilament”) or can be wrapped into a bundle (“multifilament”).

Given the variability of yarn material and structure, it is difficult to arrive at a standard characterization of yarn properties.
For instance, the diameters of fluffy, multifilament bundles are not a well-defined quantity, and they are rather characterized by their linear mass density (see
tex
,
denier
in the textile lexicon).
As such, beyond simple monofilament, there is much unknown about the mechanical (i.e. stress-vs-strain) constitutive relations of various yarn types.
Moreover, contact mechanics, also essential for capturing the structure and response of textiles, involves the added complexity of friction and contact geometry.
While experimental characterization (e.g. tensile and flexural testing) can provide useful details, yarn properties are often sensitive to a history of loading and unloading, degradation, and even humidity in the case of many natural fibers.

2.1
Hierarchical, plied yarn

Yarns and threads made from animal and plant fibers are complex, hierarchical structures. Staple fibers are short, often only a few centimeters in length
[
29
]
.
Consequently, in order to create yarn of arbitrary length, these staple fibers are combined using a technique known as “spinning.”
Spinning is an ancient practice known to humans for millennia, developed separately amongst many cultures
[
30
]
.
While modern industrial methods have automated spinning, the basic process remains the same: fibers are aligned and formed into a bundle; a tension is applied across the bundle; the bundle is twisted under tension; more fibers are added to allow the bundle to grow in length.
A drop spindle, such as the one shown in
figure
1
a
, uses gravity and rotational inertia to apply tension and torque to the fibers.
By twisting under tension, the fibers form frictional contacts, allowing the bundle to maintain its form, resulting in a single “ply” of yarn (known as singles yarn) as seen in
figure
1
b
.

While friction maintains the cohesion of a ply, there is nonetheless residual stress, in the form of a torque, arising from the imposed helical shape of the fibers; the spinning direction chooses the handedness of the helix—designated “S” or “Z,” as shown in
figure
1
c
—and thus the sign of the torque.
Such “single-ply” yarns are susceptible to relaxing residual torque through a mechanical instability that converts
twist
—related to the pitch of the helical structure of the bundle—to
writhe
, a description of the three-dimensional coiling of the bundle.
This helical instability, also known as the “plectoneme” instability, in reference to a similar phenomenon in DNA supercoiling, or the “phone cord” instability
[
31
,
32
]
, can distort the appearance of the resulting textile.
In knitted fabrics, this breaks mirror symmetries in the stitch pattern, resulting in a phenomenon known as “spirality,” shearing the relaxed shape of the fabric
[
33
]
.
In woven fabrics, this effect results in curling of opposite corners of the fabric out of the plane, where one corner curls up and the other corner curls down
[
34
]
.
In order to decrease the propensity for yarn to undergo this instability, as well as to add strength to the yarn, multiple plies are
counter-twisted
together to create a “multi-ply” or “balanced” yarn, as in
figure
1
d
.
Similarly, if multiple monofilaments are twisted together, the resulting multifilament bundles can be balanced through the incorporation of counter-twisting multiple bundles together.

2.2
Kirchhoff rod model

In its simplest consideration, yarn is treated as a 1-dimensional inextensible and bendable material. This abstraction does not account for yarn’s axial compressibility or the structures that emerge from its constituent fibers and the process of spinning them together under tension.
However, much of the fabric-level properties can be determined from a simplified view of yarn shape in which yarn is modeled as thickened space curves
\bm{\Gamma}(s)
, where
s\in[0,L]
is an arclength parameter defined such that
\partial_{s}\bm{\Gamma}=\hat{\mathbf{t}}(s)
is the unit tangent vector (see e.g.,
[
35
]
).
This curve, the “center curve,” is taken to lie along the center of mass of each small segment of the yarn throughout its length
[
32
]
.
The bending response of the yarn depends on the curvature
\kappa(s)
of
\bm{\Gamma}
, given by
\partial_{ss}\bm{\Gamma}=\partial_{s}\hat{\mathbf{t}}=\kappa\hat{\mathbf{n}}
, where
\hat{\mathbf{n}}(s)
is the unit normal vector.
The twisting response requires the definition of an orthonormal
material frame

\left\{\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{2}\right\}
where
\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{\mu}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}}=0
at each point
s
.
The instantaneous twist of the yarn
\Omega
at each point along the curve is given by
\Omega(s)\equiv\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{2}\cdot\partial_{s}\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{1}
.
For more about the definition of the material frame, see the sidebar below.

Kirchhoff rod theory gives a minimal model of yarn mechanics in terms of an effective energy functional
E
, and might be considered the “harmonic oscillator” of yarn mechanics.
While the most general version of a yarn bending and twisting energy would incorporate anisotropies in bending and twisting moduli, as well as preferred yarn curvature, for most types of yarn, it is typical that (i) the cross-section is approximately circular, (ii) gently twisting the yarn does not bend it, and (iii) there may be a preferred twist
\overline{\Omega}
in the case of un-balanced or pre-twisted yarn.
This results in a more typical yarn elastic energy of the form

E=\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}s\,\left[B\,\kappa^{2}(s)+J\left(\Omega(s)-\overline{\Omega}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(1)

where
B
and
J
are, respectively, the bending and twisting moduli of the yarn and can be measured for both monofilament and spun yarn
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
.
An additional stretching cost can be added to this model, but for most spun yarns, this cost is prohibitively large, and the yarn can be assumed to maintain a fixed length; elastomeric yarns are a notable exception.

THE FRAMING OF A CURVE

There are many ways that an orthonormal frame can be adapted to a space curve; the most common constraint is to force one of the basis vectors to align with the tangent unit vector
\hat{\mathbf{t}}(s)
.
Of these, the best known is the
Frenet-Serret frame
, which consists of the tangent vector
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
, the normal vector
\hat{\mathbf{n}}\equiv\partial_{s}\hat{\mathbf{t}}/\left|\partial_{s}\hat{\mathbf{t}}\right|
, and the binormal vector
\hat{\mathbf{b}}\equiv\hat{\mathbf{t}}\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}
.
The Frenet-Serret equations describes the infinitesimal rotations of this frame along the centerline and is given by
\partial_{s}\left\{\hat{\mathbf{t}},\hat{\mathbf{n}},\hat{\mathbf{b}}\right\}=\bm{\omega}_{\rm FS}\times\left\{\hat{\mathbf{t}},\hat{\mathbf{n}},\hat{\mathbf{b}}\right\}
, where the Darboux vector
\bm{\omega}_{\rm FS}
is given by
\bm{\omega}_{\rm FS}=\tau\hat{\mathbf{t}}+\kappa\hat{\mathbf{b}}
where
\tau(s)
is the torsion.
To fix the ambiguous definition of the Frenet-Serret frame in regions with vanishing curvature, the
Bishop frame

\left\{\hat{\mathbf{t}},\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1},\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}\right\}
imposes an additional constraint of zero twist,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}\cdot\partial_{s}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}=0
, resulting in a Darboux vector
\bm{\omega}_{\rm B}=-\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}\kappa\sin\left(\int^{s}_{0}{\rm d}s^{\prime}\tau\right)+\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}\kappa\cos\left(\int^{s}_{0}{\rm d}s^{\prime}\tau\right)

[
39
]
.
The material frame
\left\{\hat{\mathbf{t}},\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{2}\right\}
, can then be defined by rotating the Bishop frame by an angle
\theta
, as shown in
figure
1
e
.
This rotation can be described as an SO(2) gauge transformation and results in a Darboux vector given by
\bm{\omega}=\Omega\hat{\mathbf{t}}+\kappa\sin\Phi\,\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{1}+\kappa\cos\Phi\,\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{2}
where
\Omega=\partial_{s}\theta
is the material twist and
\Phi\equiv\int^{s}_{0}{\rm d}s^{\prime}(\Omega-\tau)
is the phase difference between the material and Bishop frames.

3.1
Fiber packing

As yarn is spun under tension, it is reasonable to model the undeformed state of spun yarn as a straight line with zero curvature (
\kappa=0
) and a constant twist of the material frame (
\Omega\neq 0
).
While under tension, these filaments will be subject to an aligning force that tends to minimize their distance from the yarn’s central axis; due to hard-core interactions between the filaments, they cannot all pack along the central axis.
Consequently, we might imagine the tension as applying an effective pressure or confining force
[
40
]
on the filaments in the yarn’s cross-section.
It is therefore reasonable to expect that the filaments will pack in a way that minimizes the area fraction of voids within the yarn’s cross-section, resulting in the idealized model of hexagonal packing, as shown in
figure
1
f
.

The hexagonal packing model is largely contingent on the assumption that cutting yarn perpendicular to its local tangent vector gives a 2D lattice of circular disks.
However, due to the twist of the individual filaments about the central axis of the yarn, filaments in this cross-section of the yarn bundle generally have individual cross-sections that are distorted.
This distortion increases as the twist
\Omega
of the bundle is increased and also grows with the radial coordinate of the filament from the center of the bundle, and can even result in concave and self-wrapping shapes
[
41
]
.
Since the distance between two filaments is determined by a straight line that must be orthogonal to the local tangent vectors of both filaments, this line cannot lie in the perpendicular cross-section of the twisted yarn, unless the yarn is not twisted (
\Omega=0
) or both of the filaments simultaneously occupy the center of the yarn’s core.
Therefore, the line of closest distance, when projected onto the yarn’s perpendicular cross-section, has a reduced length that depends on the radial coordinates of the two filaments in question.
This projection of inter-filament lengths gives rise to a radially-dependent metric tensor within the cross-sectional slice of the yarn, rationalizing the distortions of the fiber cross-sections.
In fact, it can be shown
[
42
]
that the induced metric is consistent with a space of positive Gaussian curvature
K_{G}
that scales as
K_{G}\sim\Omega^{2}
.
This same Gaussian curvature frustrates hexagonal packing in two dimensions, resulting in a stress distribution within the yarn that is partially relieved through the introduction of 5-fold disclination defects
[
43
]
.

Further complicating this idealized picture of twisted bundles is the issue of fiber migration.
Fibers towards the outer edge of the yarn must have larger arclengths than fibers in the core; the stretching energy of the filaments per yarn arclength then increases with radius.
In order to relax the cost of stretching filaments on the outside of the yarn will
migrate
towards the core.
In doing so, they displace filaments within the yarn, which are then forced to migrate to the outer radii, as shown in
figure
1
e
.
Meanwhile, if the density of the yarn is expected to remain translationally-invariant, these migrations must be carefully coordinated
[
44
]
.
It can then be argued that in order to maintain a translationally-invariant mass density of the yarn, the mass density cannot vary with the current radius of the migrating filament; within a small-twist approximation, this mplies radial position of a migrating filament has the form
r(s)\approx R\sqrt{s/\lambda}
, where
s
is the arclength coordinate of the yarn and
\lambda
is the migration period
[
45
]
.
While there are extensions to this idealized model
[
46
,
47
]
, much remains to be understood about the interactions between packing, migration, and contact mechanics.
Indeed, the simple assumptions of translational invariance and uniform density readily break down with the introduction of curvature
[
48
]
, with consequences for bundle mechanics that are still being explored through the language of nonlinear elasticity and gauge theory
[
49
]
.

3.2
Contact mechanics

The role of contacts is twofold: imposing a geometry and dissipating energy.
Two contacting yarns locally impose forces and moments on each other.
Within the slender elastic curve model, contacts can be interpreted as external local or distributed forces and moments.
The curve will then adapt its shape to minimize its elastic energy under these external constraints.
The geometry of yarns in a textile is then principally determined by the arrangement of contacts imposed upon crafting.

At a contact, normal forces build up as yarns cannot pass through each other, but the existence of tangential forces gives away the presence of frictional interactions.
Friction dissipates energy when the contact is sliding, but can also statically trap tension within the yarn.
For yarn mechanics, it is important to distinguish two types of frictional interactions: viscous friction and solid friction
[
50
]
.
Viscous friction is typical of lubricated surfaces and is proportional to the relative speed between the contacting strands.
It then vanishes for a structure completely at rest.
In contrast, dry friction holds tangential force until a certain threshold is reached and the contact starts sliding.
Within the simplest Amontons-Coulomb formulation of dry friction, the threshold tangential force is proportional to the normal force and, once sliding, is independent of the sliding speed.
Which interactions are predominant may depend on the time scale, the type of yarn, or external conditions.
Signatures of solid friction are found, for instance, in the hysteretic response upon cycling quasi-static tensile tests
[
51
]
, stick-slip instabilities emerging from sliding contacts
[
52
]
, or the existence of multiple rest states
[
53
]
. In contrast, viscous features are evidenced by slow creep-like responses, for instance, in the geometrical relaxation of the fabrics after fabrication
[
54
]
, or the stress relaxation of stretched fabrics
[
55
]
.

In the case where the yarn is not made from one hard monofilament, the cross-section of the yarns will undergo significant deformation in the contact zones
[
56
,
57
]
.
Normal stresses in the contact zones will generally cause yarn to adopt an elliptical, or even “stadium-shaped” cross-section with flattened edges, as shown in
figure
1
f
.
These cross-section deformations may also be the source of dissipation, in particular if the yarn is made of multiple filaments in frictional contacts.
The interplay between filament packing and extreme cross-section deformations of spun yarn has been examined at some depth experimentally
[
23
]
but beyond recent investigations into twisted bundles described above in section
3.1
, little progress has been made in physical modeling.
Phenomenological yarn-yarn deformation mechanics models rely on power law scaling in the compressed volume of the fibers
[
58
,
59
]
, motivated by van Wyk’s studies on compressed wool
[
60
]
.

4
TOPOLOGY AND SYMMETRY OF FABRICS

Now that we have our yarns, the fabrication of a fabric consists of entangling these yarns following a repeated and specific pattern.
The entanglement process gives the fabric distinctive topological and symmetrical properties.
While a large variety of fabrics have been used throughout history, woven and knitted fabrics are primarily two-periodic structures.
This makes them analogous to two-dimensional crystals, which allows us to readily adopt the language and concepts of solid-state physics and crystallography.
As is traditional in condensed matter physics, we will focus on bulk descriptions of the material, leaving the interesting complexities of boundaries and interfaces for description elsewhere.
Therefore, we will skip discussions of selvedge in wovens and hemming in knits, as well as the different structures resulting from the various cast-on and bind-off techniques.
Instead, by focusing on the doubly-periodic bulk, we can simplify the structure of fabrics by focusing on a translational unit cell.

The unit cell representation also facilitates topological characterization of these fabrics by removing the infinite set of repeated yarn crossings that result from translational symmetry.
If
\mathcal{F}
is the set of yarn paths embedded in Euclidean space, then the primitive unit cell
1
1

1

The definition of a primitive or minimal unit cell depends on the lattice being studied and which topological invariant is being considered.

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
contains the minimal segments of yarn necessary to re-build the fabric through the repeated action of the group of translations in 2D,
G_{\rm tr}
, such that
\mathcal{F}=G_{\rm tr}\circ\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
.
In other words,
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
is the quotient space (or orbit space)
\mathcal{F}/G_{\rm tr}
.
If yarn paths existed purely in two dimensions then this quotient space would be isomorphic to the two-torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
However, the yarn paths and the fabric are embedded in three dimensions, where only two of the dimensions are identified by translational symmetries; the correct quotient space is therefore
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\simeq\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I
, where
I
is an interval.
Consequently, the open curves that represent yarn in an infinite fabric become closed within the quotient space—copies of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
That is, the woven and knitted structures are equivalent to knots and links on the thickened two-torus,
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I
.
While distinct from the usual study of knots and links, topological invariants have been found for both woven and knitted fabrics
[
17
,
18
]
.

Two-dimensional tilings of the plane can be completely classified using only 17 symmetry groups, namely the wallpaper groups
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
.
The wallpaper groups are essential for understanding the structure and response of 2D crystals
[
64
]
and, as their name implies, be found in works of art, as exemplified by the tilings of M.C. Escher
[
65
]
.
However, fabrics can have symmetries through their thickness, requiring description using
sub-periodic symmetry groups
, where the number of directions with translational periodicity is less than the embedding dimension.
Of these, fabrics are described by the 80 layer groups (2D periodicity in 3D)
[
11
,
66
]
.

4.1
Wovens

Most woven fabrics are biaxial, with yarn running in perpendicular directions known as warp and weft.
When weaving on a loom, many parallel “warp” yarns are typically oriented to point towards the weaver who uses a shuttle to run a working “weft” yarn back and forth across the warp yarns.
In modern industrial looms, instead of shuttles, many other weft insertion processes are now used, including rapier, air-jet, and water-jet processes for production efficiency, although the process remains largely the same. In these looms the warp yarns are lifted up or lowered in a patterned sequence through the use of heddles, threads or cords containing eyes through which the warp yarns are threaded, forming an opening called the “shed” in which the weft is inserted, resulting in an arrangement of over and under-crossings that can vary from row to row. The Jacquard loom, due to its ability to re-configure the heddle lifting sequence using punch cards, is often considered the first computer
[
1
]
.

We will refer to an “over-crossing” (resp., “under-crossing”) as a region of the fabric where
the weft yarn passes over a warp yarn
.
As shown in
figure
2
a
, the two states of each crossing make for an easy analogy with a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
spin lattice, where the over and under-crossings are, respectively, mapped to up and down spin states.
In the case of the (3,1)-twill pattern shown, there is often a net non-zero spin to the lattice.
A
(p,q)
-twill is defined as a pattern having
p
over-crossings, followed by
q
under-crossings with subsequent rows shifting the pattern by one crossing
[
11
]
.
Therefore, the net spin of a
(p,q)
-twill is determined by the ratio of the difference in up and down-crossings
p-q
to the total number of crossings
p+q
.
By contrast to the
(3,1)
-twill, the plain weave shown in
figure
2
b
consists of an equal arrangement of over and under-crossings and may be considered a
(1,1)
-twill; this structure has a distinct antiferromagnetic arrangement of crossings.
Note that of the patterns shown in
figure
2
b-e
, only the plain weave and its generalizations in
figure
2
f
have mirror symmetries.
Due to the apparent grid-like nature of the biaxial weaves, we might consider the over/under-crossing arrangement as living on a square grid, such as that shown in
figure
2
a
.
However, since the warp yarns are under different mechanical loading conditions than the weft yarns and are often made from different materials, they are more appropriately described as a rectangular lattice, although the use of identical warp and weft yarns and careful control of stress can result in an essentially square lattice.

3\times 3
basket weave.

Given the orthogonal arrangement of yarns in biaxial weaves, it is natural to define the base repeat pattern using a translational unit cell whose edges align with the warp and weft directions; these are shown in
figure
2
b-e
in yellow.
If one considers the primitive unit cell (shown in white) of these specific structures, the resulting lattice directions generally depart from the warp and weft directions.
Since the primitive unit cells are translational units with minimal area, they also contain the fewest crossing points; consequently, they include the fewest number of yarn segments.
This makes the primitive unit cells particularly useful in the study of yarn linking topology.

The identification of a translational unit cell with the thickened two-torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I
enables a reduction of the complexity associated with classifying the topology of an infinite collection of yarn and yarn crossings in
\mathbb{E}^{3}
to the analysis of a few closed yarn paths, albeit in a space with a handle.
As shown in the right panels of
figure
2
b-e
, the use of a primitive unit cell can even reduce the number of independent yarns to two for the biaxial examples and three for the Kagome triaxial weave.
The topology of each of these patterns is invariant under smooth deformations of the yarn paths on the torus,
along with a collection of
Reidemeister moves
that define how curves can pass above and below each other without changes in knotting or linking topology.
The key complication is the addition of the torus, which has two directions or “generators” around which a loop can wrap.
In the case of the Kagome weave (also called the “mad weave”) in
figure
2
e
, the magenta and cyan loops each wrap different generators of the torus once and therefore cannot be shrunk to a point, even if the yellow curve was removed.
The generators of the torus may be regarded as providing two additional curves, one that is in the form of a closed circular ring, and the other that extends through the axis of revolution of the torus but has its antipodal ends at
\pm\infty
identified to form a second closed loop.
Therefore, the topology of the plain weave, satin, and twill patterns shown in
figure
2
can each be described through the linking of four curves (two yarn, two torus generators), whereas the Kagome weave links five curves (three yarn, two torus generators).
It should be noted, however, that there is some ambiguity through the choice of unit cell: a different choice of unit cell can change how yarn loops wrap the generators through the incorporation of
Dehn twists
without changing the overall topology of the resulting fabric
[
67
,
68
]
.
The choice of unit cell for the biaxial structures considered here requires that each closed curve traverses both generators at least once, and the number of wraps of the warp yarn around the minor axis of the torus maps to the number of warp yarns between two under-crossings in neighboring rows: one for the plain weave (i.e., (1,1)-twill), two for satin, and three for the (3,1)-twill.

Beyond the usual symmetry groups that characterize 2D materials, including woven fabrics
[
11
]
, there exists an additional “isonemal” (
iso-
meaning “same,” and
nemal
meaning “thread”) symmetry that describes the ability for two yarns to be exchanged without disruptions to the pattern.
In the most general setting, this symmetry requires that even warp and weft yarns must be able to be exchanged.
The yarn exchange can involve any uniform (homogeneous) operation consisting of isometries of the fabric
[
8
,
69
]
; this means that flipping all over-crossings to under-crossings is allowed, albeit limited to the case where there are the same number of over and under-crossings (e.g., a
(p,q)
-twill with
p=q
).
Importantly, all regular
(p,q)
-twills are isonemal
[
8
]
.
Satin structures, on the other hand, are not all isonemal.
A
(n,s)
-satin is defined by an
n\times n
box where each row consists of a single under-crossing, and there are
s
over-crossings between the under-crossings in neighboring rows.
The (5,2)-satin in
figure
2
c
is isonemal, whereas a (7,2)-satin is not
[
8
]
.
For triaxial weaves, the Kagome in
figure
2
e
is the simplest isonemal pattern
[
11
]
.

Figure
2
f
shows a comparative example of two basket weaves.
While the
2\times 2
basket weave is isonemal, the
3\times 3
basket weave (as well as
n\times n
for
n\geq 3
) is not.
Here, the difference is clear: each crossing in the
2\times 2
basket weave neighbors two over-crossings and two under-crossings, whereas the
3\times 3
basket weave also has crossings that have all under-crossing or all over-crossing neighbors, resulting in families of yarns that cannot be exchanged by an isometry.
This hints at a deep connection between the isonemal property and layer group symmetries
[
70
]
.

4.2
Knits

Knitting is fundamentally different from weaving, both on a structural level and in its creation: knitting machines use hooks and hand-knitters just require a pair of knitting needles to create fabric.
While the unit cells of weaves are crossing strands, the unit cells of knitted fabrics are linked loops. Here and throughout this review, we focus on weft knitting. The linking process for weft knitting occurs in three stages:

1.

A row of connected loops is made by tying a series of slip knots (casting on)

2.

For each of the loops in the first row, a new loop is created and linked to the existing loop. After completion of this process for every loop in the first row, the process is repeated until the fabric achieves the desired length (knitting).

3.

The loops of the final row are combined into a knot that ensures the fabric does not unravel (casting off)

In weft knitting, there is no need for warp yarns: in its most basic form, only one yarn is used at a time. Thus, instead of distinguishing fabric directions by warp and weft, the term
course
is used to describe the row-wise direction along which arrays of loops are introduced into the working yarn, and the term
wale
is used to describe the column-wise direction where loops are brought through each other.
To join two loops, either yarn may be pulled from the back of the fabric, resulting in a
knit stitch
, or the yarn may be pulled from the front, resulting in a
purl stitch
.
The underlying structure of these stitches is identical, depending only on the orientation of the fabric; a knit or
K
-stitch may be reflected or transformed by a
\pi
-rotation of the fabric about the wale axis to create a purl or
P
-stitch, as shown in
figure
3
a
.
While there are other types of stitches, such as twisted versions of the
K
and
P
stitches, most of the common knitted fabrics are made from these two. As a result, much like the two states of crossings in woven fabrics, it is possible to enumerate the
K
/
P
-based two-periodic knitting patterns using a square grid.
The canonical knitting patterns are shown in
figure
3

:
plain knit
fabric (or “stockinette” for hand-knitters, “jersey” for machine-knitters) consists of a lattice of all
K
-stitches (or all
P
-stitches when viewed from the reverse side);
garter
fabric alternates between courses of
K
-stitch and courses of
P
-stitch;
rib
fabric alternates between wales of
K
-stitch and wales of
P
-stitch;
seed
fabric has a checkerboard arrangement of
K

P

Notably, not only do these fabrics have distinct textures, but they also have remarkably distinct mechanical properties
[
59
,
71
]
.

Similar to woven fabrics, one can define a rectangular unit cell for each of these patterns.
The unit cell for plain knit fabric, shown in
figure
3

, consists of a single yarn segment.
Each of the course-wise connecting arcs bridges a mirror plane, represented by a red line.
There are also sets of two-fold rotation axes that are oriented perpendicular to the plane of the fabric; when combined with the mirror planes, these rotation axes give rise to glide planes that cut through the fabric and are oriented along the course direction.
All of these are consistent with the symmetry group pma2, which is also one of the 17 wallpaper groups.
By contrast, garter stitch has a unit cell containing a pair of
K
and
P
stitches.
Since
K
and
P
stitches are related by reversing the fabric, this gives rise to a glide symmetry through the plane of the fabric, as shown in
figure
3
d
.
When combined with the mirror planes, the result is a set of in-plane screw axes.
Finally, one set of the two-fold rotation axes is replaced with a set of inversion centers.
Unlike plain knit fabric, garter fabric contains symmetries through the thickness of the fabric and is therefore described by a layer group, pbma, which is not one of the wallpaper groups
[
72
,
66
]
.

Rib and seed fabrics, shown in
figure
3
e-f
have
K
and
P
stitches that neighbor each other in the course direction.
As a result, compared to plain knit and garter fabrics, one mirror plane is lost.
However, there is an additional in-plane glide symmetry along the course direction, with the seed fabric possessing a second glide symmetry along the wale direction.
Moreover, there are in-plane two-fold axes that serve as mechanisms to convert between
K
and
P
stitches.
The seed fabric has an additional set of in-plane screw axes that are aligned with the remaining mirror planes.
The rib fabric is described by layer group pm2a, and the seed fabric by layer group cm2e.
Note that neither of these fabrics has a two-fold axis out of the plane, unlike the plain knit and garter, or any of the woven fabrics that we have considered in this review.
There is a polar character to these fabrics that has been under-appreciated and may be of interest in the context of polar mechanical metamaterials
[
73
]
.

The topology of knitted fabrics is also distinct from that of woven fabrics.
As shown by the plain knit and rib stitch, a single closed loop can be linked with both of the generators of
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I
while additionally forming a knot with itself.
The garter and seed fabrics involve a second closed yarn loop that links the other yarn loop while giving rise to an additional knot component.
The structure of knits has been described by topological frameworks in
[
13
]
and
[
10
]
; the latter work finds a topological classification for all structures that can possibly be knit.

5
FABRIC MECHANICS AND APPLICATIONS

Although yarns and fabrics have been used for millennia for their robust mechanical properties, quantitative descriptions of their mechanics still face enormous difficulties: the emergent behavior of fabrics is generally nonlinear and anisotropic, with effective bulk properties that depend on geometric and topological features appearing at the same length scale as the fabric thickness.
The manufacturing process of ordered textiles fixes their topology, creating yarn crossing constraints that are not localized in the yarn path; this contributes greatly to computational cost in yarn-level descriptions
[
74
]
.
These topological constraints are difficult to elide because they determine the structural integrity of the fabric; in knits in particular, force is largely transmitted through yarn contact points between locally non-contiguous segments of yarn.
Additionally, the length scale of individual stitches or weaving motifs is typically comparable to the length scale of the yarn thickness.

5.1
Wovens

Biaxial woven structures induce extremely anisotropic in-plane mechanics
[
90
]
.
If one tries to pull in the warp or weft direction, parallel to the yarns, the principal modes of deformation are the tensile and compressive response of the yarns
[
91
,
92
]
, resulting in great in-plane stiffness.
The response is determined by the geometry of the serpentine path (
crimp
) that perpendicular yarn segments are forced to adopt when they are woven under tension.
Under conditions of mechanical equilibrium without external stress, the crimp adopts a characteristic spacing and curvature for both warp and weft yarns.
However, under uniaxial extension, for example, along the weft direction as shown in
figure
4
a
, the yarns aligned in the extended direction straighten, which in turn forces the perpendicular fibers to increase the amplitude of their out-of-plane undulations, resulting in a positive 2D Poisson effect.

If one pulls in a direction not aligned with the yarns, the yarns can rotate around their point of contact with only minute deformations, resulting in very soft shear modes
[
93
]
, illustrated in
figure
4
b
.
As shown in
figure
4
c
, the elastic stiffness is dominated by the uniaxial response, which is determined by crimp interchange for low strains,
\epsilon\lesssim\mathcal{O}(10^{-1})
.
For higher strains, the fabric rapidly strain-stiffens, with the modulus determined by the extensional stiffness of individual yarns.
Despite the near-negligible cost of uniform shear deformations, frictional contacts play a significant role, leading to highly hysteretic responses
[
94
,
95
]
.

Weaves are very slender sheets so their out-of-plane deformations much softer than their in-plane deformations
[
96
]
.
A woven sheet can be abstracted as rectangles formed by the intertwined warp and weft yarns.
The allowed curvatures compatible with the in-plane mechanics of the weave must keep the length of the rectangles’ sides fixed (inextensibility of the yarns) while the angles between the sides freely vary (shear soft mode).
This leads to a formal relation between the low-energy deformed shapes of woven fabrics and the mathematics of Chebyshev nets or gridshells
[
97
,
98
]
.
In-plane patterning can designed such that the fabric will wrap complex shapes with single or multiple layers of woven fabrics, which is of utmost importance for industrial applications of textiles-reinforced composites
[
99
,
100
]
.

The mechanical response of biaxial weaves is thus primarily determined by the inextensibility and slenderness of the yarns and possible rotation at the contacts.
Consequently, biaxial weave mechanics is only marginally sensitive to the type of weave (plain, twill, etc) and the corresponding intertwinement pattern.
The type of weave, however, is the primary factor influencing the touch feeling of fabrics, a crucial property for any garment application
[
101
]
.
However, if one relaxes the strictly orthogonal warp/weft structure of biaxial weaves and allows for the introduction of defects, then the 3D structure and mechanics can be greatly altered.
This is illustrated by the case of woven baskets, where angle deficits enable the creation of positive Gaussian curvature defects—namely, corners—which are significantly stiffer than the surrounding biaxially-woven structure
[
102
]
.
Woven textiles are an ideal playground for designing 3D structures through the use of disclination defects
[
103
,
104
]
.

Triaxial textile weaves have a long history as a traditional crafting method, notably in basket weaving.
Adding a series of yarns in a third direction considerably stiffens the structure, as the shear mode now also involves yarn stretching.
Triaxial weaves are usually crafted from ribbons instead of threads with circular cross-sections to allow the structure to help maintain a high cover factor with minimal out-of-plane bending of the yarns at the crossings.
Ribbons can only bend out of their plane, imposing strict in-plane geometrical constraints: ribbons must follow the geodesics of the surfaces they embed.
In contrast with biaxial structures, the pattern of triaxial weaves directly dictates their 3D shape.
Weaving the ribbons into hexagonal patterns generates flat surfaces, but introducing topological defects like pentagons or heptagons leads to dome- and saddle-shaped surfaces, respectively
[
105
]
.
The topology of the triaxial weaves and the in-plane curvature of the ribbons impose the apparent Gaussian curvature of the weaves
[
106
]
, which in turn dramatically affects the mechanical response of the woven surface
[
107
]
.

5.2
Knits

Unlike woven fabrics, knits tend to have soft in-plane and out-of-plane mechanical responses due to highly curved yarn paths within knitted stitches, which allow them to significantly deform without substantial extension of the yarn along its length.
Large yarn curvatures (typically at a similar length scale to their thickness) create geometric nonlinearities in their mechanics
[
32
]
, and knits display an abundance of yarn contacts at nontrivial angles, requiring friction forces to be modeled for quantitative accuracy even at low tensions
[
108
]
.
In addition to generalized continuum approaches also used for woven fabrics
[
109
]
, simulation methods for knits include multiscale constitutive modeling relating the complex three-dimensional structure of knitted loops with fabric-scale deformations
[
110
,
111
,
112
,
84
,
85
]
as well as yarn-level simulations
[
58
,
113
,
77
,
59
,
71
]
.
Despite the complexity involved in computational models, there is a long history of using geometric models to establish basic results governing stress-strain relationships and fabric stiffness
[
114
,
115
,
116
,
117
,
118
,
119
,
120
,
121
,
59
]
.

To outline a basic formulation of this problem, consider the relaxed configuration of an individual stitch, which is controlled by the tension under which the yarn was knit (or the length of yarn per stitch) as well as the boundary conditions imposed by surrounding stitches.
Using the yarn’s bending modulus
B
and the yarn tension
T
, one can define a
bending length

\ell_{\rm bend}\equiv\sqrt{B/T}
, which characterizes the lengthscale over which curvature is localized.
If the fabric is knit under low tension, the stitch curvature is relatively delocalized and approaches the characteristic upper lengthscale of the stitch geometry, which is controlled by the length of yarn per stitch
L
.
If the fabric is knit under high tension or an external stress is applied, the bending length decreases, approaching a characteristic lower lengthscale of the stitch, namely the yarn radius
r
.
As shown in
figure
4
d
, the curvature transitions from being delocalized throughout the stitch to being localized to the cross-over regions.
Moreover, the length
\ell_{\rm bend}
can serve as a type of interaction lengthscale between stitches: for large
\ell_{\rm bend}
, the pattern of stitches determines the mechanical response of the knit, whereas for small
\ell_{\rm bend}
, the mechanical response is determined by the stiffness of the yarn and the contact mechanics at the clasp regions between pairs of yarn segments
[
75
]
.
The pattern dependence has a rather simple rule: if two neighboring stitches are different (
K
-
P
neighbors) then there is a soft deformation mode that exists due to a near-rigid rotation of the connecting segment, as shown in F
figure
4
e
; if the neighboring stitches are the same (
K
-
K
or
P
-
P
neighbors) then the stiffness is roughly an order of magnitude larger
[
59
]
, as shown schematically in the stress-strain plot in
figure
4

.
This model confirms that the basic trends observed for knitted fabrics come from stitch geometry: plain knit is uniformly the stiffest and while rib has an extreme soft deformation when stretched in the course direction, garter and seed are the softest when stretched in the wale direction
[
59
,
71
]
.

Beyond the initial linear regime, knitted fabrics have an extended nonlinear elastic response where they smoothly strain-stiffen, exhibiting the usual J-shaped curve typical of amorphous fiber networks found in biological tissue
[
122
]
.
For inextensible yarn, this strain-stiffening eventually asymptotes at a maximum strain cutoff
\varepsilon_{\rm max}
, as shown in
figure
4

, beyond which the yarn fails; if the yarn is extensible, then this asymptote is not reached and a second, high-stress linear regime is reached, where the stiffness is governed by the extensional modulus of the yarn
[
71
]
.
The divergence of the stress as
\varepsilon\to\varepsilon_{\rm max}
in fabrics made from inextensible yarn follows the form
\sigma\sim(\varepsilon_{\rm max}-\varepsilon)^{-2}
, which can be derived as an asymptotic limit of an inextensible elastica model of yarn, where curvature is increasingly localized to infinitesimal regions under increasing tension
[
59
]
.
Notably, this same asymptotic force law is obeyed by DNA and other wormlike polymer chains as they are stretched to their maximum length, also this describes the reduction in conformational entropy under high tension rather than curvature localization effects
[
123
]
.

5.2.1
Analogies with granular mechanics

Besides sharing common features with fiber networks, the importance of contact mechanics in knitted fabrics results in similarities with granular mechanics.
An additional stiff regime can be observed at low stresses when the knitted fabric is knit in a tight or “jammed” configuration.
This jammed regime is dominated by a combination of contact mechanics and large bending stresses, but softens to the usual pattern-dependent regime after a sufficiently large applied stress
\sigma\gtrsim\sigma_{\rm jam}
as shown in
figure
4
g
.
This jammed regime has been shown to smoothly vanish (
\sigma_{\rm jam}\to 0
) as the length of yarn per stitch is increased, indicating that it is a density or volume-fraction controlled transition, similar to granular jamming
[
124
]
.

Interestingly, the analogy with granular materials also extends to the observation of
crackling noise
, which can be found in the force fluctuations measured in earthquakes and amorphous solids.
Such materials respond to external loads by local yielding events, which redistribute accumulated stresses to neighboring regions, increasing the probability that these regions also yield.
Deformations then proceed via avalanching yield events with typical power-law statistics
[
125
]
.
As a loose-knitted fabric is slowly stretched, the stress-strain curve starts to exhibit small-scale stress drops of various sizes, as illustrated in
figure
4
h
.
These stress drops are caused by local stick-slip instabilities at the yarn contacts, which redistribute stress anisotropically, creating extended slip lines. Even though the knit structure is regular, frictional instabilities create analogs of local yielding events in amorphous solids that avalanche into large-scale events with power-law statistics
[
52
]
.

This combination of fiber and granular mechanics places knits and wovens as examples within the emerging field of “elastogranular materials,” which involves the overlap of slender body mechanics, granular packing, and tensegrity
[
126
,
127
,
128
]
.

5.2.2
Hysteresis and memory of mechanical response

The tensile mechanical response of knitted fabric shows significant hysteresis, highlighting the dissipative nature of the deformation process.
Attempts to model and predict this hysteretic behavior invoked either viscoelasticity
[
129
]
or dry friction
[
51
]
.
Recent work, upon observing the independence of this effect on applied strain rate, hypothesized that this effect comes mostly from plastic deformation with a small viscoelastic effect
[
130
]
.

The latter study
[
130
]
also observed and modeled return point memory in knitted fabrics. An analogous memory effect was first observed in ferromagnets
[
131
]
.
In this type of memory, the fabric “remembers” the load at the maximum strain reached during the tensile test (the return point).
The fabric “forgets” the return point when that maximum strain is exceeded and instead remembers the new return point. This effect was observed to be independent of applied strain rates.

Another expression of the memory effect is the multiple aspect ratios that knits can adopt at rest.
The multiplicity of rest shapes poses important challenges for industry in predicting the surface of fabric from a given length of yarn, fabrication parameters, and post-processing
[
54
]
. One source of this metastability is friction at the contacts, which traps internal stresses and prevents the fabric from relaxing to its minimum energy state
[
53
]
. Depending on the deformation history, the configuration can get stuck due to friction at various aspect ratios, thereby encoding memory in the knit’s rest shape.

5.2.3
Anisotropic fracture in knits

The local structure of yarn paths within a knit fabric also leads to highly anisotropic and heterogeneous patterns under mechanical failure. When a yarn tears locally in a knit fabric, biaxial tension on the fabric may propagate
runs
or
ladders
from the initial breakage site, creating defects in the loop structure. Under biaxial tension, yarn breakage allows for two primary modes of defect propagation: (1) reptation of the newly created free ends, reducing the total number of loops in a row and thereby releasing tension in the
y
direction; and (2) loops that are no longer topologically constrained by a vertically neighboring loop may pull taut horizontally, releasing tension in the
x
direction. Due to the relative amount of friction required to be overcome in each mode of propagation, loop defects typically travel much farther in the wale (
y
) direction than the course (
x
) direction, leaving long ladder-like scars in the fabric.
The topology of the knit pattern restricts how a fracture propagates. The initial damage, typically a yarn breaking missed loop, modifies the topology of the knit, creating an effective
topological defect
. Yet, the subsequent damage propagation conserves the topology and is then limited to Reidemeister moves of the yarn
[
132
]
.
Recent work has begun to quantify fracture propagation in weft knit composites
[
133
]
, although this phenomenon has been seldom studied outside of sartorial or forensic contexts
[
134
,
135
]
. Warp knits, due to their yarns’ vertical orientation, resist laddering and are often used in applications requiring wear resistance under biaxial tension.

Curling of knit fabric

Without external forces or postprocessing, weft knitted fabrics also have a bulk tendency to curl dramatically at the fabric scale

5.2.5
Patterned geometry and mechanics

Beyond the local structure of jersey stitches, the larger-scale patterning of knit and purl stitches can have profound effects on the geometry and mechanics of a weft knitted fabric. SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK

In this review, we have examined woven and knitted fabrics through a lens that is shaped by current interests in the geometry and topology of condensed matter as well as soft mechanical metamaterials.
Similar to the realm of polymer physics, the basic building block of a textile is not a roughly convex particle or atom, but a staple fiber or filament.
Microstructure is not directly determined by covalent or metallic interactions, but by twisted bundles, friction, links, and knots.
Continuing an ascent towards the macroscale, we described efforts to rationalize how the mechanics of yarn crossings and larger-scale patterns determines the collective mechanics and shape of the fabric.

Despite the long history of textiles, there are plenty of open questions that are amenable to the tools and concepts of condensed matter.
These questions lie at all length scales of these materials, from changes in fiber packing due to clasps between different yarn segments, to a complete characterization of defects in knitted fabrics.
There is also an opportunity to push into new frontiers of textiles by attempting to generalize the notion of a knitted stitch
[
145
]
, revisiting the design of chainmail
[
146
,
147
]
, or creating higher-dimensional generalizations of traditionally 2D textiles
[
148
,
149

.
Finally, it is worth considering the grand questions of how to design bespoke garments and materials at the level of individual stitches and how to use these principles to design linked materials from the architectural scale to the molecular.
While there is much to explore, it is essential to remember that there is tremendous expertise that has been developed and curated within crafting circles and that much that we do is to simply translate that understanding into the language and formalism of our field before new contributions can be made to the understanding of textiles.
In doing so, there is the opportunity for physics to be reciprocally enriched by the weavers’ and knitters’ view of topology, symmetry, and mechanics.
