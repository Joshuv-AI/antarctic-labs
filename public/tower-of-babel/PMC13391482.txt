Synthesis, characterisation and electrochemical behaviour of calcium carbonate nanoparticles derived from automobile oxy-acetylene gas welding waste
Authors: Nnabuk Okon Eddy, Sunday Nweze, Ifeanyi Samson Eze, Rajni Garg, Raymond Taziwa, Hamimullah Watandost
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In actualizing the management of this toxic waste, resource recovery was designed to convert the Ca(OH)2 rich waste (generated by automobile welding process) to calcium carbonate nanoparticles (CaCO3NPs). The synthesized nanoparticles were characterized using XRF, SEM–EDX, XRD, FTIR, XPS, TGA/DTA, BET, DFT and EIS. Results from XRF and SEM–EDX analyses showed calcium as the dominant element, with CaO and Ca contents of 98.59 wt% and 84.30 wt%, respectively. However, based on XRD, XPS, XRF and SEM–EDX results, minor impurities, including Si Al, Fe, and Mg, were also detected at concentrations less than 0.2% respectively.. SEM micrographs also showed rough, irregular, partially agglomerated surfaces and features that reflect heterogeneous particle sizes. XRD patterns identified a dominant calcite phase (~ 81%) and minor vaterite (< 19%), with crystallite sizes ranging from 12.0 to 21.6 nm (average 16.28 nm) while the FTIR spectrum showed characteristic carbonate vibrations at 1507.71, 1149.88, and 877.79 cm⁻1, that futher confirms high purity. Thermal analysis of the nanoparticles indicated initial weight loss of less than 3% below 200 °C due to adsorbed moisture, and complete decomposition between 600 and 850 °C, with an activation energy of 178 kJ/mol and estimated purity of 97.75%. BET and DFT analyses revealed high surface areas (134 m2/g, BET; 36.6 m2/g, DFT), mesoporous structure, average pore diameter of 2.65 nm, pore volume of 0.16678 m3/g, and surface area-to-volume ratio of 819 m⁻1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy in 0.5 M H2SO4 showed two relaxation processes in Nyquist plots. The first relaxation represented a high-frequency grain boundary processes having Rct = 4.0 × 10⁶ Ω, C = 3.98 × 10⁻⁹ F, τ = 0.016 s, θmin = 43°, n = 0.38, Q = 1.66 × 10⁻⁸ S sn, and Cdl = 204 μF, indicating dispersive capacitive behaviour and significant interfacial polarization. However, the low-frequency bulk charge transfer processes had Rct = 2.4 × 10⁷ Ω, C = 2.10 × 10⁻13 F, τ = 5.03 × 10⁻⁶ s, θmin = 73°, n = 0.81, Q = 1.36 × 10⁻12 S sn, and Cdl = 34.3 μF, indicating high resistance and dielectric behaviour. Frequency-dependent capacitance analysis further confirmed strong double-layer formation at low frequencies and intrinsic insulating characteristics at high frequencies. The combination of nanoscale dimensions, high surface area, mesoporosity, thermal stability, wide bandgap (6.01 eV), and distinctive electrochemical behaviour indicated that the CaCO3NPs has potentials for dielectric fillers, corrosion-resistant coatings, adsorbents, and inert supports in electrochemical applications.

Introduction

Several nations are faced with challenges associated with waste management regarding cost, best disposal options, environmental and other barriers. Among numerous options that have been practised over the years, resource recovery, reuse and recycling have proven to be the best options, because they have the capacity to provide multiple solutions to environmental and industrial challenges, cost reduction and reductions in the volume of wastes [49].

The acetylene gas has properties that favour its application in welding metallic components of automobiles through the generation of a high temperature, localized flame, rapid combustion/energy release, controllability, reducing properties and compatibility with various metals [66, 74]. The consequence of Eq. 1 is that calcium hydroxide, which is a by-product of the process, is normally discarded into the surrounding environment without immediate usage. The generated calcium hydroxide-rich waste can negatively impact the soil and water, either directly or through interaction with other contaminants in the environment [1]. Although some beneficial roles of low Ca(OH)2 have been reported, such as the maintenance of soil pH, promotion of soil aggregation and nutrient availability [67]. There are supporting literature that have shown that at moderate concentration, the presence of Ca(OH)2 can lead to several environmental challenges including the suppresion of microbial activity in the soil, acceleration of the deficiency of essential elements (such as Fe, P, Mn, etc.), obstruction of soil aeration, reduction in the rate of nitrification and enhancement of the degradation of soil structure and associated consequences [81]. This will also provide an avenue for effective management of the automobile waste byproduct. To the best of our knowledge, this specific waste stream has not been reported before. The employment of automobile waste for the production of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{C}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{O}}_{3}\mathrm{N}\mathrm{P}s$$\end{document} has some advantages over most sources, such as biogenic (egg and crustacean shells), because resources are recovered from a toxic source, unlike biogenic and other materials. Materials and methods

Materials

The major materials that were useful for the study were Na2CO3 and waste materials containing Ca(OH)2 (obtained from some automobile welding workshop in Mechanical village, Apo, Abuja in Nigeria. The waste samples were obtained directly from the discharge tank to avoid contamination if disposed to the environment. The sample was dried under the sun and in the oven for further removal of moisture. The prepared sample was stored in a dissicator.

Methods

10 g of the Ca(OH)2 rich waste was concentrated in deionized water to obtained uniform Ca(OH)2 suspension. 2.65 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate were dissolved in 250 mL of deionized water to obtain a 0.1 M Na2CO3 solution. The Na2CO3 solution was reacted with the Ca(OH)2 suspension in a beaker and continuously stirred at room temperature to enhance dissolution and fasten the reaction rate to completion. The observed reaction produced calcium carbonate precipitate according to the following equation\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{C}\mathrm{a}\left(\mathrm{O}\mathrm{H}\right)}_{2}+ {\mathrm{N}\mathrm{a}}_{2}{\mathrm{C}\mathrm{O}}_{3} \to 2\mathrm{N}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{O}\mathrm{H}+ {\mathrm{C}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{O}}_{3}$$\end{document}

The precipitated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${CaCO}_{3}$$\end{document} particles were separated from the NaOH by vacuum filtration and were washed to ensure that all the NaOH (which is soluble in water) were removed. After precipitation, the products were subjected to vaccum filtration and extensively washed several times with deionized water, seeing that NaOH is highly soluble in water, repeated washing ensured effective removal of NaOH. Therefore, the washing process as reported in the manuscript serves as the (ii) Thermal treatment (calcination control). The completeness of washing was confirmed by testing the filtrate with pH paper and phenolphthalein indicator. The absence of a pink colouration and a neutral pH indicated that residual hydroxides had been effectively removed.

After washing, the filtrates were dried in an oven set to 80 °C for 4 h. The dried calcium carbonate was finally calcinated at 650 °C to convert it to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{C}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{O}}_{3}\mathrm{N}\mathrm{P}s$$\end{document} and to removed any residual or other unwater adsorbed materials. The calcination temperature of 650 °C was selected based on pretrial experiments, which showed that close to this temperature, the materials undergo conversion to nanoparticles. This temperature is sufficient to induce controlled thermal activation without complete structural collapse of the nanoparticles.

The synthesis route adopted in this study is inherently scalable, as it involves simple precipitation and filtration steps that can be readily translated to large-scale continuous or batch processing systems. The use of readily available Ca(OH)2-rich waste as a precursor further supports scalability by ensuring a consistent and low-cost raw material supply.

Characterization

The following characterisation techniques were employed to ascertain the product composition and other functional characteristics, namely, UV–visible, Fourier transformed infrared (FTIR, X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray fluorescence (XRF), SEM–EDX, XPS, TGA/DTA and BET.

UV–visible spectroscopy

The analysis of the optical properties of the CaCO3 nanoparticles was carried out using Shimadzu UV-1800 UV–Visible Spectrophotometer. About 10 mg of the dried CaCO3 nanoparticles was dispersed in 100 mL of deionized water to obtain a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. The suspension was ultrasonicated for 20 min to ensure uniform dispersion. Approximatly, 3 mL of the dispersion was transferred into a quartz cuvette using a 1 cm path length.

The absorbance spectrum was recorded over the wavelength range of 200–800 nm at room temperature, using deionized water as the blank reference. The recorded values were used to plot a graph of absorbance against wavelength in order to evaluate the maximum wavelength of absorption and the band gap.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The crystalline structure, phase composition, and crystallite nature of the synthesized CaCO3 nanoparticles were investigated using an Rigaku SmartLab X-ray Diffractometer operated with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), manufactured by Rigaku.

About 1.0 g of the finely powdered sample was gently ground using an agate mortar and pestle to ensure uniform particle size and then mounted onto a low-background sample holder. The surface was flattened to minimize preferred orientation effects prior to analysis [7]. The diffraction data were collected over a 2θ range of 10°–80° using a step size of 0.02° and a scanning speed of 2°/min under standard laboratory conditions. The operating voltage and current were maintained at 40 kV and 30 mA, respectively. Phase identification and qualitative analysis were performed using SmartLab Studio II, specifically the Search/Match (Qualitative Analysis) module, by comparing the obtained diffraction peaks with standard reference patterns from the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) database.

The resulting diffractogram was used to confirm the crystalline phase of CaCO3 and to identify its polymorphic form(s) (e.g., calcite, aragonite, or vaterite). The average crystallite size was estimated using the Scherrer equation based on the most intense diffraction peaks.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis

The FTIR analysis to detect functional groups and chemical bonding of the synthesized CaCO3NPs wereconducted using a Agilent Cary 630 FTIR Spectrometer (Manufacturer: Agilent Technologies), operated with MicroLab FTIR Software. 3 mg of the finely ground CaCO3NPs was directly placed onto the attenuated total reflectance (ATR) crystal. The sample was gently pressed to ensure good contact with the crystal surface for optimal signal acquisition.

The spectra were recorded over a wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm⁻1 with a resolution of 4 cm⁻1, and 32 scans were accumulated for each measurement to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. A background spectrum was collected prior to sample analysis under identical conditions and automatically subtracted.

X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis

The elemental composition of the synthesized CaCO3 nanoparticles was determined using an energy-dispersive X-ray fluorescence spectrometer, Xenemetrix X-Calibur EDXRF Spectrometer (Manufacturer: Xenemetrix), operated with XRS-FP Analysis Software (CrossRoads Scientific).

About 0.5 g of the dried sample of the CaCO3NPs was finely ground and pressed into a uniform pellet using a hydraulic press to ensure a smooth and homogeneous surface suitable for analysis. The pelletized sample was placed in the sample holder and analyzed under ambient conditions.

The instrument was calibrated using appropriate standard reference materials prior to measurement. Elemental analysis was performed over a broad energy range to detect both major and trace elements present in the sample. Each measurement was conducted with an acquisition time of 120–300 s to ensure sufficient counting statistics.

The obtained spectra were processed using the fundamental parameters method embedded in the analysis software to quantify the elemental composition.

Scanning electron microscopy and energy dispersive X-ray (SEM–EDX) analysis

The morphology and elemental composition of the synthesized CaCO3 nanoparticles were examined using a Phenom Desktop SEM equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) detector (Phenom XL G2 SEM). About 5 mg of the dried calcium carbonate nanoparticle (CaCO3NPs) sample was evenly dispersed onto a carbon-coated aluminum stub. The sample was gently pressed to ensure proper adhesion and minimize particle agglomeration. The sample was sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity and improve image quality.

The SEM analysis was conducted under high vacuum conditions at an accelerating voltage of 10–15 kV. Micrographs were obtained at different magnifications to evaluate the surface morphology, particle size, and degree of agglomeration of the nanoparticles. For elemental analysis, EDX measurements were performed at selected regions of the sample. The spectra obtained were used to identify and quantify the elemental composition of the nanoparticles.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis

The surface chemical composition and oxidation states of the synthesized CaCO3 nanoparticles were analyzed using an Kratos AXIS Ultra DLD X-ray Photoelectron Spectrometer. Approximately 103 mg of the finely powdered sample was mounted onto a conductive carbon tape attached to the sample holder. The sample surface was gently pressed to ensure uniform distribution and good electrical contact, thereby minimizing charging effects during analysis.

The measurements were carried out under ultra-high vacuum conditions (~ 10⁻⁹ mbar) using a monochromatic Al Kα radiation source (hν = 1486.6 eV). Survey scans were recorded over a binding energy range of 0–1200 eV, followed by high-resolution scans for Ca 2p, C 1 s, and O 1 s, to determine their chemical states. Charge neutralization was applied during analysis to compensate for surface charging, and all spectra were calibrated with respect to the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV. The obtained spectra were processed using appropriate background subtraction and peak fitting procedures to deconvolute overlapping peaks and assign chemical states.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal stability and decomposition behaviour of the synthesized CaCO3 nanoparticles were evaluated using a PerkinElmer TGA 4000 Thermogravimetric Analyzer equipped for simultaneous thermogravimetric (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA). About 8.0 mg of the dried nanoparticle sample was accurately weighed and placed in a platinum crucible. The analysis was performed under a nitrogen atmosphere at a flow rate of 20 mL/min to minimize oxidative effects.

The sample was programed at a constant heating rate of 10 °C/min to progressively raised the temperature from 30 °C to 900 °C. The TGA curve was used to monitor weight loss as a function of temperature, while the DTA curve provided information on the corresponding thermal events (endothermic and exothermic transitions). The prominent weight loss observed at elevated temperatures, accompanied by an endothermic peak in the DTA curve, was attributed to the thermal decomposition of CaCO3 into calcium oxide (CaO) and carbon dioxide (CO2). This thermal behaviour was used to confirm the composition and assess the thermal stability of the synthesized nanoparticles.

BET surface area analysis

Surface analysis of the nanoparticles for BET surface area, pore size and other properties were carried out using a Quantachrome Nova 4200e Surface Area Analyzer. The analysis was conducted using approximately 0.20–0.30 g of the dried sample. The sample was weighed into the sample tube and degassed under vacuum at 120 °C for 5 h to remove moisture and adsorbed gases.

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements were carried out at 77 K. The specific surface area and other properties were calculated using various adsorption isotherms such as BET and density function theory ( DFT) isotherms.

Electrochemical investigations

The electrochemical measurements were performed using a conventional three-electrode cell configuration connected to a computer-controlled potentiostat/galvanostat (specify model and manufacturer). The working electrode was prepared by coating a conductive substrate with a thin film of automobile welding waste deribed calcium carbonate nanoparticles (CaCO3NPs). To prepare the working electrode, a known amount of CaCO3NP powder was dispersed in ethanol containing 5 wt% polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) as a binder, followed by sonication for 30 min to obtain a uniform slurry. The slurry was drop-cast onto a polished glassy carbon disk electrode (diameter 3 mm) and allowed to dry under ambient conditions. The electrode surface was then gently rinsed with deionized water to remove loosely bound particles and dried before use. The geometric surface area of the coated electrode was recorded for capacitance calculations.

A platinum wire served as the counter electrode, while an Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) electrode was used as the reference electrode. All potentials were reported for this reference. The electrolyte was 0.5 M H2SO4, prepared from analytical-grade concentrated sulphuric acid diluted with deionized water. Before each measurement, the electrolyte was deaerated by purging with nitrogen gas for at least 15 min to minimize dissolved oxygen interference.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted over a frequency range from 105 Hz to 10⁻2 Hz with an AC perturbation amplitude of 10 mV (rms) superimposed on the open-circuit potential. The impedance spectra were recorded as Nyquist, Bode, phase angle, and imaginary impedance plots, from which relevant electrochemical parameters were extracted according to Eqs. (18)–(26). Capacitance–frequency measurements were obtained from the imaginary component of the impedance at each frequency, enabling analysis of frequency-dependent dielectric and interfacial properties. All measurements were conducted at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). Each experiment was repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility, and representative data are reported.

The slight difference between the two sets of data is because Tauc’s model is based on selectivity between direct, indirect and forbidden bandgaps, while the Planck model is based on the overall bandgap. However, both models confirm that the nanoparticles have a relatively wider bandgap, which is typical for insulators or transparent substances. Several reports have ascertained that CaCO3NPs have an indirect bandgap because their electronic transition is fulfilled by a change in momentum, when phnons iteract (a lattice vibration) to achieve the principle of conservation of momentum [8]. The present results also suggest that the transition of electrons from the valence to the conduction band can only occur through an indirect process compared to a direct transition, where some of the electrons remain unchanged during the transition [53]. Some literature values for the bandgap of CaCO3NPs are in close alignment wth our results. For example, Hossain et al. [29] stated a direct bandgap of 6.0 eV and an indirect bandgap of about 4.4 ± 0.35 eV for CaCO3, which is in strong agreement with the present results. Also, a bandgap of 5.36 eV was reported by Ghadam & Idrees [26], who synthesized CaCO3 nanoparticles using a reverse microemulsion technique. However, lower bandgap values have been reported in some studies involving different synthesis routes or material modifications. Such as 3.36 eV reported by Ramasamy et al. [60]. On the other hand, band gap as high as 7.1 eV has been reported by Janovec et al. [31], who used advanced theoretical methods to predict this value as a representatives of of an ideal crystalline calcite with minimal structural or compositional defects. Consequently, the high bandgap (~ 6.0 eV) observed in this study is consistent with theoretical and experimental reports. The minimal deviation can be attributed to the source of the raw materials, nanoparticle size, the presence of polymorph and finely oxidised carbonate phases with reduced surface contamination. The consequence is that the formed nanoparticle may favour quantum confinement effects, depending on the particle size. This could explain the observed difference between this study and others that employed bulk or doped forms of CaCO3. The results further suggest that the CaCO3NPs may not be directly useful in optical applications but can be functional as UV blockers or in UV-based optoelectronic devices, formulation of composites, in drug delivery, energy applications, as sensors and in quantum confinement turning applications.

TC is a measure of the anisotropy in the crystal growth, a reflection of the relative intensity of diffraction peaks and also an index for ascertaining the preferred crystallographic orientation in the sample [86], Also, differences in orientation can also influence the material’s surface energy, reactivity, and mechanical properties [58]. The calculated TC value, indicates that the material properties of the nanoparticles (such as diffusion, reactivity, and mechanical strength) can be significantly influenced by prevailing orientation. Generally, a TC value equal to 1 indicates a randomly oriented powder, but values greater than 1 indicate preferred orientation (texture)[22]. Based on the results shown in Table 2, the (202) plane shows a strong preferred orientation (TC = 1.857), which inidicates that the preferred direction for crystallite alignment is this direction, probably due to directional growth during nanoparticle formation. However, the (104) and (113) planes show moderate texture with TCs values equal to 0.953 and 1.106, respectively, whereas the (111) and (024) planes demonstrated less preferred orientation (suppression) with r TCs lower than unity. The observed moderate TC values may be due to the presence of impurities in the nanoparticles.

The listed TC values align with the findings observed in the FTIR spectrum of the nanoparticles. This also suggests that the crystalline planes with high TC values is structurally aligned to the internal vibrational modes of the carbonate group, particularly those related to calcite. The absence of significant band splitting or weakening in the spectrum confirms the dominance of a single phase (calcite) in the nanoparticles. Therefore, the agreement between the high TC values for certain planes and the strong and well-defined FTIR bands(representing calcite) validates the (i) structural uniformity and phase composition of the synthesized CaCO3NPs and (ii) exhibition of both molecular and crystallographic anisotropy by the CaCO3NPs.

The EDX spectrum and the data presented in Table 4 indicated that CaCO3 nanoparticles are predominantly enriched with calcium and carbon, with atomic concentrations of 64.17% and 34.85%, respectively, which is consistent with the expected stoichiometry of CaCO3 and validates the successful formation of calcium carbonate nanoparticles. The matching weight percentages (84.30% for Ca and 14.91% for C) also confirm the dominance of calcium due to its higher atomic mass, while the presence of Si (0.32 at.%), Fe (0.23 at.%), Al (0.02 at.%) and Na (0.01 at.% as trace elements indicates minor impurities, originating from the precursor (automobile welding waste). Although these impurities are present at very low concentrations (< 1%), they may influence the physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles by introducing lattice distortions or acting as nucleation modifiers, which aligns with the structural variations observed in the XRD analysis. Also, the absence or negligible detection of other elements such as Mg, K, Ti, P, S and Cl suggests a relatively high purity of the synthesized CaCO3NPs, thereby confirming that the nanomaterial is largely rich in calcium carbonate with only minimal contamination.

Compared with results from the XRF (Table 4), both methods confirmed a high concentration of Ca but in the SEM–EDX, Ca has a weight concentration of 84.30%, while in XRF, calcium oxide (CaO) showed 98.589%. The difference in concentrations is due to the differences in methodologies. XRF measures oxide forms, but SEM–EDX provides elemental data. The presence of carbon is identified in the SEM–EDX results, with an atomic concentration of 34.85%, consistent with the expected composition of calcium carbonate (CaCO3). Both analyses reveal trace concentrations of Si, Mg, Al and Fe. In the XRF data. The primary difference between the two techniques lies in their sensitivity to different elements. XRF is more accurate for heavier elements (e.g., Ca) and reports concentrations as oxides, while SEM–EDX provides direct measurements of elemental composition, including light elements such carbon. Both XRF and EDX results identified calcium as the dominant element, while the differences in concentrations for other elements reflect the inherent analytical limitations and differences in how each technique reports oxides.

Within these equations, resistance terms represent the opposition to charge transfer for each electrochemical process, frequency terms correspond to the characteristic relaxation behavior of the system, and capacitance-related terms describe charge storage and interfacial polarization effects. The inclusion of the constant phase element formalism accounts for non-ideal and dispersive behavior arising from surface heterogeneity, grain boundary effects, and distribution of relaxation times. Collectively, the parameters obtained using Eqs. (32)–(38) provide a coherent interpretation of the impedance response, allowing clear differentiation between grain-boundary-controlled and bulk-dominated charge transport processes and offering insight into the underlying electron transfer kinetics and dielectric properties of the material.

The deviation of the arcs from perfect semicircles indicates non-ideal capacitive behaviour, which can be linked to surface heterogeneity and morphological irregularities of the calcium carbonate nanoparticles [10]. The diameter of each arc along the Z′ axis corresponds to the charge transfer resistance for that process, calculated using Eq. (32) as 4.0 × 10⁶ Ω for the high-frequency process and 2.4 × 10⁷ Ω for the low-frequency process. These values reveal that the low-frequency charge transfer step is significantly more resistive, suggesting sluggish electron transfer kinetics, whereas the high-frequency grain boundary process is relatively more conductive.

The Bode plot in Fig. 8b displays two distinct peaks in the impedance modulus, corresponding to the two relaxation processes observed in the Nyquist plot. The frequencies at which these peaks occur, obtained using Eq. (33), are 10.00 Hz for the high-frequency process and 31,623 Hz for the low-frequency process. These frequencies represent the fmax for each process and are directly related to the capacitances and time constants of the system. The capacitance values, determined from Eq. (34) using the respective Rct and fmax, are 3.98 × 10⁻⁹ F for the high-frequency process and 2.10 × 10⁻13 F for the low-frequency process. The higher capacitance in the high-frequency process is consistent with significant interfacial polarization at the grain boundaries, while the much lower capacitance in the low-frequency process reflects the insulating nature of calcium carbonate in bulk charge transfer. The observed charge transfer resistance values (4.0 × 10⁶–2.4 × 10⁷ Ω) show a direct relationship with the calculated microstrain of the CaCO3NPs. Higher microstrain values correspond to increased lattice distortion and defect density, which enhance electron scattering and consequently increase Rct, particularly in the low-frequency process where charge transfer is more sluggish.

The phase angle versus frequency plot in Fig. 8c provides further insight into the electrochemical behaviour of the system. Two maxima are observed, located at frequencies matching the relaxation processes in the Nyquist and Bode plots. The maximum phase angles θmin, obtained from Eq. (36), are 43° for the high-frequency process and 73° for the low-frequency process. When related to the ideal cases where phase angle is − 90° for a perfect capacitor, 0° for a pure resistor, and + 90° for an inductor, these values reveal significant deviations from ideal capacitive behaviour [38]. The exponent n, calculated from Eq. (37), is 0.38 for the high-frequency process and 0.81 for the low-frequency process. These n values indicate that the high-frequency process behaves as a strongly dispersive constant phase element with substantial heterogeneity, while the low-frequency process is closer to ideal capacitive behaviour, albeit still non-ideal [37].

The imaginary impedance versus frequency plot in Fig. 8d confirms the relaxation frequencies determined from the other plots, with clear peaks at 10.00 and 31,623 Hz, which correspond to the high- and low-frequency processes respectively. These frequencies were also used in calculating the CPE magnitude Q via Eq. (38), yielding 1.66 × 10⁻⁸ S·sⁿ for the high-frequency process and 1.36 × 10⁻12 S·sⁿ for the low-frequency process. The double-layer capacitance Cdl, determined from Eq. (31), is 204 μF for the high-frequency process and 34.3 μF for the low-frequency process. The large Cdl for the high-frequency process reflects substantial double-layer formation at the grain boundaries, while the smaller Cdl for the low-frequency process aligns with the high resistance and low bulk capacitance of calcium carbonate. The relatively high double-layer capacitance (204 μF) at the high-frequency process aligns with reported values for porous dielectric fillers and nanoparticle-based systems, where enhanced surface area and interfacial polarization contribute to increased capacitance. Conversely, the lower capacitance at the low-frequency process reflects the bulk insulating behaviour typical of CaCO3 materials.

The texture coefficient values also correlate with interfacial capacitance behaviour. The (202) plane, which exhibits the highest TC (1.857), is associated with enhanced grain alignment and increased surface exposure, contributing to higher double-layer capacitance (204 μF). In contrast, planes with lower TC values correspond to reduced electroactive surface area and lower capacitance, as observed in the low-frequency process (34.3 μF).

The quantitative parameters from Figs. 8a–d link closely to each other and provide a coherent picture of the electrochemical behaviour. The high-frequency process is characterised by relatively low resistance, higher capacitance, larger double-layer capacitance, and a low n value, pointing to non-ideal capacitive behaviour dominated by surface heterogeneity. In contrast, the low-frequency process exhibits much higher resistance, very low capacitance, smaller double-layer capacitance, and a higher n value closer to unity, indicating a more uniform but highly resistive charge transfer mechanism. The high Rct for the low-frequency process implies strong resistance to electrochemical corrosion in acidic medium, while the relatively high Cdl in the high-frequency process suggests potential for interfacial polarization applications. The combination of these properties confirms that calcium carbonate in this configuration behaves as a highly insulating, corrosion-resistant material with significant dielectric properties at interfaces, making it suitable as a dielectric filler, corrosion barrier, or inert electrode support in electrochemical devices. Although CaCO3 is intrinsically insulating, its electrochemical applicability extends beyond purely dielectric functions due to its significant interfacial polarization and double-layer formation. The relatively high double-layer capacitance (204 μF) observed at the grain boundary region indicates that the material can effectively support charge accumulation at the electrode/electrolyte interface, making it suitable for applications involving interfacial charge storage rather than bulk electron conduction. In such systems, CaCO3NPs function primarily as an electrochemically inactive but structurally and interfacially active material, where their high surface area, porosity, and surface heterogeneity facilitate ion adsorption, interfacial polarization, and dielectric response. These characteristics are particularly valuable in applications such as corrosion-resistant coatings, dielectric fillers, separator materials, and electrode supports.

In this study, long-term cycling was not conducted, However, the high charge transfer resistance (up to 2.4 × 10⁷ Ω) and stable impedance response suggest strong resistance to electrochemical degradation and minimal faradaic activity. These characteristics indicate good inherent electrochemical stability under the tested conditions. The structural stability of the electrode is further supported by the dominance of the calcite phase, moderate microstrain, and calcination at 650 °C, which enhances crystallinity and reduces defect-driven degradation pathways. These factors collectively contribute to maintaining interfacial integrity during electrochemical measurements.

The combined effects of microstrain and crystallographic texture provide a structural basis for the observed impedance behaviour. Microstrain governs defect-induced resistance and charge transfer limitations, while texture coefficient controls anisotropic charge accumulation and interfacial polarization. These parameters collectively explain the dual relaxation processes observed in the impedance spectra and confirm that the electrochemical response of CaCO3NPs is intrinsically governed by their crystallographic structure.

The gradual and non-ideal transition between the high- and low-capacitance regions further supports the earlier conclusion from the constant phase element exponent n (0.38 for the high-frequency process and 0.81 for the low-frequency process) that the electrode exhibits dispersive capacitive behaviour arising from surface heterogeneity and non-uniform current distribution. The reduction in capacitance with increasing frequency also aligns with the phase angle results, where the maximum values were below − 90°, indicating deviation from ideal capacitive behaviour. The observed profile in Fig. 9 therefore integrates well with the previous impedance results, confirming that the calcium carbonate nanoparticles display strong double-layer formation and polarization at low frequencies, transitioning to intrinsic dielectric behaviour at high frequencies. This frequency-dependent capacitive response highlights the suitability of CaCO3 nanoparticles for applications where high interfacial capacitance is required at slow processes, such as in protective coatings, dielectric fillers, and inert supports in electrochemical systems.

Conclusion

This work presents an effective synthesis of high-quality calcium carbonate nanoparticles (CaCO3NPs) from the industrial waste of oxy-acetylene gas welding in automobiles, which possess potential applications in various fields as an insulator or an interfacing material. Structural, thermal, optical, and electrochemical analyses reveal that these synthesized nanoparticles are mostly calcite and, to a smaller extent, vaterite, pure, nanometric in size, and thermally stable. Their large optical band gap and highly mesoporous nature establish the insulating properties of these particles.

Electrochemical impedance spectra showed the presence of two relaxation processes associated with grain-boundary/interfacial polarization at high frequencies and bulk charge transfer processes at low frequencies. In particular, the remarkably greater charge transfer resistance for the low-frequency process (2.4 × 10⁷Ω) relative to the high-frequency process (4.0 × 10⁶ Ω) clearly reveals slow bulk electron transfer and excellent corrosion resistance in acidic solutions. In addition, high interfacial double-layer capacitance (~ 204 μF), very low bulk capacitance (≈10⁻13 F), and non-CPE behavior (n = 0.38–0.81) provide further evidence of strong dielectric and polarization contributions associated with surface inhomogeneity. The above trends of the qualitative properties and quantitative characteristics altogether make it evident that the CaCO3NPs obtained from the waste of the welding process are good insulators, corrosion resistant, and frequency-dependent with robust dielectric properties. The properties combined with its eco-friendliness make the material appropriate for use in the containing capacity of dielectric fillers, corrosion inhibitors, supporting materials for the electrode, and functional additives.

The CPE exponent values (n = 0.38–0.81) are also consistent with literature reports for heterogeneous and porous dielectric systems, where deviations from ideal capacitive behaviour arise from surface roughness, grain boundary effects, and distributed relaxation times.

The non-ideal capacitive behaviour described by the CPE exponent (n = 0.38–0.81) is quantitatively consistent with the measured microstrain values. Increased microstrain introduces a distribution of relaxation times due to structural disorder, which manifests as depressed semicircles in Nyquist plots and reduced phase angles, confirming dispersive charge storage behaviour.

The electrochemical impedance behaviour is strongly influenced by the structural parameters obtained from XRD analysis. In particular, the moderate microstrain (0.00169–0.00299) contributes to lattice defects and surface heterogeneity, which correlate with the non-ideal capacitive response observed in EIS (n = 0.38–0.81). Similarly, variations in texture coefficient (TC) reflect anisotropic crystallographic orientation, which governs charge transport pathways and interfacial polarization behaviour.

Despite the promising results, some limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The electrochemical analysis was limited to impedance-based evaluation without long-term cycling or durability testing. In addition, detailed surface area quantification–property correlation and in-depth electrochemical performance benchmarking against standard materials were not fully explored. Future studies will focus on long-term electrochemical cycling and durability testing to further evaluate the structural and interfacial stability of the CaCO3NP-based electrodes under extended operating conditions. The need for the employment of other profiling methods such as atomic force microscopy is also proposed.
