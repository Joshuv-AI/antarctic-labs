Facial expression discrimination emerges from partially overlapping neural subspaces of detection and identity
Authors: Maren Wehrheim, Shirin Taghian Alamooti, Hamidreza Ramezanpour, Kohitij Kar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
How does the human brain decode facial expressions? Addressing this question requires models that tightly link neural representations to behavior. Here, we first establish rhesus macaques as an animal model of human facial expression discrimination, showing strong behavioral correspondence across six expressions. To probe underlying neural mechanisms, we leverage artificial neural network (ANN) models of the ventral stream as explicit computational hypotheses explaining macaque behavior. Despite action unit–based stimulus generation and high classification accuracies of face-optimized models, broadly-trained ANNs best match macaque image-level behavior. Interestingly, ANNs showing the strongest behavioral alignment are those whose internal representations best resemble macaque inferior temporal (IT) representational geometry. Neural recordings further reveal that early IT responses best predict our measured expression judgments and exhibit a heterogeneous coding architecture in which identity and expression information coexist along partially overlapping dimensions. Together, these findings constrain mechanistic accounts of facial expression discrimination in high-level visual cortex.

Introduction

Discriminating facial expressions is fundamental to human social behavior, yet our understanding of the neural mechanisms driving this perceptual ability remains incomplete1,2. A significant barrier has been the lack of rigorous animal models that simultaneously capture precise human behavioral outcomes and the corresponding neural mechanisms at a resolution necessary to guide biologically realistic computational models. Rhesus macaques represent a particularly compelling model, given their extensive anatomical and functional parallels to human visual processing systems, notably within the inferior temporal (IT) cortex, critical for object3 and face recognition4,5.

Previous research in both humans and macaques highlights overlapping neural substrates within the temporal cortex specialized for processing facial identity and emotional expressions6,7. Prior foundational work has established the existence of face-selective patches and characterized how these regions encode facial identity, viewpoint, and dynamic facial features4,5,8–10. Additional studies demonstrated that neurons in macaque IT and STS encode facial expressions and dynamic facial changes11–16, and behavioral data confirm that macaques respond to facial emotional cues analogously to humans 15,17. Despite these parallels, existing macaque studies have largely relied on qualitative or simplistic behavioral paradigms and have predominantly used conspecific (macaque) faces18. This methodological choice significantly limits direct inferences regarding human neural mechanisms of facial expression processing. Consequently, a largely unresolved issue is whether macaques can serve as accurate behavioral and neural models for the purely perceptual aspects of human facial expression discrimination, specifically at the level of individual human facial images. Without rigorous validation of this behavioral alignment, the potential of macaques to inform computational hypotheses about human facial emotion recognition remains uncertain.

Moreover, even if detailed neural-behavioral patterns are observed in macaques, an essential step remains: how can such information be leveraged to update our understanding of human neural mechanisms? Ultimately, a key goal needs to be to translate insights from animal models into computational hypotheses testable in humans. Specific artificial neural networks (ANNs), which have emerged as highly predictive computational models of the primate ventral visual pathway, provide a powerful tool for this translation3,19–23. ANN models of the ventral visual stream have shown strong alignment with primate neural responses24–26 in object recognition and can partially account for neural coding in face-selective regions for identity processing22. Similarly, action unit–based approaches provide a structured, interpretable framework for facial expression recognition27,28. However, recent work indicates that both approaches leave substantial explainable variance unexplained, particularly for socially meaningful, fine-grained judgments, such as facial expressions29.

Additionally, while face-trained models often achieve high overall classification accuracy on expression tasks30,31, it remains unclear whether they reproduce the structure of behavioral errors and biases observed in primates. In particular, whether such models capture image-specific confusions and systematic misclassifications—hallmarks of biological perception—has not been systematically tested. Thus, performance evaluated solely at the level of accuracy30–32 may obscure meaningful mismatches in the perceptual decision processes underlying behavior. Moreover, models trained on general object recognition can spontaneously develop face-selective representations27, whereas models trained exclusively on faces do not necessarily show stronger similarity to neural responses28. This raises an unresolved question: whether facial expression discrimination behavior is better captured by face-specialized models or by general visual representations in which face selectivity emerges naturally as a consequence of general-purpose visual learning. Resolving this distinction requires going beyond aggregate performance to test whether either class of models captures the neural computations that support expression discrimination in primates. Thus, insights derived from macaque behavioral and neural data have the potential to critically guide the improvement of these computational models, refining their biological alignment and, consequently, their utility in modeling human facial expression discrimination.

To address these interconnected gaps explicitly, our study poses three core questions: (1) Can macaques reliably discriminate between human facial expressions, and to what extent do their image-specific behavioral patterns align with those of humans? (2) What computational hypotheses about neural processing do existing ANN models provide, and can these models explain the observed macaque behavioral patterns? (3) Can neural responses from the macaque IT cortex directly predict macaque behavioral discrimination patterns, and if so, what roles do neuronal selectivity, identity–expression representational overlap, and specific temporal windows of processing play in driving this predictive power?

We first established macaques as a valid model organism for studying the mechanisms of human facial expression discrimination using a comprehensive set of human facial expression stimuli33 (see examples in Fig. 1A) and rigorously quantifying the behavioral alignment at the image level between macaques and humans using a delayed match-to-sample paradigm (Fig. 1B). Specifically, we developed a robust behavioral paradigm (Fig. 1C) and associated behavioral metrics (Fig. 1D) in macaques, explicitly designed for direct and quantitative image-by-image behavioral comparison with human performance. Subsequently, we systematically evaluated multiple ANN architectures, including models specialized for facial action units, and ANNs with varied architectures, objectives, and learning strategies that best predict primate neural data, by comparing their facial expression discrimination behavior and the internal representations against primate behavior and neural responses recorded from the macaque IT cortex respectively (Fig. 1E, see Supplementary Fig. 1 for a schematic on the behavioral evaluation of each modality). Critically, this allowed us to determine which computational approaches best explain the neural encoding strategies underlying facial expression discrimination. Guided by hypotheses from ANN analyses, we next linked direct neural recordings from macaque IT cortex to detailed image-level behavioral performance to test whether population activity could robustly predict expression discrimination behavior. Importantly, our study specifically targets the perceptual and sensory aspects of facial expression discrimination, isolating them from emotional or affective processing. By using brief stimulus presentations followed by immediate forced-choice discrimination of the expression within the same facial identity, we partially restrict that performance reflects visual encoding rather than higher-order cognitive or affective evaluation34. This targeted approach enables a rigorous examination of the visual neural mechanisms underlying the macaque’s processing of human facial expression, likely free from emotional or experiential influences, unlike studies that use conspecific faces35.

Building on this foundation, our integrative approach—combining precise human and macaque behavioral paradigms, large-scale recordings from macaque IT cortex, and ANN modeling—directly links neural population codes to behavior and model predictions. This framework allowed us to establish that macaques replicate human image-level performance, validating them as a perceptual model for human behavior. Generalization experiments confirmed that monkeys learned to discriminate facial expressions rather than memorizing specific images, demonstrating robust transfer across identities and stimulus variations. Building on this stable behavioral alignment, we evaluated competing ANN architectures and found that although narrowly face-trained models achieve the highest overall expression discrimination accuracy, broadly trained models better capture image-level behavioral biases and align more closely with macaque IT activity, highlighting a dissociation between task accuracy and biological fidelity. At the neural level, we provide behaviorally anchored, neuron-resolved evidence for multiplexed coding of facial identity and expression within macaque IT cortex, identifying the neuronal features that most strongly predict behavioral outcomes. Our results reveal that expression discrimination depends most strongly on early-phase IT population responses, supporting a feedforward, sensory account of expression perception. Crucially, we identify a heterogeneous coding architecture within IT: neurons that multiplex identity and expression information, neurons selectively informative for identity or for expression, and expression-informative neurons that are not strongly face-selective. This neuron-level dissociation reveals how shared and specialized representations coexist within IT and directly constrains computational accounts of facial expression discrimination. This organization moves beyond the canonical view of segregated face pathways, instead defining a general coding principle by which IT flexibly supports multiple socially relevant functions. More broadly, the combination of cross-species behavior, ANN modeling, and neural population recordings establishes macaques as a quantitatively validated perceptual model for human facial expression discrimination and provides a mechanistic and generalizable blueprint for dissecting how the high-level visual cortex transforms sensory inputs into behaviorally relevant codes.

Results

As outlined above, we begin by developing a human facial expression discrimination task that can be tested without significant modifications across humans, macaques, and ANNs. We designed our behavioral paradigm with several key considerations in mind to ensure that it would capture sensory-level neural computations and remain compatible with both macaque and human testing. First, we included a wide range of emotional expressions—six distinct categories (anger, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, shame)—across 12 identities of varying gender and ethnicity. This diversity allowed us to assess performance across a broad stimulus space and to examine image-specific effects. Second, we used a relatively large number of images per category, enabling image-level behavioral metrics that could be directly compared with image-computable ANN outputs. Third, we controlled low-level image properties (luminance, contrast, and spatial frequency) using the SHINE toolbox to ensure that performance differences could not be attributed to trivial low-level cues. Fourth, we kept stimulus presentation brief (200 ms), followed by a short delay (100 ms) before the response phase to minimize the influence of higher-order cognitive strategies, keeping the task closely tied to purely perceptual processing. While we acknowledge that emotional information can be processed rapidly and that fast subcortical pathways (e.g., superior colliculus–pulvinar–amygdala circuits36) may contribute to early sensitivity to affective content, our intention was not to exclude such rapid affective influences. Rather, the brief exposure duration constrains prolonged cognitive strategies (e.g., explicit reasoning or extended evidence accumulation) while preserving early, visually driven responses. Together, these design choices allowed us to create a task that was both biologically relevant and computationally tractable, serving as a rigorous benchmark for human–macaque comparisons and ANN evaluation.

Consistent human performance with systematic variability across emotions

We first sought to establish whether human participants exhibited the behavioral properties necessary for this paradigm to serve as a rigorous benchmark for cross-species comparisons. Specifically, we asked whether image-level accuracy varied sufficiently across stimuli within each emotion category, and whether these patterns were reproducible across participants—both of which are critical for assessing whether macaques (and later, ANN models) can capture the fine-grained structure of human performance. A total of 290 human subjects on Amazon Mechanical Turk (see Methods for quality control) completed the expression discrimination task under identical timing constraints to those planned for the macaque experiments (200 ms Test stimulus, 100 ms delay, two-alternative forced choice; see Fig. 1C).

As a first validation step, we computed the psychometric functions for each emotion category. As expected, we observed that discrimination accuracy increased monotonically with emotion intensity, reflecting the greater availability of visual cues at higher intensities—a simple but important sanity check. Expression-averaged image-level accuracy scores spanned a wide range (0.7–0.82; where chance-level is 0.5), indicating that some expressions were consistently easier to discriminate than others, even at matched intensity levels (Fig. 2B, C). This variability is essential for comparisons across species at the image level. To ensure these patterns reflected robust perceptual signatures rather than noise, we computed internal consistency measures across participants. Having established that human behavior in this task displays the required reliable variance across images, we next asked whether macaques tested on the same stimuli and paradigm show comparable performance patterns.

Macaques achieve high, reliable performance in human facial expression discrimination

Training macaques to discriminate emotional expressions in human faces required multiple stages (see Methods), as this task demands generalization across unfamiliar identities and detection of image changes generated by subtle facial muscle movements. Following training, both monkeys performed the task well above chance levels (M = 0.65, Wilcoxon signed-rank test W = 60217.0, n = 360, p < 0.001). Consistent with the human behavioral results, their performance showed: (a) human-like psychometric functions (Fig. 3A), with accuracy increasing with emotion intensity for most categories (all slopes > 0); (b) varied image-level accuracy patterns (Fig. 3B; top panel), reflecting differences in relative difficulty across stimuli; and (c) high internal consistency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}_{{SB}}\,$$\end{document}rSB= 0.75–0.98) across emotions), confirming that these patterns were stable across repeated presentations (Fig. 3B; bottom panel). Furthermore, the two monkeys exhibited highly similar image-level as well as per-emotion accuracy patterns (noise corrected Pearson ρ = 0.97, p < 0.001, Fig. 3C, and Supplementary Fig. 2A), psychometric functions and internal consistencies (Supplementary Fig. 3), indicating consistent strategies within the monkeys. Macaque and human performance patterns are strongly correlated despite accuracy differences

We next compared macaque performance directly to that of human participants to quantify cross-species similarity. While overall macaque accuracy was significantly lower than that of humans (Fig. 3D, Wilcoxon signed-rank test: W = 12549, n = 360, p < .001), such a difference was expected given species-specific familiarity with human faces. Such differences in familiarity, together with reduced readout fidelity (i.e., noisier representations), may also account for the shallower psychometric curves observed in monkeys relative to humans, rather than pointing to fundamentally different underlying representations (Supplementary Fig. 4). Distributional comparisons revealed that macaque performance was less skewed toward high accuracies than human performance, except for “Shame,” where the two species overlapped closely. The more critical question was whether both species shared the same relative difficulty structure across stimuli. Indeed, expression-specific correlations (corrected for the internal consistencies of humans and macaques) were high for most categories (range of noise-corrected Pearson ρ = 0.18–0.84; Fig. 3E). However, behavioral alignment varied systematically across expressions, with highest correspondence for “Shame” and lower alignment for “Anger” and “Disgust”, indicating expression-specific similarities as well as differences in cross-species similarity. These correlations were significantly above chance (p < 0.001 permutation test) and comparable to cross-species similarity levels reported for object recognition37,38. At the overall image-level, the pooled macaque accuracy patterns correlated strongly and robustly with human image-level accuracies (noise corrected Pearson ρ = 0.51, p < 0.001, and Fig. 3F; permutation test 10,000 iterations, p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 5). Within expression categories, macaque and human image-level variability profiles show a positive but non-significant correlation (Pearson r = 0.61, p = 0.19; Supplementary Fig. 6A). While this result could suggest similar patterns of image-level variability across species within expression categories, the current dataset, comprising only six expressions, does not provide sufficient statistical power to draw firm conclusions about this finer-grained relationship. Importantly, these image-level cross-species correlations were not driven by trivial dimensions, such as identity or expression intensity (partial correlations: ρ(human, monkeys | expression intensity) = 0.312, p < 0.001; ρ(human, monkeys | identity) = 0.51, p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating that the observed cross-species alignment reflects both shared sensitivity to expression intensity and additional image-specific variability in expression discrimination. Additionally, the cross-species correlations also remained stable during test time (first 50% of trials (at least 39 per image): Pearson r = 0.49; last 50% of trials (at least 39 per image): Pearson r = 0.46; permutation test, p = 0.41), suggesting that the observed alignment was not driven by ongoing learning of specific images during testing.

In addition, although overall accuracy distributions differed between species (Cliff’s |δ | = 0.42, which captures differences in distributional location independent of normality), there was no significant difference in variance (Brown–Forsythe test, p = 0.73, which assesses differences in variability while being robust to non-normal distributions). Moreover, we did not find any significant differences between expression-specific image-level variability profiles and the internal consistency (humans: r = −0.03, p = 0.96; monkeys: r = 0.14, p = 0.79, Supplementary Fig. 8), further highlighting that cross-species differences primarily reflect shifts in overall performance level rather than fundamental differences in the structure of image-wise difficulty.

Note also that, despite overall differences in accuracy between humans and macaques, performance at the highest emotion intensity levels did not approach ceiling for both species (i.e., accuracy remained around 0.6 to 0.8 rather than approaching 1.0). This indicates that apparent plateaus in the psychometric functions do not reflect simple saturation of perceptual evidence, but instead likely arise from additional sources of uncertainty, such as ambiguity in facial expression cues, overlap between expression categories, or task demands imposed by the forced-choice paradigm. Thus, both species operate under perceptual and decisional constraints that limit maximal achievable accuracy, supporting a shared, stimulus-dependent difficulty structure across species.

In addition, we conducted two further generalization tests to assess whether macaque performance reflected genuine expression-based discrimination rather than image-specific memorization. In the first test, we introduced four new identities as both sample and choice images, each displaying fearful or joyful expressions, thereby requiring the monkeys to transfer their learned category boundaries to unfamiliar individuals (Supplementary Fig. 9). In the second test, to assess whether expression discrimination generalized beyond within-identity comparisons, the sample stimuli remained the same (i.e., the 360 MSFDE images), but the choice images were drawn from a new individual not used during training (Supplementary Figs. 10, 11). For both generalization tasks, monkeys exhibited high expression discrimination accuracy comparable to performance on the original task. Variability profiles for the original dataset and for the new-identity choice condition were nearly perfectly correlated (Pearson r = 0.98, p < 0.01; Supplementary Fig. 6B), demonstrating that macaque image-level variability generalizes robustly across identities. These generalization results demonstrate that macaques can robustly transfer facial expression categories across unfamiliar identities and stimulus sets, providing strong evidence against image-specific memorization and supporting genuine expression-based discrimination.

Together, our findings indicate that while macaques perform at a lower absolute accuracy, their behavioral patterns across images and emotional expressions closely parallel those of humans, validating them as a behavioral model for human facial expression discrimination.

ANNs reveal gaps in behavioral alignment and opposing temporal predictions

We next turned to artificial neural network (ANN) models of the primate ventral stream to generate computational hypotheses about the mechanisms underlying the observed macaque facial expression discrimination behavior. As with the behavioral analyses above, we began by asking whether these models could perform the task (Fig. 4A). All tested ANN models achieved near-perfect classification accuracy on our stimulus set, with many substantially overperforming relative to macaques (M = 0.88, range: 0.64–0.93). To enable fair comparisons of behavioral patterns, we therefore randomly subsampled features from each model until its accuracy matched the pooled macaque level (see Methods, Supplementary Fig. 12A). We focused on subsampling because it preserves the principal axes and intrinsic geometry of each model’s representation, which is critical for comparing fine-grained behavioral error patterns. Consistent with this rationale, alternative dimensionality control using random projections yielded systematically weaker behavioral alignment (Supplementary Fig. 12B), despite increasing classification accuracy with more projections, indicating that additional dimensions primarily add non–behaviorally relevant variance. This dissociation suggests that behaviorally meaningful information is embedded in a constrained subspace rather than distributed uniformly across all available features. After matching the average model accuracy across images to the average macaque levels through feature subsampling, we examined how behavioral alignment varied across the ANN space. Consistent with the idea that macaque facial expression discrimination relies on higher-order ventral stream computations, we found that features from later, putatively layers more similar to IT aligned more closely with macaque behavioral patterns than features from earlier layers (t(4) = −2.73, p < 0.05, d = −1.57, and Fig. 4B). However, we observed that even the best-performing layers failed to fully reproduce macaque image-level behavior. Despite identical overall accuracies, alignment at the image-level varied widely across models (max corrected correlation = 0.71), revealing an explanatory gap between model representations and the behavioral structure observed in macaques (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, unlike in object recognition, where behavioral alignment is often correlated with ImageNet top-1 accuracy, here no such relationship was observed (noise corrected Pearson ρ = 0.09, p = 0.87, Supplementary Fig. 12C). Notably, we observed that models trained specifically on facial identity (VGG-face) or action units (AU: a strategy originally used to generate the emotional intensities in the faces in our imageset33) aligned significantly weaker to the macaque behavioral patterns than more broadly trained networks (VGG-Face: 0.43 ± 0.05, AU: 0.44 ± 0.04, Avg. ImageNet Trained ANNs: 0.67 ± 0.04). This dissociation is particularly striking given that VGG-Face achieved among the highest raw classification accuracies prior to behavior-bounded subsampling in line with previous literature30,31 (Supplementary Fig. 12A), indicating that high task accuracy alone is insufficient to reproduce the characteristic behavioral biases and image-level error structure observed in macaques. To further examine this dissociation, we quantified the effective dimensionality of model representations using the participation ratio39. Focusing on VGG-Face and AlexNet, and subsampling 100 images from both the MSFDE facial-expression dataset and a general object discrimination dataset26, we found that AlexNet showed a significantly higher participation ratio than VGG-Face in the subset of units required to reach macaque behavioral accuracy (MSFDE: t = 26.88, p < 0.001; Kar et al. (2019): t = 238.57, p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 13A). These results suggest that broadly trained models preserve a higher-dimensional representational space, which may better capture the subtle image-specific variability underlying primate behavioral patterns.

To test potential differences between the one-vs-rest decoding used for the models and the match-to-sample paradigm in the monkeys, we repeated the full model analysis using a one-vs-one decoding scheme, which more closely mirrors the binary decision structure of the behavioral task. As expected, this more constrained readout yielded lower behavioral alignment values (mean correlation reduced from ~0.7 to ~0.5, Supplementary Fig. 14), reflecting the increased sensitivity of pairwise classifiers to noise and the loss of global category structure available in the one-vs-rest framework. Importantly, however, the qualitative pattern of results was preserved: even under this alternative readout, models continued to fall below the monkey–monkey behavioral ceiling and face trained models showed the weakest behavioral alignment. These results demonstrate that the observed explanatory gap is not an artifact of decoder choice and instead reflects fundamental differences between current ANN representations and the structure of macaque facial expression behavior.

Given that static models, despite accuracy matching, failed to fully account for the image-by-image behavioral patterns observed in monkeys, we next examined whether incorporating temporal dynamics into ANN processing could help close this explanatory gap. We evaluated two recurrent and time-extended architectures—CORNet-RT40 and ConvRNN41—that differ in how they implement temporal processing. CORNet-RT generates internal recurrent dynamics from a static input, whereas ConvRNN processes a temporally structured input sequence matching our stimulus duration. Notably, overall accuracy at each time step was matched to monkey accuracy via unit subsampling, ensuring that any differences reflect representational changes over time rather than differences in ANN performance levels. Interestingly, the two models produced opposite temporal profiles (Fig. 4D). CORNet-RT showed a marked increase in behavioral alignment over time, but doing so in later time windows, suggesting that the additional transformations due to recurrent computations might bring it closer to macaque behavior. In contrast, ConvRNN reached similar alignment earlier but exhibited a significant reduction (Mann-Whitney U test: U = 20.0, n1 = 5, n2 = 5, p < 0.001) in behavioral consistency with the macaque in later time steps, suggesting that its recurrent processing moves it away from the behavioral signatures observed in macaques. These divergent predictions provide distinct hypotheses for biological data. If IT cortex behaves more like CORNet-RT, alignment with behavior should improve at later stages of processing. If IT behaves more like ConvRNN, alignment should reduce over time. To address this, we performed large-scale neural recordings across the macaque inferior temporal cortex, applying the same behavioral alignment framework to the neural data to test whether IT population activity could predict macaque performance and to evaluate how this relationship evolved over time.

IT population activity predicts macaque behavioral patterns better than any ANN

Having established the anatomical specificity of IT for behaviorally relevant expression coding, we next asked whether these neural–behavioral relationships depend on active task engagement. To this aim, we performed a control analysis in which monkeys completed both an active object categorization task (the relevant task for us here was face vs. other objects) and passive viewing of the same stimulus set in separate sessions. Image-level decoding patterns on the subset of face images from passive and active sessions were highly similar (noise-corrected Pearson ρ = 0.91, p < 0.001, and Supplementary Fig. 17), indicating that passive IT responses preserve the behaviorally relevant structure observed during active task performance. Prior work42,43 has shown that active task engagement typically enhances stimulus selectivity and signal-to-noise ratio in IT cortex. Accordingly, our passive recordings likely provide a conservative lower-bound estimate of behaviorally relevant information, further supporting the conclusion that IT population codes during passive viewing already contain sufficient structure to support facial expression discrimination.

Having established that IT population codes carry sufficient information to discriminate facial expressions, we next asked whether those neural population predictions align with the animals’ image-level behavior. Using a time-resolved decoding approach, we trained separate linear classifiers on the population responses within sliding temporal windows and computed performance as decoding accuracy and alignment with macaque behavior. This temporal analysis revealed a distinct temporal dissociation (Fig. 5C): behavioral alignment peaked early (70–100 ms; ρ = 0.79, p < 0.001) and then declined/plateaued, whereas raw decoding accuracy continued to rise, reaching a later maximum (150–220 ms; M = 0.63, Wilcoxon signed-rank test W = 51175.0, n = 360, p < 0.001). Thus, later neural population responses contain more categorical information but are less behavior-predictive, indicating that the computations most relevant for decisions are concentrated in an early IT window. Generally, the alignment between macaque behavior and neural decoding varied systematically across expressions, mirroring the pattern observed in the monkey–human comparison, with the strongest correspondence for “Shame” and reduced alignment for “Anger” and “Disgust”, suggesting that the behavior for different expressions relies on partially distinct visual features, which are captured to varying degrees in the recorded IT population (Supplementary Fig. 2C). The decoding was implemented with a simple linear readout model, which consistently outperformed more flexible nonlinear alternatives in additional control analyses (Supplementary Fig. 18), indicating that the decision-relevant variables underlying expression discrimination are largely linearly accessible from IT population activity. Image-level neural population decoding patterns were highly consistent across monkeys for each time window (mean Pearson r = 0.49, range = 0.25–0.80). In addition, the temporal profiles of behavioral alignment were strongly similar across all neural–behavioral monkey pairings (Pearson r = 0.80–0.99), indicating robust cross-animal reproducibility. To assess sampling limits, we quantified how performance scales with population size (Fig. 5D). Extrapolations indicated that 1297 neurons (early window) or 520 neurons (late window) would be required to reach behavioral-level accuracy (fit early: MSE: 3.6e-07, fit late: MSE: 7.7e-07; see Supplementary Fig. 19 for other parametric forms). Note that higher variability at low neuron counts likely reflects reduced reliability when few task-relevant neurons are sampled. As extrapolation beyond the sampled range must be interpreted cautiously, we emphasize that these values are intended to provide an indicative scale rather than a precise estimate. The exact number of neurons required will depend on factors, such as the functional properties of the sampled population, recording quality, and task demands, and should therefore be viewed as a guide for future experimental design rather than a definitive threshold. However, this pattern—requiring more neurons to reach behavioral-level accuracy at early than at late time windows—was preserved when neurons were sampled from each monkey individually (Supplementary Fig. 20), further demonstrating that the results are robust across animals.

To test which dynamic ANN best captures this temporal profile of neural population decoding, we directly compared the time-resolved behavioral alignment of the models to that observed in IT cortex. For ConvRNN, which generates predictions at 10-ms resolution, we aligned model time steps with neural population decoding windows starting at 70 ms. As CORNet-RT provides only five discrete time states, we averaged the 10-ms neural population decoding results into five non-overlapping temporal windows to match the model’s temporal resolution. Visualizing the behavioral alignment of the first and last few time frames of each model and comparing this pattern to that observed in the neurons, reveals that the observed dynamics in the behavioral alignment of the neural data are more similar to those found in ConvRNN compared to CORNet-RT (Supplementary Fig. 21). In the neurons and ConvRNN we find a decrease towards later time windows (Mann-Whitney-U test; neurons: U = 42.0, n1 = 7, n2 = 7, p = 0.03; ConvRNN: U = 48.0, n1 = 7, n2 = 7, p = 0.001), while behavioral alignment increases in CORNet-RT (note that each set only contains 2 examples, not allowing for profound statistical testing). Additionally, using a correlation based approach of all time frames, we find a positive relationship between model and neural behavioral alignment profiles (r = 0.43, p = 0.07), while the correlation between CORNet-RT and neural behavioral alignment was weak and negative (r = −0.20, p = 0.75; Supplementary Fig. 21). These results indicate that the temporal evolution of ConvRNN predictions capture key aspects of the early-to-late transition in behaviorally relevant IT signals more closely, whereas CORNet-RT fails to reproduce this temporal structure.

Finally, we asked whether differences in ANN behavioral alignment (as shown in Fig. 4C) could be explained by how well each model’s representational geometry matched that of IT cortex, assessed using representational similarity analysis (RSA). For each model, we computed a representational similarity matrix (RSM) across all images based on the same subsampled features used for behavioral decoding, and compared these model RSMs to neural RSMs computed from population activity in corresponding time windows (see Methods). This approach allowed us to quantify the degree to which each model captured the fine-grained relational structure of IT population responses, independent of raw decoding accuracy. In the early, behaviorally optimal neural window (70–100 ms), when neural population activity is most predictive of macaque performance, we found a strong positive relationship between ANN–IT representational similarity and ANN–behavioral alignment (corrected correlation = 0.71; Fig. 5E, left). This suggests that the models whose internal geometry most closely matches IT in this critical early phase are also the ones that better capture the fine-grained behavioral structure of macaques. A similar positive relationship was also observed for the late, accuracy-optimal window (150–200 ms; corrected correlation = 0.89; Fig. 5E, right), indicating that alignment between model and brain geometry is generally beneficial for reproducing behavioral patterns, whether using early decision-related signals or later, more stable categorical representations. Importantly, extrapolating these relationships suggests that if ANNs were to reach the same representational similarity to IT as the monkeys themselves, their behavioral alignment would eventually match the level currently achieved by the neural population data (see Supplementary Fig. 22 for other parametric forms of the extrapolation function). This provides a concrete, quantitative target for ANN development: improving model–brain similarity, particularly in the early decision-relevant phase, could close the current explanatory gap between artificial and biological systems.

To further characterize temporal differences in neural–model alignment, we additionally examined how representational similarity to the early, behaviorally relevant IT window (70–100 ms) evolved across model time steps (Supplementary Fig. 21C). This analysis directly tested the hypothesis that model dynamics should progressively diverge from the early IT code, leading to reduced similarity at later time points. For ConvRNN, representational similarity to the early neural window decreased across later model time steps (Supplementary Fig. 21C left), consistent with progressive divergence from the early IT representational geometry. In contrast, for CORNet-RT, representational similarity to the early IT window increased across later model time steps, indicating that later CORNet-RT’s internal dynamics evolve toward representations that more closely resemble the early IT response (Supplementary Fig. 21C right). Together, these results highlight that the two recurrent models implement qualitatively distinct temporal transformations, and further support the conclusion that ConvRNN more closely mirrors the trajectory of IT population dynamics underlying behaviorally relevant facial expression processing.

Neural properties and coding strategies underlying facial expression discrimination in IT

The population-level analyses above show that the IT cortex contains rich, temporally dynamic information about facial expression that can predict macaque behavioral patterns. We next asked what neuronal properties best explain this predictive ability, focusing on two candidate factors: (1) a neuron’s selectivity for faces relative to other objects, and (2) its capacity to encode facial identity and support facial identity discrimination behavior.

We first examined whether a neuron’s selectivity for faces predicts its contribution to facial expression discrimination. Face selectivity was quantified as a Face Selectivity Index (FSI) computed from responses to an independent set of face and non-face object images26 (Fig. 6C). The recorded population spanned a wide range of FSIs, from neurons with no measurable preference for faces (FSI ≈ 0) to neurons responding almost exclusively to faces (FSI > 0.4, Fig. 6A). Example tuning profiles illustrate that intermediate-FSI neurons responded robustly to both faces and non-faces, whereas highly face-selective neurons responded almost exclusively to faces (Fig. 6B, left and right). To relate face selectivity to expression discrimination, we examined how each neuron’s FSI value correlated with its absolute regression coefficient from the most behaviorally aligned facial expression decoder, computed separately for each expression category (Fig. 6D). Across all six expressions, we observed a consistent positive relationship between face selectivity and decoder weight (r = 0.17–0.37, all p < 0.05), indicating that more face-selective neurons generally contributed more strongly to expression decoding. This relationship was stable across emotions, suggesting that face selectivity is a general predictor of a neuron’s importance for expression discrimination. Extensive robustness analyses confirmed that this effect was not driven by a small subset of neurons: bootstrap resampling yielded tight confidence intervals excluding zero for all expressions (10,000 iterations, N = 308, anger: mean Pearson r = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.12 to 0.39; disgust: mean r = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.13 to 0.36; fear: mean r = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.16 to 0.40), joy: r = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.31; sadness: r = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.14 to 0.38; shame: r = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.23 to 0.48), leave-one-neuron-out analyses showed minimal impact of any single neuron (max |Δr | = 0.04), and iterative removal of the most influential neurons preserved statistical significance even after removing up to 6–27% of neuron pairs (expression-dependent). We next asked whether the same neurons that carry strong expression information also support facial identity discrimination. Using a separate identity discrimination task with the same face stimuli (Fig. 7A), we found that many neurons achieved high identity decoding accuracy (Fig. 7B, M = 0.90, Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test: W = 64620, n = 360, p < 0.001). Comparing behavioral alignment across the two tasks revealed a strong positive correlation (noise-corrected Pearson ρ = 0.85, p < 0.001; Fig. 7C): time intervals where neural population responses were better aligned with behavior in the identity task were also better aligned in the expression task. This relationship persisted when comparing average decoder coefficients (noise-corrected Pearson ρ = 0.76, p < 0.001; Fig. 7D) and when comparing raw decoding accuracies (noise-corrected Pearson ρ = 0.50, p < 0.001; Fig. 7E). Robustness analyses further confirmed the stability of the neuronal-level decoding correlation between identity and expression. The relationship between a neuron’s identity-prediction accuracy and its expression-prediction accuracy remained significant after controlling for FSI (partial correlation r = 0.305, p < 0.001) and when controlling for each neuron’s response reliability (partial correlation r = 0.312, p < 0.001), suggesting a genuine partially overlapping representational subspace between identity and expression in the neural population activity. To assess whether these partial correlations exceeded levels expected from measurement noise alone, a split-half control analysis was performed in which identity-prediction accuracy from one half of trials was correlated with expression-prediction accuracy while controlling for identity-prediction accuracy computed from the other half. This procedure yielded substantially lower correlations (mean partial r = 0.094 ± 0.100 across ten random splits), providing a noise-based baseline. The marked difference between this baseline and the observed partial correlations indicates that the shared variance between identity and expression decoding reflects a genuine, partially overlapping representational subspace in IT neural population activity rather than noise-driven dependencies. Bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations, N = 308) yielded a tightly centered distribution (mean r = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.36–0.64). Leave-one-neuron-out analyses demonstrated minimal influence of any individual site (maximum |Δr | = 0.03). Color-coding neurons by FSI in these analyses revealed that many of the strongest dual-task performers fell within the mid-to-high FSI range identified in Fig. 6. Importantly, we did not observe a significant correlation between the facial identity and expression discrimination behavioral patterns for all emotion categories except shame (anger: ρ = 0.01, p = 0.92; disgust: ρ = 0.21, p = 0.12; fear: ρ = −0.06, p = 0.66; joy: ρ = −0.02, p = 0.87; sadness: ρ = 0.11, p = 0.39; shame: ρ = −0.62, p < 0.001; all ρ are noise corrected). To test whether the strong negative correlation observed for shame biased the relationship between neural population decoder alignment for identity and expression, we recomputed the analysis after excluding shame images. The correlation remained significant (Pearson r = 0.54, p < 0.001), confirming that behavioral similarity across tasks did not drive neural population decoder alignment. This reinforces the conclusion that expression discrimination emerges from a partially overlapping but functionally distinct subspace, rather than from identical identity-related codes.

The results above indicate that the neurons most predictive of behavioral performance in facial expression discrimination are often both highly face-selective and identity-informative. To test the impact of face selectivity more directly, we stratified neurons (70-100 ms) based on their FSI and repeated the neuron prediction analysis separately within high- and low-FSI subgroups (upper and lower deciles, N = 31 per group). Across both tasks—facial expression and identity discrimination—neurons in the high-FSI group exhibited significantly higher decoding accuracy and stronger behavioral alignment than neurons in the low-FSI group (Supplementary Fig. 23), demonstrating that facial tuning contributes to expression discrimination in IT. At the same time, complementary spatial and selectivity analyses (Supplementary Figs. 24−26) reveal a partial dissociation between face/identity and expression coding in IT. While face selectivity and identity discrimination show significant spatial clustering consistent with face-patch organization (Moran’s I = 0.0129, p = 0.009, a measure of spatial autocorrelation testing whether nearby recording sites show similar values), expression-related information is more spatially distributed (Moran’s I = 0.0091, p = 0.097) and overlaps preferentially with expression selectivity rather than face selectivity. These results indicate that expression and identity are supported by overlapping but non-identical neural subpopulations, consistent with a distributed, multiplexed code rather than a single localized expression module.

Finally, to further probe the differences between the broadly object-trained model (AlexNet, highest IT representational similarity) and the face-identification model (VGG-Face, lowest IT similarity), we repeated facial expression decoding using only the residual neural response after removing the variance explained by each model. Specifically, we first predicted neural activity from the subsampled model units that matched macaque behavioral accuracy, and then regressed out the model-predicted component before decoding. Removing the AlexNet-predicted component caused a significantly larger reduction in both decoding accuracy and behavioral alignment than removing the VGG-Face-predicted component (Supplementary Fig. 13B), indicating that the broadly trained model captures a larger fraction of the neural information that is specifically relevant for task performance.

To further identify the mechanistic basis of this difference, we evaluated the explained variance for functionally defined subsets of neurons (Supplementary Fig. 13C). AlexNet accounted for more variance than VGG-Face in the top 25% of neurons ranked by expression discrimination index (EDI; t = 1.92, p = 0.06), identity discrimination index (IDI; t = 5.44, p < 0.001), expression selectivity index (ESI; t = 1.74, p = 0.08), and face selectivity index (FSI; t = 2.34, p = 0.02). These results again show that broadly trained models better capture the representational structure of behaviorally informative IT neurons, further highlighting the relevance of broadly training ANNs to achieve high (functional) brain similarity.

This convergence points to a shared, partially overlapping representational subspace (and likely readout differently in the downstream areas) in IT cortex that supports both identity and expression recognition—a subpopulation that may form the core neural machinery for decoding socially relevant facial information.

Discussion

Our study provides a unified, multi-level analysis of facial expression discrimination, connecting human and macaque behavior, macaque IT neural population activity, and ANN models within the same image-level framework. By aligning behavior, neural population responses, and model predictions at high resolution, we identify candidate mechanistic features of how identity and expression are represented in the primate visual system and set quantitative targets for building more brain-like computational models.

We demonstrate that macaques not only learn a human facial expression discrimination task to high accuracy (Fig. 3A, B) but also exhibit image-by-image performance patterns that closely parallel human observers across all six expressions (Fig. 3E, F). This strong cross-species alignment—comparable to that reported in object recognition37—validates macaques as a model for probing the sensory aspects of the neural basis of human facial expression processing. It also justifies the use of human face stimuli in macaque neurophysiology, enabling direct tests of human-relevant hypotheses about the neural computations underlying social perception. By providing this benchmark, we establish a foundation for mechanistic experiments and causal interventions whose inferences can be potentially translated to human social cognition.

Evaluating a diverse set of ANN models, we find that decoders built from activations in the later, IT-like layers better align with macaque behavioral patterns than earlier layers, consistent with the hierarchical nature of ventral stream processing (Fig. 4B), and with the idea that higher stages carry behaviorally relevant invariances44. Critically, even after accuracy matching via unit subsampling (controlling for performance confounds), no static model—including those explicitly trained on facial identity or action units (AU)27—fully captures macaque behavior (Fig. 4C), underscoring a representational gap between current ANNs and primate expression discrimination. Our analyses differ from prior work by controlling for accuracy and instead asking whether models reproduce primate-like behavioral confusions. Under this constraint, narrowly face-trained and AU-based models diverge more strongly from macaque behavioral patterns despite their high task accuracy, whereas broadly trained models better capture image-level behavioral structure. This pattern is consistent with the idea that primate facial-expression behavior is supported by a broadly trained neural system shaped by diverse visual experience, rather than by a system optimized exclusively for facial stimuli. Under this view, broadly trained models may better capture the representational structure that constrains primate perceptual behavior, even if they are less efficient at the specific task of facial-expression classification. Consistent with this interpretation, face-specialized models, such as VGG-Face achieve higher task accuracy overall, as expected from their domain-specific optimization, however, broadly trained models show stronger behavioral alignment. This dissociation suggests that specialization improves task efficiency, whereas broader visual training may preserve a representational organization that more closely resembles the one underlying primate behavioral confusions. Our representational similarity analyses provide a unifying explanation for this pattern: behavioral alignment tracks the degree to which model representations resemble IT representational geometry, not accuracy alone. This relationship explains why specialized face training can improve classification performance yet over-regularize representational dimensions that are critical for reproducing primate perceptual errors, while broadly trained models retain representational structure that better supports biologically realistic behavior. This result is in line with recent AU-model evaluations showing that AU-based hypotheses explain some variance in human expression judgments but remain well below noise ceilings, indicating missing features and constraints in the current modeling space29. Likewise, work on identity-trained face ANNs demonstrates that expression-selective units can emerge and capture aspects of human-like expression perception, but only under domain-specific face experience—and still with accuracy shortfalls relative to humans—again pointing to incomplete mechanisms in off-the-shelf training regimes32. Consistent with these observations, we observed a lack of correlation between ImageNet accuracy and behavioral alignment, reinforcing that expression discrimination might depend on representational axes not emphasized or evolved by the objectives of object categorization in ANNs. Beyond static models, our time-resolved tests reveal that recurrent architectures make opposing, falsifiable predictions about neural population dynamics: CORNet-RT’s40 behavioral alignment increases over time, whereas ConvRNN’s41 alignment declines at later time bins—even when accuracy is matched at each step—implying divergent recurrent transformations over the same inputs (Fig. 4D). This divergence positions ANN modeling not merely as a descriptive tool but as a generator of specific, falsifiable predictions about neural population computations across time.

Direct IT recordings revealed a temporal dissociation between encoding and behavioral relevance. Behavioral alignment peaked early, within 70–100 ms of stimulus onset, whereas raw neural population decoding accuracy continued to rise, reaching its maximum later (150–200 ms). This indicates that the computations most relevant for guiding expression judgments in the monkeys we have trained occur during the initial feedforward sweep of IT activity, consistent with prior object recognition studies showing that early IT signals are sufficient to drive core recognition behavior45. Later IT responses, although more categorical, may reflect recurrent refinement, feedback from higher-order regions, or post-decisional processes not directly engaged by the task. An alternative, non-exclusive interpretation of the early peak in neural–behavioral alignment is that it reflects limitations in behavioral readout rather than limits of neural population representation. Later IT activity contains additional category-relevant information that may not be fully exploited by the animals’ learned decision strategies. Under this interpretation, early IT signals reflect the information most readily accessible to the animals’ decision process, whereas later representations may support improved performance under different training regimes, task demands, or readout strategies. This dissociation underscores a broader principle: encoding capacity and behaviorally deployed information are not synonymous, and early visual signals often play the decisive role in perceptual judgments. Our findings also converge with recent computational work demonstrating that activations of feedforward models can explain the behavioral variance predicted by human amygdala responses46. Taken together, these studies suggest that early ventral-stream representations are sufficient to support behaviorally relevant judgments of facial expressions. Our neural population dynamics further adjudicate the opposing predictions made by recurrent ANNs. CORNet-RT40 exhibited increasing behavioral alignment over time, while ConvRNN41 alignment declined in later time bins, despite both models being accuracy-matched at each time step. The IT data align more closely with the ConvRNN profile, where later recurrent activity does not add, but rather deteriorates, behaviorally relevant structure, providing a rare instance where ANN dynamics generate falsifiable hypotheses that are directly tested in the brain. At the same time, previous work has established the importance of late-phase IT activity for object recognition under challenging conditions, where recurrent processing helps to build more accurate codes26,47. A similar principle may hold for faces: while early IT signals dominate in standard discrimination tasks, late activity could become critical for coding expressions in more ambiguous or noisy contexts. Thus, we speculate that the current gap between neural population predictivity and the behavioral ceiling may be closed by developing more accurate dynamic decoding models that optimally integrate early feedforward signals with recurrently refined late responses.

Relating ANN–brain representational similarity (RSA) to ANN–behavior alignment revealed that models with greater IT-like representational geometry also achieved stronger behavioral alignment, particularly in the late window. Importantly, extrapolation suggests that if ANNs were trained to achieve monkey-level RSA48, they would also reach neural-to-behavior alignment. This defines a quantitative and biologically grounded target for future ANN development, moving beyond generic “brain-likeness” toward measurable alignment with both neural population activity and behavior.

Our results also provide a principled way to reconcile seemingly mixed conclusions in the facial expression literature. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of dynamic facial information and motion-sensitive pathways, particularly involving STS, for expression processing, sometimes reporting comparatively weaker expression signals in face-selective cortex under specific paradigms11. At the same time, other work has shown that IT and related temporal-lobe regions are modulated by facial expressions depending on stimulus properties and task demands35. Our findings bridge these views by demonstrating—using a controlled, image-computable discrimination task and behavior-linked neural metrics—that expression-discriminative information is enriched in face-selective IT populations, yet is not confined to a single spatially localized “expression module.” Instead, expression information is distributed within the broader face network, embedded in a shared representational substrate that supports both identity and expression discrimination.

At the single-neuron level, we find a robust positive relationship between face selectivity (FSI) and contribution to expression decoding across all expressions. This indicates that, on average, more face-selective neurons tend to contribute more strongly to expression discrimination, although some less-selective neurons also carry substantial weights. Thus, while face selectivity is not an exclusive requirement for expression coding, it biases neurons toward stronger contributions. This dependency on face selectivity also indicates a hierarchical relationship between structural and dynamic aspects of face processing—identity-stable structural encoding appears to form the scaffold upon which expression discrimination is built. In computational terms, neurons tuned to the high-dimensional subspace of face identity may be best positioned to capture within-identity variations corresponding to expressions, but our data also highlight heterogeneity in how neurons across the FSI spectrum contribute.

Our analyses disentangle several neural factors that are often conflated in prior work, including single-neuron face selectivity, neural population discrimination capacity, and behavioral alignment. While increased neural responsiveness and face selectivity bias neurons toward stronger contributions to expression decoding, neither property alone is sufficient to explain behavioral relevance. Instead, our analyses reveal that facial expression discrimination in IT cortex is not the sole function of a specialized, expression-only circuit, but instead emerges from a subpopulation of neurons that also carries substantial facial identity information. These neurons, typically in the mid-to-high range of face selectivity, form a shared representational substrate in which identity and expression are encoded along partially overlapping dimensions. This architecture challenges the long-standing view that the most face-selective neurons are the sole repository of behaviorally relevant expression information. Instead, our data show that identity- and expression-relevant codes coexist within the same subspace, but are weighted differently depending on task demands. This overlap may provide a computational advantage: by maintaining stable structural features for identity while preserving sensitivity to changeable cues, the system can flexibly support both social recognition and social communication. Importantly, this framework generates a testable prediction—perturbing these identity-informative, face-selective neurons should produce coupled deficits in both identity and expression discrimination. More broadly, our findings suggest that the principle of shared but differentially weighted subspaces may generalize to other domains in high-level vision, offering a unified model for how IT supports multiple behaviorally critical functions within a common representational geometry.

Despite the strengths of our integrative approach, several limitations remain. While macaques and humans perform the task similarly, behavioral discrepancies between the species remain, particularly for Disgust and Anger, highlighting species-specific strategies that may constrain generalization (Fig. 3E). However, these differences should not be interpreted as evidence for fundamentally different underlying representations. Instead, they are likely driven by differences in familiarity with human faces, as well as differences in behavioral readout quality (i.e., noisier or less optimized decision processes). Consistent with this interpretation, macaque psychometric functions are systematically shallower than those of humans, a pattern expected under reduced familiarity or noisier readout rather than altered representational structure. More generally, some of the pooled correlations used throughout the study are influenced not only by image-level variation within expression categories, but also by category-level structure across expressions. In particular, expressions differ in their degree of human–monkey and neural–behavioral alignment, with “Shame” showing the strongest correspondence and “Anger” and “Disgust” weaker correspondence. Accordingly, pooled correlations should be interpreted as reflecting both fine-grained image-level similarity and broader expression-level structure. These expression-specific differences likely contribute to the gap that still remains between prediction and behavior, not only for artificial models, but also for the best-predicting neurons in IT cortex, suggesting that additional factors beyond local visual representations likely contribute to behavioral performance. In addition, neural population recordings were obtained during passive viewing, whereas behavioral performance was measured during active discrimination. Although our control analyses indicate that behaviorally relevant neural population structure is largely preserved during passive viewing, this mismatch may still limit the extent to which we can capture task-dependent modulations of IT responses. In particular, active task engagement is known to enhance selectivity and signal-to-noise in IT42,43, and such effects may further shape the neural population code during explicit decision-making. We therefore interpret our neural–behavioral links as reflecting a conservative lower-bound estimate of behaviorally relevant visual information present in IT, while acknowledging that additional task-dependent or feedback-related processes may contribute during active performance. Additionally, neural population activity and behavioral performance were measured in different animals. As a result, the neural–behavioral correspondences reported here should be interpreted as reflecting population-level alignment between IT representations and behavioral discrimination patterns, rather than a direct mapping between the neural activity and decisions of the same individual. In line with previous work showing preserved neural dynamics across animals26,49, this design nonetheless provides strong evidence that the macaque IT population contains information sufficient to account for key aspects of expression discrimination behavior. Our analyses address only the visual encoding of facial expressions; however, we cannot capture how feedback from higher-order or affective brain regions may modulate these representations to infer the emotional meaning in humans. While we observed that information in IT unfolds dynamically, our current analyses did not explicitly exploit these temporal patterns, leaving open the possibility that richer dynamic signals may further improve predictions. More generally, our goal here was not to build a full multi-stage decision model, but to test whether IT population activity contains sufficient information to support expression discrimination and whether this decoding aligns with behavior on an image-by-image basis. In this sense, we follow a large literature that treats explicit decoders from population activity to behavior as minimal but concrete models of neural readout26,45,50–56. We therefore interpret our neural–behavioral alignment analyses as specifying a population-level readout framework for expression decisions, while leaving downstream circuit and algorithmic details to future work. Finally, while facial expressions are dynamic signals and dynamic stimuli may more strongly engage temporal cortical processing, our use of static images reflects a deliberate trade-off. Static stimuli afford precise experimental control and enable rigorous image-level comparisons across species, neural population data, and computational models. We therefore view dynamic facial stimuli as an important direction for future work, building on the mechanistic foundation established here rather than as a limitation of the current approach.

Taken together, our results support a model in which early IT responses from a mid-to-high FSI, identity-informative subpopulation form the core substrate for both identity and expression recognition. This shared representational subspace integrates stable and dynamic facial features, providing a flexible platform for multiple social vision tasks.

From a translational perspective, this framework also offers a mechanistic entry point into disorders of social perception. Differences in facial expression discrimination are a hallmark of autism, with autistic adults often showing atypical image-level patterns compared to neurotypical individuals46,57. By establishing macaques as reliable models of human facial expression recognition and aligning behavior, IT neural population activity, and ANN predictions at the image level, our study provides a circuit-level platform for modeling social perception differences in autism. This framework enables ANN-based predictions about how neural population coding strategies might differ in autism—for example, altered weighting of identity vs. expression dimensions—and provides a primate system in which such hypotheses can be causally tested.

From a modeling standpoint, our ANN analyses reveal both representational gaps and explicit engineering targets: models that better match IT representational geometry, especially in the early behaviorally optimal phase, should yield improved alignment with behavioral structure. From a neuroscience perspective, this framework unifies identity and expression coding, reframing them not as segregated streams but as intertwined dimensions of a common code.

Finally, the cross-species image-level alignment we establish enables causal testing of these principles: perturbations targeting mid-to-high FSI, identity-informative neurons should jointly impair identity and expression discrimination in predictable ways. More broadly, the principle of shared but differentially weighted subspaces may generalize to other high-level visual domains, offering a unified account of how IT supports multiple behaviorally critical functions within a common representational geometry.

In sum, by integrating cross-species behavior, direct neural population recordings, and model-based predictions, we establish a quantitative population-level correspondence linking primate IT representations, behavioral expression discrimination, and computational models. This framework identifies the subpopulation of neurons most strongly associated with behaviorally relevant representational structure, unifies identity and expression coding within a partially overlapping representational space, and defines quantitative targets for building models that better match both neural population geometry and behavioral patterns.

Methods

Visual stimuli

We used a combination of grayscale photographs from the Montreal Set of Facial Displays of Emotion (MSFDE) dataset33. The dataset includes six expression categories: angry, disgust, joy, fear, sadness, and shame. We selected a subset of twelve identities (six female), four individuals of each ethnicity (European, Asian, and African). Each individual was photographed while expressing an emotion, resulting in photographs with 20–100% of the specific emotion displayed. In each image, we equalized low-level properties using the SHINE (spectrum, histogram, and intensity normalization and equalization) toolbox58.

We used a separate set of 60 images to determine the face selectivity index of the neural sites. This image set contained 15 static faces, 15 objects, 15 scenes, and 15 scrambled objects.

Behavioral training and testing

Human participants

We collected large-scale psychophysical data from 290 subjects using Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk). Participants completed the tasks on MTurk, an online crowdsourcing platform, for a payment of $15 CAD/hour. At the beginning of each experimental session, informed consent was obtained from each participant. This experimental protocol involving human participants was approved by and in concordance with the guidelines of the York University Human Participants Review Subcommittee. Humans did not receive any additional training on the task. Each trial began with a brief presentation (200 ms) of a sample image with a given emotion, selected from the set of 360 images. After a 100 ms blank gray screen, subjects were shown a choice screen displaying an image of a target and a distractor emotion. Subjects indicated their choice by clicking on the image they believed matched the emotion of the sample image. Individual participants were not tested repeatedly on the same images; instead, each image was presented to many different human participants, and we treated the pooled human dataset as a single large “virtual subject” with many independent trials per image. To ensure high-quality behavioral data, we rejected all participants who performed below an accuracy of 0.6 (chance-level = 0.5) on the first 30 trials during each session. Furthermore, we evaluated the reliability of the image-level behavioral metrics as a function of the number of trials (Supplementary Fig. 27) and found that the reliability of the mean image-level accuracy pattern increased with more repetitions per image and reached high values (~0.8), nearly asymptoting at around 60 trials.

Non-human primates

We trained two adult male rhesus macaque monkeys (Macaca mulatta; monkey 1 and 2) to perform the emotional expression and identity discrimination task. All data were collected, and animal procedures were performed, in accordance with the NIH guidelines, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology Committee on Animal Care, and the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care on the use of laboratory animals, and were also approved by the York University Animal Care Committee.

The two rhesus macaques were trained to perform the two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) facial expression discrimination task using a match-to-sample paradigm59. Training was organized into sequential phases of increasing difficulty, gradually introducing additional emotion categories, identities, and subtler intensity levels. Monkeys trained 5 days per week in sessions of ~1000–2000 trials, progressing to the next phase upon reaching ≥≥80% correct performance over two consecutive sessions. Each trial began with fixation on a central dot, followed by a 500 ms presentation of a sample face (neutral or emotional; ~8° visual angle), a brief 100 ms blank delay, and a choice screen displaying two faces of the same identity: one matching the sample’s emotion category at full intensity and one with a distractor expression. In early training stages, the correct choice was identical to the sample image to facilitate learning. Monkeys indicated their choice by touching one of the two images, with correct responses rewarded by water and incorrect responses followed by a short timeout (5 s) before the next trial. This positive reinforcement protocol was maintained throughout training to promote stable and high-accuracy performance.

During behavioral testing images were presented on an iPad60. All images were shown at 8 deg of visual angle. Monkeys touched the screen to initiate a trial. Similar to the human participants, the trial started with the presentation of a sample image from the set of 360 images for 200 ms. This was followed by a blank gray screen for 100 ms, after which the choice screen was shown displaying a target and a distractor image. For the expression discrimination task, the identity was fixed, while for the identity discrimination task, the shown emotion was fixed and displayed at 100%. The monkey was allowed to view the choice screen freely for up to 1500 ms and indicated its final choice by touching the selected image. The macaques received water rewards for correct responses and brief timeouts for incorrect responses.

Using this set up, monkeys performed both the expression and the identity discrimination task, as well as the two generalization paradigms. Prior to testing in the laboratory, monkeys were trained to perform both discrimination tasks.

Artificial neural networks (ANNs)

Generating behavioral predictions from ANN activations

We first evaluated ANNs to build hypotheses about the underlying mechanisms in macaque facial expression discrimination. We therefore evaluated nine static (time-agnostic) and two dynamic (time-recursive) convolutional neural networks (CNNs, see Supplementary Table 1 for model characteristics and training objectives). These models included a diverse set of static models, such as standard architectures (AlexNet61, ResNet1862, and ResNet5062), contrastive learning models with and without language input (CLIP63 and SimCLR64, respectively), a model trained to predict facial Action Units (AU65,66), and CORNet-Z40 as representative of biologically inspired models. We also included domain-specific models trained exclusively on human faces to perform an identity discrimination task or exclusively on objects to perform object categorization22. In addition, we analyzed dynamic models, including CORNet-RT40, and ConvRNNs41, that incorporate recurrent processing to approximate temporal dynamics more closely aligned with biological systems. For each model, we extracted the unit activations from each layer in the models for every stimulus. We mimicked the macaque expression discrimination task by training six one-vs-rest logistic regression classifiers on the activations from each model layer (Fig. 4A) using 5-fold stratified cross-validation At the conclusion of ANN fine-tuning for expression prediction, we would generate 6 probabilities (one for each emotion) when a Test image was presented to the ANN.

The reported results reflect the average prediction accuracy and behavioral alignment across 50 iterations of the matched subsets.

Selection of the ANN IT-like layers

To ensure that our ANN–neural comparisons are anchored at the most biologically relevant processing stage, we selected for each model the layer with the highest alignment to macaque IT cortex as determined by the BrainScore benchmark (see Supplementary Table 1). This approach maximizes the interpretability of behavioral alignment results, as it isolates the stage in the model most likely to capture IT-like representational structure. For the Action Unit (AU) model, we instead used only the AU-based predictions to directly assess the explanatory power of action units for macaque behavior. For the face- and object-trained models, we used the final classification layer, which should, by design, have the highest discriminatory power for the categories on which the model was trained (identities for face-trained, object classes for object-trained). This choice allows us to test whether a model’s most category-selective stage, where task-relevant features are most strongly amplified, also best aligns with the behavioral patterns observed in macaques. For dynamic models, we treated each time step as an independent representational state, training a classifier at each time step and subsampling units to match macaque accuracy. This procedure allowed us to track the temporal evolution of ANN–behavior alignment and compare it directly to the dynamics observed in neural population data.

Early vs. late processing stages

To determine whether early or late processing stages better align with macaque behavior, we analyzed the first 30% (early) and last 30% (late) of layers from each static model (Fig. 4B). The action unit model was excluded from this comparison, as we only collected the general AU predictions, as were the face- and object-trained models, where only the last fully connected layers were available. For each selected layer, we computed the behavioral alignment between macaque I1 and the accuracy-matched model I1 predictions. We then averaged these alignment values within the early and late layer groups and reported the mean and standard error of these averages across models.

Electrophysiological recording and data preprocessing

We recorded neural population activity to the subset of 360 images of the MSFDE imageset, and the 60 images from the Face Object Scene Scrambled (FOSS) imageset. Each image was resized to 256 × 256 x 3 pixel size and presented within the central 8 deg.

In addition to the two behaviorally trained monkeys, we had two different male rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta; monkey 3 and 4) that were surgically implanted with a head post under aseptic conditions. All data were collected, and animal procedures were performed, in accordance with the NIH guidelines, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology Committee on Animal Care, and the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care on the use of laboratory animals and were also approved by the York University Animal Care Committee. Neural activity was recorded using two or three micro-electrode arrays (Utah arrays; Blackrock Microsystems) implanted in IT cortex (see Supplementary Fig. 28). A total of 96 electrodes were connected per array (grid arrangement, 400 μm spacing, 4 x 4 mm span of each array). Array placement was guided by the sulcus pattern, which was visible during the surgery. The electrodes were accessed through a percutaneous connector that allowed simultaneous recording from all 96 electrodes from each array.

During each recording session, band-pass filtered (0.1–10 kHz) neural activity was recorded continuously at a sampling rate of 20 kHz using Intan Recording Controllers (Intan Technologies, LLC). The majority of the data presented here were based on multiunit activity. We detected the multiunit spikes after the raw voltage data were collected. A multiunit spike event was defined as the threshold crossing when voltage (falling edge) deviated by less than three times the standard deviation of the raw voltage values. Our array placements allowed us to sample neural sites from different parts of IT, along the posterior to anterior axis. However, for all the analyses, we did not consider the specific spatial location of the site, and treated each site as a random sample from a pooled IT population.

All neural response data were obtained via a passive viewing task. In this task, monkeys fixated a white square dot (0.2°) for 300 ms to initiate a trial. We then presented a sequence of 5–10 images, each ON for 100 ms followed by a 100 ms gray blank screen. This was followed by a water reward and an inter-trial interval of 500 ms, followed by the next sequence. Trials were aborted if gaze was not held within ±2° of the central fixation dot during any point. Firing rates were computed using a non-overlapping sliding window of 10 ms to capture the temporal dynamics of the neural responses.

Generating behavioral predictions from neural population data

To ask how a downstream readout region might utilize the temporal dynamics of the IT cortex to support facial expression and identity discrimination, we modeled the binary discrimination task using one-vs-rest logistic regression classifiers (Fig. 5). Each classifier was trained with 5-fold stratified cross-validation. We generated ~200 independent decoders based on a combination of decode start time and integration time bin to evaluate how discriminability varied over time. Each neural population decoder was trained to generate 6 expression probabilities or 12 identity probabilities when shown an image at the test phase. We then convert these probabilities into neural population I1 image-level vectors following the same procedures as reported above for the ANNs

Sampling analysis

To assess how the neural population prediction’s accuracy and correlation with behavior scales with population size, we repeated the decoding procedure while systematically subsampling the number of neurons included in the decoder (Fig. 5D). Specifically, we randomly sampled an increasing number of neurons without replacement, ranging from a small subset up to the full recorded population. For each sampling size, we trained the classifier and computed the corresponding performance metric, repeating the process over 100 random draws to obtain a stable mean estimate. Predictions from single neurons

To assess the contribution of individual neurons to expression or identity decoding, we repeated the prediction procedure using the activity of single neurons in isolation (Fig. 7E). We refer to them as the expression discrimination index and the identity discrimination index for each neuron (Fig. 7E). For each neuron, we averaged the response within a specified temporal window, and trained a one-vs-rest logistic regression classifier to predict facial expressions or identities. Therefore, for each image, we generate 6 probabilities for expressions and 12 probabilities for the identities. We then convert these probabilities into neural population I1 image-level vectors following the same procedures as reported above for the ANNs and neural population decoders. Classification accuracy was evaluated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation. We report the resulting average I1 accuracy for each neuron as the corresponding expression or identity discrimination index, enabling us to identify units with above-chance predictive power and to examine how single-neuron encoding dynamics contribute to population-level performance.

Representational similarity analysis

To test whether models that share greater representational similarity with IT neural population responses also achieve higher behavioral alignment, we performed a representational similarity analysis (RSA)67 between model features and neural population responses (Fig. 5E). For each model, we extracted the same feature set used in the behavioral prediction analysis, i.e., unit activations subsampled to match macaque-level prediction accuracy. For the neural population data, we averaged the neural response within a predefined temporal window of interest. We then computed a representational similarity matrix (RSM) by calculating the Pearson correlation of all features or neurons across all 360 images. Model–brain similarity was then quantified by correlating the flattened model and neural RSMs. To account for measurement noise, the similarity values were corrected by dividing them by the square root of the split-half reliabilities of the neural RSMs estimated across trials for each neuron using Spearman–Brown correction.
