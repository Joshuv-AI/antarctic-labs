Exact Banach–Mazur distances of certain ℓ p \ell_{p} -sums and cones
Florian Grundbacher and Tomasz Kobos

Abstract
We determine certain Banach–Mazur distances involving ℓ p \ell_{p} -direct sums of finite-dimensional real normed spaces and related cone constructions of convex bodies. Using a recent characterization of the optimal Banach–Mazur position with respect to the Euclidean ball, we derive a closed formula for the distance from X 1 ⊕ p ⋯ ⊕ p X k X_{1}\oplus_{p}\cdots\oplus_{p}X_{k} to Euclidean space in terms of the distances of the spaces X i X_{i} to Euclidean space. For p = 1 p=1 we show that if d B ​ M ​ ( X , ℓ 1 n ) ≤ 3 d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{n})\leq 3 , then d B ​ M ( X ⊕ 1 ℓ 1 m , ℓ 1 n + m ) = d B ​ M ( X , ℓ 1 n ) d_{BM}(X\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m},\ell_{1}^{n+m})=d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{n}) . Interpreting ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -sums geometrically as double cones motivates a study of single cones over arbitrary convex bases, for which we establish an analogous result with the simplex replacing ℓ 1 \ell_{1} . We further show that in dimension 3 3 the distance between single cones with symmetric bases equals the distance between the bases, and that the same equality holds for double cones over planar symmetric bases in arbitrary dimension, under an additional assumption on the distance of the bases to ℓ 1 2 \ell_{1}^{2} . As consequences, we obtain an explicit isometric embedding of the 2 2 -dimensional symmetric Banach–Mazur compactum into the 3 3 -dimensional (non-symmetric) compactum and lift a recent construction of arbitrarily large equilateral sets in the 2 2 -dimensional symmetric compactum to all higher dimensions.

1
Introduction and Results

The purpose of this paper is to determine exact Banach–Mazur distances between several
\ell_{p}
-sum constructions for finite-dimensional real normed spaces,
as well as related cone constructions for general convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Before we state the main results,
we introduce the required notation and recall some basic facts.
For any integer
n\geq 1
and scalar
p\in[1,\infty]
,
we denote by
\ell_{p}^{n}
the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
defined for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\|x\|_{p}=(|x_{1}|^{p}+\ldots+|x_{n}|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}},

where for
p=\infty
we understand
\|x\|_{\infty}=\max\{|x_{1}|,\ldots,|x_{n}|\}
.
In particular,
\|\cdot\|_{1}
,
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denote the
\ell_{1}
, Euclidean, and maximum norms, respectively.
The unit ball of
\ell_{p}^{n}
is given by
\mathbb{B}_{p}^{n}
.
More generally, given
p\in[1,\infty]
and finite-dimensional normed spaces
X_{i}=(\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}},\|\cdot\|_{X_{i}})
for
i=1,\ldots,k
, the
\ell_{p}
-direct sum

X_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}X_{k}
is the space
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}}
equipped with the norm

\|x\|=\left(\|x^{1}\|^{p}_{X_{1}}+\ldots+\|x^{k}\|^{p}_{X_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where
x=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k})
with
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
for each
i=1,\ldots,k
.
For
p=\infty
, we again understand
\|x\|=\max\{\|x^{1}\|_{X_{1}},\ldots,\|x^{k}\|_{X_{k}}\}
.

For a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, its
v
-translation
by a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by
A+v=\{a+v:a\in A\}
.
Its
\rho
-dilation
for
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
is
\rho A=\{\rho a:a\in A\}
.
We say that
A
is
0
-symmetric
if
(-1)A=A
, and
(centrally) symmetric
if
A+v
is
0
-symmetric for some
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The
Banach–Mazur distance
between two real normed spaces
X,Y
of the same dimension is defined as

d_{BM}(X,Y)=\inf\|T\|\,\|T^{-1}\|,

where the infimum (in fact a minimum in the finite-dimensional setting)
is taken over all invertible linear operators
T:X\to Y

and the standard operator norms are used.
For
convex bodies

K,L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., compact convex sets with non-empty interior,
their
Banach–Mazur distance
is defined as

d_{BM}(K,L)=\inf\{\rho\geq 0:K+u\subseteq T(L+v)\subseteq\rho(K+u)\},

with the infimum (again a minimum) taken over all invertible linear operators
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
More generally, if
K
and
L
are
d
-dimensional
convex bodies, meaning their affine spans have dimension
d
,
then
d_{BM}(K,L)
is understood like above after embedding
K
and
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
When
K
and
L
are
0
-symmetric,
the translation vectors
u
and
v
may be omitted.
Under the well-known correspondence of norms and
0
-symmetric convex bodies,
the equality
d_{BM}(X,Y)=d_{BM}(B_{x},B_{y})
holds for any two normed spaces
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{X})
,
Y=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{Y})
and their respective unit balls
B_{X},B_{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The
n
-dimensional
Banach–Mazur compactum

\mathcal{BM}^{n}
is the set of affine equivalence classes of
n
-dimensional convex bodies equipped with the (multiplicative) metric
d_{BM}
.
By
\mathcal{BM}_{s}^{n}
we denote the subset of
\mathcal{BM}^{n}
corresponding to only
0
-symmetric convex bodies,
i.e., the
n
-dimensional symmetric Banach–Mazur compactum.

The
\ell_{p}
-direct sum of normed spaces is one of the fundamental constructions in the geometry of Banach spaces,
and many of their aspects have been studied in various different contexts.
In particular, estimates of the Banach–Mazur distance when
\ell_{p}
-sums are involved often play an important role, see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
22
]
.

In the vast majority of these works, the Banach–Mazur distance to
\ell_{p}
-sums is only estimated rather than determined exactly.
This reflects a broader fact about Banach–Mazur compacta:
most of their properties have been established only in the asymptotic regime, i.e., as the dimension tends to infinity.
In part, this is due to exact computations of distances requiring solutions to optimization problems that can be challenging even in very specific situations.
One notable example is given by the classical spaces

\ell_{1}^{n}
and
\ell_{\infty}^{n}
.
Their distance is known to be of order
\sqrt{n}
,
but besides the trivial cases
n=1,2
, the exact value has been determined only for
n=3
as
\frac{9}{5}
and
n=4
as
2
(see
[
17
]
).
The distance
\frac{9}{5}
appears to be the largest Banach–Mazur distance computed for
3
-dimensional real normed spaces to date.

The main goal of this paper is to investigate some situations in which the Banach–Mazur distance to an
\ell_{p}
-sum can be determined exactly,
which leads us to explicit computations of the Banach–Mazur distance between many different pairs of normed spaces or convex bodies.
In concrete cases, obtaining an upper bound on the Banach–Mazur distance is usually straightforward,
whereas establishing the matching lower bound is the main challenge, requiring appropriate tools and often restrictions.
One of the few instances in which the Banach–Mazur distance to an
\ell_{p}
-sum has been computed precisely is
[
22
, Proposition
2
]
by Tomczak-Jaegermann.
For any integer
n\geq 1
and any
1<p\leq 2
, it is proved that

d_{BM}(\ell_{p}^{n}\oplus_{p^{*}}\ldots\oplus_{p^{*}}\ell_{p}^{n},\ell_{2}^{n^{2}})=n^{\frac{2-p}{p}},

(1)

where
p^{*}=\frac{p}{p-1}\in[2,\infty)
is the conjugate exponent of
p
and there are exactly
n
identical summands.
The proof method heavily relies on the large set of symmetries present in this particular sum and thus has limited applicability.

We begin with a broad generalization of (
1
), computing the Banach–Mazur distance between the Euclidean space and an arbitrary
\ell_{p}
-sum of finite-dimensional real normed spaces in terms of the distances of the individual summands.
The result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k}\geq 1
be integers and let
X_{i}
be an
n_{i}
-dimensional real normed space for
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Then, for any
p\in[1,\infty]
,

d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}X_{k},\ell^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}}_{2})=\|(d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{2}^{n_{1}}),\ldots,d_{BM}(X_{k},\ell_{2}^{n_{k}})\|_{r},

where
r=\frac{2p}{|p-2|}\in[2,\infty]
(with the conventions
r=\infty
when
p=2
and
r=2
when
p=\infty
).

The proof is presented in Section
2
and relies on a recent characterization
[
8
]
of the optimal Banach–Mazur position with respect to the Euclidean ball by an algebraic decomposition on contact points (see Theorem
2.1
below),
inspired by an old paper of Ader
[
1
]
.
A key observation is that the
\ell_{p}
-sum operation preserves the optimal Banach–Mazur position when each summand is already optimally positioned relative to Euclidean space.
Some further consequences of Theorem
1.1
are discussed in Section
2
.

In Section
3
, we turn our attention to
\ell_{1}
-sums with
\ell_{1}^{m}
and related cone constructions.
Geometrically, if
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
is a normed space with unit ball
B_{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
then the unit ball of
X\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
is a double cone with
m
symmetric vertex pairs over the base
B_{X}
.
This operation turns out to preserve the Banach–Mazur distance to
\ell_{1}
space, provided that the initial distance is not too large.
By duality, an analogous result holds for
\ell_{\infty}
-sums with
\ell_{\infty}^{m}
,
which geometrically corresponds to forming a cylinder over the unit ball of
X
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n,m\geq 1
be integers and let
X
be an
n
-dimensional real normed space with
d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{n})\leq 3
. Then

d_{BM}(X\oplus_{1}\ell^{m}_{1},\ell^{n+m}_{1})=d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{n}).

Similarly, if
d_{BM}(X,\ell_{\infty}^{n})\leq 3
, then

d_{BM}(X\oplus_{\infty}\ell^{m}_{\infty},\ell^{n+m}_{\infty})=d_{BM}(X,\ell_{\infty}^{n}).

With the diameter of
\mathcal{BM}^{n}_{s}
being at most
n
, we obtain the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 1.3
.

For integers
n,m\geq 1
and a real normed space
X
with
\dim(X)=n\leq 3
,

d_{BM}(X\oplus_{1}\ell^{m}_{1},\ell^{n+m}_{1})=d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{n})\quad\text{ and }\quad d_{BM}(X\oplus_{\infty}\ell^{m}_{\infty},\ell^{n+m}_{\infty})=d_{BM}(X,\ell_{\infty}^{n}).

Since the
\ell_{1}
-sum corresponds to forming a centrally symmetric double cone over a symmetric base,
it is natural to consider also (non-symmetric) single cones over arbitrary convex bases.
In this setting, we obtain the following analog of Theorem
1.2
,
where the cross-polytope (i.e., the unit ball of
\ell_{1}
) is replaced by the simplex.
By
\Delta^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
we mean any
simplex
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
and by
\conv(A)
the
convex hull
of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
n,m\geq 1
be integers and let
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
be an
n
-dimensional convex body with
d_{BM}(B,\Delta^{n})\leq 2
.
Let
v^{1},\ldots,v^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
be arbitrary vectors such that
C=\conv(B\cup\{v^{1},\ldots,v^{m}\})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
is an
(n+m)
-dimensional convex body.
Then

d_{BM}(C,\Delta^{n+m})=d_{BM}(B,\Delta^{n}).

The key idea for establishing the matching lower bound for the Banach–Mazur distance in Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
is the same in both cases:
for any Banach–Mazur position of the relevant convex bodies, there exists a linear projection onto a lower-dimensional subspace that maps the (double) cones to convex bodies that are affinely equivalent to the respective cones’ bases.

This projective approach is further developed in Section
4
, where we first consider single cones in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
over planar symmetric bases.
In this setting, the distance between the cones turns out to coincide with the distance between their bases.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
B_{1},B_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
be
2
-dimensional symmetric convex bodies
and let
v^{1},v^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be arbitrary vectors such that
C_{i}=\conv(B_{i}\cup\{v^{i}\})
is a
3
-dimensional convex body for
i=1,2
.
Then

d_{BM}(C_{1},C_{2})=d_{BM}(B_{1},B_{2}).

(2)

As a corollary, we obtain an explicit isometric embedding of the
2
-dimensional symmetric Banach–Mazur compactum
\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}
into the
3
-dimensional (non-symmetric) Banach–Mazur compactum
\mathcal{BM}^{3}
.
To our knowledge, this is surprisingly the first instance of such an embedding between Banach–Mazur compacta of different dimensions (apart from the trivial embedding of the
1
-dimensional compactum).
Here and throughout the paper, by
e^{1},\ldots,e^{n}
we denote the
canonical unit basis
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Corollary 1.6
.

The mapping
\varphi\colon\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}\to\mathcal{BM}^{3}
defined by

\varphi(C)=\conv\left((C\times\{0\})\cup\{\pm e^{3}\}\right)\quad\text{for }C\in\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}

is an isometric embedding of
\left(\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s},d_{BM}\right)
into
\left(\mathcal{BM}^{3},d_{BM}\right)
.

It is natural to conjecture that an equality analogous to (
2
) holds for centrally symmetric double cones, which would strengthen Corollary
1.6
to an embedding of
\mathcal{BM}_{s}^{2}
into
\mathcal{BM}_{s}^{3}
.
We do not know whether this is true in general.
However, we can prove it under the additional assumption that at least one base is not too close to
\ell_{1}^{2}
, in exchange for embedding the double cones into arbitrary dimensions.
The necessity of this assumption is discussed after the proof of the theorem in Section
4
(see also Open Question
4.2
and Remark
4.3
for related details).

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be
2
-dimensional real normed spaces
with
d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{1}^{2})\geq d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2})
or
d_{BM}(X_{2},\ell_{1}^{2})\geq d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2})
.
Then, for any integer
m\geq 1
,

d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m},X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m})=d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2}).

By duality, an analogous result holds for the
\ell_{\infty}
-sums with
\ell_{\infty}^{m}
.
As an application, we obtain a local isometric embedding of
\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}\setminus\{\ell_{1}^{2}\}
into
\mathcal{BM}^{n}_{s}
for any
n\geq 3
.
In the following corollary, we regard the symmetric Banach–Mazur compactum
\mathcal{BM}^{n}_{s}
as consisting of isometry classes of
n
-dimensional normed spaces.

Corollary 1.8
.

For an integer
n\geq 3
, let
\psi:\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}\to\mathcal{BM}^{n}_{s}
be the mapping defined by

\psi(X)=X\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{n-2}\quad\text{for }X\in\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}.

Suppose for
X\in\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}\setminus\{\ell_{1}^{2}\}
that
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}
satisfy

d_{BM}(X,X_{i})\leq d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}
for
i=1,2
.
Then

d_{BM}(\psi(X_{1}),\psi(X_{2}))=d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2}).

In particular,
\psi
is a local isometric embedding of
\left(\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}\setminus\{\ell_{1}^{2}\},d_{BM}\right)
into
\left(\mathcal{BM}^{n}_{s},d_{BM}\right)
.

As a further consequence, we lift a recent construction of equilateral sets in the
2
-dimensional symmetric Banach–Mazur compactum
[
16
]
to higher dimensions.
Recall that a set
S
in a metric space is called
equilateral
if there exists
d>0
such that all distinct
x,y\in S
are at distance
d
.

Corollary 1.9
.

For integers
n,m\geq 2
, there exist equilateral sets of cardinality
m
in
\mathcal{BM}_{s}^{n}
.

Proofs of Corollaries
1.8
and
1.9
are given at the end of Section
4
.

Throughout the paper, we frequently use the equality
d_{BM}(X,Y)=d_{BM}(B_{X},B_{Y})
for normed spaces
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{X})
,
Y=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{Y})
and their respective unit balls
B_{X},B_{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, choosing whichever viewpoint is more convenient.
We also refer to the well-known duality of
\ell_{p}
-sums, namely

(X_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}X_{k})^{*}=X^{*}_{1}\oplus_{p^{*}}\ldots\oplus_{p^{*}}X^{*}_{k}

for any finite-dimensional normed spaces
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
and
p\in[1,\infty]
,
where
X^{*}
denotes the
dual space
of a normed space
X
and
p^{*}=\frac{p}{p-1}\in[1,\infty]
is the conjugate exponent of
p
.
Given a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
for the coordinates of
x
in the canonical unit basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2
The Distance of
\ell_{p}
-Sums to the Euclidean Space

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
.
The main ingredient is the following characterization of the optimal Banach–Mazur position with respect to the Euclidean ball.
Similar to the characterization of the maximal-volume (John) ellipsoid by the decomposition of the identity on the contact points,
the optimal Banach–Mazur distance ellipsoids
can be described by an algebraic condition on the contact points.
For the proof, historical context, and different applications of this result, the reader is referred to
[
8
]
.
By
\bd(A)
we denote the
boundary
of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
0
-symmetric convex body and let
R\geq r>0
be such that
r\mathbb{B}^{n}\subseteq K\subseteq R\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

d_{BM}(K,\mathbb{B}^{n})=\frac{R}{r}
.

(ii)

There exist integers
N,M\geq 1
, inner contact points
y^{1},\ldots,y^{N}\in\bd(K)\cap\bd(r\mathbb{B}^{n})
, outer contact points
z^{1},\ldots,z^{M}\in\bd(K)\cap\bd(R\mathbb{B}^{n})
, as well as weights
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}
,
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{M}>0
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}y^{i}(y^{i})^{T}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mu_{j}z^{j}(z^{j})^{T}.

The majority of the work for proving Theorem
1.1
is handled by the following lemma.
For
0
-symmetric convex bodies
C_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
, where
i=1,\ldots,k
, their
\ell_{p}
-sum
C_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}C_{k}
is the
0
-symmetric unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}}
of the
\ell_{p}
-sum of the corresponding normed spaces with unit balls
C_{i}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k}\geq 1
be integers and let
C_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
be a
0
-symmetric convex body for
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Suppose that for
d_{i}=d_{BM}(C_{i},\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{i}})
the inclusions
\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{i}}\subseteq C_{i}\subseteq d_{i}\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{i}}
hold for each
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Then, for every
p\in[2,\infty]
,

\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}}\subseteq C_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}C_{k}\subseteq\|(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\|_{r}\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}}

(3)

and

d_{BM}(C_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}C_{k},\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}})=\|(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\|_{r},

where
r=\frac{2p}{p-2}\in[2,\infty]
(with the conventions
r=\infty
when
p=2
and
r=2
when
p=\infty
).

Proof.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{C_{i}}
denote the norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
whose unit ball is
C_{i}
.
By assumption,

\|x^{i}\|_{C_{i}}\leq\|x^{i}\|_{2}\leq d_{i}\,\|x^{i}\|_{C_{i}}

(4)

for all
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
.
Let
C=C_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}C_{k}
and let
\|\cdot\|_{C}
be the corresponding norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}}
.
By (
4
) and
p\geq 2
, for
x=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}}
with
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
we can estimate

\|x\|_{C}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|x^{i}\|^{p}_{C_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|x^{i}\|^{p}_{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|x^{i}\|^{2}_{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\|x\|_{2}.

(5)

For the estimate in the other direction, (
4
) and Hölder’s inequality with exponents
\frac{p}{p-2}
and
\frac{p}{2}
(which extends naturally to the cases of
p=2
and
p=\infty
) yield

\begin{split}\|x\|^{2}_{2}&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|x^{i}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}^{2}\,\|x^{i}\|^{2}_{C_{i}}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right)^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|x^{i}\|^{p}_{C_{i}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}\\
&=\|(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\|^{2}_{r}\,\|x\|_{C}^{2}.\end{split}

(6)

This establishes (
3
).

It remains to show that this chain of inclusions is optimal for the Banach–Mazur distance.
By a simple inductive argument, it suffices to consider only the case of
k=2
.
Moreover, by a continuity argument, we can suppose that
p\in(2,\infty)
.
Our goal is to use the characterization of the Banach–Mazur position with respect to the Euclidean ball given in Theorem
2.1
.

Since the positions
\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{i}}\subseteq C_{i}\subseteq d_{i}\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{i}}
are optimal for the Banach–Mazur distance for
i=1,2
, Theorem
2.1
provides integers
N_{1},N_{2},M_{1},M_{2}\geq 1
,
four sets of contact points

y^{1},\ldots,y^{N_{1}}\in\bd(C_{1})\cap\bd(\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}})
,

z^{1},\ldots,z^{M_{1}}\in\bd(C_{1})\cap\bd(d_{1}\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}})
,

u^{1},\ldots,u^{N_{2}}\in\bd(C_{2})\cap\bd(\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{2}})
, and

v^{1},\ldots,v^{M_{2}}\in\bd(C_{2})\cap\bd(d_{2}\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{2}})
,
as well as weights
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},\gamma_{i},\delta_{i}>0
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\alpha_{i}y^{i}(y^{i})^{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\beta_{i}z^{i}(z^{i})^{T}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}\gamma_{i}u^{i}(u^{i})^{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{M_{2}}\delta_{i}v^{i}(v^{i})^{T}.

(7)

By pairing each contact point with its negative and normalizing, we may further assume

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\alpha_{i}y^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\beta_{i}z^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}\gamma_{i}u^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{M_{2}}\delta_{i}v^{i}=0\quad\text{ and }\quad d_{2}^{\frac{4}{p-2}}\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\beta_{i}=d_{1}^{\frac{4}{p-2}}\sum_{i=1}^{M_{2}}\delta_{i}=1.

(8)

Our goal is to determine contact points of
C=C_{1}\oplus_{p}C_{2}
with
\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
and
\|(d_{1},d_{2})\|_{r}\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}

for which an analogous algebraic decomposition exists.

We first note that for each
i=1,\ldots,N_{1}
the point
(y^{i},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
is a common point of
\bd(C)
and
\bd(\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}+n_{2}})

since such a point satisfies (
5
) with equality.
Similarly, for each
i=1,\ldots,N_{2}
the point
(0,u^{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
is a common point of
\bd(C)
and
\bd(\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}+n_{2}})
.
On the other hand, the contact points of
C
and
\|(d_{1},d_{2})\|_{r}\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
include all points of the form

(\widetilde{z}^{i},\widetilde{v}^{j})=\frac{\|(d_{1},d_{2})\|_{r}}{\sqrt{d_{1}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}+d_{2}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}}}\left(d_{1}^{\frac{2}{p-2}}z^{i},d_{2}^{\frac{2}{p-2}}v^{j}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}

for
i=1,\ldots,M_{1}
and
j=1,\ldots,M_{2}
.
Indeed,
\frac{1}{d_{1}}\|z^{i}\|_{2}=\|z^{i}\|_{C_{1}}=1
and
\frac{1}{d_{2}}\|v^{j}\|_{2}=\|v^{j}\|_{C_{2}}=1

yield that such points achieve equality in the estimate (
6
) by

\displaystyle\left\|\left(d_{1}^{\frac{2}{p-2}}z^{i},d_{2}^{\frac{2}{p-2}}v^{j}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=d_{1}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}+d_{2}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}=\left(d_{1}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}+d_{2}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right)^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\left(d_{1}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}+d_{2}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}

\displaystyle=\left(d_{1}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}+d_{2}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\right)^{\frac{p-2}{p}}\left(\left\|d_{1}^{\frac{2}{p-2}}z^{i}\right\|^{p}_{C_{1}}+\left\|d_{2}^{\frac{2}{p-2}}v^{j}\right\|^{p}_{C_{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{p}}.

To the inner contact points
(y^{i},0)
we assign the weights
\eta_{i}:=\alpha_{i}
,
to the inner contact points
(0,u^{i})
the weights
\kappa_{i}:=\gamma_{i}
,
and to the outer contact points
(\widetilde{z}^{i},\widetilde{v}^{j})

the weights

\omega_{ij}:=\frac{d_{1}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}+d_{2}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}}{\|(d_{1},d_{2})\|_{r}^{2}}\,\beta_{i}\delta_{j}.

Then,

\eta_{i}(y^{i},0)(y^{i},0)^{T}=\alpha_{i}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}y^{i}(y^{i})^{T}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right]\quad\text{ and }\quad\kappa_{i}(0,u^{i})(0,u^{i})^{T}=\gamma_{i}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&u^{i}(u^{i})^{T}\end{array}\right].

Moreover,

\omega_{ij}(\widetilde{z}^{i},\widetilde{v}^{j})(\widetilde{z}^{i},\widetilde{v}^{j})^{T}=\beta_{i}\delta_{j}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}d_{1}^{\frac{4}{p-2}}z^{i}(z^{i})^{T}&(d_{1}d_{2})^{\frac{2}{p-2}}z^{i}(v^{j})^{T}\\
(d_{1}d_{2})^{\frac{2}{p-2}}v^{j}(z^{i})^{T}&d_{2}^{\frac{4}{p-2}}v^{j}(v^{j})^{T}\end{array}\right].

Therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\eta_{i}(y^{i},0)(y^{i},0)^{T}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\kappa_{j}(0,u^{j})(0,u^{j})^{T}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\alpha_{i}y^{i}(y^{i})^{T}&0\\
0&\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\gamma_{j}u^{j}(u^{j})^{T}\end{array}\right],

and similarly

\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{2}}\omega_{ij}(\widetilde{z}^{i},\widetilde{v}^{j})(\widetilde{z}^{i},\widetilde{v}^{j})^{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{2}}\beta_{i}\delta_{j}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}d_{1}^{\frac{4}{p-2}}z^{i}(z^{i})^{T}&(d_{1}d_{2})^{\frac{2}{p-2}}z^{i}(v^{j})^{T}\\
(d_{1}d_{2})^{\frac{2}{p-2}}v^{j}(z^{i})^{T}&d_{2}^{\frac{4}{p-2}}v^{j}(v^{j})^{T}\end{array}\right].

From (
8
) it follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{2}}\beta_{i}\delta_{j}z^{i}(v^{j})^{T}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\beta_{i}z^{i}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M_{2}}\delta_{j}v^{j}\right)^{T}=0,

and in the same way
\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{2}}\beta_{i}\delta_{j}v^{j}(z^{i})^{T}=0
.
Moreover, conditions (
7
) and (
8
) yield

\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{2}}\beta_{i}\delta_{j}d_{1}^{\frac{4}{p-2}}z^{i}(z^{i})^{T}=\left(d_{1}^{\frac{4}{p-2}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{2}}\delta_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\beta_{i}z^{i}(z^{i})^{T}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\alpha_{i}y^{i}(y^{i})^{T}

and similarly
\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{2}}\beta_{i}\delta_{j}d_{2}^{\frac{4}{p-2}}v^{j}(v^{j})^{T}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\gamma_{j}u^{j}(u^{j})^{T}
.
This establishes that

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\eta_{i}(y^{i},0)(y^{i},0)^{T}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\kappa_{j}(0,u^{j})(0,u^{j})^{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{2}}\omega_{ij}(\widetilde{z}^{i},\widetilde{v}^{j})(\widetilde{z}^{i},\widetilde{v}^{j})^{T},

which shows that the required decomposition on the contact points exists.
Theorem
2.1
now yields that the chain of inclusions (
3
) is optimal for the Banach–Mazur distance between
C
and
\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
, that is,
d_{BM}(C,\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{1}+n_{2}})=\|(d_{1},d_{2})\|_{r}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

With the previous lemma established, the proof of Theorem
1.1
is straightforward.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For
p\geq 2
, the assertion follows directly from Lemma
2.2
.
Indeed, for each
i=1,\ldots,k
there exists a normed space
X^{\prime}_{i}

isometric to
X_{i}
whose unit ball
C_{i}
satisfies
\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{i}}\subseteq C_{i}\subseteq d_{i}\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{i}}
for
d_{i}=d_{BM}(C_{i},\mathbb{B}_{2}^{n_{i}})
.
Applying Lemma
2.2
to these unit balls, we obtain that the sum
X^{\prime}_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}X^{\prime}_{k}
has the required distance to the Euclidean space.
Since the spaces
X^{\prime}_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}X^{\prime}_{k}
and
X_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}X_{k}
are clearly isometric, their distances to the Euclidean space are the same.

The case
1\leq p<2
follows easily by duality,
as for any finite-dimensional normed spaces
X,Y
of the same dimension we have
d_{BM}(X^{*},Y^{*})=d_{BM}(X,Y)
.
In particular,
d_{BM}(X_{i}^{*},\ell_{2}^{n_{i}})=d_{BM}(X_{i},\ell_{2}^{n_{i}})=d_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots k
.
If now
p^{*}=\frac{p}{p-1}\in(2,\infty]

denotes the conjugate exponent of
p
, then the duality of
\ell_{p}
-sums and the first case together yield

d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}X_{k},\ell^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}}_{2})=d_{BM}(X_{1}^{*}\oplus_{p*}\ldots\oplus_{p*}X_{k}^{*},\ell^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}}_{2})=\|(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\|_{r^{*}},

where

r^{*}=\frac{2p^{*}}{p^{*}-2}=\frac{\frac{2p}{p-1}}{\frac{2-p}{p-1}}=\frac{2p}{2-p}=r,

as required.
∎

Theorem
1.1
gives a simple
1
-line proof of the well-known Banach–Mazur distance between
\ell_{2}^{n}
and
\ell_{p}^{n}
, so it in particular generalizes (
1
), as pointed out in the introduction.
Another obvious consequence is the following.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\geq 1
be integers and let
X_{i}
be an
n_{i}
-dimensional real normed space with
d_{i}=d_{BM}(X_{i},\ell_{2}^{n_{i}})
for
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Define

f(p)=d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{p}\ldots\oplus_{p}X_{k},\ell_{2}^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}})

for
p\in[1,\infty]
.
Then

\max\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\}=f(2)\leq f(p)=f\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)\leq f(1)=f(\infty)=\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+\ldots+d_{k}^{2}}.

As a further application of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain that every Hanner polytope achieves the maximal possible Banach–Mazur distance to the Euclidean ball among
n
-dimensional centrally symmetric convex bodies.
Recall that a
Hanner polytope
is a centrally symmetric polytope that arises by taking arbitrary iterated
\ell_{1}
- and
\ell_{\infty}
-sums of segments or other lower-dimensional Hanner polytopes.
These polytopes were introduced by Hanner
[
10
]
and are particularly well-known for their role in the famous Mahler conjecture,
where they are conjectured to be the only minimizers of the Mahler volume product among centrally symmetric convex bodies.
It was proved by Kim
[
15
]
that every Hanner polytope is indeed a strict local minimizer of this product with respect to the Banach–Mazur distance.
With regard to the Banach–Mazur distance itself, it appears that specialists in the field have long been aware that Hanner polytopes maximize the distance to the Euclidean ball among centrally symmetric convex bodies, where the John Ellipsoid Theorem shows that this maximal distance is at most
\sqrt{n}
.
However, to our knowledge, no proof of this fact for general Hanner polytopes has appeared in the published literature.
The following corollary provides an immediate proof via Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Every Hanner polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has the Banach–Mazur distance
\sqrt{n}
to the
n
-dimensional Euclidean ball.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the dimension
n\geq 1
.
There is nothing to prove for
n=1
,
so let us assume
n\geq 2
.
Suppose that
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Hanner polytope that is the unit ball of the normed space
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{X})
.
By the definition of a Hanner polytope, there exist normed spaces
X_{i}=(\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}},\|\cdot\|_{X_{i}})
for
i=1,2
with some Hanner polytopes as their unit balls such that
n_{1}+n_{2}=n
and either
X=X_{1}\oplus_{1}X_{2}
or
X=X_{1}\oplus_{\infty}X_{2}
.
By the inductive assumption and Theorem
1.1
, we have in both cases
that

d_{BM}(X,\ell_{2}^{n})=\sqrt{d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{2}^{n_{1}})^{2}+d_{BM}(X_{2},\ell_{2}^{n_{2}})^{2}}=\sqrt{n_{1}+n_{2}}=\sqrt{n}.\qed

Let us point out that Hanner polytopes are not the only maximizers of the Banach–Mazur distance to the Euclidean ball among centrally symmetric convex bodies when
n\geq 4
,
even in the class of polytopes (see
[
8
, Example
2.5
]
).

2.2
extends to the non-symmetric setting.
The
\ell_{p}
-sum
C_{1}\oplus_{p}\cdots\oplus_{p}C_{k}
of (not necessarily symmetric) convex bodies containing the origin in their interiors is defined analogously to before,
but using the gauge (or Minkowski) functional induced by each

instead of a norm.

Remark 2.5
.

Lemma
2.2
remains valid for general (not necessarily

-symmetric) convex bodies

satisfying

In the non-symmetric case, the characterization of the optimal Banach–Mazur position with respect to the Euclidean ball (the analog of Theorem

requires an additional balancing condition (see

), which in the considered case writes as

In the symmetric case, this vanishing condition can always be imposed by pairing each contact point with its negative and distributing the weights accordingly.
Consequently, in the non-symmetric case the equalities (

) can still be assumed,
and one readily verifies that the analogous balancing condition is also satisfied by the constructed contact points of

with

and

3
The Distance of (Double) Cones to
\ell_{1}^{n}
and the Simplex

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
.
While obtaining the exact values of the Banach–Mazur distances as upper bounds is straightforward in both cases,
verifying the matching lower bounds is more involved and relies on appropriately projecting everything onto the bases of the cones.
Clearly, for convex bodies
C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}\subseteq dC_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a linear projection
P
,
the inclusions are preserved to
P(C_{1})\subseteq P(C_{2})\subseteq dP(C_{1})
.
Thus, whenever the images
P(C_{1})
and
P(C_{2})
can be controlled, obtaining lower bounds on
d_{BM}(C_{1},C_{2})
from the lower-dimensional images becomes possible.
Controlling the projective images is manageable when the set of extreme points of the original sets is small—as in the case of
\ell_{1}^{n}
(corresponding to the cross-polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) or the simplex.
In this way, we show for any Banach–Mazur position between the cones that the projection
P
can be chosen to give images of the cones that are affinely equivalent to their bases.
A similar argument about controlling projections appears in the proof of the equality
d_{BM}(\ell_{1}^{3},\ell_{\infty}^{3})=\frac{9}{5}
in
[
17
]
.
Besides this instance, the method seems to be novel for the study of (exact) Banach–Mazur distances.

Throughout this and the next section, we shall repeatedly use the following obvious observations:
For a
d
-dimensional convex body
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
d<n
, all
n
-dimensional cones of the form
C=\conv(B^{\prime}\cup\{v^{1},\ldots v^{n-d}\})
with
B^{\prime}
an affine image of
B
and appropriate vectors
v^{1},\ldots,v^{n-d}
are affinely equivalent.
The analogous statement about double cones
\conv(B^{\prime}\cup\{\pm v^{1},\ldots,\pm v^{n-d}\})
for
0
-symmetric
B^{\prime}
holds as well.
In particular, we often assume that
B^{\prime}
is contained in the
linear span

\lin\{e^{1},\ldots,e^{d}\}

and
v^{1},\ldots,v^{n-d}
are the remaining vectors from the canonical unit basis.

Next, applying a projection with
1
-dimensional kernel to a
d
-dimensional convex body reduces its dimension by at most
1
.
It does so if and only if the kernel of the projection is parallel to the affine span of the convex body.

We also use the simple lemma below.
It is proved here to avoid repeating the argument.
By
[x,y]
we denote the closed
segment
connecting
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
B_{1},B_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex,
let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector,
and let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a linear operator.
If
B_{1}\subseteq\conv(B_{2}\cup\{v\})
,

v\notin B_{1}
,
and
T(v)\in T(B_{1})
,
then

T(\conv(B_{2}\cup\{v\}))=T(B_{2}).

If
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are
0
-symmetric, then the assumption
B_{1}\subseteq\conv(B_{2}\cup\{v\})
can be weakened to
B_{1}\subseteq\conv(B_{2}\cup\{\pm v\})
.

Proof.

For any set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is clear that

T(\conv(B_{2}\cup A))=\conv(T(B_{2})\cup T(A)),

so it suffices to prove
T(v)\in T(B_{2})
.
Now, choose any
u\in B_{1}
with
T(v)=T(u)
.
If
B_{1}\subseteq\conv(B_{2}\cup\{v\})
,
there exist some
w\in B_{2}
and
\lambda\in[0,1)
such that

u=(1-\lambda)w+\lambda v.

(9)

Then

T(w)=T\left(\frac{u-\lambda v}{1-\lambda}\right)=\frac{T(u)-\lambda T(v)}{1-\lambda}=\frac{T(v)-\lambda T(v)}{1-\lambda}=T(v)

verifies
T(v)\in T(B_{2})
.

If
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are
0
-symmetric and
B_{1}\subseteq\conv(B_{2}\cup\{\pm v\})
, it is straightforward to see that
u
can still be given as in (
9
), but possibly using
-v
instead of
v
.
If it uses
v
, then
T(v)\in T(B_{2})
follows exactly like before.
Otherwise,

T\left(\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}w\right)=\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}T\left(\frac{u+\lambda v}{1-\lambda}\right)=\frac{T(u)+\lambda T(v)}{1+\lambda}=\frac{T(v)+\lambda T(v)}{1+\lambda}=T(v)

and
\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}w\in[0,w]\subseteq B_{2}
complete the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Since
(X\oplus_{1}Y)^{*}=X^{*}\oplus_{\infty}Y^{*}
holds for all finite-dimensional real normed spaces
X
and
Y
,
it suffices to prove the result for the
\ell_{1}
-sum.
Moreover, by induction it is enough to show that for any
n
-dimensional real normed space
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{X})
with
d^{*}:=d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{n})\leq 3
, we have

d:=d_{BM}(X\oplus_{1}\mathbb{R},\ell_{1}^{n+1})=d^{*}.

We identify the underlying vector space of
X\oplus_{1}\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
in the standard way, i.e.,
the vector space of
X
is identified with the subspace
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
given by
x_{n+1}=0
.
To prove the upper bound
d\leq d^{*}
, we assume that
\|\cdot\|
is a norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is isometric to
\|\cdot\|_{X}
and satisfies

\|x\|_{1}\leq\|x\|\leq d^{*}\|x\|_{1}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then also

\|x\|_{1}+|x_{n+1}|\leq\|x\|+|x_{n+1}|\leq d^{*}\|x\|_{1}+|x_{n+1}|\leq d^{*}(\|x\|_{1}+|x_{n+1}|)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all
x_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}
.
It remains to establish the lower bound
d\geq d^{*}
.

Let
K=\conv\{\pm v^{1},\ldots,\pm v^{n+1}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a linear image of the cross-polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
satisfying

C\subseteq K\subseteq dC,

(10)

where
C=\conv(B\cup\{\pm e^{n+1}\})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is the unit ball of the space
X\oplus_{1}\mathbb{R}
for
B\subseteq Y
the unit ball of
X
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
Since
e^{n+1}\in C\subseteq K
, some vertex
v^{i}
of
K
satisfies
|v^{i}_{n+1}|\geq 1
.
We may suppose that
v^{n+1}_{n+1}\geq 1
.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, let
\pi_{Y}(x)=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},0)
denote its orthogonal projection onto
Y
.
Then

3\geq d^{*}\geq d\geq\|\pi_{Y}(v^{n+1})\|_{X}+|v^{n+1}_{n+1}|\geq\|\pi_{Y}(v^{n+1})\|_{X}+1,

so that
r:=\|\pi_{Y}(v^{n+1})\|_{X}\leq 2
.
Next, we define a vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
as

w:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle e^{n+1}+\frac{1}{r\,v^{n+1}_{n+1}}\pi_{Y}(v^{n+1}),&\text{if }r>0,\\
e^{n+1},&\text{if }r=0.\end{cases}

Now, let us take a linear projection
P:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to Y
given for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
as
P(x)=x-x_{n+1}w
.
Since
v^{n+1}_{n+1}\geq 1
,
we have
\|P(e^{n+1})\|_{X}=\|-\pi_{Y}(w)\|_{X}\leq 1

and, thus,
\pm P(e^{n+1})\in B
.
It follows that

P(C)=\conv(P(B)\cup\{\pm P(e^{n+1})\})=B.

Applying
P
to the inclusions (
10
) gives

B=P(C)\subseteq P(K)\subseteq dP(C)=dB.

(11)

Moreover, if
r=0
, then

P(v^{n+1})=v^{n+1}-v^{n+1}_{n+1}e^{n+1}=\pi_{Y}(v^{n+1})=0\in B.

If instead
r\in(0,2]
, then

P(v^{n+1})=v^{n+1}-v^{n+1}_{n+1}e^{n+1}-\frac{v^{n+1}_{n+1}}{r\,v^{n+1}_{n+1}}\pi_{Y}(v^{n+1})=\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\pi_{Y}(v^{n+1}),

so we have
\|P(v^{n+1})\|_{X}=\left|1-\frac{1}{r}\right|\cdot r=|r-1|\in[0,1]
.
In particular, we obtain
P(v^{n+1})\in B
in this case as well.
Since
P(B)=B\subseteq K
and
v^{n+1}\notin B
, Lemma
3.1
shows that

P(K)=P(\conv\{\pm v^{1},\ldots,\pm v^{n}\})=\conv\{\pm P(v^{1}),\ldots,\pm P(v^{n})\}

is a centrally symmetric polytope with at most
2n
vertices in the
n
-dimensional subspace
Y
.
As a projection of an
(n+1)
-dimensional convex body, it is
n
-dimensional and therefore a non-degenerate linear image of the
n
-dimensional cross-polytope.
Finally,
(
11
) and the definition of
d^{*}
yield
d\geq d^{*}
,
finishing the proof.
∎

Unlike the symmetric case, the non-symmetric case has no a priori guarantee for any specific centre of homothety in an optimal Banach–Mazur position.
Thus, when considering the Banach–Mazur distance between single cones and the simplex, the position of the simplex is less constrained compared to the cross-polytope in the symmetric case.
The following lemma extends the projective argument to handle this additional degree of freedom.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an
(n-1)
-dimensional convex body containing
0

and contained in the subspace
Y=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n}=0\}
.
Let
d\in[1,2]
and

The Distance of (Double) Cones With Arbitrary Planar Symmetric Bases

In this section, we further develop the projective method to study the Banach–Mazur distances between single and double cones with arbitrary planar symmetric bases.
Establishing the matching lower bounds on the distances again relies on finding linear projections in any Banach–Mazur position that simultaneously map both cones to sets affinely equivalent to their bases
or similarly easily handled.
When dealing with arbitrary cones, controlling the projective images is more involved compared to the previous section.
Our approach therefore requires a more detailed geometric analysis and, in some places, appropriate assumptions.

Our first goal is to verify Theorem

Next, we turn to Theorem
1.7
on double cones with planar symmetric bases.
The reasoning is quite similar to the one used in the proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

To prove the upper bound, suppose that the norms
\|\cdot\|_{X_{1}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{X_{2}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are chosen so that

\|x^{\prime}\|_{X_{1}}\leq\|x^{\prime}\|_{X_{2}}\leq d^{*}\|x^{\prime}\|_{X_{1}}

for all
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
d^{*}:=d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2})
.
Then, for any
x=(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2}
with
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

\|x\|_{X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m}}=\|x^{\prime}\|_{X_{1}}+\|x^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}\leq\|x^{\prime}\|_{X_{2}}+\|x^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}=\|x\|_{X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m}}

and

\|x\|_{X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m}}=\|x^{\prime}\|_{X_{2}}+\|x^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}\leq d^{*}\|x^{\prime}\|_{X_{1}}+d^{*}\|x^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}=d^{*}\|x\|_{X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m}}.

Thus,

d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m},X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m})\leq d^{*}

for every integer
m\geq 1
.

To prove the lower bound,
we proceed by induction on
m\geq 0
,
with the convention that
X\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{0}=X
.
Therefore, there is nothing to show for
m=0
.
Now, assume
m\geq 1
and that the lower bound holds for
m-1
, that is,

d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m-1},X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m-1})\geq d^{*}.

(19)

Without loss of generality, we may assume that

d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{1}^{2})\geq d^{*}.

(20)

Let
C_{1},C_{2}
be unit balls of spaces isometric to
X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m}
and
X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m}
, respectively, such that

C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}\subseteq dC_{1}

(21)

for
d:=d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m},X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m})
.
We may suppose that
C_{1}=\conv(B_{1}\cup\{\pm e^{3},\ldots,\pm e^{m+2}\})
, where
B_{1}\subseteq\lin\{e^{1},e^{2}\}
is the unit ball of
X_{1}
,
and
C_{2}=\conv(B_{2}\cup\{\pm v^{3},\ldots,\pm v^{m+2}\})
for some
2
-dimensional
0
-symmetric convex body
B_{2}
affinely equivalent to the unit ball of
X_{2}
and vectors
v^{3},\ldots,v^{m+2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2}
.

Since
e^{m+2}\in C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}
, there exists a point
v\in C_{2}
with
v_{m+2}\geq 1
.
We define a vector
w:=\frac{v}{v_{m+2}}
.
Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m+2}
be the subspace given by
x_{m+2}=0
and let
P:\mathbb{R}^{m+2}\to Y
be the linear projection onto
Y
with
P(x)=x-x_{m+2}w
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2}
. Then
P(v)=0
and

P(e^{m+2})=e^{m+2}-w=-\pi_{Y}(w)

for
\pi_{Y}
the orthogonal projection onto
Y
.
Writing
C_{1}^{\prime}=\conv(B_{1}\cup\{\pm e^{3},\ldots,\pm e^{m+1}\})
, we note that

2\geq d^{*}\geq d\geq\|\pi_{Y}(v)\|_{C_{1}^{\prime}}+|v_{m+2}|.

Consequently, by
v_{m+2}\geq 1
,

\|P(e^{m+2})\|_{C_{1}^{\prime}}=\|-\pi_{Y}(w)\|_{C_{1}^{\prime}}\leq\frac{2-v_{m+2}}{v_{m+2}}\leq 1.

It follows that

P(C_{1})=\conv(P(C_{1}^{\prime})\cup\{\pm P(e^{m+2})\})=C_{1}^{\prime}.

Applying
P
to (
21
) yields

P(C_{1})\subseteq P(C_{2})\subseteq dP(C_{1}).

(22)

To handle
P(C_{2})
, we consider two cases for the choice of
v
.
If
v
is one of the
\pm v^{i}
for
i=3,\ldots,m+2
, say
v=v^{m+2}
, then
P(v)=0
implies

P(C_{2})=\conv(P(B_{2})\cup\{\pm P(v^{3}),\ldots,\pm P(v^{m+1})\})=:C_{2}^{\prime}.

Since
C_{2}^{\prime}
is
(m+1)
-dimensional as a projection of an
(m+2)
-dimensional convex body,

P(B_{2})
is
2
-dimensional by

m+1=\dim(C_{2}^{\prime})\leq\dim(P(B_{2}))+m-1\leq m+1.

Consequently,

P(B_{2})
is affinely equivalent to
B_{2}
,
and
C_{2}^{\prime}
is the unit ball of a space isometric to
X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m-1}
.
With (
19
) and (
22
), the claim follows by

d\geq d_{BM}(P(C_{1}),P(C_{2}))=d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m-1},X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m-1})\geq d^{*}.

Now, assume that
v
belongs to
B_{2}
.
Since
P(v)=0
, the projection
P(B_{2})
is a segment.
Consequently,

P(C_{2})=\conv(P(B_{2})\cup\{\pm P(v^{3}),\ldots,\pm P(v^{m+2})\})

is a centrally symmetric polytope with at most
2(m+1)
vertices.
Since it is
(m+1)
-dimensional as a projection of an
(m+2)
-dimensional convex body,
P(C_{2})
is a non-degenerate linear image of the
(m+1)
-dimensional cross-polytope.
Therefore, Theorem
1.2
implies

d_{BM}(P(C_{1}),P(C_{2}))=d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m-1},\ell_{1}^{m+1})=d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{1}^{2}).

With the assumption (
20
) and the inclusions (
22
), we obtain

d\geq d_{BM}(P(C_{1}),P(C_{2}))=d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{1}^{2})\geq d^{*}.

Since
v
can always be chosen in one of the two ways considered above, the induction step and the theorem follow.
∎

Before we proceed with the corollaries of Theorem
1.7
,
let us discuss the necessity of the assumption on the distances to
\ell_{1}^{2}
in the theorem.
If the assumption fails, meaning that
d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{1}^{2})<d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2})
and
d_{BM}(X_{2},\ell_{1}^{2})<d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2})
,
then the conclusion of the theorem is wrong,
at least for
m\geq 2
.
Indeed, suppose that
m\geq 2
and there exists some
1\leq d<d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2})
such that
d_{BM}(X_{i},\ell_{1}^{2})\leq d
for
i=1,2
. Without loss of generality we may assume that

\frac{1}{d}\|x\|_{1}\leq\|x\|_{X_{1}}\leq\|x\|_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\|x\|_{1}\leq\|x\|_{X_{2}}\leq d\|x\|_{1}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then, for all
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+2}
with
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m-2}
,

\|(x,y,z)\|_{X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m}}=\|x\|_{X_{1}}+\|y\|_{1}+\|z\|_{1}\leq\|x\|_{1}+\|y\|_{X_{2}}+\|z\|_{1}=\|(x,y,z)\|_{\ell_{1}^{2}\oplus_{1}X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m-2}}.

Moreover,

\|(x,y,z,)\|_{\ell_{1}^{2}\oplus_{1}X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m-2}}=\|x\|_{1}+\|y\|_{X_{2}}+\|z\|_{1}\leq d\|x\|_{X_{1}}+d\|y\|_{1}+d\|z\|_{1}=d\|(x,y,z)\|_{X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m}}.

Since
\ell_{1}^{2}\oplus_{1}X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m-2}
and
X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m}
are clearly isometric,
we obtain

d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m},X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{m})\leq d<d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2}),

which shows that the conclusion of Theorem
1.7
does not hold in this case.
This naturally leads to the question whether the assumption can ever fail.

Open Question 4.2
.

Does the inequality

d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2})\leq\max\left\{d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{1}^{2}),d_{BM}(X_{2},\ell_{1}^{2})\right\}

(23)

hold for all
2
-dimensional real normed spaces
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
?

Clearly, a positive answer to this question would allow us to drop the additional assumption in Theorem
1.7
and would lead to an isometric embedding of
\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}
into
\mathcal{BM}^{n}_{s}
for all
n\geq 3
. We discuss the plausibility and some further consequences of a positive answer in the following remark.

Remark 4.3
.

While the inequality (
23
) might seem implausible at first, we are not aware of any counterexamples.
Instead, we can provide some circumstantial evidence that might speak to its validity.

First, there are several instances in the real planar setting in which
\ell_{1}^{2}
turns out to be a maximizer of the Banach–Mazur distance.
These include the diameter of the compactum, i.e., the maximal distance between arbitrary spaces (determined by Stromquist
[
21
]
),
the maximal distance to the Euclidean space
[
3
]
,
and the maximal distance between
1
-symmetric spaces
[
8
]
.
In each of these examples,
\ell_{1}^{2}
is part of every distance-maximizing pair.
A positive answer to Question
4.2
would provide at least a partial explanation of this phenomenon,
as the maximization of the distance over any class of spaces containing
\ell_{1}^{2}
would reduce to finding the space(s) farthest from
\ell_{1}^{2}
.
This would often be a significantly easier task.
For example, Stromquist established that the diameter of
\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}
equals
\frac{3}{2}
,
and that the unique pair realizing it consists of
\ell_{1}^{2}
and the space whose unit ball is the regular hexagon.
However, the proof of Stromquist is quite long and complicated.
In comparison, it was determined
20
years earlier by Asplund
[
2
]
(with a considerably simpler approach)
that the maximal distance to
\ell_{1}^{2}
equals
\frac{3}{2}
.

Second, it is not difficult to prove that the triangle inequality

d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2})\leq d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{1}^{2})\cdot d_{BM}(X_{2},\ell_{1}^{2})

is always strict if
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are non-isometric to
\ell_{1}^{2}
.
This means that a counterexample to (
23
) cannot be found by constructing a geodesic in
\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}
containing the space
\ell_{1}^{2}
in its interior.
Speaking informally,
\ell_{1}^{2}
appears to be in a “corner” of the compactum
\mathcal{BM}^{2}_{s}
.
If this “corner” is narrow enough, the inequality (
23
) becomes plausible.
However, a positive answer to Open Question
4.2
could quite possibly require a significant amount of effort.

We conclude the paper with the proofs of Corollaries
1.8
and
1.9
.

Proof of Corollary
1.8
.

We need to prove that if
X_{1},X_{2}
are
2
-dimensional normed spaces such that
d_{BM}(X_{i},X)\leq d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}
for
i=1,2
, then

d_{BM}(X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{n-2},X_{2}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{n-2})=d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2})=:d.

This follows from Theorem
1.7
,
provided that
d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{1}^{2})\geq d
.
By the assumption and the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle d_{BM}(X_{1},\ell_{1}^{2})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{2})}{d_{BM}(X,X_{1})}\geq\frac{d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{2})}{d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}}=d_{BM}(X,\ell_{1}^{2})^{\frac{2}{3}}

\displaystyle\geq d_{BM}(X_{1},X)\cdot d_{BM}(X,X_{2})\geq d_{BM}(X_{1},X_{2}),

as required.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.9
.

By
[
16
, Theorem
1
]
,
for every sufficiently large integer
N
there exist
k:=\lceil C^{N}\rceil
(with
C>1
explicit)
2
-dimensional real normed spaces
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}

such that
d_{BM}(X_{i},X_{j})=d_{N}^{2}
for all
i\neq j
,
where
d_{N}=\frac{1}{\cos(\frac{\pi}{4N})}
.
Moreover, the unit balls of the spaces
X_{i}
constructed in the proof are combinations of the regular
4N
-gon and the Euclidean disc, which satisfy
d_{BM}(X_{i},\ell_{2}^{2})\leq d_{N}
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Therefore, choosing
X=\ell_{2}^{2}
in Corollary
1.8
gives for
n\geq 3
and sufficiently large
N
with
d_{N}\leq d_{BM}(\ell_{2}^{2},\ell_{1}^{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}=2^{\frac{1}{6}}
that

d_{BM}(X_{i}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{n-2},X_{i}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{n-2})=d_{N}^{2}

for all
i\neq j
.
We conclude that the spaces
X_{1}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{n-2},\ldots,X_{k}\oplus_{1}\ell_{1}^{n-2}

form an equilateral set in
\mathcal{BM}_{s}^{n}
.
Since
k
can be arbitrarily large, this concludes the proof.
∎

In closing the paper, let us point out another possible direction for future research.
The order of the size of the
d_{N}^{2}
-equilateral sets in
\mathcal{BM}_{s}^{2}
constructed in
[
16
]
is essentially optimal,
as this order matches that of the largest
d_{N}^{2}
-separated set in
\mathcal{BM}_{s}^{2}
.
The asymptotic growth rate of the maximal cardinality of
(1+\varepsilon)
-separated sets in
\mathcal{BM}_{s}^{n}
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}

was determined by Bronstein
[
6
]
for all
n\geq 1
and equals
\exp\left(\varepsilon^{\frac{1-n}{2}}\right)
(see also
[
16
, Remark
5
]
).
While it might be possible that for all
n\geq 2
the symmetric compactum
\mathcal{BM}^{n}_{s}
contains equilateral sets with size of the corresponding order,
lifting the planar construction to higher dimensions cannot achieve this.
The process preserves the cardinality order of
\exp(\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}})
.
Therefore, constructing equilateral sets in
\mathcal{BM}_{s}^{n}
of sizes matching that of largest separated sets
requires a different approach for
n\geq 3
.
