Magnetism Induced by Periodically Driven Non-Magnetic Impurities on Surfaces with Spin-Orbit Coupling
M. Etxeberria-Etxaniz, A. Arnau, A. Eiguren

Abstract
We investigate the response of the Rashba spin-orbit system to a time-periodic scalar potential, in order to determine whether an induced magnetization exists. We approach this by employing the Floquet-Green function method within the Keldysh formalism, computing the non-equilibrium steady state of the system. We find that, even in the absence of an external magnetic field, the system evolves into a state with an oscillating magnetization density that is remarkably rich in structure. We provide a detailed physical interpretation of the results by performing a Fourier decomposition in non-local momentum-space, which helps to uncover the physical origin of the induced magnetic field in terms of Fermi surface spin polarization and the system’s dynamical character.



Spin-orbit coupling (SOC) is the lowest-order term in the relativistic regime that lifts spin degeneracy while preserving time-reversal symmetry. Therefore, it is present even in non-magnetic materials, particularly those which contain heavy elements, where it appears as an intrinsic effect. Since the photoemission experiment by LaShell and co-workers
LaShell et al. 1996
revealed energy splitting in the Au(111) surface state due to SOC, it has been recognized as a source of a wide variety of phenomena in condensed-matter physics. Notably, a new field has emerged based on the realization that SOC can lead to topological insulating electronic phases
Hasan and Kane 2010
. Surfaces are even more important in this context because it is precisely the lack of inversion symmetry that is responsible for lifting Kramers degeneracy
Bychkov and Rashba 1984
. Moreover, surfaces are directly accessible to experimental techniques such as angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES), scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), and other surface-sensitive methods
Damascelli et al. 2003
;
Binnig et al. 1982
. In this context, SOC offers the possibility of manipulating the spin without the use of any external magnetic fields and, hence, the Rashba effect is an excellent candidate for spintronic applications
Datta and Das 1990
;
Marchenko et al. 2012
;
Noël et al. 2020
. Even in light elements, like carbon, the Rashba effect could give rise to spin polarized currents in chiral nanotubes
Chico et al. 2004
. Additionally, the Rashba coupling can be enhanced by the application of an external electric field
Santos et al. 2016
.

On surfaces, impurities lead to an even more pronounced symmetry reduction by breaking the remaining translational invariance. In the non-relativistic limit, and for a free-electron-like system with a spherical Fermi surface, static impurity perturbations give rise to charge density oscillations with a wavelength corresponding to a momentum transfer equal to twice the Fermi momentum. These are known as Friedel oscillations
Friedel 1958
. The presence of spin polarization on the Fermi surface makes the problem richer and more complex, as the scattering matrices become spin-dependent. For instance, back-scattering is forbidden in the presence of spin-orbit coupling, whereas in the non-relativistic case, it is the dominant mechanism. Nevertheless, STM experiments measuring quasiparticle interference patterns reveal this physics through the role of ‘static’ impurities
Stróżecka et al. 2011
on a metal surface. It has also been shown that magnetic impurities can induce Friedel oscillations with skyrmion-like spin textures
Lounis et al. 2012
;
Bouaziz et al. 2018
.

In this letter, we investigate the dynamics of impurities on the simplest relativistic surfaces, described by the Rashba model, aiming at determining whether a non-magnetic, localized and time-periodic perturbation can induce a magnetic response in a non-magnetic surface with intrinsic SOC. It was recently demonstrated that phonons may induce an oscillating magnetization density even in non-magnetic materials, by breaking spatial translational invariance and unbalancing the spin population across the Brillouin zone
Gurtubay et al. 2020
. Similarly, our idea here is that breaking time-translational invariance should introduce magnetic oscillations in both time and space domains. As a proof of concept for this phenomenon, we choose a periodically oscillating, cylindrical-shaped perturbation to mimic a vibrating adatom or molecule on a surface. Despite the toy-model nature of the system, the resulting behavior is rich and complex, as described shortly.

We adopt the Floquet-Green function method (FGFM)
Tsuji et al. 2008
;
Aoki et al. 2014
, which provides a theoretical framework for studying periodically driven systems out of equilibrium using Green’s function formalism. As it is common in most non-equilibrium systems, the time dependence is inherently non-local. However, instead of using the time variables
t
and
t^{\prime}
, it is physically more natural to express the dynamics in terms of their relative
t_{\mathrm{rel}}\equiv t-t^{\prime}
and average
t_{\mathrm{avg}}\equiv(t+t^{\prime})/2

\begin{split}G_{n}(\omega)=\frac{1}{T}&\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\differential t_{\mathrm{avg}}\>\mathrm{e}^{in\omega_{0}t_{\mathrm{avg}}}\\
\times&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential t_{\mathrm{rel}}\>\mathrm{e}^{i\omega t_{\mathrm{rel}}}\,G(t_{\mathrm{rel}},t_{\mathrm{avg}}),\end{split}

(1)

where
\omega_{0}\equiv 2\pi/T
is the fundamental frequency.
The resulting function is referred to as the
Wigner representation
, omitting the spatial coordinates and spin indices for brevity.
The matrix form of the Green’s function is defined as

G_{mn}(\omega)\equiv G_{m-n}\left(\omega+\frac{m+n}{2}\omega_{0}\right).

(2)

This is the so-called
Floquet representation
of the Green’s function. One of the key aspects of the above expression is that the association of
\omega

\omega+(m+n)\omega_{0}/2
requires restricting the former to
\omega\in[-\omega_{0}/2,\;\omega_{0}/2)

Sambe 1973
;
Peralta Gavensky
et al. 2018

\omega_{0}
, analogous to the momentum-space Brillouin zone resulting from Bloch’s theorem.

Formally, the Dyson equation is given by

\displaystyle G_{mn}

\displaystyle(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=G^{0}_{mn}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)

(3)

\displaystyle+\int\differential\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}\sum_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}G^{0}_{mm^{\prime}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime\prime},\omega)V_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})G_{n^{\prime}n}(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega),

where, for the moment, we include the non-local spatial variables, but we will omit them where relevant for brevity.

G^{0}
denotes the unperturbed Green’s function and

Now, our objective is to study the time dependence of the charge and magnetic response of the system, which is accessible by going back to the time domain as

\displaystyle G^{<}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{mn}\int_{-\omega_{0}/2}^{\omega_{0}/2}\,\frac{\differential\omega}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i(\omega+m\omega_{0})t}\mathrm{e}^{i(\omega+n\omega_{0})t^{\prime}}G^{<}_{mn}(\omega).

(4)

In our case, the unperturbed system corresponds to the Rashba electron gas, and the Green’s function is given by

\bm{G^{0}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}G_{\mathrm{d}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)&-\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}}G_{\mathrm{od}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)\\[5.0pt]
\mathrm{e}^{i\theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}}G_{\mathrm{od}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)&G_{\mathrm{d}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)\end{pmatrix},

(5)

where we have introduced the Pauli formalism for the electron spin, in which Green’s functions become
2\times 2
spin-density matrices, denoted by bold symbols
\bm{G}
.
If the quantization axis is perpendicular to the surface plane, the off-diagonal elements represent the in-plane components of the spin polarization, while the diagonal elements represent the charge density, as no net magnetism is present in the system. In our notation,
\theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}
indicates the angle between
\bm{r}
and
\bm{r}^{\prime}
points in space, and
G_{\mathrm{d}}^{0}
and
G_{\mathrm{od}}^{0}
are given by combinations of Hankel
H_{0}^{(1)}
and
H_{1}^{(1)}
functions, respectively (see Secs. II and III of Supplemental Material
sup

t_{\mathrm{avg}}
. Therefore, the Wigner representation has a single non-zero frequency component,
\bm{G}_{\bm{n}=\bm{0}}^{\bm{0}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\bm{G^{0}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)
. The next step requires writing the full Floquet representation of
\bm{G^{0}}
using Equation

The following ingredient for the Dyson equation is the perturbation, which we choose to be a simple cylindrical potential centered at the origin and localized within a small radius
r_{V}
.
As already mentioned, our objective is primarily to study the possible magnetic response to a non-magnetic perturbation and, therefore, the impurity is a diagonal matrix in the
2\times 2
spin representation. We also assume that the time dependence is sinusoidal with frequency
\omega_{0}
. Altogether, the spin-diagonal component of the potential is modeled as

V(\bm{r},t)=v(\bm{r})\cos(\omega_0 t)
,
where
v(\bm{r})
is a localized potential; we consider a cylindrical shape
v(\bm{r})=v_{0}\,\Theta(r_{V}-r)
, with
\Theta
the Heaviside function, without loss of generality. Its Floquet representation is

V_{mn}(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\!\differential t\,\mathrm{e}^{im\omega_{0}t}V(\bm{r},t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-in\omega_{0}t}.

The physical intuition driving our investigation is that an oscillating scalar potential as described above is, up to a gauge transformation, equivalently represented by the pure-gauge vector potential
\mathbf{A}(\bm{r},t)=\nabla v(\bm{r})\sin(\omega_0 t)/\omega_{0}
, so we have
\mathbf{E}=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{A}=-\nabla v(\bm{r})\cos(\omega_0 t)
and zero magnetic field
\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}=0
by construction. To see how this impurity field couples to the Rashba mode, considering minimal coupling
\bm{p}\!\to\!\bm{p}-e\mathbf{A}
, it is easy to see that the Rashba term
H_{\mathrm{R}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\sigma}\times\bm{p})_{z}
acquires, to linear order in
\mathbf{A}
, the azimuthal, purely spin-driving coupling,

\displaystyle\delta H_{\mathrm{R}}(t)=e(r,t)\,\bm{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\bm{\phi}}=ie(r,t)\big(e^{i\theta}s_{-}-e^{-i\theta}s_{+}\big),

(6)

where
\hat{\bm{\phi}}=-\hat{\bm{\imath}}\sin(\theta)+\hat{\bm{\jmath}}\cos(\theta)
is the azimuthal unit vector,

e(r,t)=\alpha v_{0}/\omega_{0}\,\cos(\omega_0 t)\,\delta(r{-}r_{V})

is the modulus of the time-oscillating electric field localized in a ring-shaped region at radius
r_{V}
, and

s_{\pm}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{x}\!\pm i\sigma_{y})
.
Crucially, because the drive is purely azimuthal, it acts as an effective in-plane tangential field localized on the ring
r=r_{V}
. Its localized character, in turn, breaks translational symmetry and generates long-range, Friedel-like spin oscillations with polarization along
\hat{\bm{\phi}}
.

At this point, we are ready to study the coupling of the Rashba electron gas and our model time-dependent impurity by means of the Dyson equation in Eq.
3
. This is a sparse linear system and is solved directly using the
PARDISO
package
Schenk and Gärtner 2004
, considering a combined computational index comprising real-space, spin, and frequency variables to construct the matrices that represent the system. With this technique, we eventually obtain the lesser Green’s function, from which we can compute the charge and spin density variations in space and time.
In particular, we are interested in the charge and magnetization densities which are accessible from the
\bm{G^{<}}
matrix as

\begin{split}n(\bm{r},t)&=-\frac{i}{2}\lim_{(\bm{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rightarrow(\bm{r},t)}\Tr\sigma_{0}\bm{G^{<}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t,t^{\prime}),\\
m_{x,y,z}(\bm{r},t)&=-\frac{i}{2}\lim_{(\bm{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rightarrow(\bm{r},t)}\Tr\sigma_{x,y,z}\bm{G^{<}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t,t^{\prime}),\end{split}

(7)

where the traces are calculated in spin space and
\sigma_{0,x,y,z}
are the Pauli matrices.

Quite obviously, this deviates from the standard picture because it would be impossible if the impurity were static, as the spins are opposite and the overlap would be negligible. In other words, this figure shows that the dynamical nature of the problem enables scattering processes that are forbidden in the static case. These results also provide a consistent numerical check: the dominant contributions lie close to the Fermi contours, which is not imposed explicitly but rather handled by the Keldysh formalism. It is worth mentioning that our results are related to the Edelstein effect
Edelstein 1990
and spin pumping phenomena, which describe spin polarization and spin current generation under electric fields or dynamic magnetization. However, in contrast to these traditional setups, here we demonstrate that a purely time-periodic, scalar potential in a Rashba system can induce a magnetic response even in the absence of magnetic materials or spin injection.

Regarding the possible experimental detection of this phenomenon, similar to ESR-STM techniques that probe spin dynamics with microwave excitation
Choi et al. 2017
, the dynamical magnetization could be measured using spin-polarized STM
Wiesendanger 2009
;
Friedlein et al. 2019
operated under continuous wave microwave frequency modulation in the THz regime. The microwave frequency range should include the vibrational frequency of the impurity adatom or molecule. The electric field at the tunnel junction is enhanced by the presence of the STM tip and provokes the vibrational excitation
Chen et al. 2023
. In such a configuration, the spin-polarized tunneling current depends on the local magnetization, yielding higher currents for parallel alignment between the tip and the sample magnetization; thus, even small variations in the induced magnetization could be resolved with magnetized tips oriented parallel to the surface, using a lock-in technique that detects the current changes.

In conclusion, we have shown that a periodically driven, scalar potential represents a new mechanism for dynamically generating spin polarization in systems with sizable spin-orbit coupling. The dynamic nature of the perturbing potential is essential and it affects both the induced charge and magnetization densities, that are determined by scattering processes absent in the static case. As a proof of concept and comprehensible system to model a vibrating adatom or molecule on a surface, we have considered the simplest Rashba surface perturbed by a localized, cylindrical-shaped and oscillating scalar potential, that has permitted us to rationalize our results. We find that both inter and intraband scattering processes involving spin-flip transitions are determinant to produce the induced magnetization pattern. This mechanism provides an alternative route to spin control in non-magnetic systems, extending the scope of non-equilibrium spintronics.

I. Non-equilibrium treatment through the Keldysh formalism

In this work, we study non-equilibrium steady states (NESS), which occur when the energy injected by the external drive is balanced out by the energy dissipating into the environment
Ikeda and Sato 2020
. Therefore, our system needs to be coupled to the environment. For that purpose, we adopt the Keldysh formalism, and thus we write the non-equilibrium Green’s function as the following
2\times 2
matrix:

\underline{G}=\begin{pmatrix}G^{\mathrm{R}}&G^{\mathrm{K}}\\[4.0pt]
0&G^{\mathrm{A}}\end{pmatrix},

(S1)

where
G^{\mathrm{R}}
,
G^{\mathrm{A}}
and
G^{\mathrm{K}}
are the retarded, advanced and Keldysh Green’s functions. Within this framework, the coupling between the system and the environment can be represented with a self-energy correction corresponding to a free-fermion bath modelled as

\underline{\mathstrut\Sigma_{\mathrm{bath}}}(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}-i\Gamma&-2i\Gamma F(\omega)\\[4.0pt]
0&i\Gamma\end{pmatrix}.

(S2)

Here
\Gamma
indicates the spectral function of the bath, which can be assumed to be independent of
\omega
as a simple treatment of the dissipation
Aoki et al. 2014
. The Keldysh component results from the fluctuation-dissipation relation, and
F(\omega)
is a linear combination of the Fermi-Dirac distribution function, which is given by

F(\omega)=1-2f(\omega)=\tanh\left(\frac{\beta(\omega-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})}{2}\right)

(S3)

for fermions, and where
\beta
is the inverse temperature with which the bath is in equilibrium and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}
the Fermi energy of the system. Now, so as to combine this with the Floquet formalism, the previous distribution function is rewritten as

F_{mn}(\omega)=\tanh\left(\frac{\beta(\omega+m\omega_{0}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}})}{2}\right)\delta_{mn}.

(S4)

With this, the non-equilibrium Green’s function can be calculated through the following Dyson equation:

\underline{G}^{-1}=\underline{\mathstrut G_{\mathrm{eq}}}^{-1}-\underline{\mathstrut\Sigma_{\mathrm{bath}}},

(S5)

which we write in this particular form for convenience. In our calculation process, the retarded component of the equilibrium Green’s function is the direct solution of the Dyson equation (Eq.
3
in main text), and the advanced one is its conjugate transpose,

G_{\mathrm{eq},mn}^{\mathrm{A}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=G_{\mathrm{eq},nm}^{\mathrm{R}*}(\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r},\omega).

(S6)

Regarding the Keldysh component, it can be assumed that its inverse vanishes as the equilibrium system is dissipationless
Aoki et al. 2014
, and hence Equation
S5
becomes

\begin{pmatrix}G^{\mathrm{R}}&G^{\mathrm{K}}\\[4.0pt]
0&G^{\mathrm{A}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(G_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mathrm{R}}\right)^{-1}+i\Gamma&2i\Gamma F\\[4.0pt]
0&\left(G_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mathrm{A}}\right)^{-1}-i\Gamma&\end{pmatrix}^{-1},

(S7)

where we omit the
\omega
dependency for brevity. Note that, this way, the non-equilibrium Green’s function can be calculated through the generalized formula of inversion of upper triangular block matrices
Meyer 1970
, which does not require us to invert the whole right-hand side matrix, only one of its diagonals (retarded or advanced component). Likewise, it is possible to compute this inverse through the so-called Woodbury matrix identity
Henderson and Searle 1981
, in such a way that direct inversion is avoided. Once the retarded, advanced and Keldysh components are calculated, we obtain the lesser Green’s function through the
G^{<}\equiv(G^{\mathrm{K}}-G^{\mathrm{R}}+G^{\mathrm{A}})/2
definition.

Finally, note that the equations above use scalar Green’s functions. If we take into account the fact that our Green’s functions are
2\times 2
spinors, the advanced component is calculated as

\bm{G}_{\mathbf{eq},\bm{mn}}^{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\left[\bm{G}_{\mathbf{eq},\bm{nm}}^{\mathbf{R}}(\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r},\omega)\right]^{\dagger},

(S8)

and the rest of the calculation process is carried out accordingly.

II
II. Rashba Hamiltonian and calculation parameters

The Rashba Hamiltonian is

H=H_{0}+H_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{\bm{p}^{2}_{\parallel}}{2m^{*}}+\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\sigma}\times\bm{p}_{\parallel}),

(S9)

where we use atomic units,
m^{*}
is the effective mass,
\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}
is the Rashba parameter and
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y})
are Pauli matrices indicating spin orientation. The resulting eigenvalues are given by

\varepsilon_{\bm{k}\sigma}=\frac{\bm{k}^{2}}{2m^{*}}+\sigma\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}\norm{\bm{k}},

(S10)

where
\sigma=\pm 1
indicates the spin state, and a schematic representation of the corresponding spin up (
\sigma=+1
) and down (
\sigma=-1
) bands is displayed in Figure
S1
.

m^{*}=1\;\mathrm{a.u.}
,

,

and
\omega_{0}=0.004\;\mathrm{a.u.}
.

.

Rashba bands at the Fermi level, given by two concentric circles of radii
k_{\mathrm{F}+}
and
k_{\mathrm{F}-}
. The arrows depict the spin orientation, showcasing the spin-momentum locking of the Hamiltonian.

We consider the following parameters for our main calculation:
m^{*}=1\;\mathrm{a.u.}
,
\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}=0.04\;\mathrm{a.u.}
,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{F}}=0.03\;\mathrm{a.u.}
and
\omega_{0}=0.004\;\mathrm{a.u.}
(corresponding to a linear frequency of
\nu_{0}\sim 25\;\mathrm{THz}
); the representations in Figure
S1
are calculated according to these values. Here we should note that, even though the chosen parameters do not represent any system in particular, they have been selected to approximate typical ranges found for the magnitudes under consideration
Bihlmayer et al. 2015
. Regarding the cylindrical potential, previous works showed that this kind of model potentials achieve good agreement with experiments for
v_{0}=0.1\;\mathrm{a.u.}

Harbury and Porod 1996
;

, and our tests showed that a small radius of
r_{V}=2.5\;\mathrm{a.u.}
was sufficient for a proper visualization of the effect; besides,
\varphi=0
is considered for the cosine function of the time-dependence. Regarding the non-equilibrium aspect, we opt for a weak system-bath coupling of
\Gamma=10^{-5}\;\mathrm{a.u.}
and a low temperature limit of
\beta\rightarrow\infty
.

III
III. Calculation of the unperturbed Green’s function

We obtain the two-dimensional, one-particle, unperturbed Green’s function through the following definition:

\mathbf{G^{0}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{\sigma}\int\differential\bm{k}\;\frac{\psi_{\bm{k}\sigma}(\bm{r})\,\psi^{*}_{\bm{k}\sigma}(\bm{r}^{\prime})}{\omega-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}\sigma}+i\eta},

(S11)

where
\psi_{\bm{k}\sigma}
is the one-particle wave function,
\varepsilon_{\bm{k}\sigma}
the corresponding energy,
\sigma
represents the spin state and
\eta
is an infinitesimal number; the wave functions are

\psi_{\bm{k}\sigma}(\bm{r})=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\[4.0pt]
-i\sigma\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}\end{pmatrix},

(S12)

where
\theta
describes the orientation of the spin in the 2D plane. With this, we have

\bm{G^{0}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{\sigma}\int\,\differential\bm{k}\>\frac{\mathrm{e}^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})}}{2}\>\frac{\begin{pmatrix}1&i\sigma\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta}\\[4.0pt]
-i\sigma\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}&1\end{pmatrix}}{\omega-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}\sigma}+i\eta}.

(S13)

Now we decompose the
\differential\bm{k}
differential as
\differential\bm{k}=k\,\differential k\,\differential\theta
, and we get

\displaystyle\bm{G^{0}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\sum_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential k\,\frac{k}{\omega-\varepsilon_{k\sigma}+i\eta}\>\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta\>\mathrm{e}^{ik\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\rVert\cos(\theta- \theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}'})}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\sigma\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta}\\[4.0pt]
-i\sigma\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}&1\end{pmatrix},

(S14)

where
\theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}
represents the angle of the
\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}
vector, that is,

\mathrm{e}^{\pm i\theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}}=\cos\theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}\pm i\sin\theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}=\frac{(r_{x}-r_{x}^{\prime})\pm i(r_{y}-r_{y}^{\prime})}{\norm{\bm{r}-\bm{r}'}}.

(S15)

In Equation
S14
we used the fact that the
\varepsilon_{\bm{k}\sigma}
eigenvalues of the Rashba Hamiltonian depend on
\bm{k}
only through its modulus, as we can observe in Equation
S10
.

The angular part of the integrals is related to Bessel functions as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta\>\mathrm{e}^{ik\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\rVert\cos(\theta- \theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}'})}=2\pi J_{0}(k\norm{\bm{r}-\bm{r}'}),

(S16)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta\>\mathrm{e}^{ik\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\rVert\cos(\theta- \theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}'})}\,\mathrm{e}^{\pm i\theta}=2\pi i\mathrm{e}^{\pm i\theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}}J_{1}(k\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\rVert);

(S17)

and, therefore, we have

\displaystyle G_{00}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential k\>\frac{\,kJ_{0}(k\norm{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r'}})\,}{\omega-\varepsilon_{k\sigma}+i\eta},

(S18)

\displaystyle G_{01}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta_{r}}}{4\pi}\sum_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential k\>\frac{\,\sigma kJ_{1}(k\norm{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'})\,}{\omega-\varepsilon_{k\sigma}+i\eta},

(S19)

\displaystyle G_{10}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{i\theta_{r}}}{4\pi}\sum_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential k\>\frac{\,\sigma kJ_{1}(k\norm{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'})}{\omega-\varepsilon_{k\sigma}+i\eta},

(S20)

for the diagonal (note that
G_{11}^{0}
=
G_{00}^{0}
) and off-diagonal components of the unperturbed Green’s function. Regarding the radial part, we first define
G_{\mathrm{d}}^{0}\equiv G_{00}^{0}
and

G^{0}_{\mathrm{od}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)\equiv\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential k\>\frac{\,\sigma kJ_{1}(k\norm{\bm{r}-\bm{r}'})}{\omega-\varepsilon_{k\sigma}+i\eta}.

(S21)

In order to compute these, we rewrite the denominator by using the so-called Sokhotski-Plemelj formula
Economou 2006
,

\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\,\frac{1}{\omega-\varepsilon_{k\sigma}\pm i\eta}=\mathcal{P.V.}\Big(\frac{1}{\omega-\varepsilon_{k\sigma}}\Big)\mp i\pi\delta\left(\omega-\varepsilon_{k\sigma}\right),

(S22)

where
\mathcal{P.V.}
denotes the Cauchy principal value and
\delta
the Dirac delta, and then we take its imaginary part, that is,

\displaystyle\imaginary G_{\mathrm{d}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential k\>kJ_{0}(k\norm{\bm{r}-\bm{r}'})\,\delta(\omega-\varepsilon_{k\sigma}),

(S23)

\displaystyle\imaginary G_{\mathrm{od}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential k\>\sigma kJ_{1}(k\norm{\bm{r}-\bm{r}'})\,\delta(\omega-\varepsilon_{k\sigma}).

(S24)

Due to the Dirac delta, the result of the integrals above is a Bessel function (
J_{0}
for the diagonal component,
J_{1}
for the off-diagonal) multiplied by a set of parameters. With this, we can obtain the real part through the Hilbert transform, which describes the relationship between the real and the imaginary part of an analytic function. In the case of the Bessel function of the first kind, one obtains

\mathcal{H}J_{n}(x)\approx-Y_{n}(x)

Poularikas 2018
, where
n=0,1
and
Y
is the Bessel function of the second kind. At this point, it is useful to recall that Hankel functions of the first kind are given by
H_{n}^{(1)}=J_{n}+iY_{n}
. In this manner, we can write the radial integrals as

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{d}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=-\frac{im^{*}}{4}\sum_{\sigma}A_{\sigma}H_{0}^{(1)}\left(a_{\sigma}m^{*}\norm{\bm{r}-\bm{r}'}\right),

(S25)

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{od}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=-\frac{im^{*}}{4}\sum_{\sigma}\sigma A_{\sigma}H_{1}^{(1)}\left(a_{\sigma}m^{*}\norm{\bm{r}-\bm{r}'}\right),

(S26)

where the parameters
A_{\sigma}
and
a_{\sigma}
depend on
\omega
and the Rashba parameter
\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}
, as
A_{\sigma}\equiv 1+\sigma\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}/\sqrt{\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}+2\omega}
and
a_{\sigma}\equiv\sqrt{\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}+2\omega}+\sigma\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}
. Hence, the final unperturbed Green’s function is given by

\bm{G^{0}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}G_{\mathrm{d}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)&-\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}}G_{\mathrm{od}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)\\[4.0pt]
\mathrm{e}^{i\theta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}}G_{\mathrm{od}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)&G_{\mathrm{d}}^{0}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)\end{pmatrix}.

(S27)

IV
IV. Calculation of mean square deviation

We define the time average of the mean square deviation of the (normalized) magnetization over one period as

\langle\!\langle\tilde{m}_{x,y}\rangle\!\rangle_{T}\,(\bm{r})=\sqrt{\langle\tilde{m}_{x,y}^{2}(\bm{r},t)\rangle_{T}-\langle\tilde{m}_{x,y}(\bm{r},t)\rangle_{T}^{2}},

(S28)

where angle brackets indicate time averages over one period
T
and where, in our case,
\langle\tilde{m}_{x,y}(\bm{r},t)\rangle_{T}
is zero for all
\bm{r}
coordinates.
