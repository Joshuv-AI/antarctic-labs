Engineering of Yarrowia lipolytica for De Novo Biosynthesis of (2S)‑Hesperetin from Waste Cooking Oil
Authors: XiHui Wang, Tengfei Wang, Hongling Liu, Weiwei Wang, Yanpo Li, Cui Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
(2S)-hesperetin is a plant-derived natural product widely used in pharmaceutical, food, and cosmetic fields owing to its diverse biological activities. This study systematically engineered Yarrowia lipolytica through metabolic modifications to construct a microbial cell factory capable of producing (2S)-hesperetin from waste cooking oil. First, de novo synthesis of (2S)-hesperetin was achieved by heterologous expression of genes related to the (2S)-hesperetin biosynthetic pathway in Y. lipolytica. Subsequently, precursor supply was enhanced by regulating the copy number of core pathway genes and optimizing the synthesis pathways for aromatic amino acids and malonyl-CoA. Furthermore, the engineered Y. lipolytica exhibited enhanced utilization of lipids and an increased pool of precursor acetyl coenzyme A by regulating the fatty acid β-oxidation pathway. Ultimately, we achieved a (2S)-hesperetin yield of 488.7 mg/L in a 5 L bioreactor through optimized fermentation processes. This study provides a viable strategy for the green biosynthesis of flavonoid compounds and offers a sustainable solution for the high-value utilization of waste cooking oil.

Introduction

(2S)-Hesperetin is a natural dihydroflavone compound and an aglycone derivative of hesperidin, which is widely found in lemons, grapefruits, oranges and other citrus fruits of the Rutaceae family. (2S)-hesperetin exhibits remarkable biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hypolipidemic, and protective effects on cardiovascular and neural cells, making it widely used in pharmaceuticals, food, and cosmetics. Currently, (2S)-hesperetin is primarily produced through plant extraction and chemical synthesis. However, plant-based extraction methods utilize raw materials such as citrus peel, but the content of (2S)-hesperetin in these materials is extremely low (typically less than 0.5% of dry weight), with extraction yields of only 30–50%, and the process is subject to seasonal and geographical constraints. Chemical synthesis typically involves the acid hydrolysis of hesperidin to produce (2S)-hesperetin, or the construction of the flavanone skeleton via complex organic synthetic routes, and relies on toxic reagents and strict polar reaction conditions. − Therefore, developing an efficient, green, and sustainable method for producing (2S)-hesperetin is of great significance.

In recent years, the de novo synthesis of natural products using microbial cell factories has emerged as an effective strategy for overcoming bottlenecks in traditional production methods. In plants, the biosynthetic pathway of (2S)-hesperetin has been largely elucidated: The biosynthesis of (2S)-hesperetin begins with l-phenylalanine or l-tyrosine, which are converted into p-coumaric acid under the catalysis of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and cinnamic acid 4-hydroxylase (C4H) or tyrosine ammonia-lyase (TAL). − Then, p-coumaric acid undergoes a series of enzymatic reactions to be further converted into (2S)-naringenin. These reactions involve 4-hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA ligase (4CL), chalcone synthase (CHS), and chalcone isomerase (CHI). − Subsequently, (2S)-naringenin is hydroxylated by flavonoid 3′-hydroxylase (F3′H) to form (2S)-eriodictyol, which is then methylated by flavonoid 4′-O-methyltransferase (F4′OMT) to form (2S)-hesperetin. − Currently, some studies have achieved the synthesis of (2S)-hesperetin using microbial cell factories, but numerous technical challenges remain, such as the low substrate specificity and catalytic efficiency of the methyltransferase F4′OMT, as well as insufficient metabolic flux in both the shikimate pathway and the malonyl-CoA biosynthesis pathway. Therefore, to achieve efficient microbial synthesis of (2S)-hesperetin, it is essential to systematically engineer the precursor supply pathways, optimize the expression levels of key enzymes, mitigate interference from competing pathways, and balance the relationship between cell growth and product synthesis.

Waste cooking oil (WCO) refers to frying oil used at high temperatures, edible oils mixed in with food waste, and oily wastewater directly discharged into sewers. The global annual production of WCO exceeds 29 million metric tons. Notably, China is the largest producer of WCO worldwide, generating 4.5 million metric tons annually. If not properly treated, direct discharges of WCO can contaminate soil and water, clog sewer pipes, and place a heavy burden on wastewater treatment systems. Therefore, the disposal and reuse of WCO is a long-term issue that requires careful attention. − In recent years, researchers have begun exploring the use of WCO as a low-cost carbon source in microbial fermentation to produce high-value-added products such as biodiesel, citric acid, and erythritol. This “turning waste into treasure” strategy not only addresses waste disposal issues but also significantly reduces the cost of fermentation feedstocks, yielding substantial economic and environmental benefits. −

Selecting an adequate microbial chassis is crucial for the efficient synthesis of (2S)-hesperetin using WCO. Yarrowia lipolytica (Y. lipolytica) is a GRAS-grade strain that can be utilized for the production of food-grade products. − Compared to traditional microbial platforms, Y. lipolytica possesses unique metabolic advantages. The Y. lipolytica genome encodes a variety of lipases, fatty acid transporters, and degradation enzyme systems, enabling the efficient conversion of hydrophobic carbon sources such as oils and fatty acids into acetyl-CoA, thereby providing sufficient precursors for the biosynthesis of flavonoids. − Second, the high cytoplasmic acetyl-CoA flux in Y. lipolytica facilitates the supply of malonyl-CoA, thereby driving the efficient synthesis of the flavanone skeleton. Consequently, Y. lipolytica exhibits unique advantages in the biosynthesis of flavonoids. − It is worth noting that this type of microorganism has been developed to utilize WCO as a carbon source for growth and a substrate for biological transformation. − Therefore, the engineered Y. lipolytica for the biosynthesis of (2S)-hesperetin from WCO represents a sustainable and economical pathway for producing valuable and versatile chemicals.

This study aims to construct an efficient microbial cell factory capable of de novo synthesis of (2S)-hesperetin from WOC through systematic metabolic engineering of Y. lipolytica (Figure ). First, the de novo synthesis of (2S)-hesperetin was achieved in Y. lipolytica by introducing heterologous genes related to the (2S)-hesperetin biosynthetic pathway. Subsequently, precursor supply was enhanced by regulating the copy number of core pathway genes and optimizing the synthesis pathways for aromatic amino acids and malonyl-CoA. Furthermore, the fatty acid β-oxidation pathway was engineered to enhance lipid utilization and acetyl-CoA supply in the engineered strain. Finally, the potential of using engineered Y. lipolytica to produce (2S)-hesperetin from WCO was investigated. This work provides new strategies and references for developing Y. lipolytica as a microbial chassis to sustainably and economically produce valuable chemicals from waste cooking oil and fat feedstocks.

Materials and Methods

Strains and Culture Conditions

Table S1 lists all strains used in this study. E. coli DH5α was used for construction of recombinant plasmids and cultured in LB medium (0.5% yeast extract, 1% peptone, and 1% NaCl) supplemented with ampicillin (100 mg/L). Y. lipolytica Po1g-Δku70 was used as the starting strain for constructing engineered strains and was screened on solid SC medium (2% glucose, 0.67% YNB, and 2% agar powder, supplemented with leucine or uracil as necessary). Conventional cultivation of Y. lipolytica is performed in YPD medium (containing 1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, and 2% glucose) at 30 °C and 200 rpm. YP medium (containing 1% yeast extract, and 2% peptone). For shake flask fermentation, YP medium was used as the base medium, with various carbon sourcesincluding glucose, peanut oil, and WCO added if necessary. When using oils as carbon sources, 0.2% Tween 80 was added to the YP medium as an emulsifier. For 5 L bioreactor fermentation, YPO medium (containing 1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 6% WCO, and 0.2% Tween-80) was used to produce the target product. All medium used in this study was autoclaved and inoculated in a clean environment. The peanut oil/WCO is autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min. Then, the sterilized oil substrate is added to the sterilized YP medium.

DNA Manipulation and Strain Construction

The heterologous pathway-related genes TAL­(KR095306), 4CL­(KF765780), CHS­(KF765781), CHI­(KF765782), F3′H (AB480691.1; CPR, from Arabidopsis thaliana), and F4′OMT (from Citrus reticulata “Chachiensis”) were all codon-optimized and synthesized by GENEWIZ Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). The plasmid pYLXP’ is used for the expression of both single and multiple genes. For example, to construct the plasmid pYLXP’–TAL, pYLXP’ is first digested with XbaI and KpnI to obtain linearized pYLXP’. Subsequently, this linearized plasmid is ligated with the TAL gene fragment obtained via PCR amplification, generating the plasmid pYLXP’–TAL using the Gibson assembly method. Sequencing of the resulting plasmid was performed by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China). Gene integration into the Y. lipolytica genome was achieved using the Cre-loxP system.

Shake Flask Fermentation

A single colony of engineered Y. lipolytica was inoculated into a 30 mL shake flask containing 10 mL YPD liquid medium. The culture was incubated at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 12 h to prepare the seed culture. Subsequently, the seed culture was inoculated into a 250 mL shake flask containing 50 mL liquid medium to achieve an initial OD600 of 0.1. After incubation at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 72 h, cell dry weight and product concentration were measured. All shaking flask fermentation experiments included three parallel controls.

Fermentation in a 5 L Bioreactor

A single colony of engineered Y. lipolytica was inoculated into 10 mL of YPD liquid medium and cultured at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 12 h to obtain the primary seed culture. The primary seed culture was then inoculated into 100 mL of YPD liquid medium and cultured at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 12 h to obtain the secondary seed culture. Subsequently, the secondary seed culture was inoculated into a 5 L bioreactor (BLBIO-5GL, Bailun Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) to achieve an initial OD600 of 0.6, with a working volume of 3 L (containing 2% peptone, 1% yeast extract, 6% WCO, and 0.2% Tween-80). During the fermentation process, the dissolved oxygen level was maintained at 30%, the agitation speed was adjusted in accordance with cell growth (400–600 rpm), and the pH was maintained at 6.5 through the automatic addition of 4 mM NaOH. The fermentation process employed batch fermentation, with a cultivation time of 144 h. Furthermore, no additional carbon or nitrogen sources were added during fermentation.

Analytical Methods

The OD600 value was measured using a UV/vis spectrophotometer (Jinhua 752, Shanghai, China) to evaluate cell growth.

For cell dry weight, collect the cell pellets by centrifuging 10 mL of fermentation broth at 8000g for 10 min. Then wash the pellets 3× with sterile water and dry them at 60 °C until constant weight is achieved.

For quantitative detection of (2S)-hesperetin and other flavonoids, mix 1 mL of fermentation broth with 1 mL of methanol, vortexed for 10 min, and centrifuged at 8000g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected and subjected to high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis using an LC-20AT HPLC system (Shimadzu, Tianjin, China) equipped with a variable wavelength detector and a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 column (Agilent, Beijing, China). Detection employed gradient elution using solvent A (high-purity water containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid) and solvent B (methanol containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min and temperature of 40 °C. Detection wavelength was 350 nm with an injection volume of 20 μL. The gradient elution profile was as follows: 0–10 min, 10–40% solvent B; 10–20 min, 40–80% solvent B; 20–25 min, 80% solvent B; 25–30 min, 80–10% solvent B. The retention times for compounds (2S)-eriodictyol, (2S)-naringenin, (2S)-hesperetin, and (2S)-isosakuranetin were 14.5, 17.4, 20.3, and 23.9 min, respectively. Quantification was based on calibration curves using corresponding standards.

Transcriptional levels of pathway-related genes were determined using real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR). RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and RT-qPCR were performed using Trizol reagent (BBI, Toronto, Canada), Hiscript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme) kits, respectively. RT-qPCR analysis was performed according to previously described methods. ACT1 served as the reference gene, and relative transcription levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Statistics

The assay values represent the average of three independent experiments, and the error bars represent standard errors. Statistical analysis was carried out by using Student’s t-test (one-tailed; two-sample unequal variance; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Results and Discussion

Heterologous Biosynthesis of (2S)-Hesperetin in Y. lipolytica

Y. lipolytica does not possess the (2S)-hesperetin biosynthesis pathway but naturally contains the shikimate pathway, which can provide the precursors l-tyrosine and l-phenylalanine for (2S)-hesperetin synthesis. Therefore, to achieve de novo synthesis of (2S)-hesperetin in Y. lipolytica, establishing an exogenous (2S)-hesperetin biosynthetic pathway is the first step. Since C4H is a P450 enzyme and the PAL pathway is considered the rate-limiting step in the synthesis of flavonoid compounds, this study synthesized (2S)-hesperetin using l-tyrosine as a precursor to bypass the limitation imposed by C4H. Codon-optimized genes TAL, 4CL, CHS, CHI, F3′H, and F4′OMT were integrated into the genome of Y. lipolytica Po1g ΔKu70 under the control of different promoters to obtain the engineered strain Y1. Shake fermentation results showed that after 96 h of fermentation in YPD medium, the engineered strain Y1 produced 32.9 mg/L of (2S)-hesperetin (Figure AB). The growth of the Y1 strain was not affected, with an OD600 of 23.5. However, the accumulation of significant amounts of the intermediates (2S)-naringenin and (2S)-eriodictyol in the fermentation products implies that further pathway optimization is required to improve the (2S)-hesperetin yield (Figure B).

Optimization of the Core Pathway to Promote (2S)-Hesperetin Biosynthesis

The low metabolic flux in the core pathway of (2S)-hesperetin synthesis is a key factor limiting its yield, with the relevant genes being CHS, CHI, F3′H, and F4′OMT (Figure A). To enhance the flux of this core pathway, this study investigated the potential for increasing (2S)-hesperetin production by elevating the copy numbers of the CHS, CHI, F3′H, and F4′OMT genes. Engineered strains with varying gene copy numbers were constructed based on strain Y1, optimized within a range of 1–4 gene copies. Shake flask fermentation results (as shown in Figure B) showed that the engineered strain Y6 achieved a (2S)-hesperetin yield of 84.8 mg/L, representing a significant 2.6-fold increase compared to the control strain Y1 (32.9 mg/L). When the copy numbers of the CHS, CHI, and F4′OMT genes were set to 2, (2S)-hesperetin production peaked at 58.1 mg/L. However, further increasing the copy numbers of these genes resulted in decreased (2S)-hesperetin yield, suggesting that excessive copies of pathway genes may cause metabolic congestion and thereby increase cellular metabolic burden. When the copy number of gene F3′H was 1, the engineered strain Y2 accumulated large amounts of (2S)-naringenin that was not converted into (2S)-eriodictyol (Figure B). Therefore, increasing the copy number of F3′H allows more (2S)-naringenin to be converted into (2S)-eriodictyol, thereby increasing the yield of (2S)-hesperetin. As expected, the production of (2S)-hesperetin reached 84.8 mg/L when the copy number of the F3′H gene was set to 3, representing a significant 45.9% increase compared to the control strain Y2 (F3′H copy number = 1).

(A) Schematic diagram of the core pathway for (2S)-hesperetin synthesis. (B) The effect of different copy numbers of core pathway-related genes on (2S)-hesperetin production. (C) Relative transcription levels of core pathway-related genes.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) results revealed that the transcription levels of the CHS, CHI, F3′H, and F4′OMT genes in strain Y6 were 2.1-fold, 2.4-fold, 2.3-fold, and 2.5-fold higher, respectively, than those in strain Y1 (Figure C). This demonstrated that the regulation of gene copy numbers effectively enhances metabolic flux of the core pathway.

Reconfiguring Upstream Synthesis Pathways to Enhance Precursor Supply

The key precursors for (2S)-hesperetin synthesis are p-coumaric acid and malonyl-CoA, which originate from the shikimate pathway and the acetyl-CoA carboxylation pathway, respectively. To enhance precursor supply, this study reconstructed and strengthened both the shikimate pathway and the malonyl-CoA synthesis pathway.

To redirect carbon flux toward coumaric acid biosynthesis, the upstream shikimate pathway was systematically optimized based on strain Y6. We first overexpressed and knocked out individual pathway genes to identify key rate-limiting steps. Fermentation results showed that overexpression of pathway genes ARO1, ARO2, ARO4K229L, ARO7G141S, and TYR1, as well as knockout of the prephenate dehydratase gene PHA2, all positively enhanced (2S)-hesperetin yield. The introduction of the mutants ARO4K229L and ARO7G141S yielded the most favorable results, increasing (2S)-hesperetin production by 18% and 13.4%, respectively, compared to strain Y6. We believe that in the shikimate pathway, ARO4 and ARO7 are key rate-limiting enzymes, which are subject to feedback inhibition by l-tyrosine. Introducing the mutants ARO4K229L and ARO7G141S is crucial for relieving the feedback inhibition by l-tyrosine on ARO4 and ARO7, thereby enhancing the yield of (2S)-hesperetin. The (2S)-hesperetin yields of strains Y12 (overexpressing ARO1), Y13 (overexpressing ARO2), Y14 (overexpressing TYR1), and Y15 (knockout of PHA2) increased by 17.5%, 9%, 16.7% and 11.9%, respectively, compared to strain Y6. The genes ARO1 and ARO2 jointly constitute the backbone of the shikimate pathway. Overexpression of these genes enhances metabolic flux in the shikimate pathway and increases the pool of aromatic amino acids. TYR1 is a key gene in the downstream branch of the shikimate pathway, and its overexpression can increase the supply of l-tyrosine, the precursor of (2S)-hesperetin. Additionally, this study synthesized (2S)-hesperetin using tyrosine as a precursor. Knocking out the PHA2 gene reduces the carbon source consumption by the byproduct l-phenylalanine, thereby increasing the accumulation of l-tyrosine. Based on the above results, these genes were coexpressed and PHA2 was knocked out in strain Y6 to obtain the engineered strain Y16. Fermentation results are shown in Figure A, where strain Y16 achieved a (2S)-hesperetin titer of 117.1 mg/L, representing a significant 38.1% increase compared to strain Y6. These findings demonstrate that enhancing the upstream aromatic amino acid pathway substantially boosts the capacity of the engineered strain to synthesize (2S)-hesperetin.

(A) The effect of optimizing the shikimate pathway on the titer of (2S)-hesperetin. (B) The effect of optimizing the malonyl-CoA synthesis pathway on the titer of (2S)-hesperetin.

In the malonyl-CoA synthesis pathway, acetyl-CoA carboxylase ACC1 serves as the key rate-limiting enzyme, catalyzing the conversion of acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA. Therefore, we integrated the ACC1 gene from Y. lipolytica into the genome of strain Y16 under the control of the strong promoter hp4d. Concurrently, mitochondrial carnitine acetyltransferase gene CAT1 was overexpressed to enhance the transport of acetyl-CoA from mitochondria to the cytoplasm, thereby increasing cytoplasmic acetyl-CoA availability. As shown in Figure B, the engineered strain Y18 achieved a (2S)-hesperetin titer of 152 mg/L, exhibiting a 29.8% increase compared to the control strain Y16. Additionally, to reduce diversion from the malonyl-CoA pool to the fatty acid synthesis pathway, we further knocked out the fatty acid synthase gene Fas1, yielding the engineered strain Y19. Shake flask fermentation results showed that the (2S)-naringenin yield of strain Y19 increased to 160.9 mg/L (Figure B), representing a significant 5.9% improvement compared to the control strain Y18. The results above revealed that reconstructing the shikimate pathway and the malonyl-CoA synthesis pathway can effectively provide precursor pools for the synthesis of (2S)-hesperetin, offering a potential strategy for enhancing the production of flavonoid compounds.

Production of (2S)-Hesperetin Using Waste Cooking Oil as Carbon Source

Y. lipolytica possesses the natural ability to utilize oils and is considered the most promising microorganism for stable utilization of WCO. To determine the feasibility of WCO as an effective carbon source, we first replaced glucose with peanut oil as the sole carbon source for the engineered strain Y19. As shown in Figure A, after 120 h fermentation of engineered strain Y19 in YP medium supplemented with 2% peanut oil, the yield of (2S)-hesperetin reached 184.3 mg/L, which is 14.5% higher than that achieved using glucose as the carbon source (160.9 mg/L). Additionally, the OD600 of engineered strain Y19 reached 24. These results demonstrated that the engineered strain Y19 can grow robustly and efficiently produce (2S)-hesperetin from peanut oil.

(A) The titers of (2S)-hesperetin by strain Y29 when using glucose and peanut oil as carbon sources. (B) The effect of acetyl-CoA pathway optimization on the titer of (2S)-hesperetin. (C) The yield of (2S)-hesperetin using WCO at concentrations of 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10% as the carbon source, with glucose and peanut oil as controls.

To enhance the utilization of oils by strain Y19 and increase acetyl-CoA supply, we investigated pathway genes involved in converting oils into fatty acids and utilizing fatty acids to elevate precursor acetyl-CoA pool. Generally, acetyl-CoA is produced through the β-oxidation of fatty acids. In Y. lipolytica, the β-oxidation of fatty acids is initiated by six different acyl-CoA oxidases (POX1-6), which first catalyze the oxidative dehydrogenation of acyl-CoA. subsequently, the enoyl-CoA is hydroxylated and dehydrogenated by the multifunctional enzyme (MFE1) to form β-ketoenoyl-CoA, and finally thiolated by the peroxisome thiolase (POT1) to yield acetyl-CoA. , Therefore, nine genes involved in the β-oxidation pathway were overexpressed in strain Y19, including LIP2 (encoding lipase), POX1-6, MFE1, and POT1, yielding strains Y20–Y28. Fermentation results (Figure B) indicated that overexpressing genes POX2, MFE1, and POT1 positively enhanced (2S)-hesperetin yield and oil utilization. Therefore, we simultaneously overexpressed genes POX2, MFE1, and POT1 in strain Y19 to obtain strain Y29. The fermentation results showed that the titer of (2S)-hesperetin reached 233.5 mg/L after 120 h of fermentation in YP medium supplemented with 2% peanut oil by strain Y29, which was significantly higher than that of the control strain Y19 (184.3 mg/L).

To further validate the ability of WCO as a carbon source for producing (2S)-hesperetin, the engineered strain Y29 was cultured in YPO medium containing different concentrations of WCO (2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10%), with glucose and peanut oil used as controls. The results showed (Figure C) that at a WCO concentration of 6%, the yield of (2S)-hesperetin reached 276.9 mg/L­(7.6 mg/g WCO), representing increases of 37.7% and 19% compared to glucose and peanut oil as carbon sources, while OD600 increased by 40.6% and 13.4%, respectively. We consider that triglycerides in WCO undergo hydrolysis, oxidation, and polymerization reactions during repeated high-temperature heating, yielding glycerol and free fatty acids. Glycerol serves as the preferred carbon source for Y. lipolytica, and it is beneficial for the accumulation of acetyl coenzyme A and glycerol-3-phosphate within the cells, which are also the precursors for the synthesis of (2S)-hesperetin. These findings demonstrated that WCO is an effective alternative carbon source for both cell growth and (2S)-hesperetin production.

Production of (2S)-Hesperetin in a 5 L Bioreactor

To evaluate the fermentation performance of the engineered strain Y29, we conducted an expanded cultivation in a 5 L bioreactor. During the fermentation process, the stirring speed and aeration rate were controlled to maintain the dissolved oxygen concentration above 30%, and the WCO concentration was 6%. The results showed (Figure ) that (2S)-hesperetin accumulated significantly at 48–120 h, and its accumulation stabilized during fermentation at 120–144 h. Ultimately, strain Y29 achieved a (2S)-hesperetin yield of 488.7 mg/L­(13.4 mg/g WCO), and the OD600 reached a maximum of 126.9. The results above demonstrate that the engineered strain Y29 exhibits excellent stability and production performance in a 5 L bioreactor. This study successfully developed an engineered Y. lipolytica capable of de novo synthesis of (2S)-hesperetin from waste cooking oil, significantly reducing fermentation costs and showcasing strong industrial application competitiveness.

Conclusion

With growing global interest in environmental protection and sustainable development, the industrial-scale production of valuable platform chemicals using low-cost waste materials is attracting increasing attention. Here, we use the inexpensive feedstock WCO to enhance the availability of acetyl-CoA for its conversion to (2S)-hesperetin in Y. lipolytica. At the same time, by applying metabolic engineering and bioprocess optimization strategies, we achieved a (2S)-hesperetin titer of 488.7 mg/L in a bioreactor-scale system using WCO as a carbon source. Our research provides a new biomanufacturing route for the sustainable and low-cost production of flavonoids from waste resources.
