Investigating the relationship between the difference in gingival color and denture base color on patient satisfaction with complete dentures
Authors: Maryam Amini-Pozveh, Arezoo Mazaheri Nazarifar, Mohammadreza Karimipoor, Ramin Atash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient satisfaction with complete dentures is multifactorial, with aesthetics being a key determinant. The color match between the denture base and the patient’s natural gingiva is a critical aspect of denture aesthetics; however, its specific impact on satisfaction remained unclear. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between the color difference of the denture base from the natural gingiva and patient satisfaction with complete dentures.MethodsIn this descriptive-analytical study, 88 edentulous patients (62 men, 26 women) scheduled for complete denture treatment were enrolled. The closest match to the patient’s gingival color was selected by a dentist using the Ivoclar Ivobase Shade Guide. Patients also independently selected their preferred base color. All dentures were fabricated using a single shade of acrylic resin (Pref) as standard color. Patient satisfaction was assessed using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS, 0–10) at two time points: at delivery and at a one-week follow-up. Data on age, gender, education level, and color choices were collected. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27 with Mann-Whitney, Kruskal-Wallis, and Spearman’s rank correlation tests.ResultsPatient satisfaction showed no significant correlation with age, gender, education level, or the patient’s own color choice (P > 0.05). However, a significant positive correlation was found between satisfaction and the dentist’s initial color selection (P = 0.006). This relationship was stronger at the follow-up session compared to the delivery session.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that the dentist’s assessment of gingival color, leading to a closer match between the denture base and natural tissues, could be associated with patient satisfaction. Patient self-selection of color did not significantly influence satisfaction levels.

Background

Edentulism adversely affects oral function, facial aesthetics, and quality of life [1, 2], leading to reduced facial height, masticatory muscle atrophy, and speech difficulties [3–5]. While implant-supported prostheses are often the ideal treatment [6], removable complete dentures remain a common solution due to factors such as cost and medical contraindications [4]. Consequently, optimizing patient satisfaction with complete dentures is a primary clinical objective [7, 8].

Satisfaction is influenced by multiple factors, with aesthetics playing a pivotal role [9]. A significant aspect of denture aesthetics is the color of the acrylic resin base [10]. The ideal aesthetic outcome is achieved when the denture base closely mimics the shade and characterization of the patient’s natural gingiva [11, 12]. Commercial shade guides, such as those for crowns, are often used to select an appropriate pink acrylic resin [13–15].

Techniques to enhance base color range from intrinsic coloring during processing to extrinsic characterization after polymerization. While post-processing characterization with composite resins can yield highly natural results, it increases cost and laboratory time [16]. Patient perceptions of aesthetics often differ from those of clinicians [17], and neglecting this discrepancy can lead to dissatisfaction despite technically adequate treatment.

Although previous studies have examined general factors influencing denture satisfaction [18, 19] and discrepancies in tooth color selection between patients and dentists [20], a focused investigation into parameters affecting satisfaction specifically with denture base color is lacking. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of the dentist’s and patient’s color selections, along with demographic factors (age, gender, education), on patient satisfaction with the aesthetics of complete denture bases.

Methods

Study design and participants

This descriptive-analytical cross-sectional study was conducted in the Removable Prosthodontics Department of Isfahan Dental School. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (Code: IR.MUI.REC.1401.049). A formal sample size calculation was not performed; instead, the sample size was determined based on feasibility and the availability of eligible participants. A total of 88 patients (62 men, 26 women) seeking complete denture treatment were enrolled after providing informed consent. Exclusion criteria included presence of gingival inflammation, visual impairment, cognitive or psychiatric disorders, and unwillingness to participate.

Color selection procedure

A single shade guide (Ivoclar Ivobase Shade Guide, Ivoclar Vivadent) comprising six pink shades (Pink, Pink V, Pink V Implantitis, Pref, Pref Implantitis, 34 V) was used. Under natural daylight conditions (daylight lamp D55 in 35 cm distance from patient face in neutral gray back ground), a prosthodontist selected the shade guide tab deemed closest to the color of the patient’s healthy, moist gingiva. Prior to shade selection, the examiner was calibrated to ensure reliable color perception and consistency. Color vision was assessed using the Ishihara Color Vision Test to exclude any color vision deficiencies. The examiner underwent training sessions using a standardized shade guide under controlled lighting conditions. Repeated shade matching exercises were performed until intra-examiner agreement was achieved. To minimize visual fatigue and improve accuracy, shade selection was conducted in short intervals with intermittent visual rest periods.

The patient was then shown the selected tab against their own gingiva using a mirror under the same standard conditions in which the color selection was done by examiner and was asked to independently choose their preferred base color from the same guide. All dentures were fabricated using a single, standard shade of heat-cured acrylic resin (IvoBase Hybrid, Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein shade Pref).

Data collection and outcome measure

A checklist was used to record patient demographics (age, gender, education level) and color choices (patient’s choice, dentist’s choice). The primary outcome was patient satisfaction with denture base aesthetics, measured using a 10-point Visual Analog Scale (VAS), where 0 indicated “completely dissatisfied” and 10 indicated “completely satisfied.” Satisfaction was assessed at two time points: (T1) during the denture delivery appointment and (T2) one week post-delivery during a follow-up visit. Patients were instructed to rate only the aesthetic appearance of the denture base.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics software (Version 27). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which indicated deviation from normal distribution. Because the satisfaction scores were not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk, p < 0.001), Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used.

The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare satisfaction scores by gender and by match/mismatch with the standard fabrication color. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to assess the relationship between education level and satisfaction.

Results

The study population consisted of 70.5% males (n = 62) and 29.5% females (n = 26). The mean age was 57.43 years (58.22 for men, 55.48 for women). Due to non-normality of the satisfaction data (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < 0.001) Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess relationships between variables. Spearman’s correlation analysis showed no statistically significant relationship between age and satisfaction with delivery (ρ = -0.02, p = 0.83) or between age and satisfaction with follow-up (ρ = -0.14, p = 0.20), indicating that patient age did not influence either satisfaction measure.The Mann-Whitney test was used to find the gender and satisfaction relationship. It showed there is no significant relationship between gender and the level of patient satisfaction. (P value for the delivery session was 0.981 and for the follow-up session was 0.719).

Color selection frequencies

The frequency of color choices differed markedly between patients and the dentist (Figs. 1 and 2). The most frequently selected shade by patients was Pref (n = 22), followed by Pref Implantitis (n = 19). In contrast, the dentist most frequently selected Pink V Implantitis (n = 29), followed by 34 V (n = 21). Only 17 patients (19.3%) chose the same shade as the dentist.

Discussion

The present study evaluated key factors associated with patient satisfaction with the aesthetics of complete denture bases. The central finding reveals a critical distinction: while the patient’s self-reported color preference at chairside did not significantly correlate with subsequent satisfaction, the clinician’s objective assessment of gingival color was significantly associated with patient satisfaction. Satisfaction levels did not differ significantly between cases where the patient’s preferred color matched the standard and those where it did not. However, when the clinician’s selected color matched the standard, satisfaction was significantly higher. This suggests a gap between immediate preference and long-term aesthetic acceptance.

Our results align with existing literature on patient-reported outcomes in prosthodontics. The lack of significant correlation between satisfaction and demographic factors (age, gender) is consistent with findings by McCunniff et al. on removable dentures [19], suggesting that expectations for natural appearance are shared across demographic groups. The non-significant trend with education level does not establish a definitive relationship, highlighting that aesthetic appraisal is multifactorial and not solely determined by formal education.

The most significant insight is the dissociation between the patient’s initial color selection and post-delivery satisfaction. This suggests that chairside color preference may not reliably predict long-term contentment. One explanation is that the initial choice occurs in a limited context—focusing on a small shade tab in isolation—whereas ultimate appraisal occurs in a dynamic social context, where the denture must integrate with perioral tissues during speech, smiling, and interaction. The decline in correlation strength from delivery to follow-up for the patient’s own choice supports the view that evaluation criteria evolve after the prosthesis is worn.

Conversely, the strong, statistically significant correlation between the dentist’s color selection and patient satisfaction at follow-up (P = 0.006) carries substantial clinical relevance. A shade chosen through professional judgment—aimed at replicating healthy gingiva—is associated with greater patient acceptance over time. This finding aligns with the principle in tooth shade selection that professional assessment yields a more natural appearance than patient choice [20]. It extends this rationale to gingival aesthetics, suggesting that objective clinical evaluation is more strongly associated with aesthetic success than subjective patient preference.

A plausible explanation involves post-insertion adaptation and social feedback. A denture base that closely matches the mucosa is less visually disruptive, allowing faster adaptation. In contrast, an obvious mismatch may remain a visual focus and invite negative comments from the patient’s social environment, thereby reducing satisfaction. The increasing strength of the association for the clinician’s choice at one-week follow-up highlights the role of real-world evaluation in shaping final judgment.

The one-week follow-up period was selected to evaluate short-term satisfaction and early adaptation while minimizing recall bias and capturing immediate perceptions after initial use. However, this relatively short follow-up period is a limitation, as it does not permit assessment of long-term satisfaction or prosthesis performance over time.

Limitations and future research

This study has limitations that provide direction for future inquiry. First the use of a single fabrication shade, while methodologically sound for controlling variables, precluded direct assessment of satisfaction when the dentist’s precise first-choice shade was manufactured. Subsequent research should fabricate dentures according to the clinician’s selected shade to measure its true effect. Furthermore, the standard commercial shade guide may not fully capture the chromatic range of all patient populations. Investigations into more comprehensive or population-specific gingival color systems are warranted. Long-term studies are also needed to determine if the satisfaction observed at one week remains stable over extended periods. Second, the relatively short follow-up period (one week) only allowed assessment of short-term patient satisfaction and did not provide information on long-term outcomes. Third, patient satisfaction was measured using a visual analogue scale (VAS), which is subjective and may be influenced by individual perception despite its widespread use. Finally, the gender distribution was not balanced, with a higher proportion of male participants, which may limit the external validity of the results.

Finally, the influence of other aesthetic parameters, such as denture base characterization and its interaction with tooth color, was not examined and represents a valuable avenue for future research.

Clinical implications

These findings suggest a meaningful adjustment to clinical practice. While patient preference should be respectfully considered, the clinician’s expertise in color matching should be paramount. The process should include educating the patient on the goal of achieving a natural, tissue-integrated appearance that is likely to be perceived favorably over time, thereby aligning expectations with predictable aesthetic outcomes.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, it can be concluded that among the factors investigated, the color match between the denture base and the natural gingiva as determined by the clinician could be associated with patient satisfaction with complete denture aesthetics. The patient’s self-selected color preference did not significantly influence satisfaction levels. Therefore, clinical emphasis should be placed on achieving an accurate gingival color match based on professional assessment to enhance the long-term aesthetic satisfaction of complete denture wearers. Nevertheless, these findings are constrained by the use of a single denture base shade, and caution is advised when generalizing to broader clinical scenarios involving multiple shade options.
