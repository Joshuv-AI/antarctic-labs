Context-dependent regulation of IgH V(D)J recombination by cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2
Authors: Fujung Chang, Brigette Berke-Reynolds, Xinrui Yu, Emma L Bush, Jorge A Colón-Rosado, Liangliang Sun, Jianrong Wang, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCohesin-mediated loop extrusion is central to IgH V(D)J recombination in early developing B cells, driving D-to-JH rearrangements and VH-to-DJH rearrangements, with the latter step regulated by the cohesin release factor WAPL. Here, we investigate the distinct contributions of two cohesin subunits, STAG1 and STAG2, which are incorporated in the somatic cohesin variants, cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2, respectively. Using WAPL-sufficient mouse v-Abl pro-B cells to model the D-to-JH recombination stage, we show that STAG2 promotes balanced D-to-JH joining and restrains STAG1 expression. Loss of STAG2 elevates cohesin-STAG1 occupancy on chromatin, leading to premature long-range loop formation and expanded VH usage at the expense of distal DH usage. This phenotype persists in the absence of the cohesin acetyltransferase ESCO1 but requires the N-terminal YDF motif of CTCF, which protects cohesin from WAPL-mediated chromatin release. In WAPL-depleted cells modeling the VH-to-DJH recombination stage, the CTCF N-terminus is dispensable and both cohesin variants act cooperatively to establish a diverse VH repertoire. Thus, our findings uncover context-dependent and complementary roles of these two cohesin variants in shaping antibody gene diversity, and also highlight a regulatory axis between STAG1, STAG2, and CTCF in controlling long-range chromatin dynamics.Subject terms: VDJ recombination, Chromatin structure, B cells

Introduction

In developing B and T lymphocytes, V(D)J recombination assembles antigen receptor genes to generate highly diverse antibody and T cell receptor (TCR) repertoires critical to adaptive immunity1. Lymphoid-specific RAG1/RAG2 (RAG) endonuclease initiates V(D)J recombination in G1 phase cells by cutting paired recombination signal sequences (RSS) flanking V, D, and J segments2. The mouse IgH locus has served as an important model system in understanding V(D)J recombination control and gene regulation. IgH V(D)J recombination is an ordered two-step process, in which D-to-JH recombination occurs within the 3’ IgH domain, followed by joining of the DJH segment to one of many VH segments distributed across an upstream 2.4 Mb region3. RAG is enriched at a region marked by highly active chromatin that span the intronic enhancer (iEμ), J segments and the J-proximal D (DQ52), generating a “recombination center” (RC) as a hub to capture complementary RSSs for V(D)J recombination4,5 (Fig. 1a). IgH V(D)J recombination is orchestrated by multiple mechanisms, including germline transcription, DNA demethylation, active histone modifications, and subnuclear repositioning, which collectively regulate locus accessibility for RAG binding6–9. However, while chromatin accessibility permits RAG binding, it is not sufficient for efficient V(D)J recombination without appropriate 3D genome topology establishing physical juxtaposition of the complementary RSSs for RAG cleavage6–8.

Loop extrusion by cohesin, a ring-shaped protein complex, has emerged as a key mechanism shaping the 3D organization of antigen receptor loci10. Cohesin extrudes chromatin loops progressively until it encounters boundary elements, most prominently those bound by the architectural protein CTCF11–16. CTCF is a ubiquitously expressed zinc-finger protein that binds thousands of genomic sites in an orientation-dependent manner, and through interactions between its N-terminal domain and cohesin, functions as a directional barrier that impedes cohesin translocation and defines chromatin loop boundaries17–22. The mouse IgH locus has a unique organization of CTCF sites (Fig. 1a). The 3’ IgH domain is anchored upstream by the CTCF-based Inter-Genic Control Region1 (IGCR1)23 and downstream by a cluster of ten CTCF binding elements termed 3’CBEs24–26. IGCR1 lies within the VH-DH intergenic region and contains two divergent CTCF sites, CBE1 and CBE2, which together function as a developmental insulator that prevent premature VH-to-DJH recombination and enforce ordered V(D)JH recombination23,27,28. Additionally, the VH region contains a high density of CTCF sites, which are either located just downstream ( < 100 bp) of the RSS of most proximal VHs or within 10 kb of many middle and distal VHs25,29. A critical regulator of cohesin dynamics is WAPL, a cohesin release factor that promotes cohesin unloading by opening the cohesin ring and thereby limits cohesin’s residence time on chromatin30,31. In the absence of WAPL, cohesin remains bound to DNA for prolonged periods and extrudes larger chromatin loops, leading to substantial changes in chromatin loop size and higher-order genome organization32–34.

RAG off-target profiling and locus conformation studies have supported a scanning model in which RAG, bound at the RC-RSSs, scans chromatin linearly within extruded loops to locate partner RSSs, with strong bias toward usage of convergent RSSs35–37. CTCF-bound sites and other chromatin impediments can stall loop extrusion and RAG scanning, increasing dwell time and recombination frequency at these locations37. This mechanism supports efficient deletional D-to-JH recombination occurring in pre-pro-B cells37, while VH-to-DJH joining in pro-B cells further requires locus contraction, a process involving long-range chromatin looping to bring distal VH segments into proximity with the RC7,8,38,39. Developmental downregulation of WAPL in pro-B cells enables extended loop extrusion and helps overcome IGCR1 and the high density of CTCF barriers across the VH locus to permit distal VH usage40,41. Similar loop extrusion-based mechanisms likely operate at other antigen receptor loci, such as Igκ42,43 and TCRβ44, though the details remain to be fully elucidated. These findings underscore the essential role of loop extrusion in V(D)J recombination and prompt the question of whether distinct cohesin variants play differential roles in this process.

Cohesin is composed of the core subunits SMC1A, SMC3 and RAD21, which associate with either STAG1 or STAG2 in mammalian somatic cells45,46. STAG1 and STAG2 are co-expressed, although their relative abundance and chromatin distribution vary by cell type47,48. A subset of cohesin-STAG1 exhibits prolonged chromatin retention in G1 phase cells and facilitates the formation of long chromatin loops, an effect linked to preferential acetylation by ESCO1, a cohesin acetyltransferase49–52, and potentially modulated by CTCF through an as-yet undefined mechanism47. In contrast, cohesin-STAG2 is more dynamically regulated due to its stronger interaction with WAPL, resulting in shorter and more transient loops47,48. Additionally, while CTCF directly interacts with both cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2 via its N-terminus, as shown by cryogenic electron microscopy studies17,22, co-immunoprecipitation studies indicate that CTCF associates more strongly with cohesin-STAG1 than with cohesin-STAG247. Whether the two cohesin variants differ in their capacity to mediate V(D)J recombination remains an open and important question for understanding the mechanistic diversity of loop extrusion in immune development.

Here, we investigate the effects of STAG1 and STAG2 loss on V(D)JH recombination using the well-established Abelson virus-transformed (v-Abl) pro-B cell system. Their effects are assessed both in normal WAPL level and WAPL-depleted cells, modeling D-to-JH recombination and VH-to-DJH recombination stages, respectively. We find that STAG2 promotes a balanced D-to-JH joining repertoire by limiting premature, CTCF-dependent long-range loop extrusion mediated by cohesin-STAG1, whereas STAG1 is largely dispensable for D-to-JH joining. In contrast, during the VH-to-DJH recombination stage in WAPL-depleted cells, the two cohesin variants act cooperatively to support a diverse VH repertoire. These results reveal distinct, stage- and context-dependent roles of cohesin variants in shaping antibody diversity.

Results

STAG2 suppresses long-range VH-to-DJH recombination in WAPL-proficient v-Abl pro-B cells

To assess the distinct contributions of cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2 in mediating IgH V(D)J recombination, we employed CRISPR/Cas953 to generate Stag1-/- and Stag2-/- lines by deleting exon 7 of Stag1 and exon 10 of Stag2, respectively (Fig. 1b). These mutations were introduced into a previously characterized Bcl2+ v-Abl pro-B cell line harboring a catalytic-deficient RAG1(D708A) mutation4. This cell line retains germline IgH alleles and undergoes inducible V(D)J recombination upon reconstitution with wild-type (WT) RAG1 and induction to enter G1 phase54. To compare V(D)JH joining profiles between control and mutant lines, we complemented the cells retrovirally with WT RAG1, arrested them in G1 phase for 4 days using STI-571, and then performed high-throughput genome-wide translocation sequencing-adapted V(D)J-sequencing (HTGTS-V(D)J-Seq)35,37, using the JH1 coding end as bait primer (Fig. 1c).

As expected for normal v-Abl pro-B cells, the WT strain displayed efficient D-to-JH joining but limited VH-to-DJH joining that mainly involves the very proximal six VH segments, largely resembling D-to-JH recombination stage (Supplementary Figs. 1a, b, and Fig. 1c, d). Functional DH segments across the D-JH region were utilized, with DFL16.1 and DQ52 being the most frequently joined, reflecting their proximity to the CTCF-based IGCR1 loop anchor and the RC, respectively37 (Supplementary Figs. 1a, b). Stag1-/- cells showed a similar usage pattern as WT cells, indicating cohesin-STAG1 is dispensable for the D-to-JH joining step. In contrast, Stag2-/- cells showed a skewed profile, with increased DQ52 usage and reduced joining of all RC-distal DH segments, including DFL16.1 (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Therefore, cohesin-STAG2 is required for maintaining a balanced DH repertoire.

Interestingly, while the Stag1-/- strain displayed limited VH usage, closely resembling the pattern observed in WT cells, the Stag2-/- strain demonstrated a notable increase in VH-to-DJH joining across the IgH locus, particularly involving VH segments located further from the D-JH region (Fig. 1c). Overall, Stag2-/- cells showed a 6.5-fold increase in VDJH joins involving far-proximal VH segments beyond the first six, and a 9-fold increase in joins involving the middle and distal VH segments, which are hereafter collectively referred to as long-range VDJH joins, compared to WT cells (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Some individual VH segments showed more than a 20-fold increase (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Further analysis revealed that Stag2-/- cells had a significant gain in VDJH: DJH joining ratio, along with a considerable increase in the proportion of middle/distal VH usage among total VDJH joins, both contributing to the overall enhancement in the long-range VDJH recombination (Fig. 1d, e).

Previous HTGTS-V(D)J-Seq studies in WT primary B cells using each of the four individual JH segments as baits showed that all JHs yield highly similar V(D)JH recombination patterns, with JH4 exhibiting only a slightly higher VDJH:DJH ratio and somewhat lower DQ52 usage55. To determine whether the V(D)JH joining changes observed in the Stag mutants were dependent on the choice of JH bait, we performed HTGTS-V(D)J-Seq using the coding end of JH4, in addition to JH1, as bait. This analysis revealed that the recombination patterns in Stag mutant strains closely matched those obtained with the JH1 bait: Stag1-/- cells again resembled WT, whereas Stag2-/- cells consistently exhibited an elevated VDJH:DJH ratio, a higher proportion of middle/distal VH usage, and reduced joining of many RC-distal DH segments (Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). Given the closely comparable results across baits, we therefore present all subsequent HTGTS-V(D)J-Seq analyses using the JH1 bait.

Since loop extrusion facilitates most D-to-JH and VH-to-DJH recombination except for DQ52, which can access RC via short-range diffusion37, these findings likely reflect extended loop formation in Stag2-/- cells promoting distal VH-to-DJH recombination at the expense of shorter loop formation in the D-JH domain.

STAG2 loss unleashes cohesin-STAG1 to drive extended IgH loop formation

We next examined how STAG1 and STAG2 deficiency affects IgH loop formation. For this, we performed Chromosome Conformation Capture-HTGTS (3C-HTGTS) assays29 using the intronic enhancer iEμ in the RC as the bait in G1 arrested WT and mutant cells (Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Fig. 3). v-Abl pro-B cells are known to lack IgH contraction, consistent with this, the control strain showed limited RC interaction with most of the VH region, except for the very proximal portion. The Stag1-/- strain exhibited a similar interaction pattern, in line with its comparable V(D)JH recombination profile. In contrast, Stag2-/- cells showed a marked increase in RC interactions with the middle and distal VH regions (Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). This enhanced interaction closely correlated with the increased usage of VH segments in these regions (Fig. 1c, e). On the other hand, RC interactions with the IGCR1-CBE2 region, which defines the boundary of the DH-JH domain, were reduced in Stag2-/- cells, coinciding with decreased usage of RC-distal DH segments (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). These data support that, in Stag2-/- cells, where cohesin-STAG1 is the sole variant, loop extrusion promotes long-range IgH loop formation at the cost of shorter ones, driving the altered IgH recombination pattern.

Western blot analysis revealed a twofold increase in STAG1 protein levels following Stag2 knockout, whereas STAG2 levels showed only a very mild increase after Stag1 knockout, suggesting STAG1 expression is normally suppressed by STAG2 (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). To further investigate cohesin occupancy on chromatin, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-Seq)56,57. In the control cells, STAG1 and STAG2 were predominantly enriched at the CTCF sites, with the two proteins displaying a similar occupancy to the proximity of CTCF sites across the IgH locus (Supplementary Figs. 5a, b). This enrichment was evident at multiple CTCF-dependent IgH regulatory elements, including the 3’CBEs anchor, the IGCR1 insulator, and the CTCF sites within the PAX5-activated intergenic repeats (PAIR elements)58 (Supplementary Fig. 5c). In addition, moderate enrichment of both STAG1 and STAG2 was detected at the iEμ/RC region, which lacks CTCF sites (Supplementary Fig. 5c).

ChIP-Seq analysis of STAG2 and SMC3 in Stag1-/- cells revealed little to no change in cohesin-STAG2 chromatin association compared to the control (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). In contrast, in Stag2-/- cells, a genome-wide increase in chromatin binding by both STAG1 and SMC3 was observed, indicating enhanced cohesin-STAG1 complex accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Notably, although STAG1 and SMC3 were broadly elevated across the VH locus, they were not enriched near the very proximal VH segments, consistent with the minimal changes observed in VH recombination and RC interactions in this region (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Fig. 6b). Instead, their binding showed a trend toward further increase in the more distal VH regions (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Fig. 6b). This pattern cannot be attributed solely to a general increase in cohesin-STAG1 binding, but likely reflects the greater processivity of cohesin-STAG1 complexes47, which promotes extended loop extrusion and their accumulation in RC-distal regions.

We further examined the impact of STAG1 overexpression in cohesin-STAG1 binding. To this end, STAG1 cDNA were cloned under either the core or the full EF1α promoter and introduced into the control WT v-Abl pro-B cells using the piggyBac system. While the core EF1α promoter did not drive detectable STAG1 overexpression, the full EF1α promoter reproducibly resulted in approximately two-fold increased STAG1 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Consistent with this increase, STAG1 ChIP-Seq analysis revealed elevated STAG1 chromatin occupancy both genome-wide and at the IgH locus in STAG1-overexpressing cells compared with control cells, supporting that a ~two-fold increase in STAG1 protein levels is sufficient to enhance STAG1 chromatin binding (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). Interestingly, in contrast to Stag2-/- cells, where increased STAG1 binding is accompanied by a clear increase in SMC3 occupancy, SMC3 ChIP-Seq profiles were largely comparable between STAG1-overexpressing and control cells (Supplementary Fig. 7b, d). This observation may suggest that ectopic STAG1 expression does not fully recapitulate the endogenous context required for efficient cohesin complex formation. Alternatively, it may indicate that STAG2, which is present in the STAG1-overexpressed cells but absent in Stag2-/- cells, influences cohesin-STAG1 chromatin association through mechanisms beyond regulating STAG1 protein abundance, which awaits further investigation.

ESCO1 is dispensable for cohesin-STAG1-mediated long-range VH rearrangements

We next sought to elucidate mechanisms underlying extended loop formation and distal VH usage mediated by cohesin-STAG1 in v-Abl pro-B cells. A recent study in HeLa cells reported G1-specific, preferential acetylation of cohesin-STAG1 over cohesin-STAG2 by the cohesin acetylase ESCO1, producing a subpopulation of cohesin-STAG1 complexes with prolonged chromatin residence time47. This finding raises the possibility that ESCO1-dependent acetylation contributes to the enhanced ability of cohesin-STAG1 to promote long-range VH-to-DJH joining. To test the relevance of this mechanism in our system, we examined the effect of ESCO1 deficiency on V(D)JH recombination. For this purpose, we established an inducible ESCO1 degradation system using version 2 of the auxin-inducible degron (AID2) technology59, in which OsTIR1(F74G) was inserted into the Rosa26 locus to minimize leakage. ESCO1 was tagged with miniAID-tdTomato, generating the Esco1-AID2 line (Fig. 3a). Upon treatment with 1 μM 5-Ph-IAA, the tagged ESCO1 protein was efficiently degraded within 4 h and remained depleted during G1 arrest under continuous exposure to 5-Ph-IAA (Fig. 3a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 8).

We then performed HTGTS-V(D)J-Seq analysis on RAG1-complemented, G1-arrested Esco1-AID2 cells treated with 5-Ph-IAA. Unexpectedly, rather than reducing distal VH usage as predicted from the canonical role of ESCO1 in cohesin acetylation and protection from WAPL-mediated chromatin release47,52,60, ESCO1 depletion resulted in a modest increase in middle and distal VH segment usage, along with a slight increase in VDJH: DJH ratio (Fig. 3c–e). These results do not support a simple model in which ESCO1 promotes long-range interactions by counteracting WAPL activity. Instead, the observed changes in VH usage are consistent with a previously reported non-canonical role of ESCO1-mediated cohesin acetylation in restricting loop size, potentially through promoting loop boundary formation involving the cohesin regulator PDS546,47,52,61–63. In line with this interpretation, ESCO1 depletion in Stag2-/- cells had minimal additional impact on V(D)JH joining, with recombination patterns largely resembling those of Stag2-/- cells alone and only a slight increase in distal VH usage in the double mutant (Fig. 3c–e). Together, these findings indicate that ESCO1 is not required to support cohesin-STAG1-associated long-range VH recombination in v-Abl pro-B cells. While we did not directly assess cohesin acetylation or loop extrusion dynamics in this system, our results suggest that ESCO1 is not a major determinant of the functional recombination outcomes associated with cohesin-STAG1.

Cohesin-STAG1-mediated long-range VH rearrangement is critically dependent on the CTCF-YDF motif

CTCF establishes polar barriers to loop extrusion by engaging cohesin through its N-terminal Tyr-Asp-Phe (YDF) motif17,22. This interaction is thought to enhance cohesin’s processivity by preventing it from WAPL-mediated release. We therefore asked whether cohesin-STAG1-driven long-range V(D)J recombination depends on this YDF-mediated interaction. To test this, we introduced a homozygous CTCF(Y226A/F228A) mutation17, which disrupts the N-terminal YDF motif, into the Stag2-/- background, thereby generating a Stag2-/- Ctcf-Nm line (Supplementary Fig. 9a).

We then performed STAG1 ChIP-Seq to assess the effect of the CTCF(Y226A/F228A) mutation on cohesin-STAG1 chromatin association. This analysis revealed that the strong accumulation of cohesin-STAG1 observed both genome-wide and at the IgH locus in Stag2-/- cells was greatly reduced in the Stag2-/- Ctcf-Nm double mutants (Supplementary Fig. 9b, and Fig. 4a). We next performed 3C-HTGTS experiments to examine loop formation. Consistently, this analysis showed that the enhanced RC interactions with middle and distal VH regions in Stag2-/- cells were largely lost upon introduction of the CTCF(Y226A/F228A) mutation, indicating the long-range loop extrusion is severely compromised (Fig. 4b, and Supplementary Fig. 9c, f). Additionally, the already reduced interaction between RC and the IGCR1-CBE2 region in Stag2-/- cells was further diminished in the double mutant, accompanied by increased RC interactions with proximal VH regions, consistent with the N-terminal mutation disrupting CTCF-dependent loop barrier formation17 (Supplementary Fig. 9d–f). Correspondingly, HTGTS-V(D)J-Seq of RAG1-complemented cells demonstrated that the substantial increase in middle and distal VH usage in Stag2-/- cells was nearly completely abolished in the Stag2-/- Ctcf-Nm mutant (Fig. 4c). This shift was accompanied by a marked increase in the VDJH: DJH ratio, a pronounced enrichment of proximal VH usage and a further reduction in DFL16.1 usage (Fig. 4d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 9g). These changes in V(D)JH recombination pattern, particularly the strong increase in proximal VH usage, correlate well with the altered IgH interaction profiles observed by 3C-HTGTS and likely reflect two consequences of the CTCF(Y226A/F228A) mutation. First, compromised barrier insulation: impairment of the IGCR1 boundary enables more efficient VH access to the D-JH domain via direct loop extrusion and thereby promotes robust VH-to-DJH joining. In addition, weakening of the barrier function of VH-associated CTCF sites allows loop extrusion extending beyond VH5-2 (the first CTCF site-associated VH), resulting in increased usage of multiple proximal VH segments. Second, disrupted CTCF-dependent cohesin stabilization: this impairs long-range VH interactions, thereby concentrating VH recombination activity within the proximal VH region. Taken together, these data strongly suggest that cohesin-STAG1’s ability to mediate extended loop extrusion and promote distal VH usage in v-Abl pro-B cells is fully dependent on the CTCF-YDF motif-mediated interaction.

To determine whether the effects of the CTCF(Y226A/F228A) mutation are specific to the enhanced extrusion activity observed in Stag2-/- cells or also extend to baseline activity in WT cells, we further analyzed a Ctcf-Nm single-mutant line43. Similar to the double mutant, Ctcf-Nm cells displayed substantially reduced cohesin (SMC3) association at the IgH locus (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Notably, the already limited long-range RC-VH interactions observed in WT cells were further diminished in the mutant, accompanied by reduced RC contacts with the IGCR1-CBE2 region and increased RC interactions with proximal VH segments (Supplementary Fig. 10b–e). Consistent with these changes, HTGTS-V(D)J-Seq revealed a further reduction in long-range VH usage in Ctcf-Nm cells, coupled with elevated proximal VH recombination (Supplementary Fig. 11a–c). Importantly, these effects were not attributable to loss of CTCF binding itself, as ChIP-Seq showed largely comparable CTCF occupancy at IgH locus in WT and mutant cells. Moreover, consensus motif analysis revealed no obvious differences in the DNA sequence composition of CTCF-WT versus CTCF(Y226A/F228A) binding sites. These analyses suggest that the retained CTCF is largely non-functional (Supplementary Fig. 11d, e). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the CTCF N-terminal YDF motif is essential not only for the enhanced cohesin-STAG1-mediated extrusion activity in Stag2-/- cells, but also for the basal level of long-range loop extrusion in WT cells carrying both cohesin variants. This underscores a general requirement for the CTCF N-terminus in promoting cohesin processivity, with potential variant-specific differences in strength.

Cohesin-STAG1-driven VH repertoire differs from the VH repertoire promoted by WAPL depletion

WAPL-depletion reduces cohesin release and has been shown to broadly enhance long-range VH utilization in v-Abl pro-B cells, restoring VH recombination pattern similar to that observed in primary pro-B cells41, modeling VH-to-DJH recombination step. To understand how this effect compares with that of cohesin-STAG1, we directly compared the VH usage patterns in Stag2-/- cells and WAPL-depleted cells. To do this, we employed a Wapl-AID2 line43 derived from the OsTIR1(F74G)-Rosa26 line and performed HTGTS-V(D)J-Seq analysis on RAG1-complemented, G1-arrested cells untreated or treated with 1 μM 5-Ph-IAA.

The untreated cells displayed a VH recombination pattern similar to wild-type v-Abl pro-B cells, with rearrangements mostly restricted to the very proximal VH region. Upon WAPL depletion, as expected, there was a substantial increase in the VDJH: DJH ratio and the recombination of middle and distal VH segments (Fig. 5a-I, b, c). Comparison of WAPL depletion and cohesin-STAG1-mediated VH usage revealed both similarities and differences. While both conditions significantly enhanced long-range VH-to-DJH recombination, cohesin-STAG1 preferentially promoted recombination of more proximal VH segments (Supplementary Fig. 12a–c). A notable feature of these VHs is the presence of closely associated CTCF-binding sites (within 100 bp of VH-RSS) oriented with their N-termini facing the RC (Supplementary Figs. 13a). In this region, 14 of 15 VHs flanked by an immediately adjacent CTCF site showed greater usage in Stag2-/- cells than in WAPL-depleted cells, whereas all 4 VHs lacking nearby CTCF sites showed little difference between the two conditions (Supplementary Fig. 13a). This effect diminished with increasing distance, as Stag2-/- cells exhibited less usage of far-middle and distal VHs compared to WAPL-depleted cells regardless of CTCF site association (Supplementary Fig. 13b, c). These findings support the model that cohesin-STAG1-driven loop extrusion tends to pause at CTCF sites, likely reflecting its strong affinity for CTCF47,48, thereby favoring recombination at CTCF-flanked VHs. Moreover, WAPL depletion appears to enhance cohesin processivity more effectively than STAG2 loss, allowing loop extrusion to bypass CTCF barriers and enabling broader VH utilization.

Long-range VH recombination in WAPL-deficient cells is independent of the CTCF-YDF motif and requires cooperative support from cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2.

Long-range VH recombination in WAPL-deficient cells is independent of the CTCF-YDF motif

We next asked whether the long-range VH usage in WAPL-depleted and Stag2-/- cells is differentially affected by the CTCF N-terminal mutation. To address this, we employed a Wapl-AID2 Ctcf-Nm line, which carries homozygous CTCF(Y226A/F228A) mutation in the Wapl-AID2 background43. In the absence of 5-Ph-IAA treatment, these cells showed markedly elevated recombination activity concentrated in the proximal VH region, closely mirroring the Ctcf-Nm line and consistent with a role of the CTCF-YDF motif in maintaining IGCR1 integrity (Fig. 5a-II, b, c, Supplementary Fig. 11a–c).

Interestingly, upon WAPL depletion, the Wapl-AID2 Ctcf-Nm line further underwent robust recombination of middle and distal VHs, resembling the distal VH usage pattern of the Wapl-AID2 line alone, indicating that the CTCF(Y226A/F228A) mutation does not impair WAPL depletion-driven long-range VH usage (Fig. 5a-II, b, c). This result stands in sharp contrast to the effect of introducing the same mutation into the Stag2-/- background, which abolished distal VH recombination almost entirely (Fig. 4c–e). This striking difference strongly supports a model in which the CTCF N-terminus promotes cohesin-STAG1 processivity specifically by protecting it from WAPL-mediated release.

Cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2 act cooperatively to drive long-range VH recombination in WAPL-deficient cells

To gain insight into the respective contributions of cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2 to physiological VH-to-DJH joining step, we further examined whether the two variants differentially influence V(D)JH recombination under WAPL-suppressed condition, a key developmental regulation in pro-B cells that ensures both a diverse VH repertoire and proper developmental progression40,41. To this end, we generated Wapl-AID2 Stag1-/-and Wapl-AID2 Stag2-/- double mutant lines. Interestingly, both lines displayed a similar intermediate phenotype: the VDJH: DJH ratio fell between that of untreated Wapl-AID2 controls and the treated single Wapl-AID2 mutant (Fig. 5b). Moreover, both mutants showed increased usage of middle and distal VH segments compared with the untreated Wapl-AID2 line, though to a lesser degree than the treated single Wapl-AID2 mutant (Fig. 5a-III, 5c). These findings suggest that cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2 act cooperatively to fully drive long-range loop extrusion in the context of WAPL depletion. Furthermore, these results support the idea that the distinct processivity of the two cohesin variants is primarily determined by their differential sensitivity to WAPL.

Discussion

Cohesin-mediated loop extrusion plays a central role in V(D)J recombination by facilitating synapsis between distant RSSs across the IgH locus. While both cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2 are expressed in somatic cells, their respective contributions to IgH recombination have remained unclear. In this study, we examined their roles under two physiologically relevant conditions: WAPL-proficient v-Abl pro-B cells, which mimic the D-to-JH recombination stage, and WAPL-suppressed v-Abl pro-B cells, which model the VH-to-DJH recombination stage. Our findings reveal that cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2 perform distinct and context-dependent roles in shaping IgH recombination. In WAPL-proficient cells, STAG2 is required to maintain balanced D-to-JH recombination, while its suppression of STAG1 prevents premature long-range loop extrusion, thereby restricting recombination activity to developmentally regulated domains. By contrast, under WAPL-suppressed conditions, the two cohesin variants act additively and appear functionally similar, together supporting assembly of a complete VH repertoire.

In WAPL proficient cells, STAG2 loss increases cohesin-STAG1 protein level, strengthens its chromatin binding and redistributes cohesin-STAG1 toward distal IgH regions, collectively contributing to the enhanced long-range RC interactions and distal VH recombination (Supplementary Fig. 4, 6, Figs. 1, 2). This extended activity critically depends on the CTCF N-terminal YDF motif, which protects cohesin-STAG1 from WAPL-mediated release. The CTCF(Y226A/F228A) mutation abolishes these effects entirely, highlighting the central role of the CTCF N-terminus-cohesin interaction in regulating cohesin-STAG1 processivity (Fig. 4).

Comparative analysis further revealed that the long-range VH usage mediated by cohesin-STAG1 differs from that induced by WAPL-depletion (Supplementary Figs. 12, 13). Cohesin-STAG1 preferentially drives recombination of CTCF-associated VH segments, this is consistent with the reported strong interaction of cohesin-STAG1 with CTCF47,48, which may better shield cohesin-STAG1 from WAPL-release and therefore promote a greater processivity compared to cohesin-STAG2. On the other hand, WAPL-depletion produces a more evenly distributed VH repertoire, likely by enhancing cohesin processivity to a greater degree that allows extrusion to bypass CTCF barriers. Supporting this, WAPL depletion in Stag2-/- cells largely abolished the bias toward CTCF-flanked VH segments.

The extended VH recombination in STAG2-deficient cells occurs at the expense of RC-distal DH utilization, correlating with reduced short-loop formation between the RC and IGCR1-CBE2 region (Supplementary Fig. 1b, 3c, d). These findings emphasize the role of the more dynamic cohesin-STAG2 in promoting proximal synapsis events to ensure a diverse DJH repertoire. By contrast, WT and STAG1-deficient cells show nearly identical DJH recombination patterns, indicating that cohesin-STAG1 is largely dispensable at this stage. Moreover, premature VH access driven by cohesin-STAG1 may disrupt the developmental order of recombination, in which D-to-JH joining normally precedes VH-to-DJH joining3. Such premature activity may interfere with the proper establishment of allelic exclusion, a process that requires ordered and regulated recombination to ensure that only one productive IgH allele is expressed64,65, leading to impaired B cell development. Thus, a fine-tuned balance between cohesin-STAG1 and cohesin-STAG2 may be critical to preserve both repertoire diversity and developmental order.

In WAPL-suppressed cells, however, the two cohesin variants function more similarly. Loss of either STAG1 or STAG2 partially reduces VH usage under these conditions, indicating additive contributions to extended loop extrusion when cohesin residence time is prolonged (Fig. 5a-III). These findings demonstrate that WAPL levels dictate whether cohesin variants perform specialized or overlapping roles in IgH recombination.

Together, our study demonstrates that modulation of cohesin variants significantly influences both the D-to-JH and VH-to-DJH recombination during IgH rearrangement, with effects that depend on WAPL levels. We further highlight the critical role of the CTCF N-terminal region in maintaining cohesin processivity, thereby supporting long-range V(D)J recombination in cells with normal WAPL expression. This mechanism may extend to other antigen receptor loci43 and more broadly to processes involving long-range gene regulation and DNA repair. While our study focused on cohesin-STAG1, similar principles may apply to cohesin-STAG2, as the CTCF YDF motif interacts with both variants17,22 and CTCF depletion was also reported to reduce cohesin-STAG2 association with chromatin, albeit to a lesser extent47. Finally, although ESCO1 has been reported to stabilize G1-phase cohesin-STAG1 in other context47, we found that ESCO1 depletion does not affect cohesin-STAG1 promoting long-range V(D)JH recombination in our system. This suggests that ESCO1’s contribution to cohesin-STAG1 activity is context-dependent, underscoring the need for future studies to define additional mechanisms that enhance cohesin-STAG1 processivity in different contexts.

Methods

Cell lines

The control RAG1 deficient, Eμ-Bcl2 + v-Abl pro-B cell line4 was kindly provided by Dr. David Schatz. This cell line was derived from male mice; sex was not considered as a biological variable in this study. The control and the derivative v-Abl pro-B cells were cultured in RPMI1640 medium (Corning, 10-040-CV) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Bio-techne, S11150H), 50 μM 2-Mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, M3148), 100 U/ml Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), 2 mM L-Glutamine (Gibco, 25030081) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

All mutant derivatives were generated with the aid of CRISPR/Cas9. Cas9-gRNA ribonucleoproteins were assembled and introduced into cells via nucleofection using the Lonza 4D-Nucleofector system (SF solution, DN100 program). Specifically, the Stag1-/- and Stag2-/- strains were generated through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of Stag1-exon7 and Stag2-exon10, respectively. Cells were subcloned by dilution one day post nucleofection. Potential clones were screened for homozygous mutations using PCR and further confirmed through Western blot analysis.

To generate cells with STAG1-overexpression, Stag1 cDNA was PCR-amplified from pKS021 (Addgene, 156440) and cloned downstream of either the 212-bp core EF1α promoter or the 1,179-bp full-length EF1α promoter in a piggyBac vector backbone. The piggyBac backbone was derived from a published vector (Addgene, 183411) by AsiSI (New England Biolabs, R0630L) and EcoRI (New England Biolabs, R3101L) digestion and assembled with the Stag1 cDNA and EF1α promoter using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Kit (New England Biolabs, E2621L). The resulting constructs were transfected into WT v-Abl pro-B cells, selected with 400 ug/ml G418 (Gibco, 11811-031) for 7 days, and validated by PCR and western blotting. Two independent transfections were performed.

To generate the Esco1-AID2 strain, OsTIR1(F74G) was first introduced into both alleles of the Rosa26 locus43. To tag ESCO1 with miniAID-tdTomato, a targeting donor plasmid pESCO1-mAID-tdTomato was created by modifying plasmid pMK29366 (Addgene, 72831). This modification included adding homology arms flanking the ESCOI stop codon and replacing mCherry with tdTomato. The plasmid was then provided along with Cas9-gRNA ribonucleoproteins during nucleofection. tdTomato-positive cells were subcloned one day post nucleofection via fluorescence-activated cell sorting and validated through PCR and flow cytometry analysis. The WAPL-AID2 strain was generated and validated using the same strategy43. To generate the Esco1-AID2 Stag2-/- line, Stag2 exon 10 was deleted in the Esco1-AID2 background. Similarly, Stag1 exon 7 and Stag2 exon 10 were deleted in the WAPL-AID2 strain to generate WAPL-AID2 Stag1-/- and WAPL-AID2 Stag2-/- lines, respectively. All Stag deletions were validated by PCR and western blot analysis.

To generate the Ctcf-Nm43, Stag2-/- Ctcf-Nm and Wapl-AID2 Ctcf-Nm43 lines, the CTCF(Y226A/F228A) mutation was introduced to the WT, Stag2-/- and Wapl-AID2 backgrounds through CRISPR/Cas9 targeting combined with a short single-stranded DNA oligonucleotide (ssODN) template that contain the desired mutation. Cells were subcloned by dilution one day post nucleofection. Potential clones were screened for homozygous mutation by PCR and confirmed by sequencing. The oligos and primers used in this study were synthesized by Integrated DNA technologies and the sequence information can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Cultured v-Abl pro-B cells were pelleted, washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1X protease inhibitor cocktail (ThermoFisher Scientific, A32965)) to obtain whole-cell lysates. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 18,800 X g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred to fresh tubes and quantified using Pierce Rapid Gold BCA protein assay kit (Thermofisher Scientific, A53225). Equal amounts of protein per sample was mixed with 2X Laemmli sample buffer (20% glycerol, 4% SDS, 125 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8–6.9, 0.02% bromophenol blue, 10% v/v β-mercaptoethanol), heated at 95 °C for 5 min, briefly centrifuged and resolved in 7.5% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresed in 1× SDS running buffer (0.025 M Tris base, 0.192 M Clycine, 0.1% SDS) at 50 V for 30 min followed by 70 V until the loading dye front reached the bottom. Precision Plus Protein All Blue Standards (Bio-Rad, 1610373) were used as molecular weight markers.

Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, 1620177) using wet transfer in ice-cold transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, 20% methanol) at 100 V for 1 h. PVDF membranes were pre-activated in methanol for 1 min. Membranes were blocked in 3% BSA in TBS-T (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20) overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies were diluted in TBS-T containing 3% BSA and 0.01% sodium azide and incubated with membranes for 2 h at room temperature (RT). Anti-STAG1 (Fortis Life Sciences, A302-579A), anti-STAG2 (Fortis Life Sciences, A302-580A) and anti-β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, 3700 T) antibodies were all diluted at 1:1000 (antibody information is also provided in Supplementary Table 2). Membranes were washed three times (5 min each) in TBS-T and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in TBS-T with 3% BSA for 2 hours at RT. The Goat anti-Rabbit (Invitrogen, A27036) and Sheep anti-Mouse (Cytiva, NA931V) secondary antibodies were both diluted at 1:10,000. After three additional washes, signals were developed using ECL substrate (Clarity (Bio-Rad, 1705061) or Clarity Max (Bio-Rad, 1705062)) for 5 min at RT and imaged using an Amersham Imager 600 or Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imager.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry analysis was performed on parental Rosa26-OsTIR1 v-Abl pro-B cells and degron-line cells, either untreated or treated with 1 µM 5-Ph-IAA (Bio Academia, 30-003). Samples were collected at baseline (day 0), 4 h after 5-Ph-IAA treatment, and after 4 days of treatment with 3 µM STI-571 (Sigma-Aldrich, SML1027) in the continuous presence of 5-Ph-IAA. Cells were pelleted, washed once with PBS, and resuspended in PBS containing 2.5% FBS. tdTomato fluorescence was measured using an Attune CytPix flow cytometer, and data were analyzed with FlowJo v10.10. Cells were sequentially gated for live and single-cell populations, and tdTomato expression was analyzed using both histogram and dot plots to assess fluorescence shifts across treatments and timepoints.

HTGTS-V(D)J-seq

To prepare for HTGTS-V(D)J-seq37 libraries, the RAG1 deficient control and mutant v-Abl pro-B cells were first complemented with WT RAG1 through retroviral introduction of the pMSCV-RAG1-IRES-Bsr plasmid (kindly provided by Dr. David Schatz). After infection, cells were selected with 50 µg/ml blasticidin (Gibco, A1113903) for 6 days to enrich for those with viral integration. The cells were then arrested with 3 µM STI-571 for 4 days followed by genomic DNA extraction. For cell lines with the degron system, 1 µM 5-Ph-IAA was added 4 h before and maintained throughout G1 arrest. Protein degradation was confirmed by flow cytometry analysis.

To generate HTGTS-V(D)J-Seq libraries, 4 µg of genomic DNA per sample was fragmented by sonication (low-energy input, 20 s on, 60 s off, 2 cycles) using a Diagenode Bioruptor Pico. Fragmented DNA was linearly amplified with 1 µM biotinylated JH1 or JH4 bait primers. The resulting single-stranded amplification products were captured using Dynabeads MyOne C1 streptavidin beads (Invitrogen 65002) and ligated to a bridge adapter. Nested PCR was then performed using index-containing nested JH1 or JH4 primers together with primers annealing to the bridge adapter. PCR products were purified, further amplified to incorporate Illumina sequencing adapters, size-selected (400-750 bp) by gel extraction, and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform using 300 bp paired-end reads or NovaSeq X Plus platform using 150 bp paired-end reads.

Sequencing reads were aligned to the mm9 genome and processed via a published pipeline67 (http://robinmeyers.github.io/transloc_pipeline/) with the following filters applied: f.unaligned=1, f.baitonly=1, f.uncut=G10, f.misprimed=L10, f.largegap=G30, f.mapqual=1, f.sequential=1. In summary, a best-path search approach was used to identify the optimal sequence of alignments representing each read. Reads were retained only if they contained both a bait and a prey alignment, and if the bait segment did not extend more than 10 nucleotides past the defined RAG cleavage site. For quality control, all discarded alignments were evaluated against the chosen prey alignment; reads were removed if any discarded segment exceeded preset coverage and scoring thresholds, indicating poor mapping quality. To further eliminate mispriming and similar technical artifacts, the bait alignment was required to extend at least 10 nucleotides beyond the primer sequence. Additional post-processing filters were applied to exclude junctions prone to background noise, including those with insert sequence (extra nucleotides between the end of the bait and the start of the prey) sizes greater than 30 nucleotides or junctions occurring downstream of the initial bait-prey junction within the same read. Furthermore, PCR duplicates were removed by retaining only a single read per duplicate group. Because V(D)JH junction reads often contain inserted sequences generated during end processing in V(D)J recombination, PCR duplicates were defined as reads with identical bait and prey coordinates as well as identical insert sequences.

To analyze V(D)JH joining pattern, libraries were normalized to 6,278 total IgH junctions (the smallest library) and plotted using GraphPad Prism 9. Usage counts of individual VH in VDJH joins and DH in DJH joins were obtained by summing all junctions mapped within ± 50 bp of the RAG cleavage site for each specific VH or DH-RSS. To compare the relative usage of DJH junctions versus VDJH junctions, their frequencies were calculated as a percentage of the total number of DJH + VDJH junctions. To assess the efficiency of long-range VDJH recombination, we analyzed the relative usage of proximal, middle, and distal VH segments, with the frequency of each expressed as a percentage of total VDJH junctions. To analyze DH usage in VDJH join, we used a published VDJ annotation pipeline (https://github.com/Yyx2626/VDJ_annotation/)68.

3C-HTGTS

3C-HTGTS libraries29 were prepared from RAG1-deficient v-Abl pro-B cells that were G1 arrested for 4 days. 10 million cells were pelleted, resuspended in fresh medium and crosslinked with 2% formaldehyde at RT for 10 min with rotation. Crosslinking was quenched by 125 mM glycine, and cells were pelleted, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and lysed on ice for 10 min in cell lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, 1% Triton X-100, 1X Protease inhibitor cocktail) to isolate nuclei. Nuclei were pelleted and resuspended in 1.2X rCutSmart Buffer (New England Biolabs, B6004SVIAL), then sequentially treated with 0.3% SDS at 37 °C for 1 h and 1.8% Triton X-100 at 37 °C for 1 h with shaking (900 rpm). Chromatin were digested overnight with 700 U of NlaIII (New England Biolabs, R0125L) with shaking (900 rpm). After digestion, nuclei were treated with 1.6% SDS at 65 °C for 25 min, supplemented with 6.125 ml of 1.15X T4 DNA Ligation buffer (New England Biolabs, B0202S), and incubated with 1% Triton X-100 at 37 °C for 1 h with gentle shaking (200 rpm). Ligation was then performed overnight at 16 °C with 100 U T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs, M0202L). Ligation products were de-crosslinked at 65 °C overnight with proteinase K (Invitrogen, 25530-031), treated with RNase A (Invitrogen, 12091021) at 37 °C for 1 h, and purified by phenol/chloroform extraction. For phenol/chloroform extraction, samples were mixed with an equal volume of phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1), centrifuged, and the aqueous phase was recovered and re-extracted twice with chloroform. DNA was precipitated with sodium acetate (pH 5.2, 300 mM) and ice-cold ethanol, pelleted by centrifugation, washed multiple times with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 1 X Tris-EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA).

3C-HTGTS Libraries were then constructed as described for HTGTS-V(D)J-Seq, using the IgH iEμ locale as the bait, and sequenced via Illumina paired-end 300 bp or 150 bp sequencing. Because the Bcl2 transgene expression cassette present in the v-Abl pro-B cell lines contains the Eμ enhancer to promote the transgene expression69, we designed the bait primer (CCCTCTGATTATTGGTCTCCATTC) to lie upstream of the 680-bp EcoRI/XbaI fragment used to clone Eμ into the Eμ-Bcl2 transgene70. Following NlaIII digestion in the 3C-HTGTS assay, this bait primer allows selective capturing interactions at the endogenous iEµ/RC region without potential contributions from the Eµ-Bcl2 transgene. Sequencing reads were aligned to the mm9 genome, processed via a published pipeline67 (http://robinmeyers.github.io/transloc_pipeline/) with the following filters applied: f.unaligned=1, f.baitonly=1, f.uncut=G10, f.misprimed=L10, f.largegap=G30, f.mapqual=1, f.sequential=1, and deduplicated by keeping 1 read among reads with the same chromosomal coordinates. To avoid potential PCR artifacts, reads aligned to telomeric and satellite repeat regions were further filtered. Additionally, reads from the iEμ bait region (chr12:114665286-114667223) were removed to reduce the influence of self-ligation on quantitative analysis. The libraries were then size-normalized to the total junctions of the smallest library (90,000 interaction junctions) and plotted using the Integrative Genomics Viewer 2.6.2. For comparative analysis of relative iEμ/RC interactions with the indicated regions in control and mutant cells, the interaction frequency in control samples was set to 100%, and the corresponding values in mutant samples were expressed as percentages relative to the control.

ChIP-Seq

IP and Input DNA for ChIP-Seq were prepared from G1-arrested, RAG1-deficient v-Abl pro-B cells37. 20 million cells were pelleted, resuspended in pre-warmed medium, and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde at RT for 10 min with rotation. Crosslinking was quenched with 125 mM glycine at RT for 5 min, and cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS. Cells were sequentially lysed in 1 ml cell lysis buffer (5 mM PIPES pH 8, 85 mM KCl, 0.5% NP-40) on ice for 10 min, followed by 150 ul nuclei lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.1, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) at RT for 10 min to release chromatin. Chromatin was diluted with 850 ul dilution buffer (16.7 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.1, 0.01% SDS, 1.1% Triton X-100, 1.2 mM EDTA, 167 mM NaCl) and fragmented by sonication using a Diagenode Bioruptor Pico (high energy output, 30 sec on/30 sec off, 20 cycles) at 4 °C. Sonicated lysates were pelleted, and supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Chromatin was pre-cleared with 20 ul Protein A Dynabeads (Invitrogen, 10001D) for 2 h at 4 °C with rotation. A 50-ul aliquot was reserved as input. The remaining chromatin was incubated with appropriate ChIP antibody overnight at 4 °C with rotation. The following antibodies were used at 2.5 ug per sample in the IP experiments: anti-STAG1 antibody (Fortis Life Sciences, A302-579A), anti-STAG2 antibody (Fortis Life Sciences, A302-580A), anti-SMC3 antibody (Fortis Life Sciences, A300-060A) and anti-CTCF antibody (Milipore Sigma, 07-729).

Immune complexes was captured with 40 ul Dynabeads Protein A for 3 h at 4 °C with rotation and washed sequentially for 5 min each at 4 °C with chilled dilution buffer, low salt wash buffer (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.1), high salt wash buffer (0.1% SDS, 1% Trion X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH8.1), and LiCl wash buffer (0.25 M LiCl, 1% NP40, 1% NaDOC, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.1). Beads were briefly rinsed in 1X TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA), and eluted in 145 ul Elution buffer (100 mM NaHCO3 + 1% SDS). Samples were vortexed vigorously and incubated at 65 °C with shaking (800 rpm) for 25 min to release ChIP DNA. Both IP and input DNA were de-crosslinked at 65 °C overnight and purified using the MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, 28004). ChIP-Seq libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, E7645S) and sequenced with Illumina paired-end 150 bp reads. Sequencing reads were aligned to the mm9 genome using Bowtie271, processed using SAMtools72 v1.8, deduplicated using Picard (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) and normalized with bamCoverage using the --CPM flag. For genome-wide peak analysis, reference peak annotation was generated by calling peaks with MACS273 v2.11 on the control strain libraries. Heatplots were created using deepTools274, with outliers (defined as values exceeding Q3 + 50xIQR, where Q3 is the 75th percentile and IQR is the interquartile range) excluded from the analysis. To identify CTCF sites, CTCF binding peaks were first identified from WT CTCF ChIP-Seq data using the MACS2 narrowPeak calling algorithm and union peaks from two replicates were kept. CTCF motifs within each peak were then mapped using the 19-bp core motif (MA0139.1) from the JASPAR vertebrate database. Motif logos were generated using WebLogo75. To analyze the relationship between STAG occupancy and proximity to CTCF binding sites across the IgH locus, regions spanning −1 kb to +1 kb around IgH CTCF sites were extracted from WT STAG1 and STAG2 ChIP-Seq datasets and divided into 20-bp bins. Read counts per bin were calculated, normalized to reads per million (RPM) and plotted.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via unpaired, two-sided Welch t test using GraphPad Prism 9. P values are shown as exact values. No multiple comparison correction was included.
