High awareness, moderate knowledge, and prevention-oriented practices regarding β-thalassemia among young adults in Palestine: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Omar Z Harhash, Abdullah J Abu-Kamel, Mayar I Hijazi, Ayman Awwad, Seif Abu-Amar, Jude M Kawamleh, Bisan M Bannoura, Salma A Mahmoud, Tariq Sanad, Lamar N Adem, Alhareth M Amro, Salahaldeen Deeb

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundβ-Thalassemia is an inherited hemoglobin disorder caused by reduced or absent synthesis of the β-globin chain, leading to variable clinical severity and substantial lifelong health burden.Because prevention depends heavily on awareness, screening, and informed reproductive decision-making, understanding public knowledge, attitudes, and practices is essential for designing effective control strategies.MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2025 and January 2026 using a structured, self-administered Arabic questionnaire distributed online through Google Forms. The survey was adapted from a previously validated thalassemia KAP instrument and disseminated using snowball sampling through social media platforms. The minimum required sample size was 358, calculated using a single-proportion formula with an expected awareness proportion of 36.8% from a previous young-adult β-thalassemia KAP study, 95% confidence level, and 5% margin of error. A total of 424 Palestinian participants were included in the final analysis. Participants who had never heard of thalassemia were classified as unaware and excluded from domain-based KAP scoring analyses. Knowledge, attitude, and practice domains were scored using Bloom’s 60% cut-off criterion. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, t-tests, ANOVA, correlation analyses, and multivariable logistic regression were used for data analysis.ResultsOverall, 87.0% of participants had heard of thalassemia. Previous screening uptake was limited, but endorsement of premarital testing, prenatal diagnosis, and knowledge sharing was high. Knowledge score was positively correlated with practice score and independently predicted positive practice (aOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.15–1.57, p < 0.001). Female sex, increasing age, and higher education were independently associated with adequate knowledge.ConclusionsAlthough awareness of beta thalassemia among Palestinian young adults was high, important gaps remained in functionally relevant knowledge and in the translation of preventive intentions into actual screening behavior. The independent association between knowledge and positive practice suggests that strengthening practical genetic literacy may be a key pathway for improving prevention-oriented behavior. Introduction

Beta thalassemia is among the most common inherited hemoglobin disorders worldwide and continues to represent a substantial clinical and public health challenge, particularly in regions spanning the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East, and parts of Asia [1]. β-Thalassemia is an autosomal recessive hemoglobin disorder caused by reduced or absent synthesis of the β-globin chain. Clinically significant forms include β-thalassemia major and intermedia, while carrier states may be asymptomatic but remain important for reproductive risk [1]. Severe disease can require lifelong transfusion, iron-chelation therapy, and continuous medical follow-up, creating long-term physical, psychosocial, and economic burden for patients, families, and healthcare systems. Worldwide, approximately 1.5% of people are estimated to be β-thalassemia carriers, with higher frequency in the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East, and parts of Asia [2].

Although advances in supportive care have improved survival, the disease remains associated with considerable lifelong physical, psychosocial, and economic burden for affected individuals, their families, and healthcare systems [2].

In severe forms, management commonly requires sustained medical follow-up, repeated transfusion support, and long-term monitoring, underscoring the importance of preventive strategies over treatment-centered approaches alone. In this context, awareness, carrier identification, genetic counselling, and timely reproductive decision-making remain central to reducing the population burden of beta thalassemia [3].

The effectiveness of prevention programs, however, depends not only on the availability of screening services but also on the public’s knowledge of the disease, its inheritance pattern, its preventability, and the social meanings attached to carrier status and marriage [4].

Misconceptions, stigma, and limited understanding of genetic risk may undermine the uptake of screening and weaken the impact of prevention policies, even in settings where awareness initiatives exist [5]. Accordingly, assessment of knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) has become an essential public health tool for identifying educational gaps, social barriers, and behavioral readiness for prevention. Prior KAP research has shown that awareness alone is insufficient, and that young adult’s merit particular attention because this stage of life is closely linked to partner selection, premarital planning, and future reproductive choices [6].

In KAP research, knowledge refers to factual understanding of a health condition, attitudes refer to beliefs, perceptions, stigma, and acceptance, and practices refer to actual or intended behaviors related to prevention or care. In the context of β-thalassemia, knowledge includes understanding inheritance, carrier status, and screening; attitudes include stigma and willingness to accept affected individuals or carriers; and practices include premarital testing, prenatal diagnosis, genetic counseling, and knowledge sharing. Therefore, assessing KAP can identify not only whether people have heard of β-thalassemia, but also whether they possess the practical understanding and behavioral readiness needed for prevention [7].

Despite the relevance of beta thalassemia to populations in the Middle East, evidence on public KAP toward the disorder among young adults in Palestine remains limited. This knowledge gap is important because context-specific sociocultural beliefs, educational exposure, and perceptions regarding consanguinity, premarital screening, and prenatal decision-making may all shape preventive behavior. This awareness–behavior gap is also relevant in Palestine. For example, recent evidence from the southern West Bank showed that antibiotic self-medication was highly prevalent among adults and appeared to be influenced by structural and economic barriers as well as knowledge, demonstrating that health-related awareness may not automatically translate into optimal practice [8]. This supports the need to examine whether thalassemia awareness among young adults is accompanied by functional knowledge and preventive behavior. In Palestine, the implementation of prevention programs may also be affected by contextual constraints, including political instability, movement restrictions, checkpoint-related delays, violence exposure, infrastructure damage, and limited healthcare resources. These conditions can reduce access to routine, non-urgent preventive services, including premarital counseling and screening, and should be considered when interpreting gaps between willingness and completed screening behavior. Therefore, this study aimed to assess awareness and knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding β-thalassemia among young adults in Palestine. The specific objectives were: (1) to describe participant characteristics and awareness by sex; (2) to estimate knowledge, attitude, practice, and composite KAP scores and classifications; (3) to examine sociodemographic differences in KAP classifications; (4) to evaluate correlations among KAP domains; and (5) to identify independent predictors of adequate knowledge and positive practice.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This analytical cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2025 and January 2026 to assess awareness and knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding beta thalassemia among young adults in Palestine. Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire disseminated electronically via Google Forms. Snowball sampling was chosen because no complete sampling frame of eligible Palestinian young adults was available and because online recruitment enabled rapid access to geographically dispersed participants during a period when mobility and security constraints could limit face-to-face recruitment. This approach was appropriate for exploratory KAP assessment but may have increased selection bias toward digitally connected, more educated, and socially networked participants. The survey link was distributed using a snowball sampling approach through commonly used social media platforms in Palestine, including WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram, to facilitate broad geographic reach and efficient recruitment within the target age group. Given the web-based and non-probability sampling design, the study may have been subject to selection bias, including preferential participation of younger, more digitally literate, and more highly educated individuals, as well as underrepresentation of persons with limited internet access or literacy.

Participants

Eligible participants were Palestinian residents aged 18 years or older who were able to read and understand Arabic and who provided informed electronic consent before participation.

Individuals residing outside Palestine were excluded. Eligibility was verified using screening questions at the beginning of the questionnaire, including age, Palestinian residency, ability to read Arabic, consent to participate, and whether the respondent was a healthcare professional or enrolled in a health-related discipline. Respondents who did not meet eligibility criteria were excluded before analysis. To minimize professional knowledge bias and better reflect awareness levels in the general population, healthcare professionals and students enrolled in health-related disciplines, including medicine, pharmacy, nursing, midwifery, and laboratory sciences, were excluded from participation.

Sample size

The minimum required sample size was calculated using the single-proportion formula: n = Z²p(1 − p)/d². The expected awareness proportion was set at 36.8%, based on the young-adult β-thalassemia KAP study from Delhi-NCR, India. Using a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and p = 0.368, the minimum required sample size was 358 participants. Recruitment continued beyond this minimum to compensate for non-consent or incomplete records and to improve precision for subgroup analyses. A total of 437 records were identified; 424 consenting and complete responses were retained for the final analysis.

Questionnaire development and validation

The questionnaire was adapted from the previously validated instrument developed by Choudhury et al. in their cross-sectional study of knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding beta thalassemia among young adults in Delhi-NCR, India (Choudhury et al., 2025) [9]. The original instrument was developed by Choudhury et al. in a cross-sectional study among young adults in Delhi-NCR, India. For the Palestinian context, wording was culturally and linguistically adapted while preserving the original domain structure. India-specific wording was replaced with Palestine-relevant wording where needed, and items related to premarital screening, prenatal diagnosis, consanguinity, and public prevention measures were reviewed for local relevance. No item was changed in a way that altered the conceptual meaning of the knowledge, attitude, or practice domains.

Study measures and scoring

Awareness was assessed by asking participants whether they had previously heard of thalassemia. Respondents who reported never having heard of thalassemia were classified as unaware and excluded from subsequent domain-based KAP scoring analyses. Knowledge, attitude, and practice domains were scored using Bloom’s 60% cut-off criterion. Knowledge items assessed understanding of thalassemia inheritance, transmission, prevention, and screening; correct responses were assigned one point and incorrect or “don’t know” responses were assigned zero points. Attitude items evaluated stigma, misconceptions, and social distance, with scientifically accurate and non-stigmatizing responses coded as positive. Practice items captured preventive behaviors and intentions, including premarital screening, prenatal diagnosis, genetic testing, and attitudes toward consanguineous marriage; positive preventive responses were scored as one, while negative, uncertain, or “maybe” responses were scored as zero. Higher scores indicated more favorable KAP profiles, and participants were classified into adequate versus inadequate knowledge and positive versus negative attitude and practice categories according to the predefined threshold.

The knowledge domain included 9 items, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 9. The attitude domain included 10 items, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 10. The practice domain included 8 items, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 8. The total composite KAP score therefore ranged from 0 to 27. Correct knowledge responses were scored as 1, whereas incorrect and “don’t know” responses were scored as 0. For attitude items, scientifically accurate and non-stigmatizing responses were scored as 1, while stigmatizing, incorrect, uncertain, or “don’t know” responses were scored as 0. For practice items, positive preventive responses were scored as 1, while negative, uncertain, “maybe,” or “don’t know” responses were scored as 0. Using Bloom’s ≥ 60% criterion, adequate knowledge was defined as ≥ 6/9, positive attitude as ≥ 6/10, and positive practice as ≥ 5/8. The full questionnaire and scoring matrix are provided as Supplementary File 1.

Validity and reliability

The questionnaire was translated and culturally adapted from a previously used β-thalassemia KAP instrument. However, no formal content validity index or pilot study was conducted before the main survey. Internal consistency was assessed separately for each KAP domain. The reliability coefficients were 0.64 for knowledge, 0.54 for attitude, and 0.56 for practice, indicating modest internal consistency, which may reflect the multidimensional nature of KAP domains. The absence of formal local validation is acknowledged as a limitation.

Data management and statistical analysis

Survey data were exported from Google Forms into IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Data cleaning included evaluation of completeness, consistency checks, and removal of incomplete or duplicate records. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests for binary variables and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), including Welch’s ANOVA where appropriate, for variables with more than two categories. Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were applied when relevant. Associations between categorical variables were examined using chi-square tests, with effect sizes expressed as Cramér’s V. Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were computed to evaluate relationships across KAP domains.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of adequate knowledge and positive practice, with adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Variables for the multivariable logistic regression models were selected a priori based on theoretical relevance and observed bivariate associations. The adequate knowledge model included sex, age, marital status, residence type, income, and education. The positive practice model included the same sociodemographic variables, in addition to knowledge score and attitude score. Predictors were entered simultaneously. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and all VIF values were below 5, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. The highest VIF was 4.27 in the adequate knowledge model and 4.34 in the positive practice model. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, which indicated acceptable fit for both the adequate knowledge model, χ² = 9.07, df = 8, p = 0.337, and the positive practice model, χ² = 2.42, df = 8, p = 0.965.

Because the sample was not nationally representative and overrepresented females and participants from the Jerusalem district, sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, sex-post-stratified estimates were calculated using the 2025 Palestinian sex distribution. Second, the knowledge–practice correlation was repeated after excluding participants from the Jerusalem district. These analyses were used to examine the robustness of the main descriptive and correlational findings.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board/Research Ethics Committee of Al-Quds University, Palestine, approval number Ref No: 489/REC/2025. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before survey initiation. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and no personally identifying information was collected.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 437 records were identified, of which 424 consenting and complete responses were retained for analysis. The sample was predominantly female (64.9%), with a mean age of 24.85 ± 7.90 years. Most participants resided in the Jerusalem district (61.6%), followed by Bethlehem (20.3%) and Ramallah (9.9%). Sex differences were observed in age, marital status, residence type, job status, region, and awareness of thalassemia (Table 1).

Awareness of thalassemia

Overall, 369 of 424 participants (87.0%) reported that they had previously heard of thalassemia, whereas 55 participants (13.0%) were classified as unaware and excluded from KAP domain scoring. Awareness differed substantially by sex, being higher among females than males (93.8% vs. 74.5%, χ²(1) = 30.27, p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.27). Participants who were aware of thalassemia were significantly older than those who were unaware (25.55 ± 8.13 vs. 20.11 ± 3.60 years, p < 0.001). Awareness was also significantly associated with marital status, with nearly all married participants reporting prior awareness (99.2%) compared with 81.8% of unmarried participants. In addition, awareness varied by job status, with lower awareness observed among students relative to employed respondents (Table 1).

Knowledge outcomes

Among participants who were aware of thalassemia (n = 369), the mean knowledge score was 5.30 ± 1.95, and 170 participants (46.1%) met the predefined threshold for adequate knowledge, whereas 199 (53.9%) were classified as having inadequate knowledge (Table 2). Item-level analysis demonstrated relatively high recognition of key hereditary concepts: most respondents correctly identified thalassemia as a hereditary disorder (88.3%), recognized that an apparently healthy individual may be a carrier (88.6%), and were aware that consanguineous marriage increases the burden of genetic disease (87.3%). In contrast, knowledge regarding disease treatability was lower (46.3%), as was familiarity with thalassemia major (32.5%) and thalassemia minor/carrier status (47.4%). Awareness of existing governmental prevention and control measures was modest (53.1%), while knowledge regarding traditional remedies was uncommon (16.5%).

Knowledge varied significantly across several sociodemographic characteristics. Females achieved higher mean knowledge scores than males (5.60 ± 1.86 vs. 4.61 ± 2.00, p < 0.001), and adequate knowledge was more frequent among females than males (51.2% vs. 34.2%, χ²(1) = 8.28, p = 0.004). Knowledge also differed by age group, with adequate knowledge increasing from 38.6% among those aged 18–20 years to 52.4% among those aged 21–29 years and 53.2% among those aged 30–40 years. Educational attainment showed a graded relationship with knowledge, such that the lowest mean knowledge scores were observed among respondents with high school or early university education, whereas the highest scores were observed among those with Master’s-level education or above.

In the multivariable model for adequate knowledge, female sex, older age, and Bachelor’s-level education were independently associated with adequate knowledge. Female participants had higher odds of adequate knowledge than male participants (aOR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.29–3.43, p = 0.003), and each additional year of age was associated with higher odds of adequate knowledge (aOR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.13, p = 0.019). In the positive practice model, knowledge score was the only independent predictor; each one-point increase in knowledge score increased the odds of positive practice by 35% (aOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.15–1.57, p < 0.001).

In sensitivity analyses, sex-post-stratified estimates remained broadly consistent with the main findings: awareness was 84.0%, adequate knowledge was 43.6%, and positive practice was 80.8%. After excluding participants from the Jerusalem district, the knowledge–practice association remained positive and statistically significant among non-Jerusalem participants (Spearman ρ = 0.243, p = 0.003).

Attitude outcomes

Among aware participants, the mean attitude score was 6.61 ± 1.94, and 272 of 369 respondents (73.7%) were classified as having a positive attitude toward beta thalassemia (Table 2).

Attitudinal responses varied by content domain. A substantial majority rejected fatalistic and blame-oriented misconceptions, including the belief that thalassemia is caused by parental sin (71.5%) or by maternal or paternal “defects” (66.1%). Similarly, most participants rejected family-level stigma, including the notion that a family with a child affected by thalassemia is unlucky or cursed (91.9%) or that death is preferable to living with the disease (91.1%).

However, acceptance decreased when attitudes were framed in terms of close interpersonal or marital proximity. Although 62.3% indicated comfort having a neighbor with thalassemia, only 52.6% reported comfort living with an affected person, and only 27.6% expressed comfort marrying into a family in which a member had thalassemia.

In contrast to knowledge, attitude did not differ significantly by sex. Mean attitude scores were comparable between females and males (6.68 ± 1.90 vs. 6.45 ± 2.03, p = 0.315), and the prevalence of positive attitude was essentially identical in both groups (73.6% vs. 73.9%). No statistically significant associations were observed between attitude classification and age group, education, income, residence type, marital status, or job status (all p ≥ 0.606), indicating relative stability of the attitudinal profile across sociodemographic strata (Tables 2 and 3).

Practice outcomes

The mean practice score among aware participants was 5.63 ± 1.54, and 305 of 369 respondents (82.7%) met criteria for positive practice (Table 2). Practice items revealed an important divergence between previous preventive behavior and future-oriented willingness. Only 30.6% reported ever having undergone testing for thalassemia. In contrast, most respondents endorsed preventive intentions: 90.2% supported genetic testing with a fiancé(e) before marriage, 83.2% supported prenatal genetic diagnosis if both partners were carriers, and 88.9% expressed willingness to share thalassemia-related knowledge with others. By comparison, willingness to terminate a pregnancy in the event of fetal thalassemia major was reported by only 28.7% of participants. With respect to consanguinity-related indicators, 44.2% reported that consanguineous marriage was practiced in their community, 77.0% regarded it as a bad practice, and 78.6% stated that they personally objected to consanguineous marriage.

Practice varied significantly by sex and selected life-course characteristics. Females had higher mean practice scores than males (5.84 ± 1.36 vs. 5.14 ± 1.80, p < 0.001), and positive practice was more common among females than males (86.4% vs. 73.9%, χ²(1) = 7.69, p = 0.006).

Married participants also demonstrated higher practice scores than unmarried participants (6.20 ± 1.52 vs. 5.33 ± 1.47, p < 0.001), although the difference in positive practice classification was of borderline statistical significance (88.2% vs. 79.8%, p = 0.059). Practice scores further differed by income and job status, with the highest scores observed among participants reporting above-average income and among employed respondents and housewives, while students had the lowest mean practice scores. In the adjusted logistic regression model, however, knowledge score emerged as the principal independent predictor of positive practice, such that each one-point increase in knowledge was associated with a 35% increase in the odds of positive practice (aOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.15–1.57, p < 0.001). Other variables, including sex, age, marital status, education, income, residence type, and attitude score, were not independently associated with positive practice after adjustment (Table 4).

Correlation analyses demonstrated selective rather than generalized relationships across KAP domains. Knowledge score was positively correlated with practice score (Spearman ρ = 0.243, p < 0.001; Pearson r = 0.301, p < 0.001), indicating that higher knowledge was associated with more favorable preventive practices. By contrast, attitude score was not significantly correlated with either knowledge (Spearman ρ = −0.084, p = 0.105) or practice (Spearman ρ = −0.088, p = 0.090). Discussion

The present study provides important insight into the knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to beta thalassemia among young adults in Palestine. Overall, awareness of thalassemia was high in this sample; however, this relatively broad familiarity was accompanied by only moderate knowledge adequacy, indicating that recognition of the condition does not necessarily translate into a sufficiently comprehensive understanding of its inheritance, clinical implications, and preventive strategies. Although attitudes were broadly favorable at the aggregate level, the attitudinal profile was not uniformly positive, with lower acceptance observed in contexts involving close social and marital relationships. Preventive practice appeared comparatively strong, yet this pattern was driven more by expressed willingness toward premarital and prenatal preventive behaviors than by prior personal screening uptake. Notably, knowledge emerged as the most consistent and meaningful correlate of favorable practice, whereas attitude showed no significant independent association with either knowledge or practice, suggesting that informational preparedness may be more influential than attitudinal disposition in shaping prevention-oriented behavior in this population.

Taken together, the findings indicate that the principal challenge in thalassemia prevention among Palestinian young adults is not simple lack of awareness, but the incomplete conversion of awareness into functionally meaningful knowledge. Although most participants had heard of thalassemia, only a more limited proportion demonstrated adequate knowledge, and item-level responses showed that understanding was stronger for broad hereditary concepts than for clinically and programmatically actionable issues such as disease categories, treatment, and prevention policies. This pattern suggests that public familiarity with the term “thalassemia” does not necessarily reflect adequate genetic and preventive health literacy. In practical terms, the findings point to a gap between recognition-level awareness and the deeper understanding required for informed premarital, reproductive, and screening-related decision-making.

The attitudinal and behavioral findings add further nuance to this interpretation. While overall attitudes appeared broadly favorable, acceptance was notably weaker in contexts involving close social proximity and marriage, suggesting that overtly stigmatizing beliefs may be less common than more socially embedded forms of discomfort related to kinship, family formation, and perceived reproductive risk. At the same time, the contrast between relatively low prior testing uptake and strong endorsement of premarital testing, prenatal diagnosis, and knowledge sharing indicates that this population may be receptive to prevention, but that such receptivity has not yet been fully translated into completed health behavior. In this context, the finding that knowledge was positively associated with practice and independently predicted positive practice, whereas attitude was not significantly associated with either knowledge or practice, is especially important. It suggests that strengthening accurate understanding of thalassemia may represent a more immediately modifiable pathway to improving prevention-oriented behavior than relying on attitudinal change alone.

These findings carry important implications for thalassemia prevention strategies in Palestine.

First, they suggest that public health efforts should move beyond general awareness campaigns and place greater emphasis on strengthening functionally relevant knowledge, particularly regarding inheritance patterns, carrier status, the distinction between major and minor forms of the disease, and the role of premarital and prenatal screening in prevention. The observation that knowledge was independently associated with positive practice indicates that educational interventions capable of improving accurate understanding may have practical value in promoting prevention-oriented behavior [10]. In this regard, thalassemia education should be more systematically integrated into youth-focused health promotion, university and community outreach initiatives, and premarital counseling frameworks, with particular attention to groups demonstrating comparatively lower knowledge and practice scores [11].

The findings also indicate that preventive programming should not be limited to information delivery alone. Although willingness toward premarital testing, prenatal diagnosis, and knowledge sharing was high, previous screening uptake remained considerably lower, suggesting that favorable intentions do not automatically translate into completed preventive action [12].

Moreover, the persistence of reduced acceptance in marriage-related and close social contexts underscores the need for culturally sensitive approaches that address stigma, family concerns, and the social meanings attached to hereditary disease [13]. Accordingly, effective thalassemia control in Palestine is likely to require a dual strategy: one component focused on improving practical genetic literacy and facilitating timely uptake of screening services, and another focused on addressing socially embedded barriers through community engagement, premarital counseling, and contextually appropriate communication regarding reproductive risk and family health [14].

The gap between low previous testing and high willingness for future screening should not be interpreted only as an individual knowledge deficit. In Palestine, movement restrictions, checkpoint delays, exposure to violence, damaged infrastructure, and limited healthcare resources may reduce access to routine preventive services. Therefore, improving thalassemia prevention requires both education and service-access strategies, including easier screening pathways, community-based testing opportunities, and integration of counseling into accessible primary-care, university, and premarital services [15].

The most direct comparison for the present findings is the recent cross-sectional study from Delhi-NCR, India, from which the current questionnaire was adapted [9]. Relative to that study, the Palestinian sample demonstrated a substantially more favorable profile in awareness and knowledge, with 87.0% of participants having heard of thalassemia and 46.1% meeting criteria for adequate knowledge, compared with 36.8% awareness and only 7.7% adequate knowledge in the Indian cohort. Positive practice was also markedly more common in the present study than in the Delhi-NCR sample (82.7% vs. 22.6%), despite the fact that both studies identified an important gap between actual prior testing and stated willingness toward preventive behaviors.

At the same time, the two studies converged in several important respects: female participants demonstrated better knowledge profiles, and knowledge was more clearly linked to preventive practice than to attitude. Notably, both studies also suggested that apparently favorable overall attitudes may coexist with residual social discomfort in marriage-related contexts, indicating that stigma may become more visible when thalassemia is considered in relation to partner choice and family formation. These similarities support the cross-context relevance of the adapted instrument, whereas the more favorable Palestinian findings may reflect differences in public exposure, sociocultural context, or local familiarity with hereditary disease and premarital health issues.

Comparison with the multinational study conducted across Azerbaijan, Indonesia, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Thailand further strengthens the interpretation of the present findings [4]. In that survey, general awareness of thalassemia was also relatively high (85.06%), yet detailed knowledge remained limited, with only 20.08% correctly identifying thalassemia as a genetic disorder and only 5.39% recognizing it as a curable condition. The current Palestinian findings are partly consistent with that broader pattern in that awareness was high, yet clinically actionable knowledge remained incomplete. However, the Palestinian participants appeared to have a stronger grasp of several core hereditary concepts, including the inherited nature of the disorder, healthy carrier status, and the genetic implications of consanguinity, while still demonstrating weaker understanding of treatability, clinical subtypes, and policy-related prevention measures. Both studies also identified higher age and higher educational attainment as important correlates of better knowledge. A notable point of divergence, however, lies in the cross-domain structure of the results: in the multinational study, better knowledge was associated with more positive attitudes toward screening, whereas in the present study knowledge was more clearly related to preventive practice than to attitude. This difference may suggest that, in the Palestinian context, attitudinal responses are shaped not only by factual understanding but also by broader social and cultural meanings attached to hereditary disease, particularly in relation to marriage and reproductive decision-making.

The present findings may also be meaningfully compared with the nationwide study of Indonesian youth, which likewise examined knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward thalassemia in a young population [16]. That study reported that 74.5% of respondents had heard of thalassemia, most had poor knowledge (62.1%), most had positive attitudes (83.3%), and more than half had poor practice (54.4%). In addition, although 95.7% had never been tested, many participants expressed future willingness to undergo screening, particularly before marriage. The Palestinian results show a more favorable overall profile, with higher awareness, better knowledge adequacy, higher positive practice classification, and substantially greater prior testing uptake than that reported in the Indonesian youth sample. Nevertheless, the two studies display a highly similar structural pattern: in both settings, awareness and generally positive attitudes did not automatically translate into complete knowledge or consistent preventive behavior, and female participants showed more favorable KAP profiles than males. The comparison therefore reinforces a recurring finding across youth-focused thalassemia KAP research, namely that familiarity with the disease and general support for prevention are insufficient on their own; what remains critical is the conversion of awareness into accurate, operational knowledge and into timely screening-related behavior.

The findings also have direct implications for nursing practice and health education. Nurses, midwives, school health providers, and community health workers are well positioned to provide culturally sensitive thalassemia education, explain carrier status and inheritance risk, normalize premarital screening, and facilitate referral to genetic counseling or screening services. Because females showed higher knowledge and practice than males, educational interventions should intentionally include young men as well as women and should address couples, families, and university students before marriage. Nurse-led education should focus on practical genetic literacy, including the difference between thalassemia major and minor, the meaning of carrier status, the role of consanguinity, and available premarital and prenatal prevention options.

The present study is important not only because it characterizes thalassemia-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among Palestinian young adults, but also because it identifies the specific points at which prevention efforts may succeed or fail within a population entering the life stage most relevant to partner selection, premarital planning, and future reproductive decision-making.

From a public health perspective, the coexistence of high awareness with only moderate knowledge adequacy is especially consequential, as it suggests that surface-level familiarity with thalassemia may create the impression of preparedness without ensuring the depth of understanding required for informed action [17]. Equally important, the finding that prior testing uptake remained considerably lower than endorsement of premarital and prenatal preventive measures indicates that the principal challenge may no longer be simple receptiveness to prevention, but rather the effective translation of intention into behavior [18]. In settings where hereditary disorders impose lifelong clinical, psychosocial, and economic burdens, such gaps are highly relevant because they may directly limit the preventive impact of premarital counseling, genetic education, and screening programs [19].

Scientifically, this study contributes to the growing body of thalassemia KAP research by showing that the relationships among knowledge, attitude, and practice are selective rather than uniform. In the present sample, knowledge was positively associated with preventive practice and independently predicted positive practice, whereas attitude was not significantly associated with either knowledge or practice. This distinction is important because it suggests that improving factual and functional understanding of thalassemia may yield more immediate benefits for prevention-oriented behavior than relying on attitudinal change alone. At the same time, the persistence of lower acceptance in marriage-related and close social contexts indicates that stigma surrounding hereditary disease may remain embedded in familial and reproductive norms, even when overtly negative beliefs are less common. In this sense, the study has broader scientific relevance beyond the Palestinian context: it supports the view that effective thalassemia control requires not only awareness promotion, but also a more integrated framework that combines genetic literacy, service accessibility, and culturally informed engagement with the social meanings of carrier status, marriage, and reproductive risk.

The present study has several important strengths. First, it addresses a notable evidence gap by examining thalassemia-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among Palestinian young adults, a population of particular relevance to premarital planning and future reproductive decision-making. Second, the study used a culturally adapted Arabic questionnaire derived from a previously validated instrument, thereby enhancing conceptual rigor while improving contextual appropriateness for the target population. Third, by excluding healthcare professionals and students in health-related disciplines, the study was better positioned to reflect the awareness profile of the general young adult population rather than that of medically informed groups. In addition, the final analytical sample exceeded the calculated minimum sample size, allowing more stable subgroup analysis across key sociodemographic characteristics. The study also moved beyond simple descriptive reporting by incorporating comparative analyses, cross-domain correlations, and multivariable logistic regression, which strengthened the interpretive value of the findings and enabled identification of independent predictors of adequate knowledge and positive practice.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the online snowball sampling design may have introduced selection bias. The sample overrepresented females, highly educated participants, urban residents, and participants from the Jerusalem district. This differs from national Palestinian demographic patterns, where the sex distribution is relatively balanced, with approximately 2.8 million males and 2.7 million females in the State of Palestine in mid-2025, and where young people also show a near-balanced sex ratio. In contrast, 64.9% of the present sample was female. The sample also included a high proportion of participants with Bachelor’s-level education or higher and was geographically concentrated in Jerusalem, while Gaza was underrepresented or absent despite representing a large proportion of the Palestinian population.

These differences limit the generalizability of the findings to all Palestinian young adults.

Second, although sensitivity analyses using sex-post-stratified estimates and a non-Jerusalem subgroup supported the overall direction of the findings, these analyses cannot fully correct for selection bias related to education, internet access, social media use, geography, or health interest. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as reflecting the surveyed online sample rather than a nationally representative estimate. Third, the self-administered questionnaire may have introduced social desirability bias, particularly for attitudes toward premarital screening, consanguinity, and reproductive decision-making. Fourth, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, so the association between knowledge and practice should not be interpreted as proof that knowledge causes preventive behavior. Finally, if no formal local pilot testing or content validity index was conducted, this should be recognized as an additional limitation of the adapted questionnaire.

Recommendations and implications

Based on the findings, thalassemia prevention efforts in Palestine should move beyond general awareness campaigns and focus on practical genetic literacy. Educational programs should explain inheritance, carrier status, thalassemia major and minor, consanguinity-related risk, and the purpose of premarital and prenatal screening. These programs should be implemented in universities, community settings, and premarital counseling services, with specific attention to young men and younger students, who showed lower knowledge and practice scores.

Nurse-led and community-based health education may be especially useful because nurses and community health workers can provide accessible counseling, correct misconceptions, reduce stigma, and encourage timely screening. Prevention programs should also reduce practical barriers by linking education with available screening services, referral pathways, and culturally appropriate counseling. Future interventions should evaluate not only awareness and intention, but also completed screening behavior and informed reproductive decision-making.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that although awareness of beta thalassemia among Palestinian young adults was high, important gaps remained in detailed and functionally relevant knowledge, particularly in relation to prevention and informed decision-making. While attitudes were broadly favorable and preventive intentions were generally strong, lower prior screening uptake and reduced acceptance in marriage-related contexts indicate that awareness alone is insufficient to ensure effective prevention. The finding that knowledge independently predicted positive practice underscores the importance of strengthening practical genetic literacy as a key component of thalassemia control efforts. These results support the need for culturally appropriate educational strategies, premarital counseling, and community-based prevention initiatives that can translate awareness into informed action and contribute to reducing the future burden of beta thalassemia in Palestine.
