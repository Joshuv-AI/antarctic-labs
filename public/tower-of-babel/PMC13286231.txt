Alcohol‐free and low‐alcohol beverage consumption and concurrent and subsequent alcohol outcomes among young adults
Authors: Samuel Mann, Daniel Siconolfi, Anthony Rodriguez, Rachana Seelam, Eric R Pedersen, Michael Dunbar, Elizabeth J D'Amico
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsAlcohol‐Free and Low‐Alcohol Beverages (AFLABs) have grown in popularity, yet predictors and longitudinal outcomes of their use remain unclear. We aimed to measure the association between AFLAB consumption and (1) concurrent and (2) subsequent alcohol consumption, risk and change measures (e.g. consequences, quit attempts, abstention) and drinking motives among young adults.DesignObservational study using two survey waves (W15 and W16; 2022–2024) from a longitudinal cohort study (STRATA).SettingCalifornia, USA (surveys fielded 2022–2024).ParticipantsCross sectional analysis of young adults (n = 877 individuals, of which 849 were followed in W16) who report past‐month alcohol consumption in W15 from a California‐based cohort study, and analysis of those with past‐month alcohol consumption at both W15 and W16 (n = 693).MeasurementsIndividuals completed survey items on past‐month AFLAB consumption, alcohol consumption (past‐month heavy episodic drinking and past‐year abstention), alcohol risk and change measures (alcohol‐related consequences, indicators of a change effort and the alcohol contemplation ladder) and drinking motives (from the drinking motives questionnaire).FindingsTwenty‐four percent of participants reported past‐month AFLAB use at baseline. Adjusted cross‐sectional models using W15 data indicated that AFLAB use was associated with lower social drinking motives [coefficient = −0.005; 95% confidence interval (CI) = −0.011 to 0.0001] but was not statistically significantly associated with other alcohol consumption, risk or change measures. In adjusted prospective models, baseline AFLAB use was associated with a 5.1 percentage point (pp) higher probability of alcohol abstention at W16 (95% CI = 1.1–9.1) than lack of AFLAB use. In adjusted prospective models, baseline AFLAB use was not statistically significantly associated with heavy episodic drinking, alcohol‐related consequences or most change‐related measures, although there was suggestive evidence of a higher likelihood of reporting a quit attempt in the past 3 months.ConclusionsUse of alcohol‐free and low‐alcohol beverages appears to be common among young adults in California, USA, and appears to be concurrently associated with lower social drinking motives and prospectively associated with subsequent alcohol abstention.Keywords: abstention, alcohol, alcohol free and low alcohol beverages, alcohol quit attempts, alcohol risk and change behaviors, young adults

INTRODUCTION

Young adulthood is a developmental stage marked by identity exploration and changing social roles. Young adults experience higher rates of heavy episodic drinking and elevated risk for alcohol‐related harms, but have low treatment uptake for alcohol problems [1, 2]. Evidence suggests drinking norms among young adults are shifting, with rising abstinence and a growing normalization of non‐drinking [3, 4].

Against this backdrop, alcohol‐free [i.e. beverages that contain no or a minute amount of alcohol by volume (ABV); typically 0% ABV to 0.5% ABV] and low‐alcohol beverages (typically 0.5% to 1.2% ABV) (AFLABs) have expanded rapidly in availability. Studies document a rising uptake of AFLABs and highlight individual, product and environmental drivers of use, including health motivations, taste expectations and social context [5, 6]. Studies also suggest that some people use AFLABs when attempting to cut down or to experiment with non‐drinking within ‘sober curious’ practices [6, 7, 8]. However, there is mixed evidence on whether AFLAB use substitutes for, complements or leaves unchanged standard‐strength alcohol consumption.

Despite the growing popularity of AFLABs, critical evidence gaps remain. Most studies are cross‐sectional or focus on general adult samples, leaving limited evidence on AFLAB use among contemporary United States (US) young adults, a cohort with distinct drinking patterns and shifting alcohol‐related norms. Longitudinal evidence is also limited on whether AFLAB use predicts subsequent alcohol consumption, alcohol‐related consequences, intentions or attempts to reduce drinking, abstention and drinking motives after accounting for prior levels of these outcomes. To address these gaps, the present study uses multi‐wave data from a cohort of US young adults to answer two research questions: (1) is AFLAB use associated with concurrent alcohol use, consequences, motives and change‐related intentions or behaviors? (2) Does AFLAB use prospectively predict changes in alcohol outcomes and drinking motives across adjacent waves?

METHODS

Study design

Data come from a longitudinal cohort of young adults originally recruited into a middle‐school prevention trial in 2008 that took place in 16 Southern California schools. They were followed‐up annually from 2008 to 2024. Intervention effects dissipated after the first year [9]. Cohort procedures, retention and measures have been described in detail elsewhere [10]. Surveys were web‐based with monetary compensation. All participants provided informed consent, and study procedures were approved by the Human Subjects Protection Committee at RAND.

Analyses used waves 15 [W15 (the first year that questions related to AFLAB consumption were included; data collected 2022–2023)] and 16 [W16 (data collected 2023–2024)] to (1) identify correlates of AFLAB use; and (2) estimate prospective associations between AFLAB use at W15 and alcohol related outcomes at W16.

Measures

AFLAB was measured as past‐month use of alcohol‐free or low‐alcohol beverages; defined for participants as ‘At least one drink of no‐alcohol or low‐alcohol (<1.2% alcohol by volume) beer, wine or liquor (like Kaliber or Heineken 0.0%, Surely Wines, Ritual Zero Proof)’ (binary).

Demographics included age, race and ethnicity (non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non‐Hispanic Asian and non‐Hispanic other), sex (male, female), sexual identity (heterosexual, gay/lesbian, bisexual and other), educational attainment (a high school diploma or less, GED or some college, associate's degree, bachelor's degree or certificate; graduate degree).and pregnancy status (pregnant, not pregnant).

Alcohol consumption measures included past month heavy episodic drinking (binary; 5+ drinks on one occasion) and alcohol abstention (past year reporting 0 standard strength alcohol drinks).

Alcohol risk and change measures included nine alcohol‐related consequences (number of times a participant experienced negative consequences in the past year, e.g. a fight or argument because of drinking alcohol) [11], indicators of a change effort (a binary measure, which took the value 1 if someone had an alcohol quit attempt in the past 3 months) and binary self‐reported measures of reduced drinking in the past month, plans to quit and thinking about quitting, derived from the alcohol contemplation ladder [12].

Drinking motives were assessed with social (α = 0.93) and coping (α = 0.91) subscales from the Drinking Motives Questionnaire (continuous measure) [13].

Analytic samples

We restricted the sample to past‐month drinkers because several outcomes were administered only to respondents who reported alcohol use. We also excluded 50 individuals with missing pregnancy data and four with missing motive data. Our resulting analytic cross‐sectional sample consists of 877 individuals in W15. We used two analytic samples in the prospective models. First, in the models using abstention as an outcome, we included 849 individuals who responded at both waves. Second, in models using other outcomes, which were contingent on drinking at W16, we included 693 individuals who reported past‐month alcohol consumption at W16.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to compare baseline W15 characteristics by W15 past‐month AFLAB use. These comparisons were conducted in the baseline W15 sample, the prospective abstention sample and the prospective non‐abstention outcome sample. We, then, estimated cross‐sectional associations between AFLAB use and W15 outcomes. We fit separate models for each outcome. Both unadjusted and demographic adjusted models were estimated. Finally, we estimated prospective associations between W15 AFLAB use and W16 outcomes. For each W16 outcome, we estimated sequential models: (1) unadjusted models including W15 AFLAB use; (2) demographic‐adjusted models including W15 AFLAB use; (3) demographic‐adjusted models excluding W15 AFLAB use; (4) fully adjusted models including demographics, W15 AFLAB use and the baseline value of the outcome; and (5) an additional abstention model adjusting for demographics and baseline change/motivation measures. Because the abstention analysis was restricted to participants who reported past‐month drinking at W15, models for abstention could not adjust for baseline abstention. For binary outcomes, we estimated logistic regression models and report average marginal effects. For continuous outcomes, we estimated linear regression models and report unstandardized coefficients. The analysis was not pre‐registered and results should, therefore, be considered exploratory. Analyses were conducted in Stata/MP 18.

In the baseline W15 sample, people reporting AFLAB use were not significantly different from those not reporting in terms of past‐month heavy episodic drinking (48.6% versus 54.4%), recent quit attempts (22.9% versus 21.9%), reported quitting or reducing alcohol consumption (8.4% versus 6.6%), plans to quit or reduce drinking (11.7% versus 12.7%), thinking about quitting or reducing drinking (13.6% versus 13.3%), alcohol‐related consequences (means = 5.5 versus 4.8), or coping motives (8.3 versus 8.5). However, those reporting AFLAB use reported lower social drinking motives (12.6 versus 13.6).

Longitudinal associations

Table 3 reports prospective associations between W15 AFLAB use and W16 outcomes. For the abstention model, we restricted the sample to participants who reported past‐month alcohol consumption at W15 so that all participants were at risk of transitioning to abstention at follow‐up and because of this restriction, the model could not adjust for baseline abstention. Baseline AFLAB use was associated with a higher probability of past‐year abstention at follow‐up in the unadjusted model [4.9 percentage point (pp); 95% CI = 1.0–8.7] and the demographic‐adjusted model (5.1 pp; 95% CI = 1.1–9.1). This association was similar after additional adjustment for baseline indicators of recent quit attempts, reduced drinking, plans to quit or reduce drinking, thinking about quitting or reducing drinking and social and coping drinking motives (4.8 pp; 95% CI = 1.0–8.5). In the fully adjusted model, there was suggestive evidence (P < 0.10) that AFLAB use was associated with a higher likelihood of reporting a quit attempt in the past 3 months (6 pp; 95% CI = −0.9 to 12.8). All other prospective associations were statistically insignificant. In most models, adding baseline AFLAB consumption modestly improved model fit, as reflected by higher R 2 or pseudo‐R 2 values compared with otherwise similar models that excluded AFLAB use.

DISCUSSION

To date, few studies have investigated predictors of AFLAB use specifically among contemporary US young adults, and few have used longitudinal data to test whether AFLAB use predicts subsequent alcohol consumption, risk and change measures and drinking motives after accounting for prior levels of use.

We examined demographic differences in AFLAB use and whether AFLAB use was associated with concurrent and subsequent alcohol consumption, risk and change measures and drinking motives. Cross‐sectionally, AFLAB use was more common among young adult women, less common among non‐Hispanic White participants and less common among those identifying as gay or lesbian. AFLAB use was not consistently associated with indicators of alcohol risk or change in baseline comparisons, although AFLAB users reported slightly lower social drinking motives. After adjusting for demographics, AFLAB use remained associated with lower social drinking motives. Longitudinally, baseline AFLAB use was associated with a higher likelihood of alcohol abstention in the subsequent year, with suggestive evidence of a higher likelihood of reporting a past‐3‐month quit attempt.

Our findings extend a literature that has documented rising product availability, interest and mixed motivations for AFLAB use [5, 6]. In baseline comparisons, AFLAB use was not consistently associated with alcohol risk or change measures, suggesting that AFLAB use among young adults may reflect heterogeneous motivations rather than a single risk profile. However, baseline AFLAB use was prospectively associated with subsequent abstention, with suggestive evidence of an association with quit attempts, even though it was not associated with heavy episodic drinking or alcohol‐related consequences. The persistence of the abstention association after adjustment for baseline change and motivation measures suggests that AFLAB use may capture drinking‐change processes not reflected in quit attempts or plans to reduce drinking. This pattern, combined with the concurrent association with social motives, suggests AFLABs may support or accompany transitions into abstention for some young adults, including lighter or infrequent drinkers or those using AFLABs for lower‐risk substitution [6, 7, 8]. At the same time, the absence of associations with heavy episodic drinking or alcohol‐related consequences suggests that AFLAB use should not be interpreted as a broader marker of reduced alcohol risk or harm over the follow‐up period.

From a prevention and clinical perspective, findings suggest that AFLABs may be relevant to harm‐reduction approaches that emphasize feasible, lower‐risk substitutions. The association between AFLABs and subsequent abstention suggests they may be useful for some young adults experimenting with reduced drinking or non‐drinking. Campus health and clinical programs could discuss AFLABs within brief interventions, such as motivational interviewing, as one option for planned non‐drinking days or beverage substitution. However, because AFLAB use was not associated with reductions in heavy episodic drinking or alcohol‐related consequences, AFLABs should be presented as one possible tool within a broader behavior‐change strategy rather than as a standalone approach to reducing alcohol‐related harms. Public health messaging could also present AFLAB use as a socially acceptable strategy for cutting back [14, 15].

Our study is not without limitations. Outcomes were self‐reported and may be affected by recall, social‐desirability bias or seasonality. Past‐12‐month abstention may partially overlap with the pre‐baseline period for some participants, attenuating temporal interpretation. Missing data may bias estimates if related to alcohol behaviors or AFLAB use. Bounded and right‐skewed continuous outcomes should be interpreted as conditional mean differences. Although models adjusted for demographic covariates and baseline values of the modelled outcome, unmeasured confounding, bidirectional processes and motivation to cut down remain possible. Baseline AFLAB use may partly capture motivation, particularly given our single binary measure. Estimates should not be interpreted causally, and some findings may reflect chance because of multiple hypothesis testing. As such, additional research is needed to confirm the findings presented in this study in other samples.

In conclusion, AFLAB use among young adults is common and was prospectively associated with alcohol abstention, with suggestive evidence of an association with quit attempts. Findings suggest that AFLABs may be a useful tool within broader self‐regulation strategies as drinking norms continue to evolve in this developmental period. Future research should consider how findings vary in different samples and heterogeneously across demographic factors.
