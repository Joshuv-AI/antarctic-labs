Stabilizer Orthosis Rehabilitation after Bilateral Hip Disarticulation Due to Necrotizing Fasciitis: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Kei Sato, Yasuhiro Kajiwara, Motoaki Oshima, Tatsuya Fujii, Hideaki Takahata
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Bilateral hip disarticulation is a rare and devastating surgical procedure associated with poor survival and limited functional prognosis.1,2) Rehabilitation after bilateral hip disarticulation remains particularly challenging. The available literature is notably limited, with the majority of reports originating several decades ago. Necrotizing fasciitis is a fulminant soft tissue infection characterized by rapid progression and systemic toxicity. Recent epidemiological data suggest that necrotizing fasciitis incidence has increased in some settings, and rates of severe invasive group A streptococcal infections—an important cause of necrotizing fasciitis—have been rising in recent years.3,4) Although war-related trauma and traffic accidents were previously the leading causes of bilateral hip disarticulation, advances in life-saving treatments suggest that necrotizing fasciitis may emerge as a more frequent etiology in the future.5) Rehabilitation strategies and outcomes are largely undocumented.

Several reports have described the use of Canadian-type hip disarticulation prostheses, characterized by a bucket-type socket design enclosing the pelvis and lower trunk.6,7) However, these devices require high physical capacity and extensive training, and even among successful users, walking speed and distance remain limited, with only modest improvements in activities of daily living (ADL). Therefore, more feasible and practical approaches that can meaningfully improve ADL are needed. This report highlights a stabilizer-based rehabilitation strategy aimed at maximizing activity and participation within the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework, rather than pursuing prosthetic ambulation, in a patient with catastrophic limb loss.

CASE

A 46-year-old woman with a history of mycosis fungoides, who had undergone umbilical cord blood transplantation at the age of 28 years, developed necrotizing fasciitis. Nineteen days before admission, she experienced pharyngitis, followed by fever 4 days before admission and bilateral leg pain 1 day before admission. On Day 0, worsening leg pain prompted the patient to present to a secondary hospital, where she was found to have diffuse purpura on the trunk and lower extremities, along with signs of septic shock. The patient was intubated and placed on mechanical ventilation and the diagnosis of necrotizing fasciitis was made. On Day 1, the patient was transferred to a tertiary care hospital where she underwent emergency surgical debridement and was admitted to the intensive care unit. On Day 3, bilateral hip disarticulation was performed to control the infection. Over the following weeks, the patient underwent three additional debridements and received continuous local antibiotic perfusion (CLAP) therapy. After the completion of CLAP, her condition gradually stabilized.

Active rehabilitation commenced on Day 41, initially focusing on pressure relief for the wounds and training in wheelchair sitting. By Day 67, the patient had achieved unsupported sitting balance. On Day 81, she was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital. At the time of transfer, her Functional Independence Measure (FIM) score was 64 (motor, 31; cognitive, 33), her Barthel Index (BI) was 35, and her grip strength was 10.1 kg on the right and 10.8 kg on the left.

Figure 1 summarizes the rehabilitation timeline and key interventions. After transferring to the rehabilitation hospital, inpatient rehabilitation was provided by physical therapy (PT) or occupational therapy (OT) for six to nine 20-min units per day, depending on tolerance, 5 or 6 days per week. The program emphasized wheelchair skills, transfer training, seated and supported standing balance, and progressive upper-limb and trunk conditioning; upper-limb strengthening (including push-up-based exercises) was a major component, and OT contributed substantially through ADL practice and task-specific training. Self-directed exercise was introduced gradually; however, the patient performed only limited additional training outside scheduled sessions. Progression of standing time and task difficulty (e.g., chair/platform standing, ADL in standing, stairs around Day 180, and driving practice around Day 200) was guided primarily by wound/skin tolerance, safety, and fatigue.

Soon after transferring to the rehabilitation hospital, a custom-made stabilizer was introduced. Manufactured by a local orthotics company, the device was constructed from resin and synthetic leather, designed to cover the stumps and pelvis, and weighed only 1.3 kg (Fig. 2a,b). The outer shell was approximately 3 mm thick with perforations in selected areas for ventilation and weight reduction, and the inner surface was lined with sponge padding; fit was fine-tuned by adding firm sponge spacers as needed. The stabilizer was adjustable to fit the patient’s waist, and the undersurface was designed with a gentle curvature and fitted with reinforced padding to enhance stability during supported standing. Four adjustment sessions were performed over a period of about 1 month to finalize the configuration, and minor periodic adjustments have continued thereafter to accommodate body-shape changes. No major complications occurred. Sacral and wound-related skin problems, which are major obstacles to rehabilitation, were more easily managed after stabilizer introduction, facilitating training and protection. Rehabilitation focused on strengthening the upper limbs with push-up exercises, and the duration of stabilizer use was gradually increased. Initial upper-limb fatigue improved with strengthening, and a lower center of gravity and improved standing posture may have contributed to improved postural control and perceived stability (Fig. 2c,d). This improvement was also demonstrated by a decrease in the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK) score,8) which is a patient-reported measure of fear of movement and (re) injury, with higher scores higher scores indicating greater kinesiophobia. In this case, the TSK score decreased from 37 to 17 after using the stabilizer.

Use of the stabilizer facilitated easier standing on chairs or platforms, thereby enabling ADL such as eating, dressing, and household activities. The patient regained the ability to climb stairs to her second-floor room and drive a modified vehicle. At discharge on Day 228, her FIM score had improved to 115 (motor 80, cognitive 35), her BI had increased to 85, and her grip strength measured 18.7 kg on the right and 18.9 kg on the left (Fig. 3). Over time, the patient’s wounds healed steadily, and she was able to sit and stand unsupported for increasingly longer periods without the stabilizer.

After discharge, the patient reported being able to spend several consecutive hours at home without using the stabilizer, although she continued to wear it routinely for at least 1 h each morning and evening to prevent pressure ulcers and ensure proper fit. The patient achieved meaningful gains in participation (e.g., independent community mobility and social interaction), and returning to work remained as a future goal. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report and the accompanying images.

DISCUSSION

Reported cases of bilateral hip disarticulation are summarized in Table 1. Reports of rehabilitation after bilateral hip disarticulation are rare, with most published prior to the 1980s.6,7,9,10) Canadian-type prostheses and their derivatives are the mainstay devices for rehabilitation. In 2001, Spence et al.11) introduced a reciprocating gait orthosis for bilateral hip disarticulation. However, all the successful cases involved relatively young men, and wheelchair use or reliance on sockets was predominant. Previous studies have focused on prosthetic technology with limited data on functional outcomes or long-term follow-up.

Reported cases of bilateral hip disarticulation and rehabilitation outcomes

In 2021, Nakamura et al.12) reported a case of a 24-year-old man using a modified Canadian prosthesis. Although his condition was relatively favorable, his walking speed remained low (81 s per 10 m), highlighting the difficulty in achieving practical mobility. Large stabilizers covering the abdomen and stumps have been used after hemicorporectomy or hemipelvectomy; however, the only published case of a smaller stabilizer for bilateral hip disarticulation was reported in 1998 in Japan by Kaneda et al.13) Their device, which was made of soft plastic and slightly larger than ours, also contributed significantly to improvements in ADL.

A comparison of bilateral hip prostheses and stabilizers is presented in Table 2. In the present case, a stabilizer was introduced for stump protection, rehabilitation, and ADL improvement. Although hip-disarticulation prosthetic ambulation was considered, it was judged to be impractical in this case. Even unilateral hip-disarticulation prostheses require substantial physical capacity and prolonged training, and the few reported successful bilateral cases have largely involved young men and still relied predominantly on wheelchair mobility or socket-based standing for daily life. Therefore, even under favorable conditions, achieving practical walking capacity was not expected to be straightforward. In addition, the time required for prosthetic gait acquisition was unlikely to align with the patient’s priorities and clinical course, particularly given the need for ongoing wound protection. After discussing these issues with the patient, she indicated a preference for a stabilizer-based approach that focused on safe transfers, standing tasks, and activity/participation goals rather than attempting prosthetic gait. The patient’s goals included in-home mobility without a wheelchair, stair climbing, and ease of donning, doffing, and transport. Particularly in cases of disarticulation caused by infection or trauma, wound protection is a critical determinant of rehabilitation progress, and the ability to learn and apply stabilizers early is highly advantageous.

The stabilizer markedly improved the functional outcomes: FIM score increased from 64 to 115, and BI increased from 35 to 85. Grip strength was low at the start of active rehabilitation (Day 41; right 9.8 kg, left 9.1 kg) and increased to 10.1/10.8 kg at transfer (Day 81) and 18.7/18.9 kg at discharge (Day 228). At transfer to the rehabilitation hospital, the BI of 35 was mainly attributable to preserved feeding and grooming (10 and 5 points, respectively) and continence management (bowel 10, bladder 5), whereas the patient remained dependent for toileting, bathing, dressing, ambulation, and stair climbing (0 points for these items), largely because of wound protection needs and the inability to perform safe upright transfers. Similarly, the low motor-FIM score was driven by dependence in dressing and toileting and by very limited transfer and mobility performance (bed/chair, toilet, and tub/shower transfers; locomotion and stairs), whereas cognitive-FIM items were largely preserved. At discharge, the remaining limitations were mainly observed in stair negotiation and bathing-related transfers, despite substantial gains in most self-care and transfer items.

As an indicator of the patient’s perceived progress, the TSK score decreased markedly from 37 to the minimum possible score of 17 after the introduction of the stabilizer. We interpreted this change as improved perceived safety and confidence during upright tasks (e.g., standing and transfers), which may have facilitated greater engagement in rehabilitation and activity/participation goals. Although no studies have directly examined the association between stabilizer orthoses and TSK in bilateral hip disarticulation, external support that improves trunk alignment and postural stability may plausibly reduce perceived threat during movement. From an ICF perspective, the stabilizer primarily addressed body functions/structures by providing external support for postural alignment and perceived stability, which enabled activity-level gains (e.g., transfers, dressing, household tasks) and participation-level gains such as independent community mobility, driving a modified vehicle, and engagement in social roles (with returning to work remaining as a future goal). Standardized objective trunk assessments were not performed, and the relationship between stabilizer use and trunk function could not be formally evaluated. The observed functional gains likely reflected both stabilizer-assisted postural alignment and overall rehabilitation/conditioning over time. This observation remains descriptive, and causal inference is not possible in a single case.12) Importantly, the simplicity of the stabilizer allowed for smooth adoption even though none of the physicians, therapists, or orthotists had prior experience with similar devices.

Compared with previous reports, this case demonstrates that a relatively simple and lightweight stabilizer can provide substantial benefits in bilateral hip disarticulation rehabilitation. Unlike prostheses, which require high physical and technical capacity, stabilizers may offer a more practical and applicable option for a broader range of patients, including women and older individuals. Improvement in standing posture achieved with a stabilizer may help reduce the long-term risk of lower back pain and spinal deformities. Nevertheless, some limitations remain: stabilizers, such as prostheses, must be removed for toileting, and prolonged disuse can lead to poor fitting. Evidence is currently limited to single-case experiences, and future studies should include more cases and investigate long-term outcomes, including potential musculoskeletal complications. This report has limitations. Objective assessments of upper-limb and trunk function (e.g., shoulder/elbow strength or standardized trunk control measures) were not systematically performed, and grip strength alone does not fully represent functional capacity. In addition, improvements in FIM and BI were likely multifactorial, reflecting general rehabilitation, conditioning, wound stabilization, and task-specific practice over time; therefore, the specific contribution of the stabilizer cannot be isolated in a single case.

CONCLUSION

Bilateral hip disarticulation caused by necrotizing fasciitis presents significant challenges to rehabilitation. In this case, the introduction of a lightweight stabilizer facilitated stable standing posture, upper-limb strength, and substantially improved independence in ADL. Functional outcomes, including FIM and BI, showed marked improvement, allowing the patient to be discharged home and resume meaningful social participation (e.g., community outings and interactions with family and friends), while a return to work remained a future goal.

Stabilizers may offer a practical and effective option for rehabilitation in patients with bilateral hip disarticulation. Further research is needed to clarify their role in long-term management and the maintenance of functional outcomes.
