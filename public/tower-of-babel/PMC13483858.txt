Background

Endometriosis may, in rare instances, present as an acute abdomen requiring urgent surgical intervention [1]. Deep infiltrating endometriosis (DIE) represents a severe subtype of endometriosis, defined as endometriotic lesions penetrating ≥ 5 mm beneath the peritoneal surface [2]. According to the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology guideline, DIE typically involves vital pelvic structures—including the uterosacral ligaments, rectovaginal septum, bowel, and urinary tract—often resulting in severe pain, distorted pelvic anatomy, and functional impairment [3]. DIE affects approximately 15–30% of women with endometriosis [4], and its diagnostic and therapeutic complexity increases with higher American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) scores. The challenge becomes particularly significant when patients exhibit marked elevations of tumor markers such as cancer antigen 125 (CA-125) and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA 19 − 9), as the presentation can closely resemble advanced ovarian cancer, creating a major diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma [5]. The pathophysiological basis for such marked biomarker elevation in the absence of malignancy is multifactorial and not fully defined. Proposed mechanisms include direct secretion from ectopic endometrial lesions, chronic inflammation, and peritoneal irritation, which collectively contribute to elevated serum levels [6, 7]. This uncertainty contributes to the diagnostic dilemma and increases the risk of misdiagnosis. This clinical overlap introduces a dual risk: it may prompt overly radical surgery, causing irreparable harm, or it may delay intervention for a potential malignancy owning to diagnostic uncertainty, ultimately compromising prognosis. A definitive diagnosis of DIE still depends on laparoscopic exploration and histopathological confirmation [8]. In such complex situations, surgery serves to establish a definitive diagnosis, restore normal anatomy, and improve outcomes. Prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines, this report describes a severe case of DIE presenting as an acute abdomen with markedly elevated tumor marker levels. It highlights the diagnostic challenges, underscores the importance of including DIE in the differential diagnosis of acute abdomen, and demonstrates the value of early recognition and standardized surgical management in optimizing outcomes.

Case presentation

A 42-year-old woman (gravida 1, para 1) was admitted with sudden-onset severe diffuse abdominal pain lasting 5 h. Her medical history included one cesarean section, and she reported regular menstruation. She denied any prior diagnosis of endometriosis, chronic pelvic pain, or family history of malignancy. The patient had one child and expressed no desire for future fertility. Preoperatively, the possibility of hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and consequent loss of fertility was discussed, and the patient provided consent. Intraoperatively, when radical resection was deemed necessary, consent was re-confirmed with the family. Physical examination revealed board-like abdominal rigidity with diffuse tenderness and rebound tenderness.

Transvaginal ultrasonography demonstrated a complex mass measuring approximately 10.7 × 7.6 cm posterior to the uterus, along with a fluid-filled dark area approximately 4.2 cm deep in the pouch of Douglas. Abdominal computed tomography revealed a cystic adnexal lesion, suspicious for torsion or rupture (Fig. 1). Laboratory tests showed an elevated white blood cell count of 17.28 × 109/L (reference range: 4–10 × 109/L), a progressive decline in hemoglobin levels from 122 g/L to 85 g/L (reference range: 110–150 g/L), and markedly elevated CA-125 at 870.50 U/mL and CA 19 − 9 at 853.90 U/mL (reference range for both: 0–35 U/mL). Given the large, irregular pelvic mass of unclear origin and nature, CA 19 − 9 was assessed in addition to CA-125 to broaden the differential diagnosis and eliminate possible gastrointestinal involvement. These findings were highly suggestive of a ruptured ovarian malignancy with hemorrhage, representing a critical condition requiring emergency surgical intervention.

Diagnostic laparoscopy revealed multiple irregular solid-cystic masses in the mid-lower abdomen, adherent to the uterus and ovaries. Several masses had ruptured, releasing dark fluid throughout the abdominopelvic cavity. Extensive necrosis of the greater omentum was present, with widespread lesions involving both adnexa and the pelvic sidewalls. Dense adhesions extended to the uterosacral ligaments, rectum, bladder, and ureters (Fig. 2). Given the extensive lesions, dense adhesions obscuring normal anatomy, and involvement of critical structures (ureters and bowel), the procedure was converted to laparotomy to mitigate intraoperative risks and ensure complete resection. Radical resection included total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, appendectomy, excision of the affected pelvic peritoneum and portions of the abdominal wall, bilateral pelvic lymph node dissection, and partial left ureterectomy with ureteroneocystostomy owing to severe distal ureteral involvement (Fig. 3).

Discussion and conclusions

DIE is a rare and severe subtype of endometriosis that can cause marked distortion of pelvic anatomy [9]. Diagnosis becomes particularly challenging when it presents as an acute abdomen with significantly elevated tumor marker levels [10]. This case illustrates DIE as a mimicker of malignancy, whereby definitive diagnosis still relies on the gold standard of laparoscopic exploration combined with histopathological confirmation [11].

The uniqueness of this case lies in the convergence of three highly misleading features: an acute board-like abdomen, synchronous marked elevation of CA-125 and CA 19 − 9, and intraoperative findings of necrotizing omentitis and hemorrhagic ascites. Together, these created a clinical picture closely mimicking malignancy. Unlike most reported DIE cases presenting with chronic abdominal pain, this case involved an acute onset and unusually high tumor marker levels, further complicating the differential diagnosis.

Serum CA-125 and CA 19 − 9 are commonly used biomarkers in endometriosis. Marked elevation in severe cases is attributed to multiple mechanisms, including direct secretion from ectopic lesions, chronic inflammation, and endocrine dysregulation, driving serum elevation through peritoneal inflammation and high local concentrations [6, 7, 12]. CA-125 levels correlate with disease severity and are significantly elevated in patients with moderate-to-severe disease (ASRM Stage III–IV) [13]. However, specificity is limited because elevated biomarker levels also occur in ovarian cancer and pelvic inflammatory disease [6]. CA 19 − 9 serves as a supplementary marker with generally lower sensitivity and specificity than CA-125 [14]; the combined use of both markers can improve diagnostic accuracy [15]. In this case, the simultaneous extreme elevation of both markers contributed substantially to the strong preoperative suspicion of a ruptured ovarian malignancy.

Numerous reports have confirmed the potential of DIE to imitate malignancy. Similar to cases described by Brijesh et al. [16] and Kumar et al. [17], this case was initially suspected to be ovarian cancer because of the complex pelvic mass and markedly elevated CA-125. Notably, the CA 19 − 9 level in this case (853.90 U/mL) far surpassed the value of 350.13 U/mL reported by Brijesh et al. [16]. However, this case is further distinguished by its acute presentation with board-like rigidity, progressive anemia, necrotizing omentitis, and massive hemorrhagic ascites—features typically associated with advanced gastrointestinal malignancies and exceedingly rare in benign DIE. Massive hemorrhagic ascites reported by Y. Bao et al. [18] and decidualized endometriosis during pregnancy reported by L. Bao et al. [19] further expand the recognized spectrum of malignant mimicry associated with DIE.

With strong preoperative suspicion of pelvic malignancy, intraoperative findings closely resembling advanced ovarian cancer—including widespread peritoneal implants, necrotizing omentitis, and hemorrhagic ascites—and extensive disease with multiorgan involvement, the surgical team proceeded with radical resection in accordance with oncologic principles. An intraoperative frozen section was considered but deemed unlikely to yield a reliable diagnosis, given the heterogeneous and extensively necrotic nature of the lesions, which posed a substantial risk of sampling error. In the setting of an acute abdomen with ongoing hemorrhage, the potential harm of prolonging the operation to await a frozen section result—particularly one at high risk of being falsely negative—was judged to outweigh its potential benefit in guiding the extent of resection. The procedure included total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, appendectomy, excision of affected pelvic peritoneum and portions of the abdominal wall, bilateral pelvic lymph node dissection, and partial left ureterectomy with ureteroneocystostomy for severe distal ureteral involvement. This approach differs fundamentally from conventional DIE surgery, where the uterus and ovaries are typically preserved. This case also illustrates the fundamental differences between managing acute and chronic DIE: management of chronic DIE primarily involves elective surgery to improve quality of life and fertility, whereas DIE-related acute abdomen requires urgent intervention to exclude life-threatening conditions, relieve acute symptoms, and establish a definitive diagnosis. In such complex and time-sensitive situations, rapid multidisciplinary decision-making and an individualized surgical plan balancing diagnostic precision with therapeutic completeness are essential for optimizing outcomes. Postoperative histopathology confirmed DIE (ASRM score 178, Stage IV). The patient recovered uneventfully, and no recurrence was observed during 2 years of follow-up.

In conclusion, even when clinical, imaging, and biochemical findings strongly suggest malignancy, DIE should remain an important differential diagnosis in cases of acute abdomen. Advanced DIE and pelvic malignancy can both present as pelvic space-occupying lesions and induce peritoneal inflammation, tissue necrosis, and epithelial injury, leading to nonspecific elevation of serum tumor markers and overlapping imaging features. Consequently, current preoperative diagnostic tools have significant limitations in differentiating between these two conditions, and in the presence of diagnostic uncertainty with clinical urgency, surgical exploration with the intent of radical resection is often necessary. Future research should focus on improving preoperative diagnostic specificity through novel biomarkers or enhanced imaging standards.
