Negative-pressure assistance for intraoperative cone-beam computed tomography localization in lung tumor resection: comparative study
Authors: Shuenn-Wen Kuo, Pei-Ming Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the effectiveness of wound negative-pressure–assisted (NPA) localization in facilitating pulmonary expansion during intraoperative cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) localization for thoracoscopic resection of small pulmonary nodules.MethodsA retrospective comparative study included 55 consecutive patients who underwent wedge resection for small lung tumors using uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) with CBCT localization between June 2022 and December 2024. Patients were grouped by localization technique: Group 1 received NPA and Group 2 did not. Primary outcomes were CBCT localization workflow time and CBCT-defined incomplete lung expansion/residual pleural air (CTILA) occurrence. Univariate comparisons used Welch’s t-test for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression assessed factors associated with CTILA, and multivariable linear regression analyzed CBCT localization workflow time.ResultsBaseline demographic and clinical characteristics were similar between groups. CBCT localization workflow time was significantly shorter in Group 1 than in Group 2 (17.31 ± 7.13 vs. 25.69 ± 9.57 min; mean difference − 8.38 min; 95% CI − 13.00 to − 3.76; p = 0.0007; Hedges’ g = − 0.987). CTILA occurred less frequently in Group 1 (3/29, 10.3%) than in Group 2 (13/26, 50.0%) (risk difference − 39.7%, 95% CI − 61.8% to − 17.5%; Fisher’s exact p = 0.0023; odds ratio 8.67). In a multivariable model adjusted for airway management type, smoking exposure, and nodule depth, the non-NPA group remained associated with a higher likelihood of CBCT-defined incomplete lung expansion/residual pleural air (adjusted OR 7.56, 95% CI 1.03–55.61; p = 0.047).ConclusionNPA localization was associated with shorter CBCT localization workflow time and a lower rate of CTILA during intraoperative CBCT localization for thoracoscopic resection of small pulmonary nodules.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04446-6.Keywords: Cone-Beam computed tomography, Uniportal, Video-assisted thoracic surgery

Introduction

Intraoperative cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) localization has emerged as a newly developed imaging technology in recent years for patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for small pulmonary nodules [1]. CBCT facilitates the identification of lung nodules using various modalities of localization; however, intraoperative ventilation remains challenging, particularly when CBCT-defined incomplete lung expansion/residual pleural air (CTILA) obscures the radiological features of ill-defined nodules, such as ground-glass nodules (GGNs) [2]. CBCT imaging revealed atelectasis obscuring the target nodule in 20% of cases, highlighting its critical role in detecting intraoperative anatomical changes that may compromise lesion visibility and localization accuracy [3].

Maintaining lung expansion is a critical challenge during CBCT localization [4]. However, the most important consideration for CBCT localization is maintenance of a patient’s ventilation for optimal image contrast without CBCT-defined incomplete lung expansion/residual pleural air (CTILA) interruption, short holding ventilation and sufficient depth of anesthesia [5]. The purpose of this study was to investigate the utility and feasibility of suction negative-pressure–assisted (NPA) control from the uniportal wound to assist the lung expansion and CBCT localization in the resection of small pulmonary nodules.

Methods

Patient selection and grouping

From June 2022 and December 2024, 55 patients had uniportal CBCT localization VATS under general anesthesia (Table 1). The study adhered to the Declaration of the Institutional Ethics Committee (202312003RIN) and all patients provided written informed consent for each investigation. Preoperative PFTs were not routinely obtained in all patients undergoing limited wedge resection for small peripheral nodules. However, smoking exposure and CT-based parenchymal findings, including emphysema and fibrosis, are reported in Table 1. The patients received premedication via a 50 µg intravenous fentanyl dose, followed by intubation or laryngeal mask. Patients were allocated according to anesthesiologist preference and the surgical team’s evolving experience; lung parenchymal conditions such as emphysema or fibrosis were not used as primary criteria for NPA selection. Separate lung ventilation with bronchial blocker was routinely used only in intubated patients, while in laryngeal mask patients, one-lung ventilation was achieved through pneumothorax following surgical wound creation. Although pneumothorax alone may not fully deflate the lung during VATS, the lung localization procedure can still be performed during CBCT localization. During surgery, lung inflation was achieved using an anesthesia ventilator via either a laryngeal mask or intubation. Anesthesia was subsequently induced through intravenous administration of propofol (at a rate of 1.5-2 mg/kg) and fentanyl (at a rate of 1.5–2 µg/kg), all while maintaining an oxygen flow of 8 L/min. Surgical techniques

The patients were placed in the appropriate surgical position, typically lateral decubitus with arm hyperflexion for VATS. The uniportal thoracoscopic wound was covered by a wound retractor (Alexis Wound Protector, Applied Medical, Rancho Santa Margarita, CA). An iatrogenic pneumothorax was created after dissecting into the chest cavity, and the lung lesions were mapped with clipping. The appropriate clipping location was determined based on preoperative chest CT radiographic findings, relative to anatomical landmarks such as pulmonary vessels and bronchi (Fig. 1A). Following the mapping of target lesions, double lung mechanical ventilation was reinitiated. Breath-holding was required in the group without NPA. In the group utilizing suction NPA, lung expansion was achieved by controlling suction through a Jackson-Pratt (JP) drain tube inserted into the pleural cavity near the uniportal wound protector. Afterward, the wound protector was rotated, and the uniportal wound was covered with an adhesive transparent film (Op-Site, Smith & Nephew) (Fig. 1B). Mechanical ventilation was maintained continuously during CBCT localization. The suction pressure was set at -81.6 cmH2O, equivalent to approximately − 8.0 kPa. The drain tip was placed at the lateral pleural cavity and was not directed toward the lung parenchyma. Suction was applied only during the CBCT localization workflow and was discontinued after adequate image acquisition. While there are no large studies directly guiding this parameter, the relevant literature on negative pressure effects in thoracic surgery clarified the rationale [6–8]. Lung expansion was successfully achieved within one minute using the suction technique through the uniportal wound, followed by CBCT scanning. In the non-NPA group, after clip mapping, lung expansion was attempted using anesthesiologist-controlled positive-pressure ventilation and breath-holding during CBCT acquisition without JP-drain suction. The uniportal wound was temporarily approximated. If the first CBCT image did not provide adequate visualization of the clip-to-nodule relationship, additional clips were placed and CBCT acquisition was repeated. These additional steps were included in the CBCT localization workflow time. There was no specific selection criteria for lesion size between the two groups. During the study period, negative-pressure assistance was introduced as a procedural modification to improve lung re-expansion during intraoperative CBCT localization. The operative team, hybrid operating room setting, CBCT system, and general localization workflow remained consistent throughout the study period. The temporal distribution of cases by group is provided in Table 1 to allow assessment of possible chronologic or learning-curve effects. However, tumors smaller than 2 cm in diameter were included in this CBCT method, while anatomic segmentectomy or lobectomy was recommended for lesions located more than 3 cm from the pleura.

CBCT image construction and definition of intraoperative CBCT localization workflow time & residual pleural air

During lung tumor resection, intraoperative CBCT is utilized to precisely localize the mapping clip in relation to the target pulmonary nodules. This process involves a mobile CBCT system rotating around the patient, capturing a series of low-dose X-ray projections. These projections are acquired from multiple angles, generating a set of two-dimensional X-ray images. To construct a high-resolution three-dimensional (3D) volumetric image, advanced reconstruction algorithms, including back-projection and iterative reconstruction techniques, are applied. The reconstructed 3D image can be viewed in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes, allowing surgeons to accurately assess the location of the nodule relative to metallic clip markers and plan the resection accordingly.

When the clipping positions were difficult to determine relative to the target tumor, CBCT needed to be repeated after placing multiple additional clips to ensure optimal localization of the lesion. The wedge resection is approximated to a rhomboid shape, with the direction of the cut line determined by the distance between the metallic clip marker and the enhanced lesion visibility on CBCT. Besides, the resection margins were measured and confirmed by pathologists based on the resected specimen after the staple line was removed. " CBCT localization workflow time " was defined as the elapsed time from initiation of CBCT localization preparation, including patient/table positioning, lung-expansion maneuver, temporary wound sealing or NPA setup when applicable, image acquisition, reconstruction, and assessment of clip-to-nodule relationship, until a diagnostically acceptable CBCT image was obtained. If initial visualization was inadequate, additional clip placement and repeated CBCT acquisition were included in the workflow time. Intraoperative pneumothorax during localization was graded based on CBCT radiographic findings, with “mild” defined as < 2 cm separation of the lung from the chest wall and “obvious” as > 2 cm [9, 10]. Because all patients underwent thoracoscopic pleural entry, the outcome of interest was not clinical pneumothorax but CBCT-defined incomplete lung expansion/residual pleural air during the localization phase. This endpoint was assessed on intraoperative CBCT images according to the maximal separation between the lung surface and the chest wall.

Statistical analysis

The relationships between the outcomes of two sets of patient characteristics were evaluated. Univariate comparisons between groups used Welch’s t-test for continuous variables and chi-square tests for multi-level categorical variables; Fisher’s exact test was used for 2 × 2 tables. The distribution of airway management type was compared between groups, and airway type was included in sensitivity analyses when imbalance was observed. Effect sizes are reported as Hedges’ g for continuous variables, Cohen’s h for binary proportions, and Cramer’s V/phi for categorical associations. A power analysis based on our previous study was conducted to determine the appropriate sample size [7], assuming a significance level (α) of 0.05, a power of 80% (β = 0.20), and an effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.7 to detect a clinically meaningful difference in CBCT localization workflow time between groups. No formal a priori sample-size calculation was performed because this was a retrospective study of consecutive eligible cases. Effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals were reported to describe the magnitude and precision of associations. The multivariable models were kept parsimonious because of the limited number of outcome events. Multivariable logistic regression modeled CTILA events; multivariable linear regression modeled CBCT localization workflow time. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26 (SPSS Inc. in Chicago, IL).

Results

Patient demographics and nodule characteristics

The demographic characteristics of the patients enrolled in this study are summarized in Table 1. Group allocation was not randomized and was influenced by anesthesiologist preference and the surgical team’s evolving experience. The study included 55 consecutive adult patients (mean age, 59.6 ± 11.03 years; 42 women and 13 men), who underwent thoracoscopic surgery with intraoperative CBCT guidance. Among the 55 patients included, the number of target nodules per patient was distributed as follows: 42 patients (76.4%) had a single target nodule, 10 patients (18.2%) had two target nodules, and 3 patients (5.5%) had three nodules requiring localization. In patients with multiple target nodules, the nodules were located in different lobes on the same side (ipsilateral) in 5 cases and in different segments within the same lobe in 4 cases. Radiologically, the nodules were classified as pure GGNs in 47 cases (71.2%), part-solid nodules in 4 cases (6.1%), and solid nodules in 15 cases (22.7%). All resection margins were pathologically assessed after removal of the staple line. No patient required conversion to lobectomy or thoracotomy because of localization failure, and no case required additional anatomic resection for an insufficient localization margin. The mean diameter of the nodules was 9.9 ± 3.00 mm (range, 9.1–10.8 mm). Notably, 6 nodules (10.9%) were located more than 3 cm deep from the visceral pleura. In total, 66 wedge resections were performed; in three cases, a single wedge specimen contained two nodules, resulting in localization and resection of multiple lesions within the same specimen. The pathologic diagnoses included adenocarcinoma in 45 nodules (81.8%), metastatic carcinoma in 4 (7.3%), and benign in 6 (10.9%). All patients remained well at a median follow-up time of 10.7 ± 3.6 months.

Evaluation of CBCT-guided localization outcomes with versus without negative-pressure assistance for lung tumor resection

In total, 55 patients received conventional ventilation combined with intrapleural suction NPA (Group 1, n = 29) or without assistance (Group 2, n = 26). There were no significant intergroup differences in demographics, or lung lesion characteristics (Table 1). All patients completed the procedure and successfully emerged from anesthesia. The frequency and saturation of respiration and heart rate were maintained within the normal range; SpO2 was > 98% in all patients. Differences in lung parenchyma (e.g., emphysema, fibrosis) were not key factors, and no significant differences in comorbidities were observed between the two groups. The distribution of airway management type was balanced between the NPA and non-NPA groups (95% CI 0.16 ~ 2.65; p = 0.712). In sensitivity analysis adjusted for airway management type, the association between NPA and lower CTILA remained directionally consistent, suggesting that the observed effect was not solely explained by airway-management differences.

CBCT localization workflow time was significantly shorter in Group 1 than in Group 2 (17.31 ± 7.13 vs. 25.69 ± 9.57 min; mean difference − 8.38 min; 95% CI − 13.00 to − 3.76; p = 0.0007; Hedges’ g = − 0.987) (Table 2). NPA reduced CBCT localization workflow time by minimizing lung collapse, allowing for quicker lesion identification. CTILA occurred less frequently in Group 1 (3/29, 10.3%) than in Group 2 (13/26, 50.0%) (risk difference − 39.7%, 95% CI − 61.8% to − 17.5%; Fisher’s exact p = 0.0023; odds ratio 8.67). There were significant intergroup differences in the surgical duration, with a significantly lower incidence of CTILA during intraoperative localization observed in the NPA group. Multivariable logistic regression confirmed a higher CTILA risk in Group 2 (adjusted OR 7.56, 95% CI 1.03–55.61; p = 0.047). In a sensitivity multivariable model that additionally included CBCT acquisition number, each additional CBCT acquisition was associated with a higher likelihood of CTILA (adjusted OR, 6.12; 95% CI, 1.14–32.87; p = 0.035). The non-NPA group remained independently associated with CTILA in this model (adjusted OR, 6.66; 95% CI, 1.45–30.49; p = 0.015). Because repeated CBCT acquisition may reflect poor target visualization secondary to incomplete lung expansion, this variable was considered a procedural efficiency marker rather than a baseline confounder. No major intraoperative complications were observed. NPA reduced the need for repeated ventilation adjustments during CBCT localization and was not associated with wound swelling, hemothorax, infection, or lung parenchymal bleeding.

Discussion

Recent advances in localization methods and their challenges

Intraoperative identification of small pulmonary nodules, particularly GGN-dominant early-stage lung cancers, remains a considerable challenge, primarily due to the limited visual and tactile feedback available during VATS without image-guided assistance. To overcome this limitation, a variety of preoperative and intraoperative localization techniques have been developed. Perioperative dye localization using bronchoscopic methods, electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy [11, 12], intrathoracoscopic ultrasonic technique, or CT guidance [13] has demonstrated efficacy in targeting peripheral pulmonary lesions. Several factors were associated with localization failure, including diffuse pleural dye spillage, invisible dye markings, and the presence of CTILA during localization. Bronchoscopic localization, typically combined with fluoroscopy or CT image-guided techniques, has been associated with a pneumothorax risk of approximately 2.0%, according to a meta-analysis [14]. This incidence is significantly lower than the reported pneumothorax rates of 10–29% associated with transthoracic CT-guided needle localization of peripheral pulmonary lesions [15, 16]. A meta-analysis demonstrated that bronchoscopic localization typically requires general anesthesia to minimize discrepancies between the virtual navigation images and the actual anatomical location during the procedure [11]. Moreover, this approach requires additional personnel to perform bronchoscopy for lesion localization, whether preoperatively or intraoperatively, leading to increased procedure time and higher medical costs.

Contemporary CBCT localization techniques and modifications in this study

More recently, the use of CBCT thoracoscopic surgery has been investigated intensively, attempting to one step localization that follow general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation [2]. This imaging and reconstruction technique enhances intraoperative tumor localization accuracy, reduces reliance on preoperative markings, and improves surgical outcomes in lung resection, offering real-time, three-dimensional imaging for accurate localization of nodules, even those beyond the reach of conventional bronchoscopy. CBCT-guided marking provides reliable visual landmarks, enhancing the precision of minimally invasive resections, minimizing unnecessary parenchymal resection, and potentially shortening operative time. Notably, a higher number of marking attempts was significantly correlated with an increased localization success rate [11]. However, ensuring lung expansion and preventing the presence of intrapleural air interruption is also a crucial technique in successfully localizing the target lesion during CBCT scanning [17, 18]. CBCT identified atelectasis obscuring the target in 20% cases [3]. The higher event rate in the non-NPA group should be interpreted in the context of the revised endpoint definition. This study evaluated CBCT-defined incomplete lung expansion/residual pleural air during an intentional intraoperative thoracoscopic pleural-entry setting rather than spontaneous or postoperative clinical pneumothorax. Because CBCT is sensitive for detecting residual pleural air that may compromise visualization of small GGNs, the event rate is not directly comparable with pneumothorax rates reported in prior preoperative or postoperative localization studies. NPA facilitates pulmonary expansion and enables the localization of lung lesions, demonstrating clear advantages in reducing intraoperative pneumothorax complications. They can provide satisfactory gas exchange in order to allow CBCT for thoracic surgery.

No patient needed to discontinue these procedures or suspend tidal ventilation during CBCT. Safe suctioning NPA involves several critical factors, including catheter placement, suction depth and duration, and pressure settings. As the catheter advances deeper into the occupied pleural cavity, negative pressure downstream of the catheter increases due to heightened resistance to backflow [7, 8]. Consequently, the preset suction pressure may be transmitted directly to the surrounding tissues. To minimize the risk of excessive negative pressure and potential tissue or mucosal trauma, careful regulation of vacuum pressure is essential to ensure procedural safety. It is imperative to exercise caution when regulating vacuum levels, as considerations of safety, legality, and ethics become paramount when managing known risks during a procedure that may outwardly appear straightforward, such as prolonged suctioning. The extent to which clinical practice adheres to “best practice” or “evidence-based” guidelines remains a subject for further investigation [2]. However, the principle that neglecting known risks during procedures extends beyond suctioning alone and can encompass a wide array of medical practices is noteworthy.

Clinical advantages of NPA-assisted lung tumor localization

In this study, the NPA technique reduced CBCT localization workflow time and minimized excessive CTILA complications during CBCT localization. For lesions, particularly pure GGNs, that could not be localized using conventional methods due to the aforementioned limitations of dye markings or CTILA, the combination of NPA with metallic clip placement proved to be an effective alternative strategy. This strategy offers several advantages, including controlled ventilation under general anesthesia, a simple surgical workflow, and high localization accuracy. Furthermore, performing thoracoscopic surgery immediately after marking helps mitigate localization-related complications and minimizes the impact of respiratory motion, as the applied suction pressure stabilizes the surgical field and enhances localization accuracy. The present findings suggest that NPA may be most useful as a workflow adjunct during intraoperative CBCT-guided uniportal VATS rather than as a replacement for established localization methods. Compared with preoperative CT-guided hook-wire or dye localization, the technique avoids a separate preoperative puncture step and allows localization and resection in a single anesthetic session. Compared with bronchoscopic localization, it does not require additional bronchoscopy equipment or personnel. Its main practical advantage is maintenance of lung expansion and reduction of residual pleural air during CBCT imaging, which may improve visualization of small or GGN-dominant lesions and reduce repeated acquisition or additional clip placement. However, because this study was retrospective and non-randomized, these comparative advantages should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Professionals are typically aware of and acknowledge these challenges within their work, adapting their approaches to minimize associated risks. To what extent practice does and further randomized practice is yet to be examined.

Limitations

Nevertheless, we acknowledge that our study has several limitations. First, we recognize that this study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit its broader applicability. Second, the study was designed as an initial evaluation of the additional suction NPA. Because the study was retrospective and non-randomized, selection bias and chronologic bias cannot be fully excluded. The NPA technique was introduced during the study period as a technical modification to address incomplete lung expansion during intraoperative CBCT localization. Although the relatively short study interval and the use of the same operative team, CBCT platform, and localization workflow suggest that the observed differences were mainly related to the procedural effect of NPA rather than operator learning alone, a learning-curve effect cannot be completely ruled out. Future prospective studies with randomized allocation or adjustment for calendar time are needed to confirm these findings. The adjusted odds ratio for CTILA had a wide confidence interval, indicating that although the association favored NPA, the exact magnitude of the protective effect remains imprecise. These findings should therefore be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating until confirmed in larger prospective cohorts. Although smoking exposure and available CT parenchymal findings were evaluated, the retrospective design did not permit complete standardization of baseline lung compliance across groups. Therefore, residual confounding by pulmonary reserve, smoking history, or radiographic emphysema/fibrosis cannot be fully excluded. This introduces potential selection bias, which patients were assigned based on surgeon preference and real-world feasibility. NPA may not be suitable for all lung surgeries, especially those involving vascular resection. However, we have not observed an increase in lung injury or air leakage with NPA. Although the sample size was relatively small, future multi-center studies are needed to confirm our findings across different clinical settings. We acknowledge that a larger sample size would strengthen the statistical power of our conclusions. However, despite the limited number of patients, the consistency of our findings suggests a clear benefit of the additional suction assistance during CBCT localization. Our study serves as an important preliminary step, paving the way for future research with larger populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, NPA technique can provide both continuous two-lung ventilation and oxygen saturation, which may provide an effective approach for the localization of CBCT hybrid VATS.
