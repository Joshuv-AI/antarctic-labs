Abemaciclib in HR+, HER2− Breast Cancer: A Narrative Review of the Clinical Evidence
Authors: Miguel Martin, Alexandru Rosca, Alexandra Searles Vitko, Monique Coersmeyer, Katheryn Moreira, Huiping Li, Erica L Mayer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHormone receptor-positive (HR+), human epidermal growth factor 2-negative (HER2–) breast cancer comprises approximately 70% of all breast cancer cases. In early-stage breast cancer, adjuvant endocrine therapy (ET) reduces recurrence and mortality, while in advanced/metastatic breast cancer (MBC), ET prolongs survival and delays the use of chemotherapy. However, there remains a need for agents that further improve outcomes across the spectrum of breast cancer presentations. Cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors, such as abemaciclib, ribociclib, and palbociclib, are used in combination with ET as a standard of care first-line option in HR+, HER2– advanced disease to improve survival outcomes. Moreover, the addition of abemaciclib and ribociclib to adjuvant ET reduces risk of cancer recurrence, with abemaciclib also improving overall survival. Here, we provide a comprehensive analysis of the clinical trials that have demonstrated the efficacy of abemaciclib across the HR+, HER2– breast cancer continuum, improving outcomes in both high-risk, node-positive early breast cancer as well as in advanced disease. Abemaciclib has also been shown to be effective when combined with a variety of ET options, including aromatase inhibitors, tamoxifen, fulvestrant, imlunestrant, or as monotherapy, and has shown efficacy regardless of prior CDK4/6 inhibitor exposure, ESR1 or PI3K pathway mutational status, menopausal status, and in both endocrine-sensitive and endocrine-resistant breast cancer. Importantly, the safety profile of abemaciclib has been consistent across trials and allows the administration of the drug over long periods of time when needed, particularly if dose-reduction strategies are employed. Together, the data summarized in this publication help inform clinical decision making regarding the role of abemaciclib in the treatment of patients with both early and metastatic HR+, HER2– breast cancer.Keyword: Abemaciclib; early breast cancer; metastatic breast cancer

Key Summary Points

Abemaciclib, a continuously dosed cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitor, is a targeted treatment option for hormone receptor positive (HR +), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 negative (HER2 −), early and advanced/metastatic breast cancer.The phase 3 monarchE trial assessed the benefit of adding 2 years of abemaciclib to standard endocrine therapy in high-risk, node-positive HR+, HER2– early breast cancer. The addition of abemaciclib resulted in statistically significant improvements in invasive disease-free survival and overall survival, as well as clinically meaningful improvements in distant relapse-free survival.In metastatic breast cancer, abemaciclib plus endocrine therapy improved response rates and significantly improved progression-free survival in both endocrine-sensitive (MONARCH 3) and endocrine-resistant (MONARCH 2, postMONARCH, EMBER-3) populations, with demonstrated overall survival benefits in MONARCH 2.The safety profile of abemaciclib was manageable and consistent across trials.Adverse events such as diarrhea and neutropenia can be effectively managed through dose reductions and/or interruptions without compromising efficacy.

Introduction

Hormone receptor-positive (HR +), human epidermal growth factor 2-negative (HER2–) breast cancer accounts for approximately 70% of all breast cancer cases and remains a significant contributor to global cancer burden [1, 2]. While endocrine therapy (ET) has long been the cornerstone of treatment across both early and advanced disease stages, resistance to ET continues to limit long-term efficacy and patient outcomes [3]. In recent years, the addition of cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors has revolutionized the HR+ breast cancer treatment landscape, becoming a standard of care in both early and advanced/metastatic breast cancer (MBC), owing to significant improvements in clinical outcomes [2, 4, 5].

The efficacy of abemaciclib, a selective CDK4/6 inhibitor, has been demonstrated across the HR+, HER2– breast cancer continuum through an extensive body of clinical trials (Fig. 1), from high-risk, node-positive early breast cancer (EBC) to MBC (Table 1) [6–15]. Specifically, abemaciclib has been shown to improve overall survival (OS), reduce risk of recurrence, delay cancer progression, and enhance response rates [6–10, 13, 16, 17]. Moreover, abemaciclib has shown clinical benefit regardless of prior CDK4/6 inhibitor exposure, endocrine sensitivity, menopausal status, or the presence of ESR1 or PI3K pathway mutations [7, 9, 10, 18–20]. Abemaciclib is effective in combination with aromatase inhibitors (AI), tamoxifen, fulvestrant, imlunestrant, or as monotherapy [2, 4]. The safety profile of abemaciclib has been consistent across trials and is generally manageable, with dose-reduction strategies enabling effective mitigation of adverse events without compromising efficacy [6, 8–10, 14, 17, 21–23].

Abemaciclib plays a pivotal role in the ongoing efforts to improve survival and quality of life (QoL) for individuals with breast cancer. This narrative review provides a comprehensive and clinically relevant resource to guide treatment decisions and optimize patient outcomes.

Abemaciclib Mechanism of Action

Abemaciclib is a highly potent, orally bioavailable, brain-penetrant CDK4/6 inhibitor that, in enzymatic assays, has demonstrated 14-fold higher selectivity for CDK4/cyclin D1 (the activity of which drives cell proliferation in solid tumors such as breast cancer) than CDK6/cyclin D3 (which is important for hematopoiesis and differentiation of blood cells) [24, 25]. Continuous exposure to abemaciclib has been shown to block phosphorylation of retinoblastoma protein (Rb) leading to arrest of the G1/S cell cycle transition and of subsequent cell division [26, 27]. Further, dose-dependent induction of apoptosis has been observed with abemaciclib [27, 28]. Overall, these findings indicate that abemaciclib can both slow the progression of cancer cell growth through arrest of the cell cycle and can also kill cancer cells via induction of apoptosis.

Whereas total CDK6 knockout is associated with the development of neutropenia, this is not the case for CDK4 knockout, which instead results in elevated levels of myeloid progenitors but without changes in numbers of differentiated myeloid cells [29]. The lower selectivity for CDK6 than CDK4, along with higher total CDK4 inhibitory capacity, relative to other CDK4/6 inhibitors (ribociclib and palbociclib) [30], leads to less dose-limiting neutropenia with abemaciclib [25]. Therefore, unlike ribociclib and palbociclib, which require intermittent dosing to allow for bone marrow recovery [31–33], abemaciclib can be dosed continuously.

Abemaciclib has demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetics, allowing sustained CDK4/6 target engagement and inhibition with continuous dosing strategies [31]. The importance of maintaining continuous target engagement was demonstrated in the phase 2 neoMONARCH neoadjuvant study (NCT02441946), where continuous administration either as monotherapy or in combination with the AI anastrozole resulted in robust and sustained cell-cycle arrest, as shown by significant reductions in Ki-67 expression and consistent downregulation of cell-cycle-associated genes [34].

Clinical Evidence

HR+, HER2− High-Risk, Node-Positive Early Breast Cancer

Most patients with HR+, HER2− breast cancer present with early-stage disease, so we first examined the role of abemaciclib in this disease setting. Critically, effective interventions at this stage can prevent relapse over long-term follow-up and are considered potentially curative [35, 36]. Patients with higher risk HR+ breast cancer may have greater chances of recurrence in the first few years after diagnosis and treatment, justifying additional measures in that space to prevent metastatic disease [37]. However, not all patients will experience recurrence, and it is important to identify those at higher recurrence risk to justify treatment intensification. Among prognostic factors, axillary lymph node (ALN) status remains the single most significant marker in EBC. Together, the clinicopathological features of nodal positivity, tumor size, tumor grade, and Ki-67 provide reliable ways of identifying a risk of recurrence [38]. Patients with N1 high-risk disease—which can be defined by additional risk features such as tumor size ≥ 5 cm and/or grade 3—have significantly elevated risk of recurrence and mortality compared with those with N1 non-high-risk disease [39]. Real-world data has demonstrated a 14.3% absolute difference in 5-year invasive disease-free survival (IDFS) rates (75.7% versus 90.0%) and a 6.3% difference in 5-year OS rates (86.5% versus 92.8%) between these groups [40]. Moreover, in this study, patients with 4+ ALN or 1–3 ALN and a tumor size ≥ 5cm and/or a grade 3 tumor had a 5-year IDFS of 70.9% and a 5-year OS of 81.6%, similar to patients with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), who had 5-year IDFS and OS of 74.3% and 79.2%, respectively [40]. These findings underscore the importance of early intervention and more intensive treatment strategies for early-stage patients at high risk of recurrence, as such approaches may help eradicate micrometastatic disease and improve long-term outcomes.

Adjuvant Abemaciclib in High-Risk Node-Positive Early Breast Cancer: monarchE

The monarchE trial (NCT0315599) was a phase 3, open-label, randomized study that evaluated adjuvant abemaciclib added to standard-of-care ET (AI or tamoxifen) versus ET alone in patients with high-risk, node-positive HR+, HER2− EBC [41]. The intent-to-treat (ITT) population (n = 5637) comprised two patient cohorts: cohort 1 (91%) included patients with high-risk clinicopathological factors (≥ 4 ALNs or 1–3 ALN with an additional high risk feature including histologic grade 3 or tumor size ≥ 5 cm), and cohort 2 (9%), which also included patients with 1–3 ALN without grade 3 or tumor ≥ 5 cm but with centrally assessed Ki-67 ≥ 20% [15]. Recruitment for cohort 2 began after cohort 1, and was added at the request of the FDA to explore the potential benefit of abemaciclib in patients with intermediate-risk clinicopathological features and highly proliferative tumors. Patients were randomized 1:1 to receive abemaciclib 150 mg twice daily (BID) plus ET or ET alone. ET was physician’s choice, including AI or tamoxifen, with or without or a gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist in premenopausal patients [15, 41]. Abemaciclib was administered for 2 years concurrent with ET, the period of peak recurrence in high-risk EBC, and ET was subsequently continued in both arms for an additional 3–8 years as clinically indicated.

The monarchE primary endpoint of IDFS was met at 15.5 months of follow-up, with a statistically significant 25% reduction in risk of developing an IDFS event [41], leading to global approvals for abemaciclib in the EBC setting [42, 43]. Continued follow-up of the study population has demonstrated a carryover effect through 5 years [13], followed by stabilization of the curves through 7 years, with an absolute benefit in IDFS of 6.5% at 7 years (Fig. 2a) [11] in the ITT population. OS is the gold standard for establishing clinical benefit of cancer therapies [44]; however, few trials with standard of care ET have provided an OS benefit in HR+, HER2− EBC owing to the long follow-up needed to observe differences in survival, along with treatment imbalances and evolving therapies that improve outcomes across both study arms. Despite this high bar for OS, monarchE was designed to test for statistically significant OS At the primary OS analysis, abemaciclib plus ET demonstrated a statistically significant and clinically meaningful 16% reduction in the risk of death in the ITT population compared with ET alone (stratified hazard ratio [HR] 0.84; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.72–0.98; two-sided P = 0.027), with a corresponding HR for cohort 1 of 0.84 (95% CI 0.71–0.98; nominal P = 0.024) and a 2% improvement in survival rates in both the ITT population and in cohort 1 [11]. This benefit was observed despite a numerically greater use of CDK4/6 inhibitors post progression in patients with distant recurrence on the ET alone arm (294/565 patients; 52%) than those on the abemaciclib plus ET arm (137/407 patients; 34%). Of note, there were fewer patients living with metastatic breast cancer in the abemaciclib plus ET arm (6.4%) than in the ET alone arm (9.4%). Treatment benefit for IDFS, DRFS, and OS was consistent across key subgroups, including patients with N1 disease, those who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and younger patients.

Long-term safety findings in monarchE were consistent with earlier analyses [15, 41]. In the abemaciclib plus ET arm, the most common all-grade treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were diarrhea (84%), neutropenia (46%), and fatigue (41%), and in the ET-alone arm were arthralgia (38%), hot flush (23%), and fatigue (18%); TEAEs were mostly grade ≤ 2. The most frequent grade ≥ 3 TEAEs in the abemaciclib plus ET arm were neutropenia (20%), leukopenia (11%), and diarrhea (8%); in the ET-alone arm, these grade ≥ 3 TEAEs all occurred in ≤ 1% [15]. Other grade ≥ 3 adverse events (AEs) identified as of interest included venous thromboembolic events (VTE), pulmonary embolism (PE), and interstitial lung disease (ILD), which occurred in 1.4%, 1.0%, and < 1.0% of patients in the abemaciclib plus ET arm, respectively, compared with < 1.0% for all events in the ET-alone arm [15]. Of note, there is emerging evidence that suggests these AEs of interest may be a class effect of CDK4/6 inhibitors [45, 46]. The incidence of serious adverse events (SAEs) regardless of causality was approximately 7% in both arms [13]. While increases in aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase were observed in 12% and 13% of patients receiving abemaciclib plus ET, respectively, compared with 5% and 6%, respectively, for patients receiving ET alone, there were no cases meeting Hy’s Law criteria [15].

As stated, any-grade diarrhea occurred in 84% of abemaciclib-treated patients, with grade 1/2 events occurring in 76%, and grade ≥ 3 in 8%; notably, diarrhea tended to occur early during treatment (median time to onset of 8 days) [47]. The incidence of diarrhea decreased over time and in most cases lasted for < 1 week without recurrence [47]. Per protocol, patients were instructed to take anti-diarrheal agents at the first sign of diarrhea. Discontinuation owing to diarrhea was infrequent (5%) and when it did occur was mostly (84%) due to grade 1/2 diarrhea. Discontinuations occurred mainly in the first 3 months and mostly in patients without a prior dose reduction (74%) [47]. The need for dose modifications owing to diarrhea decreased over time, with ≤ 0.6% of patients receiving dose adjustments in the second year of treatment [47]. In addition, while the incidence of fatigue was also higher in the abemaciclib arm than with placebo, this was mainly low grade and infrequently associated with dose reductions or discontinuations, and patient-reported outcome data did not reflect this between-arm difference in fatigue [47].

AE management is important for an optimal patient experience on abemaciclib. In addition to appropriate use of supportive care medications, dose reductions are a key component of AE management [48]. In monarchE, dose reductions were used in 43% of patients for AE management, and did not impact treatment efficacy. More specifically, in monarchE, consistent IDFS and DRFS rates were observed regardless of relative dose intensity [21], and these findings were confirmed in a time-dependent Cox proportional hazards model which also showed no significant differences in IDFS (HR 0.91; 95% CI, 0.73–1.13) and DRFS (HR 0.94; 0.74–1.20) in patients receiving full or lower doses of abemaciclib [21]. Of note, the TRADE study in patients with HR+HER2− EBC found that an early abemaciclib dose-escalation strategy, in which patients received abemaciclib at 50 mg BID for 2 weeks, then 100mg BID for 2 weeks, then 150mg BID onward, allowed the majority of patients to reach and/or maintain target dosing and significantly reduced abemaciclib discontinuation for any reason [49]. Compared with the 40% discontinuation or dose-reduction rate at 12 weeks in monarchE, this dose-escalation strategy was associated with a discontinuation/dose reduction rate of 29.2%, with 70.8% of patients able to reach and maintain the target abemaciclib dose of 150mg twice daily at 12 weeks, and only 6.7% discontinuing abemaciclib early [49].

Long-term patient-reported outcome (PRO) data have shown preserved health-related QoL (HRQoL) with the addition of adjuvant abemaciclib to ET [50]. Patients reported that the side effect burden was low, with most reporting being bothered “a little bit” or “not at all” by side effects during treatment [50].

In summary, the monarchE trial demonstrated that adjuvant abemaciclib plus ET in patients with high-risk, node-positive HR+, HER2− EBC resulted in statistically significant improvement in IDFS and OS [11]. Importantly, the benefit extended beyond the 2-year treatment period, with a sustained reduction in IDFS and DRFS recurrence risk observed at 7 years [11, 13, 41]. These results represent the most mature efficacy data for any CDK4/6 inhibitor in the adjuvant setting. AEs were mainly low grade and manageable with supportive care and dose adjustments.

Progression From Early to Metastatic Breast Cancer

Approximately 70% of MBC cases are HR+ and patients typically receive endocrine-based therapies in the first-line setting [1]. While a minority of patients present with de novo MBC [51], the vast majority have progressed from EBC [51, 52]. When disease progresses from EBC to MBC, the therapeutic goal shifts from prevention of recurrence to control of metastatic growth and spread, prolongation of survival, and maintenance of QoL, including through delaying transition to chemotherapy for disease control [53, 54]. A substantial proportion of patients live for many years with MBC and may sequentially progress through multiple lines of ET-based systemic therapy (~60–80% from first-line to second-line, and ~60–75% from second-line to third-line).

While HR+ breast cancers in the advanced setting typically respond well to initial ET-based therapy, nearly all patients with advanced disease eventually develop resistance to ET, leading to disease progression and the need for alternative therapeutic strategies [23, 55]. Mechanisms of endocrine resistance may be mediated by a variety of mechanisms, including PIK3CA/mTOR/AKT pathway mutations or the development of mutations in the estrogen receptor (ESR1), leading to unregulated signaling [19, 56, 57]. Unlike ET-refractory disease, where tumors are no longer responsive to any form of ET, many patients with endocrine-resistant disease may still derive meaningful benefit from switching to a different ET backbone, especially when combined with targeted agents such as abemaciclib [3]. Importantly, therapies such as abemaciclib added to ET for MBC can delay the development of endocrine resistance, which has the potential to change the course of disease, delaying the need for chemotherapy, and potentially increasing OS [58, 59]. Biomarker testing (e.g., ESR1, PIK3CA/AKT) can help tailor treatment decisions in endocrine-resistant disease [19], but some therapies targeting these mutations may pose administration and toxicity challenges, and may have restricted indications [60]. These limitations underscore the value of therapies such as abemaciclib, which has demonstrated consistent benefit across patients regardless of mutational status [19].

The complexity of the HR+, HER2− MBC treatment landscape, including the evolving role of biomarker testing and the lack of clarity around optimal sequencing of both ET and non-ET therapies, makes it challenging to identify those patients most likely to derive benefit from each treatment (see Fig. 3 for a summary of the proposed place of abemaciclib in such therapy). However, the treatment goal for patients with HR+, HER2− MBC is clear: to extend survival, alleviate symptoms, and maintain QoL [53, 61].

First-Line Abemaciclib in Combination with AI in Patients with Endocrine Sensitive HR+, HER2− Advanced Breast Cancer: MONARCH 3

For patients with endocrine-sensitive MBC, including those with de novo disease or late relapse, combining ET (which targets the hormonal signaling pathway) with abemaciclib (which inhibits CDK4/6 and induces sustained cell cycle arrest, senescence, and apoptosis) reduces the risk of progression by 46% and extends chemotherapy-free survival by 16 months [7]. This is important given the primary goal of first-line treatment in MBC is to prolong progression-free survival (PFS) while maintaining QoL through delaying chemotherapy treatment [62, 63]. PFS is a highly informative first-line endpoint as it directly measures the duration of disease control and is not confounded by subsequent therapies [64].

The efficacy and safety of first-line abemaciclib plus AI was investigated in postmenopausal women with ET-sensitive HR+, HER2− MBC in the phase 3 placebo-controlled MONARCH 3 study (NCT02246621), in which PFS was the primary endpoint [23]. At baseline, 41% of patients in the abemaciclib arm had de novo metastatic disease, 53% had visceral metastases, and 22% had bone-only metastases [23]. Patients were randomized 2:1 to receive abemaciclib 150 mg or placebo BID with anastrozole 1 mg or letrozole 2.5 mg once daily (QD) until disease progression (N = 493) [23]. Abemaciclib plus AI demonstrated improved PFS as well as a clinically meaningful but not statistically significant improvement in OS compared with AI alone [7, 23, 65]. At a median of 17.8 months’ follow-up, median PFS was significantly greater in the abemaciclib plus AI arm (median not reached) than in the AI plus placebo arm (14.7 months) with a HR of 0.54 (95% CI, 0.41–0.72; P = 0.000021) [23]. This improvement in median PFS was sustained after a median follow-up of 8.1 years (29.0 months in the abemaciclib arm versus 14.8 months in the placebo arm; HR 0.535, 95% CI, 0.429–0.668; P < 0.0001) corresponding to an absolute difference at this time point of 14.3 months (Fig. 2b) [7].

In the ITT population, abemaciclib + AI provided a 20% relative risk reduction for the secondary endpoint of OS with a 13.1-month improvement in median OS, although not meeting statistical significance (66.8 months in the abemaciclib plus AI arm versus 53.7 months in the AI arm; HR 0.804; 95% CI, 0.637–1.015, P = 0.0664) [7]. In subgroup analyses, OS was numerically longer with abemaciclib plus AI versus AI alone in both patients with visceral disease as well as those with bone-only disease, although statistical significance was not reached [7]. Chemotherapy-free survival (CFS) was significantly longer in the abemaciclib arm than the placebo arm (46.7 versus 30.6 months; HR 0.69; 95% CI, 0.56–0.86; P = 0.0010) [7]. Moreover, there were no clinically meaningful differences in global HRQoL, functioning, or in most symptoms (with the exception of diarrhea) for patients receiving abemaciclib plus AI compared with AI alone [66].

Several possible factors may have contributed to the substantial numerical improvement in OS not meeting statistical significance [67]. First, post-discontinuation therapies (such as CDK4/6 inhibitors) may have contributed to slight dilution of OS benefit over time. Second, suboptimal timing of the final analysis may be a potential explanation, with the greatest separation of curves at the second interim analysis at 5.8 years whereas the final OS analysis was conducted at a median follow-up of 8.1 years [67]. In addition, as OS was not a primary endpoint of the study, the sample size was not powered to detect an OS benefit, and thus the study may have been relatively underpowered for the OS analysis.

In summary, in the pivotal phase 3 MONARCH 3 trial in postmenopausal women with HR+, HER2− MBC, abemaciclib plus an AI showed a significant improvement in PFS and a clinically meaningful increase in OS. Importantly, abemaciclib plus AI delayed the initiation of chemotherapy, an important goal in MBC [7]. The HRQoL results further support treatment with abemaciclib plus AI in this patient population [66].

First-Line or Second-Line Abemaciclib in Combination with Fulvestrant in Patients with Endocrine Resistant HR+, HER2− Advanced Breast Cancer: MONARCH 2

CDK4/6 inhibition in combination with ET has become a global standard-of-care first-line option for patients with HR+, HER2− MBC [2, 68]. While ET is generally efficacious and well-tolerated in most patients, the benefit diminishes as resistance develops, and subsequent disease progression is a major challenge [2, 69]. Resistance can emerge through multiple mechanisms and can impact both treatment efficacy and treatment sequencing [70]. Another therapeutic approach for targeting HR+ breast cancer involves directly targeting the estrogen receptor. The mechanism of AIs involves suppression of estrogen production, thus reducing estrogen availability for the estrogen receptor (ER), without direct degradation or antagonism of the receptor itself [71, 72]. In contrast, selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs) such as fulvestrant not only block ER signaling but also promote degradation of the receptor, resulting in a more complete shutdown of the estrogen signaling pathway [73]. Therefore, in patients with endocrine-resistant disease, switching the ET from AI monotherapy to a SERD and adding abemaciclib offers a more effective approach to delay progression and maintain endocrine sensitivity [74].

The phase 3 MONARCH 2 (NCT02107703) study investigated the efficacy and safety of abemaciclib plus fulvestrant in CDK4/6 inhibitor-naive patients with ET-resistant HR+, HER2− MBC who had experienced disease progression on previous ET, regardless of ESR1 or PI3K pathway mutations [14, 19]. The trial included both postmenopausal women as well as pre-/peri-menopausal women (receiving a GnRH agonist) who either progressed on first-line ET or relapsed on (neo)adjuvant or within 1 year of adjuvant ET; 59% of participants had received their most recent ET in the (neo)adjuvant setting, and ~38% in the metastatic setting [14]. Abemaciclib plus fulvestrant significantly improved median PFS (16.4 versus 9.3 months; HR 0.55; 95% CI 0.45, 0.68; P < 0.001) compared with placebo plus fulvestrant with 19.5 months’ follow-up [14]. PFS improvements were maintained after a median follow-up of 47.7 months, and a significant improvement in OS was also demonstrated with abemaciclib plus fulvestrant versus placebo plus fulvestrant (46.7 versus 37.3 months; HR 0.757; 95% CI, 0.606–0.945; P = 0.01) [74]. Benefit was generally consistent across subgroups, including a benefit in both PFS and OS across both first- and second-line subgroups, although a larger relative OS benefit was observed in subgroups associated with a poorer prognosis, including patients with visceral disease and primary resistance to ET [74]. PFS (Fig. 2c) and OS benefits were sustained with a median follow-up of approximately 80 months with absolute improvements in median PFS and OS of 7.6 months and 8.6 months, respectively [16]. Similarly, in patients with measurable disease, response rates with abemaciclib plus fulvestrant were higher than for placebo plus fulvestrant; ORR was 27% higher (48% versus 21%); and clinical benefit rate was significantly higher (73% versus 52%) [14].

Notably, abemaciclib plus fulvestrant demonstrated consistent PFS regardless of mutational status for both PIK3CA and ESR1 (Table 2) [19]. In addition, a consistent benefit was observed for abemaciclib plus fulvestrant, compared with placebo plus fulvestrant in terms of median OS in patients with PIK3CA-mutant tumors and PIK3CA-wild-type tumors, with HRs of 0.67 (95% CI 0.41–1.11) and 0.57 (95% CI 0.34–0.96), respectively, and in patients with ESR1-mutant or ESR1-wild-type tumors, with HRs of 0.71 (95% CI 0.45–1.14) and 0.40 (95% CI 0.23–0.69), respectively [19].

A post hoc analysis of MONARCH 2 data demonstrated that patients receiving abemaciclib plus fulvestrant experienced a significantly longer time to sustained deterioration compared with fulvestrant plus placebo on most patient-reported outcomes, including HrQoL, pain, function, and other symptoms [75].

These data in patients with ET-resistant HR+, HER2− MBC show that abemaciclib combined with fulvestrant significantly improved efficacy outcomes compared with placebo plus fulvestrant. Improvements in PFS and OS were observed regardless of PIK3CA or ESR1 mutation status; however, these results should be interepreted with some caution given that the analysis was post hoc and included small numbers of patients.

Abemaciclib in Combination with AI in Patients Without Prior Systemic Therapy or in Combination with Fulvestrant in Patients with Progression on Prior ET: MONARCH plus

Data from MONARCH 3 and MONARCH 2, showing improved outcome in abemaciclib-treated patients with HR+, HER2– MBC regardless of endocrine-resistance status, are further supported by the global phase 3 MONARCH plus trial (NCT02763566) [8, 76]. MONARCH plus was designed to compare the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of abemaciclib plus ET versus ET alone in postmenopausal women (n = 463) with HR+, HER2– MBC from China (~82%), Brazil (~9%), India (~7%), and South Africa (~2%), without prior systemic therapy in an advanced setting (cohort A; n = 306) or with progression on prior ET (cohort B; n = 157). Results from cohort A were consistent with MONARCH 3, showing improved median PFS and ORR with abemaciclib plus AI versus AI plus placebo [28.3 versus 14.7 months; HR 0.476, 95% CI, 0.348–0.649 (Fig. 2d) and 59% versus 32%] [8]. OS data were immature, but a trend toward OS benefit with abemaciclib plus AI was observed [8]. Similarly, results from patients experiencing progression on prior ET (cohort B) were consistent with those from MONARCH 2 [8], showing improved median PFS and ORR in patients receiving abemaciclib plus fulvestrant versus placebo plus fulvestrant [11.4 versus 5.6 months; HR 0.480, 95% CI, 0.322–0.715 (Fig. 2e) and 39% versus 11%] [8].

These data from MONARCH plus provide global confirmatory evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of abemaciclib in combination with AI in patients with endocrine-sensitive disease or fulvestrant in patients with endocrine-resistant HR+, HER2– MBC, reinforcing findings from MONARCH 2 and MONARCH 3 in a diverse patient population.

Second-Line Abemaciclib in Combination with Fulvestrant After Progression on a CDK4/6 inhibitor: postMONARCH

The positive results seen with the CDK4/6 inhibitors in MBC, along with their versatility of combinations with different endocrine therapeutics, raised the question of how these agents could be used across lines of therapy. Studies have shown that patients who progress on a CDK4/6 inhibitor have shorter PFS on their second-line therapy than those who are CDK4/6 inhibitor-naive [77]. Figure 3 summarizes the proposed place of abemaciclib in the treatment of MBC.

The efficacy and safety of abemaciclib plus fulvestrant in endocrine-resistant patients with HR+, HER2− MBC who experienced recurrence or progression on a CDK4/6 inhibitor (59% palbociclib, 33% ribociclib, 8% abemaciclib) [plus AI regardless of ESR1 or PI3K pathway mutations] was assessed in the phase 3 postMONARCH study (NCT05169567)] [10]. In this post-CDK4/6 setting, abemaciclib provided clinical benefit when added to fulvestrant; median PFS was 6.0 months in the abemaciclib plus fulvestrant group compared with 5.3 months in the placebo plus fulvestrant group, with an associated 27% reduction in risk of developing a PFS event (HR 0.73, 95% CI, 0.57–0.95) [10]. Further, numerically higher ORRs were observed in the abemaciclib plus fulvestrant versus placebo plus fulvestrant arm (17% versus 7%) [10].

Benefits were consistent across subgroups defined by duration of prior CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment and, consistent with MONARCH 2, a benefit of abemaciclib plus fulvestrant was observed regardless of mutation status (Table 2) [10]. Abemaciclib treatment benefit over placebo (PFS) was consistent between patients with (HR 0.79, 95% CI, 0.54–1.15) and without (HR 0.78, 0.54–1.12) ESR1 mutations and in patients with (HR 0.76, 0.50–1.12) and without (HR 0.80, 0.57–1.12) PIK3CA mutations [10].

These data from postMONARCH show that in patients with HR+, HER2– MBC with disease progression on a prior CDK4/6 inhibitor plus ET, continued use of a CDK4/6-containing regimen switching to abemaciclib plus fulvestrant demonstrates a statistically significant improvement in PFS [10]. Benefit was consistently observed across multiple prespecified and clinically relevant subgroups. These data therefore support the combination of abemaciclib plus fulvestrant as an additional targeted therapy option for patients with HR+, HER2− MBC even after disease progression following previous CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment [10].

First- or Second-Line Abemaciclib Plus Imlunestrant or Imlunestrant Alone: EMBER-3

Fulvestrant is a SERD broadly approved both as monotherapy and as part of combination therapy, but it has several limitations, including low bioavailability, necessitating administration via intramuscular injection (which can result in injection-related AEs), and the potential for acquired resistance during prolonged treatment [78, 79]. The introduction of oral SERDs such as imlunestrant has helped to address some of these limitations. Imlunestrant is a next-generation, brain-penetrating oral SERD and pure ER antagonist that delivers continuous ER inhibition, including in ESR1-mutated breast cancer [9, 80]. The phase 3 EMBER-3 trial (NCT04975308) enrolled patients who had experienced recurrence or progression on AI with or without a CDK4/6 inhibitor regardless of ESR1 or PI3K pathway mutations. Eligible patients had up to one prior line of ET for MBC, ± prior CDK4/6i, and were randomized to imlunestrant monotherapy, standard of care ET, or the combination of abemaciclib plus imlunestrant [8]. As monotherapy in patients with ESR1-mutated breast cancer, at a median follow-up of 27.6 months across both arms, imlunestrant demonstrated a significant improvement in median PFS compared with standard of care ET (5.5 months versus 3.8 months) with a HR for disease progression or death of 0.62 (95% CI 0.47–0.82 nominal P = 0.0007; Table 1) and, at a median follow-up of 29.5 months across both arms, a numerically favorable improvement in OS (HR 0.60; 0.43–0.86) [81, 82]. Imlunestrant monotherapy was approved in the USA and Japan in 2025, with European approval recommended by the European Medicines Agency, on the basis of these EMBER-3 trial results. In the comparison of imlunestrant plus abemaciclib versus imlunestrant monotherapy, in all patients, with a median follow-up of 25.2 months across both arms, median PFS was significantly improved with abemaciclib plus imlunestrant versus imlunestrant alone (10.9 versus 5.5 months; HR 0.59; 95% CI 0.47–0.74; nominal P < 0.0001) with a 41% reduced risk of progression compared with imlunestrant alone, regardless of mutational status (Table 2). Consistent benefits were observed regardless of visceral metastases, prior CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy, line of therapy, and mutational status [9, 81, 82]. Abemaciclib plus imlunestrant may become an option in patients with HR+, HER2– MBC, in both the first- and second-line setting and in patients with ESR1 or PI3K pathway mutations (Fig. 3) [9].

Third-Line and Beyond: Abemaciclib Monotherapy in MONARCH 1

In the third-line setting, among patients previously exposed to chemotherapy, response rates to further chemotherapy are modest (typically 10–20%), median durations of response are 4–6 months, and median PFS is ~3–4 months [83–85]. Moreover, these regimens can lead to toxicity and a decline in QoL, particularly in heavily pretreated patients.

The phase 2, single-arm, open label MONARCH 1 (NCT02102490) study demonstrated that continuous abemaciclib monotherapy dosing offers antitumor activity and manageable toxicities in patients (n = 132) with HR+, HER2− MBC who had previously progressed on or after ET and received one to two lines of chemotherapy [6], with no prior CDK4/6 inhibitor exposure. In these heavily pretreated patients with poor prognosis, abemaciclib monotherapy exhibited promising clinical activity, with an ORR of 20%, clinical benefit (complete response plus partial response plus ≥ 6 months of stable disease) rate of 42%, and median OS of 22.3 months [6]. Median PFS was 6.0 months, which represents an improvement for patients with relapsed or refractory disease, which is promising given the limited options available [6]. The oral route of administration of abemaciclib may offer advantages over other agents in this setting.

These data support the option of continuous dosing of abemaciclib monotherapy in heavily pretreated patients with HR+, HER2− MBC if not already utilized.

Abemaciclib has demonstrated a consistent safety profile in MBC when combined with an AI or with a SERD [9, 48]. Across the MBC clinical trials, almost all patients experienced ≥ 1 AE of any grade, with approximately one-half to two-thirds of all patients experiencing a grade ≥ 3 AE (Table 3) [7–10, 14].

The most common AEs (any grade) in patients receiving abemaciclib in MONARCH 2 and MONARCH 3 were diarrhea (86% and 81%), neutropenia (46% and 41%), and fatigue (40% for both), and grade ≥ 3 diarrhea occurred in 13% and 10%, grade ≥ 3 neutropenia in 24% and 21%, and grade ≥ 3 fatigue in 3% and 2%, of patients in MONARCH 2 and MONARCH 3, respectively [14, 23]. In MONARCH 1, all patients experienced ≥ 1 TEAE and 32 (24%) experienced SAEs [6]. The most common any-grade TEAEs were diarrhea (90%), fatigue (65%), nausea (64%), and decreased appetite (46%).

Across MONARCH 1, MONARCH 2, and MONARCH 3, diarrhea, which was typically low grade, occurred early in treatment and was manageable with supportive care and dose adjustments [6, 48]. In both MONARCH 2 and MONARCH 3, approximately 43% of patients had dose reductions due to AEs, most frequently grade 2 or 3 diarrhea or grade ≥ 3 neutropenia, and treatment discontinuation due to diarrhea was reported in 2.9% and 1.8% of patients, respectively, suggesting that dose modifications helped patients remain on therapy [48]. Importantly, dose reductions did not compromise efficacy [48].

In the EMBER-3 trial, there was a higher incidence of AEs in the imlunestrant–abemaciclib arm (98%) than in the imlunestrant monotherapy arm (83%) [9]. The most frequently reported AEs of any grade with imlunestrant–abemaciclib were diarrhea (86%), nausea (49%), neutropenia (48%), and anemia (44%), consistent with the safety profile of abemaciclib, with no oral SERD-specific safety signals, such as ocular or cardiac events [9]. There was also a higher incidence of grade ≥ 3 events (49% versus 17%), largely owing to a higher incidence of neutropenia (20% versus 5%), diarrhea (8% versus < 1%), and anemia (8% versus 2%).

While direct cross-trial comparisons are limited by factors such as differences in study design, patient populations, and outcome measures, there do appear to be distinct safety profiles for the different CDK4/6 inhibitors. Differences between the agents in this class in the occurrence of AEs, such as QTc prolongation, drug-induced liver injury grade ≥ 3 neutropenia, and diarrhea, suggest that each of the CDK4/6 inhibitors may have advantages and disadvantages that should be considered for each patient.

Real-World Evidence

Real-world evidence is consistent with observations from abemaciclib clinical trials, confirming both its long-term benefits and safety profile [86–88]. In patients with MBC, real-world use of abemaciclib plus ET was shown to be associated with PFS and response rates comparable to those reported in clinical trials, with median PFS in patients receiving abemaciclib as first-, second-, and third- or later-line therapy of 21.4 months, 11.0 months, and 10.1 months, respectively [88], and partial or complete responses in > 43% of patients [86]. Diarrhea is the most common toxicity in the real-world setting, affecting approximately two-thirds of treated patients and resulting in treatment discontinuation in 12% of patients; 47% of patients were prescribed anti-diarrheal medication [87].

While real-world evidence in EBC is limited, given the recent approval in this setting, available data shows high levels of persistence on abemaciclib, with 88% of patients with EBC persisting on therapy beyond 3 months, with persistence increasing to 93% in patients who received dose reductions [89].

Implications and Conclusions

Abemaciclib has demonstrated consistent and meaningful clinical benefit across the HR+, HER2− breast cancer continuum, with an extensive clinical trial portfolio involving over 9000 patients across six global phase 3 trials and multiple phase 1 and 2 trials [6, 9, 10, 14, 17, 23, 34, 41, 76, 90–94]. This is consistent with the findings for other agents in this class, with the CDK4/6 inhibitors in combination with ET considered a global standard-of-care in advanced disease [2], and both abemaciclib and ribociclib have approvals in HR+, HER2− early breast cancer with a high risk of recurrence, albeit with some differences in their exact labeled indications [22, 95].

In the adjuvant setting, the combination of abemaciclib with ET significantly improves OS [11] and reduces the risk of developing incurable metastatic disease, as evidenced by improved IDFS and DRFS compared with ET alone [15]. This benefit is particularly important for patients with high-risk, node-positive EBC, where recurrence rates remain substantial. Real world analyses show that at least one in six patients experience recurrence within 5 years, with peak risk between years 1 and 3 [96, 97]. Furthermore, 5-year IDFS rates have been reported to be 14% lower and 5-year OS rates 6% lower in patients with high-risk N1 disease compared with non-high-risk N1 disease [40]. The reduction in recurrence risk with adjuvant abemaciclib in this patient population can therefore be expected to substantially improve patient outcomes.

In advanced disease, abemaciclib has shown substantial improvements in PFS and OS in both endocrine-sensitive and endocrine-resistant populations [7, 8, 10, 74]. The efficacy of abemaciclib extends across diverse patient subgroups, including those with prior CDK4/6 inhibitor exposure, ESR1 or PI3K pathway mutations, and varying menopausal status [10, 19]. Abemaciclib has also demonstrated promising results in combination with next-generation therapies such as the oral SERD imlunestrant [9], and retains single-agent activity in heavily pretreated patients for whom ET and chemotherapy may no longer be viable options [6]. Importantly, the safety profile of abemaciclib is consistent and manageable, with dose-reduction strategies enabling effective AE management without compromising efficacy.

Available data reinforce the role of the CDK4/6 inhibitors in HR+, HER2– breast cancer, and in particular the role of abemaciclib in improving outcomes and QoL for patients with high-risk, node positive, HR+, HER2– breast cancer, and provide a strong foundation for clinical decision-making in both early and advanced settings (Fig. 3).
