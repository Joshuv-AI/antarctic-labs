Consistency Training Can Entrench Misalignment
David Demitri Africa, Arathi Mani

Abstract
Consistency training encourages a model to produce similar outputs across related inputs or sampling procedures. Such methods are simple, scalable, and largely label-free, but their effects on model alignment remain poorly understood. Could the self-bootstrapping nature of these methods amplify undesired behavior in models? We test seven consistency training methods on 108 ‘‘model organisms 1 1 1 Borrowed from biology, a “model organism” refers to a non-human species studied to understand human biological systems due to its experimental accessibility. In this context, we use the term to describe models engineered to exhibit specific misalignment failures, serving as a controlled proxy for studying safety risks and mitigations (concept introduced by 25 ). ”: open-source models (7B–70B) fine-tuned to exhibit various forms of controlled misaligned behavior. We find that outcomes vary significantly: consistency training generally suppresses reward hacking and emergent misalignment but amplifies sycophancy. We present evidence that distribution shifts induced by the consistency labeling process, rather than variation in the selection operators, may be the primary driver of systematic alignment effects. Finally, we present a unifying theoretical framework to derive conditions under which consistency training will amplify or suppress misalignment. In total, our study establishes that consistency training is not alignment-neutral, and that its use in critical systems should be carefully audited.

Introduction

Large language models increasingly rely on
consistency training
: procedures that make a model agree with itself across different samples, prompt views, answer formats, perturbations, or internal representations
(
60
;
8
;
69
)
. These procedures are attractive because they are scalable and largely label-free. Instead of requiring a human-written target for every example, they use the model’s own outputs, confidence estimates, judgments, or activations as training signal.

As such, consistency training is a natural fit for modern post-training. Iterative rejection sampling and best-of-
N
selection appear in Llama post-training pipelines
(
55
;
20
)
, DeepSeek-R1
(
22
)
, and Qwen 2.5
(
41
)
. Constitutional AI uses self-critique and revision
(
5
)
. Inference-time scaling in o1-style systems samples and selects among multiple reasoning traces
(
38
)
. In practice, this often amounts to treating consistency as a benign post-training primitive unless separate evidence shows otherwise.

But consistency is not the same as correctness, and agreement is not the same as alignment. A model can be consistently helpful, but it can also be consistently sycophantic, consistently deceptive, or consistently exploitative. If a misaligned behavior is stable under the perturbations used by a consistency method, then the method may reinforce that behavior rather than remove it. Conversely, if a misaligned behavior is brittle or unstable, consistency pressure may suppress it.

Figure 1
:
Consistency non-neutrality hypothesis.
Language models contain both aligned (blue) and misaligned (red) behavioral modes. Consistency training enforces agreement across perturbations
\tau
(e.g., different sampling strategies, prompt framings, or decoding methods). Two non-neutral outcomes are possible:
suppression
, where aligned behavior dominates the consensus and misalignment is reduced; or
amplification
, where misaligned behavior is reinforced and consistency solidifies unwanted goals.

We therefore propose the
Consistency Non-Neutrality Hypothesis
: consistency training is not, in general, an alignment-preserving transformation. This is an
a priori
claim, but not a claim that consistency always helps or always harms; rather, we find that the expected direction depends on the structure of the behavior being made consistent, where consistency pressure should tend to suppress misaligned behaviors that are brittle under perturbation, while potentially amplifying misaligned behaviors that are coherent and stable.

To investigate this hypothesis, make the following contributions:

1.

A formal account of consistency non-neutrality.
We model consistency methods as procedures that transform a model’s completion distribution, define procedure-level misalignment risk, and derive conditions under which selection-based consistency methods amplify or suppress misalignment (
Sections

2
and
3
).

2.

A large controlled evaluation across misalignment organisms.
We evaluate seven consistency methods across four controlled failure modes and seven open-weight models from 7B to 70B. We find systematic suppression for reward hacking and emergent misalignment, amplification for sycophancy, and near-neutrality for spurious correlations (
Sections

4
and
5
).

3.

A mechanistic decomposition of the effects.
Ablations show that fine-tuning on model-generated pseudo-labels, even without selection, explains much of the suppression of brittle failures, while selection and refinement mechanisms contribute to the amplification of coherent sycophancy (
Section

6
).

Code to reproduce and build on this work can be found in

2
Consistency Training

We begin by formalizing the consistency training framework and surveying existing methods.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
denote a language model with parameters
\theta
. Given an input prompt
x
, the model produces a distribution over outputs
p_{\theta}(y|x)
.
Consistency training
refers to any training procedure that encourages the model to produce similar outputs (or internal representations) across related inputs or sampling procedures, without requiring labeled target outputs.

Formally, consistency training optimizes the objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{consistency}}=\mathbb{E}_{x,\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}\left[d(f_{\theta}(x,\tau_{1}),f_{\theta}(x,\tau_{2}))\right]

(1)

where
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
represent different perturbations, views, or sampling strategies,
f_{\theta}
is some function of the model’s outputs or internal states, and
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is a distance metric. Importantly, enforcement operator
f_{\theta}
, the perturbation distribution over
\tau
, and choice of distance
d

together specify what kind of agreement is being enforced on target
x
.

This unified view serves three purposes. First, it makes explicit the design axes (perturbation source, agreement target, disagreement metric, enforcement operator) that one implicitly chooses when adopting a consistency method. Second, it enables a general theoretical treatment: we can derive amplification conditions that apply across methods
rather than re-proving results for each variant. Third, it suggests lightweight diagnostics: if we can estimate how the agreement signal correlates with misalignment, we can offer a prediction of whether a method will help or harm alignment before deployment.

2.1
Methods evaluated

We now instantiate this framework with seven concrete methods, focusing on the selection criterion each uses. We select each method to span different mechanisms for enforcing agreement. All methods act as label-generation or regularization procedures. Label-generation methods generate pseudo-labels by enforcing consistency on the validation set. Regularization methods directly optimize a consistency loss during training.

2.1.1
Label-generation methods

Self-Confidence (SC)
(
30
)

Self-confidence extends Best-of-N sampling in LLMs
(
50
)
by using the model’s probability distribution to estimate the response quality of several possible completions. Inspired by
30
, we implement this with
k=3
completions using stochastic sampling and calculate the cumulative log-probability for each sequence:
\text{score}(y)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})
. The completion with the highest total confidence is selected as the label, using mean log-probability to reduce length bias. Full implementation details are in
Section

A.1
.

Diverse-Decoding (DD)
(
58
)
.

Diverse-decoding extends Best-of-N sampling in LLMs
(
50
)
by drawing candidates from structurally different decoding procedures rather than i.i.d. temperature sampling. We implement this using greedy decoding, nucleus sampling, and beam search to explore different regions of the output space. We employ
mxbai-rerank-large-v2

(
33
)
, a Small Language Model (SLM) reranker. See
Section

A.2
for implementation details.

Multi-View Consistency (MVC)
(
29
)
.

Building on multi-view consistency methods from classical machine learning
(
64
;
59
;
17
)
and work on prompt sensitivity in LLMs
(
29
)
, we enforce agreement across answer formats. We generate responses under three formats: standard, chain-of-thought
(
13
)
, and structured JSON. Samples are retained only if consistency holds between the standard and at least one other format. See
Section

A.3
for the judge prompt and filtering procedure.

Self-Refinement (SR)
(
36
)
.

Following
36
, we generate a draft response via greedy decoding, then construct an extended prompt containing the original query, the draft, and an instruction: “Please review the draft and provide an improved final version.” The model generates a refined response from this extended context, which becomes the pseudo-label. See
Section

A.4
for the full refinement prompt.

Self-Rewarding (Rew)
(
66
)
.

Following
66
, the model serves as its own evaluator. We generate one response per prompt, then prompt the model to score it on a 0–5 scale using a task-specific rubric
(
68
)
. The score is extracted via greedy decoding and prepended to the response, yielding pseudo-labels of the form
[Score:
s
]
y
. See
Section

A.5
for rubric details.

2.1.2
Regularization methods

Bias-Augmented Consistency Training (BCT)
(
15
)
.

Consistency regularization has a long history in machine learning
(
63
;
11
)
, which
15
adapt to the alignment of large language models. They enforce in the loss function that a model’s answer should be invariant to the presence of bias-inducing cues. Following this approach, we construct paired prompts
(x_{\text{clean}},x_{\text{wrapped}})
: the clean prompt contains no biasing features, while the wrapped prompt embeds cues known to trigger misaligned behavior (
Table

1
). We generate a target completion

by sampling from the model on
x_{\text{clean}}
, and then fine-tune the model via supervised learning to produce the same
y
when conditioned on
x_{\text{wrapped}}
. The loss function and training procedure are detailed in
Section

A.6
.

Activation Consistency Training (ACT)
(
27
)
.

27

(
15
)
in using clean and wrapped prompt pairs, but instead enforce consistency at the level of internal activations rather than output tokens. For each pair, we record residual stream activations
h^{\text{clean}}_{\ell,t}
at all layers
\ell
and prompt token positions
t
when processing
x_{\text{clean}}
. We then train the model to minimize the L2 distance between activations on the wrapped prompt and the (stop-gradiented) clean activations. See
Section

A.7
for the loss function and activation extraction procedure. For ACT and BCT, Table

shows examples.

3
Theoretical Framework: When Consistency Is Not Neutral

In this section, we connect the previously mentioned consistency methods and model organisms of misalignment by treating a consistency method as an output procedure that transforms a base model’s completion distribution over
\mathcal{D}
into a new distribution over outputs.

3.1
Procedure-level misalignment risk

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
be a language model with parameters
\theta
inducing conditional completion distribution
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
.
A procedure
A
maps a prompt
x
to an output random variable
Y_{A}(x)\in\mathcal{Y}
.
For example, baseline sampling corresponds to

Y_{\text{base}}(x)\sim P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x).

Given an organism
(\mathcal{D},M)
, we define the procedure-level misalignment rate as

\mathrm{Risk}(\theta;A,\mathcal{D},M)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\mathbb{P}\big(M(Y_{A}(x))=1\mid x\big)\Big].

(2)

3.2
The
Consistency Non-Neutrality Hypothesis
(formalized)

Let
A_{\text{base}}
denote baseline sampling and let
A_{\text{ct}}
denote a consistency-based procedure
(e.g. reranking/selection, filtering and distillation, or an explicit consistency loss).
We say the consistency procedure is
\varepsilon
-non-neutral
on organism
(\mathcal{D},M)
for threshold
\varepsilon>0
if

\bigl|\,\mathrm{Risk}(\theta;A_{\text{ct}},\mathcal{D},M)\;-\;\mathrm{Risk}(\theta;A_{\text{base}},\mathcal{D},M)\bigr|\;>\;\varepsilon.

(3)

Equation (
3
) is a testable proxy for the Consistency Non-Neutrality Hypothesis which states: for a fixed organism, does applying consistency change misalignment risk at all, and in which direction?

Definition 3.1

(Amplification and suppression)
.

A consistency procedure
amplifies
misalignment on
(\mathcal{D},M)
if

\mathrm{Risk}(\theta;A_{\text{ct}},\mathcal{D},M)>\mathrm{Risk}(\theta;A_{\text{base}},\mathcal{D},M)+\varepsilon,

and
suppresses
it if

\mathrm{Risk}(\theta;A_{\text{ct}},\mathcal{D},M)<\mathrm{Risk}(\theta;A_{\text{base}},\mathcal{D},M)-\varepsilon.

3.3
A sufficient condition for label-generation consistency

Many widely used consistency methods are selection operators, which generate multiple candidates and choose the one with highest score. This includes best-of-
k
by log-probability (Self-Confidence), reranker selection (Diverse-Decoding), and best-of-
k
by judge score (a common variant of LLM-as-a-judge pipelines
(
68
)
).
In this regime, amplification vs. suppression is governed by a single relationship: do higher-scoring candidates tend to be more misaligned?

Fix a prompt
x
and draw i.i.d. candidates
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}\overset{iid}{\sim}P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
.
Let
S:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be a scalar score (method-specific), and define the selected output

Y^{\star}\in\arg\max_{i\in[k]}S(Y_{i}),

(4)

breaking ties uniformly at random.
Define the misalignment posterior as a function of score

\eta(s)\;:=\;\mathbb{P}\big(M(Y)=1\mid S(Y)=s,x\big),

(5)

where
M:\mathcal{Y}\to\{0,1\}
is the organism-specific misalignment indicator from
Section

2.2
, interpreted via a regular conditional probability (formal details in Appendix
C
).

Proposition 3.2

(Max-score selection amplifies iff higher scores imply higher misalignment)
.

Fix
x
and assume
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}
are i.i.d. from
P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
with uniform tie-breaking.

If
\eta(s)
is nondecreasing in
s
(higher score
\Rightarrow
weakly higher misalignment), then

\mathbb{P}\big(M(Y^{\star})=1\mid x\big)\ \geq\ \mathbb{P}\big(M(Y)=1\mid x\big),

and the left-hand side is nondecreasing in
k
.

If instead
\eta(s)
is nonincreasing in
s
(higher score
\Rightarrow
weakly lower misalignment), the inequality reverses and suppression strengthens with
k
.

Interpretation.

Proposition
3.2
reduces the non-neutrality question to an empirical diagnostic: estimate
\eta(s)
(or any monotone summary of score–misalignment association).

Remark 3.3

(Proposition
3.2
as a diagnostic tool)
.

If
\eta(s)
is empirically flat, Proposition
3.2
predicts that selection should have minimal effect on misalignment.
Observing large alignment changes despite flat
\eta
therefore constitutes evidence
against
score-based selection as the mechanism and
for
alternative drivers such as distributional shift.
We test this diagnostic in
Section

6
, and as we show empirically in
Section

6.1
, most observed effects fall into this latter regime.

Proof sketch.

Let
S_{(k)}=\max_{i}S(Y_{i})
.
As
k
increases,
S_{(k)}
shifts upward by first-order stochastic dominance.
By iterated expectations,

\mathbb{P}(M(Y^{\star})=1\mid x)=\mathbb{E}[\eta(S_{(k)})\mid x]

while

\mathbb{P}(M(Y)=1\mid x)=\mathbb{E}[\eta(S(Y))\mid x].

If
\eta
is nondecreasing, pushing
S
upward pushes
\eta(S)
upward in expectation, giving amplification;
if
\eta
is nonincreasing, the argument yields suppression.
A complete proof appears in Appendix
C
.

A3: Label-source sensitivity.

6.3
Why are results so heterogeneous?

Our results establish that consistency training is not alignment-neutral with striking asymmetry: the same methods that reliably suppress reward hacking consistently amplify sycophancy. This pattern suggests that the differences in types of misalignment are rich for future investigation, as they potentially have different
structures
that interact differently with consistency-based selection and filtering. Intuitively, it seems likely that this is because reward hacking behaviors are brittle and narrow (hacking specific test cases), while sycophancy is fairly coherent, where agreeing with the user is a robust strategy.

Empirical support for differential coherence.

To shed more light on the difference between reward hacking and sycophancy, we measure the symmetric KL divergence between label distributions produced by 8B-Instruct and 70B-Instruct models on identical prompts during consistency labeling. Figure

Qualitative inspection confirms this pattern: sycophantic outputs follow a rigid template of validation and praise, whereas reward hacking outputs vary dramatically, with some repeating identical questions to game count metrics, and others attempting to guess test cases. This provides mechanistic evidence that sycophancy persists under perturbation because it is a consistent mode, while reward hacking is not.

6.4
Distributional shift as the primary mechanism

Recall that there are two different ways consistency labeling can change a model. The first is a
within-prompt selection effect
: where among several candidates for the same prompt, the method may choose a more or less misaligned one. This is the mechanism captured by
\eta(s)
. The second is a
distributional effect
: the entire pseudo-label distribution may differ from the model’s original output distribution, even when there is no meaningful selection among candidates.

Let
P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
denote the model organism’s output distribution and
Q_{\mathrm{ct}}(\cdot\mid x)
the distribution over pseudo-labels produced by consistency method
\mathrm{ct}
.
We define the
consistency distributional shift
as

\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}(\mathrm{ct},\mathcal{D})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(Q_{\mathrm{ct}}(\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\right)\right].

(6)

Our evidence supports the hypothesis that
\Delta_{\mathrm{dist}}
is the primary driver of alignment effects. First, flat
\eta(s)
curves (Figure
5
) rule out score-based selection. Second,
k{=}1
(which eliminates selection entirely) achieves comparable alignment effects to higher
k
(Figure
4
). Third, label-source sensitivity (Ablation A3) shows that the
source
of labels matters more than their
quality
, consistent with distributional shift rather than label accuracy driving effects. However, a complete causal account remains an open direction.

On StrongREJECT
(
49
)
, Phase 1 organisms already score near zero (mean
=0.003
). After consistency training, raw harmful-compliance scores increase to
0.113
on average, with 489/494 runs increasing, reinforcing that consistency training should be audited as alignment-changing.

7
Conclusion

Consistency methods are scalable, label-free post-training tools, but our results show that they are not alignment-neutral. Across controlled model organisms, consistency training suppresses some brittle failures, such as reward hacking and emergent misalignment, while amplifying more coherent failures, especially sycophancy. We establish that “more consistency” has structured effects on alignment, which depends on which behaviors are stable under the procedure’s perturbations. Consistency training should therefore be treated as an alignment-changing operator, with evaluations at the intended deployment scale.

Practical recommendations.

Based on our findings, practitioners should: (i) mitigate coherent failure modes such as sycophancy before applying consistency training, e.g. via supervised alignment or RLHF; (ii) avoid treating larger
k
as a safety guarantee, since score–alignment correlations are often weak; and (iii) red-team after consistency training, not only before, especially when changing model scale.

Limitations.

Many of the task-specific misalignment evaluations rely on LLM judges and so residual judge error may affect measurements. Additionally, our model organisms covering four distinct misalignments are artificially induced, so whether these models serve as good proxies for misalignment in naturally occurring, deployed models remains an open question. Our 70B results also use fewer runs than the 7–20B setting due to compute constraints. Finally, we do not test scheming or deceptive alignment, which we expect to be a coherent, goal-directed failure mode.

Impact Statement

As consistency training becomes standard in post-training pipelines, its effects on alignment must be evaluated directly, especially as language models are increasingly integrated into critical systems in society. Our work finds that consistency training effects are heterogeneous: suppression for some organisms (reward hacking) but amplification for others (sycophancy). Safety evaluations should be performed after consistency training, not just before, and method selection should consider organism-specific effects.

Dual-use considerations.

Our finding that consistency training can amplify coherent misalignments raises a dual-use concern: an adversary aware of this effect could deliberately apply consistency training to entrench misaligned behaviours. We believe the risk is modest: (a) inducing coherent misalignment in the first place requires deliberate fine-tuning on misaligned data, which is already an adversarial act; and (b) the defensive value of our findings (enabling practitioners to audit and mitigate these effects) substantially outweighs the marginal offensive advantage.

Information asymmetry.

Frontier AI developers likely already observe these effects internally through extensive post-training evaluations. Publishing this analysis levels the information asymmetry in the broader safety community, enabling independent auditing of consistency-based pipelines.

A.1
Self-Confidence (SC)

Self-Confidence
selects the most confident completion among multiple stochastic samples, using normalized log-probability as the confidence metric (see
Section

2.1
for overview).

Algorithm.

For each prompt
x
, we generate
k=3
candidate completions using stochastic sampling with temperature
0.7
and top-p
0.9
. For each candidate
y_{i}
, we compute the length-normalized log-probability score:

\text{score}(y)=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})

(7)

The candidate with maximum score is selected as the label:
y^{*}=\arg\max_{i}\text{score}(y_{i})
. Length normalization prevents bias toward shorter sequences.

Hyperparameters.

We use
k=3
candidates, temperature
0.7
, top-p
0.9
, and maximum token length
256
.

A.2
Diverse-Decoding (DD) Implementation

Diverse-Decoding generates candidates using structurally different decoding strategies to maximize diversity, then uses a generative reranker to select the best candidate.

Algorithm.

For each prompt
x
, we generate three candidates using structurally different decoding strategies: (1) greedy decoding, (2) nucleus sampling, and (3) beam search, selecting the top beam. This diversity ensures candidates explore different regions of the probability space. All candidates are then scored using the
mixedbread-ai/mxbai-rerank-large-v2
, a 1.5B parameter causal language model optimized for ranking, and the highest-scoring candidate is selected as the label.

Hyperparameters.

We use nucleus sampling at temperature
0.8
with top-p
0.9
, and beam search with width
4
. Maximum token length is
256
for all strategies.

A.3

Multi-View Consistency
enforces robustness to prompt framing by requiring semantic agreement across different output modalities (see
Section

2.1
).

Algorithm.

For each prompt
x
, we generate responses under three views: (1)

—direct response to
x

—

0.0

A.6
BCT

Bias-Augmented Consistency Training
(BCT) enforces output-level consistency between clean and wrapped prompts using a knowledge distillation objective
(
15
)
. See
Section

2.1
for overview and
Table

1
for example prompt pairs.

Loss Function.

For each (clean, wrapped) prompt pair
(x_{c},x_{w})
, BCT minimizes a cross-entropy loss between soft labels from the clean prompt and predictions on the wrapped prompt:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCT}}=T^{2}\sum_{t\in\text{overlap}}-\sum_{v=1}^{V}\underbrace{\text{softmax}\left(\frac{z_{c}^{(t)}}{T}\right)_{v}}_{\text{soft label (detached)}}\cdot\log\underbrace{\text{softmax}\left(\frac{z_{w}^{(t)}}{T}\right)_{v}}_{\text{wrapped logits}}

(8)

where
z_{c},z_{w}
are logits from clean and wrapped prompts respectively,
T
is the distillation temperature, and the sum is over overlapping suffix positions to handle different prompt lengths.

Training Procedure.

Each training step performs two forward passes: first on the clean prompt (eval mode, no gradients) to extract target logits
z_{c}
, then on the wrapped prompt (train mode) to extract
z_{w}
. The cross-entropy loss is computed between soft labels from
z_{c}
and log-probabilities from
z_{w}
, then backpropagated to update LoRA adapter parameters.

Hyperparameters.

Training uses learning rate
10^{-5}
, batch size
1
,
3
epochs, distillation temperature
T=1.0
, and gradient clipping at norm
1.0
. Clean prompts come from the aligned distribution.

A.7
ACT

Activation Consistency Training
(ACT) enforces consistency at the level of internal representations rather than outputs
(
27
)
. See
Section

2.1
for overview.

Loss Function.

For each (clean, wrapped) prompt pair
(x_{c},x_{w})
, ACT minimizes the mean squared error between residual stream activations:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ACT}}=\frac{\alpha}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\text{MSE}\left(h_{\ell}^{(c)},h_{\ell}^{(w)}\right)

(9)

where
h_{\ell}^{(c)},h_{\ell}^{(w)}
are the residual stream activations at layer
\ell
for clean and wrapped prompts,
L
is the number of transformer layers, and
\alpha=10^{-4}
is a scaling factor to prevent numerical overflow (activations can reach magnitudes of
100
–
300
+).

Activation Extraction.

The implementation registers forward hooks on all transformer layers, supporting multiple architectures (GPT-2 style
model.transformer.h
, Llama style
model.model.layers
). Activations are aligned on the overlapping suffix between sequences. Clean activations are detached to prevent gradient flow through the reference.

Training Procedure.

Each step performs two forward passes with hooks: first on the clean prompt (eval mode) to extract reference activations
\{h_{\ell}^{(c)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
, which are detached and cloned; then on the wrapped prompt (train mode) to extract
\{h_{\ell}^{(w)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
. The scaled MSE loss across all layers is backpropagated to update parameters.

Hyperparameters.

Training uses learning rate
10^{-5}
, batch size
1
,
3
epochs, loss scale
\alpha=10^{-4}
, and gradient clipping at norm
1.0
.

Appendix C
A General Non-Neutrality Theorem for Label-Generation Consistency

C.1
Setup

Fix a prompt
x
. Let the base model induce a distribution
P(\cdot\mid x)
over completions

Y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Let
M:\mathcal{Y}\to\{0,1\}
be a misalignment indicator, where
M(y)=1

means the completion is misaligned (e.g. per an LLM judge or task-specific criterion).
Define the base misalignment rate

\pi(x)\;:=\;\mathbb{P}\big(M(Y)=1\,\big|\,x\big),\qquad Y\sim P(\cdot\mid x).

(10)

Let
S:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be a (method-specific) scalar score used for selection.
For
k\geq 1
, draw i.i.d. samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}\overset{iid}{\sim}P(\cdot\mid x)
, define

M_{i}:=M(Y_{i})
and
S_{i}:=S(Y_{i})
, and let

S_{(k)}:=\max_{i\in[k]}S_{i},\qquad\mathcal{I}^{*}:=\{i\in[k]:S_{i}=S_{(k)}\}.

(11)

Select
I
uniformly at random from
\mathcal{I}^{*}
(tie-breaking uniformly),
and output
Y^{\star}:=Y_{I}
.

Assumption C.1

(Regular conditional probability / conditional expectation)
.

There exists a version of the conditional expectation

\eta(s):=\mathbb{E}[M(Y)\mid S(Y)=s,x]

(12)

defined for
P_{S(\cdot\mid x)}
-almost all
s
.
Equivalently, there exists a measurable function
\eta
such that

\mathbb{E}[M(Y)\mid S(Y),x]=\eta(S(Y))
almost surely.

Definition C.2

(Score–misalignment monotonicity)
.

We say the score is
misalignment-increasing
if
\eta(s)
is nondecreasing in
s
,
and
misalignment-decreasing
if
\eta(s)
is nonincreasing in
s
.

C.2
Main theorem

Theorem C.3

(Max-score selection is non-neutral under monotone posteriors)
.

Fix
x
and assume
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}
are i.i.d. from
P(\cdot\mid x)
and ties are broken
uniformly at random.

(Amplification case).

If
\eta(s)
is nondecreasing in
s
, then for all
k\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\big(M(Y^{\star})=1\mid x\big)\;\geq\;\mathbb{P}\big(M(Y)=1\mid x\big)\;=\;\pi(x),

(13)

and moreover
\mathbb{P}(M(Y^{\star})=1\mid x)
is nondecreasing in
k
.

(Suppression case).

If
\eta(s)
is nonincreasing in
s
, then for all
k\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\big(M(Y^{\star})=1\mid x\big)\;\leq\;\pi(x),

(14)

and moreover
\mathbb{P}(M(Y^{\star})=1\mid x)
is nonincreasing in
k
.

Proof.

We work throughout conditional on the fixed prompt
x
.

Step 1:
S_{(k)}
first-order stochastically dominates
S_{1}
.

For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{P}(S_{(k)}\leq t\mid x)=\mathbb{P}(S_{1}\leq t,\dots,S_{k}\leq t\mid x)=\mathbb{P}(S_{1}\leq t\mid x)^{k}\leq\mathbb{P}(S_{1}\leq t\mid x).

(15)

Thus
S_{(k)}
first-order stochastically dominates
S_{1}
.

Step 2: Express the selected misalignment rate as
\mathbb{E}[\eta(S_{(k)})\mid x]
.

Let
S_{1:k}:=(S_{1},\dots,S_{k})
.
By construction,
I
is a (randomized) function of
S_{1:k}
only, i.e.
I
is conditionally
independent of
(M_{1},\dots,M_{k})
given
S_{1:k}
.

Using the tower property,

\mathbb{P}(M(Y^{\star})=1\mid x)=\mathbb{E}\big[\,\mathbb{E}[M_{I}\mid S_{1:k},x]\,\big|\,x\big].

(16)

Condition on
S_{1:k}
. Since
I
takes values in
[k]
,

\mathbb{E}[M_{I}\mid S_{1:k},x]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}(I=i\mid S_{1:k},x)\,\mathbb{E}[M_{i}\mid S_{1:k},x].

(17)

By i.i.d. sampling,
(M_{i},S_{i})
are i.i.d., and
M_{i}
is conditionally independent of

S_{-i}:=(S_{j})_{j\neq i}
given
S_{i}
(because all dependence of
M_{i}
on the sample is mediated
by
Y_{i}
). Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[M_{i}\mid S_{1:k},x]=\mathbb{E}[M_{i}\mid S_{i},x]=\eta(S_{i}).

(18)

Also,
\mathbb{P}(I=i\mid S_{1:k},x)=0
unless
i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
, and on
\mathcal{I}^{*}

we have
S_{i}=S_{(k)}
. Hence

\mathbb{E}[M_{I}\mid S_{1:k},x]=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}^{*}|}\,\eta(S_{i})=\eta(S_{(k)}).

(19)

Plugging back,

\mathbb{P}(M(Y^{\star})=1\mid x)=\mathbb{E}\big[\eta(S_{(k)})\mid x\big].

(20)

Step 3: Express the base misalignment rate as
\mathbb{E}[\eta(S_{1})\mid x]
.

By the law of total expectation and
C.1
,

\pi(x)=\mathbb{E}[M(Y)\mid x]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[M(Y)\mid S(Y),x]\mid x]=\mathbb{E}[\eta(S_{1})\mid x].

(21)

Step 4: Monotonicity of
\eta
plus stochastic dominance implies amplification/suppression.

If
\eta
is nondecreasing, then first-order stochastic dominance of
S_{(k)}
over
S_{1}

implies
\mathbb{E}[\eta(S_{(k)})\mid x]\geq\mathbb{E}[\eta(S_{1})\mid x]
.
Combining with Steps 2–3 yields amplification.
If
\eta
is nonincreasing, the inequality reverses, giving suppression.

Step 5: Monotonicity in
k
.

For
k^{\prime}>k
, the same argument as Step 1 shows
S_{(k^{\prime})}
first-order stochastically dominates

S_{(k)}
. Thus
\mathbb{E}[\eta(S_{(k^{\prime})})\mid x]\geq\mathbb{E}[\eta(S_{(k)})\mid x]
when

\eta
is nondecreasing (and the reverse when
\eta
is nonincreasing). Using Step 2 completes the proof.
∎

Corollary C.4

(Strict non-neutrality under non-degeneracy)
.

Assume the conditions of
Theorem

C.3
,
\eta
is nondecreasing and strictly increasing
on a set of positive measure under
P_{S(\cdot\mid x)}
, and
\mathbb{P}(S_{(k)}>S_{1}\mid x)>0

(e.g.
S(Y)
is non-degenerate) with
k\geq 2
. Then

\mathbb{P}(M(Y^{\star})=1\mid x)>\pi(x).

(22)

Appendix E
Compute Infrastructure

G.6

To test whether our findings extend beyond model-internal selection procedures,
we evaluate an external reward-model rejection-sampling baseline. This setting is
practically important because rejection sampling with reward models is a common
component of modern post-training pipelines. Unlike Self-Confidence or
Self-Rewarding, where the model’s own probabilities or judgments provide the
selection signal, this baseline selects among model-generated candidates using an
independently trained reward model.

Method.

For each held-out Phase 2 prompt, the Phase 1 model organism generates multiple
candidate completions. We score each candidate using

Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B
and select the highest-scoring
completion as the pseudo-label. Phase 3 then proceeds identically to the other
label-generation methods: the model organism is fine-tuned via SFT on the
resulting prompt–pseudo-label pairs, and the final model is evaluated on the
same held-out organism-specific benchmark. We run this baseline on four models
(Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Gemma-2-9B, and Mistral-7B) across all
four organisms with five seeds, giving
N=20
runs per organism.

Two patterns are notable. First, sign consistency is near-ceiling at every threshold, declining only marginally from 98.7% (threshold 20) to 96.0% (threshold 40). Instead, it disproportionately eliminates responses in the 20–40 alignment score range—responses that are borderline misaligned rather than egregiously harmful. Responses scoring below 20 (severe misalignment) are less affected, which is consistent with the hypothesis that consistency training suppresses incoherent or unstable misaligned outputs while leaving deeply entrenched misalignment relatively intact.
