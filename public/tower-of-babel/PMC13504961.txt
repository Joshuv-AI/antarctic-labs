Right anterolateral mini-thoracotomy and full sternotomy approaches for Wheat procedure: a propensity-matched comparison
Authors: Minjian Kong, Yupeng Sun, Xian Zhu, Xuebiao Li, Dongdong Wei, Aiqiang Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the prevalence and technological advancements of minimally invasive heart surgery continue to increase, aortic valve replacement(AVR) via right anterolateral mini-thoracotomy (RAMT) has matured, however, problems associated with Wheat’s procedure (replacement of the ascending aorta(AAR) concomitant AVR) via RAMT approach have not been reported. The objective of this study was to compare the surgical quality, postoperative outcomes, and patient safety in patients undergoing Wheat’s procedure via Full sternotomy (FS) versus RAMT.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 163 patients who underwent Wheat procedure between January 2022 and December 2024. Out of the 163 patients, 59 underwent the Wheat procedure via a right anterior minimally invasive incision. After 1:1 propensity score matching, by comparing various intraoperative and postoperative indicators, the outcomes of patients undergoing RAMT and FS were compared.ResultsPropensity scores identified 43 patients per group with similar baseline profiles. The results from the propensity-matched cohort are as follows. Although patients in RAMT group had longer cardiopulmonary bypass (RAMT 167 ± 57 vs FS 141 ± 40 min; P = 0.04) and cross-clamp (RAMT 128 ± 39 vs FS 110 ± 29 min; P = 0.04) time, they experienced reduced intraoperative blood loss (RAMT 317 ± 147 vs FS 456 ± 229 ml; P = 0.005). No patients in the RAMT group required intraoperative conversion to Full sternotomy. There was no statistically difference in the in-hospital mortality between the two groups (0% and 0% in RAMT and FS, respectively). RAMT was correlated with a reduced incidence of postoperative atrial fibrillation (RAMT 3 [7.0%] vs FS 13 [23.3%]; P = 0.04). Receiving RAMT can lead to a reduction in postoperative NRS pain scores and an earlier discharge for patients. RAMT not only enables shorter incisions (RAMT 6.0 cm vs FS 15.2 cm; P < 0.001) but also has no impact on the size or type of the artificial valve or vessel implanted, thereby ensuring that the surgical effection is non-inferior to the FS group.ConclusionsIn the Wheat procedure, the perioperative outcomes of RAMT closely resemble those of full sternotomy, and may be transferred in the field of cardiac surgery.Keywords: Wheat procedure, Right anterolateral mini-thoracotomy, Full sternotomy

Introduction

Aortic valve disease is one of the most common diseases in cardiac surgery, current guidelines specify that an aortic root or ascending aorta diameter of 45 mm represents the threshold at which simultaneous aortic replacement is justified. Common surgical procedures included Bentall, Wheat, David and Ross procedure[1]. The Wheat procedure indicated in this study is mainly applicable to patients with no obvious aortic sinus lesions and obvious dilation of the ascending aorta, with significant postoperative results and a low incidence of aortic adverse events [2].

With the recent developments in minimally invasive cardiac surgery and the increasing emphasis on rapid rehabilitation concept, minimally invasive heart surgery is advancing both in terms of the quantity and techniques. Minimally invasive procedures performed by RAMT have been popularized and mature in the field of valve surgery, with their safety well established [3, 4]. However, the traditional approach for wheat’s surgery is the complete median sternotomy. To reduce surgical trauma and increase patient acceptance for surgery, some cardiac surgeons have turned to RAMT for the treatment of ascending aorta and aortic root lesions [5]. Previous studies have shown that additional ascending aorta procedures can be performed during minimally invasive AVR without significantly increasing postoperative risks compared with isolated AVR[6]. However, whether the RAMT approach offers any advantages over the conventional median sternotomy has been rarely reported.

There is currently a lack of studies comparing the minimally invasive RAMT approach to FS in the wheat procedure. Only few studies reported the reliable therapeutic outcomes of RAMT access in replacement of the ascending aorta (AAR) [7, 8]. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to retrospectively analyze the clinical data of patients underwent Wheat procedure via RAMT and FS, discuss and summarize the surgical experience, compare the surgical quality, postoperative outcomes, and patient safety.

Methods

Patient selection

This single-center, retrospective cohort analysis utilized data from consecutive patients undergoing the Wheat procedure between January 2022 and December 2024. The protocol for this study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Inclusion criteria: (1) The patients with the maximum diameter of the ascending aorta ≥ 45 mm and complicated with abnormal aortic valve structure or function requiring aortic valve replacement were determined by echocardiography and chest CT before operation; (2) Intraoperative exploration showed aortic wall thinness and other conditions, and the surgeon considered that ascending aorta replacement was necessary; (3) Patients who are not contraindicated for Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) surgery. Exclusion criteria: (1) Emergency surgery and concurrent surgery, including aortic dissection, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), and other heart valve surgeries; (2) Patients with a prior history of cardiac surgery, chest trauma or surgical history; (3) Patients lost to follow-up after discharge. 25 patients were excluded in total. The ultimate sample included comprehensive clinical information for 163 patients, among whom 59 patients (36.2%) underwent Wheat procedure via RAMT and the rest through a median sternal incision.To minimize baseline confounders between the RAMT and FS approaches, propensity score matching was employed. This process yielded 43 matched pairs: a RAMT cohort and a control FS cohort, all having undergone the Wheat procedure.

Data extraction

All perioperative data were analysed from our medical records database. Patient charts were meticulously reviewed by a single investigator, data including the maximum diameter of the ascending aorta and aortic annulus diameter were measured by computed tomography (CT) and echocardiography by the same examiner.

Our primary outcome was 6-month, 30‐day or in‐hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes were intraoperative details and postoperative complications, such as cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time, operative bleeding, ventilation time, sternal wound healing disorder, reoperation for bleeding, incidence of atrial fibrillation, pneumothorax, pulmonary infection and so on.

Surgical technique

The RAMT cohort underwent surgery via a 5–7 cm incision at the 2nd or 3rd intercostal space, sparing the ribs. In contrast, the FS group received a conventional full median sternotomy.

Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) in the FS group was typically initiated by cannulating the distal ascending aorta and right atrium. For the RAMT group, venous drainage was achieved either through direct cannulation of the right atrial appendage or percutaneously via the femoral vein. Arterial inflow was established either directly in the distal ascending aorta or, in selected cases, through the femoral artery. Del Nido cardioplegia was administered in all cases to induce cardiac arrest.

Following resection of the ascending aorta, the aortic valve was exposed by placing polypropylene traction sutures. After excision of the native leaflets, a mechanical or biological prosthesis was implanted. The appropriate prosthesis size was determined intraoperatively using the manufacturer’s sizer. The annular sutures for the aortic valve replacement (AVR) were placed manually, employing interrupted, non-everted, pledgeted mattress sutures. This suturing commenced at the left coronary cusp base and advanced clockwise.

Upon completion of the AVR, the vascular graft (typically 28–32 mm) was anastomosed to the native aorta. Both the distal and subsequent proximal anastomoses were performed in a continuous fashion using a 4–0 polypropylene suture. The final length of the graft was tailored in situ, following aneurysm resection and often after completing one anastomosis, guided by surgical judgment (Fig. 1).

Follow-up data

Long-term follow-up data regarding survival and the incidence of adverse outcomes were acquired through structured telephone interviews with the patients or their next of kin. All enrolled patients were scheduled for routine postoperative assessments, which occurred at 1, 3, and 6 months after surgery, either via direct contact or through standard outpatient reviews.

Statistical analysis

A propensity-matched analysis was performed on the cohort. Patients from the two surgical groups were paired 1:1 through an automated process based on a propensity score model that adjusted for predefined covariates: age, gender, body mass index, arterial hypertension, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, and stroke history. The matching protocol utilized the nearest-neighbor approach with a caliper of 0.03.

IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25.0) served as the platform for all statistical computations. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation for continuous measures and as numbers (percentages) for categorical measures. Group comparisons involved the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous data, and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data. Survival distributions were estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method, and a p-value below 0.05 governed statistical significance.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 163 patients satisfied both the inclusion and exclusion criteria, among whom 59(36.2%) patients underwent Wheat procedure by way of RAMT and 104(63.8%) via FS. Stroke volume (SV) (RAMT 63 ± 30 vs FS 75 ± 38 ml; P = 0.04) was significantly lower in the RT group than in the FS group. A propensity score-matched analysis was performed to counteract potential treatment selection bias and confounding factors inherent in this observational study. Following this methodology, 43 patients in the RTAM group (underwent Wheat procedure via a RT approach) were successfully matched with an equivalent number of controls from the FS group. In the matched cohort, the disparities in baseline characteristics between the RAMT and FS groups were no longer statistically significant, including the patient’s previous disease history (Table 1), types of aortic valve disease, ascending aorta diameter, echocardiography data (Table 2).

Intraoperative details

There was no intraoperative conversion from mini-incision to Full sternotomy incision in all patients. Table 3 presents the intraoperative details by procedure for the all patients and for subgroups of subjects matched by propensity score. Patients who had the RAMT approach had longer cardiopulmonary bypass(RAMT 167 ± 57 vs FS 141 ± 40 min; P = 0.04) and cross-clamping time(RAMT 128 ± 39 vs FS 110 ± 29 min; P = 0.04), but less intraoperative blood loss(RAMT 317 ± 147 vs FS 456 ± 229 ml; P = 0.005) and higher intraoperative minimum body temperature(RAMT 31.9 ± 1.4 vs FS 31.2 ± 0.9 ℃; P = 0.02). These notable differences were also noted within propensity-matched cohorts. The proportion of patients in the FS group who needed to rely on electrical defibrillation to restore the heartbeat during surgery was 1.5 times higher (RAMT 11[19%] vs FS 30[29%]; P = 0.15), but did not reach statistically significant.

Postoperative outcomes

Examination of early postoperative outcomes revealed that RAMT was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative Atrial fibrillation (RAMT 4 [7%] vs FS 24 [23%]; P = 0.007) and Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores on the fourth day after surgery. Reopening for bleeding, sternal wound healing disorders, pneumothorax, pulmonary infection and blood transfusions were of similar rates in two groups. No difference was observed in 24-h postoperative drainage volume, intensive care unit length of stay, hospitalization duration and expenses. Discussion

The traditional approach to cardiac surgery is full sternotomy (FS), providing full exposure of the operative field and facilitating intraoperative procedures. However, this method results in significant surgical trauma, compromising thoracic stability and hindering recovery. Additionally, postoperative pain is pronounced, leading to greater psychological trauma and impacting the patient’s social function during recovery. Minimally invasive cardiac surgery has been developed and widely adopted as a solution to address these challenges[4, 9]. The spectrum of minimally invasive surgical access includes upper partial sternotomies (featuring T- or J-incisions) along with mini-sternotomies in various shapes, such as S, L, Z, and C. The RAMT targeted in this study has been shown to provide equivalent or even superior surgical outcomes to those following FS in AVR, without increase in perioperative risk or complication rates, as evidenced by multiple studies[10–12]. As surgical proficiency in RAMT accumulates, there is a growing propensity to address more complex pathologies of the ascending aorta and root through this minimally invasive avenue. Nevertheless, the existing body of literature dedicated specifically to RAMT for aortic conditions remains limited. While a few reports indicating low mortality rates associated with minimally invasive aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta replacement using the right anterior thoracotomy approach [5], none of these studies have conducted systematic comparisons with conventional incisions. Hence, surgeons typically exercise meticulous discretion in the selection of the RAMT incision for Wheat procedure. In this study, we consider that RAMT can ensure the surgical quality, postoperative outcomes, and patient safety for individuals undergoing Wheat procedure when compared to FS.

The most controversial issue regarding the utilization of RAMT for Wheat procedure is whether it will compromise patient safety and reduce surgical quality due to limited surgical field of view and operating space. First of all, the primary outcome is mortality. LaPietra et al. first described minimally invasive aortic valve and ascending aorta replacement via RAMT approach can be performed with low morbidity and mortality. We came to the same results: no statistically significant difference in mortality between patients in the RAMT group and FS group during hospitalization, at 1 and 6 month post-surgery. There was only 1 case of in-hospital deaths due to respiratory and cardiac arrest caused by postoperative malignant arrhythmia in FS group.

Furthermore, a comprehensive multicenter investigation revealed that the duration of aortic cross-clamping independently predicts mortality in individuals with preserved ventricular function [13] In our study, patients who had the RAMT approach had longer cardiopulmonary bypass and cross-clamping time than FS group. We consider that the technical challenges and steep learning curve required associated with RAMT have contributed to the lengthening time. Therefore, we focus on the trends over time of intraoperative details for the propensity matched patients. In the RAMT group, the third of patients who had surgery the latest had lower operation time, cross-clamp time and CPB time compared to the first third of patients, but no difference compared to the FS group (Table 6). This implies that as surgeons gain expertise in RAMT, they are able to significantly mitigate these potential risks. Additionally, we also counted end-of-surgery lactate, ventilation time and other indicators to further confirm the safety of RAMT. The RAMT group was less likely than the FS group to require defibrillation to restore the heartbeat, which, although not statistically significant, may potentially mitigating associated risks.

In terms of postoperative complications, the RAMT group had a statistically lower incidence of atrial fibrillation than the FS group. This finding holds particular significance as previous research has linked postoperative malignant arrhythmias with increased mortality rates. In the comparative analysis of traditional incision and minimally invasive incision for AVR, different studies have reached opposite conclusions regarding this indicator[14, 15]. There were no statistically significant differences in the occurrence of other complications between the two groups. The findings indicate that RAMT does not sacrifice patient safety in favor of shorter surgical incisions.

Does the reduced operating space caused by RAMT impact the surgical curative effect? In this study, on the premise that there was no difference in the preoperative aortic annulus diameter between the two groups, there was no difference in the size of artificial valves and artificial blood vessels implanted between the two groups. Denis et al. ‘s studies of AVR yielded comparable findings in the context of AVR[16]. For AAR in Wheat procedure. we measured the diameter of residual vessels 1.5 cm near the proximal end of the innominate artery by CT after surgery, so as to confirm that the RAMT group would not result in residual dilated ascending aorta due to small surgical incision. Morever, echocardiography was conducted at 1 month and 6 months post-surgery, with EF, SV and other indexes utilized to assess the postoperative cardiac function of the RAMT group, demonstrating comparable outcomes to those of the FS group.

In previous studies, Johnston and Roselli observed instances where patients opted to delay clearly necessary AVR operations out of apprehension towards sternotomy[14]. Therefore, RAMT, exhibiting comparable safety and efficacy as FS, may enhance the receptivity of patients towards surgical intervention. The lower postoperative NRS pain scores and medical costs in RAMT group shown in this study lead to a better medical experience for patients. Overall, it appears that RAMT may enhance the probability of patients opting for Wheat procedure with a sense of assurance.

Limitations

This study is subject to several important limitations inherent in its retrospective design. Firstly, the assignment to treatment groups was influenced by patient clinical status and surgeon preference, a selection bias that is partially explained by the surgical learning curve. Initially, surgeons appropriately exhibited a natural reluctance to apply minimally invasive techniques to all cases. Secondly, while propensity score matching was employed to address known confounders, residual bias from unmeasured factors may persist and could have influenced the outcome comparisons. Finally, the analysis is currently limited by the incompleteness of follow-up data, which results from the loss of a small number of patients and other reasons.

Conclusion

In the Wheat procedure, the perioperative outcomes of RAMT closely resemble those of full sternotomy, and may be transferred in the field of cardiac surgery.
