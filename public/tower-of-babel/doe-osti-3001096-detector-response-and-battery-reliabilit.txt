Purpose:

The Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) Material Control and Accountability (MC&A) measurements
team performs confirmation measurements of Special Nuclear Material (SNM) at different locations
around Sandia. The measurement team has access to two High purity Germanium (HPGe) detectors, the
AEGIS (Model: AEGIS-BEGE5030), and the ORTEC (Model: trans-SPEC-DX-100). These two can
operate purely on battery power, but the response of the detectors while on battery power is not well
documented. To enhance our knowledge with these detectors the team wants to investigate the detectors
response to changes in battery range, wall charging, and mid operation battery swaps (Hot Swaps).

From this investigation, the Team SME’s assumptions are the following:

 • Variations in the parameters of the batteries will cause some measurements to fail Quality
 Control (QC) test’s
 • Specific charge times will have the greatest effect on measurements

With this project I aim to investigate these assumptions with the AEGIS. The project will hopefully
identify any trends/patterns in relation to passing/failing QC test.

Background:

The Material Control and Accountability (MC&A) measurements team performs confirmation
measurements of Special Nuclear Material (SNM). By using gamma spectroscopy, we can confirm if the
material is SNM by identifying the specific radioisotopes. Sandia uses double confirmation measurements
to confirm two attributes of an item. The double confirmation measurements include gamma spectroscopy
and a gross weight measurement.

A confirmation measurement is a qualitative or quantitative measurement made to confirm the presence
of SNM in an item by testing whether some attribute or characteristic of the nuclear material in the item is
consistent with the expected attribute or characteristic of the material. The measurement method used for
confirmation measurements must be capable of determining the presence of a specific attribute of the
material, consistent with valid acceptance, and rejection criteria. [1]

How do HPGe Detectors work? The detector utilizes the properties of High Purity Germanium to detect
and measure the gamma rays being emitted from a source. When these two interact it creates electron-
hole pairs which correlates to the specific energy of the gamma ray. These Specific energies are later
plotted on a gamma spectrum. The AEGIS also has some interesting notes that I should talk about. First,
the AEGIS has two batteries and has charging capabilities. Secondly, the AEGIS will attempt to shut
itself down if the battery power is below 15%, which is one of the reasons the detector does not dip below
that range in our tests.

Gamma Spectroscopy:

We can use gamma spectroscopy as an attribute for confirmation. With it we can see if a source is
emitting gamma rays. We can analyze the specific energies of these gamma rays and see the specific
radioactive isotopes in the material. We see these isotopes in gamma spectrums as peaks at their specific
energy. Another way to think about it would be fingerprints. Every person has their own set of
fingerprints, and the same can be applied to isotopes. Energy Peaks are measured in kilo electron volts
(keV). The primary peaks for common SNM isotopes at Sandia fall within the 100-450keV range.

QC Process:

Figure 1 shows a gamma spectrum for our QC source. The source has 3 peaks or Regions of Interest
(ROI’s) that I will be measuring during this project. These peaks being Am-241, Cs-137, and Co-60.
Their specific/known keV’s are: 59.54 keV for Am-241, 661.66 keV for Cs-137, and 1332.49 keV for
Co-60. The general reason for these specific peaks is because it shows us the low, medium, and high
energies. If all three peaks pass our QC test, we should be confident that the detector is accurate and
working. At Sandia we do Pre-QC measurements before field measurements, basically taking the
measurement of our known source then plugging in the data into the QC test to make sure it passes. Then,
we also do Post-QC measurements after our field measurements to make sure nothing changed in that
time frame.

Genie 2000 & PeakEasy:

But how do we collect these gamma spectrums? First, we need our detector to detect the radioisotopes
which have specific energy peaks. The detector requires the Genie 2000 software to be operated. This is
done by connecting a laptop to the detector via ethernet cord. After the measurement is done, I would take
the gamma spectrum data and transfer it over to PeakEasy to take a closer look at our ROI’s. I would then
analyze the ROI’s and extract that data to an excel file. Finally, I would plug in the excel data that I
extracted from PeakEasy into our QC Test. Genie 2000 is required to the run the AEGIS. We use
PeakEasy instead of Genie 2000 to analyze the data because it is more user friendly and provides more
options for analysis.

Now let’s talk about the parameters of these types of Measurements. A couple of notes to make, I did
these measurements in order from category, Battery Range first, then Battery Charging, finally Hot
Swaps. Also, for each Sub-variation of measurement taken, 10 Quality Control (QC) measurements were
taken. This is due to my time being limited for the internship, otherwise 30 QC measurements would have
been preferred.

Battery Range:

For the first set of measurements, we have battery range. Here I took measurements at different battery
ranges. These include 80-90%, 50-60%, and 20-30% battery ranges. These battery ranges were chosen to

 get an idea of how the operational range of the AEGIS is affected when taking measurements on battery
 power.

 Battery Charging:

 For every measurement in battery charging, the initial parameter to start with is the batteries beginning at
 a range of 50-60%. After the battery had dropped to the required range, I would plug the charger into the
 AEGIS. The variations on battery charging included: Immediate (taking a measurement immediately after
 being plugged in), 10 Minute charge (taking measurement after charging for 10 Minutes), 20 Minutes, 30
 Minutes, and Full Charge (taking measurement after both batteries have reached 100%).

 Hot Swaps:

 Hot Swaps described as the ability to replace batteries without losing power to the Aegis. A major benefit
 we receive from hot swaps is the fact that the detector doesn’t lose power during the measurement. This
 ability is especially effective when the batteries in the detector start faulting or stop working which forces
 us to do a hot swap without redoing the measurement. So, it is very important for us to understand if the
 batteries affect hot swap measurements. In these tests there are certain parameters for the measurements.
 For every measurement the battery range needs to drop to 50-60% and for the actual hot swaps, I would
 swap about 150 Seconds into the live time measurement.

 bounds on Net Area (Counts). For past data it was often that net area would fail the Post QC test, this is
 shown in Figure 5. However, this could have been due to the inherently random statistics of gamma
 spectroscopy. To alleviate the amount of fails from randomness of the data the team made a new
 calculation for the bounds for Net Area. They have also added error bars to the data, this allows for the
 team to track the uncertainty of the data, and to ensure that there aren’t and negative trends. This is show
 in Figure 3 with the project data being in the new QC Chart. Figure 4 and Appendix D shows our project
 data in an older QC chart. Taking a closer look, you can see that not all our data passes the past QC test
 for Net Area. This is due to gamma spectroscopy’s random statistics, finally showing that battery has no
 real effect on our measurement data. When looking at our data for Net Area (Counts), (found in and
 appendix C), we see that all our data passes the QC test. They also have no real trends or patterns across
 all three peaks, meaning from this data set, battery does not inherently have any significance on Net Area
 (Counts).

 By looking at these graphs we see a common trend seen best in Figure 6 and Figure 7. With our expected
 value of the centroids shown in both graphs we see that three test variations are lower for all centroid
 graphs. These test variations are battery percentages of 20-30%, 50-60%, 80-90%. In Figure 6 they are the
 beginning data points that seem to be lower than the known energy. Again, these three fall under the
 battery range category where the detector runs strictly off battery power. This is a very interesting trend
 however there are slight notes to make. To start off with the ranges of these graphs are very small, so this
 does exaggerate the visual difference between the data. Second, even with this trend it passes all our QC
 test’s, meaning there is no real issue or something to be worried about. The last note to make with some
 of the graphs in Appendix A, specifically when looking at the centroids for 59.54 keV and 661.66 keV,
 that all the variations seem far from the expected value. Again, this is just exaggerated by the graphs, but
 this trend is a result from the calibration process of the detector. Additionally, overtime the HPGe energy

calibration will drift causing the centroids to shift from the known values. With all this being considered,
the batteries do not seem to have any statistical significance when affecting our measurement results.

Conclusion:

The main reason going into this project was to achieve adequate documentation of HPGe detectors
response while doing different test variation with the battery. I also talked about how in the past some QC
test would fail. The team had hypothesized that the battery was playing a part in some of the inherently
random statistics that would cause measurements to fail the QC test. So, they allowed me to investigate
this project with the thought that there would be fails in the QC test, specifically with battery percentages
and the charge times. However, as seen from Appendix A, B, and C, this was not the case. As shown with
these measurements, there was never any real statistical significance that would be cause for concern. I
did see a slight trend with centroids, as seen in the graphs, when running strictly off battery the average
centroid seemed to be slightly and consistently lower than the rest of the test variations.

Moving Forward:

Moving forward with this project there are a couple of additional things that I can do. To start with, taking
more data would be very good for this project, this is because I was only able to take 10 QC
measurements for each sub-variation. So, by taking about 30 QC measurements, it allows us to have more
accurate results, and the potential that I might find more trends in our data. When thinking about more
tests, I can try different parameters especially when concerning hot swaps, which had specific parameters.
Some of these parameters I would switch around would include Different battery percentages during
swaps, different swapping times (Swapping beginning of measurement, as well as end of the
measurement). I also think testing different things such as running the detector with wall power only, as
well as testing single batteries (since the detector has two batteries). Finally, the last thing I can do, is to
do these exact same experiments with different detectors such as the ORTEC which is one of the HPGe
detectors that we have at the lab.
