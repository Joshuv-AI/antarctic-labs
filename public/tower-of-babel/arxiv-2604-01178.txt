Screening Is Enough
Ken M. Nakanishi

Abstract
We call query–key relevance absolute when its values lie on a fixed bounded scale, depend on neither competing keys nor sequence length, require no sequence-length-dependent calibration, and can all be zero. To realize this notion, we introduce screening , whose explicit threshold transforms bounded query–key similarities into relevance values, enabling exact rejection, empty selection, and direct inspection on a common scale. In a controlled comparison of 12 attention mechanisms on a matched Transformer backbone, only screening maintains both low long-context perplexity and robust retrieval beyond the training context; notably, it does so without inference-time scaling. Building on screening, we introduce Multiscreen, a language-model architecture composed of parallel gated screening tiles. Multiscreen retains these long-context gains while achieving greater parameter efficiency, stronger general zero-shot downstream performance, lower training cost at larger scales, and lower model-side time to first token than Transformer baselines. We further develop a normalization design that keeps Multiscreen training stable even at a learning rate of 1 1 and show that an adapted version likewise stabilizes Transformer at the same learning rate.

Introduction

Handling contexts substantially longer than those seen during training remains a central challenge for large language models (LLMs).
Training directly on long sequences is computationally expensive because the cost of Transformer self-attention grows quadratically with sequence length
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
.
Models are therefore often trained on relatively short contexts and expected to generalize to much longer ones at inference time, yet increasing the nominal context length does not guarantee that they can effectively use relevant information within it.

A key difficulty lies in how relevant information is selected from the context.
In standard softmax attention, scores are unbounded and scale-dependent, while weights redistribute a fixed unit mass, so a key’s apparent relevance depends on competing keys.
Consequently, softmax cannot directly reject every key, and this competition can dilute relevant signals as contexts grow.

We call query–key relevance
absolute
when its values lie on a fixed bounded scale, depend on neither competing keys nor sequence length, require no sequence-length-dependent calibration, and can all be zero.
To realize this notion, we introduce
screening
.
Screening maps unit-normalized query–key similarities to nonnegative relevance values and uses an explicit threshold to assign exactly zero relevance to irrelevant keys.
Because these values are not normalized across keys, every key can be rejected, making both selected keys and empty selections directly inspectable on a common scale.

Validation loss alone does not reveal whether a model can retrieve and use relevant information from long contexts.
To directly evaluate retrieval, we introduce
ABCDigits
, a semantics-free key–value retrieval benchmark designed to isolate retrieval behavior from natural-language semantics and instruction-following effects.

We first isolate screening in a controlled comparison that changes only the attention mechanism on a matched Transformer backbone.
Among 12 evaluated variants, only screening maintains both low long-context perplexity and robust retrieval beyond the training context; notably, it requires no inference-time scaling.
Nested ablations isolate the contributions of screening’s windowing and positional components, while showing that RoPE alone does not explain the abrupt failure of other global mechanisms beyond the training context.

Building on screening, we introduce
Multiscreen
, whose layers comprise parallel gated screening tiles with learned screening windows and minimal positional encoding (MiPE).
MiPE becomes inactive for sufficiently large windows, so broad-context tiles do not extrapolate positional rotations beyond those seen during training.

Multiscreen models retain these long-context gains: they maintain low perplexity without inference-time scaling throughout evaluations extending to
32\times
the training context and show little retrieval degradation as context length increases.
In contrast, YaRN delays Transformer degradation but introduces a tradeoff between long-context extrapolation and perplexity at shorter contexts.

Beyond long-context behavior, the fitted scaling trends indicate that Multiscreen reaches similar validation loss with roughly 30% fewer parameters, and the 1.3B Multiscreen model improves general zero-shot downstream performance, outperforming its Transformer counterpart on eight of nine tasks.
It also has lower training cost at larger scales and lower model-side time to first token (TTFT) across all evaluated model sizes.
At the 1.3B scale, the Transformer has
2.5
–
2.9\times
the TTFT of Multiscreen across evaluated context lengths.

We further develop a normalization design that keeps Multiscreen training stable even at learning rate
1
.
Adapting this design to Transformer by combining query–key and pre-logit normalization likewise prevents divergence at the same learning rate, showing that this stability is transferable rather than specific to screening.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce
screening
, a mechanism for absolute query–key relevance, and show through a controlled comparison of 12 attention variants that it alone maintains low long-context perplexity and robust retrieval without inference-time scaling.

•

We introduce
ABCDigits
, a semantics-free completion-based retrieval benchmark designed to isolate retrieval behavior without relying on natural-language semantics or instruction-following.

•

We build
Multiscreen
from parallel gated screening tiles and show that it retains screening’s long-context gains while improving parameter efficiency, general zero-shot downstream performance, training cost, and model-side TTFT relative to Transformer baselines.

•

We develop a transferable normalization design that enables stable Multiscreen and Transformer training even at learning rate
1
.

Related Work

Attention mechanisms and relevance.

Alternatives to standard softmax modify its normalization, sparsity, or accessible key set.
Normalized variants in our controlled comparison include Softmax
1
, which adds a null alternative to the denominator
(
Miller, 2023
)
; Scalable-Softmax (SSMax), which sharpens attention as sequence length grows
(
Nakanishi, 2025
)
; Entmax, which produces sparse normalized weights
(
Peters et al., 2019
;
Correia et al., 2019
;
Vasylenko et al., 2026
)
; and Forgetting Attention, which applies data-dependent decay before normalization
(
Lin et al., 2025a
)
.
Sliding-window attention instead restricts the accessible keys
(
Beltagy et al., 2020
)
.
Sigmoid attention and
\mathrm{ReLU}^{2}
attention depart from normalized competition by weighting query–key pairs independently
(
Ramapuram et al., 2025
;
Hua et al., 2022
)
.
These mechanisms relax different constraints on total mass, sparsity, boundedness, pairwise independence, and length dependence; among those evaluated, screening alone combines all properties required for absolute relevance across context lengths.
The formal distinctions and complete evaluated configurations are summarized in
tables

8
and
7
.

Long-context positional representations.

Length generalization is also shaped by positional representations, including ALiBi, RoPE, and NoPE
(
Press et al., 2022
;
Su et al., 2024
;
Kazemnejad et al., 2023
)
, as well as RoPE extension methods such as Position Interpolation, YaRN, and LongRoPE
(
Chen et al., 2023
;
Peng et al., 2024
;
Ding et al., 2024
)
.
Our controlled comparison includes ALiBi, NoPE as used by Forgetting Attention, and RoPE with YaRN scaling.
Multiscreen instead uses minimal positional encoding (MiPE), which rotates only two dimensions for small screening windows and becomes inactive for sufficiently large windows.

Retrieval evaluation and synthetic benchmarks.

Long-context evaluation has shown that models may fail to use relevant information even when it lies within the nominal context window, as in lost-in-the-middle phenomena
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
.
Needle-in-a-haystack and passkey-style benchmarks probe long-context retrieval
(
Kamradt, 2023
;
Mohtashami & Jaggi, 2023
)
.
For natural-language needle benchmarks, semantic interference from the surrounding context can affect retrieval results
(
Shi & Penn, 2025
)
.
The original passkey evaluation uses a fixed natural-language prompt template, and subsequent work shows that apparent retrieval performance can depend strongly on its prompt and passkey configuration
(
Yuan et al., 2024
)
.
ABCDigits complements these benchmarks with a semantics-free completion task, a fixed key set, and a uniquely determined target value.

3
Model Architecture

Multiscreen realizes absolute query–key relevance through
screening
.
Screening begins with unit-normalized query–key dot products, yielding similarities on the fixed interval
[-1,1]
.
Trim and Softmask transform each similarity independently into a relevance value, without normalization across keys or calibration to the sequence length, and these values are used directly for value aggregation.
This construction permits exact rejection and empty selection, in contrast to the relative weighting imposed by softmax attention, as illustrated in
fig.

1
.

The overall architecture is illustrated in
fig.

2(a)
.
Each layer contains parallel gated screening tiles (see
fig.

2(b)
), which project token representations into query, key, value, and gate vectors, apply a screening unit to retrieve relevant context, and gate the result using a GLU-style multiplicative mechanism
(
van den Oord et al., 2016
;
Dauphin et al., 2017
;
Shazeer, 2020
)
before projecting back to the embedding dimension.
In the actual implementation, several operations are fused, and query–key pairs outside finite learned screening windows are skipped for efficiency.

3.1
Overview

Let
W_{\text{E}}=[\bm{e}_{1};\dots;\bm{e}_{|\mathcal{V}|}]
denote the embedding matrix shared by the input and language-modeling head.
We apply
embedding normalization
to each row, defining
\bar{\bm{e}}_{j}=\bm{e}_{j}/\|\bm{e}_{j}\|
.
Given a token
t_{i}
, its input representation is

\bm{x}_{i}^{(0)}=\mathrm{e}^{s_{\text{E}}}\bar{\bm{e}}_{t_{i}},

(1)

where
s_{\text{E}}
is a learned scalar.

Multiscreen then applies
N_{\text{L}}
residual layers, each composed of
N_{\text{H}}
parallel gated screening tiles.
Let
\Delta\bm{x}_{i}^{(\ell,h)}
denote the update produced for position
i
by tile
h
in layer
\ell
.
The layer update is

\bm{x}_{i}^{(\ell)}=\bm{x}_{i}^{(\ell-1)}+\sum_{h=1}^{N_{\text{H}}}\Delta\bm{x}_{i}^{(\ell,h)}.

(2)

After the final layer, we apply
pre-logit normalization
(PLNorm),
\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(N_{\text{L}})}=\bm{x}_{i}^{(N_{\text{L}})}/\|\bm{x}_{i}^{(N_{\text{L}})}\|
, and compute logits using the normalized hidden state and embeddings:

z_{ij}=\bar{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(N_{\text{L}})}\left(\mathrm{e}^{s_{\text{F}}}\bar{\bm{e}}_{j}^{\top}\right),\qquad j\in\{1,\dots,|\mathcal{V}|\},

(3)

where
s_{\text{F}}
is a learned scalar.
Neither embedding normalization nor PLNorm includes learned elementwise scales; each only projects its input onto the unit hypersphere.
We refer to the combination of these normalizations and the learned input and logit scales
\mathrm{e}^{s_{\text{E}}}
and
\mathrm{e}^{s_{\text{F}}}
as the
spherical input–output (SIO) parameterization
.
A standard softmax over logits gives next-token probabilities.

3.2
Screening Unit

A screening unit takes projected query, key, and value vectors
\bm{q}_{i},\bm{k}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d_{\text{K}}}
and
\bm{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d_{\text{V}}}
, and returns a context-dependent representation
\bm{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d_{\text{V}}}
(see
fig.

2(c)
).
Each unit has two learned scalar parameters,
s_{\text{w}}
and
s_{\text{r}}
, which define

w=\mathrm{e}^{s_{\text{w}}}+1,\qquad r=\sigma(s_{\text{r}}),

(4)

where
\sigma
denotes the sigmoid function.
Here
w
is the screening window and
r
is the acceptance width for similarity.
Under our
default inference rule
, if the learned screening window exceeds the maximum sequence length seen during training, we set
w=\infty
.
All inference evaluations use this rule unless a finite-window ablation is explicitly identified.

We first apply unit-length normalization to queries, keys, and values:

\bar{\bm{q}}_{i}=\frac{\bm{q}_{i}}{\|\bm{q}_{i}\|},\qquad\bar{\bm{k}}_{i}=\frac{\bm{k}_{i}}{\|\bm{k}_{i}\|},\qquad\bar{\bm{v}}_{i}=\frac{\bm{v}_{i}}{\|\bm{v}_{i}\|}.

(5)

In implementation, the denominator in each unit-length normalization is clipped from below by a small positive constant for numerical stability.
Normalizing queries and keys bounds query–key similarities in
[-1,1]
, making thresholding well-defined.
Prior work shows that token contributions depend on vector norms as well as attention weights
(
Kobayashi et al., 2020
;
Guo et al., 2024
)
.
We therefore normalize value vectors to unit length so that differences in their norms do not directly determine their contribution magnitudes.

Before computing similarity, we apply minimal positional encoding (MiPE; see
fig.

3
) to
\bar{\bm{q}}_{i}
and
\bar{\bm{k}}_{i}
.
MiPE is a RoPE-like rotation
(
Su et al., 2024
)
applied only to the first two dimensions, with a rotation rate modulated by the learned screening window
w
.
For position
i
, MiPE rotates by angle
\phi(i,w)=i\theta(w)
, where

\theta(w)=\begin{cases}\frac{\pi}{w}\left(1-\frac{w}{w_{\text{th}}}\right),&w<w_{\text{th}},\\
0,&w\geq w_{\text{th}}.\end{cases}

(6)

Here
w_{\text{th}}
is a fixed MiPE threshold.
Thus MiPE is active only for small screening windows and becomes the identity when
w\geq w_{\text{th}}
.

Figure 3:

Comparison between RoPE and MiPE.
RoPE applies rotations across all dimensions with fixed frequencies.
MiPE applies rotation only to two dimensions and adapts the rotation angle based on the learned screening window
w
, becoming inactive when
w\geq w_{\text{th}}
.

Let
\tilde{\bm{q}}_{i}
and
\tilde{\bm{k}}_{j}
denote the resulting position-encoded query and key vectors.
The bounded similarity is then

s_{ij}=\tilde{\bm{q}}_{i}\tilde{\bm{k}}_{j}^{\top},\qquad s_{ij}\in[-1,1].

(7)

We apply a Trim transform (see
fig.

1
) to obtain distance-unaware relevance:

\alpha_{ij}=\left[\max\left(1-\frac{1-s_{ij}}{r},0\right)\right]^{2}.

(8)

This sets relevance exactly to zero whenever
s_{ij}\leq 1-r
, and smoothly emphasizes similarities close to one.

We next apply a causal distance-aware Softmask parameterized by the screening window
w
:

m_{ij}(w)=\begin{cases}1-2\left(\frac{i-j}{w}\right)^{2},&0\leq i-j<\frac{w}{2},\\
2\left(1-\frac{i-j}{w}\right)^{2},&\frac{w}{2}\leq i-j<w,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(9)

This piecewise-quadratic form provides a cosine-like taper while avoiding trigonometric evaluation.
For
w=\infty
, we take
m_{ij}(w)=1
at every causal position.
We then define distance-aware relevance

\alpha^{\text{d}}_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}m_{ij}(w).

(10)

A key contributes only if it survives both content-based and distance-based screening.

Finally, the screening unit aggregates the surviving values:

\bm{h}_{i}=\sum_{j\leq i}\alpha^{\text{d}}_{ij}\bar{\bm{v}}_{j}.

(11)

Because the relevance values
\{\alpha^{\text{d}}_{ij}\}_{j\leq i}
are not constrained to sum to one, the unit can represent the absence of relevant context.
To softly bound the output norm while preserving direction, we define the function

\operatorname{TanhNorm}(\bm{x})=\frac{\tanh\|\bm{x}\|}{\|\bm{x}\|}\bm{x}.

(12)

For sufficiently small
\|\bm{x}\|
, we use the limiting value
\operatorname{TanhNorm}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}
.
The screening output is

\bm{u}_{i}=\operatorname{TanhNorm}(\bm{h}_{i}).

(13)

Overall, screening defines relevance independently for each key, can assign exactly zero relevance to irrelevant keys, and aggregates only the surviving values.

3.3
Gated Screening Tile

A gated screening tile (see
fig.

2(b)
) combines screening-based context retrieval with input-dependent feature selection.
Given token representations
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d_{\text{E}}}
, the tile computes

\bm{q}_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}W_{\text{Q}},\quad\bm{k}_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}W_{\text{K}},\quad\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}W_{\text{V}},\quad\bm{g}_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}W_{\text{G}},

(14)

where
W_{\text{Q}},W_{\text{K}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{E}}\times d_{\text{K}}}
and
W_{\text{V}},W_{\text{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{E}}\times d_{\text{V}}}
.

Using the screening unit from
section

3.2
, the tile retrieves

\bm{u}_{i}=\operatorname{Screening}\bigl(\bm{q}_{i},\{\bm{k}_{j}\}_{j\leq i},\{\bm{v}_{j}\}_{j\leq i},w,r\bigr)_{i}.

(15)

In parallel, it computes a gate via a nonlinear activation

\hat{\bm{g}}_{i}=\tanh(\operatorname{SiLU}(\bm{g}_{i})),

(16)

where SiLU is the sigmoid-weighted linear unit
(
Elfwing et al., 2018
)
.
The tile output is

\Delta\bm{x}_{i}=\left(\bm{u}_{i}\odot\hat{\bm{g}}_{i}\right)\left(\mathrm{e}^{s_{\text{O}}}W_{\text{O}}\right),

(17)

with
W_{\text{O}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{V}}\times d_{\text{E}}}
and learned scalar
s_{\text{O}}
.

This tile can be viewed as a generalization of GLU-style modules
(
van den Oord et al., 2016
;
Dauphin et al., 2017
;
Shazeer, 2020
)
and recent gated-attention variants
(
Qiu et al., 2025
)
, replacing the usual linear or attention-based transformation with screening-based context aggregation.
Thus, each tile jointly performs context selection, feature gating, and projection back to the model space.

To isolate retrieval ability, we introduce

The task presents a shuffled list of mappings from uppercase letters to six-digit integers (e.g.,
A=967892

L=
) requiring the model to retrieve the corresponding value.
The target mapping appears exactly once in the context, requiring the model to locate a unique occurrence.
The number of distinct keys is fixed to avoid key-cardinality confounds, while non-target frequencies are highly skewed so that the unique target does not stand out by rarity alone.

As shown in

4.4
Isolating the Screening Mechanism

To isolate screening from the remaining Multiscreen architecture, we train a controlled suite that matches the 353M Transformer in all model dimensions (including Q/K/V dimensions), initialization, and training hyperparameters, changing only the attention mechanism.
The alternatives include Softmax
1
, SSMax, Entmax (
\alpha=1.5
), Sigmoid,
\mathrm{ReLU}^{2}
, ALiBi, Forgetting Attention, and sliding-window attention, spanning normalization-based, sparse, noncompetitive, distance-decayed, and fixed-window mechanisms.
The Screening variant contains the screening unit—QKV unit-length normalization, MiPE, Trim, Softmask, and TanhNorm—but excludes the output gate and the other components of the gated screening tile.
We include two nested ablations: Screening (RoPE) replaces MiPE with RoPE, while Screening (no window) additionally removes the learned window and Softmask.

Screening is the only evaluated mechanism that maintains both low long-context perplexity and robust retrieval across all evaluated context lengths and target depths without inference-time scaling (
fig.

).
ALiBi, Forgetting Attention, and sliding-window attention maintain relatively stable long-context perplexity yet fail to retrieve distant targets, showing that perplexity alone is insufficient to assess long-range retrieval.
Even at scaling factor
1
, Screening (no window) avoids the abrupt perplexity degradation beyond the training context seen in the other full-context RoPE-based mechanisms, showing that RoPE alone does not explain this failure.
Screening (RoPE) markedly outperforms Screening (no window), demonstrating the importance of the learned window and its associated Softmask.
Full Screening further improves retrieval by replacing RoPE with MiPE, indicating that MiPE provides additional robustness.
Together, these results isolate the long-context behavior to the screening unit itself rather than the output gate or the wider Multiscreen architecture.

Model-side TTFT.

We define model-side time to first token (TTFT) as the latency of a full-context forward pass that processes all positions through the model layers but applies the vocabulary projection only at the final position to produce first-token logits.
This metric isolates model execution and excludes tokenization, sampling, request queuing, and network or serving overhead.
We evaluate TTFT across model sizes at context length
2^{17}
and across context lengths from
2^{11}
to
2^{17}
for the 1.3B models.
All measurements use a single NVIDIA GH200 GPU; the complete measurement protocol is provided in
Appendix

S
.
As shown in
fig.

8

The context-length sweep in
fig.

8

2.5
–
2.9\times
the TTFT of Multiscreen at every evaluated input length.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced
screening
to realize absolute query–key relevance on a fixed bounded scale, independently of competing keys and sequence length.
Its explicit threshold permits exact rejection and empty selection without sequence-length-dependent calibration, making relevance directly inspectable.

Using ABCDigits and long-context perplexity, a controlled comparison of 12 attention mechanisms showed that only screening maintained robust retrieval and low perplexity beyond the training context without inference-time scaling.
Multiscreen, built from parallel gated screening tiles, retained these long-context gains across all evaluated context lengths.

Beyond long-context behavior, Multiscreen improved parameter efficiency, general zero-shot downstream performance, training cost at larger scales, and model-side time to first token.
We also developed a normalization design that kept Multiscreen stable even at a learning rate of
1
and whose adaptation likewise stabilized Transformer.

These results show that directly inspectable absolute relevance can coexist with strong long-context behavior, general zero-shot downstream performance, and computational efficiency, offering an alternative to competitive renormalization and length-dependent inference-time calibration.

Appendix A
Experimental Setup

This section describes the primary Transformer and Multiscreen pretraining configurations under matched training data and token budgets.
Controlled comparisons and ablations document any deviations from this setup in their respective sections.

Tokenization.

We adopt the GPT-2 tokenizer
(
Radford et al., 2019
)
with a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens.

Training Data.

We pretrain the main-experiment models on the SlimPajama
(
Soboleva et al., 2023
)
dataset, a cleaned and deduplicated subset of RedPajama
(
Weber et al., 2024
)
.
After tokenization, documents are packed back-to-back into fixed-length input sequences without inserting EOS tokens, while document boundaries are retained as metadata.
For every nonfinal token in a document, the prediction target is the next token in that document; for the final token, the target is EOS.
For example, documents
(a,b,c,d)
and
(e,f,g,h,i)
with sequence length
8
produce input
(a,b,c,d,e,f,g,h)
and targets
(b,c,d,\langle\mathrm{EOS}\rangle,f,g,h,i)
.
The corresponding document IDs are
(0,0,0,0,1,1,1,1)
and are used only when document masking is enabled.
The tokenized documents contain approximately 627 billion tokens in total.
For these experiments, we use
2^{38}
tokens (approximately 44% of the dataset), obtained by partitioning the tokenized corpus into contiguous sequences of length
2^{12}
and selecting
2^{26}
sequences at random.
For validation, we use
2^{26}
tokens from the SlimPajama validation split, packed into sequences of the same length
2^{12}
as in training.

Document Masking.

When document masking is enabled, each query can attend only to causal keys with the same document ID.
The primary Transformer and Multiscreen runs use this mask.
The controlled attention-mechanism comparison disables document masking for all variants because several fused kernels do not support it, as detailed in
Appendix

O
.

Packing Rationale.

This construction assigns exactly one meaningful prediction target to every input token.
If EOS tokens were instead inserted into the input, each EOS position would either be trained to predict the first token of the next, unrelated document or be excluded from the cross-entropy loss.
The former trains the model to predict the first token of an unrelated document at an EOS position, although that token is not a meaningful continuation of the preceding document.
The latter makes the number of supervised targets per nominal token batch depend on the number of packed document boundaries.
In our construction, document-final tokens predict EOS and all other tokens predict the next token in the same document, so every input token is paired with exactly one meaningful supervised target.
With document masking, every prediction conditions only on tokens from the same document.
Without it, a token’s causal context may include a preceding unrelated document, so the model is trained to predict within the current document in the presence of unrelated preceding context, while document-final tokens still mark document boundaries by predicting EOS.

Model Sizes.

To analyze scaling behavior, we train both Transformer and Multiscreen models across multiple parameter scales.

For the Transformer baseline, we adopt a LLaMA-style architecture
(
Touvron et al., 2023
)
, with architecture hyperparameters based on those used in Pythia
(
Biderman et al., 2023
)
, and apply weight tying between the input embedding and the language modeling head.
We follow Pythia configurations where applicable, including model sizes and peak learning rates.
Detailed configurations of the Transformer baseline are provided in
Appendix

D
.

For Multiscreen, the architecture hyperparameters and initialization scheme are provided in
Appendices

C
and
E
.

Training Setup.

All main-experiment models are pretrained with a sequence length of
2^{12}
(4,096).
We use a global batch size of
2^{22}
tokens.
Together with the
2^{38}
-token budget, this gives
2^{16}
optimizer steps per run.

All models are optimized using AdamW
(
Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019

with
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.95)

\epsilon=10^{-8}
.

2^{12}

This warmup-then-constant schedule keeps the optimization protocol simple and comparable across architectures under a fixed token budget, and is consistent with recent work studying long stable or decay-free learning-rate phases

0.1

1.0

\theta=10{,}000

Biderman et al., 2023

.

for 353M, and

For Multiscreen, we use the same optimizer configuration as the Transformer baseline apart from the learning rate.
We use a learning rate of
2^{-4}
(i.e.,
0.0625

section

4.5
.

Appendix M
ABCDigits: Task Construction and Evaluation Details

We provide additional construction and evaluation details for ABCDigits, the synthetic key–value retrieval benchmark evaluated in
fig.

5(b)
and discussed in
section

4.3
.

Motivation.

ABCDigits builds on synthetic associative recall and key–value retrieval benchmarks studied in prior work
(
Graves et al., 2014
;
Olsson et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2024
;
Arora et al., 2024
)
.
We cast retrieval as a structured completion problem that directly requires recovering the associated value.
This provides a direct probe of retrieval behavior while reducing confounding effects from natural-language semantics, instruction following, and prompt-specific factors.

Task construction.

Each ABCDigits instance presents a shuffled list of equations mapping uppercase letters to six-digit integers, such as
A=967892
.
A query is formed by appending a target letter followed by an equals sign, such as
L=
, and the model must complete the corresponding integer.
The target mapping appears exactly once in the context, requiring the model to locate this unique occurrence.
Since each letter is consistently mapped to a single integer across the context, the task implicitly enforces a one-to-one key–value correspondence without requiring explicit instructions.

A key design choice is that the number of distinct keys remains fixed at 26 uppercase letters regardless of context length.
This removes the bias that retrieval becomes harder simply because the number of keys increases, allowing us to better isolate length-dependent retrieval behavior.

To ensure that extremely low-frequency equations are not anomalous within the context, we construct the non-target portion of the context in two stages.
First, we include exactly one instance of each of the 25 non-target equations.
We then fill the remaining context by sampling additional non-target equations from a highly skewed categorical distribution with weights proportional to
2^{0},2^{1},\ldots,2^{24}
, where the assignment from letters to weights is randomized for each instance.
This avoids making infrequent key–value pairs appear anomalous, ensuring that a range of frequencies is naturally represented within the context.
All non-target equations are then shuffled.
Finally, the unique target equation, such as
L=169428
, is inserted at a specified depth, and the query suffix
L=
is appended.
A concrete example of the resulting prompt is shown in
fig.

5(a)
in the main text.

Evaluation protocol.

Following the visualization protocol commonly used in needle-in-a-haystack evaluations
(
Kamradt, 2023
;
Arize AI, 2023
)
, we measure retrieval accuracy across a grid of context lengths and target depths.
Target context lengths range from
2^{12}
to
2^{17}
, and depths are set to
0.1
,
0.3
,
0.5
,
0.7
, and
0.9
.
Because each equation is retained in full, the realized token count may differ slightly from its target.
Depth is implemented at the equation level: a depth of
d
means that the target equation is inserted after approximately a fraction
d
of the equations in the context, before the final query.

For each context length and depth, we generate 1,000 independent ABCDigits instances.
Every model is evaluated on the same instances at each setting.
We evaluate exact-match accuracy under greedy decoding with temperature
0
.
An instance is counted as correct only if the generated integer exactly matches the target value.

Models and inference settings.

The main ABCDigits evaluation compares Transformer models with 8M, 45M, 353M, and 1.3B parameters against Multiscreen models with 4M, 28M, 286M, and 1.3B parameters.
For all models below 1.3B parameters, results are averaged over three independently trained models, with 1,000 instances evaluated per model and setting.
For the 1.3B models, we evaluate a single trained model for each architecture due to computational constraints.

For Transformer, we apply YaRN
(
Peng et al., 2024
)
with a scaling factor matched to each evaluation context length.
Specifically, for evaluation context length
L
and training context length
L_{\text{train}}=2^{12}
, we set the YaRN scaling factor to
L/L_{\text{train}}
; for example, evaluation at
L=2^{15}
uses a scaling factor of
8
.
Multiscreen has no RoPE component, so YaRN scaling does not apply.
Multiscreen’s default inference rule is fixed across context lengths: after training, any learned screening window exceeding the maximum sequence length seen during training is converted once to
w=\infty
, and all windows then remain unchanged.

Properties of the relevance rules.

Table

8
summarizes the rule-level properties and the cross-length scaling applied to each evaluated mechanism.
“Variable total mass” means that the relevance values are not constrained to sum to one, and “Pairwise independent” means that the relevance assigned to one query–key pair does not depend on the other available keys.
“Exact rejection” requires that an accessible key can receive exactly zero relevance based on its content, excluding zeros imposed only by causal or fixed-window masks, while “Empty selection” requires that all accessible keys can simultaneously receive exactly zero relevance.

“No explicit length term” means that the relevance rule contains no explicit sequence-length term, whereas “No inference scaling” means that the evaluated mechanism is used across context lengths without a context-length-dependent adjustment at inference.
Although inference-time scaling can preserve boundedness and pairwise independence at any fixed factor, it changes the calibration of relevance values across context lengths.
A check mark indicates that a property holds, while a dash indicates that it does not.

We call a relevance mechanism absolute across context lengths when it assigns each accessible query–key pair a relevance value independently on a bounded, length-invariant scale, permits exact rejection and empty selection, does not constrain the total relevance mass to one, and requires no inference-time scaling.
Under this definition, the listed properties are requirements for absolute relevance across context lengths, but satisfying them does not by itself guarantee better empirical performance.
For screening, at fixed query and key representations, adding other keys does not mechanically renormalize existing relevance values, and zero has the explicit meaning of rejection, making the resulting relevance signal easier to inspect and interpret.
We do not directly evaluate this potential interpretability benefit in the present work.

w
and the long-context perplexity and ABCDigits protocols in
Appendices

L
and
M
.

As shown in
fig.

, replacing MiPE with RoPE or NoPE changes long-context perplexity only modestly.
MiPE and NoPE maintain low perplexity without inference-time positional scaling, while the RoPE variant remains comparable under smaller YaRN scaling factors but worsens under larger factors at the longest contexts.
Thus, long-context perplexity alone provides little indication of the large retrieval differences observed below.

Retrieval sharply separates the positional-encoding variants despite their similar perplexity behavior (
fig.

22
).
NoPE retains strong retrieval, showing that the core long-context retrieval ability does not arise from positional encoding alone.
Nevertheless, MiPE outperforms NoPE in all 30 evaluated context-length–depth combinations, increasing mean accuracy across the grid from
90.71\%
to
98.76\%
.
After averaging over target depths at each context length, MiPE maintains
98.22\%
–
99.16\%
accuracy, whereas NoPE ranges from
89.44\%
to
93.80\%
.
In contrast, the RoPE variant loses most retrieval accuracy beyond the pretraining context length both with context-matched YaRN and when unscaled.

These results show that MiPE does more than avoid the failure of conventional positional rotations: it supplies positional structure that consistently improves retrieval over NoPE while preserving scaling-free length extrapolation.

Appendix R
The Role of the Output Gate

The output gate modulates the screening output before its projection back to the model dimension.
To assess its role, we compare the standard 286M Multiscreen configuration with a closely capacity-matched variant without the gate.

Removing the gate reduces the parameter count, so we increase both the number of heads and the number of layers from 32 to 38 in the no-gate control.
The resulting models contain 286.35M and 288.05M parameters, respectively, making the no-gate control approximately
0.60\%
larger than the standard model.
All other training and evaluation settings are unchanged, and both models are evaluated at
2^{36}
training tokens.
The standard Multiscreen results average the same three independently trained runs used in the model-scaling experiments in
section

4.6
, whereas the no-gate control uses a single independently trained run.

At

Because the no-gate control is slightly larger, this advantage cannot be attributed to a larger parameter count in the gated model.
The output gate therefore provides a consistent improvement in overall language-modeling quality.

The no-gate control exhibits consistently higher perplexity, in line with its validation-loss disadvantage, while retaining the same qualitative length-extrapolation behavior as standard Multiscreen (
fig.

24
).
Both variants also maintain near-perfect ABCDigits accuracy across the evaluated context lengths and target depths, with little discernible difference between them (
fig.

25
).
Together, these results indicate that the output gate primarily improves language-modeling quality rather than providing the core mechanism for long-context extrapolation or retrieval.

Appendix S
TTFT Measurement Protocol

All model-side TTFT measurements were performed on a single NVIDIA GH200 GPU with batch size 1, causal masking, and mixed precision with bfloat16 matrix multiplications.
We used the same Transformer and Multiscreen kernel implementations as in training.
Each trained model was measured 100 times, and we report the mean TTFT.
