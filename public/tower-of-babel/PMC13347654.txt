Theoretical Model and Experimental Validation of Coalbed Methane Adsorption/Desorption Dynamics in Cylindrical and Spherical Coal Particles
Authors: Fan Wu, Hao Xu, Zhenlei Li, Hongwei Yan, Shengquan He, Lijun Ning, Huzi Dong, Naigen Tan, Liming Qiu, Yingjie Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coal, as a porous medium, exhibits a strong adsorption capacity for gas. The laws governing gas adsorption and transport are critical research topics for gas disaster prevention and control, as well as for coalbed methane (CBM) extraction. This study takes coal samples from the Guobei Mine as the research subject. Isothermal adsorption experiments under constant pressure conditions were conducted on coal particles of different sizes to investigate the influence of particle size and initial pressure on gas adsorption capacity and adsorption rate. Based on the density gradient diffusion theory, mathematical models for gas adsorption in both cylindrical and spherical coal particles were established. These models were nondimensionalized, solved using the finite difference method for simulation analysis, and subsequently validated against experimental results. The research indicates that under the same initial pressure, smaller coal particle sizes result in a larger cumulative adsorption capacity and a faster adsorption rate. For particles of the same size, a higher initial pressure leads to a greater saturated adsorption capacity and a shorter time to reach adsorption equilibrium. The cumulative gas adsorption capacity conforms to an empirical model where ″the reciprocal of adsorption capacity shows a linear relationship with 1/t0.65″. The simulation results for cylindrical and spherical particles show a high degree of agreement. Under different initial pressures, the nondimensional pressure for both shapes follows a consistent pattern: “rapid surface response → internal gradient transmission → overall trend toward equilibrium”. The equilibrium nondimensional time is approximately 0.08982 at 4.0 MPa, and ranges between 0.3 and 0.45 at 0.5 MPa, with the radial pressure gradient decay rate being consistent. The initial slope, equilibrium time, and saturated adsorption capacity of the nondimensional cumulative adsorption curves are essentially identical. For 50–60 mesh coal samples at 2 MPa, the deviation in the adsorption cycle is less than 0.05 cm3/g, confirming that particle shape has no significant effect on gas adsorption and transport. The established model accurately captures the dynamic characteristics of gas transport. The fit between simulated and experimental values is excellent, with R 2 > 0.99 and an average relative error of only 3.2%. This work breaks through the limitation of the “single spherical particle” assumption, enhances the applicability of the density gradient diffusion theory, and provides reliable theoretical support for the optimization of CBM extraction and gas disaster prevention and control.

Introduction

China’s energy structure is characterized by the prominent feature of “abundant coal, insufficient oil and scarce natural gas”, with coal having long maintained a dominant position in the consumption of primary energy. ,, According to estimates, by 2030, China’s energy demand is projected to reach 5.5 billion tons of standard coal, of which coal will still account for 50%–60%. Its core role in safeguarding energy security will be difficult to replace in the short term. ,, However, with the continuous increase in the depth of coal mining and the increasingly complex geological conditions, gas disasters have become a key hidden danger restricting the safe production of coal mines. − According to the 2008 National Appraisal Report on Coal Mine Gas grades, 40.3% of key state-owned coal mines are classified as high-gas mines or coal and gas outburst mines. Moreover, as the mining depth increases, the gas pressure and gas content of coal seams rise synchronously, leading to a marked exacerbation of gas outburst risks. ,, , Gas accidents have a high rate of severe consequences; once they occur, the resulting casualties and property losses are extremely substantial. Such accidents not only lead to heavy casualties and economic losses, but also underscore the urgency of prevention and control of gas disasters. − (see Figures –)

As a porous medium with a complex pore structure, coal has a large specific surface area and a strong gas adsorption capacity. In some high-gas mines, the volume of gas adsorbed can reach more than 30 times the volume of the coal matrix itself. , Gas is mainly stored in the pores of coal, and micropores with a pore size of less than 100 μm serve as the primary storage space for gas. , The processes of gas adsorption and desorption occur continuously within coal pores; these two processes are reversible and vary with changes in coal structure and environmental conditions. When the gas content stabilizes, the adsorption and desorption rates are equal, and a state of dynamic equilibrium is achieved. The gas adsorption capacity of coal is mainly affected by factors such as coal microstructure, coal particle size, temperature, pressure, coal rank, and water content. ,, In terms of research on the influencing factors of adsorption characteristics, Ding et al. , Combined with experimental and theoretical analyses, the pore structure of coal was characterized by means of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). A fractal adsorption model of coal containing the temperature effect was established and verified based on fractal theory. The results show that the increase in reservoir burial depth and pore fractal dimension is conducive to gas adsorption, while the rise in temperature and the increase in pore size ratio will inhibit adsorption. This study provides a theoretical basis for optimizing gas storage efficiency. Since adsorption occurs at the microscopic scale, numerous experts and scholars have adopted the method of establishing prediction models to conduct research and analysis on this process. Zhang et al. two-phase seepage experiments were carried out, and it was found that the phase permeability of the adsorptive gas–water system was lower than that of the nonadsorptive system. A quantitative prediction model was established accordingly, which provides theoretical support for related engineering practices. Guo et al. addressed the issues of the Langmuir equation’s hypothetical defects and the unclear temperature attenuation law of gas adsorption capacity by constructing and deriving a fractal adsorption model coupled with pore fractal dimension (Df) and adsorption capacity attenuation coefficient (n). The results indicate that the pores of the two coal samples exhibit significant fractal characteristics, with Df values of 2.6279 and 2.93, and n values of −0.006 and −0.004. This model outperforms the Langmuir equation in terms of accuracy and theoretical value, and its temperature effect dependence has been verified. The established prediction method provides theoretical support for the development of coalbed methane (CBM) and unconventional natural gas. Li et al. characterized the pore structure of coal samples using multiple methods, proposed an improved approach for the quantitative characterization of gas adsorption capacity, and verified that coal samples of different coal ranks exhibit significant differences in microstructural properties. The calculated results from the new method show a high degree of consistency with the experimental values, and the model demonstrates excellent prediction reliability. This provides theoretical support for related engineering practices. Su et al. addressed the ambiguity regarding whether the exothermic nature of gas adsorption acts as an inducement for coal expansion. Taking highly adsorptive anthracite as the research object, they calculated the temperature rise and thermal expansion rate of coal by incorporating specific heat capacity and thermal expansion coefficient, and measured the permeability changes using the cubic law. The results show that the exothermic process of gas adsorption on coal leads to temperature rise and thermal expansion; additionally, the higher the pressure, the more significant the thermal expansion and coal deformation, indicating that adsorption-induced expansion may be a type of thermally induced expansion. Wang et al. employed Materials Studio software combined with Perl programming language to apply ultrasonic force via molecular dynamics simulations, investigating the effects of ultrasonic waves with different power levels on the desorption and diffusion of CH4 in gas-bearing coal. The results show that an increase in ultrasonic power can expand the pore volume and specific surface area of the coal matrix, increase the CH4 diffusion coefficient, and promote the migration of CH4 to pores. Moreover, ultrasonic waves exert a pore-expanding effect on coal, providing favorable channels for gas migration.

Coal permeability is a key indicator for evaluating coalbed methane recoverability and predicting gas outbursts, while the gas migration law in coal particles serves as the fundamental basis for studying permeability. , Current academic research on gas flow theory has developed various theoretical systems. Among them, gas seepage theory, which takes Darcy’s Law as its core, holds that the driving force of flow is the pressure difference. After being refined by Academician Zhou Shining and other scholars, this theory has been widely applied to describe gas flow in fractures. , Gas diffusion theory is based on Fick’s Law and focuses on the micropore diffusion process driven by concentration differences, but it has the drawback that the calculated diffusion coefficients deviate significantly from actual values. , The seepage-diffusion theory, in turn, proposes a dual-domain flow model of “Darcy flow in fractures + Fickian diffusion in micropores”, aiming to integrate the advantages of these two theoretical systems. The nonlinear flow theory explores the flow anomaly phenomena under high Reynolds numbers, yet its development has been limited due to issues related to model adaptability.

Currently, the “free–state gas density–gradient diffusion theory” has been proven via experiments and simulations to outperform Darcy’s Law and Fick’s Law. Nevertheless, this theory is founded on the ideal assumption that coal particles are spherical with a uniform particle size, which deviates from the actual scenarios where coal particles present diverse shapes and non - uniform particle sizes. In addition, existing research still has controversies regarding how the variations in coal particle shape and size affect the laws of gas adsorption and migration. Meanwhile, the issue of the permeability coefficient in Darcy’s Law showing abnormal changes with pressure has not yet been reasonably explained. Consequently, it is imperative to conduct gas adsorption experiments and model validation on coal particles with multiple particle sizes and shapes to refine the density - gradient diffusion theory, thereby providing more reliable theoretical support for the efficient extraction of coalbed methane and the accurate prevention and control of gas disasters.

Experimental

Gas adsorption and desorption experiments on coal particles are one of the important methods for studying the gas flow laws in coal particles. Coal samples collected from Guobei Coal Mine were selected for gas adsorption experiments on coal particles with different particle sizes. By investigating the variation of gas adsorption capacity of coal particles with time under different initial pressures at a constant temperature, the change laws of cumulative gas adsorption capacity of coal particles with pressure and particle size under different constant pressures were studied.

Processing of Experimental Data

The Relationship Curve of Gas Cumulative Adsorption Capacity and Time

After the experiment, the plots depicting the variation of cumulative adsorption capacity with time for coal samples of different particle sizes (5–6 mesh, 10–14 mesh, 24–26 mesh, and 50–60 mesh) under the four initial pressures were obtained, as illustrated in Figure .

The adsorption curves of coal samples with the same particle size exhibit significant pressure dependence. When the initial pressure of the GB-1 coal sample increased from 0.5 to 4.0 MPa, the saturated adsorption capacity rose from 1.0 cm3/g to 2.1 cm3/g; the saturated adsorption capacity of the GB-4 coal sample at 4.0 MPa increased by 230% compared with that at 0.5 MPa. High pressure can increase the collision probability between gas molecules and coal pores, promote their diffusion into deep pores, and fully utilize adsorption sites, thereby enhancing the saturated adsorption capacity.

The adsorption curves of all coal samples showed a three-stage characteristic of “rapid increase - slow approaching - stable equilibrium”, and the higher the initial pressure, the shorter the time required to reach equilibrium. For example, the GB-3 coal sample reached equilibrium in approximately 15 h at 4.0 MPa, whereas it took more than 25 h at 0.5 MPa. High pressure can increase the gas density gradient, strengthen the diffusion driving force, and accelerate gas migration. As the adsorption sites become saturated, the adsorption rate slows down until it balances with the desorption rate.

At the same pressure, coal samples with smaller particle sizes exhibited faster initial adsorption rates and shorter equilibrium time; at the same particle size, pressure had a more significant effect on the adsorption rate. The initial adsorption rate of the GB-2 coal sample at 4.0 MPa was three times that at 0.5 MPa. In addition, the saturated adsorption capacity of the same coal sample eventually converged to a fixed value, which was highly consistent with its own adsorption constant a, indicating that the inherent adsorption characteristics of coal play an essential restrictive role, while the initial pressure only affects the adsorption rate and equilibrium time.

The curves showing the variation of cumulative adsorption capacity with time for coal samples of different particle sizes under the same initial pressure are presented in Figure . It can be observed that under the same initial pressure, coal particle size exhibits a significant negative correlation with cumulative adsorption capacity and adsorption rate, and the higher the pressure, the more prominent the effect of particle size difference becomes. At 0.5 MPa, the saturated adsorption capacity of GB-4 was 2.3 times that of GB-1; at 4.0 MPa, the former was 3.6 times that of the latter.

Coal particles with small particle sizes have a large specific surface area and short diffusion paths, which can make full use of micropore adsorption sites and thus improve the cumulative adsorption capacity. The adsorption curves of coal samples with different particle sizes all showed the three-stage characteristic of “rapid increase - slow approaching - stable equilibrium”, and the larger the particle size, the longer the time required to reach equilibrium. At 2.0 MPa, GB-4 reached equilibrium in approximately 12 h, while GB-1 took more than 25 h. Coal particles with large particle sizes have complex internal pores and high diffusion resistance, resulting in a low initial adsorption rate. Moreover, after the surface adsorption sites are saturated, the internal diffusion gradient decreases, the adsorption rate slows down continuously, and the final cumulative adsorption capacity is relatively low.

Under the same pressure, the initial adsorption rate of coal samples with small particle sizes is much higher than that of coal samples with large particle sizes. The average adsorption rate of GB-4 within 0–5 h was 5 times that of GB-1. The saturated adsorption capacity of all coal samples was consistent with their respective adsorption constant a, confirming that the adsorption characteristics of coal are the core restrictive factor, while particle size regulates the adsorption rate and equilibrium time by affecting diffusion efficiency. The advantage of small particle size was not fully manifested at low pressure, but was amplified at high pressure, leading to a significant expansion of the gap in saturated adsorption capacity.

This confirms that the linear relationship is an inherent law of gas adsorption on coal particles under constant pressure, providing solid support for the subsequent rapid prediction of adsorption capacity and rate.

Analysis of Simulation Data for Density Gradient Cylindrical Coal Particles

Simulation Results of Dimensionless Pressure Distribution

Numerical solution generates plots depicting the variation of dimensionless gas pressure inside cylindrical coal particles with dimensionless time and dimensionless radius. Owing to space limitations, this paper presents the dimensionless simulation results of 50–60 mesh coal samples under initial gas pressures of 0.5 MPa, 1 MPa, 2 MPa, and 4 MPa, respectively, as illustrated in Figure .

As can be seen from Figure , under all initial pressure conditions, the gas pressure evolution inside cylindrical coal particles follows a three-stage pattern: rapid surface response – internal gradient conduction–overall equilibrium tendency. Moreover, the higher the pressure, the more pronounced the conduction rate and intensity. At 4.0 MPa, the pressure at the outer edge of coal particles rises sharply within an extremely short period, requiring only t ≈ 0.08982 to achieve approximate internal-external pressure equilibrium. In contrast, at the low pressure of 0.5 MPa, the conduction rate decreases significantly, with equilibrium attained at t ≈ 0.30551. This phenomenon stems from the stronger driving force provided by the larger gas density gradient under high-pressure conditions.

At the same moment, the dimensionless gas pressure inside coal particles decreases gradually with decreasing radius, and the higher the initial pressure, the more obvious the radial pressure difference. At dimensionless time t ≈ 0.01, the pressure difference between the outer edge and the central region reaches 0.7 at 4.0 MPa, while it is only 0.15 at 0.5 MPa. With the elapse of time, the radial pressure difference gradually narrows until homogenization, reflecting the internal diffusion process of gas molecules driven by density gradient. The higher the initial pressure, the shorter the dimensionless time required for coal particles to reach pressure equilibrium, and the higher the overall dimensionless pressure at equilibrium. At 4.0 MPa, the system stabilizes at t ≈ 0.45411 with an equilibrium pressure of approximately 1.4; at 0.5 MPa, the equilibrium time is longer and the equilibrium pressure is only 0.15. Sufficient gas molecules under high pressure can quickly fill the micropores, shorten the equilibrium time, and make the equilibrium pressure closer to the externally set pressure.

The flat stage of the pressure curve reflects the dynamic equilibrium between gas adsorption and diffusion, where the adsorption and desorption rates are equal and the density gradient disappears. The horizontal segment of the equilibrium curve is more stable under high pressure, whereas slight fluctuations occur under low pressure, which may be attributed to insufficient filling of gas molecules. This verifies that the density gradient theoretical model can accurately capture the characteristics of pressure evolution, providing theoretical support for the quantitative description of gas migration laws in coal particles.

Experimental Verification

The simulation results of spherical and cylindrical coal particles were compared via desorption experiments, which verified that coal particle shape exerts no influence on the variation of cumulative gas adsorption capacity with time. Since adsorption and desorption constitute a reversible process, they exert no impact on the experimental results. Therefore, this section only compares the simulation results and experimental results of spherical and cylindrical coal particles based on the density gradient theory. Similarly, the curve comparison diagrams of four particle sizes under 2 MPa pressure and those of GB-3 coal samples under 0.5 MPa, 1 MPa, 2 MPa, and 4 MPa pressures are presented as examples, and logarithmic coordinate diagrams are provided to better illustrate the matching degree of the curves, as shown in Figure –.

Comparison of simulated and experimental values of spherical and cylindrical coal particles with 24–26 mesh size at 4 MPa.

As can be seen from Figures –, the density gradient simulation curves of spherical and cylindrical coal particles almost completely overlap under different particle size and pressure conditions. For the 50–60 mesh coal samples under 2 MPa, the deviation between the two curves during the entire adsorption period is less than 0.05 cm3/g, and the curve characteristics are identical in the logarithmic coordinates. This confirms that coal particle shape has no significant effect on the temporal evolution of cumulative gas adsorption capacity, and the conclusion is universally applicable.

Under all operating conditions, the fitting degree R2 between the density gradient simulation results and the experimental data is greater than 0.99, and the average relative error is only 3.2% for 24–26 mesh coal samples under 4.0 MPa, indicating that the model can accurately capture the dynamic characteristics of gas adsorption. The simulation fitting degree is better under the conditions of small particle size and high pressure, because small particle size coal samples have uniform pore structures, and the gas flow is dominated by density gradient under high pressure, which weakens the boundary effect caused by particle shape.

This study breaks the limitation of the “single spherical shape” assumption in the density gradient theory and verifies its applicability to coal particles of different shapes, thereby providing theoretical support for simplifying assumptions and accurately predicting gas adsorption and extraction efficiency in engineering practice.

Conclusion

Taking coal samples from the Guobei Coal Mine as the research object, this paper establishes and verifies the mathematical models of gas adsorption in cylindrical and spherical coal particles based on the density gradient theory through gas isothermal adsorption experiments on coal particles with different particle sizes under constant-pressure conditions. The effects of particle size, pressure and coal particle shape on the gas adsorption and migration laws are systematically analyzed, and the main research conclusions are summarized as follows.(1)Coal particle gas adsorption is significantly affected by particle size and initial pressure: under the same pressure, the smaller the particle size, the greater the cumulative adsorption capacity and the faster the adsorption rate; under the same particle size, the higher the pressure, the greater the saturated adsorption capacity and the shorter the equilibrium time.
