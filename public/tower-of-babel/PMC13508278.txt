Epidemiology and genetic diversity of Toxoplasma gondii in rescued raptors from wildlife rehabilitation centres in Brazil
Authors: Hilda Fátima Jesus Pena, Marta Brito Guimarães, Liliane Milanelo, Bruna Farias Alves, Solange Oliveira, Juliana Aizawa Porto Abreu, Arlei Marcili, Solange Maria Gennari, Marcos Amaku, Herbert Sousa Soares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRaptors, including the orders Accipitriformes (hawks and kites), Falconiformes (falcons and caracaras), Cathartiformes (New World vultures), and Strigiformes (owls), are found in small forest fragments, parks, vacant lots, outskirts, and open areas within the Metropolitan Region of São Paulo, in the state of São Paulo, Brazil. However, few studies have examined the infectious agents that infect them, particularly protozoa. This research reports on the seroprevalence, isolation, and genetic diversity of the zoonotic parasite Toxoplasma gondii in rescued raptors from two wildlife rehabilitation centres. These birds were fed live mice from a certified institution, as well as quails and insects from commercial establishments.MethodsA total of 151 raptor specimens was sampled, comprising five Cathartiformes, 30 Accipitriformes, 31 Falconiformes, and 85 Strigiformes, representing 19 species. Anti-T. gondii IgG antibodies were identified via the Modified Agglutination Test (MAT; cut-off ≥ 20). Bioassays in mice were performed to isolate T. gondii, and the genetic diversity of the isolates was examined via PCR-RFLP and microsatellite genotyping.ResultsOf the 151 birds, serum samples were collected from 150 specimens. MAT results showed that 62 birds (41.3%) across 14 species were seropositive, including 19 of 29 (65.5%) Accipitriformes, 19 of 31 (61.3%) Falconiformes, and 24 of 85 (28.2%) Strigiformes. Some rare alleles detected included 287 for TUB2, 246 for W35, 203 for TgM-A, 364 and 366 for B17, and 273 for MIV.1.ConclusionsToxoplasma gondii is highly prevalent and genetically diverse among the wild raptors in the studied population. Background

Birds of the orders Accipittriformes (eagles, hawks, kites), Falconiformes (falcons, Old World vultures and caracaras), Cathartiformes (New World vultures) and Strigiformes (owls) are known as raptors or birds of prey, representing a global population of approximately 600 species [1]. Currently, 99 species are recognized in Brazil [2]. Raptors play a vital role in controlling populations of rodents, insects, arachnids, and snakes, as well as pigeons, which they hunt or scavenge as leftovers and carrion. However, uncontrolled urbanization can threaten the survival of these predators [1]. In the Metropolitan Region of São Paulo, Brazil, they inhabit small forest fragments, parks, vacant lots, the outskirts, and open areas, sometimes near human populations and domestic animals, and include dozens of species. There are few studies on the parasites of these birds, particularly protozoa.

Toxoplasma gondii is a cosmopolitan protozoan that can infect all homeothermic vertebrates, including humans. It is estimated that 30% of the human population has already been exposed to this zoonotic parasite [3]. Felids are the only definitive hosts, capable of excreting millions of oocysts in their faeces. These oocysts then sporulate in the environment. Infection can occur through ingesting water or food contaminated with sporulated oocysts; it can also occur through the ingestion of raw or undercooked meat containing cysts with viable bradyzoites or through transplacental transmission of tachyzoites [3]. In Brazil, T. gondii seroprevalence is high in the human population (15–80%) and reaches 100% in some areas [4, 5].

Seroprevalence studies of T. gondii in free-range chickens have been conducted in many countries, including Brazil, because these birds are considered excellent indicators of environmental contamination by oocysts, as they habitually feed on the ground [6]. In Brazil, studies indicate that domestic free-range chickens can have a high seroprevalence of T. gondii. Gennari et al. [7] reported 68.1% in the Amazon Region, and seroprevalences of 63.7% and 16.6% were reported in the Southern Region [8, 9]. Raptors may also serve as sentinel species because they prey on rodents, small mammals and other birds, which are important intermediate hosts for T. gondii. Globally, seroprevalence rates of T. gondii ranging from 8 to 100% have been reported in Accipitriformes (including Old World vultures); 5–67% in Falconiformes and 8–68% in Strigiformes [10]. For similar reasons, both domestic and wild birds may also serve as valuable indicators of the genetic diversity of T. gondii.

Over the past two decades, a dozen studies on the isolation and genotyping of T. gondii in domestic and wild animals have shown that isolates from South America are highly diverse and highly pathogenic in mouse models, unlike those from North America and Europe [11–15]. This unique diversity may affect the severity of toxoplasmosis in South America, particularly in Brazil, Colombia and French Guiana [16–18].

In Brazil, Pena et al. [11] identified 48 PCR-RFLP genotypes among 125 strains, primarily from the Southeastern Region. Over the years, cumulative studies have revealed 224 genotypes among 999 strains from humans, wild animals and livestock across Brazil´s five Regions. Some of the most prevalent genotypes are Type BrI (genotype #6), Type BrII (genotype #11), #13 and #7 [6, 7, 13, 19]. New genotypes continue to be reported. Many of these genotypes are shared among wild animals, humans and livestock. This may be due to intense urbanisation, loss of wildlife habitat, and increased contact between these hosts, which facilitates the circulation of T. gondii strains with the same genotype and the exchange of other pathogens. This has significant implications from a One Health perspective. Given Brazil’s vast territory and greatest biodiversity, information on circulating strains in wild animals, both in fragmented and isolated areas, remains limited.

Research on isolating and genotyping T. gondii in raptors is scarce [10]. The objectives of the present report were to study the epidemiology of T. gondii, including serologic diagnosis, isolation, and genotyping of strains isolated from raptors in the Metropolitan Region of São Paulo, Brazil.

Methods

Raptor sampling

A total of 151 raptor specimens were collected for this study from November 2013 to April 2018. These birds were free-living wild animals rescued due to various health issues. They were taken to one of the following wildlife rehabilitation centres in the Metropolitan Region of São Paulo (MRSP), São Paulo state, Southeastern Brazil (Fig. 1): Parque Ecológico do Tietê - PET in São Paulo municipality (132 specimens) and Centro de Triagem de Animais Silvestres - CETAS in Barueri municipality (19 specimens). These included 5, 30, 31, and 85 raptors belonging to the orders Cathartiformes, Accipitriformes, Falconiformes, and Strigiformes, respectively, covering 19 bird species (Table 1). Of the 151 animals, 60 were male, 59 were female, and 32 birds had no gender information. Gender was determined by necropsy findings (presence of testes or ovaries). No information about the animal´s ages was obtained. The data for each specimen are provided in Additional file 1: Table S1.

If a rescued bird had health conditions that prevented its reintroduction into the wild, the first option was to place it in a rehabilitation centre. If this was unsuccessful, the bird was euthanised humanely using the following protocol: the birds were first sedated with a combination of ketamine (30 mg/kg) [Cetamin 10%®, Syntec, Santana de Parnaíba, Brazil] and midazolam (2 mg/kg) [Dormium®, União Química Farmacêutica Nacional, Pouso Alegre, Brazil], via intramuscular; the sedated animal was then anaesthetised using sodium thiopental (30-50 mg/kg) [Thiopentax®, Cristália, Itapira, Brazil], via intravenous injection. At rehabilitation centres, raptors were fed according to their dietary needs. They were given live prey, which was obtained from a certified animal facility at the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine and Zootechny (FMVZ) at the University of São Paulo (mice) or from commercial establishments (insects and quails). There is no information available about how long it took from the arrival of the bird to euthanasia or blood collection. Blood and tissues were collected and processed as described in the following sections.

Serodiagnosis

Blood samples were collected from the birds by puncturing the right jugular vein, either prior to euthanasia (127 birds) or during rehabilitation, if the bird was to be released back into the wild (23 birds) (Additional file 1: Table S1). It was not possible to collect serum from a female specimen. The blood samples were centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 10 min, and the sera stored at ˗20 °C until analysis. A total of 150 serum samples were obtained and tested for IgG antibodies against T. gondii using the Modified Agglutination Test (MAT), as previously described by [20] and modified by [21], with a 1:5 dilution for screening and a 2-fold dilution for titration. Positive and negative control chicken sera were used in each test. Birds with titres ≥ 20 were considered seropositive.

Bioassays in mice

The raptors were not pre-screened for anti-T. gondii antibodies prior to the bioassays in mice. In brief, to isolate T. gondii, tissues (brain, heart, and pectoral muscle) from all the birds made available by the rehabilitation centres were collected immediately after euthanasia and then processed. A total of 128 raptors, representing 18 species, were bioassayed in mice (Additional file 1: Table S1). Tissues (8–50 g, depending on the size of the bird) were cut into small fragments, mixed and homogenized using either a domestic blender or a pestle and mortar. The tissues were then digested in acidic pepsin and neutralized using a 1.2% sodium bicarbonate solution. The homogenate was subcutaneously inoculated into three to five outbred Swiss Webster mice per group [22], which were provided by the animal facility of the Department of Pathology, FMVZ at the University of São Paulo.

The mice were examined twice daily for signs of toxoplasmosis. Any sick mice were humanely euthanised according to a scoring system that considered their coat condition and behaviour, as described by [23]. For euthanasia, the mice were first sedated with a combination of ketamine (100 mg/kg) [Vetaset®, Fort Dodge, Iowa, USA] and xylazine (10 mg/kg) [Virbaxyl 2%®, Virbac, São Paulo, Brazil], via intramuscular, followed by isoflurane (Isoforine®, Cristália, Itapira, Brazil) inhalation (3–5 mL embedded in cotton) in a sealed chamber, without direct contact with the anaesthetic. The surviving mice were bled at 45 days post-inoculation (p.i.), euthanised as previously described, and their sera tested by MAT at a dilution of 1:25 [24]. To confirm isolation, the lungs and brains of all inoculated mice were examined microscopically for the presence of T. gondii tachyzoites or cysts, respectively, as previously described [3].

The lungs and/or brains of the positive mice were macerated and homogenised separately in sterile 0.85% NaCl. One portion of the homogenate was stored in microtubes at -70 °C until molecular processing. The other portion was mixed with a solution of 10% BFS + 5% DMSO in RPMI 1640 medium and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen.

Genotyping and genetic diversity

Positive mouse homogenates (from two mice per isolate, if there were at least two infected mice) were thawed, and DNA was extracted via 250-µL aliquots using the DNeasy® Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen®, USA) following the manufacturer´s instructions.

For genotypic characterization of the isolates, Multilocus nested-PCR-RFLP (Mn-PCR-RFLP) and Microsatellite (MS) analysis were used. Nested-PCR-RFLP was performed with the markers SAG1, 5’3’SAG2, alt. SAG2, SAG3, BTUB, GRA6, c22-8, c29-2, L358, PK1, Apico [25], and CS3 [11]. Positive controls included the reference strains RH (Type I), PTG (Type II), CTG (Type III), TgCgCa1 (Cougar), MAS, and TgCatBr5. To characterise the strains, the PCR-RFLP genotypes obtained were compared to those available in the ToxoDB database (http://toxodb.org/toxo/), major review papers [10, 13], the laboratory page of Dr. Chunlei Su (https://faculty.utk.edu/Chunlei), a search platform (https://vps.fmvz.usp.br/toxo_genotypes/) and in recent publications. The conventional approach was to use the PCR-RFLP naming system for isolates typed using 10 genetic markers, with CS3 not included in this system [25]. Each genotype is represented by a number (#number). The CS3 marker was used to identify genotypic variants. Dr. Chunlei Su (personal communication) was asked for genotype numbers for the new genotypes. MS analysis was performed with eight typing markers (TUB2, W35, TgMA, B18, B17, M33, IV.1, and XI.1) and seven fingerprinting markers (N60, N82, AA, N61, N83, M48, and M102), under the conditions described by Ajzenberg et al. [26]. The reference strain PTG served as the positive control, and for characterization, the genotypes obtained were compared with MS results from previously reported Brazilian strains, summarized by Gennari et al. [7].

A NeighborNet phylogenetic network was created via SplitsTree4 (v.4.16.1) software [27, 28], on the basis of the PCR-RFLP genotyping results from this study, including the most common PCR-RFLP genotypes observed in Brazil (Type BrI, Type BrII, Type BrIII, #13, and #7), as well as the classical lineages Types I, II, II variant, and III. Simpson’s diversity index (D) [29] was employed to assess the genetic diversity of T. gondii within the studied population. The values of this index range between 0 and 1; the higher the value, the greater the diversity.

Statistical analysis

Confidence intervals for the proportions were calculated via the exact binomial distribution. Comparisons were made between the proportions of seropositive individuals by bird order and gender using Fisher’s exact test. A Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test was used to compare the titre between the groups and isolation of T. gondii. A significance level of 0.05 was adopted for all comparisons. Statistical analyses were conducted via the R program [30].

Results

Serodiagnosis and isolation

Of the 150 raptors examined, 62 (41.3%) were seropositive to T. gondii (MAT ≥ 20). This included 19/29 (65.5%) Accipitriformes, 0/5 (0.0%) Cathartiformes, 19/31 (61.3%) Falconiformes, and 24/85 (28.2%) Strigiformes (Table 2). There was a statistically significant difference in the proportion of positive animals among the orders (p < 0.001). Of the 19 species examined, 14 had animals with anti-T. gondii antibodies. There was no statistically significant difference (p = 0.14) in the proportion of seropositive individuals between females (22/58 − 38%) and males (31/60 − 52%).

Of the 447 mice inoculated in the bioassays, 372 survived until the end of the 45-day experiment (Additional file 1: Table S1). Serological analysis showed that 367 of the surviving mice were seronegative (MAT < 25), and microscopic examination confirmed the absence of T. gondii in their tissues. Five of the surviving mice were seropositive (MAT ≥ 25) and had positive tissue results for T. gondii (brain cysts). The other 75 mice exhibited acute signs of T. gondii infection and were humanely euthanised. In summary, of the 80 infected mice, 75 (93.8%) developed acute illness, with euthanasia performed at 10-20 days p.i. (62.7%), at 21-28 days p.i. (28%), and at 31-42 days p.i. (9.3%). The other five infected mice developed chronic infection without showing any clinical signs.

Further information regarding the statistical analyses can be found in Additional file 2: Tables S2 and S3.

Genotyping and genetic diversity

Sixteen distinct genotypes were identified among 27 isolates using PCR-RFLP genotyping. These included #11 (Type BrII – seven isolates), #19 (two), #21 (one), #22 (one), #33 (one), #51 (one), #69 (one), #111 (one), #162 (one), #175 (three), #206 (one), and five newly identified genotypes: #350, #351, #352, #353, and #354, each of which was represented by a single isolate. A mixed genotype was detected in the TgGeralBrSP2 isolate (Table 4). The genetic diversity (D) among the 26 isolates, excluding the mixed isolate, was 0.92. The constructed NeighborNet phylogenetic network (Fig. 2) illustrates the genetic relationships among the 16 genotypes in this study. It shows that genotypes from raptor isolates diverge from classical lineages and the most common genotypes in Brazil. However, Type BrII (seven isolates) and genotype #69 (one isolate) cluster together.

Discussion

The current study is the most extensive to date on the epidemiology and diversity of T. gondii in raptors, including 151 specimens from 19 raptor species across four orders.

A high T. gondii seroprevalence was observed among Accipitriformes and Falconiformes, contrasting with the lower seroprevalence seen in Strigiformes. Direct comparisons with other studies are difficult due to variability in analysis, including number of animals tested, serologic tests and cut-off endpoints. Variations in bird species, diets, prey availability, geographic regions, and animal’s origins also make direct comparisons difficult [10, 31]. In the present study, a cut-off of 1:20 was selected based on bioassay results as a strong indicator of true exposure, as T. gondii isolates were obtained from species from the orders Accipitriformes, Falconiformes and Strigiformes with this serologic titre, and no isolates were obtained from animals with 1:5 titres. Furthermore, isolates from birds with 1:10 titres were only obtained in the Falconiformes.

The observed exposure to T. gondii does not necessarily reflect true seroprevalence. The underlying rationale for this observation is that the samples were not collected in a random manner. Moreover, it was not possible to eliminate the possibility of a primary exposure in the rehabilitation centres, due to the absence of data on the duration of the animals’ stay in these institutions. However, the living prey included rodents from an accredited institution and quails from technologically advanced commercial establishments. Conversely, seroprevalence data on other hosts in the MRSP demonstrated 21.5% in stray cats [32] and 32.6% in bats [33]. This finding lends further support to the hypothesis that the agent under investigation is circulating within the area under study.

The present report did not detect a seroprevalence difference based on genre, corroborating the prevalence study in raptors from the USA by Lindsay et al. [34] and, in general, other studies with different hosts, including mammals [32]. However, the results could not be evaluated according to the age of the birds, which is a limitation because seropositivity is normally age-related. Older animals are generally more exposed to T. gondii throughout their lives [3].

This is the first report of anti-T. gondii antibodies in the Accipitriformes Geranoaetus albicaudatus, Parabuteo unicinctus, Buteo brachyurus, Elanus leucurus, Amadonastur lacernulatus, and Accipiter striatus.

A more extensive study of the Cathatiforme Coragypys atratus was conducted in the MRSP, which found that 13.2% of black vultures were seropositive using a lower MAT cut-off of 1:5 [35]. In the USA, Love et al. [36] reported a low seroprevalence of 12% (1/8) in black vultures (MAT cut-off 1:25). Sato et al. [37] reported seronegative results in five specimens tested via the Indirect Immunofluorescence. This observation was also made in the present study. The findings of these studies are consistent with the conclusion that the titres in black vultures are low (1:5 to 1:25), which may be indicative of vultures’ resistance to toxoplasmosis. This hypothesis is reinforced by the fact that no viable parasites have been isolated from this group of raptors to date.

The present study demonstrated antibodies against T. gondii in Falco sparverius for the first time. The species Caracara plancus exhibited the highest seroprevalence (65%), contrasting significantly with a major report result in free-ranging caracaras (5.2%; 6/115) from Recife city, state of Pernambuco, Northeast Region of Brazil [31], using a MAT cutoff of 1:25. This finding suggests that the observed variations in the study’s outcomes may be attributable to the distinct ecological environments or the infection-related pressures present in the rehabilitation centres.

The presence of anti-T. gondii antibodies was identified in six species of Strigiformes, with the first documented cases observed in Megascops choliba, Asio clamator, Asio stygius, and Pulsatrix koeniswaldiana. In comparison with Accipitriformes and Falconiformes, Strigiformes demonstrated a significantly lower seroprevalence in the present study. A more comprehensive understanding of these variations can be attained through a detailed examination of the trophic ecology of the Strigiformes species under study in the state of São Paulo, including A. clamator, A. stygius, and T. furcata, which are predominantly carnivorous in nature. In contrast, the smaller species, A. cunicularia and M. choliba (the latter with the largest sample size in this study), primarily consume insects and other arthropods [38].

In the present study, the isolation of viable parasites was predominantly successful in raptors with MAT titres ≥ 20, as observed in free-ranging chickens [7]. A robust positive correlation between antibody titre and isolation success was observed, which corroborates other findings [7, 39]. No isolates were obtained from raptors with MAT titres ≤ 5 (59 bioassays), suggesting a high negative predictive value of this serologic test for raptors. The Strigiformes order was the subject of the highest number of bioassays, however the lowest number of isolates were obtained. This outcome is consistent with the MAT titres, given that the majority of the bioassayed raptors with MAT titres ≤ 5 belonged to this order.

Toxoplasma gondii was isolated from nine out of 18 bioassayed raptor species. For the first time, viable parasites were isolated from G. albicaudatus, E. leucurus, F. femoralis, Asio clamator and Asio stygius, thereby confirming these species can be intermediate hosts for T. gondii. One isolate of F. sparverius was previously obtained by Lindsay et al. [34] and another by Dubey et al. [40] in the USA, but it is the first report in Brazil. During bioassays, most infected mice exhibited signs of acute toxoplasmosis and were humanely euthanised. The inability to ascertain the virulence of individual strains was due to the unavailability of information regarding the inoculum dose. However, it is well documented that most South American strains, when subjected to the same isolation protocol, induce severe acute disease in mice. This stands in contrast to most isolates from the Northern Hemisphere [7].

In the USA, Lindsay et al. [34] obtained 27 isolates from raptors, primarily from the genus Buteo. However, these isolates were not subsequently genotyped. There is limited information regarding genotyping T. gondii isolates from raptors worldwide, with a limited number of studies conducted in the USA and Turkey predominantly demonstrating the classical lineages II and III, as would be anticipated in the Northern Hemisphere [10].

In the present study, the genotyping of the isolated T. gondii strains from raptors demonstrated a high genetic diversity in the studied region. The high diversity of the strains is observed with various parameters. The Simpson’s Index obtained using PCR-RFLP data was high (0.92), which corroborates previously obtained results (0.96) in the Southeastern Region of Brazil [7]. The description of five new PCR-RFLP genotypes never previously reported in Brazil and elsewhere, even with more than 200 genotypes already reported across almost 1000 isolates over 20 years of studies, is also noteworthy. The high reticulation observed in the phylogenetic network and the divergence from the most common genotypes circulating in Brazil and from the classical lineages also underscore the diversity of the strains circulating in the studied region. Finally, the higher power of resolution of MS analysis [26] revealed 26 genotypes and a mixed genotype, meaning each isolate corresponded to a different genotype.

The eleven genotypes previously described in this study were also identified in the Southeastern Region of Brazil across different hosts, including free-ranging chickens, which suggests environmental contamination with T. gondii oocysts in the area. Specifically, at the MRSP, seven have already been reported, one of them in a cat, and nine are described for the first time. Furthermore, three genotypes were identified in isolates from cases of human congenital toxoplasmosis: Type BrII [41], #162 [41, 42], and #175 [43], confirming human infections usually reflect genotypes circulating in animals and environment [3]. Type BrII was identified in seven isolates, thereby confirming the extensive circulation of this lineage in Brazil [11]. It was observed among a diverse range of intermediate hosts, including chickens, sheep, rabbits, cats, pigs, wild birds, and humans.

The most prevalent genotype identified by MS analysis was South America 1 (corresponding to Type BrII by PCR-RFLP), and one isolate exhibited a mixed genotype, which was also identified by PCR-RFLP. Most genotypes exhibited one to three unique alleles at the typing markers, with some considered rare, particularly in the Southeastern Region of Brazil [7]. This is of particular significance in the context of One Health, as strains with multiple unique alleles at typing markers have been associated with severe toxoplasmosis in immunocompetent patients in French Guiana and Suriname, in the Amazon region [7]. One isolate from R. magnirostris (TgRupmaBrSP1) exhibited a combination of six unique and rare alleles, two of which (203 at TgM-A and 246 at W35) had already been reported in three cases of severe human toxoplasmosis in French Guiana [17]. It is noteworthy that Pena et al. [23] reported an isolate displaying this allelic combination in a case of rare pulmonary toxoplasmosis in an immunocompetent patient from the MRSP. Therefore, the present report supports the finding that strains with unique and rare alleles associated with severe human toxoplasmosis circulate among different hosts in MRSP, not only in the Amazon Region. These results highlight the high discriminatory power of this molecular tool in revealing the genetic diversity of T. gondii.

As previously discussed, it is not possible to definitively rule out the possibility of infection occurring in the rehabilitation centres. This affects the conclusion that wild raptors serve as sentinels for T. gondii diversity. However, it is important to note that T. gondii has previously been isolated and/or genotyped in free-living wild animals in the MRSP, including a woodpecker [44], four bats [45, 46], and a synanthropic rat [47].

Finally, it is important to situate the geographical area under study within a broader ecological context, and more specifically within the context of the epidemiology of toxoplasmosis. The MRSP covers 3% of São Paulo state and has a population of 21 million, representing 26% of the state’s total. The region is densely populated, with the capital housing 56% of its population [48]. Urban growth has encroached on the remaining Atlantic Forest remnants, causing habitat loss and fragmentation. This leads to an ecological imbalance where synanthropic animals thrive, while wild fauna is limited to isolated fragments. Despite this, São Paulo city hosts over 1,400 wild species, including 100–120 mammals and about 500 bird species, that move between green spaces and urban areas, close to humans [49]. Urban parks and squares serve as important habitats and corridors, allowing animals to feed, rest, and travel between green areas [50]. However, there are around two million stray cats and dogs just in São Paulo city [51]. Feral cats prey heavily on native wildlife, impacting populations of marsupials, birds, bats, and rodents, particularly in protected areas. Stray cats are a leading cause of mortality for birds and mammals in the USA, killing billions yearly [52]. In São Paulo city, feral cats have caused a decline in the population of songbirds and small mammals [53].

For T. gondii, even a low prevalence in some of the animals hunted by cats can lead to widespread environmental contamination through oocysts, potentially infecting many other hosts [54]. The presence of infected prey, whether live animals or carcasses, is a key factor in the epidemiology of T. gondii. This context underscores the importance of the findings from this study. By identifying the circulating genotypes of T. gondii, the results reveal the close connection that may exist between humans, wild and domestic animals, especially in areas facing urbanization and habitat fragmentation.

Conclusions

This study constitutes the most extensive investigation to date into the epidemiology and diversity of T. gondii in wild raptors, comprising 151 specimens from 19 different raptor species. The present study is the first to demonstrate that the raptor species G. albicaudatus, E. leucurus, F. femoralis, A. clamator and A. stygius are susceptible to T. gondii infection, thereby confirming their role as intermediate hosts. The existence of a high diversity of T. gondii strains circulating in the MRSP has been demonstrated, corroborating the findings of other studies conducted in various Brazilian geographic regions and thereby contributing to the advancement of knowledge concerning the circulating strains and their biological and genotypic characteristics.
