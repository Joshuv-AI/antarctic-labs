Sustainable phosphorus recovery and starch-protein-rich biomass generation from wastewater via duckweed
Authors: Yan Chen, Yuxin Cai, Yuhan Zhang, Siyi Li, Siyun Long, Xiaoqin Xiong, Min Liu, Junyan Chen, Shizhuo Ma, Liyuan Zhang, Zuoliang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEscalating phosphorus (P) pollution and resource scarcity underscore the urgent need for sustainable wastewater treatment and nutrient recovery systems. However, systematic research on P-removal efficiency and its recovery across concentrations under mixotrophic conditions is still lacking. In this study, Spirodela polyrhiza was used to investigate the physiological responses to P-containing simulated wastewater with respect to its efficacy for pollutant removal and the potential for simultaneous biomass recovery. Our results indicate that S. polyrhiza achieved a maximum total P-removal efficiency of 93.78% at 17 mg/L P, while exhibiting high adaptive plasticity. Under P deficiency, carbon was preferentially allocated to starch accumulation (up to 214 mg/g DW), while elevated P levels stimulated protein and lipid synthesis. The biomass was also enriched with essential minerals, including P, K, Mg, and Zn, supporting its potential use as animal feed, biofuel feedstock, or biofertilizer. This synergistic approach offers a practical pathway to advance the circular bioeconomy and address pressing environmental challenges related to nutrient pollution and resource scarcity.Subject areas: Environmental science, Environmental Biotechnology

Introduction

Phosphorus (P) is a nutrient essential for global food security, underpinning both agricultural productivity and ecosystem functioning.1 However, inefficient P management has led to widespread eutrophication and severe degradation of aquatic environments worldwide.2 At the same time, phosphate rock reserves, the primary source of fertilizers, are finite and geographically concentrated, creating a dual challenge of resource scarcity and environmental pollution.3,4,5 Anthropogenic activities, including intensive agriculture, livestock production, and insufficient wastewater treatment, have significantly increased global P discharges into water bodies.6,7 These wastewater sources span a wide concentration range, depending on the source. Agricultural runoff and drainage water contain 0.07–7 mg/L P.8,9 Municipal wastewater influent commonly contains 5–20 mg/L P.10 Anaerobic digestion supernatants frequently contain 50–100 mg/L P.11,12 Wastewater from high-strength distillery or pig farms can contain P concentrations ranging from 500 to 1000 mg/L.13,14 This wide variability in P loads requires a comprehensive understanding of aquatic plant responses across the variable concentration range to guide treatment design for different applications. Moreover, approximately 18 million tons of P are released into the environment annually,15,16 accelerating eutrophication processes. Such P pollution not only degrades water quality but also threatens biodiversity by promoting toxic algal blooms and hypoxia, thereby compromising drinking water supplies.17,18 Meanwhile, phosphate rock reserves are being depleted, with estimates suggesting economic exhaustion within 50–100 years.19 The prevailing linear P economy, which moves from mining to application to aquatic loss, is environmentally unsustainable. Transitioning toward a circular economy requires not only the removal of P from waste streams but its effective recovery into a usable form that can be reintroduced into productive systems. Furthermore, the global “dual carbon (C)” strategy (peak carbon, carbon neutrality) necessitates low-carbon technologies for environmental management and biomass valorization.20 Recovering P from waste streams and enabling its reuse as a valuable product rather than losing it in sludge that requires costly disposal is, therefore, critical for closing the P loop.

Conventional P removal methods, such as chemical precipitation or enhanced biological P removal, typically generate P-rich sludge that requires costly disposal and offers limited recovery potential.21,22 Phytoremediation using aquatic plants represents a sustainable alternative by leveraging natural biological processes. Within this context, mixotrophic duckweeds (Lemnaceae family), particularly Spirodela polyrhiza, have emerged as promising candidates for sustainable wastewater treatment and nutrient recycling. They combine rapid growth, high nutrient uptake, ease of harvest, tolerance to variable water conditions, and substantial biomass yield with the additional ability to thrive under mixotrophic conditions.23,24,25,26,27,28,29 In natural aquatic environments, organic carbon sources are ubiquitous and play a crucial role in plant metabolism. However, systematic studies assessing P removal efficiency by aquatic plants under mixotrophic conditions remain limited. Most duckweed research has focused on photoautotrophic or nutrient-defined systems, leaving a significant knowledge gap regarding how organic carbon and P interact to support simultaneous nutrient recovery and biomass valorization. Unlike microalgae or terrestrial energy crops, duckweed does not compete with food production for arable land and can be continuously harvested. Its biomass, rich in starch and protein, is well suited for applications in animal feed, bioenergy, and biodegradable plastics.23,30,31 Notably, S. polyrhiza exhibits exceptional P accumulation efficiency,32 with removal rates exceeding 80% reported in swine wastewater.33 The use of mixotrophy in duckweed further enhances this performance by combining photosynthesis and organic carbon uptake for robust growth and efficient recovery in complex wastewater environments.

Integrating P remediation with the co-production of starch- and protein-rich biomass directly supports China’s “dual carbon” goals, which target peak carbon emissions and carbon neutrality. Duckweed systems not only sequester atmospheric CO2 during growth but also produce biomass that can substitute for fossil fuels or energy-intensive feed ingredients, thereby contributing to a net carbon sink.34,35 Realizing this potential, however, requires a thorough understanding of how different P concentrations, ranging from nutrient-poor to highly eutrophic conditions, affect both pollutant removal (P, nitrogen [N], and chemical oxygen demand [COD]) and the biochemical composition (starch and protein) of mixotrophic duckweed.

Although previous studies have demonstrated duckweed’s capability for wastewater nutrient removal,32,33,36,37 few have systematically linked P recovery to multi-component biomass profiling across a broad P gradient under mixotrophic conditions. Moreover, the concurrent recovery of valuable macro- and micro-elements remains insufficiently explored. Most existing studies have focused either on nutrient removal efficiency in isolation or on biomass production under optimal conditions, without integrating these aspects across a comprehensive P gradient that reflects real-world wastewater variability. Furthermore, although organic carbon sources are ubiquitous in natural and engineered aquatic systems and play a crucial role in plant metabolism, systematic studies assessing P removal efficiency by aquatic plants under mixotrophic conditions remain limited. This study addresses these research gaps by: (1) systematically evaluating P removal efficiency and water quality improvement by S. polyrhiza under mixotrophic conditions across a wide range of P concentrations (0–425 mg/L); (2) comprehensively analyzing the biomass for starch, protein, lipids, and essential minerals to link P recovery with multi-component biomass valorization; and (3) elucidating a distinct carbon allocation strategy where S. polyrhiza prioritizes starch accumulation under P deficiency, offering a novel perspective for low-cost, efficient biomass production. This work aims to identify optimal P levels for maximizing both pollution removal and high-value biomass production, thereby providing a scientific basis for deploying duckweed-based systems as sustainable, multi-output technologies that align with circular economy principles and carbon neutrality goals.

Results and discussion

P-dependent growth responses and biomass accumulation

The morphological and physiological responses of S. polyrhiza exhibited a strong dependence on P availability, illustrating a clear dose-response relationship that underpins its potential for wastewater remediation and resource recovery. Under severe P deficiency (0 mg/L), fronds initially maintained normal growth for 5 days but progressively declined thereafter. By day 10, approximately 40% of fronds exhibited chlorosis, which advanced to widespread necrosis (blackening) by day 15. After 20 days, partial frond sinking was observed alongside extensive chlorosis and necrosis. At 3.4 mg/L P, growth during the first 10 days appeared normal; however, by day 15, marginal yellowing and blackening appeared, and frond quality deteriorated further by day 20 (Figure 1A). These morphological changes were directly reflected in biomass data, which showed that both fresh and dry weights (FW and DW, respectively) of S. polyrhiza increased significantly between day 5 to day 15 across all treatments (Figures 1B and 1C). After 5 days, FW under 0 mg/L P was significantly lower than other concentrations. DW measurements revealed no significant difference between 0 and 3.4 mg/L P treatments, but both were significantly lower than the 17, 85, and 425 mg/L P groups.

The integration of morphological and biomass data demonstrates that P availability acts as a primary regulator of both structural integrity and biomass productivity in S. polyrhiza. These results align with previous reports of significant biomass inhibition under low P conditions,38 confirming P as a critical limiting factor for early growth, particularly during initial treatment periods where different P levels had pronounced effects on biomass accumulation. This response pattern is consistent with the response of many aquatic plants to P nutrition.39,40

In contrast, moderate P concentrations (17 mg/L) promoted healthy frond development with sustained proliferation throughout the experimental period, with only slight chlorosis and necrosis visible after 20 days (Figure 1A). Higher P concentrations (85 and 425 mg/L) stimulated robust growth, with frond multiplication exceeding 200% over 20 days, demonstrating a strong positive correlation between P availability and vegetative propagation. Nevertheless, biomass accumulation revealed a saturation trend. Although both FW and DW generally increased with higher P concentrations, differences between the high-P groups (85 and 425 mg/L) diminished over time. For instance, DW at 10 days did not differ significantly between 17 and 85 mg/L, and by day 15, differences between 85 and 425 mg/L were also nonsignificant. By day 20, both FW and DW increased significantly with increasing P concentrations, except for no significant difference in FW between 85 and 425 mg/L (Figures 1B and 1C). This saturation effect aligns with the general law of diminishing returns of nutrient effects, wherein the growth-promoting effect of P weakens beyond a certain threshold.41,42 Although the highest P concentration (425 mg/L) performed well in DW accumulation, it did not consistently outperform the sub-high concentration group, indicating that excessive P supply provides no additional benefit and may even inhibit plant growth due to nutrient redundancy or potential toxicity.43

Chlorophyll content dynamics provided further insights into the physiological adaptations underlying these growth responses.44,45 At lower (0 and 3.4 mg/L) and moderate P (17 mg/L P) concentrations, chlorophyll content declined over time, indicating insufficient P supply for maintaining photosynthetic function. Conversely, chlorophyll levels remained consistently high in the 85 and 425 mg/L treatments, with the 85 mg/L group showing the highest overall chlorophyll content (Figure 1D). This bell-shaped trend, peaking at 85 mg/L, represents an optimal P level for photosynthetic performance.

The carotenoid content and chlorophyll a/b ratio patterns revealed additional adaptive mechanisms.46,47 Under 0 and 3.4 mg/L P stress, carotenoid synthesis was significantly inhibited, likely due to metabolic disruption and precursor deficiency caused by P limitation. At 425 mg/L P treatment, the chlorophyll a/b ratio reached a peak value of 2.87 after 10 days (Figures 1E and 1F), possibly due to phosphate-induced precipitation of Mg2+, reducing its intracellular availability.

Overall, these results demonstrate the remarkable phenotypic plasticity of duckweed under variable nutrient regimes. The optimal P supply (85–425 mg/L) enhanced both photosynthetic capacity and vegetative propagation, whereas deficiency and excess both induced stress responses. Importantly, short-term exposure to 17 and 85 mg/L P significantly promoted biomass accumulation in S. polyrhiza, but prolonged exposure to high P led to growth stabilization, indicating a balance between nutrient sufficiency and saturation. These findings provide crucial guidance for optimizing duckweed-based wastewater treatment and biomass production systems, showing that fine-tuning P concentrations can maximize both remediation efficiency and biomass yield.

Membrane integrity under P-induced stress

Evans blue staining is a reliable indicator of cell membrane damage48 and was, therefore, employed to further investigate the effects of different P concentrations on the membrane integrity of S. polyrrhiza. The results revealed a strong dependence on P availability (Figures 2A and 2B). Under lower and moderate P stress, both staining intensity and absorbance at 600 nm (A600) increased markedly over time, with the most pronounced effects at 0 mg/L P. This progressive rise reflects a gradual loss of membrane integrity under prolonged P deficiency, likely linked to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and enhanced lipid peroxidation.49,50

In contrast, fronds exposed to higher P concentrations maintained relatively stable staining intensity and A600 values across all time points, suggesting that sufficient P supply effectively preserved membrane structure and integrity.51 Notably, at 425 mg/L P stress, both parameters were significantly higher compared with the 85 mg/L P group on days 5 and 10, indicating that initial P excess triggered a mild stress response, potentially due to initial osmotic imbalance or ion toxicity. However, these differences disappeared by days 15 and 20, reflecting the capacity of S. polyrhiza to acclimate to high-P conditions without enduring toxic effects. These results collectively indicate that P deficiency leads to a significant time-dependent decline in cell membrane integrity, whereas adequate to high P levels (≥85 mg/L) effectively maintain membrane stability. This confirms the crucial role of P in preserving plant cell membrane integrity.

Starch content and localization in S. polyrhiza

Starch content serves as a crucial indicator for assessing growth vigor and environmental adaptability, playing a significant role in understanding the physiological and ecological characteristics of S. polyrhiza.52,53 In this study, the effects of P concentrations and treatment duration on starch accumulation were systematically investigated. The results revealed that starch dynamics were co-regulated by P availability and exposure time, exhibiting a significant dose-time interaction (Figure 3A). After 5 days of treatment, the starch content was significantly higher in the 17 mg/L P group compared with all other treatments. This suggests that a moderate P level can effectively promote starch synthesis under short-term culture, possibly due to a balanced state between P supply and carbon metabolism. By day 10, however, the 85 mg/L P group reached the highest starch value recorded in the entire experiment (213.81 mg/g), which significantly exceeded all other treatments. The 3.4 and 425 mg/L groups also showed significantly higher starch content compared with the 0 and 17 mg/L groups. The lower starch accumulation in the 17 mg/L group may reflect a resource investment strategy prioritizing immediate growth over storage. In contrast, both low and high P environments appear to redirect resources toward starch accumulation through stress signaling and energy-driven strategies.

At day 15, starch content in the 0, 3.4, and 17 mg/L P groups reached the highest levels (213.34, 213.26, and 214.04 mg/g, respectively). This indicates that long-term moderate and lower P conditions may enhance the starch synthesis machinery, whereas higher P levels suppress accumulation. By day 20, the 0 mg/L P group exhibited significantly higher starch content than all other groups. This increase may result from enhanced activity of ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase in the starch biosynthesis pathway.54 This demonstrates that under extreme P deficiency, S. polyrhiza modulates carbon allocation to maintain starch reserves, which is consistent with previous reports.55 This characteristic contrasts with most terrestrial crops, which typically show reduced starch accumulation under P deficiency. Notably, this peak in starch accumulation under P-deficient conditions coincided with progressively increasing membrane damage, as evidenced by Evans blue staining (Figures 2A and 2B). This correlation suggests that starch accumulation may be part of a broader stress response strategy, where the plant prioritizes carbon storage as a survival response when membrane integrity and overall metabolic function are compromised by nutrient limitation.

Histochemical staining provided further histological evidence that strongly supported the quantitative starch data. Intense blue coloration was observed in fronds from the 85 mg/L P group at day 10, followed by the 0, 3.4, and 17 mg/L P groups at day 15, and the 0 mg/L P group at day 20 (Figure 3B). This staining pattern indicates a divergent carbon partitioning strategy adopted by this aquatic plant. Moreover, the high starch accumulation in S. polyrhiza does not rely on high P supply, as the 425 mg/L P treatment did not further enhance starch production. This indicates its characteristic ability for efficient starch synthesis under low-P conditions, offering new perspectives for developing starch production systems with low cost and reduced fertilizer requirements.

Protein and lipid accumulation in P-enriched biomass

Soluble protein and triacylglycerol (TAG) contents are key indicators for evaluating the nutritional quality, energy metabolism, and environmental adaptability of plants.56,57,58 After 5 days of treatment, soluble protein contents in the 85 and 425 mg/L P groups were significantly higher than in all other treatments, reaching 15.81 and 15.25 mg/g, respectively. This early stimulation indicates that abundant P strongly promotes protein synthesis, reflecting its essential role in energy metabolism and protein translation processes.59,60 By day 10, however, protein contents converged, showing no significant differences. At day 15, the 17, 85, and 425 mg/L P groups showed comparable protein levels, all significantly higher than the 0 and 3.4 mg/L groups, with the 0 mg/L treatment consistently yielding the lowest values. This pattern suggests that protein accumulation tends to saturate under moderate to high P conditions. After 20 days, only the 0 mg/L group remained significantly lower than the 17 mg/L group (Figure 4A). These results confirm that prolonged P starvation inhibits protein synthesis.

TAG accumulation also exhibited significant P- and time-dependent dynamics. On day 5, the 425 mg/L group showed the highest TAG content, followed by the 17 mg/L group. The 3.4 and 85 mg/L treatments showed intermediate levels, significantly higher than the 0 mg/L group. This reflects an early stimulatory effect of high P on lipid biosynthesis. By day 10, TAG levels in the 85 and 425 mg/L groups were significantly higher than in all other groups and statistically comparable, while those in the 3.4 and 17 mg/L groups were higher than the 0 mg/L group. These results highlight the advantage of moderate to high P availability for TAG accumulation. At day 15, the 3.4, 17, and 425 mg/L groups had significantly higher TAG levels than the 0 and 85 mg/L groups, suggesting that moderate P supply can sustain lipid synthesis as effectively as excessive P by the mid-term stage. After 20 days, the 425 mg/L group again showed the highest TAG content, while the levels in 85 and 17 mg/L groups were comparable but significantly higher than in the low-P groups. The levels in 17 and 3.4 mg/L groups did not differ from each other, though both exceeded that in the 0 mg/L group (Figure 4B). These findings indicate that long-term high P conditions are more conducive to sustained lipid accumulation. This pattern complements our starch accumulation data (Figure 3A). Although P deficiency drove carbon toward starch storage (up to 214 mg/g DW at day 15), high P availability redirected carbon toward protein and TAG synthesis. This inverse relationship between starch and protein/TAG accumulation under different P treatments reveals a fundamental reconfiguration of carbon metabolism in response to nutrient availability, revealing a phenotypic plasticity that can be strategically exploited for targeted biomass production. These results demonstrate that P availability regulates the temporal accumulation patterns of both soluble protein and TAG in S. polyrhiza. Specifically, 85 mg/L P supply promoted strong early synthesis of both components, while 425 mg/L P supply favored sustained TAG accumulation over extended periods. These insights provide a theoretical foundation for optimizing P nutrition to enhance the nutritional value and bioenergy potential of duckweed biomass.

Macro- and micro-element recovery in S. polyrhiza biomass

The elemental composition of plant tissues is a fundamental aspect of their nutritional function and phytoremediation potential.61 The results revealed that P supply significantly modulated the uptake and allocation of most elements, with notable dose-time interactions. As the external P concentration increased, the internal P content rose significantly, confirming a pronounced enrichment effect and demonstrating direct P recovery in harvestable biomass. After 5 and 10 days of treatment, fronds from the 425 mg/L group contained 11.44- and 8.8-fold more P, respectively, than those from the P-deficient group. This concentration-dependent accumulation persisted through days 15 and 20 (except for no significant difference between the 3.4 and 17 mg/L groups on day 20), ultimately reaching a maximum value of 16.93 times the P content of the deficient group (Figure 5A). These values represent the actual recovery efficiency of the system. Rather than being merely transformed or precipitated, the P removed from wastewater is incorporated into harvestable biomass, thereby extracting it from the waste stream in a concentrated and usable form. This confirms the strong P enrichment potential of S. polyrhiza, supporting its suitability for remediating P-enriched water. K accumulation was also significantly dependent on P supply. High-P treatments generally resulted in significantly higher K contents compared with low-P groups, particularly after 10 days, when the 425 mg/L group reached its peak (Figure 5B). This synergy may be attributed to the role of P in enhancing energy metabolism and ion transmembrane transport.62 Furthermore, K plays a critical role in osmotic regulation and enzyme activation, and its enhanced accumulation under high P conditions (Figures 1B and 1C) supports the robust growth observed, further reinforcing the physiological synergy between these two nutrients. Ca and Mg exhibited more complex response patterns (Figures 5C and 5D). Ca content was significantly lower in the 17 mg/L group on days 10 and 20, whereas Mg consistently accumulated under high-P conditions. For instance, Mg content in the 85 mg/L group at day 15 was 2.72 times higher than that of the P-deficient group, indicating that P availability influences divalent cation uptake and homeostasis. Moreover, Mg is a central component of the chlorophyll molecule, and its enhanced accumulation under high P (Figure 5D) is consistent with the maintained chlorophyll content observed in these treatments (Figure 1D), indicating that P-driven Mg uptake helps to sustain photosynthetic capacity.

Micronutrient responses to P varied widely. Zn content increased significantly with rising P concentrations. On day 5, the 425 mg/L group contained 1.90–2.95 times more Zn than the P-deficient group (Figure 5G), suggesting a synergistic absorption effect between P and Zn. Similarly, Zn is an essential cofactor for numerous enzymes involved in protein synthesis, aligning with the elevated protein content measured under high-P conditions (Figure 4A), indicating that P-mediated Zn accumulation supports key metabolic processes. The maximum Zn concentration observed across all treatments was 0.70 mg/g. When considering biomass valorization as animal feed, this value exceeds regulatory thresholds established in the European Union (150 mg/kg for piglets/sows and 120 mg/kg for fattening pigs/boars),63 though it may remain within permissible ranges for certain species or under specific regulatory frameworks. Therefore, careful monitoring is essential when cultivating duckweed in P-rich environments, particularly in real wastewater contexts that may contain heavy metals such as Cd, Pb, or Hg. In contrast, Fe content showed an inverse trend. After 20 days, the Fe content in the 0 mg/L group was 2.66 times higher than in the 425 mg/L group (Figure 5E), implying that P enrichment inhibits Fe uptake. This phenomenon may be related to enhanced rhizosphere reduction capacity under P deficiency, which facilitates Fe acquisition.64,65 Mn, B, and Mo also responded significantly to P supply, though with less pronounced changes (Figures 5F, 5H, and 5I), indicating potential antagonistic or synergistic interactions among mineral nutrients.

Overall, these results demonstrate that P supply not only drives P accumulation but also broadly regulates the uptake and distribution of macro- and micronutrients in S. polyrhiza. Importantly, this co-enrichment of multiple nutrients has direct implications for the valorization of the recovered biomass. The harvested material is not only a P-rich waste product but a multi-nutrient resource containing elevated levels of P, K, Mg, and Zn under high-P conditions. For animal feed applications, harvested duckweed must be dewatered and dried because of its high moisture content, a process in which solar or mechanical drying may increase costs. For biomass that may exceed safety thresholds for direct animal feed applications, such as our Zn-enriched material from high-P treatments, several alternative valorization pathways exist. The starch-rich biomass can be hydrolyzed and fermented for bioethanol production, a pathway not constrained by elemental safety thresholds. Alternatively, the nutrient-enriched biomass represents a valuable agricultural input, serving either as a slow-release organic fertilizer that can benefit Zn-deficient soils or as a mineral-enriched feed supplement for livestock. Notably, duckweed lacks significant lignin,35 eliminating the need for costly lignocellulosic pre-treatment required for terrestrial energy crops. Economic analyses indicate that duckweed-based systems can generate positive net returns by integrating revenues from wastewater treatment services with sales of biomass co-products. However, any practical application of duckweed biomass must include a comprehensive risk assessment encompassing heavy metals, pathogens, and other potential contaminants. Such assessments are essential to ensure safe and sustainable resource recovery within a circular bioeconomy framework. These multiple valorization options demonstrate the flexibility of duckweed-based resource recovery. Although technical feasibility is established, economic optimization, process integration, and biomass safety remain important directions for further research.

Synergies for a circular P economy and carbon neutrality

Dynamic changes in key water quality parameters are essential for assessing both the performance and mechanisms of aquatic plant-based remediation systems.66 Our results reveal a synergistic effect through which exogenous P supplementation significantly enhances pollutant removal, facilitates P recovery, and promotes the generation of starch- and protein-rich biomass. Total phosphorus (TP) removal exhibited a pronounced concentration-dependent response. During the first 5 days, residual soluble P increased proportionally with rising exogenous P input. Between days 10 and 20, TP stabilized at low levels under deficient to moderate P stress, while remaining elevated in high-P groups (Figure 6A). Notably, the 17 mg/L P group achieved the highest TP removal efficiency (93.78%) by day 20, beyond which removal rates declined with increasing P (Figure 6B). This pattern suggests the existence of an optimal P range for maximum assimilation, likely mediated by enhanced biomass production and luxury uptake.67,68,69 Moreover, plant-mediated changes in water chemistry, including localized pH shifts and altered redox conditions, may promote P adsorption.70,71 These processes can also lead to precipitation as insoluble mineral phases, particularly at higher P concentrations where solution chemistry approaches saturation. The reduction in removal efficiency at supra-optimal P may result from transporter saturation or feedback inhibition.72,73,74

COD removal was significantly enhanced with P enrichment. After 5 days, COD levels were substantially lower in high-P groups than in moderate and low-P groups. By day 10, COD levels remained lowest in high-P treatments, with the 17 mg/L group also demonstrating superior performance compared with lower P concentrations. From day 15 onward, COD exhibited a continuous decreasing trend with increasing P supply (Figure 6C), culminating in the highest and lowest removal rates at 425 (50.28%) and 0 (8.36%) mg/L P, respectively (Figure 6D). This enhanced COD removal under high-P conditions can be attributed not only to stimulated duckweed metabolism but also to better-maintained membrane integrity (Figure 2), which is conducive to maintaining the overall cellular function. Additionally, the sustained chlorophyll content under high-P conditions (Figure 1D) indicates robust photosynthetic capacity, providing energy for active nutrient and organic matter uptake. These results underscore that P availability stimulates organic matter decomposition and duckweed metabolism. This interpretation is supported by the sharp initial pH decrease (pH ≈ 3.47 in high-P groups at day 10) and dynamic dissolved oxygen (DO) fluctuations (Figure S1A; Figure S2A).

N removal also demonstrated significant dependence on P supply. Total nitrogen (TN) decreased consistently in all treatments, with the lowest residual levels observed in the 17 mg/L group after 10 days, followed by the high-P groups. By day 20, TN removal reached 39.6% at 425 mg/L P, though inter-group differences remained limited (Figures 6E and 6F). Ammonia nitrogen (NH4+-N) removal also improved with increasing P concentration and incubation time. At day 5, high-P groups had the lowest NH4+-N levels. A clear concentration-dependent reduction was evident from day 10 to day 15. By day 20, the 0 mg/L P group retained significantly more NH4+-N than the high-P groups (Figure 6G). The highest NH4+-N removal rate reached 45% at 425 mg/L P (Figure 6H). Notably, the enhanced N removal under high-P conditions coincided with increased protein accumulation in the biomass (Figure 4A), suggesting that a portion of the removed N was directly assimilated into valuable protein products. This direct link between N removal from wastewater and protein enrichment in harvestable biomass exemplifies the dual-benefit nature of the duckweed system. This enhancement is likely driven by P-stimulated S. polyrhiza growth and consequent direct plant uptake and assimilation of N. Despite superior COD and N removal under high-P concentrations, effluent TP remained elevated in these groups. Therefore, practical applications should balance effluent quality with P-recovery efficiency.

Collectively, duckweed employs a multifaceted adaptation strategy to adapt to P stress, including reprogramming of carbon and N metabolism toward starch and protein storage, remodeling of membrane integrity, and selective uptake of essential minerals. These physiological adjustments are not independent but rather form a coordinated response that underpins the system’s dual functionality. The high P removal efficiency (93.78%) is achieved through this integrated physiological capacity. Meanwhile, the plant accumulates starch (>214 mg/g DW), protein, and essential minerals. These findings demonstrate that pollutant removal and resource recovery can be simultaneously optimized. Critically, P recovery is realized through its assimilation into harvestable biomass, where it is co-enriched with other valuable nutrients, transforming waste into a multi-nutrient resource. This integrated multi-product model stands in contrast to other P recovery strategies. Struvite precipitation and adsorption methods can achieve high removal efficiencies but typically recover P as single mineral products, often requiring chemical inputs and generating secondary waste streams.75 Unlike physicochemical methods that recover P as single mineral products such as vivianite or struvite,76,77 duckweed-based systems offer the added value of generating multi-purpose biomass, aligning with the growing emphasis on coupling nutrient removal with resource recovery for sustainable wastewater treatment.78 Algal systems similarly produce valorizable biomass but demand more energy-intensive harvesting and processing due to small cell sizes and dilute cultures.79 In contrast, the duckweed system demonstrated here achieves 93.78% P removal while simultaneously generating starch- and protein-rich biomass that can be directly valorized as animal feed, biofuel feedstock, bioethanol feedstock, and slow-release fertilizer without chemical addition or complex post-treatment. Furthermore, the enrichment of essential minerals, particularly P, K, Mg, and Zn under high P supply, enhances the nutritional value of the biomass for agricultural applications. These multiple pathways help address the economic viability challenge central to implementing circular economy solutions in wastewater management. However, these findings were obtained under controlled laboratory conditions using artificial wastewater. Extending them to practical applications requires further validation using real wastewater that contains heavy metals and other potentially inhibitory compounds. Despite these limitations, the findings provide an essential baseline understanding of duckweed’s intrinsic physiological capacities across a wide P gradient. This includes dose-response relationships, stress thresholds, and carbon allocation patterns, which can guide the design and optimization of pilot-scale studies with real wastewater. Future research could focus on real-world validation, assessment of biomass safety for reuse applications, and development of operational strategies to maintain performance under variable field conditions.

Based on these findings, a framework of the duckweed-based wastewater purification system was constructed, as shown in Figure 7. In this model, the duckweed-based systems convert incoming C, N, and P pollutants into valuable biomass (notably starch- and protein-rich products), while simultaneously capturing CO2 and enhancing effluent quality. This distinctive carbon allocation strategy underscores the potential of duckweed as a sustainable substitute for fertilizer-dependent energy crops. Moreover, its ability to sequester CO2, release oxygen, and reduce reliance on energy-intensive aeration further aligns duckweed-based systems with carbon neutrality objectives. Together, these synergies—efficient pollutant removal, resource recovery, and carbon mitigation—embody a circular bioeconomy model that could transform conventional wastewater management.

In summary, this study demonstrates that S. polyrhiza exhibits high phenotypic plasticity and adaptive resource allocation in response to variable P availability, enabling its dual functionality in wastewater remediation and multipurpose biomass production. Optimal P concentrations between 17 and 85 mg/L resulted in the highest P removal efficiency (93.78%) alongside significant reductions in COD and TN. Under P deficiency, starch accumulation peaked at 214 mg/g DW, whereas higher P levels promoted protein and TAG synthesis. Furthermore, S. polyrhiza exhibited strong elemental recovery capacity, accumulating elevated levels of P, K, Mg, and Zn under high P supply and Fe under P-deficient conditions. Crucially, this study provides quantitative evidence of P recovery through biomass assimilation, with harvested tissues containing up to 16.9-fold higher P than controls and co-enriched with other valuable nutrients. The recovered biomass is a real and harvestable product that can be directly reused as bio-based fertilizer or feed supplement, demonstrating that duckweed systems can achieve genuine nutrient recovery rather than mere removal. These results reveal a previously underappreciated adaptive strategy in duckweed, where P-dependent carbon reallocation enhances both bioremediation performance and resource recovery. Overall, this study provides a strategic framework for developing sustainable, integrated systems that couple wastewater treatment with biomass valorization, opening new pathways for ecological engineering and circular nutrient-water economies.

Limitations of the study

Although the study was carried out in sterile synthetic wastewater, which allowed isolation of the effects of P without interference from microbes or suspended solids, several limitations still exist. First, this study used a simplified and sterile nutrient solution rather than real wastewater. Real wastewater contains organic particles, fluctuating pH, and complex microbial communities that may influence plant growth and nutrient removal. Future studies should be carried out under field-relevant conditions using real wastewater to better understand the practical applicability of the approach. Second, the highest KH2PO4 concentration (425 mg/L) was higher than that typically found in municipal wastewater. This concentration was included to capture the full dose-response curve, but such levels are mainly relevant to specific high-strength effluents (such as pig farm wastewater or fertilizer plant discharge). Future studies should focus on lower and more environmentally relevant P ranges for most practical applications. Third, although the harvested biomass is rich in starch and protein, elevated Zn accumulation under high-P conditions was observed. A complete safety assessment, including other heavy metals, pathogens, and organic contaminants, is necessary before recommending this biomass as animal feed in real-world wastewater applications.

Method details

Plant material and growth conditions

S. polyrhiza was cultivated under controlled laboratory conditions in half-strength Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) medium, which was used as a simulated wastewater to create nutrient-rich conditions while allowing precise control over P concentrations. The complete composition of simulated wastewater included: 6.2 mg/L H3BO3, 0.025 mg/L CuSO4·5H2O, 0.025 mg/L CoCl2·6H2O, 166.1 mg/L CaCl2, 90.35 mg/L MgSO4, 16.9 mg/L MnSO4·H2O, 36.7 mg/L FeNaEDTA, 0.83 mg/L KI, 0.25 mg/L Na2MoO4·2H2O, 8.6 mg/L ZnSO4·7H2O, 950 mg/L KNO3, and 412.5 mg/L (NH4)2SO4. To establish mixotrophic conditions, the medium was supplemented with 10 g/L glucose as an organic carbon source. Five KH2PO4 concentrations (0, 3.4, 17, 85, and 425 mg/L) were used to establish a comprehensive P gradient spanning from deficiency to extreme excess. The highest P concentration (425 mg/L) was selected both to represent levels relevant to high-strength distillery or pig farm wastewater, which can range from 500 to 1000 mg/L,13,14 and to establish a full dose-response curve that captures potential toxicity thresholds. Treatments were arranged in a completely randomized design with three replicates each. Plants were grown in 200 mL glass containers under a 16/8 h light/dark cycle at a constant temperature of 25 ± 1°C and a light intensity of approximately 85 μmol/m2/s. Both the medium and the plant cultures were maintained under sterile conditions. Biomass and medium samples were collected on days 5, 10, 15, and 20 for subsequent analysis.

Photosynthetic pigment measurement

Fresh S. polyrhiza fronds (0.1 g) were extracted with 95% ethanol and kept in the dark overnight. Absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 663, 645, and 470 nm using a SpectraMax ABS microplate reader (Molecular Devices, LLC, USA). Concentrations of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total carotenoids were determined according to established protocols.28,80

Biomass determination

Biomass accumulation was assessed as fresh weight (FW) and dry weight (DW). Fronds were harvested, carefully blot-dried to remove surface moisture, and immediately weighed to determine FW. For DW analysis, the samples were subsequently oven-dried at 65°C until a constant weight was achieved.

Membrane integrity assay

Cell membrane integrity, an indicator of stress and damage, was evaluated using the Evans Blue dye exclusion method.81,82 Fresh fronds were immersed in 0.25% (w/v) Evans Blue solution for 24 h, rinsed thoroughly with distilled water, and imaged under a stereomicroscope. For quantification, bound dye was extracted with 50% (v/v) methanol containing 1% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate at 50°C for 3 days. The absorbance was measured at 600 nm, with higher values indicating greater membrane damage.

Starch content and histological staining

Total starch content was determined using the anthrone-sulfuric acid method with a commercial assay kit (Suzhou Grace Biotechnology, Suzhou, China). Dried biomass was hydrolyzed into glucose, reacted with anthrone reagent, and measured at 620 nm. Starch content was calculated against a glucose standard curve and expressed as mg per gram of dry weight (mg/g DW). For histological localization, fresh fronds were stained with Lugol’s solution (2% w/v I2-KI) for 5 min, rinsed, and examined under a bright-field microscope.83

Protein and triacylglycerol content

Soluble protein content was determined using the Bradford method with a commercial assay kit (Suzhou Grace Biotechnology, Suzhou, China), employing bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. Fresh tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized in phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), and the supernatant was reacted with Bradford reagent before reading absorbance at 595 nm. TAG content was determined by Beijing Huabo Deyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing Huabo Deyi Biotechnology, Beijing, China).

Elemental analysis

To evaluate the mineral nutrient profile and quantify P recovery in harvestable biomass, approximately 0.1 g of oven-dried (60°C) biomass was digested in Teflon vessels with concentrated nitric acid (HNO3, 65%) using a microwave digestion system (Multiwave GO, Anton Paar, Austria) at 180°C for 20 min. After cooling, digestates were diluted to 15 mL with ultrapure water, and elemental concentrations were quantified using inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Optima8000, PerkinElmer, USA).

Water quality monitoring

Water samples were collected from each replicate at 5, 10, 15, and 20 days, filtered through 0.45 μm membranes, and analyzed immediately. TP, TN, NH4+-N, and COD were determined using specific commercial test kits (Beijing Lianhua Technology Development, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. pH was measured using a portable pH meter (Shanghai Yidian Scientific Instrument, Shanghai, China), and DO was assessed with a portable DO meter from the same manufacturer. All instruments were calibrated prior to use.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s test in SPSS v26.0. A significance level of 0.05 was used to assess statistical differences (p < 0.05).
