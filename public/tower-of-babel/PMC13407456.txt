Axenic Cultivation of the First Human-Derived Breviata anathema from a Patient with Fascioliasis in West Bengal, India
Authors: Sanjib Kumar Sardar, Ajanta Ghosal, Yumiko Saito-Nakano, Jun Suzuki, Sandipan Ganguly, Seiki Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo establish axenic cultivation of a human-derived Breviata anathema (strain T76) isolated from the stool of a patient with fascioliasis and to characterize its biological properties.MethodsT76 was maintained in American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) medium 207, supplemented with sulfate-reducing Desulfovibrio desulfuricans and co-cultured with Pseudomonas aeruginosa, at 30 °C. Axenic cultivation was attempted by transferring T76 flagellates to various axenic culture media supplemented with antibiotics for parasitic intestinal protozoan species.ResultsThe 18 S rRNA gene sequence of T76 exhibited significant homology with the free-living, bacterivorous B. anathema (ATCC 50338), a member of the rare clade Obazoa. Axenic cultivation of B. anathema (T76) was achieved in YIGADHA-S medium, in which gluconic acid and dihydroxyacetone replaced glucose as energy substrates.ConclusionB. anathema (T76) was isolated from a patient with fascioliasis, exposed through consumption of aquatic plants from ponds, a known habitat of this organism. Axenic cultivation of T76 was established in YIGADHA-S medium, originally developed for Entamoeba dispar, and cyst-like spherical bodies harboring flagellates were observed and exhibited resistance to 0.1 N HCl.Keywords: Axenic cultivation, Breviata anathema, Fascioliasis, Obazoa, Cyst-like spherical body

An amoeboflagellate with filopodia (strain T76) was isolated in culture using Robinson’s medium [1] supplemented with sulfate-reducing bacterium Desulfovibrio desulfuricans [2] at 35.5 °C from the stool of a patient in Kolkata, West Bengal, India. The patient was clinically and serologically diagnosed with Fasciola hepatica-associated fascioliasis. Based on 18 S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequence analysis, T76 was identified as a free-living Breviata anathema (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC] 50338). The organism was initially deposited in 1992 as Mastigamoeba invertens [3] and later reclassified as a novel class of Breviatea, B. anathema, following phylogenetic and morphological analyses [3]. The class Breviatea is currently placed within Obazoa, a sister group to Amoebozoa within Amorphea, and includes Opisthokonta, Breviatea, and Apusomonadidae [4].

To date, two xenic isolates of B. anathema have been reported: one from brackish water under microaerobic conditions (ATCC 50338) [3] and another from a saline lake [5] as a free-living protozoan in the United States. In uranium-contaminated groundwater, acetate amendment initially promoted the growth of iron-reducing Geobacter sp. that can reduce uranium(VI) to uranium(IV). This microbial bloom was accompanied by a specific enrichment of B. anathema [6]. Notably, the Fe(III)-reducing Geobacter species involved was previously isolated from a lotus field [7]. Such environments may also serve as habitats for lymnaeid snails, the first intermediate host for F. hepatica. Infective metacercariae of F. hepatica often adhere to the stems or leaves of aquatic plants, which act as the second intermediate host for this species. These plants are subsequently consumed by definitive hosts, such as cattle, sheep, goats, and humans.

Both flagellates and cysts [3] of B. anathema can attach to the surfaces of aquatic plants and pond snails via filopodia and adhesive substances, respectively. In some rural Bengali communities, consumption of aquatic plants (water lilies and clovers) and pond snails may thus represent a potential exposure route. The T76 isolate was subsequently maintained in ATCC medium 207 containing ferric iron [Fe(III)] [8] and supplemented with anaerobic sulfate-reducing D. desulfuricans, accompanied by H2S production [2] from Robinson’s medium at 35.5 °C. Growth of T76 was significantly enhanced under these conditions, indicating an H2S-dependent metabolic interaction, involving reduction of Fe(III) to Fe(II). Notably, Fe(II) is a common metabolite produced by both Geobacter sp. and D. desulfuricans.

Stool samples were collected during epidemiological surveillance for Entamoeba moshkovskii [9], including one from a 32-year-old woman residing in a fascioliasis-endemic region, adjacent Kolkata. The patient had a history of multiple liver space-occupying lesions, with a positive serology for Fasciola hepatica. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Human Ethics Committee of the Indian Council of Medical Research and National Institute of Cholera and Enteric Diseases (IRB Number: A-1/2015-IEC). Microscopically, Fasciola sp. eggs were not detected in the stool samples, cysts morphologically consistent with Entamoeba hartmanni were found. Culture cysts using Robinson’s medium co-cultured with Escherichia coli (DH5α), supplemented with anaerobic D. desulfuricans (NBRC 13699; NITE Biological Resource Center, Chiba, Japan) and mucin at 35.5 °C, yielded E. hartmanni, Blastocystis hominis, and unidentified small flagellates (T76). H2S-producing D. desulfuricans can induce excystation in certain intestinal parasitic protozoans, including Entamoeba polecki, Entamoeba suis, Endolimax sp., Chilomastiix mesnili and Retortamonas sp [2, 10]. Cyst and trophozoite sizes of E. hartmanni were measured in 100 individuals (cyst: mean 8.99 ± 0.99 μm in diameter; trophozoite: 10–15 μm in length).

Following culture trials using various media, the T76 was transferred from Robinson’s medium to ATCC medium 207 supplemented with D. desulfuricans co-cultured with E. coli (DH5α). After genetic identification as a free-living flagellate (B. anathema), culture conditions were optimized by adjusting temperature from 35.5 to 30 °C and replacing E. coli (DH5α) with Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA: KEIO) [11] to promote microaerophilic conditions via oxygen consumption, thereby enhancing the growth of D. desulfuricans. Therefore, a 0.2 mL aliquot of D. desulfiricans co-cultured with P. aeruginosa/ATCC medium 207 was inoculated into 3.5 mL fresh ATCC medium 207 containing 2% heat-inactivated adult bovine serum (ABS) in a screw-capped glass tube (13 × 100 mm) with approximately 40% airspace, to induce H2S-production by D. desulfiricans. The growth of D. desulfiricans was confirmed by the formation of a black FeS precipitate derived from H2S and Fe(II) produced by D. desulfiricans in the medium (Fig. 1a). Subculturing of T76 was performed approximately every 2 weeks by replacing 50% of the culture medium with fresh medium containing 2% ABS. The culture conditions of T76 cells grown in the ATCC medium 207, supplemented with D. desulfuricans and P. aeruginosa, are illustrated in Fig. 1b–e. The size of flagellate bodies and their flagella, measured in 100 specimens, were 9.4–11.3 μm in body length, 6.6–7.8 μm in body breadth, and 14–18 μm in flagella length. Under these culture conditions, T76 cells adhered to glass surfaces via filopodia (Fig. 1d), proliferated (Fig. 1e), and remained viable for at least 3 weeks. Axenic cultivation was attempted by transferring T76 into various parasite culture media supplemented with antibiotics [penicillin G potassium (200 units/mL), streptomycin sulfate (1 mg/mL), and polymyxin B (300 units/mL)]. Axenic growth was ultimately achieved in YIGADHA-S medium [12], previously established as an axenic culture medium for Entamoeba dispar and contains 15% heat-inactivated ABS and gluconic acid and dihydroxyacetone, instead of glucose, as energy substrates, in a screw-capped glass tube (13 × 100 mm) with approximately 20% airspace.

Notably, cyst-like spherical bodies (CSBs) harboring flagellates (T76) (Fig. 3a–b) were observed following addition of polymyxin B (100 units/mL), a membrane-disruptive cationic cyclic polypeptide antibiotic. The CSBs (Fig. 3a–b) appeared to lack a well-defined cyst wall, consistent with that described by Walker et al. [3], as indicated by the absence of calcofluor white staining (a non-specific fluorochrome that binds with chitin and cellulose contained in the cell walls of fungi and cyst walls of Entamoeba sp., Acanthamoeba sp., and other organisms). However, they exhibited resistance to 0.1 N HCl after 15 min (3b), similar to cysts of Entamoeba histolytica [13], and retained viability, as demonstrated by subsequent proliferation after washing with Hanks’ solution by centrifugation (400×g for 3 min twice) and transfer into fresh medium (Fig. 3c–e).

DNA was extracted from cultured Entamoeba sp. trophozoites (5,000–10,000 cells) grown in Robinson’s medium and from T76 flagellates (2 × 106 cells) cultured in ATCC medium 207 using the Cica Geneus Total DNA Prep Kit (Kanto Chemical Co., Chuo-ku, Tokyo, Japan). The extracted DNA was used as a template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the 18 S rRNA genes. Universal primers targeting Entamoeba spp [14]. were used to identify Entamoeba isolates. The primer sets for T76 (Brevi-F1:5′- ACTAAGCCATGCATGTCTAAGTAT-3′ and Brevi-R2:5′- TGCTTCCTCTAGATAATAAGGT-3′) were designed based on the genomic sequences of the reference strain B. anathema ATCC50338 (AF153206) [15]. PCR cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplicons were directly sequenced using an ABI Prism BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Ready Reaction Kit on an ABI Prism 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA).

Multiple sequence alignments and phylogenetic analyses of the 18 S rRNA gene sequences from T76 were performed using ClustalW, and implemented in MEGA version 10 [16, 17]. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using neighbor-joining, p-distance, and maximum-likelihood (ML) methods. ML analysis followed a general time-reversible model with a gamma distribution and five rate categories. Node support was assessed using bootstrapping with 1,000 replicates. Ancyromonas sigmoides (DQ207565), classified as Ancyromonadida, was used as an outgroup to root the final phylogenetic tree.

The 18 S rRNA gene sequence of the morphologically identified E. hartmanni isolate exhibited high sequence similarity to reference E. hartmanni (KX618191) (data not shown). The 18 S rRNA sequence of T76 (LC887829) was identical to the reference B. anathema (AF153206) (1,644/1,644 bp). Phylogenetic analysis indicated that T76 clustered with B. anathema (AF153206) and Subulatomonas tetraspora (HQ342676) within Obazoa, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

We speculated that B. anathema (T76) was isolated from the stool of the patient based on their clinical and dietary histories, residential environment, and the habitat and culture conditions of B. anathema (T76). Specifically, B. anathema is a microaerophilic or anaerobic bacterivorous flagellate with hydrogenosome-like organelles [3, 5] and can form cystic stage [3]. A genetic survey of free-living bacterivorous protozoans indicated that B. anathema proliferates in freshwater environments containing Fe(III)-reducing bacteria (Geobacter spp.) [6]. Moreover, it has been detected in various aquatic environments with microaerophilic conditions, ranging from brackish to freshwater habitats [3, 5, 6, 18]. Iron-reducing bacteria (Geobacter spp.) and sulfate-reducing bacteria that produce H2S (Desulfovibrio spp.) are widely distributed in mineral-rich microaerophilic conditions, from freshwater to marine environments [19, 20]. These niches often overlap with those of B. anathema. Additionally, such environments are also inhabited by intermediate hosts of F. hepatica, such as lymnaeid snails and water plants. Therefore, the patient may have ingested the flagellates, cysts, or CSBs of B. anathema through the same route as F. hepatica metacercaria; that is, by consuming edible aquatic plants.

Ferrous iron [Fe(II)], a common metabolite of Geobacter sp. and D. desulfuricans, involves hydrogenosomes (protomitochondria) and ferredoxin (iron-sulfur protein) as electron transfer carriers for organisms living in anaerobic or oxygen-stressed environments. Previously, a parasitic ciliate (Nyctotherus teleacus) that possesses hydrogenosomes was cultured from tortoise stool using Robinson’s R medium supplemented with D. desulfuricans co-cultured with E. coli [21]. In contrast, aerophilic protozoans and fungi with mitochondria containing cytochrome c oxidase typically fail to grow under these culture conditions due to the presence of H2S (a cytochrome c oxidase inhibitor). A novel microaerophilic amoeboflagellate (Subulatomonas tetraspora) has been isolated from a nearshore marine site [22] and is closely associated with B. anathema based on both microscopic features and phylogenetic analyses of small subunit rDNA, actin, and alpha-tubulin gene sequences [22]. Despite ecological differences, both organisms share several key characteristics, including halotolerance, presence of hydrogenosomes, and microaerophilic bacterivorous flagellates. The hydrogenosomes found in these two species are often associated with certain anaerobic protozoan parasites. However, in the present case, we could not perform additional stool examinations, including cultivation, as the patient was transferred for specialized treatment of fascioliasis. Future studies are needed to determine whether free-living B. anathema can establish parasitic or transient infections and assess its pathogenicity.
