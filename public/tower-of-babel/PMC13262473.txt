Endovascular embolization for systemic artery to pulmonary artery fistulas supplied by 4 distinct vessels: a case report
Authors: Zhong Qiu, Zhang qirong, Wang Qian, Meng Xin, Wei Xiaodong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystemic artery to pulmonary artery fistula (SAPAF) is a rare vascular anomaly that often presents with hemoptysis and can be classified as congenital or acquired.Case presentationA 34-year-old female, presenting with recurrent hemoptysis, was diagnosed with multiple SAPAFs originating from four distinct arterial sources. Imaging using CT angiography and catheter angiography revealed complex systemic-to-pulmonary vascular connections, and the symptoms were successfully managed through transarterial embolization.ConclusionEndovascular embolization successfully treated a rare case of SAPAF, and the patient was discharged in stable condition without complications.

Introduction

Systemic artery to pulmonary artery fistula (SAPAF) is an uncommon vascular anomaly characterized by abnormal communications between systemic arteries and pulmonary vasculature [1]. Instances involving three or more aberrant vascular connections are exceedingly rare [2]. This case report presents a 34-year-old female with SAPAF supplied by four distinct arterial sources, managed successfully with transarterial embolization.

Clinical presentation

A 34-year-old female presented with recurrent hemoptysis, accompanied by cough, purulent sputum, occasional blood-streaked sputum, and fever peaking at 38.2 °C. Her medical history included pulmonary tuberculosis treated with anti-tubercular therapy for one year and the follow-up was irregular. The initial CT examination revealed a mass-like lesion in the right hilar region, which was provisionally diagnosed as pulmonary sequestration (Fig. 1). However, subsequent contrast-enhanced CT angiography with three-dimensional vascular reconstruction demonstrated an anomalous systemic arterial supply directly communicating with the pulmonary artery, without evidence of sequestrated lung tissue or an aberrant bronchial connection, leading to a final diagnosis of SAPAF. (Fig. 2). Laboratory results, including liver and renal function tests, coagulation profiles, and tumor markers, were unremarkable. Tuberculosis antibodies were weakly positive, indicating past infection.

Follow-up: The patient was informed preoperatively of the high risk of recurrence, and embolization was performed as part of a staged treatment strategy. During the one-month interval between embolization and the planned partial lung resection, the patient remained clinically stable without recurrent hemoptysis.

Interventional management

Following successful puncture of the right femoral artery, a 5 F vascular sheath was inserted. Angiography using 5 F-Yashiro, 5 F-Cobra, 5 F-Simmon1, and 5 F-MPA1 catheters identified abnormal arterial supplies from the right bronchial artery, right subclavian artery, branches of the celiac trunk, and right phrenic artery. These vessels were dilated, tortuous, and disorganized, with evidence of early pulmonary artery opacification, confirming SAPAF formation. Other intercostal arteries appeared unremarkable.Superselective catheterization of the identified vessels was performed using a 2.4–2.8 F microcatheter. Embolization was achieved using gelatin sponge particles (710–1000 μm; Ailikang, Hangzhou) and COOK embolization coils (MWCE series). Post-embolization angiography confirmed complete occlusion of the abnormal vessels and resolution of the fistulous connections (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

First described by Burchell and Clagett in 1947, SAPAF represents an abnormal communication between systemic arteries and the pulmonary vasculature, excluding bronchial arteries [1]. Subsequent studies by Remy-Jardin and Remy introduced the concept of non-bronchial systemic arterial supply, expanding understanding of this rare anomaly [3]. Common afferent vessels include the internal thoracic and intercostal arteries, though contributions from the subclavian, aorta, or other branches may also occur [4] .

SAPAF can be either congenital or acquired, thought to arise from dormant broncho-pulmonary anastomoses reactivated under conditions such as chronic inflammation, hypoxia, or vascular injury [5]. In this case, the patient’s history of pulmonary tuberculosis and chronic inflammation likely stimulated abnormal vascular development. Several published case reports have described an association between prior pulmonary TB and systemic artery–to–pulmonary artery fistulas. For example, intercostal artery–to–pulmonary artery fistulas have been reported in patients with post-tuberculosis bronchiectasis and were successfully treated with endovascular embolization [6], suggesting a link between chronic post-TB structural lung changes and abnormal vascular connections. In addition, bronchial artery–to–pulmonary artery fistulas have been described in patients with active or cavitating pulmonary TB [7], further supporting the concept that TB-related vascular remodeling may predispose to fistula formation.

Pulmonary sequestration is a congenital bronchopulmonary malformation characterized by nonfunctioning lung tissue that lacks normal communication with the tracheobronchial tree and receives its arterial blood supply from the systemic circulation rather than the pulmonary artery. Pryce classified anomalous systemic arterial supplies into three subtypes: Type I: Normal bronchial supply with anomalous collateral arteries. Type II: Anomalous vessels supplying both sequestered and normal lung segments. Type III: Vessels confined to sequestered lung segments [8]. In this case, the initial CT examination revealed a mass-like lesion in the right hilar region, which was provisionally diagnosed as pulmonary sequestration. However, subsequent contrast-enhanced CT angiography with three-dimensional vascular reconstruction demonstrated an anomalous systemic arterial supply directly communicating with the pulmonary artery, without evidence of sequestrated lung tissue or an aberrant bronchial connection, leading to a final diagnosis of SAPAF, emphasizing the pivotal role of CT and angiography in accurately diagnosing and effectively treating SAPAF.

This may be the first reported instance of a SAPAF supplied by four distinct arterial sources. The presence of multiple abnormal vessels added both complexity and risk to the intervention. Major complications, such as transverse myelitis and spinal cord ischemia, have been described when anterior spinal arteries are inadvertently embolized. And mild and transient complications, including fever, dysphagia, and tolerable chest pain, were observed following the procedure [9]. The potential presence of shunts and the risk of non-target embolization also warrant careful consideration. To mitigate this risk, several precautionary measures were implemented, including detailed pre-procedural and intra-procedural imaging, superselective microcatheterization of the feeding vessels, and controlled, stepwise embolization under continuous fluoroscopic guidance. No ischemic or non-target embolization–related complications were observed during or after the procedure.

Despite the added difficulty and risks, transcatheter embolization remains a highly effective, minimally invasive treatment for SAPAFs associated with hemoptysis [10]. This approach offers several advantages, including the lack of need for general anesthesia and a quick recovery time. Intervention is recommended to prevent complications such as recurrent hemoptysis, infection, pulmonary hypertension, or heart failure [11] .

Conclusion

Multiple aberrant vascular connections involving 4 distinct vessels are exceptionally rare. We report a unique case of multiple SAPAFs successfully managed with transcatheter embolization. The patient showed no recurrence of symptoms during follow-up, highlighting the efficacy and safety of this minimally invasive treatment approach.
