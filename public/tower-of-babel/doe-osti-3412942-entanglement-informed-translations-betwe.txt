  I. Executive Summary and Project Description

This project focused on establishing a connection between entanglement and classical/quantum
problem hardness, and leveraging this connection to inform optimal control translations between
adiabatic quantum computing (AQC) and the approximate quantum optimization algorithm
(QAOA). Although AQC and QAOA are intimately related, little is known about their relationship
in the context of computational problem hardness. Entanglement in QAOA has been a topic of
interest in recent years; however, there has yet to be a firm analytical understanding of the role
entanglement plays in solving computationally difficult problems. Similarly, while entanglement
in AQC has been extensively studied, it is unclear how entanglement generation is affected by the
hardness of the computational problem. Our approach was to leverage concepts from condensed
matter physics to determine the interplay between entanglement and computational hardness and
facilitate translations between optimally designed AQC and QAOA.

 II. Technical Accomplishments
a. Entanglement Scaling and Problem Structure in Quantum Approximate and
 Adiabatic Optimization Algorithms

The motivation for this work was to better understand how quantum entanglement—a key
computational resource in quantum algorithms—relates to problem structure and algorithmic
performance in quantum optimization. Although prior studies have characterized entanglement in
the QAOA, it remained unclear how much of the observed behavior reflects the underlying
optimization problem versus choices made in training the algorithm. We addressed this question
using MaxCut problems spanning regular, complete, and variable-density graph families, and
examined how entanglement changes with problem size, graph connectivity, and QAOA circuit
depth. We also compared QAOA with its continuous-time counterpart, AQC, to determine whether
their close mathematical relationship translates into similar entanglement requirements.

Our main technical finding is that entanglement is strongly structured rather than simply increasing
with circuit complexity. For QAOA, we found that inadequate classical parameter optimization
can obscure the true entanglement profile, particularly for highly connected problems; with a more
effective initialization and optimization strategy, complete graphs exhibit a clear entanglement
peak at relatively shallow circuit depths while solution quality continues to improve. Across graph
families, the maximum required entanglement grows approximately linearly with system size, with
more highly connected graphs generally requiring more entanglement. For sufficiently dense
graphs, the entanglement requirement can also be predicted from graph edge density, largely
independent of the individual graph instance. A particularly notable result is that the distribution
of QAOA entanglement across different subsystem partitions closely follows that of fermionic
Gaussian states, providing a substantially tighter characterization than conventional random-state
bounds; for dense graphs, the agreement becomes especially strong. In contrast, while AQC also
exhibits linear-in-system-size entanglement scaling, the amount of entanglement depends strongly
on the annealing schedule and can exceed the fermionic-Gaussian-state behavior observed for
QAOA.

The impact of these results is that we provide a more predictive framework for understanding when
and how much entanglement quantum optimization algorithms actually require, which is important

b. Connecting Stabilizer Rényi Entropy to Problem Hardness in the Quantum
 Approximate Optimization Algorithm

The motivation for this work is to understand how the non-classical resources required by QAOA
scale with the intrinsic difficulty of the optimization problem being solved. In particular, we
focused on non-stabilizerness, or “magic,” which is a quantum resource associated with states that
cannot be efficiently represented using stabilizer methods. While previous work has shown that
QAOA can generate a substantial “magic barrier” during optimization, it has not been clear how
that resource depends on recognizable features of the underlying computational problem. To
address this, we used Maximum 2-bit Satisfiability (MAX 2-SAT) as a controlled testbed because
its classical hardness can be tuned through the clause density—the ratio of constraints to
variables—and undergoes a well-known transition near unit clause density. We simulated both
random and optimized QAOA circuits and characterize magic using the stabilizer Rényi entropy,
examining its evolution through the circuit, its scaling with problem size, and the underlying
distribution of Pauli expectation values that determines it.

Our results show a clear relationship between problem structure and the amount and distribution
of magic generated by QAOA. In random circuits, magic increases with circuit depth before
saturating, with the saturation value increasing with clause density. At sufficiently high clause
density, these states approach the magic characteristics of typical or Haar-random quantum states.
For optimized QAOA, the location of the magic barrier itself changes with problem hardness. For
low clause densities, the maximum occurs near the end of the circuit, whereas around and above
the MAX 2-SAT transition it shifts toward the middle of the circuit. We attribute this change to
the shrinking solution space as additional clauses constrain the problem. More importantly, the
maximum magic exhibits volume-law scaling with system size, and the corresponding scaling rate
depends on clause density for both random and optimized QAOA. For harder, high-density
instances, the optimized-QAOA resource requirement appears to approach a common scaling
behavior that remains below the random-state limit. The Pauli-spectrum analysis provides an
additional microscopic picture: increasing clause density broadens the spectrum, and the proposed
variational model captures much of this evolution, although we note that quantitative discrepancies
remain, particularly in predicting the stabilizer Rényi entropy at low clause density and for
optimized circuits.

The impact of this work is that it begins to turn magic from an abstract quantum-resource measure
into a quantity that can be related directly to classical problem characteristics. If the observed
relationship persists more broadly, features such as clause density could provide a practical way
to anticipate how non-stabilizer resource requirements grow before running a large quantum
computation, which in turn informs both hardware requirements and the expected difficulty of
classical simulation. The results also suggest that the resource requirements of QAOA are not
determined by system size alone: two problems of comparable size can generate substantially
different magic profiles depending on their hardness and solution-space structure. This creates an
opportunity to use magic not only as a diagnostic of quantum complexity, but potentially as a tool
for algorithm design. For example, to identify where in the QAOA evolution the most classically
difficult states occur and whether alternative parameterizations could reach good solutions while
reducing that resource burden.

We are currently completing a manuscript that summarizes our findings. Upon completion, it will
be submitted to a peer-review journal for publication.

c. Band-Limited Variational Parameter Setting for Quantum Approximate
 Optimization

This work addresses one of the central practical bottlenecks in QAOA: the number of variational
parameters grows with circuit depth, making large-depth circuits increasingly difficult and
expensive to train. Standard QAOA assigns independent parameters to every layer, so improving
expressivity by increasing depth also enlarges the classical optimization problem and can
exacerbate barren plateaus and quantum-resource costs. Here, we explored whether QAOA can
retain high solution quality while operating within a much smaller parameter space. The approach
is to represent the layer-by-layer variational parameters as a functional expansion using discrete
prolate spheroidal sequences (DPSS, or Slepian sequences). DPSS form a basis widely used in
signal processing and quantum control to represent time-limited, band-limited signals efficiently.
In this representation, the bandwidth determines how rapidly QAOA parameters can vary between
layers and, simultaneously, how many coefficients must be trained. Using MAX 2-SAT and MAX
3-SAT as benchmark problems, we systematically varied problem hardness, system size, circuit
depth, and available parameter bandwidth to determine how much variational freedom QAOA
actually requires.

In this work, we found that QAOA exhibits a well-defined minimum control bandwidth, or
equivalently a critical number of trainable DPSS coefficients, above which performance improves
rapidly and below which the ansatz is too constrained to reach high-quality solutions. For both
MAX 2-SAT and MAX 3-SAT, this transition depends on problem hardness: harder instances
generally require more basis functions, with 3-SAT exhibiting stronger dependence on system size,
clause density, and circuit depth than 2-SAT. Despite this dependence, the required parameter
count remains dramatically below the canonical QAOA budget. For example, we found that only
about 3–5 basis functions are typically sufficient to reach approximation ratios around 0.9 in many
of the cases considered, corresponding to parameter reductions exceeding 95% for deep circuits.
From the numerical data, we developed an empirical scaling law that predicts the critical basis size
from clause density, system size, and QAOA depth, with cross-validation errors of only a few
percent for both problem classes. The entanglement results reinforce this picture. That is, the same

bandwidth-dependent transition appears in the maximum entanglement generated by the circuit,

This work demonstrates a path toward substantially reducing QAOA training overhead without
sacrificing much of its useful expressivity, which could make deeper variational circuits
considerably more practical. Rather than treating every QAOA layer as requiring independent
control, the results suggest that useful solutions often occupy a much lower-dimensional, band-
limited manifold whose required dimension can be estimated from the characteristics of the
problem being solved. This has direct implications for the number of classical optimization
variables, quantum evaluations, and control degrees of freedom needed in an implementation.

The control-theory interpretation is especially important in this work. Below the critical
bandwidth, QAOA suffers from a reachability limitation because it cannot generate the parameter
variations needed to access good solution states. Once that bandwidth is reached, additional
parameter freedom provides diminishing returns. That perspective also creates a natural
framework for balancing expressivity against trainability and potentially avoiding barren plateaus.

send it to a peer-review journal for publication.

 V. Project Impact and Future Development

Taken together, the studies comprising this project establish a broader framework for
characterizing and reducing the quantum resources required by variational optimization
algorithms. Our work in entanglement shows that resource requirements are strongly shaped by
problem structure and by algorithmic choices such as classical training and annealing schedules.
The magic study extends this picture by linking non-stabilizer resources directly to classical
problem hardness. Furthermore, the band-limited parameterization work demonstrates that high-

JHU/APL 4

quality QAOA solutions can often be reached within a much smaller, structured control space than
the canonical formulation would suggest. Collectively, these results point toward a future in which
quantum optimization algorithms are designed around problem-aware resource models, where
features such as connectivity, clause density, and problem size inform how much entanglement,
magic, control bandwidth, and classical training capacity are leveraged to inform the optimization
protocol.

Future directions include testing whether these relationships generalize beyond MaxCut and
MAX-SAT to broader classes of optimization problems, determining how noise and hardware
constraints modify the observed scaling laws, and using the identified resource structure to design
new ansätze, initialization strategies, and control protocols that deliberately avoid unnecessary
quantum complexity. A particularly promising direction is to integrate these ideas—using problem
structure to predict where quantum resources become necessary, while constraining the variational
parameterization to the lowest-dimensional control space capable of accessing those states. This
could improve both trainability and hardware efficiency and provide a clearer basis for identifying
regimes where quantum optimization may offer a practical advantage.
