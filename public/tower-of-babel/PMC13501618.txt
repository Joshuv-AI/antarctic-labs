Circulating angiopoietin-2 and TEK receptor tyrosine kinase-2 protein provide prognostic value in patients with acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Siyuan Tan, Xuan Hao, Yaxuan Peng, Wenyu Zhu, Zihui Yin, Zhengqi Hu, Zhaofen Zheng, Jianqiang Peng, Yi Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute myocardial infarction (AMI) is an acute heart disease that can result in high rates of disability and mortality. Biomarkers can reflect the pathophysiological progress of AMI, such as myocardial ischemia injury, ventricular remodelling and oxidative stress, and provide advantages in the prognostic evaluation of AMI. Finding novel biomarkers can help improve the risk stratification of AMI.MethodA total of 180 patients who were diagnosed with AMI in the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine of Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital from July 2020 to March 2021 were included in this study. Baseline circulating ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 levels were measured, and patients underwent a regular follow-up to record the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) after discharge.ResultsDuring the entire follow-up period with an average of 444 (356–586) days, 55 patients developed MACE. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that ANGPT-2 (HR: 2.400; 95% CI: 1.380–4.523; P = 0.002) and TIE-2 (HR: 2.004; 95% CI: 1.127–3.562; P = 0.018) were independent predictors of MACE in patients with AMI. The area under the curve (AUC) of circulating ANGPT-2 for predicting MACE was 0.756 (95% CI: 0.682–0.785; P < 0.001), and the AUC of circulating TIE-2 for predicting MACE was 0.664 (95% CI: 0.572–0.755; P < 0.001).The Kaplan-Meier survival curve revealed that patients with an ANGPT-2 level ≥ 1.896 ng/mL had a greater risk of MACE than those with an ANGPT-2 level < 1.896 ng/mL (HR = 3.189; 95% CI: 1.856–5.481; P < 0.001), and patients with a TIE-2 level ≥ 21.886 ng/mL had a greater risk of MACE than those with a TIE-2 level < 21.886 ng/mL (HR = 2.912; 95% CI: 1.681–5.042; P < 0.001).ConclusionANGPT-2 and TIE-2 are significantly correlated with the prognosis of AMI, which suggests that they might be potential biomarkers for predicting prognosis.Keywords: Acute myocardial infarction, Angiopoietin-2, TEK receptor tyrosine kinase-2, Biomarker, Major adverse cardiovascular events

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is an acute heart disease that can result in high rates of disability and mortality [1]. Despite receiving early reperfusion therapy and taking prescribed medications, some patients with AMI still develop heart failure or die, which places a heavy burden on their families and society [2, 3]. Biomarkers can reflect the pathophysiological progress of AMI, such as myocardial ischemia injury, ventricular remodelling and oxidative stress, and provide advantages in the prognostic evaluation of AMI [4–6]. Traditional biomarkers, such as NT-proBNP, are frequently used to assess the severity and prognosis of heart failure following myocardial infarction but their accuracy is easily affected by aspects such as age, obesity, and renal function [7–9]. Therefore, finding novel biomarkers can help improve the risk stratification of AMI.

The angiopoietin/tyrosine kinase receptor axis, which is an essential homeostasis regulator, regulates vascular permeability and pathological remodelling [10, 11]. Specifically, angiopoietin-2 (ANGPT-2) is a member of the angiopoietin family [12]. By weakening adhesion connections, attracting inflammatory cells and promoting development of the microvascular system, ANGPT-2 stored in the Weibel-Palade body of endothelial cells is released under hypoxia and inflammatory stimulation to increase vascular permeability and stimulate vascular remodelling [13, 14]. Moreover, ANGPT-2 and TEK receptor tyrosine kinase-2 (TIE-2) can cooperate to produce signalling complexes that enhance endothelial connections, reduce the synthesis of leukocyte adhesion molecules, activate related signal transduction and regulate vascular stability [15]. ANGPT-2 is a promising target for intervention in cardiovascular diseases due to its unique regulation of vascular homeostasis, which results in its significant role in cardiovascular development and myocardial remodelling.

Cheng et al. reported that patients with coronary atherosclerotic heart disease had significantly higher levels of ANGPT-2 compared with healthy individuals [16]. Through proteomic analysis of plasma from AMI patients, Richards et al. reported that ANGPT-2 might be utilized as a biomarker to anticipate left ventricular remodelling and cardiovascular outcomes [17]. Wang C et al. revealed that circulating TIE-2 levels may suggest ischemic necrotizing myocardial angiogenesis [18]. Chong et al. reported TIE-2 alterations in heart failure [19]. However, no study has explored whether both ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 can independently predict the prognosis of AMI patients. Our study was intended to detect circulating levels of ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 and investigate their independent prognostic value in patients with AMI.

Method

Study design and patients

Patients who were diagnosed with AMI and underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) at the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine of Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital from July 2020 to March 2021 were prospectively and continuously enrolled. The AMI diagnosis strictly followed the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction (2018) [20] and required the following two criteria: (1) typical elevation of cardiac troponin I (cTnI) above the 99th percentile upper reference limit; and (2) conforming to any one of these criteria: (1) ischaemic chest pain symptoms and chest pain lasting > 20 min; (2) new ischemic electrocardiogram changes such as new ST-segment and T-wave changes; (3) new pathologic Q waves; (4) imaging evidence of new loss of myocardial activity or new local wall motion abnormalities; and (5) coronary angiography confirming the presence of coronary thrombosis. The exclusion criteria included malignancy, peripheral vascular disease, infection, acute liver failure and end-stage renal disease (requiring dialysis or an eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2). Baseline data and clinical data of patients were collected through the electronic medical records (EMR) system. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital (2021–103). Informed consent was provided by all patients. The study flow chart is presented in Fig. 1.

Study flow chart

Blood samples

Blood samples were collected from patients with AMI at the next day of hospitalization. All blood samples were placed in a refrigerator at 4 °C for half an hour and then centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 rpm to obtain plasma. All plasma samples were frozen and stored at − 80 °C until later analysis. Plasma samples were thawed once prior to analysis. After all samples were collected, the plasma concentration was determined by a multiplex immunoassay (Protein Simple/Bio-Techne Corporation, MN, USA). The instrument (Ella-21090851, firmware version 3.7.2.0) employs a microfluidic cartridge (Simple Plex™ Kit, lot 19520, cartridge ID 219492) with triplicate measurements per sample. All samples were diluted 10-fold as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Standard curves were generated using lot-specific coefficients, with lower/upper limits of quantification of 9.91–15,124 pg/mL for ANGPT-2 and 72.24–4090 pg/mL for TIE-2; all measured concentrations fell within these ranges. Intra-assay coefficients of variation were 1.83% for ANGPT-2 and 2.11% for TIE-2. Biomarker assays were performed blinded to clinical outcomes and all other patient data.

Primary outcome and follow-up

The primary outcome was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), which included readmission for unstable angina, heart failure, nonfatal myocardial infarction and all-cause mortality. Regular telephone interviews were conducted at one month, three, six, twelve and twenty-four months. Regular telephone interviews were conducted at three, six, twelve and twenty-four months. All telephone follow-ups were comfirmed by medical records. The treatment’s impact on the patient’s illness and medication and the incidence of endpoint events were the key topics of the follow-up materials. Medical staff followed the double-blind principle during the follow-up. The follow-up period occurred from the moment of discharge until the endpoint event, or until July 31, 2022.

Statistical analysis

For continuous data, results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation for data that are normally distributed, and results are shown as medians with interquartile ranges for data that are not normally distributed. Additionally, categorical and ordinal data are presented as numbers and percentages. Differences in baseline characteristics were analysed by independent sample t-tests for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed variables, and the chi–square test was used for categorical variables. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to investigate any correlation between two variables. Collinearity among these adjustment variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). To avoid overfitting, a multivariate regression analysis was used to identify the independent predictor. We adjusted only ANGPT-2, TIE-2, Log10NT-proBNP, diabetes mellitus, D-dimer, eGFR, Killip class and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) in the multivariable analysis, and considered the two-sided P-values of the variables in the univariate Cox regression analysis and their clinical relevance. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for MACE was constructed to predict the area under the curve (AUC) and cut-off points. Then, De Long’s test was used to examine the difference in the AUC. Kaplan‒Meier curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cut-off points according to results of the ROC curve analysis, and differences between the curves were determined using the log-rank test. Harrel’s C-statistics, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated using R software. The two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY), R software (version 4.4.1), and MedCalc software 19.2.6 (MedCalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium) were used to conduct the statistical analyses.

Results

Study population

During the study period, a total of 369 patients with AMI were enrolled. This study included 192 patients with AMI who underwent PCI. 12 patients were ultimately excluded due to loss during follow-up. Therefore, 180 patients were ultimately included in the analysis. A total of 180 patients, including 133 (73.9%) males, had a mean age of 60 years (51–68 years). Among them, 125 patients had acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and 55 patients had acute non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTMEI). There was no statistically significant difference in baseline data between STEMI and NSTEMI patients. The median follow-up time was 444 (356–586) days. During the follow-up period, 55 patients experienced MACE. Compared with the no-event group, the event group had higher levels of ANGPT-2, TIE-2, NT-proBNP, diabetes mellitus and Killip class, but the eGFR levels and proportion of patients taking ACEI/ARB/ARNI drugs were lower (all P < 0.05). Associations between ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 levels and baseline characteristics

Spearman’s correlation coefficients were used to test correlations between the circulating levels of ANGPT-2 or TIE-2 and various variables. We found that ANGPT-2 correlated with TIE-2 in patients with AMI (r = 0.441, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2a). With increasing Killip class, levels of ANGPT-2 (1.875 ng/mL for Killip I/II vs. 2.365 ng/mL for Killip III/IV, P = 0.001) and TIE-2 (20.920 ng/mL for Killip I/II vs. 23.765 ng/mL for Killip III/IV, P = 0.031) increased (Fig. 2b and c). Correlation analysis revealed that the circulating level of ANGPT-2 was positively correlated with NT-proBNP and D-dimer levels was negatively correlated with eGFR and LVEF (all values of P < 0.05). The circulating level of TIE-2 was positively correlated with D-dimer and negatively correlated with eGFR (all values of P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Cox regression analysis

During the follow-up period, univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that ANGPT-2, TIE-2, Log10NT-proBNP, diabetes mellitus, D-dimer, eGFR, Killip class and LVEF were significant predictors of MACE (all values of P < 0.05). VIF values of all variables were less than 2, which indicated that there was no significant collinearity. Results of the forward stepwise multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that ANGPT-2 (HR: 2.400; 95% CI: 1.380–4.523; P = 0.002), TIE-2 (HR: 2.004; 95% CI: 1.127–3.562; P = 0.018) and D-dimer (HR: 1.384; 95% CI: 1.087–1.764; P = 0.008) were independent predictors of MACE (Table 3). When ANGPT-2 was included alone, Harrell’s C-statistic was 0.705 (95% CI: 0.638–0.773). When TIE-2 was included alone, the C-statistic was 0.638 (95% CI: 0.557–0.722). When Log10NT-proBNP, diabetes mellitus, D-dimer, eGFR, Killip class and LVEF were included, the C-statistic was 0.667 (95% CI: 0.591–0.743). When ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 were combined with Killip classification, NT-proBNP, eGFR, GRACE score, and LVEF, the C-statistic increased to 0.733 (95% CI: 0.667–0.780).

Receiver operating characteristic curves

After follow-up for 2 years, the AUC of ANGPT-2 for identifying MACE was 0.756 (95% CI: 0.687–0.785; P < 0.001) and its cut-off point was 1.896 ng/mL (Fig. 3a). The AUC of TIE-2 was 0.664 (95% CI: 0.572–0.755; P < 0.001), and the cut-off point was 21.886 ng/mL. A tenfold cross-validation confirmed the robustness of these results as follows: the cross-validated AUC for ANGPT-2 was 0.743 ± 0.032 (95% CI: 0.681–0.805; P < 0.001) and for TIE-2 was 0.652 ± 0.041 (95% CI: 0.573–0.731; P < 0.001). The AUC of NT-proBNP (a well-recognized prognostic biomarker for AMI) for MACE prediction was 0.626 (95% CI: 0.535–0.717; P < 0.001). The AUC of ANGPT-2 combined with TIE-2 was 0.765(95% CI: 0.689–0.840, P < 0.001),which is the highest. De Long’s test confirmed that ANGPT-2 had a significantly greater AUC than NT-proBNP (P = 0.007) and ANGPT-2 combined with TIE-2 had a significantly greater AUC than NT-proBNP (P = 0.001); Additionally, there was no significant difference between TIE-2 and NT-proBNP in terms of the AUC (P = 0.283) (Fig. 4).

We incorporated important clinical variables into the clinical model that included age, gender, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, prior myocardial infarction, prior percutaneous coronary intervention, killip class, diastolic blood pressure, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, Troponin I, eGFR, D-dimer and LVEF. Upon adding ANGPT-2 to the clinical model, the NRI and IDI were 0.6681 (95% CI: 0.3806–0.9594) and 0.0830 (95% CI: 0.0390–0.1257), respectively, with a Harrell’s C-statistic of 0.7499 (95% CI: 0.6816–0.8182). When TIE-2 was added, the NRI and IDI were 0.6403 (95% CI: 0.3432–0.9599) and 0.0302 (95% CI: − 0.0080 to 0.0665), respectively, and the C-statistic was 0.7228 (95% CI: 0.6539–0.7917). When both ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 were added the clinical model, the NRI is 0.7692 (95% CI: 0.4920–1.0655) and the IDI is 0.0872 (95% CI: 0.0411–0.1325), with a C-statistic of 0.7531 (95% CI: 0.6867–0.8195).

Kaplan-Meier curve

After patients were classified into two groups according to the optimal cut-off point calculated by ROC. The Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and log-rank tests were conducted to determine the difference in survival between the two groups. The Kaplan‒Meier survival curve revealed that patients with ANGPT-2 levels ≥ 1.896 ng/mL had a greater risk of MACE than those with ANGPT-2 levels < 1.896 ng/mL (HR = 3.189; 95% CI: 1.856–5.481; P < 0.001) (Fig. 5a), and patients with TIE-2 levels ≥ 21.886 ng/mL had a greater risk of MACE than those with TIE-2 levels < 21.886 ng/mL (HR = 2.912; 95% CI: 1.681–5.042; P < 0.001) (Fig. 5b).

Discussion

In this study, for the first time, we evaluated the ability of circulating ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 to predict the occurrence of MACE in patients with AMI. Further analysis also indicated that ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 were independent predictors of MACE in patients with AMI. Importantly, We further explore the AUC values of ANGPT-2 combined with TIE-2 in patients with AMI, which was significantly greater than that of any other single indicator. Moreover, when ANGPT-2 or TIE-2 was added to the commonly used clinical parameters, the reclassification was improved as assessed by NRI and IDI. We added ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 to the clinical model, which further improved reclassification.

Several clinical studies have indicated that patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) have higher peripheral blood levels of ANGPT-2, which are correlated with the degree of coronary artery stenosis [21, 22]; this may be due to the possibility of endothelial activation, pathological angiogenesis, and endothelial network instability caused by inflammation and hypoxia, which can develop and spread CHD [23]. Supraphysiological levels of ANGPT-2 can worsen tissue inflammation and microvascular remodelling, which is crucial for the aggravation of cardiac function in both chronic heart disease and acute myocardial ischemia [24]. According to a large community population study, a greater risk of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality was associated with elevated serum ANGPT-2 levels [25]. Additionally, ANGPT-2 was an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in patients with acute heart failure, acute coronary syndrome and AMI with cardiogenic shock [26–28]. Consistent with these studies, our study revealed that ANGPT-2 could be used to assess the prognosis of patients with AMI.

The Killip classification has been established as an important auxiliary tool in the prognostic evaluation of heart failure [29]. In this study, we found that patients with more severe symptoms (Killip III/IV) had higher levels of ANGPT-2 than patients with milder symptoms (Killip I/II). This finding suggests that ANGPT-2 may be a marker for the severity of acute cardiac insufficiency following AMI. According to our research, ANGPT-2 levels increase as the LVEF decreases, which suggests a tight connection between endothelial dysfunction and left ventricular dysfunction and remodelling following AMI [30]. The potential mechanism might involve ANGPT-2 continuously promoting abnormal vascular remodelling and proinflammatory macrophage polarization through inflammation and apoptosis, which worsens cardiac hypoxia and inflammation [13, 31]. Additionally, we found that circulating ANGPT-2 levels were correlated with D-dimer levels and reflected a potential relationship between coagulation dysfunction and endothelial dysfunction in AMI patients.

TIE-2, the most significant cell surface receptor of the angiopoietin protein, is widely expressed in endothelial cells and has a significant effect on the vascular system [32]. Vascular development, maintenance and instability are determined by the balance between ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 [11]. Our study confirms a strong association between ANGPT-2 and TIE-2, which is consistent with the results of previous studies. Moreover, our study demonstrated that TIE-2 is related to the degree of acute cardiac insufficiency following AMI and may predict the development of MACE in AMI patients. To our knowledge, this is the first study to document a strong association between TIE-2 and MACE in AMI patients. The potential mechanism of circulating TIE-2 may involve its ability to bind to angiopoietin and obstruct ANGPT-1/TIE-2 signalling in endothelial cells, which progressively exacerbates endothelial dysfunction [33]. We also found that circulating TIE-2 levels were significantly correlated with eGFR levels, which suggests that circulating TIE-2 levels in AMI patients may reflect renal impairment. However, the causal relationship between these two factors remains unclear, and the exact relationship between TIE-2 and eGFR levels needs to be further investigated.

Currently, NT-proBNP is commonly used for risk stratification in AMI patients [34]. By comparing the ROC curves of ANGPT-2 and TIE-2, this study revealed that ANGPT-2 was more effective at predicting MACE in AMI patients. Specifically, in our dataset, the AUC of ANGPT-2 was significantly greater than that of NT-proBNP, which is consistent with the findings of previous study [25]. The AUC of TIE-2 was comparable to that of NT-proBNP but higher than the AUC of hs-CRP reported in the literature [35]. Additionally, The AUC value of ANGPT-2 combined with TIE-2 was the greatest. These findings indicate that combining ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 may serve as a better predictive marker of MACE occurrence in AMI patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-centre study with a small sample size and a short follow-up time. Forward stepwise selection in a small cohort can yield unstable models and biased estimates and the events per variable was relatively small in our study, which may lead to model instability. In future research, we will increase the sample size and increase the time of patient follow-up to demonstrate the prognostic value of ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 in AMI patients. Second, following the incidence of AMI, we did not continuously monitor levels of circulating ANGPT-2 and TIE-2. However, Pöss et al. detected circulating ANGPT-2 levels in patients with cardiogenic shock after AMI within 3 days of onset and reported no significant difference in the level of ANGPT-2 [28]. As a result, on the first day following admission, we chose the plasma of AMI patients for detection. Third, this study did not examine the connection between inflammation and ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 even though there is a link between inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [35]. To fully understand how inflammation affects the ability of ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 in predicting the occurrence of MACE in AMI patients, more research is required.

Conclusion

Our findings imply that ANGPT-2 and TIE-2 can both predict MACE in AMI patients independently and they might be potential biomarkers for prognostic evaluation of patients with AMI in the risk stratification.
