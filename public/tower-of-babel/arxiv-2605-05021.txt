Reconstruction of non-self-adjoint anisotropic and complex inclusions in the Calderón problem
Henrik Garde, David Johansson, Thanasis Zacharopoulos

Abstract
. We generalize recent results on the monotonicity method, for inclusion detection in the partial data anisotropic Calderón problem, to very general non-self-adjoint perturbations. This involves a forward model that accounts for both the anisotropic real conductivity and the anisotropic permittivity, and the results hold in any spatial dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . We assume that the inclusion boundaries can be reached from the domain boundary via a set on which the background conductivity is self-adjoint, and that a definiteness condition holds near the inclusion boundaries. Away from the inclusion boundaries we allow general L ∞ L^{\infty} non-self-adjoint perturbations. We only require unique continuation based on the self-adjoint part of the background conductivity, thus making the methods compatible with generic unique continuation results.



Keywords: anisotropic Calderón problem, non-self-adjoint, inclusion detection, monotonicity method.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35R30, 35R05, 47H05.

1.
Introduction

We consider the partial data anisotropic Calderón problem, on reconstructing information about a matrix-valued conductivity coefficient
A
in the equation

-\nabla\cdot(A\nabla u)=0\text{ in }\Omega,

based on the Neumann-to-Dirichlet (ND) map
\Lambda(A)
on an open subset
\Gamma\subseteq\partial\Omega
. Specific to the anisotropic problem, there are significant obstructions to uniqueness in determining a coefficient
A
from
\Lambda(A)
, see e.g.
[
2
,
15
,
14
]
. To our knowledge, there are no general characterizations of the non-uniqueness in the non-self-adjoint setting of the anisotropic Calderón problem. Instead we determine the outer shape
D^{\bullet}
(Definition
3.1
) of inclusions
D=\mathop{\textup{supp}}(A_{D}-A_{0})
, from knowledge of a “background conductivity” coefficient
A_{0}
and the ND map
\Lambda(A_{D})
for an unknown coefficient
A_{D}
. A reconstruction method was recently proved in
[
5
]
for very general
L^{\infty}

self-adjoint
coefficients based on the monotonicity method. The main assumptions are a unique continuation principle (UCP) satisfied by
A_{0}
, and that
A_{D}-A_{0}
satisfies a definiteness condition near
\partial D^{\bullet}
.

We now consider the more complicated case of non-self-adjoint inclusions. Namely we write a general conductivity coefficient as

A=A^{\textup{R}}+\mathrm{i}A^{\textup{I}},

where
A^{\textup{R}}
is the
self-adjoint
part of
A
while
\mathrm{i}A^{\textup{I}}
is the
skew-adjoint
part of
A
. In particular, both
A^{\textup{R}}
and
A^{\textup{I}}
are self-adjoint as matrices. Suppose that both
A^{\textup{R}}
and
A^{\textup{I}}
have real-valued matrix-entries, then this corresponds to a mathematical model with

A^{\textup{R}}=\sigma_{\omega}\quad\text{and}\quad A^{\textup{I}}=\omega\varepsilon_{\omega},

where
\sigma_{\omega}
is the anisotropic
real
conductivity and
\varepsilon_{\omega}
is the anisotropic permittivity, both in general depending on the frequency
\omega
. In this setting,
A
would be the the anisotropic
complex
conductivity (also called an anisotropic admittivity).

We give a number of generalizations of results from
[
5
]
to the non-self-adjoint setting, using monotonicity inequalities for the self-adjoint parts of the ND maps (Theorem
7.1
), but with some limitations on the skew-adjoint part of the background conductivity
A_{0}
. Theorems
3.4
and
4.1
give exact reconstruction methods of
D^{\bullet}
provided that:

•

a definiteness condition is satisfied near
\partial D^{\bullet}
for the self-adjoint parts
A^{\textup{R}}_{D}-A^{\textup{R}}_{0}
, with a sufficiently small skew-adjoint part near
\partial D^{\bullet}
,

•

that
A^{\textup{R}}_{0}
satisfies the UCP (see e.g.
[
5
, definition 3.2]
),

•

and that all of
\partial D^{\bullet}
can be reached from the measurement boundary
\Gamma
via an open connected set on which
A_{0}
is self-adjoint.

In particular, inside
D
(away from
\partial D^{\bullet}
) we allow
A_{D}-A_{0}
to be a general
L^{\infty}
non-self-adjoint perturbation. Moreover, no explicit regularity is required from
\partial D^{\bullet}
. Theorem
4.1
is particularly useful for numerical implementation, since the test-operators are linearized and therefore can be implemented as a real-time reconstruction method.

The requirement that
A_{0}
is self-adjoint on a set that connects all of
\partial D^{\bullet}
to
\Gamma
is related to the non-optimality of the monotonicity inequalities in Theorem
7.1
. There are terms such as
A^{\textup{I}}_{j}(A^{\textup{R}}_{1})^{-1}A^{\textup{I}}_{j}
for
j\in\{1,2\}
that do not vanish even if
A_{1}=A_{2}
, meaning that the lower and upper bounds in those inequalities are non-zero in the non-self-adjoint case even when the coefficients agree. This leads to the need of some trickery, including more complicated test-operators, in order to make the
outer approach
of the monotonicity method work, and it seems to prevent very general use of the
inner approach
of the monotonicity method in this setting (see
[
5
]
for the differences between the inner and outer approach for the self-adjoint setting).

It is worth noting that we only require a UCP result for
A^{\textup{R}}_{0}
and not
A_{0}
itself, which is very useful since most UCP results are proven for self-adjoint coefficients. In particular, for spatial dimension
d=2
, for any background conductivity
A_{0}
for which
A^{\textup{R}}_{0}
is real-valued, then
A^{\textup{R}}_{0}
satisfies the UCP
[
1
]
, while for spatial dimension
d\geq 3
, a sufficient condition is to also require that
A^{\textup{R}}_{0}
is Lipschitz regular
[
7
,
16
,
17
]
.

In the case when
A_{0}
is self-adjoint and it is only the perturbation
A_{D}-A_{0}
that is non-self-adjoint, the assumptions and test-operators simplify, and the corresponding results are given in Corollary
5.2
. In
[
5
, theorem 12.2]
a general result was given using
extreme
test-operators, based on perfectly conducting and perfectly insulating parts of a coefficient. This method does not require bounds on the magnitude of the unknown perturbation. In Theorem
6.2
we show that the exact same extreme test-operators that work for very general self-adjoint perturbations also work for very general non-self-adjoint perturbations, where we again assume that
A_{0}
is self-adjoint. The proof becomes very short by rewriting the problem in a way that allows utilizing
[
5
, theorem 12.2]
based on self-adjoint coefficients.

In Appendix
A
we prove better monotonicity inequalities, in the sense that the bounds become zero when
A_{1}=A_{2}
or if the skew-adjoint parts vanish. Monotonicity-type reconstruction methods rely on localized potentials (Theorem
7.5
), to control the monotonicity inequalities using e.g. just the electric potential
u_{2}
related to coefficient
A_{2}
. We show that the extra terms originating from the skew-adjoint parts can be bounded solely based on
u_{2}
. However, these bounds are still not strong enough to avoid the extra assumptions on the background conductivity in the monotonicity method. Hence, we leave it for the inverse problems community to improve those bounds, in order to allow more general non-self-adjoint background conductivities and to enable the inner approach of the monotonicity method in this setting.

1.1.
Relation to previous results

Only few reconstruction methods of inclusions have been developed for the anisotropic Calderón problem, with
[
5
]
being a main recent result and a factorization method is given in
[
13
]
. In the real isotropic (scalar-valued) setting, we will just mention a few key results
[
6
,
3
,
12
,
11
,
18
]
related to the monotonicity method; see references within
[
5
,
8
]
for additional results.

In the complex isotropic setting, there is also a different type of factorization method in
[
10
]
for inclusion detection. The factorization method does not allow general perturbations, but require the perturbation to be bounded away from zero in a definite way (either positive or negative) throughout the entire inclusion. There is also a monotonicity method related to ultrasound modulated electrical impedance tomography
[
9
]
. Both results from
[
10
,
9
]
rely on a clever way of weighting the ND maps with admittivity coefficients. We note that such a weighting also introduces non-uniqueness in the isotropic problem (some perturbations become invisible to measurements), and we note that this approach does not appear to generalize to the anisotropic setting.

1.3.
Remarks on notation

All considered vector spaces are complex; this includes the classes of conductivity coefficients. We use the convention that inner products on complex Hilbert spaces are linear in the first entry and anti-linear in the second. We denote the Euclidean inner product as
z_{1}\cdot\overline{z_{2}}
for
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, in particular the “dot” is bilinear. The Euclidean norm is denoted
\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert
.

For self-adjoint operators
A,B\in\mathscr{L}(H)
for a Hilbert space
H
, we write
A\geq B
when
A-B
is positive semidefinite; this is the Loewner ordering of such operators. Moreover, for
A
being a positive definite operator,
A^{1/2}
denotes its unique positive definite square root.

2.
The forward problem

This section will be close to
[
5
, section 2]
, and the claims given in this section are verified there. Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for
d\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
, with connected complement. For general bounded non-self-adjoint matrix-valued functions
A\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d\times d}
, we equip the space with the norm

\lVert A\rVert_{\mathcal{H}(\Omega)}=\sup_{x\in\Omega}\lVert A(x)\rVert_{2},

for which
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{2}
is the Euclidean operator norm (spectral norm for matrices). Note that in
[
5
]
we denoted this norm as
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{*}
; we changed the notation to
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\mathcal{H}(\Omega)}
as we will also need to consider the norm of a coefficient restricted to a subset
V\subset\Omega
, which becomes
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\mathcal{H}(V)}
.

We consistently decompose
A
into a
self-adjoint part

A^{\textup{R}}
and a
skew-adjoint part

\mathrm{i}A^{\textup{I}}
, where
A^{\textup{R}}
and
A^{\textup{I}}
are self-adjoint as matrices:

A=A^{\textup{R}}+\mathrm{i}A^{\textup{I}}

with

A^{\textup{R}}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(A+A^{*}\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad A^{\textup{I}}=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\bigl(A-A^{*}\bigr).

In this notation, we may also refer to
A^{\textup{R}}
as the
real part
and
A^{\textup{I}}
as the
imaginary part
. Note that this does not relate to the actual matrix-entries, as these can be complex in both
A^{\textup{R}}
and
A^{\textup{I}}
, but rather it relates to the associated quadratic forms, which for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
satisfy:

\mathop{\textup{Re}}\bigl(A\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}\bigr)=A^{\textup{R}}\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathop{\textup{Im}}\bigl(A\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}\bigr)=A^{\textup{I}}\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}.

The general class of conductivity coefficients we will consider are given by

\mathcal{H}(\Omega)=\{\,A\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d\times d}\mid\exists c>0\colon A^{\textup{R}}\geq cI\text{ in the Loewner order in $\Omega$}\,\}.

In case the conductivity coefficient is self-adjoint, it belongs to

\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)=\{\,A\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\mid A^{\textup{I}}=0\,\}.

Consider the partial data anisotropic conductivity problem for
A\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
:

-\nabla\cdot(A\nabla u)=0\text{ in }\Omega,\qquad\nu\cdot(A\nabla u)=\begin{cases}f&\text{on }\Gamma,\\
0&\text{on }\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma.\end{cases}

(2.1)

Here
\nu
is the outer unit normal to
\Omega
,
\Gamma\subseteq\partial\Omega
is a non-empty open boundary piece, and the mean-free current density
f
belongs to

L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)=\{\,f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\mid\langle f,1\rangle=0\,\}.

We denote by
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
the standard inner product on
L^{2}(\Gamma)
, and
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
is the associated norm. We likewise denote

H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)=\{\,u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\mid\langle u|_{\Gamma},1\rangle=0\,\}.

From the Lax–Milgram lemma there exists a unique weak solution
u=u_{f}^{A}\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
to (
2.1
). We will occasionally use the notation
u_{f}^{A}
to indicate which coefficient
A
and Neumann condition
f
that is considered.

We let
\Lambda(A)\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma))
denote the associated local ND map,

\Lambda(A)f=u_{f}^{A}|_{\Gamma},

and the forward problem is the nonlinear mapping
\Lambda\colon\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\to\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma))
. It holds in general that

\Lambda(A^{*})=\Lambda(A)^{*},

(2.2)

and for any
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
and
f,g\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)
, we have

\langle f,\Lambda(A_{1})g\rangle=\int_{\Omega}A_{2}\nabla u_{f}^{A_{2}}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{g}^{A_{1}}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.3)

We also write

\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\Lambda(A)+\Lambda(A)^{*}\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}(A)=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\bigl(\Lambda(A)-\Lambda(A)^{*}\bigr),

where
\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A)
and
\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}(A)
are self-adjoint operators. In terms of the quadratic forms we thus have

\displaystyle\langle f,\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A)f\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textup{Re}}\langle f,\Lambda(A)f\rangle=\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}\nabla u_{f}^{A}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\lvert(A^{\textup{R}})^{1/2}\nabla u_{f}^{A}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\langle f,\Lambda^{\!\textup{I}}(A)f\rangle

\displaystyle=-\mathop{\textup{Im}}\langle f,\Lambda(A)f\rangle=-\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}\nabla u_{f}^{A}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.5)

3.
Nonlinear reconstruction of non-self-adjoint inclusions

We will assume that a “background conductivity”
A_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
is known. For an unknown
A_{D}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
, we call

D=\mathop{\textup{supp}}(A_{D}-A_{0})

the
inclusions
, noting that
D
may have several connected components. Our reconstruction method relates to the so-called outer shape of
D
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
outer shape

D^{\bullet}
of
D
is the smallest closed set with connected complement such that
D\subseteq D^{\bullet}
.

We will denote

M=\mathop{\textup{supp}}(A^{\textup{I}}_{0}).

We say that
C\in\mathcal{A}
for the class of admissible test-inclusions
\mathcal{A}
, provided that

(i)

C\Subset\Omega
is the closure of an open set and has connected complement.

(ii)

\partial C\cap M=\emptyset
.

Since
M
is known, this will inform on how to pick the test-inclusions. Note also that certain connected components of
M
can be contained inside
C
. Moreover, the test-inclusions can consist of several connected components.

Assumption 3.2
.

(i)

Assume there are
known
bounds on
A_{D}
, i.e. scalars
0<\alpha\leq\beta
and
\eta\geq 0
such that

\alpha I\leq A^{\textup{R}}_{D}\leq\beta I\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha I\leq A^{\textup{R}}_{0}\leq\beta I\quad

in the Loewner order in
\Omega
, and

\max\{\lVert A^{\textup{I}}_{D}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}(\Omega)},\lVert A^{\textup{I}}_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}(\Omega)}\}\leq\eta.

(ii)

Assume that
D\Subset\Omega
and is the closure of an open set.

(iii)

There exists a connected component
S
of
\Omega\setminus(D\cup M)
, such that
\partial D^{\bullet}\subset\partial S
and such that
\partial S
contains a non-empty relatively open subset of
\Gamma
.

(iv)

For every
x\in\partial D^{\bullet}
and every open neighbourhood
W
of
x
, assume there exists a relatively open connected set
V\subset D^{\bullet}\cap W
that intersects
\partial D^{\bullet}
, satisfying either of two options:

(a)

A^{\textup{R}}_{D}-A^{\textup{R}}_{0}\geq\tau^{+}I
in
V
, and there exist an open ball
B\subset V
and
c>0
such that
A^{\textup{R}}_{D}-A^{\textup{R}}_{0}\geq(\tau^{+}+c)I
in
B
, where

\tau^{+}\geq\tfrac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{3}}\lVert A^{\textup{I}}_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}(V)}^{2}.

(b)

A^{\textup{R}}_{D}-A^{\textup{R}}_{0}\leq-\tau^{-}I
in
V
, and there exist an open ball
B\subset V
and
c>0
such that
A^{\textup{R}}_{D}-A^{\textup{R}}_{0}\leq-(\tau^{-}+c)I
in
B
, where

\tau^{-}\geq\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\lVert A^{\textup{I}}_{D}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}(V)}^{2}.

(v)

Assume that
A^{\textup{R}}_{0}
satisfies the UCP.

Remark 3.3
.

In the isotropic case,
\beta^{2}/\alpha^{3}
can be replaced by
\beta/\alpha^{2}
in part (a) of Assumption
3.2
(iv). This is due to Remark
7.4
and the use of Proposition
7.3
in the proofs of Theorems
3.4
and
4.1
.

In Assumption
3.2
, part (iii) ensures that we can use localization away from the support of
A^{\textup{I}}_{0}
, up to (but not including) an interior neighbourhood (inside
D
) of
\partial D^{\bullet}
, which will be crucial.

For a measurable set
C
, we define the self-adjoint test-coefficients

A^{-}_{C}=\begin{cases}\alpha I&\text{in }C\\
A^{\textup{R}}_{0}&\text{in }\Omega\setminus C\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad A^{+}_{C}=\begin{cases}(\beta+\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{\alpha})I&\text{in }C\\
A^{\textup{R}}_{0}&\text{in }\Omega\setminus C,\end{cases}

and the corresponding test-operators

\Lambda^{-}_{C}=\Lambda(A^{-}_{C})\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda^{+}_{C}=\Lambda(A^{+}_{C}).

We also define

\langle f,\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{M\setminus C}^{+}f\rangle=-\int_{M\setminus C}A^{\textup{I}}_{0}(A^{\textup{R}}_{0})^{-1}A^{\textup{I}}_{0}\nabla u_{f}^{A_{C}^{+}}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A_{C}^{+}}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

which is the Fréchet derivative of
\Lambda
at
A_{C}^{+}
and in the direction
A^{\textup{I}}_{0}(A^{\textup{R}}_{0})^{-1}A^{\textup{I}}_{0}\chi_{M\setminus C}
, where
\chi_{M\setminus C}
is a characteristic function on the set
M\setminus C
, cf.
[
4
]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Under Assumption
3.2
(i), for any measurable
C\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
we have

D\subseteq C\quad\text{implies}\quad\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A_{D})\geq\Lambda^{+}_{C}+\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{M\setminus C}^{+}.

Under all of Assumption
3.2
, for any
C\in\mathcal{A}
we have

\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A_{D})\geq\Lambda^{+}_{C}+\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{M\setminus C}^{+}\quad\text{implies}\quad D\subseteq C.

Proof.

The proof is given in Section
8
.
∎

4.
Linearized reconstruction of non-self-adjoint inclusions

We now consider a linearized version of the monotonicity method. We define the following test-operators, via quadratic forms,

\displaystyle\langle f,\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{C}^{+}f\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{C}\bigl[\bigl(\beta+\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{\alpha}\bigr)I-A^{\textup{R}}_{0}\bigr]\nabla u_{f}^{A^{\textup{R}}_{0}}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A^{\textup{R}}_{0}}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\langle f,\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{C}^{-}f\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{C}\bigl[A^{\textup{R}}_{0}-\tfrac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha}I\bigr]\nabla u_{f}^{A^{\textup{R}}_{0}}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A^{\textup{R}}_{0}}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\langle f,\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{M\setminus C}f\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{M\setminus C}A^{\textup{I}}_{0}(A^{\textup{R}}_{0})^{-1}A^{\textup{I}}_{0}\nabla u_{f}^{A^{\textup{R}}_{0}}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{f}^{A^{\textup{R}}_{0}}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

The above test-operators are in fact Fréchet derivatives of
\Lambda
at
A^{\textup{R}}_{0}
and in particular directions, cf.
[
4
]
. Note below that we have
\Lambda(A^{\textup{R}}_{0})=\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A^{\textup{R}}_{0})
because of (
2.2
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Under Assumption
3.2
(i), for any measurable
C\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
we have

D\subseteq C\quad\text{implies}\quad\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A_{D})-\Lambda(A^{\textup{R}}_{0})\geq\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{+}_{C}+\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{M\setminus C}.

Under all of Assumption
3.2
, for any
C\in\mathcal{A}
we have

\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A_{D})-\Lambda(A^{\textup{R}}_{0})\geq\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{+}_{C}+\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{M\setminus C}\quad\text{implies}\quad D\subseteq C.

Proof.

The proof is given in Section
9
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Under the given assumptions, Theorem
3.4
gives

D^{\bullet}=\cap\,\{\,C\in\mathcal{A}\mid\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A_{D})\geq\Lambda^{+}_{C}+\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{M\setminus C}^{+}\,\},

while Theorem
4.1
gives

D^{\bullet}=\cap\,\{\,C\in\mathcal{A}\mid\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A_{D})-\Lambda(A^{\textup{R}}_{0})\geq\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{+}_{C}+\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{M\setminus C}\,\}.

Moreover, from the proofs we also conclude:

•

If in Assumption
3.2
(iv) we only arrive at case (a), we only need to check the inequalities
\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A_{D})\geq\Lambda^{+}_{C}+\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{M\setminus C}^{+}
or
\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A_{D})-\Lambda(A^{\textup{R}}_{0})\geq\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{+}_{C}+\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda_{M\setminus C}
.

•

If in Assumption
3.2
(iv) we only arrive at case (b), we only need to check the inequalities
\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A_{D})
or
\textup{D}\mkern-1.5mu\Lambda^{-}_{C}\geq\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}(A_{D})-\Lambda(A^{\textup{R}}_{0})
.

5.
When the background conductivity is self-adjoint

We will here give the corresponding results to Theorems
3.4
and
4.1
in the simplified setting when
A_{0}
is self-adjoint, that is
A_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{\textup{SA}}(\Omega)
. We will also assume that
A^{\textup{I}}_{D}=0
in a neighbourhood of
\partial D^{\bullet}
to further simplify the definiteness assumptions. In this case the class of admissible test-inclusions are

\mathcal{A}=\{\,C\Subset\Omega\mid C\text{ is the closure of an open set and has connected complement}\,\}.

Assumption 5.1
.

(i)

Assume there are
known
bounds on
A_{D}
, i.e. scalars
0<\alpha\leq\beta
and
\eta\geq 0
such that

\alpha I\leq A^{\textup{R}}_{D}\leq\beta I\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha I\leq A^{\textup{R}}_{0}\leq\beta I\quad

in the Loewner order in
\Omega
, and

\lVert A^{\textup{I}}_{D}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}(\Omega)}\leq\eta.

(ii)

Assume that
D\Subset\Omega
and is the closure of an open set.

(iii)

Assume
A^{\textup{I}}_{D}=0
in an open neighbourhood of
\partial D^{\bullet}
.

(iv)

For every
x\in\partial D^{\bullet}
and every open neighbourhood
W
of
x
, assume there exists a relatively open connected set
V\subset D^{\bullet}\cap W
that intersects
\partial D^{\bullet}
, satisfying either of two options:

(a)

A^{\textup{R}}_{D}-A_{0}
is positive semidefinite in
V
, and there exists an open ball
B\subset V
on which
A^{\textup{R}}_{D}-A_{0}
is uniformly positive definite.

(b)

A^{\textup{R}}_{D}-A_{0}
is negative semidefinite in
V
, and there exists an open ball

Appendix A
Towards improved monotonicity inequalities

In this section we prove inequalities in the style of Theorem
7.1
, but with optimal bounds in the sense that the lower and upper bounds are zero if the coefficients coincide (unlike in Theorem
7.1
for non-self-adjoint coefficients). We also prove that the additional terms that show up due to the non-self-adjointness can be controlled by the norm of
u_{2}
. Although, as will be apparent at the end of this appendix, an even more precise control in terms of
u_{2}
will be needed in order to improve the monotonicity method in the non-self-adjoint setting, related to the use of localized potentials from Theorem
7.5
.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
and
f\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\Gamma)
. For
j\in\{1,2\}
we denote
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda(A_{j})
and
u_{j}=u_{f}^{A_{j}}
. Then

\displaystyle\langle f,(\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{1}-\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{2})f\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\langle f,(\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{1}-\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{2})f\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{2}(A^{\textup{R}}_{1})^{-1}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{2}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

We start by noting that

\displaystyle\mathop{\textup{Re}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textup{Re}}\int_{\Omega}(A_{1}-\mathrm{i}A^{\textup{I}}_{1})\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textup{Re}}\int_{\Omega}A_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x-\mathop{\textup{Re}}\mathrm{i}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(A.1)

By swapping the indices in (
A
), and using that
A^{\textup{R}}_{2}
and
A^{\textup{I}}_{2}
are self-adjoint, we also have

\mathop{\textup{Re}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{2}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x-\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{2}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(A.2)

Using (
A
) and that
A^{\textup{R}}_{1}
is positive definite, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\hskip-199.16928pt

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\mathop{\textup{Re}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x-2\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\mathop{\textup{Re}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x-2\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\phantom{={}}+\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{1}\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})\cdot\overline{\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{2}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

That is,

\langle f,(\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{1}-\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{2})f\rangle\geq\int_{\Omega}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using (
A.2

A^{\textup{R}}_{2}(A^{\textup{R}}_{1})^{-1}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})
is self-adjoint, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{2}(A^{\textup{R}}_{1})^{-1}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{2}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\hskip-270.30118pt

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{2}(A^{\textup{R}}_{1})^{-1}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{1}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x-2\mathop{\textup{Re}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{2}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\langle f,(\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{1}-\Lambda^{\!\textup{R}}_{2})f\rangle\leq\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{R}}_{2}(A^{\textup{R}}_{1})^{-1}(A^{\textup{R}}_{2}-A^{\textup{R}}_{1})\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x+2\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{2}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.\qed

What remains is to establish estimates of

\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{j}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x

(A.3)

with respect to
u_{2}
, in order to be able to combine such inequalities with localization as in Theorem
7.5
.

Let
N(A)\colon f\mapsto u_{f}^{A}
be the solution operator for the PDE problem with coefficient
A
, then using Taylor’s theorem (in Banach spaces) with integral remainder term, we have

u_{1}=u_{2}+\int_{0}^{1}\textup{D}\!N\bigl(A_{2}+t(A_{1}-A_{2});A_{1}-A_{2}\bigr)f\,\mathrm{d}t.

As
A^{\textup{I}}_{j}
is self-adjoint and due to the imaginary part in (
A.3
), we have that

\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{j}\nabla u_{2}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,

so we only have to consider the remainder term from Taylor’s theorem. In the following we denote
A_{t}=A_{2}+t(A_{1}-A_{2})
,
u_{t}=u_{f}^{A_{t}}
, and let
w_{t}\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(\Omega)
be the unique solution to

\int_{\Omega}A_{t}\nabla w_{t}\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{t}\cdot\overline{\nabla v}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(A.4)

for all

. From
[
4

, we have

\textup{D}\!N\bigl(A_{2}+t(A_{1}-A_{2});A_{1}-A_{2}\bigr)f=w_{t}.

Let
\bigcup_{t\in[0,1]}\mathop{\textup{supp}}(\nabla w_{t})\subseteq W
for some measurable

, and let
M=\mathop{\textup{supp}}(A^{\textup{I}}_{j})
. Then (
A.3
) becomes

\displaystyle\lvert\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{\Omega}A^{\textup{I}}_{j}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\mathop{\textup{Im}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{W\cap M}A^{\textup{I}}_{j}\nabla w_{t}\cdot\overline{\nabla u_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert A^{\textup{I}}_{j}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}(W\cap M)}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{1}\lVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(W)^{d}}\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigr)\lVert\nabla u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(W\cap M)^{d}}.

(A.5)

If
A^{\textup{R}}_{1}\geq\alpha I
and
A^{\textup{R}}_{2}\geq\alpha I
for some

A_{1}

\displaystyle\alpha\lVert\nabla w_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(W)^{d}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\textup{Re}}\int_{\Omega}A_{t}\nabla w_{t}\cdot\overline{\nabla w_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textup{Re}}\int_{W}(A_{2}-A_{1})\nabla u_{t}\cdot\overline{\nabla w_{t}}\,\mathrm{d}x
