High-Probability Guarantees for Random Zeroth-Order Gradient Descent
Haishan Ye

Abstract
Zeroth-order optimization aims to minimize an objective function using only function evaluations, and is therefore fundamental in black-box optimization, hyperparameter tuning, bandit learning, and adversarial machine learning. While classical zeroth-order methods are well understood in expectation, much less is known about their high-probability behavior, especially for smooth and strongly convex objectives. In this paper, we establish high-probability convergence guarantees for random zeroth-order gradient descent in both deterministic and stochastic settings. For deterministic L L -smooth and μ \mu -strongly convex objectives of d d -dimension, we show that the classical two-query random zeroth-order method finds an ε \varepsilon -suboptimal solution with probability at least 1 − δ 1-\delta using 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ​ L μ ​ log ⁡ 1 ε + log ⁡ 1 δ ) \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right) function queries. Thus, compared with the standard in-expectation complexity, only an additive logarithmic dependence on the confidence parameter is needed. For stochastic objectives, under a bounded-noise condition and without assuming uniformly bounded stochastic gradients, we prove that random zeroth-order stochastic gradient descent achieves an ε \varepsilon -suboptimal solution with probability at least 1 − δ 1-\delta using 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ​ ( log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) + log ⁡ ( 1 / δ ) ) ε ) \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d\log(1/\varepsilon)\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta)\right)}{\varepsilon}\right) queries. Our results provide high-confidence counterparts to classical expectation-based zeroth-order convergence guarantees and clarify the additional cost required to obtain reliable performance guarantees.

Introduction

Zeroth-order (or derivative-free) optimization concerns the minimization of an objective function using only function evaluations, without access to explicit gradient information. Such methods are central in black-box optimization, hyperparameter tuning, bandit problems, and adversarial machine learning, where gradients are either unavailable or prohibitively expensive to compute
(
Conn et al., 2009
;
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
)
.

In this paper, we try to solve the problem of minimizing an
L
-smooth, and
\mu
-strongly convex deterministic function

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x}),

(1.1)

or a stochastic function

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[f(\bm{x};\;\xi)\right],

(1.2)

using only zeroth-order information.
Standard approaches estimate the gradient through randomized directional sampling or finite differences, and then perform (stochastic) gradient-descent steps based on these estimates
(
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
;
Duchi et al., 2015
)
.

Classical analyses of zeroth-order optimization typically provide convergence guarantees
in expectation
, bounding the average performance over algorithmic randomness or stochasticity in the objective
(
Ghadimi & Lan, 2013
;
Shamir, 2013
;
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
)
.
For example, existing results bound
\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}^{*})]
as a function of the number of queries with
\bm{x}^{*}
being the optimal point.
While useful, in-expectation guarantees do not provide explicit high-confidence assurances, which are critical in safety-sensitive or high-stakes applications.
In contrast, high-probability guarantees provide stronger assurances about the algorithm’s performance in practice.

High-probability convergence analysis of zeroth-order algorithms is substantially more challenging than expectation-based analysis. Zeroth-order gradient estimators, constructed via random directional sampling or finite differences, are inherently noisy, with variance scaling roughly linearly with the dimension
d
. The estimates are sequentially dependent: each iterate
\bm{x}_{t}
affects the next estimate, creating a martingale-like structure that complicates standard concentration analysis. Moreover, smoothing techniques introduce bias, resulting in a delicate bias–variance tradeoff across iterations. Achieving a global confidence level
1-\delta
over
T
steps further requires sophisticated time-uniform concentration inequalities, as naive union bounds would be excessively loose. These factors collectively make high-probability analysis technically intricate and require careful probabilistic reasoning.

The challenge intensifies for stochastic objectives, as in Eq. (
1.2
), where each function evaluation adds randomness atop the estimator noise. Controlling both sources simultaneously demands balancing the estimator’s smoothing bias against stochastic noise. The sequential dependence of iterates combined with stochastic function realizations produces a double-martingale structure, complicating concentration further. Ensuring a
1-\delta
confidence level now often incurs extra logarithmic factors in query complexity and amplifies dimension-dependent variance. Consequently, establishing high-probability convergence for stochastic zeroth-order methods is far more complex than in the deterministic case, requiring advanced probabilistic tools and meticulous iteration-wise analysis.

In this work, we address this gap by establishing
high-probability convergence guarantees
for zeroth-order gradient descent in both deterministic and stochastic settings. Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

Deterministic objectives:
For an
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex function, zeroth-order gradient descent converges to the global optimum with probability at least
1-\delta
, using

\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Big).

function queries.
Compared with the query complexity holding in expectation
(
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
)
, our result shows that random zeroth-order algorithms only require only
\mathcal{O}(\log\frac{1}{\delta})
extra queries to achieve a
1-\delta
probability solution.

2.

Stochastic objectives:
For
f(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[f(\bm{x};\xi)]
, the random zeroth-order gradient descent converges to the global optimum with probability at least
1-\delta
, with query complexity

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d\cdot\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}{\varepsilon}\right).

Compared with the query complexity of zeroth-order stochastic gradient holding in expectation which is
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\right)

(
Malladi et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2024
)
, our result shows that random zeroth-order stochastic gradient descent methods only take
\mathcal{O}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)
times extra queries to find an
\varepsilon
-suboptimal solution with a probability at least
1-\delta
.

1.1
Related Works

Zeroth-order optimization has received significant attention due to its ability to handle optimization problems when gradient information is unavailable. In this section, we review related works in three main directions which closely related to our work: random zeroth-order gradient descent, online convex optimization (OCO) with bandit feedback, and (zeroth-order) stochastic gradient descent.

Random Zeroth-Order Gradient Descent.

Random zeroth-order gradient descent method considers the minimization of a deterministic function when only function evaluations are available.
A seminal contribution in this direction is by
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
, established convergence guarantees of random zeroth-order gradient descent with the Gaussian smoothing.
Their method achieves convergence rates comparable to first-order methods up to a dimension-dependent factor and includes an accelerated variant for convex objectives.

Subsequent works have extended the analysis to various settings. For example,
Ghadimi & Lan, 2013
studied deterministic and stochastic first- and zeroth-order methods for nonconvex optimization, highlighting the effectiveness of multi-point gradient approximations.

Ye et al., 2025b
proposed Hessian-aware zeroth-order optimization which exploits the second order information to achieve faster convergence rate.
However, all these works focus on the convergence analysis holding in expectation.

Recently,
Ye et al., 2025a
showed that the random zeroth-order optimization with proper search direction and multi-point feedbacks can converge in high probability.
Their work relied a large batch of feedbacks to guarantee a decay holding with a high probability for each iteration.
Thus,
Ye et al., 2025a
can only achieve a query complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(d+\log(1/\delta))L}{\mu}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
.

Online Convex Optimization (OCO) with Bandit Feedback.

In the online setting, zeroth-order methods have been extensively applied to bandit convex optimization.

Flaxman et al., 2005
introduced single-point gradient estimators, providing expected regret bounds for convex losses when only function evaluations are observed.
Later works obtained sharper regret bounds for bandit convex optimization using two-point (multi-point) feedback
(
Shamir, 2017
;
Agarwal et al., 2010
)
.
Recently,
Yu et al., 2026
showed that OCO with two-point feedback can achieve the optimal regret holding in expectation, which is optimal in both the time zone and the dimension for the strongly convex function.
Almost the same time,
Ye, 2026
obtain the same result holding with a high probability.
Through the online to batch conversion technique
(
Kakade & Tewari, 2008
)
, the result of
Ye, 2026
can directly derive that random zeroth-order stochastic gradient descent can achieve a
\mathcal{O}\left(d\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon\right)
query complexity holding with high probability.
However, in the OCO setting, a bounded stochastic gradient is necessary to achieve the optimal regret.
In contrast, our analysis does
not
require the bounded stochastic gradient.

(Zeroth-order) Stochastic Gradient Descent.

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is another closely related line of work.
The classical stochastic approximation framework dates back to
Robbins & Monro, 1951
, who introduced an iterative method for solving stochastic root-finding problems.
For strongly convex objectives with bounded stochastic gradient, SGD-type methods can achieve the optimal
O(1/T)
convergence rate after
T
stochastic gradient queries holding with high probability
(
Rakhlin et al., 2012
)
.
When the function is additionally smooth and the noise has bounded variance, then SGD-type methods can achieve the optimal
O(1/T)
rate holding in expectation
(
Bottou et al., 2018
)
.
However, without a bounded-gradient assumption, it becomes substantially more challenging to achieve the optimal rate holding with high probability.
More recently,
Liu & Zhou, 2023
developed a general framework for high-probability convergence of stochastic gradient methods under subgaussian noise, achieving an
\mathcal{O}(\log T/T)
convergence rate without the unbounded gradient assumption.

Stochastic zeroth-order optimization can be viewed as a derivative-free counterpart of stochastic gradient methods.
Modern stochastic zeroth-order methods typically use randomized finite-difference or smoothing-based gradient estimators.

Duchi et al., 2015
analyzed one- and two-point zeroth-order schemes and showed that two function evaluations can lead to nearly optimal rates up to dimension-dependent factors.

Shamir, 2017
gave a simple optimal algorithm for bandit and zeroth-order convex optimization with two-point feedback.

Ghadimi & Lan, 2013
studied stochastic first- and zeroth-order methods for nonconvex stochastic programming and derived complexity bounds for finding approximate stationary points, together with improved large-deviation guarantees through post-optimization procedures.
More recently, when the objective function is smooth and strongly convex, several works showed that stochastic zeroth-order methods can achieve an expected query complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(d/\varepsilon\right)

(
Malladi et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2024
)
.

Assumptions and Preliminaries

2.1
Assumptions

First, we introduce the first assumption that is widely used in the convergence analysis.

Assumption 1
.

The function
f(\bm{x})
is
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex. That is, for any
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

\displaystyle f(\bm{y})\leq f(\bm{x})+\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|^{2},

(2.1)

\displaystyle f(\bm{y})\geq f(\bm{x})+\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|^{2}.

(2.2)

When the function
f(\bm{x})
can be represented as Eq. (
1.2
), we will introduce the following two extra assumptions.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that the stochastic gradient
\nabla f(\bm{x};\;\xi)
is an unbiased estimation of
\nabla f(\bm{x})
, that is,
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\nabla f(\bm{x};\;\xi)]=\nabla f(\bm{x})
.

Assumption 3

(Bounded Noise Assumption)
.

We also assume that with a probability
1
, it holds that

\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})-\nabla f(\bm{x};\;\xi)\right\|\leq\sigma,\mbox{ with }0<\sigma.

Assumption
2
is the standard assumption in the analysis of stochastic gradient descent.
Assumption
3
is also widely used
(
Zhang et al., 2020
)
.
A more popular assumption is the bounded variance assumption
(
Bottou et al., 2018
)
.
However, based on this assumption, we can only achieve the optimal convergence rate holding in expectation
(
Bottou et al., 2018
;
Liu & Zhou, 2023
)
.
Furthermore, if the objective function
f(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(\bm{x})
, the bounded variance assumption, that is,
\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma_{1}^{2}
, will directly imply that Assumption
3
hold.
This can be obtained as

\displaystyle\max_{i}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}=n\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq n\sigma_{1}^{2}.

Furthermore, if the noise only satisfies the bounded variance, without the extra assumption of the bounded gradient, one can
not
achieve a high probability convergence analysis to my best knowledge.
In this work, we do
not
require the bounded gradient assumption, thus, we need a more strict assumption on the noise.

Liu & Zhou, 2023
assume that the noise is subgaussian and achieve a convergence rate
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T/T\right)
when the iteration number
T
is unknown.
In this paper, we also consider the case that
T
is unknown.
Our bounded noise assumption is a little more strict than the subgaussian assumption.
In fact, our convergence analysis can be easily extend to the case of subgaussian noise.
A direct extension can be achieved using the fact that the subgaussian noise will imply Assumption
3
holds with a high probability at least
1-\delta
.
This will only introduce an extra
\log(T/\delta)
times queries.
However, our work mainly focuses on the high probability convergence of
zeroth-order
SGD.
The bounded noise assumption will help us to develop techniques to achieve high probability convergence analysis of zeroth-order SGD.

Preliminaries

In this section, we will introduce how to construct the (stochastic) gradient by two-point function queries and list the detailed algorithm description of zeroth-order gradient descent and zeroth-order stochastic gradient descent.

In the construction of gradient estimation, we will first generate a random vector
\bm{u}
of
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution. Then, we access the function at point
\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{u}
and
\bm{x}-\alpha\bm{u}
where
\alpha>0
is the smooth parameter. After obtaining two function values
f(\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{u})
and
f(\bm{x}-\alpha\bm{u})
, we can construct the approximate gradient as follows:

\bm{g}(\bm{x})=\frac{f(\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{u})-f(\bm{x}-\alpha\bm{u})}{2\alpha}\bm{u}.

(2.4)

Similar for the stochastic function, we instead obtain the function value
f(\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{u},\;\xi)
and
f(\bm{x}-\alpha\bm{u},\;\xi)
and
construct the approximate stochastic gradient at
\bm{x}
as follows:

\bm{g}(\bm{x};\;\xi)=\frac{f(\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{u};\;\xi)-f(\bm{x}-\alpha\bm{u};\;\xi)}{2\alpha}\bm{u}.

(2.5)

Once we have the (stochastic) gradient estimation, we can conduct the (stochastic) gradient descent.
The detailed algorithm descriptions are listed in Algorithm

.

Wang et al., 2024

holding in expectation.
Our complexity has extra

holding for each iteration.
The extra price for a high probability rate is very small which is up to

Remark 3
.

In Lemma

, we assume that
\{\bm{x}_{t}\}
generated by Algorithm
1
satisfies
\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|\neq 0
.
This is a reasonable assumption.
When
f(\bm{x})
is strongly convex, there is only one optimal point for
f(\bm{x})
.
At the same time,
\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|=0
implies that
\bm{x}_{t}
is the optimal point.
For a random algorithm, the probability to find an optimal point of zero measurement is also zero.

Remark 4
.

Lemma
2
is the key to our high-probability convergence of random zero-order gradient descent.
Instead of analyzing how the function value will decay after one-step, we consider the function value decay after
T
-step updates.
If we analyze the one-step update, with a proper step size, we can obtain that

f(\bm{x}_{t+1})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\leq f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}^{*})-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}))^{2}}{dL}\right).

(4.4)

Furthermore, the value
(\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}))^{2}\sim\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}\cdot\chi^{2}(1)
will dominate the convergence property.
Unfortunately, for a one-step update, we can
not
lower bound the value of
(\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}))^{2}
with a high probability since the lower bound of
\chi^{2}(1)
can not hold with a high probability.
The common way to overcome this dilemma is taking expectation since
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}(1)]=1
.
This is the reason why the convergence analysis of random zeroth-order algorithms commonly holds in expectation.
Instead, Lemma
2
analyze function value decay after
T
-step updates to overcome the problem that one can not lower bound
\chi^{2}(1)
with high probability.

Remark 5
.

We take a step size
\eta_{t}=\Omega(\frac{1}{\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2}L})
in Lemma

\Omega(\frac{1}{dL})
in the popular convergence analysis of random zeroth-order gradient descent

This is also important in our convergence analysis.
By this step size, we have

By this nice property and well-known results of beta distribution, we can obtain our high probability convergence analysis.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied high-probability convergence guarantees for zeroth-order gradient descent methods for smooth and strongly convex optimization. Unlike most classical analyses, which establish convergence only in expectation, our results provide explicit confidence guarantees for the performance of random zeroth-order algorithms.

For deterministic objectives, we proved that random zeroth-order gradient descent achieves an
\varepsilon
-suboptimal solution with probability at least
1-\delta
using

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)

function queries. This matches the classical expectation-based query complexity up to only an additive logarithmic dependence on the confidence parameter. In particular, the result shows that the standard two-query random zeroth-order method can provide strong high-probability guarantees without requiring large batches of function evaluations at each iteration.

We further considered stochastic objectives, where both randomized directional sampling and stochastic function evaluations introduce additional uncertainty. Under a bounded-noise assumption, and without imposing a uniformly bounded stochastic-gradient condition, we established a high-probability query complexity of

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d\log(1/\varepsilon)\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta)\right)}{\varepsilon}\right).

This result gives a high-confidence analogue of the standard expected convergence guarantees for stochastic zeroth-order methods, with only additional logarithmic factors in the accuracy and confidence parameters.

Overall, our analysis demonstrates that random zeroth-order gradient descent can achieve reliable high-probability performance guarantees in both deterministic and stochastic smooth strongly convex optimization. These results narrow the gap between expectation-based and high-probability theory for zeroth-order methods, and suggest several directions for future work, including improving the logarithmic factors in the stochastic setting, extending the analysis to weaker noise assumptions, and developing high-probability guarantees for broader classes of nonconvex or nonsmooth zeroth-order problems.

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq n\leq N}Z_{n}\geq y\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z_{0}]}{y}=\frac{1}{y}.

Lemma 21

(Freedman’s inequality)
.

Let
\{Y_{t}:t\geq 0\}
be a real-valued martingale adapted to a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
, with
Y_{-1}=0
. Let
Z_{t}=Y_{t}-Y_{t-1}
denote the martingale difference sequence. Assume the sequence is uniformly bounded from above:

Z_{t}\leq R\quad\text{a.s. for all }t\geq 0.

Define the predictable quadratic variation process

as:

W_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[Z_{j}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{j-1}].

Then, for any

and
\sigma^{2}>0

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists t\leq T:Y_{t}\geq\tau\text{ and }W_{t}\leq\sigma^{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+R\tau)}\right).

Lemma 22

(Conditional Hoeffding’s lemma)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a sub-
\sigma
-field. Suppose
X
is an integrable random variable such that

\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]=0,

and there exists a nonnegative
\mathcal{G}
-measurable random variable
c
such that

|X|\leq c\qquad\text{a.s.}

Then for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda X}\mid\mathcal{G}\right]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}c^{2}}{2}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.}

Proof.

Since
|X|\leq c
, we have
-c\leq X\leq c
almost surely. By convexity of
x\mapsto e^{\lambda x}
,
for every
x\in[-c,c]
,

e^{\lambda x}\leq\frac{c-x}{2c}e^{-\lambda c}+\frac{c+x}{2c}e^{\lambda c}.

Applying this with
x=X
and taking conditional expectation given
\mathcal{G}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}\mid\mathcal{G}]\leq\frac{e^{-\lambda c}}{2c}\mathbb{E}[c-X\mid\mathcal{G}]+\frac{e^{\lambda c}}{2c}\mathbb{E}[c+X\mid\mathcal{G}].

Because
c
is
\mathcal{G}
-measurable and
\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]=0
,

\mathbb{E}[c-X\mid\mathcal{G}]=c,\qquad\mathbb{E}[c+X\mid\mathcal{G}]=c.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}\mid\mathcal{G}]\leq\frac{e^{-\lambda c}+e^{\lambda c}}{2}=\cosh(\lambda c).

Using
\cosh(u)\leq e^{u^{2}/2}
for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}\mid\mathcal{G}]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}c^{2}}{2}\right).

∎

Lemma 23
.

Letting
T_{0}>1
,
T_{k}=k+T_{0}
with
k\geq 0
, and
K\geq k+1
, then we have the following results:

Appendix B
Properties of Beta Distribution

Lemma 24
.

Let
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{d})
be a
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector, and let
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed constant vector of unit length (
\|\bm{a}\|_{2}=1
). Then, the random variable defined by the squared normalized inner product,

Y=\frac{\langle\bm{x},\bm{a}\rangle^{2}}{\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}},

follows a Beta distribution with parameters
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta=\frac{d-1}{2}
. That is,
Y\sim\text{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)
.

Proof.

Because
\bm{a}
is a unit vector, we can construct an orthogonal matrix
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that its first row is
\bm{a}^{\top}
. Equivalently, this means
\bm{Q}\bm{a}=\bm{e}_{1}
, where
\bm{e}_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)^{\top}
is the first standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let us define a newly rotated random vector
\bm{z}=\bm{Q}\bm{x}
. Because the standard multivariate Gaussian distribution is invariant under orthogonal transformations, the transformed vector
\bm{z}
is also a standard Gaussian random vector, i.e.,
\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{d})
. Consequently, its components
z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{d}
are independent and identically distributed standard normal random variables,
z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

We can now express both the numerator and the denominator of
Y
in terms of
z
. For the numerator, we use the orthogonal invariance of the inner product:

\langle\bm{x},\bm{a}\rangle=\langle\bm{Q}^{\top}\bm{z},\bm{a}\rangle=\langle\bm{z},\bm{Q}\bm{a}\rangle=\langle\bm{z},\bm{e}_{1}\rangle=z_{1}.

Thus, the numerator is simply
z_{1}^{2}
.

For the denominator, the Euclidean norm is preserved under orthogonal transformations:

\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{Q}^{\top}\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}.

Substituting these into the definition of
Y
, we obtain:

Y=\frac{z_{1}^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}}=\frac{z_{1}^{2}}{z_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}z_{i}^{2}}.

Let
U=z_{1}^{2}
and
V=\sum_{i=2}^{d}z_{i}^{2}
. We analyze the distributions of
U
and
V
:

•

Since
z_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, its square follows a chi-squared distribution with
1
degree of freedom:
U\sim\chi^{2}_{1}
.

•

Since
z_{2},\dots,z_{d}
are
d-1
independent standard normal variables, the sum of their squares follows a chi-squared distribution with
d-1
degrees of freedom:
V\sim\chi^{2}_{d-1}
.

•

Because the components
z_{i}
are mutually independent,
U
and
V
are strictly independent.

A fundamental property of the Beta distribution states that if
U\sim\chi^{2}_{\nu_{1}}
and
V\sim\chi^{2}_{\nu_{2}}
are independent random variables, the ratio
\frac{U}{U+V}
follows a Beta distribution with parameters
\frac{\nu_{1}}{2}
and
\frac{\nu_{2}}{2}
.
Applying this property with
\nu_{1}=1
and
\nu_{2}=d-1
, we conclude that:

Y=\frac{U}{U+V}\sim\text{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right).

∎

Lemma 25

(Moments of the Beta distribution)
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
with parameters
\alpha,\beta>0
.
Then the mean and variance of
X
are

\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\mathrm{Var}(X)=\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)}.

Moreover, for every integer
m\geq 1
, the
m
-th raw moment is

\mathbb{E}[X^{m}]=\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{\alpha+k}{\alpha+\beta+k}.

Lemma 28
.

with
\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\neq 0

\{\bm{u}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

\bm{x}_{t}

{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}

{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t}
is the natural filtration on the
\sigma

{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t}:=\sigma(\bm{u}_{0},\ldots,\bm{u}_{t})

\delta\in(0,1)

1-\delta
, it holds that

\sum_{t=k+1}^{T-1}X_{t}\;\geq\;\frac{W_{k}}{d}-\frac{2\sqrt{2Q_{k}\log(1/\delta)}+2w_{k+1}\log(1/\delta)}{d}.

Z_{t}:=\frac{w_{t}}{d}-X_{t}=w_{t}\left(\frac{1}{d}-\zeta_{t}\right),\qquad Y_{s}^{(k)}:=\sum_{t=k+1}^{s}Z_{t},\qquad s\in\{k+1,\dots,T-1\}.

\bm{u}_{t}
is independent of
{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}

24

25

\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{t}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}]=\frac{1}{d},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\zeta_{t}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1})=\frac{2(d-1)}{d^{2}(d+2)}\leq\frac{2}{d^{2}}.

Accordingly,

\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}]=0,\qquad\mathrm{Var}(Z_{t}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1})=w_{t}^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\zeta_{t}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1})\leq\frac{2w_{t}^{2}}{d^{2}}.

\{Y_{s}^{(k)}\}_{s=k+1}^{T-1}
is a martingale.

Also, since

Z_{t}=w_{t}\left(\frac{1}{d}-\zeta_{t}\right)\leq\frac{w_{t}}{d}\leq\frac{w_{k+1}}{d},\qquad\forall\,t\in\{k+1,\dots,T-1\},

and

On the other hand,

Appendix E
Uniform lower bound for all suffix sums

Lemma 29

(Maximal Bernstein Inequality for Martingales)
.

Let
\{D_{t},{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t}\}
be a martingale difference sequence. Suppose there exist constants
v>0
and
b>0
such that for all
m\geq 2
, the conditional moments satisfy the Bernstein condition:

\mathbb{E}[|D_{t}|^{m}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}]\leq\frac{m!}{2}v^{2}b^{m-2}.

Let
M_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}
. Then for any
x>0
and integer
N>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq n\leq N}M_{n}\geq x\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2(Nv^{2}+bx)}\right).

Proof.

For any fixed
\lambda\in(0,1/b)
, we examine the conditional expectation of
e^{\lambda D_{t}}
. By Taylor expansion:

e^{\lambda D_{t}}=1+\lambda D_{t}+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{m}D_{t}^{m}}{m!}

Taking the conditional expectation on both sides and because
D_{t}
is a martingale difference,
\mathbb{E}[D_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=0
, we can obtain that

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 1+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{m}}{m!}\mathbb{E}[|D_{t}|^{m}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}].

Substituting the Bernstein moment condition
\mathbb{E}[|D_{t}|^{m}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq\frac{m!}{2}v^{2}b^{m-2}
into above equation, we can obtain that

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 1+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{m}}{m!}\left(\frac{m!}{2}v^{2}b^{m-2}\right)=1+\frac{\lambda^{2}v^{2}}{2}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}(\lambda b)^{m-2}.

Since
\lambda<1/b
, the term
\lambda b<1
, we can obtain that

\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}(\lambda b)^{m-2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\lambda b)^{k}=\frac{1}{1-\lambda b}.

Thus, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 1+\frac{\lambda^{2}v^{2}}{2(1-\lambda b)}

Finally, using the elementary inequality
1+y\leq e^{y}
, we bound the conditional moment generating function as follows:

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq\exp(g(\lambda))\quad\text{where}\quad g(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}v^{2}}{2(1-\lambda b)}

(E.1)

We define a new stochastic process
Z_{n}
:

Z_{n}=\exp\left(\lambda M_{n}-n\cdot g(\lambda)\right).

By the definition
M_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda M_{n-1}+\lambda D_{n}-n\cdot g(\lambda)\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\lambda M_{n-1}-n\cdot g(\lambda)\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{n}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:exp_up}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\exp\left(\lambda M_{n-1}-n\cdot g(\lambda)\right)\cdot e^{g(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\lambda M_{n-1}-(n-1)\cdot g(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z_{n-1}.

Because
Z_{n}\geq 0
and
\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]\leq Z_{n-1}
, the sequence
\{Z_{n}\}
is a non-negative supermartingale. Note that
Z_{0}=\exp(0)=1
.

We want to find the probability that
\max_{1\leq n\leq N}M_{n}\geq x
. Suppose this event happens. Then for some
n\leq N
,
M_{n}\geq x
.
If
M_{n}\geq x
, then because
g(\lambda)>0
and
n\leq N
, we can deduce that

\lambda M_{n}-n\cdot g(\lambda)\geq\lambda x-n\cdot g(\lambda)\geq\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda).

Therefore, the supermartingale
Z_{n}
satisfies that

Z_{n}\geq\exp\left(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda)\right),

which implies the logical inclusion of events:

\left\{\max_{1\leq n\leq N}M_{n}\geq x\right\}\subseteq\left\{\max_{1\leq n\leq N}Z_{n}\geq\exp\left(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda)\right)\right\}.

By Ville’s inequality (Lemma
20
) with threshold
y=\exp(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda))
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq n\leq N}M_{n}\geq x\right)\leq\frac{1}{\exp(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda))}=\exp\left(-(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda))\right),

(E.2)

where the inequality is also because of
Z_{0}=\exp(0)=1
.

Setting
\lambda=\frac{x}{Nv^{2}+bx}

which satisfies
\lambda b=\frac{bx}{Nv^{2}+bx}<1
and fulfill
\lambda\in(0,1/b)
, and plugging this
\lambda
into Eq. (
E.2
), we can obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq n\leq N}M_{n}\geq x\right)\leq\exp\left(-(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda))\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x^{2}}{Nv^{2}+bx}-\frac{x^{2}}{2(Nv^{2}+bx)}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2(Nv^{2}+bx)}\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Next we bound
M_{j,n}
uniformly over
n\in I_{j}
. Define the shifted filtration

\mathcal{G}_{r}:={{\mathcal{F}}}_{a_{j}+r},\qquad r\geq 0,

and the increments

D_{r}:=-Z_{a_{j}+r-1}=X_{a_{j}+r-1}-\frac{1}{d},\qquad r\geq 1.

Then
\{D_{r},\mathcal{G}_{r}\}
is a martingale difference sequence.

Instead of using Freedman’s inequality to bound
M_{j,n}
, we resort to the maximal Bernstein’s inequality for martingale (Lemma
29
).
First, we verify the Bernstein moment condition. For every integer
m\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{m}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}]=\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\frac{2r+1}{d+2r}\leq\frac{(2m-1)!!}{d^{m}}\leq m!\left(\frac{2}{d}\right)^{m},

where the last inequality is because of the fact

(2m-1)!!=\frac{(2m)!}{2^{m}m!}\leq 2^{m}m!.

Hence, for every
m\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|X_{t}-\frac{1}{d}\right|^{m}\middle|{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}\right]\leq 2^{m-1}\left(\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{m}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}]+\frac{1}{d^{m}}\right)\leq m!\left(\frac{4}{d}\right)^{m}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[|D_{r}|^{m}\mid\mathcal{G}_{r-1}]\leq\frac{m!}{2}\cdot\frac{32}{d^{2}}\left(\frac{4}{d}\right)^{m-2}.

So the Bernstein condition holds with

v^{2}=\frac{32}{d^{2}},\qquad b=\frac{4}{d}.

Now apply the maximal Bernstein inequality (Lemma
29
) to the partial sums of
\{D_{r},\mathcal{G}_{r}\}
with horizon
N_{j}
and we can obtain that, with probability at least
1-\delta_{j}
,

\max_{n\in I_{j}}M_{j,n}\leq\frac{8\sqrt{N_{j}\log(1/\delta_{j})}}{d}+\frac{8}{d}\log\frac{1}{\delta_{j}}\leq\frac{3N_{j}}{8d}+\frac{152}{3d}\log\frac{1}{\delta_{j}}.

(E.8)

By the union bound, with probability at least
1-2\delta_{j}
, both (
E.7
) and (
E.8
) hold. On this event, for every
n\in I_{j}
,

S_{n}=W_{j}+M_{j,n}\leq\frac{N_{j}}{2d}+\frac{67}{d}\log\frac{1}{\delta_{j}}.

Combining
n\geq N_{j}
(by Eq. (
E.5
)), we have

S_{n}\leq\frac{n}{2d}+\frac{67}{d}\log\frac{1}{\delta_{j}}.

Recalling (
E.4
), we conclude that

\sum_{t=T-n}^{T-1}X_{t}\geq\frac{n}{2d}-\frac{67}{d}\log\frac{1}{\delta_{j}},\qquad n\in I_{j}.

(E.9)

Finally, choosing
\delta_{j}=\frac{3\delta}{\pi^{2}j^{2}}
, it holds that

\sum_{j\geq 1}2\delta_{j}=\frac{6\delta}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{j^{2}}=\delta.

Hence, with probability at least
1-\delta
, inequality (
E.9
) holds for every block
I_{j}
, and therefore for every
n\in\{1,\dots,T-1\}
.

If
n\in I_{j}
, then
N_{j}\leq n
(by Eq. (
E.5
)), that is,
2^{j-1}\leq n
, then we can obtain that

j\leq 1+\log_{2}n.

(E.10)

Thus

\log\frac{1}{\delta_{j}}=\log\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3\delta}\Bigr)+2\log j\leq\log\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3\delta}\Bigr)+2\log\!\bigl(1+\log_{2}n\bigr).

Substituting into (
E.9
), we obtain

\sum_{t=T-n}^{T-1}X_{t}\geq\frac{n}{2d}-\frac{67}{d}\left[\log\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3\delta}\Bigr)+2\log\!\bigl(1+\log_{2}n\bigr)\right].

Note that for every
n\geq 1
,

2\log\!\bigl(1+\log_{2}n\bigr)\leq 2\log\!\bigl(2\log_{2}(2n)\bigr)\leq 4+2\log\log(2n).

Hence

\log\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3\delta}\Bigr)+2\log\!\bigl(1+\log_{2}n\bigr)\leq 4+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+2\log\log(2n).

Then, we obtain

Setting

, we can prove the result in (
E.3
).

\displaystyle|Y_{k}|\leq\frac{\rho_{K,k}}{T_{k}}\cdot\frac{|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})^{\top}\bm{u}_{k}|\cdot|\bm{u}_{k}^{\top}\bm{e}_{k}|}{\|\bm{u}_{k}\|^{2}}\leq\frac{|\rho_{K,k}|}{T_{k}}\cdot\frac{|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})^{\top}\bm{u}_{k}|}{\|\bm{u}_{k}\|}\cdot\sigma.

Moreover, by the derivation of
\rho_{K,k}
in the proof of Lemma
8
, we have that
\rho_{K,k}
is measurable with respect to

\mathcal{G}_{k}
.

Define

S_{K-1}:=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}Y_{k},\qquad c_{k}:=\frac{|\rho_{K,k}|}{T_{k}}\cdot\frac{|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})^{\top}\bm{u}_{k}|}{\|\bm{u}_{k}\|}\cdot\sigma,\qquad V_{K}:=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}c_{k}^{2}.

As shown above,
\{Y_{k}\}
is a martingale difference sequence conditional on

\{\bm{u}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
, and
|Y_{k}|\leq c_{k}
almost surely. Therefore, by the
conditional Hoeffding lemma (Lemma
22
), for every deterministic
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\lambda Y_{k}-\frac{\lambda^{2}c_{k}^{2}}{2}\right)\;\middle|\;\mathcal{\mathcal{G}}_{k-1},\bm{u}_{0},\dots,\bm{u}_{k}\right]\leq 1.

Iterating this bound and using the tower property yields

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\lambda S_{K-1}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}V_{K}\right)\;\middle|\;\bm{u}_{0},\dots,\bm{u}_{K-1}\right]\leq 1.

Now let
\tau>0
and
v>0
be deterministic. On the event

\{S_{K-1}\geq\tau,\ V_{K}\leq v\}
, we have

\lambda S_{K-1}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}V_{K}\geq\lambda\tau-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}v.

Hence, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left(S_{K-1}\geq\tau,\ V_{K}\leq v\;\middle|\;\bm{u}_{0},\dots,\bm{u}_{K-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\left(\exp\!\left(\lambda S_{K-1}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}V_{K}\right)\geq\exp\!\left(\lambda\tau-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}v\right)\;\middle|\;\bm{u}_{0},\dots,\bm{u}_{K-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\!\left(-\lambda\tau+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}v\right)\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\lambda S_{K-1}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}V_{K}\right)\;\middle|\;\bm{u}_{0},\dots,\bm{u}_{K-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\!\left(-\lambda\tau+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}v\right).

Since
v
is deterministic, we may optimize over
\lambda>0
by choosing
\lambda=\tau/v
which gives

\Pr\!\left(S_{K-1}\geq\tau,\ V_{K}\leq v\;\middle|\;\bm{u}_{0},\dots,\bm{u}_{K-1}\right)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2v}\right).

Setting
\tau=\sqrt{2v\log\frac{1}{\delta_{K}^{(Y)}}}
, we have

\Pr\!\left(S_{K-1}\geq\sqrt{2v\log\frac{1}{\delta_{K}^{(Y)}}},\ V_{K}\leq v\;\middle|\;\bm{u}_{0},\dots,\bm{u}_{K-1}\right)\leq\delta_{K}^{(Y)}.

Equivalently, with conditional probability at least
1-\delta_{K}^{(Y)}
,

S_{K-1}\leq\sqrt{2v\log\frac{1}{\delta_{K}^{(Y)}}}\qquad\text{on the event }\{V_{K}\leq v\}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
11
.

Since
f
is
L
-smooth, we have
\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|^{2}\leq 2L\Delta_{k}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(\frac{|\rho_{K,k}|\cdot|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})^{\top}\bm{u}_{k}|}{T_{k}}\cdot\sigma\right)^{2}=\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\rho_{K,k}^{2}}{T_{k}^{2}}\bigl(\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})^{\top}\bm{u}_{k}\bigr)^{2}\leq 2L\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{\rho_{K,k}^{2}}{T_{k}^{2}}\Delta_{k}\,z_{k}^{2},

where

z_{k}:=\frac{\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})^{\top}\bm{u}_{k}}{\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|}.

Because random variables
\bm{u}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
are drawn independently and independent of
\bm{x}_{k}
, we have
z_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Since
\Delta_{k}\leq\bar{\Delta}_{k}
,
we have

w_{k}\leq\bar{w}_{k}:=\frac{\rho_{K,k}^{2}}{T_{k}^{2}}\bar{\Delta}_{k}.

Hence,

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(\frac{|\rho_{K,k}|\cdot|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})^{\top}\bm{u}_{k}|}{T_{k}}\cdot\sigma\right)^{2}\leq 2L\sigma^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\bar{w}_{k}z_{k}^{2}.

Since the constants
\bar{w}_{k}
are unconditionally deterministic, and the sequence
z_{k}
are mutually independent standard Gaussians, we can apply the standard Laurent-Massart bound (Lemma
18
) to obtain that with probability at least
1-\delta_{K}^{(V)}
,

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\bar{w}_{k}z_{k}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\bar{w}_{k}+2\sqrt{\bar{Q}_{K-1}\log\frac{1}{\delta_{K}^{(V)}}}+2\bar{M}_{K-1}\log\frac{1}{\delta_{K}^{(V)}}.

Since
\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\bar{w}_{k}=\bar{W}_{K-1}
,
multiplying both sides by
2L\sigma^{2}
yields Eq. (
5.12
).
∎

Lemma 31

Letting Lemma
