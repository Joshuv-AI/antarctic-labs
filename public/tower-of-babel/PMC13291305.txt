An analysis of succinate dehydrogenase B in pleural mesothelioma
Authors: Cormac J. Jennings, Stefan J. Marciniak, Giulia Pinton, Doris M. Rassl, Laura Moro, Kenneth J. O’Byrne, Robert C. Rintoul, Stephen P. Finn, Luciano Mutti, Warren Thomas, Steven G. Gray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo investigate the expression of succinate dehydrogenase B and its potential as a therapeutic target in pleural mesothelioma (PM).MethodsThe expression of succinate dehydrogenase B was examined in cell lines using PCR. The expression of succinate dehydrogenase B mRNA was subsequently examined in a series of fresh-frozen primary patient samples using reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. The expression of succinate dehydrogenase B protein was examined by immunohistochemistry on a pleural mesothelioma tissue microarray derived from Formalin-Fixed and Paraffin-Embedded specimens to determine if succinate dehydrogenase B expression correlated with survival benefit. Using a resazurin-based assay we examined whether targeting the mitochondrial complex II (containing succinate dehydrogenase B) with a chemical inhibitor could have effects on cell proliferation.ResultsTotal ribonucleic acid was isolated from a panel of mesothelial-derived cell lines (both cancerous and non-cancerous), and expression of succinate dehydrogenase B messenger RNA was assessed by RT-PCR and found to be ubiquitously expressed. Total Ribonucleic acid was isolated from a panel of fresh-frozen surgical specimens, and succinate dehydrogenase B was significantly overexpressed in mesothelioma at the messenger RNA level. Immunohistochemical staining and analysis of a mesothelioma tissue microarray showed that expression did not correlate with any survival benefit, confirmed by in silico analyses of other datasets. However, links between DNA methylation at individual residues of the succinate dehydrogenase B gene and overall survival were identified, along with correlations to immune cell infiltrates. Targeting the mitochondrial complex II with oxaloacetic acid did not show any significant potential for higher efficacy in cell lines derived from malignant tumors compared to those derived from normal mesothelial cells.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate that succinate dehydrogenase B is overexpressed in PM but may not be a suitable candidate for therapeutic targeting in this disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04961-x.

Introduction

Pleural Mesothelioma (PM) is an aggressive inflammatory cancer, associated with prior exposure to asbestos [1]. The vast majority of patients present at an advanced stage, and as a consequence it has a median survival time of 6–12 months. Moreover, it is important to observe that in addition to exposure to man-made asbestos products, individuals can also be exposed to what are called Naturally Occurring Asbestos (NOA), and studies have clearly demonstrated an association between PM development and environmental asbestos (natural and non-natural) exposure [2, 3]. Conservative estimates suggest that the mortality rate of PM is in the order of 6.2 per million population [4], and it is estimated that 43,000 people die from this disease each year [5]. Many countries have banned the use of asbestos, and the incidence is falling in these countries [6]. The most recent epidemiological analyses suggest that while there is a moderate decline in overall incidence there remains a stable long-term burden, gender disparities, and socioeconomic influences [7]. However, in other countries such as Brazil, where asbestos consumption continues to increase, there is a 15–20-year lag in estimated peak PM mortality, and a recent study found that globally the numbers of PM cases have in fact slightly increased [8], and this may be due to a variety of factors [9].

Whilst there have been recent advances in the treatment of this disease, the current standard of care is either pemetrexed and platinum based chemotherapy [10], or more recently a combination of the two immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) ipilimumab and nivolumab [11]. Both regimens are non-curative and whilst survival outcomes have improved, there remains an urgent need to identify novel therapeutic avenues in this disease to improve patient outcomes.

Estrogen and the Estrogen Receptor Beta (ER-β) has previously been shown by us and others to be a potential therapeutic target and/or biomarker candidates in PM [12–15]. Initial analyses suggested that ER-β is an independent prognostic factor of better survival [16], and high to moderate expression of ERβ group with advanced clinical stage PM was associated with a tendency of higher overall survival (OS) and better response to chemotherapy treatment resulting in longer progression free survival (PFS). However, in contrast, the study from Helali et al. has not observed any prognostic value for ER-β in PM [17]. Nevertheless, agonists to ER-β sensitize PM cells to cisplatin [18]. However, the anti-tumor effects of anti-estrogen drugs (G15 and tamoxifen) on PM cells were found to be independent of ER-β [18, 19].

In particular high expression of ER-β was strongly associated with low expression of succinate dehydrogenase B (SDHB) (a subunit of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex II) [20]. Our study showed that knockdown of SDHB resulted in the induction of ER-β expression [20]. Furthermore, when ERβ was activated, mitochondrial complex II and IV activity was affected resulting in reduced mitochondrial ATP production, increased glycolysis dependence and impaired PM cellular proliferation [20].

Mutations within SDHB are frequently associated with hereditary paraganglioma-pheochromocytomas, a diverse group of renal cell carcinomas (RCC), and a specific subtype of gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST) [21], but recently roles for the loss of SDHB expression in RCC, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and Ovarian Cancer (OC) have been described [22–24]. It has been suggested that loss of SDHB activity results in major changes to metabolic pathways within cells creating metabolic vulnerabilities amenable to pharmacological intervention [25]. Moreover, loss or reduction of SDHB expression in cancer results in succinate accumulation which is transported to cytoplasm and subsequently secreted into the extracellular milieu [26]. This results in the activation of succinate receptor-1 (SUCNR1) promoting cancer angiogenesis and metastasis along with enhanced cancer cell migration [26]. Moreover, cancers such as paraganglioma-pheochromocytomas are known to be immunologically “cold” and intractable to ICI [27] and given our preliminary data suggests that SDHB expression may be associated with response to ICI in PM.

It has been suggested that SDHB is a candidate cancer target, and several studies have indicated that compounds that inhibit mitochondrial respiratory chain complex II have potential anti-cancer activity including compounds such as α-tocopheryl succinate (α-TOS) [28], or oxaloacetic acid [29, 30]. Our initial data suggest that SDHB may therefore play an important role in PM. However, it must be noted that other groups have not been able to show any change in SDHB expression in PM [31].

We therefore conducted an analysis of SDHB mRNA expression in a panel of mesothelioma cell lines, and fresh frozen tissue samples isolated at surgery. We also examined the expression of SDHB by immunohistochemistry in a tissue microarray of PM for potential survival benefit. In silico analyses of the TCGA-MESO database identified (a) links between SDHB mRNA expression and immune cell populations and (b) identified methylation of key CpGs within the SDHB gene associated with OS. Finally, we assessed the potential to therapeutically target SDHB in PM cells using succinate.

Benign specimens were acquired from patients never diagnosed with PM. Informed consent was obtained from each patient, and the study was conducted after formal approval from the relevant Hospital Ethics Committee (Leicestershire REC references 6742 and 6948). Samples consisted of the following: 5 benign lesions and 17 PM samples (epithelioid: n = 7; sarcomatoid: n = 4; biphasic: n = 6), and details are provided in Table 1. All research was conducted under the SJH/AMNCH research ethics committee approval (041017/8704).

Tissue microarray

Ethical approval for the use of samples and data collection was granted by the Royal Papworth Hospital Research Ethics Committee (REC reference 09/H0311/21). All cases of PM diagnosed at Papworth Hospital (Cambridge, UK) between 2005 and 2010 were identified from hospital records, and the Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biopsy tissue blocks were retrieved from the pathology department store along with their associated histology slides.

A tissue microarray containing one-hundred and thirty-five cases of mesothelioma was used in this study [32]. Sections of 3 µm were prepared and stained for SDHB.

Total RNA isolation and RT-PCR amplification

Total RNA was extracted using TRI reagent (Molecular Research Center, Montgomery Road, OH, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Prior to first strand cDNA synthesis, 1 µg of total RNA was pre-treated by digestion with RQ1 DNase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was generated using RevertAid (Thermo Scientific) and random hexamers (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

cDNAs were examined for the expression of SDHB and 18 S rRNA by RT-PCR, using primers as outlined in Table 3.

PCR cycling conditions consisted of 95 °C for 5 min followed by 35 cycles of 1 min at 95 °C, 1 min at 58 °C and 1 min at 72 °C with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

Products were electrophoresed on a 1% agarose gel. Product quantification was performed using TINA 2.09c (Raytest, Isotopenmeßgeräte GmbH, Straubenhardt, Germany) densitometry software. The mRNA expression of SDHB was normalized to 18 S rRNA controls, and was expressed as a ratio of SDHB expression: 18 S rRNA expression.

Drug treatment and cellular viability assay

Oxaloacetic acid was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA Cat# O7753), and dissolved in Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) at a final concentration of 1 M. Cells were serum starved (0.5% v/v FBS) for 24 h prior to addition of Oxaloacetic Acid, and incubated for a further 72 h. Cellular viability was assessed using a Resazurin Reduction Assay as described previously [45].

Immunohistochemistry

The primary antibody used in this study was: rabbit anti-SDHB 1: 500 (HPA002868; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Sections were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated in water. For antigen retrieval, heat induced epitope retrieval HIER was utilized. Briefly, slides were incubated in 10 mM citrate buffer (Ph 6.0) for 20 min, heat treated in a microwave for 2 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for thirty minutes. Staining was conducted with Vectastain ABC kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Non-specific binding was blocked for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with the anti-SDHB antibody at 4 °C. Following washing, the slides were incubated with Vectastain AB reagent for 30 min, washed and incubated with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (Cat# D5905; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and hydrogen peroxide. Sections were rinsed and counterstained in hematoxylin before dehydration, passage through xylene and mounting.

Detection with respective secondary antibodies was performed with Vectastain ABC (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) and DAB (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and hydrogen peroxide.

Immunohistochemical scoring

Staining was scored on an Olympus BX50 microscope using an Immuno-Reactive-Score (IRS) scoring system, giving a range of 0–12 based as a product of multiplication between positive cells proportion score (0–4) and staining intensity score (0–3) [46, 47].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Graphpad Prism 5.01 (Graphpad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed for survival curves, and statistical analysis was assessed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of surgery to death. Patients who were still alive or lost to follow-up, were treated as censored data in the survival analysis. Univariate analysis of OS was performed using Kaplan-Meier method.

Results

SDHB mRNA is ubiquitously expressed in mesothelioma cell lines

Utilizing RT-PCR, the levels of expression of SDHB mRNA were examined in a panel of cell lines including those derived from normal pleura (LP9 and Met5A) and PMs. SDHB mRNA was detectable in all cell lines as shown in Fig. 1A.

SDHB mRNA is overexpressed in PM

To assess the expression of SDHB in primary samples, RT-PCR was performed on a panel of benign pleura and PM tumor samples from patients (Fig. 1B). Densitometric analysis of the gels revealed a significant increase in the expression of SDHB in PM tumor samples compared with normal pleura (p = 0.0029) (Fig. 1C). When stratified by histology, significant overexpression of SDHB was observed in only the biphasic subtype (Fig. 1D) when compared against all other histologies using a one-way Anova (Tukey’s Multiple Comparison Test).

Finally, an examination of the TCGA-MESO dataset comprising (n = 87 PM) [48] suggests that overexpression of SDHB mRNA is rare (Fig. 1E).While in silico analyses of various other datasets suggest that there is no significant change in SDHB mRNA expression compared to normal lung/pleura (Supplementary Figure S1) [49], and in contrast to our results in the dataset from Lopez-Rios et al. [50], there is a trend towards increased expression of SDHB mRNA in the sarcomatoid subtype (p = 0.051) (Supplementary Figure S2).

Given the discrepancies between our data and the previous studies, and the observations seen for our histological subtypes we therefore re-examined the TCGA-MESO dataset with respect to histological subtype as shown in Fig. 2.

UALCAN [51] was used to assess SDHB mRNA expression stratified by histological subtype in the TCGA-MESO dataset. No significant differences between the histological subtypes were observed.

In this dataset, there were no significant differences across histological subtypes. Moreover, a query of the Beuno PM dataset (Bueno - Supplementary Tables 2 - Differentially expressed genes in sarcomatoid versus epithelioid clusters) [52], revealed that there was no significant difference in SDHB mRNA expression between sarcomatoid vs. epithelioid (unadjusted p-value = 0.06).

The differences observed between our analyses may simply reflect various differences such as (a) our analysis utilizes semi-quantitative densitometric analysis; (b) the probes used in the microarray-based studies of Lopez-Rios (Probe: 202675_at) and Gordon (214166_at) may differ; (c) the TCGA-MESO and Bueno datasets utilize NGS; or finally (d) differences in sample size.

SDHB is commonly mutated in pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas accounting for 20% of cases [53]. However, in PM the incidence of mutation is extremely rare with only a single mutation (R27G) found in one patient in a cohort of n = 53 cases [54]. In silico analyses using cBioPortal to include all existing datasets found no mutations with only one sample showing a deep deletion of SDHB in n = 123 samples (Supplementary Figure S3).

There are several common mutations associated with PM, including mutations in TP53 [55], BAP1 [56] and NF2 [57]. When examined, the expression of SDHB mRNA in the TCGA-MESO cohort [48] was found to be significantly upregulated in PM patients harboring a TP53 mutation (Wilcoxon p=1 × 10-4) as shown in Fig. 3. In contrast expression of SDHB mRNA was not significantly altered if either NF2 (Wilcoxon p = 0.57) or BAP1 (Wilcoxon p = 0.79) was mutated.

SDHB protein expression is not associated with patient survival

Previously published data by us has shown that SDHB protein is expressed in PM [20]. We subsequently made use of an existing tissue microarray of a separate series of 135 patients [32]. We subsequently stained this TMA for expression of SDHB. Unfortunately, due to TMA depletion and lack of patient clinical data it was subsequently determined that only (n = 61) patients had sufficient material/cores available for scoring. On analysis there was no significant association between SDHB expression and overall survival (Fig. 4). While this is not significant, the median survival for patients with an IRS score of ≤ 3 was 221.5 days, whereas the median survival of patients with an IRS score ≥ 4 was 493 days.

This result agrees with an in-silico analysis of SDHB mRNA in the TCGA-MESO dataset [48] as conducted using GEPIA2 [59] for both overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4 where there was also no significant OS or DFS benefit observed.

CpG methylation at the SDHB gene is associated with survival

An examination of SDHB mRNA expression using cBioPortal found an inverse association between SDHB mRNA expression and DNA methylation (Spearman R: −0.46; p = 7.844e-6) (Fig. 5A), suggesting that decreased SDHB mRNA expression may be associated with increased DNA methylation.

Analysis of the TCGA-MESO dataset [48] for promoter methylation reveals that methylation levels across all patients lie between the cut-offs considered to indicate either hyper-methylation [Beta value: 0.7−0.5] or hypo-methylation [Beta-value: 0.3−0.25] (Fig. 5B) as defined by UALCAN [51]. Interestingly, patients with TP53 mutations had lower promoter methylation at their promoter (Fig. 5C) and given that TP53 mutation also results in higher SDHB expression this suggests that potentially DNA methylation could be associated with the regulation of SDHB mRNA expression. To test for this possibility, we used RNA from the NCI-H28 cell line treated with Decitabine in our previous publication by Gray et al. [60]. To confirm reactivation of genes known to be silenced by methylation we first examined for reactivation of sFRP4 mRNA as shown in Fig. 5D. As sFRP4 mRNA is reactivated by treatment with Decitabine, we subsequently examined its effects on SDHB. However, in contrast to what might be expected, overall, we see that SDHB mRNA is decreased following treatments with a DNA hypomethylating agent (Fig. 5E). One of the limitations of the decitabine treatments is that SDHB mRNA is ubiquitously expressed across all cell lines examined including the benign pleural cell lines (LP9 and Met5A), whereas in contrast SDHB mRNA is not detectable in benign pleura from primary patients (Fig. 1B).

Correlation analysis between SDHB expression and tumor mutational burden

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) is widely considered to be a biomarker for predicting potential patient response to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [66]. Given that PM is largely considered to be a low-TMB tumor [67], it should not be expected to have predictive value, but recent reports suggest that TMB could be useful in the prediction of survival in resected patients [68, 69]. Using the methodology described by Feng and Shen [70], we examined for correlations between SDHB mRNA expression and the mRNA expression of a panel of genes associated with either the DNA Damage Response (DDR) pathway or the Mismatch Excision Repair (MMR) pathway as a proxy for TMB (Supplementary Table 1). Such an analysis represents a surrogate for TMB [70]. Whilst some individual genes were found to have some significant correlations, overall, the gene signatures for DDR, MMR or the combined DDR/MMR signatures were non-significant.

Whilst this represents a proxy for assessing the effect of SDHB mRNA expression on TMB, we subsequently reassessed the TCGA datasets for whether SDHB mRNA expression is associated with TMB using functions in UCSCXenaShiny [71] and cBioPortal [61]. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6 A TMB is not significantly associated with SDHB mRNA expression in PM (for either synonymous or synonymous mutations), as reflected in the cBioPortal data (Supplementary Fig. 6B, p = 0.527). Taken together, these results suggest that TMB is not associated with SDHB mRNA expression particularly in PM, and mirrors the low TMB association observed in PM [72].

Key miRNAs known to target SDHB may have prognostic value in PM

A number of miRNAs have been described as functionally targeting SDHB in other cancers and disease settings. These include hsa-miR-101-3p (also known as hsa-mir-101-2) [73], hsa-miR-96-3p [74, 75] and hsa-miR-142-5p [76]. Other miRNAs which have been associated just with SDHB mRNA expression include hsa-miR183 [75, 77] and hsa-miR-210 [75, 78]. Given the altered expression of SDHB in PM we examined if there were any associations between these miRNAs in the TCGA-MESO dataset using UALCAN [51]. While it is not possible to compare normal pleura to PM in this dataset, no significant changes to expression were seen when the levels of individual miRNAs were compared across patient stages. Survival data linked to these miRNAs was also examined and whilst no significant survival benefit in PM was found for hsa-miR-96-3p, hsa-miR-142-5p, hsa-miR-183 and hsa-miR-210, high expression of hsa-miR-101-3p was shown to have a significant OS benefit (p = 0.001; Fig. 8A) which was independent of sex (Fig. 8B; p = 0.027).

Interestingly Yuan et al., have recently described how a circRNA (circ0515) stabilizes SDHB mRNA in lung cancer through interactions with RNA binding motif protein 45 (RBM45) [79]. The authors found a significant positive correlation between RBM45 and SDHB mRNA in the TCGA- adenocarcinoma (LUAD) dataset. However, when we tested for this in the TCGA-MESO dataset no correlation was observed, suggesting that this circRNA does not play a role in regulating or stabilizing SDHB mRNA in PM (Supplementary Figure S5).

Effects of inhibition of mitochondrial complex II on mesothelioma cellular proliferation

It has previously been shown that oxaloacetic acid is both a potent inhibitor of mitochondrial complex II [30], and has potential anti-cancer activity in cells derived from lung adenocarcinoma [29]. As SDHB is a central component of this complex, we therefore examined the effect of oxaloacetic acid on both normal mesothelial cells (LP9) and mesothelioma cell lines (REN and NCI-H226). Oxaloacetic acid was able to induce significant effects on cellular proliferation at lower concentrations in the malignant PM cell lines compared to the normal mesothelial cell line (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Previous work by us has shown that ER-β is an important potential therapeutic target in PM [20, 80–82]. Critically, we demonstrated a link between SDHB and ER-β as part of this process [20].

In this paper we have shown that SDHB mRNA is commonly expressed in cell lines derived from both normal and malignant mesothelial cells (Fig. 1). We also demonstrated that SDHB mRNA is significantly overexpressed in primary PM tumors (Fig. 1B and C), However, an analysis of a recent TCGA NGS mesothelioma dataset (n = 87 patients) using cBioPortal [61] demonstrated upregulation of SDHB in only 3 samples (Fig. 1E). An early Affymetrix gene expression microarray study by Gordon et al. [49], did not find any significant overexpression of SDHB mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 1), while data from another microarray data set by López-Ríos et al. whilst demonstrating that SDHB is expressed in Me, demonstrated a trend for increasing SDHB mRNA expression as the epithelioid/biphasic/sarcomatoid, but this was not significant (Supplementary Fig. 2). This discrepancy remains to be resolved.

When separated according to histological subtype we observed significant overexpression of SDHB in only the biphasic subtype (Fig. 1D). However, the number of samples in our analysis is too small to make any real comparisons and our results do not reflect those of Lopez-Rios et al. [50], who observed higher expression of SDHB in the sarcomatoid subtype (Supplementary Figure S2). However, a much larger cohort of specimens would be required to truly identify changes in SDHB mRNA and protein expression associated with PM histological subtype.

Immunohistochemical staining of a PM tissue microarray was then conducted to see if expression levels of SDHB could be associated with patient survival. No significant survival benefit was however observed (Fig. 3) and these results are in agreement with other extant datasets such as the TCGA-MESO dataset which also shows no OS or DFS benefit (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Loss of SDHB expression by mutation is more commonly associated with various cancers [21]. In mesothelioma however, mutations of SDHB are rare. Indeed, in the initial discovery of the BAP1 mutation in 53 mesothelioma patients, only one patient was also found to have an SDHB mutation [54]. A subsequent analysis in other cohorts also only found one mutation in 123 patients (Supplementary Fig. 3). As such mutation of SDHB would appear to be uncommon in PM which is unfortunate as a recent clinical trial found that sunitinib had anti-tumor efficacy in progressive metastatic phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas [83]. The lack of mutations in SDHB in PM may therefore reflect why sunitinib was ineffective or showed limited efficacy in clinical trials [84–86].

Our analyses indicate that high expression of SDHB mRNA is associated with lower levels of DNA CpG methylation and vice versa (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, whilst stratification across histological subtypes in the TCGA-MESO dataset did not identify any significant changes in methylation between histological subtypes (Fig. 5B), significantly lower levels of methylation were found in PM patients with TP53 mutation. This may explain in part why SDHB levels are elevated in such patients (Fig. 3). As DNA methylation may be involved in regulating SDHB expression in PM, treatment of the NCI-H28 cell line with DNA hypomethylating agent Decitabine demonstrated that loss of methylation resulted in decreased SDHB mRNA (Fig. 5E). Given that lower DNA methylation is associated with increased expression of SDHB mRNA in TP53 mutated patients, this contrasting result suggests that DNA methylation may not therefore play a role in regulating SDHB mRNA expression. As a caveat, however, because SDHB mRNA is ubiquitously expressed across all cell lines tested including normal cell lines (Fig. 1A), whereas its expression is absent in benign pleural tissues (Fig. 1B), this may suggest that DNA methylation analysis in cell lines will be insufficient to delineate if DNA methylation is truly playing a role in regulating SDHB mRNA expression. Intriguingly, an analysis of DNA methylation at the SDHB locus of a subset of patients from the TCGA-MESO dataset with methylation data available showed that the CpG island of the SDHB promoter shows uniform methylation (Fig. 6 A). In contrast, three CpG residues within the CpG Shore nearby show differential methylation across these patients (Fig. 6 A). MethSurv analysis of these three CpG residues demonstrate that higher methylation at these CpG residues is associated with a better prognostic survival value (OS) (Fig. 6B), suggesting further studies are warranted in a larger number of samples to confirm if this OS survival value is maintained, and to also assess if loss or decreased SDHB expression is directly associated with the methylation status of these CpG residues.

In addition to DNA methylation, a number of miRNAs have also been associated with the regulation of SDHB expression. With this in mind we also examined whether key miRNAs which have been shown to target SDHB also have any prognostic value in PM. Our results show that of five candidate miRNAs, only one demonstrated significant prognostic OS value in PM (Fig. 8).

Despite PM being described as an “immunologically cold” tumor [87, 88], analysis of immune cell infiltrates identified that SDHB mRNA expression was correlated with dendritic cells (Table 4a), while neutrophils were associated with survival (Table 4b, Fig. 7).

Given that we have shown that SDHB mRNA is generally overexpressed in PM, this may make SDHB potentially amenable to therapeutic targeting. We therefore tested whether a known inhibitor of mitochondrial complex II, oxaloacetic acid, could affect PM cellular proliferation. When treated cell lines derived from PMs were more sensitive to oxaloacetic acid than cells derived from normal mesothelial cells (Fig. 9), indicating that targeting mitochondrial complex II with oxaloacetic acid may be a novel therapeutic avenue of approach in PM. Oxaloacetic acid was shown to significantly prolong the survival of rats and mice with brain-implanted gliomas [89], and is currently designated by the FDA as an orphan drug (Designation: 12–3704), for the treatment of patients with Glioma in a formulation known as “CRONaxal Medical Food”, a medical food composed of oxaloacetate and ascorbic acid. However, in a clinical study no significant changes in blood levels of oxaloacetate were found from a daily oral dose of 200 mg [90], and a later trial with higher doses (up to 2000 mg daily) found that OAA levels in plasma samples did not increase [91], and as such it may only be that supraphysiological doses of oxaloacetate may be required limiting the potential utility of this approach. In this regard, a more suitable inhibitor of mitochondrial complex II is α-tocopheryl succinate, (or α-TOS), which has shown significant pre-clinical efficacy in models of PM [92–96]. Critically, when succinate quinone reductase (a key component of complex II) was depleted, PM cells became resistant to α-TOS [92].
