Comonotonic improvement under feasibility constraints
Christopher Blier-Wong, Jean-Gabriel Lauzier

Abstract
Regulatory and contractual constraints on individual exposures are standard in insurance and reinsurance markets, but a poorly designed constraint can distort the economic incentives of risk-averse agents. In the unconstrained problem, the classical comonotonic improvement theorem guarantees Pareto-optimal allocations that are nondecreasing in the aggregate loss. A constraint that is not stable under risk reduction can destroy this property. We show by example that Value-at-Risk caps lead to optimal allocations that are non-comonotonic in the aggregate loss. We identify componentwise convex-order solidity as a sufficient condition on the feasible set that restores the comonotonic improvement under constraints. If replacing any agent’s allocation by a less risky one preserves feasibility, then every feasible allocation admits a feasible comonotonic improvement for all convex-order-consistent preferences. This criterion covers many constraints typical in risk management, but excludes Value-at-Risk caps and idiosyncratic deductibles. We illustrate the implications of our main result in a mean-variance risk-sharing application.



Keywords:
Risk-sharing, regulatory constraints, convex order, Pareto-optimal allocations, mean-variance risk-sharing

Introduction

Under convex-order-consistent preferences, the classical comonotonic
improvement theorem states that every Pareto-optimal allocation of
an aggregate loss
S
among
n
agents admits a comonotonic
representative with the same marginal distributions. The allocation problem
therefore collapses to a one-dimensional rearrangement of
S
. The
result was established for expected-utility agents by

9
, extended preference-free by

19
, and sharpened to integrable
aggregate losses on atomless spaces by

22
. In parallel, the
inf-convolution tradition of
3
,

18
,
17
,

1
, and
25

recasts the problem on spaces of monetary risk measures, and
identifies convex-order consistency as the structural feature that
forces optimal allocations to be comonotonic.
The preference class it covers is broad. It
contains every law-invariant coherent risk measure

(
4
)
, every law-invariant convex risk
measure on an atomless space
(
18
)
, and every
distortion risk measure with concave distortion

(
26
)
, a family that includes Expected
Shortfall, spectral risk measures, proportional hazard transforms,
and Wang transforms with a concave generator.
A parallel to the comonotonic improvement theorem is well-known
in general equilibrium theory under a different label:
in equilibrium, each risk-averse agent’s consumption bundle is
nondecreasing in the aggregate endowment, so that Pareto-optimal
allocations are comonotonic
(
23
)
.
Comonotonic reduction
is the starting point of most optimal risk-sharing analyses.

In practical situations, risk-sharing problems are rarely unconstrained.
Capital regulation imposes ceilings on each entity’s exposure based
on its own capital, assets, and reserves. Solvency II

(
16
)
and the Swiss Solvency Test cap one-year
changes in own funds through a high-level
\mathrm{VaR}
and
\mathrm{ES}

respectively. The revised Basel III market-risk framework

(
5
)
replaces the earlier
\mathrm{VaR}
ceiling with an

\mathrm{ES}_{0.975}
at the trading-desk level.
Reinsurance treaties impose capacity limits, quota-share
ratios, and excess-of-loss layer caps

(
2
)
. Catastrophe bonds and
insurance-linked securities introduce attachment points, exhaustion
points, and step-up capacities indexed to the aggregate loss

(
11
)
. Peer-to-peer pools can cede an upper tail to
a reinsurer
(
13
)
, and every sharing agreement
must satisfy individual rationality, so that each agent prefers the
pool to walking away
(
18
)
. Each such
arrangement carves out a strict subset of the clearing allocations
and raises the question of whether comonotonic reduction still
applies.

We seek a condition on the feasible set that preserves
comonotonic reduction under constraints, and identify it as
componentwise convex-order solidity, the property that
every clearing allocation whose components are convex-order
dominated by those of a feasible allocation is itself
feasible. Informally, making any one agent’s
share less risky cannot push the allocation out of the feasible
set. Once the feasible set is solid, the classical comonotonic
improvement theorem carries over with essentially the same proof;
Theorem
2
, our constrained comonotonic
improvement theorem, reduces to the unconstrained one in a single
step. Isolating this property is the substantive step, and it forces the
constrained problem back into the geometry where the classical
argument applies.

The definition is substantive because it excludes constraints
commonly written into regulation and contracts, and this has direct
consequences for the structure of the pool and for the incentives
facing its participants. Solidity fails in two qualitatively
distinct ways. The first arises when feasibility depends on
information beyond the aggregate loss. If each agent retains a
deductible on its own portfolio, feasibility is driven by
each agent’s own portfolio loss, whereas the components of a comonotonic
allocation are
\sigma(S)
-measurable.
Optimal allocations then cannot be functions of
S
alone, and
must condition on each agent’s individual loss.
The second mode arises even when the constraint is

\sigma(S)
-measurable. A
\mathrm{VaR}
ceiling at a fixed level is the
canonical example.
\mathrm{VaR}
is not convex-order consistent, so a firm
under such a cap can lower its requirement by splitting its
portfolio across subentities and shifting mass into the tail that

\mathrm{VaR}
does not see.
28
shows that under
Solvency II a firm can shrink its
\mathrm{VaR}
capital requirement
almost to zero by splitting into enough subentities.
This mechanism echoes observations in

29
and resonates with the
counter-monotonic quantile-based optima of

15
and

20
;
21
.

6
show that an insurer subject to a
\mathrm{VaR}

regulatory ceiling optimally purchases reinsurance for moderate
losses but leaves large losses entirely uninsured, since coverage
above the quantile does not affect the binding constraint. This
tail-insensitivity of
\mathrm{VaR}
runs directly counter to the purpose
of the regulation, and reflects the same mechanism that breaks
convex-order solidity in our setting. The regulatory
incentive therefore, points in the opposite direction from a cap based on a
convex-order-consistent risk measure: instead of rewarding each
agent for flattening the tail of its share, a
\mathrm{VaR}
cap rewards
pushing mass further out. Expected
Shortfall at the same level dominates
\mathrm{VaR}
and is convex-order
consistent, so replacing the cap with an
\mathrm{ES}
cap at the same level
restores solidity. To the best of our knowledge,
neither the loss of
\sigma(S)
-measurability induced by
own-endowment deductibles, nor the breakdown of comonotonic
reduction under a
\mathrm{VaR}
ceiling with risk-averse decision criteria,
has been isolated in the prior risk-sharing literature.

On the positive side, solidity admits a broad class of operational
constraints: deterministic caps and floors, premium budgets,
coherent risk-measure ceilings such as Expected Shortfall, and
individual rationality. Solidity is preserved under intersection,
so combinations of these are again solid, and the constrained
problem admits the same comonotonic reduction as the unconstrained
one. A complementary thread studies constrained bilateral sharing without
a common probability measure.
8
consider a
decision maker with subjective expected utility facing a
rank-dependent counterparty in a setting with no aggregate loss to
share, impose deterministic exposure caps, and obtain optima that are
monotone in the likelihood ratio rather than comonotonic. Their
failure of comonotonicity is preference-side, driven by heterogeneous
beliefs and a nonconvex distortion, whereas our non-comonotonic
examples come from the constraint side under shared
convex-order-consistent preferences. We fix convex-order-consistent
preferences and identify the structural property on the feasible set
that preserves the comonotonic reduction.

As a concrete illustration, we revisit the mean-variance
risk-sharing problem. In the unconstrained case, the optimum is
proportional, with each agent’s share of
S
tied to that agent’s
risk tolerance
(
1
)
. Under deterministic
capacity caps, which are solid, the optimum remains proportional,
but only in pieces. Once an agent saturates its capacity, the
remaining agents absorb the residual loss in proportion to their
own risk tolerances. The sharing rule transitions to a new linear
regime at each saturation point, and the truncated-affine form shows
how a solid constraint reshapes the pool without destroying the
comonotonic structure. Replacing the cap with a
\mathrm{VaR}
ceiling at
the same level breaks solidity, and we exhibit by example an
optimal allocation whose components fail to be comonotonic with

S
.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.
Section
2
fixes the setup, introduces the notation,
and recalls the unconstrained comonotonic improvement theorem.
Section
3
defines componentwise convex-order solidity
and proves the constrained comonotonic improvement theorem.
Section
4
exhibits three classes of constraints
that violate solidity, each yielding an optimal allocation whose
components may not be comonotonic with
S
.
Section
5
verifies solidity for the constraint
families typically used in practice and discusses a funding-cost
adjustment on the objective side. Section
6
applies the
constrained reduction to the mean-variance problem.

2
Setup and preliminaries

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be an atomless probability space. Each
agent
i\in[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
is endowed with a standalone loss

\zeta_{i}\in L^{1}
, and the aggregate loss shared by the pool is

S:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}\in L^{1}
. Each agent evaluates losses through a
law-invariant risk measure
\rho_{i}:L^{1}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
consistent with
risk aversion, in the loss convention where higher values of
\rho_{i}(X_{i})

correspond to worse loss distributions. The unconstrained risk-sharing
problem is

\inf_{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i});\qquad\mathbb{A}_{n}(S):=\left\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in(L^{1})^{n}:\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=S\ \text{a.s.}\right\}.

(1)

The feasible set
\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)
collects every clearing allocation of

S
. Under mild regularity conditions, a minimizer of
(
1
) is Pareto optimal up to
deterministic cash transfers between the agents.

An allocation
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)
is comonotonic with the
aggregate loss
S
if there exist nondecreasing functions

f_{1},\dots,f_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
X_{i}=f_{i}(S)
a.s. for every
i
and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(s)=s
for
F_{S}
-a.e.
s
(equivalently,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(S)=S
a.s.); see
12
.
We denote
the set of comonotonic clearing allocations by

\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S):=\left\{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S):\ X_{i}=f_{i}(S)\ \text{a.s.\ for nondecreasing }f_{1},\dots,f_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\right\}.

For any constraint system
\mathbb{C}
, we define

\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(S):=\{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S):\bm{X}
satisfies every
constraint in
\mathbb{C}\}
. Let

\mathop{\kern 3.99994pt\square\mathrlap{{}_{\mathbb{C}}}\kern 3.99994pt}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(S)\ :=\ \inf_{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(S)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathop{\kern 3.99994pt\boxplus\mathrlap{{}_{\mathbb{C}}}\kern 3.99994pt}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(S)\ :=\ \inf_{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(S)\cap\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i})

denote the constrained inf-convolution and its comonotonic
restriction, respectively. The unconstrained symbols

\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(S)
and
\mathop{\boxplus}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(S)
are
obtained by removing the constraint system
\mathbb{C}
. The unconstrained
reduction
\mathop{\square}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(S)=\mathop{\boxplus}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(S)
,
recalled in Theorem
1
, is what we seek to extend
to the constrained setting. The central equality we study is whether

\mathop{\kern 3.99994pt\square\mathrlap{{}_{\mathbb{C}}}\kern 3.99994pt}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(S)\;=\;\mathop{\kern 3.99994pt\boxplus\mathrlap{{}_{\mathbb{C}}}\kern 3.99994pt}\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(S),

(2)

where the right-hand side is the same infimum restricted to

\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathbb{C}}(S)\cap\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S)
.

Definition 1

(Pareto optimality)
.

Given a feasible set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)
, an
allocation
\bm{X}^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}
is
Pareto optimal in

\mathcal{X}
if no
\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{X}
satisfies

\rho_{i}(Y_{i})\leq\rho_{i}(X^{\star}_{i})
for every
i\in[n]
with at least
one strict inequality.

Any minimizer of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i})
over
\mathcal{X}
is Pareto
optimal in
\mathcal{X}
.

2.1
Convex order

The convex order formalizes the notion that one random variable is less
dispersed than another while preserving the mean.

Definition 2

(Convex order)
.

For
X,Y\in L^{1}
, we write
Y\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X
if
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]
and

\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y)]\leq\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)]
for every convex
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Equivalently,
Y\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X
if and only if
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]
and

\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}_{Y}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\leq\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}_{X}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u
for every

p\in[0,1]
. In the loss convention,
Y\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X
says
Y
is a
mean-preserving contraction of
X
in the sense of

24
; equivalently, in the wealth
convention,
-Y
second-order stochastically dominates
-X
with equal
means. Every risk-averse agent with finite mean prefers
Y
to
X
.

Assumption 1

(Convex-order-consistent preferences)
.

Each agent’s risk measure
\rho_{i}:L^{1}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is
convex-order consistent:
Y\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X
implies
\rho_{i}(Y)\leq\rho_{i}(X)
.

Throughout we use the extended-arithmetic conventions

a+(+\infty)=+\infty
for
a\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, and we write

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i})=+\infty
as soon as
\rho_{i}(X_{i})=+\infty
for
some
i
. Infima over empty sets are
+\infty
.

Assumption
1
covers expected
concave-utility functionals in the loss convention, i.e.
X\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\ell(X)]
with
\ell
convex and nondecreasing (equivalently

X\mapsto-\mathbb{E}[u(-X)]
for concave
u
, where
-X
is the wealth
representation); it also covers law-invariant coherent risk measures

(
4
)
, distortion risk measures with concave
distortion
(
26
)
, and law-invariant convex monetary
risk measures on atomless probability spaces
(
18
)
,
under the standard regularity conditions on the underlying function
space that render these classes convex-order consistent.

Throughout the paper,
\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}
denotes the lower

\alpha
-quantile,

\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(X):=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}(X\leq x)\geq\alpha\}
, for

\alpha\in(0,1)
.

2.2
Unconstrained comonotonic improvement

The following is the comonotonic improvement theorem of

22
, which extends to integrable
aggregate losses the construction of

19
for the bounded discrete case.
The original statements are phrased in terms of a stop-loss order;
for
L^{1}
aggregate losses with equal means, this coincides with
the convex order used here.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be atomless and
S\in L^{1}
. For every

\bm{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)
there exists

\bar{\bm{X}}=(\bar{X}_{1},\dots,\bar{X}_{n})\in\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S)
such that

\bar{X}_{i}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X_{i}
for every
i\in[n]
.

Comonotonicity also has a desirable incentive interpretation,
sometimes called the no-sabotage property.
If
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S)
,
so each share is a nondecreasing function of the aggregate loss

X_{i}=f_{i}(S)
, then an agent cannot reduce its own allocation by
secretly inflating its own contribution to
S
, since a larger
S

weakly increases every
X_{i}
. See
10
for
further discussion of this property and its economic relevance.
Non-comonotonic allocations generally
lack this stability property.

Remark 1
.

Two features of the comonotonic improvement are relevant to Section
3
.
First, it is componentwise:
\bar{X}_{i}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X_{i}
holds separately for
each
i
, with no cross-component comparison. Second, it is
law-based:
\bar{X}_{i}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X_{i}
compares the marginal distributions of

\bar{X}_{i}
and
X_{i}
alone and imposes no restriction on their
pathwise coupling. Both features are preserved by feasibility
constraints expressed through componentwise convex-order-consistent
functionals of each
X_{i}
.

3
The constrained comonotonic improvement theorem

This section identifies the structural condition on the feasible set
under which the unconstrained comonotonic improvement theorem remains
valid. We first introduce componentwise convex-order solidity as a
property of the constrained feasible set, and then state and prove the
constrained comonotonic improvement theorem.

3.1
Componentwise convex-order solidity

The comonotonic improvement in Theorem
1

replaces each component
X_{i}
by a convex-order smaller
\bar{X}_{i}
.
What a feasible set must tolerate for the comonotonic improvement
to stay inside it is therefore exactly this operation: any componentwise
convex-order reduction of a feasible allocation should remain
feasible.

Definition 3

(Componentwise convex-order solid feasible set)
.

A feasible set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)
is
componentwise
convex-order solid
if, for every
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{X}
and every

\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)
,

Y_{i}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X_{i}\ \text{for every }i\in[n]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{X}.

Solidity ensures that the comonotonic rearrangement of
Theorem
1
, which replaces each component by a
convex-order smaller one, cannot leave the feasible set. The
condition is componentwise in convex order, but joint through the
clearing constraint
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)
, and it is preference-free,
in that the reduction applies to every convex-order-consistent
preference family once
\mathcal{X}
is solid. Solidity is also
preserved under arbitrary intersections, by direct verification of
Definition
3
.
Marginal constraints are compatible only when their admissible marginal
sets are downward closed in convex order; in particular, ceilings based
on convex-order-consistent law-invariant functionals are compatible,
whereas law-invariance alone is not enough, with Value-at-Risk ceilings
in Section
4
as the canonical counterexample. By
contrast, constraints coupling
X_{i}
to variables outside
\sigma(S)

are typically not compatible.
Section
4
exhibits three failure modes and
Section
5
catalogues the admissible families.

3.2
The constrained comonotonic improvement theorem

The main result of this paper shows that componentwise convex-order
solidity is sufficient for the comonotonic improvement in
Theorem
1
to remain within the feasible set.

Theorem 2
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be atomless, let
S\in L^{1}
, and let

\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)
be nonempty and componentwise
convex-order solid. Then:

(i)

for every
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{X}
there exists a comonotonic
allocation
\bar{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S)
with

\bar{X}_{i}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X_{i}
for every
i\in[n]
;

(ii)

if
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n}
satisfy
Assumption
1
, then

\inf_{\bm{X}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i})\ =\ \inf_{\bm{X}\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i});

(3)

(iii)

under Assumption
1
, if

\bm{X}^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}
is Pareto optimal, then there exists

\bar{\bm{X}}^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}\cap\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S)
with

\rho_{i}(\bar{X}^{\star}_{i})=\rho_{i}(X^{\star}_{i})
for every
i
.

Proof.

Fix
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{X}
. By Theorem
1
, there is

\bar{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S)
with
\bar{X}_{i}\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X_{i}
for every
i
.
Componentwise convex-order solidity of
\mathcal{X}
gives

\bar{\bm{X}}\in\mathcal{X}
, proving (i).

For (ii), the inequality “
\leq
” follows from

\mathcal{X}\cap\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{X}
. The reverse
inequality follows from (i) and Assumption
1
:

\sum_{i}\rho_{i}(\bar{X}_{i})\leq\sum_{i}\rho_{i}(X_{i})
for every
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{X}
.

For (iii), apply (i) to
\bm{X}^{\star}
. Assumption
1

gives
\rho_{i}(\bar{X}^{\star}_{i})\leq\rho_{i}(X^{\star}_{i})
for every
i
, and
Pareto optimality rules out any strict inequality.
∎

Theorem
2
provides a direct recipe: to justify
the reduction to the comonotonic subclass on a constrained problem,
one verifies componentwise convex-order solidity of the feasible set,
which decomposes over intersection; see Section
3.1
.
Section
5
carries this out for the constraint
families typically used in practice. When solidity fails, the comonotonic
restriction still returns an upper bound on the constrained
inf-convolution, but the bound may be strict.

4
Constraints that preclude comonotonic reduction

Three examples show how comonotonic reduction can fail.
Section
4.1
shows that constraints
coupling
X_{i}
with state variables outside
\sigma(S)
, such as an
agent’s own deductible, measurable with respect to its individual
loss, need not be admissible.
Section
4.2
shows that

\sigma(S)

is not
sufficient either: a fixed retention plus a catastrophe-only capacity
top-up produces a strict gap in

2

Section
4.3
shows that a Value-at-Risk ceiling produces
a strict gap even when the initial endowments are comonotonic and
feasible.

4.1
Private deductibles on idiosyncratic state

Consider an agent

4.3

The Value-at-Risk is not convex-order consistent: a degenerate

5
Constraint families admitting comonotonic optima

This section gathers the constraint families most relevant to risk
management and regulation that generate convex-order-solid feasible
sets and therefore admit the reduction of
Theorem
2
. We collect four marginal constraint
families whose induced feasible sets are componentwise convex-order
solid in Theorem
3
. Convex piecewise-linear
funding-cost adjustments folded into the objective are handled
separately at the end of this section.

Before turning to concrete families, we record a one-dimensional
version of solidity that we use to verify each family componentwise.

Definition 4

(Marginally convex-order solid)
.

A set
\mathfrak{C}\subseteq L^{1}
is
marginally convex-order
solid
if
Z\in\mathfrak{C}
and
W\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}Z
imply
W\in\mathfrak{C}
.

If each component constraint
X_{i}\in\mathfrak{C}_{i}
is marginally
convex-order solid, then the feasible set

\{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S):X_{i}\in\mathfrak{C}_{i}\ \text{for every }i\}

is componentwise convex-order solid in the sense of
Definition
3
. This is the mechanism through which
Theorem
3
below combines its four
items; intersections close under both marginal and componentwise
solidity, as established in Section
3.1
.

Theorem 3

(Solid constraint families)
.

Each of the following constraint sets is marginally convex-order
solid, and componentwise convex-order solidity of the induced
feasible set follows from the paragraph preceding the theorem,
with closure under intersections noted in
Section
3.1
:

(a)

Deterministic pathwise bounds

\{Z\in L^{1}:\ \ell\leq Z\leq u\ \text{a.s.}\}
with

\ell\in[-\infty,+\infty)
,
u\in(-\infty,+\infty]
,
\ell\leq u
.

(b)

Linear expectation constraints
\{\mathbb{E}[Z]\leq c\}
,

\{\mathbb{E}[Z]=c\}
,
\{\mathbb{E}[Z]\geq c\}
.

(c)

Orlicz-type bounds
\{\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Z)]\leq c\}
for convex

\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
bounded below, including
L^{p}
-norm and
entropic bounds; variance bounds
\{\mathrm{Var}(Z)\leq v\}
follow
from
\varphi(x)=x^{2}
together with preservation of the mean.

(d)

Risk-measure ceilings
\{\varrho(Z)\leq c\}
for any
convex-order-consistent
\varrho:L^{1}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We verify that each family is marginally convex-order solid; the
componentwise statement for the induced feasible set was recorded in
the paragraph preceding the theorem. In each case, we fix

Z\in\mathfrak{C}
and
W\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}Z
, and check that
W\in\mathfrak{C}
.

For item (a), suppose first that
u<+\infty
and consider the convex
hinge
\phi_{u}(x)=(x-u)^{+}
. Because
Z\leq u
almost surely, one has

\phi_{u}(Z)=0
a.s., so
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{u}(Z)]=0
. Definition
2

applied to
\phi_{u}
then yields
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{u}(W)]\leq 0
, and since

\phi_{u}\geq 0
this forces
\phi_{u}(W)=0
a.s., that is,
W\leq u
a.s.
The lower bound
W\geq\ell
a.s. follows by the same argument with

\phi_{\ell}(x)=(\ell-x)^{+}
whenever
\ell>-\infty
. If
u=+\infty
or

\ell=-\infty
, the corresponding inequality is vacuous.

For item (b), both
x\mapsto x
and
x\mapsto-x
are convex, so
Definition
2
yields
\mathbb{E}[W]\leq\mathbb{E}[Z]
and
-\mathbb{E}[W]\leq-\mathbb{E}[Z]
,
hence
\mathbb{E}[W]=\mathbb{E}[Z]
. The three sets
\{\mathbb{E}[Z]\leq c\}
,
\{\mathbb{E}[Z]=c\}

and
\{\mathbb{E}[Z]\geq c\}
are therefore each preserved under
\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}
.

For item (c), the test function
\varphi
is convex by hypothesis,
so Definition
2
gives
\mathbb{E}[\varphi(W)]\leq\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Z)]\leq c
;
the boundedness below of
\varphi
ensures that the extended
expectations are well defined. The variance bound follows by taking

\varphi(x)=x^{2}
: combining
\mathbb{E}[W^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]
with
\mathbb{E}[W]=\mathbb{E}[Z]

from item (b) gives
\mathrm{Var}(W)\leq\mathrm{Var}(Z)\leq v
.

For item (d), convex-order consistency of
\varrho
is exactly the
implication
W\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}Z\Rightarrow\varrho(W)\leq\varrho(Z)
, so

\varrho(W)\leq\varrho(Z)\leq c
directly.
∎

Item (d) applies to every law-invariant coherent risk measure and to
every distortion risk measure with a concave distortion, including
Expected Shortfall, spectral measures, Wang transforms with concave
generator, and proportional hazard transforms; see

27
and
14
for the
convex-order properties of distortion risk measures.
Expected Shortfall ceilings
\mathrm{ES}_{\alpha_{i}}(X_{i})\leq R_{i}
, such as
those used in the Basel FRTB framework and the Swiss Solvency Test,
therefore generate convex-order-solid feasibility sets, while VaR-based
regimes such as the Solvency II standard capital requirement fall
under the caveat in Section
4.3
. Heterogeneity of

\alpha_{i}
across firms does not break solidity.

Corollary 1

(Capacity caps)
.

For deterministic constants
K_{i}\in[0,+\infty]
, the feasibility set

\{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S):\ 0\leq X_{i}\leq K_{i}\ \text{a.s.\ for every }i\}

is componentwise convex-order solid, so
Theorem
2
applies under the standing
hypotheses whenever this set is nonempty.

Corollary
1
covers limited-liability clauses,
per-member capacity limits in reinsurance pools, layer widths in
excess-of-loss reinsurance programmes, and public–private pool structures with
heterogeneous governmental backstop caps. The analysis of inadmissible constraints in
Section
4
shows why the cap
K_{i}
must be a
deterministic constant rather than a function of
S
with over-unit
slope, and rules out caps depending on state variables outside

\sigma(S)
.

Aggregate-indexed pathwise bounds
\ell(S)\leq X_{i}\leq u(S)
with
nondecreasing
\ell,u
are not automatically admissible:
Example
2
shows that monotonicity alone does
not prevent the comonotonic rearrangement from violating the envelope.
Such caps should be treated as potentially inadmissible unless a
separate solidity argument is supplied.

Premium budgets
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\leq P_{i}
and actuarial-fairness constraints

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{i}]
, which equate each agent’s expected
post-allocation loss with its expected initial loss, are admissible
by item (b); equality and lower-bound forms are admissible as well, a
special feature of the linearity of
\mathbb{E}
.

Each agent’s preferences may combine a baseline risk measure with a
convex loading of the post-allocation loss without disturbing
solidity of the feasible set. In
tiered-capital or cost-of-funds models, agent
i
’s objective takes
the form
\rho_{i}(X_{i})+\mathbb{E}[\varphi_{i}(X_{i})]
, where
\varphi_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is
convex, nondecreasing, piecewise linear, and bounded below (so that

\mathbb{E}[\varphi_{i}(X_{i})]
is an extended expectation with integrable
negative part, taking values in
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
). A typical
specification is

\varphi_{i}(x)=\alpha_{i}(x-R_{i})^{+}+(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})(x-R_{i}-B_{i})^{+}
,
with
R_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
a retention threshold,
B_{i}\geq 0
a layer width, and

0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
so that the slopes are nonnegative and
nondecreasing, encoding a reserve, collateral, and fire-sale ladder. The
adjusted objective is convex-order consistent: convex-order consistency
of
X\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\varphi_{i}(X)]
follows by applying
Definition
2
to the convex test function
\varphi_{i}
, and
summing with the convex-order-consistent
\rho_{i}
preserves the
property, so Theorem
2
applies directly on any
convex-order-solid feasibility set.

6
Application: constrained mean-variance risk sharing

We illustrate Theorem
2
on the mean-variance
risk-sharing problem. The unconstrained mean-variance planner admits a
well-known affine quota-share optimum. Under deterministic caps and
floors, Theorem
3
(a) (and
Corollary
1
in the special case
L_{i}=0
) licenses the
comonotonic reduction, and
the constrained optimum takes a truncated-affine form driven by a single
statewise shadow price. Hard caps produce a piecewise linear sharing
rule with slopes determined by the active set of uncapped agents.

6.1
Unconstrained mean-variance sharing

Fix risk-aversion parameters
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}>0
, and assume

S\in L^{2}
throughout this section. Each agent

evaluates its
share through the mean-variance risk measure

\rho_{i}(X)\ :=\ \mathbb{E}[X]+\delta_{i}\,\mathrm{Var}(X),\qquad X\in L^{2}.

(4)

The functional
\rho_{i}
is law-invariant and convex-order consistent:
if
Y\leq_{\mathrm{cx}}X
with
X\in L^{2}
, then Definition
2

\varphi(x)=x^{2}
gives
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]<\infty
, so

,
and combined with
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]
this yields

\mathrm{Var}(Y)\leq\mathrm{Var}(X)
and therefore
\rho_{i}(Y)\leq\rho_{i}(X)
.
Assumption
1
is in force on the domain
L^{2}
.

The clearing condition forces
\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[S]
, so the
unconstrained risk-sharing problem

\inf_{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)\cap(L^{2})^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i})

(5)

\mathbb{E}[S]
, to the pure
variance-minimization problem

\inf_{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)\cap(L^{2})^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\,\mathrm{Var}(X_{i}).

The following proposition is the mean-variance instance of the
proportional sharing rule for dilated risk measures, noted in

1
; see

3

7
for a related statement. We state it to fix
the notation used in the constrained problem.

Proposition 1
.

The unconstrained mean-variance problem (
5
)
admits the optimum

X_{i}^{\star}\ =\ \frac{\delta_{i}^{-1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}^{-1}}\,S\,+\,\beta_{i},

(6)

where
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sum_{i}\beta_{i}=0
;
the optimizer is unique
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. up to cash transfers
\bm{\beta}
.

6.2
Constrained mean-variance sharing

Impose deterministic capacity bounds on each share:

L_{i}\ \leq\ X_{i}\ \leq\ U_{i}\qquad\text{a.s.,}\qquad i\in[n],

(7)

with
L_{i}\in[-\infty,+\infty)
,
U_{i}\in(-\infty,+\infty]
,
L_{i}<U_{i}
,
and
\sum_{i}L_{i}\leq S\leq\sum_{i}U_{i}
a.s. in the extended sense. Let

\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathrm{cap}}(S)\ :=\ \left\{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}(S)\cap(L^{2})^{n}:\ L_{i}\leq X_{i}\leq U_{i}\ \text{a.s.}\ \forall i\right\}.

The constrained mean-variance problem is

\inf_{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathrm{cap}}(S)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(X_{i}).

(8)

Applied componentwise, Theorem
3
(a) shows that

\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathrm{cap}}(S)
is componentwise convex-order solid in
the sense of Definition
3
, and convex order preserves

L^{2}
membership, so the comonotonic improvement stays in
(L^{2})^{n}
.
Theorem
2
therefore yields the reduction

\inf_{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathrm{cap}}(S)}\sum_{i}\rho_{i}(X_{i})\ =\ \inf_{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathrm{cap}}(S)\cap\mathbb{A}_{n}^{+}(S)}\sum_{i}\rho_{i}(X_{i}),

so we may restrict attention to comonotonic allocations of the form

X_{i}=f_{i}(S)
with
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to[L_{i},U_{i}]
nondecreasing and

\sum_{i}f_{i}(s)=s
for
F_{S}
-a.e.
s
. Let

\mathcal{S}:=\mathrm{supp}(F_{S})
denote the topological support of

S
, that is, the smallest closed set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with

\mathbb{P}(S\in K)=1
. Since the optimum has the form
X_{i}^{\star}=f_{i}^{\star}(S)
,
the functions
f_{i}^{\star}
are pinned down only on
\mathcal{S}
;
statements below about the shape of
s\mapsto f_{i}^{\star}(s)
therefore
refer to
s\in\mathcal{S}
and hold up to
F_{S}
-null sets.

The following theorem identifies the form of the constrained optimum.

Theorem 4
.

Any solution
\bm{X}^{\star}
to problem (
8
) has the form

X_{i}^{\star}\ =\ \Pi_{i}\!\left(c_{i}^{\star}+\delta_{i}^{-1}\,\eta^{\star}(S)\right)\quad\text{a.s.},\qquad\Pi_{i}(x)\ :=\ \min\!\left\{U_{i},\ \max\!\left\{L_{i},\ x\right\}\right\},

(9)

with
c_{i}^{\star}=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{\star}]
and a nondecreasing

\eta^{\star}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by the clearing condition

\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}(c_{i}^{\star}+\delta_{i}^{-1}\eta^{\star}(s))=s.

Furthermore, the map
s\mapsto f_{i}^{\star}(s)
is
affine with slope

\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{i}^{\star}}{\mathrm{d}s}\ =\ \begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{i}^{-1}}{\sum_{j\in A}\delta_{j}^{-1}},&i\in A,\\[8.99994pt]
0,&i\notin A\end{cases}

(10)

on any subinterval of
\mathcal{S}
along which the active set

A(s):=\{i\in[n]:L_{i}<f_{i}^{\star}(s)<U_{i}\}
is constant and equal to some nonempty
A\subseteq[n]
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3
(a), applied componentwise, the
constraint set
\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathrm{cap}}(S)
is componentwise
convex-order solid in the sense of Definition
3
.
Theorem
2
(i) then produces a feasible
allocation comonotonic with
S
that componentwise dominates any
given optimizer in convex order; since each
\rho_{i}
is consistent
with convex order, this comonotonic allocation is itself optimal. Fix such an optimizer, write it
as
X_{i}^{\star}=f_{i}^{\star}(S)
, and set

c_{i}^{\star}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{\star}]
. Since
\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[S]
is fixed
across
\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathrm{cap}}(S)
, the expectation part of the
objective in (
8
) is constant, and only the
variance term remains.

We first show that, for
F_{S}
-a.e.
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the vector

f^{\star}(s)=(f_{1}^{\star}(s),\ldots,f_{n}^{\star}(s))
solves the
finite-dimensional statewise program

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(x_{i}-c_{i}^{\star})^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=s,\ L_{i}\leq x_{i}\leq U_{i}.

(
Q_{s}
)

If this failed on a set of
s
-values with positive
F_{S}
-mass,
replacing
f^{\star}(s)
on that set by the unique minimizer of

(Q_{s})
would produce another feasible allocation

Y_{i}=g_{i}(S)\in\mathbb{A}_{n}^{\mathrm{cap}}(S)
with

\sum_{i}\rho_{i}(Y_{i})\ =\ \mathbb{E}[S]+\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\,\mathrm{Var}(Y_{i})\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}[S]+\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\,\mathbb{E}[(Y_{i}-c_{i}^{\star})^{2}],

whereas, using

\mathrm{Var}(X_{i}^{\star})=\mathbb{E}[(X_{i}^{\star}-c_{i}^{\star})^{2}]
at the
optimizer,

\sum_{i}\rho_{i}(X_{i}^{\star})\ =\ \mathbb{E}[S]+\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\,\mathbb{E}[(X_{i}^{\star}-c_{i}^{\star})^{2}].

A statewise improvement on a positive-mass set makes the right-hand
side strictly smaller, contradicting optimality of
\bm{X}^{\star}
.
Hence
f^{\star}(s)
solves
(Q_{s})
for
F_{S}
-a.e.
s
.

It remains to solve
(Q_{s})
. This is a strictly convex quadratic
program with affine and box constraints, so the KKT conditions are
necessary and sufficient. With Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(x,\eta,\lambda,\mu)\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(x_{i}-c_{i}^{\star})^{2}-2\eta\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}-s\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(L_{i}-x_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}(x_{i}-U_{i}),

the KKT system

\sum_{i}x_{i}=s,\qquad L_{i}\leq x_{i}\leq U_{i},\qquad 2\delta_{i}(x_{i}-c_{i}^{\star})-2\eta-\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i}=0,

\lambda_{i}(x_{i}-L_{i})=0,\qquad\mu_{i}(U_{i}-x_{i})=0,\qquad\lambda_{i},\mu_{i}\geq 0,

is equivalent to

x_{i}\ =\ \Pi_{i}\!\left(c_{i}^{\star}+\delta_{i}^{-1}\eta\right),\qquad\Pi_{i}(x)=\min\{U_{i},\max\{L_{i},x\}\}.

The clearing condition
\sum_{i}x_{i}=s
then determines the multiplier

\eta^{\star}(s)
through

H(\eta^{\star}(s))\ =\ s,\qquad H(\eta)\ :=\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pi_{i}\!\left(c_{i}^{\star}+\delta_{i}^{-1}\eta\right),

and, since
H
is continuous and nondecreasing, a nondecreasing
measurable selector
s\mapsto\eta^{\star}(s)
exists. Combining,

X_{i}^{\star}\ =\ \Pi_{i}\!\left(c_{i}^{\star}+\delta_{i}^{-1}\eta^{\star}(S)\right)\quad\text{a.s.,}

which is the truncated-affine form (
9
).

Finally, on any subinterval of
\mathcal{S}
along which the active
set
A=\{i\in[n]:L_{i}<f_{i}^{\star}(s)<U_{i}\}
is constant, the inactive
shares are fixed at their bounds and the clearing condition reduces
to

\sum_{i\in A}\left(c_{i}^{\star}+\delta_{i}^{-1}\eta^{\star}(s)\right)+\sum_{i\notin A}f_{i}^{\star}(s)\ =\ s,

so
\eta^{\star}
is affine in
s
on that interval. Differentiating
yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{i}^{\star}}{\mathrm{d}s}\ =\ \frac{\delta_{i}^{-1}}{\sum_{j\in A}\delta_{j}^{-1}}\ \text{for }i\in A,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{i}^{\star}}{\mathrm{d}s}\ =\ 0\ \text{for }i\notin A,

which is (
10
). When
L_{i}=-\infty
and
U_{i}=+\infty

for every
i
,
\Pi_{i}
is the identity and
A=[n]
, and the formula
reduces to the unconstrained affine allocation
(
6
).
∎

Remark 2

(Existence and measurability of the shadow-price selector)
.

Theorem
4
asserts that every comonotonic optimum has the
truncated-affine form (
9
) through a nondecreasing
measurable function
\eta^{\star}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
of the
aggregate loss. Two facts must hold for this claim to make sense:
the statewise minimizer must be unique, and the multiplier that
generates it must be choosable as a Borel function of
s
, even though
the clearing condition does not pin
\eta
down uniquely when several

\eta
-values give the same total. We record both here.

For fixed
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the statewise program

(

Q
s

)
of the proof of Theorem
4
has a
strictly convex, coercive objective and a convex polyhedral
feasible set, so it admits a unique minimizer
\hat{x}(s)
. Dualizing
the adding-up constraint yields

\hat{x}_{i}(s)\ =\ \Pi_{i}\!\left(c_{i}^{\star}+\delta_{i}^{-1}\,\eta(s)\right)

(11)

for any
\eta(s)
satisfying
H(\eta(s))=s
, where

H(\eta):=\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}(c_{i}^{\star}+\delta_{i}^{-1}\eta)
is continuous and
nondecreasing. The preimage
H^{-1}(\{s\})
is therefore a nonempty
closed interval, possibly unbounded at boundary values of
s
, and

\hat{x}(s)
is constant along it, so the non-uniqueness of
\eta(s)

does not affect the allocation on any flat region of
H
. Any
nondecreasing measurable selector
s\mapsto\eta^{\star}(s)
of the
multivalued inverse of
H
therefore defines a finite, single-valued
and measurable
\eta^{\star}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, so

\hat{X}_{i}:=\hat{x}_{i}(S)
is a Borel allocation comonotonic with
S
of
the form claimed by Theorem
4
.

Theorem
4
records three structural features. Globally,
the allocation is truncated-affine through a single nondecreasing
statewise shadow price
\eta^{\star}(S)
, pinned down by the adding-up
constraint. Locally, sharing is proportional to risk tolerances among
active agents, reproducing the unconstrained slopes

\delta_{i}^{-1}/\sum_{j\in A}\delta_{j}^{-1}
on each region where the active set
A
is
constant. Capped agents contribute zero marginal sharing on that
region, and the remaining active agents re-share incremental losses
with renormalized slopes; slope discontinuities in

s\mapsto f_{i}^{\star}(s)
occur at levels where an agent transitions
between interior and boundary.

6.3
Two-agent example

Consider
n=2
agents with risk-aversion parameters
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
.
Agent 1 faces a hard upper cap
U_{1}=C>0
and a lower bound

L_{1}=0
; agent 2 is uncapped in the sense that
L_{2}=-\infty
and

U_{2}=+\infty
. Assume
S\geq 0
a.s.
The feasible set is

\mathbb{A}_{2}^{\mathrm{cap}}(S)\ =\ \left\{(X_{1},X_{2}):\ X_{1}+X_{2}=S,\ 0\leq X_{1}\leq C\right\}.

Write
a:=\delta_{1}^{-1}/(\delta_{1}^{-1}+\delta_{2}^{-1})\in(0,1)
for the unconstrained quota
share of agent 1, and assume the cap binds on a nontrivial set of
states, that is,
\mathbb{P}(aS>C)>0
, equivalently
\esssup S>C/a
.

Corollary 2
.

The constrained mean-variance optima in the two-agent pool above take
the form

X_{1}^{\star}\ =\ \min\!\left\{C,\ \max\!\left\{0,\ aS+\beta^{\star}\right\}\right\},\qquad X_{2}^{\star}\ =\ S-X_{1}^{\star}\quad\text{a.s.,}

(12)

where
\beta^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
is any solution of the fixed-point condition

\beta^{\star}\ =\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\min\!\left\{C,\ \max\!\left\{0,\ aS+\beta^{\star}\right\}\right\}\right]-a\,\mathbb{E}[S].

(13)

The fixed-point equation (
13
) admits at least one
solution
\beta^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
, and any such
\beta^{\star}
yields
an optimum of the form (
12
); the solution set
is a closed interval
[\beta_{-},\beta_{+}]
of deterministic balanced
cash transfers.

Proof.

Agent 2 is uncapped, so
\Pi_{2}
is the identity in
Theorem
4
. On the states where the active set
equals
\{1,2\}
, the slope formula (
10
) gives

\mathrm{d}X_{1}^{\star}/\mathrm{d}s=a
; on the cap regions
X_{1}^{\star}
is pinned
at
0
or
C
. Continuity of
s\mapsto f_{1}^{\star}(s)
forces

X_{1}^{\star}\ =\ \Pi(aS+\beta^{\star}),\qquad\Pi(y):=\min\{C,\max\{0,y\}\},

for some
\beta^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
identified from the interior identity

X_{1}^{\star}=aS+\beta^{\star}
as
\beta^{\star}=c_{1}^{\star}-a\mathbb{E}[S]
,
using
c_{1}^{\star}+c_{2}^{\star}=\mathbb{E}[S]
. Combining with

c_{1}^{\star}=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{\star}]=\mathbb{E}[\Pi(aS+\beta^{\star})]
yields the
fixed-point equation (
13
). Existence of

\beta^{\star}
follows from existence of the optimum.

Let
h(\beta):=\mathbb{E}[\Pi(aS+\beta)]
. Since
\Pi
is nondecreasing and

1
-Lipschitz, so is
h
, and

) is
a closed interval
[\beta_{-},\beta_{+}]
.
∎

The allocation (
12
) is the classical
quota-share-with-limit rule up to a deterministic transfer.
Below the attachment

\{X_{1}^{\star}=0\}
does not bind (for instance whenever

transitioning from interior to upper cap; below it the

(a,1-a)
, above it they are
(0,1)
. The formula

transfer
\beta^{\star}
that the paper’s convention usually absorbs
into a premium. The comonotonic reduction in
Theorem
2
, instantiated by
Theorem
3
(a) componentwise
(Corollary
1
covers the symmetric

what licenses restricting to this shape in the first place.

7
Conclusion

The classical unconstrained comonotonic reduction of

9
,
19
, and

22
makes optimal risk sharing under
convex-order-consistent preferences tractable when the feasible set
is the full clearing simplex. We extend the reduction to constrained
problems. Theorem
2
shows that the comonotonic
improvement theorem survives whenever the feasible set is
componentwise convex-order solid, and
Theorem
3
identifies the operational
constraint families that qualify, together with their stability under
finite intersection.

Not every constraint is admissible. Sections
4.3

and
6.5
show that
\mathrm{VaR}
ceilings, which Solvency II
imposes on insurers, escape the reduction even when every agent is
risk-averse. Risk aversion alone does not guarantee comonotonic
optima: under
\mathrm{VaR}
constraints, the optimal sharing rule can combine
comonotonic and counter-monotonic regions over the state space, so the
dependence structure of the allocation departs from what the unconstrained
intuition suggests.
