Feasibility and safety of awake extracorporeal membrane oxygenation for operating room extubation after lung transplantation
Authors: Chengfu Xian, Lihua Ao, Shangxi Zeng, Jiayu Li, Zhuolin He, Shujun Wu, Furong Lin, Fengfa Yang, Lan Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and safety of awake extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) for operating room (OR) extubation after lung transplantation (LTx).MethodsLTx patients, with intraoperative ECMO and extubation in the OR under ECMO support between January 1, 2024, and June 30, 2025, were classified as the awake ECMO + no mechanical ventilation (MV) group. From September 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023, the LTx patients, who weaned from ECMO in OR and subsequently extubated in the intensive care unit (ICU) were defined as the No ECMO + MV group, while those weaned from ECMO and extubated both in the ICU were classified as ECMO + MV group.ResultsForty-four LTx patients were extubated in the OR under awake ECMO support. After propensity score matching (PSM), 26 LTx patients were respectively included in three groups. No significant differences of vital signs were observed at the end of transplantation among the three groups (P>0.05), except that a lower central venous pressure and lower driving pressure in the ECMO + No-MV group (P=0.05 and P=0.001). The duration of invasive MV and ICU stay were shortest in ECMO + No MV group (P<0.001 and P=0.01). The ECMO-related complications and complications within postoperative 7 days had no difference among three groups (P=0.07 and P=0.053). The total cost of hospitalization was lowest in the No ECMO + MV group (P=0.02). The follow-up 3- and 6-month survivals were lowest in the ECMO + MV group (P<0.001 and P=0.02), while the follow-up 12-month survival was comparable among three groups (P=0.20).ConclusionsExtubated awake veno-arterial (VA)-ECMO is feasible and safe for OR extubation following LTx and may facilitate postoperative recovery by reducing invasive MV and ICU stay.Keywords: Lung transplantation (LTx), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), respiratory distress syndrome, respiratory insufficiency

Introduction

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) provides an alternative form of circulatory and respiratory support to conventional mechanical ventilation (MV) (1-3). Postoperative ECMO assistance may be required in some cases of lung transplantation (LTx). Whether to discontinue ECMO while maintaining MV or to continue both ECMO and MV after LTx depends on the overall condition of the patient. Nevertheless, postoperative MV is inevitable and may lead to detrimental effects, including ventilator-induced lung injury (4), pneumonia (5), and diaphragmatic atrophy or myotrauma (6). Extubation in the operating room (OR) following LTx may avoid the MV, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and hospital stay, as well as reducing postoperative complications (7,8).

Awake veno-arterial ECMO (VA-ECMO) is safe and effective for perioperative acute heart failure during cardiac surgery (9), as well as a promising preoperative bridging strategy for patients with chronic respiratory failure awaiting LTx (4,9-11). However, whether awake ECMO can facilitate OR extubation and serve as an alternative to MV after LTx remains uncertain. A single case reported by Prada et al. provides insufficient evidence to confirm this feasibility (12).

Therefore, the primary objective of this cohort study was to describe our experience with OR extubation in LTx patients supported by awake VA-ECMO, and to evaluate its clinical feasibility and safety. Based on previously reported favorable outcomes of OR extubation at our center (7), this cohort study hypothesize that LTx patients can be feasibly extubated in the OR under the awake ECMO support, which would potentially decrease the duration of postoperative MV and improve early postoperative recovery compared with conventional intubated management. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0600/rc).

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective real-world implementation study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (No. ES-2024-183-02). Informed consent was waived in this retrospective study. All organs were allocated through the China Organ Transplant Response System (COTRS), and all recipients received an organ acceptance confirmation from the transplantation center before transplantation.

Patient selection

LTx patients extubated in the OR under ECMO support between January 1, 2024, and June 30, 2025 were classified as the awake ECMO + No MV group. From September 1, 2019 to December 31, 2023, the LTx patients, who weaned from ECMO in OR and subsequently extubated in the ICU were defined as the No ECMO + MV group, while those weaned from ECMO and extubated in the ICU were classified as ECMO + MV group.

Inclusion criteria were patients aged over 18 years who received either double or single LTx with intraoperative ECMO and/or postoperative ECMO support. Exclusion criteria included multi-organ transplantation, donor from ex vivo lung perfusion, intraoperative resuscitation.

ECMO procedures

The criteria for intraoperative ECMO implantation was in accordance with the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization guidelines (13) and previous reports (14). VA-ECMO was generally established via the femoral vein with a 21F outflow catheter, and via the femoral or right axillary artery with a 19F or 17F inflow catheter, depending on the patient’s condition and the surgeon’s experience. The selection of the 21F drainage cannula reflects our specific institutional practice, primarily driven by the generally small body mass index. When necessary, a 17F inflow catheter was inserted via the right internal jugular vein to establish veno-arterio-venous (VAV) ECMO. Although relatively small, this cannula size consistently allowed us to initiate VA-ECMO at a baseline blood flow equivalent to 60% of the cardiac output, and typically adjusted between 60–80%. The blood flow-to-gas flow ratio was adjusted to 1:1 or 1:0.5. The fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) was adjusted based on arterial blood gas, typically between 50–100%.

OR extubation under awake ECMO after LTx

The indications for extubation after LTx were primarily based on Felten’s extubation protocol (15). The surgical strategy, LTx procedure, and ECMO management followed the methods previously described by the Vienna Lung Transplant Group (16). At the end of transplantation, ECMO support was tapered by lowering FiO2 and reducing blood flow to 1 L/min. After 5 minutes, an oxygenation index (OI = PaCO2/FiO2) greater than 300 was considered the indication for ECMO weaning. ECMO support was continued in these patients to maintain a PaCO2 of 40–50 mmHg and an OI >300, during which patients gradually resumed spontaneous breathing. Extubation was performed once LTx patients regained consciousness under ECMO support, demonstrated stable hemodynamics (norepinephrine <0.1 µg/kg/min and dopamine <5 µg/kg/min), maintained breathing (tidal volume 4–6 mL/kg, respiratory rate 12–20 breaths/min, and driving pressure <16 cmH2O), and could respond with eye opening and nodding. Sugammadex and flumazenil were then administered. After extubation, non-invasive MV was initiated using bilevel positive airway pressure with an inspiratory pressure of 12–16 cmH2O, expiratory pressure of 6–8 cmH2O, respiratory rate of 12–20 breaths/min, and FiO2 of 60–80%. If patients exhibited difficulty breathing, restlessness, or unstable hemodynamics despite ECMO support, and their OI remained <300, the ECMO and MV were continued. These patients were subsequently weaned from ECMO and extubated in the ICU.

Outcomes of the study

The primary outcome was the safety and feasibility of the awake ECMO strategy for postoperative extubation in LTx patients. The secondary outcome was the difference in perioperative changes between the extubated awake and intubated non-awake VA-ECMO strategies.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 22.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as number (percentages) and compared between groups using the Chi-Square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, or median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data. The t-test was for two group comparison or one-way analysis of variance followed by post hoc Newman-Keuls test was for multiple group comparison. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test (two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (multiple groups).

Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to adjust for intergroup confounding and to verify the robustness of the results. The matching caliper value was set to 0.1, with ratios of 1:1:1, and factors such as age, sex, diagnosis, coronary, preoperative MV, and preoperative ECMO support, were used to patient’s match. The standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to evaluate inter group balance. When the SMD value was less than or nearest 0.2 after PSM, the inter group balance was considered to be good.

P value of two-sided less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All statistical analysis were carried out with R language version 4.4.1.

No significant difference were found in the parameters of intraoperative ECMO support, including pump-rate (P=0.58), flow-rate (P=0.24), and gas-flow (P=0.48) (Table 2).

No significant differences in vital signs at the end of LTx were observed among the three groups (P>0.05), except that a lower central venous pressure (10 vs. 12 vs. 12 cmH2O, P=0.050) and lower driving pressure (15 vs. 19.5 vs. 16 cmH2O, P=0.001) in the ECMO + No-MV group, but a higher median pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) (26.5 vs. 27.5 vs. 22.5 mmHg, P=0.02) was in the No ECMO + MV group.

There were comparable values in the surgical time (P=0.55), urine output (P=0.48), blood lose (P>0.99), infusion of red blood cell and plasma (P=0.42 and P=0.60), while less liquid infusion (1,887.5 vs. 3,512.5 vs. 3,325 mL, P<0.001) and longer anesthesia time (681.5 vs. 557.5 vs. 602 min, P=0.006) in the ECMO + No-MV group (Table 2).

Comparison of postoperative rehabilitation

The postoperative hospital stay, total hospital stay, the incidence and the cause of re-intubation, were comparable among three groups (P>0.05). Compared with the ECMO + MV group, the duration of invasive MV (0 vs. 9 vs. 12 days, P<0.001) and ICU stay (7 vs. 11 vs. 20 days, P=0.01) were shortest in ECMO + No MV group (Table 3).

Discussion

We implemented OR extubation under awake ECMO following LTx and preliminarily evaluated its feasibility and safety. The patients in the ECMO + No MV group exhibited no need for MV and the shortest ICU stay. But the hospital stay and re-intubation, the ECMO-related complications, complications with postoperative 7 days, and follow-up 12 months survival, were similar with the traditional methods of ECMO + MV and No ECMO + MV.

In the traditional ECMO + MV group, patients requiring postoperative ECMO support must also receive simultaneous invasive MV. However, our results indicated that in patients with stable breathing and hemodynamics, postoperative invasive MV can be avoided by relying solely on ECMO support. Therefore, invasive MV can be avoided. It is reported that patients receiving awake ECMO have better survival than those receiving MV (5), owing to reduced duration of postoperative MV, as well as shorter ICU and hospital stays (17), which is consistent with our results.

Preoperative awake ECMO can be a bridge to LTx in patients with end-stage pulmonary disease awaiting a lung donor (4,10,11). Perioperative awake ECMO has also been reported in cardiac surgery (11). Although postoperative awake ECMO for LTx was first reported by Prada et al., it was limited to a single case (12). We have accumulated experience from 44 cases and found that initiating OR extubation under awake VA-ECMO support for LTx patients was consistent with that reported by Prada et al. Continuing ECMO support after LTx raises concerns about potential complications. However, the incidence of ECMO-related complications, complications with postoperative 7 days, and deep vein thrombosis after LTx were comparable among the three groups.

In addition, re-intubation after LTx remains a major concern. In 2003, Hansen et al. reported a 20.8% re-intubation rate in patients undergoing OR extubation during LTx (18). Recent studies by Augoustides et al. and Felten et al. reported re-intubation rates of 9.5% and 8.9%, respectively (15,19). In our study, although the re-intubation rate was 31% in the ECMO + No MV group, mainly due to respiratory failure caused by infection, it had no difference with those in the ECMO + MV and No ECMO + MV groups. However, the ECMO + MV group showed the highest in-hospital mortality with 62%, highest total cost of hoppitalization, and lowest follow-up 6-month survival. However, the in-hospital mortality was respectively 15% in the No ECMO + MV and ECMO + No MV groups, the total cost of hospitalization and the follow-up 12-month survival were also comparable between these two groups. These findings suggest that ECMO + No MV has similar survival and economic burden with No ECMO + MV, it may be a promising alternative to traditional method for rehabilitation after LTx. Therefore, we may propose a new rehabilitation process for LTx patients who need ECMO and MV support at the end of transplantation (Figure S1). The potential factors of benefit may come from that the extubated awake ECMO after LTx reduces ribcage displacement (20) and preserves diaphragmatic contractions (2,21), helping maintain optimal ventilatory function. In addition, spontaneous breathing after LTx enhances venous return (22), supports cardiac output, and reduces pulmonary edema (23). Finally, staying conscious allows patients to communicate with ICU staff and family members, improving mental health. Therefore, the “awake VA-ECMO” strategy fully aligns with the application indications of ECMO outlined in the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) consensus, effectively preventing ventilator-associated complications (24-26). The clinical practice in this study (utilizing VA-ECMO to achieve ultra-fast extubation in the OR) is a perfect embodiment of this concept.

Adhering to indications for OR extubation is a successful key for awake VA-ECMO after LTx. Our extubation occurs when OI nears 300, hemodynamics is stable, and there’s no shortness of breath or airway exudation. When ECMO removes 100% of CO2, minute ventilation drops to apnea (27-29), reducing respiratory muscle workload and oxygen consumption by up to 50%. This helps prevent respiratory muscle fatigue (2,25). However, hypoventilation from CO2 removal can lower the ventilation-to-perfusion ratio, risking atelectasis (20). A balance is needed between lung rest and ventilation load. ECMO must provide enough oxygenation for spontaneous breathing after transplantation to prevent atelectasis. Alternative support modes like bilevel positive airway pressure should be applied to ensure lung recruitment after extubation.

This study has several limitations. The sample size of the ECMO + No MV group was small, and the data was obtained from a single center. However, PSM was applied to minimize intergroup bias. Furthermore, our analysis could not include a central determinant of early postoperative respiratory status of primary graft dysfunction (PGD) for analysis, which due to the varying circumstances regarding the need for postoperative ECMO support. Additionally, our institutional practice of utilizing a relatively small 21F outflow catheter may restrict maximum flow adequacy in patients requiring higher extracorporeal support. But 21F is a commonly recommended size, which allowed to maintain baseline blood flow equivalent to 60% of cardiac output for patients with a median BMI of 20 in our study. Finally, this retrospective cohort study only assessed the survival within the follow-up 12 months and did not evaluate the long-term impact of awake ECMO, which warrants prospective study.

Conclusions

It suggests that avoiding invasive MV through the application of awake ECMO may be a feasible and alternative clinical approach to traditional intubated non-awake ECMO after LTx. The intraoperative conditions and rehabilitation of patients who underwent extubation with awake ECMO support were similar to those of patients who underwent ECMO weaning first in OR and continuously MV.
