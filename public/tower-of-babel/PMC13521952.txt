Hybrid bioelectrochemical process enables hierarchical C, N, and P utilization towards negative carbon emission wastewater treatment
Authors: Chao Li, Yamei Ma, Chengcheng Ji, Jing Zhang, Xiayu Yuan, Kexin Yi, Wulin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBioelectrochemical system (BES) can provide sustainable solutions for wastewater treatment through lower energy consumption, reduced carbon emissions, and nutrient capture and reuse. Herein, we propose a hybrid bioelectrochemical assembly (HBA)-centered purification-power-cultivation process to achieve hierarchical C, N, and P metabolism, graded power generation, and reusable water production. The hybridization of abiotic and biotic cathode BESs in HBA enables hierarchical C metabolism, with effluent COD directly meeting the Class Ⅲ surface water standard. The customized water-permeable biofilm separators sequentially create anaerobic-anoxic-aerobic zones within HBA, enabling complete N removal. Subsequently, the P-rich tailwater of HBA is channelled into a hydroponic irrigation system for P utilization, ultimately producing reusable clean water. Notably, a high power density of 0.43 W m–2 is obtained, and this process achieves a net carbon emission of –0.413 kg-CO2 m–3. This study presents a prototype for sustainable wastewater purification, particularly in low-resource areas with carbon emission reduction and carbon neutrality targets.Subject terms: Pollution remediation, Chemical engineering, Bioremediation

Introduction

Wastewater treatment is crucial to the global water cycle and it is intrinsically linked to sustainable development of society1,2. Conventional wastewater treatment is historically focused on the removal of three main components of organic carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P)3,4. These processes typically use multiple separate processes, resulting in high energy demand and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Specifically, it consumes 3–5% of global electricity production5,6 and contributes 2–5% to anthropogenic GHG emissions7,8. However, wastewater contains organic C that could be a potential bioenergy source as well as a nonrenewable P resource, and thus new processes are of interest that can achieve sustainable wastewater treatment and resource recovery9,10. Currently, contingent upon the emerging requirements on sustainable development and carbon emission reduction goals, there raises an urgent need to shift from traditional pollutant removal to sustainable alternatives2,11, transitioning from a single purification function to multi-target energy or resource recovery12.

Wastewater purification is essentially a process involving electron transfer coupled with pollutant conversion. Traditional processes remove organics through intensive microbial catabolism, leading to a major waste of electrons and energy. In contrast, the bioelectrochemical system (BES) could spatially decouple anodic oxidation of organic C and cathodic reduction of oxygen, nitrate, etc., enabling the aerobic metabolic reaction in an anaerobic pathway13,14. Such metabolic pattern of BES provides great promise of achieving high electron utilization rates for organic C degradation and N conversion, and harvesting electricity, which is advantageous over traditional technologies14,15. For example, a modularized BES achieves an ultra-short hydraulic retention time (HRT) of 2 h for organic C removal in low-strength wastewater purification16, and another pilot-scale BES doubles organic C removal loading compared to the traditional activated sludge process17. However, previous BES designs mostly focus on the removal of C or N, without fully tailoring electron flow from C-oxidation to N-reduction to realize optimal C/N removal and system-level energy output.

The two main types of BES are abiotic cathode BES (AcBES) and biotic cathode BES (BcBES), which differ in terms of suitable wastewater, energy efficiency, and effluent quality. AcBES can achieve high coulombic efficiency for organic C conversion and electron utilization because the biotic anode executes a solely anaerobic metabolic process and the abiotic cathode minimizes side reactions18. However, AcBES typically requires a high organic concentration (Chemical oxygen demand, COD, > 200 mg L−1) to provide sufficient electrons and protons for exoelectrogen growth and to maintain a low anode working potential, which often leads to insufficient organic removal and failure to directly meet effluent discharge standards19,20. In addition, AcBES cannot remove N efficiently due to suppressed denitrification at the oxygen-rich cathode and the lack of a suitable aerobic environment for nitrification21,22. In contrast, BcBES employs a biotic cathode where biofilm uses electrons from anodic C oxidation to reduce nitrate to N2, enabling simultaneous C and N removal23,24. However, BcBES are not suitable for treating high-strength wastewater because excessive organic loading normally induces the proliferation of non-electroactive microbes at the cathode. This inhibits cathode function, disrupts redox balance, and reduces denitrification efficiency, ultimately leading to low coulombic efficiency20,25,26. Therefore, rational design of BES with guided electron flow, particularly by integrating the benefits of AcBES and BcBES, is critical for achieving effective C/N removal, optimal energy recovery, and broad wastewater treatment capabilities.

In this article, we develop a hybrid bioelectrochemical assembly (HBA) that integrates both AcBES and BcBES modules in a single BES, with these modules hydraulically interconnected by customized biofilm separators. The HBA device is then coupled with a hydroponic irrigation system to realize a purification-power-cultivation concept. The primary goal of this study is to leverage guided electron flow to achieve complete removal and recovery of C, N, and P within a short HRT via a hierarchical metabolism design. Secondary objectives include enhancing bioelectrical energy output through a graded energy recovery design and ultimately producing reusable water, thereby contributing to net-negative carbon emissions. Overall, this process is expected to provide a sustainable alternative to traditional wastewater treatment processes by emphasizing nutrient and energy recovery, especially in remote and low-resource rural areas, and to shift the paradigm of wastewater treatment towards negative carbon emission.

Results

Pilot-scale hybrid bioelectrochemical assembly (HBA) culturing and operation

The HBA device, which consisted of four AcBES modules (Rxᵃ, x = 1–4) and four BcBES modules (Rxᵇ, x = 1–4), was connected in series between a primary settling tank and a secondary discharge tank (Fig. 1a). Two water-permeable biofilm separators (BSs) created separate anaerobic, anoxic, and aerobic zones within the HBA device (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). After startup (Supplementary Table 1), biofilms were successfully enriched on all biotic electrodes and separators (Fig. 1c). The dissolved oxygen (DO) concentrations confirmed that the different zones were maintained in anaerobic, anoxic, and aerobic states (Fig. 1d), resembling the classic anaerobic-anoxic-oxic process. Correspondingly, the anodes of the Rxa (a_An) and Rxb (b_An) modules, and the cathodes of the Rxb (b_Ca) module were set in series for hierarchical COD degradation, while nitrification and denitrification took place in the b_An and b_Ca zones. The Functional Annotation of Prokaryotic Taxa (FAPROTAX) analysis showed that more than 15% of operational taxonomic units in all biofilms were closely associated with heterotrophic processes (carbon metabolism), while 2.4–7.3% of the total sequence abundances were directly related to nitrogen respiration (Fig. 1e).

Hierarchical C and N metabolism and graded energy production

Electron flow guided hierarchical carbon metabolism

In the HBA device, the anaerobic, anoxic, and aerobic zones are hydraulically connected in series by water-permeable biofilm separators (Fig. 1b). This configuration, leveraging its distinct metabolic niches through a sequential arrangement of units along the hydraulic stream, could achieve hierarchical metabolism of organics with varying concentrations at different zones. Consequently, it enhanced the system’s adaptability to changing organic loadings (Fig. 2a). The HBA device effectively removed organics across a broad COD range of 400–1200 mg L–1 (Fig. 2b). The effluent COD concentrations consistently met the first-grade A standard of the Discharge Standard of Pollutants for Municipal Wastewater Treatment Plants in China (DSPMWTP, GB 18918-2002) at an HRT of 12 h (400–1000 mg L−1). The removal efficiency exceeded 93% (Fig. 2b), and the COD removal rates reached up to 2.3 kg m−3 day−1 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Taking C500 as an example, about 41% of organic matter was metabolized in anaerobic a_An zone (Fig. 2c). Subsequently, b_An zone reduced COD (32%) to acceptable levels for b_Ca zone (below 80 mg L−1), and further in-depth purification (22%) was achieved through intense aerobic catabolism and anabolism (sludge production) in b_Ca zone (Fig. 2c). During this process, the electron flow distribution map revealed that the organics in a_An and b_An zones were converted for catabolism (mineralization, with denitrification occurring in the b_An zone), power generation, and anabolism (sludge) (Fig. 2d). The sludge yield coefficients were 0.014, 0.012, and 0.109 g-VSS g-COD−1 in a_An, b_An, and b_Ca zones (Supplementary Fig. 4), which implied that approximately 2, 2, and 11% of COD were converted into sludge by microbial anabolism (Fig. 2d). Biofilm composition analysis also confirmed the spatially decoupled metabolic process when the HBA device was used to treat real wastewater (Supplementary Table 2). Specifically, the a_An biofilm harbored a consortium dominated by fermenting microbes and exoelectrogens (Fig. 2f). The former performed the hydrolysis and acidification of the complex organics, while the latter metabolized the resulting simple organics for current production (Fig. 2f). In the b_An biofilm, exoelectrogens were dominant species (Supplementary Table 2), further degrading these simple organics to generate bioelectricity, thereby reducing the organic load from the upstream wastewater. In the b_Ca biofilm, aerobic bacteria, capable of metabolizing residual organics, were highly enriched, thereby achieving in-depth purification of upstream wastewater. Notably, the COD removal analysis at component-level of the HBA device indicated that the efficiency of a singly-installed module is severely limited, highlighting the necessity of this hybrid design (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Synergistic nitrogen conversion mechanism

The water-permeable biofilm separators (BS and b_BS) separated the HBA device into independent anaerobic, anoxic, and aerobic zones without interruption of hydraulic continuity, in which aeration-driven internal circulation enabled the exchange of nitrogen species between the anoxic b_An zone and the aerobic b_Ca zone (Fig. 1b). Consequently, the HBA device achieved consistent effluent total nitrogen (TN) below 15 mg L−1, meeting the first-grade A discharge standard, with an influent TN of approximately 40 mg L−1 (Supplementary Fig. 6)—an influent concentration representative of typical municipal wastewater27. Notably, when the influent C/N ratio fluctuated within the range of approximately 4–17, the nitrogen removal capacity of the HBA device remained essentially constant, specifically 0.06 ± 0.004 kg m–3 day–1 for TN (Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating that the electron flow available for denitrification in wastewater remained largely consistent. This nitrogen removal was independent of influent COD concentrations, thereby enabling more balanced C and N removal and broadening its applicability under various wastewater treatment conditions.

Taking C500 as an example, the b_Ca zone contributed the most to nitrification, accounting for ~50% of the ammonia nitrogen (NH4+-N) oxidation process, without significant nitrite and nitrate accumulation (Fig. 2e), suggesting that nitrite and nitrate were either directly reduced in situ or recycled into the b_An zone for denitrification. Biodiversity analysis revealed that the biofilms of both separators and electrodes contributed to the nitrogen respiration (Supplementary Fig. 8). For instance, the filamentous, heterotrophic Thiothrix, which possesses denitrification capability, played pivotal roles in organic matter and nitrate conversion and served as a structural skeleton for the biofilms on b_BS and b_Ca. The PICRUSt2 analysis predicting gene expression also revealed putatively complete pathways of nitrogen metabolic processes (Supplementary Fig. 9). The metagenomic sequencing results indicated that nitrification, denitrification, and dissimilatory nitrate reduction were the main pathways for nitrogen metabolism in the b_An and b_Ca biofilms (Fig. 2g). Notably, the enzyme NosZ, specifically catalyzing N2O reduction to N2, showed higher expression abundance, implying lower N2O emission risk of the HBA device (Supplementary Fig. 9)28. Electron balance analysis revealed that approximately 16% of the electrons derived from organics were utilized via a synergy of biological and bioelectrochemical reactions for N removal (Fig. 2d). Overall, by rationally directing electron flow and leveraging synergistic microbial metabolism, the HBA device enabled highly efficient C and N removal from wastewater.

Graded energy production

As independent circuits, Rxa and Rxb modules outputted separate cell voltages along the wastewater stream depending on hierarchical C degradation, enabling graded energy production (Fig. 3a). During testing, the Rxa modules demonstrated greater stability (~550 mV) compared to the fluctuating output voltages of the Rxb modules (290–750 mV) under a wide range of organic concentrations (Fig. 3a). The stability of the Rxa modules originated from their metabolic design specializing in high-concentration organics, thereby maintaining consistent electrode potentials (Supplementary Fig. 10). The highest output voltage of the Rxb modules was reached under C500 condition (Fig. 3a), as their cathodic potential was closest to theoretical potential for ORR (Supplementary Fig. 10)14. Correspondingly, the maximum power density (Pmax) of the Rxa modules remained stably around 0.21 ± 0.01 W m−2, while the Rxb modules reached a peak Pmax of 0.32 ± 0.01 W m−2 at C500 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 11). These corresponded to an energy production of 33 and 25 Wh m−3 in the Rxa and Rxb modules (Supplementary Fig. 12).

During operation periods, output voltage of Rxb modules dropped rapidly when influent COD exceeded 1200 mg L−1. Conversely, a voltage drop also occurred when COD fell below 400 mg L−1 (Fig. 2a). These results indicated an optimal COD range required for the stable operation of the HBA device. Notably, COD levels below 150 mg L−1 destabilized the b_An biofilm, whereas concentrations exceeding 100 mg L−1 inhibited the b_Ca biofilm activity (Fig. 3c). Apparent internal resistance analysis showed that the b_Ca potential fluctuated most dramatically (Supplementary Fig. 13), and its minimum resistance at C500 coincided with the Pmax generation. From field testing results, fluctuations of the b_Ca potentials in high COD potentially resulted from nonfunctional filamentous bulking (Supplementary Fig. 14)25. Thus, the influent COD of the HBA device should be below 1200 mg L–1 to avoid this biofilm bulking. The probe testing demonstrated that the high-COD-induced bulking biofilm highly restricted oxygen diffusion in the radial direction of the b_Ca biofilm (Fig. 3d), thereby limiting cathodic ORR. Based on the foregoing analysis, the optimal COD loadings for balancing wastewater treatment efficiency and energy output performance were estimated to be 0.8 ± 0.24, 0.41 ± 0.19, and 0.05 ± 0.03 kg m–3 day–1 (Supplementary Fig. 15), while the higher COD loadings can be addressed by stacking a_An and b_An electrodes (zones) (Supplementary Fig. 16).

HBA-centered purification-power-cultivation process for real wastewater treatment

Real wastewater purification performance

To achieve complete real wastewater treatment, an integrated purification-power-cultivation process was developed by coupling the HBA device with a hydroponic irrigation system for the utilization of C, N, and P elements, power generation, crop cultivation, and reusable water production (Fig. 4a). The real wastewater was collected from the fine screen of the wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) using a membrane bioreactor (MBR) process at Peking University (Beijing, China) with raw COD of 660 ± 84 mg L−1 and soluble COD (SCOD) of 485 ± 56 mg L−1 (Supplementary Fig. 17). As a result, COD removal reached 97% with a low effluent COD of 20 ± 5 mg L–1 (SCOD: 15 ± 6 mg L−1) during this long-term test (Supplementary Fig. 17). This effluent quality was far below the DSPMWTP discharge standards and met the surface water quality requirements (Class Ⅲ standard, GB 3838-2002, China). The hydrolase activity analysis (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Table 3), metagenomic sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 19), and electron transport system (ETS) activity analysis (Supplementary Fig. 20 and Supplementary Table 4) also confirmed a spatial decoupling of C metabolic processes (hydrolysis and VFAs metabolism) along the wastewater stream in different zones (Supplementary Fig. 21). Effective N removal was also achieved with an NH4+-N removal of 94% and an effluent of 3 ± 2 mg L−1, while those of TN were 82% and 10 ± 3 mg L−1 (Supplementary Fig. 17). The Suspended solids (SS) removal was 99 %, with an effluent SS of 3 ± 1 mg L−1 (Supplementary Fig. 17). The observed sludge yield coefficient was estimated to be 0.103 g-TSS g-COD−1 (or 0.089 g-VSS g-COD−1) (Supplementary Fig. 22), which was significantly lower than the values observed in the activated sludge process (0.5–0.8 g-sludge g-COD–1)29. Notably, all these parameters mentioned above were comparable to, or better than, those from energy-intensive MBR processes (Supplementary Fig. 17). Owing to the low sludge yield of the HBA device, P removal was limited to approximately 25%, leading to a relatively high P concentration in effluent (3.5 mg L–1) (Fig. 4a), which can be characterized as P-rich tailwater.

Improved electron flow enhances mass and energy metabolism rate

Electron flow can be improved by reducing external resistance, which in turn increases the metabolic rate of microbes29. Herein, external loads of Rxa and Rxb modules were adjusted from 500 Ω (R_500) to 15 Ω (Maximum power density point, MPDP_15) to direct electron flow towards improved power output and organic removal rates. Simultaneously, the HRT of the HBA device was halved from 12 to 6 h. As a result, the current densities of Rxa and Rxb modules rapidly increased to 535 ± 48 and 677 ± 59 mA m–2, which were 22.6 and 21.3 times higher than those under R_500 (Fig. 4b). The Pmax values of Rxa and Rxb modules were measured to be 0.14 ± 0.02 and 0.29 ± 0.01 W m−2, yielding an overall power density of 0.43 W m−2 (Fig. 4c). Meanwhile, a coulombic recovery of 9.7% was recorded (Supplementary Fig. 23), indicating that one-tenth of the chemical energy in wastewater can be recovered by the HBA device. Notably, the COD removal still met the requirements of surface water discharge standard (Class Ⅲ standard, GB 3838-2002, China) during long-term tests, while the effluent of SCOD, TN, NH4+-N, and SS were also comparable to, or better than, those from the MBR process (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 24). Compared with reported pilot-scale BES systems17,19,30–37, the HBA device achieved the highest pollutant removal and an energy output of 0.43 W m−2 with an increase of over 100% (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 5).

Phosphorus-containing tailwater as irrigation and replenishment water

By coupling with a hydroponic irrigation system, untreated P could be readily utilized by the crops. The discharged P concentration of the rice hydroponic system decreased to below 0.2 mg L−1 (Class III standard, GB 3838-2002) within only half a day with a P removal of 96%, which was higher than the 25% removal observed for wheat seedlings (Fig. 4f). After 7 days of hydroponic irrigation, the rice seedlings were approximately 43% taller than those irrigated with surface water, which was associated with a high P uptake rate exceeding 5 mg L−1 day−1 per 1000 seedlings (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 25). Subsequently, the P-depleted effluent was reused as replenishment water in an aquatic system (carp, koi, river snails, freshwater shrimp, and plants), and the ecosystem remained stable over a six-month observation period (Supplementary Fig. 26). Overall, coupling the HBA device with hydroponic irrigation and aquatic systems provided a sustainable approach to P utilization, enabling the effective recycling of P and treated water to maximize the value of domestic wastewater.

Energy balance, cost analysis, and carbon emission evaluation

Graded power production and operational energy balance

The bioelectricity yield of the HBA device was estimated from the Pmax of Rxa and Rxb modules. As a result, it produced an overall output energy of 4.5 × 10–2 kWh m–3, which could theoretically offset the energy consumed by reactor feeding (2.1 × 10–4 kWh m–3), biotic cathode aeration (1.99 × 10−3 kWh m−3), and sludge disposal (1.77 × 10–3 kWh m–3) (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 6). Ultimately, this system achieved a net energy output (4.1 × 10–2 kWh m–3) (Fig. 5a), which is superior to those reported in existing studies and represents a more advanced solution compared to conventional energy-intensive wastewater treatment processes. For example, the energy consumption of MBR was reported to be 0.5–0.7 kWh m−3 (Fig. 5a)38.

Technical and economic analysis

Cost is a critical factor for technology deployment, thus the capital cost of the HBA device was estimated (Supplementary Table 7). From the results, the electrode manufacturing constituted the largest portion of total cost (61.4%), comprising the cost of carbon brushes ($582.9 m−3) and rolling cathodes ($557.8 m−3). Notably, the customized water-permeable biofilm separators accounted for only 4.8% of total cost (Fig. 5b). In addition, the HBA tank was built using polycarbonate at a cost of $514.3 m−3 (27.7%). Other accessories, such as resistors, wires, connection tubes, bolts, and nuts, contributed a minor fraction (~6.1%). Compared with previous studies, the HBA device showed a much lower cost of ~$1859 m−3 (Fig. 5b). Notably, this estimated value is based on current laboratory-scale prices. The capital cost of the HBA device could be significantly reduced by using alternative, lower-cost materials (Fig. 5c).

Carbon emission intensity evaluation

Reducing carbon emissions has now evolved into a key consideration in developing new wastewater treatment processes39. Thus, true N2O and CH4 emission factors (EFN2O and EFCH4) of the HBA device were evaluated based on the total emissions of N2O and CH4 and the removal amounts of TN and COD (Supplementary Table 8). Gas composition analysis indicated that the N2O emission originated primarily from the b_An zone, whereas CH4 emission occurred in both the a_An and b_An zones (Supplementary Fig. 27). Accordingly, the true EFN2O and EFCH4 of the HBA device were determined to be 0.0021 kg-N2O kg-TN−1 and 0.0013 kg-CH4 kg-COD−1, which were much lower than the values recommended by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) for conventional biological processes (Fig. 5d). Ultimately, the carbon equivalent emissions of N2O and CH4 were determined to be 0.0191 and 0.0363 kg-CO2 m–3, which constituted the direct carbon emissions (DCE) of the HBA device (Fig. 5e). By comparison, the DCE of MBR was estimated at 0.104 kg-CO2 m−3 (Supplementary Table 8). The indirect carbon emissions (IDCE) are multifaceted, encompassing emissions resulting from electricity consumption, chemical usage, and other sources40. Using a conservative carbon equivalent emission factor for electricity production (Supplementary Table 9), the current system, owing to its hierarchical treatment design, significantly reduced carbon emission from electricity consumption (0.0014 kg-CO2 m−3) compared to MBR process (0.288 kg-CO2 m−3) (Supplementary Table 10). Low sludge yield also reduced chemical consumption for sludge dewatering (0.007 kg-CO2 m−3), while MBR has higher emissions (0.035 kg-CO2 m–3) (Fig. 5e). Additionally, the benefits from P (−0.004 kg-CO2 m−3) and tailwater recycling (−0.446 kg-CO2 m−3) also offset the electricity and chemical emissions (Fig. 5e). Overall, this HBA-centered purification-power-cultivation process achieved a negative carbon emission of −0.413 kg-CO2 m–3, presenting high environmental advantages compared to conventional wastewater treatment technologies41–46. For example, the carbon equivalent emission of MBR can be as high as 0.53 kg-CO2 m−3 (Fig. 5f).

Discussion

Aiming for negative carbon emission water purification, we proposed a HBA-centered purification-power-cultivation process for hierarchical C, N, P utilization, graded bioelectricity generation, and reusable water production. In the HBA device, the customized water-permeable biofilm separators are the core components. These component consistently maintained exceptional water permeability and cross-separator ion transport performance throughout long-term operation without any clogging (Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29), thereby establishing a robust foundation for the sustained BES efficiency. In the unlikely event of clogging, its low-cost, modular plug-in design allows for easy maintenance through direct replacement, starving treatment (temporary operation pause)25, or artificial cleaning. A recently developed abiotic cathode-biofilm separator assembly was also used to replace typical simple abiotic cathode (Fig. 1b)47, which can prevent biofilm fouling and maintain stable power output of the Rxa modules throughout long-term operation (Supplementary Fig. 30). Compared with reported configurations (Supplementary Table 11), this hybrid design established hydraulic continuity within the HBA device. Along the wastewater stream, the customized water-permeable biofilm separators enabled the creation of distinct metabolic zones (anaerobic, anoxic, and aerobic) that align with the inherent redox gradients and the stepwise degradation process (hydrolysis and acidification, VFAs metabolism, and aerobic metabolism) of complex organics (Supplementary Fig. 31). This spatial organization not only intensified C metabolism but also enabled the establishment of dual, functionally differentiated redox circuits for increased energy recovery.

The HBA device successfully integrated the AcBES and BcBES modules, thereby realizing hierarchical C metabolism decoupling along the wastewater stream, enabling effluent COD to directly meet the surface water class Ⅲ standard during long-term test (GB3838-2002, China) at a short HRT (6 h). Meanwhile, the customized water-permeable biofilm separator (b_BS) enabled internal recirculation between the b_An and b_Ca zones, which was crucial for achieving robust and efficient nitrogen removal during long-term operation. Recently, a customized BES with three functionalized chambers has also highlighted a long-term stable strategy for simultaneous water purification and ammonium recovery, providing a flexible pathway to N recycling48. Temperature control tests indicated that the operating temperature needs to be maintained above 12 °C; below this temperature, the bioactivity of functional microbes and pollutant removal are inhibited (Supplementary Fig. 32). After C/N removal, the P-rich tailwater was sequentially utilized in a hydroponic irrigation system, which promoted seedling growth by ~43% and ultimately produced reusable clean water. Notably, when irrigation is not feasible, numerous flexible strategies exist to remove or recover P from P-rich tailwater. For example, a P management tank can be integrated after the HBA device (Supplementary Fig. 33), where P can either be removed on-site via conventional adsorption, precipitation, or flocculation methods, or be directly converted into value-added products such as FePO4 and MgNH4PO449,50. The HBA device also demonstrated remarkable performance in heavy metal control from wastewater. The effluent concentrations were far below discharge standards (GB 3838-2002, China), with the vast majority of heavy metals accumulated in the sludge (Supplementary Table 12). Consequently, the heavy metal content in the cultivated biomass was far below the safety threshold (GB13078-2017, China), ensuring the feasibility of the irrigation practice51. In addition, disinfection tests showed that pathogenic bacteria in HBA effluent were effectively controlled (Supplementary Fig. 34), which resulted in minimal incidence of plant disease during the hydroponic cultivation period. Overall, the HBA-centered process has demonstrated effective wastewater purification performance. Future studies should consider the negative impact of inhibitory components (e.g., heavy metals and antibiotics), particularly those present at higher concentrations, on BES performance in more complex wastewater streams, such as pharmaceutical and petrochemical wastewater.

For energy recovery, the Rxᵃ and Rxᵇ modules function as independent primary cell circuits. Within these circuits, the a_Ca and b_Ca cathodes catalyze the ORR via chemically and biologically driven processes, while the a_An and b_An anodes catalyze the oxidation of organics via the metabolism of exoelectrogens. As a result, a high power density of 0.43 W m−2 was achieved via HBA’s graded energy recovery design. This power output can theoretically cover BES’s electricity consumption and yield a net energy output (Fig. 5a). Herein, the electricity is generated as direct current (DC), which can be used to drive low-power pumps or supply low-voltage sensors and lighting35,52. At the current stage, given the limited treatment capacity of pilot-scale BES, conversion to alternating current (AC) for grid feeding is not yet economically viable. However, for future full-scale BES applications, scaling effects are expected to significantly amplify the total electricity production, making AC conversion and grid integration economically feasible.

It is worth noting that the manufacturing cost of the HBA device is estimated at ~$1,859 m−3, which is far below that of comparable products (Fig. 5b). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the cost impact of separator replacement is negligible due to the use of inexpensive biofilm separators (Supplementary Fig. 35a). The most significant cost factors are the investments in carbon brush electrodes (a_An, b_An, and b_Ca) and abiotic rolling cathode assembly (a_Ca) (Supplementary Fig. 35b, c), which represent the key opportunity for cost reduction through the use of low-cost materials or design simplification. For example, Zhang et al. proposed starch-based carbon foams as the anode of BES, which increased the power density by about 160% and had a low-cost of ~$5 m−3, compared to carbon brush electrodes (~$380 m−3) (Supplementary Table 7)53. This carbon foam possesses high mechanical strength, robust chemical and physical stability, and good reproducibility for large-scale application. Regarding rolling cathodes (a_Ca), the electrode fixing assembly constitutes the largest portion (21.5%) (Supplementary Table 7). This is primarily due to the use of expensive polycarbonate with a relatively complex fixing structure. Notably, a_Ca cathode can also be replaced by recently developed phase-inversion electrodes that are cheaper and easier to produce on a large scale54, thereby reducing its cost. Apart from electrode fabrication, the reactor tank also employs polycarbonate. Therefore, it is advisable to use more affordable materials (e.g., high-density polyvinylchloride, PVC17) and simplify the assembly structure to further reduce costs (Supplementary Fig. 36). Accordingly, the projected capital cost for future full-scale application is estimated at $680 m−3 (Supplementary Table 13), which is comparable to current mainstream technologies (e.g., MBR)26. Notably, the expected revenue from reclaimed water was conservatively estimated at $0.15 m−3 based on reported cases (Supplementary Table 14). Consequently, the investment payback period for the HBA device is projected to be 3 years via the use of low-cost materials in future full-scale manufacturing; by the tenth year of operation, it is expected to generate a profit of approximately $0.1 m−3 (Fig. 5c), demonstrating its promising application potential.

According to carbon emission analysis results, the HBA-centered purification-power-cultivation process achieved a negative carbon emission of –0.413 kg-CO2 m−3, which is attributed to the fact that the N2O and CH4 emission factors of the HBA device are far lower than those of conventional biological processes. Beyond wastewater purification, this BES-based process can also serve as a technical platform for carbon valorization (e.g., converting inorganic carbon to renewable fuels or chemicals). All these highlight the remarkable advantage of BES in carbon emission reduction55. Overall, this BES-centered process offers a viable alternative to traditional energy-intensive wastewater purification technologies, and establishes a sustainable water-nutrient cycle paradigm particularly suited for resource- and energy-limited rural regions, thereby contributing to carbon-emission reduction and carbon-neutrality goals. Notably, the HBA-centered processes may also be particularly applicable for medium-sized to large-scale centralized WWTPs, since scale effect can significantly amplify the benefits in terms of sludge yield reduction, reusable water production, resource recovery, and carbon emissions reduction. In the future, efforts should focus on addressing the challenges in terms of manufacturing of large-scale facilities, clarifying investment and financial returns, and progressively promoting the application of these technologies.

Methods

Fabrication of the hybrid bioelectrochemical assembly (HBA)

The HBA device consisted of four abiotic cathode (Rxa) and four biotic cathode (Rxb) modules (Fig. 1a). All modules were housed in a 50 L plexiglass tank (L: 46 cm × W: 33 cm × H: 45 cm), with each module secured in place by fixing channels within the tank (Supplementary Fig. 1). Each Rxa module consisted of two rolling abiotic cathodes (a_Ca, L: 18 cm × W: 12 cm) connected to three graphite fiber brush anodes (a_An, Φ: 5 cm × L: 42 cm). The abiotic cathodes were constructed in an electrode-separator assembly configuration (See details in Supplementary Fig. 1b)47, in which the abiotic cathode and the biofilm separator were combined with zero spacing. In the Rxb modules, both anodes (b_An) and cathodes (b_Ca) were constructed from three graphite fiber brushes (Φ: 5 cm × L: 42 cm). Aeration strips were installed at the bottom of b_Ca zones. The nylon fabric (0.25 mm thickness, 1800 PPI) was fixed by the perforated plate to serve as a skeleton matrix for the biofilm separator (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 28). All modules were connected to external resistors to close the circuits. The HBA-centered process consisted of a primary settling tank, an HBA reactor, and a hydroponic tank. The HBA device was operated at room temperature (18–22 °C). During operation, raw wastewater was precisely pumped (BT 100, Langer Co., China) into the HBA device; subsequently, the effluent flowed into the hydroponic irrigation tank on demand, and finally the purified clean water was used to replenish the water requirement of an aquatic ecosystem (50 L).

Analysis of water quality parameters

Water quality parameters were determined using a series of rapid test kits (HACH, USA), including COD (2125925-CN), TN (2714100-CN), NO3−-N (2605345-CN), NO2−-N (2107569-CN), and TP (2742645-CN). The NH4+-N concentration was determined using the Nessler’s reagent spectrophotometry (HJ 535-2009, China). SS were quantified using the gravimetric method (GB11901-89, China), which was also used to measure the excess sludge yields. The DO concentration in the electrolytes, as well as its evolution in the radial direction of the b_Ca biofilms, was measured using a non-consumptive fiber optic DO probe (NeoFox, FOSPOR).

Characterization of bioelectrochemical performance

The output voltage (U) of Rxa and Rxb modules was recorded using a data acquisition system (DAQ6510, Keithley, USA). The current density (j, A m−2) was calculated as j = U/(RA), where R is the external resistance and A is the projected area of the abiotic cathode (equal to the area of separator). The power density (p, W m−2) was calculated as p = Uj. The polarization and power density curves were obtained through stepwise reduction of external resistors (open circuit, 500, 100, 51, 20, 15, 11, 7.8, 5.6, and 3.4 Ω). Polarization curves of electrodes (a_Ca, a_An, b_An, and b_Ca) were plotted using the recorded electrode potential values. The apparent internal resistance (Rin) of each electrode was determined by the electrode potential slope method17, whereby the slope at the point near maximum power density represents the electrode’s Rin (Current in A, voltage in V).

Measurement of hydrolytic enzyme activity

Activities of protease, α-glucosidase, alkaline phosphatase, and acid phosphatase were assessed using established methods (See details in Supplementary Table 15). The activity of α-amylase was measured using the α-Amylase Activity Assay Kit (Micromethod, D799324, Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The electron transfer system (ETS) activity in various biofilms was evaluated using the 2-(p-iodophenyl)-3-(p-nitrophenyl)-5-phenyltetrazolium chloride (INT) electron transport system (INT-ETS) method56, with the absorbance measured at 485 nm on a spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan) (See details in Supplementary Table 15). All hydrolase activities (U) were normalized to the mass of the electrode (U mg–1).

Analysis of microbial composition and function

The biomass on the electrodes and separators was estimated using the Bicinchoninic Acid kit (C503051, Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China)57, in which the biomass was quantified in terms of protein content, assuming that proteins comprise 55% of biomass by dry weight. The composition and function of the biofilms were analyzed using the 16S rRNA gene sequencing (primers: 338 F and 806 R) and metagenomic sequencing methods. All biofilm sample pretreatment and testing were performed by Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd., and all biodiversity and composition analyses were conducted using their cloud platform (www.majorbio.com).

Evaluation of energy balance and carbon emissions

Energy balance of this system was calculated using a standard method17, which consisted of the electrical energy generated by the HBA and theoretical electrical energy consumption for operation (See details in Supplementary Table 6). True emission factors of CH4 and N2O were calculated based on the total emissions from the HBA device and the removal amounts of COD and TN (See details in Supplementary Table 8). The CH4 and N2O concentrations in the collected emission samples were determined using a high-precision gas analyzer (G2508, Picarro, Inc., USA).

Statistics and reproducibility

The experimental data were analyzed by two-tailed Student’s t-test. *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, and ***p ≤ 0.001 were considered to be statistically significant. No data were excluded from the analyses.
