Sample efficient inductive matrix completion with noise and inexact side information
Yuepeng Yang, Cong Ma

Abstract
Low-rank matrix completion is a widely studied problem with many variants. Inductive matrix completion (IMC) incorporates row and column side information to significantly narrow the search space. Prior work falls into two regimes: methods that exploit this structure to achieve reduced sample complexity but only in noiseless settings, and methods that handle noise but require sample complexity matching the ambient matrix dimension—forfeiting the sample efficiency that side information should provide. In this paper, we close this gap by studying noisy IMC with a nonconvex projected gradient descent algorithm with spectral initialization. Our main technical contribution is establishing a regularity condition for the IMC loss function that holds at the reduced sample complexity determined by the effective problem size—scaling with the side information dimension a a rather than the ambient dimension n n . This directly yields linear convergence and an estimation error that both depend only on the effective problem size rather than the ambient matrix dimension. We further extend our analysis to the inexact side information setting, demonstrating that the reduced sample complexity is maintained and the estimation error is order-optimal with respect to the inexactness of the side information. Extensive simulations and real-world experiments on the MovieLens dataset validate our theoretical findings.

1.2
Related Work

Matrix completion without side information.

The classical matrix completion problem has been studied extensively
over the past two decades. In the noiseless setting, pioneering convex
approaches based on nuclear norm minimization established exact recovery
under incoherence and near-optimal sampling conditions
[
2
,
3
,
26
]
.
In the noisy setting, subsequent work showed that one can achieve
stable recovery and near-optimal estimation error guarantees from
partial noisy observations
[
1
,
25
,
9
]
.
These results provide the benchmark statistical rates for recovering
a low-rank matrix when no side information is available.

There is also a large literature on computationally efficient nonconvex
algorithms for matrix completion, including alternating minimization,
spectral initialization followed by gradient refinement, and leave-one-out
analyses
[
18
,
17
,
15
,
35
,
22
,
12
,
29
]
.
Taken together, these works give a sharp picture of both the statistical
and algorithmic behavior of ordinary matrix completion.

Inductive matrix completion with exact side information.

Inductive matrix completion incorporates side information on the rows
and columns to restrict the target matrix to a lower-dimensional subspace.
When the side information exactly spans the true row and column spaces,
one expects recovery to require substantially fewer observations than
ordinary matrix completion, with the sample complexity governed by
the effective feature dimensions rather than the ambient matrix size.
Early theoretical work confirmed this intuition in noiseless settings.
In particular, gradient-based and convex-style approaches were shown
to achieve exact recovery with significantly reduced sample complexity
under suitable incoherence and conditioning assumptions
[
30
]
.
Related algorithmic developments include alternating-minimization-type
procedures and other efficient approaches tailored to the inductive
structure
[
14
,
32
]
.

More recent work has also studied nonconvex formulations of inductive
matrix completion from an optimization perspective. For example,
[
37
]

analyzes the landscape of a nonconvex IMC formulation and shows the
absence of spurious local minima under suitable conditions, while

[
32
]
develops a multi-phase Procrustes flow method
with favorable sample and runtime guarantees. These results clarify
the optimization landscape of IMC in exact-side-information settings.

Inductive matrix completion with noisy observations.

Compared with the noiseless exact-side-information setting, the theory
of inductive matrix completion is less complete when observations
are noisy or the side information is imperfect. One line of work studies
noisy IMC and establishes estimation guarantees under general side
information models, but the resulting rates or sample requirements
do not fully capture the reduced effective dimension suggested by
the inductive structure
[
11
]
. More recently,
[
4
]

analyzes a nonconvex formulation of noisy IMC and proves a sharp estimation
error guarantee. However, its sample complexity scales at the level
required for ordinary matrix completion, and therefore does not fully
realize the sample-efficiency gains that side information should enable.
From a complementary perspective,
[
19
]

studies generalization guarantees with a Lipschitz-bounded loss function for IMC in a low-noise regime, which is not directly comparable to the sharp optimization and estimation guarantees sought here.

Other approaches to matrix completion with side information.

Broader work on side information for matrix completion considers settings
where auxiliary covariates are informative but do not exactly determine
the target row and column spaces, as well as application-driven feature
constructions in recommendation, multi-label learning, and graph-based
problems
[
28
,
36
,
31
,
27
]
.
While they address different formulations, they are closely related in spirit in that they incorporate side information into matrix completion.

Problem Setup

We study the problem of recovering a low-rank matrix from partial,
noisy observations when side information about its column and row spaces
is available. This section formalizes the model and assumptions, introduces
the notation used throughout the paper, and presents the projected
gradient descent algorithm at the heart of our approach.

2.1
Model formulation with exact side information

Recall that in inductive matrix completion we observe a subset of entries
of a noisy low-rank matrix
\bm{L}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
.
More precisely, if
\Omega\subset[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]
denotes the
set of observed entries and
\bm{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}

is the noise matrix, then the data take the form

\bm{M}=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{L}^{\star}+\bm{E}).

In addition, we are given side information matrices

\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times a_{1}}
and
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times a_{2}}

that encode prior information about the column and row spaces of

\bm{L}^{\star}

2
2

2

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
have orthonormal columns.
. The central idea is that if these side-information
matrices are informative, then one can recover
\bm{L}^{\star}
by
estimating a lower-dimensional core matrix rather than working directly
in the ambient space.

We first consider the setting with exact side information. More rigorously,
we assume the following to hold:

Assumption 1
.

\mathrm{col}(\bm{L}^{\star})\subset\mathrm{col}(\bm{X})
and
\mathrm{row}(\bm{L}^{\star})\subset\mathrm{col}(\bm{Y}).

This immediately implies that
\bm{L}^{\star}=\bm{X}\bm{Z}^{\star}\bm{Y}^{\top}

for some rank-
r
matrix
\bm{Z}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{a_{1}\times a_{2}}

and the observed matrix can be written as

\bm{M}=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{X}\bm{Z}^{\star}\bm{Y}^{\top}+\bm{E}).

We let
n\coloneqq n_{\max}\coloneqq n_{1}\vee n_{2}
and
n_{\min}\coloneqq n_{1}\wedge n_{2}
,
where
\vee
and
\wedge
denote the maximum and minimum, respectively.
To enable the analysis, a recurring concept that is used in matrix
completion literature (e.g.,
[
2

1
]

Definition 1

(incoherence)
.

We say a rank-

is

\|\bm{U}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu r}{n_{1}}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\bm{V}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu r}{n_{2}}}

where
\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}
is the compact SVD of
\bm{M}
.
Here for any matrix
\bm{A}
,
\|\bm{A}\|_{2,\infty}\coloneqq\max_{i}\|\bm{A}_{i,\cdot}\|_{2}

is the 2-to-infinity norm (i.e. maximum row norm).

Assumption 2
.

Throughout this paper we assume the following properties
hold:

(a)

(Uniform sampling)
For each
entry
(i,j)\in[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]
,
(i,j)
is observed in
\Omega
independently with
probability
p>0
.

(b)

(Singular values)
Denote
the maximum and minimum non-zero singular value of
\bm{Z}^{\star}

as
\sigma_{\max}
,
\sigma_{\min}
and let the condition number
be
\kappa\coloneqq\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}
.

(c)

(Incoherence)
We
assume
\bm{L}^{\star}
is
\mu_{0}
-incoherent,
\bm{X}
is
\mu_{1}
-incoherent,
and
\bm{Y}
is
\mu_{2}
-incoherent.

Informally, a small value of the incoherence parameter
\mu
means that the singular vectors
of a matrix are reasonably spread out across rows and columns, rather than
concentrated on a few entries.
The incoherence
of
\bm{L}^{\star}
is standard in matrix completion and rules out
low-rank matrices whose mass is too concentrated on a
small number of entries. The incoherence of
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}

ensures that the side information is sufficiently spread out across
coordinates, which is what allows the effective problem size to be
smaller than the ambient dimension.

Remark 1
.

In most cases we will treat
\kappa
and
\mu_{0}

as constant order parameters. One can also show that
1\leq\mu_{1}\leq n_{1}/a_{1}

and
1\leq\mu_{2}\leq n_{2}/a_{2}
. As we will see later,
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}

control how informative the side information is: the smaller the quantities

\mu_{1}a_{1}
and
\mu_{2}a_{2}
are, the smaller the effective problem
size becomes in our sample complexity bounds.

2.2
Main Algorithm: Projected Gradient Descent

Our algorithm is inspired by a standard nonconvex formulation for the
ordinary matrix completion problem, in which a rank-
r
matrix is
parameterized as a product of two thin factors and fitted to the observed
entries by minimizing

\min_{\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r},\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r}}\frac{1}{2p}\|\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{U}\bm{V}^{\top}-\bm{M})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Despite being a nonconvex optimization problem, this formulation is well understood to be statistically and computationally efficient for ordinary matrix completion; see, for example,

[
17
,
15
,
35
]
. Our IMC
algorithm follows the same logic, but exploits the side information

\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
to work in the smaller latent space.

Under Assumption
1
, the unknown matrix takes
the form
\bm{L}^{\star}=\bm{X}\bm{Z}^{\star}\bm{Y}^{\top}
. Thus,
instead of estimating all
n_{1}n_{2}
entries of
\bm{L}^{\star}

directly, we can estimate the much smaller core matrix
\bm{Z}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{a_{1}\times a_{2}}
.
Writing the compact SVD of
\bm{Z}^{\star}
as

\bm{U}^{\star}\bm{\Sigma}^{\star}\bm{V}^{\star\top}
, it is natural
to factor

\bm{Z}^{\star}=\bm{A}^{\star}\bm{B}^{\star\top},\qquad\text{where}\quad\bm{A}^{\star}\coloneqq\bm{U}^{\star}\bm{\Sigma}^{\star 1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{a_{1}\times r},\quad\bm{B}^{\star}\coloneqq\bm{V}^{\star}\bm{\Sigma}^{\star 1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{a_{2}\times r}

This turns IMC into
the task of fitting two lower-dimensional factors. Motivated by this viewpoint, we search over matrices

\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{a_{1}\times r}
and
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{a_{2}\times r}

by minimizing
the nonconvex objective function

f(\bm{A},\bm{B})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2p}\|\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{X}\bm{A}\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{Y}^{\top}-\bm{M})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{16}\|\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}-\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{B}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

(3)

The term
\frac{1}{16}\|\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}-\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{B}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

here acts as a balancing term to keep the singular values of
\bm{A},\bm{B}

close. Inspired by the success of analogous methods for ordinary matrix completion (e.g.
[
35
]
), we solve this minimization problem with projected gradient
descent with spectral initialization in Algorithm
1
.
The spectral initialization provides a starting point close enough
to the ground truth to guarantee convergence, followed by projected
gradient descent steps that improve the factor fit to the observed
entries. Concretely, the update equations in Algorithm
1

are gradient steps for the objective
f(\bm{A},\bm{B})
, followed
by projection onto an incoherence-preserving constraint set.

3.1
Main Result

We start by introducing the central quantity in the analysis. The
effective noise size

\gamma_{E}\coloneqq\frac{1}{p}\left\|\bm{X}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{E})\bm{Y}\right\|,

\bm{X}

\bm{Y}

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumption
1

and
2

size and the noise level satisfy

3.2
Analysis

The proof of Theorem
1
rests on two key lemmas that
are of independent interest. Lemma
1
establishes
that the inductive spectral initialization lands within the basin
of attraction of the ground truth at the reduced sample complexity.
Lemma
2
—the central technical contribution of this
work—establishes a regularity condition for the IMC loss function
at the same reduced sample complexity, directly yielding linear convergence.
Together, these two lemmas show that the favorable local landscape
properties of the standard matrix completion objective persist in
the IMC setting, but at the substantially lower sample rate determined
by the effective problem size
\mu_{1}a_{1}\vee\mu_{2}a_{2}

regularity condition also forms the foundation for the inexact side
information analysis in Section

We begin by introducing notation used in the proofs. For any two

When referring to the

As with many nonconvex algorithms, the proof has two main components:

Suppose that

4.3
Interpolation Scheme

When the side information is inexact, IMC can outperform non-inductive
MC in the low-sample regime, while MC can become preferable once
the sample size is sufficiently large. A natural way to bridge these
two regimes is to penalize deviation from the side-information subspaces
rather than enforcing it exactly. To this end, we solve the following
nonconvex objective for
\widetilde{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r}

and
\widetilde{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r}
:

\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{\bm{A}},\widetilde{\bm{B}})=\frac{1}{2p}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}\left(\widetilde{\bm{A}}\widetilde{\bm{B}}^{\top}-\bm{M}\right)\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{16}\|\widetilde{\bm{A}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{A}}-\widetilde{\bm{B}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{B}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\widetilde{\bm{A}}\widetilde{\bm{B}}^{\top}-\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{A}}\widetilde{\bm{B}}^{\top}\bm{Y}\bm{Y}^{\top}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

(17)

Here
\lambda\geq 0
controls the strength of the side-information
penalty. When
\lambda=0
, this reduces to the usual non-inductive
MC objective. When
\lambda
is large, the estimate is encouraged
to align closely with the side-information subspaces, so the method
behaves more like IMC.

This interpolation objective provides a pragmatic way to hedge against
side-information misspecification: smaller values of
\lambda
place more weight on
the observed entries more heavily, whereas larger values of
\lambda

exploit the inductive structure more aggressively. We study this scheme
empirically in Section

6
Discussion

This paper shows that inductive matrix completion can retain its main
statistical advantage beyond the noiseless exact-side-information regime.
Under exact side information, we prove that noisy recovery is possible
at sample complexity governed by the side-information dimensions rather
than the ambient matrix size. Under inexact side information, we show
that this reduced sample complexity can still be preserved, with estimation
error controlled by the level of inexactness. We also propose an interpolation
scheme that balances the low-sample efficiency of IMC against the robustness
of ordinary matrix completion to side-information mismatch.

Several important questions remain open for matrix completion with side
information. We highlight a few directions that seem especially worthwhile.

•

The (un)necessity of the projection step.
In our algorithm,
a projection step is used at each iteration to ensure the matrices
of interest are incoherent. Empirically, however, the projection is
inactive in our simulations; see Appendix
F.2
.
This raises the question of whether one can prove comparable guarantees
for Algorithm
1
without projection. A main technical difficulty
that sets IMC apart from non-inductive MC is that the subspace restriction
onto
\mathrm{col}(\bm{X})
and
\mathrm{col}(\bm{Y})
couples the row
and column dependencies induced by the observation pattern. This makes
it difficult to decouple the iterates in the way needed for the powerful
leave-one-out arguments used
in
[
22
,
5
]
for non-inductive MC.

•

Entrywise guarantees and inference.
Our paper establishes
Frobenius-norm error guarantees. It would also be interesting to develop
entrywise error bounds, confidence intervals for individual entries,
and related inferential guarantees, in the spirit of the corresponding
results for non-inductive MC
[
22
,
10
,
9
]
.

•

Partial side information.
In our model for exact side information,
we assume the side information covers the whole column and row space
of
\bm{L}^{\star}
. It would be interesting to understand settings
where the available side information is exact but incomplete, for example
when only a subset of informative features is observed or when only
part of the true subspace is known. Although our inexact-side-information
theory can technically cover such cases, it may not provide the sharpest
or most interpretable characterization of this type of structural information.

is generated once and then kept fixed
throughout the synthetic experiments. For each trial, the observed
index set
\Omega
is resampled with independent entrywise sampling
probability
p
. In the noiseless setting, we observe

\bm{M}=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{L}^{\star}),

while in the noisy setting we observe

\bm{M}=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{L}^{\star}+\bm{E}),

where the entries of
\bm{E}
are i.i.d. Gaussian with mean
0

and standard deviation
\sigma=0.001
.

For experiments with inexact side information, we replace

\bm{X}^{\star},\bm{Y}^{\star}
by matrices
\bm{X},\bm{Y}
with
prescribed inexactness level
\delta
in the sense of
Assumption
3
. We describe the construction
for the column side information
\bm{X}
; the row side information

\bm{Y}
is generated analogously by applying the same procedure to

(\bm{L}^{\star})^{\top}
.

Let
\bm{U}^{\star}
be the left singular matrix of
\bm{L}^{\star}
.
We first choose target principal angles

0\leq\theta_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\theta_{r}=\arcsin\delta
, with the first

r-1
angles drawn uniformly from
[0,\arcsin\delta]
. Next, we extend

\bm{U}^{\star}
to an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and select
orthonormal matrices
\bm{U}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and

\bm{U}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(a-r)}
such that both are orthogonal
to
\bm{U}^{\star}
and
\bm{U}_{2}
is also orthogonal to
\bm{U}_{1}
.
We then define the first
r
columns of
\bm{X}
by

\bm{X}_{\cdot,i}=\cos(\theta_{i})[\bm{U}^{\star}]_{\cdot,i}+\sin(\theta_{i})[\bm{U}_{1}]_{\cdot,i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq r,

where
[\bm{U}^{\star}]_{\cdot,i}
and
[\bm{U}_{1}]_{\cdot,i}
are the
i
-th columns of
\bm{U}^{\star}
and
\bm{U}_{1}
, respectively. We then take the remaining
a-r
columns from
\bm{U}_{2}
.

This construction
ensures that the principal angles between
\mathrm{col}(\bm{X})

and
\mathrm{col}(\bm{U}^{\star})
are exactly

\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r}
, and hence

\|\sin\bm{\Theta}_{1}\|=\delta
.
For each
1\leq i\leq r
, the vector
\bm{X}_{i}
lies in the
two-dimensional plane spanned by
\bm{U}^{\star}_{i}
and

(\bm{U}_{1})_{i}
, and makes angle exactly
\theta_{i}
with

\bm{U}^{\star}_{i}
. These planes are mutually orthogonal because
the columns of
\bm{U}^{\star}
and
\bm{U}_{1}
are orthonormal,
and the remaining columns from
\bm{U}_{2}
are orthogonal to

\mathrm{col}(\bm{U}^{\star})
. Therefore

\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{U}^{\star}
is diagonal in these coordinates, with
diagonal entries
\cos(\theta_{1}),\dots,\cos(\theta_{r})
. Hence
the singular values of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{U}^{\star}
are exactly

\cos(\theta_{1}),\dots,\cos(\theta_{r})
, which means the principal
angles between
\mathrm{col}(\bm{X})
and
\mathrm{col}(\bm{U}^{\star})

are precisely
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r}
.

F.2
Redundancy of Projection Steps in Algorithm
1

The projection step in Algorithm
1
is unnecessary in the setup of our simulated experiments.
In the following, we show empirically that the projection step is trivial. We use the same steps as in Section
5.1

to generate the ground truth matrix
\bm{L}^{\star}
and exact side
information matrices
\bm{X},
\bm{Y}
, with parameters
n_{1}=n_{2}=n=1000,a_{1}=a_{2}=a=50,r=10
,
and sampling rate
p=0.01
. Matrix observations are noiseless, so
that we observe
\bm{M}=\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\bm{L}^{\star})
. The
IMC objective (
3
) is optimized with Algorithm
1

for 200 iterations. The experiment is repeated for 20 independent
trials, where
\Omega
is resampled each time. For each iteration

t
, we verify that the inequalities

\|\bm{X}\bm{A}\|_{2,\infty}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{0}r}{n}}\|\bm{Z}^{0}\|,\|\bm{Y}\bm{B}\|_{2,\infty}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{0}r}{n}}\|\bm{Z}^{0}\|

are satisfied. For one of the 20 experimental trials, Figure
8

shows the norms
\lVert\bm{X}\bm{A}\rVert_{2,\infty}
and
\lVert\bm{Y}\bm{B}\rVert_{2,\infty}

for each iteration and a reference line at
2\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{0}r}{n}}\|\bm{Z}^{0}\|
.
Both norms always lie below the reference line, which shows that the
projection step onto the set

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}\\
\bm{B}\end{bmatrix}:\|\bm{X}\bm{A}\|_{2,\infty}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{0}r}{n_{\min}}}\|\bm{Z}^{0}\|,\|\bm{Y}\bm{B}\|_{2,\infty}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{0}r}{n_{\min}}}\|\bm{Z}^{0}\|\right\}

is trivial at every iteration. The same phenomenon is observed for
the other trials.
