Visual acuity degradation under positive lens–induced myopic defocus at nominal chart contrast levels in young adults: a repeated-measures study
Authors: Shivani Sah, Suraj Chettri, Pragati Gautam, Gauri Shankar Shrestha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo quantify visual acuity (VA) degradation across nominal chart contrast levels under graded positive lens–induced myopic defocus and to determine whether corrected low myopes differ from emmetropes in their acute response to optical blur.Methods and analysisIn this repeated-measures study, 300 adults aged 18–35 years (150 low myopes and 150 emmetropes) underwent monocular logMAR VA assessment at 6 m. Positive spherical lenses (+0.50 to +2.00 D) were added over best correction to induce myopic defocus. VA was measured at five nominal chart contrast levels (100%, 70%, 50%, 25% and 13%) under natural pupil conditions. A repeated-measures general linear model evaluated the effects of defocus and contrast on VA, with refractive group and gender as between-subject factors and age as a covariate.ResultsDefocus produced a significant decline in VA (p<0.001, partial η²=0.471), with progressive degradation as defocus increased. Progressively lower nominal chart contrast levels also significantly worsened VA (p<0.001, partial η²=0.237). A small but significant Defocus×Contrast interaction was observed (p<0.001, partial η²=0.015), indicating slightly greater blur-related degradation at lower nominal contrasts. No clinically meaningful interactions involving refractive group were observed. Although myopes demonstrated slightly poorer overall VA than emmetropes (mean difference 0.041 logMAR; p<0.001, partial η²=0.056), refractive status had little influence on the overall pattern of acuity decline across defocus or contrast levels.ConclusionsVA degradation under positive lens–induced myopic defocus is primarily determined by the magnitude of optical blur, with reduced contrast exerting an additional but smaller effect. Corrected low myopes and emmetropes show broadly comparable functional responses to acute defocus under natural viewing conditions, although subtle subgroup differences cannot be excluded.

Introduction

Optical defocus reduces retinal image quality by enlarging the blur circle and attenuating high spatial frequency information, resulting in decreased spatial resolution and visual acuity (VA).1 2 Positive spherical defocus produces blurred retinal images with reduced edge definition and contrast, and even small dioptric changes can impair visual performance, particularly for tasks requiring fine visual detail.

VA remains the most widely used clinical measure of spatial resolution.3 LogMAR charts provide improved linearity and repeatability compared with traditional Snellen charts, enabling more precise detection of small changes in visual performance.4 5 However, conventional high-contrast VA testing may not fully reflect functional vision because optical blur and stimulus contrast interact. Previous studies have shown that reducing contrast increases susceptibility to optical blur and further reduces visual performance.6 Therefore, assessment across nominal chart contrast levels may provide a broader understanding of visual function under optical degradation.

Experimentally induced defocus using spherical lenses is a well-established method for studying the effects of retinal blur. Incremental positive lenses produce predictable reductions in VA and characteristic defocus curves in both emmetropic and ametropic eyes.5 7 8 Although visual performance under defocus is primarily determined by optical image quality, previous studies have suggested that factors such as neural adaptation and contrast processing may influence visual responses under some experimental conditions.9 10 However, these mechanisms were not evaluated in the present study.

Whether habitual refractive status influences tolerance to acute defocus remains uncertain. Some studies have reported differences in blur adaptation or defocus responses between myopes and emmetropes.9–11 Differences in experimental conditions, accommodative control, contrast conditions and pupil manipulation may contribute to these inconsistent findings. A recent review has also highlighted the multifactorial nature of myopia and the role of optical factors in refractive development.12 However, the short-term functional effects of acute-induced defocus on VA under varying contrast conditions have not been comprehensively characterised under standardised clinical VA testing conditions.

Although retinal defocus has been discussed in relation to optical treatment strategies for myopia,13 14 the present study evaluated only acute central VA changes under experimentally induced spherical defocus. Therefore, these findings should not be directly extrapolated to real-world performance with myopia-control interventions.

Therefore, the present study aimed to characterise VA degradation across nominal chart contrast levels under graded positive lens–induced myopic defocus and to determine whether corrected low myopes and emmetropes exhibit different patterns of VA change under standardised clinical testing conditions. We hypothesised that increasing defocus and lower contrast would independently worsen VA, whereas refractive status would have minimal influence on the overall pattern of acuity degradation.

Material and methods

Study design and settings

This hospital-based, repeated-measures study evaluated the effect of positive lens–induced myopic defocus on VA across multiple contrast levels. Defocus and contrast conditions were systematically varied within participants at a single study visit using controlled, standardised measurement procedures.

The study was performed at the B.P. Koirala Lions Center for Ophthalmic Studies (BPKLCOS), Institute of Medicine, Tribhuvan University, Kathmandu, Nepal, between October 2024 and September 2025.

Participants contributed to the conduct of the study through their involvement in VA testing procedures. However, patients or the public were not involved in the design of the research question or dissemination of results.

Study population

A total of 300 adults aged 18–35 years were enrolled, including 150 low myopes (spherical equivalent −0.50 to −2.00 D) and 150 emmetropes (±0.25 D). This age range was selected to minimise age-related accommodation variability and early presbyopic changes. Participants were recruited consecutively from the General Outpatient Department and Refraction Unit to reduce selection bias. Eligible participants were screened according to refractive status, and recruitment continued until the predefined sample size of 150 participants had been achieved in each refractive group. The target sample size was determined to provide adequate statistical precision for repeated-measures comparisons across defocus and contrast conditions and to ensure sufficient power to detect potential interaction effects between refractive group, defocus and contrast.

Inclusion criteria required participants to be 18–35 years of age and to have VA of 0.1 logMAR or better under their appropriate refractive condition determined by subjective refraction. Participants requiring refractive correction were required to achieve best-corrected visual acuity of 0.1 logMAR or better following subjective refraction. Participants for whom subjective refraction indicated that no optical correction was required were eligible if their uncorrected VA was 0.1 logMAR or better. All experimental VA measurements were subsequently performed using the study-determined refractive correction or plano trial lenses when no correction was required, incorporated into standardised trial frames. Exclusion criteria included ocular pathology, amblyopia, strabismus, ocular surgery, significant astigmatism (>1.50 D), contact lens use, ocular trauma, corneal scarring, binocular vision disorders or systemic conditions affecting vision.

Refraction was determined by autorefractometry and retinoscopy, refined with subjective refraction to obtain the best subjective sphero-cylindrical correction for each participant. During testing, all participants wore this study-determined correction incorporated into standardised trial frames rather than their habitual spectacles. Trial frames were individually adjusted to achieve a stable and comfortable fit before testing. Proper frame positioning was verified to ensure that the pupils were unobstructed during all VA measurements. The trial frame remained in position throughout the experiment while additional positive lenses were introduced sequentially over the correcting lenses. Vertex distance was not measured quantitatively; however, it was kept as consistent as possible by maintaining the fitted trial frame in position throughout testing. Although the study protocol excluded participants with astigmatism greater than 1.50 D, all participants included in the final analysis had astigmatism of 1.00 D or less.

VA assessment

Baseline VA was measured monocularly in both eyes at a testing distance of 6 m using a digital LCD logMAR chart (Aurolab, India). The repeated-measures protocol, including sequential positive lens–induced defocus at different nominal chart contrast levels, was performed only on the right eye to avoid inter-eye correlation and only right-eye data were included in the statistical analysis. Letter-by-letter scoring was employed to improve measurement precision, with VA recorded in logMAR units. The digital chart software automatically randomised optotype presentation for each testing condition and letter sequences were refreshed across repeated defocus and contrast measurements. Therefore, optotypes were not presented sequentially or systematically repeated, minimising potential memorisation or learning effects during repeated VA measurement.

Examinations were performed in a standardised clinical refraction room under controlled ambient illumination (approximately 160–200 lux), measured using Digital Light Metre (YF-170, Taiwan). The luminance of the LCD logMAR chart was maintained according to the manufacturer’s specifications (Wide angle LED FULL HD 22-inch Display Panel; 1920*1080 Resolution; and background luminance approximately 200 cd/m2).

VA was assessed at manufacturer-defined nominal contrast levels of 100%, 70%, 50%, 25% and 13%, as provided by the chart’s internal software settings. These manufacturer-defined nominal chart contrast levels were applied consistently for all participants and testing conditions. However, independent photometric calibration of the display was not performed; therefore, the reported contrast levels should be interpreted as relative nominal chart contrast levels rather than exact Weber contrast measurements. Consequently, the analyses primarily evaluate relative changes in VA across progressively lower chart contrast conditions under standardised testing levels. Testing was conducted under natural pupil conditions without pharmacological dilation or artificial aperture control.

Induction of myopic defocus

Participants with significant astigmatism (>1.50 D cylinder) were excluded irrespective of refractive group. During testing, all participants wore their full subjective sphero-cylindrical correction incorporated into standardised trial frames, including emmetropic participants with low cylindrical refractive errors despite near-plano spherical equivalent values. Only participants with no refractive error received plano correction alone. Positive spherical lenses of +0.50, +1.00, +1.50 and +2.00 D were then sequentially placed over the correcting lens of the tested eye to induce nominal myopic defocus under natural viewing conditions. Participants were instructed to maintain clear fixation at 6 m, and no proximal cues were present to minimise accommodative compensation for the induced defocus. VA was measured at each defocus level across all contrast levels.

Participants were allowed approximately 1–2 min to adapt to each new defocus lens before testing to stabilise initial perceptual and accommodative responses. Although previous studies indicate that blur adaptation can continue over 4–6 min, the shorter adaptation period in this study was considered sufficient for initial stabilisation while minimising prolonged testing time.

To minimise order effects, the sequence of the five defocus levels (+0.00, +0.50, +1.00, +1.50 and +2.00 D) was randomised for each participant using a computer-generated randomisation schedule in Microsoft Excel before participant enrolment. After each randomly selected defocus lens was introduced and the participant had completed the adaptation period, VA was measured at all five nominal chart contrast levels (100%, 70%, 50%, 25% and 13%). The order of contrast presentation under each defocus condition was also prespecified for each participant according to the pre-generated randomisation schedule. Thus, all five contrast levels were assessed once under each defocus level, with the order of contrast presentation varying between participants. The pre-generated randomisation schedule was followed throughout VA assessment.

Following completion of testing at one defocus level, the next randomly selected defocus lens was introduced, and participants were again allowed approximately 1–2 min to adapt before testing resumed. Additional 1–2 min breaks were provided if participants reported visual strain or if signs of visual fatigue were observed (eg, excessive blinking, squinting or delayed responses). The reported VA assessment time of approximately 25–35 min represented the active testing time only and excluded the adaptation periods and any additional breaks. Participants were continuously monitored throughout testing, and testing was temporarily paused if reduced attention or inconsistent responses were noted. No participant required termination of testing due to visual fatigue.

All measurements were performed by a single trained optometrist following standardised testing procedures. Examiner masking to defocus level was not feasible because lens manipulation was directly visible during testing. To minimise potential measurement bias, standardised instructions, automated optotype randomisation, fixed testing distance, controlled illumination and letter-by-letter scoring procedures were applied consistently for all participants and testing conditions.

Data analysis

Assumptions of normality were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and inspection of residual plots confirmed approximate normal distribution of model residuals. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean±SD.

A repeated-measures general linear model (GLM) was conducted to examine the effects of defocus (+0.00 to +2.00 D) and nominal contrast (100% to 13%) on VA (logMAR) in emmetropic (n=150) and myopic (n=150) participants. Refractive group and gender were included as between-subject factors, while age was included as a continuous covariate.

Assumption testing indicated violation of sphericity (Mauchly’s test, p<0.001; online supplemental table S1); therefore, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied, and corrected df are reported for all within-subject effects. Partial eta squared (η²) was reported as a measure of effect size for all model effects.

A repeated-measures GLM framework was selected because the study employed a fully balanced repeated-measures design with complete observations across all defocus and contrast conditions for each participant and no missing within-subject data. Under these conditions, repeated-measures GLM provides robust estimation of within-subject effects and interactions. Assumptions of residual normality and sphericity were formally evaluated. Violations of sphericity identified by Mauchly’s test were addressed using Greenhouse–Geisser corrections for all within-subject effects.

Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed for significant main effects. Estimated marginal means were reported for between-group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS V.27 (IBM, USA).

To provide context for the observed findings, a post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power V.3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) for repeated-measures within–between interaction effects. Based on the observed repeated-measures interaction effect size (partial η²=0.008; approximately Cohen’s f=0.09), α=0.05, two refractive groups and five repeated measurements, the achieved sample size of 300 participants was considered adequate for detecting an interaction effect of the observed magnitude. This analysis is descriptive, and should not be interpreted as evidence that the study was prospectively powered to confirm the absence of refractive group differences or support the null hypothesis.

A total of 300 participants (150 myopes and 150 emmetropes) were analysed. Myopes were significantly older than emmetropes (24.1±4.3 vs 22.6±4.2 years; p=0.003). Sex distribution differed between groups (p=0.031), with more females among emmetropes (table 1). Spherical equivalent refraction differed significantly (p<0.001).

Simple effects and effect sizes

Simple effects analyses demonstrated significant worsening of VA with each incremental increase in induced defocus (all p<0.001; online supplemental table S2). Mean logMAR differences increased progressively from 0.163 between 0.00 and 0.50 D to 0.825 between 0.00 and 2.00 D, confirming a dose-dependent decline in VA across all contrast conditions.

Between-group comparisons showed consistently poorer VA in myopic participants compared with emmetropes across all defocus levels (online supplemental table S5). The mean group differences were relatively small, ranging from 0.030 to 0.063 logMAR, with the largest difference observed at +1.00 D defocus. These findings are consistent with the modest overall between-group effect and the absence of significant group-related within-subject interactions reported in table 2.

Discussion

This study evaluated VA degradation under graded positive lens–induced myopic defocus at nominal chart contrast levels in corrected low myopes and emmetropes under routine clinical conditions. The results demonstrate that the magnitude of optical defocus is the primary determinant of acuity degradation, while reduced contrast exerts an additional but smaller effect. VA declined progressively with increasing dioptric blur across all nominal chart contrast levels (100%13%), consistent with previous studies reporting predictable reductions in spatial resolution under spherical defocus.7 8 15

Reduced nominal chart contrast independently worsened VA, supporting evidence that lower contrast increases susceptibility to blur-related visual degradation.6 16 Because contrast levels were based on manufacturer-defined software settings without external photometric calibration, the findings should be interpreted as relative effects across progressively lower nominal contrast conditions rather than precise contrast-dose relationships. Although the Defocus×Contrast interaction was statistically significant, the small effect size suggests that contrast primarily acts as a secondary modifier of performance rather than substantially altering the effect of optical blur. These findings reinforce the dominant role of retinal image quality in determining VA under induced defocus.

Corrected low myopes exhibited slightly poorer overall VA than emmetropes after adjustment for age and gender (mean difference=0.041 logMAR, corresponding to approximately half a line). The absence of significant Defocus×Group and higher-order interactions indicates that functional responses to acute myopic defocus were broadly similar in both groups within the tested range (+0.50 to +2.00 D). However, the refractive groups were not completely matched for age, sex or baseline VA. Therefore, despite statistical adjustment, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the small between-group difference may partly reflect sampling differences in addition to refractive status itself. These findings suggest that any refractive group–specific neural adaptation, such as short-term blur adaptation, is small relative to the imposed optical degradation.9 10 17 18 At higher levels of induced defocus, between-group differences in VA appeared relatively less distinct; however, no formal ceiling/floor analysis was performed, and these observations should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

A significant Group×Gender interaction further indicated that the modest difference between refractive groups was not uniform across males and females. Simple-effects analyses showed that the between-group difference was primarily driven by female participants, whereas no significant difference was observed among males. Because the effect size was small (partial η²=0.028) and the study was not specifically designed or powered to investigate sex-specific visual responses, this finding should be considered exploratory. Future studies with balanced recruitment and pre-specified subgroup analyses are needed to determine whether biological or behavioural factors contribute to these observed differences.

Similarly, although the Defocus×Contrast×Group interaction did not reach conventional statistical significance (p=0.0504), the result was close to the significance threshold. Together with its very small effect size (partial η²=0.006), this finding suggests that any refractive group differences in the combined effects of defocus and contrast are likely to be subtle rather than absent. Accordingly, the present findings support broadly comparable response patterns between refractive groups under the tested conditions, while not excluding the possibility of small subgroup-specific differences that may become evident in larger or more targeted studies.

Experimental conditions were intended to approximate routine clinical viewing conditions without artificial pupil control or pharmacologically controlled apertures or wavefront-guided blur.7 19 20 However, pupil diameter was not directly measured, and its contribution to retinal image quality, depth of focus and visual performance could not be quantified. Standardised clinical luminance conditions were maintained throughout testing, although minor differences in defocus curve slopes relative to previous reports may reflect variations in chart characteristics, scoring protocols, or adaptation procedures.7 15 19 Although participants were allowed only brief adaptation (1–2 min) at each defocus level, monotonic defocus curves and low within-subject variability suggest stable initial responses. Longer exposure could elicit modest blur adaptation, but acute effects likely predominate in routine clinical or therapeutic contexts.17

Strengths of this study include a large and balanced sample, inclusion of both refractive groups, randomised sequencing of defocus and contrast, controlled ambient illumination, standardised digital logMAR assessment with letter-by-letter scoring and statistical adjustment for relevant covariates. The repeated-measures design enhanced sensitivity to detect within-subject changes while minimising inter-individual variability.

Several limitations should be considered. Pupil diameter was not directly measured, which represents an important limitation because inter-individual variation in pupil size may influence retinal image quality, depth of focus and the magnitude of defocus-related visual degradation. Contrast levels were manufacturer-defined and were not independently calibrated using external photometric methods; therefore, the reported contrast conditions should be interpreted as relative nominal chart contrast levels rather than precise Weber contrast measurements. Accordingly, the study was designed to evaluate relative changes in VA across standardised chart contrast conditions rather than establish exact quantitative contrast-dose relationships. In addition, accommodative responses were not objectively measured during testing, and the imposed blur levels should therefore be interpreted as nominal lens-induced defocus rather than exact retinal defocus. The cross-sectional design captures acute effects of induced defocus and does not evaluate longer term neural adaptation or accommodative dynamics. The study was conducted at a single tertiary eye centre, which may limit generalisability. In addition, the emmetropic and myopic groups were not fully matched for age, sex distribution or baseline VA. Although these variables were statistically adjusted in the repeated-measures model, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded when interpreting the modest between-group differences. Finally, examiner masking was not feasible because lens manipulation was directly visible during testing. Although standardised protocols, automated optotype randomisation and objective logMAR scoring procedures were used to minimise observer-related variability, the possibility of subtle measurement or expectancy bias cannot be completely excluded. This limitation is inherent to many experimental defocus paradigms in which lens changes are apparent to both the examiner and participant.

Although repeated-measures GLM was appropriate for the present balanced dataset with complete repeated observations, linear mixed-effects modelling may provide additional flexibility for handling hierarchical variance structures, participant-specific random effects and incomplete repeated-measures data in future studies.

These findings indicate that optical blur is the primary determinant of acuity degradation under induced myopic defocus, with reduced contrast further impairing performance. High-contrast VA assessment alone may underestimate functional reductions encountered in low-contrast environments. The findings primarily characterise short-term VA responses under induced central spherical defocus and controlled clinical conditions. Although optical strategies such as multifocal or myopia-control designs intentionally introduce retinal defocus,14 21 these systems involve more complex optical profiles, including simultaneous vision optics and peripheral defocus components, which were not simulated in the present experiment. Therefore, any relevance of the current findings to myopia-control interventions should be considered indirect and speculative.

Conclusions

Positive lens–induced myopic defocus produces a progressive reduction in VA, with the magnitude of defocus being the primary determinant of performance. Progressively lower nominal chart contrast levels further worsened VA, although their effect was smaller than that of optical defocus. Corrected low myopes demonstrated slightly poorer overall acuity than emmetropes; however, this modest between-group difference should be interpreted cautiously because the refractive groups were not fully matched at baseline. Although corrected low myopes and emmetropes demonstrated broadly comparable patterns of VA decline across defocus and nominal chart contrast levels, small subgroup differences cannot be excluded, particularly in view of the observed Group×Gender interaction and the borderline three-way interaction. Overall, these findings suggest that optical blur is the principal determinant of short-term visual performance under induced defocus, while reduced contrast provides an additional but smaller influence. The results indicate broadly comparable patterns of VA response between corrected low myopes and emmetropes under the conditions studied, although modest subgroup-specific effects warrant further investigation.
