Associations of critical thinking and social media addiction with eHealth literacy among adolescent social media users
Authors: İlknur Dolu, Ecem Çiçek, Mahir Can Gökçe, Hilal Büyüktopaç Çakıcı, Feyza Demir Bozkurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study aims to investigate the impact of social media addiction and critical thinking on eHealth literacy in adolescents. The cross-sectional study was conducted between March and July 2025 among high school students. A census sampling method was employed, with 80.2% of students agreeing to participate. Data were collected using a questionnaire comprising socio-demographic information, the eHealth Literacy Scale for Adolescents, the Critical Thinking in Everyday Life Questionnaire, and the Social Media Addiction Scale for Adolescents. Of the participants, 62.8% were female, and the most commonly used social media platforms were Instagram (85.5%). The study found a positive and strong correlation between eHealth literacy and critical thinking in everyday life, whereas no significant relationship was found between eHealth literacy and social media addiction. According to the linear regression analysis, daily internet use (hours) and critical thinking in everyday life were significantly associated with eHealth literacy. This study highlights that eHealth literacy in adolescents is significantly influenced by daily internet usage and critical thinking skills, rather than social media addiction. These findings suggest that future interventions should prioritize the development of critical thinking to empower adolescents as informed health users.Keywords: eHealth literacy, Social media addiction, Critical thinking; Adolescent, School nurse

Introduction

In the digital age, the pervasive use of the internet and social media has significantly shaped behaviours related to the seeking and exchange of health-related information. Young people, in particular, commonly rely on these platforms, which are characterised by their rapid and convenient access, to obtain diverse forms of health information [1]. In particular, social media platforms encompass both accurate and misleading health information, underscoring the importance of users developing robust health literacy skills [2, 3]. Social media can play a significant role in fostering social connections and supporting identity development among adolescents and young adults, and it also serves as an important source of health information [1, 3]. A recent study reported a substantial prevalence of underage social media engagement among early adolescents aged 11 to 15. Overall, 69.5% maintained at least one social media account. Among these users, the most frequently utilized platforms were TikTok (67.1%), YouTube (64.7%), and Instagram (66.0%) [4]. Social media use among adolescents poses several potential risks, including depression, sleep disturbances, behavioural problems, body image concerns, reduced physical activity, anxiety and addiction [5]. On the other hand, social media can serve as an important source of health information for adolescents. It is well established that adolescents frequently use social media platforms to obtain health-related information, particularly on sensitive topics such as sexual and reproductive health [4]. Social media is also considered to have the potential to support adolescents in developing appropriate health behaviours [6].

Recent data from the World Health Organization (WHO) indicate a marked increase in problematic social media use, which is characterized by addiction-like symptoms, among adolescents. The findings are based on a 2022 survey of nearly 280,000 young people aged 11, 13, and 15 across 44 countries and regions in Europe, Central Asia, and Canada [7]. A considerable number of individuals turn to social media as a means of coping with stress or loneliness. Although these platforms may foster a sense of belonging, they can also intensify feelings of inadequacy and isolation by exposing users to idealized depictions of others’ lives [7]. Excessive social media use is associated with higher levels of anxiety and depression in adolescents and young adults, and can also reduce attention to academic responsibilities, negatively affecting educational performance [8]. Social media addiction among adolescents is an emerging concern. It is characterized by impaired control over use, withdrawal symptoms when access is restricted, neglect of other activities, and negative effects on daily functioning [7]. As a preventive strategy against social media addiction, the literature highlights media literacy as particularly effective, as it provides individuals with the skills to engage with social media responsibly and critically [9].

Media literacy is a core component of eHealth literacy, particularly emphasizing critical thinking in the evaluation of media content. eHealth literacy is defined by Norman and Skinner as ‘the ability to seek, find, understand, and appraise health information from electronic sources and apply the knowledge gained to addressing or solving a health problem [10]. This concept integrates multiple literacy competencies and applies them to the promotion of health and the provision of care. At its core are six fundamental literacies: traditional literacy, health literacy, information literacy, scientific literacy, media literacy, and computer literacy [10]. In the updated model of eHealth literacy, Norman and Skinner emphasized the importance of computer literacy, information literacy, and media literacy, providing enhanced definitions that reflect technological advancements and contemporary paradigms [11]. The concept of eHealth literacy incorporates critical thinking within the domain of media literacy, which is defined as ‘the critical and reflective skills to analyse, evaluate, and utilize media content to form judgments and guide actions.’ However, evidence on the effect of everyday critical thinking on eHealth literacy is limited. When considering the broader concept of critical thinking, numerous definitions can be identified in the literature. Although various definitions of critical thinking exist, Ennis’s (1985) formulation is frequently cited, as its breadth allows for application across diverse disciplines and contexts [12]. Ennis defines critical thinking as ‘reflective and reasonable thinking that is focused on deciding what to believe or do’. Evidence also indicates that young learners’ critical thinking skills can be enhanced through approaches such as the careful selection of subject content that encourages students to engage in critical dialogue [12]. By examining the relationships between social media addiction, critical thinking, and eHealth literacy levels, as well as the factors influencing them in adolescents, this study is expected to enhance awareness among professionals working in adolescent health. Consequently, it may inform the development of future strategies aimed at preventing health risks in this population at an early stage. In light of the existing literature, the present study aims to investigate the impact of social media addiction and critical thinking on eHealth literacy in adolescents. The research hypothesis posits that adolescents’ levels of social media addiction and critical thinking may serve as predictors of their eHealth literacy levels.

Methods

Design and settings

The cross-sectional study was carried out between March and July 2025, involving students enrolled in high schools under the jurisdiction of the Bartın Provincial Directorate of National Education. The present study was conducted at a high school located in the city center, comprising 581 students, 55 teachers, and one school nurse. At the time of the study, this school was the only institution within the province to employ a school nurse. In Türkiye, school nursing was first introduced in 1983 within public boarding schools under the authority of the Ministry of National Education [13]. Since 2020, regulations have required schools with 250 or more students to employ a school nurse [14]. Although the presence of school nurses is mandated in private schools, only approximately 1,000 school nurses are currently employed across public schools nationwide, a figure substantially below the level required to meet existing needs [15]. The school nurse was responsible for conducting routine health assessments, implementing preventive measures against infectious diseases, and delivering educational programs aimed at promoting healthy lifestyle behaviors. In emergency situations, the nurse provided first aid and coordinated with healthcare facilities as required. Additionally, the school nurse collaborated closely with the Guidance and Counseling Service, teaching staff, and families to address and support the psychosocial needs of students [14].

The study defined participants as adolescents who were high school students aged between 13 and 19 years, in accordance with the World Health Organization’s definition of adolescence as the period between 10 and 19 years of age [16]. Census sampling method was used. All 581 students were invited to participate in the study, and 466 of them agreed to take part (80.2%). Only participants who reported active social media use were included in the final analysis. Non-users were excluded to ensure the validity of the results and to minimize potential bias related to a lack of relevant experience, resulting in a sample of 358 students (Fig. 1). The findings were reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines [17].

Participant flow diagram for study recruitment and inclusion

The Bartın University Ethics Committee provided ethical approval for this study (2024-SBB-0779). The authors assert that all procedures contributing to this work comply with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional committees on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013. In Türkiye, obtaining consent from both the individual and their legal guardian is a legal requirement for participants aged between 13 and 18 years [18]. Accordingly, both the participants and their legal guardians were informed about the purpose and methodology of the research, as well as the voluntary nature of participation. They were also notified that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. Written informed consent forms were obtained separately from the participants and their parents/legal guardians prior to inclusion in the study. The necessary institutional approvals were obtained from the Provincial Directorate of National Education in the region where the study was conducted.

Data collection tools

For data collection, a questionnaire was administered that included socio-demographic information, the eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS) for adolescents, the Critical Thinking in Everyday Life Questionnaire, and the Social Media Addiction Scale for Adolescents. Data were collected by the school nurse, and completing the form took approximately 15 to 20 minutes.

A Questionnaire Form: This form included questions on students’ age, gender, perceived income level, and daily internet usage time.

eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS) in Adolescent: The eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS) was adapted into Turkish by [19]. This 5-point Likert scale consists of 10 items, two assessing internet use and eight evaluating internet-related attitudes. Total scores range from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher levels of eHealth literacy. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the Turkish version was reported as 0.78 [19] and was 0.90 in the present study.

Critical Thinking in Everyday Life Questionnaire (CTDLQ): CTDLQ, which was validated in the Turkish context by [20], was used to assess the life skills of young people. This 17-item questionnaire comprises four sub-dimensions: analysis and information processing (items i1, i7, i8, i9, and i14), questioning (items i2, i3, i5, and i6), flexibility (items i10, i11, i12, and i13), and reasoning and evaluation (items i4, i15, i16, and i17). Total scores range from 17 to 85, with higher scores indicating higher levels of critical thinking skills. The questionnaire uses a 5-point Likert-type scale, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the Turkish version was reported as 0.87 [20] and was 0.91 in the present study.

Social Media Addiction Scale for Adolescents (SMASA): This 5-point Likert-type scale, developed by [21], consists of 9 items, with total scores ranging from 9 to 45. The total score is calculated by summing the responses to all items, with higher scores indicating higher levels of social media addiction. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient was reported as 0.90 [21] and was 0.84 in the present study.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, were used to summarize the study variables. The normality of the data distribution was assessed by examining skewness and kurtosis values. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between variables. Additionally, linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the effects of participants’ selected characteristics, critical thinking, and social media addiction on their eHealth literacy levels. There were no missing data, as all data were collected face-to-face by the fourth author. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted for all statistical analyses.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the association of social media addiction and critical thinking with adolescents’ eHealth literacy. The findings indicated that daily internet use (in hours) and critical thinking in everyday life were significantly associated with eHealth literacy. School nurses play a key role in health promotion, and eHealth literacy is important for developing healthy behaviours. In adolescents, higher eHealth literacy is associated with better health knowledge, preventive behaviours, attitudes, and well-being [23–26].

This study showed that critical thinking is strongly associated with adolescents’ eHealth literacy, and the level of critical thinking in everyday life was relatively high in our sample. Although critical thinking and health are included in high school curricula, explicit instruction on applying critical thinking to health issues is often lacking, leaving many individuals unable to make informed health-related decisions [27]. Recent evidence, which is consistent with our findings, demonstrates that teaching critical thinking about health information and choices in high schools leads to substantial improvements in students’ ability to critically evaluate health-related decisions [28]. Emerging evidence indicates that interactive learning methodologies have a greater impact on critical thinking and on improving health literacy among adolescents [29]. Building on these points, incorporating critical thinking skills into educational programs delivered by school nurses in high schools, particularly by teaching adolescents to critically evaluate the credibility of online information, may further enhance their eHealth literacy, which is essential for the practical application of health-related knowledge in daily life.

Our study also found that daily internet use (hours) was an important predictor of adolescents’ eHealth literacy, with a mean of 4.19 hours (±1.83) in our sample. Similar to the present study, a recent study in Türkiye reported that adolescents use the internet for an average of 4.43 hours per day (±2.37) [30]. Similar patterns have been observed in other countries, including Switzerland (3.5–4.5 hours) [31], the United States (≥4 hours) [32], and the United Kingdom (around 3 hours per day) [33]. Current evidence suggests that internet use has both benefits and risks. While excessive use may reduce physical activity and face-to-face interaction, potentially harming physical and mental health, it also supports learning and access to health information [34], contributing to adolescents’ knowledge and literacy skills [35, 36]. Evidence also indicates that there is no simple, linear relationship between internet use, time spent online, and well-being outcomes in adolescent [34]. Therefore, future research and interventions should consider both the positive and negative aspects of internet use rather than focusing solely on its harmful effects.

This study did not identify any relationship between social media addiction and eHealth literacy. In the present study, social media addiction levels were slightly below the moderate range, whereas eHealth literacy levels were slightly above moderate. Social media addiction involves compulsive use that disrupts daily functioning and well-being, including constant checking, anxiety when offline, and negative impacts on relationships and responsibilities [8]. In our study, females comprised 62.8% of the total sample, and no significant gender differences in social media addiction were observed. Some studies have reported that male adolescents are more prone to social media addiction [37, 38], whereas other evidence suggests that it is more prevalent among female adolescents [39]. A recent study among adolescents reported that social media addiction is positively associated with functional and interactive health literacy, but strongly negatively associated with critical health literacy, with no significant association observed for overall health literacy, which is consistent with our study finding [38]. Compulsive or excessive social media use does not necessarily affect the ability to find, evaluate, or apply online health information, as the skills required for eHealth literacy appear to be distinct from problematic usage patterns [40]. This finding highlights the role of critical thinking as a key component in enhancing adolescents’ eHealth literacy. Future programs should focus on fostering critical thinking and search skills rather than assuming that frequent or ‘addictive’ social media users possess inherent digital health advantages. Evidence also has highlighted that eHealth literacy may moderate the negative impact of social media addiction on well-being [41]. Moreover, initiatives that enhance social media self-efficacy, an individual’s confidence in effectively using social media to achieve specific goals, may also promote higher eHealth literacy among young people [42, 43]. The implementation of these strategies is therefore critical for promoting healthier online behaviours, enhancing eHealth literacy, and ultimately improving overall well-being in adolescents. Therefore, it is also essential to understand the underlying reasons for adolescents’ tendency to use social media as a search engine and for health authorities to engage with these platforms to protect young people from misleading or inaccurate information.

Limitation

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the generalizability of the findings is limited due to the use of a convenience sample from a single school, which may not represent all adolescents. In addition, participants were self-selected and included only social media users, introducing potential selection bias. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design limits the applicability of the results across different time points and populations. Second, although we assessed the duration of internet use, we did not examine the purposes for the types of digital activities they engaged in, which may influence their eHealth literacy. Third, the presence of a school nurse in the study setting may have influenced the findings, as access to on-site health support could affect students’ health awareness, behaviours, or use of health information. Finally, the school included in this study was the only one with a school nurse, and the educational strategies provided by the nurse may have affected the study outcomes, further limiting the generalizability of the findings to other schools in the region or country.

Conclusion

This study aimed to investigate the impact of social media addiction and critical thinking on adolescents’ eHealth literacy. The results showed that the number of hours spent online daily and the level of critical thinking in daily life were significant determinants of eHealth literacy. School nurses play a key role in providing health education and are therefore ideally placed to consider adolescents’ eHealth literacy when developing health education programs, especially during a stage when young people are forming their own understanding and practices related to health. Integrating critical thinking into school nurses’ programs, especially teaching adolescents to assess online information, can strengthen eHealth literacy and support the practical use of health knowledge in daily life.

Modern society is undergoing a significant shift in which digital screens are no longer merely sources of entertainment but are increasingly integrated into daily life, enabling internet use to foster the development of knowledge and literacy skills in adolescents and ultimately enhance their overall well-being. Further research is needed on adolescents’ use of social media as a search tool and on how school nurses’ educational strategies influence their eHealth literacy. Additionally, it is important for health authorities to prioritize engagement with these increasingly prominent platforms to protect adolescents from misleading or inaccurate health information.
