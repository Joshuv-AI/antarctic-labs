Augmented Lagrangian Method for Last-Iterate Convergence for Constrained MDPs
Michael Lu, Max Qiushi Lin, Mo Chen, Sharan Vaswani

Abstract
We study policy optimization for infinite-horizon, discounted constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs). While existing theoretical guarantees typically hold for the mixture policy, deploying such a policy is computationally and memory intensive. This leads to a practical mismatch where a single (last-iterate) policy must be deployed. Recent theoretical works have thus focused on proving last-iterate convergence, but are largely limited to the tabular setting or to algorithmic variants that are rarely used in practice. To address this, we use the classic inexact augmented Lagrangian ( AL ) method from constrained optimization, and propose a general framework with provable last-iterate convergence for CMDPs. We first focus on the tabular setting and propose to solve the AL sub-problem with projected Q-ascent ( PQA ). Combining the theoretical guarantees of PQA and the standard AL analysis enables us to establish global last-iterate convergence. We generalize these results to handle log-linear policies, and demonstrate that an efficient, projected variant of PQA can achieve last-iterate convergence with comparable guarantees as prior work. Finally, we demonstrate that our framework scales to complex non-linear policies, and evaluate it on continuous control tasks.

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has found success in a variety of applications such as video games
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
, robotics
(
Kober et al. 2013
)
and fine-tuning large language models
(
Uc-Cetina et al. 2023
)
. While standard RL algorithms are typically designed to optimize a single performance objective, real-world systems must also operate under operational and safety constraints. For instance, in a wireless communication system, a network controller aims to maximize the bitrate delivered to users while ensuring that total energy consumption does not exceed a given budget
(
Julian et al. 2002
;
Buratti et al. 2009
)
. A common approach is to model such problems as a constrained Markov decision process (CMDP)
(
Altman 2021
)
. In this setting, an agent must maximize a cumulative reward (e.g. bitrate), while ensuring that the cumulative constraint reward (e.g. energy consumption) satisfies a prescribed threshold. Unlike standard MDPs, the optimal policies for CMDPs are stochastic, making it challenging to design algorithms that reliably recover a near-optimal policy.

A typical algorithmic template
(
Jain et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2025b
;
Ding et al. 2020
;
Bura et al. 2022
)
to solve CMDPs is to form the Lagrangian and iteratively perform alternating updates of the policy (primal variable) and the associated Lagrange multipliers (dual variable). While such primal–dual (
PD
) methods are guaranteed to output a policy that maximizes the reward while satisfying the constraints, such guarantees only hold for the
mixture policy
. In particular,
PD
methods iteratively generate a sequence of policies, and maintaining a mixture policy requires storing all intermediate policies, making this approach memory intensive. Moreover, in order to deploy a mixture policy, a policy is randomly sampled from the set of intermediate policies, and the theoretical guarantees hold in expectation over this random selection. From a safety perspective, relying on such a mixture policy can be dangerous, because an individual sampled policy may significantly violate the constraints. Consequently, practitioners use
PD
methods and deploy the final policy, trading theoretical guarantees for ease of implementation. Unfortunately, this practical variant is typically sensitive to hyper-parameters and the final policy can exhibit oscillatory behavior (for example, see
Fig.

1
).

This concern has motivated recent works
(
Ding et al. 2023
;
Mondal and Aggarwal 2024b
;
Müller et al. 2024
;
Moskovitz et al. 2023
;
Ying et al. 2022
;
Montenegro et al. 2024
)
that aim to design iterative approaches that have theoretical guarantees for the final stochastic policy. Such
last-iterate convergence
is desirable from both a theoretical and empirical perspective. All such recent works rely on adding additional regularization to both the primal and dual variables. Despite their theoretical appeal, these last-iterate algorithms are either difficult to implement in practice, or are designed for simplified settings, limiting their applicability. In fact, to the best of our knowledge, there is no last-iterate algorithm that is both principled (has guarantees in simplified settings) and practical (can be efficiently implemented for large problems).

Motivated by this gap, and in contrast to the previous work on last-iterate convergence, we employ the
augmented Lagrangian (
AL
) method
. Such methods are a classic approach in the constrained optimization literature, and are widely used in practice (see
Deng et al. 2025
for a recent survey). Under suitable assumptions,
AL
methods enjoy strong theoretical guarantees, including last-iterate global convergence
(
Xu 2021
;
Liu et al. 2019
)
. Empirically,
AL
methods tend to be stable and more robust when compared to common
PD
approaches
(
Birgin and Martínez 2014
)
. For example,
Fig.

1

2
Problem Formulation & Background

We consider an infinite-horizon discounted constrained Markov decision process (CMDP)
(
Altman 2021
)
defined by the tuple
({\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{P}},\rho,\gamma,r,\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m})
. Here,
{\mathcal{S}}
is the set of states with
S=\lvert{\mathcal{S}}\rvert
,
{\mathcal{A}}
is the set of actions with
A=\lvert{\mathcal{A}}\rvert
,
{\mathcal{P}}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to\Delta_{{\mathcal{S}}}
is the transition probability function,
\rho\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
is the initial state distribution,
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount factor and
r:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to[0,1]
is the reward function. The constraint reward functions are denoted by
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
where
c_{i}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to[0,1]
and
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is the set of corresponding non-negative thresholds.

For policy
\pi:{\mathcal{S}}\to\Delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}
, the
state-action value function

Q^{\pi}_{\diamond}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as
Q^{\pi}_{\diamond}(s,a)\coloneq\mathbb{E}[\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{\tau}\diamond(s_{\tau},a_{\tau})|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a]
such that for
\tau\geq 1
,
s_{\tau+1}\sim{\mathcal{P}}(\cdot|s_{\tau},a_{\tau})
,
a_{\tau+1}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{\tau})
and
\diamond:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded (pseudo) reward function equal to either
r
or
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
or their linear combination. The
value function

V^{\pi}_{\diamond}:{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as
V^{\pi}_{\diamond}(s)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{a\sim{\pi(\cdot|s)}}[Q^{\pi}_{\diamond}(s,a)]
and we use
V^{\pi}_{\diamond}(\rho):=\mathbb{E}_{s_{0}\sim\rho}[V^{\pi}_{\diamond}(s_{0})]
to denote the expected value function. The
reward state-action value function
and
reward value function
are denoted as
Q^{\pi}_{r}
and
V^{\pi}_{r}
. Similarly, for each constraint
i\in[m]
, we denote the
constraint state-action value function
as
Q^{\pi}_{c_{i}}
and the
constraint value function
as
V^{\pi}_{c_{i}}
.

The objective is to find a policy
\pi
that maximizes cumulative reward while satisfying the constraints. Formally, we aim to solve the following problem:

Problem 1
.

\max_{\pi}V^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad V^{\pi}_{c_{i}}(\rho)\geq{b_{i}}\quad\forall i\in[m]
.

Unlike MDPs, the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
of
1
is stochastic but stationary
(
Altman 2021
)
. A common approach to solving
1
is to form its Lagrangian and consider the following problem:

\max_{\pi}\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)\coloneq V^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\,[V^{\pi}_{c_{i}}(\rho)-b_{i}]
,
where
\lambda
is the Lagrange multiplier. Primal-dual (
PD
) methods solve the above max-min problem by iteratively and alternatively updating the policy and the Lagrange multiplier. For example, the policy is updated using natural policy gradient, whereas the Lagrange multiplier is updated using gradient descent
(
Ding et al. 2020
)
.

For a target
\varepsilon>0
, prior work
(
Jain et al. 2022
;
Ding et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2025b
)
prove that it is possible to output a mixture policy
\bar{\pi}_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{t}
which is an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution to
1
,

\displaystyle V^{\bar{\pi}_{T}}_{r}(\rho)\geq\max_{\pi}V^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)-\varepsilon,\text{and}\quad V^{\bar{\pi}_{T}}_{c_{i}}(\rho)\geq b_{i}-\varepsilon,\quad\forall i\in[m].

(1)

However, maintaining the mixture policy requires storing all the policies
\{\pi_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, making this memory intensive. Moreover, in order to deploy a mixture policy, a policy is repeatedly chosen uniformly at random from
\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{T}\}
, and the guarantees for the mixture policy only hold in expectation over this random selection. This implies that constraints are only approximately satisfied in expectation. In many safety-critical settings, such behavior is undesirable because a randomly sampled policy may significantly violate the constraints. These disadvantages significantly limit the practicality of
PD
methods, and motivate the development of principled algorithms that do not rely on mixture policies. To that end, prior work
(
Mondal and Aggarwal 2024b
;
Ding et al. 2023
;
Müller et al. 2024
;
Ying et al. 2022
)
has developed techniques that ensure
last-iterate
convergence meaning that the resulting guarantees hold for the final stochastic policy. These works employ regularization such as entropy-regularization on the policy and
\ell_{2}
regularization on the dual variables. In contrast, we propose using the
AL
method for CMDPs, and describe it next.

3
Augmented Lagrangian Policy Optimization Framework

The
AL
method
(
Bertsekas 2014
;
Powell 1969
;
Rockafellar 1976
)
is a classical approach for constrained optimization problems. It adds a quadratic penalty term to the Lagrangian to discourage constraint violations. In contrast to
PD
methods, the
AL
method considers the following problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\min_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}^{\beta}(\pi,\lambda)\;\text{;}\;\mathcal{L}^{\beta}(\pi,\lambda)

\displaystyle\coloneq V^{\pi}_{r}(\rho)+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\,\left\lparen-\min\left\{V^{\pi}_{c_{i}}(\rho)-b_{i}-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\beta},0\right\}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\right\rparen

(2)

Here,
0<\beta<\infty
is the penalty parameter, and the min operator within the quadratic term corresponds to be the constraint violation for the
i^{\text{th}}
constraint.
Intuitively, a larger parameter
\beta
increases the weight of the quadratic penalty term which forces the policy to satisfy the constraints, improving feasibility.
However, choosing
\beta
too large leads to an ill-conditioned optimization problem.
As
\beta\to 0
, the
AL
reduces to the standard Lagrangian.

To find a solution to
Eq.

2
,
the inexact
AL
method iteratively and alternatively updates the policy (primal variable) and the Lagrange multiplier (dual variable).
Specifically at iteration
t\geq 1
, for a target sub-optimality
{\epsilon}_{t}>0
and dual variable
\lambda_{t}
, the inexact
AL
method seeks an approximate solution
\pi_{t+1}
to the
AL

subproblem
, such that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\beta}(\pi_{t+1},\lambda_{t})\geq\argmax_{\pi}\,\mathcal{L}^{\beta}(\pi,\lambda_{t})-{\epsilon}_{t}.

(3)

Once an approximate primal solution is obtained, the dual variable is updated according to:
\forall i\in[m]

\lambda_{t+1}[i]=\lambda_{t}[i]-\frac{\beta}{2}\,\left\lparen V^{\pi_{t+1}}_{c_{i}}(\rho)-[b_{i}+\xi_{i}(\pi_{t+1})]\right\rparen

(4)

where
\xi_{i}(\pi)\coloneq\max\left\{V^{\pi}_{c_{i}}(\rho)-b_{i}-\frac{\lambda_{t}[i]}{\beta},0\right\}
acts as a slack variable and prevents overly aggressive updates to the dual variable. For instance,
\xi_{i}(\pi_{t+1})>0\iff V^{\pi_{t+1}}_{c_{i}}(\rho)>b_{i}+\frac{\lambda_{t}[i]}{\beta}
. As a result, when constraint
i
is well satisfied, the corresponding dual variable is only perturbed slightly. This avoids the excessive penalization of already-feasible constraints and prevents the dual variables from oscillating. On the other hand, if the constraint is not satisfied, the resulting update is akin to gradient descent w.r.t.
\lambda
and enforces feasibility. Compared to
PD
methods that regularize the primal/dual variables, the
AL
method employs a quadratic regularization on the constraint violation. Unlike
PD
methods, the step-size for the dual update in
Eq.

4
is directly determined by the penalty
\beta
.

Unlike the conventional use of the
AL
method in concave settings, the main difficulty of applying it to CMDPs is that
\mathcal{L}^{\beta}(\pi,\lambda)
is non-concave w.r.t
\pi

(
Agarwal et al. 2021
)
, making the
AL
subproblem challenging to solve accurately. Hence, we first assume access to the solver
\texttt{Oracle-AL}(\mathcal{L}^{\beta},\lambda_{t},{\epsilon}_{t})
that returns a policy satisfying
Eq.

3
for all
t\geq 1
. This abstraction separates the analysis of the
AL
iterations from the choice of the policy optimization method used to solve the subproblem.

4
Instantiating the
AL
Oracle: Tabular Setting

We first derive the

4.1
The
AL
Policy Gradient

We first consider the
direct representation
in the tabular setting, in which the policy is parameterized by its state–action probabilities, i.e.,
\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{A}
for all
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
. In the standard unconstrained RL setting, a recent line of theoretical works
(
Mei et al. 2020
;
Lu et al. 2024
;
Agarwal et al. 2021
;
Bhandari and Russo 2021
;
Lan 2023
;
Shi et al. 2023
;
Zhan et al. 2023
)
establishes global convergence guarantees for PG methods under various structural assumptions (e.g., smoothness or gradient domination).

5
Handling Function Approximation

We first describe a framework to handle general FA, and then specialize it to the class of log-linear policies for which we derive theoretical convergence guarantees.

In particular, we consider the general parameterized policy class:
\Pi_{\theta}\coloneq\left\{\pi\mid\exists\,\theta\text{ s.t. }\pi=\pi_{\theta}\right\}
, where
\pi_{\theta}
is the policy with learnable parameters
\theta
, and the model parameterizing the policy is implicit in the definition. For instance, this model could be chosen to be a neural network. Given
\Pi_{\theta}
, the only modification to our framework is that we now solve the
AL
subproblem within this parameterized class of policies, i.e.,
\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{\beta}(\pi,\lambda)
.

In order to do so, we note that the PG for the
AL
sub-problem can be calculated similar to that in
Section

4
, i.e. if
Q^{\pi}_{\Gamma(\pi)}
is the state-action value function for the pseudo-reward
\Gamma(\pi)
,
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\beta}(\pi(\theta))=\sum_{s}\frac{d^{\pi}(s)}{1-\gamma}\sum_{a}\,Q^{\pi}_{\Gamma(\pi)}(s,a)\frac{d\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}{d\theta}
, where,
\Gamma(\pi)=r-\beta\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\,\min\left\{V^{\pi}_{c_{i}}(\rho)-b_{i}-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\beta},0\right\}
. Note the pseudo-reward
\Gamma(\pi)
decomposes linearly into the original reward and constraint components. Consequently, its state–action value function
Q^{\pi}_{\Gamma(\pi)}
admits to the following decomposition:
Q^{\pi}_{\Gamma(\pi)}=Q^{\pi}_{r}-\beta\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}Q^{\pi}_{c_{i}}\min\left\{V^{\pi}_{c_{i}}(\rho)-b_{i}-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\beta},0\right\}
. As a result,
Q^{\pi}_{\Gamma(\pi)}
can be estimated using approximations of
Q^{\pi}_{r}
,
Q^{\pi}_{c_{i}}
, and
V^{\pi}_{c_{i}}(\rho)
. Hence, the same estimation tools employed in standard RL algorithms can be used to implement this gradient. This flexibility allows common PG methods, such as
PPO
,
TRPO

(
Schulman et al. 2015
)
, or
SPMA
to be used to solve the
AL
subproblem.

Following
Section

4
, we use policy optimization to solve the
AL
problem. Inspired by
Vaswani et al. 2021
;
Asad et al. 2024
, we adopt a surrogate optimization approach for updating the parameterized policy. In particular, if
K
is the total number of policy optimization iterations, at iteration
k\in[K]
, we first construct an intermediate policy using a generic PG method:
\pi_{k+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\texttt{PGMethod}(\pi_{k},Q^{\pi_{k}}_{\Gamma(\pi_{k})})
. We refer to the policy
\pi_{k+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
as the idealized policy update if there were no restrictions on the policy class. Note that we can use a generic policy optimization method such as
PQA
,
NPG
or
SPMA
to produce this intermediate policy. In the presence of FA,
\pi_{k+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
may not lie in the set of feasible policies. As a result, we project
\pi_{k+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
onto the set of feasible policies using the forward KL divergence and solve the following optimization problem:

\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\theta}}\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}d^{\pi_{k}}(s)\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{k+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\cdot|s)\|\pi(\cdot|s))
.

We use the forward KL divergence due to its mode-covering behavior which encourages exploration resulting in better practical performance. Note that the subsequent framework does not depend on this choice, and we could use the reverse KL or any other
f
-divergence. Solving the above optimization problem exactly is typically intractable since
\pi_{\theta}
is typically non-convex.
Following
(
Vaswani et al. 2021
;
Asad et al. 2024
;
Asad et al. 2025
;
Tomar et al. 2020
;

, we transform this projection problem into an unconstrained optimization and form the following surrogate as a function of

\ell_{k}(\theta)=\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}d^{\pi_{k}}(s)\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{k+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\cdot|s)}[-\log\pi_{\theta}(a|s)]\;\text{;}\;\theta_{k+1}\approx\argmin_{\theta}\ell_{k}(\theta)\;\text{;}\;\pi_{k+1}=\pi_{\theta_{k+1}}\,.

(8)

In practice, this minimization is carried out approximately using a finite number of gradient steps.

Remark
. For large problems where

cannot be computed exactly, the surrogate

can be efficiently approximated using sampling. In particular, following
Vaswani et al. 2021
;
Asad et al. 2024

\pi_{k}

(using

7

This framework supports off-policy updates
(
Vaswani et al. 2021

similar to

and

and perform multiple parameter updates. Next, we specialize this general framework to log-linear policies

6
Conclusion

We developed a policy optimization framework with the
AL
method for CMDPs that provides last-iterate guarantees. In the tabular setting, we prove the framework attains an
{\mathcal{O}}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{6}}})
last-iterate rate and under standard assumptions, a comparable rate under linear function approximation. The resulting approach is both principled and practical, demonstrating competitive performance in tabular environments as well as in large-scale continuous-control tasks. The main limitation of our result is the strong dependence on
\rho_{\min}
in the tabular setting and
{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{bias}}
for log-linear policies. Future directions include weakening these dependencies and incorporating actor–critic or off-policy methods into the theoretical framework.

This proof follows from

E.2
Linear Functional Approximation Setting

To handle large state/action spaces, we consider linear function approximation using log-linear policies. Given features
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}^{SA\times d}
, the policy is parameterized as

\pi_{\theta}(a|s)=\frac{\exp({\left\langle\varphi(s,a),\theta\right\rangle})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}\exp({\left\langle\varphi(s,a^{\prime}),\theta\right\rangle})}\,,

where

are the learnable parameters with

.
Since scaling the features can always be absorbed into a rescaling of
\theta
, we may, without loss of generality, normalize them so that

.
As a result, we consider the following policy class

\Pi_{\theta}:=\{\pi|\exists\theta\text{ s.t }\pi=\pi_{\theta}\}

where the model parameterizing the policy is implicit in the definition.

In this setting, to update the policy, we consider the following projected
PQA
(
PPQA
) update using the forward KL-divergence.
For all
k\geq 1
, using a fixed step-size

\displaystyle\pi_{k+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\pi\in(\Delta_{A})^{S}}\left[\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\frac{d^{\pi_{k}}(s)}{1-\gamma}\,{\left\langle Q^{\pi_{k}}_{\Gamma(\pi_{k})}(s,\cdot),\pi(\cdot|s)-\pi_{k}(\cdot|s)\right\rangle}-\frac{1}{2\,\eta}\,\sum_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\frac{d^{\pi_{k}}(s)}{1-\gamma}{\left\lVert\pi(\cdot|s)-\pi_{k}(\cdot|s)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}\right]\,,

(

Lemma 16

(General Utility Value Difference Lemma)
.

For any policies
\pi
, and
\pi^{\prime}
with general utility pseudo-reward
\Gamma(\pi)=\left.\frac{d{\mathcal{F}}(x)}{dx}\right|_{x=\mu^{\pi}}

Proof.

According to the value difference lemma (
Lemma

15
), for any value function
V^{\pi}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
with utility

and any policies
\pi^{\prime}
,
\pi
,

\displaystyle V^{\pi^{\prime}}_{u}-V^{\pi}_{u}

\displaystyle=(I_{S}-\gamma P_{\pi^{\prime}})^{-1}[{\mathcal{T}}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{u}V^{\pi}_{u}-V^{\pi}_{u}]

(
{\mathcal{T}}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{u}V^{\pi}_{u}=u^{\pi^{\prime}}+\gamma P^{\pi}V^{\pi}_{u}
)

\displaystyle\implies\rho\,[V^{\pi^{\prime}}_{u}-V^{\pi}_{u}]

\displaystyle=\rho\,(I_{S}-\gamma P_{\pi^{\prime}})^{-1}[{\mathcal{T}}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{u}V^{\pi}_{u}-V^{\pi}_{u}]

\rho

\displaystyle\implies\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho}[V^{\pi^{\prime}}_{u}(s)-V^{\pi}_{u}(s)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi^{\prime}}}\left[[{\mathcal{T}}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{u}V^{\pi}_{u}](s)-V^{\pi}_{u}(s)\right]

(

\displaystyle V^{\pi^{\prime}}_{\Gamma(\pi)}(\rho)-V^{\pi}_{\Gamma(\pi)}(\rho)

\displaystyle={\left\langle\Gamma(\pi),\mu^{\pi^{\prime}}-\mu^{\pi}\right\rangle}

\Gamma(\pi)

\pi

\mu^{\pi}

\displaystyle G(\pi^{\prime})-G(\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi^{\prime}}}\left[[{\mathcal{T}}^{\pi^{\prime}}_{\Gamma(\pi)}V^{\pi}_{\Gamma(\pi)}](s)-V^{\pi}_{\Gamma(\pi)}(s)\right].
