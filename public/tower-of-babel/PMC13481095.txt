Silicone Axillary Lymphadenopathy Associated With Rupture of Fourth-Generation Cohesive Silicone Breast Implants Following Breast Augmentation
Authors: Stephen D Bresnick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSilicone implant rupture is a common complication of breast augmentation, with silicone axillary lymphadenopathy (SAL) a recognized sequela.ObjectivesTo evaluate SAL associated with surgically confirmed rupture of fourth-generation cohesive silicone breast implants and correlate imaging with operative findings.MethodsA retrospective review included 260 consecutive patients undergoing surgery for removal of one or more ruptured cohesive silicone implants identified by non-contrast breast/axillary MRI and ultrasound. The implant-side was the unit of analysis, with bilateral ruptures counted separately. Implant characteristics, including manufacturer, volume, rupture type, and implant age at explantation, were correlated with ipsilateral axillary findings. All patients had primary augmentation with Allergan plc (Irvine, CA) or Mentor Worldwide LLC (Irvine, CA) fourth-generation cohesive implants and available implant records. Operative findings confirmed implant type, size, rupture presence, and rupture characteristics.ResultsAmong 260 patients, 299 ruptured implant-sides were identified, with imaging findings corroborated surgically. Intracapsular rupture accounted for 81.6% of ruptured implant-sides with 18.4% extracapsular ruptures. SAL occurred with both intracapsular and extracapsular rupture types but was more commonly seen with extracapsular rupture. Increasing implant age at explantation showed a strong independent association with SAL, while extracapsular rupture demonstrated a modest independent association. No significant differences in SAL were observed by implant manufacturers.ConclusionsSAL was frequently observed in patients undergoing surgical management of ruptured smooth, fourth-generation cohesive silicone implants. Increasing implant age at explantation was independently associated with SAL, while extracapsular rupture showed a modest independent association. These findings strongly influence patient counseling and surgical evaluation of aging fourth-generation silicone implants. Further study of newer fifth-generation highly cohesive implants is warranted.Level of Evidence: 4 (Therapeutic) For image description, please refer to the figure legend and surrounding text.

“Fourth generation” breast implants were developed by Allergan plc (Irvine, CA) and Mentor Worldwide LLC (Irvine, CA) in the early 1990s and were distinguished by thicker, more cohesive gel, thicker shells, and improved manufacturing controls than prior implant generations.1 Fourth-generation implants are still sold world-wide. The term, “fourth generation” describes the post-moratorium era of silicone gel implants, which began after US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approvals in November 2006 in the United States and prior to the “fifth generation” highly cohesive implants which have gained in popularity more recently. In 2019, Allergan introduced their “fifth generation” line of breast implants and phased out the fourth-generation implants over time. Mentor has continued to market and sell fourth generation “Memory Gel” silicone implants.

Although the exact number of fourth-generation silicone breast implants placed worldwide is not available, reliable procedural volume data provide a strong basis for estimating their prevalence.2,3 The Aesthetic Society (The American Society for Aesthetic Plastic Surgery) and American Society of Plastic Surgeons statistics demonstrate that more than 300,000 breast augmentation procedures have been performed annually in the United States between 2006 and 2019, the period during which fourth-generation cohesive silicone implants constituted a substantial proportion of silicone devices in use in the United States. Taken together, sustained high procedural volumes over nearly 2 decades and ongoing global availability strongly support the conclusion that several million fourth-generation silicone breast implants have been implanted worldwide since their introduction in 2006.

Prior investigations have reported rupture rates of fourth generation silicone implants,4-6 but the rates of axillary silicone lymphadenopathy for fourth-generation implants is unknown. The majority of studies investigating silicone axillary lymphadenopathy (SAL) are older studies with less cohesive implants, have not reported the implant types involved, their manufacturers, silicone gel type, previous surgeries, or implant age.7-9 Our goal was to delineate SAL occurrence with fourth generation implants specifically and correlate SAL to implant age, size, manufacturer, and type of rupture in a large cohort of patients.

METHODS

A retrospective study was performed for 260 consecutive female patients who had a history of primary silicone breast augmentation with fourth generation Allergan “Natrelle” or Mentor Worldwide LLC “Memory Gel” smooth-surface breast implants placed in a dual plane position. The study was performed under IRB supervision (Tier IRB, Redfield, KS). Patients were treated between October 2021 and October 2025 by a single surgeon. Patients with a history of breast or axillary surgery prior or following the initial breast augmentation were excluded. Patients presented with an implant rupture diagnosed with non-contrast breast MRI. If patients presented with an MRI supporting rupture, a high-resolution breast ultrasound was obtained to both confirm the rupture and to further evaluate the axillary region. Evidence of enlarged node(s) with silicone signal on MRI and/or echogenic axillary silicone on ultrasound was considered positive for the presence of axillary silicone. Patients without imaging at presentation had both tests performed as part of the work-up. All patients studied had implant records available with manufacturers’ data and implantation dates. Patients found at surgery to have “false positives” or no implant rupture, were excluded from the study.

Rupture rates and the occurrence of silicone axillary lymphadenopathy were evaluated for breast implants through the review of both the MRI and ultrasound records. The implant/side was the primary unit of analysis. Each breast implant and its ipsilateral axilla were evaluated. For patients with bilateral ruptures, each side was treated as a distinct observation. Data recorded were side-specific implant characteristics, such as manufacturer, implant volume, implant age (time implanted), rupture type, and side-specific SAL status based on ipsilateral axillary imaging findings.

Medical records were evaluated to determine implant manufacturer, implant volume, age of implant at explantation, nature of the rupture found at surgery, and corroboration of rupture type described by imaging studies. Patients were excluded from the study if the documentation of existing implants did not match surgical findings such as implant manufacturer or size, or if imaging studies did not match surgical findings in relation to rupture type (intracapsular vs extracapsular).

Variables

The primary outcome was the presence of silicone-associated axillary lymphadenopathy (SAL), with primary explanatory variables included implant manufacturer (Allergan vs Mentor), rupture type identified by imaging and corroborated with surgical findings (intracapsular vs extracapsular), implant size (cc) and implant age at the time of implant rupture surgery (continuous, in years).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize implant characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations. Comparisons of categorical variables between manufacturers were performed using chi-square analysis.

The primary unit of analysis was the implant-side. Silicone axillary lymphadenopathy (SAL) was defined as the presence of silicone-consistent axillary lymph node findings on imaging in the ipsilateral axilla. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate the independent association of implant age at explantation (continuous, in years), implant volume (continuous), rupture type (extracapsular vs intracapsular), and manufacturer with SAL.

Because some patients contributed bilateral implant-side observations, correlation within patient was accounted for using clustered logistic regression with generalized estimating equations and an exchangeable working correlation structure. Linearity of implant age on the log-odds scale was assessed and the linear specification was retained. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P-value < .05. All analyses were performed using R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

For ruptured implants, intracapsular rupture was more common than extracapsular rupture (81.6% vs 18.4%), for the fourth-generation implants studied. Among 299 ruptured implant-sides, 89 were Allergan Natrelle (including 10 bilateral cases) and 210 were Mentor MemoryGel (including 29 bilateral cases). For Allergan implants, 75 ruptures were intracapsular, 14 were extracapsular. SAL was noted in 11 implant-sides (14.6%) for intracapsular rupture and 2 implant-sides (14.2%) for extracapsular ruptures. For Mentor implants, 169 demonstrated intracapsular ruptures, while 41 had extracapsular ruptures. Intracapsular ruptures were associated with silicone axillary lymphadenopathy in 23 implant-sides (13.6%) while extracapsular ruptures were found in 10 implant-sides (24.4%).

DISCUSSION

Fourth-generation silicone breast implants were introduced in the United States in 2006 with the intent of improving safety compared with earlier designs, and they remain widely used. In this study, silicone axillary lymphadenopathy was frequently identified among ruptured fourth-generation implants undergoing surgical management. Implant age at the time of explantation showed a strong and highly statistically significant association with SAL and was the most important factor related to axillary silicone involvement. SAL was observed with both intracapsular and extracapsular rupture and was seen more often in older implants. After adjustment for implant age, implant volume, and manufacturer, extracapsular rupture showed a modest but statistically significant independent association with SAL. Taken together, these findings indicate that implant age showed the strongest association with SAL in the adjusted model, and extracapsular rupture showed a modest additional association.

Implant integrity continues to be a major health concern among the public regarding implant safety. Silicone implants were removed from the US market in 1992 as the FDA stated there was not enough evidence that the devices were safe in the long term.10 In 2006, following prospective clinical trials, the FDA approved cohesive silicone implants for general use in the United States for both aesthetic and reconstructive purposes.11 Cohesivity was increased to reduce silicone migration with shell failure, reduce bleed, and meet stricter regulatory requirements.12,13 Plastic surgeons soon came to realize that these devices had rupture rates which increased over time.14 The prospective core Allergan Natrelle study showed rupture rates of 7.7% at 10 years with implants and 13% for patients undergoing serial MRI scans.6 Mentor data through a short-term follow-up of 3 years showed rupture rates of .5% for primary augmentation and 7.7% for revision augmentation; however, post-approval Mentor studies through 8 years showed primary augmentation rupture rates of 13.6%, while revision augmentation rupture rates were 15.5%.4,5,15 Higher rates of rupture have been published in the literature.16,17 The results of these studies demonstrate that silicone implant rupture is common with fourth generation implants.

Most prior studies evaluating silicone breast implant rupture and silicone axillary lymphadenopathy (SAL) have been retrospective and imaging-based.18-20 Although these reports generally suggest an association between rupture and axillary silicone involvement, many are limited by incomplete implant histories, inclusion of mixed implant generations, unknown manufacturer or implant age, and reliance on MRI or ultrasound without operative confirmation. Rupture type has also not been consistently defined, and prior surgical findings have often been unavailable, making it difficult to determine the independent contribution of rupture characteristics to the development of SAL.

The present study was designed to address these limitations by restricting analysis to patients with known smooth surface, fourth-generation silicone implants of documented age and manufacturer, with rupture confirmed at the time of explantation. This yielded a large series of surgically verified ruptures and allowed more precise assessment of axillary silicone involvement within a single implant generation. To our knowledge, this study represents the largest cohort of surgically confirmed ruptured fourth-generation silicone breast implants evaluated for the presence of silicone axillary lymphadenopathy.

Multivariable clustered logistic regression showed that older implant age at the time of explantation was associated with higher odds of silicone axillary lymphadenopathy among ruptured implant-sides. Implant age was analyzed as a continuous variable, reflecting an association between aging implants and the presence of axillary silicone at the time of surgical evaluation. Figure 1 presents age-grouped proportions of SAL, while inference regarding the relationship between implant age and SAL is based on the regression analysis. Although a small elevation in the observed SAL proportion is seen in the 4- to 6-year implant age group, this finding likely reflects sampling variability related to the small number of ruptured implant-sides in this age-grouping rather than a true biologic effect. This is supported by the wide confidence interval and substantial overlap with adjacent age groups. In contrast, older implant age groups demonstrate a more stable and progressive increase in SAL prevalence, consistent with an age-dependent effect. In the context of known rupture rates for fourth-generation devices, this association is relevant for patient counseling and may help inform discussions regarding surveillance and management of aging fourth-generation silicone breast implants.

The results of this study suggest that the rates of SAL for both implant manufacturers were similar for both intracapsular and extracapsular ruptures, which is not surprising based on implant generation and the likely source for silicone gel when these implants were manufactured. Prior to 2006, Dow Corning (Midland, MI) was the leading supplier for medical grade silicone used for medical devices in the United States.21 However, in early 2007, Dow Corning withdrew from the breast-implant materials market. Industry sources have suggested that similar platinum-cured, cross-linked silicone gels were supplied to multiple manufacturers during this period by a single manufacturer, which may partly explain the concordant findings between implant brands.22 Although the physical properties, degree of cross-linking, densities, and other physical parameters of the gels used in Allergan and Mentor implants were not required to be disclosed to the FDA due to proprietary factors, the gels may have been similar in nature and may explain the similarity of the SAL findings in this study.

Silicone implant rupture appears to represent a dynamic process rather than a static event. This concept is nicely demonstrated by several patients in our cohort with a history of MRI findings suggestive of rupture without SAL who did not undergo treatment for several years after their initial rupture diagnosis. They presented to our practice with dated imaging and were re-evaluated with updated MRI and ultrasound analysis. Findings at re-evaluation supported that rupture had progressed to extracapsular with or without SAL. In other cases, intracapsular rupture without SAL at historic imaging progressed to intracapsular rupture with SAL with updated imaging. Findings like these suggest that prolonged rupture duration without surgical management may allow further silicone dissemination beyond the capsule and into regional lymphatics. Although this study cannot establish causality, these findings support early consideration of surgical treatment once rupture is identified to potentially limit silicone extension beyond the breast.

In-office ultrasound screening of fourth-generation breast implants may be the most practical way to routinely evaluate patients with aging fourth-generation silicone breast implants. This simple office procedure may increase patient compliance in silicone implant integrity monitoring. This procedure, which can be performed during a routine office visit, greatly reduces patient cost, speed, and logistical barriers to more formal imaging studies. The technique is effective in the detection of silent rupture to avoid some of the problems associated with rupture progression. It allows earlier identification of intracapsular rupture and can be repeated easily without advanced imaging. For patients who delay surgery, ultrasound may be useful for interval monitoring and for recognizing progression to extracapsular silicone spread, helping guide timing of further imaging or intervention.

We did not find an association between implant size and the presence of SAL. This is not surprising because we studied if SAL was detected on imaging studies, rather than a quantitative measure of the amount of silicone present in the axilla. It may be that more silicone may extravasate in larger silicone implants, or that with larger volumetric ruptures, more silicone may enter the lymphatic system, but future studies would be required to investigate this issue. One of the limitations of our study was that volumetric analysis was not performed. Our data was also dependent on imaging results with some potential variation on the interpretation of imaging, and some potential variation in the facilities which conducted the MRI/ultrasound studies.

Limitations

This study was not designed to evaluate for rupture rates, causes of rupture, or the time course in which rupture occurred. We did not know when actual rupture occurred or the time of rupture to the presence of silicone in the lymph nodes. We did know that the presence of SAL in our cohort strongly correlated with implant age, but the timing of when rupture occurred is not known. The rates of SAL only applied to implant-sides with confirmed rupture requiring surgical intervention, not the broader implant population. SAL incidence among patients with intact implants was not assessed and would require further study. The data did not allow evaluation of how intracapsular or extracapsular rupture may evolve into silicone axillary lymphadenopathy, although the literature does suggest how that process likely occurs.23 While the single surgeon/single center design of the study has some limitations to generalization of the findings, it reduces inter-observer variability in operative assessment. Further study of silicone implant rupture and associated SAL would be useful in fifth generation implants.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings of the current investigation show that for fourth-generation silicone breast implants, silicone axillary lymphadenopathy is common and more often seen with extracapsular rupture. Intracapsular ruptures are more common than extracapsular ruptures. The age of fourth generation implants and extracapsular rupture correlates with SAL. These findings may assist surgeons in counseling patients with aging fourth-generation implants regarding axillary findings on imaging and the potential implications of implant rupture. Timely management of ruptures is encouraged. Further study of newer, fifth-generation highly cross-linked implants and the occurrence of SAL with rupture is recommended.
