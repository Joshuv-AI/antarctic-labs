Arrival-time distributions as a probe of the preferred foliation in relativistic Bohmian mechanics


Abstract
Relativistic extensions of de Broglie-Bohm theory postulate a preferred foliation of space-time, an additional structure essential for defining simultaneous configurations on Minkowski space-time, but conventionally believed to be empirically undetectable at quantum equilibrium. In this paper, we outline an experimental protocol for empirically detecting the preferred foliation, which is assumed to be flat for simplicity. Building on the arrival-time distributions for spin-1/2 particles predicted by Das and Dürr [S. Das and D. Dürr, Sci. Rep. 9 : 2242 (2019)], we show that in an EPRB-type experiment with spacelike-separated spin and arrival-time measurements, the observed arrival-time statistics will depend crucially on the temporal order of these measurements relative to the preferred foliation of space-time. This dependence offers a potential experimental signature of the preferred foliation postulated by relativistic Bohmian models. Moreover, it implies the possibility of superluminal signaling.

1  Introduction

The widely accepted “peaceful coexistence” between quantum mechanics (QM) and special relativity rests on a crucial mathematical pillar: no-signaling theorems
[
28
,
30
]
. These theorems guarantee that spacelike-separated quantum measurements performed on entangled particles cannot be used for superluminal communication. However, they are not unconditional. Their proofs rely–explicitly or implicitly—on the assumption that every conceivable experiment yields statistics described by a Positive Operator-Valued Measure (POVM)
[
8
]

[
65
, Sec. 5.5.9 and 7.6.2]
. If a quantum experiment were to produce statistics outside the scope of the POVM framework, the central premise of these theorems would break down, and the door to superluminal signaling would, in principle, be opened.

Arrival-time measurements present a promising setting for investigating this possibility. This is because, unlike position or momentum, time is not represented by a Hermitian operator in standard QM, and despite decades of research, no satisfactory POVM for describing arrival-time experiments has been established. Existing proposals—such as the Aharonov–Bohm–Kijowski distribution
[
1
,
43
]
—are essentially limited to freely moving particles and lack compelling extensions to general contexts
[
54
,
13
]
. More recent POVM proposals, such as those based on an absorbing boundary condition
[
64
]
, have been shown to be empirically inadequate
[
7
]
. This widely recognized theoretical gap, the so-called “quantum arrival-time problem”
[
56
]
—the absence of a widely accepted, empirically promising POVM for arrival times—makes arrival-time experiments a privileged arena for exploring potential violations of no-signaling constraints.

In 2019, Das and Dürr (DD)
[
11
]
introduced spin-dependent arrival-time distributions calculated within the framework of de Broglie–Bohm theory (dBB), also known as Bohmian mechanics (BM). These distributions, which have recently been proven to be non

POVM by Goldstein, Tumulka, and Zanghì (GTZ)

,

\tfrac{1}{2}
particle moving in a waveguide exhibit

-
signaling and foliation

The insight that DD’s predictions could, in principle, enable superluminal signaling was, to our knowledge, first recognized by Sandro Donadi and Siddhant Das (private communication with Siddhant Das). Tim Maudlin subsequently popularized this idea in several public discussions
[

Notably, both Ref.

In the present work, we adopt a different perspective: we explore the consequences that would follow if these arrival-time distributions—as drastic as they are—were even

From this extension, we derive an explicit protocol for superluminal signaling. More importantly—and this constitutes the central novel contribution of this paper—we demonstrate that, if DD’s predictions are approximately correct, they provide a method for the direct experimental detection of the

While the potential connection between DD’s predictions and superluminal signaling has been noted before, the possibility of using these predictions to detect the preferred foliation has received less attention. To our knowledge, Maudlin was the first to publicly mention this possibility

Second, and crucially, our protocols do not require exact confirmation of DD’s predictions. 2  From Bohmian Trajectories to Arrival-Time Statistics

Arrival-time experiments probe a foundational question in QM: When does a particle, prepared in a known initial state, trigger a detector placed at a specified location? In a typical experimental setup, an initially well-localized particle—described by an initial wave function
\Psi_{0}
—is released from a source and propagates toward a detection surface
\mathcal{D}
, leading to a detection event at some time
\tau
. By repeating the experiment under identical conditions, one accumulates a statistical distribution for
\tau
. Any satisfactory quantum theory should predict this distribution: a probability density
\Pi^{\Psi_{0}}(\tau)
such that
\Pi^{\Psi_{0}}(\tau)d\tau
gives the probability that the particle is detected in
\mathcal{D}
between times
\tau
and
\tau+d\tau
.

Within the standard formalism of QM, however, predicting such a distribution remains an open problem, the so-called “quantum arrival-time problem”
[
56
]
. The main roadblock is that unlike position or momentum, time is not represented by a Hermitian operator; consequently there is no Born rule and associated projection postulate for arrival-time experiments. Consequently, multiple competing proposals have been advanced to define and compute
\Pi^{\Psi_{0}}(\tau)
, but no consensus has emerged
[
56
]
.

In this landscape, the dBB theory, given its explicit particle ontology, offers a distinctive conceptual advantage over most existing quantum theories. In the words of Dürr and Teufel, this theory is “tailor-made to answer [the arrival-time question], since the notion of where and when the particle crosses a surface is a natural one when trajectories exist”
[
27
, pp. 345]
. By providing explicit, deterministic trajectories, the Bohmian approach yields arrival-time predictions with conceptual clarity and mathematical precision. This trajectory-based method is applicable to a wide range of physical scenarios—an advantage that stands in sharp contrast to the severely restricted operator-based proposals
[
1
,
43
]
. Pioneered by Leavens
[
44
]
, the Bohmian-trajectory based treatment of arrival-time experiments has since been consolidated and extended, becoming a central tool in the field (see [56, 61, 110–124] of
[
15
]
). We now outline how this method works.

2.1  The de Broglie-Bohm Theory for a Non-Relativistic Spin-1/2 Particle

The de Broglie-Bohm (dBB) theory
1
1

1

[
6

4

35

65

26

, also known as Bohmian mechanics (BM), is a heterodox quantum theory that posits the existence of point-like particles following well-defined trajectories at all times, including outside measurement contexts. In this theory, a particle follows a deterministic trajectory
\mathbf{X}(t)
determined by its initial position
\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{X}_{0}
and the initial quantum state
\Psi(\textbf{x},0)=\Psi_{0}(\textbf{x})
. The quantum state of a non-relativistic spin-
\frac{1}{2}
particle is a two-component spinor:
\Psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, governed (in the absence of external magnetic fields) by the Schrödinger-Pauli equation:

i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}(\mathbf{x},t)=\left[-\,\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\nabla\right)^{2}+V(\mathbf{x},t)\right]\Psi(\mathbf{x},t),

(2.1)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
denotes the vector of Pauli matrices. For a spin-
\frac{1}{2}
particle, the probability density is given by the usual Born rule:
\rho(\mathbf{x},t)=\Psi^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x},t)\Psi(\mathbf{x},t)
.

As in the spinless case, a Bohmian particle is guided by a velocity defined as the ratio of a probability current to the probability density:

\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{dBB}}\left(\mathbf{X}(t),t\right),\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{dBB}}\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)=\frac{\mathbf{J}_{\text{Pauli}}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\rho(\mathbf{x},t)}.

(2.2)

This guiding equation ensures the equivariance of the Bohmian dynamics. The Pauli current
\mathbf{J}_{\text{Pauli}}
, together with the probability density
\rho(\mathbf{x},t)=\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi
, solve the quantum continuity equation:

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}(\mathbf{x},t)+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\text{Pauli}}(\mathbf{x},t)=0

(2.3)

which follows from the single-particle Pauli equation (
2.1
).

Some authors
[
57
]
propose to define the guiding equation (
2.2
) by relying on the simplest current that satisfies this continuity equation:

\mathbf{J}_{\text{Pauli}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\!\left(\Psi^{\dagger}\nabla\Psi\right)\!.

(2.4)

However, this expression is not unique. The continuity equation (
2.3
) admits a gauge freedom: given any scalar function
a(\mathbf{x},t)
and vector field
\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x},t)
satisfying
\partial_{t}a+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{b}=0
, the transformations
\rho\to\rho+a
and
\mathbf{J}\to\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{b}
preserve the conservation law (
2.3
)
[
41
]
. Such transformations preserve probability conservation but can significantly alter the resulting Bohmian trajectories. The expression for the probability current density
\mathbf{J}_{\text{Pauli}}
is not uniquely determined by the continuity equation (
2.3
) alone.

The resolution lies in an additional physical constraint: the Pauli current must arise as the non-relativistic limit of the Dirac current, thereby inheriting the correct relativistic kinematics. Following the analyses of Holland
[
41
]
and Hodge
et al.

[
39
]
, and adopting the expression used by Das and Dürr
[
11
]
, we take:

\mathbf{J}_{\text{Pauli}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\underbrace{\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\left[\Psi^{\dagger}\nabla\Psi\right]}_{\text{{convective flux}}}+\underbrace{\frac{\hbar}{2m}\nabla\times\left(\Psi^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Psi\right)}_{\text{{spin flux}}}.

(2.5)

This current is comprised of two physically distinct contributions. The first term, the
convective flux
, resembles the well-known current density of a spinless particle and captures the motion resulting from the phase gradient of the wave function. The second term, the
spin (magnetization or Gordon) flux
, arises from the spin degrees of freedom. Crucially, the spin flux is divergenceless, so (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) have the same divergence, and therefore, fulfill the quantum continuity equation (
2.3
). Substituting this current into the dBB velocity formula, Eq. (
2.2
), yields a velocity field comprising a
convective velocity
(arising from the first term) and a
spin velocity
(arising from the second). The spin velocity induces a precessional component

2

).
in the particle trajectories while preserving the overall equivariance of the theory.

2.2  The
de Broglie correspondence principle
: From Microscopic Trajectories to Empirical Predictions

Any rigorous quantum theory postulates certain fundamental entities and specifies their behaviour. To make observable predictions, one must connect this microscopic behaviour to macroscopic features of a measurement apparatus—for example, the position of a pointer or the appearance of a dot on a screen. Ideally, one would derive empirical predictions by applying the quantum theory to the
\sim 10^{23}
particles that compose the measurement apparatus itself. However, such a calculation is practically impossible, and no “theory of everything” is currently available. Consequently, every quantum theory must rely on some form of idealisation to extract concrete, testable predictions from its formalism (see
[
53
]
for a more detailed analysis). We call such a predictive method, which bridges the gap between abstract formalism and empirical data, a
correspondence principle
(private conversation with Will Cavendish and Siddhant Das).

The widely used Bohmian method for predicting arrival times leverages what may appropriately be called the
de Broglie correspondence principle
.
3
3

3

The idea of such a correspondence principle can be traced back to Louis de Broglie, who, inspired by Einstein’s theory of the photon, developed his theory of the double solution and the pilot wave (see, in particular,
[
19
, pp. 447-448]
and
[
20
, pp. 273-274]
). The first “Bohmian” formulation (i.e., pilot
-
wave version) of this principle was proposed by Kennard in 1928
[
42
]
, anticipating Bohm’s results by 24 years. For a broader historical analysis related to the origin of this principle, see
[
24
]
.
This trajectory
-
based correspondence principle can be stated as follows:

For a given initial wave function
\Psi_{0}
and a hypothetical initial configuration
\mathbf{X}_{0}
, the time and location at which a real detector fires when placed at a surface
\mathcal{D}
is well approximated by the time and location at which the Bohmian trajectory
\mathbf{X}\left(t\right)
—computed from the same
\Psi_{0}
and
\mathbf{X}_{0}
but
in the absence of the detector
—first crosses the mathematical surface
\mathcal{D}
.

In other words, the idealised crossing time of the unperturbed Bohmian trajectory serves as a proxy for the actual detection time. This correspondence rule is the essential link between the microscopic ontology (trajectories) and the macroscopic reading of a clock.

For a hypothetical scenario in which the precise initial configuration
\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{X}_{0}
of the particle were known, the principle is implemented as follows. One computes the Bohmian trajectory
\mathbf{X}(t)
by solving the free Schrödinger–Pauli equation (
5.2
) and the guiding equation (
2.2
) with the initial conditions
(\Psi_{0},\mathbf{X}_{0})
. From this trajectory one then calculates the
first arrival time

\tau(\mathbf{X}_{0})
at the detection surface
\mathcal{D}
as the first crossing time:

\tau(\mathbf{X}_{0})=\min\{t\mid\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathcal{D}\text{ and }\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\operatorname{supp}[\Psi_{0}]\},

(2.6)

where
\operatorname{supp}[\Psi_{0}]
denotes the support of the initial wave function.

Because the precise initial configuration
\mathbf{X}_{0}
is not known in a single experimental run, Eq. (
2.6
) does not yield a deterministic prediction for an individual event. Instead, it defines a random variable
\tau
whose probability distribution is obtained by averaging over the distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{0}
in the ensemble of identically prepared systems.

At this point a second, logically independent hypothesis enters: the
quantum equilibrium hypothesis
. It states that for an ensemble of particles all prepared with the same wave function
\Psi_{0}
, the initial positions
\mathbf{X}_{0}
are distributed according to
|\Psi_{0}|^{2}
. Combining this hypothesis with the de Broglie correspondence principle yields the arrival
-
time distribution

\Pi_{\text{dBB}}^{\Psi_{0}}(\tau)=\int_{\operatorname{supp}[\Psi_{0}]}\delta\!\left(\tau(\mathbf{X}_{0})-\tau\right)|\Psi_{0}(\mathbf{X}_{0})|^{2}\ d^{3}\mathbf{X}_{0},

(2.7)

where
\tau(\mathbf{X}_{0})
satisfies (
2.6
). Thus, the correspondence principle motivates the definition of the arrival
-
time distribution in terms of the crossing statistics of unperturbed trajectories, while the equilibrium hypothesis supplies the specific probability measure over the initial positions.

The distribution
\Pi_{\text{dBB}}^{\Psi_{0}}(\tau)
is often called an
ideal
or
intrinsic
arrival
-
time distribution, because the influence of the detector on the particle’s motion is deliberately ignored. For a detailed exposition see
[
27
, Ch. 16]
. This approach has been successfully applied to scattering experiments, where it reproduces measured distributions of arrival positions and joint arrival
-
position
-
time statistics (the so-called “screen problem”
[
55
]
) with high accuracy
[
16
,
17
,
10
]
. Such empirical success lends strong support to the de Broglie correspondence principle as a pragmatic tool for extracting predictions from Bohmian mechanics.

Because Bohmian trajectories depend non
-
linearly on the initial wave function (through the guiding equation), the distributions
\Pi_{\text{dBB}}^{\Psi_{0}}(\tau)
need not be compatible with any Positive Operator
-
Valued Measure (POVM). The first proof of this incompatibility was given by Ruggenthaler
et al.

[
60
]
. However, in the simple one
-
dimensional setups studied prior to the work of Das and Dürr, this non
-
POVM character was not particularly manifest—likely because robust quantum backflow (see Sec.
8.4
) was absent. Consequently, the potential incompatibility with POVM statistics remained an abstract possibility without concrete experimental signatures. The spin
-
polarisation
-
dependent arrival
-
time distributions derived by Das and Dürr provide the first explicit, qualitatively distinct predictions that fall outside the POVM framework, thereby opening the door to the novel effects analyzed in this paper.

2.3  A Brief Outline of Das and Dürr’s Predictions

3  The Preferred Foliation: A Necessary Structure in Relativistic Bohmian Mechanics

In this section we explain why the
preferred foliation
—a space-time structure postulated by every relativistic extension of de dBB theory—emerges naturally, not only from the non-relativistic version of the theory, but even from the mathematical framework of
standard
QM. Understanding this connection is essential for appreciating why the foliation, though often regarded as an extra-theoretical postulate, is in fact deeply rooted in the conceptual foundations of standard QM.

3.1  Non-Relativistic Bohmian Mechanics and Newtonian Simultaneity

So far our introduction of BM has been restricted to single-particle systems, yet the non-locality of the dynamics appears in multi-particle systems. In non-relativistic BM, the complete state of an
N
-particle system at time
t
is specified by two elements: the wave function
\Psi_{t}
and the actual configuration
\mathbf{X}(t):=(\mathbf{X}_{1}(t),\dots,\mathbf{X}_{N}(t))
of this system. The dynamics of the system is governed by two deterministic equations. First, the wave function always evolves according to an
N-
particle wave equation (Schrödinger or Pauli), which always implies a continuity equation of the form:

\partial_{t}\rho(\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{N},t)+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\nabla_{k}\cdot\mathbf{J}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{N},t)=0,

(3.1)

where
\nabla_{k}=(\partial_{x_{k}},\partial_{y_{k}},\partial_{z_{k}})
denotes the gradient with respect to the coordinates of the
k
-th particle,
\rho(\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{N},t)=|\Psi_{t}|^{2}
is the probability density and
\mathbf{J}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{N},t)
is the probability current for the
k
-th particle. Second, each particle moves along a trajectory
\mathbf{X}_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
(for
k=1,\cdots,N
) that satisfies a
guiding equation
of the generic form:

\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{k}(t)=\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{dBB}}^{(k)}\big(t,\mathbf{X}_{1}(t),\dots,\mathbf{X}_{N}(t)\big)=\frac{\mathbf{J}^{(k)}}{\rho}\big(t,\mathbf{X}_{1}(t),\dots,\mathbf{X}_{N}(t)\big).

(3.2)

Each particle is thus guided by a velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{dBB}}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{N},t):=\mathbf{J}^{(k)}/\rho
that depends, crucially, on the instantaneous positions of
all

N
particles.
5
5

5

The structure of this guiding field—i.e.
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{j}/\rho
with
\mathbf{j}
and
\rho
satisfying a continuity equation (
3.1
)—ensures
equivariance
: if the initial configuration
\mathbf{X}(0)
is distributed according to
|\Psi_{0}|^{2}
, then at any later time

the distribution of
\mathbf{X}(t)
is given by
|\Psi_{t}|^{2}
. See
[
6
, Sec. 5.C]
for a rigorous proof.
This non-local coupling—the dependence of each particle’s motion on the simultaneous positions of all others—embodies the essential non-locality of quantum phenomena within the Bohmian framework.

The structure of this non-local dynamics (
3.2
) is intimately tied to the Newtonian concept of
absolute simultaneity
. This connection becomes apparent when we examine the mathematical framework of standard QM, which is built upon the concept of
configuration space

\Gamma\cong\mathbb{R}^{3N}
—a point
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N})\in\Gamma
represents a possible (ordered) spatial configuration of the
N
particles composing the system. Here a subtle but crucial tension arises. Standard QM explicitly employs configuration space as the domain of its central mathematical object, the wave function, yet it declines to populate this space with actual configurations
(\mathbf{X}_{1}(t),\dots,\mathbf{X}_{N}(t))
of particles. As de Broglie pointed out, “[i]t seems a little paradoxical to construct a configuration space with the coordinates of points which do not exist”
[
2
, p. 379]
. BM resolves this tension by taking configuration space literally.

In dBB theory, at any time
t
, the
N
-particle system occupies a well-defined configuration
(\mathbf{X}_{1}(t),\dots,\mathbf{X}_{N}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
that corresponds to a specific point in the abstract configuration space
\Gamma
. By definition, such a configuration represents the positions of all
N
particles
at a single instant
. As Maudlin emphasizes, “[c]onfigurations are
configurations at a time
; they specify where all of the particles in a system are
at a given moment
”
[
50
, p. 198]
. Consequently, by committing to the literal existence of configurations, the dBB theory commits itself to a notion of absolute simultaneity.

This point deserves emphasis because it is often overlooked in critiques of dBB theory. The formalism of orthodox QM is itself built upon configuration space and therefore
already implicitly presupposes
a notion of absolute simultaneity—the same Newtonian simultaneity that underlies the time derivative in the Schrödinger equation. However, standard QM avoids confronting this commitment by treating configuration space as a mere mathematical convenience rather than an abstract space representing a feature of physical reality. BM, by taking the formalism seriously and populating configuration space with actual configurations of particles, makes this implicit commitment explicit.

In the non-relativistic context, this commitment is unproblematic: both Newtonian and Galilean space-time come equipped with a preferred foliation into simultaneity hyperplanes
[
51
, Chap. 3]
. This intrinsic structure gives physical meaning to the notion of “at a given moment” in those classical space-times. In the relativistic context, however, the situation differs fundamentally. Because Minkowski space-time lacks any notion of absolute simultaneity, a Bohmian theory formulated in this setting must introduce a
preferred foliation
of Minkowski space-time into spacelike hypersurfaces—an additional space-time structure that provides the notion of “at a given moment” needed to define simultaneous configurations. Thus, while many physicists criticize the preferred foliation as an unwelcome addition to relativity, it is in fact the natural extension of a commitment already present—though unacknowledged—in the standard quantum formalism.

3.2  Hypersurface Bohm-Dirac Models: Making the Foliation Explicit

The primary relativistic extension of dBB theory is the class of models known as Hypersurface Bohm-Dirac (HBD) models
[
25
]
. These models preserve the theory’s commitment to deterministic particle trajectories while generalizing the dynamics to Dirac particles and achieving a form of formal Lorentz covariance—meaning the laws can be written in a coordinate-free geometric language. However, this covariance is purchased at the price of introducing an additional space-time structure: a
preferred foliation
of Minkowski space-time into spacelike hypersurfaces.

The simplest and historically first member of this class is the Bohm-Hiley-Dirac model
6
6

6

Often called the
Bohm-Dirac model

[
25
]
; we use the fuller terminology to acknowledge Basil Hiley’s contribution.

[
5
, Chap. 12]
. In this model, one begins by singling out a preferred Lorentz frame
F
, with coordinates
(t,\mathbf{x})
. For
N
Dirac particles, the wave function
\Psi(t,\mathbf{x}_{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{(N)})
satisfies the many-particle Dirac equation written in the coordinates of this frame:

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(c\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{k}+mc^{2}\beta_{k}\big)\Psi,

(3.3)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}:=\mathbb{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{I}\otimes\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathbb{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{I}
denotes the Dirac matrices acting on the
k

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2},\alpha^{3})
placed at the
k
-th position, and
\beta_{k}
defined in a similar manner. The particle velocities (again expressed in
F
) are given by the guiding equation:

\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{(k)}(t)=\frac{\Psi^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\Psi}{\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi}\big(\mathbf{X}_{(1)}(t),\dots,\mathbf{X}_{(N)}(t)\big).

(3.4)

Thus, at the common time
t
in frame
F
, the positions of all particles are simultaneously defined, and each particle’s velocity depends on the simultaneous positions of all others. The frame
F
therefore provides the notion of “at a given moment” that is required to give meaning to a configuration at an instant.

Each trajectory
\mathbf{X}_{(k)}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
solving Eq. (
3.4
) corresponds to a worldline
X_{(k)}:\mathbb{R}\to M
in Minkowski space-time, parametrized by the preferred frame’s time coordinate:
X_{(k)}^{\mu}(t):=\big(t,\mathbf{X}_{(k)}(t)\big)
, with
\mu=0,1,2,3
and
k=1,\cdots,N
. Together, these
N

X_{(k)}
—often called an
N-path

X:\mathbb{R}\to M\times\cdots\times M\equiv M^{N}
—describe the complete history of the system.

Crucially, if one were to write the Bohm-Hiley-Dirac dynamics (Eqs. (
3.3
) and (
3.4
)) in a different Lorentz frame, the resulting worldlines would differ, thus changing the predicted history of the system. Qualitatively, this can be seen from the fact that formulating these equations in a different frame would implement non-locality as an instantaneous influence in that frame—i.e., a synchronization of particle worldlines along a different family of hyperplanes
[
5
, Chap. 12]
. More formally, in the preferred frame
F
, the guiding field in Eq. (
3.4
) has components:

v^{\mu}_{(k)}\left(x_{(1)},\cdots,x_{(N)}\right)=\left(1,\frac{\Psi^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{(k)}\Psi}{\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi}\right)=\frac{\bar{\Psi}\gamma_{(1)}^{0}\cdots\gamma_{(k-1)}^{0}\gamma_{(k)}^{\mu}\gamma_{(k+1)}^{0}\cdots\gamma_{(N)}^{0}\Psi}{\bar{\Psi}\gamma_{(1)}^{0}\cdots\gamma_{(k-1)}^{0}\gamma_{(k)}^{0}\gamma_{(k+1)}^{0}\cdots\gamma_{(N)}^{0}\Psi},

(3.5)

with
\bar{\Psi}:=\Psi^{\dagger}\gamma^{0}\otimes\cdots\otimes\gamma^{0}
and where
\gamma_{(k)}^{\mu}:=\mathbb{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{I}\otimes\gamma^{\mu}\otimes\mathbb{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{I}
acts on the
k
-th particle’s spinor index. This guiding field is
not
a Lorentz-covariant 4-vector field. Under a Lorentz boost, its components would mix in a way that does not preserve the form of the equation: the temporal component would no longer equal
1
, and the spatial part would transform non-covariantly. Hence the functional form of the guiding equation changes when written in a different frame—meaning the Bohm-Hiley-Dirac model is not Lorentz invariant.

Consequently, the choice of
F
to write down the equations of motion is not merely a mathematical convenience, but a genuine physical commitment to a specific reference frame. This apparent conflict with special relativity can be made less conspicuous by reformulating the dynamics in a coordinate-free geometric language, at the cost of introducing an additional space-time structure.

The first step toward this coordinate-free reformulation is to re-express the preferred frame as a preferred
foliation
of space-time. The non-local coupling in Eq. (
3.4
) links space-time points
\big(t,\mathbf{X}_{(1)}(t)\big),\dots,\big(t,\mathbf{X}_{(N)}(t)\big)
lying on the same hyperplane
\Sigma_{t}
of constant time
t
in
F
. Mathematically, the family of constant-time hyperplanes
\mathcal{F}=\{\Sigma_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
defines a
foliation
of Minkowski space-time:

M=\bigcup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Sigma_{t},\quad\Sigma_{t}:=\{(t,\mathbf{x})\in M\mid\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}.

(3.6)

Physically, each leaf
\Sigma_{t}\in\mathcal{F}
supplies a notion of absolute simultaneity, allowing quantum non-locality to be implemented as a synchronization along these planes. At time
t
, the space-time configuration
X\left(\Sigma_{t}\right):=(X_{(1)}(t),\dots,X_{(N)}(t))
lies on the leaf
\Sigma_{t}
, and the guiding equation (
3.4
) evolves configurations from
\Sigma_{t}
to the next leaf
\Sigma_{t+\delta t}
.

From a mathematical standpoint, the time coordinate
t
of the preferred frame
F
is a scalar field
t:M\to\mathbb{R}
on Minkowski space-time, and the constant-time hyperplanes of
F
correspond to the level sets of this
time function

t(x)
. Formally, for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the hyperplane of constant time
t=s
in
F
is:

\Sigma_{s}:=t^{-1}(s)\equiv\{x\in M\mid t(x)=s\}.

(3.7)

The leaves of the preferred foliation
\mathcal{F}
are precisely these hyperplanes
\Sigma_{s}
. Furthermore, any hypersurface—and therefore any foliation—can be characterized by its future-pointing unit normal vector field
n(x)
. Since moving along
\Sigma_{s}
does not change the value of
t(x)
, the gradient
\partial t(x)
is everywhere orthogonal to
\Sigma_{s}
. This yields the relation between the future-pointing unit normal covector field and the gradient of the time function
t(x)
:

n(x)=\frac{\partial t(x)}{\sqrt{\partial t(x)\cdot\partial t(x)}},

(3.8)

where the dot denotes the Minkowski scalar product:
\partial t(x)\cdot\partial t(x)=\partial_{\mu}t(x)\partial^{\mu}t(x)=|\partial t(x)|^{2}
(using the Einstein summation convention). Because the foliation of the Bohm-Hiley-Dirac model consists of parallel hyperplanes, its normal vector field is constant throughout space-time:
n(x)=n
, with coordinates
n^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
in the preferred frame
F
—i.e.,
n
corresponds to the time axis unit vector of
F
. This timelike 4-vector field is now treated as a genuine part of the space-time structure, thus enabling coordinate-free notations.

Using the unit normal vector field
n(x)
to
\mathcal{F}
, the Bohm-Hiley-Dirac guiding field (
3.5
) can be re-expressed in a coordinate-free way as:

v_{(k)}\left(x_{(1)},\cdots,x_{(N)}\right)=\frac{\bar{\Psi}\left(\gamma_{(1)}\!\cdot\!n\right)\cdots\left(\gamma_{(k-1)}\!\cdot\!n\right)\gamma_{(k)}\left(\gamma_{(k+1)}\!\cdot\!n\right)\cdots\left(\gamma_{(N)}\!\cdot\!n\right)\Psi}{\bar{\Psi}\left(\gamma_{(1)}\!\cdot\!n\right)\cdots\left(\gamma_{(k-1)}\!\cdot\!n\right)\left(\gamma_{(k)}\!\cdot\!n\right)\left(\gamma_{(k+1)}\!\cdot\!n\right)\cdots\left(\gamma_{(N)}\!\cdot\!n\right)\Psi},

(3.9)

and the worldlines
X_{(k)}:\mathbb{R}\to M
(for
k=1,\cdots,N
) satisfy the guiding equation:

\dot{X}_{(k)}\left(\Sigma_{s}\right)=v_{(k)}\left(X_{(1)}\left(\Sigma_{s}\right),\cdots,X_{(N)}\left(\Sigma_{s}\right)\right),

(3.10)

where
\gamma\cdot n=\gamma^{\mu}n_{\mu}
reduces to
\gamma^{0}
in
F
coordinates. The worldlines are now parametrized by the same parameter
s\in\mathbb{R}
that labels the preferred hypersurfaces (
3.7
), i.e.,
X_{(k)}\left(\Sigma_{s}\right)\equiv X_{(k)}(s)\in\Sigma_{s}
, so that
\dot{X}_{(k)}\left(\Sigma_{s}\right)=\frac{dX_{(k)}}{ds}\left(\Sigma_{s}\right)
.

In essence, while we have achieved a coordinate-free notation, this dynamics remains physically equivalent to the original Bohm-Hiley-Dirac dynamics (Eqs. (
3.3
) and (
3.4

F

F
, The HBD dynamics (Eqs. (
3.9
) and (
3.10

\mathcal{F}

However, a significant difference between the Bohm-Hiley-Dirac model and HBD models is that the latter allows for potentially curved foliations, i.e., families of curved preferred hypersurfaces, unlike the original model which was restricted to flat hyperplanes.
The preferred foliation is therefore an ontological postulate, not a mere mathematical convenience. Given the one-to-one correspondence between a preferred frame and its associated flat foliation (
3.6
), both formulations postulate the same additional geometrical structure
\mathcal{F}
—a structure with no counterpart in the standard special-relativistic description of space-time.

Crucially for our experimental proposal, while special relativity assigns no objective temporal order to spacelike-separated events
A
and
B

t(A)-t(B)

is the time function whose level sets define the leaves of the foliation (
3.7
).

In a nutshell, the complete specification of an HBD model requires three elements: the (multi

\Psi

\mathcal{F}

(\Psi,X,\mathcal{F})
constitutes the ontology of the theory
[
62

. Although special relativity treats all foliations as mathematically equivalent, an HBD model elevates a particular one to the status of a physically real structure. As a geometrical structure, the preferred foliation
\mathcal{F}
is not part of the special-relativistic description of space-time, which only postulates a light-cone structure attached to every event

Despite its centrality to the HBD ontology, the preferred foliation is commonly asserted to be empirically undetectable

“Can one observe the time foliation? That is, can one determine experimentally which

3-

This paper takes up that very challenge. We propose a concrete experimental protocol whose outcome, if the underlying assumptions hold, would indeed reveal the preferred foliation
\mathcal{F}

Note that for the purposes of the present work, we do not need the full machinery of HBD models. Our calculations will be performed in the Bohm-Pauli theory for non-relativistic spin-

Given this simplification, a potential concern is that DD’s predictions—and consequently our predictions—are derive from the non

5  Bohmian Mechanics for Two Entangled Spin-1/2 Particles

Our experimental proposal involves a system of two spin-

\Psi(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},t)\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}\equiv\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\right)\otimes\left(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\right)\simeq L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{4}.

To describe this state efficiently, we employ a formalism based on spin projection along an arbitrary spatial direction
\hat{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

\chi_{s\hat{n}}
denote a one-particle spinor quantized along the
\hat{n}

\{\chi_{+\hat{n}},\chi_{-\hat{n}}\}

\mathbb{C}^{4}

\{\chi_{s_{1}\hat{n}}\otimes\chi_{s_{2}\hat{n}}\}_{s_{1},s_{2}=\pm 1}

\Psi(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},t)=\sum_{s_{1},s_{2}=\pm 1}\psi_{s_{1}\hat{n},\ s_{2}\hat{n}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},t)\left[\chi_{s_{1}\hat{n}}\otimes\chi_{s_{2}\hat{n}}\right],

(5.1)

where the coefficients
\psi_{s_{1}\hat{n},s_{2}\hat{n}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},t)

\mathbf{x}_{1}

\mathbf{x}_{2}

This

[

40

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\left[\sum_{k=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2m}\left(-i\hbar\nabla_{k}-q\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}_{k},t)\right)^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{2}\frac{q\hbar}{2m}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}\cdot\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x}_{k},t)+V(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},t)\right]\Psi,

(5.2)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}\otimes\mathbb{I}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}:=\mathbb{I}\otimes\boldsymbol{\sigma}

\nabla_{k}
denotes the gradient with respect to
\mathbf{x}_{k}

\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{1}\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\text{Pauli}}^{(1)}+\nabla_{2}\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\text{Pauli}}^{(2)}=0
, with the probability density
\rho(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},t)=\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi
and the associated probability current:

\mathbf{J}_{\text{Pauli}}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},t)=\underbrace{\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\left[\Psi^{\dagger}\nabla_{k}\Psi\right]}_{\text{{convective flux}}}+\underbrace{\frac{\hbar}{2m}\nabla_{k}\times\left(\Psi^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}\Psi\right)}_{\text{{spin flux}}}.\quad k=1,2,

(5.3)

The Bohmian velocity for particle
k

\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{dBB}}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},t):=\frac{\mathbf{J}^{(k)}_{\text{Pauli}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},t)}{|\Psi(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},t)|^{2}},\quad k=1,2.

(5.4)

At time

(5.5)

so that the particles’ trajectories are synchronized along the hyperplanes of constant time

in the chosen reference frame.

Although the Bohm-Pauli model is formulated on Galilean space-time, the time coordinate
t
of the frame in which the equations (

) and (
5.5
) are written plays exactly the same dynamical role as the preferred foliation does in a relativistic HBD model: it provides the notion of simultaneity required to define a joint configuration at an instant and to evolve it deterministically.

For the purposes of our analysis, we regard the Galilean frame
(t,\mathbf{x})
used for the formulation of the Bohm-Pauli dynamics ((
5.2
) and (
5.5
)), as an approximation to the preferred Lorentz frame

postulated the Bohm-Hiley-Dirac model, valid in the regime where particle velocities
\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{k}(t)
(solving (
5.5

5.5

with coordinates
X_{(k)}^{\mu}(t):=(t,\mathbf{X}_{(k)}(t))
. The foliation function
t:M\to\mathbb{R}
and the parameter
s
can then be identified to the time coordinate
t
of the Galilean frame used for the formulation of the Bohm-Pauli dynamics, and the constant-time hyperplanes of this frame as the leaves
\Sigma_{s}
of the (flat) preferred foliation.

Thus, although our explicit calculations are going to be performed within the non-relativistic Bohm-Pauli framework, they can be understood as approximations to predictions of the “relativistic” HBD model formulated in the Galilean frame whose time coordinate coincides with the foliation parameter
s
. The foliation parameter

—and hence the temporal ordering of spacelike-separated events relative to
\mathcal{F}
—then plays a direct dynamical role in determining the trajectories and, consequently, the arrival-time statistics.

Equipped with this general Bohmian formalism for two spin-
\frac{1}{2}
particles, we now specialize to the entangled
singlet state
that constitutes the quantum resource for our proposed experiment.

7  Definition of the Temporal Order

To rigorously analyze how Bob’s arrival-time distribution depends on the relative timing of Alice and Bob measurements, we must precisely define the notion of temporal order that appears in our subsequent derivations.

Although the arrival-time distribution observed by Bob results from an experimental run that involves an ensemble of
\sim 10^{5}
pairs (as described in Section
4
), the dynamics of each pair is independent and governed by the same laws. By analyzing a single representative pair, we can determine how the trajectory of particle 2—and consequently its contribution to the statistical distribution—depends on the temporal order of the two measurements performed on that pair. The observed arrival-time distribution for the full ensemble then emerges from averaging over the initial conditions of many such pairs, as per Eq. (
2.7
).

To describe the relative timing of the two measurements, we must first identify specific events (i.e., space-time points) that define the “measurement” for each particle within a single entangled pair. We therefore introduce:

•

Event
A
: Particle 1’s exit from the inhomogeneous magnetic field inside Alice’s Stern-Gerlach magnet. This event marks the completion of the spin-measurement interaction; after this moment, the two spin components of particle 1’s wave function have been spatially separated (Eq. (
8.1
)), and the non-local influence on particle 2 has been fully determined.

•

Event
B
: The release of particle 2 from its initial confinement near
z=0
inside Bob’s waveguide. This event initiates the arrival-time measurement; it corresponds to the moment
t=0
in the analysis of Das and Dürr
[
12
]
(see Section
2
).

The choice of event
A
is motivated by the fact that the non
-
local influence of Alice’s spin measurement on particle 2’s trajectory becomes operative only
after
the two spin components of particle 1’s wave function have separated spatially. A meaningful definition of temporal order in our setup must therefore distinguish whether Bob’s release of particle 2 (event
B
) occurs
before
this separation or
after
it. Event
B
is chosen because it marks the beginning of the dynamical evolution that determines particle 2’s arrival time at the detection screen.

The preferred foliation’s time function
t:M\to\mathbb{R}
assigns coordinates
t_{A}:=t(A)
and
t_{B}:=t(B)
to these events. Two fundamentally distinct cases arise for each entangled pair, according to their temporal order relative to the preferred foliation:

(i)

Spin measurement before arrival-time measurement:
According to the preferred foliation, Alice’s measurement event occurs before Bob’s, i.e.
t_{A}<t_{B}
.

(ii)

Arrival-time measurement before spin measurement:
According to the preferred foliation, Bob’s measurement event occurs before Alice’s, i.e.
t_{B}<t_{A}
.

A clarification is in order. Our explicit calculations in Sections
8
and
9
are performed within the non
-
relativistic Bohm–Pauli framework, which is formulated on Galilean space
-
time. Galilean space
-
time comes equipped with an absolute time foliation; consequently, the Bohm–Pauli theory does not need to postulate an additional foliation—it inherits one directly from the space
-
time structure. In this framework, the times
t_{A}
and
t_{B}
that appear in our derivations are simply the absolute time coordinates of events
A
and
B
in a Galilean frame, and the sign of
t_{A}-t_{B}
(i.e., the temporal order of
A
and
B
) is the same in every Galilean frame.

Our ultimate goal, however, is to detect the preferred foliation postulated by HBD models as an additional geometrical structure on Minkowski space
-
time. Therefore, when we construct our foliation detection protocol in Section
11
, we will treat
A
and
B
as points in Minkowski space
-
time and interpret the sign of
t_{A}-t_{B}
—the temporal order determined by our non
-
relativistic calculations—as the order fixed by the preferred foliation of the HBD model.

We now derive, for a single entangled pair, the explicit velocity fields governing particle 2’s motion in each of the two temporal scenarios.

11  Mapping a Flat Preferred Foliation: An Experimental Protocol

The predictions summarized in Table
1
can be systematically exploited to map the geometry of the preferred hypersurfaces. While HBD models generally allow curved foliations, our detection protocol is specifically designed for a flat foliation—i.e., a family of parallel hyperplanes.

As established in Section
3
, any foliation of Minkowski space
M
is completely characterized by its future-pointing unit normal vector field
n(x)
(see Eq. (
3.8
)). Measuring
n(x)
is therefore equivalent to detecting the foliation, which is precisely what our protocol aims to achieve. Before describing the protocol itself, we first establish the mathematical framework underlying it.

For the flat foliation
\mathcal{F}
considered here, the normal vector field is constant throughout space-time, i.e.,
n(x)=n
. In the preferred frame
F
, its components are simply
(1,0,0,0)
. During the first stage of the protocol (introduced below), they will establish a common Lorentz frame
\Lambda
using standard synchronization procedures. In this frame
\Lambda
, the future-pointing unit normal vector of the preferred foliation has unknown coordinates

(n^{0},n_{x},n_{y},n_{z})\equiv(n^{0},\mathbf{n}),

where
\mathbf{n}=(n_{x},n_{y},n_{z})
denotes the spatial part. The objective of our detection protocol is to determine these four components—a task that requires four independent constraints.

The first and most obvious constraint is the normalization condition imposed by the Minkowski metric (with signature
(+,-,-,-)
):

|n|^{2}=(n^{0})^{2}-(n_{x}^{2}+n_{y}^{2}+n_{z}^{2})=1.

Since the foliation is future-oriented,
n^{0}>0
, which fixes the sign when solving for
n^{0}
:

n^{0}=\sqrt{1+n_{x}^{2}+n_{y}^{2}+n_{z}^{2}}.

(11.1)

Thus, once the three spatial components
\mathbf{n}
are determined, the time component
n^{0}
follows directly from Eq. (
11.1
). The problem therefore reduces to finding three independent equations for
\mathbf{n}
.

The key physical constraint is the simultaneity condition itself. Two events
P_{A}
and
P_{B}
that are simultaneous with respect to the preferred foliation lie on a common preferred hyperplane
\Sigma\in\mathcal{F}
. Their separation vector
P_{A}-P_{B}
is therefore tangent to
\Sigma
and, by definition, orthogonal to
n
in Minkowski space. For a pair of simultaneous events
(P_{A},P_{B})
with coordinates
(T_{A},\mathbf{X}_{A})
and
(T_{B},\mathbf{X}_{B})
in the common Lorentz frame
\Lambda
, this orthogonality condition reads:

(P_{A}-P_{B})\cdot n=(T_{A}-T_{B})\,n^{0}-(\mathbf{X}_{A}-\mathbf{X}_{B})\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,

(11.2)

where
(\mathbf{X}_{A}-\mathbf{X}_{B})\cdot\mathbf{n}=(X_{A}-X_{B})n_{x}+(Y_{A}-Y_{B})n_{y}+(Z_{A}-Z_{B})n_{z}
.

Given two additional pairs of simultaneous space-time points,
(P^{\prime}_{A},P^{\prime}_{B})
and
(P^{\prime\prime}_{A},P^{\prime\prime}_{B})
, the simultaneity condition provides two further equations analogous to (
11.2
):

\displaystyle(P^{\prime}_{A}-P^{\prime}_{B})\cdot n

\displaystyle=(T^{\prime}_{A}-T^{\prime}_{B})\,n^{0}-(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{A}-\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{B})\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,

(11.3)

\displaystyle(P^{\prime\prime}_{A}-P^{\prime\prime}_{B})\cdot n

\displaystyle=(T^{\prime\prime}_{A}-T^{\prime\prime}_{B})\,n^{0}-(\mathbf{X}^{\prime\prime}_{A}-\mathbf{X}^{\prime\prime}_{B})\cdot\mathbf{n}=0.

(11.4)

Inserting
n^{0}=\sqrt{1+\mathbf{n}^{2}}
from Eq. (
11.1
) into Eqs. (
11.2
)–(
11.4
) yields three equations for the three unknowns
n_{x},n_{y},n_{z}
.

These equations have a unique solution if and only if the three separation vectors
P_{A}-P_{B}
,
P^{\prime}_{A}-P^{\prime}_{B}
,
P^{\prime\prime}_{A}-P^{\prime\prime}_{B}
are linearly independent. In other words, they must not all lie in a common 2
-
dimensional subspace of Minkowski space-time; equivalently, they must span the full 3
-
dimensional tangent space of the hyperplane
\Sigma
.
18
18

18

Analogously, to determine the unit normal vector of a 2
-
plane in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

-

Our aim here is not to advocate for either camp but rather to anticipate potential criticisms and to argue for the value of an empirical test of both DD’s predictions and our own.

Because our conclusions derive from DD’s arrival-time distributions, which have not yet been tested and are the subject of ongoing debate, the present work might be dismissed as overly speculative. On one side stands Einstein’s famous admonition to Heisenberg: “it is the theory which alone decides what is measurable”
[
37
]
. With this remark, Einstein was objecting to Heisenberg’s claim that a quantum theory should be built solely upon observable quantities. A stronger interpretation of the claim, however, is this: one must construct a precise theoretical framework in order to determine which experiments are feasible within it. The Bohmian version of the POVM theorem aims to do exactly that—to establish from Bohmian first principles that “the statistics of the result of every quantum experiment is governed by a POVM”
[
33
]
. In other words, one cannot conceive of a quantum experiment that would yield non-POVM statistics; according to any quantum theory, such an experiment is simply not feasible. In its Bohmian formulation, the POVM theorem rests on the following reasoning: for any quantum experiment, if one applies the Bohmian dynamics to “all the particles involved in the experiment”
[
26
, pp. 137]
—including both the target system and the
\sim 10^{23}
particles composing the measurement apparatus—the theory can
in principle
predict the distribution of the final macroscopic configuration. By equivariance, at quantum equilibrium this configuration will be distributed according to the Born rule. If experimental outcomes are identified with the final configuration of a macroscopic pointer—a configuration that results from applying the Bohmian dynamics to all particles composing the apparatus—then one can prove that, at quantum equilibrium, BM yields only POVM statistics. This theorem therefore establishes a formal constraint on the empirical content of any quantum theory.

Crucially, however, this constraint rests on a premise that has never been subjected to direct empirical test. The POVM theorem assumes that the empirical predictions of a quantum experiment are fully captured by applying the Born rule to the composite wave function of the
entire macroscopic apparatus
(see, e.g., Eq. (5.37b) in
[
65
]
). While this reasoning follows from BM first principles, it does not reflect the actual application of the theory to real experiments. In practice, no one computes the wave function of a macroscopic device, evolves it through the measurement process, and then applies the Born rule to its pointer coordinates. The empirical success of QM rests instead on applying the Born rule to systems of very limited size—individual particles, atoms, or small molecules—and then using idealized correspondence principles to connect those microscopic distributions to macroscopic records. For this reason, existing data do not actually support the application of the Born rule to macroscopic ensembles of particles—in particular, to measurement apparatuses—in the way the POVM theorem takes for granted.

This observation does not, of course, disprove the claim that the presence of a real detector would strongly influence the outcome of DD’s experiment, nor does it refute the assertion that the true arrival-time statistics in DD’s setup will ultimately be described by a POVM. It does, however, call into question the empirical authority of the POVM theorem as a decisive argument against DD’s predictions and, by extension, against our own.

On the other side of this methodological tension lies Einstein’s complementary insight, offered immediately after the remark quoted above: “[One] must appreciate that observation is a very complicated process”
[
37
]
. As Maudlin emphasizes, Einstein later stressed the necessity of idealizations and approximations for making numerical predictions in physics
[
29
,
53
]
. The underlying idea is this: because measurement is generally a highly complex process that cannot be analyzed with full rigor within any existing theoretical framework, one is always compelled to introduce well-informed idealizations and to invoke phenomenological laws that the theory is ultimately expected to justify at a later stage
[
53
]
. This situation is particularly acute in quantum mechanics, where the detection and amplification of a microscopic event must lead to a macroscopic signal, e.g. the configuration of a “pointer”. Yet, no current quantum theory is in a position to describe the dynamics of a pointer with sufficient precision to extract numerical predictions for a real experiment. For purely technical reasons, the quantum treatment of a measurement interaction must stop somewhere, even though the Bohmian dynamics should in principle apply to “all the particles involved in the experiment.” Any quantum prediction therefore relies on some idealization.

Within Bohmian mechanics, the standard tool for making such predictions is the de Broglie correspondence principle. This idealization neglects the detailed modeling of detectors with finite spatial and temporal resolution (such as fluorescent screens, photomultipliers, or microchannel plates), yet experimental evidence to date shows it to be remarkably effective. It has been successfully applied to scattering processes
[
16
,
17
,
27
]
and interference phenomena
[

68
,
61

[

and
[

When applied to the DD setup, however, the de Broglie correspondence principle yields arrival-time distributions that
cannot
be described by any POVM
[
33

In this novel regime, accepting the de Broglie correspondence principle leads directly to the results discussed in this article—most notably, the possibility of superluminal communication and the empirical detectability of the preferred foliation.

One might reasonably object that the de Broglie correspondence principle will fail precisely in this yet unexplored regime, which is characterized by robust quantum backflow. It could be argued, for instance, that the presence of the detection screen will wash out the spin dependence of the arrival-time distribution, thereby restoring POVM statistics. However, such a claim cannot be substantiated by a generic appeal to the Born rule applied to the apparatus pointer; it would require a concrete, physically motivated model of a real detector suitable for use in DD’s experiment.

Despite decades of peaceful coexistence between the de Broglie correspondence principle and the Bohmian formulation of the POVM theorem, DD’s arrival-time predictions have brought their latent incompatibility to the surface—a conflict anticipated as early as 2005 by Ruggenthaler
et al.

[

We prefer to leave this question open. Ultimately, only the experiment proposed by Das and Dürr can settle the debate over the correctness of their arrival-time predictions. As long as those predictions remain unrefuted—and remain firmly anchored in the well-established, empirically successful applications of de Broglie–Bohm theory—it is legitimate to explore their revolutionary consequences and to critically re-examine the authority of theorems that would rule them out. Such an attitude is all the more justified given (i) the novelty of the physical regime investigated by DD (robust, controllable backflow), (ii) the absence of any well-established theoretical framework for arrival-time experiments, and (iii) the current lack of compelling, POVM-based predictions for the DD setup.

In any case, the logical implication established in this paper confirms the boldness of DD’s prediction—a boldness that is no defect:
