Interfacial proton-electron donor synergy enables sustainable oxime electrosynthesis via dual-path reduction
Authors: Shuo Wang, Ying Zhao, Zhuoran Feng, Yuqian Liao, Dan Song, Zhiqiang Sun, Xiaofu Sun, Buxing Han, Jun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe electrosynthesis of value−added organonitrogen chemicals offers a promising strategy to address the anthropogenic unbalance of global nitrogen cycle. The challenge lies the dynamic asynchrony between proton and electron transfer at electrocatalytic interface, resulting in low Faradaic efficiency (FE) and selectivity. Here, we engineer a synergistic proton−electron donor interface by integrating protonated polyaniline with graphite sheets (p-PANI − GS), enabling dual−path reduction of nitrate (NO3−) to *NH2OH, i.e., spontaneous chemical reduction and enhanced electrocatalytic reduction. When coupled with cyclohexanone, this configuration delivers a high cyclohexanone oxime (C6H11NO) productivity of 67.1 µmol h−1 cm−2 with 69.9% FE, while maintaining robust stability across pH of 3.0 − 11.0. Mechanistic investigations unveil that autonomous proton donation synergizes with hetero−interfacial electron redistribution to achieve concerted proton−electron delivery at catalytic sites. Such donor synergy accelerates proton−coupled electron transfer kinetics and stabilizes *NH2OH intermediates in NO3− reduction, thereby steering selectivity toward C6H11NO. Additionally, the solar−driven system and machine learning prediction support the practical extensibility of this oxime electrosynthesis strategy. Our work pioneers an atomic−scale interface engineering framework enabling sustainable value−added oxime electrosynthesis.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Renewable energy, Electrocatalysis, Sustainability

Introduction

Organonitrogen chemicals are pivotal in propelling global advancements across diverse fields, including agriculture, manufacturing, and materials science, while holding substantial academic and economic values1–3. Among these, cyclohexanone oxime (C6H11NO), a key precursor of caprolactam—the monomer of Nylon−6, is deemed an indispensable feedstock in the fiber and plastic industry4. Current industrial synthesis of C6H11NO heavily relies on energy-intensive nucleophilic addition between cyclohexanone (C6H10O) and hydroxylamine (NH2OH), which stems from hydroxylamine sulfate or ammonia (NH3) oxidation with O2/H2O2 (Fig. 1a)5,6. However, these routes are challenged by harsh reaction conditions, high energy consumption, environmental and safety risks6. Recently, the renewable energy−powered electrosynthesis of C6H11NO stands out as an emerging and sustainable synthetic route7–9. This approach can be realized through C−N coupling between C6H10O and hydroxylamine intermediates (*NH2OH), which are generated in situ from the electroreduction of nitrate/NOx waste streams. It exemplifies a waste−to−value transformation that contributes to restoring the nitrogen cycle10–12. Despite the promise, the electrosynthesis of C6H11NO is still in its early stages, particularly hindered by the low Faraday efficiency (FE) and the competitive formation of NH3 during nitrate (NO3−) electroreduction. Consequently, developing effective strategies to enhance the conversion of NO3−−to −*NH2OH for C6H11NO electrosynthesis is of critical importance.

The conversion of NO3−−to −*NH2OH is governed by sequential proton-coupling electron transfer (PCET) steps at the catalytic interface13,14. This complex process utilizes proton/electron pairs (H+/e−) to drive the reduction of NO3− to nitrite (NO2−), followed by NO2− hydrogenation to subsequent N-containing intermediates15. A critical yet underappreciated kinetic bottleneck lies in the dynamic asynchrony between proton and electron transfer16–21. Specifically, the sluggish proton transfer kinetics relative to electron injection disrupt the temporal coordination required for N−containing intermediate stabilization, leading to incomplete hydrogenation and parasitic pathways (e.g., N−N coupling or NH3 formation). Prevailing strategies collectively aim to improve interfacial proton donor via: (i) catalyst−enabled aqueous proton activation, where advanced electrocatalysts (e.g., transition metal phosphides/sulfides) accelerate water dissociation to generate surface−adsorbed hydrogen species (*H)22–24; and (ii) electrolyte engineering employing pH buffers, concentrated acids, or cations to augment proton accessibility25,26. However, these approaches address proton availability yet neglect the kinetic diffusion limitation of proton delivery and its dynamic coupling with electron transfer. Protons remain reliant on liquid-phase diffusion pathways, distinct from solid-state electron transfer, precluding PCET synchronicity. More importantly, existing electrocatalysts lack dual-functional interfaces that can integrate intrinsic proton donors with directed electron transfer pathways. This deficiency intensifies the mismatch in PCET, thereby compromising both reaction efficiency and selectivity.

In this work, we propose an electrode design philosophy centered on constructing a dual-functional platform that synergizes proton and electron transfer by atomic−scale interface engineering. Our strategy leverages the integration of protonated polyaniline with graphite sheet (p-PANI−GS). The surface-bound −NH⁺•− of p-PANI establishes an intrinsic proton donor, while the GS creates a built−in electric field (BIEF) that redistributes the interfacial electron. It directs electron transfer to adsorbed intermediates, creating synergistic proton−electron donors for concerted PCET. This synergistic donor mechanism enables dual-path NO3− reduction reaction (NO3RR): (i) Spontaneous PCET under open-circuit conditions, namely chemical NO3RR (Chem−NO3RR); (ii) Electrocatalytic PCET under applied bias, namely electrocatalytic NO3RR (EChem−NO3RR). Such a design fundamentally redefines the interfacial dynamics by decoupling proton and electron donor limitations, thereby optimizing the sequential hydrogenation of NO3−−to −*NH2OH intermediates while suppressing over-hydrogenation to NH3 (Fig. 1b). Hence, the optimized p-PANI−GS composite demonstrates remarkable efficiency in converting aqueous NO3− and C6H10O into C6H11NO, achieving a high productivity rate of 67.1 µmol cm−2 h−1 and a FE of 69.9% at 30 mA cm−2. Moreover, it exhibits excellent electrocatalytic durability and broad pH (3.0−11.0) adaptability. Complementary in situ characterizations and theoretical calculations confirmed that this unique interface helped in the stabilization of *NH2OH, and meanwhile promoting C−N coupling between *NH2OH and C6H10O. Finally, the application of solar-driven system on C6H11NO synthesis and machine learning to predict oxime synthesis efficiency pioneered a universal design framework for future intelligent industrial production of C6H11NO.

Results

Design and characterization of an electrocatalyst

p-PANI-GS was fabricated via a controllable in-situ polymerization method, offering a straightforward and scalable preparation process (Fig. 2a). Ball-milled GS featured enhanced surface oxygen functional groups and edge defects, which potentiated aniline adsorption, thus accelerating and stabilizing p-PANI polymerization via π–π interaction. The layered structure observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) signified the existence of GS that exhibited a rough texture, presumably a consequence of the p-PANI coating (Supplementary Fig. 1). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) detailly captured the spatial distribution and morphology of p-PANI−GS composite. GS layers were clearly discernible, adorned with rod−shaped p-PANI structures firmly anchored to their surface, affirming the effective integration of p-PANI with GS matrix (Supplementary Fig. 2). This was further substantiated by the robust interfacial interactions observed between the periodic lattice fringes of p-PANI chain and the (002) planes of GS in high resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Fig. 2b). EDX mapping demonstrated a uniform distribution of C and N across the material, confirming the presence of p-PANI embedded on the GS (Fig. 2b). Additionally, the atomic force microscopy (AFM) clearly illustrated the uniform distribution of p-PANI chains across GS (Supplementary Fig. 3), confirming the presence of a seamless conductive network that spans the entire electrode surface, thereby ensuring continuous electron transfer during electrolysis.

The molecular information of p-PANI−GS was characterized using an array of spectroscopic techniques. The Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR, Fig. 2c) verified the concurrent presence of C−H plane bending vibration at 1150 cm−1, alongside the distinctive –NH+•– band at 1252 cm−1, underscoring the protonation of p-PANI in an acidic doping environment27. Notably, 15% p-PANI−GS exhibited the most pronounced intensity for these characteristic peaks, surpassing all other samples. Furthermore, the coexistence of C−N stretching of secondary aromatic amines, as well as the C=C stretching vibration corresponding to the quinoid ring and benzenoid ring were also witnessed, respectively28. X−ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to analyze the alterations in the compositional and electronic state of p-PANI−GS. The survey spectrum revealed that p-PANI−GS was a nonmetallic catalyst consisting exclusively of C, N, and trace O (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The XPS N 1s spectrum revealed a pronounced discrepancy in the abundance of −NH− and −NH⁺•− species on the catalyst surface29,30. Distinct peaks corresponding to surface −NH⁺•− and −NH− were observed in various p-PANI−GS samples, however, no such peaks were observed on GS (Supplementary Fig. 4b). This phenomenon occurred because GS acted as electron acceptor, drawing electrons from p-PANI matrix through π−π stacking interactions during the polymerization. It diminished the electron density on the amino groups within the p-PANI chains, facilitating the formation and stabilization of the protonated amine salt structures. Based on the deconvolution of N 1s envelop, the relative content of −NH⁺•− species, ranked from highest to lowest, was as follows: 15% p-PANI−GS, 20% p-PANI−GS, 10% p-PANI−GS, and 5% p-PANI− GS, with percentages of 43%, 36%, 32%, and 19%, respectively (Fig. 2d). The −NH⁺•− moiety could function as intrinsic proton donor and plays an instrumental role in the early stages of nitrate electroreduction, ensuring immediate proton availability and enhancing the kinetics of the subsequent protonation steps30. Conversely, the trend in the −NH− content exhibited an opposite pattern, with the 15% p-PANI−GS exhibiting the lowest −NH− content (57%). In the C 1s, the peaks of C−C bonds, graphitic C, and trace amounts of C=O bonds were detected on GS, signifying a structurally intact yet slightly oxidized surface31 (Fig. 2e). While the appearance of C−N peaks confirmed the effective integration of p-PANI onto the p-PANI−GS. Moreover, the C 1s spectrum showed a slight shift to lower binding energies for p-PANI−GS versus GS, suggesting increased electron density at the C atoms due to the protonation of N sites (Supplementary Fig. 4c). It hinted at a synergistic interaction between p-PANI and GS that optimized the electron distribution on the catalyst surface.

In the course of synthesis, the strong electronegative nitrogen atoms (−NH− or =N−) on the p-PANI chain engaged in coordination bonding with the GS surface, leading to electron localization, which in turn generated more active unpaired electrons. These played a pivotal role in promoting electrocatalytic reactions. Additionally, the polymerization process had the potential to produce oxidative radicals, which also might modify the electronic configuration of catalyst. Hence, the electronic structure characteristics of p-PANI−GS composites were systematically examined. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns revealed that the characteristic intensity of graphite diminishes nonlinearly with the introduction and gradual increase of p-PANI content32, indicative of heightened surface defect density from in-situ p-PANI polymerization (Supplementary Fig. 5). This was corroborated by selected area electron diffraction (SAED) findings, with p-PANI addition causing diffraction spot loss (Fig. 2b). Additionally, for p-PANI−GS catalyst, the intensity ratio of the D band to the G band (ID/IG) in Raman spectrum was notably elevated compared to the GS (0.23), with the highest ID/IG ratio of 0.50 for 15% p-PANI−GS (Fig. 2f). It once again indicated that the introduction of p-PANI enhanced the defect concentration and disorder in p-PANI−GS structure33. The formed defects acted as an electron trap to alter the electronic state on the surface of p-PANI−GS, giving rise to electron localization. This, in turn, created a greater number of unpaired electrons, which served as active sites in electrocatalytic reactions. It has been validated through electron spin resonance (ESR) tests conducted on GS and a range of p-PANI−GS composites (Fig. 2g). No ESR signal was detected for the GS. However, a pronounced signal at g = 2.004, attributable to unpaired electrons, was observed in various p-PANI−GS composites, with varying intensities34. Notably, 15% p-PANI−GS exhibited the most pronounced peak, suggesting the highest concentration of paramagnetic centers along with a greater number of unpaired electrons. Collectively, the optimized electronic structure synergized with intrinsic proton-donating ability of −NH⁺•− species to establish a highly functional electrocatalytic interface.

Performance of nitrate−to−oxime electrosynthesis

We initially explored the feasibility of the electrosynthesis of C6H11NO from NO3− and C6H10O, both at a concentration of 0.02 M, utilizing serialized p-PANI−GS composites. Electrolysis was performed in a three−electrode H−type cell with a Nafion117 membrane at a constant current density of 30 mA cm−2 in 0.5 M K2CO3 electrolyte. The target product was quantified by liquid chromatograph−mass spectrometry (LC−MS), and also identified using gas chromatography−mass spectrometry (GC−MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)7. It is astonishing that the signal of C6H11NO was detected via LC−MS in the initial reaction mixture before electrical stimulation—an unprecedented phenomenon (Supplementary Fig. 6). As measured, 9.9% of NO3− was spontaneously chemical reduced by 15% p-PANI−GS, which then reacted with C6H10O to produce C6H11NO through C−N coupling under open−circuit condition (Fig. 3a). Subsequently, upon electrification, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) revealed a raised current density for 15% p-PANI−GS upon introducing C6H10O and NO3−, suggesting accelerated NO3− reduction and subsequent C−N coupling (Supplementary Fig. 7). Meanwhile, the formation of C6H11NO was clearly detected with the increasing concentration, accompanied by a clearly decline in C6H10O levels (Fig. 3a). Additionally, we recorded 1H NMR spectra of NO3−, C6H10O, and 15% p-PANI−GS electrode coexisting simultaneously before electrolysis, shown in Fig. 3b. The appearance of weak peaks corresponding to the pure C6H11NO spectrum confirmed the successful conversion of C6H10O to C6H11NO via spontaneous chemical reduction of NO3−35. Following electrolysis, the C6H10O peaks almost disappeared, while obvious C6H11NO peaks were observed without additional signals appeared. The 13C NMR spectrum obtained for the reaction mixture before and after electrolysis also supported the chemical and electrochemical synthesis of C6H11NO (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 8). These findings reveal a dual−path for the synthesis of C6H11NO, wherein NO3− undergone pre-electrolysis chemical activation by p-PANI−GS, followed by coupling electrolysis process appeared to significantly amplify C6H11NO production.

The performance of pure GS, p-PANI, and p-PANI−GS electrocatalysts was compared (Fig. 3d). Negligible production of C6H11NO was observed on the GS electrode before powering on, implying that GS by itself was insufficient for chemical reduction of NO3−. While p-PANI alone enabled spontaneous chemical reduction of NO3− under open-circuit with C6H11NO productivity of 8.7 µmol h−1 cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 9), implying that the p-PANI itself possessed intrinsic reductive capability. After a 60 min constant current test at 30 mA cm−2, GS and p-PANI delivered a limited acetamide C6H11NO synthesis performance with a yield of 11.3 and 44.9 µmol h−1 cm−2, respectively. In stark contrast, the productivities of C6H11NO definitely increased across all p-PANI − GS catalysts, displaying a volcano-type tendency with values ranging from 32.4 to 67.1 µmol h−1 cm−2. The trend in the FEs was consistent with the observed productivity, with values ranging from 15.1% to 69.9%. Particularly, the 15% p-PANI − GS sample exhibited the highest C6H11NO productivity (67.1 µmol h−1 cm−2) and FE (69.9%) compared to other catalysts. Moreover, 15% p-PANI−GS delivered the most significant current response with the presence of NO3− and C6H10O, suggesting its much better catalytic activity toward C6H11NO yield (Supplementary Fig. 10). Thus, these suggested that the incorporation of p-PANI might modulate the interfacial characteristic on p-PANI−GS, not only triggering the chemical reduction of NO3−−to−*NH2OH, but also accelerating the dual−path synthesis of C6H11NO.

Next, we investigated the influence of different current densities on the productivity and FE of C6H11NO over 15% p-PANI−GS. The mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of 113.1 and 98 given by LC−MS, which aligned with the C6H11NO and C6H10O structure, respectively (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 11). The results revealed that the successful synthesis of C6H11NO in all tested systems and the highest efficiency was achieved at a current of 30 mA cm−2 due to the complete consumption of C6H10O. Increasing the current density from 10 to 30 mA cm−2 enhanced the C6H11NO productivity from 45.4 to 67.1 µmol h−1 cm−2, though the FE dropped by approximately 12.8% (Fig. 3f). Continue to raise to 50 mA cm−2, the C6H11NO yield significantly declined to attributable to the enhanced electrocatalytic reduction of NO3− to NH3 at higher current densities. Nitrogen selectivity was evaluated by tracking the temporal evolution of nitrogen species over the 15% p-PANI−GS electrode (Supplementary Fig. 12-14). Prior to electrolysis, partial conversion of NO3– to NO2– was observed, accompanied by 4.2% C6H11NO formation. NO2– built up as a kinetically significant intermediate during electrolysis, achieving a maximum yield of 35.1% after 15 min. Upon prolonged operation (60 min), N2 remained negligible (<3%), while C6H11NO and NH3 emerged as dominant products with yields of 34.9% and 47.5%, respectively. Normalized to nitrate conversion, the C6H11NO and NH3 yield was 57.6% and 42.4%. This outcome highlights the preference for the nitrate-to-oxime conversion pathway over competing routes, such as N–N coupling and over-hydrogenation.

Controlled current co-electrolysis of NO3− and C6H10O over 15% p-PANI−GS was further investigated within the pH range of 3.0 to 11.0 (Fig. 3g). The normalized C6H11NO production consistently exceeded 91% throughout this range (98.2% at pH 5), demonstrating exceptional pH-universal activity for C6H11NO synthesis, which boded well for its promising application potential. The slight attenuation under extreme pH conditions (91.3% at pH 3; 92.4% at pH 11) results from dominant hydrogen evolution via accelerated HER kinetics in acidic media and compromised proton donation due to reduced −NH⁺•− protonation in alkaline environments (Supplementary Fig. 15-16). The p-PANI−GS cathode maintained long−term electrochemical stability and reproducibility (Fig. 3h). The productivity and FE of C6H11NO production remained around 65% and 67%, respectively, even after the 20th run under optimal reaction conditions. It could be attributed to the dynamically sustainable proton-donor interface during extended operation, confirmed by post-reaction XPS and FTIR analyses (Supplementary Fig. 17). This study further underscored the strong promise of non-metallic electrocatalysts for C6H11NO synthesis at relatively low current densities, delivering performance competitive with representative metal-based counterparts reported in the literature (Supplementary Table 1).

Witnessing the interfacial proton−electron donor synergy

The NO3RR served as a decisive step in the production of C6H11NO, involving a highly complex seven−proton coupled six-electron transfer processes (NO3− + 6e− + 7H+ → *NH2OH + 2H2O). Such PCET process was critical to achieving high catalytic performance in the NO3RR, particularly through the precise coordination of proton and electron kinetics at the catalyst interface. Thus, we elaborated the proton−electron donor synergy mechanism on the p-PANI − GS interface during the NO3RR. p-PANI supported on GS was utilized to create an intrinsic proton donor, which mediated the proton transfer reactions involved in the conversion of NO3−−to−*NH2OH. The proton donor mechanism of the p-PANI was studied. The effect of p-PANI in p-PANI−GS as a solid−state proton reservoir should depend on the source of proton. Therefore, we examined the catalytic activity of the electrode by systematically varying the source of proton in the electrolyte. As evidenced from the measurement of the kinetic isotope effect (KIE), which was a key parameter to evaluate the specific rate-determining step in electrocatalytic reactions20,36. As shown in Fig. 4a, the KIE values for all the examined electrodes with GS and p-PANI−GS composites, were above 1.0, indicating that the rate-determining step in all those cases involved proton transfer during NO3RR37. GS gave the highest KIE value of 3.6. In contrast, the KIE values of p-PANI−GS composites dramatically decreased, signifying that the proton transfer was largely accelerated with the assistance of the PANI. The proton donor ability of p-PANI originates from its protonated −NH⁺•− on the polymer chain, as confirmed by XPS and FTIR data. Notably, a slight variation in KIE value is observed by varying the content of p-PANI on p-PANI−GS, where the 15% p-PANI−GS exhibited the lowest KIE value of 1.2. That observation manifested that p-PANI with the highest protonation degree led to the most effective proton−shuttling effect for the NO3−−to− *NH2OH conversion. Additionally, we observed that the productivity of C6H11NO increased linearly with the relative content of −NH⁺•− on different p-PANI−GS composites, regardless of whether it was electrified or not (Fig. 4b, R2 = 0.968 and 0.967). Therefore, we supposed that p-PANI could modulate the interfacial microenvironment of p-PANI−GS and provide a positively charged catalytic surface, which largely facilitates the enrichment and adsorption of NO3− anions. Simultaneously, the dynamic protonation−deprotonation process in p-PANI not only served as a proton source but also ensured that the surface remains proton-rich, which was essential for the efficient mediation of PCET steps involved in the reduction of NO3−−to− *NH2OH.

On the other hand, we explored the interfacial electron transfer behavior at the p-PANI−GS interface, which complemented the PCET process by modulating the electron dynamics. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) was utilized to obtain work functions (Φ) values of GS and p-PANI were 5.41 eV and 5.51 eV, respectively, showing a relatively large ΔΦ of 0.10 eV at the p-PANI−GS interface38 (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 2). It indicated a pronounced interfacial charge transfer, which was further confirmed by DFT calculations of WF for electron gain and loss39 (Fig. 4d). The calculated WF for GS (5.22 eV) was lower than that of p-PANI (6.15 eV), which possibly drive electron transfer from high level (GS) to low level (PANI) until reaching an equilibrium. As expected, such a large difference in WF after the contact of the two phases to form a heterostructure (Schottky heterojunction) created BIEF (Fig. 4e) to make the WF balanced, combining the analysis of ultraviolet−visible spectroscopy (UV−Vis, Supplementary Fig. 18). The formation of BIEF was instrumental in facilitating oriented electron transfer across the interface, as it created an energetically favorable path for the reduction of NO3−40. The analysis of the electron density difference (EDD) and planar-averaged charge density variations at the interface further corroborated the existence of significant electron redistribution (Fig. 4f), establishing a strong BIEF in the p-PANI-GS composite (Fig. 4e). This strong electric field not only enhanced the charge carrier mobility but also contributed to the accumulation of unpaired electrons (Fig. 2g), enhancing interfacial electron transfer dynamics within the p-PANI-GS composite. The BIEF creates an electron-deficient GS region and an electron-rich p-PANI region, promoting directional electron transfer from p-PANI to adsorbed NO3− intermediates.

To further explicit the effects of BIEF on the electronic structure across the interface, we performed density of states (DOS) calculation (Fig. 4g). By integrating, the p-band centers (εp) of pristine GS and p-PANI-GS were calculated as +2.35 eV and −1.56 eV, respectively, reflecting distinct electronic configurations induced by the BIEF. Of note is that the εp of p-PANI−GS lies below the Fermi level (Ef), in contrast to GS (+2.35 eV above Ef), meaning a higher electron-donating character at the heterointerface, thus beneficial to improve its electronic conductivity and ameliorate relevant reaction kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 19). Additionally, the p-PANI−GS delivered a closer εp (−1.56 eV) toward the Ef than its individual components, which had a higher occupancy of antibonding states in the hybridized orbitals, which strengthens the adsorption of reaction intermediates like *NH2OH41.

Subsequently, we employed femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations to directly capture the concerted PCET. fs-TA spectra were conducted to interrogate the interfacial charge transfer dynamics. The pristine GS/NO3− system exhibited a broad negative ΔA signal across 540-660 nm, characteristic of stimulated emission (SE) (Supplementary Fig. 20). The kinetic trace monitored at 613 nm revealed an excited−state lifetime of τGS = 390 fs (Fig. 4h)42, reflecting intrinsic electron-phonon scattering and radiative recombination within the GS framework. Strikingly, the 15% p-PANI–GS heterostructure showed a drastically quenched SE signal and a significantly shortened lifetime of τ15% p-PANI–GS = 49 fs when immersed in a nitrate-containing solution. This accelerated decay kinetics unambiguously indicated that the excited electrons were efficiently channeled away from radiative pathways toward interfacial reduction processes. We attribute this rapid electron extraction to the concerted action of the BIEF and the autonomous proton donation at the p-PANI−GS heterointerface, which collectively establish a synergistic proton−electron transfer pathway, thereby accelerating the overall NO3RR reaction kinetics and lowers the energy barrier for the overall reaction.

To directly visualize the proton-transfer kinetics and the synergy between proton and electron donors, we conducted AIMD simulations43. We first constructed two models, non-protonated polyaniline with graphite sheet (PANI-GS) and p-PANI-GS interface, both adsorbing a nitrate ion to simulate the proton delivery process (Supplementary Fig. 21, Supplementary Data 1). For PANI-GS, proton delivery to the adsorbed NO3− proceeded via a two-step sequence: initial water dissociation to form an adsorbed hydrogen species (*H), followed by H migration to NO3−. While p-PANI-GS interface enabled a direct proton shuttle from the surface-bound –NH+•– group to the NO3−, achieving proton delivery. The radial distribution function (RDF) analysis confirmed the distinct proton-transfer mechanisms (Fig. 4i). A sharp peak at ~2.1 Å (g(r) > 50) for p-PANI-GS signified O–H covalent bond formation, evidencing direct proton transfer from –NH+•– groups. Conversely, the absence of this feature in PANI-GS, which only showed a broad peak at ~2.8–7.5 Å from solvent interactions, underscored its reliance on weak hydrogen bonding. Successive snapshots from AIMD trajectory (Supplementary video 1-2) revealed that p-PANI-GS enabled direct proton transfer from –NH+•– to NO3−, bypassing the requisite water dissociation in the PANI-GS system, thereby achieving kinetically synergistic proton–electron donation. In parallel, DFT result provided electronic-level evidence for this synergy. The N atom bonding with NO3− in the PANI-GS model showed higher electron density, whereas this accumulation was significantly reduced in the p-PANI–GS system (Supplementary Fig. 22). It implied that the intrinsic proton donor not only shuttled protons at an accelerated rate but also optimally preconditions the electronic configuration of the active site, working in conjunction with the BIEF to enable concerted PCET.

Consequently, we employed in situ ATR-SEIRAS to monitor the dynamic evolution of key intermediates during the NO3RR process on both GS and 15% p-PANI-GS electrodes. On the pristine GS electrode, characteristic bands for *NO2− (~1287 cm−1), *NO (~1540 cm−1), *NH2OH (~1180 cm−1), and *NH3 (~1465 cm−1) were clearly detected44. Notably, these species exhibited pronounced and persistent accumulation throughout the reaction, particularly for * NO2− and * NH3 (Fig. 4j), indicating severe kinetic bottlenecks in the sequential hydrogenation steps arising from sluggish and decoupled proton delivery. However, the 15% p-PANI-GS electrode under identical conditions revealed a fundamentally distinct dynamic (Fig. 4k). Although the same intermediates were observed, their spectral intensities were markedly weaker and highly transient. The fleeting appearance of NO2− and NH2OH signatures demonstrated accelerated consumption of these species. We attributed the suppressed accumulation of intermediates to the concerted PCET at the heterointerface, which facilitated rapid and continuous hydrogenation, thereby steering the reaction flux along the desired pathway toward NH2OH.

Mechanism insights into dual-path nitrate reduction for C6H11NO synthesis

Such interfacial proton−electron donor synergy on the p-PANI−GS composite offered a precisely tailored platform to execute the spontaneous Chem−NO3RR process. The critical *H in the NO3RR process, which could promote the hydrogenation of NO3− toward *NH2OH production, was examined. We probed the weak ESR signal of the DMPO−H adduct with an intensity ratio of 1:1:2:1:2:1:2:1:1 in the mixture of 15% p-PANI−GS and NO3− solution without electrolysis45 (Fig. 5a), whereas no signal was observed when using GS under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 23). It indicated the formation of *H within the intrinsic proton induced by p-PANI. The ion chromatography was employed to monitor the dynamics of NO3− and its intermediates during Chem−NO3RR process. A new characteristic peak corresponding to *NO2 emerged, accompanied by a reduction in the NO3− peak intensity (Supplementary Fig. 24). This observation signified the NO3− hydrogenation to *NO2−, facilitated by the well−coupling of *H and interfacial electron transfer mediated by p-PANI−GS. Subsequently, the production of C6H11NO was identified through LC−MS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6), providing definitive evidence for the successful execution of the Chem−NO3RR process in synthesizing C6H11NO. Upon electrolysis, the hydrogenation capability at the p-PANI−GS interface was significantly amplified as evidenced by the pronounced increase in the H* signal (Fig. 5a). The integration of electrochemical stimulation facilitated the PCET, enabling more effective hydrogenation of *H with NO3− (or intermediates), thus improving EChem−NO3RR efficiency. Additionally, the accumulation of *NO2−, a key intermediate generated via Chem−NO3RR process (Supplementary Fig. 24), likely lowered the energy barrier for subsequent EChem−NO3RR steps. This effect not only accelerated the reduction kinetics but also enhanced the overall energy efficiency of the process by leveraging the pre−activation of NO3−.

To confirm the origin of C6H11NO production, D2O and K15NO3 were used as the H and N sources to replace H2O and KNO3, respectively. LC−MS demonstrated the acquisition of 15N−labeled C6H11NO before and after electrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 25). Meanwhile, no C6H11NO was detected when removing C6H10O or KNO3 (Supplementary Table 3), demonstrating C6H10O and KNO3 were the C and N sources in whole C6H11NO synthesis process, respectively. The reaction pathway was elucidated through systematic control experiments and in situ ATR−FTIR (Supplementary Fig. 26). As shown in Fig. 5b, *NH2 (1121 cm−1) and *NH2OH (1015 cm−1) were detected by in situ ATR−SEIRAS using C6H10O and KNO3 as the feedstock, both before and after electrolysis7,15. Furthermore, we used D2O as the background solvent since the wide H2O peak at approximately 1639 cm−1 overlapped with the peaks of C=N and NO. The vibration bands at 1644 cm−1 and 1449 cm−1, assigned to the stretching vibrations of C=N and O−H in C6H11NO, were observed (Fig. 5c). Besides, in situ ATR−SEIRAS isotope−labeling experiments using K15NO3 as the N source were conducted to verify the above analysis (Fig. 5d, e). The vibrations of *15NH2 (1106 cm−1), *15NH2OH (1019 cm−1), and C=15N (1640 cm−1) shift to lower wavenumbers46. These blueshifts were attributed to isotope effects. These results confirmed the successful synthesis of C6H11NO and the formation process of *NO, *NH2, and *NH2OH, which might serve as the active intermediate for oxime formation. To verify the N−containing active species for C6H11NO formation, control experiments using C6H10O as C source and NO2−, NH4+, NH2OH as N sources were carried out under standard conditions (Supplementary Table 3). All three entries produced C6H11NO, indicating these were all involved in C6H11NO formation. Considering that NH2OH was the more reduced intermediate than NO or NO2− in NO3− reduction, it is reasonable to regard NH2OH as the key N−containing active species for C6H11NO formation.

Additionally, operando SR−FTIR spectra were employed to identify the dynamics of *NH2OH in overall NO3RR process over 15% p-PANI−GS (Fig. 5f). No *NH2OH signal was detected in pure KNO3 solution or electrolyte containing KNO3. The stretching vibration peaks at 2852 and 2925 cm−1, corresponding to N−H bonds of *NH2OH8,47,48, were detected when p-PANI−GS was introduced before electrolysis, which was attributed to NO3−−to−*NH2OH based on Chem−NO3RR process. The characteristic *NH2OH signal increased with the electrolysis of 15 min, demonstrating the improved accumulation of *NH2OH under electrical stimulation. Moreover, *NH2OH could be continuously and massively generated with the electrolysis time prolonged. In addition, the observed results suggested the C–N coupling between C6H10O and NH2OH occurred spontaneously, even in the absence of electrolysis (Supplementary Table 3). However, the synthesis of C6H11NO was not achieved when C6H10O was introduced at the end of NO3− electroreduction process, likely due to the adsorption of the generated *NH2OH on the catalyst surface, hindering its desorption into the electrolyte and subsequent reaction with C6H10O. This hypothesis was supported by none signal UV–vis analysis (Supplementary Fig. 27), which revealed almost no NH2OH presence in the electrolyte during and after the reaction. Together with the weak but measurable enrichment of C6H10O at the p-PANI-GS interface (Supplementary Fig. 28), these results demonstrated that C6H11NO was formed by condensation of surface-confined *NH2OH and interfacially enriched C6H10O, rather than in the electrolyte solution7.

Furthermore, the reaction pathways of NO3RR and the Gibbs energies of intermediates over p-PANI−GS and bulk GS were calculated, respectively. The computational model was established, where the protonated N site was energetically more favorable for nitrate adsorption than the carbon framework (Supplementary Fig. 29-31). Accordingly, as shown in Fig. 5g, nitrate could be chemically adsorbed by the −NH+•− site on p-PANI−GS to initially form *NO3 with a decrease in free energy. Then, the deoxygenation and hydrogenation steps were continuously proceeded by a series of PCET steps to form *NO2 and *NO. The conversion of *NO2 to *NO was endergonic and acted as the rate−determining step (RDS), which was consistent with the detection of NO2− byproducts during NO3RR process. Subsequently, the *NO intermediate was gradually converted to *NOH, *NHOH, and finally to *NH2OH under hydrogenation49. As a result, the RDS of p-PANI−GS had a lower barrier to overcome compared with the GS counterpart, further indicating the higher ability on p-PANI−GS for driving the NO3RR to *NH2OH. These indicated that the incorporation of p-PANI on GS enabled the regulation of interfacial proton−electron transfer, leading to a significant reduction in the energy barrier of the reaction and the facilitation of C6H11NO synthesis. On the other hand, the intrinsic proton donor on p-PANI-GS interface contributed to stabilize these adsorbed *NH2OH by mitigating charge accumulation, preventing premature desorption (Supplementary Fig. 32) or over−hydrogenation to NH3 (Fig. 5g).

Thus, the plausible pathway for C6H11NO synthesis on p-PANI−GS was finally proposed employing Chem− and EChem−NO3RR process (Fig. 5h). Specifically, the construction of p-PANI − GS precisely manipulated the interfacial microenvironment, endowing it with the unique property of proton−electron donation, which facilitated the execution of Chem−NO3RR process to initiate synthesis C6H11NO prior to electrolysis. The Chem−NO3RR proceeded on p-PANI−GS in the following reaction pathway: NO3− → *NO3 → *NO2 → *NO → *NOH → *NHOH → *NH2OH. This preliminary process facilitated the adsorption and enrichment of NO3− at the p-PANI−GS interface and promoted its partial conversion into more reactive intermediates before electrolysis. Subsequently, PCET kinetics were significantly promoted in EChem−NO3RR process, thereby enhancing the overall reduction performance. The EChem−NO3RR was as follow: *NO3/*NO2 → *NO → *NOH → *NHOH → *NH2OH. Notably, the pre-reduction of NO3− to NO2− during Chem−NO3RR facilitated to lower kinetic barriers for the following electrocatalytic reduction because the bent NO2− geometry exposed a reactive nitrogen center, thus enhancing adsorption and accelerating bond−breaking kinetics relative to resonance-stabilized planar NO3−. Therefore, Chem−NO3RR acted as an initiation step that pre-activated the interface, whereas EChem−NO3RR functioned as an amplification step that accelerated downstream hydrogenation under bias. The seamless integration of the Chem− and EChem−NO3RR processes on p-PANI−GS enabled a cascading effect, where the intermediates formed in Chem−NO3RR were effectively utilized and rapidly consumed in EChem−NO3RR. Additionally, the Chem−NO3RR was not merely a transient, initial event but a dynamically maintained interfacial process that synergized with EChem−NO3RR to enhance both production and selectivity of C6H11NO. Such synergism underscored the critical role of Chem−NO3RR in complementing the EChem−NO3RR process by preconditioning the interface for a more kinetically favorable and efficient route toward C6H11NO synthesis.

Solar-driven system and machine learning prediction for oxime synthesis

Solar−driven systems represented a promising avenue for the generation of clean energy. The system was adapted from a commercial solar panel with a rated voltage of 6 V (3 W) without a reference electrode. LC−MS analysis was used to monitor the production of C6H11NO under different lighting environments (Fig. 6a, b, Supplementary Fig. 33). During 3 h of electrocatalytic reaction, the average current intensities of solar-driven reactions were 44.3 mA (Sunny Harbin) and 6.2 mA (Cloudy Harbin), respectively. Remarkably, under natural sunlight, the production of C6H11NO reached 0.81 g, with productivity of 164 mmol h−1 cm−2 and FE of 64.1%. In contrast, under cloudy conditions, the production rate of C6H11NO significantly decreased to just reach 0.27 g, with productivity of 24 mmol cm2 h−1 and FE of 21.6%. These results laid the foundation for the development of sustainable and efficient solar-driven processes for the synthesis of C6H11NO, providing a valuable roadmap for advancing renewable energy technologies in industrial-scale applications.

The machine learning predictions demonstrated robust performance in C6H11NO synthesis validation. As shown in the Prediction Confidence plot (Fig. 6c), the model’s confidence scores remained stable (0.6–0.8 range) with increasing numbers of predicted tokens, indicating reliable judgment consistency even for structurally complex intermediates. Analysis of Top-k accuracy by simplified molecular-input line-entry system (SMILES) length bins (Supplementary Fig. 34, Supplementary Table 4) revealed that the model maintained >80% accuracy across all complexity levels (10–80 + SMILES characters), with Top-5 accuracy reaching 93% for lengthy SMILES strings, underscoring the model’s strong robustness in predicting complex cyclohexanone oxime derivatives. These results validate the practical utility of the machine learning approach in extending experimental research: The model not only effectively predicts products under known experimental conditions but also provides reliable candidate schemes for designing cyclohexanone oxime structures, thereby reducing experimental trial-and-error costs. Additionally, the high-confidence metrics offer quantitative support for risk assessment in multi-step synthesis pathways, bridging computational predictions with practical synthetic applications.

Discussion

This work marked the demonstration of a non−metallic p-PANI−GS catalyst to capable of dual−path reduction of NO3−−to− *NH2OH, establishing not only a robust platform for efficient C−N coupling toward oxime electrosynthesis, but also a mechanistic framework in which spontaneous chemical pre-activation preceded electrocatalytic amplification at the interface. Central to this breakthrough was the dual functional interface engineering that concerted proton and electron transfer. The p-PANI component served as a solid−state proton reservoir through its −NH⁺•− moieties. Meanwhile, its integration with GS triggered interfacial electron redistribution, which worked in synergy to expedite the oriented electron transfer to adsorbed intermediates. Combining in situ characterization and theoretical calculations, this synergistic donor mechanism enhanced PCET kinetic and stabilized *NH2OH intermediates in the NO3− reduction network, steering selectivity toward C6H11NO. Our system achieved a considerable C6H11NO productivity of 67.1 µmol h−1 cm−2 and a FE of 69.9% at 30 mA cm−2, while exhibiting excellent electrocatalytic durability and broad pH (3.0−11.0) adaptability. Beyond mechanistic insights, this work pioneered solar-driven oxime synthesis by coupling p-PANI−GS with a photovoltaic cell, achieving a productivity of 164 mmol h−1 cm−2 and a FE of 64.1%. Machine learning models trained on experimental datasets further predicted the feasibility of oxime production, establishing a framework for intelligent manufacturing. These findings pioneered a sustainable electrocatalytic strategy for converting nitrate pollutants into high−value oxime, overcoming longstanding kinetic limitations in nitrogen cycle remediation while establishing an innovative platform for future scale-up of green organonitrogen electrosynthesis.

Methods

Chemicals

Carbon felt (19 × 19 cm), aniline (≥99%), ammonium persulfate (APS, ≥98%), sodium salicylate (≥99%), sodium hypochlorite (6–14% active chlorine basis), 1,10-phenanthroline (≥98%) are purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Graphite powder, sodium hydroxide (≥96.0%), hydrochloric acid (36.0–38.0%), potassium permanganate (≥99.5%), potassium carbonate (99.0%), and potassium nitrate (≥98.5%) are purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., P. R. China. Ethanol (>99.9%), 5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO, 99%), formic acid (50% in water), acetonitrile (≥99.99%), benzoic acid (≥99%), and cyclohexanone (99%) are purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. The above chemicals were analytical grade reagents. All chemicals were used directly without any purification. Deionized water was employed for preparing all of the solutions. The solar panels (6 V, 3 W) were purchased online from Guangdong Taiqifeng Electronics Co., Ltd. and used after being modified.

Fabrication of p-PANI−GS electrodes

Graphite sheets (GS) were produced through a top-down dry ball milling technique using zirconia balls (4 mm diameter) and graphite sheet powder at a 50:1 mass ratio without additional agents. The milling process occurred in a planetary ball mill (QM−3SP2J, China) at 450 rpm for 3 h. For the synthesis of p-PANI−GS, aniline, and 1 M HCl were sonicated and mixed with GS, followed by the addition of APS in 1 M HCl to form emerald green p-PANI rods on the GS surface. The composite was filtered, washed, and dried at 80 °C for 12 h, resulting in p-PANI−GS samples with varying p-PANI contents (5−20%). Electrodes were fabricated by treating carbon felt (CF, 1.0 cm2) with KMnO4, washing, and drying before applying the p-PANI−GS catalyst using a conductive adhesive, achieving a loading of approximately 10 mg.

Characterizations

The crystalline structure was analyzed by X-ray diffractometry (Rigaku TTR-III) with Cu Kα radiation (0.15406 nm) in the 2θ range of 10–90°. Surface morphology was examined using scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi S-4800). Microstructures were observed via transmission electron microscopy and high-resolution TEM (FEI Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN). Atomic force microscopy was performed using Digital Instrument Nanoscope IIIA. Diffuse reflection spectra were obtained using a UV–vis spectrometer (Shimadzu UV-2550). Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy was conducted with Thermo Fisher Esca Lab 250Xi (He I, 21.22 eV). Raman spectra were recorded with a Raman spectrometer (Nanofinder 30 R, 532 nm laser) and a Horiba XploRA (532 nm laser). Elemental analysis was carried out via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Kratos XSAM 800, Al Kα, hν = 1361 eV). Electron spin resonance spectra were obtained using a Bruker EMX plus X-band CW EPR spectrometer (9.83 GHz, 2.00 MW). Functional groups were identified by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher Nicolet iS50). Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS) data were acquired using a Helios Fire pump-probe spectrometer (Ultrafast Systems), featuring a motorized optical delay line that provides a maximum time delay of ~8 ns. The 800 nm ultrashort laser pulse, generated by a regenerative amplifier (Coherent Astrella, 800 nm, 100 fs, 7 mJ, 1 kHz) seeded with a mode-locked Ti:sapphire oscillator (Coherent Vitara-S, 800 nm, 80 MHz) and pumped with a LBO laser (Coherent Evolution-50C, 1 kHz system), was split by a beam splitter. One beam was frequency-converted to a 340 nm pump pulse via an optical parametric amplifier (OPerA Solo), with its power adjusted to ~73 μW by a neutral-density filter. The other beam was focused onto a CaF2 crystal to generate a white-light continuum (420–800 nm) serving as the probe pulse.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical experiments were performed on the electrochemical workstation (CHI 660E, Shanghai CH Instruments Co., Ltd.). Electrolysis was conducted in an H-type cell with a three-electrode configuration at a controlled room temperature of 25 °C, which consisted of a working electrode of p-PANI − GS, a platinum sheet as a counter electrode and Ag|AgCl electrode as the RE. Electrolysis was performed under constant-current mode, which provided a well-defined and steady electrochemical state for product evaluation (Supplementary Fig. 35). The reaction cell was composed of a quartz reactor with two chambers separated by a cation exchange membrane, and the lid was equipped with an electrode insertion port. 100 mL of 0.5 M K2CO3 electrolyte with 0.02 M KNO3 and 0.02 M C6H10O was used as the catholyte, while 100 mL of 0.5 M K2CO3 was used as the anolyte. Before the test, the cathode chamber was purged with Ar (99.99%) gas to purify the electrolyte for approximately 30 min. All linear scanning voltammetry (LSV) was repeated at a rate of 5 mV s−1 until the curves basically coincide, the open circuit potential was carried out at different applied potentials for 3600 s under a stirring rate of 300 rpm. All the curves were used without iR compensation. For the kinetic isotope effect (KIE) test, D2O was used instead of H2O.

In situ ATR−SEIRAS measurements

In situ ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was conducted utilizing a Bruker INVENIO S spectrometer, which was fitted with an MCT detector. For the experiment, a gold-coated, hemicylindrical silicon prism functioned as both the conductive substrate for catalyst deposition and the infrared reflection element. The working electrode was prepared by depositing p-PANI-GS suspensions onto the gold-coated surface. The electrochemical cell was completed with Ag|AgCl (0.5 M K2CO3) as the reference electrode and Pt foil as the counter electrode. The electrolyte used was a 0.5 M K2CO3 solution in H2O/D2O, supplemented with C6H10O and KNO3/K15NO3. Spectra collection occurred at a potential of −0.9 V relative to Ag|AgCl. Prior to each measurement series, a background spectrum of the catalyst electrode was obtained at open-circuit voltage.

Operando SR−FTIR measurements

Operando synchrotron radiation−Fourier transform infrared (SR-FTIR) spectroscopy measurements were conducted at the BL01B infrared beamline of the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory (NSRL), China. A ~60 nm Au thin film was chemically deposited on a Si prism via a two-step wet process. Prior to deposition, the IR-reflective Si surface was polished with diamond suspension, ultrasonically cleaned in water, and soaked in piranha solution (7:3 v/v 98% H2SO4: 30% H2O2) for 2 h. Subsequently, 30 μL of ink was deposited and dried on the Au-film working electrode. The ink-coated prism was assembled into a homemade spectroelectrochemical cell as the working electrode, with a Pt mesh (1 cm × 1 cm) counter electrode and an Ag|AgCl reference electrode positioned near the working electrode via a Luggin capillary. All spectra are presented as ΔR/R = (Es−ER)/ER, where Es and ER denote sample and reference spectra, respectively. The spectral resolution was 4 cm−1 for all the measurements if not otherwise mentioned. The setup incorporated an FTIR spectrometer (Bruker 70 v/s), a potassium bromide (KBr) beamsplitter, and a liquid nitrogen-cooled mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector. Infrared data acquisition utilized an infrared microscope (Bruker Hyperion 2000) equipped with a 15× objective. Spectra were acquired in reflection mode by averaging 128 scans. Background correction was performed at open−circuit potential before each measurement series.

DFT calculations

Our spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) calculations50,51 were carried out in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) based on the plane-wave basis sets with the projector-augmented-wave method52,53. The exchange-correlation potential was treated by using a generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) parametrization54. The van der Waals correction of Grimme’s DFT-D3 model was also adopted55. Lattice parameters of graphene were a = 29.8 Å, b = 12.3 Å, and c = 20.4 Å. The energy cutoff was set to be 450 eV. 1 × 2 × 1 Gamma k-point sampling is used for the Brillouin zone integration56. The structures were fully relaxed until the maximum force on each atom was less than 0.05 eV/Å, and the energy convergence standard was 10−5 eV. Vibrational frequency calculations provide zero-point energies for both free molecules and adsorbates. For electrochemical steps involving proton-electron transfer, the free energy change is determined using the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) model. This technique defines zero voltage relative to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE), where the hydrogen evolution reaction is thermodynamically equilibrated under standard conditions: 0 V applied potential, H2 at 1 MPa, and arbitrary pH and temperature. Consequently, the CHE model equates the free energy of a proton-electron pair (H+ + e−) to half the free energy of gaseous H2 at 0 V.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics simulation serves as a pivotal approach for investigating interactions among microscopic particles. In this study, all simulations were performed with the Materials Studio software package. The composite structure was constructed using the Amorphous Cell module, which employs a Monte Carlo algorithm to generate a random molecular morphology within a three-dimensional periodic cell under periodic boundary conditions (PBC) applied in all directions. To eliminate local energy concentrations and achieve a stable equilibrium state, the structure underwent geometry optimization and molecular dynamics simulation in the Forcite module. This was followed by a 100 ps NVT simulation using the COMPASSII force field for both intramolecular and intermolecular interactions. Temperature was regulated via the Nosé–Hoover thermostat, with charges assigned by the forcefield, while van der Waals and electrostatic interactions were computed using the atom-based summation method and the PPPM approach, respectively, with a Q ratio of 0.01.

Artificial intelligence model

The model employed in this work is built upon the Transformer architecture57, which was initially developed for neural machine translation tasks. The key architectural innovation compared to prior sequence-to-sequence models used for reaction prediction lies in the complete elimination of recurrent neural network (RNN) components, relying entirely on attention mechanisms for feature interaction.

The architecture follows an encoder-decoder paradigm, combining multi-head attention layers with position-wise feed-forward networks to enable autoregressive predictions. The encoder module employs multi-head attention to extract contextual features from input sequences and encode them into latent representations. The decoder incorporates two distinct attention mechanisms: a masked self-attention layer that maintains causal dependencies during generation, and a cross-attention layer that dynamically integrates encoder outputs with previously generated target sequence information to fuse source-target contextual relationships.

where dk is a scaling factor depending on the layer size. This mechanism enables adaptive focus on relevant contextual elements, where queries guide attention weighting, keys represent feature significance, and values carry content information.

A critical advantage of the Transformer over traditional sequence models is its parallelized multi-head attention, allowing simultaneous extraction of multi-dimensional features in the encoder and dynamic context retrieval in the decoder. To compensate for the absence of recurrence, the architecture incorporates sinusoidal positional encodings added to token embeddings. These encodings use phase-shifted trigonometric functions to inject absolute and relative position information into the embedding space, enabling the network to discern token order while maintaining computational efficiency. This design significantly enhances the modeling of long-range dependencies while preserving parallelizability.

Following the work58, the top-k predictions are generated through beam search decoding, with a beam width fixed at 5. The implementation builds on the PyTorch framework provided by OpenNMT. During training, we utilized the ADAM optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 and a token-based batch size of 4096.
