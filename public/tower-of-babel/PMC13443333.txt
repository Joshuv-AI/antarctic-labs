Greater burden of Alzheimer's Co-pathology in women with Parkinson's disease dementia
Authors: Nathaniel Dunckley, Erika D. Driver-Dunckley, Nan Zhang, Geidy E. Serrano, Holly A. Shill, Shyamal H. Mehta, Christine Belden, Cecilia Tremblay, Alireza Atri, Charles H. Adler, Thomas G. Beach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate sex differences in Alzheimer's disease (AD) related pathology among autopsy-confirmed Parkinson's disease (PD) cases.BackgroundAD and PD frequently co-occur in older adults, yet the influence of sex on AD pathology in the context of PD remains unexplored.MethodsAll subjects were enrolled in the Arizona Study of Aging and Neurodegenerative Disorders (AZSAND) and Brain and Body Donation Program (BBDP) and had annual standardized research clinical assessments by neuropsychologists, subspecialty behavioral and movement disorders neurologists, as well as comprehensive neuropathological examinations after death.ResultsAmong 230 autopsy-confirmed PD cases, females exhibited greater amyloid plaque pathology burden than males, regardless of a co-occurring AD diagnosis. Specifically, females with PD had significantly higher mean cortical total plaque scores (mean 6.5/15 vs. 4.9/15, p = 0.045) and greater CERAD neuritic plaque density (mean 1.7/3 vs. 1.3/3, p = 0.035). Females were also more likely to have a higher cortical plaque burden (plaque total ≥ 5: 56.8% vs. 39.7%, p = 0.015). In multivariable logistic regression models, female subjects showed greater than twice the odds of having an amyloid plaque burden ≥5 compared to males (OR = 2.18; 95% CI = 1.17–4.06; p = 0.014), when controlling for ApoE ε4 status, Lewy body density score, and age at death.ConclusionsFemale sex is associated with increased amyloid plaque pathology in PD, independent of ApoE ε4 status. These findings highlight a sex-specific vulnerability to AD pathology in PD patients and support the need for sex-informed approaches to reseach in mixed neurodegenerative disease.

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD) are prevalent among neurodegenerative disorders in aging populations, each contributing uniquely to cognitive and motor decline. While AD is primarily characterized by β-amyloid plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles, PD is defined by dopaminergic neurodegeneration and the accumulation of α-synuclein aggregates. 1 These pathologies often co-occur within the same individuals, particularly in older adults with PD who subsequently also develop dementia. Autopsy studies frequently reveal overlapping AD and PD pathology, suggesting a shared or compounding mechanism of neurodegeneration. 2 Neuropathological data from the Arizona Brain and Body Donation Program (BBDP) indicates that over half of PD dementia cases exhibit significant comorbid AD pathology, evident by both amyloid plaque and tau pathology. 3

Sex is an essential biological variable in both diseases. Men are more frequently affected by PD, while women are more likely to develop AD and may experience faster cognitive decline with more extensive tau and amyloid pathology.4–6 The mechanisms underlying these sex differences are complex, potentially involving hormonal and genetic factors. Despite known disparities in disease prevalence and progression, limited research has examined whether sex influences AD pathology in patients with PD.

In this study, we analyzed clinical, cognitive, and neuropathological data from a large autopsy-confirmed cohort of PD patients, assessing differences in β-amyloid plaque and tau pathology burden, in relation to ApoE genotype, between males and females. This analysis aims to determine whether the well-documented pattern of increased AD pathology in women persists even in the context of comorbid PD, which could enhance clinical awareness and inform more nuanced models of disease progression.

Materials and methods

Research subjects

Approval of this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) of Banner Sun Health Research Institute. Participants provided informed consent and were enrolled in the AZSAND/BBDP at Banner Sun Health Research Institute (www.brainandbodydonationprogram.org).2,7 Recruitment primarily targeted independently living retirees in Maricopa County, Arizona, particularly the Sun Cities, resulting in a predominantly Caucasian, middle to high income demographic. Along with neurological controls, individuals with dementia and parkinsonism were also recruited.

Clinical assessments

Research-dedicated physical, neurological, cognitive, and movement examinations have been previously detailed.2,7 Participants underwent at least one research visit; with most completing several annual visits. Standardized neuropsychological test batteries covered global assessments and specific cognitive domains. 2 Subspecialist cognitive-behavioral neurologists perform comprehensive evaluations leading to a cognitive diagnosis at the consensus conference involving neuropsychologists, neurologists and cognitive neurology subspecialists. All participants receive annual standardized movement disorders examination, including the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS, original version) and a clinical diagnosis by movement disorders fellowship-trained neurologists (EDD, CHA, SHM, HAS). Assessment of smell, sleep, and autonomic function along with medical comorbidities were conducted as previously published. 2 This study examined clinical diagnoses from the first and last examination visits, as well as the final clinical diagnosis which is made after death at a consensus conference, by review of all prior clinical assessments as well as review of the private medical records, prior to availability of the neuropathological examination results.

Case selection

Subjects included in this study had completed neuropathological examinations between 2008 and 2023, with at least one complete set of AZSAND/BBDP clinical assessments, including a final clinical diagnosis (considering all available clinical data before autopsy results) and a final clinicopathological diagnosis (with autopsy results considered).

Tissue processing and neuropathologic diagnosis

Autopsies were conducted by AZSAND/BBDP. Both clinical and neuropathological methods have previously been published.2,7 The same neuropathologist (TGB), blinded to clinical findings, conducted all neuropathological examinations. A clinicopathological diagnosis of PD required two or more cardinal clinical signs, as well as Lewy bodies and pigmented neuron loss in the substantia nigra. 8 Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) was distinguished from PD with dementia according to consensus criteria published by the DLB Consortium 9 ; the diagnosis of DLB was assigned if dementia was diagnosed prior to or within one year of the onset of parkinsonism and if the distribution and density of Lewy body (LB)-type pathology met “intermediate” or “high” criteria. Subjects with LB-related histopathology were classified according to the Unified Staging System for Lewy Body Disorders (USSLB).10,11 Neuropathological AD diagnoses were defined using the criteria according to the National Institute on Aging – Alzheimer's Association (NIA-AA) combined with a history of clinical dementia. 12 Formaldehyde-fixed paraffin-embedded sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H & E) and immunohistochemical stains for phosphorylated a-synuclein and phosphorylated TDP-43.2,7 Large-format, formaldehyde-fixed sections (40–80 µm-thick) were also stained with H & E, as well as with methods targeting amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, including Gallyas silver, Thioflavin-S and Campbell-Switzer silver stains.2,7 The dataset further included summary regional brain density measures for total amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary (NF) tangles (for both: summary score of 5 regional semi-quantitative 0–3 density scores for a maximum possible total of 15 in frontal, temporal and parietal lobes plus hippocampal CA1 and entorhinal/transentorhinal area), and assignment of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) neuritic plaque density, Braak neurofibrillary stage, and AD neuropathological change levels of Low, Intermediate or High, as described previously.2,12

Statistical analysis

The analyses utilized SAS statistical software (Version 9.4 SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and Sigma Plot 12.0 (Systat Software, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). Group comparisons used the Kruskal-Wallis test, Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression was used to further assess the sex association with greater presence of plaque burden after adjusting for potential confounders (age at death and ApoE status). For all tests, 0.05 was chosen as the criterion for statistical significance.

Results

We analyzed 230 autopsy-confirmed PD cases (74 females, 156 males), comparing clinical and pathological characteristics by sex. There were no significant sex differences in PD symptom onset age, disease duration, Hoehn & Yahr stage, or UPDRS scores. Age at death was also similar between groups (mean 81.8 ± 6.7 years in females vs. 80.4 ± 7.0 years in males; p = 0.094), as was the proportion with a coexistent diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease (32.4% in females vs. 30.1% in males; p = 0.724). In contrast, males had a significantly higher prevalence of REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) compared to females (66.9% vs. 42.9%; p = 0.043). Olfactory function, assessed using the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT), was significantly better in females (mean 16.0 ± 5.2) than in males (13.0 ± 4.8; p = 0.002), although both groups demonstrated overall olfactory impairment. Additionally, years of education differed by sex, with males having completed more years of schooling than females (15.9 ± 2.8 vs. 14.7 ± 2.6 years; p = 0.003) (Table 1).

Comorbid variables

Rates of comorbid hypertension, diabetes, obesity, depression, anxiety, and hyperlipidemia were similar between groups, although males had higher rates of sleep apnea (21.2% vs. 2.7%; p < 0.001), smoking (52.6% vs. 18.9%, p < 0.001), and alcohol consumption (66.0% vs. 47.3%, p = 0.007). Rates of minor traumatic brain injury were similar between groups (33.8% females vs. 34.6% males; p = 0.901) and family histories of dementia or parkinsonism were also not statistically significant. Cognitive function

No statistically significant sex differences were observed in cognitive test scores or dementia diagnosis rates; except on the AVLT total learning score; females scored higher compared to males (30.9 ± 12.2 vs. 24.4 ± 10.2, p = 0.003). MMSE scores were equivalent (22.7 ± 6.9 in females vs. 22.0 ± 6.3 in males; p = 0.190). At final clinical evaluation, 55.4% of female subjects and 68.6% of male subjects had a dementia diagnosis, a difference that was not significant (p = 0.089). Similarly rates of MCI were not statistically different (13.5% females vs. 12.8% males; p = 0.089). [Table 3].

Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body pathology

Female subjects had significantly greater amyloid plaque burdens than male subjects. Figure 1 provides an example of amyloid plaque density. The mean total plaque score was higher in females (6.5/15 ± 5.4) than in males (4.9/15 ± 5.5; p = 0.045). A larger proportion of females also met criteria for a dichotomously higher plaque burden (plaque total ≥ 5: 56.8% vs. 39.7%, p = 0.015). CERAD neuritic plaque density was also significantly higher in female subjects (mean 1.7/3 ± 1.2 vs. 1.3/3 ± 1.2; p = 0.035), with more females classified as having frequent neuritic plaques (37.8% vs. 26.3%). The Thal amyloid phase was significantly different between the two groups (mean 2.0 (1.5) vs. 1.6 (1.5), p = 0.034). In line with higher Thal phases, females were more likely than males to have amyloid plaques in the striatum, substantia nigra and cerebellar cortex. In contrast, tau pathology did not differ significantly between sexes. Total tangle burden was similar between females and males (5.7/15 ± 3.0 in females vs. 5.1/15 ± 2.4 in males; p = 0.353), and Braak neurofibillary stage did not differ by sex (p = 0.459). Likewise, there were no significant sex differences in Unified Lewy body stage or overall Lewy body density (Table 3).

Subgroup analyses by cognitive Status

In the PD subgroup without dementia (cognitively normal or MCI; n = 82), no significant sex differences were observed in Lewy body stage, Braak stage, plaque burden, or other neuropathologic measures (Supplemental Table 1). In contrast, among subjects with Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD; n = 148), several sex differences emerged (Supplemental Table 2). Females demonstrated greater Lewy body density (31.0 ± 6.7 vs. 28.8 ± 6.2; p = 0.030). Notably, Alzheimer's disease-related pathology was significantly more pronounced in females with PDD. Females had higher total plaque burden (7.6 ± 5.6 vs. 5.5 ± 5.6; p = 0.028), were more likely to meet criteria for elevated plaque burden (plaque total ≥5: 63.4% vs. 43.9%; p = 0.034), and had higher CERAD neuritic plaque density (1.9 ± 1.2 vs. 1.4 ± 1.2; p = 0.018). Thal amyloid phase was also significantly higher in females (2.4 ± 1.4 vs. 1.7 ± 1.5; p = 0.019), with a greater proportion of females in higher Thal phase groupings (p = 0.020). In contrast, tangle burden and Braak stage did not differ significantly between sexes.

Multivariable analysis of plaque burden

Implementing ROC analysis, the final pathology diagnosis of AD was used as the outcome to find the optimal cut-off of total plaque score to differentiate AD vs. non-AD. From this a plaque score of >=5 was chosen as the optimal cut-off for AD diagnosis. In multivariable logistic regression models adjusting for age at death, ApoE ε4 status, and Lewy body density score, female sex was associated with more than a two-fold increase in the odds of having elevated plaque burden (plaque total ≥ 5) compared to males (OR 2.18, 95% CI 1.17–4.06; p = 0.014). The presence of an ApoE ε4 allele was a strong independent predictor of higher plaque burden (OR 6.79, 95% CI 3.25–14.19; p < 0.001), while increasing age at death was also associated with greater plaque burden (OR 1.05 per year, 95% CI 1.00–1.10; p = 0.032) (Table 4). An interaction term between sex and Lewy body density score was initially included to assess potential effect modification but was not statistically significant (p = 0.086) and was therefore removed from the final model. In the adjusted model, female sex remained independently associated with increased odds of elevated AD plaque burden (OR 2.10, 95% CI 1.04–4.22; p = 0.038), confirming the robustness of this association after accounting for Lewy body pathology.

Discussion

This autopsy-based study of individuals with Parkinson's disease, found no sex differences in clinical features, demographics, or comorbidities. However, female subjects exhibited significantly greater AD-related pathology, particularly amyloid plaque burden. This was observed across multiple measures including total plaque score, CERAD neuritic plaque density, and Thal amyloid phase. These differences were robust and remained significant after adjustment for age, ApoE ε4 status, and Lewy body density. Importantly, this increased plaque burden in females was not accompanied by differences in tau pathology, as measured by tangle burden and Braak stage. This suggests that sex differences in PD may be driven more by amyloid-related processes than by tau pathology, at least within this cohort. The multivariable analysis further strengthens this conclusion. Female sex was independently associated with approximately a two-fold increased odds of elevated plaque burden, even after controlling for major contributors such as ApoE ε4 genotype and age. While ApoE ε4 was the strongest predictor of plaque burden overall, the persistence of a sex effect suggests additional biological mechanisms beyond known genetic risk factors.

An important finding of this study is that sex differences appear to be stage-dependent, emerging primarily in the context of PDD rather than earlier disease stages. Among PD subjects without dementia (cognitively normal or MCI), there were no significant sex differences in Lewy body stage, Braak stage, amyloid plaque burden, or other neuropathologic measures. In contrast, among subjects with PDD, females demonstrated greater Lewy body density and, more prominently, significantly higher AD-related amyloid pathology. These findings suggest that sex-related vulnerability to amyloid pathology may play a key role in the development of dementia in PD, rather than influencing early disease processes. This interpretation is consistent with the broader literature indicating that mixed pathologies, particularly AD co-pathology, are major determinants of cognitive impairment in PD. 3 The observation of higher Lewy body density in females with PDD further suggests that the interaction between synuclein and amyloid pathology may be particularly relevant in this group. While Lewy body pathology alone is a key driver of PD, the combination of increased synuclein burden and greater amyloid deposition in females may produce a cumulative or synergistic effect on cognitive decline. These findings suggest that women may be more susceptible to amyloid-driven pathology in the context of PD, echoing patterns observed in clinically diagnosed AD populations. 4

Global cognitive performance and rates of dementia did not differ significantly between sexes, despite females demonstrating a superior performance on measures of verbal learning (AVLT). The absence of sex-based differences in tau pathology may partly explain the lack of differences in overall cognitive impairment, given the well-established relationship between neurofibrillary tangles and cognitive decline. In AD, higher neuritic plaque densities are also associated with greater cognitive impairment; however, this relationship appeared less pronounced in females with PD in our cohort. This attenuation may reflect the relatively small sample size and will require further study. Alternatively, these findings may suggest a degree of relative cognitive resilience in females, potentially reflecting sex-related cognitive reserve or compensatory mechanisms. Similar observations have been reported in AD, where females may tolerate a greater burden of neuropathology before exhibiting clinical symptoms. 6

Strengths of this study include a well-characterized autopsy cohort with comprehensive neuropathological assessments and detailed clinical data, allowing for robust comparisons by sex and cognitive status. Limitations include the relatively modest sample size for subgroup analyses and the inability to account for unmeasured factors that may contribute to observed sex differences.

In summary, this study demonstrates that female sex is independently associated with greater AD-related pathology in PD, particularly in the context of dementia. While clinical features are largely similar between sexes, underlying neuropathological differences suggest that females may experience a distinct disease trajectory characterized by increased amyloid burden. These findings underscore the importance of considering sex as a biological variable in PD research and highlight the need to account for mixed pathologies in both clinical practice and therapeutic development.
