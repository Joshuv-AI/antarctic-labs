Generative Artificial Intelligence in Health Informatics Education: A Qualitative Exploration of Student Engagement, Perceived Educational Implications, and Ethical Considerations
Authors: Anas Ali Alhur, Nouf Khalid Al-Kahtani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGenerative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is increasingly used in higher education and health sciences education. However, limited qualitative evidence has examined how undergraduate Health Informatics (HI) students in Saudi Arabia engage with GenAI and interpret its educational, cognitive, ethical, and institutional implications.AimThis study aimed to explore HI students’ perceptions and self-reported experiences of GenAI use within academic learning contexts in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA qualitative study informed by an interpretivist approach and reflexive thematic analysis was conducted across six Saudi public universities offering undergraduate HI programmes. Thirty undergraduate HI students with prior academic experience using GenAI were purposively recruited. Semi-structured interviews were conducted over a two-month period in 2026 using face-to-face and online modalities. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, anonymized, and analyzed iteratively using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis approach with support from ATLAS.ti software.ResultsThree themes were identified: engagement with GenAI; perceived benefits and limitations; and ethical and institutional considerations. Participants reported using GenAI to summarize materials, understand complex concepts, organize academic tasks, and support assignments. They perceived GenAI as useful for efficiency and comprehension but also described concerns about over-reliance, reduced independent learning, passive acceptance of outputs, plagiarism, lack of source transparency, and unclear institutional policies.ConclusionUndergraduate HI students in Saudi Arabia perceived GenAI as a useful academic support tool, while also identifying cognitive, ethical, and institutional challenges. These exploratory findings reflect perceptions and self-reported experiences rather than objectively measured educational outcomes. Structured AI literacy, clear institutional guidance, and responsible curriculum integration are needed in HI education.

Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has attracted substantial attention across multiple sectors in recent years, particularly within higher education and health sciences education. The rapid advancement of large language models and AI-powered educational technologies has created new opportunities for supporting learning, improving academic engagement, enhancing access to information, and assisting students in complex educational tasks. Previous studies have demonstrated that GenAI can support self-regulated learning, improve student engagement, and facilitate personalized educational experiences.1,2 Furthermore, AI has been recognized for its broader potential to transform education and healthcare by supporting personalized learning, enhancing evaluative judgement, improving clinical decision support, and facilitating data-driven healthcare innovation.3–6 Investigations examining the performance of generative AI systems such as ChatGPT in professional and medical examinations have also reported promising results, including passing performance in components of the United States Medical Licensing Examination.7,8

Within educational environments, GenAI has been increasingly used to explain complex concepts, generate summaries, support assignments, and improve efficiency in academic tasks.3–6,9 In health sciences education, students frequently use GenAI tools to assist with understanding clinical concepts, preparing educational materials, and supporting learning activities.9–16 Recent evidence also suggests that integrating GenAI into Health Informatics education may contribute to student engagement, learning experiences, technological competencies, and broader applications related to clinical decision support and healthcare AI implementation.17–20 Because Health Informatics (HI) combines healthcare, information systems, digital technologies, data governance, clinical documentation, decision support, and data-driven decision-making, students in this field occupy a distinct position compared with many other health professions students. Their academic preparation requires them to understand both healthcare practice and the digital infrastructures that increasingly support care delivery, quality improvement, population health, and AI-enabled decision-making.

This disciplinary position makes HI students an important group for studying GenAI adoption. Unlike students whose AI exposure may be limited to general academic writing or clinical knowledge support, HI students are expected to develop competencies related to health data, digital systems, interoperability, information governance, analytics, and responsible technology use. Their engagement with GenAI is therefore not only a learning support issue but also part of their broader professional preparation for digitally enabled healthcare environments.

Despite these potential benefits, the increasing use of GenAI in higher education has generated substantial academic, ethical, and cognitive concerns. Researchers have highlighted challenges related to misinformation, hallucinated content, algorithmic bias, lack of transparency, ethical implementation, academic professionalism, and over-reliance on AI-generated outputs.12,21–35 Additional concerns relate to the possible influence of GenAI on students’ critical thinking, problem-solving abilities, independent learning, and evaluative judgment.28,36–41 Several investigations have suggested that students may become increasingly dependent on AI-generated answers without engaging in sufficient reflection, analysis, or verification of information.36,38–41

Academic integrity has emerged as one of the most frequently discussed concerns regarding the educational use of GenAI. Existing literature indicates that generative AI tools may create challenges related to plagiarism, authorship, originality, fairness in assessment, and ethical conduct in academic research.33,34,42 At the institutional level, many universities continue to demonstrate inconsistency in policies and guidance regarding the acceptable use of GenAI within educational environments.43–51 This lack of institutional clarity may create uncertainty among students and educators regarding ethical boundaries, responsible use, and evaluation standards for AI-assisted academic work.

Globally, research examining GenAI in higher education has expanded rapidly. However, most studies remain quantitative in nature and primarily focus on general attitudes, adoption, or technology acceptance. Existing qualitative studies within health sciences education have largely concentrated on medical or nursing students, with limited attention given to HI students despite the highly technological and interdisciplinary nature of the field.9–16,52,53 In Saudi Arabia, research investigating GenAI among university students has similarly focused mainly on quantitative assessments of perceptions, acceptance, or adoption patterns across general educational and health sciences populations.54–61

The Saudi Arabian context is particularly relevant because higher education and healthcare are undergoing rapid digital transformation, with growing national attention to artificial intelligence, data-driven services, digital health, and workforce development. Studying GenAI use among HI students in this context can provide insight into how future digital health professionals interpret AI-supported learning and how institutional policies may support responsible AI integration within health-related education. These developments are consistent with broader literature describing both the opportunities and challenges associated with AI in education, including implications for teaching and learning, critical thinking, healthcare innovation, and the evolving role of human expertise.2,62–67

Limited qualitative evidence currently exists regarding how undergraduate HI students in Saudi Arabia interpret and integrate GenAI within their learning practices, how they perceive its influence on their educational experiences and cognitive processes, and how they navigate ethical and academic integrity concerns associated with these technologies. In addition, little is known about how institutional environments, training opportunities, and policy ambiguity shape students’ engagement with GenAI in educational contexts. Therefore, this study aimed to generate an in-depth understanding of how undergraduate HI students in Saudi Arabia engage with, perceive, and interpret the use of GenAI within their educational environments.

Methodology

Study Design

This study adopted a qualitative research design to explore undergraduate Health Informatics students’ experiences, perceptions, and interpretations regarding the use of generative artificial intelligence within educational contexts. A qualitative methodology was appropriate because the study aimed to gain an in-depth understanding of how students engage with and make sense of GenAI within their learning practices rather than measuring usage frequency or attitudes quantitatively.

The study was situated within an interpretivist paradigm, which recognizes that individuals construct meaning through their experiences and interactions within specific social and educational contexts. Accordingly, students’ perceptions and experiences of GenAI were understood as being shaped by their academic environment, prior technological exposure, institutional context, and personal interpretations.

The study was partially informed by selected constructs from the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), particularly perceived usefulness, ease of use, social influence, and facilitating conditions.68 These constructs informed the development of several interview questions; however, the study extended beyond UTAUT to explore broader educational, cognitive, ethical, and institutional dimensions related to GenAI use in Health Informatics education.

Study Setting and Participants

The study was conducted across six public universities in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia offering undergraduate Health Informatics programmes. The participating institutions represented different geographical regions of the country, including King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences, Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University, Umm Al-Qura University, Qassim University, University of Hail, and Jazan University.

Participants were undergraduate students enrolled in Health Informatics programmes who had previous experience using generative artificial intelligence tools for academic purposes. Students without prior experience using GenAI were excluded because the study focused on lived experiences and interpretations of actual use.

A purposive sampling strategy was employed to recruit information-rich participants capable of providing meaningful insight into the use of GenAI within academic settings. A total of 30 undergraduate students participated in the study. Sample adequacy was guided by the concept of informational power and by the conceptual richness of the interview data rather than by statistical power or a fixed saturation threshold. The sample was considered adequate because the study aim was focused, the participant group was specific, participants had direct experience with GenAI for academic purposes, and the interviews generated detailed accounts relevant to the research questions.

Recruitment and data collection proceeded iteratively alongside data familiarization and preliminary coding. During later interviews, participants continued to provide nuance, examples, and variation within existing patterns of meaning, but no substantially different thematic direction emerged. The research team therefore determined that the dataset provided sufficient depth, breadth, and conceptual richness to support the development of coherent themes and address the study objectives.

Data Collection

Data were collected using semi-structured interviews developed through a review of existing literature on generative artificial intelligence in higher education and health sciences education, alongside selected UTAUT constructs. The interview guide explored areas related to patterns of GenAI use, perceived educational value, ease of use, social and institutional influences, cognitive implications, ethical considerations, academic integrity, and future expectations regarding GenAI in Health Informatics education.

Data collection was conducted over a two-month period in 2026 using both in-person and online interview modalities. In-person interviews were conducted in private university settings to ensure confidentiality and minimize interruptions. Online interviews were conducted through Microsoft Teams in situations where face-to-face interviews were not feasible. The same semi-structured interview guide was used across both modalities to support consistency while allowing participants to elaborate on issues that were important to them.

The research team considered the possible influence of interview modality during data collection and analysis. Face-to-face interviews sometimes allowed more direct rapport and observation of non-verbal cues, whereas online interviews provided flexibility and may have supported participation from students in different regions. Across both modalities, participants provided detailed accounts of their experiences, and the analysis did not indicate that one modality produced systematically different themes. Nevertheless, modality was considered as a contextual factor when interpreting the data.

All participants received an information sheet explaining the study purpose, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality procedures, and audio-recording process prior to participation. Written or electronic informed consent was obtained before each interview commenced. Interviews lasted approximately 30–45 minutes and were audio-recorded with participants’ permission. Participants were encouraged to elaborate on their experiences and perceptions to support depth and richness of the data.

Transcription and Data Management

All interviews were transcribed verbatim and reviewed multiple times to ensure transcription accuracy. Identifying information was removed during transcription, and participants were assigned anonymized identifiers (eg, P1, P2, P3). All data were stored in password-protected systems accessible only to the research team. Encrypted backup systems were used to maintain confidentiality and data security.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis approach. This method was selected because it supports systematic identification, interpretation, and reporting of patterns across qualitative datasets while acknowledging the active interpretive role of the researcher.

The analysis process began with repeated reading of interview transcripts to achieve familiarity with the data. Initial codes were then generated inductively from participants’ accounts while also being sensitized by selected UTAUT concepts relevant to technology use and acceptance. Coding was iterative rather than linear; codes were refined, merged, separated, or renamed as the research team moved between individual coded extracts and the complete dataset.

Candidate themes were developed by grouping related codes according to shared patterns of meaning. The research team then reviewed these candidate themes against the coded extracts and the complete dataset to assess coherence, distinctiveness, and relevance to the research questions. Analytical decisions were documented through memos, including decisions about theme boundaries, overlap between educational and cognitive interpretations, and the placement of ethical and institutional concerns. Final themes were defined, refined, and supported with representative participant quotations.

ATLAS.ti software version 25 was used to facilitate data organization, coding, retrieval, memo-writing, and thematic management throughout the analytic process. The software supported data management but did not determine theme development or interpretation.

Researcher Reflexivity

Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study to acknowledge the researcher’s active role in interpreting participants’ experiences and constructing meaning from the data. The study was situated within an interpretivist paradigm, which recognizes that data interpretation is influenced by the researcher’s academic background, experiences, and perspectives.

The researcher’s academic training in Health Informatics and familiarity with generative artificial intelligence technologies were recognized as factors that could shape data interpretation and thematic development. To support reflexive engagement, reflective memos were maintained throughout data collection and analysis to document assumptions, analytic decisions, emerging interpretations, and personal reflections related to the research process.

This reflexive process supported transparency in interpretation and contributed to maintaining close alignment between the identified themes and participants’ experiences.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was established by addressing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability throughout the research process. Credibility was enhanced through in-depth semi-structured interviews, repeated engagement with interview data, and the use of participant quotations to support thematic interpretations.

Dependability was strengthened through transparent documentation of the recruitment process, interview procedures, transcription methods, coding strategies, and thematic analysis process. A clear audit trail was maintained throughout the study to support consistency and transparency.

Confirmability was supported through reflexive memo-writing and systematic documentation of analytic decisions throughout the study. Reflective notes were maintained during coding and theme development to critically examine emerging interpretations and reduce the influence of unexamined assumptions on the findings.

Transferability was enhanced through detailed description of the study context, participant characteristics, institutional settings, and methodological procedures. Including participants from multiple universities across different geographical regions of Saudi Arabia further supported diversity of perspectives and provided a richer contextual understanding relevant to similar educational environments.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University (IRB No. IRB-PGS-2026-03-0386) before commencement of data collection. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and all participants provided written or electronic informed consent before participating in the interviews.

Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, their right to withdraw at any stage without consequence, and the procedures used to maintain confidentiality and privacy. All interviews were audio-recorded only after obtaining participants’ consent. The informed consent process included permission for the publication of anonymized responses and direct quotations.

Confidentiality was maintained through anonymization of transcripts and the use of coded participant identifiers (eg, P1, P2, P3). Identifying information was removed from transcripts during data processing and analysis. All digital files, interview recordings, transcripts, and related research materials were securely stored in password-protected systems accessible only to the research team. Encrypted backup systems were also used to maintain data security and confidentiality.

For interviews conducted online through Microsoft Teams, participants were encouraged to join from private and quiet locations to support confidentiality and minimize interruptions during the interview process.

Overview of Themes

Following repeated review and familiarization with the interview transcripts, initial codes were generated systematically across the dataset. Codes were subsequently organized into categories and broader themes based on recurring patterns identified during the analysis process. Reflexive thematic analysis resulted in the identification of three overarching themes: (1) Engagement with Generative Artificial Intelligence, (2) Perceived Benefits and Limitations, and (3) Ethical and Institutional Considerations.

The first theme focuses on how students used GenAI and how their use evolved through experience. The second theme focuses on students’ perceived educational and cognitive benefits and limitations, including efficiency, comprehension, over-reliance, and independent learning. The third theme focuses on ethical, academic integrity, and institutional issues, including plagiarism concerns, source transparency, policy uncertainty, and the need for training. These distinctions were used to reduce conceptual overlap between practical engagement, perceived cognitive-educational implications, and governance-related concerns (Figure 1). Theme 1: Engagement with Generative Artificial Intelligence

Patterns of Use

Participants described early and progressively increasing engagement with generative artificial intelligence tools throughout their educational journey. Several students reported initial exposure to GenAI before entering university, while others began using these technologies during their undergraduate studies. Over time, participants explained that their familiarity and confidence improved as they developed more effective prompting and interaction strategies. This finding aligns with the UTAUT construct of effort expectancy, suggesting that increased experience with GenAI may reduce perceived difficulty and support greater confidence in its use.

One participant explained: I started using them a little before entering university, around the end of secondary school, and since then, I have continued using them during my studies as part of how I learn and deal with different academic tasks. (P1)

I started using them a little before entering university, around the end of secondary school, and since then, I have continued using them during my studies as part of how I learn and deal with different academic tasks. (P1)

Another participant stated: I started using them when I entered university, and over time, my use improved because I learned how to use them better and how to phrase my questions more clearly so that I could get more useful and accurate answers. (P2)

I started using them when I entered university, and over time, my use improved because I learned how to use them better and how to phrase my questions more clearly so that I could get more useful and accurate answers. (P2)

Similarly, another student reflected: At the beginning, I was not very familiar with it and did not use it much, but over time I became more comfortable using it and started using it more frequently, especially when I needed help in my studies. (P17)

At the beginning, I was not very familiar with it and did not use it much, but over time I became more comfortable using it and started using it more frequently, especially when I needed help in my studies. (P17)

These findings suggest that students’ engagement with GenAI evolved progressively through practice, familiarity, and repeated academic use.

Contexts of Application

Participants described using GenAI across various academic activities, particularly for summarizing educational materials, organizing academic tasks, understanding complex concepts, supporting assignments, and preparing presentations. Many students described GenAI as part of their daily academic routine, especially when reviewing lectures, planning study schedules, preparing presentation content, and checking their understanding of complex material. Most students emphasized that they used GenAI as a supportive educational aid rather than as a complete replacement for independent learning.

One participant stated: I use it for organizing schedules, summarizing content, and helping with assignments, but I do not depend on it completely, and I always try to check the information myself to make sure it is correct. (P4)

I use it for organizing schedules, summarizing content, and helping with assignments, but I do not depend on it completely, and I always try to check the information myself to make sure it is correct. (P4)

Another participant explained: I used it for assignments, helping with tasks, checking certain information, and also preparing presentations, especially when I needed support in understanding something or organizing my work better. (P5)

I used it for assignments, helping with tasks, checking certain information, and also preparing presentations, especially when I needed support in understanding something or organizing my work better. (P5)

Similarly, one participant described the role of GenAI in simplifying difficult concepts: It contributes a great deal, especially when something is difficult or unclear, because it simplifies the information and explains it in a way that makes it easier for me to understand. (P22)

It contributes a great deal, especially when something is difficult or unclear, because it simplifies the information and explains it in a way that makes it easier for me to understand. (P22)

Overall, participants viewed GenAI as a practical academic support tool that assisted with organization, understanding, and task completion.

Theme 2: Perceived Benefits and Limitations

Cognitive Support and Educational Benefits

Participants consistently described GenAI as helpful in improving efficiency, supporting understanding, and simplifying complex educational content. Students frequently perceived these technologies as valuable supplementary learning tools when used alongside traditional learning methods. These perceptions reflect the UTAUT construct of performance expectancy, as participants believed that GenAI enhanced their academic work by facilitating access to information, improving task completion, and supporting comprehension of challenging concepts. These findings should be interpreted as perceived benefits rather than objectively measured learning outcomes.

One participant explained: It helps in understanding topics and completing tasks more efficiently, but only when it is used as support and not as a replacement for actual learning or studying by myself. (P6)

It helps in understanding topics and completing tasks more efficiently, but only when it is used as support and not as a replacement for actual learning or studying by myself. (P6)

Another participant stated: It summarizes and explains things for me in general, which makes it easier to understand lectures and study materials that I might not fully understand on my own. (P24)

It summarizes and explains things for me in general, which makes it easier to understand lectures and study materials that I might not fully understand on my own. (P24)

Similarly, one participant highlighted both benefits and limitations: It helps explain difficult topics, but sometimes it removes important details when summarizing, so you still need to be careful and not rely on it completely without reviewing the original content. (P3)

It helps explain difficult topics, but sometimes it removes important details when summarizing, so you still need to be careful and not rely on it completely without reviewing the original content. (P3)

These accounts show that students perceived GenAI as beneficial for supporting comprehension and improving academic efficiency while simultaneously recognizing the importance of active engagement and verification of AI-generated information.

Risks of Over-Reliance and Divergent Views

Despite the perceived benefits, participants expressed significant concerns regarding excessive dependence on generative artificial intelligence tools. Students frequently discussed concerns related to reduced independent learning, diminished problem-solving engagement, and passive acceptance of AI-generated responses. However, participants did not view GenAI as uniformly harmful. Several students differentiated between guided use that supports learning and excessive use that replaces effort, suggesting a more nuanced interpretation of GenAI’s perceived cognitive role.

One participant explained: If I keep depending on it, it may stop me from trying to solve the problem by myself, and I may not develop my own way of thinking or understanding the material properly. (P1)

If I keep depending on it, it may stop me from trying to solve the problem by myself, and I may not develop my own way of thinking or understanding the material properly. (P1)

Another participant stated: Yes, negatively. If you stop returning to real sources, and if you stop building your own knowledge, it will affect you badly over time and reduce your ability to understand things on your own. (P5)

Yes, negatively. If you stop returning to real sources, and if you stop building your own knowledge, it will affect you badly over time and reduce your ability to understand things on your own. (P5)

Similarly, one participant reflected: It may make students forget how to study on their own and how to depend on themselves, especially after using it for long time and too much for summaries instead of reading the full material. (P18)

It may make students forget how to study on their own and how to depend on themselves, especially after using it for long time and too much for summaries instead of reading the full material. (P18)

Participants also highlighted concerns regarding critical thinking and cognitive engagement. One participant explained: It makes it easier to accept answers without thinking in depth, because everything is given quickly and directly, so you might not stop to analyze or question the information. (P7)

Taken together, these findings indicate a perceived tension between educational convenience and the maintenance of independent cognitive engagement. The data suggest that students distinguished between responsible use of GenAI as a learning support tool and over-reliance that may reduce active learning, verification, and independent thinking.

Theme 3: Ethical and Institutional Considerations

Academic Integrity Concerns

Participants raised multiple concerns regarding academic integrity, particularly issues related to plagiarism, lack of source transparency, fairness in assessment, and uncertainty regarding acceptable use of GenAI within academic environments.

One participant stated: Sometimes AI provides information without mentioning the original source, even though the information may actually come from researchers or other existing sources, and that creates a problem. (P19)

Sometimes AI provides information without mentioning the original source, even though the information may actually come from researchers or other existing sources, and that creates a problem. (P19)

Another participant explained: If an instructor has put in effort teaching you and you then just let ChatGPT shorten everything for you, then there is reason for concern about fairness and whether you are really doing your own work in the right manner. (P25)

If an instructor has put in effort teaching you and you then just let ChatGPT shorten everything for you, then there is reason for concern about fairness and whether you are really doing your own work in the right manner. (P25)

Similarly, one participant highlighted institutional uncertainty: There is no clear direction from instructors, so sometimes it is not clear what is allowed and what is not when using these tools in assignments. (P12)

There is no clear direction from instructors, so sometimes it is not clear what is allowed and what is not when using these tools in assignments. (P12)

These findings demonstrate that students experience uncertainty regarding ethical boundaries and appropriate academic use of generative artificial intelligence tools.

Need for Training and Institutional Guidance

A recurring issue across participant accounts was the limited availability of formal training and institutional guidance regarding responsible and effective GenAI use. Most students reported relying primarily on self-directed learning and personal experimentation when using these technologies.

One participant stated: I have not received any formal training on how to use it properly or responsibly in my academic work, so most of what I know comes from my own experience. (P17)

I have not received any formal training on how to use it properly or responsibly in my academic work, so most of what I know comes from my own experience. (P17)

Another participant similarly explained: No proper training is available, and most of what we know about using it comes from our own experience and trying things ourselves. (P15)

No proper training is available, and most of what we know about using it comes from our own experience and trying things ourselves. (P15)

Participants also emphasized the importance of structured educational support and awareness initiatives. One participant explained: There should be more awareness and education for students about how to use AI, rather than only treating it as something negative or trying to limit its use without guidance. (P23)

There should be more awareness and education for students about how to use AI, rather than only treating it as something negative or trying to limit its use without guidance. (P23)

Overall, participants identified a clear gap between widespread student engagement with GenAI technologies and the limited institutional support available to guide responsible and effective use.

Overall, participants demonstrated widespread engagement with generative AI and recognized its value in supporting academic tasks. However, concerns regarding over-reliance, reduced critical engagement, and ethical use were prominent. The lack of clear institutional policies and training further contributed to uncertainty, emphasizing the need for structured guidance and educational support.

Discussion

This study explored how undergraduate Health Informatics students in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia engage with, perceive, and interpret the use of generative artificial intelligence within their academic learning environments. The findings indicated widespread engagement with GenAI tools and identified perceived educational benefits, concerns regarding cognitive engagement, ethical challenges, and institutional gaps associated with their use.

One important finding was that students described increasing familiarity and confidence in using GenAI over time. Participants reported that repeated exposure and continued interaction with these tools helped them formulate clearer prompts, refine their questions, and obtain more useful outputs. This suggests that the perceived ease of using GenAI may improve as students gain practical experience, which may subsequently encourage more frequent academic use. This finding is consistent with previous literature indicating that effective interaction with GenAI requires practice, familiarity, digital competence, and the development of AI literacy skills.69–71

Participants frequently described GenAI as a supportive educational tool that assisted them in summarizing learning materials, simplifying complex concepts, organizing academic tasks, preparing presentations, and completing assignments more efficiently. These findings are broadly consistent with previous studies suggesting that GenAI can support personalized learning, facilitate access to educational information, and improve students’ engagement with academic activities.11,72–77 However, the findings should be interpreted carefully because this study did not objectively measure academic performance, knowledge acquisition, or learning outcomes. Rather, it examined how students perceived and described the educational value of GenAI within their academic routines.

The practical relevance of these findings is particularly evident in the ways students incorporated GenAI into their daily learning practices. Participants described using these tools to review lectures, summarize readings, organize study tasks, prepare presentations, clarify difficult concepts, and plan assignments. These uses indicate that GenAI has already become part of students’ academic routines. In Health Informatics education, this creates a need to teach students how to use GenAI as a supplementary learning tool while maintaining independent reading, disciplinary reasoning, source verification, and engagement with original academic materials.

Despite these perceived benefits, participants expressed substantial concerns regarding excessive dependence on GenAI. Several students believed that over-reliance on AI-generated responses could reduce independent learning, problem-solving engagement, and students’ willingness to construct knowledge through their own efforts. Participants also raised concerns about accepting AI-generated information without sufficient analysis, questioning, or verification. These findings are consistent with previous literature discussing the possible effects of GenAI on critical thinking, cognitive effort, evaluative judgement, and intellectual autonomy.36,38–41,78

Importantly, participants did not describe GenAI as uniformly beneficial or harmful. Instead, they differentiated between guided use that supports learning and excessive use that replaces independent effort. This distinction suggests that the educational consequences of GenAI may depend less on whether the technology is used and more on how it is used. Passive use may reduce cognitive effort, whereas structured use may support deeper learning when students are required to question outputs, compare information with reliable sources, identify inaccuracies, generate alternative explanations, and reflect on the reasoning behind AI-generated responses.

The findings related to critical thinking, problem-solving, and independent learning represent students’ perceptions rather than objectively measured cognitive outcomes. Therefore, the study does not establish that GenAI causes cognitive decline or directly improves cognitive performance. Students’ concerns may reflect their awareness of the risks associated with passive or excessive use. An alternative interpretation is that GenAI may reduce cognitive engagement when used as a substitute for learning but may strengthen learning when incorporated into structured educational activities that require verification, reflection, and active reasoning.

Academic integrity was another major concern identified by participants. Students discussed uncertainty related to plagiarism, authorship, originality, fairness in assessment, and the absence of transparent sources in AI-generated outputs. These concerns are consistent with previous research identifying challenges surrounding academic integrity, attribution, responsible authorship, and ethical GenAI use in higher education.33,34,43,79,80 Participants also described inconsistency in instructors’ expectations and uncertainty regarding what forms of GenAI use were acceptable in assignments and assessments.

The lack of clear institutional expectations may create confusion for both students and educators. When acceptable and unacceptable uses are not clearly defined, students may make decisions based on personal judgement, peer practices, or assumptions about individual instructors’ preferences. This may lead to inconsistent practices across courses and unequal expectations among students. Institutions should therefore provide clear guidance on disclosure, attribution, verification, authorship, privacy, and the appropriate use of GenAI in academic work.

A particularly important finding was the limited availability of formal training and institutional guidance. Most participants reported that they had learned to use GenAI through personal experimentation rather than through structured educational programmes. This gap is important because effective GenAI use requires more than basic technical ability. Students also need to understand the limitations of AI-generated information, potential bias, fabricated references, privacy risks, inappropriate disclosure of sensitive information, and the need to verify outputs using credible academic sources.

These findings support the growing emphasis on AI literacy in higher education.69–71 AI literacy within Health Informatics education should include responsible prompting, source evaluation, recognition of inaccurate or fabricated information, verification of health-related content, awareness of algorithmic bias, privacy protection, academic integrity, and critical reflection on the role of AI in decision-making. Such training should be integrated into the curriculum rather than provided only as optional or isolated workshops.

The findings are especially relevant to Health Informatics education because students in this discipline are preparing for roles involving health information systems, clinical documentation, data governance, interoperability, analytics, decision support, privacy, and digitally enabled healthcare. Their use of GenAI therefore has implications beyond academic task completion. Students may later encounter AI-enabled systems in professional environments where inaccurate information, inappropriate data disclosure, or uncritical acceptance of automated recommendations could affect healthcare quality, patient safety, and organizational decision-making.

Health Informatics programmes may therefore consider integrating GenAI into the curriculum through structured and transparent learning activities. For example, students could be required to compare AI-generated responses with peer-reviewed evidence, identify errors or unsupported claims, evaluate generated clinical or informatics recommendations, document how GenAI was used, and reflect on the ethical implications of relying on automated outputs. These activities may help students move from unstructured experimentation toward responsible, accountable, and educationally meaningful use.78,81–83

Assessment practices may also need to be reconsidered. Assignments that can be completed through direct reproduction of AI-generated content may provide limited evidence of students’ actual understanding. Educators may instead use assessments that require justification of reasoning, comparison of multiple sources, explanation of decision-making processes, oral defence of submitted work, and reflection on how GenAI contributed to the final output. Such approaches may help maintain academic integrity while acknowledging that GenAI has become increasingly accessible to students.

Institutional responses should move beyond either unrestricted use or complete prohibition. Restrictive policies alone may not prevent students from using GenAI and may discourage transparent disclosure.84 A more practical approach would involve clear guidance, programme-level expectations, instructor training, student education, and assessment redesign. Policies should distinguish between acceptable supportive use, such as brainstorming or language assistance, and unacceptable practices, such as submitting AI-generated work without acknowledgement or using GenAI during restricted assessments.85–87

Overall, this study contributes to the growing literature on GenAI in higher education by providing qualitative insight into the experiences of undergraduate Health Informatics students in Saudi Arabia. The findings indicate that students perceive GenAI as useful for academic support, efficiency, and comprehension, while also recognizing risks related to over-reliance, reduced independent engagement, academic integrity, and unclear institutional expectations. These findings highlight the importance of structured AI literacy education, transparent institutional guidance, and curriculum approaches that support responsible use while preserving active learning and independent reasoning.

Strengths and Contributions of the Study

This study contributes to the growing literature examining generative artificial intelligence within higher education by focusing specifically on undergraduate Health Informatics students in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. One major strength is its focus on a specialized and technology-oriented discipline within the health sciences, where students are highly likely to interact with emerging AI technologies during both academic training and future professional practice.

Another important strength is the multi-site qualitative design, which included participants from six universities representing different geographical regions of Saudi Arabia. This approach enhanced the diversity of participant perspectives and supported broader understanding of students’ experiences across different institutional contexts.

In addition, the study employed an in-depth qualitative methodology that enabled exploration of students’ perceptions, interpretations, and experiences beyond what is typically captured through quantitative surveys. The use of semi-structured interviews and reflexive thematic analysis allowed participants to discuss educational, cognitive, ethical, and institutional dimensions of GenAI use in considerable depth. The study also contributes by highlighting student-centered perspectives regarding AI literacy, academic integrity, institutional ambiguity, and concerns about over-reliance on AI technologies.

Limitations of the Study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the study focused exclusively on undergraduate Health Informatics students enrolled at selected universities within Saudi Arabia, which may limit transferability of the findings to other health disciplines, academic programmes, or international educational contexts.

Second, the study adopted a qualitative exploratory design, which aimed to generate in-depth understanding of participants’ experiences and perceptions rather than measure causal relationships or objectively assess educational and cognitive outcomes. Consequently, the findings reflect students’ subjective experiences and interpretations rather than direct measures of critical thinking, problem-solving ability, academic performance, or learning outcomes.

Third, all participants had prior experience using GenAI and volunteered to participate. This may have introduced self-selection bias because students with stronger interest in AI technologies, greater digital confidence, or more positive experiences may have been more likely to participate. Therefore, the findings may not fully represent students who do not use GenAI, use it less frequently, or hold more critical views of these technologies.

Fourth, interviews were conducted using both face-to-face and online modalities. Although the same semi-structured guide was used across modalities, differences in rapport, comfort, privacy, and interaction style may have influenced the depth or nature of participant responses. This was considered during analysis, but modality-related differences cannot be fully excluded.

Finally, participants’ perspectives may have been influenced by differences in prior technological exposure, digital literacy, educational background, and personal experiences with GenAI tools. The rapidly evolving nature of GenAI technologies also means that students’ experiences and perceptions may change over time as these systems continue to develop and become more integrated into educational environments.

Recommendations for Future Research

Future research should examine the actual educational and cognitive effects of generative artificial intelligence using quantitative, longitudinal, experimental, and mixed-methods approaches. There is a particular need for studies investigating how GenAI may influence critical thinking, problem-solving, decision-making, independent learning, and academic performance over time.

Further investigations should also include students from different academic disciplines, healthcare programmes, and educational institutions both within and outside Saudi Arabia to improve understanding of disciplinary and cultural differences related to GenAI use. Comparative studies across Health Informatics, medicine, nursing, pharmacy, and public health may help identify discipline-specific needs for AI literacy and curriculum design.

Additionally, future studies should explore the perspectives of faculty members, administrators, policymakers, and educational leaders on institutional governance, academic integrity, AI literacy, and the ethical implementation of generative artificial intelligence in higher education. Research examining effective institutional policies, training programmes, assessment redesign, and educational interventions supporting responsible AI use would also be valuable.

Conclusion

This exploratory qualitative study found that undergraduate Health Informatics students in Saudi Arabia widely engaged with generative artificial intelligence tools within their educational practices and perceived several educational benefits associated with their use, particularly in simplifying complex concepts, improving efficiency, and supporting academic tasks.

However, the findings also highlighted important perceived ethical, cognitive, and institutional concerns, including excessive reliance on AI systems, passive acceptance of AI-generated information, concerns regarding critical thinking and independent learning, uncertainty surrounding academic integrity, and the absence of clear institutional policies and educational guidance. Statements related to critical thinking and cognitive development should therefore be understood as participants’ perceptions rather than established effects.

The findings suggest that educational institutions should move beyond purely restrictive approaches toward more structured and responsible integration of generative artificial intelligence within higher education. Strengthening AI literacy, supporting critical evaluation skills, and establishing clear institutional policies and guidance may help promote ethical, informed, and responsible educational use of these technologies.

Overall, this study contributes to understanding how Health Informatics students interpret and experience the use of generative artificial intelligence within educational contexts and provides a foundation for future research exploring the educational, ethical, and cognitive implications of GenAI within higher education.
