 Background
• The National Lab of the Rockies (NLR) has collaborated with Great Lakes ports
  to conduct analysis on potential risks and opportunities for making additional
  fuels available at their ports.
• Broadly, we have looked at existing and anticipated fuel production within
  the region, as well as current port infrastructure and activities. We’ve
  provided a demonstration on the Alternative Energy for Ports Explorer (APEX)
  tool to help ports consider how existing energy resources and infrastructure
  could support port goals or development.
• The project is supported by the U.S. Department of Energy, Office of
  International Affairs with assistance from the U.S. Department of
  Transportation, Great Lakes St. Lawrence Seaway Development Corporation.
                                                                 National Laboratory of the Rockies | 3

 Energy Addition
• Proposed fuel production facilities in the Great Lakes region could produce an
  additional 1.5 million tons marine diesel oil-equivalent (MDO-eq) by 2031.
• 1,336 acres of available land around ports have the potential to contain
  energy-producing facilities with production of
  ◦ Up to 44 million tonnes of LNG per year OR
  ◦ Up to 9,545 million gallons of biodiesel per year OR
  ◦ Up to 7 million tonnes of e-methanol (produced using CO2 and electricity) per year
• New bunkering at ports could expand available fuels and open new markets
  for imports and bunkering in the Great Lakes.

 Regional supply and demand at the Great Lakes
• Biodiesel could be a near-term solution for alternative fuel supply:
 ◦ The Great Lakes region has a large biodiesel production capacity
 ◦ According to producers’ data, biodiesel production capacity is currently underutilized
 ◦ Excess capacity could potentially go toward marine bunkering
 ◦ Biodiesel bunkering is mature in terms of safety procedures and is already established in two of the chosen ports
 ◦ Biodiesel is a highly-compatible solution that generally does not require engine modifications or newly built ships to
 accommodate diesel/biodiesel blends, which is in line with the limited fleet turnover in the Great Lakes
 ◦ Shipping companies in the Great Lakes have conducted pilots with Lakers operating on B100 without modifications
• Nearly all chosen ports have regional operational ethanol facilities providing fuel for blending with gasoline, hydrogen facilities
 supporting refineries, ammonia facilities supporting the agricultural sector, and natural gas processing plants. These facilities are
 unlikely to supply fuel to the ports in the near-term (e.g., new fuels require new vessels/significant modifications, or high
 utilization of existing facilities). However:
 ◦ Most of the planned added capacity by 2031 will come from ammonia, methanol, and hydrogen facilities.
 ◦ Canadian shipping companies (i.e., Algoma, Canada Steamship Lines (CSL)) are building methanol-ready vessels that
 could potentially purchase methanol from the plants in the U.S.

Discussion

 Key Takeaways
• Biodiesel could be a near-term alternative fuel candidate in the Great Lakes.
 ◦ Substantial regional production capacity (1.37 million tons MDO-eq), meaningful unused capacity (0.3 million tons MDO-eq),
 and existing use as a bunker fuel (and currently planned additional infrastructure) position biodiesel as a highly deployable
 near-term option.
 ◦ Near drop-in compatibility aligns with the existing Great Lakes fleet. Biodiesel blends generally don’t require engine
 modifications, which is critical given low turnover rate of U.S. fleet.
 ◦ Logistics favor near-term deployment at ports. Established rail and road networks enable relatively straightforward transport of
 biodiesel to ports without major new infrastructure investments.
 ◦ Key risks are operational and market-driven. Higher freeze points may limit shoulder-season use, and production volumes
 remain sensitive to feedstock availability, pricing, and competing markets.
• LNG could also be a near-term candidate, although with some limitations.
 ◦ Substantial regional processing capacity and limited but meaningful bunkering already taking place at Hamilton.
 ◦ Key challenge is ship-readiness. Use requires new vessel builds or major retrofits – potentially better aligned with the more
 flexible Canadian fleet.
 ◦ Advantages include low current fuel costs and potential ability to switch to bio-LNG or methanol in the future.

                                                                                                              National Laboratory of the Rockies | 29

 Key Takeaways
• Ammonia, methanol, and hydrogen show the greatest planned capacity growth (although still small, 1.4 million
  tons MDO-eq planned). Actual future capacity will likely depend heavily on market and policy signals. Use would
  require new vessel builds or major retrofits – potentially better aligned with the more flexible Canadian fleet.
• Ethanol also represents a promising long-term pathway due to large regional production. Use would require new
  vessel builds or major retrofits – potentially better aligned with the more flexible Canadian fleet.
• Permitting and safety/spill protocols are major considerations for the addition of any new fuel or infrastructure,
  representing a key challenge to alternative fuel introduction in the Great Lakes.
