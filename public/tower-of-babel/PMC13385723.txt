Integrated eco-physiological and anatomical responses of Capparis decidua to salinity and water deficit stress
Authors: Ayesha Kafeel, Rameez Hussain, Ahlam Khalofah, Suad AL-Ruzayza, Aamir Ali, Naima Huma Naveed, Toqeer Abbas, Rubina Abbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe eco-physiological and structural flexibility of Capparis decidua at specific locations along an environmental gradient in the Sargodha region of Pakistan is examined in this work. C. decidua is a xerophyte that thrives in extremely dry and semi-arid environments. Shahpur exhibited the highest electrical conductivity and ionic concentrations (Na⁺ = 29.5 mg kg⁻¹; Cl⁻ = 23.07 mg kg⁻¹) indicating strong salinity stress. The most organic matter (0.83%) was found in Chak 48 NB, which indicates a less harsh environment. There were notable phenotypic differences (p < 0.001) with Shahpur exhibiting greater plant height (139.13 cm), canopy area (3.04 m²), and root length (46.17 cm) associated with saline and drought-prone conditions. Anatomical traits included thicker epidermis (29.5 μm), increased sclerenchyma that are connected to structural elements relating to water conservation. Strong photoprotective mechanisms were suggested by the Shahpur population’s stable chlorophyll levels and elevated carotenoid content. PCA biplots showed clear separation of Shahpur from Chak 48 NB and Chak 89 NB along PC1, delineating the Shahpur population and directly correlating it with stress-adaptive anatomical suites. These results suggested that a number of morphological, anatomical, and physiological adaptation features provide a specialized approach to improve drought and salinity tolerance. Introduction

Pakistan has many different ecological zones that are home to a wide variety of therapeutic plant species of which there are 6000 plant species that can be used in bio-prospecting. One of the medicinally and nutritionally significant wild plant species is Capparis decidua1. Pakistan is a subtropical country with a wide range of topographic variation, from arid coastal zones in the south to alpine highlands in the north. It extends over an area of 79.6 million hectares between 61–75ºE and 24–37º N. The country is mostly arid with 40.9 hectares lying in arid zones including 11 million hectares of desert land. The major deserts include Cholistan, Thal, Thar and Chagi-Kharan2.

Capparis decidua also known as karir is a densely branched xerophytic shrub growing in dry regions of Pakistan3. C. decidua is common in arid regions of the Middle East, Africa, parts of South Asia especially the deserts of South Punjab in Pakistan. This plant is well suited to desert climates and is extremely drought tolerant and are commonly found under mild winter conditions and freely develop root suckers4. C. decidua is a deciduous xerophytic small tree or large shrub that grows to a height of four to five meters. It produces pink flowers in tiny clusters along the leafless stalks which form corymbs. Fruit matures in May and October, while the vivid red blooms bloom every two years, usually from March to April and August to September. It can withstand moderate frost and is highly drought-resistant5. The plant can tolerate salty and drought conditions in dry places, and it can withstand high temperatures6. The fruit is a tiny, cherry-sized berry that is globose to ovoid in shape that turns black upon drying. The size of leaf is tiny and the life cycle is short on young branches Additionally, the plant has a near-aphyllous habit, meaning that leaves only momentarily develop on immature stems7. It can easily thrive in desert circumstances which include water scarcity, salinity and low-nutrient land conditions and temperature between − 8 and + 48 ◦C or more. Soil ecological conditions include dryness, salinity, and insufficient nutrient levels. Generally, kair is naturally propagated via seeds, root suckers, hardwood cuttings, and tissue culture, even though plant survival is extremely low8.

There are over 250 species in Capparis, one of the 28–39 genera that make up the Capparidaceae family4. Traditionally, this plant was used to treat variety of diseases. It has been shown that certain plant parts exhibit pharmacological qualities including hepatoprotective, antibacterial, anti-hyperlipidemic, antihypertensive, and antidiabetic effects9. As C. decidua is a desert plant that produces a lot of volatile essential oils, sterols are necessary for both its medicinal potential and drought resistance4. C. decidua is ecologically important because it reduces soil erosion and desertification in arid and semi-arid areas by increasing soil quality and mediating ecological succession6.

Drought, salinity, severe temperature and interrelated aspects of oxidative stress change the water interactions of the plant at the cellular and whole plant levels resulting in both specific and unspecific reactions. This causes a series of morphological, physiological, biochemical and molecular alterations that negatively impact plant development and production10.

Their morphological and physiological characteristics such as deep root systems and thicker cuticles are examples of xeromorphic characteristics that allow C. decidua to survive in drought, salt, and nutrient-deficient environments. These plants maintain biodiversity by providing food and habitat for numerous organisms. They are also associated with carbon sequestration even in difficult conditions11. The main aim is to evaluate C. decidua’s morphological, anatomical, and physiological adaptability to the many environmental stresses of the Sargodha region, this study seeks to clarify the eco-physiological strategies of the species. The main objective is to assess its morphological, anatomical and physiological changes in response to drought, salinity, and temperature stress and their association with survival and ecological roles in response to these diverse environments. Materials and methods

Study sites

Three locations are uncultivated sandy, cultivated loamy, and saline agricultural, representing a heterogeneous environmental gradient were chosen in the vicinity of Sargodha region. Chak no 89 NB is an uncultivated area having sandy soil while Chak no 48 NB is a cultivated area having loamy soil and Shahpur is an agricultural land.

Three mature, independent C. decidua individuals were chosen with three replicates at each study site. In order to ensure independent sampling and reduce bias, the chosen plants were geographically isolated within each site. For morphological, anatomical, physiological, and biochemical studies, individual plant samples from each replication were gathered in labeled plastic bags.

Soil samples were collected using soil augur at a depth of 0 to 30 cm. After being cleaned and blotted dry, plant samples were either processed right away for physiological tests or oven-dried at 70 °C to a consistent weight for biochemical analysis. Soil and Water samples were analyzed at soil and water laboratory, University of Sargodha (Table 1).

Morphological parameters

The samples collected were washed using distilled water to eliminate dust particles and were dried at room temperature under shade till completely dry.

Plant fresh weight (g)

Plant fresh weight was taken by using weight balance machine.

Plant dry weight (g)

The dry weight of plants was determined with the samples stored in an oven at 65 °C±2 °C. Plant dry weight was recorded using digital weight balance.

Shoot length

The length of shoot was measured using measuring tape.

Root length

Measurements of root length, which are given in centimeters (cm), were taken from excavated root sections close to the plant base.

Root diameter

Measuring tape was used to measure the diameter of root.

Anatomical parameters

Samples of roots and stem were collected and washed with distilled water. For long term storage samples were transferred to ethanol solution 25% and 75% ethanol. Transverse sections were prepared using a sharp razor blade. The section was dehydrated by using a series of ethanol 30%, 50%, 70%, 90% and 100%. A double staining technique was used using safranin to stain lignified tissues Parenchymatous tissues were stained using fast-green. Stained sections were cleared using xylene and fixed in the slides using Canada balsam and covered with cover slips. Slides were examined under compound light microscope. Determination of total phenolic content

The total phenolic contents were assayed via the method of Singleton and Rossi (1965). A total of 0.2 g of sample was extracted on an orbital shaker at 200 rpm with 2 mL of 80% methanol containing 1% HCl. Afterwards, the mixture was centrifuged at 1000 × g for fifteen minutes. The supernatant was separated, and the pellets were extracted again via the same procedure. The supernatant was obtained again and was used with a previous one for assaying phenolics. One hundred µl of the extract was mixed with 0.75 mL of Folin Ciocalteu reagent and incubated for 5 min at 22 °C, followed by the addition of 0.75 mL of sodium bicarbonate (60 g/L) solution, and the mixture was incubated again for 90 min. The absorbance was read at 765 nm, and the concentrations were expressed as gallic acid equivalents.

Units and data expression

All measurements were expressed using standardized SI units throughout the manuscript. Soil and metal concentrations were reported as mg kg⁻¹, biochemical parameters as mg g⁻¹ fresh weight (f. wt.), and anatomical measurements as µm². Physiological traits

The chlorophyll data depicts differences in photosynthetic pigments such as chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid levels across populations growing at Chak 48 NB, Chak 89 NB, and Shahpur. Chlorophyll b and total chlorophyll concentration were significantly higher in Chak 89 NB, indicating improved photosynthetic efficiency under quite moderate environmental circumstances. In contrast, Shahpur plants showed increased carotenoid accumulation, indicating that photoprotective and antioxidant defence systems were activated in response to high salinity and drought stress. Chlorophyll a levels varied only slightly between sites, indicating that primary photosynthetic activity is relatively stable. The higher carotenoid concentration in Shahpur shows an adaptive physiological response for protection against oxidative damage and excessive light exposure in xeric conditions (Fig. 4b).

The sites under investigation (Chak 48 NB, 89 NB, and Shahpur) had different physiological characteristics. While total chlorophyll and chlorophyll b were highest at 89 NB and lowest in Shahpur, chlorophyll a showed no change. Shahpur, on the other hand, had the highest carotenoid content. These variations show how photosynthetic pigment accumulation varies geographically among populations (Fig. 4b).

PCA morphological traits

Principal component analysis (PCA) of morphological traits

PC1 and PC2 explained 63.1% and 35.6% of the overall variation, respectively, with a cumulative variability of 98.7%, according to PCA of morphological features. While shoot fresh weight was favorably correlated with PC2, plant height, canopy area, root length, root breadth were all highly positively correlated with PC1. On the other hand, PC1 was negatively correlated with root fresh and dry weights. While Chak 48 NB and Chak 89 NB formed distinct groups, showing variance in physical features among the research sites, the PCA biplot clearly separated Shahpur along PC1 (Fig. 7a).

(a-b) PCA biplot of Morphological traits (a) and Stem anatomy (b) of C. decidua.

PCA stem anatomical traits

The sampled populations showed significant anatomical variance, according to PCA. PC2 explained 13.18% of the overall variation, whereas PC1 explained 86.78%. Phloem, sclerenchyma, endodermis, cortex, and epidermis all made significant contributions to population differentiation. Sclerenchyma and metaxylem contributed positively along PC2, but the epidermis had a negative correlation. The PCA biplot showed that populations from various research locations were clearly separated (Fig. 7b).

PCA root anatomical traits

For root anatomical traits, PC1 and PC2 explained 86.6% and 13.3% of the total variation, respectively. Cortex, metaxylem, sclerenchyma, phloem, and pith were closely grouped, indicating strong positive relationships among these tissues. Epidermis showed weak positive association, whereas endodermis was negatively associated along PC2. The PCA also demonstrated distinct separation of Shahpur from other sites, reflecting enhanced root structural variation under saline and drought-prone conditions (Fig. 8a).

(a-b) PCA biplot of root anatomy (a) and leaf physiology (b) of C. decidua.

PCA physiological traits

Physiological traits of C. decidua shows that PC1 explains 78.2% and PC2 explains 19.2%, together covering 97.4% of variation. Total chlorophyll and carotenoids lie on the positive side of PC1 (around + 1.5), indicating a strong positive correlation. Chlorophyll a is associated with the positive side of PC2 (about + 2.5), while chlorophyll b is linked to negative PC1 (about − 1.5) and PC2 (about − 1.2) (Fig. 8b).

PCA total flavonoid content

For flavonoid content, PC1 explained 69.4% and PC2 explained 30.5% of the total variation. Fruits and shoots clustered closely together, indicating similar flavonoid accumulation patterns, while flowers and roots were positively associated along PC2. The PCA reflects tissue-specific variation in flavonoid accumulation among plant organs.

PCA of phenolic content

The phenolic content PCA revealed that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 67.8% and 32.2% of the overall variation, respectively, with close positive associations for flowers and fruits, and negative associations for roots and shoots. These patterns suggest that phenolic accumulation varies by organ in various habitats.

Root anatomical traits

The correlation matrix of root traits of C. decidua shows that most anatomical traits are strongly and positively correlated. Cortex, metaxylem, phloem, and pith exhibit very high positive correlations (close to 1.00), indicating that they vary together in a similar trend. Epidermis is also positively correlated with these traits but at slightly lower values (0.74–0.91). In contrast, endodermis shows weak to negative correlations with several traits (− 0.50 to − 0.88), highlighting its distinct and opposite pattern of association compared to other root traits. Thus, cortex, xylem, phloem, and pith are strongly related, while endodermis has the weakest correlations (Fig. 12b).

Discussion

Soil studies

The present study revealed considerable difference in soil physicochemical properties. The soil was slightly alkaline, which normally promotes plant growth but, in saline conditions, may reduce the availability of essential micronutrients through nutrient sequestration. Electrical conductivity was highest at Shahpur while other sites exhibited moderate levels. Organic matter indicated moderate fertility. Potassium was limited except at site 3 and phosphorous was limited. The soils were moderately fertile and due to salinity have limited nutrient availability. Same results were observed by12,13. The increased soil pH may be due to decreased level of organic matter14 leading to reduced nutrient content. Soil phosphorous is low due to calcareous nature of soil and alkaline nature further reduces its availability. Reduced potassium availability was observed in Pakistan15.

Water studies

Shahpur has highest salinity and ionic concentration with increased EC, Na⁺, and Cl⁻ that exceeded by previous values reported16,17. Chak 89 NB has moderate salinity and mineral enrichment. Chak 48 has lower EC but an increased RSC value. Concentration of Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ were higher than recorded by13. SAR remained within the acceptable limits at 48 and 89NB and higher at Shahpur (Masood et al., 202318;. Salinity increases Na⁺/K⁺ and Na⁺/Ca²⁺ in soils that restrict the uptake of essential nutrients, destabilize membrane instability and inhibit enzymatic functioning19. Excess carbonates precipitate Mg+ and Ca+ that enhance sodium dominance in soil solution.EC and SAR are the indicators for assessing salt affected soil20. High RSC accumulates sodium lowering water quality21. “Plants growing under relatively higher salinity and lower water availability tended to exhibit reduced height.exhibited shorter height and smaller canopy area.”22,23. Current findings revealed deeper and wider roots, which were associated with sites having higher salinity and reduced water availability.”24. Site 3’s leaves were somewhat larger; this could be an opportunistic growth strategy during short bursts of moisture availability that enhance photosynthetic efficiency. The root stem biomass in the current study were found to be higher than in earlier studies25. Compared to previous research, the observed increase in root length and biomass under high salinity may be explained by the localized nutritional composition of the soil and the plants’ innate ability to adapt to stressful situations.

Studies showed that root biomass decreased and stem biomass increased as plant size increased. Eziz and Fang26,. Leaf structural changes may be associated with cell enlargement and increased cell number. Plant height varied across sites with different salinity levels and CO2 increased cell area. Increased succulence may enhance leaf longevity and larger leaf area was mainly by an increase in cell number27. Root traits such as length and biomass were found to be highest under high saline soils28.

Photosynthetic pigments

C. decidua exhibited variation in the total chlorophyll content. Chlorophyll a decreased as compared to previous studies. Reduction may be due to environmental factors, while chlorophyll b was elevated at Chak 48 and 89 NB but lower at Shahpur. Carotenoid content increased across all locations preventing photodamage under heat conditions. Same results were demonstrated by29,30 as they demonstrated C. decidua and other desert species have similarly shown increased carotenoid accumulation under stress, where carotenoids shield photosynthetic machinery from oxidative and photooxidative damage. Similar patterns have been observed in xerophytic and halophytic species, where chlorophyll b either stays relatively stable or increases to maintain light harvesting efficiency while chlorophyll a decreases under environmental stress due to chloroplast damage and inhibition of chlorophyll biosynthesis. Decreased level of Chl a and total Chlorophyll is due to combined effect of salinity and drought with Chlorophyll b remained stable31. The reduction is due to damage of chloroplast and inhibited biosynthesis32.

Root

In comparison with earlier reports C. decidua showed similarities with xerophytic species. Epidermis thickness was similar to the previous research and higher at some sites. Phloem was more advanced than earlier studies and has ability to transport assimilate. Pith was not developed as compared to previous studies showed structural variation among sites. Increased growth of lignified and supporting tissues may be linked to the somewhat smaller pith area seen at some locations. This could support structural stability and stress tolerance in arid environments. Parenchyma and collenchyma were thicker than other xerophytic plants which could be connected to mechanical assistance.

Xylem were less developed than other species, strongest at 48NB lower value at Shahpur. Vascular bundle was similar to the previous research but moderately reduced at Shahpur (Akram et al., 2022; IQBAL et al., 2021;22.Stem epidermis increased with the increase in salinity but decreased under high salinity. Sclerenchyma thickness decreased slightly under saline soils but become thicker under highly saline soils. Vascular bundle area becomes expanded under high salinity but phloem and pith increased for low to highly saline sites. Reduced water loss and preservation of physiological activity may be linked to these structural characteristics (Akram et al., 2022).

Stem

Epidermal thickness was highest at 48NB but lower at 89NB and Shahpur. Increased sclerenchyma across all sites which may be associated with mechanical support. Reduced phloem and cortex thickness associated with differences in nutrient uptake and water transport. Metaxylem area showed site specific differences with values increasing from 89NB to 48NB. Pith area showed variations depending on different sites showing modifications for storage and conduction of water. In the least saline soil, the stem had the smallest cross-sectional area, indicating a relationship between secondary growth and salinity. Similar results were reported by Akram et al., 202228,33,. Plants showed larger cortex regions and thicker epidermis in hypersaline soils34. In moderately saline areas, larger vascular bundles that may be associated with hydraulic conductivity (Akram et al., 2022). Phloem tissues were well developed in hyper saline soils increasing nutrient distribution under stress and in least saline environment had the smallest phloem and pith regions. Smaller epidermal cells were observed during extreme salinity which may be associated with reduced water loss. Sclerenchyma was thickest under moderate stress strengthening root structure. Vascular bundles were more developed under extreme salinity associated with long-distance transport33.

Total flavonoid content

Flavonoid contents were higher in Chak 48 and Shahpur and 89NB has lower flavonoid content in show suggesting that climatic factors influence physiological processes. Flowers accumulate more flavonoids in Chak 48NB as compared to other two sites whereas previous studies reported different trends. Root tissues illustrate higher flavonoid content in Chak 48 and 89NB, results are similar to earlier studies35. Hazrati et al.36, demonstrated that flavonoid content is more in ripe fruits while lower in flower and fruiting stages. In roots flavonoid accumulation was stronger whereas in previous studies shoots showed moderate associations. The inhabitants of Shahpur showed the greatest accumulation of flavonoids. Previous studies were conducted under less stressful conditions on Capparis spinosa showed that flavonoids spread equally among plant parts. The total flavonoid concentration at Shahpur demonstrated an intriguing organ-specific divergence, with flowers exhibiting the lowest values across all locations and shoots exhibiting the largest accumulation. This divergent reaction could be the result of distinct metabolic resource allocation in extreme salinity and drought stress situations. Vegetative tissues, like shoots, may increase flavonoid biosynthesis in extremely stressful conditions to shield photosynthetic structures from oxidative damage, ionic toxicity, and excessive sunlight. On the other hand, when plant resources are preferentially directed toward survival, osmotic adjustment, and maintenance of vegetative growth rather than floral metabolism, reproductive organs may exhibit decreased flavonoid accumulation.

With protective secondary metabolites preferentially accumulating in actively exposed vegetative tissues at Shahpur, this tissue-specific variation implies that C. decidua employs different adaptive strategies in various organs under harsh environmental conditions. Several xerophytic and halophytic species have been shown to exhibit similar stress-induced increase of flavonoid production and organ-specific metabolic regulation under salinity and drought circumstances37,38.

Total phenolic content

In fruits flavonoid accumulation varied between locations indicating soil characteristics, nutrient availability and salinity play an important role in synthesis of secondary metabolites. Flower exhibited higher flavonoid in Chak 89 and 48NB as compared to Shahpur suggesting an adaptive role in response to environmental stresses such as high temperature fluctuations. Shahpur had a greater content of flavonoid in Shahpur. Site specific variation observed in roots indicating the impact of environment and function of roots as storage organs35,39–42.

Conclusion

The study of C. decidua exhibited Eco-physiological responses in three different areas of Sargodha region that were associated with different environmental conditions such as high salinity, drought conditions and changing nutrient content. Morphologically, under harsh environments plants exhibited comparatively longer and thicker roots under stressful conditions.and larger leaves, root to shoot ratios, “observed under low water availability conditions” and high salinity and were associated with differences in water and nutrient relations”. Physiologically Chl content is regulated in response to different stress conditions under high salinity, temperature and drought conditions. Reduction in levels of Chl a while Chl b and carotenoid increasing, recommending that photoprotective mechanisms be activated in order to reduce oxidative stress and maintain photosynthetic pathways. Anatomically both roots and stems undergo structural adaptations including thickened endodermis, modified cortex and pith and changes in phloem, xylem, parenchyma and sclerenchyma that were associated with ionic uptake, water transport, and structural support under adverse conditions. The observed differences in root and stem anatomy, photosynthetic pigments, and secondary metabolite accumulation among sites suggest that C. decidua possesses substantial eco-physiological flexibility in response to varying environmental conditions. “These traits were observed in plants growing under moderate and extreme environmental conditions.” and maintain essential metabolic functions highlighting its resilience in arid and semi-arid conditions. The environmental characterization of the study sites was primarily based on soil and water physicochemical properties. Climatic variables such as temperature, rainfall, and relative humidity were not directly measured and may also influence plant eco-physiological responses. Therefore, the observed variations among sites should mainly be interpreted in relation to edaphic and salinity-related environmental differences.

Future recommendations

Present study highlight more detailed work is needed in future to better understand the Eco-physiological adaptations of C. decidua. Current study demonstrates clear differences in root and stem anatomy, photosynthetic pigments, osmolyte synthesis and antioxidant activity across different locations. There is a need of more detailed work to perform both in field and experimental work that expose plant to different stress factors such as salinity, drought conditions and high temperature. Long term observations in field help us to understand how seasonal and climatic changes affect adaptive traits of plants. Comparative analysis with other xerophytic species will be carried out to clarify how C. decidua depends on adaptive strategies or has advanced modifications. Traditional methods are important including germplasm preservation and seed banking to ensure long term survival of this plant species. Future work that includes physiological, anatomical, biochemical, molecular and ecological perspective will provide framework how plant adapt to different conditions.
