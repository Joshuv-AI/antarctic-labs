Morphology-Controlled Growth of Silver Dendrites and Flowers on Gold Films Irradiated by Femtosecond Laser: Roles of Ascorbic Acid and Sodium Citrate and Their SERS Performance
Authors: Naushad Ahmad Khan, Risandi Try Ananda, Yi-Han Kou, Yun-Cheng Ku, Kuo-Yung Hung, Ming-Tsung Sun, Jiunn-Woei Liaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid and controlled fabrication of silver microstructures with a three-dimensional (3D) hierarchical morphology is crucial for enhancing the performance of surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) substrates. In this study, we utilized a femtosecond (fs) laser-assisted photochemical approach to synthesize hierarchical silver dendrites and flowers on gold films in two distinct chemical solutions. Silver dendrites were synthesized from aqueous silver nitrate (AgNO3) solutions in the presence of ascorbic acid, whereas silver flowers were formed by adding both ascorbic acid and sodium citrate. Ascorbic acid acts as a primary reducing agent to initiate the reduction of silver ions (Ag+), whereas sodium citrate functions as both a stabilizing ligand and a capping agent, promoting anisotropic platelike growth by inhibiting growth in the thickness direction. When femtosecond laser pulses were tightly focused on the gold-film-coated glass, localized photochemical reduction of silver ions occurred. In this process, ascorbic acid serves as the primary reducing agent, while laser irradiation provides localized energy input that enhances reduction kinetics, promotes silver cluster formation, and enables the assembly of complex hierarchical 3D silver microstructures within minutes. The resulting dendritic and flower-like hierarchical structures, characterized by sharp tips, edges, and petal- or branch-like gaps, possess a high surface-to-volume ratio and numerous electromagnetic hotspots, making them highly effective for SERS applications. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirm their morphology and composition. SERS measurements using Rhodamine 6G as a probe molecule reveal significantly enhanced Raman signals compared to those of bare gold substrates. This mask-free, room-temperature method offers a fast and scalable strategy for fabricating high-performance SERS substrates with potential applications in chemical and biosensing.

Introduction

Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) has emerged as a powerful analytical technique for ultrasensitive molecular detection, with applications spanning chemical analysis, biosensing, medical diagnostics, and environmental monitoring. , Central to the success of SERS is the development of substrates that offer intense electromagnetic field enhancement, particularly through nanoscale features that generate localized “hotspots.” , Through various previous studies, it has been established that these “hotspots” arise from the highly branched structures that provide nanoscale gaps or sharp edges and tips. Among noble metals, silver is widely regarded as the most efficient plasmonic material for SERS due to its strong localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) in the visible light range. Silver metal also shows the ability to form highly branched, rough morphologies such as three-dimensional (3D) dendrites and flowers, thereby providing a large number of hotspots for SERS enhancements. −

The synthesis of such branched silver nanostructures is a tedious process traditionally involving a time-taking wet-chemical process or focused ion beam (FIB)-based nanostructure patterning. ,,,, While wet-chemical synthesis involves the reduction of silver nitrate using ascorbic acid or sodium citrate for the synthesis of silver nano/microstructures often requiring elevated temperature or use of potential differences or longer reaction times to enhance the reduction of silver ions to silver atoms, FIB milling offers precise control but is very costly and time-intensive. Flower-like and dendritic silver structures have been widely reported through purely wet-chemical reduction methods, particularly using ascorbic acid, where morphology is governed by diffusion-limited growth and reaction kinetics, as demonstrated by Fukuyo and Imai.

To address these challenges, laser-assisted fabrication techniques have attracted increasing attention for their capability to drive site-specific, mask-free nanostructure growth with high spatial precision. − Continuous-wave (CW) lasers have been shown to facilitate the reduction of silver ions on gold nanostructures via hot-electron ejection. , In comparison, femtosecond (fs) lasers offer notable advantages due to their ultrashort pulse durations, which enable nonlinear two-photon excitation, minimize thermal diffusion, and facilitate the excitation of hot electrons from the gold substrate, playing a crucial role in synthesizing silver structures through the photoreduction of silver nitrate. − Under fs laser excitation, localized energy is deposited at the gold film–solution interface through nonlinear optical processes. This localized excitation enhances the rate of chemically driven reduction of Ag+ ions by ascorbic acid and promotes nucleation at spatially confined regions. ,, In this system, ascorbic acid acts as the primary reducing agent, while laser irradiation serves to accelerate reaction kinetics and influence growth localization. Although energetic carrier generation may occur under such conditions, its direct role in silver-ion reduction is not conclusively established in the present study. In the absence of citrate, growth proceeds under diffusion-limited conditions, where silver atoms deposit preferentially at tips and high-curvature regions, resulting in dendritic, fractal-like architectures. , In contrast, when sodium citrate is present, it serves as both a mild stabilizer and a facet-selective capping agent, binding preferentially to low-energy {111} planes and thereby directing anisotropic growth. This facet stabilization, coupled with oriented attachment of silver or gold nanocrystals, facilitates the outward radial extension of branches that evolve into densely packed, petal-like protrusions.

In addition, light-driven self-assembly with oriented attachment of plasmonic nanoparticles, such as gold nanorods, has been reported both experimentally and theoretically. , Such morphology control parallels earlier reports demonstrating that crystallization routes can be steered from dendrites to flower-like structures by tuning growth kinetics and capping chemistry and is further consistent with studies showing reversible switching between dendritic and flower-like silver architectures via modulation of reaction kinetics. , Collectively, the photoreduction of silver ions, light-induced self-assembly, and the capping abilities of citrate give rise to the complex hierarchical microsized 3D silver dendrites or flowers, which have great potential as SERS substrates.

In our study, 3D hierarchical silver structures, specifically dendrites and flowers, were synthesized by focusing an fs laser on a gold film. The synthesis used either ascorbic acid alone or a combination of ascorbic acid and sodium citrate as reducing agents to reduce silver ions on a gold film-coated glass substrate within an inverted microscope. Femtosecond laser irradiation enhances local reaction kinetics and promotes rapid nucleation and growth. Furthermore, these silver clusters undergo rapid hierarchical growth for the synthesis of silver dendrites. When sodium citrate is introduced in the reduction solution, this rapid growth is inhibited into two-dimensional (2D) silver plates which undergo self-assembly under light-induced optical force to form 3D hierarchical silver flowers. Figure shows the inverted microscope setup for two-photon fs laser irradiation and the corresponding FE-SEM images of the silver structures obtained with different reducing agents. Newly formed silver clusters attach to the tips of silver dendrite branches, that is, local hotspots, during fs laser irradiation, leading to the growth of hierarchical 3D dendritic structures. In contrast, when sodium citrate as a capping agent is introduced, the newly reduced silver clusters attach only to the edges of planar 2D petals and the light-driven self-assembly of these petals gives rise to complex 3D silver flowers without any temperature elevation.

Two-photon excitation at the gold film–solution interface provides localized energy input that facilitates photochemical reduction of Ag+ to Ag0 in the presence of ascorbic acid. Experiments

In this study, two types of silver structures were synthesized on a gold-coated glass substrate by irradiating two different solutions with focused femtosecond laser pulses. Driven by the two-photon effect of the fs laser, hot electrons may be generated from the gold film to facilitate the reduction of silver nitrate, producing hierarchical silver dendrites in the presence of ascorbic acid alone and silver flowers when sodium citrate was added (Figure ).

Gold Film Coating

Silica coverslips with a diameter of 10 mm were coated with a 10-nm-thick gold film using a Hitachi E-1010 ion sputtering machine. These gold-coated substrates were used without further treatment for silver dendrite or flower growth under femtosecond laser irradiation in the presence of the reduction solution. FE-SEM images of the gold film are shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). The uneven gold surface may generate local field enhancement sites during fs laser irradiation, thereby promoting localized energy deposition and enhancing reaction kinetics at the gold–solution interface.

Reduction Solution Preparation

The reduction solution contains the aqueous solution of silver nitrate and ascorbic acid as a reducing agent. Here, a 5 mM aqueous solution of silver nitrate (AgNO3) is prepared and kept in the dark in a 50 mL centrifuge tube, while another 20 mM aqueous solution of ascorbic acid is prepared in another 10 mL centrifuge tube along with a 4 mM solution of sodium citrate. Reduction solution is prepared by adding 4 μL of freshly prepared silver nitrate, 4 μL ascorbic acid, and/or 4 μL of sodium citrate in an Eppendorf containing 100 μL of dd water. For silver dendrite and flower growth synthesis, we used 50 μL of these as-prepared reduction for fs laser irradiation. All of the chemicals were bought from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received without further treatment. A few control experiments were conducted, including the synthesis of silver structures without any reducing agent (Figure S2, Supporting Information) and the synthesis by chemical reduction only (Figure S3, Supporting Information).

Fs Laser Irradiation

The light irradiation of the fs pulse laser was carried out using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM) (Zeiss LSM 510 meta, Germany) which is an inverted type of confocal microscope. We utilized a Ti:Sapphire fs laser with a wavelength of 800 nm (Mai Tai, Spectra Physics) for the irradiation process. The fs laser operated at a repetition rate of 80 MHz and was set to 0.35% power, equivalent to 10 mW for the irradiation. We employed a 20× magnification objective lens for the irradiation process and focused on the interface between the gold film and the reduction solution. Fs laser irradiation was performed using a 2D point scan within a 450 × 450 μm2 area with a pixel size of 2048 × 2048. The pixel dwell time for laser irradiation for each point was 3.2 μs. A total of 4 areas were irradiated as shown in Figure .

(a) Two-photon fs laser irradiation setup within an inverted laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM). The laser beam is directed upward from the bottom and focused precisely at the interface between the thin gold film and the reduction solution. (b) Schematic of the scanned area showing four irradiation squares (450 × 450 μm2 each) used for the synthesis of silver dendrites and flowers.

FE-SEM Characterization

A JEOL 7610 field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used for the characterization.

SERS Substrate Preparation

After the experiment for the synthesis of silver dendrites or flowers is completed, the sample is dried overnight and then dipped in Rhodamine 6G concentrations ranging from 10–6 M to 10–8 M and then dried and used for SERS measurement.

SERS Measurement

SERS measurements were conducted using the LabRAM HR Evolution system at room temperature, employing a 633 nm He–Ne laser as the excitation source. A 100× objective lens was used to focus the laser beam on a spot approximately 2 μm in diameter. To prevent interference from ambient light, the Raman spectroscopy was performed in darkness. The excitation laser power was maintained below 5 mW, and the integration time was set between 5 and 10 s. Raman signals were recorded at three different locations and subsequently averaged.

Results and Discussion

Silver Dendrites

In this study, a two-photon femtosecond pulsed laser integrated into an inverted confocal microscope was employed to drive the photochemical reduction of silver nitrate in the presence of ascorbic acid, resulting in the formation of hierarchical silver dendrites on a thin gold film substrate. We hypothesize that intense, localized excitation from the focused femtosecond laser enhances local reaction kinetics at the gold-solution interface, thereby accelerating chemically driven reduction of Ag+ ions and promoting spatially confined growth. This mechanism may explain the direct, template-free, and single-step synthesis of hierarchical silver dendrites on the substrate. Figure shows the representative FE-SEM images of the as-synthesized hierarchical silver dendrites along with the corresponding EDS elemental mapping, confirming the coexistence and spatial distribution of silver and gold, validating successful photochemical deposition.

(a) FE-SEM images show hierarchical silver dendrites and (b) a magnified view of a single dendrite synthesized on a gold-coated glass substrate with a solution of silver nitrate and ascorbic acid dropped on the glass irradiated by an upward fs laser. (c–e) EDS analyses confirm that the dendrites are composed of silver with the presence of gold. Scale bar in (a) is 10 μm, (b) 1 μm, and (c–e) 2.5 μm, respectively.

Figure (a, b) shows the number of silver dendrites and a single dendrite synthesized on the top side of a gold film coated on a glass substrate. The reduction of silver nitrate in the presence of ascorbic acid and assistance from a two-photon fs laser involves several sequential steps in the synthesis of silver dendrites. First, a small nucleation occurs in the near field of the gold film by the chemical reduction of silver ions into silver atoms. These clusters anchor strongly to the gold substrate via Au–Ag interfacial interactions i.e., “pinning effect” and subsequently undergo directional growth, resulting in hierarchical dendritic structures. Figure a shows the result of fast fabrication of silver dendrite with fs laser-assisted reduction of silver ions using ascorbic acid as a reducing agent. Figure b shows details of a single silver dendrite showing its hierarchical structure of branches and sub-branches, making it an excellent substrate for SERS enhancements. Figure (c–e) EDS mapping confirms that the structures are composed of silver with the presence of gold. During this process, newly reduced silver clusters attach to the tips of silver dendrite branches, serving as local hotspots, through light-driven assembly, thereby promoting the growth of 3D dendrite-like structures. Without irradiation from the focused fs laser, no dendritic structures are observed (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Although limited chemical reduction of silver ions occurs, the resulting deposits lack any hierarchical morphology. This observation highlights the essential role of fs laser excitation in driving both rapid reduction and anisotropic growth. The laser provides localized energy input that enhances reaction kinetics and promotes anisotropic growth through spatially confined nucleation and field-assisted assembly. These near-field plasmonic effects promote oriented attachment by directing newly reduced silver atoms toward high-field regions at the branch tips. As this process continues, successive deposition leads to the formation of three-dimensional, dendritic architectures, as clearly evidenced in Figure . Ascorbic acid serves as the primary reducing agent, initiating the reduction of silver ions and promoting the formation of initial silver nuclei on the gold surface. The subsequent irradiation by the fs laser enhances this process by further enhancing local reaction kinetics and promoting continued growth of the dendritic structures, which accelerate the reduction of remaining Ag+ ions and facilitate their deposition onto growing silver structures, ultimately yielding well-defined micrometer-sized dendritic architectures. In the absence of ascorbic acid or any reducing agent, the reduction of silver ions is negligible, resulting only in sparse, irregular silver clusters rather than dendritic structures, as shown in Figure S2 (Supporting Information). These observations indicate that while ascorbic acid governs the reduction process, fs laser irradiation plays a key role in enhancing nucleation kinetics and enabling the formation of hierarchical dendritic structures.

The observed dendritic morphologies are consistent with previously reported silver structures obtained via ascorbic acid reduction, as demonstrated by Fukuyo and Imai, where similar dendritic architectures were formed under diffusion-limited growth conditions. This comparison indicates that the fundamental growth mechanism in the present system follows the established chemically driven pathways. In this context, femtosecond laser irradiation does not fundamentally alter the intrinsic growth mechanism but instead enhances nucleation kinetics and spatial localization on the substrate.

Silver Flowers

In this section, we demonstrate the synthesis of hierarchical silver flowers via the two-photon fs laser-induced photoreduction of silver nitrate, using ascorbic acid as a reducing agent and a small amount of sodium citrate as a capping agent to facilitate reduction and control the morphology of the resulting silver flowers. The sodium citrate is a well-known capping agent and is used in “Turkevich” method for gold nanoparticles synthesis. , Here, the capping properties of sodium citrate are exploited for the synthesis of hierarchical silver flowers in the near field of gold film. The addition of sodium citrate as a capping agent in the reduction solution opens a whole new area of research in which thin petals of silver flowers are synthesized and can be used as a SERS substrate for the detection of Rhodamine 6G molecules.

Figure shows the formation of a substantial number of hierarchical silver flowers synthesized via the photoreduction of silver nitrate in the presence of ascorbic acid and sodium citrate as the reducing and capping agents, respectively, under two-photon fs laser irradiation. Compared to the synthesis of silver dendrites, the key distinction lies in the addition of sodium citrate, which plays a critical role in controlling the crystal growth pathway. Sodium citrate selectively adsorbs onto specific crystallographic facets of silver nuclei, effectively inhibiting growth along the thickness direction while promoting lateral expansion to form 2D petal-like structures. Under continued laser irradiation, these thin petals self-assemble into 3D flower-like architectures. The light plays a dual role, i.e., enhancing the rate of chemically driven reduction of silver ions through localized energy output and providing a driving force in the self-assembly of these silver flowers. These clusters subsequently interact through dipole–dipole coupling and near-field plasmonic confinement, leading to optical binding and aggregation at specific energetically favorable sites. , Simultaneously, the localized photothermal gradient contributes to thermophoretic motion, aiding mass transport of silver species toward the illuminated region along with the capping properties of citrate, only allowing certain preferential growth of these clusters. , Therefore, the self-assembly driven by light energy along with the capping property of citrate gives rise to silver flowers with a 3D structure. , However, the exact synthesis mechanism of these complex 3D silver flowers is still uncertain and requires further research. These silver flowers act as an excellent substrate for the SERS enhancement of Rhodamine 6G probe molecules. Several studies have reported methods for producing 3D hierarchical silver dendrites ,,, or silver flowers ,,,, separately.

In addition, similar flower-like silver morphologies have been reported through purely chemical reduction processes, particularly using ascorbic acid–based systems, as demonstrated by Fukuyo and Imai. This indicates that such structures can arise from diffusion-limited growth and facet-controlled mechanisms that are independent of laser irradiation. In the present work, the role of fs laser irradiation is not to alter the intrinsic growth pathway but to enhance nucleation kinetics and enable spatially localized growth, while the addition of sodium citrate governs the formation of flower-like architectures through facet-selective capping.

In contrast, we demonstrate a laser-assisted variation of chemically driven growth that enables the synthesis of both structures within the same system by modifying the reduction chemistry. Silver dendrites are formed using ascorbic acid alone, while silver flowers are produced when both ascorbic acid and sodium citrate are used. 3D hierarchical silver structures possess not only a high surface-to-volume ratio and multiple hotspots but also superhydrophobic behavior.

It is worth noting that an X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was not performed in this study. Although the individual silver structures reach micrometer-scale dimensions, they are sparsely distributed and localized only within laser-irradiated regions, with large areas of the substrate remaining uncovered. As a result, the overall diffracting volume of silver is relatively low when averaged over the entire illuminated area during the XRD measurement. Since the XRD signal intensity depends on the total crystalline volume rather than the size of individual structures, the diffraction signal from silver is expected to be weak compared to the dominant background from the substrate. In contrast, studies such as Jia et al. involve continuous or densely distributed nanostructured films that provide sufficient scattering volume for reliable XRD detection. Therefore, in the present case, SEM and EDS analyses were considered to be more appropriate for confirming morphology and composition.

Conclusion

The synthesis of hierarchical 3D silver dendritic and flower-like microstructures on gold film substrates was achieved through fs laser-assisted photochemical reduction of silver nitrate. In this process, ascorbic acid serves as the primary reducing agent, driving the formation of silver nuclei and subsequent growth. Femtosecond laser irradiation delivers localized energy at the focal point, accelerating reaction kinetics and confining nucleation to rapidly forming hierarchical architectures. In the presence of ascorbic acid, newly formed silver clusters preferentially attach to the tips of existing branches during continuous irradiation, driving the growth of complex 3D dendritic morphologies. In contrast, the formation of flower-like structures requires the addition of sodium citrate as a capping agent, which directs anisotropic growth by selectively binding to specific crystallographic facets. This results in the formation of thin, petal-like features that assemble into three-dimensional flower-like architectures under continued laser-assisted growth. The results demonstrate that while the fundamental growth mechanism is governed by chemically driven reduction processes, the femtosecond laser plays a critical role in enhancing nucleation kinetics and enabling spatially localized growth. Although laser-induced energetic carrier effects may be present, their direct contribution to silver-ion reduction is not conclusively established in the present study. Both dendritic and flower-like structures exhibit excellent SERS performance, as demonstrated using Rhodamine 6G as a probe molecule, showing strong signal enhancement and high sensitivity, highlighting their potential as efficient SERS substrates.
