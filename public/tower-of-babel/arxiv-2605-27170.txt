Regular black hole solutions and the quark chemical potential at the QCD phase transition
Gaetano Lambiase, Ali Övgün, V. Vertogradov

Abstract
Motivated by Refs. 27 ; 98 , we investigate whether quantum chromodynamics (QCD)-inspired matter at finite quark chemical potential can dynamically support regular black-hole interiors during gravitational collapse. To this end, we couple two effective equations of state, namely a three-flavor chiral quark model at finite temperature and chemical potential and a cold-QGP mean-field model with a dynamical gluon mass, to a spherically symmetric advanced Eddington–Finkelstein geometry. The matter source is treated as an effective anisotropic fluid. Rather than assuming a regular mass profile a priori, we determine the radial temperature dependence from the local conservation law and reconstruct the mass function from the Einstein equations. In the chiral model, the conservation equation admits an exact Lambert-function solution, but the physical coefficients select a singular near-center branch. In the cold-QGP model, the exact implicit temperature-radius relation drives the temperature to diverge near the center, causing the thermodynamic source terms and the reconstructed mass function to become incompatible with the regular-center condition. We therefore find that, within the effective framework adopted here, finite quark chemical potential reshapes the thermodynamics of the collapse phase but does not by itself provide a self-regularizing black-hole core. Any regular completion must invoke an additional inner vacuum-like phase or further microphysics beyond the two QCD-inspired equations of state considered in this work.

II
Brief review of a QCD effective model with finite chemical potential

To make the discussion self-contained, we first summarize the three-flavor chiral quark model with finite chemical potential introduced in Ref.
27
. The main idea is to encode the QCD dynamics near a first-order phase transition in an effective scalar-pseudoscalar chiral field coupled to quarks, and to derive an improved EoS for the QGP phase, including the dependence on the quark chemical potential
\mu
.

At the thermodynamic level, the matter content in the quark-gluon plasma (QGP) and hadron phases is described by the equations of state

p_{QGP}=\frac{g_{QGP}\pi^{2}}{90}T^{4}-V(T)\,,\qquad\rho_{QGP}=\frac{g_{QGP}\pi^{2}}{30}T^{4}+V(T)\,,

(II.1)

and

p_{H}=\frac{\rho_{H}}{3}=\frac{g_{H}\pi^{2}}{90}T^{4}\,,

(II.2)

where
p
is the pressure,
\rho
the energy density, and
T
the temperature. The functions
g_{QGP}
and
g_{H}
denote the effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom in the QGP and hadron phases, respectively, while
V(T)
encodes self-interaction effects (e.g. the bag contribution) in the QGP phase. The hadronic phase is modeled as a relativistic gas with the conformal relation
p_{H}=\rho_{H}/3
.

These equations follow from the effective Lagrangian density

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{f}}\left[i\bar{\psi}_{k}\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\psi_{k}-g\bar{\psi}_{k}(\sigma+i\tau\cdot\pi\gamma_{5})\psi_{k}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\sigma\partial^{\mu}\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\pi\partial^{\mu}\pi-V(\sigma^{2}+\pi^{2})\,,

(II.3)

where
\psi_{k}
are the quark fields,
\sigma
is a scalar meson field, and
\pi
denotes the pion triplet. The Yukawa coupling
g
controls the quark mass generated by chiral symmetry breaking. The self-interaction potential
V
is responsible for the shape of the chiral effective potential and the existence of (meta)stable minima.

It is convenient to rewrite the Lagrangian in terms of the chiral radius

\xi=(\sigma^{2}+\pi^{2})^{1/2}

and a unitary matrix
U
describing the direction of the chiral field in isospin space. In this parametrization one finds

{\cal L}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\rm f}}\left[i\bar{\psi}_{k}\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\psi_{k}-g\xi(\bar{\psi}_{k}^{\rm L}U\psi_{k}^{\rm R}+\bar{\psi}_{k}^{\rm R}U^{\dagger}\psi_{k}^{\rm L})\right]+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\xi\partial^{\mu}\xi+\frac{1}{4}\xi^{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}(\partial_{\mu}U\partial^{\mu}U^{\dagger})-V(\xi)\,.

(II.4)

Here
\psi^{\rm L,R}
denote left- and right-handed quark fields, and
V(\xi)
depends only on the magnitude of the chiral field and acts as an order-parameter potential. The dynamics of
\xi
capture the chiral symmetry breaking and restoration.

The self-interaction potential is chosen as

V(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}f_{\pi}^{2}\left(\lambda^{2}-\frac{12B}{f_{\pi}^{4}}\right)\xi^{2}\left(1-\frac{\xi}{f_{\pi}}\right)^{2}+B\left[1+3\left(\frac{\xi}{f_{\pi}}\right)^{4}-4\left(\frac{\xi}{f_{\pi}}\right)^{3}\right]\,,

(II.5)

where
f_{\pi}
is the pion decay constant and
B
plays the role of a bag constant, i.e. the vacuum energy difference between the perturbative and nonperturbative phases. The structure of
V(\xi)
is such that it has a global minimum at
\xi=f_{\pi}
(chiral symmetry broken) and a local minimum near
\xi\simeq 0
(chirally restored phase), allowing for a first-order phase transition as temperature or chemical potential are varied.

To incorporate finite quark chemical potential
\mu
and temperature
T
, one adds the fermionic contribution to the thermodynamic potential,

\omega_{f}(T,\mu)=-T\left[\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\ln\left(1+e^{-(E(k)-\mu)/T}\right)+\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\ln\left(1+e^{-(E(k)+\mu)/T}\right)\right]\,,

(II.6)

where
E(k)=\sqrt{k^{2}+m_{s}^{2}}
for the strange quark (here
m_{s}
is the mass of strange quark); the up and down quarks are treated as effectively massless. The first and second integrals represent the contributions of quarks and antiquarks, respectively. Expanding
\omega_{f}
in powers of
T
and
\mu
for a non-zero strange quark mass
m_{s}
yields corrections to the effective potential:

V_{T,\mu}(\xi)=B-\alpha_{T}T^{4}-\frac{3\mu^{2}}{2}T^{2}-\alpha_{\mu}\mu^{4}+\gamma_{T}T^{2}+\gamma_{\mu}\mu^{2}\,,

(II.7)

with

\alpha_{T}=\frac{7\pi^{2}}{20},\qquad\alpha_{\mu}=\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}},\qquad\gamma_{T}=\frac{m_{s}^{2}}{4},\qquad\gamma_{\mu}=\frac{3m_{s}^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\,.

(II.8)

These coefficients encode thermal and density corrections to the vacuum energy and effectively renormalize the bag parameter and the curvature of the potential.

Combining the above ingredients, one arrives at the improved EoS for the QGP phase:

\displaystyle p_{QGP}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{37\pi^{2}}{90}+\alpha_{T}\right)T^{4}+\left(\frac{3\mu^{2}}{2}-\gamma_{T}\right)T^{2}+\alpha_{\mu}\mu^{4}-\gamma_{\mu}\mu^{2}-B\,,

(II.9)

\displaystyle\rho_{QGP}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{37\pi^{2}}{30}-\alpha_{T}\right)T^{4}-\left(\frac{3\mu^{2}}{2}-\gamma_{T}\right)T^{2}-\alpha_{\mu}\mu^{4}+\gamma_{\mu}\mu^{2}+B\,.

(II.10)

The
T^{4}
terms are dominated by the relativistic quark and gluon degrees of freedom, while the
T^{2}
and
\mu^{4}
terms encode the effects of the strange quark mass and finite baryon density, respectively. In the limit
\alpha_{T}=\gamma_{T}=\alpha_{\mu}=\gamma_{\mu}=0
one recovers the standard MIT bag relation
p=\frac{1}{3}(\rho-4B)
.

The critical temperature
T_{c}
of the phase transition is obtained by imposing pressure equality between the QGP and hadron phases,
p_{QGP}(T_{c},\mu)=p_{H}(T_{c})
. Using the above EoS one finds

T_{c}=\left[\frac{\frac{3\mu^{2}}{2}-\gamma_{T}}{\frac{\pi^{2}}{45}\Delta g+2\alpha_{T}}\,\left(-1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{\left(\frac{2\pi^{2}}{45}\Delta g+4\alpha_{T}\right)\left(\alpha_{\mu}\mu^{4}-\gamma_{\mu}\mu^{2}-B\right)}{\left(\frac{3\mu^{2}}{2}-\gamma_{T}\right)^{2}}}\right)\right]^{1/2}\,,

(II.11)

where
\Delta g=g_{QGP}-g_{H}
is the jump in the effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom across the transition. The physically relevant branch corresponds to the choice of sign that yields a real and positive
T_{c}
. The dependence of
T_{c}
on
\mu
will be important later when we consider collapse scenarios with non-negligible quark chemical potential.

A particularly useful combination of the above equations is

p_{QGP}=-\,\rho_{QGP}+\frac{74\pi^{2}}{45}T^{4}\,.

(II.12)

This relation shows that, within this model, the deviation from a vacuum-like equation of state
p=-\rho
is controlled entirely by the
T^{4}
term, while the bag and chemical-potential contributions cancel in
p+\rho
. This structure will play a key role when we embed the EoS into a dynamical black hole geometry.

Before discussing special limits, it is important to stress that the physical coefficients of the chiral model already fix the asymptotic exponent. Using

\alpha_{T}=\frac{7\pi^{2}}{20},\qquad\xi=\frac{37\pi^{2}}{30}-\alpha_{T}=\frac{53\pi^{2}}{60},\qquad\zeta=\frac{74\pi^{2}}{45},

(II.13)

one obtains

\frac{\zeta}{\xi}=\frac{296}{159}\approx 1.8616,\qquad\frac{2\zeta}{\xi}=\frac{592}{159}\approx 3.7233.

(II.14)

III
Chiral QCD equation of state in the Eddington–Finkelstein geometry

We now couple the chiral QCD equation of state discussed in
Sec.
II
to a dynamical, spherically symmetric geometry.
We work in advanced Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates,

ds^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2M(v,r)}{r}\right)dv^{2}+2\varepsilon\,dv\,dr+r^{2}d\Omega^{2},

(III.1)

where
M(v,r)
is a generalized mass function and

\varepsilon=\pm 1
distinguishes ingoing and outgoing null flows. In
the collapse setting considered below we take
\varepsilon=+1
.

The effective stress-energy tensor compatible with the generalized
Vaidya form (
III.1
) may be written as an anisotropic
matter source supplemented by a radial null flux,

T_{\mu\nu}=\sigma\,l_{\mu}l_{\nu}+(\rho+p_{r})(l_{\mu}n_{\nu}+l_{\nu}n_{\mu})+p_{r}\,g_{\mu\nu}+(p_{t}-p_{r})\,q_{\mu\nu},

(III.2)

where
l_{\mu}
and
n_{\mu}
are two radial null one-forms satisfying

l_{\mu}l^{\mu}=n_{\mu}n^{\mu}=0
and
l_{\mu}n^{\mu}=-1
, while

q_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}+l_{\mu}n_{\nu}+l_{\nu}n_{\mu}
projects onto the
two-sphere. In the present reconstruction only the radial sector is
needed, and the Einstein equations give

\sigma(v,r)=\frac{2\dot{M}(v,r)}{r^{2}},\qquad\rho(v,r)=\frac{2M^{\prime}(v,r)}{r^{2}},\qquad p_{r}(v,r)=-\frac{M^{\prime\prime}(v,r)}{r},

(III.3)

where
F(v,r)=1-2M(v,r)/r
.
where the dot and prime denote derivatives with respect to
v
and

r
, respectively. Here
\sigma
is the density of the null flux,

\rho
is the effective energy density, and
p_{r}
is the effective
radial pressure. The tangential pressure
p_{t}
is not fixed independently unless an
additional anisotropic equation of state is supplied.

Equivalently, in the coordinate basis

(v,r,\theta,\varphi)
, the relevant contravariant components of the
effective stress tensor may be written as

\displaystyle T^{vv}

\displaystyle=0,

(III.4)

\displaystyle T^{vr}=T^{rv}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\,\rho,

(III.5)

\displaystyle T^{rr}

\displaystyle=\sigma+\left(1-\frac{2M(v,r)}{r}\right)p_{r},

(III.6)

\displaystyle T^{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{t}}{r^{2}},

(III.7)

\displaystyle T^{\varphi\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{t}}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta},

(III.8)

where
p_{t}
denotes the tangential pressure. In the present
reconstruction only the radial sector is needed; a separate equation of
state for
p_{t}
would correspond to a genuinely anisotropic extension
of the model.

The radial component of the contracted Bianchi identity,

T^{\mu\nu}{}_{;\nu}=0
, gives

r\,\rho^{\prime}(v,r)+2\left[\rho(v,r)+p_{r}(v,r)\right]=0.

(III.9)

Once the Einstein equations are imposed, this equation is automatically
satisfied. Here, however, we use it as an effective radial conservation
law for the chosen QCD equation of state. The mass function is then
reconstructed from

M(v,r)=M_{0}(v)+\frac{1}{2}\int\rho(r)\,r^{2}\,dr,

(III.10)

where
M_{0}(v)
is an integration function. It reduces to the Vaidya
mass parameter in the absence of an additional matter contribution and
should not be interpreted as
M(v,0)
.

We introduce

u(r)\equiv T^{2}(r)

(III.11)

and rewrite the chiral QCD equation of state in the quadratic form

p_{QGP}=a\,u^{2}+b\,u+c,\qquad\rho_{QGP}=d\,u^{2}-b\,u-c,

(III.12)

with

a=\frac{37\pi^{2}}{90}+\alpha_{T},\qquad d\equiv\xi=\frac{37\pi^{2}}{30}-\alpha_{T},\qquad b=\frac{3\mu^{2}}{2}-\gamma_{T},\qquad c=\alpha_{\mu}\mu^{4}-\gamma_{\mu}\mu^{2}-B.

(III.13)

It is useful to define

\eta\equiv-b=\gamma_{T}-\frac{3\mu^{2}}{2},\qquad\zeta\equiv a+d=\frac{74\pi^{2}}{45}.

(III.14)

Then

\rho_{QGP}=\xi u^{2}+\eta u-c,\qquad p_{QGP}+\rho_{QGP}=\zeta u^{2}.

(III.15)

Substitution into the conservation equation
(
III.9
) gives the first-order differential equation

\frac{du}{dr}=-\frac{2\zeta u^{2}}{r(2\xi u+\eta)}.

(III.16)

Its implicit integral is

2\xi\ln u-\frac{\eta}{u}=-2\zeta\ln r+C,\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad u^{2\xi}e^{-\eta/u}=\kappa r^{-2\zeta},

(III.17)

where
\kappa>0
absorbs the integration constant and irrelevant
normalization factors.

Equation (
III.17
) can be solved explicitly in terms of
the Lambert
W
function. Defining

y\equiv\frac{\eta}{2\xi u},

(III.18)

one obtains

ye^{y}=\frac{\eta}{2\xi\,\kappa^{1/(2\xi)}}\,r^{\zeta/\xi},

(III.19)

and hence

u(r)=T^{2}(r)=\frac{\eta}{2\xi\,W\!\left[\dfrac{\eta}{2\xi\,\kappa^{1/(2\xi)}}\,r^{\zeta/\xi}\right]}.

(III.20)

For the values of
\mu
relevant to the high-density QGP-collapse
regime one typically has

\eta=\gamma_{T}-\frac{3\mu^{2}}{2}<0.

(III.21)

In this case the Lambert-
W
solution admits two real branches in the
appropriate domain,
W_{0}
and
W_{-1}
. The principal branch
W_{0}

corresponds to the high-temperature QGP branch and gives the near-center
scaling derived below. The
W_{-1}
branch instead drives

T^{2}\to 0
as
r\to 0^{+}
, and therefore lies outside the
high-temperature collapse regime assumed here.

W
solution for the temperature
profile in the chiral model. Panel (a):
T^{2}(r)
on a log–log scale
for representative values of the quark chemical potential
\mu
,

T^{2}\propto r^{-\zeta/\xi}=r^{-296/159}
. Panel (b): fractional

\mu
-dependent

r\to 0^{+}
.

On the principal branch,
W_{0}(z)\sim z
as
z\to 0
. Therefore, in
the near-center limit,

u(r)=T^{2}(r)\sim\kappa^{1/(2\xi)}r^{-\zeta/\xi},\qquad r\to 0^{+}.

(III.22)

Consequently,

p_{QGP}+\rho_{QGP}=\zeta T^{4}\sim\zeta\,\kappa^{1/\xi}r^{-2\zeta/\xi}.

(III.23)

Thus the deviation from the vacuum-like relation
p_{r}=-\rho
grows
without bound near the center. Figure
1
illustrates
this universal behavior: different values of
\mu
change only the
subleading corrections, while the leading power law is fixed by

\zeta/\xi
.

The mass function follows from

\rho=\frac{2M^{\prime}}{r^{2}}.

(III.24)

Using Eq. (
III.12
), the exact density is

\rho(r)=\xi u(r)^{2}+\eta u(r)-c,

(III.25)

where
u(r)
is given by Eq. (
III.20
). Hence

M(v,r)=M_{0}(v)+\frac{1}{2}\int\left[\xi u(r)^{2}+\eta u(r)-c\right]r^{2}\,dr.

(III.26)

The integral is not generally expressible in a simple elementary form,
but its near-center behavior is fixed directly by
Eq. (
III.22
). Since the
u^{2}
term dominates,

\rho(r)\sim\xi\,\kappa^{1/\xi}r^{-2\zeta/\xi},\qquad r\to 0^{+}.

(III.27)

Therefore,

M(v,r)\sim M_{0}(v)+\frac{\xi\,\kappa^{1/\xi}}{2\left(3-2\zeta/\xi\right)}r^{3-2\zeta/\xi}.

(III.28)

For the physical chiral-model coefficients,

\frac{\zeta}{\xi}=\frac{296}{159},\qquad\frac{2\zeta}{\xi}=\frac{592}{159},

(III.29)

and therefore

M(v,r)\sim M_{0}(v)+\hat{D}\,r^{-115/159},

(III.30)

where
\hat{D}
is a constant. This behavior is incompatible with the
regular-center condition

M(v,r)=\mathcal{O}(r^{3}),\qquad r\to 0.

(III.31)

Even if
M_{0}(v)
is set to zero, the power-law correction in
Eq. (
III.30
) remains singular.

summarizes the obstruction. The
principal-branch chiral solution gives a mass function that diverges as

r\to 0
, whereas a regular de Sitter-like core requires

M\propto r^{3}
. Thus finite quark chemical potential changes the
thermodynamics of the collapse phase, but within this chiral equation of
state it does not generate a self-regularizing black-hole core.

IV
Cold–QGP model and the quark chemical potential

We now consider a complementary effective description of quark matter based on the cold-QGP model developed in Ref.
85
. This framework treats the gluon field in a relativistic mean-field approximation, where soft and hard momentum modes are separated and the hard modes acquire a dynamical mass
m_{g}
. The resulting EoS is particularly simple in the high-temperature regime and allows for analytic control in curved spacetime.

The general expressions for the pressure
p
and energy density
\rho
in this model are

\displaystyle p_{\text{cold-QGP}}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\pi\alpha_{s}}{4{m_{g}}^{2}}{n_{q}}^{2}-B+\sum_{f}\,\frac{\gamma_{f}}{6\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\frac{k^{4}}{\mathcal{E}_{f}}\,\Big(d_{f}+\bar{d}_{f}\Big)+\frac{\gamma_{g}}{6\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d{k}\,\frac{k^{3}}{e^{k/T}-1}\,,

(IV.1)

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{cold-QGP}}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\pi\alpha_{s}}{4{m_{g}}^{2}}{n_{q}}^{2}+B+\sum_{f}\,\frac{\gamma_{f}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,k^{2}\,\mathcal{E}_{f}\,\Big(d_{f}+\bar{d}_{f}\Big)+\frac{\gamma_{g}}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d{k}\,\frac{k^{3}}{e^{k/T}-1}.\,

(IV.2)

Here
\mathcal{E}_{f}=\sqrt{m_{f}^{2}+k^{2}}
is the quark single-particle energy,
d_{f}
and
\bar{d}_{f}
are the Fermi-Dirac distribution functions for quarks and antiquarks with chemical potential
\nu_{f}
, and
n_{q}
is the quark number density. The parameters
\alpha_{s}
and
m_{g}
represent the strong coupling and the dynamical gluon mass, respectively, while
B
is the bag constant. The factors
\gamma_{f}
and
\gamma_{g}
account for spin and color degeneracies of quarks and gluons.

For a system with two light quarks (
u
and
d
) in the high-temperature regime (
T\gg\nu_{f}
,
T\gg m_{f}
), the integrals can be evaluated analytically. In this limit the EoS simplifies to

\displaystyle p_{\text{cold-QGP}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{37\pi^{2}}{90}+\frac{3\pi\alpha_{s}\mu^{2}}{4{m_{g}}^{2}}\right)T^{4}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}T^{2}-B\,,

(IV.3)

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{cold-QGP}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{37\pi^{2}}{30}+\frac{3\pi\alpha_{s}\mu^{2}}{4{m_{g}}^{2}}\right)T^{4}+\frac{3\mu^{2}}{2}T^{2}+B\,,

(IV.4)

where the chemical potential is taken equal for the two flavors,
\mu\equiv\nu_{u}=\nu_{d}
. The first terms in Eqs. (
IV.3
)-(
IV.4
) describe the contribution of relativistic massless quarks and gluons, corrected by an interaction term proportional to
\alpha_{s}\mu^{2}/m_{g}^{2}
, while the
T^{2}
terms originate from finite chemical potential and the bag constant
B
provides the vacuum energy offset. In the limit
\alpha_{s}\rightarrow 0
these expressions reduce to the MIT bag EoS for a two-flavor QGP.

The critical temperature of the phase transition is obtained by imposing pressure equality between the QGP and hadron phases,

p_{\rm cold\text{-}QGP}(T_{c},\mu)=p_{H}(T_{c})=\frac{g_{H}\pi^{2}}{90}\,T_{c}^{4}.

(IV.5)

Using Eq. (
IV.3
), one finds

A_{\rm cold}\,T_{c}^{4}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\,T_{c}^{2}-B=0,

(IV.6)

where

A_{\rm cold}\equiv\frac{\pi^{2}}{90}(37-g_{H})+\frac{3\pi\alpha_{s}\mu^{2}}{4m_{g}^{2}}.

(IV.7)

Solving for
T_{c}^{2}
, the physically relevant branch is

T_{c}^{2}=\frac{-\mu^{2}/2+\sqrt{\mu^{4}/4+4A_{\rm cold}B}}{2A_{\rm cold}}.

(IV.8)

This expression replaces the chiral-model critical-temperature formula and is the appropriate one for the cold–QGP equation of state used here.

Combining Eqs. (
IV.3
) and (
IV.4
) we obtain

\rho_{\rm cold\text{-}QGP}=3p_{\rm cold\text{-}QGP}+4B-\frac{3\pi\alpha_{s}\mu^{2}}{2m_{g}^{2}}T^{4}

(IV.9)

and hence

p_{\rm cold\text{-}QGP}=\frac{1}{3}\rho_{\rm cold\text{-}QGP}-\frac{4B}{3}+\frac{\pi\alpha_{s}\mu^{2}}{2m_{g}^{2}}T^{4}.

(IV.10)

The cold-QGP fluid therefore behaves as a radiation-like fluid
p\simeq\rho/3
with a negative vacuum contribution
-4B/3
, plus an interaction correction proportional to
\alpha_{s}\mu^{2}T^{4}/m_{g}^{2}
. When
\alpha_{s}\to 0
we recover exactly the MIT-bag form
p=\tfrac{1}{3}(\rho-4B)
.

V
Black holes supported by the quark chemical potential (cold–QGP model)

We now embed the cold-QGP EoS into the EF geometry (
III.1
) and analyze the resulting mass function. We assume that the pressure and energy density are given by Eqs. (
IV.3
) and (
IV.4
). For a static configuration the continuity equation (
III.9
) reduces to

\rho_{\text{cold-QGP}}^{\prime}r+2p_{\text{cold-QGP}}+2\rho_{\text{cold-QGP}}=0.

(V.1)

This equation expresses radial energy-momentum conservation in the presence of anisotropic pressure and can be rewritten as a differential equation for the temperature
T(r)
.

For convenience we introduce the notation

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{3\mu^{2}}{\gamma},\qquad\beta\equiv\frac{4\mu^{2}}{\delta},

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{74\pi^{2}}{15}+\frac{3\pi\alpha_{s}\mu^{2}}{m_{g}^{2}},\qquad\delta\equiv\frac{148\pi^{2}}{45}+\frac{3\pi\alpha_{s}\mu^{2}}{m_{g}^{2}}.

(V.2)

In terms of these parameters one finds

\gamma\left(T^{3}+\alpha T\right)T^{\prime}r+\delta\left(T^{4}+\beta T^{2}\right)=0.

(V.3)

Introducing
x(r)\equiv T^{2}(r)
, Eq. (
V.3
) becomes

\gamma\,r\,(x+\alpha)\,x^{\prime}+2\delta\,x(x+\beta)=0,

(V.4)

or equivalently

\frac{x+\alpha}{x(x+\beta)}\,dx=-\frac{2\delta}{\gamma}\,\frac{dr}{r}.

(V.5)

Using the partial-fraction decomposition

\frac{x+\alpha}{x(x+\beta)}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\frac{1}{x}+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)\frac{1}{x+\beta},

(V.6)

we obtain

\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\ln x+\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)\ln(x+\beta)=-\frac{2\delta}{\gamma}\ln r+C.

(V.7)

Therefore the exact implicit relation can be written as

\left(\frac{T^{2}}{T_{\star}^{2}}\right)^{\alpha/\beta}\left(\frac{T^{2}+\beta}{T_{\star}^{2}}\right)^{1-\alpha/\beta}=\kappa\,r^{-2\delta/\gamma},

(V.8)

where
T_{\star}
is an arbitrary reference scale introduced to make the left-hand side dimensionless, and
\kappa>0
is an integration constant fixed by boundary data.

For fixed
\kappa
, Eq. (
V.8
) implies
T\to\infty
as
r\to 0^{+}
and
T\to 0
as
r\to\infty
. In the high-temperature regime
x=T^{2}\gg\beta
, Eq. (
V.8
) reduces to

x(r)\propto r^{-2\delta/\gamma},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad T^{2}(r)\propto r^{-2\delta/\gamma}.

(V.9)

Hence

\rho_{\rm cold\text{-}QGP}\sim T^{4}\propto r^{-4\delta/\gamma},\qquad p_{\rm cold\text{-}QGP}+\rho_{\rm cold\text{-}QGP}\propto T^{4}\propto r^{-4\delta/\gamma},

(V.10)

which diverges as
r\to 0^{+}
. Therefore the cold–QGP phase alone does not regularize the core.

In the opposite regime
x=T^{2}\ll\beta
, one finds

x^{\alpha/\beta}\propto r^{-2\delta/\gamma},

(V.11)

and since

\frac{\beta}{\alpha}=\frac{4\gamma}{3\delta},

(V.12)

it follows that

T^{2}(r)=x(r)\propto r^{-8/3}.

(V.13)

Thus the temperature decreases toward zero at large radius, but the cold–QGP phase should in any case be regarded as an inner high-density phase and matched to an outer hadronic or vacuum region.

and hence

M(v,r)\;\sim\;M_{0}(v)+D\,r^{\,3-4\delta/\gamma},

(V.17)

where
D
is a constant determined by the microphysical parameters, provided

\delta/\gamma\neq 3/4
.

the power-law integration is replaced by a logarithm,

which is also singular as

. For the physical parameter range

(V.20)

-1<3-\frac{4\delta}{\gamma}\leq\frac{1}{3}.

approaches the regular-center behavior

.

V.1
Curvature-based regularity criterion

A convenient way to state the regularity condition is in terms of the mass function. For a static spherically symmetric geometry with

f(r)=1-\frac{2M(r)}{r},

(V.22)

one finds the Ricci scalar

R=\frac{2\big(rM^{\prime\prime}+2M^{\prime}\big)}{r^{2}},

(V.23)

while the Kretschmann scalar
K\equiv R_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}R^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}
is also fully determined by
M
,
M^{\prime}
, and
M^{\prime\prime}
. If

M(r)\sim c\,r^{n}\qquad(r\to 0),

(V.24)

then

R\sim 2c\,n(n+1)\,r^{\,n-3},\qquad K\sim\mathcal{O}\!\left(r^{\,2n-6}\right).

(V.25)

Therefore, finiteness of the curvature invariants requires

n\geq 3.

(V.26)

The special case
n=3
corresponds to a de Sitter-like core,

M(r)\sim\frac{\Lambda_{\rm eff}}{6}\,r^{3},

(V.27)

for which

R\to 4\Lambda_{\rm eff},\qquad K\to\frac{8}{3}\Lambda_{\rm eff}^{2}.

(V.28)

This criterion will be used below to assess whether the QCD-supported solutions are genuinely regular at the center.

VI
Transition of baryonic matter into quark matter

The analysis so far shows that neither the chiral QCD model nor the cold-QGP phase, by themselves, guarantee the formation of a regular core: in both cases the high-temperature behavior drives
p+\rho
to large positive values near the center, and the mass function does not naturally scale as
r^{3}
. However, the collapse process is expected to involve nontrivial microphysics beyond a single homogeneous phase. In particular, once certain critical densities or temperatures are reached, baryonic matter can convert into quark matter, releasing energy in the form of radiation.

Following Refs.
100
;
98
;
99
, we consider a scenario in which the collapsing matter consists initially of baryons, and, above a threshold density, undergoes a transition into quark matter accompanied by electromagnetic (and possibly neutrino) radiation. During this conversion, the individual energy-momentum tensors of baryons, radiation, and newly formed quark matter are not separately conserved, although the total energy-momentum tensor remains divergence-free.

We model the rate of conversion by introducing a dimensionless transition function
\beta=\beta(v,r)
, which depends on both advanced time and radius and is assumed to decrease with radius,
\beta^{\prime}<0
, so that the transition proceeds more rapidly in denser inner regions. Denoting by
\rho_{r}
and
\rho_{b}
the energy densities of radiation and baryonic matter, respectively, and by
P_{r}
,
P_{b}
their pressures, the transition can be described by the system
99

\displaystyle\rho_{r}^{\prime}r+2P_{r}+2\rho_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta(v,r)\,\rho_{r},

\displaystyle\rho_{b}^{\prime}r+2P_{b}+2\rho_{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\beta(v,r)\,\rho_{r}.

(VI.1)

The first equation states that radiation is produced at a rate proportional to
\beta\rho_{r}
, while the second shows that the baryonic sector loses energy at the same rate. The sum of the two equations reproduces the usual conservation law for the total matter sector (baryons + radiation).

We assume that radiation obeys the relativistic equation of state

P_{r}=\frac{1}{3}\rho_{r}
,
while baryonic matter is characterized by a barotropic relation

P_{b}=\alpha\rho_{b}
,
with constant
\alpha
. Solving Eqs. (
VI
) under these assumptions yields

\displaystyle\rho_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{0r}\exp\left(\int\frac{\beta-\frac{8}{3}}{r}\,dr\right),

\displaystyle\rho_{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{-(2\alpha+2)}\left[C(v)-\rho_{0r}\int r^{1+2\alpha}\beta\exp\left(\int\frac{\beta-\frac{8}{3}}{r}\,dr\right)dr\right],

(VI.2)

where
\rho_{0r}
and
C(v)
are integration constants. The first expression shows explicitly how the radiation energy density is modulated by the transition rate
\beta
, while the second encodes the depletion of baryonic matter as conversion proceeds. These expressions are understood for
r>0
in regions where
\beta(v,r)
is sufficiently smooth; in addition, the later inversion formula (
VI.5
) requires
\alpha\neq\tfrac{1}{3}
.

The total energy density of the new quark phase is

\rho_{\text{new}}=\rho_{b}+\rho_{r}.

(VI.3)

In Ref.
99
it was shown that, given an EoS for the new phase specified by
\rho_{\text{new}}(r)
and
P_{\text{new}}(r)
, the transition rate
\beta
and radiation density
\rho_{r}
can be expressed as

\beta=\frac{\frac{2}{3}\alpha\rho_{\text{new}}-\left(2\alpha+\frac{8}{3}\right)P_{\text{new}}-P^{\prime}_{\text{new}}r}{\alpha\rho_{\text{new}}-P_{\text{new}}},

(VI.4)

and

\rho_{r}=\frac{\alpha\rho_{\text{new}}-P_{\text{new}}}{\alpha-\frac{1}{3}}.

(VI.5)

These relations invert the problem: given a desired EoS for the new quark phase, one can reconstruct the transition rate required to realize it dynamically.

In our context,
\rho_{\text{new}}
and
P_{\text{new}}
are given either by the chiral QGP EoS (
II.9
)-(
II.12
) or by the cold-QGP EoS (
IV.3
)-(
IV.4
). As an illustration, inserting Eq. (
II.12
) into Eq. (
VI.5
) yields

\rho_{r}=\frac{\left[\left(\alpha+1\right)\rho_{\text{QGP}}-\frac{74\pi^{2}}{45}T^{4}\right]}{\alpha-\frac{1}{3}}.

(VI.6)

Alternatively, expressing
\rho_{\text{QGP}}
in terms of temperature using Eq. (
II.9
) leads to

\rho_{r}=\frac{1}{\alpha-\frac{1}{3}}\Bigg[\left(\alpha+1\right)\left(\left(\frac{37\pi^{2}}{30}-\alpha_{T}\right)T^{4}-\left(\frac{3\mu^{2}}{2}-\gamma_{T}\right)T^{2}-\alpha_{\mu}\mu^{4}+\gamma_{\mu}\mu^{2}+B\right)-\frac{74\pi^{2}}{45}T^{4}\Bigg],

(VI.7)

where the temperature
T
should be evaluated at the phase transition given by Eq. (
II.11
). For the chiral QCD model, the conservation law leads to an implicit radial temperature profile and, through the exact Lambert-
W
solution, to a mass function whose near-center behavior is singular for the physical coefficients of the model.

Finally, substituting the cold-QGP relation (
IV.9
) into Eq. (
VI.5
) yields

\rho_{r}=\frac{1}{\alpha-\frac{1}{3}}\left[\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{3}\right)\rho_{\rm cold\text{-}QGP}+\frac{4B}{3}-\frac{\pi\alpha_{s}\mu^{2}}{2m_{g}^{2}}T^{4}\right],

(VI.8)

where the critical temperature from Eq. (
IV.8
) is used for estimation in collapse scenarios passing through the QCD phase transition. This expression shows explicitly how the radiation component produced during the baryon-to-quark conversion depends on the underlying QCD parameters
(B,\alpha_{s},m_{g},\mu)
and the local temperature.

The central point of this section is that an appropriately tuned conversion process, encoded in the function
\beta(v,r)
, can in principle arrange for the newly formed quark phase to approach an EoS with
P_{\text{new}}\simeq-\rho_{\text{new}}
in the innermost region, thus dynamically generating a de Sitter-like core. The radiation emitted in the process carries away the excess energy density that would otherwise make the center singular, while the total energy-momentum tensor remains conserved.

VII
Explicit matching to an inner vacuum-like core

In this section, we consider an effective completion in which the high-density QCD phase is matched, below a transition radius
r_{c}
, to an inner vacuum-like core satisfying

p_{\rm core}\simeq-\rho_{\rm core}.

(VII.1)

The purpose of this section is not to derive such a phase from first-principles QCD, but rather to determine the minimal conditions under which a smooth and curvature-regular interior can be constructed consistently with the exterior QCD-supported collapse solution.

For the metric (
III.1
), regularity at
r=0
requires that the mass function behave as

M(v,r)=\frac{\Lambda_{\rm eff}(v)}{6}\,r^{3}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4}),\qquad r\to 0,

(VII.2)

so that the lapse function approaches a de Sitter-like form,

1-\frac{2M(v,r)}{r}=1-\frac{\Lambda_{\rm eff}(v)}{3}r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{3}).

(VII.3)

Equivalently, one needs

M(v,0)=0,\qquad M^{\prime}(v,0)=0,\qquad M^{\prime\prime}(v,0)=0,

(VII.4)

which guarantee that the effective density and pressure,

\rho(v,r)=\frac{2M^{\prime}(v,r)}{r^{2}},\qquad p_{r}(v,r)=-\frac{M^{\prime\prime}(v,r)}{r},

(VII.5)

remain finite at the center. In particular, Eq. (
VII.2
) implies

\rho(0)=\Lambda_{\rm eff},\qquad p_{r}(0)=-\Lambda_{\rm eff},

(VII.6)

showing explicitly that regularity requires an inner vacuum-like equation of state.

Since the QCD mass functions obtained in
Secs.
III
and
V
are singular when
extended all the way to
r=0
, strict regularity requires that the QCD
branch not be evaluated at the origin. A purely hyperbolic-tangent
interpolator is exponentially small inside the core, but not identically
zero; therefore, it is useful for numerical illustrations but does not remove, by itself, a power-law singularity in an exact mathematical sense.
For an exactly regular construction, one should either use a piecewise
matching or a compact-support smooth switching function that vanishes
identically in the inner core and becomes unity only outside the
transition layer.

displays the lapse function

f(r)=1-2M_{\rm eff}(r)/r
of the matched geometry for several
representative parameter choices. Two features are worth
emphasizing. First,
f(0)=1
for every matched configuration,
a direct consequence of the regularity
conditions (
VII.4
) and of the de Sitter-like
scaling
M_{\rm eff}(r)\sim(\Lambda_{\rm eff}/6)\,r^{3}
at the

for which
f(r)\to-\infty
as
r\to 0^{+}
. Second, the matched
profile generically exhibits a pair of horizons: an inner horizon

r_{-}
located close to the transition radius
r_{c}
, and an
outer horizon
r_{+}
which, whenever
r_{c}<2M_{*}
, coincides
with the Schwarzschild value
2M_{*}
to numerical precision.
This double-horizon structure is the distinctive geometric
fingerprint of regular black holes of the
Bardeen–Hayward–Dymnikova family
15
;
49
;
39
, and it persists here despite
the fact that the inner region is matched to a QCD-motivated
outer phase rather than to a nonlinear electrodynamics or other
effective source. As the core parameter
\Lambda_{\rm eff}
is
increased, the two horizons move apart and the de Sitter core
becomes progressively deeper, while the exterior metric remains
essentially unchanged for
r\gg r_{c}+\Delta
. This explicitly
confirms that the regularity of the interior is invisible to
any exterior probe in the regime
r_{c}<2M_{*}
.

By construction, Eq. (
VII.7
) reduces to a de Sitter-like core deep inside the transition layer,

M_{\rm eff}(v,r)\simeq\frac{\Lambda_{\rm eff}(v)}{6}\,r^{3},\qquad r_{c}-r\gg\Delta,

(VII.12)

while sufficiently far outside the layer one recovers the original QCD-supported solution,

M_{\rm eff}(v,r)\simeq M_{\rm QCD}(v,r),\qquad r-r_{c}\gg\Delta.

(VII.13)

This provides the simplest explicit realization of a regular interior consistent with the no-go results of the previous sections.

Using Eq. (
VII.7
), the effective density becomes

\rho_{\rm eff}(v,r)=\frac{2}{r^{2}}\,\partial_{r}M_{\rm eff}(v,r),

(VII.14)

that is,

\rho_{\rm eff}(v,r)=f(r)\,\rho_{\rm QCD}(v,r)+\left[1-f(r)\right]\Lambda_{\rm eff}(v)+\frac{2f^{\prime}(r)}{r^{2}}\left[M_{\rm QCD}(v,r)-\frac{\Lambda_{\rm eff}(v)}{6}r^{3}\right].

(VII.15)

Similarly, the effective radial pressure is

p_{\rm eff}(v,r)=-\frac{1}{r}\,\partial_{r}^{2}M_{\rm eff}(v,r).

(VII.16)

Differentiating Eq. (
VII.7
) twice with respect to
r
, and using
\partial_{r}(r^{3})=3r^{2}
and
\partial_{r}^{2}(r^{3})=6r
, one obtains

\displaystyle p_{\rm eff}(v,r)

\displaystyle=f(r)\,p_{\rm QCD}(v,r)-\left[1-f(r)\right]\Lambda_{\rm eff}(v)-\frac{2f^{\prime}(r)}{r}\,\partial_{r}M_{\rm QCD}(v,r)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(r)}{r}\left[M_{\rm QCD}(v,r)-\frac{\Lambda_{\rm eff}(v)}{6}r^{3}\right]+\Lambda_{\rm eff}(v)\,r\,f^{\prime}(r).

(VII.17)

These expressions make it explicit that the interpolation layer contributes additional effective stresses proportional to
f^{\prime}(r)
and
f^{\prime\prime}(r)
. In a realistic microscopic model such terms would be generated by the dynamics of the transition region itself. Here they are treated phenomenologically.

A physically sensible composite solution should satisfy continuity of
M_{\rm eff}
and
\partial_{r}M_{\rm eff}
at the transition radius, i.e.
C^{1}
continuity. In practice, this means requiring

M_{\rm eff}(v,r_{c}^{-})=M_{\rm eff}(v,r_{c}^{+}),\qquad\partial_{r}M_{\rm eff}(v,r_{c}^{-})=\partial_{r}M_{\rm eff}(v,r_{c}^{+}).

(VII.18)

If one further demands continuity of the effective radial pressure, then
\partial_{r}^{2}M_{\rm eff}
should also be continuous across the transition layer. For the smooth interpolation (
VII.10
), these conditions are automatically implemented provided
M_{\rm QCD}
is regular for
r>r_{c}
.

The parameters
(r_{c},\Delta,\Lambda_{\rm eff})
characterize the effective completion. Their allowed range is constrained by three requirements:

1.

regularity at the center, Eq. (
VII.4
);

2.

positivity of the total energy density in the transition region,
\rho_{\rm eff}\geq 0
;

3.

existence of at least one apparent horizon, determined by

1-\frac{2M_{\rm eff}(v,r)}{r}=0.

(VII.19)

These conditions can be used to delimit the region of parameter space in which the geometry describes a regular black hole rather than a horizonless compact object.

The effective completion necessarily modifies the energy conditions in the interior. In the core region one has

\rho_{\rm eff}+p_{\rm eff}\simeq 0,

(VII.20)

so the null energy condition is saturated at leading order. In the transition layer, however, the derivatives of
f(r)
generate additional terms in Eqs. (
VII.15
) and (
VII.17
), and these may lead to local violations of the null or strong energy conditions. This is not unexpected: any regular de Sitter-like core requires a departure from ordinary matter behavior in the deep interior.

A useful diagnostic is the combination

\rho_{\rm eff}+p_{\rm eff},

(VII.21)

which measures the local departure from vacuum-like matter. If this quantity is positive everywhere outside the transition layer and only becomes small or negative inside the core, then the regularization can be interpreted as a localized inner effect rather than a modification of the entire collapse solution.

To verify that the matched solution is genuinely regular, one should evaluate the curvature invariants constructed from
M_{\rm eff}(v,r)
. In particular, regularity requires that the Ricci scalar
R
and the Kretschmann invariant

K\equiv R_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}R^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}

(VII.22)

remain finite as
r\to 0
. For the de Sitter-like core (
VII.2
), one expects

R\to 4\Lambda_{\rm eff},\qquad K\to\frac{8}{3}\Lambda_{\rm eff}^{2},\qquad r\to 0,

(VII.23)

up to corrections suppressed by higher powers of
r
. This provides a direct numerical check of the regularity of the composite configuration.

The matched solution (
VII.7
) should be regarded as an effective completion of the QCD-supported collapse geometry. Its purpose is not to claim that the inner vacuum-like phase has been derived from first-principles QCD, but rather to make explicit what additional structure is required in order to convert the no-go result of the previous sections into a regular black-hole solution. In this sense, the role of the finite-
\mu
QCD phase is twofold: it determines the outer high-density collapse dynamics, and it sets the conditions under which an additional inner component must take over if regularity is to be achieved.

This construction also provides a practical starting point for phenomenology. Once
M_{\rm eff}(v,r)
is specified, one can study how the transition radius
r_{c}
, the core scale
\Lambda_{\rm eff}
, and the thickness
\Delta
affect the horizon structure, the photon sphere, and the quasinormal-mode spectrum. Once such a matched mass profile is specified, its horizon structure, photon sphere, and linear perturbations can be studied directly.

VIII
Conclusion

We have studied whether two QCD-inspired equations of state at finite quark chemical potential can support a regular black-hole interior in a spherically symmetric collapse geometry. The matter sector was coupled to an advanced Eddington–Finkelstein metric, and the radial temperature profile was determined from the effective conservation equation rather than prescribed externally. The mass function was then reconstructed from the Einstein equations, allowing the near-center regularity condition to be tested directly.

For the chiral QCD model, the conservation equation admits an exact Lambert-
W
solution. The high-temperature branch relevant for the QGP collapse regime leads to a divergent temperature profile and to a mass function whose leading near-center behavior is incompatible with
M(r)\sim r^{3}
. The second real Lambert branch exists mathematically for
\eta<0
, but it drives the temperature to zero as
r\to 0^{+}
and therefore does not describe the assumed high-density QGP regime. In the cold–QGP model, the implicit
r
–
T
relation similarly forces
T\to\infty
near the center, so that both
\rho
and
p+\rho
diverge. The resulting mass function scales as
M(r)\sim r^{3-4\delta/\gamma}
, which never reaches the regular de Sitter-like behavior in the physical parameter range.

The present analysis should therefore be interpreted as a restricted
no-go result rather than as a complete model of QCD-driven regular black
hole formation. The two finite-
\mu
QCD equations of state considered
here describe high-density collapse phases, but their near-center
behavior remains too stiff and too singular to generate the vacuum-like
condition
p_{r}\simeq-\rho
required for curvature regularity. A regular
black-hole interior can be obtained only after supplementing the QCD
phase by additional short-distance physics, modeled here through a
smooth matching to an inner de Sitter-like core.

Future work should
derive this inner phase dynamically, for example from color
superconductivity, magnetic-field effects, nonperturbative QCD vacuum
structure, semiclassical gravity, or other ultraviolet completions. Once
such a microphysical completion is specified, the resulting mass profile
can be used to study horizon structure, photon spheres, shadows, lensing
and quasinormal spectra.
