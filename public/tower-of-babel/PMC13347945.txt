BMI-genome interactions regulate global gene expression with emphasis in brain and gut
Authors: Rebecca Signer, Carina Seah, Hannah Young, Kayla Retallick-Townsley, Julia Ciarcia, Agathe De Pins, Alanna Cote, Seoyeon Lee, Meng Jia, Jessica Johnson, Keira J.A. Johnston, Jiayi Xu, Kristen J. Brennand, Laura M. Huckins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGenome-wide association studies identify single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with disease in a population but do not reliably account for individual environmental effects, despite evidence that environment mediates SNP functional regulatory capacity. Body mass index (BMI) is associated with physiologic processes across disorders but hasn’t been modeled as an environment for disease-associated SNPs. We use an interaction approach to identify SNPs that contextually regulate gene expression across the BMI spectrum, called BMI-dynamic expression quantitative trait loci (BMI-eQTL). We found BMI-eQTL across tissues, including brain and gut, while the main effects of BMI were confined to endocrine tissues. We demonstrate that cell type, putative enhancers, and/or inflammatory cytokines underlie BMI-eQTL. We develop models to predict gene expression using BMI-by-SNP interactions and identify more replicating disease-associated genes than SNP-only models. While neither genetics nor BMI is sufficient as a standalone measure to capture the complexity of downstream cellular consequences, including environment helps power disease gene discovery.

Introduction

The utilization of genetics in medicine has drastically expanded over the last 30 years through the identification of a growing body of rare and common genetic variation that contributes to diseases across specialties. While high penetrance rare variation can be tested in the clinic and is relevant to the clinical management of a subset of mendelian cases,1,2 clinical translation of common variation, which cumulatively represents the bulk of heritability in complex traits, has proven more challenging.3,4 Much success in identifying trait-associated common variants has come from genome-wide association studies (GWASs). This population-level approach maximizes power to identify disease-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs),5 yielding hundreds of genome-wide significant loci across a very large number of complex traits. However, discovery of causal variants from GWAS is challenging; this difficulty stems from the complexity in pinpointing causal alleles from complex linkage disequilibrium (LD) structures, translating population-level statistics to individual-level associations, and, on a larger scale, because many of the common diseases in the clinic are multifactorial, i.e., a combination of genomic and environmental factors.6 Moreover, GWASs fail to identify SNPs that are associated in only certain environmental contexts or SNPs with varying gene-by-environment (GxE) effect sizes.7 Dynamic variant effects may be a core reason, in addition to ancestry and diagnostic bias, population-level predictors derived from GWAS (e.g., polygenic risk scores) perform poorly on an individual level and have not successfully been robustly translated to patient care. To truly capture the predictors of multifactorial disease development, we must consider both genetics and environment in disease risk models.

A global finding from GWAS shows that disease-associated SNPs are enriched for expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL)—variants with the functional capacity to regulate gene expression.8 This enrichment of eQTLs may imply that the primary modality by which GWAS variants affect disease is through a cumulative impact of gene expression in disease-relevant tissues. Understanding eQTL architecture and integrating it with GWAS summary statistics yields biological insights and interpretability, for example allowing researchers to implicate a gene, tissue, or cell type involved in the complex trait. However, to date, studies of eQTLs have focused on static associations (i.e., based on germline genotype, without consideration for environmental effects) despite evidence that eQTL activity may be highly context-specific, with dynamic effect sizes across tissues, cell-types, sex, hormonal state, developmental stage, and immune activation,9,10,11,12,13,14 with implications for risk-estimates of common immunologic disorders and cell types.10,11

We propose that studies that integrate only static eQTL estimates fail to capture important context-specific associations. By characterizing and incorporating dynamic estimates into eQTL architecture and integrating these estimates with GWAS, we hope to move beyond static estimates and into a personalized understanding of dynamic risk. Here, we focus on body mass index (BMI), a key physiological context with potential relevance to many complex traits. Obesity, often defined as BMI > 30, is rising across the globe,15 with important physiologic impacts, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and hormonal dysregulation.16,17,18 The combined morbidity of obesity-associated cardiometabolic, oncologic, psychiatric, and neurologic conditions is substantial,.19,20,21 Many efforts have been undertaken to use GWAS to identify factors associated with genomic susceptibility to these complex disorders,22,23,24 yet no studies have evaluated the impact of the environment of obesity on genomic risk estimates. In this study, we test whether BMI (kg/m2), here used as a proxy for the endocrinologic physiologic environment, interacts with the genome to regulate gene expression across tissues. We aim to identify BMI-dynamic genomic variants, postulate cell-type specific regulatory BMI-dynamic mechanisms, and identify genes and pathways relevant to complex disease, which are missed with conventional GWAS approaches. As BMI is itself a heritable, polygenic trait, we evaluate the concordance of the identified genes that are responsive to BMI with genes that are implicated in the heritability of adult BMI using the most recent BMI GWAS.25

Previous work has shown that cell type proportion may be a key mediator in eQTL and environmental interactions26 and is a vital consideration in defining gene-by-environment findings. To parse the impact of cell type on our BMI-dynamic eQTL effects (Box 1), we perform cell type deconvolution across body systems and identify associations of BMI and cell fraction. We postulate cell-type-specific dynamic mechanisms using transcription factor-binding affinity prediction and human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived glutamatergic neurons treated with pro-inflammatory factors. Results

Defining the impact of BMI on the regulation of gene expression

We implemented an eQTL linear regression framework to test for the main and interactive effects of SNPs and BMI on gene expression (Figure 1A) using matched genotype, phenotype, and tissue-specific gene expression data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project v827 freeze. First, we followed the classic cis-eQTL approach (Exp ∼ SNP + PC1:20) to identify the main effects of SNPs on expression without regard for environmental context, which we term base-eQTL and base-eGenes. To determine the direct impact of BMI on gene expression, we tested for differentially expressed genes in the context of BMI (BMI DE-genes, Figures 1C and 1D; Table S2). We identified 7,563 total BMI DE-genes (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05), 4,199 of which are negatively associated with BMI and 3,364 are positively associated (Figure 1C), including known BMI-associated genes such as LEP,28 SLC27A2,29 and GPD1L.30 BMI-DE genes were significant in 18 tissues, with most in the endocrine system, including subcutaneous adipose (n = 2,341 genes), skeletal muscle (n = 1,894 genes), and skin-not sun exposed (n = 1,823 genes) (Figure 1D; Table S1). We did not identify any BMI-DE genes in any brain tissue.

We next examined whether BMI impacts gene expression indirectly through interactions with SNPs, called BMI-eQTL, and their associated eGenes, called BMI-eGenes, by adding an interaction term to the classic framework (Exp ∼ SNP × BMI + SNP + BMI + PC1:20). To establish a threshold for significance, we applied a hierarchical permutation-based approach (see methods). As with base-eGenes, we observe a significant relationship between tissue sample size and discovery power (Figure 1E). Subcutaneous adipose and tibial nerve contained the most BMI-eGenes among the large tissues, mucosa and muscularis of the esophagus had the most BMI-eGenes among medium tissues, and cerebellar hemisphere and nucleus accumbens (NAc) had the most BMI-eGenes across the small tissues (Figure 1E; Table S3). When comparing across the base-BMI and DE-gene sets, we found that most BMI-eGenes are not significant base-eGenes or BMI-DE genes but some overlap is present (Figure S3A). Base-eGenes have greater across-tissues overlap compared to BMI-eGenes (average SS overlap coefficient in base-eGenes SSAv = 0.68 and BMI-Genes SSAv = 0.12, Figure S3B). Frontal cortex base-eGenes and BMI-eGenes both demonstrated replication in the CommonMind Consortium (CMC) frontal cortex dataset (pi1Base = 0.87 and pi1BMI = 0.46, Figures S1B–S1E).

Characterizing BMI-eGenes function

To characterize BMI-eGenes, we assessed constraint scores and gene set enrichments. We assessed constraint using the probability of loss-of-function intolerance (pLI) score, where a high pLI (≥0.9 on a scale from 0 to 1) indicates a gene is evolutionarily constrained in the population, and therefore likely to impact longevity and/or reproductive fitness. Base-eGenes are generally found to be tolerant to loss-of-function variation, as opposed to GWAS hits that demonstrate larger constraint and are therefore more likely to impact reproductive fitness.31 BMI-eGenes are enriched for constrained genes in subcutaneous adipose (p = 6.0 × 10−33), brain cortex (p = 0.025), and skeletal muscle (p = 0.040) (Figure 2A), while base-eGenes had no enrichment of constrained genes. We identified enrichments of functional gene sets across BMI-eGenes, including immunologic systems, such as immunoglobulin complex in the transverse colon (pFDR = 8.1 × 10−5) and network map of SARS-CoV-2 signaling in caudate basal ganglia (pFDR = 7.6 × 10−3). We identified metabolic gene sets including familial hyperlipidemia type 1 enrichment in aortic artery (pFDR = 0.028) and provided evidence that BMI-eGenes are enriched in signaling pathways across tissues, including G-protein coupled receptors in caudate basal ganglia (pFDR = 0.014), transmembrane signaling receptor activity in NAc (pFDR = 0.023), signaling receptor activity in pancreas (pFDR = 0.014), and phosphatidylinositol bisphosphate (PIP2) binding in spleen (pFDR = 1.5 × 10−3) (Figure 2B; Table S4).

Characterization of BMI by SNP interactions

For subsequent analyses, we focused on tissues across the sample size categories with the most BMI-eGenes, including subcutaneous adipose and tibial nerve, esophagus mucosa and muscularis, and lastly cerebellar hemisphere and NAc. We added the transverse colon as an additional digestive tissue due to the presence of numerous gene set enrichments and cortex as an additional brain tissue due to the enrichment for functional constraint. In total, we prioritize eight tissues for further study.

To identify the number of independent SNPs that regulate each eGene in the cis-window, we performed stepwise conditional analysis. Each conditionally independent SNP in the cis-window is referred to as the “top SNP” and the credible set comprises the top SNP and SNPs in high LD with that top SNP. Across tissues, base-eGenes had more credible sets (i.e., more independent top SNPs) in the cis-region locus than BMI-eGenes, but there was a shared pattern of distribution across tissues (Figure S3C; Table S5).

To determine whether BMI × SNP interactions have a predominant direction, i.e., if high BMI is most often associated with a reduction of eQTL activity, we characterized the directionality of the independent BMI × SNP relationships. Using a decision tree based on categorical BMI models (Figures 1A and S4A), we identified four possible directions: positive (absolute eQTL effect size, i.e., beta, is larger at high BMIs, Figure 2C), negative (absolute eQTL activity is larger at low BMI, Figure 2D), flipped (eQTL activity is significant in high and low in opposite direction, Figure 2E), and uncertain (Figure 2F). Across the eight tissues, 4,413 of the top SNPs across BMI-eQTL credible sets are positive, 3,184 are negative, 2,919 are flipped, and 1,617 are uncertain. Generally, we found that each tissue had BMI-eQTL in all possible directions (Figure S4B; Table S5). In all three digestive tissues (mucosa and muscularis of the esophagus and transverse colon), most of the BMI-eQTL had flipped directions. However, the brain categories were not consistent, as the cerebellar hemisphere and NAc BMI-eQTL were most often positive and cortex BMI-eQTL were most often negative (Figure 2G).

We identified 46 significant TFBS enriched in BMI-eQTL across tissues, comprising 40 unique TF motifs (Figure 3A; Table S5). Our study uses bulk RNA sequencing data, including a combination of mRNA from all cell types in the sample. To determine whether cell type proportion plays a role in BMI-eGene discovery, we applied cell type deconvolution (Figure 3B; Table S6). The association of BMI and cell fraction has been previously characterized in the adipose and endocrine system32; therefore, we focused on brain and gut tissues with available single-cell reference panels, which included mucosa in the esophagus, transverse colon, cortex, cerebellar hemisphere, and NAc.

BMI was negatively correlated with the estimated proportion of microglia in NAc (cor = −0.17, p = 6.6 × 10−3) and with myeloid cells in the transverse colon (cor = −0.12, p = 0.013) (Figure 3C). In the cortex, we identified correlations with somatostatin (SST+) interneurons, where SST+/CALB1+ interneurons positively correlated with BMI (cor = 0.15, p = 0.014) and SST+/CALB1− interneurons negatively correlated with BMI (cor = −0.15, p = 0.017).

To determine the impact of cell fraction on BMI-eGene discovery, we repeated the BMI-eQTL analysis, correcting for cell fraction. 36%–94% of BMI-eGenes remain significant after adjusting for cell type proportion (Figure S5A; Table S5), suggesting that both cell type proportion and disruption of cis-regulatory motifs underlie the BMI-dynamic signal identified.

We next examined eQTL activity across cell types in eight cortical BMI-eGenes that remained significant after cell type correction and were predicted to impact the TF binding affinity (Figure 3D). We imputed cell-type-specific expression and called within-cell-type eQTLs to compare the effect direction and magnitude of BMI-eQTL across cell types. Effect directions were opposite between SST+ interneurons and other significant cell types (Figure 3E).

Deciphering mechanisms of BMI dynamic effects using in vitro models

Inflammatory processes secondary to BMI have been reported with evidence that overactivated microglia release proinflammatory cytokines that crosstalk with neurons.33,34,35 To empirically resolve whether inflammatory signals mediate cortical BMI-eQTL activity in neurons, we conducted assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) on hiPSC-derived NGN2-induced glutamatergic neurons at baseline and in the context of three acutely treated (48 h) pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 (60 ng/μL), INFα-2b (500 UI/mL), and TNF-α (100 ng/mL).36 We applied an adapted activity-by-contact (ABC) model using STARE37 to predict dynamic enhancer-gene interactions across inflammatory contexts, linking BMI-eQTL to predicted target genes and fine-mapping eGenes with high LD. In the cortical BMI-eGene analyses, we found a trade-off where the global FDR correction < 0.1 exhibited the strongest external replication, while the FDR < 0.25 threshold sacrificed replication for more potential BMI-eGenes. We further employ in vitro data to assess the choice of global FDR threshold by testing for enrichment of contextually active enhancer relationships in BMI-eQTL compared to the base across thresholds.

To determine whether BMI-eQTL are enriched for loci that show contextual chromatin accessibility, we first defined the ABC-shift as the absolute value of the difference between the ABC score at baseline (Veh) and the ABC score in each context. We defined an ABC-shift for an eQTL as significant if any of the contexts (baseline or inflammatory) had an ABC score > 0.1 and the ABC-shift > 0.015, in line with previous ABC studies.38,39 We find that at the FDR < 0.25 threshold, loci in the BMI-eQTL credible sets are enriched for having a significant ABC-shift compared to the base-eQTL credible sets (p = 0.045). At the FDR < 0.1 BMI-eGene threshold, this enrichment was not significant (p = 0.97). At FDR < 0.25, we identified 770 BMI-eQTL that were active in any context in glutamatergic neurons, of which 186 had a significant ABC-shift in one of the inflammatory contexts (compared to 322 of the 1494 active base-eQTL). Among the contexts, most of the BMI-eQTL, ABC scores were shifted in the INF-α2b (n = 94), followed by IL-6 (n = 64) and TNF-α (n = 63).

Of the 186 cortical BMI-eQTL with dynamic ABC activity, 23 were significantly predicted to disrupt TF binding affinity (Figure 4A; Table S7). The TFs that were enriched in the overall BMI-eQTLs in the cortex (Figure 3A), such as ZNF354C, SP2, and FOXA1, are represented in this glutamatergic ABC-shift set. We demonstrate that the BMI-eGene ALKBH7 is dynamically regulated in the context of INF-α and TNF-α and predicted to be mediated by disrupted ZNF354C binding. In this glutamatergic neuronal context, EHF was the most numerous TFBS that overlapped ABC shifts (9 out of 23 overlapping BMI-eQTL). We used the intersection of ABC-shift and TF binding affinity to fine-map BMI-eQTL credible sets. Each of our eGenes had only one credible set (and therefore one top SNP), but each top SNP had anywhere from 0 to 17 SNPs in LD with the top SNP (Figure 4B). We show an example of a fine-mapped locus SLC16A10, a monocarboxylate transporter also called MCT10, that showed context-wide regulatory shifts at two SNPs (rs6927687 and rs4945850), one of which (rs6927687) had a predicted impact on MZF1 binding and so was likely the BMI-dynamic regulatory SNP (Figure 4D), which had pan context-specific accessibility at its promoter region (Figure 4C).

BMI-dynamic models predict gene expression using main effects and interactions

Our results identify dynamic eQTLs; associations where both BMI and genotype interact to (dys)regulate gene expression. Predicting gene expression from genotype is well established and allows researchers to add gene- and tissue-level context to GWASs, through a penalized regression framework to select variables that significantly predict expression. However, available models have focused on predicting gene expression only using genomic data, called genetically regulated gene expression (GREx). Here, we created predictor models that incorporate SNP, BMI, and BMI-SNP interactions, allowing prediction of dynamic, context-specific gene expression. The “BMI-dynamic models” refer to all models made using the interaction-aware regularization approach using glinternet and were allowed to select from SNP, BMI, and SNP × BMI interactions to predict expression; however, not all BMI-dynamic models will select BMI and may only be predicted using SNPs.

We tested if the crossfold-R2 significantly predicts the 4th fold hold out R2 in each tissue (Figures S6A–S6H; Table S8) and found a significant prediction across tissues tested with R2 ranging from 0.314–0.765. We find that sample size is vital to learning interactions. In tissues with larger sample sizes, the CV-R2 (cross-validation R2) better predicts the hold-out validation R2. We also see that the association between CV-R2 and hold-out R2 is similar across models that contain only main effects as well as interactions. In smaller tissues, we find that models with interactions have poorer hold-out validation, indicating that the sample size is paramount in fitting interaction predictor models.

We compared our BMI-dynamic models to existing GREx predictors using PredictDB-PrediXcan40 (Figure 5A), also built from GTEx data. BMI-dynamic models significantly predicted fewer genes (NGREx = 4851–10012 and NBMI-dynamic = 2389–6657) (Figure 5B), with both shared and unique genes compared to GREx predictor models (Nunique = 262–350 and Nshared = 2,099–6348) (Figure S7B). For genes that have models made using both methods, BMI-dynamic models that include interaction terms explained significantly more variance in gene expression than PredictDB-GREx models (padipose = 2.6 × 10−35, pcortex = 5.1 × 10−23, pNAc = 3.1 × 10−23, ptibial nerve = 8.4 × 10−29, pesophagus mucosa = 1.3 × 10−25, pesophagus muscularis = 1.4 × 10−25, and pcolon = 4.0 × 10−21, Figure 5C). We found that in subcutaneous adipose tissue, BMI is included as a main effect without interactions almost as frequently as interactions occur; however, in the rest of the non-endocrine tissues, most of the models with BMI include interactions, which mirrored the results from the BMI DE-gene and BMI-eGene analysis (Figure 5D).

We assessed for overlap among the two approaches to learn BMI interactions: the BMI-eGenes and the genes with a predictor model that selected a BMI interaction. We evaluated overlap at three different BMI-eGene correction thresholds: the first that uses only a local correction by adjusting for the number of tests estimated based on tensorqtl permutations with the adjusted p value < 0.05, second the locally corrected p value that is further globally FDR-adjusted at FDR < 0.25, and last the approach with the local correction and a global FDR < 0.1 threshold (Figure S7C; Table S10). For the larger tissues, including subcutaneous adipose and tibial nerve, we saw the most overlap among the FDR < 0.25 set (75% in adipose and 86% in nerve), while in the smaller brain tissues, we observed the most overlap using only the local adjustment (34% in brain, 39% in cerebellar hemisphere, and 44% in cortex).

Across tissues, we identified more disease-associated genes using the BMI-dynamic models than the published predictor models (Figures 6B, S8B, and S8C; Tables S9 and S10). In subcutaneous adipose, we identified 42 associations specific for GERD that replicated at least nominally in the external dataset, 35 gene associations specific for depression, and 409 associations shared across traits, compared to the GREx models, which replicated four associations in depression and none in GERD (Figure 6B). When we examined the predictor composition in the models that were shared across depression and GERD, we found in adipose that most of the models had BMI main effects with no interactions (Figure 6C). The replicating BMI-dynamic disease-gene associations are largely tissue-specific, with the most overlap across tissues in the associations that are shared between depression and GERD (Figures S9A–S9C).

Thus far, we have evaluated the interaction of observed BMI on the predicted functional activity of genomic variants but have not examined the role of BMI-dynamic genes in the heritability of BMI. To probe if BMI-dynamic associations are enriched for significance in the BMI GWAS,25 we subset the significant associations from the BMI-dynamic association into two groups: one set that contains genes that only selected SNPs in the predictor model vs. a second group of genes with models that selected BMI with or without interactions and/or SNPs in the model. We tested if the group with BMI had more genes that were significant in the BMI GWAS and found that the significant associations with a BMI predictor were depleted for significance in the BMI GWAS compared to the models with just SNPs (p = 7.8 × 10−4, 25% of models with BMI vs. 31% of models with only SNPs). We further tested for enrichment in significance in a component of BMI, height, and again found that models with BMI are under-represented in height functional significance compared to models with SNPs (pheight=7.1 × 10−33, 53% vs. 76%).

We further assessed the replicability of our BMI-dynamic models by testing for enrichment among the depression associations with the nominally significant S-PrediXcan loci from the recent major depression (MD) GWAS42 and found enrichment across tissues including NAc (p = 0.011), esophagus mucosa (p = 0.023), esophagus muscularis (p = 0.40), and subcutaneous adipose (p = 0.03) (Figures S10A–S10C). Among the genes in NAc that validated in an external dataset and intersected with the MD GWAS S-PrediXcan, we find various relationships with the underlying BMI and height traits. In the NAc, predicted expression of HLA-G and SRGAP2B had a negative relationship with height (Zheight = −6.4 and Zheight = −2.3, respectively), while RPAP1 and STRC have a negative association with BMI but not height (ZBMI = −2.6 and ZBMI = −3,6, respectively), and ST6GALNAC6 has a positive relationship with height and a negative relationship with BMI (ZBMI = −3.2 and Zheight = 4.7), with many genes not significant in either trait (Figure 6D; Table S10).

When we examined gene sets enriched in genes that replicated in the BMI-dynamic associations, we identified enrichment in NAc signal across traits for structural constituent of eye lens (pGERD = 0.040 and pdepression = 0.042), driven by CRYGA and CRYBB2 (Figure 6E), which encode crystallin gamma A and crystallin beta-2. We found disease-specific enrichment in the muscularis of the esophagus in depression, including NADH pyrophosphate activity (p = 6.0 × 10−3), driven by genes NUDT17 and NUDT13, and in the subcutaneous adipose, identified enrichment in lipid binding (p = 0.016).

Discussion

In this work, we demonstrate that BMI interacts with the genome to (dys)regulate gene expression within and beyond the endocrine system. Gene-by-BMI effects on gene expression in the brain are most pronounced in the reward regions, including the basal ganglia and cortex and in cerebellar regions, but not in the classic obesity-associated hypothalamus, which did not have any significant BMI by SNP interactions. The observation that the cortex, cerebellum, and striatum have more BMI-dynamic genetic regulation than the hypothalamus expands our understanding of the variety of brain regions involved in metabolic response. We note that we did not identify any FDR-significant BMI-differentially expressed genes in the brain; instead, BMI DE-genes were primarily found in endocrine tissues. This is intuitive as the endocrine system is responsible for adapting to the fed or fasted state, while the brain is protected from direct BMI effects. We found that both endocrine and brain cortical interactive BMI-eGenes were enriched for genes that are highly constrained, and thus more vital for reproductive fitness. Previous work has shown that classic eQTL is generally less constrained, while GWAS hits demonstrate greater constraint,31 which constitutes a core difference between eQTL and GWAS that limits colocalization. We demonstrate that context-specific eQTL looks more like GWAS hits in terms of loss-of-function intolerance and are more likely to be disease-associated than traditional eQTL.

The epidemiological associations of BMI with adverse outcomes, such as all-cause mortality, exhibit a U-shaped pattern of effect, where mortality is increased at low and high ends of the BMI spectrum.43 To identify eQTL effect differences at high vs. low BMI, we implemented a directionality analysis of BMI-eQTL using categorical BMI eQTL models. We find that BMI-eQTL can exhibit multiple patterns, where some eQTL are more active at high and others at low, and there is no apparent pattern across tissues. In the adipose, BMI-eQTL are most often active at high BMI, whereas in the digestive tissues, most are more active at low BMI, but eQTL in all directions exist in both. This highlights the pattern of BMI-dynamic effects described, some people may have more eQTL active and be more susceptible at high BMI and others at low. Further work about context-wide susceptibility scores is needed to precisely sum the effects of disease risk in the context of environmental factors.

Previous literature has shown that obesity impacts cell type proportion in adipose tissue, where macrophage infiltration and abundance increase in the context of obesity,44 and in the brain, where volume loss and microglial activation are noted.45,46 We see correlations between estimated microglia cell fraction and BMI in the nucleus accumbens; however, the correlation is negative. Previous reports have shown that over-activated microglia are targets of apoptosis,47 which may be an explanation for the negative relationship. We found that the surrogate variables used to residualize the expression matrix correlated with cell fraction and are therefore corrected for in our BMI-eQTL equation; however, we preserved the effects of BMI in the residualization and, therefore, a portion of our signal may still be due to BMI effects on cell type that are difficult to deconvolute. This is a limitation of the study and should be addressed in single-cell datasets with BMI as a measure in donors to validate our findings.

We hypothesize that in the brain, overactive microglia secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines48,49,50 which crosstalk with neurons,33,51 and induce the BMI-dynamic loci identified in this study. We tested this hypothesis by washing a glutamatergic iPSC neuronal system with pro-inflammatory cytokines, measuring baseline and context-specific chromatic accessibility with ATAC-seq in neurons, and testing for overlapping effects with BMI-eQTL using an activity-by-contact model. We find enrichment in BMI-eGenes for contextually dynamic chromatin accessibility and perform fine-mapping of multiple BMI-dynamic loci. We observed a pattern in the cortical single-cell analyses where the eQTL effect direction was flipped between excitatory neurons and SST-positive interneurons; further work across cell types may provide additional meaningful context for our findings. An additional limitation is that iPSC systems model fetal-like cell states, while the effects found here were in postmortem adult. Synchronization of developmental stages may identify even more overlap.

We have shown that the functional capacity to regulate gene expression for a subset of SNPs is not static in a population and instead is dynamic in the context of their physiology. This has implications for modeling BMI-associated complex disease genetic associations. We have shown that by adapting genomic predictor models to include the effects of BMI, we find more disease-associated genes with stronger replication in a pan-ancestry cohort. We find more disease-associated genes despite a lower number of significant models compared to the gold-standard GREx approach using Predixcan-PredictDB. This may be due to differences in quality control, such as a more lenient MAF threshold or the decision to use the entire ancestry cohort.52 It also may be a byproduct of the different machine learning models used, reflected by the differing number of features selected, where perhaps the BMI-dynamic models select fewer predictors with more biological relevance. A final contributor is the increased variance explained in gene expression denoted by BMI-aware models, which capture additional direct and indirect effects of BMI. If the field can adapt GWAS and post-GWAS approaches, including polygenic risk scores to be environment-aware and fit for the individual, we likely will see better predictive performance in clinical settings.

Using the BMI-dynamic models in depression and GERD, we increased our understanding of the underlying biology of the disorders. We show that BMI-dynamic associations overlap the MHC region in the brain and gut in the recent depression GWAS and may be a locus that is contextually active, dependent on BMI. We find evidence that BMI impacts the genomic regulation of crystallin gamma (CRYGA) and beta (CRYBB2) proteins in the NAc, which were relevant across disorders and are proteins that, as of now, are poorly characterized in the brain and more studied in the eye. However, the crystallin proteins are highly expressed in the brain with reported functions, including responding to neuroinflammation by microglia,53 although most of the work in neuronal crystallins has been regarding alpha-crystallins. We identified enrichment in nudix hydrolases NUDT13 and NUDT17 in the esophagus depression associations. The nudix hydrolase subfamily is reported to regulate mitochondrial dynamics and oxidative stress,54 mRNA decapping,55 and is associated with cell cycle and esophageal cancer.56,57

Our results indicate that specific genotypes interact with BMI to dysregulate gene expression (BMI-eQTLs), which can be utilized in TWAS (transcriptome-wide association study) to identify more disease relationships using BMI-dynamic models. We show that disease-associated BMI-dynamic genes are underrepresented in the BMI GWAS compared to genes regulated only by SNPs, suggesting that the heritability of BMI and the response to BMI are often distinct processes. These results provide a personalized insight into how BMI may interact with a given individual’s genotype to impact the transcriptome. For example, within any given population, we might expect that some individuals will have a higher genome-wide burden of BMI-eQTLs, while others have a lower burden. Those with a higher burden may experience a greater transcriptomic impact of BMI changes than those with lower genome-wide BMI-eQTL burdens.

This work supports the guidance that medical counseling on BMI as an isolated measure is insufficient to capture the personalized nature of risk for obesity-associated disorders. BMI is a useful tool in that it is free, easy to measure, and represents an individual’s weight change over time, but we are continually recognizing that BMI as a stand-alone is inadequate when defining individual risk or comparing across populations. Genetic effects, which can be elicited with a family history, in combination with environmental effects, such as BMI changes, sex, age, and psychosocial factors, need to be considered in unison while providing counseling and applying genomic models related to define disorder risk.

Limitations of the study

We used the publicly available dataset GTEx to explore genomic regulatory relationships; however, GTEx is a postmortem dataset and does not represent the full range of BMI, especially the very low and very high BMI ranges. Additionally, the associations of BMI and health outcomes are reported to be related to sex, age, and developmental stage,58,59 which are not represented in just one dataset and may impact replication when applying to other datasets with different demographic features. Further research into the timing of BMI-SNP relationships in the context of age and sex is vital to parsing the impacts of the interactions identified above.

In general, replication of interaction eQTL studies is also challenged by the various choices in significance threshold and correction methods. We observed that the current approaches used to adjust the local cis-window for interaction eQTL, such as eigenMT provided highly variable adjustments in the literature and our study. We developed a local correction protocol that infers the number of tests for adjustment based on the tensorqtl permutation-based estimates, which outperformed the current approaches for local adjustment. From a biological perspective, we reason that the challenge in local multiple testing adjustment is defining the number of independent blocks of correlated SNPs tested, which should not drastically change between eQTL equations in base and BMI analyses. However, if software could calculate empirical distributions from the observed interaction architecture, instead of relying on the permutations derived in the base, we might add specificity to the local correction.

We also note a discordance in the number of interactions estimated in the small tissues across the interaction eQTL and the BMI-dynamic predictor models. For example, NAc had four FDR < 0.1 significant associations but over 100 BMI-dynamic models that selected an interaction term, and these models showed the strongest association replication outside of adipose in the GERD association analysis, indicating that there may be more interactions than estimated by the interaction eQTL analysis. One explanation could be the strict 10% MAF threshold recommended in the literature for eQTL,60 which is lower for the published models41 and the models in this study, where we used a 5% MAF threshold, which demonstrated the least over-fitting.

A second potential impact is the global adjustment of the locally adjusted p value. When we assess the overlap in NAc between BMI-eGenes and BMI-dynamic models with interaction terms at FDR < 0.1, we observed no overlap across the two approaches but at local adjustment threshold, we identified that 25% of BMI-eGenes overlap with BMI dynamic models with interactions in the NAc. This suggests that the global FDR correction may be too strict and remove the biological signal. Future studies should be completed to define a more biologically meaningful global correction, such as adjusting for the number of genes in high gene density regions, where each SNP is tested for many nearby genes, instead of all eGenes in the genome.

Experimental model and study participant details

Genotype-Tissue Expression program (GTEx)

We analyzed transcriptome samples released in the GTEx freeze v8.27 In brief, GTEx is a publicly available resource which includes deceased donor health questionnaire, postmortem whole genome sequencing, and transcriptome (mRNA) sequencing from one or more tissues. For genomic association testing, we required each tissue have a sample size of 100 samples, which resulted in 48 tissues included in subsequent analyses. A detailed description of biospecimen collection, analyte extraction, and GTEx-completed quality control (QC) is available in the version 8 release text and supplement.

We plotted and visualized tissue sample attributes to remove samples with outlying RIN, end base mismatch rate, transcripts detected, alternative alignment, brain weight, and split reads. We excluded height, weight and BMI outliers (+- 3SD) as well as individuals with an amputation. We excluded four individuals who had outlying height values and one individual with an outlying weight listed. The GTEx dataset ranges in age from 21 to 70 years with a median age of 55 and is 67% male. Race of the cohort is reported to be 86% white, 11% black, 1% Asian with remaining contributions from Native Americans and unknown. We chose to include all ancestries in eQTL generation to maximize sample size and application.52 The BMI range in GTEx is 17.03–35.05 kg/m2. The median and mean BMI in this study are 27.37 and 27.33 kg/m2 respectively, compared to the mean BMI of 30 kg/m2 in the United States in 2020.85

CommonMind Consortium

We assess replication for the GTEx Brain-Cortex base-eQTL and BMI-eQTL using the CommonMind Consortium (CMC)61 dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) from CMC release 3. The CMC includes paired genotype and RNA sequencing data from postmortem subjects that is comprised of individuals with psychiatric disorders including schizophrenia and bipolar disorder along with matched controls. We removed one subject with XXY chromosomes and six samples with outlying height, weight, and BMI values from subsequent analysis. Subjects of European descent were selected for testing replication. We ran ancestry analyses using the 1000 genomes reference panel and the unsupervised analysis in the admixture tool.86 In our final DLPFC replication dataset, we had 313 postmortem paired samples of which 60% are male and 40% are female. The mean age at death was 60 (17–98) years, the mean BMI was 27.2 (13.0–48.7).

UK Biobank

The UK Biobank is a cohort of ∼500,000 individuals from the United Kingdom with genotype, lifestyle, health, and anthropometric data.87 For the UK biobank genotype quality control, we implemented an INFO >0.8 threshold and filtered relatedness >0.0625 and subset to European individuals as that is the population used in the published Predixcan models. We selected depression and current smoking as psychiatric traits due to epidemiologic associations with BMI,21,88 the availability of case status and BMI at UKB assessment center visits, and the large sample sizes. To obtain depression and GERD case status, we used data field 20002, which is medical history obtained from verbal interview at the assessment center. We chose to use the depression diagnosis from the assessment center and not use a scale-based approach such as what is present in the Mental health questionnaire because we prioritized data that accompanied a BMI measurement, as is the case in the assessment center and is not available for the online mental health questionnaires. Additionally, the online questionnaires are largely skewed toward female participants, while the assessment center is required of all individuals. BMI values were obtained from data field 21001 from the assessment center physical measures.

Mount Sinai BioMe Biobank

The Mount Sinai BioMe Biobank is a New York City hospital-based electronic health record with paired genomic data for over 30,000 individuals. BioMe has ancestral diversity reflective of the New York City area, and is comprised of roughly 1/3 African American, 1/3 Hispanic American, and 1/3 European American.89 In this analyses we used the freeze 1 data and to retain power, we separated by ancestry and meta-analyzed using METAL, with full information on the ancestry protocol delineation outlined in previous work.75 We filtered relatedness >0.0625 within each ancestry. We defined case status for depression as having two F32.XXX codes and case status for GERD as two K21.XXX codes. The Mount Sinai freeze1 data was genotyped using the global screening array and subsequently imputed with TopMed and filtered with an INFO score >0.7.

Method details

Transcriptome (RNA) sequencing processing

The GTEx transcriptome release transcripts per million (TPM) and read counts, which were aligned to human reference genome GRChg38/hg38 and quantified based on GENCODE release v26. We retained transcripts annotated as autosomal lncRNAs, protein coding, and immune-related genes and filtered out lowly expressed genes, requiring genes have greater than or equal to 6 reads and greater than or equal to 0.1 TPM in at least 20% of samples in each tissue. We normalized read counts between samples using TMM in the edgeR v3.32.0 package.73 We applied a winsorize function, so that any samples deviating more than three standard deviations (sd) from the mean were set to the three sd limit. We used voom from the limma72 v3.46.0 package to transform the normalized count data to log2 counts per million (logCPM). In order to detect hidden sources of variation such as batch or age that might confound association analyses while still retaining the effect of the environmental variable BMI we used the surrogate variable analysis v3.30.1 package.74 We calculated the number of surrogate variables (SVs) to create using the “be” method in the sva package. Finally, we residualized the logCPM matrix with limma to correct for the SVs. This tissue-specific residual gene expression matrix was used for subsequent linear models. Quality control for the CMC transcriptomics analysis mirrored the QC performed for the GTEx dataset.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) processing

We began our quality control with the final v8 phased VCF file provided by GTEx, which contains 46,526,292 sites. A detailed description of QC to generate this final is available from the GTEx consortium v8 supplement. Using plink v2,90 we retained only autosomes and removed insertion-deletion (indel), multi-allelic sites, and ambiguous SNPs. We removed variants with missingness greater than 1% (--geno 0.01) or that deviated from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) (--hwe 0.000001). We removed samples with greater than 1% genotype missingness (--mind 0.01). To identify the largest sources of variance in the genotype data due to factors such as population structure we generated principal components (--pca) which were later used as covariates in eQTL linear models.

In contrast to GTEx and WGS data, CMC genetic data is imputed genotype calls to the HRC platform. For genotype QC, we filtered for imputation quality, Rsq ≥ 0.3,91 removed sex mismatch, and ran relatedness in plink 1.9, removing a related sample if IBD >0.125. In plink2, we applied missingness flags -geno 0.01, mind 0.01, and HWE 1 × 10−6. We computed principal components in plink for later use in the eQTL model as covariates.

We filtered based on minor allele frequency (MAF) in accordance with the size of the tissue,60 as follows: Large tissues (N > 500), MAF >1%; medium tissues (500 > N > 200), MAF >5%; small tissues (N < 200), MAF >10%.

ATAC sequencing in induced NGN2-glutamatergic neurons

Clonal human induced pluripotent stem cells (hIPSCs) were generated from two European donors with no significant medical history (control donors #3182 (XX) and #2607 (XY)).69 The hIPSCs where then induced into excitatory NGN2-glutamatergic neurons (iGLUTs) and harvested at day 24.70,71 The mature iGLUTs (22 days in vitro) underwent 48-h acute exposure to either INFα (500 IU/mL), IL-6 (60ng/mL), TNFα (100ng/mL), or vehicle (0.1% FBS in ultrapure H2O).36 The experimental schematic included two replicates per donor per cue/vehicle treatment. ATAC library prep and sequencing was performed by the Yale Sequencing Core and aligned to hg38 with the full bioinformatic pipeline available elsewhere.36

We next generated three sets of eQTLs using tensorqtl76 with a cis-eGene window distance of 1e6. First, “base-eQTLs”, (Equation 2) are akin to the bulk cis-eQTLs called the seminal GTEx v8 manuscript, with slight differences in QC such as the choice of latent factor correction in the RNA sequencing matrices. The second set of eQTLs are “BMI-eQTLs” and generated by adding a BMI-SNP interaction term (with both main effects) to the base model (Equation 3) with the MatrixEQTL_v2.377 modelLINEAR_CROSS function. Lastly, we calculated a “categorical-eQTL” set by subsetting the within-tissue cohort into BMI quintiles and calculating base-eQTLs (Equation 2) within quintile. For replication analyses, we called base-eQTLs and BMI-eQTLs in CMC according to the same equations and assessed replication using the p1 statistic from the qvalue R package.78

Expression quantitative trait loci significance determination

The standard practice in eQTL studies to define significance utilizes a hierarchical approach, where the smallest raw p-value from each eGene is first adjusted for the number of tests completed within the cis-region (i.e., the number of independent SNPs tested per each eGene) and then adjusted globally for the number of eGenes across the genome. The gold standard for local correction is a permutation-estimated approach, such as used in tensorqtl with the downside that the software does not complete significance assignments on interaction coefficients and instead returns the statistics from the SNP main effect.

For interaction eQTL the common approach is eigenMT,79 which is built to correct the local estimations from the output of the MatrixQTL interaction model, which defined significance based on the statistics of the coefficient of the BMI × SNP interaction term. We found that depending on the package dependencies, eigenMT produced highly variable adjustments and therefore we defined an alternate approach to define interaction significance for eQTLs, “permutation-based estimation” (Equation 4) which assumes the correlation between variants in the genome does not change depending on the statistical test used, and therefore infers the number of effective tests in an eGene window using the tensorqtl output from the base analysis.(Equation 4)Permutation−estimatednumberofteststoadjustinthelocalwindow=0.05/pval_nominal_thresholdtensorqtl−base

We apply the permutation-based adjustment by selecting the SNP in each eGene with the most significant BMI-SNP interaction coefficient p-value and multiple that p-value by the permutation-based estimated number of tests. We tested the replication of Base- and BMI- eGene discovery in Brain-Cortex using the CMC DLPFC data (described above) of each of local correction approaches – the depreciated version of eigenMT, the current version of eigenMT, and permutation-based estimation, all of which were followed by a global Benjamini & Hochberg (BH) FDR correction60 at FDR <0.05, FDR <0.1, and FDR <0.25. (Figure S2A). Based on the replication results in the Cortex, we used the permutation-based estimate at a global BH-FDR <0.1 threshold to define the BMI-eGene set for the study. We identify significant BMI-eGenes using the method defined above and apply the categorical models only to annotate the directionality of the significant interactions, described below. For the base-eGenes we filtered the tensorqtl qval <0.01 to define the base-eGene set.

Testing assumptions of OLS in the significant eQTL models

We next sought to determine if the significant BMI-eQTL models met the assumptions of ordinary least squares (OLS) regression as often as base-eQTL and were robust significant associations. There are four assumptions of OLS: linearity of the predictor and response, independence, normality of the errors, and homoscedasticity (constant error variance). The relationships of SNP and expression are assumed to be linear, as is the case in the eQTL literature. Additionally, relationships of BMI and SNP on expression were visualized and are depicted in Figure 2 and in the supplement. Independence also is satisfied as the data is not time-series. We statistically tested the assumption of normality for each model using the Shapiro-Wilk test, shapiro.test() in stats base R. We assessed the assumption of homoscedasticity using the Koenker’s studentized version of the Breusch-Pagan test, bptest() in the lmtest package, v 0.9–38.

Loss-of-function intolerance enrichment

To test for enrichment of functionally relevant genes in our sets, we obtained the probability of loss-of-function intolerance (pLI) score from gnomAD/2.1.162 for base-eGenes, BMI-eGenes, BMI-DE genes. pLI represents the under-enrichment of loss of function (LOF) variants in the general population in the context of the number of LOF variants expected by chance based on the size of the gene. If a gene is depleted for LOF variants in the population, it indicates the gene is vital for reaching reproductive age and successfully reproducing and therefore is likely to impact human health. We assessed for enrichment for high pLI (pLI >0.90) genes in each category compared to the background of genes using a binomial test, (binom.test in the stats package of R) where x = number of eGenes that are high pLI and significant, n = number of significant genes, and p = number of high pLI genes in this tissue/all genes in background of tissue.

Gene set enrichment

To define enriched gene sets in the sets of BMI-dynamic loci identified, we implemented gene set enrichment using the gprofiler2 package92 with the ‘fdr’ correction method and a custom background set defined as all genes expressed in the tissue of interest. We tested GO, KEGG, REACTOME and Wikipathways. For plotting, we only visualize biologically unique sets, where if two gene intersections are overlapping with an overlap coefficient >0.75, we only plot the gene set with the smallest FDR association.

Conditional analysis

To probe the number of independent signals in each eGene loci we employed stepwise conditional analysis in R for the base-eGenes and BMI-eGenes. For each eGene, we generated a linkage disequilibrium (LD) matrix using the genotype data from the eQTL analysis and the cor() function in R with the pairwise complete observation setting. We identified the top SNP signal, which is the SNP with the smallest SNP coefficient p-value and all SNPs with r > 0.999 correlation. We added the most significant SNP in the model and re-ran the eQTL model for the eGene window SNPs. If the SNP coefficient p-value of new most significant SNP in the iteration was less than 0.001, it was added into the model and a subsequent iteration was run. The conditional analysis iterated until the top p-value was ≥ 0.001.

For the interaction conditional analysis, we repeated this process except at start of the conditioning we add the top SNP, the top SNP × BMI interaction, and BMI into the model. The top SNP for the iteration model is the SNP with the smallest p-value of the interaction coefficient. At each iteration if the interaction coefficient p-value >0.001, we add the top SNP and the top SNP × BMI terms as covariates.

We identified credible sets of base-eQTL and BMI-eQTL for each eGene by selecting the top SNPs that were conditionally independent and had an original eQTL p-value that was locally significant. Each top SNP marks a discrete credible set that also includes SNPs in LD r2 > 0.8.

Interaction directionality assignment

For the top SNP in each BMI-eQTL credible set we characterized the direction of the interaction of SNP and BMI into four categories: positive, negative, flipped, and uncertain, using the categorical BMI models. The full decision tree is available in Figure S3.

Transcription factor binding site analysis

To test if BMI-eQTL disrupt specific transcription factors compared to base-eQTL we implemented transcription factor binding site (TFBS) affinity analysis using the atSNP package80 and the Jaspar2020 motif library.63 We tested all the variants in each credible set as our TFBS input, annotated to dbSNP151 and computed the affinity scores using ComputeMotifScore() and p-values using ComputePValues(). We assessed the significance of the binding affinity change using an FDR correction of the p-value rank output. We implemented a one-sided binomial test using binom.test in the stats package of R to determine if there is an enrichment for disruption of TFs in the BMI-eQTL. The expected probability p is the number of significant SNPs in a motif X in base-eQTLs/number of SNPs tested in motif X in base-eQTL. The trials n equals the number of SNPs tested in motif X in BMI-eQTLs and the successes x are the number of FDR-significant SNPs in motif X in the BMI-eQTLs. We implemented an FDR correction to the binomial p-values to correct for the number of motifs tested.

Cell type deconvolution

We downloaded single cell data across tissues across studies to deconvolute the GTEx bulk data with the most BMI-eQTL. Data was available for cortex,64 nucleus accumbens,65 cerebellar hemisphere,64 transverse colon,66 and mucosa of the esophagus.67 We converted all counts data to CPM and used Cibersortx81 to construct a signature matrix. We applied that signature matrix to deconvolute the GTEx bulk tissue and obtain estimated cell type fractions for each GTEx sample. We tested if cell type was controlled for in the RNA sequencing quality control, completed with SVA. The estimated cell fractions correlated robustly with surrogate variables (Figure S4A) however because we preserved the effect of BMI in SV creation and found BMI correlates with cell fraction there may be residual impacts on discovery. Using the cell fraction and RNA sequencing, we apply Bayesian estimation using bMIND82 to estimate cell type-specific gene expression matrices. Of note, when using bMIND only a set number of cell type-specific matrices can be imputed, so at this step we collapse cell types of the same kind (i.e., Excitatory neuron population 1 and 2 are collapsed, Table S6). We then repeat our primary eQTL framework within each cell type to construct cell-type specific eQTL statistics.

Activity by contact scoring of interaction-eQTL with ATAC sequencing

We selected base- and BMI-eGene credible sets significant in excitatory neurons from deconvolution. We use adapted activity-by-contact (ABC) model using STARE37 to score relative regulatory activity of the eQTL in the interaction terms using the R package. The ABC score is calculated using by integrating “activity”, which here is defined by -log10(p-value) of the SNP × BMI interaction coefficient and “contact” which we define using ATAC-sequencing in glutamatergic iPSC neurons along with the distance to eGenes to determine predicted regulatory activity of an SNP toward a specific gene. We assess a baseline contact of ATACseq peaks with no inflammatory markers applied and ATACseq in the context of IL-6, TNFα, and interferon-α. The methods of cell culture can are cited elsewhere.93 We visualized the distribution of ATAC seq scores across contexts and consulted the literature,38 and chose to set a threshold requiring the absolute value of the ABC score difference between vehicle and context >0.015 and ABC score in either vehicle or the context to be >0.1.

Creation of BMI-dynamic predictor models

To predict gene expression from SNPs, BMI and SNP × BMI interactions, we use the cv.glinternet() from the glinternet R package.83 Glinternet employs a Hierarchical Group-Lasso Regularization to select predictive features. The software is intended to learn interactions, and if an interaction coefficient is non-zero then both main effects are included with a non-zero coefficient. We employ a 4-fold approach whereby we train the models using a 3-fold cross validation and hold out the fourth fold for within-sample validation. We ensured the hold out validation fold has an even distribution of BMI using used StratifiedKFold from the scikit-learn package in python3.7.3. We treat SNP as a continuous measure, so models can be applied to dosage data. We used the same genotype QC steps described in the eQTL set with a MAF threshold to 5%, which demonstrated reduced overfitting than 1%. For BMI-Interaction model to be significant the cross-fold R2 >0.1 and p < 0.05. Additionally, in the hold-out fold the predicted values must significantly predict the observed values (p-holdout <0.05).

We compare our interaction-aware models to the published GTEx v8 elastic net predictor models on PredictDB,41 which use Predixcan40 to predict expression. Elastic net accounts for only the main effects of SNPs using a 1% MAF threshold and the models have other analysis-specific differences including latent factor correction. For genes that had a significant model in both the published and BMI-dynamic we calculated the difference in R-Squared and used a t test to evaluate if models with model that select BMI (with or without an interaction) had a larger gain in R-squared compared to the published data.

BMI-dynamic predictor model application

To calculate predicted expression using the PredictDB models, called GREx, we used the Predixcan python package and the elastic net v8 GTEx PredictDB predictor models. To calculate predicted expression for the BMI dynamic models, we manually multiply each predictor by the beta coefficient output by glinternet in R. The glinternet package scales the input predictors while building the model but returns the unscaled coefficients.

Once predicted expression is calculated, we test the association with psychiatric phenotype using a logistic regression in R using glm() with family = “binomial”. For the trait association analyses, we used age, sex, BMI, and PC1-10 as covariates in the Predixcan-PredictDB models and age, sex, PC1-10 as covariates in the BMI-Interaction model. We tested replication using a pi1 statistic from the qvalue package or the FDR significant associations in the UK Biobank.

We tested if the BMI-dynamic models replicated published associations with depression using the S-PrediXcan84 output of the recent major depression GWAS,42 using the summary statistics that do not contain the UK Biobank or 23andMe. We applied a binomial test to assess if the depression associations found in the UK biobank BMI-dynamic depression association were enriched for nominally significant GWAS MD depression loci. In addition to major depression, we downloaded GWAS summary statistics from the most recent BMI25 and height68 GWAS and ran S-PrediXcan in our tissues of interest. Using this data, we evaluated if our BMI-dynamic models are also implicated in the genomic regulation of BMI and height by running a binomial enrichment for significance in the BMI-dynamic models that selected BMI vs. the models that only select SNP effects.
