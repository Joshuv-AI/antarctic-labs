 ABSTRACT
This report investigates the embedding of Cu-coated silica optical fibers into stainless steel substrates and
specimens using directed energy deposition (DED) and high-temperature brazing to support structural
health monitoring in advanced nuclear reactors. To overcome thermal expansion mismatches at extreme
operational temperatures (400◦ C–800◦ C), both manufacturing methods were evaluated based on their abil-
ity to induce pre-compressive residual strains while ensuring fiber survivability. For the DED approach,
process parameter optimization established that protecting the fiber within a steel capillary tube alongside
a co-placed braze wire successfully mitigates thermal shock, generating a repeatable residual compressive
strain of approximately −5,000 µm/m. For the brazing approach, utilizing a BAg-8 eutectic filler metal
(liquidus 779◦ C) in a vertical orientation provided superior volumetric filling compared to horizontal se-
tups by leveraging gravity and capillary action. However, brazing longer lengths led to localized Cu coat-
ing solubilization into the molten pool and subsequent fiber breakage during cooling, identifying shorter
embedding lengths as the more feasible configuration for point sensing applications with these material
choices specifically. Leveraging these insights, a short, vertically brazed fiber specimen was utilized to fab-
ricate a Fabry–Perot cavity (FPC) point sensor tack-welded to a representative component. Dynamic strain
testing conducted under thermal cycles up to 700◦ C demonstrated exceptional frequency tracking agree-
ment (within 1 Hz) between the integrated FPC sensor and a reference laser Doppler vibrometry (LDV)
system, alongside minimal cavity length hysteresis (< 4 µm). These results validate the mechanical ro-
bustness and high-fidelity diagnostic capabilities of the developed sensor integration strategies for harsh,
next-generation reactor environments.
 1. INTRODUCTION
As optical fiber–based sensors continue to be developed to operate and function in increasingly harsh en-
vironments, sensing a wider array of different measurands, they become more and more promising as sen-
sors that can support the existing and developing fleet of nuclear power plants. These types of sensors are
immune to electromagnetic radiation, have a generally minimal footprint due to their small size, can often-
times withstand extreme temperatures, and in certain cases enable distributed sensing in addition to point
sensing capabilities. As such, there are numerous potential sensing applications geared toward nuclear re-
actors, such as temperature mapping (Hyer, Giuliano, and Petrie 2023), local power monitoring (Birri and
Blue 2020), online corrosion monitoring (Daniel C Sweeney, Adrian M Schrell, and Christian M Petrie
2021), and distributed strain measurements (Holden C. Hyer and Christian M. Petrie 2024), among sev-
eral other possibilities (Fleming et al. 2018; Arora et al. 2021; Weathered and Anderson 2017). The abil-
ity to measure up to extremely high temperatures is of particular interest to the nuclear industry, as Gen
IV reactors that operate at significantly higher temperatures than light-water reactors are in development
(Chen et al. 2021). Moreover, recent research on radiation-induced attenuation in silica optical fibers indi-
cates that proper dopant selection can mitigate darkening for doses relevant for long-term reactor operation
(Sweeney and Petrie 2024; Shafir et al. 2022; Gallet et al. 2021), and the fundamental understanding of
radiation-induced sensor drift has been progressed such that one may better define which sensing measur-
ands are appropriate to target depending on the temperatures and expected dose rates (Petrie et al. 2019;
Petrie and Sweeney 2023; Kashaykin et al. 2023).
One manner by which optical fiber sensors can be implemented in nuclear reactor applications is to con-
duct structural health monitoring (SHM); generally speaking, this involves measuring either static or dy-
namic strain of structural components or systems in order to assess if any degradation or failure modes
have occurred. In light-water reactors, conventional approaches have been implemented involving piezo-
electric sensors to perform such measurements (e.g., acoustic emission or resonant frequency monitor-
ing), but such implementations are limited to ex-core components, in which operational temperatures are

 1

modest (not exceeding 400◦ C) (Meyer et al. 2011; Hutton, Friesel, and Dawson 1993; Bamford and Rishel
2002; Nakamura and Terada 1985; Cheng 2007). Optical fibers present an attractive solution to capture
data identical to data captured by piezoelectric sensors to support a reactor’s SHM needs, as they have a
higher resilience to radiation (expanding the potential application cases), they could acquire distributed
measurements or quasi-distributed measurements in a cascaded sensor, and they will not be subject to elec-
tromagnetic interference (which may be a concern with piezoelectric devices). One of the primary chal-
lenges with using optical fibers to collect SHM data is that they may require direct or indirect bonding or
embedment into nuclear reactor components or structural materials. Fortunately, for temperatures relevant
for Gen III reactors, numerous techniques for bonding and embedment have been demonstrated, such as
ultrasonic additive manufacturing (UAM) or laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) (Petrie et al. 2019; Holden C.
Hyer and Christian M. Petrie 2024; Havermann et al. 2015). These bonding methods involve elevated lo-
cal temperatures to allow the fiber to be put in a state of residual compressive strain, but this compressive
strain is not as high as it would be if the bulk component were at a high temperature.
For Gen IV reactors, for which the operating temperatures can be generally between 400◦ C and 800◦ C,
the limited pre-compressive strains induced from UAM or LPBF will pose a high risk of the fiber break-
ing due to tension from thermal expansion mismatch between the fiber and the component to which it may
be bonded. As such, there are a handful of techniques that have been investigated that involve bonding at
higher temperatures, such as directed energy deposition (DED) (Snider et al. 2023; Zou et al. 2018; Zou
et al. 2020), brazing (Sandlin and Hokkanen 2003; Sandlin et al. 2007; Birri et al. 2025), and spark plasma
sintering (Zhang et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025). It should be noted that DED involves local laser heating
like with LPBF, but the larger laser power and beam size result in more heat being dumped into the com-
ponent during bonding. Any of these methods would be expected to put the fiber under more compressive
strain after bonding and cooldown, enabling the fiber to operate at higher temperatures relevant for Gen IV
reactors. Based on previous experience with both techniques at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL)
(Birri et al. 2025; Hyer et al. 2026), this report focuses on the implementation of both DED and brazing to
make functional optical fiber sensors that are capable of either static or dynamic strain monitoring. There
is a stronger emphasis on brazing in this report because the bulk of the developmental work toward the
DED bonding technique was reported on in great detail previously (Hyer et al. 2026).
Of course, either brazing or DED may be the more advantageous option, depending on the desired nuclear
reactor application. This will depend on numerous factors such as if the fiber is intended for internal rout-
ing through complex or dense components, if the fiber is to be bonded during or after component fabri-
cation, or the operating temperature of the component during reactor operations. Brazing would gener-
ally be utilized for bonding to a sensing element, which is then bonded to a reactor component of inter-
est; this would be amenable to long pipes, thinner-walled components, and larger containment structures.
DED, contrarily, would be more relevant for integration into the reactor component itself; this would be
amenable to printed components, which could involve complex geometries, such as a heat exchanger. DED
may be more limited in terms of the maximum operational temperature of the embedded sensor because
the bonding technique ultimately relies on local heating, which may result in significant tensile strain that
can be imposed on the fiber at high temperature. Contrarily, a proper implementation of the brazing tech-
nique should put the fiber under compression for the majority of the temperature range, from room temper-
ature up to the melting point of the braze filler.
All these nuances should be considered in the context of this report, in which both brazing and DED are
explored as bonding techniques that could enable the use of optical fiber sensors for SHM in advanced
reactor applications. The experimental progressions reported herein are therefore focused on advancing
either technique to yield a bonded optical fiber sensor that is properly robust in the associated thermal con-
ditions. As such, a successful bond is generally characterized based on the level of pre-compressive strain
achieved, and a successful sensor, which implements either bonding technique, is measured based on the

 2

ability to accurately resolve vibrational frequencies of advanced reactor–relevant components to which the
sensor is bonded.

 2. MATERIALS AND EQUIPMENT USED
2.1 MATERIALS AND EQUIPMENT FOR OPTICAL FIBER BRAZING
All brazing experiments conducted in this work consisted of Cu-coated silica optical fibers. The Cu-coated
fibers used herein were Cu1300 optical fibers (IVG Fiber; Toronto, Ontario, Canada). These fibers have a
Ge-doped core (9 µm diameter), a pure silica cladding (125 µm diameter), and a Cu coating with an ad-
ditional inner carbon layer (total coating diameter of 165 µm). Copper was chosen as the coating material
because it enables limited optical losses for typical optical interrogation wavelengths up to Gen IV nu-
clear reactor–relevant temperatures in an inert environment (Popov et al. 2012). Gold was not chosen as
a coating material to assess herein because it has been observed that Au coatings tend to be more fragile
(Laarossi, Quintela-Incera, and López-Higuera 2019), previous brazing experiments exhibited poor strain
coupling (Birri et al. 2024), and Au-coated fibers can have high levels of optical attenuation at low temper-
atures (< 200◦ C) (Reinsch and Henninges 2010).

 Figure 1. High-temperature equipment used for brazing of optical fibers. The two furnaces used are
(a) the Evenheat HF TAP 1210B and (b) the Thermo Scientific Lindberg Blue M Model, along with (c) an
 apparatus that can be backfilled with inert Ar and house specimens to which fibers are bonded during
 brazing experiments.

The braze material used for the brazing experiments was BAg-8 (a recognized material for joining both
Cu and steel), which is a eutectic composition of Cu-Ag with 28 wt % Cu. Because BAg-8 is a eutectic
composition, this alloy has the benefit of transitioning rapidly to a fine lamellar structure of α-Cu and β-Ag
upon cooling below the liquidus point (Qin et al. 2024) (whereas off-eutectic compositions will precipitate
out either α- or β-rich phases, resulting in liquation, which can affect joint quality). Furthermore, BAg-8
has an ideal liquidus point for optical fiber brazing (779◦ C) (Xie and Zhang 1998); this temperature is suf-
ficiently above expected operational temperatures for most Gen IV reactor designs but low enough to en-
sure that the optical fiber does not undergo optical losses due to phenomena such as devitrification (which
can start to become severe at temperatures > 1,000◦ C for annealing times relevant to brazing processes)
(Rose 2002). Off-eutectic compositions of Cu-Ag can approach 1,000◦ C when they are particularly rich in
Cu or Ag. Finally, it is worth noting that since BAg-8 does not contain any volatile elements that may typ-
ically be used to lower the melting point (such as Zn, Cd, or Sn), the brazing can be done in inert furnace

 4

conditions, in which the associated braze times would otherwise cause outgassing of such volatile compo-
nents, affecting the braze metal’s wettability; this was observed in a previous study in which BAg-7 (which
contains Sn) was used in an attempt to braze Cu-coated fiber in an inert furnace braze (Birri et al. 2025).
There were multiple specimens to which Cu-coated fibers were bonded in the brazing experiments con-
ducted in this work. In this case, the term specimen refers to a bulk component that both houses the fiber
and can be bonded to a reactor component for the sake of monitoring it for structural health. In all cases,
these specimens were SS316. Both tubular specimens and rectangular channel specimens have been con-
sidered for housing the Cu-coated optical fibers. In the case of the tubular specimens, braze filler wires
are inserted into tubes in co-location with the Cu-coated optical fibers, such that they can be melted and
re-solidified, bonding the Cu-coated fiber internally within the tube; these braze processes occur with the
tubular specimens oriented vertically. In the rectangular channel specimens, the Cu-coated fibers are laid
in co-location with braze filler wires, and the braze is melted to form a pool in the channel and re-solidified
to embed the Cu-coated fiber within the channel; these braze processes occur with the rectangular channels
oriented horizontally. More details on the exact dimensions and geometric descriptions of these specimens
are provided in Section 3.1.
The high-temperature furnaces used for fiber brazing are shown in Figure 1(a) and 1(b). The high-temperature
furnace in Figure 1(a) is a Thermo Scientific Lindberg Blue M Model and was used for horizontal braz-
ing experiments, in which the fiber was laid within a rectangular channel specimen along with braze filler
wires. The Lindberg Blue M furnace has a heated length of 61 cm (24 in.) and a total furnace length of ap-
proximately 85 cm. The high-temperature furnace in Figure 1(b) is an Evenheat HF TAP 1210B and was
used for vertical brazing experiments, in which the fiber was packed within the tubular specimens along
with similar braze material. The Evenheat furnace has a heated height of 33 cm and a total furnace height
of 48 cm. For some of the vertical brazing experiments with the tubular specimens, the tubes themselves
were backfilled with inert Ar during the brazing experiments. For the other brazing experiments, the spec-
imens were placed in a custom apparatus with a fiber feedthrough, as shown in Figure 1(c). Temperatures
in all furnace experiments were monitored with a type K thermocouple, which was interrogated at 1 Hz
with a Squirrel data logger (2020 Series, Grant Instruments).

2.2 MATERIALS AND EQUIPMENT FOR DIRECTED ENERGY DEPOSITION EMBEDDING
The DED equipment used herein for fiber embedding was an InssTek (Daejeon, South Korea) MX-Lab
system. This is a three-axis DED machine with a 150 × 150 × 150 mm build volume. The MX-Lab uti-
lizes a 500 W ytterbium-fiber laser with a 400 µm beam diameter. Powder delivery is managed by a built-
in vibratory method feeding system featuring a multi-hopper setup and providing accurate control over the
SS316L powder feed rate. Argon is used as the cover gas for the embedding process. SS304 build plates
were used as the starting substrate, and pre-alloyed SS316L powder acquired from PAC (Ohio, USA) was
used as depositing material.
Process optimization studies were conducted to determine the optimal parameters that would be used to
embed Cu1300 optical fibers. The exact details of this process optimization study are described in a pre-
vious report (Hyer et al. 2026) but are summarized herein. Several experiments were conducted, centered
around a central composite design (CCD) to establish a viable processing window for consolidating dense,
defect-free SS316L cubes. Parameter sweeps evaluated the independent and coupled effects of laser power,
travel speed, scan strategy, and powder mass flow rates on volumetric density and geometric retention.
Preliminary evaluations revealed that lower powder feed rates (< 4.0 g/min) failed to achieve sufficient
dimensional buildup, while excessively elevated laser powers induced severe overmelting and melt pool
instability. Ultimately, it was determined that the optimal parameters were a powder feed rate of 5.0 g/min,
a 240 W laser power, 950 mm/min travel speed, 0.32 mm toolpath spacing, 0.15 mm layer thickness, and

 5

a contour-fill-contour (CFC) plus zigzag scan strategy. This specific parameter matrix yielded the best di-
mensional accuracy both in terms of height and maintaining the rectangular cross section. It also ensured
rapid, dimensionally accurate sensor coverage without concurrently amplifying the risk of thermal degra-
dation to the sequestered optical fiber.

2.3 OPTICAL INTERROGATION EQUIPMENT
There were three different optical interrogators employed in this work to either assess the integrity of the
fiber bonding/embedment in a given brazing/DED experiment, or to provide static or dynamic strain data
from a bonded fiber, demonstrating its use as a sensor for SHM. These optical interrogators are described
in this section and are listed as follows: (1) a Luna Innovations OBR-4600 system, (2) a Luna Innovations
Hyperion si155, and (3) a custom low-coherence interferometry (LCI) system. These three systems have
different functions in terms of use cases and the data that can be obtained from them. The OBR-4600 can
be used for interrogation before, during, and after heated bonding processes and can provide distributed
data on strain and optical losses; this makes the OBR-4600 particularly powerful as a diagnostics tool,
specifically for longer lengths of bonded or embedded fiber. The Hyperion si155 can obtain optical spec-
tra from embedded or bonded point sensors—such as a Fabry–Perot cavity (FPC) or fiber Bragg grating
(FBG)—at a data acquisition rate up to 1 kHz, which makes this system useful as a diagnostics tool as well
(e.g., to assess sensor reflectivity). These spectra can be used to assess dynamic strain based on the dy-
namic shifts in peaks in the reflected spectra. The custom LCI system is specifically tailored to interrogate
FPCs with high precision and extremely high sampling rates (> 100 kHz), making it particularly power-
ful for dynamic strain monitoring, such that even weak, high-frequency vibrations can be detected. These
three interrogators are summarized and compared in Table 1, and the optical network topologies of each
interrogator are shown in Figure 2.

To describe the Luna Innovations OBR-4600 system further, it is coupled with an FOS-008 optical switch,
and was employed to interrogate Rayleigh-backscattered signals from optical fibers during brazing exper-
iments. Operating on the principles of optical frequency domain reflectometry (OFDR), the system uses
a tunable laser source to supply light for a Mach–Zehnder interferometer. The Mach–Zehnder interfer-
ometer enables measurement of light intensity as a function of optical frequency, which is converted to
the time-of-flight domain via Fourier transform. By isolating discrete fiber segments and performing in-
verse Fourier transforms, the resulting spectral intensities are cross-correlated against reference scans to
resolve distributed spectral shifts. These working principles of the OBR-4600 are described in more detail
in a previous paper (Daniel C. Sweeney, Adrian M. Schrell, and Christian M. Petrie 2020). This method-
ology enabled continuous monitoring of the fiber’s simultaneous response to elevated temperatures and
differential thermal expansion throughout thermal cycling. Furthermore, comparing pre- and post-bonding
spectral shifts allowed for the calculation of residual strain and the identification of the neutral-strain bond-
ing temperature, while amplitude data as a function of fiber length provided a diagnostic means to correlate
reflectivity losses or mechanical breaks with specific phases of the bonding processes.
The Hyperion si155, like the OBR-4600, uses a tunable laser source, but it does not involve the complexity
associated with OFDR with Mach–Zehnder interferometry; rather, the Hyperion si155 is a simple, multi-
channel swept-wavelength interferometer. This instrument relies on a tunable laser source that is swept

 6

over a wavelength range of 1,460–1,620 nm. The transmitted light is compared with a known reference
wavelength to produce an interference pattern, which is recorded with a photodetector to track the instan-
taneous wavelength of the tunable laser source. Light that is reflected from intrinsic sensors (FPCs, FBGs)
is returned to a second photodetector that measures the reflected intensities as the wavelength of the tun-
able laser is varied. This produces a spectral interference pattern. Sweeney et al. provide a more detailed
description of the mathematics associated with relating the changes to the interference pattern to measure-
ment of dynamic strain (Sweeney and Petrie 2022). The Hyperion si155 was thus employed in this work
for diagnostic assessments of brazed FPCs as they were fabricated and while they were tested in high-
temperature vibration monitoring experiments, as described in Section 5.
The all-fiber LCI system, as mentioned previously, is engineered specifically for high-speed monitoring
of FPC displacement (which is directly linked to dynamic strain imposed on the FPC). The LCI system
has a broadband light source coupled into an FPC through an optical circulator before returning through
the circulator and entering a Mach–Zehnder interferometer. Two arms of the interferometer are offset by a
constant phase and are co-located in a benign environment to mitigate unsymmetrical polarization changes
between each leg. The light entering each leg passes through a delay line and a phase shifter before be-
ing reflected by a Faraday mirror and recombining in a 3 × 3 coupler. After recombination, the interfering
light enters two photodetectors, and the change in FPC length can be demodulated from the interference
patterns measured by the two photodetectors, based on the time-dependent amplitude ratio between them.
This demodulation scheme is described in further detail by Sweeney et al. (Sweeney, Sweeney, and Petrie
2021). Because the system can be implemented in hardware to reach speeds over 1 MHz, it is uniquely
capable of capturing the high-frequency resonant shifts necessary for detecting structural damage in real
time.

3.2 DED FIBER EMBEDDING EXPERIMENTS
A series of fiber embedding experiments were performed with DED, as described in full detail in a pre-
vious report (Hyer et al. 2026). These experiments are summarized in Table 3. These experiments are
given designation numbers “D1, D2, etc.” to differentiate them from the brazing experiments, which are
described in Section 3.1. In these DED experiments, the Cu1300 optical fiber was embedded into a SS304
base plate, and the DED system used SS316L powder. A variety of different features were varied from
experiment to experiment. In most experiments, the fibers were protected in capillary tubes of SS316L
held in place with rectangular support structures. The support structures were either the height of the cap-
illary tube OD, just above the capillary tube diameter, or just below the capillary tube diameter. In some
experiments, a strapping process was implemented in which single track lines of SS316L material were
deposited transversely across the capillary at a periodic spacing of 1–3 mm across the embedment region.
In experiment D1, optimized parameters determined from cube optimization studies (described in Section
2.2) were used to directly embed the Cu1300 optical fiber into a SS304 base plate, but this process resulted
in too significant a thermal shock to the thinly coated Cu1300 fiber. As such, experiments D2–D6 included
a capillary tube to protect the fiber, while varying heights of support structures were used to hold the cap-
illary tube in place, as depicted in Figure 6(a). Building the supports to the height of the OD appeared to
be the optimal solution, but embedding with 5–10 g/min either resulted in fiber failure or showed no sign
of fiber embedding. As such, in experiments D7–D8, to circumvent the excessive thermal shock associated
with continuous direct deposition over the fixtured tube, a segmented laser-tacking or strapping strategy
was implemented. However, this method caused fracturing at the ingress and egress boundaries for the
fiber. Finally, in experiments D9–D10, a BAg-1 braze wire (with a 0.8 mm diameter) was introduced into
the capillary tube, parallel to the fiber. These braze wires enabled the successful braze of the Cu1300 fibers
(assumedly by reducing the thermal shock to the fibers); the residual strain data were collected by collect-
ing OBR scans of the fibers before and after embedment.

 4. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS FROM BONDING EXPERIMENTS
4.1 BRAZING RESULTS
The experimental results from the brazing experiments, summarized in Table 2, are presented in this sec-
tion. For experiments B1–B4, spectral shift data that were actively collected during experimentation with
the OBR were converted to strain based on the effective photoelastic coefficient of fused silica. Experi-
ments B1–B2 were inspected visually in post-analysis, while experiments B3–B4 were inspected with
x-ray radiography using a Zeiss VoluMax 9 Titan, with a process reported previously (Birri et al. 2025).
One of the assemblies from experiment B5 was destructively evaluated by cutting the brazed section of the
tube in half with a diamond wire and polishing the cross section of the tube with lapping paper. The cross-
sectional interface was optically imaged with a Keyence VHX-6000 optical microscope, and backscatter
electron micrographs were taken with a Tescan MIRA3 operating at 20 kV.
Photographs of experiments B1 and B2 are shown in Figure 7(a) and 7(b), respectively. In experiment B1,
there was a short region (∼5 cm) of optical fiber in which the braze material fully encased the fiber, while
the remaining length had separation between the fiber and the braze material. In experiment B2, the braze
material did not appropriately wet to the confined specimen. This resulted in the braze material consolidat-
ing into balls and not bonding to the fiber. This wettability issue highlights the challenge with the horizon-
tal brazing configuration associated with experiments B1 and B2; there is a general lack of a driving force,
such as appropriate capillary action or gravity, to assist in the braze. For example, recommended gap sizes
with pre-placed Ag as the filler metal for brazing range from 0.05 to 0.2 mm (Lemma et al. 2025) to ensure
appropriate capillary action.

The measured strain on the fiber throughout experiment B1 is shown in Figure 8. The spatially and tem-
porally dependent data of Figure 8(a) show that the fiber was only bonded near the beginning and the end
of the braze region of the experiment, as consistent with the visual inspection photographs of the fiber pro-
vided in Figure 7(a). The distinct region of positive “strain” in Figure 8(a) is really associated with the

periment B1, the fiber bonded to the BAg-8 shortly after solidification, which shows that brazing with this

the observed braze region lengths in Figure 9(c) and 9(d) and that the molten braze material drew down
to shorter lengths as excess spatial volume between the fibers and the specimen tubes was filled. What is
observed from the optical images of the experiment B3 lead-in fiber is that the braze material partially sol-
ubilized and stripped off the Cu coating as it drew down in length, leaving lengths of exposed fused silica.
Figure 9(b) notably showcases this phenomenon, in which a solidified braze bead is bonded to the fiber,
just underneath an exposed region of fiber, suggesting that the stripped Cu coating is solubilized within this
solidified bead. This Cu stripping effect therefore presents a fragility risk at the insertion point in the braze,
and this should be considered for vertically oriented braze configurations in which the coating has good
solubility in the braze filler.
On a more positive note, the x-ray radiographs in Figure 9(c) and 9(d) are promising in the sense that rea-
sonably long lengths of uniform braze regions were formed in these experiments; this was especially the
case for the larger-ID tube in experiment B3. This shows that the vertical orientations of braze apparatuses
for experiments B3 and B4 have the benefit of gravity acting as a driving force to ensure that the braze ma-
terial fills the desired volume, whereas the horizontal orientations of braze apparatuses for experiments B1
and B2 have challenges because neither gravity nor capillary action are leveraged to force the braze mate-
rial to wet appropriately.
The measured strain on the fibers throughout experiments B3 and B4 is shown in Figure 10. The spatially

and temporally dependent data in Figure 10(a) and 10(c) indicate that the lengths associated with the braze

ably lower than those for experiment B1, but a zero-strain state at these temperature values would likely
still enable application at Gen IV reactor–relevant temperatures, as the tension would not be so severe that
it would exceed the high-temperature tensile strain capacity of the Cu-coated silica fiber. The reason for
which experiment B1 had considerably higher levels of pre-compressive strain on the fiber in comparison
with experiments B3 and B4 is unclear at this time.
In general, there are a few lessons that were learned from experiments B1–B4. First, it was discovered that
bonding long lengths of Cu-coated fiber with BAg-8 braze material presents challenges from a Cu solu-

 17

bility standpoint and that material choices (either with the braze filler material or the fiber coating) could
be optimized to prevent this issue. Second, the poor braze wettability in the horizontal brazing experi-

a vertical configuration provides a gravitational force that ensures the braze material bonds appropriately.
Finally, the microscopy and x-ray radiography results from experiments B3 and B4 reveal that the braze
drawdown, associated with trying to vertically braze longer lengths of fiber, is a driving factor for the strip-
ping of the Cu coating from the fiber; this suggests that attempting to bond shorter lengths of Cu-coated
fiber, with an improved filler material packing factor, may prevent drawdown and thus increase the chance
of fiber survivability.

from the apparatus was hermetically sealed with Loctite EA 11C epoxy. The proximal end of the lead-in

The FPC was tack-welded with small SS316 strips to a 0.91 m (3 ft) long rod at the approximate axial cen-
ter of the rod, as shown in Figure 14(c) and 14(d). The rod had a diameter of 1.27 cm (0.5 in.). The rod
would act as a representative high-temperature component that could be vibrationally excited at elevated
temperatures and would have an easily calculated set of resonant frequencies (fundamental mode, second
harmonic, third harmonic, etc.) that would be expected to be measured by the tack-welded FPC, assuming
sufficient mechanical coupling. Hereafter, this rod is called the “vibrational rod.”

5.2 HIGH-TEMPERATURE EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
A schematic of the high-temperature experiments conducted on the brazed FPC, described in the previous
section, is shown in Figure 15. The vibrational rod was suspended inside an Evenheat Model SB 718 SAP
furnace such that approximately 75% of the rod was within the furnace. The rod was held by a steel wire
that was fed through a 1/16 in. hole through the top of the rod. The small SS316 tube containing the lead-
in fiber was backfilled with inert Ar (99.999%, Airgas, Inc) to prevent oxidation of the Cu coating of the
lead-in fiber. This was done by evacuating the fiber-containing tube with a vacuum pump ∼10 times and

 22

refilling with Ar after each pump down. After the final backfill, the fiber-containing tube was maintained
at ∼5 psi to prevent any potential air ingress. A laser Doppler vibrometry (LDV) system (Birri et al. 2024)
was used as a non-contact confirmatory measurement system to enable simultaneous capture of vibrational
data alongside the LCI system during high-temperature testing. The system consists of a VibroFlex Neo
and a VibroFlex Connect Front-End (Polytec, Plymouth, Michigan).

 Figure 15. Schematic of the high-temperature test conducted on the brazed FPC, utilizing the
 brazed Cu-coated fiber from experiment B5.

Three different experiments were conducted in which the brazed FPC was taken up to high temperature
and allowed to return to room temperature. These experiments are summarized in Table 4. The maximum
furnace setpoint temperature was set in each experiment to 300◦ C, 500◦ C, and 700◦ C, in that order, to pro-
gressively test the FPC at Gen III reactor operation temperatures and then toward Gen IV reactor oper-
ation temperatures. The temperature histories involved temperature steps of 100◦ C increments, with 30
min holds at each temperature. The furnace was ramped as fast as possible between temperature steps. At
each temperature step, the FPC was interrogated with the LCI system and the Hyperion si155. When the
LCI system interrogation was occurring, the vibrational rod was lightly struck with a hammer multiple
times to excite it at its resonant frequencies. The LDV and LCI systems were set to collect data concur-
rently. The total time windows over which data were collected during hammer strikes were between 10
and 20 s. Interrogations with the Hyperion si155 were taken periodically during furnace cooldown and ul-
timately when the furnace reached room temperature. For report brevity, analysis of the reflected spectra
from Hyperion si155 scans is limited to the scans taken before furnace firing, at maximum temperature,
and after the furnace completely cooled down. It should be noted that when the 500◦ C test was attempted,
the reflection rod in the FPC became debonded due to a tack-weld failure; this tack-weld was ultimately re-
performed, with additional SS316 strips added to hold the reflection rod in place, and the 500◦ C test was
performed once again with these repairs in place.

5.3 HIGH-TEMPERATURE EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
The data collected from the brazed FPC and LDV during the high-temperature testing described in Section
5.2 were in the form of displacement; in the case of the LDV, this was displacement caused by the vibra-
tional rod itself, whereas in the case of the FPC, this was displacement of the FPC cavity length. These
displacement data were converted to the frequency domain via a fast Fourier transform. This way, peaks
in the frequency domain, which correspond to the vibrational rod’s fundamental frequencies, could be
compared between the FPC and the LDV. In the context of SHM for nuclear reactors, this would be ap-
plicable to monitoring the vibrational frequencies of, for example, a high-temperature pump shaft to en-
sure that the frequency content stays consistent with what is expected during normal operations. The LDV
and FPC measured frequency data were compared against a previously described theoretical model of an
Euler–Bernoulli beam with free–free boundary conditions; this model was used to calculate the discrete
fundamental modes that would be expected based on the vibrational rod’s dimensions (Birri et al. 2025). In
said model, temperature is an input in order to account for thermal expansion effects and reduction to the
Young’s modulus, as these variables directly affect the fundamental modes.
In Figure 16, the change in the second fundamental mode, as measured by the FPC and LDV, is plotted for
the three high-temperature experiments described in Table 4. Specifically, Figure 16(a) corresponds to a
maximum temperature of 300◦ C, Figure 16(b) corresponds to a maximum temperature of 500◦ C, and Fig-
ure 16(c) corresponds to a maximum temperature of 700◦ C. Note that both the FPC and LDV had resolv-
ability challenges with the first fundamental mode (which is theoretically 67 Hz), likely due to this mode
being too damped as a result of the vibrational rod not truly being in a free–free configuration. For this rea-
son, the second mode (which is theoretically 185 Hz) was tracked, as it resolved clearly for the LDV and
FPC. The theoretical models in Figure 16(a)–(c) were calculated assuming temperatures that were 100%
and 75% of the furnace setpoint temperature in order to provide a realistic envelope in which the experi-
mentally measured frequency data should fit, given that the vibrational rod was protruding from the fur-
nace and thus was not a uniform temperature.
What is evident from Figure 16(a)–(c) is that the agreement between the FPC and the LDV measured
changes to the vibrational rod’s second mode is extremely strong. In general, in each of the high-temperature
tests, the FPC and LDV agree within 1 Hz, and this level of agreement is consistent across all temperature
ranges in the experiments. Furthermore, the FPC and LDV data generally fit within the theoretical enve-
lope that would be expected, suggesting that the consideration of the vibrational rod as an Euler–Bernoulli
beam in a free–free configuration is a reasonable assumption. This theoretical validation also gives confi-
dence that the LDV and FPC are both measuring vibrational phenomena that would be expected of a rod
with these dimensions and that the existence of the tack-welded FPC is not perturbing the system so signif-
icantly that the temperature-dependent vibrational characteristics of the rod are fundamentally altered.

 6. CONCLUSION
In this study, both DED and brazing were demonstrated as two bonding techniques that can enable SHM
for advanced reactors using optical fiber sensors. A significant emphasis was placed on the brazing ap-
proach in this study, as DED has been reported on at length previously (Hyer et al. 2026). Regarding braz-
ing, several experiments were conducted herein to assess the most appropriate manner by which Cu-coated
optical fibers could be bonded to specimens. These experiments involved a range of specimen geometries,
orientations, and target lengths of fiber to be brazed. Regarding DED, several experiments were also con-
ducted in order to assess the ideal process conditions to achieve good bonding without damaging the Cu-
coated fiber. These experiments involved use of different capillary tube sizes to house the fiber, different
powder feed rates, use of different support heights, use or non-use of a strapping technique, and co-use or
non-use of a braze wire. In conducting these different brazing and DED experiments, the following conclu-
sions were drawn:
 • Both DED and brazing techniques can achieve significant pre-compressive strain on the fiber to en-
 sure that the fiber is not under significant tension at temperatures relevant to Gen IV reactor opera-
 tion; these strain values are generally more significant than in the previously considered approaches,
 such as UAM.
 • In order to achieve successful and consistent bonding with DED, the co-use of a braze wire within
 the capillary tube containing the fiber is essential to prevent thermal shock that could break the fiber.
 Having a powder feed in addition to the braze wire is not necessary and potentially not recommended.
 • Brazing of optical fibers is generally more successful in vertical orientations rather than horizontal
 orientations to ensure that there is a driving force to ensure the molten braze fills the desired volume
 and overcomes surface tension effects.
 • Brazing short lengths of Cu-coated fiber with BAg-8 specifically is more feasible than brazing long
 lengths due to the solubilization of the Cu coating into the molten braze, which can strip the fiber
 protruding from the braze region.
Furthermore, given the success exhibited with brazing short lengths of Cu-coated optical fiber in these
experiments, this outcome was leveraged to fabricate an FPC in which the cavity is formed between the
polished end face of the fiber brazed within a tube and a reflective rod. This FPC was used to monitor the
fundamental vibrational modes of a larger rod to which this FPC was tack-welded. The vibrational rod was
heated up to 300◦ C, 500◦ C, and 700◦ C in three separate experiments. Good agreement with confirmatory
LDV measurements and minimal hysteresis were shown. These results yield confidence that a sensor of
this particular configuration would be able to survive and collect accurate SHM data in an advanced reac-
tor application.
