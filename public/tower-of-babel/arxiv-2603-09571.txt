An Optimal Control Approach to Transformer Training
Kağan Akman kagan.akman@bilkent.edu.tr, Naci Saldı naci.saldi@bilkent.edu.tr, Serdar Yüksel yuksel@queensu.ca

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a rigorous optimal control-theoretic approach to Transformer training that respects key structural constraints such as (i) realized-input-independence during execution, (ii) the ensemble control nature of the problem, and (iii) positional dependence. We model the Transformer architecture as a discrete-time controlled particle system with shared actions, exhibiting noise-free McKean-Vlasov dynamics. While the resulting dynamics is not Markovian, we show that lifting it to probability measures produces a fully-observed Markov decision process (MDP). Positional encodings are incorporated into the state space to preserve the sequence order under lifting. Using the dynamic programming principle, we establish the existence of globally optimal policies under mild assumptions of compactness. We further prove that closed-loop policies in the lifted is equivalent to an initial-distribution dependent open-loop policy, which are realized-input-independent and compatible with standard Transformer training. To train a Transformer, we propose a triply quantized training procedure for the lifted MDP by quantizing the state space, the space of probability measures, and the action space, and show that any optimal policy for the triply quantized model is near-optimal for the original training problem. Finally, we establish stability and empirical consistency properties of the lifted model by showing that the value function is continuous with respect to the perturbations of the initial empirical measures and convergence of policies as the data size increases. This approach provides a globally optimal and robust alternative to gradient-based training without requiring smoothness or convexity.

1
Introduction

Transformers, introduced by
Vaswani et al. 2017
, form a significant class of neural networks with a special mechanism called
self-attention
. Many large language models (LLMs), such as GPT-4, use Transformers as their backbones, and they are proven to be very efficient and successful, see
Achiam et al. 2023
’s technical paper. A Transformer is a back-to-back concatenation of so-called
feed-forward layers
with
self-attention blocks
. Here, a feed-forward layer is a composition of two affine functions with a sigmoidal nonlinear function in between, providing useful approximation properties where some of them can be reviewed from the work of
Hornik et al. 1989
. A
self-attention block
is considered as a function processing a sequence of vectors that "relates" them using a kernel described by two matrices called
query
and
key
matrices. This process returns a probability distribution called attention scores and maps each vector to a weighted average of vectors obtained from attention scores and one more matrix called
value
matrix. In applications, a Transformer is usually endowed with
encoder
,
decoder
blocks, and normalization between each pair of layers and blocks, which can be seen in the work of
Vaswani et al. 2017
.

Conventionally, a Transformer, like many classes of neural networks, is trained by means of gradient-descent-based models. The existence of an optimal solution is guaranteed to be achievable by gradient descent methods if the objective function is convex and sufficiently smooth
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
, see Chapter 9)
. However, the loss structure of a Transformer does not possess either of these properties in general. Therefore, gradient-descent-based approaches may only guarantee convergence to a stationary point, which might not be a global optimum, or even a local one. Despite this, there are several studies showing that, under strong conditions, such as over-parametrization
(
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
;
Du et al. 2018
)
or linearity
(
Arora et al. 2018
)
, it is possible to reach a global minimum.

In this paper, following
Geshkovski et al. 2025
’s contribution, we form an optimal control—theoretic framework for Transformers. In particular, we regard Transformers, at the particle level, as a finite-horizon, discrete-time dynamical system exhibiting McKean—Vlasov flow similar to the setup used by
Pham and Wei 2016
; yet in our case, we have shared controls applied to each particle simultaneously and therefore, this formulation can also be viewed as an ensemble control problem as in the work of
Li and Khaneja 2009
. Then, we proceed with defining an optimal control problem by specifying a data set, formulating an appropriate information structure, appropriate controls, a cost structure, and the corresponding optimality notion. We observe that this dynamical system, at the particle level, exhibits a non-Markovian nature since the measure dependence of the dynamics breaks the Markovian property.

A solution to this issue is lifting the problem to the space of probability measures, similar to the methods utilized by
Bäuerle 2023
;
Carmona et al. 2023
;
Sanjari et al. 2025
. However, this lifting to empirical measures results in the loss of positional information. Therefore, before the lifting procedure, we incorporate positional encodings to the state space so that we restore the positional relations between particles. This is important because Transformers can approximate sequence-to-sequence functions, which is shown by
Yun et al. 2019
. Then, we pose the corresponding dynamic programming equations for the final MDP and show the existence of a globally optimal solution that comes in the form of a
closed-loop
policy, or in other words, deterministic and Markovian. Exploiting the deterministic flow of Transformers, it is possible to obtain
open-loop
policies that are optimal. Using such policies, we fix the weights and consequently fix the structure of the Transformer. By fixing the weights, we establish consistency with the realized-input-independence during execution.

We propose a quantization based training algorithm for our formulation since the dynamic programming for continuous state-space MDPs is computationally expensive. By quantizing the state space, the measure space on the quantized state space, and the action space, we define a new measure-valued MDP with finite state and action spaces for which the existence of optimal controls are guaranteed. Then, we show that optimal policies for the triply quantized model are near-optimal for the lifted MDP, and therefore, for the original problem. Finally, we provide an illustrative example to demonstrate the performance of optimal policies obtained from the triply quantized problem with respect to the level of quantization.

Beyond existence and near-optimality results, our lifted formulation enjoys stability properties. Owing to the weak Feller structure and a compactness assumption, the resulting value function depends continuously on the underlying empirical measures. This ensures that as the data set becomes a better sampling of the underlying true distribution, the corresponding optimal control actions become near-optimal for the true distribution, which we establish rigorously.

1.1
Related Work

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper that formulates and provides an alternative approach to train Transformers using the theory of Markov decision processes and dynamic programming. Because of its nature, this work stands on the crossroads of many research areas.

Control–theory approaches to neural networks.
An important line of research focuses on interpreting deep neural networks as dynamical systems. A pioneering work in such perspective is done by
Weinan 2017
where deep neural networks are considered as discretizations of the continuous-time dynamical systems. An approach by regarding deep learning as a parameter estimation problem for a nonlinear dynamical system is followed in the work of
Haber and Ruthotto 2017
, where they also provide stability and well-posedness results. For a PDE-based treatment of convolutional neural networks, see the study of
Ruthotto and Haber 2020
. In the study of
Chen et al. 2018
, residual neural networks are seen as ordinary differential equations. A comprehensive study in control of neural ordinary differential equations is provided by
Ruiz-Balet and Zuazua 2023
.

Optimal control formulations of training neural networks.

Li et al. 2018
develop training algorithms for deep residual neural networks using Pontryagin’s maximum principle. The authors show that the resulting algorithm coincides with the continuous-time counterpart of backpropagation (i.e. gradient descent). Consequently, the obtained controls are generally suboptimal, which is natural since the maximum principle provides only necessary conditions for optimality. The connection between deep learning and ensemble control problems -to which they refer as "mean-field"- is further explored by
E et al. 2019
, where residual networks are interpreted as continuous-time control systems. They consider each input in the training data as a part of an ensemble, for which a central controller tries to pick optimal weights. Moreover, they derive the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equations for this continuous-time control system. A broader overview of optimal control perspectives on neural networks is surveyed by
Benning et al. 2021
.

Mean-field control and McKean—Vlasov dynamics.
Due to the attention mechanism in transformer architectures, the resulting dynamics can naturally be viewed as a McKean—Vlasov type control problem. This differs fundamentally from residual neural networks, since here the empirical distribution of the particles explicitly enters the dynamics. Such distribution-dependent dynamics were studied in the context of stochastic processes induced by stochastic differential equations by
McKean Jr 1966
. From a control-theoretic perspective, McKean—Vlasov control problems —and the closely related mean-field control or mean-field game problems — have been extensively investigated in both continuous-time and discrete-time settings by
Bayraktar et al. 2018
;
Carmona and Delarue 2015
;
Carmona et al. 2013
;
Motte and Pham 2022
;
Pham and Wei 2017
;
Pham and Wei 2016
;
Pradhan and Yüksel 2025
. Our primary objective is to build on but also extend this rich body of literature by adapting the machinery to the specific operational constraints of Transformers training, in order to develop algorithms which are optimal in a mathematically precise sense. In addition to optimality, we also arrive at near optimal solutions in an algorithmic and computational sense.

Mathematical formulation on Transformer design
.
Geshkovski et al. 2025
provide a rigorous formulation of Transformers by modeling them as a continuous-time
uncontrolled
dynamical system of interacting particles. In contrast to our control-theoretic perspective, their focus is on analyzing the intrinsic dynamics and establishing asymptotic clustering properties, following their earlier work
(
Geshkovski et al. 2023
)
. Although control of Transformers is mentioned there as a possible research direction, it is not developed. This direction is precisely what we pursue in the present paper.
The framework provided by
Geshkovski et al. 2025
is extended to broader classes of Transformer architectures with different self-attention mechanisms, and the well-posedness of the associated continuity equation is analyzed by
Castin et al. 2025
. Since these works do not formulate Transformers within an optimal control framework with shared controls—as we do here—our contribution can be viewed as complementary.

Generalization problem and robustness to incorrect priors.
Recent advances in generalization problem have taken in multiple directions.
Xu and Raginsky 2017
provide an information—theoretic approach to generalization problem by controlling the error using the mutual information between the data set and output.
Lugosi and Neu 2022
take a convex-analytic path by replacing the mutual information with a general strongly convex dependence functional of the joint data–output distribution.
Mwigo and Dasgupta 2025
derive a Transformer-specific generalization bound for shallow Transformers trained with gradient descent.

1.2
Contributions

This paper provides a rigorous and implementation-faithful optimal control–theoretic formulation of Transformer training. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

(i)

Control theoretic formulation compatible with Transformers architecture.
We formulate Transformer architectures as discrete-time controlled dynamical systems of interacting particles evolving under shared control actions with the following salient attributes.

–

Ensemble control.
This perspective leads to an ensemble control problem with deterministic McKean—Vlasov dynamics, distinguishing our framework from exchangeable mean-field models and classical single-agent control problems while being equivalent to the original training problem.

–

Open-loop design and compatibility with fixed weights in Transformers design.

To obtain data-independent coefficients, which are inevitably open-loop, we establish and build on the following novel equivalence properties: a closed-loop policy of a lifted problem is equivalent to an initial-distribution dependent open-loop policy (owing to the deterministic and ensemble nature of the lifted problem), which is, in turn, realized-input-independent and thus a genuine open-loop policy. This procedure corresponds exactly to fixing the weights after the training phase in classical neural network training, and thereby satisfies realized input-independence during execution (Figure
1
).

–

Explicit treatment of positional dependence and MDP lifting.
We lift the problem to the space of probability measures, which induces a measure-valued MDP with finite-horizon terminal cost criterion equivalent to the particle-level problem. However, measure lifting destroys the positional information. Therefore, we incorporate positional encodings into the state space before the lifting procedure to recover positional information.

–

Existence of globally optimal controls.
We prove that the lifting dynamics have a Markovian transition kernel satisfying weak Feller property under compactness assumptions (Corollary
4
). This allows the application of dynamic programming and establishes the existence of optimal controls that are globally optimal for the lifted MDP (Theorem
7
).

The Proposed Optimal Control Formulation of Transformers

In this section, we interpret the forward dynamics of Transformers as a discrete-time controlled dynamical system of interacting particles and show how the training procedure can be formulated as a finite-horizon optimal control problem. Since each particle evolves through the interactions with the empirical measure of the ensemble, the resulting dynamics is not Markovian at the particle level. The Markovian property is essential in our formulation of Transformers as an optimal control problem as it allows us to decompose the training problem into tractable and smaller subproblems using the dynamic programming principle, which guarantees the existence of optimal weights, and also provides a structural description of them.

In the work of
Geshkovski et al. 2025
, Transformers are described via the flow map of a continuous-time dynamical system. We adopt this approach, but, rather than considering the continuous-time system, we study a discrete-time version of the resulting dynamics that models a Transformer block with a feed-forward layer and single-headed self-attention. The formulation presented in this paper can be generalized to the multi-headed self-attention case. We therefore restrict ourselves to the single-headed self-attention for brevity and simplicity of the presentation.

More precisely, we view Transformers as sequence-to-sequence maps and, motivated by the universal approximation result of
Yun et al. 2019
, our goal is to identify an optimal Transformer architecture that best explains the training data. We assume that the data are generated, possibly in a noisy manner, by an underlying sequence-to-sequence mapping. For a meaningful comparison between input and output sequences, the position of each element must be preserved. To this end, we equip particles with positional encodings, which allow us to recover positional information after lifting the system to the space of probability measures in order to obtain a Markovian representation. Although this lifting breaks the exchangeability property, a mean-field analysis remains possible by exploiting the underlying McKean—Vlasov structure of the particle dynamics.

2.2
Data set-induced dynamics and information structure

In the classical training of Transformers, given some data set, the goal is to minimize some cost functional over the set of weights. We now formulate the corresponding scenario as an optimal control problem. To this end, suppose that we are given a data set
\mathcal{D}:=\{(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathbf{y}^{k}):k=1,2,\ldots,K\}\subseteq\mathrm{S}^{N}\times\mathrm{S}^{N}
, for some
K\in\mathbb{N}
. In some applications, the learning goal is to approximate a classifier function
T:\mathrm{S}^{N}\to\mathcal{Y}
where
\mathcal{Y}
is a finite set of certain classifications. In such problems, the output vector is mapped to a class label by a known function. In our formulation, we consider the case in which Transformer maps feature vectors to label vectors, and the corresponding classes of the latter are known. Conceptually, this is equivalent to previously mentioned sequence-to-sequence function approximation paradigm and captures most learning scenarios.

For each
k\in\mathcal{K}:=\{1,\ldots,K\}
, the feature vector
\mathbf{x}^{k}=(x^{1,k},\ldots,x^{N,k})\in\mathrm{S}^{N}
gives rise to an ensemble of particles
(X_{t}^{1,k},\ldots,X_{t}^{N,k})
that evolves with (
2

X_{0}^{i,k}=(p_{i},x^{i,k})
for each
i\in\mathcal{N}
. In our problem, we consider a
centralized information structure

\mathcal{I}_{t}^{i,k}=\mathcal{I}_{t}:=\{X_{s}^{i,k},U_{s}:i\in\mathcal{N},k\in\mathcal{K},0\leq s\leq t-1\}\cup\{X_{t}^{i,k}:i\in\mathcal{N},k\in\mathcal{K}\}

(3)

t\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}\setminus\{0\}

i\in\mathcal{N}

\mathcal{I}_{0}=\{X_{0}^{i,k}:i\in\mathcal{N},k\in\mathcal{K}\}
. The equality
\mathcal{I}_{t}^{i,k}=\mathcal{I}_{t}
indicates that each particle
X_{t}^{i,k}
has access to the same information structure. In optimal control problems, the actions are chosen using a
policy

However, choosing an action probabilistically is not compatible with Transformers since they work with concrete fixed weights. Therefore, we need policies of the form
(\gamma_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
consisting of functions of the form
\gamma_{t}(\mathcal{I}_{t})=U_{t}\in\mathbb{U}
for each

. In Section 3, we show that the choice of actions can be done in a deterministic way if we lift our problem to the space of probability measures. However, we subsequently show that the deterministic policies based on feedback (closed-loop policies) are not compatible with Transformers either. Therefore, we introduce the initial-condition-dependent open-loop policies obtained from closed-loop policies that are optimal and fully compatible with the usual training paradigm of Transformers.

We note that the choice of actions is not performed by a single particle or a group of particles that exclude some other particles. On the contrary, the choice is done by observing all of the ensembles. This is called an
ensemble control problem
since we are trying to find optimal controls that are optimal for each member of the ensemble.

The choice of information structure is intentional since we seek conformity with the current training configurations in which similar mechanisms are used. In the classical training of Transformers, the weights are trained using the information accumulated across all tokens and all samples, the controls in our formulation have to depend on all past information as well as the current information, which naturally results in a centralized information structure. This property of the centralized information structure is called the
perfect recall
property, that is, we make decisions while having all possible information available. In general, an information structure might be more complicated, see the book of
Yüksel and Başar 2024
for a detailed analysis of information structure-dependent properties of team optimal control problems.

In the next subsection, we complete our optimal control problem formulation of the training procedure of Transformers by introducing the cost functional and giving a sensible optimality criterion.

2.3
Cost functional and optimality criterion

Having specified the dynamics and the information structure, we now complete our formulation of Transformer training as an optimal control problem by defining a cost functional and the associated optimality criterion. In standard training, the objective is to minimize the empirical risk over the data set; in our formulation, we minimize a cost measuring the average discrepancy between the terminal states and the target vectors.

\Gamma

. We compare the feature and label vectors using a loss function
L:\mathrm{S}\times\mathrm{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The minimization task is as follows

\displaystyle\underset{\displaystyle\underline{\gamma}\in\Gamma}{\text{minimize}}\quad\frac{1}{NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}L(x_{T,\underline{\gamma}}^{i,k},y^{i,k})

\displaystyle\text{subject to: }x_{t,\underline{\gamma}}^{i,k}\text{ evolving according to \eqref{eq:dynamics}},

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008ptx_{0,\underline{\gamma}}^{i,k}=x^{i,k}\text{ for all }i\in\mathcal{N},k\in\mathcal{K},t\in\mathcal{T}.

(OP)

In Section
5
, we discuss the robustness properties of the value function associated to the lifted formulation presented in Section
3
. This is the optimal control–theoretic analogue of the generalization problem in neural network. To this end, we provide another representation of the problem expressed as a finite-horizon terminal-cost-only expected cost functional minimization. Let
P_{\mathcal{D}}
denote the true distribution of the data set
\mathcal{D}
, that is
P_{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{S}^{N}\times\mathrm{S}^{N})
. For convenience, we write

\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(x^{i},y^{i})

for all
\mathbf{x}:=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{N})
,
\mathbf{y}:=(y^{1},\ldots,y^{N})\in\mathrm{S}^{N}
. We consider a scenario in which the central controller try to find an policy that is optimal for the underlying distribution.

\displaystyle\hskip-30.00005pt\underset{\displaystyle\underline{\gamma}\in\Gamma}{\text{minimize}}\quad\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathcal{D}}}^{\underline{\gamma}}\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{x}_{T},\mathbf{y}).

(ROP)

Here
\mathbf{x}_{T}
denotes the terminal state of the process, whose initial distribution is the first marginal of
P_{\mathcal{D}}
, evolving in the trajectory specified by the Transformer dynamics (
1
) and by the policy
\underline{\gamma}\in\Gamma
.

There are several benefits of the presentation of the optimization problem as if the initial data is random:

(i)

This captures the standard setup given in (OP) above, where the data is empirically given, in which case, the distribution is the realized empirical distribution for training.

(ii)

As noted, this allows us to formulate the robustness to initial training data and asymptotic consistency as the data converges weakly or in the Wasserstein sense to a true distribution based on which data is generated.

(iii)

This formulation makes the subtle point of open-loop/closed-loop policy discussion transparent: The policy we obtain is closed loop in the distribution, but open loop in the realization during the implementation of the Transformer for any source once the Transformer is trained.

In the definition of cost functional, we only consider feature vectors and omit positional encodings. Since
(p_{i},x_{T,\underline{\gamma}}^{i,k})
and
(p_{i},y^{i,k})
have the same positional encodings for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
and all
k\in\mathcal{K}
, any sensible metric on
\mathrm{PE}_{N}
would provide a zero contribution to the cost structure. The reason why we introduce the positional encodings is motivated by preserving the positional information while lifting the problem to probability measures.

In this paper, we primarily will consider the quadratic distance criterion, i.e.

L(x,y):=\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}

(4)

for all
x,y\in\mathrm{S}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In practice, for example when training LLMs, the cost function differs from the one we use in this paper, see the contribution of
Phuong and Hutter 2022
. Such variations do not disturb the analysis presented this paper as long as we have a jointly continuous loss function. However, it may result in a different cost structure which might be computed along the time-horizon instead of a terminal-cost.

Although the cost function
(\mathrm{S}^{N})^{K}\ni(x^{i,k})_{i\in\mathcal{N},k\in\mathcal{K}}\mapsto\frac{1}{NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|x^{i,k}-y^{i,k}\|^{2}
is strictly convex, the constraints given by Transformer dynamics (
1
) are highly nonconvex. Therefore, applying methods requiring convexity such as gradient descent can only guarantee convergence to local minima, and in general, do not provide guarantees of global optimality. In Section 4, we propose a quantization based training method guaranteeing near-optimality, which is not dependent on convexity, but rather requiring mild assumptions on the state and action spaces.

Definition 1
.

A strategy
\underline{\gamma}^{*}\in\Gamma
is called
globally optimal
if it attains the minimum in (
OP
).

We conclude our formulation of Transformers as an optimal control problem by noting that the particle-level dynamics are not Markovian. Indeed, the evolution of each particle depends on the empirical measure of the ensemble through the self-attention mechanism in (
1
). Therefore, the dynamic programming principle is not directly applicable with the current formulation. In the next section, we show that the particles have a McKean—Vlasov-type dynamics that can be lifted to the space of probability measures, which restores the Markovian property.

3
McKean—Vlasov Structure and Measure-Valued MDP

In Section
2
, we formulated an optimal control problem modeling Transformer training and argued that, after constructing superstates, the dynamics can be lifted to the space of probability measures, giving rise to a Markov decision process. In this section, we make this lifting explicit, and we establish that the topological requirements for the application of dynamic programming hold under some compactness assumptions.

In particular, we (i) formalize the deterministic McKean—Vlasov representation of the dynamics, (ii) lift the particle-level dynamics to the space of probability measures, (iii) verify the weak Feller property of the resulting transition kernel, (iv) prove the existence of optimal closed-loop policies, and (v) relate these closed-loop policies to initial-data-dependent open-loop policies from the viewpoint of implementation and conformity with the standard training paradigm.

3.1
Deterministic McKean—Vlasov representation of the particles.

We begin with defining the function
f:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\to\mathbb{X}
for all
(p,x)\in\mathbb{X}
,
U=(W,A,b,

Q,K,V)\in\mathbb{U}
, and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
by

f((p,x),U,\mu):=\left(p,W\sigma(Ax+b)+\dfrac{1}{\int_{\mathrm{S}}\mu_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,Kz\rangle}}\int_{\mathrm{S}}\mu_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}\tilde{z})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,K\tilde{z}\rangle}V\tilde{z}\right),

(5)

which models the flow of a single particle. To make this concrete, take
(p,x)=(p_{i},x_{t}^{i,k})=X_{t}^{i,k}
,
U_{t}\in\mathbb{U}
as in (
2
), and
\mu_{t}^{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(p_{i},x_{t}^{i,k})}
for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
k\in\mathcal{K}
, and
t\in\mathcal{T}
. Then

\displaystyle f(X_{t}^{i,k},U_{t},\mu_{t}^{k})

\displaystyle=\left(p_{i},W_{t}\sigma(A_{t}x_{t}^{i,k}+b_{t})+\frac{1}{\int_{\mathrm{S}}\mu_{t}^{k}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Q_{t}x_{t}^{i,k},K_{t}z\rangle}}\int_{S}\mu_{t}^{k}(\mathrm{d}z)\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Q_{t}x_{t}^{i,k},K_{t}z\rangle}V_{t}z\right)

\displaystyle=\left(p_{i},W_{t}\sigma(A_{t}x_{t}^{i,k}+b_{t})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Q_{t}x_{t}^{i,k},K_{t}x_{t}^{j,k}\rangle}}{\sum_{r=1}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Q_{t}x_{t}^{i,k},K_{t}x_{t}^{r,k}\rangle}}V_{t}x_{t}^{j,k}\right)=X_{t+1}^{i,k}.

Note that the flow of any particle does not solely depend on its previous realizations, but it depends explicitly on the empirical of the ensemble
\mu_{t}^{k}
. This shows that the particles do not have a Markovian transition regime. The positional encodings are fixed in the definition of
f
since they are not dynamical variables over the time horizon, but merely structural labels whose purpose is to preserve the order of the particles.

3.2
The lifted system and its continuity properties.

We continue with precisely explaining the lifting procedure. Then, we prove that the underlying dynamics are continuous under the following assumption:

{assume}

The particles’ state space
\mathrm{S}
and the action space
\mathbb{U}
are compact.

As discussed earlier, we justify Assumption
3.2
by widespread usage of compactly supported data in most applications for the state space
\mathrm{S}
. For the compactness of
\mathbb{U}
, it can be viewed as a regularization of actions such as substituting
\mathbb{U}
with a closed, norm-bounded subset of it. In addition, compactness of
\mathrm{S}
implies the compactness of
\mathbb{X}
since
\mathrm{PE}_{N}
is finite set, and therefore, is compact in discrete topology. This observation is crucial since we endow the space of probability measures with the weak
*
-topology, that is a sequence of probability measures
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\text{weak}^{*}
-convergent
to
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
if, for any continuous and bounded
h:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)h(x)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)h(x).

Under Assumption
3.2
, one of the nicest properties of
\text{weak}^{*}
-topology is that
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
is compact. Moreover, we have many other choices for a suitable metric. In particular, we consider a modified version of
p
-Wasserstein distance,
p\in[1,+\infty)
, defined by

W_{p,\lambda}(P,Q):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\pi(\mathrm{d}X,\mathrm{d}Y)\|X-Y\|_{p,\lambda}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

(6)

where
\|X-Y\|_{p,\lambda}^{p}=\|x-y\|_{p}^{p}+\lambda|p-q|^{p}
,
X=(p,x),Y=(q,y)\in\mathbb{X}
,
\lambda>0
, and

\Pi(P,Q):=\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}):\pi(\cdot\times\mathbb{X})=P(\cdot)\text{ and }\pi(\mathbb{X}\times\cdot)=Q(\cdot)\}.

Here
\Pi(P,Q)
is the set of couplings of
P
and
Q
, i.e., all joint probability measures on
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}
such that the respective marginals of each
\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)
are
P
and
Q
. This set is always non-empty since it contains
P\otimes Q
. Moreover,
\Pi(P,Q)
is compact whenever
\mathbb{X}
is since it is a closed subset of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X})
, a compact space. Since the functional
\Pi(P,Q)\ni\pi\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}h\ \mathrm{d}\pi
is continuous for all
h\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X})
, by Weierstrass theorem and the fact that
h(X,Y)=\|X-Y\|_{\lambda}^{p}
is continuous for all
p\in[1,+\infty)
, there exists a
\pi^{*}\in\Pi(P,Q)
such that
\pi^{*}
minimizes (
6
). Here the value of
\lambda
is critical for enforcing the model to take the positional information into account. Since
\mathrm{S}
is compact, then for any
x,y\in\mathrm{S}
and
p,q\in\mathrm{PE}_{N}
, we have

\|(p,x)-(q,y)\|_{p,\lambda}^{p}=\|x-y\|_{p}^{p}+\lambda|p-q|^{p}\leq\operatorname{diam}(\mathrm{S},\|\cdot\|_{p})^{p}+\lambda<+\infty.

The choice of
\lambda
affects the behavior of the minimization problem in (
6
). If
\lambda
becomes larger, then the choice of optimal product measure
\pi^{*}
will prioritize minimizing the cost contribution of positional encodings, which is what we want since it tolerates the lost positional information in the correct way. Later, we provide an explicit bound for
\lambda
to ensure this. Considering
p
, we are more interested in the case
p=2
since it corresponds to minimizing the square loss, which is one of the most popular cost option in deep learning problems.

In our formulation in Section
2
, we have
K
-many samples from which we construct the empirical measures
\mu_{0}^{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(p_{i},x^{i,k})}
for each
k\in\mathcal{K}
. Note that this measure representation not only encodes the particle state information, but also the positional information. We define the map
\Phi:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathbb{U}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
and
U\in\mathbb{U}
by calculating the following push-forward measure

\Phi(\mu,U):=\mu\circ T_{\mu,U}^{-1}

(7)

where
T_{\mu,U}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{X}
is given by
T_{\mu,U}(X):=f(X,U,\mu)
for all
X\in\mathbb{X}
. This can be viewed as the discrete-time analogue of the continuity equation in
(
Geshkovski et al. 2025
)
. Consequently, we have a concrete functional representation of the measure flow in this particular ensemble controlled McKean—Vlasov system. It follows that, by a change of variables argument, for any measurable
g:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathcal{K}
and
t\in\mathcal{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{X}}\Phi(\mu_{t}^{k},U_{t})(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)g(p,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu_{t}^{k}\circ T_{\mu_{t}^{k},U_{t}}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)g(p,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(p_{i},x_{t}^{i,k})}(T_{\mu_{t}^{k},U_{t}}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x))g(p,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(T_{\mu_{t}^{k},U_{t}}(p_{i},x_{t}^{i,k}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(f((p_{i},x_{t}^{i,k}),U_{t},\mu_{t}^{k}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(p_{i},x_{t+1}^{i,k})=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu_{t+1}^{k}(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)g(p,x).

Thus,
\Phi(\mu_{t}^{k},U_{t})=\mu_{t+1}^{k}
for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
and
k\in\mathcal{K}
. This indicates that each empirical measure evolves with a Markovian structure. Since our objective is to minimize a cost criterion over all training data, and consequently, all empirical measures, we consider a multi-variable generalization of
\Phi
as
\bm{\Phi}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}\times\mathbb{U}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}
is given, for all
(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{K})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}
and
U\in\mathbb{U}
, by

\bm{\Phi}(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{K};U)=(\Phi(\mu^{1},U),\ldots,\Phi(\mu^{K},U)).

(8)

This generalization allows us to control the minimization problem over the data set, a similar approach can be found in
(
E et al. 2019
)
. On the other hand, we can now see the ensemble process
((\mu_{t}^{1},\ldots,\mu_{t}^{K}))_{t\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}}
, initialized from the data set
\mathcal{D}
and evolving with
\bm{\Phi}
, as a deterministic and measure-valued Markov decision process. This ensemble view allows us to use a cost criterion to which all points in the data set contribute. To continue, we now establish the continuity of
\Phi
. To do this, since we construct
\Phi
using
f
, we first need the continuity of
f
under Assumption
3.2
.

Proposition 2
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, the function
f
in (
5
) is jointly continuous on
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
where
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
is endowed with
\text{weak}^{*}
-topology.

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that
\mathrm{S}\ni x\mapsto W\sigma(Ax+b)
is continuous. Since continuous functions form a vector space, it suffices to analyze the remaining term. Since
\mathrm{S}
and
\mathbb{U}
are compact, then for any
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
U=(W,A,b,Q,K,V)\in\mathbb{U}
, the function
\mathrm{S}\ni z\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,K\tilde{z}\rangle}
is bounded. Also, it is continuous. Therefore, for any sequence
(P_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{S})

\mathrm{w}^{*}
-converging to
P^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{S})
we have

\int_{\mathrm{S}}P_{n}(\mathrm{d}\tilde{z})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,K\tilde{z}\rangle}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathrm{S}}P^{*}(\mathrm{d}\tilde{z})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,K\tilde{z}\rangle}.

This convergence is uniform on compact subsets of the action space, which suffices for the entire action space under Assumption
3.2
. Of course, this statement is meaningful if we remind that if a sequence of probability measures
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
is
w^{*}
-convergent to
\mu^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
, then the marginal of each
\mu_{n}
on
\mathrm{S}
converges to the marginal of
\mu^{*}
on
\mathrm{S}
in
\text{weak}^{*}
-topology since the projection map is continuous
(
Billingsley 2013
, Theorem 2.7)
. On the other hand, since
\mathrm{S}\ni z\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,K\tilde{z}\rangle}
is bounded and we are working on finite measures, dominated convergence theorem
(
Cohn 2013
, Theorem 2.4.5)
is applicable. Therefore, for any
Q^{(n)}\to Q
in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
,
K_{n}\to K
in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
,
x_{n}\to x
in
\mathrm{S}
, we have

\int_{\mathrm{S}}P(\mathrm{d}\tilde{z})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Q^{(n)}x_{n},K_{n}\tilde{z}\rangle}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathrm{S}}P(\mathrm{d}\tilde{z})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,K\tilde{z}\rangle}.

Note that this convergence is not dependent on the choice of measure
P
. Since we have uniform convergence in each separate argument, we have joint continuity.

Next, we examine the continuity of
(\mu,Q,K,V,x)\mapsto\int_{\mathrm{S}}\mu_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}\tilde{z})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,K\tilde{z}\rangle}V\tilde{z}
. It is important to see that the integration here is in the sense of Bochner integration in finite dimensions. The strong measurability of
(Q,K,V,x)\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,K\tilde{z}\rangle}V\tilde{z}
is routine and the continuity of the same function is an application of dominated convergence theorem for Bochner integration
(
Cohn 2013
, Theorem E.6)
. Since the continuous functions form an algebra,
f
is (uniformly) continuous in the control argument
U
.

Finally, it is immediate that
f
is uniformly continuous in
p
. Therefore,
f
is jointly continuous.
∎

As a consequent of Proposition
2
, we establish the continuity of
\Phi
in the next result.

Proposition 3
.

Under Assumption
3.2
,
\Phi
defined in (
7
) is jointly continuous on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathbb{U}
.

Proof.

First, we show the uniform convergence in each separate argument. Let
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
that is
W_{1,\lambda}
-convergent to
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
. The topology generated by
W_{1,\lambda}
is equivalent with
W_{2,\lambda}
for compact state space. For simplicity, write
X
for the generic element of
\mathbb{X}
. Then, for any
g:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with minimal Lipschitz constant
1
and
U\in\mathbb{U}
, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{X}}(\Phi(\mu_{n},U)-\Phi(\mu,U))(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)g(p,x)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)g(T_{\mu_{n},U}(p,x))

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt-\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)g(T_{\mu,U}(p,x))\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)\big(g(T_{\mu_{n},U}(p,x))

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt-g(T_{\mu,U}(p,x))\big)\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad+\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{X}}(\mu_{n}-\mu)(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)g(T_{\mu,U}(p,x))\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)|g(f(p,x;U,\mu_{n}))

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt-g(f(p,x;U,\mu))|

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{X}}|\mu_{n}-\mu|(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)|g(f(p,x;U,\mu))|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(p,x)\in\mathbb{X}}|f(p,x;U,\mu_{n})-f(p,x;U,\mu)|

\displaystyle\qquad+\|g\|_{\infty}W_{1,\lambda}(\mu_{n},\mu)

where the last inequality is due to the Lipschitz property of
g
and the definition of
W_{1,\lambda}
distance. Since
\mu_{n}\xrightarrow{W_{1,\lambda}}\mu
and
f
is continuous by Proposition
2
, we have uniform convergence in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
.

Now let
U_{n}\to U
in
\mathbb{U}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
be arbitrary. Then,

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{X}}(\Phi(\mu,U_{n})-\Phi(\mu,U))(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)g(p,x)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)g(T_{\mu,U_{n}}(p,x))

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt-\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)g(T_{\mu,U}(p,x))\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}}\mu(\mathrm{d}p,\mathrm{d}x)|g(T_{\mu,U_{n}}(p,x))-g(T_{\mu,U}(p,x))|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(p,x)\in\mathbb{X}}|f(p,x;U_{n},\mu)-f(p,x;U,\mu)|.

By the continuity of
f
again, we have the uniform convergence in
\mathbb{U}
as well.
Therefore,
\Phi
is indeed jointly continuous on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathbb{U}
since it is uniformly continuous in each argument.
∎

For
(\mu_{t}^{1},\ldots,\mu_{t}^{K})_{t\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}}
, we define its transition kernel
\eta
by

\displaystyle\eta(B\mid\mu_{t}^{1},\ldots,\mu_{t}^{K};U_{t})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}((\mu_{t+1}^{1},\ldots,\mu_{t+1}^{K})\in B\mid\mu_{t}^{1},\ldots,\mu_{t}^{K};U_{t})

(9)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{\bm{\Phi}(\mu_{t}^{1},\ldots,\mu_{t}^{K};U_{t})\in B\}=\delta_{\bm{\Phi}(\mu_{t}^{1},\ldots,\mu_{t}^{K};U_{t})}(B),

(10)

where

. Here, we endow

with the product

-topology and obtain the Borel
\sigma
-field structure.

Note that the continuity of
\Phi
implies the continuity of
\bm{\Phi}
, which is critical since we want the transition kernel

, that is, for any continuous and bounded function

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}\times\mathbb{U}\ni(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{K};U)\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}}\eta(\mathrm{d}\mu_{t+1}^{1},\ldots,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t+1}^{K}\mid\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{K};U)g(\mu_{t+1}^{1},\ldots,\mu_{t+1}^{K})

(11)

is a continuous function. The next assertion, which is a corollary to

, indeed verifies this hypothesis.

Corollary 4
.

The transition kernel
\eta
defined in (

) is weak Feller under Assumption
3.2
.

Proof.

Due to Proposition
3
,
\Phi
is jointly continuous. Therefore, so is
\bm{\Phi}
. Then, let
(\bm{\mu}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}
converging to
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}
in weak
∗
-topology and
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be another sequence in
\mathbb{U}
converging to

. Then for any continuous and bounded

, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}}\eta(\mathrm{d}\bm{\mu}_{t+1}\mid\bm{\mu}_{n};U_{n})g(\bm{\mu}_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}}\delta_{\bm{\Phi}(\bm{\mu}_{n},U_{n})}(\mathrm{d}\bm{\mu}_{t+1})g(\bm{\mu}_{t+1})

\displaystyle=g(\bm{\Phi}(\bm{\mu}_{n},U_{n}))

for all

. Since both

are continuous, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}g(\bm{\Phi}(\bm{\mu}_{n},U_{n}))=g(\bm{\Phi}(\bm{\mu},U))=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}}\eta(\mathrm{d}\bm{\mu}_{t+1}\mid\bm{\mu};U)g(\bm{\mu}_{t+1}).

Thus,

is weak Feller.
∎

3.3
Existence of optimal policies

We now employ the framework of Markov decision processes. First, we observe that the ensemble of empirical measures

With dynamic programming, we first minimize starting from inside to outside, meaning that we first minimize starting from the terminal cost. At each time-step, we work with only optimization of one term and get optimal control action for each state. This procedure is more efficient than checking each action individually even if we have a finite set of admissible actions. However, we have a continuum of actions, which is not quite easy to work with currently. In the next section, we reduce this set to a finite one at the cost of having near-optimality. However, by doing this, we depend on the compactness of state and action spaces instead of relying on the convexity and smoothness of the problem, which is not the case in most applications.

3.4
Open-loop policy design via closed-loop policy design for the lifted problem.

Let

We now discuss why open-loop policies constitute the appropriate class of policies in the context of Transformers. An

(U_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}

of the form

for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
. Basically, a closed-loop policy yields an action based on the current state.

In the operational context of Transformers, one would like to fix the architecture after the training process is finished. For example, if we were to use gradient descent for training provided that it is feasible to use it, we would have terminated the process when the gradient of loss function with respect to actions vanishes. Then, we would have a collection of actions, and whenever we feed some other data, the system returns an output by running the model with the weights obtained from that gradient descent-based training. This is exactly an open-loop policy since weights are not subject to change when the system is given a new data. However, in the case of closed-loop policies, the controls are calculated at each layer
t\in\mathcal{T}
, which is not computationally efficient, and also, not compatible with classical paradigm.

We establish and build on the following equivalence for classes policies:

A

4
Triply Quantized Training Scheme for Transformers

We have so far developed a mathematical framework and control theoretic formulation on optimal design of Transformer architecture. In this section, building on this formulation, we present a rigorous approximation method which is provably near optimal, thus facilitating a numerical implementation for Transformer design with performance guarantees.

Under Assumption
3.2
, we exploit the total boundedness of
\mathrm{S}
. More concretely, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it is possible to find a set
\{s^{(n),j}:j\in J(n)\}\subseteq\mathrm{S}
such that

\min_{j\in J(n)}\|x-s^{(n),j}\|<\frac{1}{n}

for all
x\in\mathrm{S}
. Note that compactness, thereby total boundedness, of
\mathrm{S}
ensures that the indexing set

is finite. It is possible to include the initial information into the quantizer set. However, the analysis provided in this section is asymptotically consistent even if we do not include them. So, write

\mathrm{S}_{n}:=\{s^{(n),j}:j\in J(n)\}

(17)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The set
\mathrm{S}_{n}
is finite and its cardinality is
|J(n)|
.

In order to continue, we need to introduce the positional encodings for the dynamics defined on these sets, meaning that we now have
\mathbb{X}_{n}:=\mathrm{PE}_{N}\times\mathrm{S}_{n}
, which is also a finite set with
N|J(n)|

\tilde{Q}_{n}(p,x):=(p,\operatorname{argmin}_{s\in\mathrm{S}_{n}}\|x-s\|),\quad(p,x)\in\mathbb{X},

(18)

where the ties are broken in a way to protect measurability.

Moreover, to have a feasible learning algorithm, we need to quantize the action space
\mathbb{U}
. Recall that we endow
\mathbb{U}
with the product Frobenius norm, that is
\|U\|_{\mathbb{U}}^{2}=\Tr(WW^{T})+\Tr(AA^{T})+\Tr(bb^{T})+\Tr(QQ^{T})+\Tr(KK^{T})+\Tr(VV^{T})
for all

. By Assumption
3.2
, we also know that
\mathbb{U}
is compact, as a result, for any

Although the problem is reduced from an infinite-dimensional problem to a finite-dimensional one, it remains computationally expensive. If we were to consider the model where only the states are quantized, we would have a product of probability simplices

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}_{n})=\{p=(p_{a})_{a=1}^{|\mathbb{X}_{n}|}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbb{X}_{n}|}:p_{a}\geq 0\text{ and }\sum_{a=1}^{|\mathbb{X}_{n}|}p_{a}=1\}.

\{1,2,...,|\mathbb{X}_{n}|\}\ni a\mapsto\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(\bigg\lfloor\frac{a}{|\mathrm{S}_{n}|}\bigg\rfloor+1,a\text{ mod }|\mathrm{S}_{n}|+1\right)&\text{ if }|\mathrm{S_{n}}|\text{ does not divide }a\\
\vskip 5.0pt\\
\displaystyle\left(\frac{a}{|\mathrm{S}_{n}|},|\mathrm{S}_{n}|\right)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The requirement for going back to a non-quantized measure from a quantized one is because of the definition of

, where we use the original

. Here the first component is associated with the corresponding positional value and the second component is the index of a particle state in

\bm{\Phi}^{(n)}(\hat{\mu}^{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}^{K};U):=(\Phi^{(n)}(\hat{\mu}^{1},U),\ldots,\Phi^{(n)}(\hat{\mu}^{K},U)).

\mathsf{MDP}^{(\ell,n,m)}
, whose state space is
\mathcal{P}^{(\ell)}(\mathbb{X}_{n})^{K}
and its action space is
\mathbb{U}_{m}
. We define the corresponding dynamics via

\Phi^{(\ell,n,m)}(\hat{\mu},U^{(m)}):=R_{\ell}(\Phi^{(n)}(\hat{\mu},U^{(m)}))

(26)

for all
\hat{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}^{(\ell)}(\mathbb{X}_{n})
and
U^{(m)}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}
. Like we have done before, its multivariable version

\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell,n,m)}(\hat{\mu}^{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}^{K};U^{(m)})=(\Phi^{(\ell,n,m)}(\hat{\mu}^{1},U^{(m)}),\ldots,\Phi^{(\ell,n,m)}(\hat{\mu}^{K},U^{(m)}))

(27)

allows us to define the transition kernel

\eta^{(\ell,n,m)}(\cdot|\hat{\mu}^{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}^{K};U^{(m)}):=\delta_{\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell,n,m)}(\hat{\mu}^{1},\ldots,\hat{\mu}^{K};U^{(m)})}(\cdot).

(28)

We end the specification of
\mathsf{MDP}^{(\ell,n,m)}
by indicating that we use a similar cost structure given by an
|\mathbb{X}_{n}|\times|\mathbb{X}_{n}|
-matrix,

\mathbf{C}_{a,b}:=\|X_{a}-X_{b}\|_{2,\lambda}^{2}

for all
X_{a},X_{b}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
and
a,b\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathbb{X}_{n}|\}
, which induces the measure-level cost

\hat{W}_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu}):=\min_{\bm{\hat{\pi}}\in\hat{\Pi}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu})}\sum_{a=1}^{|\mathbb{X}_{n}|}\sum_{b=1}^{|\mathbb{X}_{n}|}\mathbf{C}_{a,b}\bm{\hat{\pi}}_{a,b}

where
\hat{\Pi}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu}):=\{\hat{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbb{X}_{n}|\times|\mathbb{X}_{n}|}:\bm{\hat{\pi}}_{a,b}\geq 0,\ \sum_{a=1}^{|\mathbb{X}_{n}|}\bm{\hat{\pi}}_{a,b}=\hat{\nu}_{b},\ \sum_{b=1}^{|\mathbb{X}_{n}|}\bm{\hat{\pi}}_{a,b}=\hat{\mu}_{a}\}
.

For this MDP, invoking Blackwell’s theorem (see
(
Blackwell 1964
)
), it is sufficient to use the Markovian information structure, which is given by

\mathcal{I}_{t}^{(\ell,n,m)}:=(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k})_{k\in\mathcal{K}}

for all
t\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}
. We denote the collection of deterministic Markovian (feed-back) strategies by
\Gamma_{\mathrm{DS}}^{(\ell,n,m)}
whose elements are finite sequences
(\hat{\gamma}_{t}^{(\ell,n,m)})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
of measurable functions such that

\hat{\gamma}_{t}^{(\ell,n,m)}(\mathcal{I}_{t}^{(\ell,n,m)})=U_{t}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}

for each
t\in\mathcal{T}
. Note that we denote the terminal cost functional for
\mathsf{MDP}^{(\ell,n,m)}
as follows

\hat{V}(\underline{\hat{\gamma}}^{(\ell,n,m)}):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{W}_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{\underline{\hat{\gamma}}^{(\ell,n,m)},k},\hat{\nu}^{k})^{2}

(29)

for all
\underline{\hat{\gamma}}^{(\ell,n,m)}\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{DS}}^{(\ell,n,m)}
. Here we have
\hat{\nu}^{k}:=R_{\ell}(\nu^{k})
and
\hat{\mu}_{T}^{\underline{\hat{\gamma}}^{(\ell,n,m)},k}
is the quantized terminal measure-state initialized from
\hat{\mu}_{0}^{k}
evolving with
\Phi^{(\ell,n,m)}
under
\underline{\hat{\gamma}}
for each
k\in\mathcal{K}
.

Remark 9
.

Since both
\mathcal{P}^{(\ell)}(\mathbb{X}_{n})
and
\mathbb{U}_{m}
are finite, the appropriate topology on
\mathcal{P}^{(\ell)}(\mathbb{X}_{n})^{K}\times\mathbb{U}_{m}
is discrete topology. That makes
\bm{\Phi}^{(\ell,n,m)}
jointly continuous on its domain. Recalling the proof of Corollary
4
, we know that
\eta^{(\ell,n,m)}
is weak Feller. Moreover, finiteness of
\mathcal{P}^{(\ell)}(\mathbb{X}_{n})^{K}\times\mathbb{U}_{m}
automatically ensures continuity and boundedness of the cost structure.

Theorem 11
.

Let Assumptions
3.2
,
3.3
and
4
hold. Then, there exists a sequence of nonnegative real numbers
(\varepsilon^{(\ell,n,m)})_{\ell,n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\varepsilon^{(\ell,n,m)}\xrightarrow{\ell,n,m\to\infty}0

such that for all
\ell,n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and all optimal controls
\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}=(U_{t,\ell,n}^{(\ell,n,m)*})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}
for
\mathsf{MDP}^{(\ell,n,m)}
, we have

V(\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*})\leq\inf_{\underline{\gamma}\in\Gamma}V(\underline{\gamma})+\varepsilon_{\ell,n,m}.

Proof.

We utilize the following processes that helps in intermediate steps of this proof

(N1)

\mu_{t+1}^{k}=\Phi(\mu_{t}^{k},U_{t})
with cost functional
V(\underline{U}):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})^{2}
where
\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
and
V^{*}=\inf_{\mathbb{U}^{T}}V
.

(N2)

\bar{\mu}_{t+1}^{k}=\Phi^{(n)}(\bar{\mu}_{t}^{k},U_{t})
with
V_{n}(\underline{U}):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})^{2}
where
\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
and
V_{n}^{*}=\inf_{\mathbb{U}^{T}}V_{n}
.

(N3)

\hat{\mu}_{t+1}^{k}=\Phi^{(\ell,n,m)}(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k},U_{t}^{(m)})
with
V^{(\ell,n,m)}(\underline{U}^{(m)}):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(m)}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))^{2}
where
\underline{U}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}
and
V^{(\ell,n,m)*}=\inf_{\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}}V^{(\ell,n,m)}
.

for all
t\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}
,
k\in\mathcal{K}
,
(U_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
,
(U_{t}^{(m)})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}
. Let
\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}=(U_{t,\ell,n}^{(\ell,n,m)*})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}
be a sequence of optimal control actions for (N3). Then, we compose the objective in the following way

\displaystyle|V(\underline{U}^{(m),*})-V^{*}|

\displaystyle\leq|V(\underline{U}^{(m),*})-V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m),*})|

\displaystyle+|V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m),*})-V^{(\ell,n,m)}(\underline{U}^{(m),*})|

\displaystyle+|V^{(\ell,n,m)*}-V_{n}^{*}|

\displaystyle+|V_{n}^{*}-V^{*}|

Step-1:
In
|V(\underline{U}^{(m),*})-V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m),*})|
, we only have error contribution from the state quantization. We prove a more general claim

\sup_{\underline{U}\subset\mathbb{U}^{T}}|V(\underline{U})-V_{n}(\underline{U})|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0.

Fix any
\underline{U}\subset\mathbb{U}^{T}
, then

\displaystyle|V(\underline{U})-V_{n}(\underline{U})|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})^{2}-W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})^{2}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})-W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})|\cdot|W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})+W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})|

For each
k\in\mathcal{K}
, using reverse triangle inequality we have

|W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})-W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})|\leq W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}}).

For the second term

\displaystyle W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})}\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\pi(\mathrm{d}(p,x),\mathrm{d}(q,y))(\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda|p-q|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\nu^{k})}\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\pi(\mathrm{d}(p,x),\mathrm{d}(q,y))(\operatorname{diam}(S)^{2}+\lambda)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diam}(S)^{2}+\lambda<+\infty.

Therefore, we obtain

|V(\underline{U})-V_{n}(\underline{U})|\leq\frac{2}{K}\sqrt{\operatorname{diam}(S)^{2}+\lambda}\sum_{k=1}^{K}W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}}).

Now the focus is on
W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{t}^{k,\underline{U}},\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}})
for which the relation

\displaystyle W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{t+1}^{k,\underline{U}},\hat{\mu}_{t+1}^{k,\underline{U}})

\displaystyle=W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi(\mu_{t}^{k,\underline{U}},U_{t}),\Phi^{(n)}(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}},U_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi(\mu_{t}^{k,\underline{U}},U_{t}),\Phi(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}},U_{t}))+W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}},U_{t}),\Phi^{(n)}(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}},U_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq L_{\Phi}W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{t}^{k,\underline{U}},\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}})+\frac{1}{n}

holds for all
k\in\mathcal{K}
. The last inequality is due to the Lipschitz property of
\Phi
(Corollary
16
) and the asymptotic stability of the state quantizer
Q^{(n)}
(Lemma
17
). By induction, we obtain

W_{2,\lambda}(\mu_{T}^{k,\underline{U}},\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}})\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{s=0}^{T}L_{\Phi}^{s}.

Hence,

|V(\underline{U})-V_{n}(\underline{U})|\leq\frac{2}{n}\sqrt{\operatorname{diam}(S)^{2}+\lambda}\sum_{s=0}^{T}L_{\Phi}^{s}.

We write
\alpha_{n}:=\frac{2}{n}\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{diam}(S)^{2}+\lambda}\sum_{s=0}^{T}L_{\Phi}^{s}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
for simplicity.

Step-2:
The term
|V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m),*})-V^{(\ell,n,m)}(\underline{U}^{(m),*})|
contributes the total error because of measure quantization. To compute a bound, we apply a similar machinery

\displaystyle|V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m),*})-V^{(\ell,n,m)}(\underline{U}^{(m),*})|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},\nu^{k})^{2}-W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))^{2}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},\nu^{k})-W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot|W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},\nu^{k})+W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))|.

Since
\mathcal{P}^{(\ell)}(\mathbb{X}_{n})
can be embedded into
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
isomorphically,
\mathbb{X}_{n}\subset\mathbb{X}
, and
\mathbb{U}_{m}\subset\mathbb{U}
, we have

|W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},\nu^{k})+W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))|\leq 2\sqrt{\operatorname{diam}(S)^{2}+\lambda}.

The first term is decomposed into

\displaystyle|W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},\nu^{k})-W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))|

\displaystyle\leq|W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},\nu^{k})

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt-W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))|

\displaystyle+|W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt-W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))|.

Using reverse triangle inequality and Proposition
8
, it follows that

|W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},\nu^{k})-W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))|\leq W_{2,\lambda}(\nu^{k},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))\leq\rho_{\ell}\xrightarrow{\ell\to\infty}0^{+}.

We treat the other term by using reverse triangle inequality and a reasoning similar to the previous case

|W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))-W_{2,\lambda}(\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},R_{\ell}(\nu^{k}))|\leq W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},\hat{\mu}_{T}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}})

for which we have the following recursive relation

\displaystyle W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{t+1}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},\hat{\mu}_{t+1}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}})

\displaystyle=W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi^{(n)}(\bar{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},U_{t}^{(\ell,n,m)*}),R_{\ell}(\Phi^{(n)}(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},U_{t}^{(\ell,n,m)*})))

\displaystyle\leq W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi^{(n)}(\bar{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},U_{t}^{(\ell,n,m)*}),\Phi^{(n)}(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},U_{t}^{(\ell,n,m)*}))

\displaystyle\qquad+W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi^{(n)}(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},U_{t}^{(\ell,n,m)*}),R_{\ell}(\Phi^{(n)}(\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},U_{t}^{(\ell,n,m)*})))

\displaystyle\leq L_{\Phi^{(n)}}W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}},\hat{\mu}_{t}^{k,\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}})+\rho_{\ell}

for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
and
k\in\mathcal{K}
, where we use Proposition
8
and Corollary
18
. Consequently, we have

|V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m),*})-V^{(\ell,n,m)}(\underline{U}^{(m),*})|\leq 2\rho_{\ell}\sqrt{\operatorname{diam}(S)^{2}+\lambda}\sum_{s=0}^{T}L_{\Phi^{(n)}}^{s}=:\beta_{\ell,n}.

Note that proof of Corollary
18
asserts that the Lipschitz constant of
\Phi^{(n)}
is not dependent of
n
. So we simply write
\beta_{\ell}
instead of
\beta_{\ell,n}
.

Step-3:
We continue with the third estimate
|V^{(\ell,n,m)*}-V_{n}^{*}|
in which we have two types of errors, namely the errors due to measure quantization and action quantization. Let
\underline{U}^{(\ell,n,m)*}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}
be optimal for
V^{(\ell,n,m)}
and
k,\underline{U}^{*}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
be optimal for
V_{n}^{*}
. Then we consider

|V^{(\ell,n,m)*}-V_{n}^{*}|\leq|V^{(\ell,n,m)*}-\inf_{\underline{U}^{(m)*}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}}V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m)*})|+|\inf_{\underline{U}^{(m)*}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}}V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m)*})-V_{n}^{*}|.

The first summand on the majorizing side is dominated by
\sup_{\underline{U}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}}|V^{(\ell,n,m)}(\underline{U}^{(m)})-V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m)})|\leq\beta_{\ell}
by the preceding part involving the second term in the main decomposition. Define
V_{n,m}^{*}:=\inf_{\underline{U}^{(m)\prime}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}}V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m)\prime})
. For any
r>0
, pick
\underline{U}^{r}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
so that
V_{n}(\underline{U}^{r})\leq V_{n}^{*}+r
exploiting the approximation property of infimum. Then, there is
\underline{U}^{(m),r}\in\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}
such that
\max_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\|U_{t}^{(m),r}-U_{t}^{r}\|_{\mathbb{U}}<\frac{1}{m}
. Let
\omega_{n}
be the modulus of continuity of
V_{n}
, i.e.
\omega_{n}(\delta):=\sup\{|V_{n}(\underline{U})-V_{n}(\underline{U}^{\prime})|:\max_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\|U_{t}-U_{t}^{\prime}\|<\delta\}
,
\delta\in\overline{R}_{+}
. Note that
V_{n}
is a composition and sum of Lipschitz functions, see Corollary
18
, thereby, a uniformly continuous function on
\mathbb{U}^{T}
, and in the special case
\mathbb{U}_{m}^{T}
. Then, we get

V_{n,m}^{*}\leq V_{n}(\underline{U}^{(m),r})\leq V_{n}(\underline{U}^{r})+\omega_{n}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\leq V_{n}^{*}+r+\omega_{n}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right).

Since
r>0
is arbitrary, we get
0\leq V_{n,m}^{*}-V_{n}^{*}\leq\omega_{n}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)
. Moreover
\omega_{n}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\to 0
as
n,m\to\infty
since
V_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is uniformly continuous and so is the limit function
V
. Thus, we get

|V^{(\ell,n,m)*}-V_{n}^{*}|\leq\beta_{\ell}+\omega_{n}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)=:\xi_{\ell,n,m}\xrightarrow{\ell,n,m\to+\infty}0.

Step-4:
Lastly, recall that we have
\sup_{\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}}|V(\underline{U})-V_{n}(\underline{U})|\leq\alpha_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to+\infty}0
. Let
\underline{U}_{n}^{*}
be optimal for
V_{n}
and
\underline{U}^{*}
be optimal for
V
. Then

V_{n}(\underline{U}^{*})\leq V(\underline{U}^{*})+\alpha_{n}=V^{*}+\alpha_{n}\implies V_{n}^{*}\leq V^{*}+\alpha_{n}

and

V(\underline{U}_{n}^{*})\leq V_{n}(\underline{U}_{n}^{*})+\alpha_{n}\leq V_{n}^{*}+\alpha_{n}\implies V^{*}\leq V_{n}^{*}+\alpha_{n}.

Combining both, we obtain
|V^{*}-V_{n}^{*}|\leq\alpha_{n}
.

Step-5:
Finally, the original decomposition yields

|V(\underline{U}^{(m),*})-V^{*}|\leq 2\alpha_{n}+\beta_{\ell}+\xi_{\ell,n,m}.

Defining
\varepsilon_{\ell,n,m}:=2\alpha_{n}+\beta_{\ell}+\xi_{\ell,n,m}
for all
\ell,n,m\in\mathbb{N}
concludes this proof.
∎

We now know that an optimal policy for
\mathsf{MDP}^{(\ell,n,m)}
is near-optimal for the original problem. Then, we can easily solve the dynamic programming equations provided in Theorem
10
and obtain optimal controls for
\mathsf{MDP}^{(\ell,n,m)}
. We know that they perform well enough for finer quantization, in other words, the model can be more accurate if the quantization levels are chosen higher.

5
Robustness to Distributional Initialization Errors, Asymptotic
Consistency and
\Gamma
-convergence to Optimality for the Generalization Problem

In training of Transformers, the optimal control actions are chosen for a given data set. This begs the question "how would the trained Transformer perform for unseen data?". This questions is called the
generalization problem
. In this section, we provide an answer to it by proving that the optimal controls are robust under distributional perturbations. In other words, as a sequence of empirical initial laws converges to the true distribution, we prove the continuity of value function under weak
*
-topology.

For this part, we extend our formulation as follows: Given a sequence of data sets
\mathcal{D}_{r}:=\{(\bm{x}^{k,r},\bm{y}^{k,r}):k=1,2,\ldots,K_{r}\}
, where
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we lift them to the empirical measures of the following form

\mathbb{P}_{r}:=\frac{1}{K_{r}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{r}}\delta_{(\mu_{0}^{k,r},\nu^{k,r})},

where
\mu_{0}^{k,r}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{i,k,r}}
and
\nu^{k,r}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{y^{i,k,r}}
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
and all
k\in\{1,\ldots,K_{r}\}=:\mathcal{K}_{r}
. Hence, we see
\mathbb{P}_{r}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
. Now, we define the cost functional (with an abuse of notation), for all
\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
and
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
, as

V(P,\underline{U}):=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})}P(\mathrm{d}\mu,\mathrm{d}\nu)W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi_{T}(\mu,\underline{U}),\nu)^{2},

where we end up with
\Phi_{T}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathbb{U}^{T}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
in a recursive fashion, using

\Phi_{t}(\mu,\underline{U}|_{t})=\Phi(\Phi_{t-1}(\mu,\underline{U}|_{t-1}),U_{t}),\quad\forall t\in\mathcal{T},

and
\Phi_{1}(\mu,\underline{U}|_{1})=\Phi(\Phi(\mu,U_{0}),U_{1})
. Here we use the notation
\underline{U}=(U_{0},U_{1},\ldots,U_{T-1})\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
and
\underline{U}|_{t}=(U_{0},\ldots,U_{t})
for all
t\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}
. In short,
V
is the value function for the empirical measure of a given data set that passes the first marginal through the Transformer flow using a trajectory of control actions, and compares the resulting distribution with the second marginal. The cost functional here is defined by
\mathfrak{c}((\mu,\nu),\underline{U}^{T}):=W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi_{T}(\mu,\underline{U}),\nu)^{2}
for all
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
and all
\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
. For an empirical measure, for example considering
\mathbb{P}_{r}
, the value function collapses to

V(\mathbb{P}_{r},\underline{U}):=\frac{1}{K_{r}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{r}}W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi_{T}(\mu_{0}^{k,r},\underline{U}^{T}),\nu^{k,r})^{2}.

Therefore, this is indeed the right generalization since
\Phi_{T}(\mu_{0}^{k,r},\underline{U}^{T})
corresponds to the terminal state under the Transformer dynamics as it is illustrated in Figure
4
. Moreover, this generalization is more powerful from the point of our analysis than the previous ensemble formulation we have discuss earlier. However, it is computationally less tractable because of the double lifting. Therefore, we prefer one lifting formulation when discussing the training problem. Because of the intrinsic properties of the problem, we have such nice equivalences that allows us to go back and forth between these formulations.

We endow
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
with the induced weak
*
-topology. That is,
P_{j}\to P
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
, if given a continuous and bounded map
\varphi:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})}P_{j}(\mathrm{d}\mu,\mathrm{d}\nu)g(\mu,\nu)\to\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})}P(\mathrm{d}\mu,\mathrm{d}\nu)g(\mu,\nu).

Under Assumption
3.2
, the underlying space
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
is compact, thereby,
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
is also compact, and can be equipped with a metric generating the same topology. Note that we give
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
the respective Borel
\sigma
-field structure to make it a measurable space.

Lemma 12
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, the value function
V
is jointly continuous on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))\times\mathbb{U}^{T}
.

Proof.

In metric spaces, the continuity is characterized by sequential continuity. So, let
(P_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of measures on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
converging to
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
in the weak
*
-topology of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
, and let
(\underline{U}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of sequences of control actions in
\mathbb{U}^{T}
, converging to
\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
. Under the same assumptions, we know that
\Phi
is uniformly continuous in each of its arguments, see the proof of Proposition
3
.

First, we fix
\underline{A}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
and consider

V(P_{j},\underline{A})=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})}P_{j}(\mathrm{d}\mu,\mathrm{d}\nu)W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi_{T}(\mu,\underline{A}),\nu)^{2}\qquad\forall j\in\mathbb{N}.

We know that
W_{2,\lambda}
is Lipschitz continuous in each of its arguments, and, under Assumption
3.2
, it is bounded. Therefore, the cost function
\mathfrak{c}
is a continuous and bounded function on its domain. By the definition of weak
*
-convergence, we have

V(P_{j},\underline{A})\to V(P,\underline{A})

and this convergence is uniform. Second, we fix
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
and consider

V(P,\underline{U}_{j})=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})}P(\mathrm{d}\mu,\mathrm{d}\nu)W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi_{T}(\mu,\underline{U}_{j}),\nu)^{2}\qquad\forall j\in\mathbb{N}.

The function
\mathfrak{c}
is bounded and measurable since we use Borel
\sigma
-fields and
\mathfrak{c}
is continuous. Using dominated convergence theorem
(
Cohn 2013
, Theorem 2.4.5)
,
we get

V(P,\underline{U}_{j})\to V(P,\underline{U}).

Note that this convergence is uniform as well. Finally, since
V
is uniformly convergent in both of its arguments, we have the joint continuity on its domain.
∎

This lemma is sufficient for us to prove the robustness of value function to varying distributions.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{r}
be the empirical distribution associated to the data set
\mathcal{D}_{r}
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
\mathbb{P}_{r}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}^{*}}\mathbb{P}
. Let
\underline{U}_{r}^{*}
be a sequence of optimal control actions for
\mathbb{P}_{r}
in the sense that if we train the Transformer with the underlying data set,
\underline{U}_{r}^{*}
is optimal. Also, let
\underline{U}^{*}
be a sequence of optimal control actions for
\mathbb{P}
in the sense that
V^{*}(\mathbb{P}):=\inf_{\underline{A}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}}V(\mathbb{P},\underline{A})=V(\mathbb{P},\underline{U})
. Then
V(\mathbb{P},\underline{U}_{r}^{*})\to V^{*}(\mathbb{P})
.

Proof.

For any
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we can perform the following decomposition

|V(\mathbb{P}_{r},\underline{U}_{r}^{*})-V^{*}(\mathbb{P})|\leq|V(\mathbb{P},\underline{U}_{r}^{*})-V(\mathbb{P}_{r},\underline{U}_{r}^{*})|+|V(\mathbb{P}_{r},\underline{U}_{r}^{*})-\underbrace{V(\mathbb{P},\underline{U}^{*})}_{V^{*}(\mathbb{P})}|

The second term on the dominating side goes to zero as
r\to+\infty
since the value function is continuous (Lemma
12
) and since the model is weak Feller (Corollary
4
). For the first term, construct the family of functions

\mathcal{G}:=\{\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}:\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}\}\subset\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})),

where
\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}(\cdot):=\mathfrak{c}(\cdot,\underline{U})
. Since
\mathfrak{c}
is continuous on a compact space, it is uniformly continuous. Meaning that for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
and all
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
, we have

W_{1}(P_{1},P_{2})<\delta\implies|\mathfrak{c}(P_{1},\underline{U})-\mathfrak{c}(P_{2},\underline{U})|<\epsilon.

Here,
W_{1}
is, with an abuse of notation, the product
1
-Wasserstein distance on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
, which generates the weak
*
-topology on the same space. However, the above expression can be equivalently written as for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}\in\mathcal{G}
and all
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
, we have

W_{1}(P_{1},P_{2})<\delta\implies|\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}(P_{1})-\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}(P_{2})|<\epsilon.

By definition,
\mathcal{G}
is equicontinuous. Moreover, by also using the continuity on compact space, we conclude that

\sup_{\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}\in\mathcal{G}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})}|\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}(P)|=\sup_{\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})}|\mathfrak{c}(P,\underline{U})|<+\infty.

Hence,
\mathcal{G}
is also uniformly bounded. By Arzelà-Ascoli theorem
(
Conway 1994
, Theorem VI-3.8)
, it follows that
\mathcal{G}
is totally bounded in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
with sup norm. Hence, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\underline{U}^{1},\ldots,\underline{U}^{M}
in
\mathbb{U}^{T}
such that for all
\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
there is
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
satisfying
\|\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}-\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}^{i}}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon
.

Fix any
\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
and let
\underline{U}^{i}\in\{\underline{U}^{j}:j=1,\ldots,M\}
be the closest one to
\underline{U}
in sup-metric, then consider the following

\displaystyle|V(\mathbb{P},\underline{U})-V(\mathbb{P}_{r},\underline{U})|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{r}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\bigg|\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}^{i}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\bigg|}_{<\epsilon}+\bigg|\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}^{i}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}^{i}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{r}\bigg|+\underbrace{\bigg|\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}^{i}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{r}-\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{r}\bigg|}_{<\epsilon}

\displaystyle<2\epsilon+\bigg|\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}^{i}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}^{i}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{r}\bigg|.

Then we have

\sup_{\underline{U}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}}\bigg|\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{r}\bigg|<2\epsilon+\max_{1\leq i\leq M}\bigg|\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}^{i}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\int\mathfrak{c}_{\underline{U}^{i}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{r}\bigg|.

By the uniform continuity of the value function, again Lemma
12
, the second term on the right hand side goes to zero as
r
goes to infinity. From here, it follows that

|V(\mathbb{P},\underline{U}_{r}^{*})-V(\mathbb{P}_{r},\underline{U}_{r}^{*})|\xrightarrow{r\to+\infty}0.

This concludes the proof of the statement using the decomposition given before.
∎

In principle, given a sequence of data sets
\mathcal{D}_{r}
that are qualitatively getting better, in the sense that they get close to the true distribution, then the resulting control actions are near optimal for the true distribution. If the data used for training comes from an i.i.d. sequence under a fixed probability measure; then the policies obtained for finitely many samples are asymptotically near optimal for the limit problem (with infinite training data). Furthermore, the sequence of generated actions converges, possibly along a subsequence, to an optimal policy for the limit problem, which is often termed as
\Gamma
-convergence.

Corollary 14
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{r}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{w}^{*}}\mathbb{P}
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))
. With the notation of Theorem
13
and under Assumption
3.2
, if
\underline{U}_{r}^{*}=(U_{t,r}^{*})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\in\operatorname{argmin}V(\mathbb{P}_{r},\cdot)
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\{\underline{U}_{r}^{*}:r\in\mathbb{N}\}
has a limit point
\underline{U}^{*}=(U_{t}^{*})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
such that
\underline{U}^{*}\in\operatorname{argmin}V(\mathbb{P},\cdot)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{U}
is compact, so is
\mathbb{U}^{T}
. Therefore, there is a subsequence
(\underline{U}_{r_{j}}^{*})_{j=1}^{\infty}
converging to some
\underline{U}=(U_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{U}^{T}
. Lemma
12
asserts that the value function is jointly continuous. Therefore, we have
V(\mathbb{P},\underline{U})=\lim_{j\to\infty}V(\mathbb{P}_{r_{j}},\underline{U}_{r_{j}}^{*})=\lim_{j\to\infty}V^{*}(\mathbb{P}_{r_{j}})=V^{*}(\mathbb{P})
, where the last equality is due to Theorem
13
.
∎

Numerical Experiment

This section is an evaluation of the proposed triply quantized training scheme on a toy problem of approximating the following function

T((x^{i})_{i=1}^{N}):=\left(\dfrac{1}{\sum_{s=1}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{0.3\langle x^{i},x^{s}\rangle}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{0.3\langle x^{i},x^{j}\rangle}x^{j}\right)_{i=1}^{N}.

In short, we try to approximate the self-attention layer with identity weights of a Transformer with another Transformers with data we generate using
T
. Here is the experiment setup:

•

The inputs
(x^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
live in the space
([-1,1]^{2})^{N}
. Using the previous notation, it corresponds to
\mathbb{X}=[-1,1]^{2}
.

•

We restricted our actions with
k=l=2
. Recall that
W_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times l}
,
A_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times d}
,
b_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
,
Q_{t},K_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
, and
V_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. We also applied the following constraint: Elements of any of these matrices and vectors are limited between
[-1,1]
.

•

In this experiment, we used sequences of length
N=4
and
\beta
was chosen as
0.5
.

•

The activation function was chosen as
\mathrm{ReLU}
.

•

The time horizon (the number of layers) is

.

•

The number of data points generated for training is
K_{\text{train}}=35
.

•

The number of data points generated for testing is
K_{\text{test}}=15
.

The distortion metric used in this example is the quadratic loss, that is after generating
K
-many data points
\bm{x}^{k}:=(x^{i,k})_{i=1}^{N}
,

\mathcal{D}:=\{(\mathbf{x}^{k},T(\mathbf{x}^{k})):k=1,2,\ldots,K\}

\hat{T}

\|T(\mathbf{x}^{k})-\hat{T}(\mathbf{x}^{k})\|^{2}

For the quantization levels, we fixed the state quantization level

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we formulated Transformers as a discrete-time McKean—Vlasov dynamics system and lifted this dynamics to a measure-valued discrete-time Markov decision process. This lifting yielded the Markov property for the ensemble of probability measures that are obtained from initial data, which allowed us to use the dynamic programming principle to establish near-optimality. Although the optimal policies obtained from dynamic programming principle are of closed-loop nature, we argued that they should be seem as a theoretical tool, but not a practical one. Then we showed that from the deterministic flow of measure-valued MDP and the deterministic nature of closed-loop policies, we can get initial-data-dependent open-loop policies, which are compatible with the orthodox training methods of Transformers by fixing neural network weights after training. Therefore, such policies are the correct abstraction of neural network weights from the perspective of optimal control theory. After that, we proposed a quantization-based learning algorithm, which is compatible with the theory presented in this paper, and we showed that it is possible to get near-optimal policies that are computationally more feasible than running dynamic programming on the infinite-dimensional measure-valued space.

Although, by quantizing, we reduced the infinite-dimensional measure-valued space to a finite-dimensional probability simplex, then to a finite set, the training-by-quantization is not designed as a scalable solver of this problem, but as a helper to conduct numerical experiment that is compatible with the theory. The main contribution of the framework presented is providing another perspective on Transformers to understand its structure better and guarantee the existence of optimal weights for it, rather than providing an efficient and scalable algorithm. Also, the subjects of this paper are not a competitor to gradient-descent-based techniques, however; since many applications of Transformers use gradient-descent methods for, in general, a non-convex and non-differentiable system, which indicates that the underlying structure of Transformers is not really understood.

The present work so far is mainly theoretical and aiming to provide a structural comprehension of Transformers from the viewpoint of optimal control theory. As for future research directions, it is possible to consider mean-field case (
N\to\infty
) for this formulation and seek connections with mean-field games. Moreover, making the learning process with dynamics programming more tractable is critical since it has problems with high-dimensional data as many MDP-based systems do. We believe that these directions may have a contribution to understand the connections between Transformers and optimal control theory.

Auxiliary Results used in Section 4

In this part, we provide all of the necessary lemmata and correlated results that are used to prove the main results of this section.

Lemma 15
.

Under Assumption
3.2
,
f
satisfies the following Lipschitz condition for all
(p,x),(q,y)\in\mathbb{X}
,
U\in\mathbb{U}
, and all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})

\|f((p,x);U;\mu)-f((q,y);U;\nu)\|_{2,\lambda}\leq L_{f}(\|(p,x)-(q,y)\|_{2,\lambda}+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu))

for some
L_{f}\geq 0
.

Proof.

Since the estimations are tedious, we go step-by-step. First, for fixed
U\in\mathbb{U}
and for any
(r,z)\in\mathbb{X}
and

f((r,z);U,\omega)=\left(r,W(\sigma(Az+b))+\frac{N(z,\omega)}{D(z,\omega)}\right)

where
N(z,\omega):=\int_{\mathrm{S}}\omega_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qz,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}Vz^{\prime}
and
D(z,\omega):=\int_{\mathrm{S}}\omega_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qz,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}
. By Assumption
3.2
, we have

•

\|N(z,\omega)\|\leq\mathrm{e}^{\beta\|Q\|\|K\|\|z\|^{2}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\beta(\operatorname{diam}(\mathbb{U})\operatorname{diam}(\mathrm{S}))^{2}}\operatorname{diam}(\mathbb{U})\operatorname{diam}(\mathrm{S})=:B_{N}
.

•

|D(z,\omega)|\leq\mathrm{e}^{\beta(\operatorname{diam}(\mathbb{U})\operatorname{diam}(\mathrm{S}))^{2}}=:B_{D}
.

Since
x\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}Vz^{\prime}
is a smooth function for any
z^{\prime}\in\mathrm{S}
, it is also a globally Lipschitz function. Write
L_{1}
for the corresponding Lipschitz constant. From here, it follows that
N
and
D
satisfy the following Lipschitz conditions:

\displaystyle\|N(x,\mu)-N(y,\nu)\|

\displaystyle=\|N(x,\mu)-N(y,\mu)+N(y,\mu)-N(y,\nu)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|N(x,\mu)-N(y,\mu)\|+\|N(y,\mu)-N(y,\nu)\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{\mathrm{S}}\mu_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})(\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}-\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qy,Kz^{\prime}\rangle})Vz^{\prime}\right\|

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\|\int_{\mathrm{S}}\mu_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qy,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}Vz^{\prime}-\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nu_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qy,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}Vz^{\prime}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}\operatorname{diam}(\mathrm{S})\operatorname{diam}(\mathbb{U})\|x-y\|+B\cdot W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)

where the last inequality is obtained as follows: The first summand is a consequence of Lipschitz nature of the map
x\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qx,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}Vz^{\prime}
. Since both
\mathrm{S}
and
\mathbb{U}
are compact, the map
(Q,K,V,z,z^{\prime})\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qz,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}Vz^{\prime}
is bounded and say
B
is the minimal bound. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{\mathrm{S}}\mu_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qy,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}Vz^{\prime}-\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nu_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qy,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}Vz^{\prime}\right\|

\displaystyle=B\cdot\bigg\|\int_{\mathrm{S}}\mu_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qy,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}Vz^{\prime}}{B}

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt-\int_{\mathrm{S}}\nu_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathrm{d}z^{\prime})\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta\langle Qy,Kz^{\prime}\rangle}Vz^{\prime}}{B}\bigg\|

\displaystyle\leq B\cdot W_{1}(\mu_{\mathrm{S}},\nu_{\mathrm{S}})\leq B\cdot W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu).

Here, we normalize the inner function to obtain a function with unit Lipschitz constant. This maneuver enables us to use the dual form of
W_{1}

(
Villani 2008
, Remark 6.5)
. In addition, we have
W_{1}(\mu_{\mathrm{S}},\nu_{\mathrm{S}})\leq W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)
since
p
-Wasserstein distances increase as
p
increases
(
Villani 2008
, Remark 6.6)
and
W_{2}(\mu_{\mathrm{S}},\nu_{\mathrm{S}})\leq W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)
as we include the distance between positional encodings to the cost structure.

So, by writing

\|N(x,\mu)-N(y,\nu)\|\leq\max\{L_{1}\operatorname{diam}(\mathrm{S})\operatorname{diam}(\mathbb{U}),B\}(\|x-y\|+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu))

we establish Lipschitz property of
N
. We write
L_{N}=\max\{L_{1}\operatorname{diam}(\mathrm{S})\operatorname{diam}(\mathbb{U}),B\}
in order to be a little bit more concise. With a similar argument, we also have

|D(x,\mu)-D(y,\nu)|\leq L_{D}(\|x-y\|+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)).

Before moving on to estimating
f
, we remind that the compactness assumptions force
D(z,\omega)
to be bounded away from zero. Write
C_{\text{min}}:=\min_{z,\omega}D(z,\omega)
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|\left(W\sigma(Ax+b)-W\sigma(Ay+b)+\frac{N(x,\mu)}{D(x,\mu)}-\frac{N(y,\nu)}{D(y,\nu)}\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\|W\sigma(Ax+b)-W\sigma(Ay+b)\bigg\|_{2}

\displaystyle+\bigg\|\frac{D(y,\mu)N(x,\mu)-D(x,\mu)N(y,\nu)}{D(x,\mu)D(y,\nu)}\bigg\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|W\|\cdot\|A\|\cdot\operatorname{Lip}(\sigma)\cdot\|x-y\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{C_{\text{min}}^{2}}\left\|(D(y,\nu)-D(x,\mu))N(x,\mu)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{C_{\text{min}}^{2}}\left\|D(x,\mu)(N(x,\mu)-N(y,\nu))\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{diam}(\mathbb{U})^{2}\|x-y\|_{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{C_{\text{min}}^{2}}(L_{D}B_{N}+L_{N}B_{D})(\|x-y\|_{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)).

Letting
L_{2}:=\operatorname{diam}(\mathbb{U})^{2}(L_{D}B_{N}+L_{N}B_{D})C_{\text{min}}^{-2}
, we get

\left\|\left(W\sigma(Ax+b)-W\sigma(Ay+b)+\frac{N(x,\mu)}{D(x,\mu)}-\frac{N(y,\nu)}{D(y,\nu)}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq L_{2}(\|x-y\|_{2}+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\|f(p,x;U,\mu)-f(p,y;,U,\nu)\|_{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(W\sigma(Ax+b)-W\sigma(Ay+b)+\frac{N(x,\mu)}{D(x,\mu)}-\frac{N(y,\nu)}{D(y,\nu)}\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq L_{2}(\|x-y\|_{2}+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)).

A simple application of quadratic mean-arithmetic mean inequality and sub-additivity of square root gives us:

\displaystyle\|f(X;U;\mu)-f(Y;U;\nu)\|_{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda|p-q|^{2}+L_{2}(\|x-y\|_{2}+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu))^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda|p-q|^{2}+2L_{2}(\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{1,\sqrt{2L_{2}}\}\sqrt{(\lambda|p-q|^{2}+\|x-y\|_{2}^{2})+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{1,\sqrt{2L_{2}}\}(\|(p,q)-(q,y)\|_{2,\lambda}+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)).

∎

From now on, we write
L_{f}
for
\max\{1,\sqrt{2L_{2}}\}
in order to make the notation easier to follow. The Lipschitz nature of
f
and the compositional structure of the flow map
\Phi
yield us the following Lipschitz type estimate, which comes handy while showing the convergence of the quantized problem to the original one.

Corollary 16
.

For any
\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})^{K}
and
U\in\mathbb{U}
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{K}W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi(\mu^{k},U),\Phi(\nu^{k},U))\leq L_{\Phi}\sum_{k=1}^{K}W_{2,\lambda}(\mu^{k},\nu^{k})

for some
L_{\Phi}\geq 0
. We here write
\bm{\mu}=(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{K})
and
\bm{\nu}=(\nu^{1},\ldots,\nu^{K})
.

Proof.

We prove the simple case
K=1
, from which the general case follows. Indeed, let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
and
U\in\mathbb{U}
, then let
\pi^{*}
be an
W_{2,\lambda}
-optimal coupling of
\Phi(\mu,U)
and
\Phi(\nu,U)
. Define

\tilde{\pi}(\mathrm{d}(\tilde{p},\tilde{x}),\mathrm{d}(\tilde{q},\tilde{y})):=\pi^{*}(f^{-1}(\mathrm{d}(\tilde{p},\tilde{x}),U,\mu),f^{-1}(\mathrm{d}(\tilde{q},\tilde{y}),U,\nu)).

By a change of variables argument and by Lemma
15
, we get

\displaystyle W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi(\mu,U),\Phi(\nu,U))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\tilde{\pi}(\mathrm{d}(\tilde{p},\tilde{x}),\mathrm{d}(\tilde{q},\tilde{y}))\|(\tilde{p},\tilde{x})-(\tilde{q},\tilde{y})\|_{2,\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\pi^{*}(\mathrm{d}(p,x),\mathrm{d}(q,y))\|f((p,x);U;\mu)-f((q,y);U;\nu)\|_{2,\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq L_{f}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\pi^{*}(\mathrm{d}(p,x),\mathrm{d}(q,y))(\|(p,x)-(q,y)\|_{2,\lambda}^{2}+W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)^{2})

\displaystyle=2L_{f}^{2}W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu)^{2}.

Thus, writing
L_{\Phi}:=\sqrt{2}L_{f}
,

W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi(\mu,U),\Phi(\nu,U))\leq L_{\Phi}W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\nu).

∎

Lemma 17
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
. Then
W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\mu\circ(Q^{(n)})^{-1})<\frac{1}{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be a coupling for the law of
((p,x),Q^{(n)}((p,x)))
with
(p,x)\sim\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X})
. Then,

\displaystyle W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\mu\circ(Q^{(n)})^{-1})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\pi(\mathrm{d}(p,x),\mathrm{d}(q,y))\|(p,x)-(q,y)\|_{2,\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{(p,x)\sim\mu}\|(p,x)-Q^{(n)}((p,x))\|_{2,\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\pi(\mathrm{d}(p,x),\mathrm{d}(q,y))\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

In the last inequality, we use
\|(p,x)-Q^{(n)}((p,x))\|_{2,\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{n}
, given by the definition of
(Q^{(n)})^{-1}
. Thus,
W_{2,\lambda}(\mu,\mu\circ(Q^{(n)})^{-1})\leq\frac{1}{n}
.
∎

Corollary 18
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is
L_{\Phi^{(n)}}\geq 0
such that for all
\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}_{n})
and
U\in\mathbb{U}
, we have

W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi^{(n)}(\bar{\mu},U),\Phi^{(n)}(\bar{\nu},U))\leq L_{\Phi^{(n)}}W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}).

Proof.

Let
\pi^{*}
be an optimal coupling between
\Phi^{(n)}(\bar{\mu},U)
and
\Phi^{(n)}(\bar{\nu},U)
. We define

\tilde{\pi}(\mathrm{d}(\tilde{p},\tilde{x}),\mathrm{d}(\tilde{q},\tilde{y})):=\pi^{*}(f_{n}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}(\tilde{p},\tilde{x}),U,\bar{\mu}),f_{n}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}(\tilde{q},\tilde{y}),U,\bar{\nu})).

Then

\displaystyle W_{2,\lambda}(\Phi^{(n)}(\bar{\mu},U),\Phi^{(n)}(\bar{\nu},U))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\tilde{\pi}(\mathrm{d}(\tilde{p},\tilde{x}),\mathrm{d}(\tilde{q},\tilde{y}))\|(\tilde{p},\tilde{x})-(\tilde{q},\tilde{y})\|_{2,\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\pi^{*}(\mathrm{d}(p,x),\mathrm{d}(q,y))\|f_{n}((p,x);U,\bar{\mu})-f_{n}((q,y);U,\bar{\nu})\|_{2,\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\pi^{*}(\mathrm{d}(p,x),\mathrm{d}(q,y))\|f((p,x);U,\bar{\mu})-f((q,y);U,\bar{\nu})\|_{2,\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq L_{f}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\pi^{*}(\mathrm{d}(p,x),\mathrm{d}(q,y))(\|(p,x)-(q,y)\|_{2,\lambda}^{2}+W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{f}^{2}W_{2,\lambda}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu})^{2}.

In these estimations, we use Lemma
15
and use the fact that
\|Q^{(n)}(p,x)-Q^{(n)}(q,y)\|_{2,\lambda}\leq\|(p,x)-(q,y)\|_{2,\lambda}
, which is evident by the definition of
Q^{(n)}
and the reverse triangle inequality.
∎
