Abstract:

This paper studies finite-horizon stochastic linear-quadratic optimal control problems with random coefficients and Poisson jumps, where the weighting matrices may be random and indefinite. Under a uniform convexity condition on the cost functional, we prove that the associated stochastic Riccati equation (SRE) with jumps admits a unique strongly regular solution. As a consequence, the open-loop optimal control admits a closed-loop representation. The proof does not rely on a global representation of the form
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
or on any nonsingularity condition on the jump multiplier
I_{n}+E
in the state equation. Instead, we construct
P
from the stochastic value flow, and derive the strong regularity of the Riccati solution by a small-interval localization method. In addition, sufficient conditions are obtained for uniform convexity, and examples are presented to illustrate indefinite terminal and control weighting matrices and a nonzero jump martingale component in the SRE.

Key words:
stochastic linear-quadratic optimal control, random coefficient, Poisson jump, stochastic Riccati equation, value flow, open-loop optimal control, closed-loop representation

Introduction

1.1
Background and literature

The study of stochastic linear-quadratic (SLQ) optimal control problems was initiated by Wonham
[
25
]
in 1968 and has since attracted extensive research; see Athans
[
2
]
, Bismut
[
4
,
5
]
, Davis
[
8
]
, Bensoussan
[
3
]
, and the comprehensive treatment in Chapter 6 of the book by Yong and Zhou
[
26
]
. A central question in SLQ theory is the solvability of an associated Riccati equation, which connects the value function to a state-feedback optimal control.

For LQ problems with
deterministic coefficients
, the Riccati equation is an ordinary differential equation (ODE), and the solvability theory is relatively well understood. Under the classical
standard condition
—requiring
Q\geqslant 0
,
G\geqslant 0
,
S=0
, and
R\geqslant\delta I_{m}
for some
\delta>0
—both open-loop and closed-loop solvabilities are guaranteed. Molinari
[
15
]
showed that
Q\geqslant 0
and
G\geqslant 0
are not necessary for open-loop solvability of deterministic LQ problems, though
R\geqslant 0
remains necessary in the deterministic setting.
In contrast, for SLQ problems with Brownian motion, Chen, Li, and Zhou
[
6
]
discovered that even the condition
R\geqslant 0
is not necessary for solvability—the so-called
indefinite
case. This remarkable finding, unique to the stochastic setting, triggered extensive subsequent research on indefinite SLQ problems, including Lim and Zhou
[
13
]
, Chen and Zhou
[
7
]
, Ait Rami, Moore, and Zhou
[
1
]
, and Hu and Zhou
[
10
]
. For SLQ problems with deterministic coefficients, Sun and Yong
[
20
]
and Sun, Li, and Yong
[
18
]
gave a very satisfactory treatment, establishing the equivalence between the strongly regular solvability of the Riccati equation and the uniform convexity of the cost functional.

For SLQ problems with
random coefficients
, the Riccati equation becomes a backward stochastic differential equation (BSDE), usually called the stochastic Riccati equation (SRE), which is highly nonlinear due to the quadratic dependence on the martingale component. Under the standard condition, Tang
[
22
]
proved the unique solvability of the SRE by connecting it to the stochastic Hamilton system; a key step in his approach is to show that the optimal state fundamental matrix
\mathbf{X}
is globally invertible, so that the Riccati process can be recovered via the representation
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
, where
\mathbf{Y}
is the adjoint process. Later, Tang
[
23
]
gave an alternative proof based on the dynamic programming principle and the Doob–Meyer decomposition, bypassing the stochastic flow argument. These works resolved the standard-condition version of an open question raised by Bismut
[
4
]
and Peng
[
16
]
. Under the weaker uniform convexity condition, Sun, Xiong, and Yong
[
19
]
extended the theory to the
indefinite
case with random coefficients. Their proof also relies on the global representation
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
, but requires a new and delicate argument to establish the invertibility of
\mathbf{X}
under the weaker condition. A convenient summary of their method is

\text{Uniform convexity}\;\Rightarrow\;P~\text{bounded, left-continuous}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathbf{X}~\text{invertible}\;\Rightarrow\;P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{SRE solvable},

(1.1)

where
P
is the process such that the stochastic value flow admits the quadratic representation
\widehat{V}(t,\xi)=\langle P(t)\xi,\xi\rangle
, and
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
are the matrix-valued optimal state and adjoint processes.

When the state equation involves
Poisson jumps
, the SRE acquires additional unknown components driven by the compensated Poisson random measure, and the analysis becomes considerably more challenging. Under the standard condition, Zhang, Dong, and Meng
[
27
]
proved the solvability of the SRE with jumps. Their approach combines the dynamic programming principle with a piecewise inverse-flow construction on inter-jump intervals: the time interval
[0,T]
decomposes into finitely many inter-jump subintervals on which the state equation reduces to a pure diffusion SDE and the fundamental matrix is invertible; the Riccati process is then recovered by a semimartingale aggregation and gluing method across jump times. For the indefinite case with jumps, Li, Wu, and Yu
[
12
]
studied a special case under an additional relax-compensator assumption. In the direction of Markovian regime switching, Zhang, Li, and Xiong
[
28
]
and Wen, Li, Xiong, and Zhang
[
24
]
studied the SLQ problem with coefficient jumps driven by a finite-state Markov chain.
In a broader context, Meng, Dong, Shen, and Tang
[
14
]
studied stochastic HJB equations with jumps for non-Markovian control problems with random coefficients, and observed that the dynamic programming principle at random times and the semimartingale property of the value field are both nontrivial in the jump setting, and that their general framework does not cover the LQ problem due to the unbounded control region. These observations further motivate the dedicated LQ analysis in the present paper.

1.2
Main difficulties and contributions

The present paper studies the indefinite SLQ problem with random coefficients and Poisson jumps under the uniform convexity condition. It can be viewed as extending the pure-diffusion result of Sun, Xiong, and Yong
[
19
]
to the jump setting, and simultaneously extending the jump result of Zhang, Dong, and Meng
[
27
]
from the standard condition to the indefinite setting. As we explain below, the combination of jumps and indefiniteness introduces essential new difficulties that cannot be resolved by either approach alone.

(a) Failure of the global
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
approach.
In the pure diffusion case
[
19
]
, the value process
P
is continuous (in particular left-continuous), and a stopping-time contradiction argument shows that the optimal state fundamental matrix
\mathbf{X}
is invertible. With
\mathbf{X}
invertible, the Riccati process is recovered via
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
, and the SRE solvability follows. In the jump setting, however, the aggregated process
P
is only càdlàg, and the optimal state fundamental matrix may lose invertibility at a jump time. We demonstrate this through two explicit counterexamples:
Example
6.1
shows that even with uniform convexity,
\mathbf{X}
can become singular at the first Poisson jump time;
Example
6.2
shows that this obstruction persists even when the control enters the jump coefficient and
I_{n}+E
is nonsingular. Therefore, the entire method (
1.1
) of
[
19
]
breaks down in the jump setting.

(b) Positive definiteness of
\hat{R}
must be derived.
Under the standard condition in
[
27
]
, the uniform positive definiteness of the aggregated control weight
\hat{R}=R+D^{\top}P_{-}D+\int_{Z}F^{\top}(\zeta+P_{-})F\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)
follows directly from the positive conditions
R\geqslant\delta I_{m}
,
Q\geqslant 0
,
G\geqslant 0
, and the nonnegativity of
P
. In the indefinite setting,
Q
,
G
,
R
may all fail to be positive semidefinite, the cross-weighting matrix
S
may be nonzero, and
P
need not be nonnegative. Thus, the positive definiteness of
\hat{R}
cannot be read off from the coefficients and must instead be
deduced
from the control problem itself.

Our proof proceeds in three steps.
First, we establish a Hilbert space formulation for the jump-diffusion SLQ problem,
which yields the quadratic representation of the cost functional and the
characterization of open-loop optimality in the presence of Poisson jumps
(
Section
3
–
Section
5
).
Second, we construct the stochastic value flow and
aggregate it into a bounded càdlàg special
semimartingale via a stopping-time extension and the
T-system framework
of
[
9
,
27
]
(
Section
6
).
Third, we prove the positivity of
\hat{R}
by a
small-interval localization argument and identify the
Riccati drift on inter-jump intervals (
Section
7
).

The main results and contributions are summarized as follows.

(i)

We develop a value-flow construction of the Riccati process for indefinite jump-diffusion SLQ problems with random coefficients. The quadratic value field is aggregated into a bounded càdlàg special semimartingale, without relying on a global representation of the form
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
or on any nonsingularity condition on the jump multiplier
I_{n}+E
. The counterexamples in
Section
6.3
show that this distinction is intrinsic to the jump setting.

(ii)

We prove that the uniform convexity of the cost functional implies the strong regularity and unique solvability of the SRE with jumps. The key step is a small-interval localization argument which derives the uniform positive definiteness of the effective control weighting process from the control problem itself, without imposing positive definiteness conditions on the terminal, state, or control weighting matrices. The Riccati drift is then identified on inter-jump intervals, and a jump-Itô completion-of-squares argument yields the closed-loop representation of the open-loop optimal control.

(iii)

We obtain verifiable sufficient conditions for uniform convexity and provide examples illustrating the genuinely indefinite nature of the problem. In particular, the theory covers cases with indefinite terminal and control weighting matrices, singular jump multiplier
I_{n}+E
, and a nonzero jump martingale component in the Riccati equation.

Section

establishes open-loop optimality and the equivalence between Problems (SLQ-J) and
\widehat{(\mathrm{SLQ}\text{-}\mathrm{J})}
.
Section

constructs the stochastic value flow and the aggregated process

, and explains why the global representation
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
is unavailable in the jump setting.
Section

Section

Section

Section

(i)

Remark 2.2
.

The notion of closed-loop optimal strategy in Definition 2.1(ii) is a

must be optimal simultaneously for all initial states

with a feedback matrix

We conclude this section with a brief account of the T-system aggregation framework, which will be used in

Definition 3.3
(T-system and RCE;
[
9
,
27
]
).

A
T
-system is a family
\{\mu(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}[0,T]}
such that, for every
\tau\in\mathcal{T}[0,T]
,
\mu(\tau)
is
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-measurable, and for every
\tau,\sigma\in\mathcal{T}[0,T]
,

\mu(\tau)=\mu(\sigma)\text{a.s. on }\{\tau=\sigma\}.

We say that the
T
-system is of class
(D)
if the family
\{\mu(\tau):\tau\in\mathcal{T}[0,T]\}
is uniformly integrable. The T-system is said to satisfy
right-continuity in expectation
(RCE) if, for every sequence of stopping times
\tau_{n}\downarrow\tau
a.s., one has
\mathbb{E}[\mu(\tau_{n})]\to\mathbb{E}[\mu(\tau)]
.

Proposition 3.4
(RCE aggregation;
[
27
, Proposition 2.3]
).

Let
\{\mu(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}[0,T]}
be a T-system of class (D) satisfying the RCE condition. Then there exists a unique (up to indistinguishability) càdlàg adapted process
\bar{\mu}
such that
\bar{\mu}(\tau)=\mu(\tau)
a.s. for every
\tau\in\mathcal{T}[0,T]
. If, in addition,
\{\mu(\tau)\}
is a submartingale system, then
\bar{\mu}
is a càdlàg submartingale and admits the Doob–Meyer decomposition.

In
Section
6
we will show that the zero-control value system associated with the SLQ-J problem forms a class (D) submartingale T-system satisfying the RCE.
Proposition
3.4
then produces the bounded càdlàg aggregation of the value process
P
; see

6.8
for the precise statement.

u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,T]
. Define
u=u_{1}\mathbf{1}_{A}+u_{2}\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}
. Then
u\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,T]
, and the corresponding state and conditional cost satisfy

X^{u}=X^{u_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{A}+X^{u_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}},\qquad\widehat{J}(\sigma,\xi;u)=\widehat{J}(\sigma,\xi;u_{1})\mathbf{1}_{A}+\widehat{J}(\sigma,\xi;u_{2})\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}.

Proof.

Since
A\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
, the process
u
is again
\mathbb{F}
-predictable and square-integrable on
[\sigma,T]
. Let
X^{1}
,
X^{2}
, and
X
be the state processes corresponding to
(\sigma,\xi,u_{1})
,
(\sigma,\xi,u_{2})
, and
(\sigma,\xi,u)
, respectively. By linearity of the state equation, the process
X^{1}\mathbf{1}_{A}+X^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}
solves the same stochastic differential equation as
X
with the same initial state
\xi
at time
\sigma
. It follows from uniqueness that

X=X^{1}\mathbf{1}_{A}+X^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}.

Substituting this identity into the definition of the conditional cost and splitting the expectation over
A
and
A^{c}
yield the stated formula for
\widehat{J}
.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\tau=\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}
be a simple stopping time with
0\leq t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}\leq T
and
A_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}
forming a partition of
\Omega
. Define

P(\tau):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}P(t_{i})\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}.

Then
P(\tau)\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{n})
and

\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)=\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle,~\forall\xi\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Proof.

Let
\xi\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Since
\tau
is simple, there exist
\xi_{i}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}.

For each
i
,
Theorem
6.2
yields a unique optimal control
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}[t_{i},T]
for the deterministic-time problem started from
(t_{i},\xi_{i})
, and

\widehat{J}(t_{i},\xi_{i};u_{i})=\widehat{V}(t_{i},\xi_{i})=\langle P(t_{i})\xi_{i},\xi_{i}\rangle.

Define
u:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}
. By repeated use of
Lemma
6.3
,

\widehat{J}(\tau,\xi;u)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{J}(t_{i},\xi_{i};u_{i})\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle P(t_{i})\xi_{i},\xi_{i}\rangle\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}=\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle.

Hence
\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)\leq\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle
.

Conversely, let
v\in\mathcal{U}[\tau,T]
be arbitrary and extend it in any convenient way to each deterministic interval
[t_{i},T]
(for instance, by zero on
[t_{i},\tau)
when
t_{i}<\tau
). Using
Lemma
6.3
again, one has on each atom
A_{i}
,

\widehat{J}(\tau,\xi;v)\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}=\widehat{J}(t_{i},\xi_{i};v)\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}\geq\widehat{V}(t_{i},\xi_{i})\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}=\langle P(t_{i})\xi_{i},\xi_{i}\rangle\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}.

Summing over
i
gives
\widehat{J}(\tau,\xi;v)\geq\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle
a.s. Taking the essential infimum over
v\in\mathcal{U}[\tau,T]
yields the reverse inequality.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
\sigma\leq\tau
be simple stopping times valued in
[0,T]
. Then, for every
\xi\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{u\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau]}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}\ell(s,X(s),u(s))\mathrm{d}s+\big\langle P(\tau)X(\tau),X(\tau)\big\rangle\Big|\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\Big].

Proof.

Set

\mathscr{Y}(u):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}\ell(s,X(s),u(s))\mathrm{d}s+\langle P(\tau)X(\tau),X(\tau)\rangle\Big|\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\Big],~u\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau].

Let
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,T]
be arbitrary, and write
u=\bar{u}|_{[\sigma,\tau]}
. Splitting the cost over
[\sigma,\tau]
and
[\tau,T]
gives

\widehat{J}(\sigma,\xi;\bar{u})=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}\ell(s,X(s),u(s))\mathrm{d}s+\widehat{J}\big(\tau,X(\tau);\bar{u}|_{[\tau,T]}\big)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\Big].

By
Proposition
6.4
, one has

\widehat{J}\big(\tau,X(\tau);\bar{u}|_{[\tau,T]}\big)\geq\widehat{V}\big(\tau,X(\tau)\big)=\langle P(\tau)X(\tau),X(\tau)\rangle,

and therefore
\widehat{J}(\sigma,\xi;\bar{u})\geq\mathscr{Y}(u)
. Taking the essential infimum over
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,T]
yields

\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)\geq\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{u\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau]}\mathscr{Y}(u).

For the reverse inequality, fix
u\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau]
and let
X^{u}
be the corresponding state process on
[\sigma,\tau]
. Write
\tau=\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}
. For each
i
, the random variable
X^{u}(\tau)\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}
-measurable, so by deterministic-time solvability there exists an optimal continuation
v_{i}\in\mathcal{U}[t_{i},T]
for the initial pair
(t_{i},X^{u}(\tau)\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}})
. Define
v:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}\in\mathcal{U}[\tau,T]
and concatenate in time:

\bar{u}:=u\mathbf{1}_{[\sigma,\tau]}+v\mathbf{1}_{(\tau,T]}\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,T].

By construction and
Proposition
6.4
,

\widehat{J}\big(\tau,X^{u}(\tau);v\big)=\widehat{V}\big(\tau,X^{u}(\tau)\big)=\langle P(\tau)X^{u}(\tau),X^{u}(\tau)\rangle.

Hence

\widehat{J}(\sigma,\xi;\bar{u})=\mathscr{Y}(u),

and therefore
\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)\leq\mathscr{Y}(u)
. Taking the essential infimum over
u\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau]
proves the claimed identity.

For simple stopping times
\sigma\leq\tau
and

\xi\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, define

J^{\tau}(\sigma,\xi;u):=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\Big[\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}\ell(s,X^{u}(s),u(s))\,\mathrm{d}s+\langle P(\tau)X^{u}(\tau),X^{u}(\tau)\rangle\Big],\qquad u\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau].

By
Proposition
6.5
,

\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{u\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau]}J^{\tau}(\sigma,\xi;u).

Proposition 6.6
.

There exists a constant
K>0
such that for all simple stopping times
\sigma\leq\tau
and all
\xi\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\big|\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)\big|\leq K(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\big(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\tau-\sigma|\big)^{1/2}.

(6.3)

Proof.

Fix simple stopping times
\sigma\leq\tau
and set

\Delta:=\tau-\sigma.

We prove in two steps.

Step 1: coercivity on
[\sigma,\tau]
.

For every
u\in\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau]
,

J^{\tau}(\sigma,0;u)\geq\varepsilon\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}|u(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(6.4)

Indeed, concatenate
u
on
[\sigma,\tau]
with an optimal continuation from

\tau
to
T
; then (
6.1
) applied to the concatenated
control yields (
6.4
).

Step 2: Hilbert-space representation on
[\sigma,\tau]
.

Repeating the construction of
Section
4
on the stochastic interval

[\sigma,\tau]
with terminal weight
P(\tau)
, we obtain bounded operators

\mathcal{N}^{\tau}_{\sigma}:\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau]\to\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau],\qquad\mathcal{L}^{\tau}_{\sigma}:L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{U}[\sigma,\tau],

and a bounded symmetric random matrix

M^{\tau}(\sigma)\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{n})
such that

J^{\tau}(\sigma,\xi;u)=\llbracket\mathcal{N}^{\tau}_{\sigma}u,u\rrbracket_{\sigma,\tau}+2\llbracket\mathcal{L}^{\tau}_{\sigma}\xi,u\rrbracket_{\sigma,\tau}+\langle M^{\tau}(\sigma)\xi,\xi\rangle,

(6.5)

where

\llbracket u,v\rrbracket_{\sigma,\tau}:=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}\langle u(s),v(s)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s.

Moreover, (
6.4
) implies

\mathcal{N}^{\tau}_{\sigma}\geq\varepsilon I,

and the same argument as in
Proposition
4.2
(ii), with the interval

[t,T]
replaced by
[\sigma,\tau]
and terminal weight
G
replaced by

P(\tau)
, yields

\llbracket\mathcal{L}^{\tau}_{\sigma}\xi,\mathcal{L}^{\tau}_{\sigma}\xi\rrbracket_{\sigma,\tau}\leq K|\xi|^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|.

Hence the unique minimizer of
u\mapsto J^{\tau}(\sigma,\xi;u)
is

u^{*}=-(\mathcal{N}^{\tau}_{\sigma})^{-1}\mathcal{L}^{\tau}_{\sigma}\xi,

(6.6)

and

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}|u^{*}(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq K|\xi|^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|.

(6.7)

Upper bound.

Let
X^{0}
be the state on
[\sigma,\tau]
driven by the zero control. Then

\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)\leq J^{\tau}(\sigma,\xi;0)-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)\leq{}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}\langle Q(s)X^{0}(s),X^{0}(s)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s+\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\Big[\langle P(\tau)(X^{0}(\tau)-\xi),X^{0}(\tau)+\xi\rangle\Big].

Since the coefficients are bounded and
u\equiv 0
, the standard estimate for
linear jump-diffusions gives

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\sup_{r\in[\sigma,\tau]}|X^{0}(r)-\xi|^{2}\leq K(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|.

(6.8)

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}|X^{0}(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq 2|\xi|^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|+2T\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\sup_{r\in[\sigma,\tau]}|X^{0}(r)-\xi|^{2}\leq K(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|\leq K(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\big(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|\big)^{1/2},

and, since
P(\tau)
is bounded,

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\langle P(\tau)(X^{0}(\tau)-\xi),X^{0}(\tau)+\xi\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\leq K\big(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|X^{0}(\tau)-\xi|^{2}\big)^{1/2}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{1/2}\leq K(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\big(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|\big)^{1/2}.

Thus

\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)\leq K(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\big(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|\big)^{1/2}.

(6.9)

Lower bound.

Let
u^{*}
be the minimizer in (
6.6
), and let
X^{*}

be the corresponding state on
[\sigma,\tau]
. Then

\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)=J^{\tau}(\sigma,\xi;u^{*})-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle.

Using the boundedness of
Q,S,R,P
, we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)\geq{}

\displaystyle-K\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}\big(|X^{*}(s)|^{2}+|u^{*}(s)|^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s-K\big(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|X^{*}(\tau)-\xi|^{2}\big)^{1/2}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{1/2}.

By the standard stability estimate for the state equation and
(
6.7
),

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\sup_{r\in[\sigma,\tau]}|X^{*}(r)-\xi|^{2}\leq K\Big[(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|+\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}|u^{*}(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big]\leq K(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|.

(6.10)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\int_{\sigma}^{\tau}\big(|X^{*}(s)|^{2}+|u^{*}(s)|^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq K(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|\leq K(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\big(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|\big)^{1/2},

and

\big(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|X^{*}(\tau)-\xi|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\leq K(1+|\xi|^{2})^{1/2}\,\big(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|\big)^{1/2}.

Hence

\widehat{V}(\sigma,\xi)-\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)\geq-K(1+|\xi|^{2})\,\big(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}|\Delta|\big)^{1/2}.

(6.11)

Combining (
6.9
) and (
6.11
) proves
the claim.

Theorem 6.7
.

For every stopping time
\tau\in\mathcal{T}[0,T]
, there exists a unique bounded
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-measurable random matrix
P(\tau)\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{n})
such that

\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)=\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle,\qquad\forall\xi\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Proof.

For
n\geq 1
, define the simple stopping time on the uniform grid
\{jT/2^{n}\}_{0\leq j\leq 2^{n}}
by

\tau_{n}:=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{(j+1)T}{2^{n}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\frac{jT}{2^{n}}\leq\tau<\frac{(j+1)T}{2^{n}}\}}+T\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau=T\}}.

Then
\tau\leq\tau_{n}\downarrow\tau
a.s.
Since
\tau_{n+1}\leq\tau_{n}
are simple,
Proposition
6.6
gives, for each
i
,

\mathbb{E}\big|\widehat{V}(\tau_{n+1},e_{i})-\widehat{V}(\tau_{n},e_{i})\big|\leq K\Big(\frac{T}{2^{n+1}}\Big)^{1/2},

and the same estimate holds with
e_{i}+e_{j}
in place of
e_{i}
. Hence the sequences
\{\widehat{V}(\tau_{n},e_{i})\}_{n}
and
\{\widehat{V}(\tau_{n},e_{i}+e_{j})\}_{n}
are Cauchy in
L^{1}(\Omega)
. Define

P_{ii}(\tau):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\widehat{V}(\tau_{n},e_{i}),\qquad P_{ij}(\tau):=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big[\widehat{V}(\tau_{n},e_{i}+e_{j})-\widehat{V}(\tau_{n},e_{i})-\widehat{V}(\tau_{n},e_{j})\Big].

By construction,
P(\tau):=(P_{ij}(\tau))
is symmetric. Moreover, every
P_{ij}(\tau_{n})
is
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}
-measurable, and right-continuity of the filtration implies
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}=\bigcap_{n}\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}
; therefore
P(\tau)
is
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-measurable. The uniform boundedness of
P(\tau)
follows from the uniform boundedness of the deterministic-time matrices
P(t)
and
Proposition
6.4
.

Now let
\xi=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}x_{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{B_{\ell}}
be a bounded
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-simple random vector. Since
B_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}
for every
n
,
Proposition
6.4
yields

\widehat{V}(\tau_{n},\xi)=\langle P(\tau_{n})\xi,\xi\rangle,n\geq 1.

Passing to the
L^{1}
-limit gives

\lim_{n\to\infty}\widehat{V}(\tau_{n},\xi)=\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle.

This identifies the unique possible extension of the value field from simple stopping times to the present
\tau
, and we therefore set

\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi):=\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle

for bounded
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-simple
\xi
.

For a general bounded
\xi\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, choose bounded
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-simple vectors
\xi^{m}
such that
\xi^{m}\to\xi
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Since
P(\tau)
is bounded,

\langle P(\tau)\xi^{m},\xi^{m}\rangle\to\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle\qquad\text{in }L^{1}(\Omega),

so the same identity extends to all bounded
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-measurable initial states. Finally, for
\xi\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, let
\xi^{N}:=\xi\mathbf{1}_{\{|\xi|\leq N\}}
. Again by boundedness of
P(\tau)
,

\langle P(\tau)\xi^{N},\xi^{N}\rangle\to\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle\qquad\text{in }L^{1}(\Omega),

which yields the stated representation on
L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Uniqueness is immediate from polarization.

6.2
Aggregation into a càdlàg semimartingale and the stopped problem

Proposition 6.8
.

The family
\{P(\tau)\}_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}[0,T]}
admits a bounded càdlàg adapted aggregation, still denoted by
P
. Moreover,
P
is a bounded special semimartingale:

P(t)=P(0)-\mathscr{A}_{P}(t)+\mathcal{M}(t),\qquad t\in[0,T],

(6.12)

where
\mathscr{A}_{P}
is a predictable finite-variation
\mathbb{S}^{n}
-valued process with
\mathscr{A}_{P}(0)=0
, and
\mathcal{M}
is a càdlàg local martingale. By the predictable representation property in the Brownian–Poisson filtration,

\mathcal{M}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda(s)\mathrm{d}W(s)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{Z}\zeta(s,z)\widetilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z),

(6.13)

for predictable integrands
(\Lambda,\zeta)
that are locally square-integrable.

Proof.

The proof follows the inter-jump aggregation and gluing method
of
[
27
, Theorem 3.5]
.
We first aggregate suitable scalar value systems on each
inter-jump interval and then recover
P
via the local inverse flow.

Let
(T_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
denote the successive jump times of the
Poisson random measure, and set
T_{0}:=0
.
For each
k\geqslant 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

X^{T_{k}\wedge T,\,x,\,0}
denote the state process on

[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T]
starting from
x
at time
T_{k}\wedge T

under the zero control, and define the scalar value system

J_{k}^{x}(\tau):=\widehat{V}\!\bigl(\tau,\,X^{T_{k}\wedge T,\,x,\,0}(\tau)\bigr)+\int_{T_{k}\wedge T}^{\tau}\ell\!\bigl(s,\,X^{T_{k}\wedge T,\,x,\,0}(s),\,0\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\tau\in\mathcal{T}[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T].

(6.14)

By standard moment estimates for the state equation,

\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T]}\mathbb{E}|J_{k}^{x}(\tau)|<\infty
,
so
\{J_{k}^{x}(\tau)\}
is of class (D).
Moreover,
Proposition
6.5
yields the submartingale
property for simple stopping times,
and the continuity estimate in

Proposition
6.6

together with the same dyadic approximation as in

Theorem
6.7
yields the RCE property.
Therefore, by
Proposition
3.4
, for every
k\geqslant 0
and
every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\{J_{k}^{x}(\tau)\}
is aggregated by a càdlàg adapted
submartingale, still denoted by
J_{k}^{x}(t)
,

t\in[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T]
.

For
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
, polarization gives an
\mathbb{S}^{n}
-valued family

\Gamma_{k}(\tau):=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(J_{k}^{e_{i}+e_{j}}(\tau)-J_{k}^{e_{i}-e_{j}}(\tau)\Bigr)_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n},\qquad\tau\in\mathcal{T}[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T].

(6.15)

Since each
J_{k}^{x}
has a càdlàg semimartingale
aggregation, so does
\Gamma_{k}
.

Next, let
\Phi_{k}(t):=\bigl[X^{T_{k}\wedge T,\,e_{1},\,0}(t),\dots,X^{T_{k}\wedge T,\,e_{n},\,0}(t)\bigr]

denote the
n\times n
state flow matrix.
Note that
\Phi_{k}(t)
is defined for all

t\in[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T]
;
on the inter-jump interval
[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T_{k+1}\wedge T)

the state equation is a pure diffusion SDE (no jumps occur),
so
\Phi_{k}
is continuous there and, exactly as
in
[
27
, Theorem 3.5(ii)]
,

\Phi_{k}(t)
is invertible on

[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T_{k+1}\wedge T)
a.s.
We write

\Psi_{k}(t):=\Phi_{k}(t)^{-1},\qquad t\in[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T_{k+1}\wedge T).

(6.16)

By linearity of the state equation,

X^{T_{k}\wedge T,\,x,\,0}(\tau)=\Phi_{k}(\tau)\,x

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\tau\in\mathcal{T}[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T]
.
Combining with

\widehat{V}(\tau,\xi)=\langle P(\tau)\xi,\xi\rangle

from
Theorem
6.7

and
\ell(r,\Phi_{k}(r)x,0)=\langle Q(r)\Phi_{k}(r)x,\,\Phi_{k}(r)x\rangle
,
the polarization identity yields

\Gamma_{k}(\tau)=\Phi_{k}(\tau)^{\top}P(\tau)\,\Phi_{k}(\tau)+\int_{T_{k}\wedge T}^{\tau}\Phi_{k}(r)^{\top}Q(r)\,\Phi_{k}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r,\qquad\tau\in\mathcal{T}[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T].

(6.17)

Consequently, on each inter-jump interval we may
recover
P
by

P_{k}(t):=\Psi_{k}(t)^{\top}\Bigl(\Gamma_{k}(t)-\int_{T_{k}\wedge T}^{t}\Phi_{k}(r)^{\top}Q(r)\,\Phi_{k}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\Bigr)\Psi_{k}(t),\qquad t\in[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T_{k+1}\wedge T).

(6.18)

Indeed, substituting (
6.17
)
into (
6.18
) gives

P_{k}(t)=\Psi_{k}(t)^{\top}\Phi_{k}(t)^{\top}P(t)\,\Phi_{k}(t)\Psi_{k}(t)=P(t)

on
\{T_{k}\leqslant t<T_{k+1}\}
.
Since
\Gamma_{k}
,
\Phi_{k}
,
\Psi_{k}
, and the
finite-variation integral are all semimartingales
on
[T_{k}\wedge T,\,T_{k+1}\wedge T)
,

P_{k}
is an
\mathbb{S}^{n}
-valued semimartingale there.
Thus the T-system
\{P(\tau)\}
is
aggregated by the càdlàg adapted process

P(t):=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}P_{k}(t)\,\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{k}\wedge T\leqslant t<T_{k+1}\wedge T\}},\qquad t\in[0,T],

(6.19)

where the sum is a.s. finite.
By the same gluing argument as
in
[
27
, Theorem 3.5(ii)]
,

P
is a càdlàg semimartingale.
It is bounded
by the value field
established in
Theorem
6.2
.
A bounded semimartingale is special
(see
[
17
, Chapter III, Theorem 33]
),
so the canonical decomposition (
6.12
)
exists with the stated properties.
The representation (
6.13
)
then follows from the predictable representation property
in the Brownian–Poisson filtration.

.

6.3
Why the global
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
method fails

At this point, one might still hope to imitate the pure-diffusion argument of
[
19
, Theorem 6.2]
and recover the Riccati process from a global representation of the form
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
. The next two examples show that this is impossible in general: the first is the simplest degeneracy case, and the second shows that the same obstruction persists even when the control enters the jump coefficient.

Example 6.1
(Failure of invertibility of
\mathbf{X}
).

Let
n=m=1
and take

A=B=C=D=F=Q=S=G=0,R\equiv 1,E\equiv-1.

Then the state equation under the zero control becomes

\mathrm{d}X(s)=\int_{Z}\big(-X(s-)\big)\widetilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z),\qquad X(0)=1.

At the first Poisson jump time
T_{1}
, one has
\Delta X(T_{1})=-X(T_{1}-)
, hence
X(T_{1})=0
and therefore
X(s)\equiv 0
for all
s\geq T_{1}
. On the other hand,

J(0,0;u)=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s,

so (
6.1
) holds with
\varepsilon=1
, and the unique optimal control is
u^{*}\equiv 0
. However,
\mathbf{X}(s)=X(s)=0
for
s\geqslant T_{1}
on the event
\{T_{1}\leqslant T\}
, which has probability
1-e^{-\lambda(Z)T}>0
. Nevertheless, the Riccati equation is perfectly solvable:
P\equiv 0
,
\Lambda\equiv 0
,
\zeta\equiv 0
, and
\hat{R}\equiv 1
.

This shows that, in the presence of jumps, the strategy of proving global invertibility of the optimal state flow and then setting
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
is unavailable under mere uniform convexity.

In Zhang et al.
[
27
, Remark 3.1]
, the condition
I_{n}+E\geqslant\delta I_{n}

is identified as sufficient for the global existence of the stochastic inverse flow.
One might hope that this condition, together with uniform convexity,
would ensure the invertibility of the optimal state fundamental matrix.
The following example shows that this is not the case:
even when
I_{n}+E
is bounded away from zero (so that the

uncontrolled
fundamental matrix is invertible),
the
optimal closed-loop
fundamental matrix can still become singular.

Example 6.2
.

Let
n=m=1
,
Z=\{z_{0}\}
, and
\lambda(Z)=\frac{1}{2}
. Take

A=C=D=S=0,B=1,E=0,F=1,R\equiv 1,Q\equiv G\equiv 2.

Then the state equation is

\mathrm{d}X(s)=u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{Z}u(s)\,\widetilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z),\qquad X(0)=1,

and the cost functional is

J(0,1;u)=\mathbb{E}\bigg[2X(T)^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\Big(2X(s)^{2}+|u(s)|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg].

Since
Q\geq 0
,
G\geq 0
, and
R>0
, the cost functional is uniformly convex and the optimal control is unique. Consider the constant triple
P\equiv 2
,
\Lambda\equiv 0
,
\zeta\equiv 0
. A direct computation gives

\hat{R}=R+\int_{Z}F^{\top}PF\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)=1+\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 2=2,\qquad\hat{S}=B^{\top}P+\int_{Z}F^{\top}PE\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)+S=2,

and
\Gamma^{\circ}=Q+\int_{Z}E^{\top}PE\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)=2
. Hence
\Gamma^{\circ}-\hat{S}^{\top}\hat{R}^{-1}\hat{S}=2-4/2=0
, so
(P,\Lambda,\zeta)
solves the SRE-J with feedback law

\Theta=-\hat{R}^{-1}\hat{S}=-1.

The optimal closed-loop state therefore satisfies

\mathrm{d}X^{*}(s)=-X^{*}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{Z}\big(-X^{*}(s-)\big)\,\widetilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z),\qquad X^{*}(0)=1.

At every jump time
T_{j}
of
N
one has

X^{*}(T_{j})=X^{*}(T_{j}-)+(E+F\Theta)X^{*}(T_{j}-)=(1+E+F\Theta)X^{*}(T_{j}-)=0,

because
I+E=1\neq 0
while
I+E+F\Theta=1+0+1\cdot(-1)=0
. Thus
X^{*}(s)=0
for all
s\geq T_{1}
. Since
\mathbb{P}(T_{1}\leq T)=1-e^{-T/2}>0
, the optimal state flow is singular with positive probability.

This example shows that the obstruction is not caused by the degenerate choice in
Example
6.1
. Even when the control enters the jump coefficient and
I_{n}+E
is nonsingular, the optimal closed-loop jump multiplier
I_{n}+E+F\Theta
may still be singular. Therefore the global representation
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
cannot be used in general.

A(\rho)\leq k
a.s. by (
7.11
), and
A(\rho)\to 0
a.s. as
h\downarrow 0
. For the remainder part, by (
7.18
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\int_{(t,\rho]}|R^{k,h}(s-)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}A(s)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{h}\Big(\mathbb{E}\sup_{r\in[t,\rho]}|R^{k,h}(r)|^{4}\Big)^{1/2}\big(\mathbb{E}A(\rho)^{2}\big)^{1/2}\leq C|\eta|^{2}\big(\mathbb{E}A(\rho)^{2}\big)^{1/2}\to 0.

For the pure-jump part, the pathwise jump formula gives, for
s\in(t,\rho]
,

|J^{k,h}(s-)|^{2}=\int_{t}^{s-}\!\int_{Z}\Big(2\langle J^{k,h}(r-),F(r,z)\eta\rangle+|F(r,z)\eta|^{2}\Big)N(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z).

Integrating with respect to
\mathrm{d}A(s)
and using Fubini for Lebesgue–Stieltjes integrals yields

\int_{(t,\rho]}|J^{k,h}(s-)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}A(s)=\int_{t}^{\rho}\!\int_{Z}\Big(2\langle J^{k,h}(r-),F(r,z)\eta\rangle+|F(r,z)\eta|^{2}\Big)\big(A(\rho)-A(r)\big)N(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z).

Taking expectations, one cannot use the compensator naively because the factor
A(\rho)-A(r)
is not predictable. Instead, since the remaining factor is predictable, the dual predictable projection gives, for every nonnegative predictable
H
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\rho}\!\int_{Z}H(r,z)\big(A(\rho)-A(r)\big)N(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z)=\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\rho}\!\int_{Z}H(r,z)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[A(\rho)-A(r)\mid\mathcal{F}_{r-}\right]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)\,\mathrm{d}r.

Therefore, by the boundedness of
F
, (
7.17
), and Cauchy–Schwarz,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{(t,\rho]}|J^{k,h}(s-)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}A(s)

\displaystyle\leq C|\eta|\int_{t}^{\rho}\mathbb{E}\Big[|J^{k,h}(r-)|\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[A(\rho)-A(r)\mid\mathcal{F}_{r-}\right]\Big]\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+C|\eta|^{2}\int_{t}^{\rho}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\!\left[A(\rho)-A(r)\mid\mathcal{F}_{r-}\right]\Big]\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq C|\eta|\int_{t}^{\rho}\big(\mathbb{E}|J^{k,h}(r-)|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\big(\mathbb{E}(A(\rho)-A(r))^{2}\big)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}r+C|\eta|^{2}\int_{t}^{\rho}\mathbb{E}\big[A(\rho)-A(r)\big]\,\mathrm{d}r.

Since
0\leq A(\rho)-A(r)\leq A(\rho)\leq k
, we have
(A(\rho)-A(r))^{2}\leq k(A(\rho)-A(r))
. Moreover,

\int_{t}^{\rho}\mathbb{E}\big[A(\rho)-A(r)\big]\,\mathrm{d}r=\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\rho}\big(A(\rho)-A(r)\big)\,\mathrm{d}r\leq h\,\mathbb{E}A(\rho).

Hence another application of Cauchy–Schwarz in
r
yields

\mathbb{E}\int_{(t,\rho]}|J^{k,h}(s-)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}A(s)\leq C|\eta|^{2}h\big(\mathbb{E}A(\rho)\big)^{1/2}+C|\eta|^{2}h\,\mathbb{E}A(\rho).

Dividing by
h
and using
\mathbb{E}A(\rho)\to 0
by dominated convergence, we conclude that

\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\int_{(t,\rho]}|J^{k,h}(s-)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}A(s)\to 0.

Since
X^{k,h}=R^{k,h}+J^{k,h}
, combining the estimates for
R^{k,h}
and
J^{k,h}
proves (
7.15
).

Lemma 7.5
(Uniform positive-definiteness of
\hat{R}
).

Under the assumptions of
Theorem
7.3
, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that (
7.8
) holds.

Proof.

Fix a localization sequence
(\tau_{k})
satisfying
(
7.11
).
The proof proceeds in four steps:
(i) a localized spike test,
(ii) error estimation,
(iii) Lebesgue differentiation,
(iv) and a monotone-class extension.

Step (i): Localized spike test.

Fix
t\in[0,T)
,
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

A\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
, and
k\geq 1
.
Set

A_{k}:=A\cap\{t<\tau_{k}\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t},

and let
\rho_{k,h}
and
X^{k,h}
(abbreviated

X^{\prime}
below) be as in (
7.12
).
Consider the localized spike control

u_{k,h}(s):=\eta\,\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\,\mathbf{1}_{(t,\rho_{k,h}]}(s),\qquad s\in[t,T].

The corresponding state is

X_{k,h}=\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\,X^{\prime}
.
By
Proposition
6.11
applied to the stopped
problem on
[t,\rho_{k,h}]
,

J^{\rho_{k,h}}(t,0;u_{k,h})\geq\varepsilon\,|\eta|^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}(\rho_{k,h}-t)\big].

(7.19)

Since
\rho_{k,h}\leq\tau_{k}
, the localization
(
7.11
) ensures that the stopped
stochastic integrals in the Itô expansion are true
martingales on
[t,\rho_{k,h}]
.
Repeating the proof of
Lemma
7.1
on this
interval yields the stopped identity

\displaystyle J^{\rho_{k,h}}(t,0;u_{k,h})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\rho_{k,h}}\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\Big(\langle\hat{R}(s)\eta,\eta\rangle+2\langle\hat{S}(s)X^{\prime}(s\!-),\eta\rangle\Big)\mathrm{d}s

(7.20)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\int_{(t,\rho_{k,h}]}\langle X^{\prime}(s\!-),\,\mathrm{d}\mathscr{N}_{P}(s)\,X^{\prime}(s\!-)\rangle\Big].

Step (ii): Error terms vanish.

Since
\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\leq 1
and
\hat{S}^{(b)}
is bounded,

\frac{1}{h}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\rho_{k,h}}2\,\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\,\langle\hat{S}^{(b)}(s)X^{\prime}(s\!-),\eta\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\leq\frac{C|\eta|}{h}\,\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\rho_{k,h}}|X^{\prime}(s\!-)|\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C|\eta|\,\Big(\mathbb{E}\sup_{r}|X^{\prime}(r)|^{2}\Big)^{\!1/2}\leq Ch^{1/2}|\eta|^{2}\to 0.

For the unbounded part and the residual term,

\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\leq 1
and

Lemma
7.4
give

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\bigg|\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\rho_{k,h}}2\,\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\,\langle\hat{S}^{(u)}(s)X^{\prime}(s\!-),\eta\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{h}\,\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\rho_{k,h}}\big|\langle\hat{S}^{(u)}(s)X^{\prime}(s\!-),\eta\rangle\big|\,\mathrm{d}s\longrightarrow 0,

(7.21)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\int_{(t,\rho_{k,h}]}|X^{\prime}(s\!-)|^{2}\,|\mathrm{d}\mathscr{N}_{P}(s)|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{h}\,\mathbb{E}\int_{(t,\rho_{k,h}]}|X^{\prime}(s\!-)|^{2}\,|\mathrm{d}\mathscr{N}_{P}(s)|\longrightarrow 0.

(7.22)

Combining (
7.19
)–(
7.22
)
and noting that

(\rho_{k,h}-t)/h\to\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\tau_{k}\}}
a.s., we
obtain

\liminf_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{s\leq\tau_{k}\}}\,\langle\hat{R}(s)\eta,\eta\rangle\Big]\mathrm{d}s\geq\varepsilon\,|\eta|^{2}\,\mathbb{P}(A_{k}).

(7.23)

Step (iii):
L^{1}(\Omega)
-valued Lebesgue differentiation.

Fix
k
and
\eta
, and define

Y_{k,\eta}(s,\omega):=\mathbf{1}_{\{s\leq\tau_{k}(\omega)\}}\langle\hat{R}(s,\omega)\eta,\eta\rangle,\qquad s\in[0,T].

Since
R,D,F,P
are bounded and (
7.11
) controls the

\zeta
-term on
[0,\tau_{k}]
, one has

|Y_{k,\eta}(s)|\leq C|\eta|^{2}\Big(1+\int_{Z}|\zeta(s,z)|^{2}\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)\Big)\mathbf{1}_{\{s\leq\tau_{k}\}},

hence

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}|Y_{k,\eta}(s)|\,\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

Therefore
Y_{k,\eta}\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
. By the Lebesgue
differentiation theorem in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, there exists a null set

\mathcal{N}_{k,\eta}\subset[0,T]
such that for every

t\notin\mathcal{N}_{k,\eta}
,

Z_{h}(t):=\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}Y_{k,\eta}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\longrightarrow Y_{k,\eta}(t)\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\Omega)\quad\text{as }h\downarrow 0.

(7.24)

Fix
t\notin\mathcal{N}_{k,\eta}
and let
A\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
be arbitrary.
Since
A_{k}=A\cap\{t<\tau_{k}\}
in (
7.23
), we may rewrite
(
7.23
) as

\liminf_{h\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{A}\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\tau_{k}\}}Z_{h}(t)\Big]\geq\varepsilon|\eta|^{2}\,\mathbb{P}(A\cap\{t<\tau_{k}\}).

(7.25)

Passing to the limit in (
7.25
) by
(
7.24
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{A}\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\tau_{k}\}}\big(\langle\hat{R}(t)\eta,\eta\rangle-\varepsilon|\eta|^{2}\big)\Big]\geq 0,\qquad\forall A\in\mathcal{F}_{t}.

(7.26)

Step (iv): Conclusion.

Take

A=\Big\{\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\tau_{k}\}}\big(\langle\hat{R}(t)\eta,\eta\rangle-\varepsilon|\eta|^{2}\big)<0\Big\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}

in (
7.26
). Then

\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\tau_{k}\}}\big(\langle\hat{R}(t)\eta,\eta\rangle-\varepsilon|\eta|^{2}\big)\geq 0,\text{a.s.}

for every
t\notin\mathcal{N}_{k,\eta}
. Letting
k\to\infty
and using

\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\{t<\tau_{k}\}=\Omega
a.s., we obtain

\langle\hat{R}(t)\eta,\eta\rangle\geq\varepsilon|\eta|^{2},\text{a.s., for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

Finally, taking
\eta\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
and using continuity of the quadratic form

\eta\mapsto\langle\hat{R}(t)\eta,\eta\rangle
, we conclude that

\hat{R}(t)\geq\varepsilon I_{m},\qquad\text{a.s. for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

7.3
Identification of the Riccati drift

Lemma 7.6
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem
7.3
, let
\mathscr{R}_{P}
be the completed residual measure defined in (
7.6
). Then:

(a)

\mathscr{R}_{P}\equiv 0
;

(b)

the predictable finite-variation part satisfies

\mathrm{d}\mathscr{A}_{P}(s)=\Big(\Gamma^{\circ}(s)-\hat{S}(s)^{\top}\hat{R}(s)^{-1}\hat{S}(s)\Big)\mathrm{d}s;

(c)

if
(\bar{X},\bar{u})
is an optimal pair, then

\bar{u}(s)=-\hat{R}(s)^{-1}\hat{S}(s)\bar{X}(s-),\qquad\text{a.e.
}s\in[t,T],\ \text{a.s.}

(7.27)

In particular,
(P,\Lambda,\zeta)
satisfies the SRE-J (
7.1
).

Proof.

By
Lemma
7.5
, the matrix process
\hat{R}
is uniformly positive definite, so the stopped completed identity (
7.7
) is available. On any stochastic interval
[t,\tau]
for which the integrals are well-defined, substituting an admissible pair
(X,u)
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle P(\tau)X(\tau),X(\tau)\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle P(t)\xi,\xi\rangle+\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\tau}\ell(s,X(s),u(s))\,\mathrm{d}s

(7.28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\tau}\Big|\hat{R}(s)^{1/2}u(s)+\hat{R}(s)^{-1/2}\hat{S}(s)X(s-)\Big|^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\mathbb{E}\int_{(t,\tau]}\langle X(s-),\mathrm{d}\mathscr{R}_{P}(s)\,X(s-)\rangle.

To identify the drift, we make explicit the two key steps from the
piecewise inverse-flow argument of

[
27
, Theorem 4.1(i)]
. The logic is local in time:
first we show the residual measure vanishes between successive jump
times, then we rule out atoms at the jump times by predictability.

Step 1: Vanishing on each inter-jump interval.

Since
\lambda(Z)<\infty
, the Poisson random measure has finite
activity. Denote the jump times by

0=T_{0}<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{N}<T_{N+1}=T
. On each stochastic interval

(T_{k-1},T_{k})
, the state equation reduces to a pure diffusion SDE
with bounded coefficients, and the associated fundamental matrix

\Phi_{k}
is invertible a.s. Applying the completion-of-squares identity
(
7.28
) on
[T_{k-1},T_{k}\wedge\tau]
for suitable
stopping times
\tau
and performing the change-of-variables
argument of
[
27
, Theorem 4.1(i)]
on this
diffusion interval yields

\mathscr{R}_{P}\big|_{(T_{k-1},T_{k})}\equiv 0,\qquad k=1,\dots,N+1.

Step 2: No atoms at jump times.

By definition (
7.6
),

\mathrm{d}\mathscr{R}_{P}(s)=(\Gamma^{\circ}(s)-\hat{S}(s)^{\top}\hat{R}(s)^{-1}\hat{S}(s))\mathrm{d}s-\mathrm{d}\mathscr{A}_{P}(s)
.
Both terms on the right are predictable, so
\mathscr{R}_{P}
is a
predictable finite-variation matrix measure. In the
Brownian–Poisson filtration, the jump times
T_{1},\dots,T_{N}
are
totally inaccessible stopping times. A predictable finite-variation
measure cannot charge totally inaccessible times
(see, e.g.,
[
17
, Chapter I, Theorem 2.24]
).
Hence
\mathscr{R}_{P}(\{T_{k}\})=0
for
k=1,\dots,N
.

Combining Steps 1 and 2,
\mathscr{R}_{P}\equiv 0
on
[0,T]
.
Consequently,

\mathrm{d}\mathscr{A}_{P}(s)=\Big(\Gamma^{\circ}(s)-\hat{S}(s)^{\top}\hat{R}(s)^{-1}\hat{S}(s)\Big)\mathrm{d}s,

which proves
(a)
and
(b)
.

Finally, let
(\bar{X},\bar{u})
be an optimal pair. Since
\mathscr{R}_{P}\equiv 0
, the residual term in (
7.28
) vanishes for every
\tau
, and passing
\tau\uparrow T
via dominated convergence (using
P\in S^{\infty}
,
\bar{X}\in S^{2}
, and the nonnegativity of the completed-square term) gives

0=\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}\Big|\hat{R}(s)^{1/2}\bar{u}(s)+\hat{R}(s)^{-1/2}\hat{S}(s)\bar{X}(s-)\Big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

hence

\bar{u}(s)=-\hat{R}(s)^{-1}\hat{S}(s)\bar{X}(s-),\text{a.e.\ }s\in[t,T],\ \text{a.s.}

This proves (
7.27
).

7.4
Feedback representation and uniqueness

Lemma 7.7
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem
7.3
, the local martingale

\mathcal{M}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda(s)\mathrm{d}W(s)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{Z}\zeta(s,z)\widetilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)

is in fact a BMO martingale, i.e.

\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}[0,T]}\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\int_{\tau}^{T}\Big(|\Lambda(s)|^{2}+\int_{Z}|\zeta(s,z)|^{2}\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)\Big)\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

In particular,

\Lambda\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;\mathbb{S}^{n}),\zeta\in G^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T,\lambda;\mathbb{S}^{n}).

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary stopping time
\tau\in\mathcal{T}[0,T]
. Apply Itô’s
formula to
|P(s)|^{2}
on
[\tau,T]
and take conditional expectation with
respect to
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
. Using the Riccati drift identified in

Lemma
7.6
, the boundedness of
P
, and the nonnegativity of

\hat{S}^{\top}\hat{R}^{-1}\hat{S}
, we obtain the desired conditional
square-integrability bound exactly as in

[
27
, Theorem 4.1(ii)]
. Taking
\tau=0
gives the
announced
L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;\mathbb{S}^{n})\times G^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T,\lambda;\mathbb{S}^{n})
integrability.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
(P^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})
be an adapted solution of the SRE-J
(
7.1
) such that
P^{\prime}\in S^{\infty}_{\mathbb{F}}([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{n})
,

(\Lambda^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;\mathbb{S}^{n})\times G^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T,\lambda;\mathbb{S}^{n})
, and

\hat{R}^{\prime}\geq\delta I_{m}
for some
\delta>0
.
Define
\Theta^{\prime}:=-(\hat{R}^{\prime})^{-1}\hat{S}^{\prime}
.
Then, for every
(t,\xi)\in\mathcal{D}
, there exists a unique
admissible pair
(X^{\prime},u^{\prime})
with
u^{\prime}(s)=\Theta^{\prime}(s)X^{\prime}(s-)
, and

J(t,\xi;u)=\mathbb{E}\langle P^{\prime}(t)\xi,\xi\rangle+\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}\Big|\hat{R}^{\prime}(s)^{1/2}\bigl(u(s)-\Theta^{\prime}(s)X(s-)\bigr)\Big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

(7.29)

for every admissible
u\in\mathcal{U}[t,T]
with state
X
.

Proof.

Since
\hat{R}^{\prime}\geq\delta I_{m}
and
B,D,F
are bounded,

|\Theta^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\leq C(1+|\Lambda^{\prime}(s)|^{2}+\int_{Z}|\zeta^{\prime}(s,z)|^{2}\lambda(\mathrm{d}z))
,
so
\Theta^{\prime}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
.

Step 1: Well-posedness of the closed-loop SDE.

Consider the SDE

\mathrm{d}X^{\prime}(s)=(A+B\Theta^{\prime})X^{\prime}(s-)\,\mathrm{d}s+(C+D\Theta^{\prime})X^{\prime}(s-)\,\mathrm{d}W+\int_{Z}(E+F\Theta^{\prime})X^{\prime}(s-)\,\widetilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z),\quad X^{\prime}(t)=\xi.

(7.30)

Since the coefficients
A+B\Theta^{\prime}
, etc., are predictable and locally
square-integrable (by the
L^{2}
integrability of
\Theta^{\prime}
), the SDE
admits a unique strong solution up to a possible explosion time.
Define the stopping times

\gamma_{j}:=T\wedge\inf\{s\geq t:|X^{\prime}(s)|\geq j\}
,
j\geq 1
.
On
[t,\gamma_{j}]
the process
X^{\prime}
is bounded, so

u^{\prime}_{j}(s):=\Theta^{\prime}(s)X^{\prime}(s-)
is square-integrable on
[t,\gamma_{j}]
.

Step 2: Localized Itô identity.

Since
(P^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})
solves the SRE-J, the residual measure

\mathscr{R}_{P^{\prime}}\equiv 0
. Applying the Itô formula to

\langle P^{\prime}(s)X^{\prime}(s),X^{\prime}(s)\rangle
on
[t,\gamma_{j}\wedge\theta^{\prime}_{k}]

(where
(\theta^{\prime}_{k})
localizes the martingale integrals) and using

\mathscr{R}_{P^{\prime}}\equiv 0
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\langle P^{\prime}(\gamma_{j}\wedge\theta^{\prime}_{k})X^{\prime}(\gamma_{j}\wedge\theta^{\prime}_{k}),X^{\prime}(\gamma_{j}\wedge\theta^{\prime}_{k})\rangle+\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\gamma_{j}\wedge\theta^{\prime}_{k}}\ell(s,X^{\prime},u^{\prime}_{j})\,\mathrm{d}s=\mathbb{E}\langle P^{\prime}(t)\xi,\xi\rangle.

Here the completion-of-squares term vanishes because

u^{\prime}_{j}=\Theta^{\prime}X^{\prime}_{-}
on
[t,\gamma_{j}]
.

Step 3:
L^{2}
estimate for the feedback control and non-explosion.

Set
\tau^{\prime}:=\gamma_{j}\wedge\theta^{\prime}_{k}
. Introduce the stopped cost functional with terminal weight
P^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime})
,

\mathfrak{J}^{\tau^{\prime}}_{P^{\prime}}(t,\xi;u):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{t}^{\tau^{\prime}}\ell(s,X^{u}(s),u(s))\,\mathrm{d}s+\langle P^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime})X^{u}(\tau^{\prime}),X^{u}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle\Big],

where
X^{u}
denotes the state on
[t,\tau^{\prime}]
driven by
u
. Repeating the Hilbert-space construction of Section 4 on the stochastic interval
[t,\tau^{\prime}]
with terminal weight
P^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime})
, we obtain bounded operators

\mathcal{N}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}:\mathcal{U}[t,\tau^{\prime}]\to\mathcal{U}[t,\tau^{\prime}],\qquad\mathcal{L}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}:L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{U}[t,\tau^{\prime}],

and a bounded symmetric random matrix
M^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{n})
such that

\mathfrak{J}^{\tau^{\prime}}_{P^{\prime}}(t,\xi;u)=\llbracket\mathcal{N}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}u,u\rrbracket_{t,\tau^{\prime}}+2\llbracket\mathcal{L}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\xi,u\rrbracket_{t,\tau^{\prime}}+\mathbb{E}\langle M^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\xi,\xi\rangle,

where

\llbracket u,v\rrbracket_{t,\tau^{\prime}}:=\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\tau^{\prime}}\langle u(s),v(s)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s.

By
Proposition
6.11
, the stopped problem is uniformly convex, hence

\mathcal{N}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\geq\varepsilon I.

Moreover, the same argument as in
Proposition
4.2
(ii) yields

\llbracket\mathcal{L}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\xi,\mathcal{L}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\xi\rrbracket_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\leq C\,\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{2},

with
C
independent of
j,k
.

By Step 2,

\mathfrak{J}^{\tau^{\prime}}_{P^{\prime}}(t,\xi;u^{\prime}_{j})=\mathbb{E}\langle P^{\prime}(t)\xi,\xi\rangle.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\varepsilon\,\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\tau^{\prime}}|u^{\prime}_{j}(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\llbracket\mathcal{N}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}u^{\prime}_{j},u^{\prime}_{j}\rrbracket_{t,\tau^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{J}^{\tau^{\prime}}_{P^{\prime}}(t,\xi;u^{\prime}_{j})-2\llbracket\mathcal{L}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\xi,u^{\prime}_{j}\rrbracket_{t,\tau^{\prime}}-\mathbb{E}\langle M^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\xi,\xi\rangle

\displaystyle\leq C\,\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{2}+2\big|\llbracket\mathcal{L}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\xi,u^{\prime}_{j}\rrbracket_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\big|.

Using Young’s inequality and the bound on
\mathcal{L}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}
, we obtain

2\big|\llbracket\mathcal{L}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\xi,u^{\prime}_{j}\rrbracket_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\big|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\tau^{\prime}}|u^{\prime}_{j}(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\,\llbracket\mathcal{L}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\xi,\mathcal{L}^{P^{\prime}}_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\xi\rrbracket_{t,\tau^{\prime}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\tau^{\prime}}|u^{\prime}_{j}(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s+C\,\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{2}.

Hence

\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\tau^{\prime}}|u^{\prime}_{j}(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C\,\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{2},

with
C
independent of
j,k
. Applying the standard state estimate to the closed-loop equation on
[t,\tau^{\prime}]
gives

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{s\in[t,\tau^{\prime}]}|X^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\Big]\leq C\,\mathbb{E}\Big[|\xi|^{2}+\int_{t}^{\tau^{\prime}}|u^{\prime}_{j}(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big]\leq C\,\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{2}.

Letting
k\to\infty
and then using Fatou’s lemma, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{\gamma_{j}}|u^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{s\in[t,\gamma_{j}]}|X^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\Big]\leq C\,\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{2},\qquad j\geq 1.

Since
|X^{\prime}(\gamma_{j})|\geq j
on
\{\gamma_{j}<T\}
, the above estimate implies

so
\gamma_{j}\uparrow T
a.s. and the closed-loop SDE (

) does not explode. Finally, letting
j\to\infty
and using Fatou’s lemma once more, we conclude that
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}[t,T]
and
X^{\prime}\in S^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(t,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Step 4: Verification identity for general

.

For any admissible
u\in\mathcal{U}[t,T]
with state
X
, applying the
Itô formula to
\langle P^{\prime}(s)X(s),X(s)\rangle
on
[t,T]
, using

\mathscr{R}_{P^{\prime}}\equiv 0
, and completing the square yields
(
7.29
) by the same localization and
dominated-convergence argument as in
.

Corollary 7.9
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem
7.3
, for every
admissible pair
(X,u)
with initial pair
(t,\xi)\in\mathcal{D}
,

J(t,\xi;u)=\mathbb{E}\langle P(t)\xi,\xi\rangle+\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}\Big|\hat{R}(s)^{1/2}\bigl(u(s)+\hat{R}(s)^{-1}\hat{S}(s)X(s-)\bigr)\Big|^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

(7.31)

Proof.

By
Lemma
7.6
,
\mathscr{R}_{P}\equiv 0
, so the
residual term in the stopped identity vanishes. By

Lemma
7.7
,
(\Lambda,\zeta)\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}\times G^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}
, hence all terms in the localized identity
(

) are globally integrable. Passing to
the limit along
\theta_{k}\uparrow T
via dominated convergence
(for the terminal term, using
P\in S^{\infty}
and

)

).

Proof of
Theorem
7.3
.

Existence (Parts (i)–(iv)).

Part (i) follows from
Lemma
6.1
and
Theorem
5.2
.
Theorem
6.7
identifies the random-time value field (with the extension from
L^{\infty}
to
L^{2}
already built in), and
Proposition
6.8
yields a bounded càdlàg special semimartingale
P
together with a local martingale representation. Part (iii) is
Lemma
7.5
, and Part (iv) is
Lemma
7.6
. Finally,
Lemma
7.7
upgrades the local martingale representation to

\Lambda\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T;\mathbb{S}^{n}),\zeta\in G^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}(0,T,\lambda;\mathbb{S}^{n}),

so Part (ii) is complete.

Feedback representation (Part (v)).
Fix
(t,\xi)\in\mathcal{D}
, and let
(X^{*},u^{*})
be the unique open-loop optimal pair given by Part (i).
Lemma
7.6
(c) yields

u^{*}(s)=-\hat{R}(s)^{-1}\hat{S}(s)X^{*}(s-)=\Theta(s)X^{*}(s-),\qquad\text{a.e. }s\in[t,T],\ \text{a.s.}

Substituting this identity into the state equation shows that
X^{*}
satisfies the closed-loop equation (
2.8
). This proves Part (v).

Uniqueness.

Let
(P^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})
be another solution of the SRE-J
(
7.1
) in the class stated in Part (iv). By

Lemma
7.8
,

\widehat{V}(t,\xi)=\langle P^{\prime}(t)\xi,\xi\rangle,\qquad\forall(t,\xi)\in\mathcal{D}.

On the other hand, Part (ii) gives

\widehat{V}(t,\xi)=\langle P(t)\xi,\xi\rangle,\forall(t,\xi)\in\mathcal{D}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\langle(P^{\prime}(t)-P(t))\xi,\xi\rangle=0,\forall\xi\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Taking first
\xi=\mathbf{1}_{A}e_{i}
and then

\xi=\mathbf{1}_{A}(e_{i}+e_{j})
, with
A\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
and

1\leq i,j\leq n
, yields equality of all diagonal and

a.s. for every

.
The càdlàg property then implies indistinguishability, and

and
\zeta^{\prime}=\zeta
.

Remark 7.10
(Comparison with
[
27
]
).

The structural backbone of our proof is the same as in
[
27
]
: semimartingale aggregation via the T-system, followed by piecewise inverse-flow and gluing on inter-jump intervals. What changes is the origin of the positive definiteness of the control weight. In
[
27
]
, one assumes the standard condition and obtains
\hat{R}\geq\delta I_{m}
from the positive-definiteness conditions on the data. In the present indefinite setting,
Q
,
G
, and
R
may all fail to be positive semidefinite; the matrix
\hat{R}
becomes positive because the cost functional is uniformly convex, and
Lemma
7.5
extracts this positivity by a localization argument.

This distinction is precisely why
Example
6.1
is important. In the jump setting, the global inverse-flow construction
P=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}^{-1}
is unavailable under mere uniform convexity. The correct method is instead:
value field
\Rightarrow
semimartingale aggregation
\Rightarrow
positivity of
\hat{R}
from uniform convexity
\Rightarrow
Zhang’s drift-identification method
.

Remark 7.11
(Invertibility under additional assumptions).

If one additionally assumes that
I_{n}+E(s,z)+F(s,z)\Theta(s)
is uniformly nonsingular, then the optimal closed-loop state fundamental matrix is invertible, and one recovers the more classical decoupling relations of the form
\mathbf{Y}=P\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Z}=(\Lambda+P_{-}(C+D\Theta))\mathbf{X}
on the pre-jump level. This is an optional refinement, not a prerequisite for the solvability theory developed here.

8
Sufficient Conditions for Uniform Convexity

We present some sufficient conditions on the coefficients of the state equation and the weighting matrices that guarantee the uniform convexity (
6.1
). These extend the results of
[
19
, Section 7]
to the jump-diffusion setting. We begin with the standard conditions; the more refined results in Theorems
8.2
–
8.4
below show that neither (
4.18
) nor (
4.19
) is necessary for the uniform convexity.

Proposition 8.1
(Standard sufficient conditions).

Under conditions (
H1
)–(
H2
), suppose that either (
4.18
) or (
4.19
) holds. Then the mapping
u\mapsto J(t,0;u)
is uniformly convex for all
t\in[0,T)
.

Proof.

(i) Suppose (
4.18
) holds. Consider the state SDE with
\xi=0
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}X^{(u)}\left(s\right)=&\left[A\left(s\right)X^{(u)}\left(s-\right)+B\left(s\right)u\left(s\right)\right]\mathrm{d}s+\left[C\left(s\right)X^{(u)}\left(s-\right)+D\left(s\right)u\left(s\right)\right]\mathrm{d}W\left(s\right)\\
&+\int_{Z}{\left[E\left(s,z\right)X^{(u)}\left(s-\right)+F\left(s,z\right)u\left(s\right)\right]\widetilde{N}\left(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z\right)},\quad s\in[t,T],\\
X^{(u)}(t)=&0.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(8.1)

Under condition (
4.18
), since
Q\geqslant 0
,
G\geqslant 0
,
S=0
, and
R\geqslant\varepsilon I_{m}
:

\displaystyle J(t,0;u)=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}\left[\langle GX^{(u)}(T),X^{(u)}(T)\rangle+\int_{t}^{T}{\langle Q(s)X^{(u)}(s),X^{(u)}(s)\rangle+\langle R(s)u(s),u(s)\rangle}\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{\langle R(s)u(s),u(s)\rangle}\mathrm{d}s\geqslant\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{|u(s)|^{2}}\mathrm{d}s.

(ii) Suppose (
4.19
) holds. We may assume
F(s,z)^{\top}F(s,z)\geqslant\varepsilon I_{n}
(the case
D^{\top}D\geqslant\varepsilon I_{n}
is similar), so
F(s,z)^{-1}
exists and is uniformly bounded. By the uniqueness of SDEs (
Lemma
3.1
), we can rewrite (
8.1
) in terms of a BSDE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}Y\left(s\right)=~&\Bigg\{\left[A\left(s\right)-B\left(s\right)\frac{1}{\lambda\left(Z\right)}\int_{Z}{F\left(s,z\right)^{-1}E\left(s,z\right)\lambda\left(\mathrm{d}z\right)}\right]Y\left(s\right)+B\left(s\right)\frac{1}{\lambda\left(Z\right)}\int_{Z}{F\left(s,z\right)^{-1}r\left(s,z\right)\lambda\left(\mathrm{d}z\right)}\Bigg\}\mathrm{d}s\\
&+Z\left(s\right)\mathrm{d}W\left(s\right)+\int_{Z}{r\left(s,z\right)\widetilde{N}\left(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z\right)},\quad s\in[t,T],\\
Y(T)=&~X^{(u)}\left(T\right),\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(8.2)

with
Y(s)=X^{(u)}(s)
,
Z(s)=C(s)X^{(u)}(s-)+D(s)u(s)
,
r(s,z)=E(s,z)X^{(u)}(s-)+F(s,z)u(s)
.
By
Lemma
3.1
, the unique adapted solution
(Y,Z,r)\in S_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(t,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(t,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times G_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(t,T,\lambda;\mathbb{R}^{n})
of (
8.2
) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\mathrm{sup}}\limits_{t\leq s\leq T}|Y(s)|^{2}+\int_{t}^{T}{|}Z(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t}^{T}{\int_{Z}{|r(s,z)|^{2}}\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}\mathrm{d}s\right]\leqslant K\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{(u)}(T)|^{2}\right],

(8.3)

where
K>0
is independent of
X^{(u)}(T)
. By (
H1
), there exists
\beta>0
such that
\int_{Z}{|E(s,z)|^{2}\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}+|C(s)|^{2}\leqslant\beta
. Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
satisfy
2(\gamma^{-1}-1)T\beta<1
. Using
(a+b)^{2}\geqslant(1-\gamma)a^{2}-(\gamma^{-1}-1)b^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{|}Z(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{|D(s)u(s)|^{2}}\mathrm{d}s-(\gamma^{-1}-1)\beta T\mathbb{E}\mathop{\mathrm{sup}}\limits_{t\leq s\leq T}|X(s)|^{2},

(8.4)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{\int_{Z}{|r(s,z)|^{2}}\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}\mathrm{d}s\geqslant(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{\int_{Z}{|F(s,z)u(s)|^{2}}\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}\mathrm{d}s-(\gamma^{-1}-1)\beta T\mathbb{E}\mathop{\mathrm{sup}}\limits_{t\leq s\leq T}|X(s)|^{2}.

(8.5)

Combining (
8.4
)–(
8.5
) and noting
D(s)^{\top}D(s)+\int_{Z}{F(s,z)^{\top}F(s,z)\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}\geqslant\varepsilon I_{n}
yields

\displaystyle K\mathbb{E}|X^{(u)}(T)|^{2}\geqslant(1-\gamma)\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{|}u(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Finally, since
Q,R\geqslant 0
,
S=0
, and
G\geqslant\varepsilon I_{n}
:

J(t,0;u)\geqslant\mathbb{E}\langle GX^{(u)}(T),X^{(u)}(T)\rangle\geqslant\varepsilon\mathbb{E}|X^{(u)}(T)|^{2}\geqslant\frac{(1-\gamma)\varepsilon^{2}}{K}\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{|}u(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let (
H1
)–(
H2
) hold. Suppose that
B(\cdot)=0
,
C(\cdot)=0
,
E(\cdot,\cdot)=0
,
S(\cdot)=0
. Let
\Phi=\{\Phi(s);0\leqslant s\leqslant T\}
be the solution to the random ODE

\mathrm{d}\Phi(s)=A(s)\Phi(s)\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\Phi(0)=I_{n}.

Let
\lambda_{G}
and
\lambda_{Q}(s)
be the essential infimums of the smallest eigenvalues of
G
and
Q(s)
, respectively. If for some
\delta>0
,

\left[\frac{\lambda_{G}}{\|\Phi(T)^{-1}\|_{\infty}^{2}}+\int_{r}^{T}{\frac{\lambda_{Q}(s)}{\|\Phi(s)^{-1}\|_{\infty}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s}\right]\frac{1}{\|\Phi(r)\|_{\infty}^{2}}\left[D(r)^{\top}D(r)+\int_{Z}{F(r,z)^{\top}F(r,z)\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}\right]+R(r)\geqslant\delta I_{m},

(8.6)

a.s., a.e.
r\in[0,T]
, then the mapping
u\mapsto J(t,0;u)
is uniformly convex for every
t\in[0,T)
.

Proof.

The proof follows the same line as
[
19
, Theorem 7.2]
, with the additional jump contribution. When
B=C=E=S=0
, for each
u\in\mathcal{U}[t,T]
, the state with initial condition
\xi=0
is

X(s)=\Phi(s)\int_{t}^{s}{\Phi(r)^{-1}\left[D(r)u(r)\mathrm{d}W(r)+\int_{Z}{F(r,z)u(r)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}z)}\right]}.

By the Itô isometry and Fubini’s theorem, one estimates

\mathbb{E}\langle GX(T),X(T)\rangle\geqslant\frac{\lambda_{G}}{\|\Phi(T)^{-1}\|_{\infty}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{\frac{1}{\|\Phi(r)\|_{\infty}^{2}}\left(|D(r)u(r)|^{2}+\int_{Z}{|F(r,z)u(r)|^{2}\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}\right)\mathrm{d}r},

and similarly for the running cost. Combining with condition (
8.6
) yields the result.

Remark 8.3
.

Condition (
8.6
) allows
R
to be negative definite provided the combination
D^{\top}D+\int_{Z}{F^{\top}F\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}
is sufficiently positive definite, or allows both
G
and some
Q(s)
to be negative. Thus, neither (
4.18
) nor (
4.19
) need hold.

Theorem 8.4
.

Let (
H1
)–(
H2
) hold. Let
t\in[0,T)
and
Q_{0}\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{\infty}(t,T;\mathbb{S}^{n})
with
Q_{0}(s)>0
a.e.
s\in[t,T]
, a.s. Let
(\Pi,\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2})\in S_{\mathbb{F}}^{\infty}(t,T;\mathbb{S}^{n})\times L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(t,T;\mathbb{S}^{n})\times G_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(t,T,\lambda;\mathbb{S}^{n})
be the adapted solution to the Lyapunov BSDE over
[t,T]
:

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}\Pi(s)=-\left[\Pi A+A^{\top}\Pi+C^{\top}\Pi C+\Pi_{1}C+C^{\top}\Pi_{1}+Q-Q_{0}\right.\\
\hskip 40.00006pt\left.+\displaystyle\int_{Z}{[\Pi_{2}E+E^{\top}\Pi_{2}+E^{\top}(\Pi_{2}+\Pi)E]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}\right]\mathrm{d}s+\Pi_{1}\mathrm{d}W(s)+\displaystyle\int_{Z}{\Pi_{2}\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)},\\
\Pi(T)=G.\end{cases}

(8.7)

If for some
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle R+D^{\top}\Pi D+\int_{Z}{F^{\top}(\Pi_{2}+\Pi)F\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}-(B^{\top}\Pi+D^{\top}\Pi C+D^{\top}\Pi_{1}+\int_{Z}{[F^{\top}\Pi_{2}+F^{\top}(\Pi_{2}+\Pi)E]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}+S)^{\top}

(8.8)

\displaystyle\cdot Q_{0}^{-1}\cdot(B^{\top}\Pi+D^{\top}\Pi C+D^{\top}\Pi_{1}+\int_{Z}{[F^{\top}\Pi_{2}+F^{\top}(\Pi_{2}+\Pi)E]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}+S)\geqslant\delta I_{m},\quad\text{a.e. on }[t,T],~\text{a.s.},

then
u\mapsto J(t,0;u)
is uniformly convex.

Proof.

The proof follows
[
19
, Theorem 7.3]
. For any bounded
u\in\mathcal{U}[t,T]
, let
X_{0}
be the state with
\xi=0
. Apply Itô’s formula (with jumps) to
\langle\Pi(s)X_{0}(s),X_{0}(s)\rangle
on
[t,T]
:

\displaystyle\langle GX_{0}(T),X_{0}(T)\rangle-\langle\Pi(t)\cdot 0,0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}\Big\{\langle\Pi(AX_{0}+Bu),X_{0}\rangle+\langle X_{0},\Pi(AX_{0}+Bu)\rangle+\langle(CX_{0}+Du),\Pi(CX_{0}+Du)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\langle\Pi_{1}(CX_{0}+Du),X_{0}\rangle+\langle X_{0},\Pi_{1}(CX_{0}+Du)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\langle[\Pi A+A^{\top}\Pi+C^{\top}\Pi C+\Pi_{1}C+C^{\top}\Pi_{1}+Q-Q_{0}+\int_{Z}(\Pi_{2}E+E^{\top}\Pi_{2}+E^{\top}(\Pi_{2}+\Pi)E)\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)]X_{0},X_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{Z}\langle[(EX_{0}+Fu)^{\top}(\Pi_{2}+\Pi)(EX_{0}+Fu)-X_{0}^{\top}\Pi X_{0}+X_{0}^{\top}\Pi_{2}X_{0}]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)\rangle\Big\}\mathrm{d}s+\text{(martingale terms)}.

After canceling the Lyapunov BSDE terms, taking expectations, and adding the running cost
\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}[\langle QX_{0},X_{0}\rangle+2\langle SX_{0},u\rangle+\langle Ru,u\rangle]\mathrm{d}s
, one obtains

\displaystyle J(t,0;u)=\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}\Big\{

\displaystyle\left|Q_{0}^{1/2}X_{0}+Q_{0}^{-1/2}\big(B^{\top}\Pi+D^{\top}\Pi C+D^{\top}\Pi_{1}+\textstyle\int_{Z}[F^{\top}\Pi_{2}+F^{\top}(\Pi_{2}+\Pi)E]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)+S\big)^{\top}u\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\langle\mathcal{R}(s)u,u\rangle\Big\}\mathrm{d}s,

where
\mathcal{R}(s)
is the left-hand side of (
8.8
). Since the perfect square term is nonnegative, condition (
8.8
) gives
J(t,0;u)\geqslant\delta\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}|u|^{2}\mathrm{d}s
. The result then follows for bounded controls and extends to all
u\in\mathcal{U}[t,T]
by density.

Remark 8.5
.

Comparing with the sufficient conditions for the pure diffusion case in

[
19
, Theorem 7.3]
, the jump-diffusion criterion
(
8.8
) contains three additional Poisson terms:

\int_{Z}\Big[\Pi_{2}E+E^{\top}\Pi_{2}+E^{\top}(\Pi_{2}+\Pi)E\Big]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z),\quad\int_{Z}\Big[F^{\top}\Pi_{2}+F^{\top}(\Pi_{2}+\Pi)E\Big]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z),\quad\int_{Z}F^{\top}(\Pi_{2}+\Pi)F\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}z).

When

, these terms vanish and (
8.8
)
reduces exactly to condition (87) in
[

]
.
Moreover, because the Lyapunov BSDE (

\widetilde{N}
, the solution

is càdlàg rather than continuous;
accordingly, the solution space is

instead of

L_{\mathbb{F}}^{\infty}(\Omega;C([t,T];\mathbb{S}^{n}))
.

9
Illustrative Examples

We present three examples demonstrating that the SRE-J can be solvable with indefinite data.

Example 9.1
(Scalar example:
G<0
and
P
negative).

Let
n=m=1
,
T>0
, and
\lambda(Z)=1
. Take the coefficients

\displaystyle A(s)=B(s)=C(s)=0,\quad D(s)=1,\quad E(s,z)=0,\quad F(s,z)=1,\quad G=-\tfrac{3}{4},\quad Q(s)=S(s)=0,\quad R(s)=s^{2}+2.

Note that
G<0
, so the terminal weighting is
negative
(in particular, not positive semi-definite). The state equation is
\mathrm{d}X(s)=u(s)\mathrm{d}W(s)+\int_{Z}{u(s)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)}
,
X(t)=\xi
.

We claim that the SRE-J admits the (constant, deterministic) adapted solution

P(t)\equiv-\tfrac{3}{4},\qquad\Lambda(t)\equiv 0,\qquad\zeta(t,z)\equiv 0.

Verification.
Since
A=B=C=E=S=0
,
D=F=1
, and
\Lambda=\zeta=0
:

\displaystyle\hat{S}(s)

\displaystyle=B^{\top}P_{-}+D^{\top}\Lambda+D^{\top}P_{-}C+\int_{Z}{[F^{\top}\zeta+F^{\top}(\zeta+P_{-})E]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}+S=0,

\displaystyle\hat{R}(s)

\displaystyle=D^{\top}P_{-}D+\int_{Z}{F^{\top}(\zeta+P_{-})F\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}+R(s)=P_{-}+P_{-}+R(s)=2\cdot\bigl(-\tfrac{3}{4}\bigr)+(s^{2}+2)=s^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}.

In particular,
\hat{R}(s)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
for all
s\in[0,T]
. The SRE drift becomes

\displaystyle A^{\top}P_{-}+P_{-}A+Q+C^{\top}P_{-}C+C^{\top}\Lambda+\Lambda C+\int_{Z}{[\zeta E+E^{\top}\zeta+E^{\top}(\zeta+P_{-})E]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}-\hat{S}^{\top}\hat{R}^{-1}\hat{S}=0

which is consistent with
P\equiv-\frac{3}{4}
having zero drift and zero martingale parts (i.e.,
\Gamma\equiv 0
,
\Lambda\equiv 0
,
\zeta\equiv 0
). The terminal condition
P(T)=G=-\frac{3}{4}
is satisfied. Thus
(P,\Lambda,\zeta)
solves the SRE-J.

Uniform convexity.
Since
\hat{S}=0
, for any
u\in\mathcal{U}[0,T]
with
\xi=0
, the completion-of-squares identity gives

J(t,0;u)=\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{\hat{R}(s)|u(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s}\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{|u(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s}.

Hence the cost functional is uniformly convex. The unique optimal control is
u^{*}(s)=-\hat{R}^{-1}(s)\hat{S}(s)X^{*}(s-)=0
, and the optimal feedback law is
\Theta\equiv 0
.

Example 9.2
(Multi-dimensional example:
P
indefinite and
R<0
).

Let
n=2
,
m=1
,
T>0
, and
\lambda(Z)=1
. Take

\displaystyle A=0,\quad B=0,\quad C=0,\quad D=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad E=0,\quad F=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad G=\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad Q=0,\quad S=0,\quad R=-9.

Note that
G
is indefinite (with eigenvalues
5
and
-1
) and
R=-9<0
is negative. The state equation is

\mathrm{d}X(s)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}u(s)\mathrm{d}W(s)+\int_{Z}{\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}u(s)\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)},\qquad X(t)=\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

so that
X_{1}(s)=\xi_{1}+\int_{t}^{s}u\,\mathrm{d}W+\int_{t}^{s}\int_{Z}u\,\tilde{N}
and
X_{2}(s)\equiv\xi_{2}
.

The SRE-J admits the constant adapted solution

P=\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\Lambda\equiv 0,\zeta\equiv 0.

Verification.
We compute
\hat{S}=0
and

\hat{R}=D^{\top}PD+\int_{Z}{F^{\top}PF\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}+R=5+5+(-9)=1>0.

The SRE drift is
0+0+0+0-0=0
, matching the constant
P
with
P(T)=G
.

Uniform convexity.
Direct computation gives

J(t,0;u)=\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{\hat{R}|u(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s}=\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{|u(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s}\geqslant\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{|u(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s},

so uniform convexity holds with
\varepsilon=1
, despite
R=-9<0
and
P
having a negative eigenvalue. This demonstrates that the SRE-J can be solvable with indefinite weighting matrices, as long as the uniform convexity condition is satisfied.

Example 9.3
(Malliavin-derivative example:
P
random,
\Lambda
nontrivially random,
\zeta\neq 0
).

This example is the jump-diffusion analogue of Example 8.1 in
[
19
]
. It demonstrates that the SRE-J can admit a random adapted solution
(P,\Lambda,\zeta)
in which
P
is
not
positive definite,
\Lambda
is nontrivially random, and the jump martingale component
\zeta
is
nonzero
.

Let
n=2
,
m=1
,
T>0
,
\lambda(Z)=1
. Let
\eta\in L_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
be a Malliavin differentiable random variable with square-integrable Malliavin derivative
D_{t}\eta
(with respect to
W
) and difference operator
D_{t,z}\eta
(with respect to
\tilde{N}
). By the Clark–Ocone–Haussmann formula in the Brownian–Poisson filtration,

\eta=\mathbb{E}\eta+\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}[D_{s}\eta|\mathcal{F}_{s-}]\,\mathrm{d}W(s)}+\int_{0}^{T}{\int_{Z}{\mathbb{E}[D_{s,z}\eta|\mathcal{F}_{s-}]\,\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)}}.

Let
\lambda_{W}(t)
denote a predictable modification of
\mathbb{E}[D_{t}\eta|\mathcal{F}_{t-}]
and let
\lambda_{N}(t,z)
denote a predictable modification of
\mathbb{E}[D_{t,z}\eta|\mathcal{F}_{t-}]
. Define

\mu(t)=\mathbb{E}\eta+\int_{0}^{t}{\lambda_{W}(s)\,\mathrm{d}W(s)}+\int_{0}^{t}{\int_{Z}{\lambda_{N}(s,z)\,\tilde{N}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}z)}},\qquad t\in[0,T].

(9.1)

Take the coefficients of the state equation to be

A(s)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad B(s)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad C(s)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad D(s)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
2\end{pmatrix},\quad E(s,z)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad F(s,z)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
2\end{pmatrix},

and the weighting coefficients

G=\begin{pmatrix}-(1+T^{2})&T\\
T&1+T^{2}\end{pmatrix}+\eta\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\
-2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad S(s)=(0,0),

Q(s)=\begin{pmatrix}2s&s^{2}\\
s^{2}&-4s\end{pmatrix}+4\mu(s)\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad R(s)=-(1+s^{2}).

(9.2)

Note that
F=D
, which is the key algebraic relation enabling the verification below.

Claim.
The SRE-J admits the adapted solution

P(t)=\begin{pmatrix}-(1+t^{2})&t\\
t&1+t^{2}\end{pmatrix}+\mu(t)\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\
-2&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\Lambda(t)=\lambda_{W}(t)\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\
-2&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\zeta(t,z)=\lambda_{N}(t,z)\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\
-2&1\end{pmatrix}.

(9.3)

Note that
P(t)
is random and generically not positive definite (for suitable
\mu(t)
), and
\Lambda
is a nontrivially random process (determined by the Brownian Malliavin derivative of
\eta
) that may fail to be essentially bounded for appropriate choices of
\eta
.

Verification.
Set
G_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\
-2&1\end{pmatrix}
. The crucial algebraic property is:

D^{\top}G_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\
-2&1\end{pmatrix}=(0,0),\qquad D^{\top}G_{1}D=0,\qquad F^{\top}G_{1}F=D^{\top}G_{1}D=0,

(9.4)

where the last equality uses
F=D
. Consequently,
D^{\top}\Lambda=\lambda_{W}D^{\top}G_{1}=0
,
D^{\top}\zeta=\lambda_{N}D^{\top}G_{1}=0
, and
F^{\top}\zeta=\lambda_{N}F^{\top}G_{1}=\lambda_{N}D^{\top}G_{1}=0
. Since
B=C=E=S=0
, the aggregated cross term vanishes:

\hat{S}=B^{\top}P_{-}+D^{\top}\Lambda+D^{\top}P_{-}C+\int_{Z}{[F^{\top}\zeta+F^{\top}(\zeta+P_{-})E]\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}+S=0.

For
\hat{R}
: since
D^{\top}G_{1}D=F^{\top}G_{1}F=0
, the random parts of
P
cancel in
D^{\top}P_{-}D
and
F^{\top}P_{-}F
. Setting
P_{0}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}-(1+t^{2})&t\\
t&1+t^{2}\end{pmatrix}
, we compute

D^{\top}P_{0}D=(1,2)\begin{pmatrix}-(1+t^{2})&t\\
t&1+t^{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
2\end{pmatrix}=3(1+t^{2})+4t,

and since
F=D
and
\lambda(Z)=1
:

\hat{R}(s)=D^{\top}P_{-}D+\int_{Z}{F^{\top}(\zeta+P_{-})F\,\lambda(\mathrm{d}z)}+R=2\cdot D^{\top}P_{0}D+R=2[3(1+s^{2})+4s]-(1+s^{2})=5+5s^{2}+8s.

In particular,
\hat{R}(s)=5(1+s^{2})+8s\geqslant 5
for all
s\geqslant 0
.

Since
\hat{S}=0
, the SRE-J drift reduces to
PA+A^{\top}P+Q
. We verify entry by entry that the
\mu
-dependent terms cancel. Indeed,
G_{1}A+A^{\top}G_{1}=(0,4;4,-4)
, and
Q_{1}:=4\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}=(0,-4;-4,4)=-(G_{1}A+A^{\top}G_{1})
, so the coefficient of
\mu
in
PA+A^{\top}P+Q
vanishes. The deterministic part satisfies
P_{0}^{\prime}(t)=(-2t,1;1,2t)=-(P_{0}A+A^{\top}P_{0}+Q_{0})
, confirming the SRE drift. The martingale parts match by construction:
\Lambda=\lambda_{W}G_{1}
and
\zeta=\lambda_{N}G_{1}
correspond to
\mathrm{d}\mu
.

Uniform convexity.
Since
\hat{S}=0
, the completion-of-squares identity gives

J(t,0;u)=\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{\hat{R}(s)|u(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s}\geqslant 5\,\mathbb{E}\int_{t}^{T}{|u(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s},

so the cost functional is uniformly convex with
\varepsilon=5
.

A concrete instance with
\Lambda
nontrivially random and
\zeta\neq 0
.
Take

\eta=\tfrac{1}{16}\sin(W(T)^{2})+\tfrac{1}{8}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{N(T)\geqslant 1\}},

where
N(T)=N([0,T]\times Z)
is the Poisson count. Then
\mathrm{ess\,sup}\,|\eta|\leqslant\frac{1}{16}+\frac{1}{8}=\frac{3}{16}<\frac{1}{4}
. Its Malliavin derivative with respect to
W
is
D_{t}\eta=\frac{1}{8}W(T)\cos(W(T)^{2})
, which is unbounded. Its
difference operator
with respect to
\tilde{N}
is

D_{t,z}\eta=\tfrac{1}{8}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{N(T)=0\}},\qquad t\in[0,T],

since adding a point at
(t,z)
turns
\{N(T)\geqslant 1\}
from
0
to
1
exactly when
N(T)=0
. The corresponding
predictable projection
is

\lambda_{N}(t,z)=\mathbb{E}[D_{t,z}\eta|\mathcal{F}_{t-}]=\tfrac{1}{8}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\{N(T)=0\}}|\mathcal{F}_{t-}]=\tfrac{1}{8}\,e^{-(T-t)}\mathbf{1}_{\{N(t-)=0\}},

which is bounded and
nonzero
before the first Poisson jump. (Here we used
\{N(T)=0\}=\{N(t-)=0\}\cap\{N(T)-N(t-)=0\}
and the independence of
N(T)-N(t-)
from
\mathcal{F}_{t-}
.) As for the Brownian component,
\lambda_{W}(t)=\mathbb{E}[D_{t}\eta|\mathcal{F}_{t-}]
is a nontrivial random process. In general, one may choose bounded
\eta
(e.g., via a suitable Malliavin-differentiable truncation) so that
\Lambda=\lambda_{W}\cdot G_{1}
is not essentially bounded; the point of this example is that the SRE-J framework accommodates such random, potentially unbounded martingale components, and the jump component
\zeta=\lambda_{N}\cdot G_{1}\neq 0
is a genuinely new phenomenon that does not arise in the pure diffusion setting.

10
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we studied an indefinite stochastic linear-quadratic optimal control problem with random coefficients and Poisson jumps (with finite activity) under a uniform convexity condition on the cost functional. We developed a proof strategy based on three main steps: (1) a T-system aggregation approach (following the architecture of
[
27
, Theorem 3.5]
) to construct the value process
P
as a bounded càdlàg special semimartingale; (2) a small-interval localization argument to establish
\hat{R}\geqslant\delta_{0}I_{m}
directly from the uniform convexity, without any positive-definiteness conditions on the weighting matrices; (3) a residual-measure technique on inter-jump intervals, using the piecewise inverse-flow (available in the diffusion setting between jumps) and the optimal pair, to identify the drift of
P
as the SRE-J and derive the closed-loop feedback representation.

This approach bypasses the
global
invertibility of the optimal state fundamental matrix, which holds in pure diffusion models but fails in the jump setting (as demonstrated by
Example
6.1
). The key conceptual advance over
[
27
]
is the reversal in the source of positive definiteness for
\hat{R}
: rather than inheriting it from positive condition (
Q\geqslant 0
,
G\geqslant 0
,
R\geqslant\delta I
), we deduce it from the functional-level uniform convexity of the cost. The sufficient conditions in
Section
8
and the examples in
Section
9
—where
G
can be negative or indefinite,
R
can be negative, and yet the cost functional remains uniformly convex—demonstrate that this approach is strictly more general than the standard condition framework.

The proof architecture relies on two structural properties: the RCE (right-continuity in expectation) of the zero-control cost system, established via the continuity estimate (
Proposition
6.6
); and the invertibility of the local diffusion fundamental matrix on inter-jump intervals, which holds because no Poisson atoms are present between jumps. The passage from the aggregated submartingale to a special semimartingale uses Doob–Meyer for class (D), and the identification of the SRE drift uses the piecewise inverse-flow approach of
[
27
]
once
\hat{R}\geqslant\delta_{0}I_{m}
is known from
Lemma
7.5
.
