Collagen gene expression profiles predict recurrence and progression of DCIS to IDC
Authors: Naeimeh Heiranizadeh, Mina Mohammad-rezaei, Mina Noroozbeygi, Saeed Azad Armaki, Parisa Aziminezhadan, Soheil Rahmani Fard, Bahareh Shateri Amiri, Nahid Nafissi, Elahe Safari, Maryam Kadivar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer is a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality globally. Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is a non-invasive precursor to invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), and understanding the molecular mechanisms driving this transition is critical. This study explores the role of collagen genes (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, COL5A2) in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and tumor progression. Pathology samples were collected from patients at Rasoul Akram, Asia, and Khatam Al-Anbia Hospitals (2013–2023), including 42 cases (recurrent DCIS and IDC) and 32 controls (primary DCIS without recurrence). RNA was extracted, reverse transcribed, and analyzed via real-time PCR to assess collagen gene expression. Statistical analyses, including ROC curve analysis, were performed to evaluate the predictive performance of collagen gene expression. Results revealed significant upregulation of COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and COL5A2 in the case group, with fold increases of 9.71, 5.38, 3.16, and 4.63, respectively (p ≤ 0.05). Overexpression was most pronounced in ER-negative, PR-negative, and high Ki67 subtypes, indicating a link to aggressive tumor behavior. ROC analysis demonstrated the potential predictive utility of collagen gene expression profiles, with AUC values ranging from 0.765 to 0.859. These findings underscore the role of collagen genes in breast cancer progression, highlighting their potential as predictive biomarkers and therapeutic targets.Keywords: DCIS, IDC, COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, COL5A2, ER, PR, HER2Subject terms: Cancer, Breast cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer remains one of the most frequent malignanceis to be diagnosed in females worldwide and comprises over 10% of all new cancer cases. It also ranks the second cause of cancer-related death among females1. It has been reported that in 2022, incidence of breast cancer new cases was 2,296,840 in the world with the mortality rate of approximately 13 per 100,000 people in all age. Moreover, it is predicted by 2050, the global breast cancer new cases will show 54.7% increase2. Despite remarkable progress in early detection and treatment, breast cancer is a heterogeneous disease with dissimilar outcomes related to prognosis, largely because of its molecular subtypes, stages at diagnosis, and molecular and transcriptomic characteristics3.

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is a non-invasive precursor of invasive breast cancer. Although DCIS is confined within the milk ducts, with no invasion to surrounding tissues, in about 25–30% of patients, it will develop into invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC)4, the most common form of invasive breast cancer associated with poorer prognosis, higher recurrence rates, and metastasis5. Several transcriptomic, molecular, and microenvironmental factors influence DCIS progression to IDC. Despite all the progress made in understanding DCIS biology, the molecular mechanisms underlying its progression to IDC are still not fully understood. Current clinicopathological markers are insufficient to distinguish slow-growing, low-risk DCIS from lesions that become invasive6. Recent studies have demonstrated that intraoperative radiation therapy and breast-conserving surgery significantly reduce the risk of DCIS recurrence, though challenges remain in predicting which cases will progress to invasive carcinoma7. Generally, positive estrogen and progesterone receptor status means a good prognosis and a favorable response to hormone therapies8. However, those that are ER-negative, PR-negative, or HER2-negative, the course is often highly aggressive, with limited sensitivity to traditional therapies9. Alterations in the tumor microenvironment, particularly, the extracellular matrix (ECM) and stromal remodeling, has become into consideration as key contributors to tumor progression10. Collagen seems to be a crucial player there11, and genes, especially those encoding type I (COL1A1, COL1A2), type III (COL3A1), and type V (COL5A2) collagens, have been implicated in breast cancer progression due to their role in ECM remodeling12, and are upregulated in advanced stages of breast cancer and are associated to aggressive tumor behavior13.

Building upon our previous in silico analysis, which identified specific collagen genes—including COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and COL5A2—as potential key candidates involved in the progression of ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) to invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), the present study was designed to validate these transcriptomic findings14. By analyzing the expression of these candidate genes in clinical specimens, we aim to evaluate their potential as predictive biomarkers for disease progression and further elucidate their role in the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Patients and tissue sampling

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Rasoul Akram Hospital, Asia Hospital, and Khatam Al‑Anbia Hospital, Iran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (Ethics Code: IR.IUMS.REC.1403.814). All procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

This retrospective case–control study investigated molecular transcriptional mechanisms involved in the progression from ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) to invasive breast cancer. Pathology blocks from patients diagnosed with DCIS between 2013 and 2023 at Rasoul Akram Hospital, Asia Hospital, and Khatam Al‑Anbia Hospital were collected. The Case group included patients whose DCIS progressed to invasive breast cancer, whereas the Control group included patients with primary DCIS without recurrence. Cases and controls were matched for age and first occurrence of DCIS. Control patients underwent standardized follow‑up, including clinical examination and ultrasound every three months during the first two years and every six months thereafter, with annual mammography, and had at least 10 years of follow‑up confirming the absence of recurrence.

Inclusion criteria required available and complete pathology reports and clinical data. Cases were defined as patients initially diagnosed with DCIS who later developed invasive breast cancer and had undergone surgical treatment at the participating hospitals.

For the Case group, two formalin‑fixed paraffin‑embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks were analyzed for each patient: the primary DCIS block and the corresponding recurrent or invasive carcinoma block. Each block was evaluated histopathologically and used for RNA extraction, resulting in 84 samples (42 patients × 2 blocks). In the Control group, only the primary DCIS block was analyzed for each patient, yielding 32 samples (32 patients × 1 block). Analyses were performed on a per‑sample basis, including comparisons between primary DCIS and matched recurrent/invasive tissues in the Case group, and between Case‑DCIS and Control‑DCIS samples.

RNA extraction from paraffin blocks

To ensure high tumor purity and normalize the tumor content across samples, all formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks were first reviewed by an experienced breast pathologist. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to identify regions with the highest density of DCIS foci or IDC cells. Areas containing at least 70% tumor cellularity were demarcated and manually macrodissected from subsequent unstained sections for RNA extraction in order to minimize stromal contamination.

RNA was extracted from paraffin-embedded tissue samples using the GenExir FFPE RNA Extraction Kit (Azma Exir Pajooh, Iran). Briefly, tissue samples (50–200 mg) were deparaffinized with G Solution and washed with ZR Solution. After digestion with TR Buffer and α Activator Reagent at 65 °C and 85 °C, the lysate was processed through a series of washing steps using VR and SE Buffers. RNA was then purified using a spin column, followed by washing with Wash Buffer. After drying, RNA was eluted with Elution Buffer and stored for further analysis. RNA concentration and purity was assessed using a NanoDrop™ 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered suitable for reverse transcription and real-time PCR.

Ki67 assessment and stratification

Ki67 proliferative index was evaluated on immunohistochemically stained slides by an experienced breast pathologist. The percentage of positively stained tumor cell nuclei was recorded. For analytical stratification, a cutoff value of 20% was applied, which is commonly used to distinguish low‑proliferative from high‑proliferative tumors. Accordingly, tumors with Ki67 expression < 20% were classified as Ki67‑low, and those with Ki67 expression ≥ 20% were classified as Ki67‑high. This cutoff was consistently used in all comparative analyses involving Ki67 status.

Necrosis assessment

Tumor necrosis was evaluated histopathologically on hematoxylin and eosin–stained sections using a semi‑quantitative scoring system (0–3), where 0 = no necrosis and 3 = extensive necrosis. Scores were recorded by an experienced breast pathologist.

Reverse transcription (cDNA synthesis)

The synthesis of cDNA was conducted by using the cDNA synthesis kit (Azma Exir Pajooh, Iran). cDNA synthesis was performed using a two-step protocol. First, a Master Mix A was prepared by adding 1 µl of Oligo dT primer, 1 µl of Random Hexamer primer, and 1 µl of dNTP Mixture per sample. After mixing, 3 µl of Master Mix A was aliquoted into 0.5 ml reaction tubes, and samples were kept on ice. Then, a Master Mix B was prepared by adding 0.5 µl of RT enzyme, 2.5 µl of 5X RT Buffer, and 4 µl of Nuclease Free Water per sample, and kept on ice. Next, 10 µl of total RNA (containing 500 ng RNA) was added to each reaction tube containing Master Mix A. The samples were incubated at 65 °C for 5 min, followed by 4 °C for 1 min. Then, 7 µl of Master Mix B was added to each tube, and the samples were incubated at 47 °C for 60 min and at 80 °C for 8 min. After incubation, the cDNA was briefly spun down and stored at -20 °C for further use. The final volume of synthesized cDNA was 20 µl, which was stored at − 20 °C until use.

Real-time PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Azma Exir Pajooh, Iran) on a GeneRunner Real-Time PCR System (BioER, China). Real-time PCR was performed by quantifying the following gene expressions of interest: COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and COL5A2. Target gene primers were designed based on sequences available in the NCBI database and reviewed in silico for specificity and efficiency. The design process involved ensuring that the primers were specific to the target genes and avoided potential secondary structures that could interfere with PCR amplification. Amplification was done by the SYBR Green Master Mix. Reactions were prepared in a 20-µL volume comprising 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 pM), 1 µL cDNA (equivalent to approximately 25 ng RNA), and 7 µL RNase-free water. Real-time PCR was performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by amplification for 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Fluorescence was measured after extension in each cycle. All reactions were performed in technical duplicates. GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalization of gene expression data. GAPDH is a commonly used housekeeping gene that is assumed to have stable expression across different samples. Additionally, a no-template control (NTC) was included in all PCR experiments to ensure the specificity of amplification and to detect any contamination. Then, the relative expressions of target genes were normalized against that of housekeeping gene GAPDH, and data analysis was done using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distribution was assessed prior to statistical testing. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the unpaired Student’s t‑test for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non‑parametric data.

Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the chi‑square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies were small.

To examine the effects of clinical grouping and receptor status on collagen gene expression, two‑way analysis of variance (two‑way ANOVA) was performed. The model evaluated the main effects of group (Case vs. Control) and receptor status (ER, PR, or Ki67), as well as the interaction between these factors. When appropriate, post‑hoc multiple comparison tests were conducted. A p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Gene expression of collagen genes in the case vs. control groups

Expression analysis demonstrated significant upregulation of all four collagen genes (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and COL5A2) in the Case group compared with the Control group (p ≤ 0.05 for all). The magnitude of upregulation varied among genes, with COL1A1 showing the highest fold increase (9.71‑fold), followed by COL1A2 (5.38‑fold), COL5A2 (4.63‑fold), and COL3A1 (3.16‑fold) (Fig. 1 A–D). These results indicate a consistent elevation of collagen‑related gene expression in recurrent or invasive lesions compared with non‑recurrent DCIS.

Comparison of hormonal receptor status between control and case groups

Hormonal receptor status, including the expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2, was further evaluated due to their established prognostic value in breast cancer. A statistically significant difference was observed in the distribution of ER-positive cases, with the Control group exhibiting a significantly higher proportion of ER-positive samples compared to the Case group (p = 0.003; Fig. 2). This finding aligns with the known profile of primary DCIS, which typically demonstrates greater hormone receptor positivity. Similarly, PR positivity was also significantly more frequent in the Control group (p = 0.0019; Fig. 2), further supporting the association between hormone receptor expression and less aggressive tumor behavior. In contrast, no significant difference was found in HER2 expression between the two groups (p = 0.3365; Fig. 2), suggesting that HER2 overexpression may not be strongly linked to recurrence or invasiveness in this cohort.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of collagen gene expression levels to distinguish Case samples from Controls. All four genes demonstrated good discriminatory performance. The area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.859 for COL1A1 (95% CI: 0.774–0.944), 0.788 for COL1A2 (95% CI: 0.686–0.891), 0.777 for COL5A2 (95% CI: 0.665–0.889), and 0.765 for COL3A1 (95% CI: 0.645–0.885), with p < 0.0001 for all comparisons (Fig. 5 A–D).

These findings suggest that collagen gene expression profiles may serve as potential predictive biomarkers for identifying DCIS patients at increased risk of progression.

Overall, these analyses indicate that elevated expression of extracellular matrix–related collagen genes is consistently associated with the Case phenotype and is compatible with a molecular signature accompanying progression from DCIS to invasive/recurrent disease. Importantly, the clinical grouping was used as a comparative framework and should not be interpreted as causally driving gene expression changes. ER, PR, and Ki67 status showed limited association with collagen gene expression patterns in this cohort.

Discussion

Breast cancer is a biologically heterogeneous disease, and progression from ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) to invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) represents a critical step associated with increased recurrence and mortality4,6. However, current clinicopathological parameters remain insufficient to accurately predict which DCIS lesions will progress to invasive disease15. In this study, we evaluated the expression of four collagen genes—COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and COL5A2—in order to explore their potential association with disease progression.

The principal finding of this study was the consistent upregulation of all four collagen genes in recurrent DCIS and IDC compared with non‑recurrent DCIS. This pattern suggests that collagen gene dysregulation accompanies the progression from DCIS to IDC. Collagens are key structural components of the extracellular matrix (ECM), and their altered expression reflects the dynamic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment that occurs during cancer progression10,16,17. ECM remodeling increases tissue stiffness, alters cell–matrix interactions, and promotes integrin‑mediated signaling pathways that facilitate tumor invasion and survival12,17.

Among the investigated genes, COL1A1 and COL1A2 encode type I collagen, the predominant fibrillar collagen in breast tissue. Increased type I collagen deposition has been associated with enhanced tumor cell migration and ECM stiffening, both of which promote invasive behavior12,18. COL3A1 contributes to stromal remodeling and angiogenesis, processes that are also linked to tumor invasion11. Although less extensively studied, COL5A2 has been reported to correlate with aggressive tumor phenotypes and unfavorable outcomes in breast cancer19. In our analysis, COL5A2 expression also showed a modest association with ER status, suggesting a possible interaction between ECM remodeling and hormonal signaling pathways.

These observations support the concept that the tumor microenvironment is an active regulator of cancer progression rather than a passive structural scaffold16,17. Increased collagen deposition and alignment can modify the mechanical properties of breast tissue, activate signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK, and create a microenvironment favorable for tumor cell migration and invasion12,20. Therefore, the elevated collagen gene expression observed in recurrent DCIS and IDC likely reflects molecular changes accompanying the transition toward invasive disease.

Clinically, these molecular findings were consistent with several clinicopathological characteristics observed in the Case group. Tumors in this group were significantly larger and more frequently ER‑negative and PR‑negative, features commonly associated with aggressive disease and poorer prognosis21,22. Higher collagen gene expression was also associated with these adverse characteristics, supporting previous reports that increased stromal collagen density correlates with tumor size and unfavorable outcomes in breast cancer23. Together, these findings suggest that collagen gene dysregulation is closely linked with tumor aggressiveness and may contribute to the stromal conditions that facilitate tumor growth and invasion.

The proliferation marker Ki67 was also significantly higher in the Case group, indicating increased tumor aggressiveness. Ki67 is widely used as a prognostic marker reflecting cellular proliferation23–25. However, two‑way ANOVA analysis in our study indicated that collagen gene expression differences were primarily associated with disease grouping rather than Ki67 status itself. This suggests that stromal remodeling represented by collagen gene upregulation may capture a biological dimension of tumor progression that is distinct from cellular proliferation. While Ki67 reflects intrinsic tumor growth potential, collagen remodeling reflects alterations in the tumor microenvironment that may facilitate invasion and dissemination17,18.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis further demonstrated that all four collagen genes showed good performance in distinguishing recurrent or invasive cases from controls, with COL1A1 showing the highest discriminative power (AUC = 0.859). These findings indicate that collagen gene expression profiles may have potential utility as molecular biomarkers for identifying DCIS patients at higher risk of progression. Integration of collagen gene expression with established clinical markers such as ER, PR, and HER2 could improve risk stratification and contribute to earlier detection of high‑risk lesions26.

Beyond their predictive value, collagen‑related pathways may also represent potential therapeutic targets. Experimental studies have shown that inhibition of collagen crosslinking enzymes such as lysyl oxidase (LOX) can reduce ECM stiffness and limit tumor progression27. Antifibrotic agents that suppress collagen synthesis, including halofuginone and pirfenidone, have also demonstrated potential anti‑tumor effects in preclinical models28. In addition, targeting collagen–integrin signaling interactions may impair tumor cell migration and improve treatment responses29. These strategies highlight the possibility that modulation of the tumor stroma could complement conventional therapies, particularly in aggressive breast cancer subtypes characterized by pronounced ECM remodeling30.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, collagen expression was evaluated only at the RNA level, and protein‑level validation using immunohistochemistry or collagen‑specific staining methods such as picrosirius red was not performed. Such approaches would provide direct visualization of collagen localization and organization within the tumor microenvironment. Second, RNA integrity was not assessed using advanced platforms such as Bioanalyzer or TapeStation, and RNA quality evaluation relied on Nanodrop measurements of concentration and purity. Third, due to the retrospective design and limited tissue availability, the stromal‑to‑epithelial ratio could not be quantified, which may influence collagen gene expression measurements. Finally, although patients generally underwent standardized treatment approaches, variations in surgical procedures could potentially influence recurrence outcomes.

Despite these limitations, our study provides evidence that increased expression of collagen genes is associated with recurrent DCIS and invasive breast cancer and reflects ECM remodeling accompanying disease progression. These findings support the potential value of collagen gene expression as a biomarker for identifying DCIS patients at increased risk of progression and highlight the importance of the tumor microenvironment in breast cancer biology. Further studies incorporating larger cohorts and combined transcriptomic and protein‑level analyses will be necessary to validate these observations and clarify the clinical utility of collagen‑based biomarkers.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights the critical role of collagen genes in the progression from DCIS to IDC, underscoring their potential significance both as predictive biomarkers and as therapeutic targets. We observed consistent upregulation of COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and COL5A2 in recurrent and invasive breast cancer compared to non-recurrent DCIS, suggesting that dysregulation of these genes is a hallmark of more aggressive disease states. Importantly, ROC analysis confirmed their strong predictive power, particularly for COL1A1, which demonstrated the highest discriminative ability.

These findings contribute to a growing body of evidence that the extracellular matrix (ECM) and its remodeling through collagen gene activity are central to breast cancer biology. Unlike classical markers such as ER, PR, and HER2, collagen gene expression captures the stromal contribution to tumor aggressiveness, providing a complementary dimension to risk assessment. Incorporating collagen gene profiling into clinical practice could therefore enhance risk stratification, prognostication, and personalized treatment planning.

Despite the robustness of our results, several limitations should be acknowledged, including the lack of protein-level validation and structural characterization of collagen fibers within tumor tissue, as well as the retrospective nature of the study. Addressing these limitations in future research through integrated transcriptomic, proteomic, and histopathological approaches will be essential for translating our findings into clinical utility.

Overall, this study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying breast cancer progression and strongly supports the future exploration of collagen-targeted strategies within the framework of precision oncology. Such approaches may ultimately improve outcomes for patients with high-risk DCIS and invasive breast cancer, particularly those with hormone receptor–negative or treatment-resistant disease.
