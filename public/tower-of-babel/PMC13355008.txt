Introduction

A 34-year-old male with no past medical history presented to a local hospital in Greenville, South Carolina with a diffuse rash and burning and pain in his lower extremities. The rash had started as a lesion on his arm and then spread over weeks to his whole body (Fig. 1). He was originally from Guam and emigrated to California in 2013 where he worked as a chef. Question 1a: what is your diagnosis?

Lepromatous leprosy

Hansen’s disease, also known as leprosy, is a curable, chronic, granulomatous infection that generally presents reddish or hypopigmented skin lesions (macules, papules, nodules, or patches) [1], [2]. Classically the skin lesions have decreased sensation. Non-dermatological symptoms can include uveitis, neuritis, nasal septum perforation, and paresthesias of the hands or feet. A person with hypoesthesia may present with burns, ulcers or traumatic lesions with reports of not having felt the injury. A hand in a claw position or a foot that drags on the ground while walking can be strongly suggestive of leprosy.

Hansen’s disease is not an easily transmitted infection; many people exposed never develop clinical disease indicating either immune clearance or latency. The primary route of transmission is likely through respiratory droplets or nasal secretions from close, long-term exposure to an infected person. Nine-banded armadillos are a zoonotic reservoir of leprosy in the southeastern United States and other countries of the world [3].

Early diagnosis and treatment of Hansen’s disease is necessary to prevent irreversible nerve damage and resulting disability. The WHO classification guides treatment in resource-limited settings where pathology might not be available. Our patient was diagnosed with lepromatous leprosy [2], [4].

Treatment course

He was treated at by the National Hansen’s Disease Center with daily rifampin, minocycline, and dapsone for 2 years (2019–2020). He was retreated with directly observed monthly moxifloxacin, minocycline, and rifampin for 1.5 years 2023–2024. Most of his lesions improved on treatment, leaving residual scarring and the patient was thought to be cured. Question 1b: what is your diagnosis?

Erythema nodosum leprosum

Our patient was diagnosed with erythema nodosum leprosum and started on a prolonged steroid taper as well as thalidomide 100 mg daily.

Pathologic immune reactions are common in Hansen’s disease, occurring in approximately one-fifth of patients. They can occur before, during, or after treatment. Reversal reaction (type 1) is clinically recognized by erythema, edema, and ulcerations of pre-existing cutaneous plaques or nodules. Erythema nodosum leprosum (type II reaction) is clinically recognized by new painful, erythematous subcutaneous nodules which are thought to be a result of antigen-antibody complex deposition with subsequent activation of the complement immune system. Reactions are often recurrent for many years with long-term corticosteroids or other immunomodulatory medications such as clofazimine, methotrexate, or thalidomide required to control symptoms [2].
