A Letter to the Editor: The Role of Artificial Intelligence in Dental Education—A Tool or a Crutch?
Authors: Soukaina Abidi, Youssra Azzouz, Yasmine Hanine, Rime Chraibi, Oumaima Finnaoui, Hanae Bouzakhnin, Saliha Chbicheb
Published: 2026

To the Editors—Artificial intelligence (AI) is becoming increasingly prevalent in dentistry at all levels, as highlighted in the recent article “AI Acceptability in Dentistry: Insights From Dental Professionals and Students in the Netherlands: A Pilot Study.”1 Although AI offers clear advantages such as enhanced diagnostic support, improved efficiency, and reduced human error,2,3 its rising presence in dental education raises concerns regarding the future competencies of dental students.4

A major concern is the potential deterioration of fundamental knowledge. Dentistry is inherently a practical, judgement-based discipline that requires a deep understanding of anatomy, pathology, biomechanics, and clinical reasoning.5,6 Early or unsupervised exposure to AI tools risks turning them into replacements rather than supplements. Students’ motivation to master critical skills may diminish if they become overly reliant on AI-generated diagnoses, treatment plans, or radiographic interpretations.7,8 Under such circumstances, the profession may produce dentists who function more as AI-guided technicians than as autonomous, self-governing clinicians.9

It is essential to differentiate between novices and experienced professionals. AI can serve as a valuable extension—a second opinion enhancing accuracy and workflow—for qualified practitioners with established clinical judgement and manual skills.10 However, AI may inadvertently accelerate learning for students who lack the cognitive framework to evaluate the appropriateness, safety, or validity of AI outputs.11 These students are at risk of relying uncritically on AI due to insufficient foundational knowledge.12

Therefore, the integration of AI into dental education must be carefully structured and supervised. Institutions should avoid unrestricted access in early training stages, instead introducing AI gradually after students demonstrate competency in core principles.3,5 AI should supplement learning rather than supplant it. Additionally, curricula must explicitly address AI system operations, limitations, potential biases, and proper use cases.2,4,11 Students will only be able to evaluate AI-assisted outputs effectively if they possess a solid understanding of dentistry and the underlying mechanisms of AI.6,10

In conclusion, AI has the potential to enhance dentistry, but only if future practitioners remain grounded in the principles that define clinical excellence. To maintain integrity, autonomy, and expertise, dental students must first learn, comprehend, and internalise fundamental knowledge. The key to ensuring technology supports rather than undermines the profession is a balanced, pedagogically controlled integration of AI.7,12
