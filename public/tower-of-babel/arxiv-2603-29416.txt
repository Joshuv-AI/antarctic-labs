Force Geometry and Irreversibility in Nonequilibrium Dynamics
Erez Aghion, Swetamber Das

Abstract
Recent experiments have revealed heterogeneous dissipation in optically trapped systems, often anticorrelated with local positional fluctuations, exposing a structural gap in the scalar stochastic thermodynamic description. While the conventional scalar framework successfully quantifies dissipation through currents and entropy production rates, it does not reveal the underlying vectorial force geometry that shapes spatial dissipation patterns. Here, we bridge this gap by identifying force geometry as an organizing principle for nonequilibrium thermodynamics and introducing force alignment as a geometric determinant of irreversibility. We show that entropy production depends not only on force magnitudes but also on the relative orientation between deterministic driving forces and entropic gradients, vanishing only under exact anti-alignment with matched magnitudes. We formalize this geometric alignment through a time-dependent force-correlation coefficient, quantifying the relative orientation between the forces. This yields an instantaneous geometric lower bound on entropy production that remains valid even when force magnitudes are matched. For overdamped dynamics, perfect anti-alignment defines a thermodynamic stall where net transport vanishes and the lower bound on entropy production is saturated. This force-level perspective provides a structural explanation for the experimentally observed fluctuation-dissipation anticorrelation and nonuniform dissipation. We construct geometric control charts for both constant dragging and sinusoidal driving protocols, explicitly locating experimental operating points within this force-space representation. Together, these results position force geometry as a unifying structural perspective on irreversibility, spanning active biological systems, microrheology, and naturally extending to underdamped dynamics.



Introduction

Irreversibility is a defining feature of nonequilibrium dynamics, commonly quantified by finite entropy production. Its physical origin, however, is typically framed statistically rather than in terms of the forces that drive the dynamics
Sekimoto1998Langevin
. In stochastic thermodynamics, time-reversal asymmetry appears as probability currents, while the entropy production rate can also be expressed at the level of the underlying forces. Yet in practice, analysis proceeds primarily at the level of currents, treating dissipation as an aggregate quantity rather than resolving the organization of the contributing forces. In systems maintained out of equilibrium, entropy production arises from the interplay of competing forces, highlighting the need to understand how irreversibility is organized through force correlations, in space and time.

Recent spatially resolved measurements provide a concrete setting in which the limitations of current-centric descriptions become evident. In particular, Ref.
DiTerlizzi2024
reported pronounced spatial inhomogeneity in entropy production across driven red blood cell membranes, together with counterintuitive anti-correlations between dissipation and positional fluctuations. Their observation revealed nonuniform heat dissipation along the cell contour, demonstrating that energetic cost can vary substantially across space even under steady driving. This spatial heterogeneity points to a missing structural layer in nonequilibrium thermodynamics, essential for understanding how dissipative processes are structured and regulated in living systems.

At the same time, these observations also expose a conceptual gap. While variance-based sum rules and force-free kinetic inference approaches enable experimental estimation of local entropy production
DiTerlizzi2024
;
DiTerlizzi2025
, they do not explain how entropy production is organized at the force level, or how the relative alignment of underlying forces shapes spatial patterns in dissipation. More broadly, established frameworks in stochastic thermodynamics – including current-based formulations, thermodynamic uncertainty relations and Fisher-information speed limits – provide powerful scalar bounds on entropy production, precision, and timescales
Barato2015Thermodynamic
;
Gingrich2016Dissipation
;
Seifert2019TUR
;
horowitz2020thermodynamic
;
Nicholson2020TimeInformation
;
DechantSasa2021PhysRevX11.041061
. However, these frameworks do not directly capture the geometric structure encoded in thermodynamic forces’ vectorial alignment and its role in organizing dissipation.

Here, we show that irreversibility admits a geometric interpretation at the force level, governed by the relative orientation between external driving forces and entropic (information) gradients. Entropy production vanishes only when these forces cancel exactly – equal in magnitude and perfectly anti-aligned – whereas any deviation in alignment or magnitude generates dissipation. We introduce an instantaneous force–correlation coefficient
r(t)
that quantifies this alignment and provides an experimentally accessible measure of force organization. Applying this framework to moving harmonic traps as analytically tractable examples, we construct control charts that organize entropy production, transport, and driving strength across protocols. These charts provide a geometric account of the experimentally observed spatial heterogeneity and dissipation–fluctuation anti-correlation. Together, these results establish force geometry as an organizing principle of nonequilibrium thermodynamics, complementing current-based bounds by linking the microscopic force structure to macroscopic irreversibility.

\rho(x,t)

An alternative representation, valid for overdamped isothermal dynamics and often more
physically transparent in providing a mechanistic perspective on stochastic
thermodynamics, is obtained by rewriting the entropy production rate in terms of the net thermodynamic force:

\dot{S}_{i}(t)=\frac{D}{k_{B}T^{2}}\,\bigl\langle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{net}}^{2}\bigr\rangle,\qquad\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{net}}=F_{\mathrm{ext}}+F_{\mathrm{info}}.

(2)

Here,
F_{\mathrm{ext}}=-\nabla U
is the externally imposed driving force and

F_{\mathrm{info}}=-k_{B}T\nabla\ln\rho
is the entropic restoring force arising from spatial variations of the probability density. The latter acts locally to oppose spatial probability gradients. In the absence of nonequilibrium driving, this entropic force leads to relaxation toward the equilibrium distribution by counteracting local probability inhomogeneities. At equilibrium, the entropic force
F_{\mathrm{info}}
exactly balances the mechanical force
F_{\mathrm{ext}}
, whereas under external driving the two forces compete to determine the structure of entropy production. The angular brackets denote an average of the net thermodynamic force
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{net}}^{2}(x,t)
with respect to the instantaneous probability density
\rho(x,t)
. This expression follows directly from the overdamped Fokker–Planck equation
SinghDasAghionPrep
(see Supplemental Material,
SM I
for the derivation). While mathematically consistent with standard current-based expressions for the entropy production rate
Seifert2005Entropy
, Eq. (
2
) exposes a geometric structure that is otherwise obscured in scalar observables and information-theoretic characterizations of nonequilibrium dynamics.

Equation (
2
) reveals a geometric constraint on reversibility arising from the force decomposition.
The entropy production rate vanishes only when the two forces are perfectly anti-aligned and equal in magnitude,
F_{\mathrm{info}}=-F_{\mathrm{ext}}
.
Anti-alignment is therefore a necessary condition for reversibility; matching force magnitudes alone is not sufficient.
In other words, equilibrium requires both directional and magnitude cancellation, whereas nonequilibrium arises from failure of either, independently of whether the system is in a steady state or undergoing transient dynamics.
This geometric requirement is not explicit in conventional current-based formulations of stochastic thermodynamics.
Within the present force-based representation, irreversibility corresponds to a geometric asymmetry between mechanical and entropic contributions in local thermodynamic force space.

Expanding the square explicitly yields:

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{k_{B}T^{2}}\left\langle(F_{\mathrm{info}}+F_{\mathrm{ext}})^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{k_{B}T^{2}}\left[\langle F_{\mathrm{info}}^{2}\rangle+\langle F_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}\rangle+2\langle F_{\mathrm{info}}F_{\mathrm{ext}}\rangle\right].

(3)

The entropy production rate therefore consists of three contributions:
(i) an information cost associated with maintaining nonequilibrium probability gradients,
(ii) a driving cost due to external forcing,
and (iii) a correlation term capturing constructive or destructive interference between forces.
Because this structure follows directly from the generic form of thermodynamic
forces in overdamped Langevin dynamics, it does not depend on confinement
or linear response assumptions.
The information-force variance
\langle F_{\mathrm{info}}^{2}\rangle
is directly proportional to the Fisher information of the instantaneous probability distribution, linking this contribution to statistical measures of distinguishability.
By contrast, the force correlation term, often unemphasized in scalar descriptions,
encodes the genuinely geometric organization of entropy production at the force level.

(F_{\mathrm{ext}},F_{\mathrm{info}})
force space is shown in Fig.

.

This decomposition reveals a key structural feature of entropy production.
For fixed force variances
\langle F_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}\rangle
and

\langle F_{\mathrm{info}}^{2}\rangle
, and for fixed transport and noise parameters
(
D
,
\gamma
,
T
), the entropy production rate is
not
uniquely determined.
Instead, it depends explicitly on the cross term

\langle F_{\mathrm{ext}}F_{\mathrm{info}}\rangle
, which reflects the relative
orientation of the two forces.
As a result, two systems with identical current-based observables and identical
fluctuation scales can dissipate very differently, depending solely on their force
geometry. This degree of freedom is invisible in scalar descriptions based on currents,
variances, or state-space distances, but plays a central role in shaping
irreversibility.

Throughout, we focus on overdamped dynamics where the thermodynamic force admits
a decomposition into external and entropic contributions; extending the framework
to systems with additional force components or active noise would require a
corresponding extension of the force-space description.

The natural dimensionless quantity emerging from
Eq. (
3
) is the instantaneous Pearson correlation
coefficient between the external and information forces:

r(t)=\frac{\langle F_{\mathrm{ext}}F_{\mathrm{info}}\rangle_{t}}{\sqrt{\langle F_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}\rangle_{t}\,\langle F_{\mathrm{info}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}},

(4)

where the subscript
t
denotes averaging with respect to the instantaneous
probability distribution
\rho(x,t)
. This coefficient quantifies the geometric alignment of fluctuating forces and
serves as a real-time indicator of irreversibility at the force level. Importantly, both
\dot{S}_{i}(t)
and
r(t)
are defined instantaneously, without assuming that the system is at steady-state or cycle-averaged, so force geometry and stall apply equally to steady, periodic, and
transient nonequilibrium dynamics. The Pearson form of the coefficient
r(t)
arises naturally from the quadratic force decomposition of the entropy production rate; other reasonable measures of force alignment would capture the same cross-force structure without changing the underlying physics.

Perfect anti-alignment, quantified by a force correlation coefficient
r(t)=-1
, defines the

stall condition
of overdamped dynamics, where correlated cancellation of external and
entropic forces leads to a vanishing
mean
net force,

\langle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{net}}\rangle=0
, and arrests macroscopic transport.
Yet such thermodynamic stall—a nonequilibrium steady state—does not imply reversibility.
Local force imbalances,
F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t)\neq-F_{\mathrm{info}}(x,t)
, generally persist,
sustaining microscopic probability currents and finite entropy production. In fact, at stall,

\dot{S}_{i}\propto\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{net}})
, showing that entropy production
is entirely sustained by fluctuations of the net force.
While residual local force imbalance may not be directly accessible in coarse-grained
experiments, its presence is thermodynamically unavoidable whenever stall does not coincide
with reversibility. The box in Fig.
2
summarizes this conceptual hierarchy.

True thermodynamic reversibility is recovered only under the stricter, pointwise condition
F_{\mathrm{info}}=-F_{\mathrm{ext}}
, which eliminates all probability currents and renders microscopic trajectories statistically time-symmetric. Any departure from this perfect local force cancellation—whether through force misalignment or unequal magnitudes—necessarily generates entropy production. We define
geometric waste
as the total entropy production arising from departures from the
reversible force-cancellation condition, including both global misalignment captured by
deviations of
r(t)
from
-1
and residual local force imbalances that persist even at
thermodynamic stall. In this sense,
r(t)
quantifies proximity to stall, whereas geometric waste quantifies distance from true reversibility. Because
r(t)
captures only global force alignment, it resolves proximity to stall, whereas residual local force imbalance contributes to geometric waste without being fully resolved by this quantity.

Crucially, this framework highlights alternatives to quasistatic or near-reversible
operation, where entropy production is minimized by suppressing dynamics and transport.
By appropriately organizing system dynamics to enhance force anti-alignment,
entropy production can be reduced while finite transport is maintained.
The correlation coefficient
r(t)
thus functions as a geometric alignment measure,
integrating entropy production directly to asymmetries in fluctuating forces in a manner
that remains experimentally accessible. In overdamped dynamics, the condition
r(t)=-1
therefore serves as a diagnostic of stall,
distinct from the stricter condition of true reversibility.

A tunable geometric lower bound on entropy production

The force decomposition yields a natural geometric constraint on entropy production.
Using the inequality

\langle(F_{\mathrm{ext}}+F_{\mathrm{info}})^{2}\rangle_{t}\geq(\sqrt{\langle F_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}-\sqrt{\langle F_{\mathrm{info}}^{2}\rangle_{t}})^{2}
,
we obtain the instantaneous bound:

\dot{S}_{i}(t)\geq\frac{D}{k_{B}T^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\langle F_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}-\sqrt{\langle F_{\mathrm{info}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}\right)^{2}.

(5)

The bound is saturated when the two forces are perfectly anti-aligned
(
r(t)=-1
), corresponding to the stall condition of zero mean net force.
As shown in Fig.
2
, except for the special case of exact pointwise force cancellation, this lower
bound remains greater than zero.
Reversible stochastic operations (
\dot{S}_{i}=0
) require the additional condition
that the force magnitudes are matched locally,

F_{\mathrm{info}}(x,t)=-F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t)
.
Notably, unlike many thermodynamic bounds whose saturation requires fine-tuned or
asymptotic conditions and lacks a clear operational interpretation, saturation of the
present geometric bound corresponds directly to a physically meaningful regime—namely,
the stall condition of zero mean transport.

Exact force decomposition and correlation

The geometric origin of entropy production and its spatial heterogeneity becomes explicit when we examine the instantaneous forces acting on a particle in a harmonically trapped system driven by time-dependent protocols. For definiteness, we take
\Delta<0
in Fig.
3
, but this sign choice is purely conventional and leaves the classification of force-correlation regions unchanged. Overdamped dynamics in a harmonic potential is a linear Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with additive Gaussian noise. Hence, starting from an initially Gaussian shape around
x=0
, the particles’ spatial distribution remains Gaussian at all times, with time-dependent mean and variance
Risken1989Fokker
. Accordingly, the external driving force and the information-theoretic restoring force are both linear in position, but are centered on distinct reference points (see Fig.
3
):

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=-k\bigl(x-\lambda(t)\bigr),\qquad

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{info}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{B}T}{\sigma^{2}(t)}\bigl(x-\mu(t)\bigr),

(6)

where
\lambda(t)
denotes the trap center and
\mu(t)
the mean particle position. At each instant,
F_{\mathrm{info}}
is directed outward from the mean

\mu(t)
, while
F_{\mathrm{ext}}
is directed inward toward the trap center
\lambda(t)
. A finite lag

\Delta(t):=\mu(t)-\lambda(t)\neq 0
signals nonequilibrium transport.

Crucially, the local product of the two forces:

F_{\mathrm{ext}}F_{\mathrm{info}}=-\frac{kk_{B}T}{\sigma^{2}(t)}\bigl(x-\lambda(t)\bigr)\bigl(x-\mu(t)\bigr),

(7)

changes sign across space: it is negative when
x
lies outside the interval
[\lambda,\mu]
and positive within it. This sign reversal reflects local constructive or destructive interference between driving and entropic restoring forces, thereby generating spatial heterogeneity in entropy production.

The force statistics follow exactly,

\displaystyle\langle F_{\mathrm{ext}}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=k^{2}(\Delta^{2}(t)+\sigma^{2}(t)),

\displaystyle\langle F_{\mathrm{info}}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{(k_{B}T)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(t)},

\displaystyle\langle F_{\mathrm{ext}}F_{\mathrm{info}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-kk_{B}T,

(8)

leading to a compact expression for the Pearson correlation coefficient,

r(t)=-\frac{\sigma(t)}{\sqrt{\Delta^{2}(t)+\sigma^{2}(t)}}.

(9)

This result shows that force anti-alignment is controlled neither by the lag
\Delta

nor by the fluctuations
\sigma
alone, but by their dimensionless ratio

|\Delta|/\sigma
.
When fluctuations dominate (
|\Delta|\ll\sigma
), the forces approach perfect
anti-alignment (
r(t)\to-1
) and entropy production is reduced.
Conversely, when lag dominates (
|\Delta|\gg\sigma
), force alignment weakens
(
|r(t)|\ll 1
) and irreversibility increases.
The spatial organization of driven transport is thus captured by a single geometric
parameter that can be evaluated instantaneously from the force statistics.

Experimental connection and geometric explanation

The spatial heterogeneity and fluctuation–dissipation anticorrelation reported in Ref.
DiTerlizzi2024
provide a natural testbed for the geometric framework developed here. Using a variance-based sum rule, these experiments demonstrated that regions exhibiting
large fluctuations can dissipate weakly, while comparatively quiescent regions can act
as entropy production hotspots.
While this approach provides a powerful and experimentally accessible method for

inferring
local entropy production, it remains agnostic to the mechanism that generates the observed nonuniform dissipation.

Our framework identifies this missing structure.
In a driven harmonic trap, entropy production is controlled by the dimensionless ratio

|\Delta|/\sigma
, which fixes the force–force correlation coefficient
r
.
Regions with poor force alignment (
|r|\ll 1
, corresponding to
|\Delta|\gg\sigma
)
necessarily generate strong entropy production, whereas regions approaching force
anti-alignment (
r\approx-1
, or
|\Delta|\ll\sigma
) dissipate minimally.
As a result, positional fluctuations alone are not generally predictive of the entropy production: patches
with small fluctuations can be highly dissipative if their response lags the drive,
while strongly fluctuating regions can operate near force cancellation and dissipate
weakly.

This force-level perspective provides a geometric framework for the experimentally
observed anti-correlation between variance and entropy production, as well as the
spatial heterogeneity characterized in the same measurements.
Rather than reflecting anomalous fluctuations or proximity to equilibrium, the apparent efficiency of certain membrane regions emerges from spatially varying force anti-alignment (see Fig.
3
), which buffers the energetic cost of active fluctuations.
From this perspective, spatial heterogeneity in entropy production reflects not
heterogeneous activity or noise intensity, but spatial variation in force geometry. A visual summary of this framework is shown in
Fig.
4
.
Force geometry thus complements force-free inference schemes
DiTerlizzi2025
, providing a
fundamental organizational framework that can guide the interpretation and design of
experiments probing nonequilibrium entropy production.

, lies in a regime of strong force anti-alignment, with
r_{\mathrm{ss}}\approx-0.96
.
Its operation close to the anti-aligned force condition provides a geometric interpretation of the observed anticorrelation between fluctuations
and dissipation, indicating that strong force anti-alignment enables
high-amplitude flickering at comparatively low metabolic cost.

Sinusoidal driving: force geometry and microrheological operation

In optical-tweezer microrheology and related experiments, the center of a harmonic trap
is often modulated sinusoidally with frequency
\omega
:

\lambda(t)=A\cos(\omega t)
, to probe frequency-dependent mechanical response

Kumar2021Multi
;
CatalaCastro2025
.
For overdamped dynamics, the mean particle position
\mu(t)
follows the drive with a
phase lag
\phi
determined by

\tan\phi=\gamma\omega/k
, where
k
is the trap stiffness and
\gamma
the effective
friction coefficient.

For this protocol, the force–correlation coefficient, time-averaged over one driving
period
\tau=2\pi/\omega
, can be evaluated exactly (see
SM V
for derivation and discussion):

\langle r\rangle_{\tau}:=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}r(t)\,dt=-\frac{2}{\pi}\,K\!\left(-Q\sin^{2}\phi\right),

(11)

where
K(\cdot)
denotes the complete elliptic integral of the first kind and

Q=kA^{2}/(k_{B}T)
is a dimensionless measure of the driving amplitude relative to thermal
fluctuations.
The extent of force anti-alignment is therefore governed by the single geometric
combination
Q\sin^{2}\phi
.

Perfect force anti-alignment (
\langle r\rangle_{\tau}\to-1
) is approached only in the
quasi-static limit
\phi\to 0
, where the system remains close to instantaneous
equilibrium and cycle-averaged entropy production vanishes.
At finite phase lag, force cancellation is incomplete and irreversible dynamics are
unavoidable.

The same geometric structure organizes energetic cost.
For sinusoidal driving, the time-averaged injected mechanical power takes the form:

\langle P\rangle_{\tau}=\frac{k_{B}T\,k}{2\gamma}\,Q\sin^{2}\phi,

(12)

where
\langle P\rangle_{\tau}:=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}P(t)\,dt
. Both
\langle r\rangle_{\tau}
and
\langle P\rangle_{\tau}
thus depend exclusively on the same
geometric parameter
Q\sin^{2}\phi
, implying that contours of constant force correlation
coincide with contours of constant injected power throughout the
(Q,\phi)
plane.
For linear sinusoidal driving, force organization and energetic cost are therefore mathematically equivalent and
geometrically linked within this model, without reference to additional microscopic
details.

A natural reference point occurs at
\phi=\pi/4
, corresponding to driving at the
intrinsic relaxation rate
\omega=k/\gamma
.
At this impedance-matched condition, elastic and dissipative contributions to the response are equal, representing a crossover between quasi-static and dissipation-dominated operation.
The corresponding force correlation:

\langle r\rangle_{\tau}=-\frac{2}{\pi}\,K\!\left(-\frac{Q}{2}\right),

(13)

depends smoothly on
Q
and characterizes finite-power nonequilibrium operation away
from both quasistatic and strongly dissipative limits.

Figure
6
summarizes this geometric organization as a chart.
Dashed curves indicate contours of constant force correlation which, as noted, coincide with contours of constant time-averaged injected power.
Also shown is the experimentally relevant driving strength
Q_{\mathrm{ext}}=486.6
, fixed by the oscillation amplitude
A
, trap stiffness
k
, and thermal
scale
k_{B}T
, corresponding to the parameters used in Ref.
CatalaCastro2025
. Details of the parameters used to map experimental conditions onto the
theoretical framework for both driving protocols (including the rescaling
required for constant-velocity driving) are provided in
SM VI
.
Varying the driving frequency corresponds to traversing this line across the accessible
range of phase lags.

Within this representation, the fixed-
Q_{\mathrm{ext}}
trajectory intersects contours
of relatively strong force anti-alignment (
\langle r\rangle_{\tau}\lesssim-0.9
) at small
phase lags.
Because the phase lag is set by the driving frequency through

\tan\phi=\gamma\omega/k
, the chart highlights frequency ranges in which
force anti-alignment is enhanced at fixed energetic cost.
This geometric organization provides a principled basis for navigating
trade-offs between dissipative cost and mechanical response in
microrheological operation.
From this viewpoint, dynamical regimes already exploited in microrheological
experiments acquire a unified interpretation as manifestations of an
underlying force geometry. By resolving how forces are organized, this
framework makes explicit structural features of entropy production that
remain implicit in energetic analyses.

Relation to existing thermodynamic bounds

Thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs) bound the precision of a prescribed
current in terms of the entropy production rate, placing fundamental limits on
fluctuations at a given energetic cost

Barato2015Thermodynamic
;
Gingrich2016Dissipation
.
These results play an essential role in characterizing the reliability of
nonequilibrium processes, but they address a distinct aspect of
nonequilibrium behavior to the present framework.
TURs assume a fixed entropy production budget and ask how precisely a current
can be realized; by contrast, the force-correlation framework focuses on how
entropy production is structured through force organization.
In this sense, TURs constrain performance
given
entropy production,
whereas force geometry clarifies a mechanism by which entropy production itself
is generated. Notably, at stall where the mean current vanishes, the TUR becomes
trivial, while the entropy production rate remains finite and is determined entirely by fluctuations of
the net force.
Together, these perspectives provide a more complete thermodynamic description,
combining scalar bounds on precision with a geometric view of force alignment.

A parallel standpoint is provided by Fisher-information–based speed limits discussed in Ref.
Nicholson2020TimeInformation
.
These bounds constrain the minimal time required for a system to evolve between
probability distributions by relating entropy production to the statistical
distinguishability of states, and they apply even to underdamped dynamics.
While powerful, such limits remain agnostic to the organization of forces:
the same state-space displacement can be realized through distinct force
configurations with different entropy production.
By contrast, the force-correlation framework resolves the vectorial organization
of thermodynamic forces, clarifying how alignment between driving and entropic
forces shapes entropy production.

Related geometric views on entropy production have also been developed,
e.g., through decompositions based on information geometry and
thermodynamic length
Ito2020Geometric
.
These approaches operate at the level of probability distributions and currents,
providing valuable bounds on entropy production in finite-time processes.
Our analysis complements these perspectives by explicitly resolving
force alignment within the thermodynamic description.

Taken together, these perspectives illuminate distinct structural aspects of
nonequilibrium dynamics.
Thermodynamic uncertainty relations constrain the precision of currents at fixed
entropy production, Fisher-information speed limits bound the minimal time required for
state evolution, and force geometry clarifies how entropy production is generated through
the geometric organization of competing forces.
Integrating these viewpoints places force geometry within the broader
framework of nonequilibrium constraints, alongside statistical bounds
on speed and precision.

Summary and Outlook

In this work, we identify force alignment as a structural determinant
of irreversibility in overdamped nonequilibrium dynamics.
By decomposing the thermodynamic force into deterministic and information-theoretic components,
we show that entropy production depends not only on force magnitudes, but also on their
geometric organization. We quantify this organization through a force correlation coefficient.
This perspective complements scalar current-based bounds on thermodynamic observables by restoring the vectorial structure of thermodynamic forces.

In overdamped systems, thermodynamic stall follows from the proportionality between mean velocity and mean net force
(\langle\dot{x}\rangle\propto\langle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{net}}\rangle)
. Perfect anti-alignment cancels the average force and eliminates mean transport, while entropy production remains finite, Fig.
2
. In this case, entropy production is zero only if forces cancel pointwise in space and time. This separation between transport suppression and true reversibility highlights
that geometric anti-alignment represents a distinct nonequilibrium condition.
Extending this perspective beyond overdamped dynamics (see
SM VII
), where additional
degrees of freedom such as momentum are present, suggests a richer relationship
between force geometry and transport.
In underdamped dynamics, momentum acts as a thermodynamic buffer that partially decouples force geometry from transport. This allows for the coexistence of strong positional anti-alignment and directed motion – a dynamical state where transport persists even as positional force cancellation suppresses instantaneous entropy production. Such dynamics identify potential “coasting” regimes that scalar descriptions, lacking access to the underlying vectorial organization, would otherwise conflate with simple dissipative dragging in the presence of friction. Recent experiments on underdamped micro-mechanical oscillators
Ludovic2021

provide a natural setting to explore how force organization, transport,
and irreversibility are jointly controlled.

A complementary direction is suggested by recent experiments in living systems. In Ref.
DiTerlizzi2024
, steady-state entropy production was inferred from high-resolution flickering experiments in human red blood cells, including cell stretching and contour fluctuations analyzed as a proxy for metabolic activity. From the present perspective, these observations invite a geometric reinterpretation of low-dissipation operation in living matter. Sustained activity far from equilibrium may reflect the dynamical orchestration of internally generated and externally imposed forces. In overdamped environments, proximity to the stall condition (
r\rightarrow-1
) identifies a regime in which internally generated forces nearly cancel external loads, suppressing entropy production while the transport remains finite. In this view, force anti-alignment is the geometric mechanism through which entropy production and transport decouple in overdamped active biological systems.

Beyond inertia, spatial dimensionality introduces additional geometric freedom. Our geometric view extends directly to higher dimensions, where forces become vector fields and scalar products are replaced by inner products, without requiring additional assumptions. While force geometry in one-dimensional confinement is necessarily binary (aligned or anti-aligned), higher dimensions permit continuous angular reorganization of competing forces, allowing partial cancellation and transport to coexist. Together with recent optimal-transport bounds on finite-time entropy production
Oikawa2025
, these considerations suggest that force geometry offers a unifying structural perspective and a potential framework for organizing and regulating nonequilibrium operation across driven systems.

SM I
Derivation of entropy production rate from the overdamped Fokker-Planck equation

To derive Eq. (2) in the main text, we first note that all the different entropy rates can be formulated through the information-theoretic quantity surprisal,
I(x,t)=-\ln\rho(x,t)
.
We start from the overdamped Langevin equation, describing the dynamics of a particle under the influence of thermal white noise, and a potential
U(x,t)
:

\displaystyle\dot{x}=-U^{\prime}(x,t)+\sqrt{2D}\Gamma(t),

(1)

where
\Gamma(t)
is Gaussian white noise with

\langle\Gamma(t)\rangle=0
and

\langle\Gamma(t)\Gamma(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.

The corresponding Fokker-Planck equation, describing an ensemble of such particles is:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\rho}(x,t)=D\partial_{x}^{2}\rho(x,t)+\frac{D}{k_{B}T}\partial_{x}\left[U^{\prime}(x,t)\rho(x,t)\right].

(2)

According to standard formulations of stochastic thermodynamics

Seifert2012Stochastic
,
the time derivative of the system entropy and the entropy flow to the environment
can be expressed in terms of the time-dependent probability density
\rho(x,t)
as

\displaystyle\text{(i)}\quad\dot{S}(t)

\displaystyle=-k_{B}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dot{\rho}(x,t)\,\ln\rho(x,t)\,dx,

(3)

\displaystyle\text{(ii)}\quad\dot{S}_{e}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dot{\rho}(x,t)\,U(x,t)\,dx,

(4)

where
T
denotes the bath temperature. The entropy production rate is then defined as

\dot{S}_{i}(t)\equiv\dot{S}(t)-\dot{S}_{e}(t)
.
The entropy production rate is thus

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{S}_{i}&\equiv\dot{S}-\dot{S}_{e}\\
&=-k_{B}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dot{\rho}(x,t)\ln\rho(x,t)\,dx-\frac{1}{T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dot{\rho}(x,t)\,U(x,t)\,dx\\
&=-k_{B}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigg[D\,\partial_{x}^{2}\rho(x,t)+\frac{D}{k_{B}T}\,\partial_{x}\bigl(U^{\prime}(x,t)\rho(x,t)\bigr)\Bigg]\ln\rho(x,t)\,dx\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigg[D\,\partial_{x}^{2}\rho(x,t)+\frac{D}{k_{B}T}\,\partial_{x}\bigl(U^{\prime}(x,t)\rho(x,t)\bigr)\Bigg]U(x,t)\,dx\end{split}

(5)

Integrating by parts and assuming vanishing boundary contributions, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{S}_{i}&=k_{B}D\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\bigl(\partial_{x}\rho(x,t)\bigr)^{2}}{\rho(x,t)}\,dx+2\frac{D}{T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}U^{\prime}(x,t)\,\partial_{x}\rho(x,t)\,dx\\
&+\frac{D}{k_{B}T^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl(U^{\prime}(x,t)\bigr)^{2}\,\rho(x,t)\,dx\\
&=k_{B}D\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(x,t)\Bigl[\partial_{x}\ln\rho(x,t)+\frac{U^{\prime}(x,t)}{k_{B}T}\Bigr]^{2}dx.\end{split}

Defining the information force

F_{\mathrm{info}}(x,t)=-k_{B}T\,\partial_{x}\ln\rho(x,t)
,
this can be written compactly as

\dot{S}_{i}=\frac{D}{k_{B}T^{2}}\Bigl\langle\bigl[F_{\mathrm{info}}(x,t)+F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t)\bigr]^{2}\Bigr\rangle,

(6)

where
F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t)=-U^{\prime}(x,t)
and
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes an average
with respect to
\rho(x,t)
.

This result, which leads to Eq. (2) in the main text, complements the
information-theoretic formulations of the total entropy rate and entropy flow rate
derived in Refs.
Nicholson2020TimeInformation
,
DechantSasa2021PhysRevX11.041061
:

\displaystyle\dot{S}=-k_{B}\left\langle\dot{I}(x,t)\,I(x,t)\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{e}=\frac{\dot{\mathcal{Q}}}{T}=-\left\langle U(x,t)\dot{I}(x,t)\right\rangle,

(7)

where
\dot{\mathcal{Q}}
denotes the heat
absorbed by the system
from the thermal bath.
The entropy production rate is
\dot{S}_{i}=\dot{S}-\dot{S}_{e}\geq 0
.

SM II
Integrated Bounds for Finite-Time Processes

From Instantaneous to Integrated Constraints

While instantaneous bounds provide moment-by-moment constraints on entropy production rate, practical
experiments and engineered protocols operate over finite durations.
Here we derive integrated bounds that constrain the total entropy production over
an arbitrary protocol time
\tau
.

Derivation of the Integrated Bound

Starting from the instantaneous inequality derived in the main text, we integrate the
entropy production rate over the protocol duration
\tau
,

\Sigma_{\tau}=\int_{0}^{\tau}\dot{S}_{i}(t)\,dt\geq\frac{D}{k_{B}T^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{ext}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}-\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{info}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}\right)^{2}dt,

(8)

where
\Sigma_{\tau}
is the total entropy production in units of
k_{B}
.

Defining:

g(t)=\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{ext}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}-\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{info}}^{2}\rangle_{t}},

and applying Jensen’s inequality to the convex function
g(x)^{2}
, we obtain the averaged bound

\Sigma_{\tau}\geq\frac{D\tau}{k_{B}T^{2}}\left(\overline{\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{ext}}^{2}\rangle}}-\overline{\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{info}}^{2}\rangle}}\right)^{2},

(9)

with time averages defined as

\displaystyle\overline{\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{ext}}^{2}\rangle}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{ext}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}\,dt,

\displaystyle\overline{\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{info}}^{2}\rangle}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{info}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}\,dt.

(10)

Scope and applicability.

The integrated bounds derived here rely only on the Markovian overdamped
Fokker–Planck description and therefore apply far from equilibrium, without
invoking linear-response or quasistatic assumptions.
They hold for arbitrary protocol durations
\tau>0
, from instantaneous limits
to finite-time processes, and are independent of the specific initial state preparation.
Moreover, the bounds are valid for general time-dependent driving protocols

U(x,t)
and do not assume any specific functional form of the driving.

Physical Interpretation.

The integrated bound shows that the total entropy production over a finite-time protocol is
constrained by the time-averaged magnitudes of the external and information forces.
The instantaneous force-correlation structure determines how closely this bound can be
approached over the protocol duration.
Protocols that maintain favorable force anti-alignment throughout the protocol
accumulate minimal excess entropy production, whereas protocols with poor force alignment incur
large geometric waste gaps, even when leading to identical macroscopic outcomes.
In the overdamped setting considered here, favorable force anti-alignment corresponds
to proximity to stall rather than true reversibility, since pointwise force cancellation is not imposed.

Relation to Current-Based Integrated Bounds

Unlike thermodynamic uncertainty relations and related integrated bounds, which provide
scalar constraints on total entropy production or current fluctuations, the present
inequality retains explicit information about the time-resolved force structure along the protocol.
This decomposition makes it possible to identify which segments of a protocol contribute most strongly to
entropy production, thereby enabling a localized attribution of entropy production rather than a purely global
estimation.
Correlation-aware thermodynamic bounds have been studied in other contexts, where incorporating correlations
between observables can tighten traditional scalar bounds such as the thermodynamic uncertainty
relation
DechantSasa2021PhysRevX11.041061
, but these frameworks operate at the level of stochastic observables,
rather than directly resolving the geometric organization of thermodynamic forces considered here.

Implications for Work Bounds

For isothermal processes coupled to a single thermal reservoir and using the Sekimoto
definition of stochastic work (with Stratonovich interpretation), the total excess work
satisfies

W_{\tau}-\Delta F=k_{B}T\,\Sigma_{\tau},

(11)

which implies the bound

W_{\tau}\geq\Delta F+\frac{D\tau}{T}\left(\overline{\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{ext}}^{2}\rangle}}-\overline{\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{info}}^{2}\rangle}}\right)^{2}.

(12)

This inequality establishes a minimum energetic cost required to execute a protocol of
duration
\tau
, independent of the detailed time dependence of the driving.
Unlike quadratic-response bounds or speed limits, this constraint explicitly links
energetic expenditure to the geometric mismatch between external and information forces.

Experimental Utility.

The integrated bound provides experimentally actionable diagnostics for interpreting protocol performance.
Reducing excess entropy production does not require suppressing dynamics or extending protocol durations,
but rather maintaining close correspondence between

\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{ext}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}
and

\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{info}}^{2}\rangle_{t}}
throughout the protocol.
This time-resolved viewpoint enables systematic identification of geometrically inefficient segments in complex driving protocols, clarifying where excess entropy production accumulates.

SM III
General Framework for Moving Harmonic Traps

System Description and Dynamics

We consider a Brownian particle immersed in a viscous fluid at temperature
T
, subject to a harmonic trapping potential whose centre
\lambda(t)
can be externally controlled
Risken1989Fokker
,
vanKampen2007Stochastic
. The particle experiences deterministic restoring forces from the trap and random thermal fluctuations from the surrounding medium
Sekimoto2010Stochastic
.

The potential energy is

U(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}k\big[x-\lambda(t)\big]^{2},

(13)

The corresponding deterministic force is

F_{\text{ext}}(x,t)=-\nabla U=-k\big[x-\lambda(t)\big],

(14)

which always points toward the instantaneous trap centre
\lambda(t)
.

In the overdamped regime, where inertia is negligible compared to viscous
friction, the dynamics are governed by the Langevin equation

\gamma\frac{dx}{dt}=-k\big[x-\lambda(t)\big]+\sqrt{2\gamma k_{B}T}\,\xi(t),

(15)

where
\gamma
is the drag coefficient,
k_{B}
is Boltzmann’s constant, and

\xi(t)
is Gaussian white noise with

\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0
and

\langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.

It is convenient to introduce the relaxation rate and diffusion constant,

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{\gamma},\quad D=\frac{k_{B}T}{\gamma}.

(16)

In terms of these parameters, the Langevin equation becomes

\frac{dx}{dt}=-\Gamma x+\Gamma\lambda(t)+\sqrt{2D}\,\xi(t).

(17)

Because this equation is linear, a Gaussian initial distribution remains
Gaussian for all times.

Lag and Nonequilibrium Driving.

A central quantity characterizing nonequilibrium driving is the
lag

\Delta(t)=\mu(t)-\lambda(t),

(18)

defined as the displacement between the mean particle position
\mu(t)
and
the trap centre
\lambda(t)
. The lag quantifies the system’s inability to
instantaneously follow the external driving protocol.

When
\Delta(t)=0
, the distribution is centred at the trap minimum and, for
overdamped dynamics, the system remains in instantaneous equilibrium,
corresponding to quasi-static or stall-like motion. When
\Delta(t)\neq 0
, the trap moves faster than the
relaxation dynamics, generating irreversible entropy production. The magnitude of

|\Delta|
relative to the thermal width
\sigma
controls the degree of
nonequilibrium driving.

The mean restoring force exerted by the trap is

\langle F_{\text{ext}}\rangle(t)=-k\langle x-\lambda\rangle=-k\Delta(t),

(19)

and the mean particle velocity is

\langle\dot{x}\rangle=-\frac{k}{\gamma}\Delta(t).

(20)

For forward dragging with
\lambda(t)=vt
and
v>0
, the lag
\Delta<0
and the
mean velocity is positive, indicating directed transport.

SM IV
Constant Drag Protocol

Steady-State Solution

For constant-velocity dragging,
\lambda(t)=vt
, the system reaches a
nonequilibrium steady state. Solving the mean dynamics yields

\mu(t)=vt-\frac{\gamma v}{k},

(21)

while the positional variance attains its equilibrium value

\sigma^{2}=\langle(x-\mu)^{2}\rangle=\frac{k_{B}T}{k},

(22)

independent of the dragging velocity. The steady-state lag is therefore

\Delta=\mu-\lambda=-\frac{\gamma v}{k},

(23)

with the negative sign indicating that the distribution mean trails the trap
centre.

Force Correlation Coefficient

The external force exerted by the moving trap is

F_{\text{ext}}(x,t)=-k(x-vt),

(24)

while the information-theoretic (entropic) force associated with the
probability density
\rho(x,t)
is

F_{\text{info}}(x,t)=-k_{B}T\,\frac{\partial\ln\rho}{\partial x}=k(x-\mu),

(25)

where the second equality follows from the Gaussian form of
\rho(x,t)
.

Using Gaussian statistics, the force moments are

\displaystyle\langle F_{\text{ext}}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=k^{2}\big(\sigma^{2}+\Delta^{2}\big),

(26)

\displaystyle\langle F_{\text{info}}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=k^{2}\sigma^{2},

(27)

\displaystyle\langle F_{\text{ext}}F_{\text{info}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-k^{2}\sigma^{2}.

(28)

The force correlation coefficient defined in the main text is therefore

r(t)=\frac{\langle F_{\text{ext}}F_{\text{info}}\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle F_{\text{ext}}^{2}\rangle\langle F_{\text{info}}^{2}\rangle}}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\Delta(t)^{2}/\sigma(t)^{2}}}.

(29)

For a harmonic trap at finite temperature, the correlation satisfies

-1\leq r<0
. The strict negativity of
r
reflects the one-dimensional harmonic geometry, in
which external and entropic forces can only align or anti-align.

Substituting the steady-state expressions

\Delta=-\gamma v/k
and
\sigma^{2}=k_{B}T/k
yields

r_{\text{ss}}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\dfrac{\gamma^{2}v^{2}}{kk_{B}T}}}.

(30)

For fixed
r_{\text{ss}}
, this implies the scaling relation

k\propto v^{2}\qquad\text{for constant }r_{\text{ss}}.

(31)

Injected power under constant dragging

For a time-dependent potential, the instantaneous injected mechanical power is
defined in the standard stochastic-thermodynamic sense as

P(t)=\left\langle\frac{\partial U(x,t)}{\partial t}\right\rangle.

(32)

For a harmonically trapped particle with center
\lambda(t)=vt
, this yields

P(t)=-k\langle x(t)-\lambda(t)\rangle\,\dot{\lambda}(t)=-k\,\Delta(t)\,v.

(33)

In the nonequilibrium steady state, the lag
\Delta=-\gamma v/k
is constant,
so that the time-averaged injected power becomes

\langle P\rangle=\gamma v^{2}.

(34)

This expression coincides with the steady-state dissipation rate,
as expected for overdamped dynamics in contact with a single thermal reservoir.

SM V
Sinusoidal Driving Protocol

Periodic Steady State

For sinusoidal driving, the trap centre oscillates as

\lambda(t)=A\cos(\omega t),

(35)

where
A>0
is the amplitude and
\omega
the angular frequency.
The mean position obeys

\dot{\mu}(t)+\Gamma\mu(t)=\Gamma A\cos(\omega t),

(36)

whose periodic steady-state solution is

\mu(t)=AR\cos(\omega t-\phi),

(37)

with

R=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega/\Gamma)^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\gamma\omega/k)^{2}}},\qquad\tan\phi=\frac{\omega}{\Gamma}=\frac{\gamma\omega}{k},

(38)

where
\phi\in[0,\pi/2)
is the phase lag and
R\leq 1
the amplitude reduction factor.

Instantaneous Correlation Coefficient

The instantaneous lag between the distribution mean and the trap centre is

\displaystyle\Delta(t)

\displaystyle=\mu(t)-\lambda(t)=A\!\left[R\cos(\omega t-\phi)-\cos(\omega t)\right]

\displaystyle=A\sin\phi\,\sin(\omega t-\phi),

(39)

where we used
R=\cos\phi
.

The instantaneous force-correlation coefficient is therefore

r(t)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\Delta^{2}(t)/\sigma^{2}(t)}}.

(40)

Introducing the dimensionless driving strength

Q\equiv\frac{A^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{kA^{2}}{k_{B}T},

(41)

we can write

r(t)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+Q\sin^{2}\phi\,\sin^{2}(\omega t-\phi)}}.

(42)

Here
Q
measures the driving strength relative to thermal fluctuations. Here
\sigma^{2}=k_{B}T/k
denotes the steady state variance of the harmonic trap, which remains time-independent under sinusoidal driving.

Time-Averaged Force Correlation and Injected Power

For periodic driving, the force–correlation coefficient is naturally averaged over
one driving period
\tau=2\pi/\omega
,

\langle r\rangle_{\tau}=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}r(t)\,dt.

(43)

Introducing the phase variable
\theta=\omega t
, this becomes

\langle r\rangle_{\tau}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1+Q\,\sin^{2}\phi\,\sin^{2}(\theta-\phi)}}.

(44)

Exploiting the periodicity and symmetry of the integrand, the integral reduces to

\langle r\rangle_{\tau}=-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1+Q\sin^{2}\phi\,\sin^{2}\theta}}.

(45)

This time average isolates the geometric contribution of force alignment over a
driving cycle, independent of the instantaneous phase. Writing

\alpha\equiv Q\sin^{2}\phi,

(46)

the integral can be expressed exactly in terms of the complete elliptic integral
of the first kind with negative parameter,

\langle r\rangle_{\tau}=-\frac{2}{\pi}\,K(-\alpha),\qquad K(-\alpha)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1+\alpha\sin^{2}\theta}},

(47)

which is valid for all values of
Q
and
\phi
.

For sinusoidal driving in the linear-response regime, the time-averaged injected
mechanical power is controlled by the same geometric parameter
\alpha
,

\langle P\rangle_{\tau}=\frac{k_{B}T\,k}{2\gamma}\,\alpha.

(48)

Thus, both force correlation and injected power are organized by the same
combination of driving amplitude and frequency, a consequence of the harmonic
structure of the protocol rather than a generic feature of driven systems.

A geometrically distinguished reference point occurs at the impedance-matched
condition
\phi=\pi/4
, corresponding to equal storage and loss components of the
linear response. At this phase lag,

\langle r\rangle_{\tau}=-\frac{2}{\pi}K(-Q/2),

(49)

showing explicitly that even at fixed phase the degree of force anti-alignment
varies with driving strength. This reflects the intrinsic coupling between force
geometry and driving amplitude encoded in
\alpha
.

At the impedance-matched condition, force cancellation is incomplete but
structured: finite transport is sustained while entropy production remains controlled,
with neither reversible nor strongly irreversible contributions dominating the
dynamics. The experimental operating point lies far from this geometric balance,
reflecting the objective of broadband mechanical probing, which prioritizes
frequency coverage over force correlation at any single operating point. Within
the geometric landscape defined by
\alpha
, the experiment therefore operates in
a low-correlation regime despite strong driving.

Driven-Dominated Regime (
Q\gg 1
).

In the driven-dominated regime, where the driving amplitude is large compared to
thermal fluctuations (
Q\gg 1
), the integral in
Eq. (
47
) admits a controlled asymptotic expansion.

When the geometric parameter

\alpha=Q\sin^{2}\phi

is large, corresponding to strong driving combined with a substantial phase lag
between the trap motion and the particle response, the complete elliptic integral exhibits logarithmic decay. In this
limit, the time-averaged force correlation behaves as

\langle r\rangle_{\tau}\simeq-\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\alpha}}\ln(16\alpha),\qquad\alpha\gg 1.

(50)

This asymptotic follows directly from the large-argument expansion of the
complete elliptic integral of the first kind,

K(-\alpha)\sim\frac{\ln(16\alpha)}{2\sqrt{\alpha}},\quad\alpha\to\infty.

(51)

Thus, for fixed phase lag
\phi
, the magnitude of the time-averaged force
correlation decays as
Q^{-1/2}
, up to a logarithmic correction, in the
strong-driving limit. Large driving amplitudes alone therefore do not guarantee
strong force anti-alignment; the geometric phase lag plays a decisive role
through the combination
\alpha
.

For the experimentally relevant parameters discussed in the next section,
\alpha

lies deep in this asymptotic regime, and the approximate expression
Eq. (
50
) is in quantitative agreement with the exact
elliptic-integral evaluation. This places the corresponding operating point in a
low-correlation regime despite strong driving.

Accordingly, while the asymptotic form captures the scaling behavior, the exact
expression in Eq. (
47
) remains essential for precise quantitative
interpretation whenever
\alpha
is large.

Interpretation and geometric implications.

The force–correlation framework exposes a geometric trade-off underlying finite-time
nonequilibrium operation.
Near-perfect force anti-alignment (
\langle r\rangle_{\tau}\to-1
) corresponds to quasi-static or stall-like
dynamics in which external and entropic forces nearly cancel.
While this suppresses entropy production, it also yields vanishing injected power.
Finite power output therefore requires operating at a controlled distance from this
limit, where force cancellation is incomplete and irreversibility is finite.

For sinusoidal driving, this trade-off is constrained by geometry.
Because the time-averaged force correlation
\langle r\rangle_{\tau}
and the injected power

\langle P\rangle_{\tau}
both depend on the same geometric parameter
x=Q\sin^{2}\phi
,
they cannot be tuned independently: improving force anti-alignment necessarily
reduces the power that can be delivered at a given driving strength.
The impedance-matched condition
\phi=\pi/4
, where elastic and dissipative response
components are equal, marks a reference point in this geometric landscape.
At this phase, the correlation takes the explicit form

\langle r\rangle_{\tau}=-\frac{2}{\pi}K(-Q/2)
, which varies continuously with the
driving strength
Q
.
This illustrates that even at a fixed phase, the degree of force anti-alignment is not
fixed but is instead modulated by the driving amplitude, reflecting the intrinsic
coupling between force geometry and energetic cost.

Importantly, a large magnitude of the force–correlation coefficient does not by itself
imply efficient or high-performance operation.
Values
|\langle r\rangle_{\tau}|\approx 1
can arise from distinct physical regimes:
(i) quasi-static driving, where entropy production and power both vanish, and
(ii) weak driving (
Q\ll 1
), where correlations are dominated by thermal fluctuations.
Discriminating between these regimes requires considering both the correlation
coefficient and the driving strength.

Taken together, these results clarify the role of force correlation as a geometric
organizing variable.
For sinusoidal driving, correlation and energetic cost are intrinsically linked,
whereas other protocols may allow partial decoupling.
The framework therefore provides a unified and protocol-sensitive perspective on
nonequilibrium operation, enabling meaningful comparison across driving schemes
without invoking protocol-independent optimality.

SM VII
The Underdamped Opportunity: A Supporting Argument

Here we provide a minimal analytical argument supporting the conjecture that
underdamped dynamics can sustain strong force anti-alignment while maintaining
finite currents, a situation that does not arise in overdamped systems.

Underdamped Dynamics and Information Geometry

We consider one-dimensional underdamped Langevin dynamics,

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{t}}{m},

(55)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{t}

\displaystyle=F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x_{t},t)-\frac{\gamma}{m}p_{t}+\sqrt{2\gamma k_{B}T}\,\xi_{t},

(56)

where
F_{\mathrm{ext}}=-\partial_{x}U(x,t)
.
The phase-space density
\rho(x,p,t)
obeys the Klein–Kramers equation.

In general,
\rho(x,p,t)
does not factorize and both information forces
depend on both position and momentum.
Exact factorization of the phase-space distribution,

\rho(x,p,t)=\rho_{x}(x,t)\rho_{p}(p,t)
, occurs only under special conditions
(e.g. equilibrium) or when enforced by additional constraints discussed below.

We define position- and momentum-space information geometric quantities:

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{info}}^{(x)}

\displaystyle=-k_{B}T\,\partial_{x}\ln\rho,

(57)

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{info}}^{(p)}

\displaystyle=-k_{B}T\,\partial_{p}\ln\rho,

(58)

which are defined on full phase space. The dimension of
F_{\mathrm{info}}^{(x)}
is that of a force,
MLT^{-2}
,
whereas
F_{\mathrm{info}}^{(p)}
has the dimension of a velocity,
LT^{-1}
. Upon marginalization over momentum,

F_{\mathrm{info}}^{(x)}
reduces to the
standard overdamped information force.

Perfect Position-Space Anti-Alignment

Assume that the external force is perfectly anti-aligned with the
position-space information force,

F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t)=-F_{\mathrm{info}}^{(x)}(x,p,t),

(59)

for all
(x,p)
with
\rho>0
.
This condition is highly restrictive and is not expected to hold
generically in driven underdamped systems.
Since
F_{\mathrm{ext}}
is independent of
p
, this condition implies that

\partial_{x}\ln\rho
is independent of
p
, and therefore

\rho(x,p,t)=\rho_{x}(x,t)\rho_{p}(p,t).

(60)

Equation (
59
) then yields

\rho_{x}(x,t)\propto e^{-U(x,t)/k_{B}T},

(61)

i.e. the position distribution instantaneously satisfies detailed balance,
despite the system being driven.

Thus, perfect force anti-alignment in position space enforces equilibrium
structure only in the positional sector,
without constraining the momentum distribution.

Entropy Production and Current

For standard underdamped Langevin dynamics with linear friction and a single
thermal reservoir, the entropy production rate is entirely determined by the
irreversible momentum current
Seifert2012Stochastic
,
SpinneyFord2012
:

\dot{S}_{i}(t)=\frac{\gamma}{k_{B}T}\iint\rho(x,p,t)\Bigl(\frac{p}{m}-F_{\mathrm{info}}^{(p)}\Bigr)^{2}\,dx\,dp.

(62)

If the phase-space distribution factorizes,

\rho(x,p,t)=\rho_{x}(x,t)\rho_{p}(p,t)
, which occurs only under the strong condition of
perfect position-space force anti-alignment, this reduces to

\dot{S}_{i}(t)=\frac{\gamma}{k_{B}T}\Bigl\langle\Bigl(\frac{p}{m}-F_{\mathrm{info}}^{(p)}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr\rangle_{p}.

(63)

Hence, imposing perfect force anti-alignment in position space places no
direct constraint on entropy production, which remains generically nonzero
unless the momentum distribution is exactly Maxwellian.

Meanwhile, the mean transport current is
J(t)=\langle\dot{x}\rangle=\frac{1}{m}\langle p\rangle_{p}
,
which depends only on the first moment of the momentum marginal
\rho_{p}(p,t)
.
Although the current is defined as a position-space observable, for underdamped
dynamics it is entirely controlled by the momentum sector, since
\dot{x}=p/m
.

Thus, even when forces are perfectly anti-aligned in position space, the momentum
sector can sustain finite mean transport.
Crucially, the current and the entropy production are governed by different
moments of the momentum distribution.

Implication

Equations (
63
)
show that underdamped dynamics permit the coexistence of
perfect force anti-alignment in position space, finite directed current,
and finite entropy production.
This coexistence does not arise in overdamped systems, where perfect force
anti-alignment (
r=-1
) necessarily implies a vanishing mean current.
In overdamped dynamics, entropy production vanishes only under exact
pointwise force cancellation, while finite entropy production may
persist at stall in the absence of transport, thereby precluding finite
power operation.

Momentum therefore acts as a thermodynamic buffer that decouples force geometry
from transport, allowing underdamped systems to approach ideal geometric
alignment in position space without suppressing current.
This observation supports the conjecture that inertia can be exploited as a
design resource for high-efficiency stochastic machines.
