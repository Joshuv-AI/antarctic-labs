Several key issues should be emphasized in assessing clinical benefits of a regional block for elderly patients undergoing hip arthroplasty under spinal anesthesia
Authors: Ru-Ping Li, Fu-Shan Xue, Yi Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis matters arising article was written in response to the recent article by Chen et al regarding “Effect of perioperative ultrasound guided fascia iliaca compartment block in elderly adults with hip fractures undergoing arthroplasty in spinal anesthesia-a randomized controlled trial”, which is published on BMC Geriatrics at February, 2023. The main findings of Chen et al’ study are that the fascia iliaca compartment block (FICB) before spinal anesthesia reduces positioning pain, shorten procedure time of spinal anesthesia, prolongs duration of analgesia and improves quality of early postoperative recovery, but reduces postoperative muscle strength. While the efforts of using the existing technique by Chen et al. to optimize patient comfort during spinal anesthesia procedure should be acknowledged, we have noted several issues about methods and results of this study that are not well addressed, such as the lack of detailed perioperative analgesic scheme, clinical significance of reduced positioning pain during spinal anesthesia procedure with the FICB, adverse motor effect of the FICB, time needed for additional FICB, and others. We believe that clarification of these issues is helpful for quality improvement of further randomized clinical trials assessing the benefits of a regional block in elderly patients with hip fractures undergoing arthroplasty under spinal anesthesia.Keywords: Hip fracture, Hip arthroplasty, Fascia iliaca compartment block, Spinal anesthesia, Elderly patients

This matters arising article was written in response to the recent article by Chen et al. regarding “Effect of perioperative ultrasound guided fascia iliaca compartment block (FICB) in elderly adults with hip fractures undergoing arthroplasty in spinal anesthesia-a randomized controlled trial” [1], in which the FICB performed before spinal anesthesia reduced positioning pain during spinal anesthesia procedure, shortened procedure time of spinal anesthesia, prolonged duration of analgesia and enhanced early postoperative recovery defined by the quality of recovery-15 score, but reduced postoperative muscle strength. Many aspects of Chen et al.’ research have been well done and their findings have the potential implications. However, as a randomized controlled trial determining the clinical benefits of an intervention, other than the limitations described by the authors in the discussion section, we have noted several questions about methodology and results of this study that need further clarification and discussion.

First, after completion of spinal anesthesia, patient was asked to assess most severe positioning pain during spinal anesthesia procedure using a visual analogue scale (VAS) score. Reporting the maximum VAS score during spinal anesthesia procedure provided a valuable insight, but Chen et al. [1] did not clearly state whether preoperative pain level was comparable between the two groups and if a consistent perioperative analgesic scheme was used in all patients. Given that patients with hip fractures often experience severe preoperative pain [2], the current enhanced recovery after surgery practice for knee and hip arthroplasty actually recommend the use of perioperative multimodal analgesia strategies, which include a serial of non-opioid analgesics, such as paracetamol, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or cyclooxygenase-2 specific inhibitors, gabapentinoids and dexamethasone, as well as opioid analgesics on needed [3]. Especially, it is required that administration of analgesics should be started before surgery to decrease pain with hip fracture and improve patients’ outcomes. We are concerned that any significant between-group imbalance in the preoperative pain levels and analgesic uses would have affected the results of positioning pain evaluation during spinal anesthesia procedure.

Second, the median VAS score of most severe positioning pain during spinal anesthesia procedure was significantly decreased in the FICB group compared with the control group, but the net between-group difference of median VAS score is 1.5, with large interquartile ranges. We would like to remind the authors and readers that the recommended minimal clinically important differences of pain scores in the available literature are 1.5 points at resting and 1.8 points at movement when pain levels are assessed by a 0–10 VAS in undergoing knee and hip arthroplasty [4]. That is, the between-group difference of most severe positioning pain intensities during spinal anesthesia procedure in this study did not exceed the critical value of the recommended minimal clinically important difference of 1.8 points at movement. Most important, this study did not assess patient satisfaction with pain control or comfort during spinal anesthesia procedure, a patient-centered outcome measurement, albeit VAS score is a subjective variable and pain intensity alone cannot well capture the full patient experience. In these cases, it is somewhat difficult for the readers to determine whether reduced positioning pain during spinal anesthesia procedure with the FICB should be considered as being clinically important. While the efforts of using the existing technique by Chen et al. to optimize hip fracture patient comfort during spinal anesthesia procedure should be acknowledged as a reasonable and clinically grounded approach, the above issues need to be noted when interpreting the results of this study.

Third, as early mobilization is one of the cornerstones of enhanced recovery after surgery practices for knee and hip arthroplasty, motor-sparing regional blocks have become popular as they can minimize pain and opioid requirements while preserving motor function and promoting early postoperative ambulation [2, 3, 5, 6]. In the Chen et al.’ study, however, up to 57.8% of patients receiving the FICB only had a grade 3 of operated side quadriceps muscle strength when the postoperative non-operative side quadriceps muscle strength had reached grade 4. This is not in line with the basic requirements of current enhanced recovery after surgery practices for knee and hip arthroplasty [3]. Despite of the potential of decreasing pain intensity during spinal anesthesia procedure with the FICB, the adverse effect of weakened lower extremity muscle function on postoperative rehabilitation in elderly patients undergoing hip arthroplasty should not be ignored. In recent literatures, the distal pericapsular nerve group block with motor-sparing benefits has been shown as a promising alternative to proximal nerve blocks with muscle weakness in the perioperative multimodal analgesia regimen of patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty under spinal anesthesia, as it can provide more complete analgesia to the hip by depositing local anesthetic within the myofascial plane of the psoas muscle and superior pubic ramus and may be performed with the patient in the supine position [7, 8]. It has been shown that for patients with hip fracture, pericapsular nerve group block compared to the FICB can resulted in a lower dose of local anesthetic, a shorter procedure time and earlier analgesic effect [9]. We believe that clarifying the merits and demerits of these two widely used regional block techniques may help inform clinical decision-making and select the optimized pathways of anesthesia and perioperative analgesia for patients with hip fracture surgery according to individualized clinical scenarios.

Finally, the FICB was performed after patients entered into the operating room, rather than before surgery. The FICB shortened the median procedure time of spinal anesthesia by 8 min, but Chen et al. [1] did not report the time needed for the FICB procedure. Given that the total time of anesthetic interventions in the FICB groups should include the times needed for the FICB and spinal anesthesia procedures, it is unclear whether the addition of the FICB will affect the turnover of a high-efficient operating room. As unstable bones of hip fracture can cause severe pain in the slightest movement, moreover, it is best that regional blocks should be implemented before surgery to improve the compliance of patients with preoperative diagnostic examination and therapeutic procedures [2]. It has been shown that this strategy may save preoperative opioid consumption, decrease total time of anesthetic interventions and improve patients’ outcomes [10]. We argue that addressing these methodological concerns would enhance the clarity and impact of their findings. Such methodological refinement is also essential for translating the valuable evidence into current clinical practice.
