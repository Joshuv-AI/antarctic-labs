Application of retrospective teaching method in stomatology undergraduate education

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUndergraduate orthodontic education requires students to integrate multiple diagnostic sources and develop individualized treatment plans, yet lecture-based learning may limit opportunities for integrating theoretical knowledge with clinical practice. This study evaluated the effectiveness of a case review-based clinical learning improving academic performance and learning-related outcomes.MethodsForty-two undergraduate orthodontic students were randomly assigned to either a case review-based learning group (experimental, n = 21) or a lecture-based learning group (control, n = 21). The experimental group engaged with complete patient cases in a stepwise, longitudinal format, linking each stage from consultation and record-taking to appliance therapy and retention with underlying theoretical knowledge. The same group of instructors delivered both teaching interventions using an identical curriculum and teaching schedule. Academic performance was assessed via orthodontic examination scores. Additional outcomes included self-reported learning interest and self-directed learning ability.ResultsStudents in the experimental group achieved significantly higher orthodontic examination scores than those in the control group (95.2 ± 1.7 vs. 66.2 ± 3.5, p < 0.001). The experimental group also reported significantly higher ratings for the appropriateness of course design (9.71 ± 1.27 vs. 7.29 ± 1.31, p < 0.001) and overall satisfaction (9.05 ± 0.80 vs. 6.76 ± 1.18, p < 0.001). In addition, 9 of 10 instructor members (90%) expressed willingness to adopt the structured case-based learning approach in future skills teaching, compared with 1 of 10 (10%) for the lecture-based learning.ConclusionsThe case-based learning was associated with improved academic performance, learning interest, and self-directed learning among undergraduate orthodontic students. Integrating complete clinical cases in a stepwise, longitudinal manner may provide a useful pedagogical approach for bridging theoretical knowledge with clinical practice in orthodontic education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09665-y.Keywords: Orthodontics, Undergraduate education, Case-based learning, Clinical reasoning, Teaching effectivenes

Introduction

Orthodontic treatment requires multidimensional diagnosis, individualized planning, and continuous clinical judgment [1, 2]. Clinicians integrate patient history, examinations, dental casts, and radiographs to guide treatment. Developing clinical reasoning that links theory with real-world decision-making is central to undergraduate orthodontic education, yet many students struggle to apply abstract concepts to complete cases, indicating limitations of conventional teaching approaches [3].

Lecture-based learning (LBL) remains the predominant instructional approach in most dental schools. This method provides a structured theoretical foundation but offers limited opportunities for students to engage with complete cases or to practice diagnostic and therapeutic reasoning aligned with authentic clinical workflows, particularly in disciplines that require integrative analysis of multiple clinical factors, such as orthodontics [4]. Teaching hours in orthodontics are often limited, and treatment typically spans several months, making it difficult for students to observe entire treatment processes [5]. Consequently, formal clinical rotations are frequently unavailable, restricting exposure to longitudinal clinical reasoning and decision-making [6].

To address this gap, case‑based learning (CBL) has been increasingly adopted in medical and dental education. CBL situates learning within authentic clinical scenarios, prompting students to analyze clinical data, justify diagnostic decisions, and formulate treatment plans. Evidence from dental education suggests that CBL is associated with improved theoretical understanding, enhanced clinical skills, and greater overall learning performance compared with traditional lecture‑based instruction, while also enhancing student satisfaction [7].

CBL is widely used in dental education to support the development of clinical reasoning through the use of real or simulated patient cases. However, conventional CBL approaches often focus on selected clinical scenarios or isolated decision points, which may limit students’ exposure to complete treatment processes. In orthodontic education, where patient management is inherently longitudinal and involves multiple stages of diagnosis and treatment, there is a need for more structured approaches that better reflect clinical workflows. This study therefore implemented a structured longitudinal CBL model based on complete case review to enhance the integration of theoretical knowledge with clinical application.

In orthodontic education, emerging evidence supports the value of case-oriented teaching approaches. A randomized controlled study comparing CBL and LBL in orthodontic case diagnosis reported that students in the CBL group achieved better diagnostic performance and higher satisfaction levels than those receiving traditional lectures [8]. In addition, a cross-sectional study of undergraduate orthodontic education found that students perceived case-based instruction as effective in improving diagnostic reasoning and treatment planning skills [9]. Studies in related dental domains also indicate that case-oriented teaching supports development of clinical reasoning and diagnostic competence [10].

Case review-based clinical teaching represents such a structured approach, emphasizing comprehensive review of complete records, stepwise diagnostic interpretation, and rationale behind treatment decisions, closely reflecting real clinical practice. Evidence for its effectiveness in undergraduate orthodontic education is limited. This study therefore aimed to evaluate a case review-based teaching model compared with LBL to determine its impact on students’ clinical reasoning and overall teaching quality.

Methods

Study participants

The study recruited all fifth-year undergraduate dental students from the 2019 cohort at the Air Force Medical University. Students were randomly assigned to either the LBL group or CBL group. All participants received instruction according to the standard orthodontic curriculum. Eligible students were full-time fifth-year undergraduates who provided written informed consent. Students who did not complete the required orthodontic coursework or withdrew consent were excluded from the study.

Orthodontic teaching instructors involved in the study were required to hold a valid Practicing Dentist Certificate in China, a professional title of attending physician or higher, and at least five years of independent clinical experience in orthodontics. Instructors were also required to have a minimum of three years of teaching experience in orthodontics, including theoretical lectures, laboratory instruction, or clinical supervision. All instructors participanted demonstrated adequate physical and mental health, had no significant speech or articulation impairments that could interfere with teaching, and had no record of professional ethics violations. Instructors were excluded if they had participated in major training programs in the preceding three months that could influence teaching methods, had conflicts of interest, were unable to commit the necessary time or effort for the study, or refused to provide written informed consent. A total of 10 instructors participated in the teaching intervention. The same instructors delivered both the experimental (CBL) and control (LBL) groups to minimize inter-instructor variability. The teaching intervention was conducted over one academic semester (approximately five months), covering the full orthodontics curriculum.

Teaching program

This study employed a controlled teaching design, randomly assigning undergraduate orthodontic students to either the LBL group or the CBL group to compare the effects of the two instructional approaches on students’ clinical reasoning and overall learning outcomes. Both groups followed the same curriculum and used Orthodontics (7th edition) as the core textbook [11], covering foundational theoretical knowledge (e.g., tooth movement biology, craniofacial growth) as well as clinical applications.

The LBL group followed structured, instructor-led PowerPoint lectures. Teaching content advanced from foundational theoretical concepts to clinical applications, offering a comprehensive overview of core orthodontic knowledge. Student participation focused on theoretical content and discrete clinical examples rather than full patient case analysis.

The CBL group implemented a structured, longitudinal case review model. Representative completed orthodontic cases were selected according to standardized treatment protocols, completeness of records, and availability of photographic and video documentation.

Teaching sessions presented the full treatment process of each case, including initial consultation, record-taking, auxiliary examinations, study model preparation, facial and intraoral documentation, diagnosis, treatment planning, fixed appliance procedures (bracket bonding, archwire placement, alignment and leveling, extraction space closure, finishing, and detailing), and retention. Each step was linked to corresponding theoretical knowledge, such as diagnostic analysis using aesthetic evaluation, cephalometric measurements, and model analysis, or biomechanical principles applied during tooth alignment and space closure. Retention strategies, including Hawley, vacuum-formed, and lingual retainers, were described with reference to clinical indications and fabrication procedures. Multimedia materials were used to support students’ observation and understanding of complete cases and clinical decision-making processes.

Assessment of academic performance, self-directed learning, and learning interest

At the end of the course, students completed a 40-minute closed-book examination, which comprised multiple-choice questions and two case analysis problems (total score 100 points) (Supplementary Material 1). Examinations were graded by instructors to evaluate both knowledge acquisition and clinical reasoning abilities. In addition, anonymous questionnaires were administered to students and instructors to assess satisfaction with the teaching approaches, enabling comparison between the LBL and CBL groups.

Students’ self-directed learning ability was measured using an 18-item questionnaire encompassing six dimensions: self-motivation, learning beliefs, goal-setting, self-monitoring, information acquisition, and collaborative learning. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with reverse scoring applied to negatively worded items. Subscale scores and total scores were calculated by summing or averaging relevant items for subsequent statistical analyses.

Orthodontics scores and basic course scores represented students’ previous academic performance and were obtained from institutional academic records using a 100-point scale. Students’ learning interest was assessed using a 12-item questionnaire covering four dimensions: interest in professional course content, interest in clinical practice and exploration, interest in self-directed learning and knowledge expansion, and recognition of professional value. All items were positively worded and rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Dimension and total scores were computed by summing or averaging item responses for further analysis.

Instructor satisfaction assessment

A structured questionnaire was administered to instructors to evaluate their perceptions of the two teaching methods. The questionnaire included five domains: (1) enhancement of students’ learning interest, (2) improvement of students’ theoretical knowledge, (3) reduction in lesson preparation time, (4) reduction in teaching material review workload, and (5) willingness to adopt the teaching method in future skills training. The first four domains were rated on a 10-point Likert scale, while the fifth domain was recorded as a binary response (yes/no). Instructors completed the questionnaire after the teaching intervention for both the experimental and control teaching methods.

To minimize potential confounding bias, the same group of instructors delivered both teaching interventions. Both groups followed an identical curriculum, used the same textbook, and were taught over the same time period according to a standardized teaching schedule. Therefore, the only systematic difference between the two groups was the instructional method.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent t-tests. Categorical variables were expressed as counts or percentages and compared using chi-square tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because the study included all eligible students within a fixed academic cohort. A post hoc sensitivity analysis indicated that, given the sample size (n = 21 per group), the study was capable of detecting large between-group differences (achieved power = 0.817 at α = 0.05), indicating sufficient sensitivity to detect large between-group differences.

Results

Baseline characteristics

All 42 students completed the study. The experimental group (n = 21) and the control group (n = 21) were comparable in terms of age, gender distribution, educational background, admission GPA, previous orthodontics and basic dental course performance, and English proficiency. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups at baseline (all P > 0.05), indicating good comparability prior to the teaching intervention (Table 1).

Learning outcomes following the teaching intervention

Following the teaching intervention, students in the experimental group achieved significantly higher orthodontic examination scores than those in the control group (95.2 ± 1.7 vs. 66.2 ± 3.5, P < 0.01, Fig. 1).

Satisfaction outcomes of students and instructors between the experimental and control groups

Questionnaires were distributed to undergraduate students in both the experimental group (n = 21) and the control group (n = 21), as well as to 10 instructors, with a 100% response rate in all groups.

Students in the experimental group reported significantly higher satisfaction scores than those in the control group across all evaluated domains (Table 2, P < 0.001). Similarly, instructors rated the experimental teaching method higher than the LBL in all domains, and instructors showed a significantly higher willingness to adopt the CBL group than the LBL group for future clinical teaching (90% vs. 10%, Table 3, P = 0.001). Overall, both students and instructors expressed greater satisfaction with the CBL approach compared with the LBL. Detailed distributions of satisfaction ratings are presented in Fig. 2.

Discussion

At the undergraduate level, orthodontic education often remains largely theoretical, with students relying on rote memorization to pass examinations. Opportunities to apply theoretical knowledge to clinical cases and to develop integrative clinical reasoning are limited. CBL may provide a framework to bridge this gap by facilitating longitudinal exposure to clinical cases and supporting the integration of theoretical and practical knowledge. Orthodontics involves complex diagnostic integration and individualized treatment planning, posing challenges for undergraduate learners when applying theoretical knowledge to comprehensive clinical cases [2, 12, 13].

The higher examination scores observed in the experimental group (95.2 ± 1.7 vs. 66.2 ± 3.5, p < 0.001), suggest that CBL may be associated with improved learning outcomes and clinical application ability compared with LBL. Student-reported outcomes were also more favorable in the experimental group, particularly regarding the appropriateness of course design, learning initiative, and clinical thinking skills. Additionally, 90% of instructors expressed support for implementing the CBL in future teaching, further highlighting its perceived value and feasibility, suggesting good acceptability and potential feasibility for implementation in orthodontic education.

From an educational perspective, the CBL differs from LBL in its longitudinal and interactive design. By facilitating repeated exposure to clinical cases and integrating theoretical knowledge across diagnostic and treatment stages, this approach may support conceptual integration and more structured learning processes. Consistent with constructivist learning theory, embedding knowledge within clinical contexts may shift learning from passive reception toward active cognitive processing [14, 15]. These features may enhance the organization of knowledge structures, improve contextual understanding, and increase perceived relevance of learning materials, which may in turn support learning motivation and self-directed learning behaviors [16].

In this study, the CBL simulated clinical workflows by guiding students through complete patient cases, linking each stage from consultation and record-taking to appliance therapy and retention with relevant theoretical knowledge. This hierarchical structure approximates real clinical workflows and provides repeated exposure to treatment processes spanning multiple years within a condensed learning period [9]. As an image-assisted learning approach, it may further facilitate understanding of treatment planning, tooth movement, and procedural details. While student engagement and contextual understanding may be enhanced, examination performance may also be influenced by multiple factors, including assessment alignment, instructional design, and student motivation. Therefore, the observed differences should be interpreted within the broader context of educational research rather than attributed solely to the instructional method.

Scaffolded CBL has been found to increase the number of correct clinical decisions made by dental students compared with LBL, indicating that CBL supports the development of integrated clinical reasoning competencies [17]. In addition, students participating in CBL methods have reported positive perceptions of the learning experience and found the approach valuable for improving diagnostic interpretation in radiology education [10]. In line with these reports, students in the present study’s experimental group demonstrated higher academic performance, learning interest, and self-directed learning ability than those in the control group, reinforcing the practical benefits of scaffolded case-based approaches in orthodontic and broader dental curricula.

Several limitations should be noted. First, CBL cannot replace hands-on skill training, as basic orthodontic procedures still require repeated practice in simulation or clinical sessions. Second, although the examination included both theoretical and case-based components, the assessment format may have partially aligned with the instructional characteristics of the experimental group, which may have contributed to the observed differences in examination performance. Third, preparing representative case materials is time-consuming, and clinical records may occasionally be incomplete. In addition, the use of two-dimensional imaging may omit important spatial information; three-dimensional technologies could further enhance learning outcomes. Additional limitations include the single-institution design, small sample size, reliance on self-reported questionnaires, and evaluation of short-term outcomes. Future studies should examine long-term effects on knowledge retention, clinical competence, and professional development.

The relatively large difference in examination scores should be interpreted with caution. It may reflect multiple contributing factors, including instructional design, assessment alignment, and student engagement. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as being solely attributable to the instructional method.

Conclusion

The CBL module was associated with improved academic performance, learning interest, and self-directed learning among undergraduate students in orthodontics. By presenting complete clinical treatment processes, it may help facilitate the connection between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice. These findings suggest that CBL may have potential educational value in orthodontic curricula.
