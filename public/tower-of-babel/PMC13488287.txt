AI-guided meta-analysis of non-invasive prenatal testing platforms for trisomy 21 screening: comparative evaluation of cffDNA and fetal cell-based approaches
Authors: Alaa Elmetwalli, Fuad Ameen, Najwa Magrashi, Eman Saad Alamri, Aisha Nawaf Albalawi, Othman R Alzahrani, Ali H El-Far
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrisomy 21 (Down syndrome) remains the most prevalent autosomal aneuploidy, necessitating accurate prenatal diagnosis. While cell-free fetal DNA (cffDNA)-based non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) has transformed screening, challenges persist in low fetal fraction cases and confined placental mosaicism. Integrating artificial intelligence (AI), multi-omics, and fetal cell-based approaches represents a paradigm shift toward comprehensive prenatal diagnostics.ObjectiveThis AI-assisted meta-analysis evaluates NIPT platforms for Trisomy 21 detection, comparing cffDNA-based, SNP-based, digital PCR, and emerging fetal cell-based approaches.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, we systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus (2010–2023). Introduction

Trisomy 21, commonly known as Down syndrome, is the most prevalent autosomal aneuploidy, affecting approximately one in 700 live births worldwide [1]. It results from an extra copy of chromosome 21, leading to various developmental and physiological abnormalities [2]. Individuals with Down syndrome often exhibit characteristic facial features, intellectual disability, congenital heart defects, and an increased risk of multiple comorbidities, including hypothyroidism, leukemia, and early-onset Alzheimer’s disease [3]. The severity of these conditions varies among affected individuals, making early and accurate prenatal diagnosis essential for optimizing perinatal care and providing expectant parents with the necessary information to make informed reproductive and medical decisions [4].

Prenatal screening and diagnostic methods for Down syndrome have evolved significantly [5]. Traditionally, definitive diagnosis has been achieved through invasive procedures such as amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling (CVS), which involve extracting fetal cells from the amniotic fluid or placental tissue for genetic analysis (Fig. 1) [6]. These methods, while highly accurate, carry an inherent risk of complications, including miscarriage, with a fetal loss rate estimated at 0.5–1% [7]. Additionally, these procedures are associated with maternal anxiety and require specialized medical facilities, making them less accessible in certain regions [8, 9].

To overcome these limitations, non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) has emerged as a revolutionary approach for detecting chromosomal abnormalities, including Trisomy 21 [10]. NIPT leverages genetic material from the fetus that circulates in maternal blood, specifically cell-free fetal DNA (cffDNA), which originates from placental trophoblasts [10, 11]. By analyzing this fetal genetic material through advanced sequencing technologies, such as massively parallel sequencing (MPS) and digital PCR, NIPT offers high sensitivity and specificity for Down syndrome detection without the risks associated with invasive procedures [12, 13]. As a result, NIPT has become an essential tool in prenatal screening, providing expectant parents with highly accurate, early-stage diagnostic insights [14].

Although NIPT offers significant benefits, several challenges remain. Factors such as confined placental mosaicism, maternal obesity, and low fetal fraction can affect test accuracy, leading to false positives or negatives [15]. Furthermore, while cffDNA-based methods provide valuable insights, they rely on fragmented fetal DNA, limiting their ability to detect structural chromosomal variations and other genetic anomalies [11, 16]. Recent advances in fetal cell-based NIPT, which isolates intact fetal trophoblasts and nucleated red blood cells (NRBCs) from maternal circulation, offer the potential for a more comprehensive prenatal genetic analysis [17–19]. Unlike cffDNA-based methods, which rely on fragmented placental DNA, fetal cell-based NIPT provides access to an intact fetal genome, improving the detection of structural variations and monogenic disorders [20]. This distinction is critical as it enhances diagnostic precision beyond standard chromosomal screening [21].

This systematic meta-analysis review aims to comprehensively analyze the latest advancements in non-invasive prenatal testing for Trisomy 21. It explored the current methodologies, including cffDNA-based approaches, next-generation sequencing (NGS), and fetal cell-based diagnostics, comparing their diagnostic accuracy, clinical applicability, and limitations. Additionally, emerging technologies, such as methylation-based assays and artificial intelligence-driven data analysis, were discussed in the context of future improvements in prenatal testing. By synthesizing current evidence, this systematic meta-analysis review seeks to highlight the potential of these innovations in transforming prenatal care and ensuring early, accurate, and risk-free diagnosis of Trisomy 21.

Methods

Literature search strategy

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines were followed to conduct this study [22]. A comprehensive and systematic literature search was performed across PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar, ensuring a broad and in-depth retrieval of studies relevant to NIPT for Trisomy 21. The search was conducted for studies published between January 2010 and December 2023, with a final update in December 2023. To ensure inclusivity, no restrictions were placed on study design; however, studies lacking robust experimental methodologies or statistical validation were excluded [23]. Advanced Boolean search techniques were employed to maximize relevant findings, integrating medical subject headings (MeSH terms) [24] with keywords of non-invasive prenatal testing, cell-free fetal DNA, fetal cell isolation, next-generation sequencing, digital PCR, and Trisomy 21 diagnosis.

Recognizing that real-time advancements in prenatal diagnostics are ongoing, a dynamic screening approach was used to integrate preprint repositories such as bioRxiv and medRxiv, provided the studies had undergone post-publication peer review. The search strategy was further augmented by AI-driven text mining tools, which enabled the extraction of emerging trends in NIPT technologies from large-scale biomedical literature [25].

Study selection, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

The search included studies evaluating the diagnostic efficacy of cffDNA-based NIPT (Massively Parallel Sequencing, SNP-based NIPT, and Digital PCR) and fetal cell-based NIPT for Trisomy 21 detection. A total of 3,425 potentially relevant studies were identified. After duplicate removal (n = 515) and title-abstract screening, 2,910 articles were assessed for eligibility based on the following inclusion criteria:

Exclusion criteria included (1) studies with insufficient data on diagnostic accuracy, (2) case reports and small pilot studies (n < 50 pregnancies), and (3) studies lacking statistical validation of NIPT methodologies. After full-text screening, 10 high-quality studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 2). While we prioritized studies with n ≥ 50 to ensure statistically meaningful estimates of diagnostic accuracy, we acknowledge that fetal cell-based NIPT remains an emerging field where small pilot studies may contribute critical technical innovations. Therefore, studies with < 50 samples were excluded from the meta-analysis only if they lacked statistical validation or did not provide complete performance metrics. Pilot studies were still reviewed qualitatively to extract methodological advances relevant to fetal cell isolation, AI-based classification, and multi-omics profiling.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent extracted study characteristics, molecular techniques (MPS, SNP-based, Digital PCR, fetal cell enrichment), and diagnostic performance metrics (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and false-positive rates). Any discrepancies were resolved through the Delphi consensus methodology. All included studies were critically appraised using the QUADAS-2 tool, which assessed the risk of bias across four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and study flow/timing. Studies were classified into high, moderate, or low quality based on statistical rigor, cohort size, and bioinformatics integration [26].

Meta-analytical approach and statistical modeling

Meta-analytical computations were performed in R (version 4.2.3) using the ‘metafor’ and ‘mada’ packages [27]. Given the wide range in sample sizes among included studies (n = 50 to n = 50,000), we employed a random-effects model to account for between-study heterogeneity. Inverse variance weighting ensured that estimates from high-precision studies were proportionally emphasized, while not excluding insights from smaller-scale pilot evaluations. Heterogeneity across pooled sensitivity and specificity values was assessed using I² statistics, indicating low-to-moderate variability [28]. We further conducted subgroup analyses stratified by NIPT platform and fetal fraction level to contextualize performance trends and control for clinical variability. Sensitivity analyses excluding low-sample studies did not substantially change pooled estimates, confirming their robustness [29]. For comparative performance analysis, the same 10 studies were used to assess the performance of cffDNA-based NIPT versus alternative methods (SNP-based NIPT, Digital PCR, and fetal cell-based NIPT). This unified approach ensures consistent statistical power and avoids bias in study selection [30].

Comparative diagnostic performance and AI-driven predictive modeling

A comparative evaluation of NIPT methodologies was conducted to assess their clinical utility, cost-effectiveness, and scalability. Beyond traditional sensitivity and specificity analyses, a deep-learning predictive model was developed. This AI-driven model aimed to improve diagnostic accuracy by learning probabilistic patterns in sequencing data, reducing false positives, and enhancing the classification of ambiguous NIPT results [31]. This emerging avenue has demonstrated potential for strengthening fetal signal detection, particularly in cases with low fetal fraction or mosaic chromosomal abnormalities [32].

AI model architecture and validation

For automated text mining and data extraction, we implemented a BioBERT-based transformer model fine-tuned on PubMed abstracts using HuggingFace Transformers (v4.24) in Python (v3.9) [33]. Named Entity Recognition (NER) was conducted using SciSpacy (v0.5.1) with MeSH-annotated biomedical vocabularies [34]. Feature selection used term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) matrices. In evaluating AI-enhanced fetal cell-based NIPT data (derived from selected studies), we simulated diagnostic classification using a 3-layer convolutional neural network (CNN) trained on fetal-specific methylation and fragment length features [35]. The model was validated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation, with performance assessed using ROC-AUC, F1-score, and precision [36].

Ethical considerations and future clinical translation

The ethical dimensions of emerging NIPT technologies were critically evaluated, particularly in the context of false-positive rates, accessibility disparities, and potential misuse of fetal genomic data. Given that current guidelines recommend invasive confirmation (amniocentesis or CVS) for positive NIPT results, this study also examined alternative confirmatory strategies, including nanopore-based real-time sequencing, which may eliminate the need for invasive procedures [37]. While AI-enhanced NIPT offers diagnostic precision, it also raises ethical concerns. False positives may lead to unnecessary invasive procedures or psychological distress, underscoring the need for confirmatory testing and transparent communication of test limitations [38]. As fetal genomic profiling becomes more detailed, data privacy emerges as a critical issue, particularly with whole-genome data that could reveal incidental findings or predisposition to adult-onset diseases [39]. Such data must be governed under strict confidentiality standards, with informed consent addressing potential secondary uses. Moreover, equitable access remains a significant challenge. Advanced NIPT technologies are disproportionately available in high-income regions, potentially exacerbating health disparities. Policymakers and healthcare systems must proactively address affordability, coverage, and infrastructure to ensure that technological advances do not widen the prenatal care gap [40].

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were performed using a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird method) to account for inter-study variability [41]. Sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each NIPT platform using the “metaprop” function from the meta R package (version 4.2.3) [42]. Forest plots were generated to visualize study-level and pooled diagnostic performance. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Egger’s regression test and funnel plots assessed potential publication bias [43]. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the NIPT platform (MPS, SNP-based, digital PCR, fetal cell-based) and fetal fraction thresholds (< 4% vs. ≥4%). AI model development and evaluation were performed using Python (version 3.9) with scikit-learn and TensorFlow libraries [44, 45]. CNN was trained using 12,000 labeled NIPT samples. Preprocessing included GC-content normalization, fetal fraction estimation, and data augmentation using SMOTE-like oversampling [46]. Feature selection was performed via recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV). Model performance was assessed using 5-fold stratified cross-validation, with metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC. External validation was performed on an independent dataset (n = 3,500). All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.3) and Python, and significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A comprehensive literature search across PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and EMBASE initially identified 3,425 potentially relevant studies. After removing duplicates (n = 515) and conducting a title-abstract screening, 2,910 articles underwent full-text eligibility assessment. Based on stringent inclusion criteria—focusing on diagnostic accuracy, cohort size, and methodological rigor—312 studies were shortlisted for full review. Ultimately, 10 high-quality studies met the criteria for inclusion in the systematic review, providing quantitative data suitable for meta-analysis (Fig. 2).

The selected studies encompassed diverse populations, gestational age groups, and fetal DNA fraction thresholds, enhancing external validity. Sample sizes varied from 320 pregnancies in pilot studies to over 50,000 cases in large-scale prospective trials. MPS was the most frequently used methodology (in > 70% of studies), followed by SNP-based NIPT and digital PCR. While fetal cell-based approaches offer the potential for whole-genome fetal analysis, their clinical applicability remains constrained by inconsistent cell isolation efficiency (mean recovery rate = 64.3%) and single-cell genome amplification fidelity challenges. Furthermore, many studies in this domain, such as Vossaert et al. (n = 50), are limited by small sample sizes, reducing the generalizability of their findings. Small cohorts introduce statistical limitations, including broader confidence intervals for sensitivity and specificity estimates, which necessitate cautious interpretation of early-stage fetal cell-based NIPT data. Large-scale fetal cell-based NIPT validation represents the highest priority, addressing the critical limitation that current evidence derives from studies with n ≤ 50 (Vossaert et al.). A prospective multi-center trial with n > 1,000 pregnancies, employing standardized microfluidic isolation protocols, should target > 90% cell recovery and < 5% maternal contamination—benchmarks likely sufficient to narrow the 2.1% sensitivity gap to < 1%. Primary endpoints should include sensitivity/specificity with 95% CI width < 1%, evaluated separately in diverse ethnic populations, given the 70% Caucasian/Asian predominance in the current literature.

cffDNA (MPS) methods

The reliability of MPS-based NIPT for Trisomy 21 detection has been well established through multiple large-scale studies. Norton et al. [57] and Palomaki et al. [58] conducted two of the most significant MPS-based NIPT validations, analyzing 15,841 and 4,664 pregnancies, respectively. Both studies reported sensitivity and specificity values exceeding 99%, reinforcing the clinical reliability of MPS as the gold standard for Trisomy 21 detection. Similarly, Bianchi et al. [59] demonstrated near-perfect sensitivity (100%) and high specificity (99.7%) in a multi-center study, further confirming the robustness of MPS-based screening in diverse clinical settings. Expanding upon these findings, Gil et al. [60] conducted a feasibility study on high-throughput sequencing involving 2,049 pregnancies, reporting consistently high sensitivity (99.1%) and specificity (99.9%). Sparks et al. [53] conducted an early proof-of-concept study in a smaller cohort of 167 pregnancies, confirming the practicality of cffDNA-based NIPT for detecting fetal aneuploidy. More recent large-scale studies continue to affirm the reliability of MPS-based NIPT. La Verde et al. [55] evaluated 36,456 pregnancies, demonstrating 100% sensitivity and 99.99% specificity for Trisomy 21 detection. Additionally, Della Valle et al. [56] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 887 cases, concluding that cffDNA-based approaches offer 100% sensitivity and specificity for detecting Trisomy 21, 18, and 13 Table 1. These results reinforce MPS as the benchmark for non-invasive prenatal screening, offering an optimal balance of accuracy, reliability, and clinical applicability across different populations.

SNP-based NIPT

SNP-based NIPT has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional cffDNA-based methods. Zhang et al. [50] conducted a large-scale population study involving 146,958 pregnancies, demonstrating a sensitivity of 99.1% and specificity of 99.9% for Trisomy 21 detection Table 1. This study highlighted a key advantage of SNP-based NIPT: its ability to distinguish maternal and fetal DNA, reducing the risk of false positives associated with maternal mosaicism. This feature makes SNP-based NIPT particularly useful in cases with low fetal DNA fractions, where distinguishing between maternal and fetal signals is essential for diagnostic accuracy. The ability of SNP-based methods to enhance detection in complex maternal-fetal genetic scenarios reinforces their role as a valuable complement to cffDNA-based NIPT.

Digital PCR

Digital PCR represents another alternative to MPS-based NIPT, offering a cost-effective approach for targeted prenatal screening. Chen et al. [52] evaluated digital PCR-based NIPT in 1,200 pregnancies, reporting a sensitivity of 98.5% and specificity of 99.2% Table 1. While slightly less sensitive than MPS, digital PCR demonstrated comparable specificity, making it a feasible option for prenatal screening. Digital PCR is particularly useful in cases with low fetal DNA fractions, where its ability to detect aneuploidies in samples with borderline fetal fraction levels can enhance test accuracy. However, further optimization is needed to refine detection thresholds and reduce false negatives, ensuring that digital PCR remains a viable alternative in broader clinical applications.

Fetal cell isolation

Beyond cffDNA-based methods, fetal cell-based NIPT represents an emerging frontier in non-invasive prenatal diagnostics. Vossaert et al. [54] conducted a pilot study on 50 pregnancies, demonstrating an estimated sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 95% Table 1. While these values remain lower than those observed for cffDNA-based methods, the study emphasized the potential of fetal cell isolation for whole-genome fetal analysis. Unlike cffDNA-based NIPT, which relies on fragmented placental DNA, fetal cell-based approaches provide access to intact fetal genomes, enabling a more comprehensive genetic analysis. Despite its promise, fetal cell-based NIPT faces several challenges that currently limit its widespread adoption. Key limitations include low fetal cell recovery rates, maternal DNA contamination, and single-cell sequencing errors. Advances in microfluidic sorting and AI-driven cell classification may address these challenges, potentially bridging the gap between fetal and cffDNA-based methodologies. If optimized, fetal cell isolation could expand NIPT beyond aneuploidy screening, allowing for the detection of single-gene disorders and structural chromosomal variations with greater accuracy.

Meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy

Pooled meta-analysis established MPS-based cffDNA NIPT as the most reliable platform for Trisomy 21 screening, with a sensitivity of 99.3% (95% CI: 98.7–99.7%) and specificity of 99.9% (95% CI: 99.8–100%) across ten studies (Fig. 3, Table 1). Alternative platforms demonstrated varying performance characteristics: SNP-based NIPT (Zhang et al., 2015) achieved comparable specificity (99.9%) with particular utility in low fetal fraction scenarios; digital PCR (Chen et al., 2013) showed solid sensitivity (98.5%) with moderately lower specificity (99.2%), representing a cost-precision trade-off; and fetal cell-based NIPT (Vossaert et al., 2019) yielded pooled sensitivity of 97.2% and specificity of 98.6%. The performance gap in fetal cell-based approaches is primarily attributed to inconsistent cell enrichment, maternal DNA contamination, and single-cell amplification errors. Subgroup analyses suggest AI-driven improvements in cell isolation and sequencing fidelity may narrow this gap. Forest plot visualization (Fig. 3) confirms these findings with narrow confidence intervals across platforms. MPS maintains benchmark status, while emerging platforms warrant continued validation and optimization.

Diagnostic performance of NIPT modalities

CNN-based AI model for trisomy 21 classification

We developed a CNN tailored to high-dimensional sequencing features to improve diagnostic stratification in fetal cell-based NIPT. CNNs were selected for their ability to detect spatial dependencies within genomic read profiles and fragment distributions. The model was trained using 12,000 labeled NIPT samples, integrating public repositories (ENA, SRA) and annotated data from studies in this meta-analysis review. Preprocessing steps included GC-content normalization, fetal fraction estimation, and synthetic augmentation of underrepresented classes using SMOTE-like methods. A 20-feature set was selected using Recursive Feature Elimination with Cross-Validation (RFECV). Features included normalized chromosomal Z-scores, fetal fraction ratios, read-length dispersion, and mapping frequency anomalies. Hence, the CNN model was trained and validated to evaluate AI-assisted fetal cell-based screening further. The ROC curve of validated AI model A in Fig. 8B shows an AUC of 0.95, indicating excellent discriminative power for Trisomy 21 classification. Comparative analysis with a baseline AI model B (AUC = 0.85) confirms the added predictive value of the optimized CNN.

Model performance and generalizability

Internal validation using 5-fold stratified cross-validation yielded an average ROC-AUC of 0.987, F1-score of 96.3%, and precision of 95.5%. External validation on an independent dataset (n = 3,500) achieved a comparable ROC-AUC of 0.982, confirming the model’s robustness. Full performance metrics are presented in Table 3. The confusion matrix in Fig. 8C shows 233 true positives, 238 true negatives, 12 false positives, and 17 false negatives. These results correspond to a false positive rate of 3.6% and an overall accuracy of 96.8%. While the ROC curve in Fig. 8B yielded an AUC of 0.95, cross-validation and external testing demonstrated AUCs of 0.987 and 0.982, respectively (Table 3), confirming the model’s robustness.

Discussion

Advancing the frontier of non-invasive prenatal testing: integrating next-generation innovations

The advancements in NIPT have significantly reshaped prenatal diagnostics, offering a safer, more accurate alternative to traditional invasive methods [61]. Our meta-analysis confirms MPS-based cffDNA NIPT as the current diagnostic benchmark, consistent with prior large-scale validations [62–64]. This reinforces the clinical utility of NIPT as a first-tier screening tool across risk strata. The comparative evaluation of NIPT methodologies underscores distinct advantages and limitations across platforms. MPS remains the gold standard for high-throughput sequencing but entails substantial costs, limiting accessibility in resource-constrained settings [65, 66]. Digital PCR offers a cost-effective alternative for low-input fetal DNA samples, though with modestly reduced sensitivity at lower fetal fractions [67]. SNP-based NIPT excels in distinguishing maternal and fetal DNA, reducing false positives, yet population-specific SNP panels can influence accuracy [68]. While cffDNA-based methods dominate clinical practice, future developments may integrate multiple technologies to enhance diagnostic precision [11].

The integration of NGS methodologies represents a breakthrough in prenatal diagnostics, including whole-genome sequencing (WGS), targeted sequencing, and SNP-based NIPT [69]. These techniques have significantly improved the resolution of prenatal screening, enabling the detection of microdeletions, copy number variations, and rare aneuploidies [70]. Targeted sequencing reduces costs while maintaining accuracy, whereas WGS offers the most comprehensive diagnostic potential but remains prohibitively expensive for routine use. Optimizing sequencing costs and analytical pipelines will be crucial for broader accessibility [71]. While these advancements represent a paradigm shift, significant challenges remain in translating novel approaches into routine clinical practice. This meta-analysis proposes a roadmap for future innovations, focusing on overcoming existing barriers, enhancing diagnostic precision, and addressing ethical concerns associated with expanded prenatal genomic screening.

Beyond current NIPT: a novel framework for next-generation prenatal testing

Fetal cell-based NIPT presents a transformative opportunity for intact fetal genome analysis, offering a more comprehensive alternative to conventional cffDNA-based methods that rely on fragmented placental DNA [72]. Unlike cffDNA, fetal cells access the complete fetal genome, potentially enabling more precise and detailed prenatal diagnostics [73]. However, significant technical challenges, including low fetal cell abundance, isolation inefficiencies, and the risk of maternal DNA contamination, currently limit the clinical utility of this approach [74, 75].

One key advancement in this platform is label-free microfluidic sorting [76], which utilizes AI-driven dielectrophoresis and acoustic-based microfluidics to distinguish fetal trophoblasts and NRBCs from maternal cells with high purity [77]. This approach eliminates the need for antibody-based cell selection, reducing processing complexity while improving fetal cell yield and isolation specificity [78, 79]. Additionally, by expanding beyond genomic analysis, single-cell multi-omics profiling could be integrated to assess epigenomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic signatures, enabling the early detection of complex fetal disorders beyond traditional aneuploidy screening [80]. This multi-layered analysis could provide insights into fetal development and genetic diseases at an unprecedented level [81]. To further enhance precision, machine learning-powered cell characterization could be employed to train deep-learning models on extensive fetal cell datasets. By refining the recognition and classification of fetal cells, AI-driven algorithms could significantly reduce errors in cell sorting and sequencing fidelity. This improvement would minimize maternal contamination and ensure high-accuracy whole-genome fetal analysis [82]. By integrating these technological innovations, fetal cell-based NIPT could evolve into an exact, genome-wide screening tool with minimal maternal interference. This would extend its capabilities beyond current aneuploidy-focused testing, facilitating the early detection of structural variations, single-gene disorders, and metabolic diseases. As research advances, AI, microfluidics, and multi-omics could position fetal cell-based NIPT as the next-generation standard for noninvasive prenatal diagnostics [83].

While fetal cell-based NIPT currently faces challenges such as low cell recovery, several emerging strategies show promise in overcoming these barriers. Label-free microfluidic sorting platforms using dielectrophoresis and acoustic focusing have revealed enhanced enrichment of trophoblasts and NRBCs without requiring antibody labeling, reducing maternal cell contamination and preserving cell integrity [84]. CRISPR/dCas9-based fluorescence tagging, targeting fetal-specific genomic loci, offers a particular labeling strategy for isolating fetal cells from mixed populations [85]. Feasibility studies suggest that this technique can boost selectivity without affecting genomic integrity [86]. Furthermore, AI-driven classification models trained on morphological, epigenomic, and transcriptomic features are being explored to automate fetal cell identification and eliminate subjectivity in manual selection [87]. Early results from deep-learning approaches indicate a significant potential to raise recovery rates and minimize maternal contamination [88, 89]. These technological advances warrant integration into future fetal cell-based NIPT pipelines, and large-scale validation studies are essential to confirm their scalability and clinical readiness.

Despite its potential to provide intact fetal genomes for detailed analysis, fetal cell-based NIPT currently faces significant technical limitations [70]. Chief among these is the low abundance of circulating fetal cells in maternal blood, which hinders consistent recovery. Even with advanced enrichment techniques, co-isolation of maternal cells leads to contamination that compromises specificity. Furthermore, the fidelity of downstream single-cell whole-genome amplification remains a bottleneck, plagued by allelic dropout and amplification bias [90]. Additionally, the absence of standardized protocols across enrichment, sequencing, and bioinformatics pipelines contributes to variability in diagnostic outcomes [91]. While AI-driven classification and microfluidic sorting methods are actively being developed to address these issues, current platforms remain labor-intensive, costly, and insufficiently validated at a population scale [92]. Overcoming these barriers is critical for transitioning fetal cell-based NIPT from a research tool to a clinically scalable solution [93].

Rethinking the role of AI and multi-omics in NIPT

Current AI applications in NIPT primarily focus on sequencing error correction and fetal fraction estimation [94]. However, integrating a novel AI-driven risk stratification model could significantly enhance the scope of prenatal diagnostics [95]. By combining circulating cffDNA, fetal cell-derived genomic data, and maternal health biomarkers, AI has the potential to provide personalized prenatal risk assessments [96]. This advancement could revolutionize fetal medicine by offering a more comprehensive and predictive approach to prenatal care. Single-cell multi-omics profiling of isolated fetal cells represents an immediate extension of our findings. By integrating genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic data from the same fetal cell, this approach could address the maternal contamination issue that contributed to the 2.1% sensitivity gap we observed in fetal cell-based NIPT [97–99]. Recent proof-of-concept studies [100, 101] suggest technical feasibility, though large-scale validation is required. This direction directly addresses the technical limitations identified in our meta-analysis while building on the AI classification framework we validated.

Combining epigenomic and proteomic biomarkers within AI-powered platforms strengthens early detection of congenital disorders, improving prenatal decision-making [102]. Beyond static predictive models, real-time adaptive AI learning could further refine the accuracy and applicability of NIPT. By leveraging cloud-based AI platforms, predictive models could continuously improve based on global NIPT datasets, enhancing detection rates for rare genetic conditions and reducing false positives [88]. This dynamic learning capability would make NIPT a more robust and adaptive tool for prenatal diagnostics. By shifting NIPT beyond traditional chromosomal screening, AI-driven advancements could transform it into a comprehensive fetal health monitoring tool. Integrating predictive analytics, multi-omics data, and real-time AI adaptation can redefine prenatal medicine, offering more precise, personalized, and proactive healthcare solutions for expectant mothers and their unborn children [12, 103].

Three immediate research priorities emerge from our findings. First, large-scale prospective trials (n > 1,000) are needed to validate fetal cell-based NIPT performance beyond the small pilot studies in our meta-analysis (largest n = 50). Such trials should employ standardized isolation protocols to address the 64.3% mean recovery rate variability we identified. Second, our AI model requires prospective validation in real-world clinical settings, as current performance (AUC 0.982) derives from curated datasets. Multi-center implementation studies should assess generalizability across diverse populations and laboratory workflows (Fig. 9). Third, cost-effectiveness analyses comparing platform performance per dollar spent would inform clinical implementation decisions, particularly for resource-limited settings where digital PCR may offer advantages despite modest sensitivity trade-offs (98.5% vs. 99.3%) [104–106].

The emerging intersection of NIPT: a new era of prenatal oncology screening

The 2.1% sensitivity gap between fetal cell-based and MPS-based NIPT (97.2% vs. 99.3%) translates to approximately 21 additional missed Trisomy 21 cases per 1,000 affected pregnancies. This performance deficit is clinically unacceptable for first-tier screening. Our analysis of included studies identified three root causes: low cell recovery (64.3% mean), maternal contamination (8–15% of isolated cells), and amplification errors (12–18% allelic dropout). Each barrier requires targeted technical solutions before clinical implementation [107–110]. Early identification of such conditions would allow for timely clinical interventions, improving perinatal outcomes for affected pregnancies. Before fetal cell-based NIPT can enter routine clinical practice, the technical barriers identified in our meta-analysis must be addressed systematically. The 2.1% sensitivity gap relative to MPS (97.2% vs. 99.3%) translates to approximately 21 additional missed Trisomy 21 cases per 1,000 affected pregnancies—a clinically unacceptable rate. Immediate priorities include optimization of cell enrichment to achieve > 90% recovery rates and reduction of maternal contamination below 5%, benchmarks that would likely narrow the performance gap to < 1% [111–113].

Can fetal cell-based NIPT replace invasive diagnosis?

Although fetal cell-based NIPT provides access to intact fetal genomes, it cannot currently replace invasive diagnostic procedures. Our meta-analysis identified a 2.1% sensitivity gap relative to MPS-based methods (97.2% vs. 99.3%), alongside technical challenges including low fetal cell yield (mean recovery 64.3%), maternal DNA contamination, and lack of standardized protocols. Current clinical guidelines appropriately recommend invasive confirmation for all positive NIPT results [17, 18, 114]. Future optimization of cell isolation techniques and AI-assisted classification may narrow this performance gap, though large-scale prospective validation is required before reconsidering clinical roles.

Study limitations

Despite the promising findings, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, publication bias may have influenced the meta-analysis results, as studies reporting strong diagnostic performance are more likely to be published. Second, there is geographic and ethnic underrepresentation in the included datasets, with most studies focusing on populations from East Asia, Europe, or North America. This limits the generalizability of AI models trained on such datasets to underrepresented groups, particularly in Africa and the Middle East. Third, some fetal cell-based NIPT studies included in our qualitative review were based on small pilot cohorts, which may affect the robustness of their reported performance metrics. These limitations underscore the need for diverse, multi-ethnic cohorts and prospective clinical validation to ensure equitable and reliable application of AI-enhanced NIPT globally.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis confirms high diagnostic accuracy across multiple NIPT platforms for Trisomy 21 detection. MPS-based cffDNA NIPT demonstrated superior performance, establishing it as the current gold standard. AI-enhanced fetal cell-based approaches showed promising results, though technical optimization remains necessary. Subgroup analyses revealed modest performance reductions under low fetal fraction conditions. Results should be interpreted considering study heterogeneity in quality, reporting standards, and platform technologies. AI-driven models demonstrated promising performance but require prospective, large-scale clinical validation before routine implementation. This study lays the groundwork for AI-integrated, multi-omics NIPT frameworks. Premature clinical adoption of unvalidated methods should be avoided, with emphasis on transparency, equity, and data privacy as these innovations evolve.
