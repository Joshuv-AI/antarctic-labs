CAR T‐cell expansion after idecabtagene vicleucel is a predictor of outcomes in multiple myeloma
Authors: Benoît Ferment, Amani Ouedrani, Alexis Talbot, Naouel Mouri, Pauline Zarnitzky, Romain Stammler, Tristan Vaugeois, Antoine Diep, Stephanie Harel, Bruno Royer, Floriane Theves, Nathalie Forgeard, Vincent Allain, Sophie Caillat‐Zucman, Bertrand Arnulf
Published: 2026

Idecabtagene vicleucel (ide‐cel), a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy targeting B‐cell maturation antigen (BCMA), has demonstrated significant efficacy in patients with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM). 1 Ide‐cel has been commercially available since 2021 in France, where it remains the only anti‐BCMA CAR T‐cell product available to date, 2 and its clinical utility should be maintained due to a favorable safety profile. In patients with large B‐cell lymphoma, baseline factors such as high tumor burden 3 and an altered immune landscape, including elevated monocyte counts and impaired T‐cell fitness, negatively affect the efficacy of anti‐CD19 CAR T‐cells.4, 5, 6 Conversely, a higher proportion of early memory T cells in the apheresis or infusion product, and robust CAR T‐cell expansion post‐infusion are associated with improved clinical outcomes. 7 However, in RRMM, the influence of immune‐related parameters on ide‐cel efficacy has been less studied.8, 9, 10 In this real‐world study, we aimed to identify potential immune biomarkers associated with clinical response and progression‐free survival (PFS) in patients treated with ide‐cel.

We retrospectively analyzed all consecutive patients with RRMM who received commercial ide‐cel between October 2021 and December 2024 in Saint‐Louis hospital. Response to treatment was assessed at 6 months post‐infusion according to IMWG criteria. 11 Ongoing response (OR) was defined as achieving at least a partial response or better. Treatment failure (TF) was defined as stable disease, progressive disease, or death. The study was approved by the local ethics committee, and all patients provided written informed consent.

Baseline complete blood count and immune profiling were performed at the time of apheresis. Lymphocytes and T‐cell subsets (naïve, memory, CD4, CD8) were quantified by multiparameter flow cytometry. The infused CAR T‐cell dose was extracted from the manufacturer's release certificate.

CAR T‐cell kinetics were evaluated by flow cytometry using the BCMA CAR detection reagent (Miltenyi Biotec) on infusion bag residuals and peripheral blood samples collected on Days 4, 7, 10, 14, 21, and 28 (±2 days), as per national guidelines. 12 The peak CAR T‐cell count (Cmax, cells/µL), time to peak within 28 days (Tmax) and time to last detection of CAR T‐cells (Tlast) were defined.

Serum soluble BCMA (sBCMA) was measured on Day 0 and Day 7 (±1) using the Ella Simple Plex immunoassay (Bio‐Techne). Functional effector‐to‐target (E:T) ratio was defined as Cmax normalized by baseline sBCMA levels, as described. 13

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0). Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test and categorical variables using the chi‐square test. PFS was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier estimates with comparison between groups based on log‐rank tests, and Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify predictors of PFS. For uni‐ and multivariable analyses, missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), with five imputations performed. Variables with wide distributions were log2‐transformed before analysis to facilitate interpretation as the effect of doubling. Univariable analyses were conducted for each variable, and a multivariable model was constructed including variables with a univariable P‐value < 0.20. Due to collinearity, ratios and parameters included in the previously validated composite MyCARe score 14 were excluded in the multivariable model. All tests were two‐sided, with P‐values < 0.05 denoting statistical significance.

A total of 90 patients were included in the final analysis. Their main clinical and biological characteristics are shown in Table 1. All patients had an evaluable response at 6 months, of whom 32 (36%) experienced TF, and 58 (64%) showed OR (Figure S1). The median follow‐up time was 12 months. The median PFS was 13.1 months (Figure S2).

At apheresis, no significant differences were observed between the TF and OR groups in monocyte counts (median [range], TF: 490/μL [100–1220]; OR: 460/μL [140–3450], P = 0.30) and T‐cell counts (TF: 770/μL [141–2068]; OR: 891/μL [306–2576], P = 0.31). The proportions of regulatory T cells (Tregs), and of naïve, memory, and effector CD4 and CD8 T‐cell subsets were also similar in both groups (Figure S3A,B).

Analysis of the infusion product revealed no difference in the total number of infused CAR T‐cells (TF: 436 × 106 [348–485]; OR: 446 × 106 [339–500], P = 0.24), the proportion of CAR T‐cells among T‐cells (TF: 63% [26–78]; OR: 58% [26–86], P = 0.73), or the distribution of CD4 and CD8 subsets among CAR T‐cells. CD4 CAR T‐cells were predominant in both groups (CAR CD4:CD8 ratio, TF: 4.27 [0.66–12.13]; OR: 6.11 [0.97–27.54], P = 0.24).

Following infusion, peak CAR T‐cell expansion occurred at a median of 9 days in both groups (Tmax, TF: 9 [5–16] days; OR: 9 [6–17] days, P = 0.91). However, responder patients exhibited significantly higher peak CAR T‐cell values (Cmax, TF: 46 CAR T/μL [2–1416]; OR: 398 CAR T/μL [7–3078], P < 0.001) (Figure 1A), with no difference in the CAR CD4:CD8 ratio (TF: 0.24 [0.05–2.13]; OR: 0.26 [0.01–3.34], P = 0.44). Additionally, CAR T‐cells persisted longer in responders (Tlast, TF: 21 days [7–89]; OR: 28 days [8–248], P = 0.01) (Figure 1B). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis identified a Cmax threshold of 100 CAR T/μL to predict TF, with a sensitivity of 66% and specificity of 86%. Patients with Cmax above this threshold had significantly improved PFS compared to those with lower peak expansion (median PFS: 18.9 vs. 3.3 months; P < 0.0001, log‐rank test) (Figure 1C and Table S1). Consistently, this threshold was also associated with deeper clinical responses at Months 3 and 6, as well as a higher rate of best overall response (Figure S4).

Baseline sBCMA levels were not significantly different between groups (Day 0, TF: 90.7 μg/L [0.9–1813]; OR: 66.2 μg/L [5.8–1433], P = 0.66). However, a greater reduction in sBCMA levels during the first week after infusion was observed in OR patients, resulting in a higher Day 0 to Day 7 sBCMA ratio (TF: 1.38 [0.79–5.11]; OR: 3.24 [1.28–11.03], P < 0.001) (Figure 1D). The effector‐to‐target (E:T) ratio was significantly higher in responder patients (TF: 0.44 [0.00–21.82]; OR: 6.79 [0.09–63.80], P = 0.01), highlighting more efficient CAR T‐cell expansion relative to disease load. Notably, Cmax and D0:D7 sBCMA ratio were highly correlated (r = 0.73, P < 0.001, Spearman test) (Figure 1E).

In multivariable Cox regression analysis (Figure 1F), Cmax ≥ 100 CAR T/µL was significantly associated with longer PFS (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] = 0.18; 95% CI: 0.08–0.38; P < 0.001). Conversely, a higher MyCARe score remained a strong independent predictor of shorter PFS (adjusted HR for intermediate/high vs. low risk = 2.57; 95% CI: 1.20–5.51; P = 0.02). Higher sBCMA at D7, assessed as log2(sBCMA), was not significantly associated with altered PFS (adjusted HR = 1.16; 95% CI: 0.97–1.38; P = 0.11).

To evaluate the combined impact of CAR T‐cell expansion and MyCARe risk, patients were stratified into four subgroups (Figure 1G). In the Cmax high group, PFS was similar between MyCARe low and intermediate/high risk patients. Conversely, patients with both low expansion and intermediate/high MyCARe risk exhibited the shortest PFS. Even in the MyCARe low‐risk group, low expansion was associated with reduced PFS compared to high expansion.

Finally, we found that expansion above the threshold was associated with a higher incidence of Grade ≥2 cytokine release syndrome (P = 0.02) (Figure S5). No significant association was observed with any‐grade immune effector cell‐associated syndrome.

This real‐world study investigated immunological correlates of response in 90 patients with RRMM treated with ide‐cel. The key finding was that patients achieving an OR displayed significantly higher CAR T‐cell expansion, and this expansion was independently associated with prolonged PFS. Notably, the CD4:CD8 ratio inverted between the infusion product and peak expansion in both groups, suggesting that in vivo proliferation is predominantly driven by CD8 CAR T‐cells, in contrast to cilta‐cel. 9 A peak expansion above a threshold of 100 CAR T‐cells/μL emerged as a strong predictor of PFS, consistent with prior findings by Caillot et al. 8 in a smaller cohort of ide‐cel–treated RRMM patients. Similarly, Mejia Saldarriaga et al. 15 demonstrated that absolute lymphocyte count peaks around Day 11 after BCMA CAR T therapy correlated with clinical outcomes and may serve as an indirect marker of CAR T‐cell expansion. Together, these results highlight the value of cellular kinetics as a real‐time biomarker of therapeutic efficacy and support the rationale for early CAR T‐cell monitoring to identify patients who may benefit from adjunctive interventions, such as immunomodulatory drugs, 16 immune checkpoint inhibitors or interleukin agonists 17 in cases of suboptimal expansion.

We did not observe significant differences in monocyte counts or T‐cell subset profiles at the time of apheresis between patients with TF or OR. This contrasts with findings in large B‐cell lymphoma, where baseline monocyte levels and T‐cell fitness influence CAR T‐cell efficacy. 6 Whether the increased expansion we observe in responders reflects intrinsic T‐cell characteristics remains to be determined.

Likewise, baseline or early post‐infusion sBCMA levels did not differ between groups, in contrast to prior observation showing that tumor burden, measured by sBCMA and metabolic tumor volume, is a major determinant of CAR T‐cell efficacy. 18 In our cohort, a higher E:T ratio was strongly associated with better outcomes at 6 months. Importantly, the decline in sBCMA levels during the first week post‑infusion was more pronounced in patients who achieved sustained response at 6 months and correlated with CAR T‐cell expansion, potentially indicating more effective early tumor clearance driven by greater expansion.

The recent MyCARe study identified multiple independent baseline disease characteristics that were predictors of relapse following BCMA‐directed CAR T therapy. 14 In our cohort, both greater CAR T‐cell expansion and a low MyCARe risk score were independently associated with longer PFS, further validating the MyCARe model while emphasizing the additional prognostic value of early CAR T‐cell kinetics. Importantly, our subgroup analysis revealed a modulating effect of expansion on baseline MyCARe risk: robust expansion appeared to neutralize the adverse prognosis of a high‐risk profile, with PFS being comparable in the high expansion group regardless of the MyCARe score.

Collectively, these findings underscore the value of monitoring ide‐cel expansion as an early indicator of TF risk. They suggest that, while baseline MyCARe risk is a key determinant, it is not inevitable, since the quality of expansion acts as a major modulator of clinical outcome. Importantly, this observation appears to extend to other BCMA‐targeted CAR T‐cell products, such as ciltacabtagene autoleucel, not yet available in France. 9 , 13 Furthermore, the potential benefit of adjuvant therapies aimed at enhancing CAR T‐cell expansion warrants investigation, as such strategies may rescue patients with unfavorable baseline characteristics and improve overall outcomes.
