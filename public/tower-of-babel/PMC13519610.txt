Biomarker changes in cerebral adrenoleukodystrophy after gene therapy or allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplant
Authors: Troy C Lund, Stephan Kemp, Parmida Alikhani, Annika Willmert, Jennifer Braun, Ashish Gupta, Dan J Loes, David R Nascene, Paul J Orchard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral adrenoleukodystrophy (CALD) is a progressive immune-inflammatory neurologic disease that can be arrested with autologous hematopoietic cell transplant-based gene therapy (GT) or allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (Allo-HCT). We compared transplant types by evaluating changes in two biomarkers, C26:0-lysophosphatidylcholine (C26:0-lysoPC) and neurofilament light chain (NfL), before and 12 months after therapy. Disease severity was quantified by Loes score on MRI. Baseline C26:0-lysoPC was 489 nmol/L for GT recipients and 487 nmol/L for Allo-HCT recipients; it decreased by 6.7% after GT and 48.4% after Allo-HCT (p < 0.0001). Baseline NfL was 17.3 for GT recipients and 35.9 pg/mL for Allo-HCT recipients; it increased by 143% after GT and decreased by 43% after Allo-HCT (p = 0.0006). Loes score progression was greater with GT (+3.0 vs. +1.0 points, p = 0.0134). Multivariate analyses to understand which pre-HCT variable was best associated with disease progression indicated that modality of transplant, GT, was the only significant factor (p = 0.0302).Keywords: adrenoleukodystrophy, biomarkers, transplant, gene therapy, neurofilament light chain, lysophosphatidylcholine

Introduction

Disruption of the gene ABCD1 leads to the accumulation of very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs), including C26:0-lysophosphatidylcholine (C26:0-lysoPC), due to a lack of functional transport from the cytoplasm to the peroxisome for their degradation. This results in the X-linked disorder adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD, OMIM #300100). It is characterized by a high rate of adrenal insufficiency (AI) by age 18. Additionally, 30%–40% of boys develop cerebral adrenoleukodystrophy (CALD), a progressive inflammatory demyelinating brain disorder marked by gadolinium-enhancing lesions on MRI. AI is treated through lifelong replacement of glucocorticoid, and sometimes mineralocorticoid, hormones. In boys, CALD can be arrested with autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplant-based gene therapy (GT) or allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (Allo-HCT).1,2

While both GT and Allo-HCT can arrest active CALD, the products differ in cellular composition, which may lead to distinct biochemical changes following treatment. GT involves autologous cell isolation with lentiviral-mediated gene addition, whereas Allo-HCT involves isolation of normal hematopoietic cells from a healthy “wild-type” donor.

To understand changes in biomarkers after each type of transplant, we compared the 12-month (12M) changes in two groups of boys with active CALD undergoing either GT or Allo-HCT. The GT group was composed of 21 individuals with a median age of 6.9 years (4.9–13.1) at transplant. The Allo-HCT group was composed of 35 individuals with a median age of 7.9 years (4–13.4) at transplant. The amount of cerebral disease in CALD can be quantified by a scoring system developed by Loes et al. with assigned for neuroanatomical structure affected by disease.3 The median Loes score pre-transplant was 2.0 in the GT group and 4.0 in the Allo-HCT group.

Results

C26:0-lysoPC is a validated VLCFA biomarker used in newborn screening for ALD. It may have some predictive value for ALD-related phenotypes, such as AI and CALD.4,5,6 The median baseline C26:0-lysoPC was 489 nmol/L (280–664 nmol/L) for the GT group and showed no correlation with pre-transplant Loes score (p = 0.7275; Figures 1A and 1B). The 6-month (6M) and 12-month (12M) C26:0-lysoPC values were not significantly different from those before transplant (p = 0.0921 and 0.2811, respectively; Figure 1A).

The median baseline C26:0-lysoPC for the Allo-HCT group was 487 nmol/L (309–759 nmol/L) and showed no correlation with pre-transplant Loes score (p = 0.9335; Figures 1C and 1D). The 6M and 12M C26:0-lysoPC values were significantly lower than those before transplant (p = 0.0040 and p < 0.0001, respectively; Figure 1C).

Individual trajectories in C26:0-lysoPC levels revealed a clinically meaningful divergence between the two treatment groups. GT patients showed little to no reduction at 6M (mean increase by 31%) and mean decrease by only 6.7% at 12M (Figures 1E and 1G). In contrast, Allo-HCT patients showed a progressive mean decrease in C26:0-lysoPC levels: 29.6% at 6M and 48.4% at 12M after transplant (Figures 1F and 1G). These differences were statistically significant at both time points (p = 0.0031 and 0.0029, respectively; Figure 1G).

Neurofilament light chain (NfL) is a brain-derived plasma biomarker associated with neuronal injury.7 It correlates with the presence and extent of CNS disease in CALD and may serve as an early biomarker of cerebral involvement.8,9 The median baseline NfL was 17.3 pg/mL for the GT group and showed a significant correlation with pre-transplant Loes score (p = 0.0007; Figures 1H and 1I). The median pre-transplant NfL was 35.9 pg/mL for the Allo-HCT group, and it also showed a significant correlation with the pre-transplant Loes score (p = 0.0001; Figures 1J and 1K). There was no change in NfL absolute values from pre-HCT to the 6M or 12M time points in the GT group (p = 0.2733 and 0.1847, respectively; Figure 1H). NfL decreased significantly from pre-Allo-HCT to the 12M time point (p = 0.0022; Figure 1J). Individual changes in NfL are shown in Figures 1L and 1M. On an individualized basis, NfL at the 12M time point was increased by 154% (median) in the GT group from pre-transplant and decreased by 43% (median) from pre-transplant in the Allo-HCT group (p = 0.0358; Figure 1N). Across both biomarkers, responses after Allo-HCT were more consistent and directionally uniform, whereas responses after GT were more heterogeneous.

After transplant, CNS disease progression, as measured by Loes score, is usually greatest in the first 12 months.10 For the GT group, the baseline median Loes score was 2.0 (1.0–8.0; Figure 2A). For the Allo-HCT group, the baseline median Loes score was 4.0 (1.0–17.0; Figure 2B). The change in Loes score from pre-transplant to 12M time point (12M delta Loes) reflects disease progression. Progression was greater in the GT group than in the Allo-HCT group despite higher baseline Loes scores in the Allo-HCT group (p = 0.0327; Figure 2C), indicating that the observed differences were unlikely to be driven by baseline disease severity. To assess factors associated with 12M delta Loes, we performed multivariate analysis using pre-transplant biomarkers, disease severity, and treatment type and identified transplant modality as the most significant factor (p = 0.0302; Figure 2D).

Discussion

In this single-institution study of boys with CALD, we describe the disease progression and dynamic changes in key ALD-related biomarkers after GT or Allo-HCT. MRI findings in ALD are often thought to lag behind the ALD-related cellular processes causing demyelination. Therefore, for any patient going through either type of transplant, the goal is efficient and timely disease arrest. MRI progression is often observed before disease stabilization, with the greatest changes occurring within the first 12 months of transplant.

In the cohort we assessed, MRI progression was greater in the GT group than in the Allo-HCT group, despite higher baseline Loes scores in the Allo-HCT group, which, historically, can predict greater progression.1,10 While NfL can be linked to disease progression, C26:0-lysoPC is not. The GT group showed less efficient normalization of C26:0-lysoPC and NfL biomarkers, suggesting that Allo-HCT had better metabolic correction. Despite the unequal metabolic correction, published data indicate that many boys treated with GT ultimately achieve disease arrest.2

Differences in disease and biomarker changes may reflect underlying biological mechanisms at work. A leading hypothesis is that the gene correction of hematopoietic stem cells in the transplanted GT product and resulting myeloid progeny is less than that in Allo-HCT. The published data show that at 12 months after GT, 10%–20% of CD14+ myeloid cells express the ABCD1-encoded protein, ALDP, while after Allo-HCT, 100% of cells express ALDP (assuming a normal wild-type donor).2 This disparity in wild-type ALDP expression may explain some of the observed differences we found in biomarkers.

There remains controversy regarding the mechanism by which transplant arrests peroxisomal demyelination in CALD, though multiple mechanisms may be involved. CNS donor myeloid engraftment is known to occur after transplant.11,12 The rate of CNS engraftment can be directly assessed only in autopsy studies in humans and murine models, but two contemporary studies have shown that about 8% of the myeloid-derived (microglia-like) cells are donor derived after myeloablative transplant.11,12 If ALDP-replete donor cells contribute to CALD arrest, then in the Allo-HCT setting, 100% of that 8% donor-derived myeloid cells in the CNS would express ALDP. By contrast, after GT, only 10%–20% of corrected cells express ALDP, potentially resulting in <2% of the CNS donor-derived myeloid cells expressing ALDP, which may contribute to slower disease attenuation.

Another difference between GT and Allo-HCT was that the Allo-HCT group, but not the GT group, demonstrated marked improvement in peripheral C26:0-lysoPC. C26:0-lysoPC has been implicated, but not proven, as a pathological contributor to the pediatric ALD disease phenotypes of CALD and AI.4,13 Its reduction after transplant may contribute to the disease arrest. However, its independent contribution is difficult to quantify given concurrent brain engraftment and other immune system changes happening concurrently after any transplant.

In conclusion, we compared changes in two key biomarkers of disease activity in CALD following GT and Allo-HCT. These biomarkers will be useful as current cell and gene therapies for this disease continue to be evolved. While both GT and Allo-HCT transplant modalities can lead to remission of active cerebral disease, biomarker changes in the Allo-HCT group appeared more efficient and consistent.

Materials and methods

All protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Minnesota. Plasma samples were also analyzed using Simoa assay for NfL and GFAP (Quanterix, Billerica, MA). Plasma C26:0-lysoPC was measured, as previously described.14

Statistics. Ordinary least squares (OLS) simple linear regression was used to assess associations between continuous variables. Outliers were identified using the ROUT method (Prism, version 10.5.0). Normal distribution was tested by Shapiro-Wilk analysis. Two-way comparisons were performed using a paired Student’s t test for normally distributed data or a Mann-Whitney test for non-normally distributed data. Comparisons within the same patient were performed using a paired t test; otherwise, an unpaired t test was used. Multivariate analyses were performed using JMP (version 18.2.2).
