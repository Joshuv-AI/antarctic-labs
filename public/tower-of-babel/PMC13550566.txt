Design and field validation of an automated sensor-integrated high-capacity corn plot seeder
Authors: Yan Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient and accurate plot seeding is essential for modern crop breeding and precision agriculture. However, existing systems demonstrate limitations in terms of automation, throughput, and sensing capabilities. To address these challenges, an automated, sensor-integrated, high-capacity planting system was developed for corn plot seeders. The mechanical dynamics of seed delivery were analyzed, and key parameters, including opener tip angle, tilt angle, pushrod speed, and opening position, were optimized using response surface methodology. The optimal parameter combination (30°, 49°, 90 mm/s, and − 9 mm, respectively) yielded a seeding qualification rate of 99.33%. A three-stage sensor module comprising QR code recognition, cartridge positioning, and seed-drop detection was integrated to ensure accurate seed–plot matching. Field experiments demonstrated a 76% reduction in manual intervention, with an error tolerance of 0.1%. Therefore, the proposed system provides an effective and scalable solution for precision plot seeding and offers a practical framework for upgrading existing agricultural equipment.

Introduction

Chinese plot seeders continue to exhibit technological limitations compared to their international counterparts1–3. The global market is currently dominated by advanced models developed by industry leaders such as Wintersteiger (Austria), Almaco (USA), and Haldrup (Germany)4,5. These systems feature sophisticated automated planting technologies that not only fulfill automatic seeding requirements but also significantly improve operational efficiency6,7. The planting system is a core component of the plot seeder8 and plays a critical role in plot breeding and seeding operations9. At present, agricultural practices in China predominantly rely on manual seeding methods, using either cone systems (gravity-fed funnels through which seeds flow into furrows) or magazine systems (cartridge-based dispensers requiring manual reloading after several plots)10–13. In contrast, automated high-capacity planting systems offer clear operational advantages over these traditional approaches. In particular, these systems can enable precise seed spacing within sowing plots, the continuous planting of multiple seed varieties, and the automated collection of used seed cartridges.

Extensive research has been conducted globally to enhance planting efficiency and performance. Current planting systems for plot seeders can be broadly classified into two categories: manual and automatic. Manual seeding involves pre-distributing seeds into individual paper bags, which are then manually delivered using a central seed dispenser. However, this method is labor-intensive and makes maintaining seeding precision and uniformity challenging. For instance, Liu et al.14 designed an electromagnetically driven coaxial double-disc automatic planting system that was compatible with plot breeding and seeding equipment. However, its application was limited to a small number of plots. Meanwhile, Xu et al.15 developed a cone-belt seed metering device for plot seeders, but its seeding uniformity was sensitive to cone structure and the seed distribution speed. In addition, Engel et al.16 designed a manual plot seeder and fertilizer applicator using a simple seed cartridge device produced by Hege. Moreover, Yuan et al.17 developed an oil–electric hybrid air-suction sorghum plot seeder to improve seeding accuracy and reduce seed damage, but its specialized configuration limited its applicability to sorghum and similarly sized grains. For theoretical optimization, Ma et al.18 proposed a seeding-trajectory-based farmland boundary estimation method using curvature analysis and clustering, enabling efficient and accurate digital mapping for agricultural automation. Moreover, Zhao et al.19 optimized a hand-pushed precision planter by determining optimal hole dimensions and chamber angles through EDEM simulation and orthogonal testing, addressing the issues of low flexibility, high cost, and poor sowing quality in existing corn breeding seeders. Beres et al.20 developed two fully operational prototype drills to overcome the limitations of conventional drills for conservation farming and to enable the accurate replication of zero-tillage practices in field plot studies.

Automatic seeding systems, in contrast, rely on preloaded seed cartridges that are delivered via a magazine-type automated planting mechanism. While this method is suitable for small-scale breeding and seeding trials, it requires the frequent manual replacement of seed cartridges in larger trials, leading to potential delays and replacement errors during prolonged operation. Li et al.21 designed a seed distributor equipped with a magazine system for precision plot seeders, thereby reducing the number of operators required during trials. Wang22 developed an automatic seed delivery system for soybean plot seeders, although its application was limited to continuous seeding for up to 12 plot groups per cycle. Tabal et al.23 employed a mechatronic planting system for a simple hand-pushed corn seeder. More recently, Almaco (USA) introduced a large-scale automated planting system equipped with a magazine system, capable of seeding 5,760 plots when fully loaded24. However, the high cost of importing such devices remains a significant barrier. Moreover, the proprietary cartridge design and fixed opener kinematics of this system restrict its adaptability to variable domestic conditions across China. Similarly, Haldrup’s systems lack integrated seed–plot verification sensors, which can lead to mismatches during field trials25,26.

This study addresses several challenges in existing plot seeder planting processes, including high operational intensity during planting and cartridge replacement, difficulties in maintaining seeding precision, limited planting areas, and the high cost of imported systems. To overcome these limitations, an automated, high-capacity planting system for corn plot seeders was developed to provide a reference framework for future research. Unlike existing systems, the proposed system offered three key improvements tailored to practical requirements: real-time sensory verification, adaptive opener kinematics, and an open retrofitting platform. Its design was based on three core hypotheses: (1) optimization of opener geometry and kinematic parameters would increase seeding qualification rates; (2) integration of a three-stage sensing system would reduce seed–plot mismatches; and (3) the system would decrease manual intervention while maintaining field operational speeds. Key performance indicators, including seeding qualification rate, error tolerance, labor reduction, and cartridge cycle time, were used to evaluate the improvements achieved by this system.

Working principle

Before the planting system is operated, seed cartridges (each row pre-filled with seeds) are first arranged in compartments separated by dividers according to the designated plot sequence. The storage cart is then placed on the sliding platform. A push–pull cartridge compactor, connected by a linkage mechanism, drives the three compactors to secure the storage cart, while the tow hook on the support rod of the storage cart engages with the support rod of the seed cartridges. During operation, the DC servo motors provide the driving force. DC servo motor 1 drives the synchronous belt via a pulley, causing the storage cart’s sliding platform to move along the rail toward the right. This causes the movement of the storage cart. Throughout this movement, the tow hook on the storage cart support rod constrains the movement of the seed cartridge support rod. Guided by position sensors, the storage cart moves to a predetermined position, where a seed cartridge is released onto the holder. Once the seed cartridge is in position, DC servo motor 2 is activated, driving a second synchronous belt via a pulley system. This action moves the seed cartridge pushrod along the rail toward the right, pushing the seed cartridge from the holder to the opener. The opener causes the seed cartridge to open, allowing seeds to fall through the discharge port. The empty seed cartridge then continues to move backward and is collected by the cartridge collection unit. The DC servo motor drives the pushrod of the seed cartridge in a reciprocal manner, enabling the continuous dispensing of all seed cartridges within the storage cart. Throughout the dispensing process, position sensors and seed cartridge code recognition sensors ensure that all seed cartridges are correctly dispensed. After all seed cartridges have been dispensed, the DC servo motors return all components to their initial positions. To further illustrate the coordination between the sensor module and the actuator system, the control logic of the automated planting system is presented in Fig. 2. The proposed system employs a three-stage sensing module to ensure accurate seed cartridge handling and reliable seed delivery. The sensing module comprises: (1) a seed cartridge detection sensor, (2) a recognition sensor based on QR code identification, and (3) a seed drop monitoring sensor. These sensors are strategically installed along the seed cartridge transport path. The detection sensor is installed at the cartridge loading position to verify the presence of the cartridge. Meanwhile, the recognition sensor is positioned at the identification station to ensure correct seed–plot matching. Finally, the seed drop monitoring sensor is located near the discharge outlet to confirm successful seed release. Sensor signals are transmitted to a PLC-based control system, which coordinates actuator operations sequentially. During operation, the system first performs initialization and self-check. Upon receiving the start command, the cartridge is transported to the target position. The pushrod mechanism is then activated based on sensor feedback to open the cartridge. The recognition sensor verifies that positioning is correct, and the seed drop sensor confirms the successful release of seeds. If abnormalities such as blockage or misalignment are detected, the system initiates an error-handling procedure and resets to ensure safe operation. Sensor signals are continuously sampled and processed in real time by the control unit, leading to synchronized sensing and actuation operations.

Design of key components

Calculation of seed quantity for a single plot

The internal structural dimensions of the seed cartridge serve as a key determinant of the overall size of the planting system. Typically, the seed spacing for corn plot seeders ranges from 30 to 40 cm, alongside a row spacing of 0.5–0.6 m. However, for summer corn, the seed spacing is typically between 25 and 33 cm. To facilitate subsequent experiments, a seed spacing of 30 cm and a row spacing of 0.5 m were selected to calculate the total seed quantity required for a single plot, as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q=\left(\left[\frac{L}{P}+\left(\frac{L}{P}\times n\right)\right]\right)\times r$$\end{document}

Herein Q—Total seed quantity for a single plot; L—Planting length (m); P—Seed spacing (cm); n—Excess seed ratio (approximately 10%); r—Number of planting rows.

The excess seed ratio n compensates for potential seed losses. Planting an excess number of seeds can lead to issues such as seed mixing and delays in the subsequent seed clearing process. Given existing seeding equipment, an excess seed ratio of about 10% is appropriate for the planting area of a single plot. For a minimum plot area of 2 m2, each planting row should include an additional two to three seeds to prevent issues such as seed shortage or uneven distribution during the subsequent seed dispensing process.

Based on the above calculations, a plot with a maximum area of 8 m2 requires approximately 58.7 corn seeds. Unlike field seeding, plot seeding requires a specific quantity of seeds for smaller plots11,27,28, and the plot seeding area typically ranges from 2 to 8 m2.

Design of the structural parameters of the seed cartridge

Issues such as the premature opening of the seed cartridge or its failure to open during planting can result in seed spillage or missed seeding. To prevent such issues, the seed cartridge is designed such that it contains an opening on the long side, allowing the seed cartridge opener to work effectively. Meanwhile, the short side is equipped with a fold-over slot that connects the upper cartridge and the bottom cover, preventing the bottom cover from falling into the seed dispenser.

For our analysis, a random sample consisting of 100 seeds belonging to three corn varieties widely cultivated in the Harbin region—Ruipu 909 (blue), Yangguang No. 1 (red), and Dongnong 248 (yellow)—was selected. The volume of the corn kernels was measured using graduated cups. Among the three varieties, Yangguang No. 1 showed the highest volume (100 seeds = approximately 60 ml), while Dongnong 248 showed the lowest volume (100 seeds = 35 ml). Determination of planting time

The typical operating speed for small plot seeders ranges from 2.0 to 2.5 km/h. To ensure adequate time for planting29, agronomic requirements and actual seeding conditions must be considered. A plot seeder efficiently seeds a two-row, 2 m2 plot in roughly 3.6 s; however, when tasked with seeding a two-row, 8 m2 plot, the time required rises to approximately 14.3 s. The total planting time should be less than half of the total operation time for a single plot (including the time taken for the seed cartridge to move from the initial point to the seed discharge port, the time taken for seeds to be released from the seed cartridge to the seed dispenser, and the time taken for the seed dispenser to move seeds to the seeding position on the seed metering device).

Mechanical analysis of the seed dispensing process of the planting system

Force and motion analysis during seed delivery

The planting system involves a significant amount of particle motion. This motion is influenced by various forces, including gravity. To characterize the forces and motion of seeds within the cartridge, an analysis of the planting process is necessary. Since the air resistance acting on corn particles during free fall is negligible1,30,31, their motion after exiting the cartridge is governed predominantly by gravity and can be approximated as linear free-fall motion in accordance with Newton’s second law.

A Cartesian coordinate system can be considered, with the centroid of a seed inside the cartridge serving as the origin, the horizontal line passing through the origin acting as the x-axis, and the vertical line acting as the y-axis. The forces acting on an individual seed include tangential resistance ft, the normal support force N from the bottom cover of the seed cartridge, the pressure from the upper seed layer Nj, the gravitational force G, and the normal support force from the lower seed layer Nv. Seed cartridge travel analysis

The post-collision velocities v1 and v2 of corn particles can be determined based on the conservation of momentum and the coefficient of restitution. This approach models momentum exchange during particle interactions within the seed cartridge. For two colliding particles with masses m1 and m2, the post-collision velocities are distributed around the velocity of their center of mass. The relative velocity after collision is expressed as e(v1-v2), where e is the coefficient of restitution, representing collision elasticity. The velocities of the corn particles after collision can be calculated as follows:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V=\frac{m_{1}{v}_{1}+m_{2}v_{2}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}+e(v_{1}-v_{2})\frac{m_{1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}$$\end{document}

During sowing, corn particles undergo complex motion under the combined influence of multiple forces such as gravity, inertia, and contact friction. To further examine seed dynamics within the cartridge, the planting process can be analyzed using a three-dimensional coordinate system, where the seeding direction is defined as the positive x-axis, the downward vertical direction as the positive z-axis, and the cartridge opening direction as the positive y-axis. The primary component influencing seed motion is the velocity Vx, which aligns with the cartridge’s direction of travel (x-axis). In practical applications, the overall planting speed can be regulated by adjusting the pushrod speed, which controls cartridge movement to meet specific sowing requirements.

Analysis of seed delivery stages

Assuming that corn particles glide smoothly and unhindered from the bottom cover of the seed cartridge into the seed metering device, and neglecting external influences such as air resistance, our analysis focuses on two dominant forces: gravity acting on the corn particles and friction between the particles and the cartridge cover. Using a simplified mechanical model, the motion of corn particles on the cartridge’s bottom cover can be divided into three stages: (1) an initial opening stage, in which particles rotate along with the cartridge cover around the axis of the bottom cover; (2) a sliding stage, in which particles begin to slide along the cover once the opening angle reaches a certain level; and (3) a projectile motion stage, in which particles are discharged and lose contact with the cover, following a parabolic motion with an initial velocity. This process is illustrated in Fig. 9.

Experimental design

Experimental platform and field conditions

To evaluate the operational effectiveness of the planting system under actual field conditions, a field trial was conducted. The device was installed on the 2BQX-2 pull-type plot seeder developed by the Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Machinery Engineering. During the trial, the overall operating speed of the machine was maintained between 2.0 and 2.5 km/h, with seeding operations conducted across two rows. The automated planting system was mounted on the rear frame of the plot seeder and operated in coordination with the seed metering and furrow opening units. The integrated system is explained in Fig. 11. As illustrated in the figure, the automated planting system was installed above the seeding units, functioning synchronously during operation. The interaction between the planting system and the seeding mechanism under actual operating conditions can be inferred from the working configuration shown in Fig. 11b. Seeds belonging to the Yangguang No. 1 variety were used for the field trials, with a Lovol M554-B tractor providing the required power. Preliminary trials were carried out to establish the relationship between the tractor’s gear positions and its forward speed. The main trial was then conducted in the second low gear, and based on the optimal settings identified during the pre-trials, subsequent experiments were conducted.

Orthogonal optimization experiment

Given the lack of national standards for evaluating automated high-capacity planting systems32, the qualification rate of a single container during continuous planting was adopted as the criterion for assessing the performance and stability of the seed delivery technology developed in this study.

To examine the impact of the tip angle of the opener (A), the tilt angle of the opener (B), and the pushrod operating speed (C) on planting performance, a 3-factor, 5-level quadratic rotation orthogonal combination experiment was conducted. In total, 23 groups were established. The test followed the guidelines outlined in Testing Methods of Single Seed Drills (Precision Drills) (GB/T 6973–2005) and relevant scholarly research22,33–36, with the three aforementioned factors serving as independent variables. The qualification rate (Y1) was chosen as the corresponding indicator and was calculated using the following formula:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_{1}=\frac{n_{1}}{N_{1}}\times 100\%$$\end{document}

Herein \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_{1}$$\end{document}—Qualification rate; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{1}$$\end{document}—Total number of seed cartridges tested; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_{1}$$\end{document}—Number of qualified seed cartridges.

To ensure the clarity and reproducibility of the assessment, a “qualified seed cartridge” was explicitly defined. A seed cartridge was considered qualified only when all the following conditions were simultaneously satisfied: (1) all seeds were completely discharged from the cartridge, without any residue; (2) no missed seeding or blockage occurred during the seed delivery process; (3) the seed flow was continuous and stable, without any interruption; and (4) the seeds were released at their designated positions in accordance with the preset operation sequence. During the experiments, the performance of each seed cartridge was evaluated through direct observation and counting. The total number of qualified seed cartridges was recorded, and the qualification rate was calculated accordingly. In total, 3 cartridges containing 150 seed cartridges each were examined, and each test was repeated three times. The average value of these measurements was taken for analysis.

Optimized parameter test

To maximize the qualification rate (objective function) given the constraints of various experimental factors (8.18° ≤ A ≤ 41.82°, 23.18° ≤ B ≤ 56.82°, and 55.31 mm/s ≤ C ≤ 111.36 mm/s), multi-objective optimization of the regression equation was performed using Design Expert software. Bench verification test

To verify the reliability of the field experiment data, in July 2023, a laboratory bench test was conducted at the Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Machinery Engineering. The JPS-12 computer vision seed metering bench was employed to evaluate the planting system. First, upon powering up the host computer, the main interface of the control system was activated, automatically connecting the system with the PLC controller. Once the planting preparation for the plots was complete, the plot length and the number of plots was set, the initial parameters were calibrated, and the planting operation was executed. This planting operation could be controlled either manually or automatically. In the manual control mode, the angle of the opener and pushrod operating speed were manually adjusted to preset values by observing the motion of the seed cartridge and storage cart. These parameters were then added as system inputs. Meanwhile, under the automatic planting control mode, continuous planting was executed thereafter according to the selected parameters. This ensured the flexibility of parameter adjustment and planting stability, providing a reproducible test environment for structural optimization.

Field validation

The field trial, conducted in August 2023 in an experimental field of the Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Machinery Engineering, involved a well-designed experimental setup comprising twelve 8 m2 plots. To minimize experimental errors, a seed delivery tube was connected to the seed discharge port of the planting system, extending 20–30 mm above the ground of the plot. To clearly observe the planting efficiency of the seed cartridge, the soil covering device was not used in any of the three field trials. After the metrics for each trial were recorded, the seeds within the trial area were cleared prior to the subsequent trial.

Influence of different factors on seeder performance

Response surface methodology involves the use of graphical techniques to display the functional relationship between two factors and their response values, allowing us to intuitively observe the relationship between the experimental values and response values of various variables, as well as the types of interactions between the two measured variables. Accordingly, optimal conditions can be selected for experimental design. In this study, based on the regression equation, response surfaces were computed to visualize the interaction effects of the experimental factors on the seed metering qualification rate. These factors included the tip angle and tilt angle of the opener, the operating speed of the pushrod, and the opening position of the seed cartridge, as shown in Fig. 12. The response surface analysis (Fig. 12) further indicated that when the tip angle of the opener tip approached the center value (30°), the tilt angle increased to 46°, and the pushrod operating speed increased to 110 mm/s, the qualification rate tended to peak. This was consistent with the optimization results.

Discussion

This study presents the design and experimental validation of an automated, high-capacity corn plot seeding system aimed at improving sowing efficiency and accuracy. Through laboratory experiments and field trials, the optimal system parameters, including opener tip angle, opener tilt angle, pushrod operating speed, and seed cartridge opening position, were determined. The optimized parameter combination achieved a seeding qualification rate of 99.33%, demonstrating the system’s effectiveness and applicability under practical conditions.

In addition, benchmarking against leading international systems indicated measurable performance improvements4,5,24. For example, the developed system achieved a 1.6–3.0% higher qualification rate than leading commercial systems, while maintaining robust performance under varying field conditions. It reduced manual intervention by 76%, although with slightly longer cycle times (4.5 s vs. 3.2 s for industry gold standards). A key innovation lied in its enhanced adaptability, with approximately 300% greater tolerance to variations in soil hardness without the need for manual recalibration. From an economic perspective, the modular architecture could enable a cost reduction of approximately 77% relative to conventional imported systems, while incorporating advanced sensing capabilities that are not commonly available in existing systems.

Although the proposed system demonstrated strong performance under optimized conditions, the field experiments did not include a direct control group (e.g., manual seeding or conventional mechanical systems). This could limit the rigor of comparative assessments. To address this, system performance was compared with results reported in the literature and with data from commercial systems.

A comparative analysis with representative international plot seeding systems, such as those developed by Wintersteiger and Almaco, is presented in Table 8. These systems are widely recognized for their high levels of automation and operational efficiency. Compared with these systems, the proposed system achieves comparable or superior seeding qualification rates while significantly reducing manual intervention. Moreover, the integration of a three-stage sensing module enables real-time seed-to-plot verification, a feature not commonly implemented in existing systems. The modular design also enhances adaptability and contributes to overall cost reduction.

The design and experimental trials presented in this study are of considerable significance for improving the sowing efficiency and accuracy of Chinese corn plot seeders. Currently, many domestic applications still rely on manual methods or basic seed supply equipment, which are characterized by low efficiency, limited precision, high labor intensity, and substantial human resource requirements. To address these limitations, our research institute has developed a high-capacity automated seed supply system capable of supporting various sowing methods and seed types. By integrating technologies such as seed cartridge code recognition, automated dispensing, and cartridge recycling, the system enables continuous, large-scale, and precise seed delivery without operational interruption. Compared with existing devices, which are often hindered by their limited capacity, low reliability, and poor scalability, the proposed system provides a more robust and efficient solution for modernizing seed supply operations in mechanized agriculture.

Nevertheless, several potential sources of seed delivery error must be acknowledged. These include occasional seed bridging under high-humidity conditions, seed misplacement due to uneven vibration transmission on rough terrain, and disruptions in seed flow owing to minor deformations in reusable cartridges after extended use. Moreover, seed detection accuracy may degrade over time without the appropriate recalibration of the optical sensors, especially under dusty or low-light field conditions. These technical vulnerabilities highlight the need for robust error-detection mechanisms and redundant sensing strategies in future iterations.

Real-world adoption also presents operational challenges beyond those encountered under controlled experimental conditions. For instance, performance consistency on terrains with slopes exceeding 15° or with highly variable soil compaction (e.g., transitions between sandy and clay soils) remains uncertain. Furthermore, maintaining sensor alignment, ensuring cartridge cleanliness, and managing power supply stability in remote environments may limit field adoption without appropriate training and technical support. Maintenance considerations, such as the availability of spare parts, diagnostic tools, and remote calibration support, may further constrain large-scale adoption, particularly in regions with limited agri-tech infrastructure.

The scope of this study is also influenced by limitations related to available resources, costs, and time. Although the system yields significant advancements in automated seeding, certain practical constraints remain. First, the field validation was conducted under typical agronomic conditions for corn cultivation; however, the performance under extreme conditions (e.g., heavy clay soils or steep terrain) remains to be evaluated. Second, the mechanical wear observed during extended operation suggests the need for further material optimization for high-friction components. Third, while the sensing system performed reliably under controlled conditions, additional calibration may be required for deployment under variable weather conditions. Moreover, the current design focuses primarily on seed delivery mechanics and does not incorporate post-sowing processes such as soil compaction monitoring, which could be addressed in future studies through integrated agronomic approaches. Future work will focus on both short-term improvements and long-term technological integration from the perspective of practical considerations. In the short term (0–2 years), efforts should be focused on expanding compatibility with different seed types through modular cartridge redesign, validating system performance under challenging field conditions (e.g., slopes > 15° and high-clay soils), and enhancing the durability of high-wear components through accelerated life testing. Over the longer term (3–5 years), development should target closed-loop agronomic systems that integrate real-time soil monitoring with adaptive sowing parameters, incorporate AI-assisted crop monitoring using UAV-based multispectral imaging, and explore blockchain-enabled traceability from sowing to harvest.

Conclusions

This study describes the development of a sensor-integrated, high-capacity automated planting system for corn plot seeders, addressing key limitations of existing systems in terms of automation, accuracy, and operational efficiency. By combining mechanical optimization with a three-stage sensing mechanism, the proposed system achieves reliable seed delivery and accurate seed–plot matching. The results demonstrate that the integration of sensing and control frameworks significantly enhances system stability and reduces manual intervention, providing a practical solution for improving the efficiency of plot-based breeding operations. Furthermore, the modular architecture facilitates flexible adaptation under varying field conditions and supports the upgrading of existing seeding equipment. Future work will focus on advancing system robustness under complex field environments, expanding compatibility with different crop types, and developing closed-loop intelligent control strategies for adaptive seeding.
