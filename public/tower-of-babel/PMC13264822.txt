Surveillance of the antiviral activity and mechanism of action of newly synthesized spirocyclic thio-pyrimidinones against human adenovirus type 40
Authors: Esraa A Manie, Sherif S Ragab, Ayman M Sweed, Sherif Nasser, Abdou K Allayeh, Emad M Elzayat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman adenoviruses (HAdVs) cause multiple common human infections; they are responsible for several upper and lower respiratory system and gastrointestinal diseases. There are no available approved antiviral treatments specific to HAdVs thus far; only a wide-spectrum antiviral can be used, and this creates the need for potent antiviral medications to treat illnesses that pose a serious threat. On the basis of our previous research that examined spirocyclic thio-pyrimidinone derivatives against human herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2), in this study, we concentrated on exploring the antiviral activity of these compounds against adenovirus type 40 (HAdV-40) and their mode of action. Spiro pyrimidinone derivative 3 showed promising antiviral activity among the eleven synthesized compounds, with a selectivity index of 11.88. Compound 3 mainly inhibited HAdV-40 during the virucidal stage by 35%, viral multiplication by 26%, and viral absorption by approximately 20%. Additionally, the downregulation of the gene expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL10, and CCL-5 reflects the effect of compound 3 on viral suppression, indicating that the inflammatory pathway is modulated. Additionally, docking studies were performed to evaluate the 3 mechanisms of action of the compounds for viral inhibition. The results revealed some interactions that point to a potential mechanism by which compound 3 prevents the adenovirus from adhering by interfering with the ability of the short-fiber knob to function while the compound and virus are in contact.Keywords: Human adenovirus type 40 (HAdV-40), Antiviral activity, Mechanism of action, Spirocyclic Thio-pyrimidinones, RT‒qPCR, Short-fiber knob

Introduction

Human adenoviruses (HAdVs) are a group of nonenveloped double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid (dsDNA) viruses [1]. They are ubiquitous human pathogens that cause significant infections involving the upper and lower respiratory tract, ocular, and gastrointestinal illnesses [2]. Adenoviruses have more than 50 serotypes that cause a wide range of clinical syndromes, which are typically present in young children, immunocompromised patients, and military populations [1, 3].

Adenoviruses are distributed worldwide, and infections occur throughout the year without significant seasonal variability. Most infections occur as sporadic events. As HAdVs are typically transmitted person-to-person through aerosols or droplets and through fecal-oral and fomite routes [4].

To date, there is no antiviral agent against adenovirus that has been licensed, and its management is still based on hygienic and supportive measures alone [5]. The major problem associated with AdV infections is not only the absence of chemotherapeutic agents for clinical practice but also the absence of strong anti-adenovirals in experimental research. Evidently, the development of effective antiviral treatments is a principal task [6].

To achieve this goal, many research groups have aimed primarily to investigate drugs or other potential compounds with antiviral activity against the adenovirus. Many antiviral agents have been shown to be effective in the in vivo and in vitro studies in animal models and in preclinical trials, but lack reliability in large, controlled clinical investigations. The design of such studies, however, presents several hurdles because of the nature and specific characteristics of adenovirus-induced ocular diseases [5]. Among the most versatile bioactive molecules, compounds possessing pyrimidine motifs have a wide range of pharmacological properties, including antiviral activity [7].

In fact, a number of well-known antiviral drugs contain pyrimidine moieties. Some of these drugs that have been recently used to treat adenovirus are shown in Fig. 1. As shown in Fig. 1, cidofovir is an acyclic monophosphate nucleotide analog with an aminopyrimidinone fragment [8]. On the other hand, ganciclovir and filociclovir are acyclic guanine nucleoside analogs that share the same aminopurine backbone (imidazopyrimidine motif) but differ only at the 9-position [9, 10]. Zalcitabine is a derivative of the naturally occurring deoxycytidine, which is accessed by replacing the hydroxyl group at position 3’ with a hydrogen [11]. It should be mentioned that medicinal chemists face significant challenges in designing and synthesizing non-nucleoside versions of pyrimidine analogs with potent antiviral activity to overcome problems such as toxicity and drug resistance.

Molecular design

We realized a variety of chemical modifications at different positions on the representative drug cidofovir as a basis for the main core of the molecular design rationale (Scheme 1).

Rationale for the molecular design of new antiviral agents

The first modification was achieved at the C5 position of cidofovir, which involves the insertion of the isosteric cyano group. Many advantages could be attained from this modification, where the nitrile group is known to act as a hydrogen bond acceptor [12, 13]; consequently, it could bind to the active sites by an important interaction [10], and the aromatic π–system with the cyano group became more polar [11]; finally, it could have more favorable effects with respect to pharmacokinetics and solubility [14]. The C6 position was modified through the insertion of the Spiro-cyclohexyl fragment. This motif is superior to rigid flat rings for drug design because it could improve conformational entropy once the interaction with the protein target starts [15]. The third modification occurred at the N1 position and involved the removal of the sugar moiety. The C = O and -NH2 groups at the C-2 and C-4 positions were replaced with C = S and C = O, respectively, to generate the thiol parent compound. The latter could be subjected to either S-alkylation or diazocoupling. The latter could also be achieved on the S-alkylated compounds. This rationale of molecular design allowed the generation of new hybrid structures with different biological isosteres (Scheme 1).

Materials and methods

Preparation of the compounds, cells and virus

Adherent human HEp-2 cells, which is fundamentally a derivative from human cervical adenocarcinoma cell line (HeLa cells) were cultivated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco™), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and a 1% antibiotic penicillin/streptomycin mixture. All synthetic compounds were prepared in accordance with the procedures of Ragab et al. [16] and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to create a 100 mg/mL stock. The Nawah-Scientific Center Inc. (Mokatam, Egypt) generously provided the human adenovirus type 40.

The half maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of each compound was determined by crystal violet assay

Each well of a 96-well cell culture plate was seeded with 100 µL of cell suspension. To create a confluent monolayer of cells, the seeded plates were then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a 5% humidified CO2 incubator. To start with a 1000 µg/mL concentration for the 2-fold serial dilution, we placed 198 µL and 2 µL of the drug of interest in the first row. We then transferred the three dilutions onto the cell monolayers and incubated them for 72 h at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2. The cells were fixed with 100 µL of 10% formaldehyde, incubated at room temperature for 20 min, and then washed five times with running water after the supernatant was discarded and the cells were washed with PBS once. Afterward, 50 µL of 0.1% crystal violet was added (LOBA CHEMIE.LTD; C.I.No 42555) and incubated for 15 min. After the cell monolayers were washed and dried, 200 mL of methanol was added to each well to dissolve the crystal violet dye for 20 min. Ultimately, a plate reader (FLUOstar Omega at =570 nm) was used to measure the absorbance.

The antiviral activity and inhibitory concentration 50 (IC50) were determined by crystal violet assay

As previously mentioned, the CC50 values were calculated for the safest substances. To create a confluent monolayer of cells, the HEp-2 cell suspension was placed in 96-well tissue culture plates and then cultured for 24 h at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2. Afterward, 1× PBS was used to wash the cell monolayer once. Cells were co-incubated in triplicate with an adenovirus (MOI = 1), followed by serial dilution of the drug and incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Three untreated cells were allowed to control the virus. The cell monolayer was rinsed with 1x PBS following a 72-hour incubation period. Then, 50 µL of 0.1% crystal violet was added (LOBA CHEMIE.LTD; C.I.No 42555) and incubated for 15 min. After the cell monolayers were washed and dried, 200 mL of methanol was added to each well to dissolve the crystal violet dye for 20 min. Ultimately, a plate reader (FLUOstar Omega Ω at =570 nm) was used to measure the absorbance. The absorbance was then measured using a plate reader, and the CC50 - IC50 values were computed using nonlinear regression analysis by plotting the log inhibitor versus the normalized response using the GraphPad Prism 5 analysis program (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA).

Detecting the mechanism of action for promising candidates using the MTT assay

We investigated three mechanisms, viral adsorption, viral replication, and viral fusion (virucidal), to better understand how each compound’s antiviral effectiveness blocks particular steps in the viral cycle. The 96-well cell culture plates were seeded with 100 µL of cell suspension under the previously stated conditions to test the adsorption mechanism. After 24 h, the adherent cells were washed with 1x PBS, and the medium was discarded. The cells were then treated in triplicate with 100 µL of each potential chemical at a safe cytotoxic dose (CC50) that had been previously determined. After that, the plates were incubated for an hour at 4 °C to ensure that the chemical particles adhered to the cell receptors. Following incubation, the chemical was eliminated, and one PBS wash was performed on the cells. After that, we filled each well with 100 µL of virus dilution (MOI = 1) and incubated them in a CO2 incubator for an hour at 37 °C. After the virus was eliminated, the cells were subjected to another 1x PBS wash. Finally, 100 µL of maintenance medium supplemented with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was added to the plates, which were then incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 72 h.

To study the replication mechanism, we pre-seeded a 96-well cell culture plate, added 100 µL of virus dilution (MOI = 1), and incubated it in a CO2 incubator for one hour at 37 °C. Following incubation, 100 µL of each substance was added in duplicate after the virus withdrawn. The plates were then placed in a CO2 incubator and incubated for one hour at 37 °C. Each well was then filled with 100 µL of maintenance medium.

To understand the virucidal process, we combined the viral dilution (MOI = 1) with an equal amount of the safe concentration of the pretested compound, and we allowed the mixture to sit at room temperature for an hour. The cells were then incubated for one hour at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator after we made twofold serial dilutions of the mixture and injected it into them. Following incubation, 100 µL of maintenance medium was added, the cells were removed, and the supernatant was discarded. After that, the plates were incubated in an incubator with 5% CO2 for 72 h at 37 °C. After the incubation times, we removed the media and used 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to wash the cell monolayer. The plates were then incubated for two hours at 37 °C after we added 25 µL of 0.5 mg/mL MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide; Sigma‒Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA). Following incubation, we disposed of the MTT reagent and added 200 µl of acidified isopropanol (1 N HCl in 99.9% isopropanol) to dissolve the generated formazan crystals. Finally, we used a plate reader (FLUOstar Omega ꭥ) to measure the absorbance at λ = 570 nm.

Total RNA extraction, RT‒PCR and RT‒qPCR for cells treated with promising compounds at different time intervals

A 3 mL cell suspension was cultured in 6-well cell culture plates and then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h. After incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell monolayer was washed with 3 mL of 1x PBS. Afterward, the cell monolayers were treated in duplicate with the suitable IC50 and the previously detected drug’s mechanism of action, leaving duplicates for the cell and virus control, and the cell pellets were collected at 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h post infection. Total RNA was extracted using a RNeasy Mini Kit (Cat. No. 74104). A 350 µL volume of RLT buffer was added to a (1 × 106) cell pellet and mixed well. The lysate was then transferred directly into a QIAshredde column and centrifuged for 2 min at full speed. Afterward, the flowthrough supernatant was transferred to a new Eppendorf tube, and an equal volume of 70% ethanol was added to the lysate. Then, the mixture was transferred to a RNeasy spin column and centrifuged for 15 s at ≥ 8000 xg. The flowthrough was discarded, and DNase digestion enzyme (thermoscientific REF. NO. EN0521) was added to purify the samples from the DNA, which were subsequently washed with 700 µL of Buffer RW1 and 500 µL of Buffer RPE and centrifuged for 15 s at ≥ 8000 xg each time. RNA samples were eluted in 30 µL of RNAse-free water. cDNA was subsequently synthesized using a reverse transcriptase kit and a Thermosceintific RevertedAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (REF. NO. k1622). Gene expression of the inflammatory markers IL-6, TNF-α, CCL-5, and CXCL-10 in the cell pellets was subsequently measured by a Bio-Rad CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR System using a Perfectstart® Green qPCR SuperMix kit (Cat.No. AQ601) with a two-step qPCR method (94 °C; 30 s; 94 °C; 5 s, then 60 °C; 30 s) for 45 cycles.

Molecular docking

The 3D crystal structure of the Human Adenovirus Serotype 41 Fiber Head (PDB ID 2BZU) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank [17]. The protein was prepared by adding missing hydrogen atoms at a target pH of 4.0 to simulate the acidic conditions of the gastrointestinal tract, and partial charges were assigned via the AMBER ff14SB force field within UCSF Chimera [18]. To identify potential binding sites, the CASTpFold server was used, which revealed a consistent pocket located at the interface between each pair of chains within the trimeric structure [19]. Compound 3 was drawn using ChemSketch, followed by the addition of hydrogen atoms, the calculation of partial charges via AM1-BCC, and energy minimization using 1000 steps of the steepest descent algorithm in UCSF Chimera [18, 20]. GOLD (version 5.8) was used for molecular docking simulations, generating 50 GA solutions with ChemPLP used as the primary scoring function [21, 22]. The resulting docking poses were clustered at a 1.5 Å threshold using the complete linkage method. The best-ranked pose from the most populated cluster was then exported as a protein–ligand complex. Finally, the binding interactions were analyzed using the protein–ligand interaction profiler (PLIP) and subsequently visualized via PyMOL [23, 24].

Statistical analysis

ANOVA (analysis of variance) and Student’s t-test were performed using the SPSS statistical software package version 20. The data are presented as the means and standard deviations (SDs), with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. The plots were constructed using GraphPad Prism analysis software version 5 for Windows (developed by GraphPad Software, Inc., USA).

The cytotoxicity and antiviral activity of the compounds (CC50, IC50)

Recent studies have assessed the antiviral activity of highly bioactive azo dye derivatives synthesized by diazocoupling aryl diazonium compounds with spirocyclic thiouracil (Scheme 2) against DNA and RNA viruses [11–13].

The authors were prompted to investigate the antiviral action of these compounds against DNA viruses, specifically human adenovirus type 40, guided by their prior performance against RNA coronavirus and human herpesvirus type 2 [25, 26]. Therefore, the authors assessed the Hep-2 cell viability of the 11 newly synthesized pyrimidine derivatives. After the serially diluted compounds were added to the cell culture, the cell viability was assessed using a crystal violet assay 72 h later. The results demonstrated that the majority of novel derivatives were not harmful to HEp-2 cells. After the half maximal cytotoxic concentrations of the compounds were detected, the safest doses of the compounds were used to detect antiviral activity compared with that of ribavirin, with the FDA-approved antiviral used as comparative control rather than a definitive positive control, acknowledging its limitations against some serotypes of adenoviruses [27].

The inhibition mechanisms of action

Compounds 2, 3, and 6a were the most promising new inhibitors of human adenovirus type-40 (HAdV-40) among all the compounds tested according to the evaluation of antiviral activity (Fig. 2). These compounds are used as antiviral models for DNA virus inhibition. These substances showed various levels of antiviral activity against HAdV-40. The 50% inhibitory concentrations (IC50s) of these active substances varied starting from 237 µg/mL (Table 1). Interestingly, compound 3 successfully inhibited HAdV-40 with a favorable selectivity index of 11.88, suggesting that it is a promising option for the development of additional antiviral medications. To better understand the inhibitory effects of these three substances on HAdV-40, this study also investigated their mechanisms of action. According to these results, compounds 2, 3 and 6a significantly affected the virucidal and replication mechanisms, respectively, through which HAdV-40 inhibited infection.

The plots of the CC50s and IC50s of the compounds with the highest SIs (2, 3, and 6a) are shown in Table 1

In particular, compound 3 led to a 20% or greater reduction in HAdV-40 adsorption, the mechanism by which the virus binds to and penetrates host cells. Additionally, the data revealed that compound 3 affected viral replication, with 26% inhibition and 35% virucidal activity, which are critical stages of the HAdV-40 life cycle, during which treatment directly alters the structure of the virus. Compound 6a exhibited 17% inhibition by a virucidal mechanism, 40% replication, and 20% adsorption inhibition. Finally, compound 2 inhibited viral infection by 30%, the replication mechanism by 21%, and the adsorption mechanism by 15%. (Fig. 3). Furthermore, compared with those of the control cells and the virus control cells, the effects of compound 3-induced HAdV-40 suppression on HEp-2 cells occurred via a virucidal mechanism (Fig. 4).

Molecular docking

Human adenovirus types 40 and 41 (HAdV-40 and HAdV-41) are distinguished by their enteric tropism and specialized adaptation to the gastrointestinal environment. This is evidenced by the presence of a unique short fiber, in addition to the long fibers found in all adenovirus types. While adenovirus long fibers play an essential role in cell attachment through high-affinity interactions with Coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor (CAR), the precise function of the short-fiber knob was initially unclear [17]. In HAdV-F40 and HAdV-F41, the ratio of short fibers to long fibers was found to be approximately 6:1, indicating a significant functional role for short fibers. Furthermore, under low-pH conditions of the gut, the binding of the long-fiber knob to CAR is inactivated, suggesting the presence of an alternative attachment pathway for these enteric adenoviruses [28]. Rajan et al. reported that the short-fiber knob binds to heparan sulfate, which serves as a primary attachment factor for both HAdV-F40 and HAdV-F41 [29]. Since our antiviral compound has a significant effect on both viral replication and viral adsorption, we investigated its proposed binding to the short-fiber knob trimer by docking studies. The hypothesis is that by occupying the cavity between the fiber knob monomers, compound 3 could physically hinder the adsorption process. Because the 3D crystal structure of the Human Adenovirus Serotype 40 Fiber head is not yet available, docking studies were performed using the 3D crystal structure of the Human Adenovirus Serotype 41 Fiber Head (PDB ID 2BZU) [17].

As illustrated in Fig. 8, compound 3 is located in the cavity at the interface between two chains of the trimer fiber knob. Several hydrogen bonds are formed with residues Thr268, Val327, Tyr328, Lys332, and Thr378. CH-π stacking is observed between the benzene ring of compound 3 and the side chain of Tyr353. Additionally, the complex is stabilized by several hydrophobic contacts with residues Phe253, Val327, and Ile384 on one side and Tyr328, Lys332, Ala334, and Val351 on the other side of the pocket. These interactions suggest a possible mechanism by which compound 3 hinders adenovirus adsorption by interfering with the short-fiber knob function during contact between the compound and the virus.

Discussion

Adenoviruses are non-enveloped DNA viruses that are prevalent in people and animals and often affect both adults and children. Adenoviruses are classified into seven species (A to G). Serotypes F40 and F41, which have a strong preference for the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, are found in species F [30]. Adenoviruses are present in more than 100 serologically distinct varieties, of which 49 infect humans. The virus can infect multiple organ systems, although most infections are asymptomatic [31]. Human adenovirus species F (HAdV-F), types 40 and 41, are commonly associated with acute diarrheal disease (ADD) worldwide, especially among children as well as elderly individuals [32, 33].

In a previous study, a new derivative group of spirocyclic thio-pyrimidinones was created by integrating several bioactive moieties (pyrimidine, cyano, azo, sulfonamide, diketone, and Spiro cycles) into a single hybrid. On the basis of the abovementioned previous studies, one or more members of the same spirocyclic thio-pyrimidinone derivatives have good antiviral efficacy against 229E and HSV-2 [25, 26]. In this context, we aimed to assess the antiviral activity of eleven novel synthetic spirocyclic thio-pyrimidinone derivatives on human adenovirus type 40 (HadV-40) in the HEp-2 cell line, detecting the mechanisms of viral inhibition and gene expression of the inflammatory markers IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL10, and CCL-5 in infected Hep2 cells and treating them with synthetic compounds by RT‒qPCR. Next, the molecular docking interactions of those compounds were investigated. to find a promising antiviral compound that can be used against HAdV-40 in the future.

In terms of the cytotoxicity level, the data in Table 1 reveal that compound 3 has the highest SI of 11.88, with an IC50 of 304.7 µg/mL, while compounds 2 and 6a have an SI of 7.28 and 4.93.

and an IC50 of 603.6 and 237 µg/mL, respectively. However, compound 6a had the best IC50 value and viral inhibition percentage in terms of the replication mode of action (40%), and its SI was only 4.93, indicating its toxicity to normal cells, with a CC50 of 1170 µg/mL. Compound 3 was safe for normal cells, with a CC50 of 3620 µg/mL, and its viral inhibition activity on both replication and the virucidal mode of action was 26% and 36%, respectively, and compound 2’s best viral inhibition mode was virucidal, with a value of 30%. Additionally, our previous study revealed that compound 3 has proven activity against HSV-2 [26]. Therefore, compound 3 was chosen for downstream investigations, including an in-depth molecular docking study, to determine the best binding mode that inhibits viral infection.

Additionally, the anti-inflammatory effects of newly synthesized compounds, such as those of IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL-10, and CCL-5, which are known to occur during viral infections [33–35], were assessed. In particular, the expression of CXCL10 is important in a variety of viral infections and could aid in eliminating the virus [34]. The promising candidates resulted in varied expression levels in HAdV-40-infected HEp-2 cells, as determined by real-time PCR, compared with the expression levels in HAdV-40 virus control cells. The RT‒PCR plots revealed a significant decrease in inflammatory marker levels at 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h post infection in the 3 treated groups, with p values < 0.005. However, slight changes in the expression levels of these genes were detected in the 2- and 6a-treated cells. Therefore, compound 3 inhibited HAdV-40 viral infection, as reflected by the downregulation of IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL-10, and CCL-5 gene expression at different time intervals post infection.

Since the ketone group is known to increase the inhibitory efficacy of similar compounds against a variety of viruses, including the Mayaro virus and West Nile virus NS2BNS3 proteases, the antiviral activity of compound 3 may be attributed to its diketone moiety (PhNHOC-CH-COMe) [36, 37]. Additionally, in our earlier study, we reported that adding a PhNHOC-CH-COMe group to spiro-pyrimidine compounds doubled their antiviral activity against human coronavirus 229E (HCoV-229E) [25].

Docking studies have shown that the best possible binding mode for compound 3 is between two chains of the trimer’s fiber knob. This binding mode is stabilized by several hydrogen bonds either with the amino acid backbone (with Val327) or with polar side chains (with Thr268, Thr378, and Lys332), as well as through hydrophobic interactions with the two hydrophobic sides of the binding site. The placement of the compound and its ability to maintain several interactions within the binding site together might shed light on how the compound interferes with short-fiber knob function, justifying its high percentage of viral inhibition by both adsorption and replication mechanisms.

Finally, this research effectively identified compound 3 (2-(5-cyano-4-oxo-1,3-diazaspiro[5.5]undecan-2-yl)thio)-3-oxo-N-phenylbutanamide) as an effective inhibitor of human adenovirus type 40 (HAdV-40), aiding in the continuous search for efficient antiviral treatments against adenoviruses. These results show that compound 3 has a positive selectivity index and low cytotoxicity to normal cells while simultaneously interfering with crucial stages of the HAdV-40 life cycle, replication, and viral structure. The correlation between the antiviral activity of 3 and its structure, specifically its diketone moiety, highlights the possibility of creating novel antiviral drugs on the basis of comparable chemical structures. A new mechanism of action as an entry inhibitor is suggested by these docking studies, which shed light on the binding interactions of chemical compound 3 with short-fiber knobs. Overall, our findings support the antiviral properties of compound 3 and open the door for additional research and refinement of its structural formula and its derivatives as potential treatments for adenovirus infections.
