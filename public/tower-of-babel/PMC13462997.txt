Negative-curvature interfaces enable highly synergistic strength-ductility-toughness at 77 K
Authors: Dongdong Zhang, Jinyu Zhang, Mengyuan Hao, Jianjun Bian, Hengchao Shi, Ranming Niu, Julie Cairney, Song Ni, Kang Cheung Chan, Yiu Wing Mai, Min Song, Wenhai Ji, Ping Miao, Ting Zhu, Jun Sun, Evan Ma, Zibin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecipitation hardening is a well-known strategy that can raise the yield strength of alloys to well over 1 GPa, including at 77 K, but is less potent in offering strain hardening than twinning/transformation-induced-plasticity (TWIP/TRIP) mechanisms, which have been essential for the high ductility and fracture toughness of established cryogenic alloys. Here we demonstrate an innovative strategy to tailor the coherent nanoprecipitates by purposely designing negative-curvature interfaces (NCIs). This morphological control uses the geometric curvature and curvature-gradient effects to generate additional local stress, high elastic energy density, and substantial strain gradients to make NCIs prolific sources of dislocation nucleation. The proliferation of partial dislocations builds up ultra-dense hierarchical stacking-faults dynamically all over the deforming volume, substantially enhancing strain-hardening and toughening. The resulting NiCoCrAlTa alloy exhibits excellent cryogenic mechanical properties, achieving a high yield strength of 1.26 GPa, a product (~90 MPa%) of ultimate tensile strength (~1.80 GPa) with tensile ductility (~50%) and a fracture toughness of 213 MPa·m1/2 at 77 K—representing a record-high combination among all reported alloys to date. Our interface design strategy may be applicable to all precipitation-hardened alloys, transforming the precipitates from merely passive strengtheners to active and tunable agents regulating the plastic flow.Subject terms: Metals and alloys, Mechanical properties

Introduction

Modern cryogenic technologies, such as fusion reactors, superconducting magnets, and hydrogen-storage systems, require structural materials that exhibit concurrently high strength, ductility, and fracture resistance under low-temperature conditions1–5. These demanding applications necessitate alloys with a combination of high yield strength (YS > 1 GPa), ductility (uniform elongation, UE > 40%), and fracture toughness (KJIc > 200 MPa·am1/2) at 77 K. However, achieving these properties simultaneously remains a significant challenge6–9. For example, the widely used conventional cryogenic alloys such as 316LN stainless steel (SS) rarely attain a YS above 1 GPa, although their twinning/transformation-induced-plasticity (TWIP/TRIP) mechanisms allow excellent strain hardening and hence high UE and KJIc10. Cold-worked MP35N (CoNiCrMo) alloy11, on the other hand, readily surpasses 1 GPa in YS, but does not offer adequate UE or KJIc. The improvement in synergy is also a challenging issue, even for the recently developed multi-principal-element medium/high-entropy alloys (M/HEAs) invented for cryogenic use1–3,12–15: their YS rarely reached 1 GPa, and only a couple of HEAs with YS well below 1 GPa have reported fracture toughness on par with 316LN SS1–3,10.

To reach the desirable combinations of YS, UE, and KJIc, one naturally thinks of precipitation-hardened HEAs16. Recent studies on coherent spherical L12 nanoprecipitates (NPs) in FCC HEAs have demonstrated their potential for mechanical property enhancement12,16–22. However, while nanoprecipitation is known to be effective in strengthening HEAs to surpass GPa-level in YS and simultaneously can act as a mechanism to enhance strain-hardenability23,24, it is doubtful if such precipitates could sustain a high strain hardening comparable to that mediated by TWIP/TRIP in 316LN SS to attain a large UE and high toughness at 77 K. In this study, we propose an effective approach to achieve high strength–ductility–toughness synergy in a model NiCoCrAlTa alloy at low temperature. Our central idea is to purposely engineer negative-curvature precipitate/matrix interfaces (NCIs) of the L12-NPs in the FCC matrix to harness curvature-driven stress effects. The extended surface elastic model, integrated with strain gradient plasticity theory, predicts that high curvature (H, defined as the reciprocal of radius) and curvature gradients (∇H, representing spatial variations in curvature) induce additional interface stress (ΔτH ∝ (H + ∇2H)), rooted in the additional non-uniform accumulation of elastic strain energy caused by localized geometric incompatibility (Supplementary Text S1 and Fig. S1)25–27. Thus, this local stress amplification in the NCI region is expected to create widespread sites for, and enhance the nucleation efficiency, of dislocations and stacking-faults (SFs), which dynamically refine the microstructure along with tensile deformation and crack propagation to sustain strain hardening, therefore achieving high UE and KJIc in combination with the high YS enabled by precipitates.

The primary challenge lies in fabricating interfaces with high H and ∇2H. By revisiting the Ostwald ripening process, we strategically froze the coalescence stage of neighboring NPs during aging28,29, reshaping conventional coherent interfaces. The resulting non-equilibrium, peanut-shaped L12 NPs exhibit negative Gaussian curvature due to the saddle-shaped geometry at the connecting neck (Fig. S1), while transitions from high/negative to positive curvature at their body generate pronounced curvature gradients—a stark contrast to spheroids with uniform/positive Gaussian curvature and negligible gradients. The modeling results below further suggest that the NCIs featuring high elastic energy and strain have the potential to serve as dislocation sources to proliferate dislocation debris during plastic deformation.

Results and discussion

Phase-field and molecular dynamics simulations

Thermodynamically, the driving force for precipitate coalescence (surface energy reduction) implies that negatively curved interfaces experience additional stress directed toward the matrix, further amplifying the local stress level. Phase-field (PF) simulation tracking NP evolution revealed that NCI regions possess high elastic energy density (U) and significant stress/strain gradients (φ), as shown in Fig. 1. The simulation examined three distinct precipitates: two closely spaced spheroidal pairs (A/B) and an isolated spheroid (C), as illustrated in Fig. 1a1–1a3. During thermal coarsening, a neck develops between the neighboring NPs (A/B) undergoing coalescence, resulting in the formation of peanut-shaped precipitates interconnected by NCIs where the curvature-center directs toward the matrix. By contrast, the curvature-center of the ordinary spheroid-C always directs toward the precipitates and features positive-curvature precipitate/matrix interfaces (PCIs). Obviously, a sufficiently small interparticle spacing would facilitate coalescence, and this morphological transformation arises from the overlapping diffusion fields between adjacent precipitates, driven by a unique coupling of constituent element diffusion fluxes toward and away from the NPs28. Detailed analysis of the elastic energy distribution reveals significant changes before and after coalescence (Fig. 1b1–1b3). Prior to coalescence, the NP/matrix interfaces exhibited relatively low elastic energy density. Following coalescence, however, substantial elastic energy concentration emerged specifically in the NCI regions (indicated by white arrows in Fig. 1b2, 1b3). The elastic energy re-distribution upon coalescence is a result of the interactions between misfit strain and local stress-field30. In contrast, the isolated spheroid-C maintained a homogeneous energy distribution with negligible variation throughout the simulation.

Figure 1b3, 1c and 1d semi-quantitatively display the normalized energy landscape regarding the precipitates, whose magnitude is plotted in a line profile across both the NCI and PCI (denoted by red and pink arrows in Fig. 1b3, respectively). The results reveal that the peak elastic energy (Up, 1.76 × 10−2) on the precipitate side of the NCI is approximately twice that of the PCI (8.91 × 10−3), as shown in Fig. 1c, 1d. Furthermore, the elastic potential (ΔU), the magnitude difference between peak-energy and valley-energy at the matrix-side, for the NCI (1.74 × 10−2) is about five times greater than that of the PCI (3.06 × 10−3). From the perspective of solid mechanics, the relationship U = σ′ε′/2 directly correlates the stored energy density with local stress (σ′) and strain (ε′) fields31. Therefore, we derived the equivalent stress/strain gradient (φ) at each interface by considering the ΔU distributed in a given physical-diffuse-interface width. The NCI exhibits a fourfold higher φ value (2.87 × 10−3) compared to the PCI (7.65 × 10−4). Due to such a pronounced stress concentration and strain gradient, these NCIs are expected to function as effective dislocation nucleation sites, as schematically depicted in Fig. 1e. This prediction is further confirmed by our molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, which utilizes the same physical model as the prior PF simulation. Consistent with the PF results, the NCI regions exhibit markedly higher initial shear strain (indicated by yellow arrows in Fig. 1f) compared to other sites prior to deformation. Upon loading, dislocations are observed to nucleate first at the NCI (Fig. 1g), demonstrating that NCIs serve as preferential nucleation sites for dislocations.

Manufacturing the negative-curvature-interfaced nanoprecipitates

We successfully achieved a high-density population of coalescence-stage peanut-shaped L12-NPs featuring NCIs, in a Ta (and Al)-modified MEA with a nominal composition of (NiCoCr)92Al6Ta2 (at%, denoted Al6Ta2 hereafter), as demonstrated in Fig. 2. This was realized through careful control of the aging protocol (750 °C for 1 h), as detailed in Text S2 and Figs. S2 and S3. Comparative analysis with an Al-only modified reference alloy ((NiCoCr)94Al6, denoted Al6) revealed that Ta plays two critical roles in NCI construction (Texts S3 and S4, Figs. S4–S8). First, as a potent stabilizer and former of L12, Ta enhances the number density (increased by 324%, shown later) and volume fraction (increased by 196%) while reducing interparticle spacing (decreased by 70%). This heightened NP population promotes NP coalescence. Second, the intrinsically low diffusivity of Ta kinetically slows down Ostwald-ripening (Figs. S5d, S6, and S7), thereby stabilizing the metastable peanut-shaped geometry and preventing rapid coalescence into spheroids.

Figure 2 presents a multiscale characterization of the NCI-structured Al6Ta2 alloy. Electron-backscatter-diffraction (EBSD) analysis reveals an equiaxed grain structure with an average grain size (dg) of ~6.2 μm (Fig. 2a). High-angle-annular-dark-field scanning transmission-electron-microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image (Fig. 2b) demonstrates a homogeneous distribution of predominantly interconnected peanut-shaped L12-NPs, which constitute over 90% of the total precipitate population. The nanoparticles are clearly distinguishable due to the pronounced Z-contrast of tantalum, and Fig. 2c displays a typical enlarged peanut-shaped precipitate. Figure 2c1–c3 presents the fast-Fourier-transform (FFT) patterns corresponding to the regions marked by the red dashed squares in Fig. 2c. These patterns clearly confirm the superlattice L12 structure in both the body and the neck region of the peanut-shaped precipitate. Quantitative analysis yields an average NP size in the body (dp) of ~7.1 nm, interparticle spacing of 2.7 nm, and number density (Nv) of ~8.9 × 1023 m−3, corresponding to a volume fraction of 16.6% (Figs. S6a1 and S8). Atomic-resolution imaging confirms the coherent matrix/precipitate interface with a minimal lattice misfit (0.31%) and absence of misfit dislocation (Fig. 2c). The corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping reveals elemental partitioning between the L12 and matrix, with Ni, Al, and Ta preferentially segregating to the L12 while other elements are enriched in the FCC matrix. Furthermore, chemical fluctuations are prevalent both within the L12 and the matrix on length scales from sub‑nanometer to a few nanometers (Fig. S9). The compositional fluctuation is significant in a multi-principal-element system, and often coupled with local chemical ordering that results from the negative mixing enthalpy of some constituent elements, which is large for Ni–Ta (and Ni–Al) pairs in this alloy32. These induce appreciable variation in the spatial distribution of the constituent elements. Three-dimensional architecture of the interconnected NP network is further elucidated through atom-probe-tomography (APT) reconstructions using Ni, Al, and Ta iso-concentration surfaces (Figs. 2d–2f and S10). The distinctive morphology is particularly evident in the magnified view of typical NPs featuring NCIs (Fig. 2f). The peanut geometry is similar to that in the neck region, where Gaussian curvature K < 0 (Fig. S1). The proxigram (Fig. 2i), which shows the compositional profile across the interface for all analyzed NPs, reveals elemental partitioning between the nanoparticles and the matrix, in agreement with the EDS results. The sub-hour aging and straightforward processing route enable industrial scalability and cost-effectiveness, offering a facile microstructural (interface) feature that can be readily controlled to engineer non-equilibrium alloys.

Mechanical properties at 77 K

Figure 3 presents the uniaxial tensile and fracture toughness properties of the NCI-structured Al6Ta2 MEA at 77 K. The YS of the Al6 reference alloy (783 ± 31 MPa) was elevated significantly to 1122 ± 26 MPa after adding Ta, respectively, for these two alloys containing spherical NPs (Figs. S4 and S11). However, this enhancement came at a substantial cost to ductility, with UE decreasing from 51.5 ± 1.8% to 32.7 ± 1.3% (corresponding to the engineering strain at peak stress). Upon introducing NCI, the Al6Ta2 alloy achieved a YS of 1258 ± 39 MPa and a UTS of 1800 ± 31 MPa (Fig. 3a), representing 65% and 36% improvements in strength, respectively, over the Al6 reference alloy, while retaining a similarly high UE of 45.1 ± 1.2% (total elongation, 49.6 ± 1.6%). Notably, the peanut-structured variant of the Al6Ta2 MEA, when compared with the spheroid-precipitate counterpart with comparable particle size and volume fraction (Fig. S11), exhibited a major improvement in UE and evident enhancement in strength. These substantial improvements underscore the crucial efficacy of the NCI nanostructure in upgrading the strength–ductility balance. Previous studies have indicated that non-spherical precipitates, such as those with a rod-like morphology, strengthen a material more effectively than their spherical counterparts33–35. This is attributed to a reduced effective inter-precipitate spacing for a given precipitate volume fraction when aspect ratios are higher, which increases the density of obstacles encountered by gliding dislocations. Consequently, the critical stress required for dislocation bowing or shearing through these obstacle arrays is elevated, resulting macroscopically in a higher YS. Therefore, the increased aspect ratio of the peanut-shaped precipitates is the primary reason for their marginally higher YS compared to the spheroidal-precipitate variant.

As mentioned earlier, a sustained high strain-hardening rate (SHR) is the key to high ductility and toughness36–39. The availability of sustainable SHR in NCI alloy is indeed confirmed in Fig. 3b. In general, the Al6 reference alloy exhibits a low SHR. The spherical-NP Al6Ta2 variant, although initially showing a comparable SHR with the NCI sample, also suffers from a rapid decline in SHR after ~20% true strain. In contrast, the NCI-structured Al6Ta2 (purple curve) MEA maintains a higher SHR over a larger strain regime, thereby enabling prolonged uniform strain. Clearly, the NCIs offer additional strain-hardening, which will be explained in more detail later.

The fracture toughness of the Al6Ta2 alloy was evaluated at 77 K using standard compact-tension (CT) specimens. The crack-resistance curve (Fig. 3c), plotting the nonlinear elastic energy release rate (JR) against crack extension (Δa), yields a crack-initiation toughness (KJIc) of 212.8 ± 3.5 MPa m1/2 for the peanut-structured Al6Ta2 alloy. This magnitude is even comparable to the renowned FeCoCrNiMn HEA1, demonstrating exceptional fracture resistance. Furthermore, the peanut-structured Al6Ta2 alloy exhibits a substantial ~74% improvement in toughness over its spheroid counterpart (KJIc: 122.5 ± 13.4 MPa m1/2). These results suggest that the NCI design also enhances the damage tolerance of this alloy.

The Ashby map in Fig. 3d involving high YS, (UTS × UE) product (~81 GPa %) demonstrates the exceptional strength–ductility synergy of our alloy, which contains peanut-shaped L12 NPs and is governed by SF-mediated deformation (discussed later). This performance surpasses that of HEAs strengthened by spherical L12 precipitates, as well as most TRIP-mediated HEAs12,40. Figure 3e, 3f involving high YS vs KJIc and UTS vs KJIc, respectively, further reveal the superior strength–toughness combination of the alloy. To the best of our knowledge, the NCI-engineered Al6Ta2 delivers the most desirable strength-ductility-toughness combination at 77 K among all reported alloys for cryogenic applications, including advanced TRIP/TWIP steels, high-Ni steels, 316 L/316LN stainless steels, titanium alloys, aluminum alloys, and HEAs (Tables S3 and S4). The above results collectively substantiate this advancement in mitigating the strength–ductility–toughness trade-off at 77 K in this work. This success positions our NCI-engineered Al6Ta2 MEA as a promising candidate for cryogenic applications.

NCIs as prolific dislocation sources

At the outset of this paper, we proposed to use the NCIs in peanut-shaped NPs as efficient dislocation sources throughout the deforming volume. This is further confirmed in detail via EBSD and TEM analysis of the deformed alloy tested at 77 K (Fig. 4).

Consistent with our predictions, HAADF-STEM imaging indeed revealed a high density of dislocations, preferentially located at NCIs (highlighted by azure rectangles in Fig. 4a1). While dislocations were also observed at PCIs (marked by white rectangles), their occurrence was significantly less frequent. Figure 4a2 presents a representative magnified view of a concave NCI region with associated dislocations, and Fig. 4a3 displays the corresponding atomic configuration of paired full and partial dislocations, as revealed by Burgers circuit analysis and corroborated by geometric phase analysis (GPA). These experimental (and simulation) findings confirm our hypothesis that NCIs serve as preferential dislocation nucleation sites during deformation (schematically illustrated in Fig. 1e), although it is rather challenging to quantitatively evaluate the NCI effects for the efficacy of dislocation nucleation yet. This mechanism is projected from our analysis of the curvature-driven stress, PF and MD simulation results. First, the elevated energy density at NCIs substantially reduces the energy barrier for dislocation nucleation. Second, the curvature-driven stress, coupled with the pronounced stress/strain gradient, offers additional help in reaching the threshold stress needed to nucleate dislocations25,27,41. Taking no account of the influences of local deformation and crystal orientation in the NCI alloy, the approximate onset stress for dislocation nucleation at the NCI was determined as ~1200 MPa (Fig. S12). While interfacial dislocation sources have been widely reported in metallic materials42–45, curvature-promoted dislocation nucleation has hitherto remained unreported, and this study provides its initial documentation.

To further validate the role of NCI as prolific dislocation sources, we tracked geometrically necessary dislocation (GND) density evolution for the peanut-shaped and spherical precipitate-hardened samples from low to high tensile strains (3%, 18%, and 33%), shown in Text S5 and Fig. S13. The results indicated that the GND density in the peanut-shaped precipitate sample is always higher than that in the spherical counterpart throughout plastic strain, confirming the crucial role of NCI on dislocation multiplication. The dislocation proliferation and SF configuration are further revealed in Fig. 4b, 4c via TEM analysis, as well as Figs. S14 and S15, which illustrate the microstructural evolution. At a low strain (~3%) in the tensile test, initial plasticity occurs primarily through planar-slip of a/2 〈110〉 dislocations, which frequently dissociate into a/6 〈112〉 Shockley partials (Fig. S15a). With increasing strain (~18%), activation of multiple slip systems leads to extensive intersections of SF-bundles (Figs. S15b, c), forming dense SF networks that intensify with further deformation to failure (~45%, Figs. 4b1, 4b2 and S15d). Concurrently, high-density immobile L–C locks gradually form through SF interactions (Figs. 4b2 and S15e). Quantitative analysis reveals a two-order-of-magnitude reduction in SF spacing (λ) from 305 ± 117 to 5.9 ± 2.2 nm with progressive straining (Fig. S14). These results demonstrate the SF-dominated deformation of NCI alloy at 77 K, contrasting with the full-dislocation-mediated plasticity at 298 K (Text S6 and Fig. S16). The NCI-enabled SF-debris dynamically refines the microstructure and extensively shears precipitates, sustaining strain hardening as discussed next.

Superior strain-hardening sustained by NCI-generated SF-debris

As is well known, the high SHR stems from the multiplication and accumulation of dislocations/SFs, along with the interactions with microstructural heterogeneities that further encourage dislocation entanglement46–50. Therefore, the dislocations/SFs proliferation assisted by NCIs, as revealed above, translates into a sustained high rate of strain hardening for the following two reasons. First, the NCI-enabled dislocation sources distributed everywhere generate dislocations and SFs at numerous sites, which not only spread out the plastic strain all over the deforming volume but also increase the chances for dislocation entanglement and reactions, producing sessile junctions that dynamically harden the alloy. Second, the role of ultra-dense SFs is particularly noteworthy in our alloy. In fact, previous research has established ultra-dense SFs as a key mechanistic contributor to strain hardening50–52. It was observed that the SF spacing λ in the NCI-sample (9.1 ± 3.2 nm) was significantly smaller than that in the spherical-NP containing sample (15.8 ± 4.1 nm) at an equivalent strain (~33%), despite both alloys having comparable precipitate-size and the same Al6Ta2 composition (Figs. 4b3 and S17). A higher density of SFs in the NCI alloy agrees with the above result of higher GND density. In fact, a comparative analysis further reveals that the NCIs enable a substantially higher density of SFs, with the minimum spacing as small as 5.9 nm (Fig. 4b3), than that observed in other HEAs containing conventional spheroids when SFs dominated their deformation upon fracture at 77 K (Fig. 4b3 and Table S5). This finding underscores the pivotal role of NCI as a dislocation/SF-enabler, which is crucial for the superior strain-hardening of this alloy (discussed later). Herein, the observed prevalence of partial dislocation activities (and hence profuse SFs) over full dislocations mainly arises from two aspects. Beyond the low-temperature effect2, the elemental re-partitioning induced by the precipitate formation (costing the Ni/Al while expelling Co elements) decreases the overall stacking fault energy (SFE) of materials. Beyond the low-temperature effect, the elemental re-partitioning induced by the precipitate formation (costing the Ni/Al while expelling Co elements) decreases the overall SFE of materials by ~29% (from ~31 mJ/m2 to ~20 mJ/m2, see Supplementary Methods S4.4). Furthermore, the pronounced chemical fluctuations—present in both the precipitates and matrix cause the spatially variable local SFE and thus provide a wide range of energy barriers for persistent nucleation of partial dislocations while restricting their propagation53,54.

As observed, these SF debris deposited along with tensile straining lead to continuously increasing intersection of SFs, substantially and dynamically refining the microstructure through faulting interfaces. Specific mechanisms of the ultra-dense SFs causing strain hardening include the nucleation of sessile dislocations via intersected unparallel SFs and the elastic repulsion between parallel SF nano-bundles51. Furthermore, the continuously intersected SFs throughout straining develop into denser and denser nanoscale SF-grids. They not only impede dislocation motion but also effectively store dislocations to enhance strain hardening50. Concurrently, the dual roles of abundant L–C locks—operating as Frank-Read dislocation sources to multiply dislocations while obstructing their movement—further amplify strain hardening by facilitating dislocation accumulation55,56.

Furthermore, the extensive SF/dislocation-precipitate interactions also significantly contribute to strain-hardening. HAADF-STEM images reveal pronounced shearing of SFs through the NPs (Figs. 4b, 4c and S18). In fact, the non-spherical morphology increases the specific interfacial area of peanut-shaped NPs (spheroids minimize surface area for a given volume). This geometry enables greater interface density, thereby increasing the probability of defect-precipitate interactions. Furthermore, previous modeling indicated that a higher aspect ratio of precipitates increases the SHR, for a given volume fraction of precipitates34. Our MD simulation (Fig. S19) further reveals that the mobile dislocation requires a higher critical shear stress for de-trapping from peanut-shaped precipitates (~390 MPa) compared to separated spheroidal precipitates (~340 MPa). Thus, the enhanced dislocation resistance in NCI-alloy causes more sluggish dislocation motion and improves the chances for dislocation interactions/interlocking. This encourages dislocation entanglement and accumulation, contributing to strain hardening.

Moreover, within the ordered precipitates, various deformation-induced planar defects are observed, including antiphase boundary (APB) and dominated complex intrinsic/extrinsic SFs (Figs. 4c2 and S18), arising from shearing by partial and full dislocations, respectively. The cutting process necessitates overcoming those high-energy barriers, leading to an increase in flow stress (strengthening)17,19. Crucially, the act of cutting generates residual defects (APBs, complex SFs, even locks) within the precipitates, refining them into stronger obstacles to subsequent dislocation motion. As plastic deformation progresses, the increasing accumulation of these residual defects delivers strain-hardening. Those mechanisms are more pronounced in NCI-alloy than in its spheroid counterpart, given the higher shearing probability (higher SF density).

Note that the precipitates are repeatedly sheared during straining, degrading the NCI structure after large strains. However, the NCI-enabled high-density SFs in early deformation stages have already been set up to promote the subsequent strain hardening. In comparison, the spherical-precipitate alloy exhibits a rapid decline in SHR at later stages (Fig. 3b), due to its low density of SFs.

High fracture toughness enabled by NCI nanostructure

Previous studies have conclusively demonstrated that the exceptional damage tolerance of ductile FCC-structured HEA systems primarily stems from their sustained strain-hardening capacity36. Our analysis clearly reveals that the NCI structure enables persistent, high strain-hardenability in this alloy. Therefore, the deforming volume around the crack also activated excellent strain hardenability within the plastic zone during fracture. This process not only dynamically hardens the crack tip but also effectively inhibits strain localization, thus preventing sudden crack extension and enhancing crack resistance7,36.

Further characterization of the deformed microstructure near the crack tip in CT specimens for both spherical and peanut-shaped precipitate-hardened alloys (Fig. S20 and Text S7) was conducted to elucidate the intrinsic toughening mechanism responsible for the higher fracture toughness of the NCI alloy. It is important to note that the mechanism of NCI-assisted dislocation generation is largely independent of loading mode. Consequently, extensive dislocation proliferation is also expected at the crack front in the NCI alloy. EBSD and TEM characterizations of the crack vicinity confirm a higher activated density of dislocations and SFs in the NCI alloy, which is crucial for its pronounced toughening. Specifically, the GND density in the peanut-shaped precipitate sample (4.2 × 1014 m−2) is approximately 83% higher than in the spherical precipitate sample (2.3 × 1014 m−2), accompanied by a smaller average SF spacing (~50 nm vs. ~80 nm). Furthermore, GNDs exhibit a more homogeneous distribution in the sample with peanut-shaped precipitates. This homogeneity results from the relatively low energy barriers for dislocation nucleation induced by NCIs within the deforming volume near the crack. Such a high defect density with uniform distribution facilitates the effective dissipation of strain energy and dispersion of localized damage away from the crack tip during fracture, helping to mitigate stress concentration and delay crack propagation. In addition to the NCI-induced increase in ordinary dislocations and SFs, the resulting higher probability of precipitate shearing further generates a greater density of high-energy planar faults, such as APBs and CSFs. The formation of these complex defects requires additional energy dissipation. Furthermore, these extensive plasticity activities promote crack-tip blunting (Fig. S20a4), which reduces the stress intensity ahead of the crack tip and thereby lowers the driving force for crack propagation36.

Beyond the defect-mediated toughening mechanisms previously outlined, the geometric effects introduced by the NCI nanostructure further enhance fracture resistance. First, simulations have demonstrated that the unique interface architecture combining NCIs and PCIs, resembling our peanut-shaped precipitates, homogenizes internal stress distribution57,58. This homogenization contributes to the observed uniform distribution of GNDs around the crack—in addition to the NCI-facilitated dislocation nucleation discussed earlier—by mitigating stress concentrations and absorbing additional strain energy under a given applied loading. Moreover, our MD result (Fig. S19) and prior studies have established that non-spherical precipitates strengthen a material more effectively than their spherical counterparts for a given precipitate volume fraction33–35. Consequently, the irregular precipitate morphology in our alloy strengthens the crack front zone, thereby increasing its resistance to cracking. As a result, a higher far-field load is required to drive unstable crack propagation in the NCI alloy, leading to improved fracture toughness. Collectively, the synergistic toughening effects enabled by the NCI nanostructure contribute to the exceptional fracture toughness of this peanut-structured Al6Ta2 alloy at 77 K.

In summary, this work presents an innovative interface design strategy that achieves a desirable synergy of strength, ductility, and toughness at cryogenic temperatures. The NCI design activates a unique dislocation-source mechanism governed by curvature effects, localized energy concentrations, and strain gradients. This mechanism generates an unusually large population of dislocations and especially SFs, creating debris that promotes strain hardening. In a nutshell, the NCI strategy makes precipitates more effective in sustaining a high SHR, to a level not far from that via conventional mechanisms such as TWIP/TRIP, while offering the YS benefits from precipitation hardening. The result is a set of superior cryogenic mechanical properties, combining GPa-level YS with large UE and serious damage tolerance. In general, this study redefines the conventional role of coherent precipitates—transforming them from passive strengthening contributors to actively tunable regulators of plastic deformation. Crucially, the proposed NCI strategy may exhibit universal applicability across precipitation-hardened systems, including superalloys, age-hardening steels, and aluminum alloys, where the connected-precipitates fraction can be judiciously tailored through controlled aging28. This straightforward approach offers a cost-effective and scalable pathway for designing high-performance structural materials for industrial applications.

Methods

Phase-field simulation

Phase-field simulations were employed for a semi-quantitative study of the precipitate evolution in terms of morphology and elastic energy density, and a model of Ni–Al system was utilized here. The thermodynamics of the γ/γ′ (i.e., FCC/L12) interface is treated within the framework of the Kim–Kim–Suzuki (KKS) model59. To provide alloy-specific input parameters to the PF model, thermodynamics and diffusivity data for γ and γ′ phases are derived from the Pandat database, see Supplementary Methods 4.1 for details.

Molecular dynamics simulation

MD simulations were carried out by the large-scale atomic/molecular massively parallel simulator (LAMMPS)60. The interactions among atoms were described with the embedded atom method (EAM)61, employing a potential parameter set developed by Farkas et al.62 for the NiCoCr-based HEAs. The matrix was modeled as a random NiCoCr solid solution, while the precipitates were represented by the Ni3Al-type L12 phase. Two groups of atomic models were constructed to investigate (i) atomic-level strain of peanut-shaped precipitates and dislocation nucleation behavior at NCI sites, and (ii) the critical stress of dislocation for de-trapping from peanut-shaped precipitates and isolated spherical precipitates, see Supplementary Methods 4.2 for details.

Materials preparation

The 5 kg grade (NiCoCr)92Al6Ta2 cast ingot was prepared by the vacuum levitation melt method using pure metals with a purity greater than 99.9 % under a highly pure argon atmosphere. The reference Al6 alloy ingot in 200 g was prepared by the arc-melting technique using highly pure metals under a protective argon atmosphere, and the ingot button was flipped over and re‑melted at least five times during the arc‑melting process to ensure compositional uniformity. Then, all ingots were homogenized at 1225 °C for 24 h, followed by water quenching. Afterward, the homogenized specimens were cold-rolled to a sheet with a thickness reduction of ~70%, followed by annealing at 1100 °C for 3 min to obtain a fully recrystallized microstructure. The recrystallized samples were then aged at 750 °C for various durations to introduce L12-precipitates with different morphologies, as detailed in Text S2.

Tensile properties and fracture toughness tests

For the tensile test, flat dog-bone shape samples of alloys with a gauge size of 8 × 2.0 × 1.5 mm3 were truncated from aged sheets along the rolling direction. Before testing, the surfaces of the gauge region were polished using 1000-grit sandpaper to eliminate scratches. Uniaxial tension with an extensometer at 298 and 77 K was performed on an Instron mechanical machine under a strain rate of 10−3 s−1. To evaluate the fracture toughness of the Al6Ta2 alloy at 77 K, the methodology based on the standard ASTM E1820-24 was employed using the standard compact-tension samples with a thickness of B = 7 mm and a width of W = 20 mm, approximately, see Supplementary Methods 4.3 for details. All the samples were immersed in liquid nitrogen and stabilized for about 10 min for subsequent cryogenic mechanical tests, and the samples were also always immersed in liquid nitrogen during the test. All the tests were repeated at least three times to guarantee data reliability.

Microstructural characterizations

EBSD scans under an acceleration voltage of 15 kV were carried out in a scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS Gemini 300) equipped with the Oxford Instruments-AZtecCrystal system. EBSD samples were first mechanically polished and then electrolytic-etched under 30 V for 20 to 40 s at room temperature using a mixed electrolyte of 90% ethanol with 10% perchloric acid. The GND density evolution was evaluated via EBSD experiments for Al6Ta2 samples from small to large tensile strains via interruption. Electron channeling contrast imaging (ECCI) was conducted to reveal the large-area deformation features at the grain scale for deformed samples. The detailed structures in microns, nanometers, and the atomic scale before and after deformation were mainly characterized by TEM (FEI Talos F200X and Spectra 300). The disks for TEM observation were initially mechanically ground to a thickness of 20–30 μm and subsequently subjected to ion beam milling using a Gatan precision ion polishing system (PIPS II 695), equipped with two Penning ion guns using argon ions for thinning and polishing. The ion milling process employed sequential beam voltages under 5 kV and 3 kV, with corresponding milling angles of ±6° and ±4° for the rough and fine milling, respectively. TEM foils for deformed microstructure observation were taken from the uniform deformation region in tensile samples. TEM foils at the crack-tip in compact–tension specimens after fracture toughness tests were taken at mid-thickness (B/2) to ensure that deformation conditions were in full plane strain. The average diameter and aspect ratio of precipitates were determined based on several HAADF-STEM images63. SF spacing λ was also statistically determined from several representative TEM images for the cryogenically deformed samples. The neutron diffraction experiment for the bulk samples was conducted on the time-of-flight high-resolution neutron diffractometer (TREND) (https://csns.cn/31113.02.CSNS.TREND) at China Spallation Neutron Source (CSNS) (https://csns.cn/31113.02.CSNS). The Rietveld refinement on neutron diffraction patterns was performed on the GSAS-II software to derive the volume fraction of precipitates for representative samples18,64. Chemical compositions and precipitate reconstruction were analyzed using APT in a local electrode atom probe CAMECA LEAP 4000X SI and a 6000 INVIZO. Needle-shaped APT samples were fabricated by Thermo Fisher Helios plasma-focused-ion-beam SEM. The APT experiment was conducted under a laser pulse frequency of 200 kHz, a laser pulse energy of 50 pJ (in 4000) or 100 pJ (in INVIZO). APT data were reconstructed using a CAMECA integrated visualization and analysis software (IVAS 6.3).
