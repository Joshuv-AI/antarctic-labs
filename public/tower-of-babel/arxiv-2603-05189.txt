Small Changes, Big Impact: Demographic Bias in LLM-Based Hiring Through Subtle Sociocultural Markers in Anonymised Resumes
Bryan Chen Zhengyu Tan 1,4 Shaun Khoo 2 Bich Ngoc Doan 3 Zhengyuan Liu 4 Nancy F. Chen 4 Roy Ka-Wei Lee 1,2 1 Singapore University of Technology and Design (SUTD) 2 GovTech Singapore 3 EPFL 4 Agency for Science, Technology and Research (A*STAR), Singapore

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in resume screening pipelines. Although explicit PII (e.g., names) is commonly redacted, resumes typically retain subtle sociocultural markers (languages, co-curricular activities, volunteering, hobbies) that can act as demographic proxies. We introduce a generalisable stress-test framework for hiring fairness, instantiated in the Singapore context: 100 100 neutral job-aligned resumes are augmented into 4100 4100 variants spanning four ethnicities and two genders, differing only in job-irrelevant markers. We evaluate 18 18 LLMs in two realistic settings: (i) Direct Comparison (1v1) and (ii) Score & Shortlist (top-scoring rate), each with and without rationale prompting. Even without explicit identifiers, models recover demographic attributes with high F1 and exhibit systematic disparities, with models favouring markers associated with Chinese and Caucasian males. Ablations show language markers suffice for ethnicity inference, whereas gender relies on hobbies and activities. Furthermore, prompting for explanations tends to amplify bias. Our findings suggest that seemingly innocuous markers surviving anonymisation can materially skew automated hiring outcomes.

Research Questions.

We organise our investigation around three core research questions:

RQ1 (Demographic Recoverability):
Can LLMs infer gender and ethnicity from anonymised resumes containing only sociocultural markers, and which marker categories drive this inference?

RQ2 (Outcome Bias):
How do demographic markers alter hiring outcomes under two complementary evaluation paradigms: (a)
Direct Comparison
(a 1v1 forced-choice between two candidates) and (b)
Score & Shortlist
(independent scoring of a pool to find top-scoring candidates), and how sensitive are these outcomes to prompt variations?

RQ3 (Demographic Disparities):
Which demographic groups receive the most and least favourable treatment, and how do these disparities manifest across models and settings?

Methodology

Our experimental framework comprises four sequential stages: (1) job description curation and neutral resume generation, (2) demographic marker injection, (3) bias evaluation under dual settings, and (4) demographic recoverability and ablation analysis. This design ensures that any observed disparities can be attributed solely to the injected sociocultural markers, as all job-relevant qualifications remain constant across demographic variants.

3.1
Job Descriptions and Resume Generation

We curated
100

engineering
,

,
healthcare

retail
, and
services

We standardised job descriptions to specify required qualifications, experience levels, and core competencies, controlling for stylistic variation while preserving authentic role requirements.

For each job description, we generated a single “neutral” baseline resume using a state-of-the-art LLM (gemini-2.5-pro), instructed to produce realistic candidates with minor, plausible weaknesses (slightly less experience than required, or missing one secondary skill). These controlled imperfections prevent ceiling effects: For example, if baselines scored 100/100, marker injection could only reveal disadvantages, precluding detection of demographic-specific
advantages
. Each neutral resume contains placeholder fields in an “Additional Information” section: [LANGUAGES], [ACTIVITIES], [VOLUNTEERING], and [HOBBIES]. These placeholders serve as injection points for demographic markers while ensuring that the core job-relevant content (education, work experience, skills) remains identical across all variants. Examples of job descriptions and generated baseline resumes can be found in Appendix
B

3.4
Evaluation Settings

We utilise two complementary evaluation settings that attempt to mirror realistic deployment scenarios for LLM-based hiring tools.

3.4.1
Setting A: Direct Comparison (1v1)

This setting simulates a head-to-head candidate comparison: recruiters or AI systems must choose between two applicants. For each demographic-augmented resume, we construct a comparison task against its neutral baseline (same job, identical job-relevant qualifications). To control for position bias, we run each comparison twice with positions swapped (A=augmented, B=neutral; then A=neutral, B=augmented) and average the results.

The LLM judge outputs a verdict:
A
(prefers first resume),
B
(prefers second resume), or
Tie
. We convert verdicts to points (win=1.0, tie=0.5, loss=0.0) and compute per-comparison win-rates as the mean across both positions. The
ideal win-rate is 0.5
for every demographic group: since augmented and neutral resumes are equivalent in job-relevant content, a fair evaluator should show no systematic preference. Deviation from 0.5 indicates bias induced by the presence (or absence) of demographic-associated sociocultural markers. However, this setting inherently frames evaluation as a choice between two candidates, which may not reflect real-world hiring workflows where multiple candidates are evaluated independently
(
10
)
from a large pool of applicants.

3.4.2
Setting B: Score & Shortlist

This setting simulates shortlisting based on scoring and ranking, reflecting hiring workflows where a pool of candidates is scored independently and top candidates are shortlisted.
Each resume is scored on a 1 to 100 scale against its job description.

For each job, we identify all resumes achieving the maximum score (ties permitted) out of the
40
augmented and
1
neutral resumes. We then compute
top-score appearance rates
for each demographic group across all jobs. The
ideal appearance rate is 100%
: if demographic markers are truly job-irrelevant, all
41
resumes for a given job should tie at the maximum score. Disparities in appearance rates quantify opportunity loss due to demographic bias
(
27
)
.

3.4.3
Prompt Sensitivity Analysis

Stakeholders deploying AI hiring systems increasingly demand interpretability that spans multi-stage decision pipelines and supports transparency, contestability, and auditability
(
52
)
. Rationale-augmented outputs provide a seemingly accessible form of explanation, though recent work demonstrates that chain-of-thought explanations can be unfaithful, rationalising biased decisions without revealing their true causes
(
56
)
. This tension motivates our comparison of prompts with and without rationale requirements.

Each evaluation setting comprises two prompt variants:

No rationale:
The prompt requests only the verdict or score.

With rationale:
The prompt requests a 2–3 sentence explanation before the verdict or score (e.g., “Explain your reasoning in 2–3 sentences, then provide your verdict.”).

This design tests whether chain-of-thought style prompting, often advocated as a means to improve reasoning quality
(
20
)
and interpretability, affects bias magnitude or direction. Temperature is set to 0 for all experiments to maximise reproducibility; small-scale experiments confirmed that temperature=0 yields stable results with largely minimal variance across repeated runs (see Appendix
D
for prompt templates and Appendix
F.2
for temperature stability analysis).

3.5
Model-Level Bias Metrics

We employ two model-level metrics:

Normalised Demographic Performance Disparity:
The gap between the highest- and lowest-performing demographic groups
g
, normalised to lie in
[0,1]
. For
Direct Comparison (1v1)
setting, this is the range of group win-rates:
\max_{g}\mathrm{WR}_{g}-\min_{g}\mathrm{WR}_{g}
(since
\mathrm{WR}\in[0,1]
). For
Score & Shortlist
setting, this is the range of top-score appearance (in %):
(\max_{g}\mathrm{TopTier}_{g}-\min_{g}\mathrm{TopTier}_{g})/100
.

Normalised Deviation from Ideal:
How far the macro-average is from the ideal, normalised to lie in
[0,1]
. For
Direct Comparison (1v1)
setting, the ideal win-rate is 0.5 and we compute
|\overline{\mathrm{WR}}-0.5|/0.5
. For
Score & Shortlist
setting, the ideal top-score appearance is 100% and we compute
|\overline{\mathrm{TopTier}}-100|/100
.

The choice of ideal values reflects our core assumption that the injected sociocultural markers (languages, activities, volunteering, hobbies) are
job-irrelevant
and should not affect hiring suitability. For the first setting, an ideal win-rate of 0.5 means augmented resumes are selected at the same rate as neutral baselines, indicating that demographic markers confer neither advantage nor disadvantage. For the second setting, an ideal top-score appearance (the proportion of resumes achieving the maximum score per job after independent scoring) of 100% means all
41
variants for a given job tie at the maximum score. We assume that in practice, hiring systems often shortlist only top-scoring candidates; any deviation from 100% top-score appearance indicates that demographic markers induced score variation where none should exist.

3.6
Demographic Recoverability and Ablation

To validate that injected markers are effective demographic proxies, we conduct recoverability experiments: LLMs are prompted to classify the gender and ethnicity of candidates based solely on resume content. High accuracy indicates that markers encode recoverable demographic stereotypes.

We further conduct ablation studies to identify which marker categories drive demographic inference. Starting from fully-marked resumes, we progressively remove marker categories (hobbies→volunteering→activities→languages) and measure the impact on recoverability F1 scores for gender and ethnicity separately. This reveals the relative importance of each marker type for different demographic dimensions.

Human Validation

To validate realism and provide a human baseline, we ran a small study with 32 volunteer annotators: a brief resume quality check, followed by a five-step progressive revelation protocol (Step 0: no “Additional Information”; Steps 1–3: three marker categories revealed in random order; Step 4: all markers revealed, with languages always last; Appendix
G
).

5
Discussion

Implications for Hiring Practices.

Our findings demonstrate that
anonymisation limited to explicit PII is insufficient
for ensuring fair AI-assisted hiring.
Sociolinguistic cues that routinely survive redaction (such as language and culturally-relevant resume content) enable implicit demographic inference and can propagate into outcome disparities.
Organisations that seek to deploy debiased LLM-based hiring tools should:
(1) Conduct demographic stress-tests using controlled resume variants prior to deployment;
(2) Prefer models with low disparity and deviation from ideal, validated on demographically representative test sets;
(3) Recognise that certain settings like
rankings derived from scores can amplify small preference differences
into binary inclusion/exclusion decisions;
(4) Implement ongoing monitoring and auditing to detect bias drift as models are updated.

The Limits of Prompting for Explanations.

In practice, prompting for explanations is typically used for interpretability or to elicit more careful reasoning, rather than as an explicit fairness intervention. In our setting, this did not reliably improve fairness. On average across models, requiring brief rationales increases deviation from the ideal in both
Direct Comparison (1v1)
(deviation
0.198\rightarrow 0.288
, disparity
0.063\rightarrow 0.066
) and
Score & Shortlist
(deviation
0.730\rightarrow 0.772
, disparity
0.062\rightarrow 0.071
) (Figures
3
and
4
). This pattern is consistent with prior works which assert that post-hoc explanations can preserve or even amplify stereotypes rather than correcting them
(
56
;
63
)
. Effective mitigation likely requires more than prompt-engineering, such as model-level interventions (data curation, fine-tuning, or alignment) or system-level controls (calibration and post-hoc ranking constraints).

Generalisability of the Framework.

While our empirical results are grounded in the Singapore context,
the experimental framework generalises to any setting
where demographic groups exhibit distinguishable sociocultural patterns. The methodology requires: (1) defining demographic baskets relevant to the target context; (2) developing culturally authentic job-irrelevant marker variations through community consultation; (3) adapting job descriptions to local labour markets. We encourage researchers to replicate our approach in diverse cultural contexts to build a comparative understanding of how LLM hiring biases vary across societies.

6
Conclusion

We show that subtle sociocultural markers surviving anonymisation enable LLMs to infer demographic attributes and can induce measurable disparities in automated hiring outcomes. Evaluating
18
models across dual settings (win-rate and scoring-to-ranking), we find: (1) languages primarily drive ethnicity inference whilst hobbies/activities drive gender inference; (2) Chinese and Caucasian males are systematically favoured, whilst Malay and Indian females experience compounded intersectional disadvantage; (3) rationale prompting does not reliably reduce bias and can amplify it in certain models; (4) substantial heterogeneity across models necessitates per-deployment audits. Practitioners should combine stress-testing with demographic variants, careful model selection, ongoing bias monitoring, and policy safeguards to prevent demographic harm in LLM-assisted hiring. Our framework is flexible and can be extended to diverse cultural contexts worldwide.

Limitations

We interpret our findings within the specific context of Singapore’s demographic landscape. While the experimental framework is transferable, the empirical results reflect local sociocultural dynamics and may differ in regions with alternative ethnic compositions. Furthermore, our current analysis operationalises gender as binary to establish a baseline for measurement; we acknowledge that this simplifies the spectrum of gender identities and suggest that future research extends to non-binary frameworks. Methodologically, we utilise controlled, LLM-generated resumes to ensure comparability. While we introduced realistic weaknesses to mimic authentic candidates, these documents provide a standardised approximation rather than the full unstructured variability of human-authored resumes. Similarly, our demographic markers were selected to function as distinct stress-tests; consequently, the magnitude of observed disparities reflects a response to clear sociocultural signals, which may differ from the response to potentially subtler markers in naturally occurring data. Finally, our evaluation captures single-turn decision-making at a specific point in time; given the rapid update cycles of large language models, behaviour in interactive workflows or future model versions may evolve.

Ethical Considerations

We deliberately inject stereotypical demographic cues into synthetic resumes to surface vulnerabilities in ostensibly anonymised hiring workflows. This adversarial testing approach is intended to
identify
bias so that it can be mitigated, not to provide a blueprint for discriminatory deployment. The demographic variations we develop encode cultural stereotypes intentionally; we acknowledge the representational harms inherent in stereotyping and emphasise that our markers are designed as bias probes rather than accurate characterisations of demographic groups.

Our study uses “Caucasian” in experiments. We recognise this term has problematic historical origins in outdated racial classification systems
48
. Contemporary demographic reporting uses White” as a more neutral descriptor. We retained “Caucasian” to ensure consistency across our prompts and experimental instruments; future research should adopt updated terminology. Additionally, our framework occasionally uses “Tamil” and “Indian” interchangeably when referring to Singapore’s broader Indian ethnic category. Whilst Tamil represents one specific South Asian group, our markers capture stereotypical associations with the wider Indian demographic as defined in Singapore’s multiethnic context
28
. This oversimplification does not reflect the rich diversity within South Asian communities.

Our findings should inform model selection, auditing practices, and policy frameworks for fair AI hiring; they should not be used to justify or enable discriminatory practices. Deployment of AI hiring tools must comply with applicable employment law, anti-discrimination regulations, and emerging AI governance frameworks.

Appendix G
Human Validation

We conducted a parallel progressive revelation study with 32 human annotators (volunteers within our organisation) to validate the realism of our LLM-generated resumes and establish a human baseline for comparison. Annotators completed two tasks: (i) a resume quality check assessing authenticity, and (ii) a five-step progressive revelation task for demographic inference.

Study Design.

Each annotator fully annotated 5 resumes, yielding 160 total resume-level assessments. For each resume, the annotator first completed a quality check (Figure
19
), rating whether the resume “Looks okay” or “Looks unusual.” Next, the annotator proceeded through a five-step progressive revelation protocol in which the same resume was displayed with incrementally more “Additional Information” revealed:

1.

Step 0
: No additional information (Figure
20
)

2.

Step 1
: First marker revealed (Figure
21
)

3.

Step 2
: Two markers revealed

4.

Step 3
: Three markers revealed

5.

Step 4
: All four markers (including languages)

The order of marker categories (hobbies, volunteering, activities) was randomised across annotators, with languages always revealed last. This randomisation controls for order effects while ensuring that the final step always includes linguistic cues, which our LLM ablation identified as the primary driver of ethnicity inference.
At each step, annotators guessed the candidate’s gender (Male / Female / Cannot determine) and ethnicity (Chinese / Malay / Indian / Caucasian / Cannot determine).

Authenticity Results.

Of 160 authenticity assessments, 88.8% rated resumes as realistic (“Looks okay”), confirming that LLM-generated resumes pass human scrutiny and can serve as valid experimental stimuli.

Demographic Recoverability Results.

Human demographic recoverability exhibited a pattern consistent with LLM performance (Figure
17
). Starting from minimal information (Step 0, no markers), humans achieved low performance (gender F1 = 0.42, ethnicity F1 = 0.20). As markers were progressively revealed, performance improved monotonically. With all four markers revealed (Step 4), human accuracy matched or exceeded LLM performance (gender F1 = 0.96, ethnicity F1 = 1.00). The “Cannot determine” rate decreased correspondingly: for ethnicity, it dropped from 87.5% (Step 0) to 3.8% (Step 4); for gender, from 78.1% to 22.5% (Class-level trends in Figure
18
).

This convergence of human and LLM performance to near-perfect accuracy validates that these sociocultural markers constitute genuine demographic proxies embedded in both cultural knowledge and algorithmic training. The results also suggest that effective anonymisation would require removing not just names and explicit identifiers, but also linguistically informative content and personal-interest information; interventions that may substantially reduce resume informativeness for legitimate evaluation purposes.
