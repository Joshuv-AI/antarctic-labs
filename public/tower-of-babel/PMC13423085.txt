What are the symptom heterogeneity and network characteristics among lung cancer survivors in China? A cross-sectional latent profile and network analysis
Authors: Huxing Cao, Xiaolong Wang, Yufei Li, Ailin Zhang, Shengchang Ye, Nan Dang, Cuiwen Tian, Guihua Hao, Junjun Cao, Qiaoqiao Ma, Lili Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify latent symptom subgroups, compare symptom network characteristics across subgroups and examine factors associated with subgroup membership among lung cancer survivors.DesignCross-sectional study using latent profile analysis and symptom network analysis.SettingFour hospitals in Shanghai, China, including a national thoracic oncology centre, two tertiary general hospitals and one regional general hospital.ParticipantsA total of 942 lung cancer survivors who had completed surgical treatment or received at least one course of initial antitumour therapy and were in a stable follow-up phase or treatment interval. The mean age was 64.43±10.70 years and 65.10% were male.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were latent symptom subgroups identified by latent profile analysis and symptom network characteristics derived from partial correlation networks. Secondary outcomes were socio-demographic, clinical, functional and psychosocial factors associated with subgroup membership, assessed using multivariable multinomial logistic regression following least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) variable selection.ResultsThree symptom subgroups were identified: a low-symptom group (n=488, 51.80%), a moderate-symptom group (n=364, 38.64%) and a high-symptom group (n=90, 9.55%). Network density was descriptively higher in the high-symptom group than in the low-symptom group (0.468 vs 0.175). Central symptoms differed across subgroups, with cough in the low-symptom group, vomiting in the moderate-symptom group and distress in the high-symptom group. After LASSO selection and collinearity assessment, 22 predictors were entered into the final multivariable multinomial logistic regression model. Compared with the low-symptom group, surgery with adjuvant therapy was associated with higher odds of membership in the moderate-symptom group (adjusted OR (aOR)=4.054, 95% CI 2.094 to 7.850), whereas better exercise capacity was associated with lower odds of membership in the high-symptom group (6-minute walk distance ≥450 m: aOR=0.101, 95% CI 0.027 to 0.372). The final model had a Nagelkerke pseudo-R² of 0.522.ConclusionsSymptom burden among lung cancer survivors is heterogeneous and differs in both severity profiles and network structure. Integrating latent profile analysis with symptom network analysis may provide a useful framework for stratified symptom assessment and individualised symptom management in survivorship care.Trial registration numberMR-31-24-027806 (https://www.medicalresearch.org.cn/clinicalResearch/researchInfo?id=eb897346-a67b-4465-a553-1d1a3488bc30).Keywords: Cancer Survivors, China, Chronic Disease, Clinical Relevance, Cross-Sectional Studies, Patient-Centered Care

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related death in China, imposing a substantial burden on patients, families and the healthcare system.1 Advances in early detection, surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy have improved survival, leading to a growing population of lung cancer survivors.2 3 Cancer survivorship is commonly conceptualised as beginning at diagnosis and continuing throughout the remainder of life, including periods of initial treatment, transition after treatment and long-term survival.3 In this study, lung cancer survivors refer to clinically stable patients with histologically or cytologically confirmed primary lung cancer who were in the follow-up phase or a treatment interval after completing surgery or at least one course of initial antitumour therapy. However, longer survival does not necessarily mean lower symptom burden. Many patients continue to experience fatigue, pain, cough, dyspnoea, nausea and vomiting and emotional distress, all of which may persist over time and adversely affect treatment tolerance, functional status and quality of life.4 5

Symptom experience in lung cancer survivors is rarely limited to isolated symptoms. Instead, patients often experience multiple co-occurring and interacting symptoms. Previous studies have shown that lung cancer-related symptoms often occur as symptom clusters, and that interactions among symptoms may amplify overall burden and lead to poorer clinical outcomes.6 7 Symptom patterns are also not randomly distributed across patients,8 but are associated with disease characteristics, treatment exposure, functional status, psychosocial factors and health behaviours.9–11 Therefore, focusing only on overall symptom severity may be insufficient to capture the complexity of symptom experience and the heterogeneity within this population.

Traditional symptom research has largely relied on variable-centred approaches, such as factor analysis and cluster analysis.12 These approaches are useful for identifying co-occurring symptoms, but they are limited in distinguishing latent symptom phenotypes within patient populations.13 Latent profile analysis is a person-centred approach that identifies distinct subgroups based on individuals’ response patterns across multiple symptom dimensions, providing a more refined understanding of symptom heterogeneity.14 Examining factors associated with subgroup membership may further clarify the clinical and psychosocial conditions linked to specific symptom patterns. However, subgroup identification alone does not explain how symptoms interact within each subgroup.

Symptom network analysis conceptualises symptoms as interconnected nodes and examines relationships among them to reveal the internal structure of symptom systems.15 16 Compared with traditional symptom cluster approaches, network analysis provides a more direct representation of symptom inter-relationships and may help identify symptoms that warrant closer clinical attention.17 18 Latent profile analysis and symptom network analysis are therefore complementary. The former identifies types of patients, while the latter describes how symptoms are organised within each subgroup. Integrating these two approaches may provide a more comprehensive understanding of symptom heterogeneity among lung cancer survivors.

To date, research on symptom burden in lung cancer has focused mainly on individual symptom clusters, overall symptom levels or specific treatment phases. Few studies have simultaneously identified latent symptom subgroups among treated lung cancer survivors, compared symptom network structures across subgroups and examined associated clinical and socio-demographic factors. Without considering both subgroup heterogeneity and symptom network structure, current approaches may overlook how symptom burden differs across patients and how symptoms are organised within distinct profiles. Therefore, this study aimed to identify latent symptom subgroups among treated lung cancer survivors, compare symptom network characteristics across subgroups and examine clinical and socio-demographic factors associated with subgroup membership, with the goal of informing stratified symptom assessment and individualised symptom management.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study used a convenience sampling approach to recruit lung cancer survivors between February 2023 and November 2024 from four hospitals in Shanghai, China, including a national thoracic oncology centre, two tertiary general hospitals and one regional general hospital. Because participants were recruited using a non-probability sampling design, the sample was not weighted and was not intended to be statistically representative of all lung cancer survivors in Shanghai or China. Sampling weights were not applied because no appropriate external sampling frame was available for the joint distribution of key characteristics among treated lung cancer survivors, including age, sex, disease stage, treatment modality and hospital source. Applying weights under these conditions would require strong assumptions and could introduce additional bias.

For participant selection, lung cancer survivors were operationally defined as clinically stable adults with histologically or cytologically confirmed primary lung cancer who had completed surgical treatment or received at least one course of initial antitumour therapy and were in the follow-up phase or a treatment interval at the time of symptom assessment.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) histologically or cytologically confirmed primary lung cancer; (3) completion of surgical treatment or at least one course of initial antitumour therapy, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy or targeted therapy; (4) being clinically stable in the follow-up phase or during a treatment interval at the time of symptom assessment; (5) no severe cardiac, hepatic or renal failure and no significant cognitive impairment, with the ability to understand and complete the questionnaire; and (6) ability to perform basic daily activities and ambulate independently or with minimal assistance. The exclusion criteria included the presence of other active malignancies that limited physical activity or the occurrence of severe complications during the follow-up period.

Sample size was estimated according to Kendall’s principle, which recommends 5–10 participants per estimated parameter.19 The latent profile analysis (LPA) model included 32 estimated parameters; therefore, at least 320 participants were required. Allowing for a 10% invalid response rate, the target sample size was 352.

Measures

Standardised instruments and objective functional assessments were used to collect data on socio-demographic and clinical characteristics, symptom burden, psychological status, social support, health behaviour self-efficacy and physical activity.

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

A self-developed questionnaire was used to collect socio-demographic, lifestyle, clinical, treatment-related and functional information. A copy of this questionnaire is provided in online supplemental appendix C.

Symptom burden

Symptom burden was assessed using the MD Anderson Symptom Inventory (MDASI) and its validated Chinese lung cancer module.20 The scale consists of 19 symptom items, each rated on an 11-point numerical scale from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. For the latent profile analysis, these symptoms were grouped into five predefined analytical symptom domains based on symptom content, clinical relevance and previous symptom-cluster literature, rather than derived post hoc from the present dataset.21 22 The five domains were systemic symptoms (pain, fatigue, sleep disturbance, weight loss), emotional/cognitive symptoms (distress, sadness, forgetfulness), lung cancer-specific respiratory symptoms (dyspnoea, cough, sputum production, haemoptysis, chest tightness), treatment-related gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, poor appetite, dry mouth, constipation) and treatment-related neurotoxicity symptoms (drowsiness, numbness). This grouping was intended to reduce the number of indicators in the latent profile analysis, improve model parsimony and enhance the clinical interpretability of the latent profiles. To retain symptom-level information, all 19 individual symptom items were subsequently included as nodes in the symptom network analysis. The Chinese version has demonstrated good validity (content validity index=0.944) and internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.733).

Psychological status

Anxiety and depression were assessed using the Chinese version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).23 The scale includes 14 items, with 2 subscales measuring anxiety and depression, each consisting of 7 items. Each item is rated on a 4-point scale, with subscale scores ranging from 0 to 21. Scores of 0–7 indicate no symptoms, 8–10 indicate possible symptoms and 11–21 indicate definite symptoms. The Chinese version has shown good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.879).

Social support

Social support was measured using the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS).24 The scale includes 10 items across three dimensions: objective support, subjective support and support utilisation. Total scores range from 11 to 60, with higher scores indicating higher levels of social support. The scale has demonstrated good test–retest reliability (r=0.920).

Health behaviour self-efficacy

Health behaviour self-efficacy was assessed using the Chinese version of the Self-Rated Abilities for Health Practices Scale (SRAHP).25 The scale consists of 28 items covering four domains: nutrition, exercise, health responsibility and psychological well-being. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale from 0 to 4, with total scores ranging from 0 to 112. Higher scores indicate greater self-efficacy.

Physical activity

Physical activity was assessed using the Chinese version of the long form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ).26 The questionnaire captures physical activity over the past 7 days across four domains: occupational, transportation, household and leisure-time activities. All activities were converted into metabolic equivalent minutes per week according to standardised IPAQ scoring guidelines.27 The test–retest reliability ranged from 0.674 to 0.934.

Data collection

Data were collected using a standardised electronic questionnaire. Each hospital designated a principal investigator and trained research staff, all of whom received standardised training prior to data collection. Eligible patients were informed about the study objectives and their rights, and written informed consent was obtained. Socio-demographic information was collected within 24 hours of admission. Objective measurements, including body mass index (BMI), handgrip strength, 6-minute walk distance (6MWT) and lung function, were assessed by trained investigators and clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical record system. Participants completed the MDASI, HADS, SSRS and SRAHP before discharge or during a clinically stable follow-up or treatment-interval stage. Symptom burden was not assessed at initial diagnosis or during acute severe complications. For patients receiving chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy or targeted therapy, questionnaire assessment was arranged during routine follow-up, between treatment sessions, or before the next scheduled treatment whenever feasible, to reduce the influence of acute treatment-related fluctuations. Physical activity was assessed by telephone 1 month after discharge using the IPAQ, with timing adjusted according to treatment intervals when necessary.

To ensure data quality, all questionnaires were reviewed for completeness before submission. Obvious inconsistencies were checked with participants when possible, and unresolved missing, inconsistent or implausible values were handled according to the predefined data-cleaning procedures described in the Statistical analysis section.

Statistical analysis

All data were cleaned, coded and checked for completeness, range errors and implausible values before analysis. Questionnaires with substantial missing data, defined as incomplete questionnaires or questionnaires with more than 10% missing items in the predefined study dataset, were excluded before analysis. Participants with missing 1-month physical activity assessment data were excluded because the missing follow-up physical activity module resulted in more than 10% missing items in the predefined dataset. For continuous variables, values beyond ±3 SDs were regarded as implausible and recoded as missing. The plausibility of the missing-at-random assumption was considered based on the source and pattern of missingness. Most missing values were due to item non-response or implausible values identified during data cleaning, and missingness was judged to be conditionally compatible with the missing-at-random assumption after accounting for observed socio-demographic, clinical, functional, psychosocial and symptom variables.

Remaining missing values in variables included in the final analyses were handled using multiple imputation in SPSS. Multiple imputation was performed using fully conditional specification. Five imputed datasets were generated with 10 iterations, and the imputation model included socio-demographic, clinical, functional, psychosocial and symptom variables used in the final analyses. Estimates from the imputed datasets were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. After data cleaning and imputation, no missing values remained for the variables included in the latent profile analysis, multivariable multinomial logistic regression and symptom network analysis. To assess the robustness of the missing-data handling approach, a complete-case sensitivity analysis was conducted by repeating the final multivariable multinomial logistic regression model among participants with complete data for the outcome variable and all predictors retained after least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) selection and collinearity assessment. The same model specification and reference group were used as in the primary imputed-data analysis. Descriptive and inferential analyses were performed using SPSS V.25.0. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed visually using histograms and Q–Q plots and was supplemented by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were presented as median and IQR, while categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages.

Latent profile analysis was conducted using Mplus V.8.3, with the mean scores of the five predefined analytical symptom domains as indicators. Models with increasing numbers of classes were estimated sequentially from a one-class solution. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion, sample-size-adjusted BIC, entropy, the Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test. The final model was selected based on statistical fit, classification accuracy, theoretical interpretability, class size and clinical relevance.

Univariate analyses were used to identify candidate variables associated with symptom subgroup membership at p<0.10. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for continuous variables, and χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables, as appropriate. Because physical activity was assessed 1 month after discharge and was not temporally aligned with symptom assessment, it was described only as a subgroup characteristic and was not included in the regression analyses.

A multinomial LASSO regression model was constructed using the glmnet package in R to further select candidate variables identified in univariate analyses. 10-fold cross-validation was applied, and the optimal penalty parameter was determined based on the minimum binomial deviance. To reduce multicollinearity, only subscale scores of psychometric instruments were included, while total scores were excluded. Collinearity among predictors retained after LASSO selection was further assessed using variance inflation factors, and variables showing structural overlap were not retained simultaneously in the final model. Predictors retained after LASSO selection and collinearity assessment were then entered simultaneously into a multivariable multinomial logistic regression model to estimate adjusted ORs (aORs) and 95% CIs, with the low-symptom group used as the reference category.

Symptom network analysis was conducted using R V.4.5.2 with the qgraph and bootnet packages. Before network estimation, symptom scores were adjusted for 17 socio-demographic, clinical, treatment-related and functional covariates by extracting regression residuals. The adjusted covariates included age, BMI, education level, living status, employment status, occupational type, monthly household income, smoking status, alcohol consumption, lung function, 6-minute walk distance, cancer stage, lymph node stage, distant metastasis status, treatment modality, surgical approach and type of resection. These covariates were selected because they were clinically relevant and could potentially influence symptom severity and symptom inter-relationships. The residuals were then used to construct partial correlation networks using the estimateNetwork function with the default ‘pcor’ method. Edges represented partial correlations between pairs of symptoms after controlling for all other symptoms in the network. Networks were visualised using a Fruchterman-Reingold layout. Centrality indices, including strength, expected influence, betweenness and closeness, were calculated. Network stability was assessed using case-dropping bootstrap procedures with 1000 resamples and evaluated using centrality stability coefficients. A coefficient above 0.25 was considered acceptable, and a value above 0.50 was considered preferable. Network analyses were conducted in the overall sample and within each symptom subgroup.

Patient and public involvement

No patient was involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study, nor in the selection of research questions or outcome measures.

Results

Participant characteristics

The participant recruitment and selection process is shown in figure 1. Of 1160 patients assessed for eligibility, 1008 provided written informed consent and were enrolled. During data cleaning, 66 patients were excluded because of incomplete baseline/core questionnaires or more than 10% missing items (n=38), or non-completion of the 1-month IPAQ follow-up module, which met the predefined >10% missing-item exclusion criterion (n=28). Finally, 942 lung cancer survivors were included in the analysis. The mean age of participants was 64.43±10.70 years, and 65.10% were male. Most participants were married (91.50%), lived in urban areas (77.40%) and resided with family members (95.20%). Clinically, 62.90% were diagnosed at stage III or IV, and 30.50% received systemic therapy. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in online supplemental appendix table S1. Latent profile analysis of symptom clusters

LPA was conducted using the mean scores of five predefined analytical symptom domains: systemic, emotional/cognitive, lung cancer-specific respiratory, treatment-related gastrointestinal and treatment-related neurotoxicity symptoms. Five models were fitted sequentially from one to five classes. As shown in table 1, the three-class model provided the most appropriate balance between model fit, classification accuracy, class size and clinical interpretability. Although the two-class model showed acceptable fit, it mainly separated participants into lower-symptom and higher-symptom groups and did not capture the clinically meaningful moderate-symptom profile. The four-class and five-class models showed lower information criteria but had non-significant LMR-LRT values and produced very small classes (n=23 and n=16, respectively), limiting their interpretability and potential stability. The average posterior probabilities for the three-class model ranged from 0.935 to 0.961, indicating good classification accuracy. Therefore, the three-class model was selected as the optimal solution, and the distribution of symptom severity across the three subgroups is shown in figure 2.

Candidate variables identified by univariate analysis

Univariate analyses identified 28 candidate variables associated with symptom subgroup membership at p<0.10 (online supplemental appendix table S2). These variables covered socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, disease-related variables, functional status and psychosocial factors, including anxiety, depression, social support and health behaviour self-efficacy.

LASSO regression results

The 28 candidate variables identified in univariate analyses were entered into a multinomial LASSO regression model (figure 3). Using 10-fold cross-validation, the optimal penalty parameter was λ.min=0.023. A total of 23 variables were retained, while 5 variables were excluded: handgrip strength, histological type and 3 SRAHP domains. The retained variables were further assessed for collinearity before construction of the final multivariable multinomial logistic regression model.

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression for selecting predictors of symptom subgroups.

Multinomial logistic regression results

Collinearity assessment indicated structural overlap between surgical treatment and type of resection; therefore, surgical treatment was not retained in the final model. The remaining 22 predictors were entered into the multivariable multinomial logistic regression model, with the low-symptom group as the reference. The overall model was statistically significant (p<0.001), with a Nagelkerke pseudo-R² of 0.522. The results are shown in figure 4, and detailed estimates are provided in online supplemental appendix table S3.

Bubble plot of adjusted ORs for factors associated with symptom subgroups in lung cancer survivors. 6-MWT, 6-minute walk distance; aOR, adjusted OR; BMI, body mass index; CNY, Chinese yuan; HADS, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale; SRAHP, Self-Rated Abilities for Health Practices Scale; SSRS, Social Support Rating Scale.

Compared with the low-symptom group, membership in the moderate-symptom group was associated with surgery with adjuvant therapy (aOR=4.054, 95% CI 2.094 to 7.850, p<0.001), targeted therapy (aOR=2.204, 95% CI 1.051 to 4.621, p=0.036), segmentectomy (aOR=5.305, 95% CI 1.931 to 14.575, p=0.001) and higher utilisation of social support (aOR=1.226, 95% CI 1.110 to 1.355, p<0.001). Membership in the high-symptom group was associated with living with family members (aOR=6.308, 95% CI 1.055 to 37.709, p=0.043) and segmentectomy (aOR=7.783, 95% CI 1.275 to 47.524, p=0.026), whereas greater exercise capacity (6MWT ≥450 m: aOR=0.101, 95% CI 0.027 to 0.372, p<0.001), higher subjective support (aOR=0.900, 95% CI 0.828 to 0.977, p=0.012) and higher exercise self-efficacy (aOR=0.897, 95% CI 0.853 to 0.943, p<0.001) were associated with lower odds of high-symptom membership.

The complete-case sensitivity analysis included 791 participants and yielded broadly consistent findings, particularly for the moderate-symptom group. However, several estimates for the high-symptom group were attenuated and had wider CIs, reflecting the smaller complete-case sample size; therefore, the regression findings, especially those for the high-symptom group, should be interpreted as exploratory (online supplemental appendix table S6).

Symptom networks and centrality across latent profiles

As shown in figure 5, symptom networks were estimated for the overall sample and each symptom subgroup after adjustment for 17 socio-demographic, clinical, treatment-related and functional covariates. Network density was descriptively higher in the high-symptom group than in the low-symptom group (0.468 vs 0.175). In descriptive comparisons of the estimated networks, cough–sputum, nausea–vomiting and dyspnoea–chest tightness appeared to be prominent symptom associations. Edge-level subgroup comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because bootstrapped edge-weight accuracy was not assessed. Detailed edge-weight estimates for the overall sample and each symptom subgroup are provided in online supplemental appendix table S5.

Symptom network diagram. (a) Full sample, (b) high-symptom group, (c) moderate-symptom group, (d) low-symptom group.

Based on strength centrality, vomiting was the most central symptom in the overall sample and the moderate-symptom group, whereas cough and distress had the highest centrality in the low-symptom and high-symptom groups, respectively (online supplemental appendix figure S1). Case-dropping bootstrap analysis showed that expected influence had acceptable to good stability across the overall sample and all symptom subgroups (CS, case-dropping correlation stability coefficient=0.444–0.750), whereas strength was less stable in the moderate-symptom and high-symptom groups and betweenness and closeness were generally less stable (online supplemental appendix figure S2). Therefore, centrality findings, particularly for the moderate-symptom and high-symptom groups, should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

This study integrated latent profile analysis, LASSO selection, multivariable multinomial logistic regression and symptom network analysis to characterise symptom heterogeneity among lung cancer survivors. Three distinct symptom subgroups were identified, indicating that symptom experience in this population is not homogeneous but exhibits clinically meaningful stratification. This finding is consistent with previous person-centred research showing that symptom burden varies across patient subpopulations rather than following a uniform pattern.28 29 These results suggest that reliance on overall symptom severity alone may be insufficient to capture underlying heterogeneity.

Beyond subgroup identification, the findings suggest that heterogeneity may also be reflected in symptom network structure. Network density was descriptively higher in the high-symptom group, suggesting a tendency toward stronger symptom interconnections as symptom burden increased. This finding is consistent with network theory, which proposes that densely connected symptom systems may be more likely to sustain and amplify symptom activation.15 Similar changes in symptom connectivity have been reported in perioperative lung cancer populations,30 and the present findings extend this perspective to lung cancer survivorship. At the symptom-pair level, cough–sputum and nausea–vomiting were dominant connections in the low-symptom and moderate-symptom groups, whereas dyspnoea–chest tightness and distress-related cross-domain connections became more prominent in the high-symptom group. The prominence of nausea–vomiting is consistent with evidence that gastrointestinal symptoms play an important role during cancer treatment and may be linked to impaired nutritional intake and increased symptom burden.31–33 In contrast, the prominence of dyspnoea–chest tightness in the high-symptom group is consistent with previous evidence linking respiratory symptoms to disease progression, prognosis, quality of life and functional outcomes in lung cancer.34–36 Together, these descriptive findings suggest possible differences in symptom connectivity across subgroups, but they should not be interpreted as statistically confirmed edge-level differences.

Central symptoms differed across subgroups, suggesting that symptom networks may be organised differently at different levels of symptom burden. Cough was central in the low-symptom group, vomiting in the moderate-symptom group and distress in the high-symptom group. The centrality of cough is consistent with its persistent and disease-specific nature in lung cancer and with previous network studies in this population.30 The centrality of vomiting in the moderate-symptom group may reflect the role of treatment-related gastrointestinal toxicity, which is consistent with evidence linking nausea and vomiting to nutritional decline and reduced treatment tolerance.37 38 In the high-symptom group, distress showed the highest centrality. Previous studies have shown that symptom burden can influence anxiety and depression through illness perception,39 and that psychological distress is closely linked to overall symptom experience and coping capacity.40 Although these studies did not adopt a network perspective, they support the view that psychological symptoms are integral to the symptom system rather than merely secondary outcomes. This finding is also consistent with the bio-psycho-social model, which emphasises the role of psychological factors in adaptation to cancer.41

Regarding associated factors, subgroup membership appeared to reflect different clinical and psychosocial vulnerability profiles. The moderate-symptom group was mainly associated with treatment-related factors and reduced functional capacity, which reflect symptom burden related to treatment exposure and declining physiological reserve. This is consistent with evidence that symptom burden in lung cancer is influenced by treatment modality and disease characteristics.4 In contrast, membership in the high-symptom group was associated with clinical, functional and psychosocial factors. Better pulmonary function, longer walking distance and higher perceived support were associated with lower odds of high-symptom membership, aligning with previous findings that physical capacity42 and psychosocial factors43 are important determinants of quality of life in lung cancer survivors. Exercise-based interventions have also been shown to improve symptom outcomes and functional status.10

Several findings require cautious interpretation. The association between segmentectomy and higher symptom subgroup membership should also be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect patient selection, surgical timing, combined treatment exposure or residual confounding rather than a direct effect of segmentectomy itself. The association between living with family members and higher symptom burden may reflect reverse causality, whereby patients with greater care needs are more likely to cohabit with family, rather than a detrimental effect of cohabitation itself. Socio-environmental factors, including residential context and access to care, have also been reported to influence disparities in lung cancer outcomes.44 Similarly, the positive association between social support utilisation and the moderate-symptom group may indicate increased support needs rather than adverse effects of support use. Prior research has highlighted the complex relationship between social support, symptom distress and self-efficacy in patients with lung cancer.11 In addition, the central role of distress in the high-symptom group, although not entirely intuitive, is supported by evidence linking symptom burden with psychological distress,39 41 suggesting that the role of psychological symptoms in symptom networks may be underestimated.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support a shift from total score-based assessment toward a stratified and network-informed approach to symptom management. By integrating subgroup classification, identification of central symptoms and consideration of clinical and psychosocial factors, this approach provides a more precise basis for identifying intervention priorities. Because this study used a cross-sectional network design, central symptoms should not be interpreted as confirmed causal drivers or proven intervention targets. Rather, they may serve as potential priorities for symptom assessment and hypothesis-generating targets for future longitudinal and interventional studies. In clinical care, this implies that symptom management should move toward a more dynamic and integrated pathway, where patient-specific symptom patterns and key symptoms are continuously assessed. Within this context, nursing practice is well positioned to operationalise this approach through ongoing symptom assessment, early identification of high-risk profiles and coordination of individualised care strategies.45 For patients with high symptom burden or limited support, earlier integration of multidisciplinary and supportive care may further improve symptom outcomes and overall well-being.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow assessment of transitions between symptom subgroups over time. Therefore, central symptoms identified from the network analysis should be interpreted as potential priorities for assessment and hypothesis generation, rather than confirmed causal drivers or intervention targets. Second, although participants were clinically stable at the time of assessment, radiological progression status was not systematically available for all participants. This cohort should therefore be interpreted as a clinically stable treated follow-up or treatment-interval population, rather than a uniformly disease-free survivorship cohort. Third, symptom assessment was conducted during clinically stable, non-acute assessment windows whenever feasible, but assessment timing was not identical across treatment modalities. Fourth, some socio-demographic information and questionnaire-based measures were self-reported, and the convenience sampling design may limit generalisability. Fifth, physical activity data were collected 1 month after discharge and were used only descriptively because they were not temporally aligned with symptom assessment. Although the complete-case sensitivity analysis yielded broadly consistent findings, estimates for the high-symptom group remained less stable and had wider CIs because of the smaller complete-case sample size. Exclusion of participants with missing 1-month physical activity data may have introduced selection bias. Finally, the high-symptom group was relatively small, and several regression estimates had wide CIs; therefore, subgroup-specific regression findings should be considered exploratory. Formal network comparison tests and bootstrapped edge-weight accuracy analyses were not conducted, so differences in network density, structure and specific edge weights should be interpreted descriptively. These findings should be validated in larger independent samples.

Conclusions

This study identified three distinct symptom subgroups among lung cancer survivors using an integrated analytical approach combining latent profile analysis, symptom network analysis and multivariable modelling. The findings indicate that symptom experience is heterogeneous and can be systematically characterised within a subgroup-informed and network-informed framework. These results provide a conceptual basis for stratified symptom assessment and management, supporting a shift from overall symptom severity toward subgroup identification and cautious prioritisation of key symptoms in survivorship care. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs to examine the transitions of symptom subgroups over time and to evaluate the generalisability of symptom network patterns across diverse populations and care settings.
