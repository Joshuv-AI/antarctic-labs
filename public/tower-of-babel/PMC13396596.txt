Geographical Information Systems–Based Wildfire Risk Analysis of Canadian Dialysis Facilities
Authors: Daniel McCollin, Raja Sengupta, Saly El Wazze, Shaifali Sandal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPeople receiving in-center hemodialysis face heightened disaster vulnerability. Strengthening system resilience is essential, and a risk assessment can strategically guide these efforts. We conducted a risk analysis of Canadian dialysis facilities (DFs) from wildfires.MethodsUsing a Geographical Information Systems-based method, we integrated the location of DFs with multiple publicly available datasets to assess historical wildfire exposure, conduct a current wildfire risk assessment, and estimate patients at risk.ResultsWe mapped 341 DFs using georeferenced point data that have 5294 dialysis stations, where 23,185 patients receive in-center hemodialysis. Between 1994 and 2024, a significant increase in wildfires was noted— 8524, 12,947, and 15,994 wildfires were observed from 1994 to 2013, 2004 to 2013, and 2014 to 2024 (excluding 2020), respectively (P < 0.001). Based on historical wildfire data and current facility locations, we estimate that 29.3% (n = 100) of DFs located in 8 provinces and 1 territory were within a 5-kilometer buffer of at least 1 wildfire. Based on the current Fire Danger Risk Index, 6.2%, 29.9%, 51.3%, 10.6%, and 2.1% of DFs were in areas classified as low, moderate risk, high, very high, and extreme risk, respectively, placing 67.3% of patients at risk. There was variability in risk across provinces, and provinces with both high and low historical exposure now have DFs distributed across a wide range of risk categories.ConclusionAlthough these findings should be interpreted as estimates of potential exposure under the current distribution of DFs, our findings highlight the vulnerability of dialysis patients and systems to disasters, and that historical exposure does not necessarily correlate with current risks.

Globally, an escalation in both the frequency and extent of natural and man-made disasters has been reported, driven by a complex array of climatic, environmental, geo-political, and societal factors.1,2 A disaster is a “serious disruption of the functioning of a community or a society at any scale because of hazardous events interacting with conditions of exposure, vulnerability, and capacity, leading to 1 or more of the following: human, material, economic, and environmental losses and impacts.”3 From 2000 to September 2025, the International Disaster Database recorded 16,436 natural and technological disasters that led to almost 1.8 million deaths, affected about 4.8 billion people, and caused $5 quadrillion in damages.4 These numbers are likely an underestimate, potentially because of reporting bias and poor data availability from some regions.

Individual and system susceptibility to a disaster varies by the hazard, exposure, and vulnerability.5 People living with kidney disease, especially those dependent on dialysis, are highly vulnerable, as are the kidney care systems responsible for delivering this life-sustaining therapy.6, 7, 8, 9 The survival of patients on dialysis depends on complex community and health system structures to deliver this therapy to patients multiple times a week. It requires the uninterrupted availability of equipment, electricity, large quantities of clean water, trained personnel, transportation, and an intact DF, all of which might be disrupted by a disaster. Indeed, patient-level analyses report an increased risk of hospitalizations and mortality among dialysis patients with disaster exposure.10, 11, 12, 13 In the US between 1997 and 2017, hurricane exposure was associated with a 13% higher mortality risk when compared with other nonexposed dialysis patients.11 These risks are likely much higher in resource-constrained settings.14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Thus, international bodies have called for stronger disaster preparedness to mitigate the vulnerabilities of people living with kidney diseases and those at risk of developing kidney diseases.15,19, 20, 21, 22, 23

Conducting a risk analysis is a critical first step in disaster risk management, ensuring that efforts are strategic, cost-effective, context-specific, and aligned with broader system priorities.8,24, 25, 26 The Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015 to 2030 places strong emphasis on risk assessment (analysis and evaluation) as a foundation for disaster preparedness and resilience building.27 This entails using available information to estimate the risk to individuals or populations, property, or the environment from various hazards. Van Westen has outlined the following 5-step approach for risk analysis that can inform disaster management: hazard identification, hazard assessment, elements-at-risk and/or exposure analysis, vulnerability assessment, and risk estimation.28 Applying these risk assessments can strategically inform capacity building, contingency planning, and other elements of disaster preparedness. However, limited work has been undertaken in nephrology to operationalize such assessments.

Canada, the second-largest country in the world, comprising 10 provinces and 3 territories over 9,093,507 km2 of area, is at-risk for severe weather and geological events because of its northern location, varied landforms, and weather types.29 In our ongoing vulnerability and adaptation assessment of kidney care systems,30 healthcare professionals and administrators have reported that their DFs have endured several disasters and emergencies, some of which are reported in the lay press.31, 32, 33, 34 However, without a systematic risk assessment, resources cannot be effectively allocated, nor can organizational support be secured for disaster management. Wildfires are 1 of the most pressing climate hazards that have triggered mass evacuations, causing loss of life, destroying infrastructure, and affecting air quality across North America.8,35, 36, 37, 38 In this study, we aimed to conduct a wildfire-focused risk analysis using historical fire data and the current Fire Danger Risk Index (FDRI). Specifically, our objective was to estimate risk and assess vulnerability of the following 2 critical elements at risk: DFs and patients receiving in-center hemodialysis. Our overarching goal is to strengthen capacity building and contingency planning using a risk-informed approach.

Methods

Research Design and Scope

This project follows a quantitative spatial analysis framework grounded in the hazard-exposure-vulnerability model. We used a Geographical Information Systems-based method that allowed the integration of multiple datasets to address our research objectives.

Ethics Statement

This was a secondary analysis of deidentified data from multiple publicly available federal, provincial, and academic sources. As per Article 2.2 of the Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans, it was exempt from institutional ethics review.

Data Sources

DF Locations

We used the Canadian Organ Replacement Register (CORR) Directory available through the Canadian Institute for Health Information to obtain the addresses of DFs that participate in CORR as of 2024.39 We also obtained other data related to the prevalence of people receiving in-center hemodialysis and the number of stations per DF. DF locations were treated as a static representation of the national dialysis infrastructure as of 2024 and were used to estimate retrospective wildfire exposure based on present-day facility distribution, rather than to reconstruct the exact historical configuration of facilities over the study period.

Historical Wildfire Records

We used data from the Canadian National Fire Database.36 Specifically, we used the National Burned Area Composite (NBAC), which covers fire records from 1972 to 2024. This dataset estimates the total forest area burned each year across the country by integrating information from multiple sources and methodologies. These historical records were essential for understanding fire location, frequency, and its extent, offering context for current risks and clues to future trends. For this study, we restricted our analysis to data from 1994 to 2024 to ensure the analysis is contemporary. However, this represents 30 years of data, as the data for the year 2020 was not captured, presumably because of the COVID-19 pandemic.

We defined a wildfire as an unplanned vegetation fire event captured within the NBAC, represented by mapped polygons of burned area provided by provincial, territorial, and federal agencies. Although the database uses the neutral term “fire event,” the scope of the NBAC is specifically wildland fire disturbance (including forest, shrubland, and grassland), and therefore, the term “wildfire” is used consistently throughout this analysis. Trends in wildfires were assessed using the F-statistic generated by Microsoft Excel’s LINEST function (Microsoft, Redmond, WA).

Current Fire Danger Risk Index

The FDRI combines weather data with local fuel availability and flammability to assess the potential for wildland fires.40 In Canada, they are represented by the Canadian Forest Fire Danger Rating System, consisting of the following 2 subsystems: The Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index System and the Canadian Forest Fire Behavior Prediction System. We used the 2025 FDRI from the Canadian Fire Weather Index System to estimate current wildfire risk.36 This index reflects the likelihood of fire ignition, potential fire behavior, and suppression difficulty based on real-time meteorological and environmental data. These categories are used nationally by fire agencies to communicate wildfire threat levels. The 2025 FDRI was selected to represent current and near-term wildfire risk conditions rather than to directly align with historical wildfire events. Preliminary assessment of earlier annual indices (e.g., 2024) showed limited spatial variability in risk classification at the national scale, which reduced their utility for differentiating exposure across regions. In contrast, the 2025 FDRI provided greater variation in risk levels, allowing for a more informative assessment of present-day wildfire risk to dialysis facilities. Our analysis reflects the FDRI from January 2025 to July 2025. Fire risk was assessed as low (fires unlikely to spread), moderate (surface fires possible, but manageable with basic equipment), high (fire behavior intensifies, may require aircraft or heavy tools), very high (likely crown fire activity, suppression is difficult), and extreme (fast-moving and high-severity, suppression is rarely effective).

Population Data

Population data was sourced from 2021 Statistics Canada open-source data.

Data Preparation and Processing

Before spatial analysis was conducted, all raster and vector datasets were reprojected into a common geospatial coordinate system to ensure consistent spatial alignment across layers. Given the national extent of the study area, the Canada Albers Equal Area Conic projection was used for all analyses. This projection was selected because it is well suited for large-scale Canadian geospatial studies and preserves areal relationships across broad geographic regions.

To identify DFs, the centroids of 6-digit postal codes were initially used as georeferenced locations. However, postal code centroids do not always correspond precisely to the physical location of healthcare facilities and may introduce spatial inaccuracies in both urban and rural settings. Therefore, geographic coordinates were manually refined to improve locational accuracy. Facility locations were carefully verified and adjusted using satellite imagery and visual inspection in Google Earth. A combination of reverse geocoding and high-resolution imagery was also used to validate and correct coordinates where necessary.

Data and/or Spatial Analysis

All processing and analysis were carried out using ArcGIS Pro (version 3.3.0, Environmental Systems Research Institute, Redlands, CA). Multiple thematic maps were created using this software to visualize different aspects of the wildfire risk and DF exposure. Current FDRI, historical fire data, census-based population estimates, DF location, and other data were all overlaid and examined together.

First, to establish a consistent geographic frame, a map of Canada showing provincial and territorial boundaries and a map specifying the distribution of DFs across Canada were created with insets for high-density urban areas (Supplementary Figures S1–3). This base layer established a consistent geographic frame for spatial analysis and provided the administrative context for risk classification and vulnerability assessments across regions. It was used throughout the mapping process to ensure visual and analytical consistency. Then we analyzed the DFs in relation to historical wildfire perimeters from 1994 to 2024. DF locations were layered over the NBAC. As facility location data were available only for 2024, this analysis represents a retrospective projection of wildfire exposure based on current facility distribution, rather than a reconstruction of historical facility locations

To conduct a current wildfire risk assessment, the 2025 FDRI was overlaid with the DF’s locations. The FDRI values were grouped into 5 categories representing the potential intensity and likelihood of wildfire activity (low, moderate, high, very high, and extreme). A 5-kilometer circular buffer was applied around each georeferenced DF. This buffer distance was informed by previous geospatial wildfire research. In particular, in the study by Parisien et al.,41 concentric distance bands of 5 km were applied to assess wildfire exposure gradients around communities. In that context, 5 km represents a standardized spatial unit of analysis rather than a fixed threshold of wildfire impact. Consistent with this approach, the buffer in our study was used as a proxy to capture both direct and indirect effects of wildfire activity, including evacuation zones, access disruptions, and smoke exposure that may affect healthcare service delivery. This approach reflects the operational reality that wildfire impacts often extend beyond the immediate fire perimeter and may disrupt surrounding infrastructure and access to care. Alternative buffer distances may yield different exposure estimates; however, the use of a 5 km buffer provides a balance between sensitivity to indirect impacts and avoidance of overestimation, while maintaining consistency with existing literature.

Then, a spatial join and risk classification were conducted, whereby each DF was assigned a risk category based on whether its buffer intersected areas marked with 1 of the 5 FDRI risk categories. DFs with such intersections were flagged as exposed. A spatial join operation facilitated this classification. If a DF buffer did not intersect with any FDRI polygons, it was classified as “low risk.” No DF buffers overlapped with more than 1 FDRI category. Dialysis facilities were classified as urban or rural based on their location relative to census metropolitan area and census agglomeration boundaries, where facilities located within census metropolitan area and census agglomeration boundaries were categorized as urban and those outside were categorized as rural.

Finally, to assess the population of patients served by at-risk DFs, we first mapped the population density per province (Supplementary Figure S4). Then, we conducted an FDRI assessment in relation to population density by linking historical fire risk data and the current fire risk assessment. To estimate how many people rely on each DF involved, we combined population data from Statistics Canada with prevalence information obtained from CORR. This helped produce an estimate of the population dependent on each DF, which is essential for understanding the potential human impact if a facility were affected by wildfire.

Capacity and Vulnerability Assessment

The national average of patients who receive in-center dialysis/station was 4.38. However, this ranges widely across regions with Alberta (Northwest Territories/Nunavut; 5.2), Saskatchewan (4.9), Manitoba (4.6), Quebec (4.5), and Ontario (4.4) having higher than the national average patients/station. In contrast, British Columbia/Yukon (4.1), New Brunswick (3.7), Newfoundland and Labrador (3.6), Nova Scotia (3.5), and Prince Edward Island (2.3) fall below the national average. However, another metric we assessed was the population on in-center hemodialysis at risk. The current national prevalence of patients on in-center hemodialysis is 578.2 per million population (pmp). The provinces of Newfoundland (998.9 pmp), Manitoba (982.2 pmp), New Brunswick (791.9 pmp), Nova Scotia (679.1 pmp), and Saskatchewan (647.6 pmp) have a proportion much higher than the national average. The prevalence for the rest of the provinces was as follows: British Columbia and/or Yukon 463.7 pmp, Alberta and/or Northwest Territories and/or Nunavut 460 pmp, Ontario 593.3 pmp, Quebec 555.5 pmp, Prince Edward Island 489.1 pmp (Supplementary Figure 5).

Historical Wildfires and Trends

Between 1994 and 2024, the province of Manitoba had the largest number of documented wildfire events (8869), followed by British Columbia (7511) and Alberta (5396). The 2 largest and most densely populated provinces also recorded several wildfires; Ontario: 2288, and Quebec: 3671. The Northwest Territories and Yukon have recorded 3541 and 1480 wildfires, respectively, despite low population densities (Table 1). Estimating Historical Wildfire Exposure of DFs

Our spatial analysis identified critical patterns in the geographic distribution of DFs across Canada and their exposure to historical wildfires. Based on historical data, we estimate that 29.3% (n = 100) of DFs located in 8 provinces and 1 territory would fall within a 5-kilometer buffer of at least 1 wildfire from 1994 to 2024 (excluding 2020; Figure 2). These exposures were relatively evenly distributed between rural and urban areas, with 51.0% (n = 51) of affected DFs located in rural regions based on current facility distribution, and a full list of facilities is provided in Supplementary Table S1. We estimate that British Columbia (n = 20), Nova Scotia (n = 17), and Quebec (n = 27) had the highest number of affected dialysis facilities, followed by Manitoba (n = 12), Alberta (n = 10), and New Brunswick (n = 7). Fewer affected facilities were reported in Newfoundland and Labrador (n = 2), Ontario (n = 3), Saskatchewan (n = 1), and the Northwest Territories (n = 1). Current Wildfire Risk Assessment of DFs

Based on the current FDRI, 64.0% of DFs are within a 5-kilometer buffer where the likelihood of wildfire activity have been rated as high, very high, or extreme. Overall, 6.2% of DFs were in areas classified as low risk, 29.9% as moderate risk, 51.3% as high risk, 10.6% as very high, and 2.1% as extreme risk (Figure 3). Risk varied across provinces and territories, and in some regions the majority, if not all DFs, were designated to be in the 5-kilometer buffer of a high, very high or extreme FDRI region, as follows: Alberta and/or Nunavut = 59.4%, British Columbia and/or Yukon = 92.9%, Manitoba = 45.5%, New Brunswick = 0, Newfoundland and Labrador = 6.2%, Nova Scotia = 21.1%, Northwest Territories = 100.0%, Ontario = 85.7%, Prince Edward Island = 100.0%, Quebec = 44.8%, and Saskatchewan = 100.0%. Estimating Wildfire Exposure Risk of Patients on In-Center Dialysis

Dialysis facility-level FDRI categories were used as a location-based proxy for exposure, with provincial estimates weighted by the number of in-center hemodialysis patients. Nationally, we estimate that 67.3% of patients are being dialyzed at a DF in the 5-kilometer buffer of a region assigned as high, very high, or extreme FDRI. The provincial breakdown of the percentage of patients at risk of fire exposure is essentially the same as the proportion of the DFs at risk, as listed above.

Comparing Historical Wildfire Exposure with the Current FDRI of DFs

Current fire danger assessments show that provinces with both high and low historical exposure now have DFs distributed across a wide range of risk categories. Although Manitoba had the highest number of recorded wildfires, 45.5% of DFs are now classified as high FDRI, and none were in the very high or extreme category. Provinces with fewer and moderate historical fire activity also show elevated present-day risks. British Columbia and Alberta, which have recorded higher numbers of fires in the past, show higher present-day danger levels. In British Columbia, the majority of DFs are classified as high, very high, or in the extreme danger category. Discussion

In this wildfire-focused risk and vulnerability analysis, we estimate that 29.3% of DFs based on current facility locations were within a 5-kilometer buffer of at least 1 wildfire from 1994 to 2024 (excluding 2020), of which 51.0% were located in rural and/or remote regions. Additionally, using the current FDRI, 64.0% of DFs are within a 5-kilometer buffer where the likelihood of wildfire activity has been rated as high, very high, or extreme, placing 67.3% of the prevalent patients on in-center hemodialysis at risk. We also report that previous wildfire exposure may not necessarily correspond with current FDRI. Although these findings should be interpreted as estimates of potential exposure under the current distribution of dialysis infrastructure, our findings highlight the vulnerability of kidney patients and kidney care systems to disasters. Also, we highlight the need for contemporary risk assessments to inform disaster preparedness efforts, and how these can be accomplished by integrating disciplines and existing datasets with the ultimate goal of strengthening health system resilience.

As has been reported globally for different kinds of disasters,1,2 we note a significant increase in the number of wildfires in Canada over the past 3 decades. The largest proportional increase was observed in natural-caused wildfires, which rose from 991 events between 1994 and 2003 to 7366 events between 2004 and 2013 and 9926 events between 2014 and 2024 (excluding 2020). Human-caused wildfires also rose substantially, whereas the number of undetermined causes declined, reflecting improved fire attribution and reporting practices. Nevertheless, the increase in the proportion of wildfires of all causes is likely driven by anthropogenic climate change. Factors such as decreased snowpack, low soil moisture, and elevated drought conditions contribute to wildfires in Canada that are more frequent, larger, and challenging to contain.43 Overall, our data adds to the growing evidence advocating for climate mitigation efforts to strengthen health system resilience, as suggested by the Alliance for Transformative Action on Climate and Health.44

Based on the current FDRI, 67.3% of patients on in-center dialysis are at high, very high, or extreme wildfire risk. Patients may be directly affected through mandatory evacuations, loss of their homes to fire, or disruptions in care when DFs, roads, power grids, or other infrastructure become inoperable. They may also be indirectly affected, as patients with kidney failure tend to be very comorbid, increasing other health risks, particularly related to poor air quality. Wildfires generate high concentrations of fine particulate matter (PM2.5), and this poor air quality is associated with a significantly higher mortality risk for patients on dialysis.37,45,46 For example, among patients on dialysis, a 10 μg/m3 increase in wildfire PM2.5 was demonstrated to be associated with a 4% increased risk in all-cause mortality on the same day and 7% increased risk cumulatively over 30 days following exposure.46

Although all patients on dialysis are at a higher risk of being negatively impacted by disasters because of the nature of their life-sustaining treatment, our data raises concerns regarding the inequitable impact of wildfires on DFs in remote and rural regions. Based on the locations reported in the supplementary tables, we speculate that Indigenous patients on dialysis have been the most impacted, reflecting broader evidence that disasters have inequitable impacts on Indigenous communities.47 In Canada, although 5% of the population identifies as Indigenous, more than 42% of wildfire evacuations have been from communities that are more than half Indigenous.48 Furthermore, kidney diseases also tend to impact populations inequitably, and multiple studies have reported the higher prevalence of diabetic and nondiabetic kidney failure among Indigenous Canadians compared with non-Indigenous Canadians.49, 50, 51 Overall, we respectfully raise concerns about equity; however, there is a need for more granular data to characterize risks by individual patient characteristics.

A last point of discussion is that the distribution of risk and vulnerability of DFs varies across provinces, as different facilities are operating at different capacity levels. Some provinces have a higher number of patients and/or dialysis stations, when compared with the national average, indicating more patients are vulnerable if the DF shut down. Furthermore, the prevalence of patients on in-center dialysis per million was substantially higher in some provinces than the national averages, indicating a larger proportion of the population that resides in those provinces is at risk. As per our assessment, patients in the provinces of Manitoba and Saskatchewan are the most vulnerable, as both these metrics are higher than the national averages. The province of Saskatchewan, in particular, had all DFs categorized with a high, very high, or extreme FDRI. The adaptation efforts in these regions may need to be particularly robust. Overall, our findings highlight that even within the same country, preparedness strategies must be tailored to local contexts, as the types and levels of disaster risk vary across regions. Conducting risk assessments of this nature provides a critical foundation for developing targeted, evidence-informed preparedness efforts.

Our analysis has some important limitations tied to the quality and completeness of available data across multiple databases used. The population data used were drawn from the 2021 census, whereas some of the other datasets were more recent. Although we believe the temporal variation is minimal and unlikely to meaningfully alter our findings, the mismatch in data vintages should be acknowledged. The wildfire dataset spans a 30-year period and includes a mix of human-caused, natural, and undetermined fire events. The relative contribution of each type of fire varies substantially by region and context, which is an important consideration when developing tailored risk mitigation strategies. The NBAC may not capture all wildfire events, particularly smaller fires or those occurring in regions with limited satellite mapping coverage. Although complementary datasets such as those from the Canadian Interagency Forest Fire Centre provide national-level fire occurrence statistics, they do not include geospatially explicit burned area data and therefore cannot be directly integrated into this spatial analysis. Nevertheless, NBAC remains the most comprehensive geospatial dataset available for national-scale wildfire assessment in Canada. The use of a single-year FDRI snapshot (2025) reflects current conditions and may not capture interannual variability in wildfire risk; however, it provides a relevant approximation of present-day exposure patterns for preparedness planning. Lastly, a key limitation of this analysis is the use of a static (2024) dataset of DF locations to estimate historical wildfire exposure over a 30-year period. This approach does not account for temporal changes in the distribution, opening, or closure of facilities, which may influence the accuracy of time–space relationships. As such, our estimates of historical exposure should be interpreted as retrospective projections based on the current configuration of dialysis infrastructure, rather than precise reconstructions of past exposure.

However, we believe this approach remains appropriate for system-level risk assessment, as it reflects the present-day spatial distribution of dialysis services, which is the most relevant context for current and future disaster preparedness planning. This allows for the identification of geographic patterns of vulnerability within the existing healthcare system, even in the absence of longitudinal infrastructure data. As such, this analysis provides estimates of exposure and vulnerability rather than direct measures of realized exposure, but identifying specific facilities at risk offers a foundation for more granular, patient-level analyses in future work. Future studies incorporating time-resolved facility data would further refine these estimates. Furthermore, our findings are relevant to the global nephrology readership, as risk assessment is essential in kidney care planning to identify and mitigate vulnerabilities that result in inequitable impacts of disasters on people living with kidney diseases.8,20 Although some disasters occur with little warning, many are increasingly predictable through climate monitoring and modeling.27,52 Existing global datasets curated by climate scientists can be leveraged to inform such assessments and guide preparedness strategies.53, 54, 55 Our analysis, conducted by an interdisciplinary team, exemplifies how this can be operationalized in kidney care.

In summary, to our knowledge, we have conducted the first wildfire-focused risk analysis to estimate risk and assess vulnerability of DFs and patients receiving in-center dialysis. Our findings reveal that approximately two-thirds of DFs in Canada, and two-thirds of patients who receive in-center hemodialysis, are located within a 5-kilometer buffer of areas classified as high, very high, or extreme wildfire risk. Importantly, our analysis demonstrates that previous wildfire exposure does not necessarily correlate with current risks. This risk analysis will guide our national capacity planning and strengthen system resilience while also offering an approach to conduct similar risk assessments in different countries for similar and other climate hazards.
