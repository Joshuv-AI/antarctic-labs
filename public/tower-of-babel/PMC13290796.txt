Umbilical vein–ductus venosus angle: a novel ultrasound marker for predicting optimal umbilical venous catheter placement
Authors: Kamil Gierek, Aleksandra Mikołajczak, Małgorzata Stefańska, Marta Kusibab-Mytych, Katarzyna Różańska, Renata Bokiniec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Umbilical venous catheterization is a common procedure performed in intensive care units. Point-of-care ultrasonography enables efficient vascular access and reliable assessment of catheter tip position. This prospective study aimed to determine whether the angle formed between the terminal segment of the umbilical vein (UV) and the initial segment of the ductus venosus (DV) can predict successful umbilical venous catheter (UVC) placement in the inferior vena cava, and to evaluate the effectiveness of ultrasound-guided navigation. Between 2021 and 2023, 78 neonates in a single-center study underwent umbilical venous catheterization. Ultrasonography, performed by an experienced ultrasonographer, was used to assess UVC tip position, DV caliber, and the UV–DV angle. After identifying catheter tip malposition, infants were classified into two groups: low and correct catheter positions. Ultrasound-guided maneuvers were applied to achieve optimal tip placement. The umbilical vein–ductus venosus (UV–DV) angle differed significantly between low and correctly positioned UVCs, median 136.9° (IQR 128.8–151.5°) vs. 157.4° (IQR 151.4–164.6°), p < 0.001. In multivariable analysis, both the UV–DV angle (p = 0.0032) and ductus venosus caliber (p = 0.0047) were independent predictors of correct catheter positioning. The catheter was correctly positioned on the first attempt in 73% of cases, increasing to 85% after adjustment maneuvers. Conclusion: These findings highlight the usefulness of ultrasound navigation for effective UVC placement and the UV–DV angle appears to be a promising ultrasound marker for predicting optimal catheter positioning. The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Warsaw No. KB/110/2020 and retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07434102) on 25.02.2026. Introduction

The umbilical vein (UV) and artery (UA) catheterizations are the most common procedures performed in preterm and term neonates [1]. These catheters are used in infants who are critically ill to ensure reliable central access, allowing for the administration of parenteral nutrition and medications, monitoring of vital parameters, and collecting blood for examination. The UV catheterization involves numerous complications due to incorrect catheter positioning [2, 3]. Catheter tip may be malpositioned, with reported failure rates of up to 50% [3, 4]. Estimation of the optimal depth for umbilical venous catheter (UVC) insertion is commonly derived from either the shoulder–umbilicus distance, as described by Dunn [5], or from the neonate’s body weight, according to the method proposed by Shukla and Ferrara [6].

Thoracoabdominal radiography (TAR) has long been the standard approach for confirming catheter tip position. However, studies have demonstrated imprecision in the radiological assessment of standard UVC tip positioning [3, 7]. Although TAR is still routinely used in neonatology wards, it is increasingly being replaced by a faster, less invasive, and more readily available ultrasonographic method. Clinical studies have consistently demonstrated inaccuracies in the preliminary assessment of catheter insertion depth and substantial discrepancies between radiologic and ultrasonographic evaluations in determining central catheter tip position [8–11].

The primary aim of this study was to assess the angle formed between the terminal segment of the UV and the initial segment of the ductus venosus (DV), herein referred to as the umbilical vein–ductus venosus (UV–DV) angle, and to determine whether the UV–DV angle can predict difficult UVC advancement and guide procedural strategy. The secondary objective was to evaluate the effectiveness of ultrasound-guided navigation combined with corrective maneuvers in achieving the correct UVC placement.

Methods

Study design

This observational, prospective, single-center, population-based cohort study was conducted at the Neonatal and Intensive Care Department of the University Frederic Chopin Hospital of Rzeszów (Poland) between 2021 and 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Bioethical Committee of the Medical University of Warsaw, Poland (KB/110/2020) and retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07434102; protocol version 25.02.2026). Caregivers received full information about the purpose and procedures of the study, and all provided written informed consent.

Participants

Participants were consecutively enrolled from children undergoing umbilical vein catheterization in the Neonatal and Intensive Care Department of the University Frederic Chopin Hospital of Rzeszów (Poland) between September 2021 and January 2023.

Inclusion criteria encompassed neonates of varying gestational ages who required parenteral nutrition during the early postnatal period. Individuals with congenital gastrointestinal tract malformations, agenesis of the ductus venosus, or those delivered prior to 24 weeks of gestation were excluded from the study.

Study size

The study included 78 neonates; clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records. Participants were consecutively enrolled from children born between September 2021 and January 2023. Given the novel nature of this study and the limited availability of prior data, a formal sample size calculation was not feasible.

The participants were divided into two groups based on UVC tip position: one group with a correctly positioned (high) catheter in the inferior vena cava (IVC) (achieved within a maximum of three attempts) and one group with low catheter tip positioned within the UV.

Variables

Primary outcome was the magnitude of the UV–DV angle defined as the angle formed between the terminal segment of the UV and the initial segment of the DV measured within the first 72 h of life.

Secondary outcome included (1) comparison of the UV–DV angle between low and high UVC tip position groups, (2) success rate of ultrasound-guided maneuvers, and (3) causes of UVC malposition.

The UV–DV angle was analyzed according to the length of UVC tip position: low and correct. A low UVC tip position within the UV was defined as a location below the lower edge of the liver, whereas the correct (high) position was defined as a tip located within the IVC at the entrance to the right atrium (RA).

The analysis also incorporated the maneuvers used to correct low-positioned UVCs, including gentle ultrasound-guided transducer compression at the level of DV/portal sinus alignment, which appears to be the most anatomically coherent approach; and maneuver II, consisting of trunk flexion.

Data sources

After confirming the indications for catheter placement, the DV anatomy was assessed using ultrasound. The Philips HD Affinity 70 System (Philips N.V., Amsterdam, Netherlands) with a high-frequency linear probe (L3–12 MHz) was used to evaluate the UV, DV, UV–DV angle (in degrees), and the position of the UV catheter tip. An experienced ultrasonographer [KG] scanned all infants to obtain images showing the junction of the UV with the DV, adjacent to the left branch of the portal vein (LPV) and the IVC (Fig. 1a). The transducer was placed in the subcostal region in a longitudinal abdominal plane. Gentle rotation and angulation of the transducer allowed visualization of the junction between the UV and DV, with the probe marker directed slightly to the left. During post-processing, an experienced ultrasonographer reviewed the results in the high-quality video recordings obtained during the study. In the next stage, UV catheterization was performed following standard neonatal protocols, with the neonate placed in the supine position.

Under sterile conditions, a 4-French catheter was inserted into the UV to a depth calculated using the Shukla and Ferrara’s formula,based on the neonate’s body weight, or advanced to a low position if resistance was encountered.

Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records.

Intervention procedures

The next ultrasound examination evaluated UVC placement. In cases of unsuccessful catheter insertion into the UV, the secondary objective was to identify the causes of malposition and assess the effectiveness of US-guided maneuvers in achieving successful correction to the proper position. If the UVC was positioned intrahepatically (Fig. 2b), an attempt was made to reposition it under ultrasound guidance, depending on the underlying cause. The success rate was defined as the proportion of participants with initially malpositioned UVCs in whom correct catheter tip positioning was achieved following ultrasound-guided maneuvers.

Bias

Potential sources of bias included measurement bias and observer variability related to ultrasound assessment. To minimize bias, all measurements were performed using a standardized protocol by trained clinicians. Intraobserver and interobserver variability was assessed using repeated measurements from recorded ultrasound images.

Statistical analysis

In the first stage of the analysis, descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Differences between categories of qualitative variables were assessed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, with a cell count of < 5 used as the threshold for applying Fisher’s exact test. Quantitative variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test because of deviations from normality identified on Q–Q plots.

Multivariable analysis was performed using a generalized linear model with logistic regression. The optimal model was selected according to the Akaike information criterion (AIC). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was then used to determine the optimal cutoff value.

A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS/STAT software, release 15.2.

Additionally, analyses were performed using Python. Intra- and interobserver agreement were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; absolute agreement models). Agreement was further evaluated using the Bland–Altman analysis, including calculation of mean differences and 95% limits of agreement.

A total of 78 neonates underwent UV catheterization. In 21 infants, the catheter tip was malpositioned within the portal circulation, whereas in 57 infants, the catheter tip was positioned above the DV within the IVC after the first attempt. Among the 21 cases of unsuccessful catheter positioning, corrective maneuvers were applied to advance the catheter to the proper anatomical location. Maneuver I, the first-line option involving ultrasound-guided transducer compression at the DV/portal sinus alignment site, was applied first due to its anatomical coherence and used when the catheter deviated into the portal circulation and the anatomy was still potentially favorable. When the first maneuver failed, trunk flexion was used as a second maneuver (maneuver II) to advance the catheter to its optimal position. After maneuver I, successful repositioning was achieved in 7 of 21 cases. Maneuver II resulted in successful positioning in an additional two cases, yielding an overall success rate of 9 of 21 (43%).

In the remaining 12 cases, advancement of the catheter to the optimal position was not achieved. Overall, the initial attempt at optimal catheter positioning was unsuccessful in 21 of 78 cases (28%). After the application of corrective maneuvers, the catheter remained in a low position in 12 of 78 patients (15%).

Analysis demonstrated significant differences between low- and correctly (high)–positioned catheter placements for both the UV–DV obtuse angle and DV caliber. The low-position group showed a smaller median UV–DV angle compared with the correctly positioned group (136.9° vs 157.4°, p < 0.001). Similarly, the median DV caliber was lower in the low-position group than in the correctly positioned group (0.75 cm vs 1.00 cm, p = 0.0065). Detailed results are presented in Table 2. To analyze factors associated with correct UVC positioning, a multivariable regression model was performed. The final model was selected using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) as a measure of model fit. The candidate variables included UV–DV angle, DV length, DV caliber, neonatal birth weight, sex, umbilical arterial blood pH, and antenatal steroid administration. Among these, the UV–DV angle (p = 0.0032) and DV caliber (p = 0.0047) were independent significant predictors of correct UVC positioning.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to select cutoff points. In the first step, ROC analysis based on the UV–DV angle alone demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8649. The optimal cutoff value for the UV–DV angle was 145.7°, yielding a sensitivity of 94% and a specificity of 67%.

In the second step, the optimal model including both the UV–DV angle and DV caliber was analyzed. This multivariable optimal model showed a superior diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.9672. The optimal probability threshold for the model was 0.76, corresponding to a sensitivity of 95% and a specificity of 83%. To assess intraobserver and intraobserver variability, 78 sonographic measurements (obtained from video review) were analyzed, yielding a variability of 0.41 ± 1.67%. The mean difference (MD) was 0.66; 95% CI = [− 0.928, 2.257] for intraobserver; the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) demonstrated excellent agreement (ICC = 0.97; 95% CI = 0.96–0.98). Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to introduce the UV–DV angle as a promising ultrasound marker of difficult UVC advancement and may support bedside procedural planning. We derived a clinically actionable cutoff value for the UV–DV angle based on ROC analysis; however, we emphasize that the presented data are preliminary, and external validation is required to establish this parameter as a definitive clinical tool.

In successful UVC advancement, DV caliber was recognized as an important parameter, whereas the UV–DV angle emerged as a novel marker.

UV catheterization is one of the most frequently used methods for achieving central vascular access in neonatal intensive care units. The catheter inserted into the UV follows the natural direction of blood flow from the placenta to the fetal heart. It first passes through the umbilical vein, which courses medially, cephalad, and superficially, then turns posteriorly, bypassing the left branch of the portal vein. It continues through the DV and enters the IVC near the junction of the hepatic veins, ultimately reaching the RA. After umbilical venous return enters the fetal heart, the eustachian valve directs the flow toward the left atrium [12, 13].

The catheter tip was considered to be in the optimal position when it was located at the junction between the IVC and the RA, which is reached after entering the UV and passing through the DV [1, 3]. However, the UVC may follow a collateral course into the portal system and end up in an intrahepatic branch of the portal vein [1]. Accurate evaluation of catheter position is essential to prevent complications.

Ultrasound evaluation of UAC and UVC placement dates back to 1981, when Oppenheimer successfully visualized UAC tips but had difficulty imaging the intrahepatic course of UVCs [14]. Subsequent studies confirmed the superiority of targeted neonatal echocardiography over chest radiography for determining UVC tip position [15–17]. Advances in ultrasonography have increased its use for assessing UVC position. Modern point-of-care ultrasonography enables real-time guidance and continuous monitoring of UVC placement [13]. Establishing precise guidelines that facilitate accurate catheter tip placement is essential for preventing procedural failure and minimizing related complications.

Our results showed a statistically significant difference in the UV–DV obtuse angle between the groups with low and correct placements (UV–DV angle: median 136.9° vs. 157.4°; p < 0.001). A larger UV–DV obtuse angle was associated with a higher likelihood of successful catheter tip placement in the correct position. This anatomical parameter may therefore serve as a useful predictor of potential challenges during catheterization. Moreover, we derived a clinically actionable cutoff value for the UV–DV angle based on ROC analysis.

Real-time ultrasonography also enabled identification of factors contributing to unsuccessful catheter insertion. Failure to achieve correct positioning was associated with a smaller UV–DV angle, and with a narrow DV caliber. Based on these causes of insertion failure, specific corrective maneuvers were applied to facilitate accurate catheter placement.

This study highlights the importance of ultrasound-guided navigation and the use of adjunctive maneuvers to improve procedural success. In particular, we would like to emphasize the liver compression maneuver (maneuver I), consisting of gentle ultrasound-guided transducer compression performed at the level of the DV/portal sinus alignment (posterior liver maneuver), which appears to be the most anatomically rational and effective approach for facilitating catheter advancement. In contrast, trunk flexion of the infant (maneuver II) appeared to be less effective.

Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported that hepatic compression is the most effective maneuver for UVC placement [4, 18, 19]. Kishigami et al. reported a 93% success rate in achieving optimal catheter tip positioning using this technique. In our study, we achieved 85% success rate in correctly advancing the catheter, a result consistent with that of Patel [18] but notably lower than that of Kishigami [19]. The implementation of new catheterization techniques further reduced the failure rate from 27 to 15%. Conversely, Kieran et al. reported that placing a newborn in the right lateral decubitus position during UVC insertion did not improve the success rate [20].

Recently, guidelines have recommended using real-time ultrasound to locate the UVC tip as it is more reliable, faster, and free of side effects [4, 6–9, 14]. A remaining challenge is in providing additional training for medical staff in ultrasound and echocardiography [21]. Rubertone et al. demonstrated a significant increase in bedside ultrasound use following the implementation of a dedicated training program.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size (n = 78), which may limit statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the study population may not be fully representative of other clinical settings. The limited sample size may also have reduced the robustness of the multivariable model and precluded more detailed subgroup analyses.

Second, no formal sample size calculation was performed due to the exploratory nature of the study and the limited availability of prior data. Although statistical power was not formally assessed, the sample size represents a pragmatic compromise; however, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating.

Third, although a provisional clinical threshold for the UV–DV angle is proposed, external validation is required to establish a clinically applicable cutoff value.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrated that the UV–DV angle appears to be a promising ultrasound marker of difficult UVC advancement and may support bedside procedural planning.

The findings of this preliminary study suggest a potential practical procedural approach and ultrasound-guided algorithm: (1) pre-procedural ultrasound assessment of the UV–DV angle, and DV caliber; (2) identification of potentially challenging anatomy; (3) application of targeted gentle ultrasound-guided probe compression, if the catheter deviates into the portal circulation and the anatomy is still potentially favorable; (4) limitation of repeated attempts, with early consideration of alternative venous access when anatomical conditions appear clearly unfavorable.

Ultrasound-guided navigation may increase the likelihood of correct catheter placement and helps identify factors contributing to insertion failure. However, these findings are preliminary and require external validation prior to clinical implementation.
