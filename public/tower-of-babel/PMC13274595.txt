Metaceramic enables ultrahigh-temperature record rectification and programmable 3D thermal control
Authors: Yishu Su, Haoliang Huo, Qianqian Wu, Shuihua Yang, Chengrui Yan, Zongbing Chen, Jiecai Han, Xinghong Zhang, Paolo Colombo, Cheng-Wei Qiu, Jian Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thermal rectification enables asymmetric heat flow and offers transformative potential for thermal management under extreme environments, yet its practical development has been constrained by a fundamental trade-off between high-temperature stability and nonlinear thermal response. Conventional rectifiers face intrinsic limitations, with operating temperatures below 900 kelvin and rectification ratios under 3.5. Here, we present a metaceramic, a monolithic metamaterial ceramic, architected with multiscale graded porosity that synergistically integrates four nonlinear heat transfer mechanisms: ion-tailored conduction, cavity-modulated radiation, chaotic advection–enhanced convection, and spontaneous convective dissipation. This metaceramic achieves a record thermal rectification ratio of 8.5 at 2473 kelvin, surpassing previous benchmarks by over 140%. In addition, the design’s continuum-like, functionally partitionable nature enables its extension into a triaxial ultrahigh-temperature rectifier, which provides programmable, volumetric thermal flow control along three independent axes, effectively elevating rectification from a scalar to a tensor-like property. By decoupling and recombining multimodal nonlinearities within a single material, we overcome the classical stability-nonlinearity conflict. Our work establishes a metamaterials platform for breaking thermal reciprocity under extreme conditions, with implications for heat management in hypersonic systems, spacecraft, and energy technologies.

INTRODUCTION

Ultrahigh-temperature environments, such as spacecraft reentry, hypersonic vehicle surfaces, and next-generation nuclear reactor cores, pose unprecedented challenges (1, 2) to the thermal stability of materials and thermal management technologies (3–5). Nonreciprocal thermal transport (6, 7) enables asymmetric heat flow control (8, 9), facilitating heat transmission in one direction while blocking it in the reverse, delivering an innovative approach (10, 11) for advanced thermal management (12, 13) under extreme environments (14). In hypersonic vehicles, this technology can limit aerodynamic heating to the heat-resistant area surface while effectively dissipating waste heat from internal combustion chambers. In interstellar missions, such as the exploration of Venus, which has a surface temperature approaching 773 K, the directional regulation of heat flow is essential for ensuring the thermal protection of equipment. However, a persistent and seemingly intractable materials dilemma fundamentally constrains the transformative potential of this technology. Current state-of-the-art thermal rectifiers face an insurmountable performance ceiling in extreme conditions: Maximum operating temperatures plateau at ~900 K (15), while rectification ratios struggle to exceed 2.2 at high temperatures and remain below 3.5 even at room temperature (16). This critical bottleneck stems from a deep-seated conflict inherent in conventional material systems: Materials exhibiting exceptional high-temperature stability, such as ultrahigh-temperature ceramics (17, 18), typically lack the pronounced nonlinear thermal conductivity response essential for strong nonreciprocal heat transport above 2000 K. Conversely, materials offering strong nonlinearity often suffer structural, physical, and chemical degradation at elevated temperatures.

Conventional design paradigms have failed to achieve strong nonreciprocal heat flow under extreme conditions. Approaches using composite architectures, whose efficacy relies on the temperature-dependent evolution of thermal conductivity and structural disparities (19), are severely hindered by the scarcity of material combinations that can maintain synergistic nonlinear compatibility across a broad temperature range. Phase change materials (16, 20), which can be considered as discrete nonlinear elements (fig. S1A), are capable of producing abrupt nonlinear jumps in properties. However, their application is critically limited by fixed phase transition temperatures and a lack of viable high-temperature phase change material options, thereby rigidly constraining their operational window and making efficient modulation highly challenging. Another strategy exploits phonon transport (21, 22) in asymmetric nanostructures (fig. S1B), where a nonequilibrium phonon spectrum overlapping under forward thermal bias can enable asymmetric heat flow regulation (9, 23). Nevertheless, the efficacy of such phonon-mediated mechanisms decays drastically with increasing length scales, precluding the achievement of substantial thermal rectification at macroscopic dimensions. Conventional rectification strategies based on single materials or simple interfaces are fundamentally constrained by the mutual exclusivity between thermal stability and nonlinearity (24, 25). Previous theoretical frameworks for thermal rectification have primarily focused on conduction-based mechanisms in natural materials. However, the coupling of radiation and convection with conductive transport, which becomes increasingly pronounced at elevated temperatures, offers substantial untapped potential for overcoming the intrinsic nonlinearity limitations of natural materials and achieving higher rectification efficiencies.

The central challenge in ultrahigh-temperature rectifier design thus hinges on overcoming this stability-nonlinearity conflict. While refractory materials like ceramics (26) and superalloys are indispensable for stability, their thermal conductivity often displays insufficient temperature dependence for enabling effective rectification (27). A transformative path forward lies not in seeking elusive “ideal” monolithic materials, but in rationally engineering material architectures to create and orchestrate the required nonlinearity. Materials with intrinsically low thermal conductivity (28, 29) provide a fertile foundation, as their thermal pathways can be strategically manipulated via advanced processing techniques (30–32) to induce robust, tunable nonlinear responses. Compounding this materials challenge is the stark efficiency ceiling encountered by all existing thermal rectification strategies (33). Current approaches, even when combining the most favorable properties of known materials, are fundamentally constrained to low rectification ratios, particularly under ultrahigh-temperature conditions. Crucially, the pursuit of a single-material strategy, which is essential for circumventing the interfacial degradation and delamination that plague multimaterial components at extreme temperatures, has yielded no viable blueprint for high efficiency. This critical gap underscores the urgent need for a paradigm shift in thermal rectifier design. We propose that such a breakthrough can be achieved by leveraging the often-overlooked yet intrinsically strong nonlinearities of heat convection and radiation, which become dominant and highly nonlinear at elevated temperatures. By engineering a specific distribution of effective thermal conductivity modes within a monolithic structure, it becomes possible to orchestrate these nonlinearities and achieve markedly enhanced rectification performance. We posit that to overcome this stability-nonlinearity impasse, a paradigm shift in material architecture is required: moving away from the search for ideal materials or simple bilayer composites and toward the rational design of a single, thermally stable structure that can spontaneously generate and orchestrate strong nonlinearities. This can be achieved by leveraging the often-overlooked yet intrinsically strong nonlinearities of heat convection and radiation, which become dominant and highly tunable at elevated temperatures.

Extending such nonreciprocal thermal transport from one to three dimensions presents fundamental physical and architectural challenges. In conventional, monolithic thermal diodes, rectification is a global property of an inseparable structure; it cannot be described by a meaningful local rectification coefficient (6, 7). This absence of a spatially defined functional field makes genuine three-dimensional designs intrinsically unattainable. Any attempt to scale such designs beyond a single axis confronts an insurmountable conflict: Achieving independent directional control requires spatially varying material properties and asymmetric nonlinearities, yet the global, indivisible nature of the rectification mechanism prevents the required functional partitionability and spatial programmability. Consequently, prior work has been confined to layered or low-symmetry one-dimensional layouts, leaving programmable volumetric thermal control an open frontier.

RESULTS

Rationale for the monolithic metaceramic thermal architecture

Here, drawing upon the principles of metamaterials, we introduce metaceramics as architected monolithic multiscale porous ceramics in which engineered effective properties arise from architecture rather than composition alone, enabling them to fundamentally resolve the stability-nonlinearity conflict (Fig. 1A). This work integrates and couples four distinct nonlinear heat transfer mechanisms, namely, tailored conduction, modulated radiation, chaotic advection–enhanced convection, and spontaneous dissipation, within a single, thermally stable, and functionally continuous architecture. This integration shatters the long-standing operating temperature barrier of ~900 K (Fig. 1B) that previously constrained thermal rectifiers. In addition, the continuum-like, locally defined rectification behavior of this meta-ceramic surpasses the fundamental efficiency limit under which previous performance improvements were confined to incremental gains, such as an increase in rectification ratio from 1.2 in 2006 to only 3.5 by 2021 (Fig. 1C). This metaceramic exhibits strong nonreciprocal heat transport and demonstrates a record thermal rectification ratio of 8.5 at 2473 K. Crucially, its rectification function is spatially integrable, allowing the design to be extended into a triaxial rectification architecture that enables three-dimensional thermal control. This work establishes a class of thermal metamaterials that break reciprocity in heat flow under extreme conditions, opening a path toward programmable thermal management in high-temperature systems.

The exceptional rectification efficiency achieved in our meta-ceramic originates from the deliberate conjunction of two fundamental conditions within its monolithic architecture: structural asymmetry and anticorrelated temperature-dependent thermal conductivities. Effective thermal rectification first requires a violation of spatial inversion symmetry in the heat conduction pathway. This is achieved by engineering a continuous spatial gradient in the local microarchitecture, described by a volume fraction function X(x) that satisfies X(x) ≠ X(L−x), where L is the length of the system. The effective thermal conductivity can be modeled as κeff(x,T) = κ1(x,T)X(x) + κ2(x,T)[1−X(x)], where κ1 and κ2 represent two distinct, temperature-dependent thermal conductivity modes governed by the local microstructure. However, structural asymmetry alone yields only limited rectification. The key to high efficiency lies in the programmed anticorrelated temperature dependence of these effective thermal conductivity modes, κ1(T) and κ2(T), which are governed by distinct local heat transfer mechanisms (e.g., radiation-dominated versus convection-enhanced; Fig. 1A). When one mode’s conductivity decreases with temperature while the other’s increases, a powerful synergistic effect is unlocked. Under a given thermal bias, this creates a self-reinforcing feedback loop between the emergent temperature field and the spatially graded conductivity profile, enabling a vast disparity between forward and reverse heat flow resistances.

This operational principle is elucidated by considering the coupled state of the spatial gradient and the temperature-dependent conductivities under opposing biases. In the forward bias configuration, the hot end of the gradient is dominated by the material mode [e.g., mode 1, κ1(T) decreasing with T] which, at the elevated temperature, resides in its high-conductivity regime. Simultaneously, the cold end is dominated by the opposing mode [e.g., mode 1, κ1(T) decreasing with T], which, at the lower temperature, is also in its high-conductivity state. This alignment creates a coherent, low-resistance pathway across the entire structure. Conversely, under reverse bias, the roles are inverted: Heat enters through mode 2 at the hot end, where it is forced into a low-conductivity state, and must exit through mode 1 at the cold end, which is also in a low-conductivity state. This misalignment establishes a dual thermal bottleneck at both ends of the heat flow path, resulting in a markedly increased overall thermal resistance. The profound contrast between the synergistic low-resistance forward state and the dual-bottleneck high-resistance reverse state is the direct physical origin of the record-breaking rectification ratio demonstrated in our work.

On the basis of the identified strategy for ultrahigh rectification efficiency, which requires materials with opposing thermal conductivity–temperature dependencies, the intuitive approach would be to combine two different materials incorporating both nonlinearity and asymmetry. However, such dual-material design, despite its capacity to incorporate both nonlinearity and asymmetry, faces severe challenges in ultrahigh-temperature environments. Particularly in brittle ceramic systems, thermal expansion mismatch across material interfaces induces significant thermal stresses, often leading to delamination or catastrophic failure. To overcome this fundamental limitation, we developed a metaceramic-based solution: a single ceramic matrix with spatially programmed functional asymmetry, achieved through architected porosity across dual scales and graded triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) structures (Fig. 2A). This monolithic design delivers its exceptional functionality by enabling two critical, scale-dependent mechanisms within a single component. The graded TPMS geometry provides (i) inherent geometric asymmetry through pore density gradients to break thermal reciprocity and (ii) integrated millimeter-scale cavities that amplify spontaneous chaotic advection under thermal bias (Fig. 2B), thereby generating a convective heat transfer component with a strong positive temperature dependence. Concurrently, at the microscale, the base ceramic matrix is engineered with a fine distribution of pores, the size of which is meticulously tailored to be comparable to the dominant wavelength of thermal radiation at the target temperature. According to Mie theory, which governs scattering in this size regime, this precise pore sizing maximizes scattering efficiency, thereby suppressing radiative heat transfer through the solid matrix. It is this meticulous microscale design that effectively tailors the solid phase to exhibit a negative correlation between its effective thermal conductivity and temperature. The ingenious coupling of this negative temperature-dependent conductive base with the positive temperature-dependent convective contribution, all integrated within the continuous TPMS structure, artificially creates the crucial cross-property relationship in thermal conductivity (Fig. 2C), thus fulfilling the fundamental condition for record-breaking rectification (Fig. 2D).

Material engineering and synergistic coupling of mechanisms

The thermal performance of the microporous base matrix is paramount. Achieving the desired negative correlation, where thermal conductivity decreases with rising temperature, requires precise control over pore size, distribution, and volume fraction to dominate and alter the intrinsic temperature-dependent conduction and radiation properties of the ceramic material. We use porous zirconia (ZrO2) ceramic to modulate the temperature-dependent thermal conductivity by regulating radiative heat transfer via engineering of both pore size and shape and by adjusting the matrix’s thermal conductivity by ion doping. At 2473 K, the submicrometer-scale pores (0.4 to 2 μm in diameter) within the ZrO2-based ceramic (porosity 10.8 vol %) effectively suppress radiative heat transfer through Mie scattering, as the peak thermal wavelength approaches ~1.3 μm (Fig. 2E). Concurrently, magnesium oxide doping introduces point defects that substantially intensify phonon scattering (see Materials and Methods). This combined effect, comprising pore-limited radiation and defect-enhanced phonon scattering, reduces the effective thermal conductivity to ~1.1 W m−1 K−1, markedly lower than the ~1.96 W m−1 K−1 typical of conventional zirconia at this temperature. We note that the uncertainty in thermal conductivity measurements increases at ultrahigh temperatures (error bars shown in Fig. 2C), which is attributable to the laser flash analysis method requiring a thin carbon coating on the sample surface. At temperatures exceeding 2000 K, carbon infiltration into the porous zirconia structure becomes increasingly pronounced, introducing greater variability in the measured thermal diffusivity and consequently larger SDs in the calculated thermal conductivity. In contrast, at room temperature, the same Mg2+ doping produces an opposite effect: It increases the thermal conductivity from about 2.9 W m−1 K−1 (characteristic of dense, undoped zirconia) to 5 W m−1 K−1, likely by modulating grain-boundary phonon transport. This deliberate, doping-enabled reversal of the thermal conductivity’s temperature trend, which decreases at high temperature while increasing at low temperature, is engineered to create the strongly anticorrelated temperature dependence between distinct conduction modes, which is essential for achieving high-efficiency thermal rectification. Furthermore, Mg2+ doping effectively suppresses the polymorphic phase transitions in zirconia, which enhances its resistance to external stresses, thereby reducing the challenges associated with manufacturing complex test specimens and significantly extending their operational life span.

On the basis of the preceding analysis, this multiscale porous ceramic architecture achieves a deliberately engineered, cross-distributed nonlinear thermal conductivity κ(T). Specifically, the millimeter-scale pores architected via TPMS structures induce spontaneous chaotic advection, resulting in a convective contribution whose effective conductivity increases with temperature. Concurrently, the Mg2+-doped zirconia matrix exhibits the opposite trend, as its lattice conductivity decreases substantially with temperature, a behavior further accentuated by the Mie-scattering suppression of radiative transfer enabled by the incorporated submicrometer pores. By integrating these opposing temperature dependencies within a spatially graded TPMS framework, the system physically realizes the conditions required for high-efficiency thermal rectification. While effective medium theory predicts diminished rectification as κair approaches zero (21) because of volume-averaged conductivity κ(T)X(x) + κair[1 − X(x)], our graded TPMS overcomes this limitation by having millimeter-scale cavities that enable two asymmetric effects. First, geometric asymmetry arising from the programmed pore density gradient intrinsically breaks thermal reciprocity. Second, these millimeter-scale TPMS channels induce spontaneous chaotic advection, generating a convective contribution whose effective thermal conductivity exhibits a strongly nonlinear temperature dependence. This convective enhancement is described by κcov(T) = htra κair (T/T0)3, where the cubic temperature dependence explicitly captures the strongly nonlinear nature of radiation-coupled natural convection at elevated temperatures, and T0 denotes the reference temperature of the hot side. The parameter htra, analogous to the Nusselt number, quantifies the geometry-induced flow enhancement relative to pure conduction and reflects the efficiency of convective heat transfer within the specific TPMS architecture. The overall effective thermal conductivity integrates these pore-mediated conduction/radiation and enhanced convective contributions, establishing the pronounced nonlinear and nonreciprocal thermal response observed. The effective thermal conductivity could be expressed asκeff(x,T)=κs(T)X(x)+htraκair(TT0)3[1−X(x)](1)where κs(T) describes the pore-mediated conduction-radiation nonlinearity that can be fitted by the curves in Fig. 2C and X(x) represents a Boolean-engineered TPMS gradient structure (Fig. 2A). To maximize convective channels, X(x) is generated by subtracting a 10 to 5% gradient TPMS model from a 20 to 90% gradient one (fig. S9), creating interconnected millimeter-scale cavities that amplify chaotic advection. Concurrently, surface convective dissipation interacts with pore-engineered κ(T) nonlinearities and internal flow vortices, collectively generating a robust thermal diode effect that shatters theoretical performance ceilings.

Natural convective dissipation breaks thermal conservation, thus also enabling nonreciprocal heat transport (34, 35). Under natural heat transfer conditions, the structure inevitably dissipates heat to the ambient air via convection, transforming the system from a conservative to a nonconservative regime. The system satisfies the following equation∇⋅(κeff∇T)+hdis/C(T−TA)=0(2)where κeff is determined by Eq. 1, hdis is the heat dissipation coefficient, TA is the ambient temperature, and C is the circumference of the sample cross section. Under fixed-temperature boundary conditions, we measured the heat flux through the entire system at the cold end for a gradient TPMS structure, with the cold side maintained at room temperature and the hot side held at elevated temperatures ranging from 1673 to 2473 K, as shown in Fig. 2D. From these experimental data, the convective heat transfer coefficients (htra) and convective heat dissipation coefficients (hdis) were assessed. To rigorously determine the two unknown parameters, we introduced a well-controlled contrast system: an identical TPMS structure fabricated from porous silica ceramic. Owing to their identical macroscopic geometry, the convective dissipation coefficient (hdis) is the same for both the zirconia and silica systems. This strategic experimental design provided two independent sets of performance data under identical thermal boundary conditions, enabling a well-constrained fitting procedure for the two unknowns. In the silica system, the pore size was specifically engineered to avoid the Mie scattering regime, resulting in a matrix whose effective thermal conductivity increases significantly with temperature (see the Supplementary Materials, section S1.1 and fig. S2). This positive temperature dependence aligns with, rather than counteracts, the trend introduced by convective heat transfer, leading to a lower rectification ratio. The measured efficiency of 3.5 at 1673 K, while lower than that of zirconia, matches the state-of-the-art performance for other high-temperature rectifiers, thereby validating the reliability of our experimental measurements. The final fitted values, htra = 100 and hdis = 50 W/(m2·K) for the zirconia system, are thus physically justified and empirically grounded. This parameterization not only closes the model but also powerfully validates the underlying framework we established, demonstrating its ability to accurately capture and predict the thermal rectification behavior across different material systems.

Guided by Eqs. 1 and 2, we conducted a decoupled analysis of the individual nonlinear modes governing thermal transport. The conduction suppressed-radiation mode, corresponding to the case where T = T0 and htra = 1, demonstrated only marginal rectification, a result clearly reflected in the heat flux profiles shown in Fig. 3A. The introduction of convective heat transfer, represented by the parameter set T = T0 and htra = 1 in combination with the conduction radiation base, yielded a substantially stronger improvement in rectification performance compared with coupling with convective dissipation alone, which is defined by htra = 1 and hdis = 50 W/(m2K). Notably, the heat fluxes under both forward and backward bias conditions reached magnitudes on the order of 104 W/m2 across all configurations. These values substantially exceed the heat flux levels typically encountered in previous studies reporting high rectification ratios, making the achievement of high efficiency under such intense thermal loads particularly challenging. The maximum performance was ultimately realized through the integrated hybrid mode, which incorporates conduction radiation, convective transfer, and convective dissipation simultaneously. This synergistic combination resulted in a record rectification ratio of 8.56 at 2473 K. For comparison, we also analyzed a silica system under analogous conditions, as illustrated in figs. S2 to S6. In silica, the conduction-enhanced radiation mechanism aligns with, rather than counteracts, the convective transfer mode, leading to a less effective hybrid mode that achieved a rectification ratio of 3.5 at 1673 K. Further analysis details on convective heat transfer and dissipation mode can be found in the Supplementary Materials (sections S1.2 and S1.3, S14, and S15). The designed anticorrelated thermal conductivity profile, which couples opposing thermal transport mechanisms, more effectively harnesses convective transfer enhancement to achieve superior thermal rectification efficiency compared with systems with correlated thermal conductivity (figs. S10 and S11). In addition, the rectification ratio increases monotonically with sample height and convective dissipation strength, emphasizing that geometric and environmental factors are critical to maximizing performance (see the Supplementary Materials, section S1.4 and figs. S14 and S15). Our findings revolutionize ultrahigh-temperature thermal management by substituting delicate unstable interfaces between different materials with spatially designed multimodal interactions in a monolithic material with bespoke porosity, enabling resilient and efficient rectification in extreme environments (fig. S12).

Manufacturing and experimental results

The metaceramic structures were manufactured using digital light processing (DLP) additive manufacturing technology using formulations developed in-house. We fabricated 3 TPMS configurations, Diamond, FKS (Fischer-Koch surface), and Gyroid, in single-layer or bilayer forms, with height ratios of 1:2 and 1:3, while having the same geometric surface area (details in the Supplementary Materials, section S1.2). Furthermore, the proposed monolithic, single-material fabrication strategy enables large-scale manufacturing and can be readily extended to the manufacturing of sandwich structures for enhanced mechanical and thermal performance (Fig. 4A). In addition, this approach supports full customization to meet site-specific thermal management requirements within arbitrarily shaped domains. As illustrated in Fig. 4B, we successfully fabricated and demonstrated functional thermal rectifiers in diverse geometries, including triangular, clover-shaped, and butterfly-like regions, highlighting the versatility and adaptability of our architecture for complex real-world applications. While this demonstrates manufacturability, practical deployment in extreme environments, such as hypersonic vehicle surfaces or reusable thermal protection systems, will require further validation of long-term thermochemical stability under sustained thermal load and oxidizing atmospheres, as well as resistance to cyclic thermal shock during repeated heating-cooling cycles. Future work should also explore integration strategies with existing thermal protection systems, potentially through the development of graded interfacial layers or hybrid joining techniques that leverage the freeform shaping capabilities demonstrated here to create seamless, multifunctional thermal management solutions. Regarding the scalability of the DLP fabrication process, current size constraints are primarily dictated by the build envelope of commercially available DLP systems. The debinding and sintering stages also introduce further dimensional limitations, as large components are prone to cracking during thermal processing due to nonuniform shrinkage and gas evolution. To overcome these constraints, several pathways for larger-scale manufacturing can be envisaged, including the development of large-format DLP systems with expanded build areas, hybrid manufacturing approaches that combine DLP with complementary additive techniques, and modular design strategies wherein large components are printed as smaller subunits for subsequent assembly. These developments suggest that the scalability challenge is addressable through continued innovation in both printing hardware and postprocessing protocols.

We tested a total of 24 models under Cartesian coordinates for bidirectional heat transfer performance (fig. S13). A heat flow sensor (model HT50) concurrently measured both heat flux and the temperature of the contact surface. The ultrahigh temperature sensor used for calibration is the NS-T51WRe5 type, capable of consistently outputting a test signal of 2473 K. During testing, we established the hot-side temperature using an oxy-acetylene torch, and maintained the cold side at room temperature. To precisely control the hot-side temperature, we performed a calibration procedure. We adjusted the flame temperature by controlling the oxygen-to-fuel ratio, and carefully varied the distance between the torch nozzle and the heating surface before the measurement to achieve the desired interface temperature. The heating surface itself was fabricated from an ultrahigh-temperature ceramic plate, which served as a stable thermal interface in contact with either the temperature sensor or the test sample. For calibration, we placed a high-temperature sensor (model: NS-T51WRe5) in direct contact with the interface where the sample would later be positioned. We measured the interface temperature as we iteratively adjusted the flame conditions and nozzle distance until the fixed target temperatures (2473, 2273, 2073, 1873, and 1673 K) were each achieved and stabilized. Once the calibration was completed for a given temperature setpoint, we replaced the sensor with the actual test sample, and steady-state forward and reverse heat flux measurements were conducted under identical flame and positioning conditions. This procedure ensured that the thermal boundary conditions applied to the sample were both well defined and reproducible across all experiments. As shown in Fig. 4D, the cylindrical Gyroid configuration with a diameter-to-height ratio of 1:3 achieved a thermal rectification ratio of 8.5, outperforming other TPMS geometries under identical conditions (fig. S13). Figure 4C presents a comparative analysis of experimental and simulated temperature profiles for zirconia Gyroid structures at varying temperatures. Experimental validation using calibrated oxy-acetylene heating demonstrates that the Gyroid structure with a 1:3 diameter-to-height ratio achieves maximum rectification efficiency, surpassing alternative Diamond and FKS architectures (details in the Supplementary Materials, section S1.2). This performance variation is directly attributable to the distinct convective heat transfer coefficients (htra) inherent to different structural architectures, which ultimately govern the rectification efficiency. For instance, the Gyroid topology exhibited a superior htra value compared with Diamond or FKS structures (see fig. S16), directly correlating with its enhanced rectification ratio. This underscores that beyond material selection, the precise geometric design of the porous medium is a critical determinant of performance, as it dictates the intensity of advection-driven nonlinearity. Optimizing these architectural parameters provides a powerful pathway to further boost thermal rectification capabilities.

While the rectification ratio of 8.5 achieved in this work represents a substantial advance over prior benchmarks, it does not represent a fundamental upper limit for this material system. Further enhancement can be pursued through several interconnected strategies. Central to rectification efficiency is the magnitude of nonlinearity in the two anticorrelated thermal conductivity modes; amplifying this nonlinearity, particularly in the convective transfer contribution, would increase the asymmetry between forward and reverse heat flow. This can be achieved by developing previously unexplored TPMS architectures that generate more intense chaotic advection to increase the convective transfer coefficient htra and by refining material engineering to enhance the temperature-dependent nonlinearity of the solid-phase conductivity κs(T) through optimized dopant concentration and pore size distribution. Equally important is the symmetry between these two modes, for when both exhibit strong nonlinearity with well-matched magnitudes, they contribute synergistically to the rectification effect. The alumina system illustrates this principle: Despite its intrinsically strong thermal nonlinearity, its order-of-magnitude higher absolute conductivity compared with zirconia creates poor symmetry with the convective contribution, limiting overall performance. The convective dissipation coefficient htra offers a third, independent lever for optimization, as its monotonic positive correlation with rectification ratio demonstrated in figs. S14 and S15 underscores the importance of environmental coupling; strategies such as incorporating active cooling features or optimizing external surface geometry to promote air flow could further amplify the rectification effect. Continued progress in all three directions, including architectural refinement to maximize htra, materials processing to enhance and match κs(T), and engineering of external dissipation pathways, provides a clear roadmap for achieving even higher rectification efficiencies in future work.

Triaxial thermal rectifier

Building on the demonstrated high-efficiency uniaxial thermal rectification at extreme temperatures, extending this principle to multidimensional control presents a fundamental challenge. Conventional rectifiers, whose function is an emergent property of an indivisible whole, lack a spatially definable rectification field. This renders them incapable of genuine three-dimensional scaling, as independent control of heat flow along orthogonal axes requires a material whose nonreciprocal function is both partitionable and programmable at a local level. To further extend the concept of unidirectional thermal rectification into multidimensional heat control, we proposed a triaxial ultrahigh-temperature rectifier (TUTR; Fig. 4E). This cubic meta-ceramic monolith incorporates orthogonally aligned architectural gradients derived from a uniaxial cylindrical prototype, enabling programmable thermal asymmetry along all three spatial dimensions. The TUTR device demonstrated consistent thermal rectification capabilities along the x, y, and z axes, with measured rectification ratios of 1.54, 1.52, and 1.73, respectively, at 2473 K. Although these values are modest compared with uniaxial devices, resulting from shallower architectural gradients (e.g., 90%:40.3%, 40.3%:20.8%, and 20.8%:10% between vertex pairs, versus 85%:10% in unidirectional designs), they nevertheless represent a successful proof of concept for three-dimensional thermal rectification. This transformative approach effectively elevates the thermal rectification ratio from a scalar quantity to a tensor-like property that characterizes the direction-dependent nonreciprocal thermal response, introducing an additional design dimension for programmable thermal management and analog thermal computing systems. Unlike conventional one-dimensional rectifiers, our TUTR enables spatially tailored heat flow control in three dimensions, marking a substantial expansion in the functionality and application scope of solid-state thermal devices.

DISCUSSION

This work establishes a paradigm for breaking thermal reciprocity under extreme conditions through a monolithic metamaterial architecture. By structurally engineering asymmetric and anticorrelated thermal transport pathways, we demonstrate giant nonreciprocal heat flow, achieving a rectification ratio of 8.5 at 2473 K, and realize three-dimensional thermal rectification through the triaxial thermal rectifier (TUTR), introducing a tensor-like thermal property. Our approach transcends the classical stability-nonlinearity trade-off, transforming heat flow into a spatially programmable quantity. These advances offer practical solutions for thermal shielding in aerospace systems, heat regulation in hypersonic vehicles, and thermal management (36–38) in next-generation nuclear reactors. Future endeavors will concentrate on extending this architecture to additional refractory materials (39) and enhancing various multimaterial TPMS geometries. Our work breaks the long-standing trade-off between thermal stability and nonlinearity, opening up possibilities for smart heat-flow control in thermal environments that were previously impossible to work with. Beyond its immediate contributions to ultrahigh-temperature ceramics, this work establishes a design paradigm for architected metamaterials that could inform broader advances in thermal management systems, energy conversion devices, and adaptive thermal logic circuits. The demonstrated ability to engineer nonlinear, nonreciprocal heat transport through multiscale architecture rather than mere compositional variation alone underscores the transdisciplinary value of this approach, offering a versatile platform for controlling energy flow across length scales and application domains. The engineered nonreciprocal response in the metaceramic, achieved through structural asymmetry and nonlinear heat transfer mechanisms rather than magnetic fields or external biases, shares conceptual parallels with recently explored non-Hermitian (40) and topological systems (41) for wave and heat transport. This suggests that architected materials of this class could also serve as a platform for investigating topological effects in diffusive thermal transport, potentially enabling phenomena such as thermal edge states or nonreciprocal heat steering. Future work may explore whether the direction-dependent thermal conductivity tensor engineered here exhibits topological invariants or non-Hermitian characteristics analogous to those studied in electronic and photonic systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Thermal conductivity of architected porous metaceramics

As shown in Fig. 2, we engineered the thermal transport properties of the metaceramic through architected porosity and Mg2+ doping to achieve a strongly negative correlation between effective thermal conductivity and temperature. In parallel, a silica-based reference system was designed to exhibit a positive correlation through tailored pore size distributions, as detailed in section S1.1.

The magnesium-doped zirconia metaceramics demonstrate a remarkable negative temperature dependence, achieving an unprecedented thermal conductivity reduction at ultrahigh temperatures (Fig. 2E). Despite introducing controlled porosity using sacrificial pore formers, ~10.8 vol % with pore sizes spanning 0.4 to 2 μm (figs. S2 and S8), the room temperature conductivity increased from 2.9 W/(m·K) for the dense reference to 5.0 W/(m·K) in the doped porous structure. This initial enhancement stems from Mg2+ incorporation into the zirconia lattice, as confirmed by Rietveld refinement showing co-occupancy of Zr and Mg sites (0.948: 0.052 ratio) within a stabilized monoclinic structure (a = 5.154 Å, b = 5.203 Å, c = 5.319 Å, β = 99.24°; Rwp = 8.68%, GOF (goodness of fit) = 1.84). The dopants introduce point defects that alter phonon scattering, marginally enhancing conduction at room temperature while markedly suppressing it at high temperatures because of intensified phonon-defect interactions.

At 2473 K, the material’s thermal conductivity drops precipitously to 0.5 W m−1 K−1, markedly below values for dense zirconia and conventional ceramics. This anomalous reduction arises from the deliberate matching of pore size to the peak blackbody radiation wavelength {λpeak ≈ 2898/[T (K) − 273] μm, e.g., ~1.3 μm at 2473 K}, inducing strong Mie scattering that quenches radiative heat transfer. Although total porosity is moderate, the high concentration of pores within the critical 0.4- to 2-μm range (figs. S7B and S8B), combined with dopant-tuned optical properties, ensures dominant radiative suppression. Thus, even as solid conduction declines, radiatively blocked heat flux drives the ultralow effective conductivity.

This metaceramic design demonstrates that strategic pore architecture and dopant integration can invert conventional temperature-conductivity relationships, enabling exceptional thermal insulation under extreme conditions through tailored photon and phonon scattering.
