Variational Routing: A Scalable Bayesian Framework for Calibrated Mixture-of-Experts Transformers
Albus Yizhuo Li, Matthew Wicker

Abstract
Foundation models are increasingly being deployed in contexts where understanding the uncertainty of their outputs is critical to ensuring responsible deployment. While Bayesian methods offer a principled approach to uncertainty quantification, their computational overhead renders their use impractical for training or inference at foundation model scale. State-of-the-art models achieve parameter counts in the trillions through carefully engineered sparsity including Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layers. In this work, we demonstrate calibrated uncertainty at scale by introducing Variational Mixture-of-Experts Routing (VMoER), a structured Bayesian approach for modelling uncertainty in MoE layers. VMoER confines Bayesian inference to the expert-selection stage which is typically done by a deterministic routing network. We instantiate VMoER using two inference strategies: amortised variational inference over routing logits and inferring a temperature parameter for stochastic expert selection. Across tested foundation models, VMoER improves routing stability under noise by 38%, reduces calibration error by 94%, and increases out-of-distribution AUROC by 12%, while incurring less than 1% additional FLOPs. These results suggest VMoER offers a scalable path toward robust and uncertainty-aware foundation models.

Introduction

Foundation models are increasingly deployed in open-world settings characterised by distributional shift, partial observability, and high-stakes decision-making
(
4
;
47
)
. In such settings, the ability to quantify uncertainty is central to responsible deployment, governing when predictions should be trusted, deferred, or interrogated
(
27
;
1
;
54
)
. However, contemporary foundation models overwhelmingly rely on deterministic training and inference pipelines that obscure epistemic uncertainty and yield overconfident predictions outside their training support
(
20
)
. While Bayesian methods provide a principled framework for uncertainty quantification, their computational and memory costs have rendered them largely incompatible with the scale at which modern foundation models operate
(
51
)
. The tension between the necessity of uncertainty-aware reasoning and the engineering realities of trillion-parameter architectures has resulted in a critical gap between theory and practice. Bridging this gap requires uncertainty estimation mechanisms that are both statistically grounded and natively compatible with the sparsity and modularity that enable foundation model scaling.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layers have become the standard for scaling foundation models, replacing dense Feed-Forward Networks (FFN) with a set of specialised “experts” networks to achieve massive parameter counts at constant inference cost
(
46
)
. At the time of writing, every non-proprietary foundation model in the top fifty of LMArena’s text leaderboard employs an MoE architecture
(
11
)
.

Central to MoEs is the
routing mechanism
, a lightweight network that selects a sparse subset of experts for each token.
In standard implementations, this router operates in three stages:
(1) a
linear projection
computes similarity scores (logits);
(2) a
Softmax
function projects them onto probability simplex;
and (3) a hard
Top-K
operation enforces sparsity.

While efficient and scalable, routing networks are prone to various failure modes. During training, MoE routers may experience expert collapse where routers send a disproportionate fraction of tokens to a small subset of experts
(
22
)
; during fine-tuning, selection drift can substantially hinder performance
(
59
)
, and routing decisions are often sensitive to numerical precision and input perturbations
(
18
;
31
)
. In Figure
1(a)
we illustrate this instability by demonstrating the brittleness of routing decisions in response to small random input noise.

One effective approach to mitigating routing failures is the introduction of stochasticity
(
18
)
which has been a tried-and-trusted method to stabilise training dynamics, improve robustness, and capture uncertainty. In Figure
1(b)
we show that replacing hard Top-K with low-temperature sampling (
T<1
) not only improves robustness (as shown in
(
18
)
) but significantly reduces calibration error (ECE). In this paper, the critical question we raise is
how
to add stochasticity. We adopt a principled Bayesian perspective adding stochasticity into MoE layers. Prior Bayesian approaches in foundation models focus on weight uncertainty, modelling uncertainty within attention or experts and often incurring substantial inference-time overheads
(
16
)
.

We propose
Variational Routing
for MoE (
VMoER
), a framework that shifts the site of inference from the parameters of attention or experts themselves to the router’s decisions. Viewing MoE routing probabilistically, we observe that Top-K routing is inherently multi-label, while existing routers rely on single-label likelihoods (softmax vectors), preventing modelling of expert correlations. Further, a Bayesian perspective allows us to view previous stabilisation strategies (load balancing regularisation
(
31
)
and auxiliary losses
(
59
)
) as implicit priors.

To structure our Bayesian approach, we propose two complementary forms of variational inference for routing networks: (1)
Logit-Space Inference:
we consider applying amortised variational inference directly to the similarity scores to capture expert correlations and (2)
Selection-Space Inference:
which learns a latent, input-dependent temperature to dynamically scale decision boundaries. Both approaches side-step the need for and computational burden of weight-space posteriors.

Probabilistic Mixture-of-Experts Routing

We begin by describing and formalising the standard, deterministic routing network. We consider a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer embedded within a Transformer block.
Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denote the input token representation and
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be a set of
N
expert networks parameterised by weights
\mathbf{W}_{\text{exp}}
.
The subject of our focus is the
routing mechanism
, parameterised by learnable weights
\mathbf{W}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
, which determines the sparse assignment of tokens to experts.

Deterministic Routing

In standard implementations, the router operates as a deterministic directed graph, processing variables in the following sequence (as shown in Figure
2
):

Logits:

\displaystyle\mathbf{l}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{W}_{r}

(1)

Probs:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}

\displaystyle=\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{l}),\quad\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{N-1}

(2)

Selection:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\text{Top-K}(\mathbf{p}),\quad\mathbf{z}\in\{0,1\}^{N}

(3)

The final layer output
\mathbf{y}
is the weighted sum of expert outputs, gated by both the probability parameters
p_{i}
and the discrete selection mask
z_{i}
:
\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}\cdot p_{i}\cdot E_{i}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{W}_{\text{exp}})

2.1
MoE as a Latent Variable Model

To reason about how to conduct inference, we reformulate MoE layer as a probabilistic generative model, visually depicted as a PGM in Figure
3
.
We postulate that the observed output
\mathbf{y}
is generated through a latent decision process involving logits
\mathbf{l}
, probability simplex
\mathbf{p}
, and discrete selection mask
\mathbf{z}
.

Generative Process

We define the generative process for a single token as follows:

(1)

Latent Logit Generation (
\mathbf{l}
):

Given input
\mathbf{u}
and router weights
\mathbf{W}_{r}
, the model generates a latent logit vector from the conditional distribution
p(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u},\mathbf{W}_{r})
.
In deterministic routing, this collapses to a Dirac delta:
\delta(\mathbf{l}-\mathbf{u}\mathbf{W}_{r})
.

(2)

Probability Projection (
\mathbf{p}
):

The logits are transformed into the simplex via
\mathbf{p}=\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{l})
.
The uncertainty in the logits propagates through the Softmax function, establishing the dependency relation
p(\mathbf{p}|\mathbf{l})
.

(3)

Sparse Expert Selection (
\mathbf{z}
):

The discrete selection mask
\mathbf{z}
is sampled based on the probabilities
\mathbf{p}
, subject to the sparsity constraint
\|\mathbf{z}\|_{0}=k
. We denote this conditional dependency as
p(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{p})
.

(4)

Observation (
\mathbf{y}
):

Finally, the output is generated by the selected experts, conditioned on the active path defined by
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{p}
:

\mathbf{y}\sim p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{W}_{\text{exp}})
.

The Bayesian Inference Challenge

Ideally, for a new input
\mathbf{u}^{*}
, we wish to compute posterior predictive distribution by marginalising over all global parameters
\Theta=\{\mathbf{W}_{r},\mathbf{W}_{\text{exp}}\}
and latents:

2
2

2

We’re using
\int
to denote marginalisation over both continuous and discrete variables (
\mathbf{z}
) for notational clarity.

\begin{split}&p(\mathbf{y}^{*}|\mathbf{u}^{*},\mathcal{D})\\
=&\int\!\!\underbrace{\iiint p(\mathbf{y}^{*}|\mathbf{u},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{p},\Theta)\,p(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{p})\,p(\mathbf{p}|\mathbf{l})\,p(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u}^{*},\Theta)\,d\mathbf{l}\,d\mathbf{p}\,d\mathbf{z}}_{\text{Latent Activation Marginalisation}}\\
&\times\!\!\!\!\underbrace{p(\Theta\mid\mathcal{D})\,d\Theta}_{\text{Weight Posterior Integration}}\end{split}

(4)

Standard MoE training performs Point Estimation (MAP/MLE), effectively approximating the weight posterior with delta functions at
\hat{\Theta}
.
However, even with fixed weights, the inner integral over the latent dependency (
\mathbf{l}\to\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{z}
) remains.
Standard routing ignores this by taking a single deterministic path (Top-K), thereby discarding the predictive uncertainty.

2.2
The Weight-Space Critique

:

Probabilistic graphical model formulation of MoE routing treating decisions as latent variables.

Figure 4
:
Weight-Space Inference.
Uncertainty is modeled by sampling global parameters
\mathbf{W}_{r}\sim p(\mathbf{W}_{r}\mid\mathcal{D})
.

The conventional approach to intractable posterior inference is to approximate the distribution over global parameters,
p(\mathbf{\Theta}\mid\mathcal{D})
, often referred to as
Weight-Space
methods (e.g., MC Dropout
(
19
)
, SWAG
(
35
)
, Deep Ensembles
(
30
)
).
In the context of MoE routing, this corresponds to approximating the
outer
integral in Equation
4
while ignoring the inner marginalisation over latent states. While theoretically sound, weight-space inference is indirect for the task of routing.
Ideally, one might target the discrete selection
\mathbf{z}
directly, however, this is combinatorially intractable and insufficient, as the final output
\mathbf{y}
also relies on the continuous probabilities
\mathbf{p}
for expert weighting.
Consequently, our primary interest lies in modelling a posterior over the
routing decision variables
—specifically the logits
\mathbf{l}
and probabilities
\mathbf{p}
—which serve as the differentiable precursors to both selection and weighting.
Weight-space methods, by contrast, rely on linear projections to propagate parameter noise from
\mathbf{W}_{r}
onto these variables. This induces an indirect posterior over the routing decision variables which makes prior selection challenging and may result in an undesirable uncertainty profile.
Instead of modeling the indirect cause (weight uncertainty), it is more effective to directly model the proximal effect (routing decision uncertainty).

3
Logit-Space Variational Inference

Figure 5
:
Variational Gaussian Logit Router (VGLR) Architecture.
Latent logits are sampled from a posterior parameterised by a shared-trunk network using the reparameterisation trick, then averaged to marginalise routing uncertainty.

To overcome the structural bottlenecks of weight-space inference, we propose treating the routing logits
\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
not as deterministic values, but as stochastic latent variables governed by the input
\mathbf{u}
.
Our goal is to approximate the intractable posterior
p(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u})
using a variational distribution
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u})
parameterised by a lightweight inference network
\phi
.
This constitutes an
amortised inference
approach, where the global network
\phi
learns to map any input token directly to the
sufficient statistics
of its approximate posterior.
We adopt the standard variational approach with multivariate Gaussian posteriors i.e., optimizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u})}[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{l},\mathbf{u})]-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u})\,||\,p(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u}))

(5)

Note that the
reconstruction term

p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{l},\mathbf{u})
remains conditioned on the input
\mathbf{u}
, as the final MoE output depends on both the expert selection (derived from
\mathbf{l}
) and the experts’ computation on the token itself.
The second term is the
KL divergence term
, which regularises the posterior against a prior belief
p(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u})
whose strength is governed by
\beta
.

3.1
Residual Learning & The Centred Prior

A critical challenge in fine-tuning is preserving the routing performance of the pre-trained model.
To ensure stability, we employ a
residual learning
strategy.
Instead of predicting the posterior mean from scratch, the inference network learns a
residual correction

\Delta\bm{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})
added to the deterministic logits:

\bm{\mu}_{post}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{l}_{det}+\Delta\bm{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{u}),\quad\text{where }\mathbf{l}_{det}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{W}_{r}

(6)

This architectural choice induces what we term as a
centred prior
:
a Gaussian prior,
p
, centered on the deterministic solution:
p(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{l}_{det},\mathbf{I})
.
This formulation offers a significant theoretical advantage: by centering the prior on the deterministic baseline, the KL divergence simplifies to the distance between the learned residual and zero, that is:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal{N}(\Delta\bm{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{u}),\bm{\Sigma}_{post})\,||\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}))

(7)

3.2
Architecture: Variational Gaussian Logit Router

We term this framework the
Variational Gaussian Logit Router (VGLR)
, illustrated in Figure
5
. The architecture employs a heteroscedastic network:
a common backbone extracts features from the input token, which then branch into distinct heads to predict the residual mean
\Delta\bm{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})
and the covariance parameters.

The Correlation Challenge

Standard variational approaches typically adopt a
mean-field (MF)
approximation, assuming the latent dimensions are statistically independent.
While computationally efficient (
O(N)
), this assumption is suboptimal for routing because expert suitabilities are often highly correlated (e.g., experts specialising in similar domains).
Unlike high-dimensional image latent spaces, the MoE routing space are relatively small (
N\leq 64
).
This allows us to move beyond the naive mean-field baseline and explicitly model expert correlations via
full covariance (FC)
modelling.
We propose a
full-covariance
formulation where the inference network predicts the entries of a lower-triangular Cholesky factor
\mathbf{L}_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})
, defining a dense covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{post}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
.
Sampling is performed via the multivariate reparameterisation trick:

\mathbf{l}=\mathbf{l}_{det}+\Delta\bm{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})+\mathbf{L}_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})\bm{\epsilon},\quad\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})

(8)

By capturing the off-diagonal terms in
\bm{\Sigma}_{post}
, VGLR-FC models the joint distribution of expert suitability, providing a richer uncertainty signal than deterministic or mean-field baselines.
Although this incurs
O(N^{2})
complexity, it remains negligible for standard expert counts.
The analytic KL loss is defined as:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{post})+\|\Delta\bm{\mu}\|_{2}^{2}-N-\log|\bm{\Sigma}_{post}|\right)

(9)

where the log-determinant term
\log|\bm{\Sigma}_{post}|
is efficiently computed as
2\sum\log(\text{diag}(\mathbf{L}))
.

3.3
Inference Strategy

While the training procedure resembles a standard VAE, the inference strategy requires adaptation because the posterior predictive distribution over the final decision space involves a non-linear Softmax transformation.
Since the integral of a Gaussian passing through a Softmax is intractable, we rely on Monte Carlo (MC) sampling.

Training
We use a single sample to compute reconstruction loss, leveraging mini-batch stochasticity for efficiency.

Inference
To obtain a robust estimate of the routing distribution, we draw
S
samples
\{\mathbf{l}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}
from the approximate posterior
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u})
.
The final routing probabilities are computed by averaging the softmax outputs:

\mathbf{p}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s}\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{l}^{(s)})
.
These averaged probabilities are then passed to the Top-K operator to yield the final discrete expert assignment
\mathbf{z}
.
This process effectively marginalises over the uncertainty in the logit space, ensuring that decisions are robust to noise in the router’s internal state.

4
Selection-Space Variational Inference

While Logit-Space inference (VGLR) provides a rich uncertainty representation, it relies on Monte Carlo averaging (
S>1
) to approximate the high-dimensional posterior integral, creating a computational bottleneck for latency-critical applications.
It may be more efficient to directly modeling the discrete posterior
p(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{u})
, but this is combinatorially intractable due to the explosive search space
\binom{N}{k}
. Thus, we seek an efficient mechanism that captures the
magnitude
of uncertainty directly on the decision boundary without the overhead of multiple samples or full covariance modeling.

To address this, we propose an Bayesian approach to
temperature scaling
. Rather than learning a generic posterior, we restrict variational family
q_{\phi}
to a physically meaningful 1D sub-manifold: the trajectory of distributions defined by scaling the fixed deterministic logits with a latent temperature, as in Figure
6
. By learning an input-dependent temperature
T_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})
, we can effectively reshape the probability simplex, allowing the use of a stochastic
Sample-K
operator to introduce necessary variance into selection space.

Figure 6
:
Geometry of Variational Temperature Scaling.
The temperature
T
acts as a radial coordinate on the simplex. A low
T
(green) pushes the distribution toward the vertices (certainty), while a high
T
(blue) pulls it toward the centre (uniformity). By learning
T_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})
, the model dynamically expands or contracts its
variational search space
(yellow region) based on input ambiguity.

4.1
Variational Formulation

We define the variational posterior
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{p}|\mathbf{u})
not as a generic distribution over the simplex, but as a specific point estimate constrained to a 1D manifold.
This manifold is parameterised by the fixed deterministic logits
\mathbf{l}_{\text{det}}
and governed by a latent, input-dependent temperature
T_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
:
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{p}|\mathbf{u})=\text{Softmax}\left(\mathbf{l}_{\text{det}}/T_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})\right).
As shown in Figure
6
, by varying
T_{\phi}
, the model can slide the posterior
\mathbf{p}
along the trajectory connecting the sharp deterministic solution (
T\to 0
) to the maximum-entropy centre of the simplex (
T\to\infty
).
The final discrete decision is obtained by applying the
Sample-K
operator to this posterior probability vector. We note that as
T_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})\to 0
, the distribution
\mathbf{p}_{\phi}
approaches a one-hot vector, and the Sample-K operation mathematically converges to the deterministic Top-K selection.

Epistemic Prior

To formalise the regularisation, we define the prior
p(\mathbf{p})
as the
uniform categorical distribution
over the
N
experts:

p(\mathbf{p})=\left[\frac{1}{N},\frac{1}{N},\dots,\frac{1}{N}\right]
.
This prior represents a state of “maximum epistemic uncertainty.”
It reflects the Bayesian belief that in the absence of strong evidence (e.g., OoD data or ambiguous tokens), the router should revert to a balanced, unbiased allocation rather than arbitrarily favouring a specific expert.

Entropy as a Variational Objective

As previously discussed, entropy regularization emerges in our framework as a consequence of posterior inference. Our objective,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{\phi,k}\log q_{\phi,k}+\log N

can be written in terms of the Shannon entropy:
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=-\mathcal{H}(q_{\phi})+\text{const}

Thus, minimising the KL divergence is mathematically equivalent to
maximising the entropy
of the routing policy.
This provides a Bayesian justification for entropy penalties in sparse routing as implicit priors.

4.2
Variational Temperature Scaling Router (VTSR)

The architecture of the VTSR is depicted in Figure
7
.
It consists of a lightweight MLP that runs in parallel with the deterministic router to predict the scalar temperature
T_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})
.
As computing gradients for Shannon entropy can be unstable, we employ a proxy objective, namely
a regularisation term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}=-\log T_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})
.
This pushes the router toward higher stochasticity (the prior) unless the data log-likelihood (reconstruction term) strongly demands a sharp, deterministic decision. One minor downside is that the discrete sampling step requires us to use
Gumbel-Softmax

(
25
)
to compute gradients for the learning process.

Experiments

We evaluate our proposed varational routing methods across three dimensions: (1) In-Distribution (ID) reliability, (2) Out-of-Distribution (OoD) detection capability, and (3) robustness to input perturbations. Finally, we analyse the efficiency of our approach to demonstrate practicality.

5.1
Experimental Setup

We evaluate VMoER across three dimensions: In-Distribution (ID) reliability, Out-of-Distribution (OoD) detection, and routing stability. This section details the models, datasets, and metrics used to ensure a rigorous comparison.

Models and Baselines

To verify the generalisability of our approach, we conduct experiments on three distinct Mixture-of-Experts architectures varying in scale:

(

)
,

(

)

(

We compare our two proposed families of VMoER, Logit-Space (

,

First, we establish a deterministic baseline using the standard

Second, we evaluate a heuristic baseline (

) which uses a fixed global temperature hyperparameter.
Finally, we compare against Weight-Space Bayesian baselines, specifically MCDropout (
MCDR
) and SWAG (

All evaluations are performed on Multiple-Choice Question Answering (MCQA) tasks under two distinct settings.

)

For OoD detection, we treat the task as binary classification (ID vs. OoD) and report

ID Performance and Calibration

5.5
Practicality: Efficiency Analysis

For Bayesian routing to be viable in large-scale foundation models, the computational overhead must be negligible relative to the base model.
We analyse the theoretical complexity and empirical cost (Activation Memory and FLOPs) of our methods against weight-space baselines.
Detailed derivations for all calculations are provided in Appendix

Activation Memory

As detailed in Table

S

O(S)

\sim

On the Granite-3B-MoE model, VGLR-FC adds only
\sim

Related Work

MoE Routing and Reliability.

Top language mdoels such as
14
and
56
demonstrate that sparse expert routing enables scalable foundation models.

46
and
22
identify early routing failures, including expert collapse and severe load imbalance.

31
and
18
show that deterministic Top-K routing is brittle to numerical precision and input perturbations, leading to unstable expert assignments under low-precision or noisy settings.

59
further demonstrate selection drift during fine-tuning and transfer, resulting in expert under-utilisation and degraded performance.
In practice, these issues are mitigated through precision management
(
31
)
, explicit load-balancing losses
(
46
;
18
)
, and controlled stochasticity such as noisy or softened Top-K routing
(
18
;
59
)
; however, these mechanisms remain heuristic and deterministic at inference time, and do not explicitly model uncertainty over expert selection.

Uncertainty Quantification in LLMs.

Current uncertainty quantification methods for LLMs are largely post-hoc and output-centric.
Black-box approaches elicit verbalised confidence but are often poorly calibrated or require supervised tuning
(
33
;
26
)
.
Logit-based metrics, such as semantic entropy
(
29
;
17
)
, estimate uncertainty by aggregating over the final vocabulary distribution.
In contrast, Variational Routing extracts a latent epistemic uncertainty signal
(
27
)
directly from the model’s internal decision-making process prior to token generation.

Variational Inference in Transformers.

Prior applications of variational inference (VI) in Transformers primarily target self-attention, modeling attention scores as Gaussian processes
(
9
;
10
;
6
)
, or inject noise into weights for approximate inference
(
16
)
.
These methods address uncertainty in feature extraction rather than expert selection.
Closest in spirit is Mixture-of-Variational-Experts
(
23
)
, which applies VI to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in continual learning.
In contrast, our framework applies amortised variational inference
(
28
)
directly to the router’s logit space to address the brittleness and miscalibration of deterministic expert selection.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we propose
Variational Routing (VMoER)
, a framework that reformulates the Mixture-of-Experts Transformer’s routing mechanism as a
probabilistic latent variable model
. We introduced targeted
variational inference
methods operating directly on the decision variables:
Logit-Space
inference (VGLR) to capture semantic expert correlations, and
Selection-Space
inference (VTSR) to learn input-dependent routing stochasticity.

Our empirical analysis yields three key findings.
First, the VMoER’s internal variance serves as a distinct and superior signal for Out-of-Distribution detection compared to standard predictive entropy. Second, VMoER significantly improves calibration on in-distribution tasks, mitigating the overconfidence typical of deterministic Top-K selection.
Third, we demonstrate that these benefits are achievable with minimal computational overhead (
<1\%
FLOPs), confirming that rigorous uncertainty quantification can be achieved without compromising efficiency.

In future works we hope to address remaining limitations, particularly regarding VTSR method, which, while efficient, exhibits training instability (temperature collapse) without careful initialisation.
Additionally, our current evaluation is restricted to Multiple-Choice Question Answering (MCQA) benchmarks that rely on next-token prediction uncertainty.
Future works will focus on stabilising optimisation dynamics, scaling VMoER to larger 70B+ parameter architectures,
and extending the framework from token-level uncertainty to holistic sequence generation tasks.

Impact Statement

This work contributes to the development of more reliable and uncertainty-aware foundation models by introducing a scalable Bayesian framework for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) routing. By explicitly modelling uncertainty in expert selection, our approach improves calibration, routing stability under perturbations, and out-of-distribution detection, all while incurring negligible computational overhead. These properties are particularly relevant for high-stakes or open-world deployments, where overconfident predictions and brittle internal decision-making can lead to silent failures.

At the same time, the methods proposed here address a specific class of reliability challenges related to uncertainty quantification and routing robustness in MoE architectures. They do not, on their own, prevent other important failure modes such as adversarial prompt manipulation, data poisoning, or the emergence of triggered backdoors within experts or routing networks. Moreover, while improved calibration and epistemic uncertainty signals can support safer downstream decision-making, they do not guarantee correct behavior in all deployment contexts.

Appendix A
Mathematical Derivations

In this section, we provide the full derivations for the variational objectives used in both the Logit-Space (VGLR) and Selection-Space (VTSR) frameworks.

A.1
KL Divergence for VGLR with Centred Prior

In Section
3
, we stated that defining the prior
p(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u})
as a Gaussian centred on the deterministic logits simplifies the KL divergence significantly. We derive this result here.

Problem Setup

Let the variational posterior
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u})
be a multivariate Gaussian with mean
\bm{\mu}_{\text{post}}
and covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}
.
We parameterise the posterior mean using a residual formulation:

\bm{\mu}_{\text{post}}=\mathbf{l}_{\text{det}}+\Delta\bm{\mu},\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}

(10)

We define the prior
p(\mathbf{l}|\mathbf{u})
as a fixed multivariate Gaussian centred on the deterministic solution with identity covariance:

\bm{\mu}_{\text{prior}}=\mathbf{l}_{\text{det}},\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{prior}}=\mathbf{I}

(11)

General Gaussian KL Formula.

The Kullback-Leibler divergence between two multivariate Gaussians
q=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\Sigma}_{1})
and
p=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0})
of dimension
N
is given by:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,||\,p)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{1})+(\bm{\mu}_{0}-\bm{\mu}_{1})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}_{0}-\bm{\mu}_{1})-N+\log\frac{|\bm{\Sigma}_{0}|}{|\bm{\Sigma}_{1}|}\right)

(12)

Substitution and Simplification

Substituting our specific parameters (
\bm{\Sigma}_{0}=\mathbf{I},|\bm{\Sigma}_{0}|=1,\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}=\mathbf{I}
):

1.
Trace Term:

\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{1})=\text{tr}(\mathbf{I}\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}})=\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}})

(13)

2.
Quadratic Term:

The term
(\bm{\mu}_{0}-\bm{\mu}_{1})
becomes
(\mathbf{l}_{\text{det}}-(\mathbf{l}_{\text{det}}+\Delta\bm{\mu}))=-\Delta\bm{\mu}
. Thus:

(\bm{\mu}_{0}-\bm{\mu}_{1})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}_{0}-\bm{\mu}_{1})=(-\Delta\bm{\mu})^{\top}\mathbf{I}(-\Delta\bm{\mu})=\|\Delta\bm{\mu}\|_{2}^{2}

(14)

3.
Log-Determinant Term:

\log\frac{|\bm{\Sigma}_{0}|}{|\bm{\Sigma}_{1}|}=\log 1-\log|\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}|=-\log|\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}|

(15)

General Form

Combining these terms yields the standard KL loss for a Gaussian posterior against a standard normal prior:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}})+\|\Delta\bm{\mu}\|_{2}^{2}-N-\log|\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}|\right)

(16)

Parameterisation via Cholesky Factor

In the Full-Covariance (VGLR-FC) variant, we parameterise the covariance matrix using its Cholesky decomposition
\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{L}
is a lower triangular matrix with positive diagonal entries. Substituting this into the general equation:

1.
Trace Term:
Using the cyclic property of the trace and the definition of the Frobenius norm:

\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}})=\text{tr}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top})=\sum_{i,j}L_{ij}^{2}=\|\mathbf{L}\|_{F}^{2}

(17)

2.
Log-Determinant Term:
The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries:

\log|\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}|=\log|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}|=\log(|\mathbf{L}||\mathbf{L}|)=2\log\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}L_{ii}\right)=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log L_{ii}

(18)

Substituting these back yields the final computational objective used in our implementation (Algorithm
):

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\Delta\bm{\mu}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{L}\|_{F}^{2}-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log L_{ii}-N\right)

(19)

This formulation is numerically stable and confirms that minimising the KL divergence drives the residual
\Delta\bm{\mu}\to\mathbf{0}
and the Cholesky factor
\mathbf{L}\to\mathbf{I}
(since
\|\mathbf{I}\|_{F}^{2}=N
and
2\sum\log 1=0
, the minimum is zero).

A.2
Proof of Entropy Equivalence for VTSR

In Section
4
, we claimed that for the Selection-Space Router (VTSR), minimising the KL divergence between the posterior and a uniform prior is analytically equivalent to maximising the entropy of the routing distribution.

Problem Setup.

Let
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{p}|\mathbf{u})
be the discrete variational posterior distribution (a probability vector of size
N
).
Let
p(\mathbf{p})
be the uniform categorical prior:

p_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,N\}

(20)

Derivation.

The definition of the KL divergence for discrete distributions is:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{\phi,k}\log\frac{q_{\phi,k}}{p_{k}}

(21)

Expanding the logarithm term:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{\phi,k}(\log q_{\phi,k}-\log p_{k})

(22)

Substituting
p_{k}=1/N
:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{\phi,k}\log q_{\phi,k}-\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{\phi,k}\log\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)

(23)

Since
\log(1/N)
is a constant with respect to
k
, it can be pulled out of the summation. Furthermore, the sum of probabilities
\sum q_{\phi,k}=1
:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{\phi,k}\log q_{\phi,k}}_{-\mathcal{H}(q_{\phi})}-\log\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\cdot\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{\phi,k}}_{1}

(24)

Recognising the definition of Shannon entropy
\mathcal{H}(q_{\phi})=-\sum q_{\phi,k}\log q_{\phi,k}
, we obtain:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\phi}\,||\,p)=-\mathcal{H}(q_{\phi})+\log N

(25)

Since
\log N
is a constant determined solely by the model architecture (number of experts), minimising the KL divergence is mathematically identical to maximising the Shannon entropy
\mathcal{H}(q_{\phi})
.
∎

D.2
Experiment I: In-Distribution Performance & Training Details

In this experiment, we evaluate the capacity of the VMoER framework to improve model calibration on In-Distribution tasks without degrading predictive accuracy. Unlike the OoD settings, here the router is trained and evaluated on the same domain (using the splits defined in Table
6
).

Training Setup

To ensure the variational routers operate on a high-quality representation space while maintaining efficiency, we employ a
Two-Stage Training Protocol
combined with a selective application strategy:

1.

Stage 1: Deterministic MAP Adaptation (3 Epochs).

We first fine-tune the base model using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to establish a strong deterministic baseline (the Maximum A Posteriori estimate). LoRA adapters are applied to the attention modules (Q/K/V projections) and the Expert networks. This stage runs for 3 epochs to ensure convergence of the task-specific weights.

2.

Layer Selection Strategy (Top-10 Sensitivity).

Instead of replacing all routers, we apply VMoER selectively to the
10 most sensitive layers
. These layers were identified via the brittleness analysis in Appendix
B.1
(Figure
9(b)
), corresponding to the depths where routing decisions were most fragile to perturbation. All other layers remain standard deterministic MoE layers.

3.

Stage 2: Variational Inference Learning (10 Epochs).

We
freeze
all model parameters—including the pre-trained router weights
\mathbf{W}_{r}
and the LoRA adapters learned in Stage 1. We then initialise and optimise
only
the variational inference networks
\phi
on the selected 10 layers for 10 epochs. This isolation ensures that any improvement in calibration is strictly attributable to the probabilistic routing in the critical layers.

Hyperparameters & Optimisation

All models were trained using the
AdamW
optimiser.
To evaluate the robustness and transferability of our method, we performed hyperparameter grid search
exclusively on the Granite-MoE-3B model
. The optimal settings identified on Granite were then transferred directly to Qwen-MoE and DeepSeek-MoE without further tuning.

The final settings used for the reported results are:

•

Learning Rate:

1\times 10^{-4}
with a cosine decay schedule (warmup ratio 0.05).

•

Batch Size:
Variable per model size (2, 4, or 8) with gradient accumulation steps adjusted to maintain a consistent
effective batch size of 16
.

•

Epochs:
10 (with early stopping based on validation NLL).

•

KL Weight
\beta
:
Selected per dataset (on Granite), typically
\beta\in\{0.01,0.1\}
for VGLR and
\beta\in\{0.05,0.1\}
for VTSR to balance the trade-off between calibration (entropy) and accuracy.

•

Monte Carlo Samples:

S=1
during training (reparameterisation),
S=35
during evaluation (to estimate ECE/NLL robustly).

Compute Resources

Experiments were conducted on NVIDIA A100 (80GB) GPUs.
Due to the lightweight nature of the inference networks (adding
<1\%
parameters) and the frozen backbone, training typically converged within 1–3 hours per dataset.

Experiment II: Out-of-Distribution Detection & Uncertainty Analysis

In this experiment, we assess the ability of the learned variational routers to detect distributional shifts. We use the
OBQA
dataset as the In-Distribution (ID) source and evaluate on the Near-OoD (
ARC
) and Far-OoD (
SciQ, MMLU, MedMCQA
) datasets defined in Table
6
.

Uncertainty Signals

To quantify the router’s epistemic uncertainty, we evaluate four distinct signals. We denote the input token as
\mathbf{u}
and the number of experts as
N
.

1.

Gate Entropy (Gate-Ent):

The standard baseline metric, defined as the Shannon entropy of the final expert selection probabilities. High entropy implies the router is ”confused” between experts.

\mathcal{U}_{\text{Ent}}(\mathbf{u})=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\log p_{i}

(29)

For
VTSR
, this metric naturally adapts to the learned uncertainty by computing the entropy of the temperature-scaled probabilities:
\mathcal{H}(\text{softmax}(\mathbf{l}_{\text{det}}/T_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})))
.

2.

Inferred Logit Variance (Inf-Logit-Var):

Specific to
VGLR-FC
, this signal leverages the analytically predicted covariance. It is calculated as the trace of the posterior covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
, representing the total magnitude of uncertainty in the logit space.

\mathcal{U}_{\text{VGLR}}(\mathbf{u})=\text{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\text{post}})=\|\mathbf{L}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}L_{ij}^{2}

(30)

3.

Inferred Temperature (Inf-Temp):

Specific to
VTSR
, this signal is the raw scalar temperature predicted by the inference network. A higher temperature explicitly indicates that the model desires a flatter (more uniform) distribution to account for ambiguity.

\mathcal{U}_{\text{VTSR}}(\mathbf{u})=T_{\phi}(\mathbf{u})

(31)

4.

Monte Carlo Logit Variance (MC-Logit-Var):

A general metric for any stochastic router (including VGLR at inference). We perform
S
stochastic forward passes to generate a set of logit vectors
\{\mathbf{l}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{S}
. The signal is the total variance of the logit vector norm:

\mathcal{U}_{\text{MC}}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{S-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S}||\mathbf{l}^{(s)}-\bar{\mathbf{l}}||^{2}_{2}

(32)

Appendix E
Efficiency Analysis Details

We provide the derivations for the memory and computational overhead analysis presented in Section
5.5
.
We assume a
Parallel Execution
environment, where all
S
Monte Carlo samples are processed in a single batch to minimize wall-clock latency.

Measurement Protocol

Theoretical complexity is derived using standard Big-O notation.
Empirical measurements (Actual Add. GFLOPs reported in the main text) were conducted using the
fvcore.nn.FlopCountAnalysis
library
(
45
)
on a single forward pass of the Granite-3B-MoE model.

Notation

•

L
: Number of modified MoE layers (
10
).

•

N
: Experts per layer (
40
).

•

D
: Hidden dimension (
1536
).

•

S
: Monte Carlo samples (
35
).

•

H
: Inference network dimension (
D/4=384
).

E.1
Activation Memory Overhead

The critical constraint is the peak memory required to hold parameters during the forward pass.

Weight-Space Baselines (MCDR, SWAGR)

Typically, MC-Dropout (MCDR) is considered memory-efficient because it requires no extra parameters. However, in a
latency-critical
setting, running
S
sequential passes is unacceptable. To parallelise MCDR, we must replicate the masked weight matrices
S
times in memory (or store
S
distinct large masks).
Thus, both MCDR and SWAGR scale linearly with
S
:

\mathcal{M}_{\text{Weight}}=L\times(S-1)\times(D\cdot N)

(33)

For
S=35
, this results in storing 34 additional copies of the dense router weights, causing the high overhead (
\sim
2.6%) observed in Table
4
.

VMoER Methods

Our methods rely on fixed inference networks. The memory cost is constant regardless of
S
, as samples are transient vectors, not matrices.

•

MFVR:
Backbone (
DH
) + Mean/Var Heads (
2HN
).

•

VGLR-FC:
Backbone (
DH
) + Mean Head (
HN
) + Cholesky Head (
H\cdot\frac{N(N+1)}{2}\approx\frac{1}{2}HN^{2}
).

\mathcal{M}_{\text{FC}}\approx L\cdot H\cdot(D+N+0.5N^{2})

(34)

•

VTSR:
Backbone (
DH
) + Scalar Head (
H
).

E.2
Computational Overhead (FLOPs)

We assume a standard linear layer requires
2\cdot\text{in}\cdot\text{out}
FLOPs.

Weight-Space Methods

Every sample requires a full pass through the router’s projection layer (
D\to N
).

\mathcal{C}_{\text{Weight}}=L\times S\times(2DN)

(35)

VMoER Methods

The heavy feature extraction (
D\to H
) runs only once per token (
S=1
). The scaling with
S
affects only the cheap sampling step.

•

VGLR-MF:

Double-head one-hidden-layer MLP adds approximately
2DH+4HN
FLOPs.
Reparameterisation trick for
S
samples adds
S\times 2N
FLOPs.
Total overhead is the sum of these two.

•

VGLR-FC:
The most expensive variant involves generating the Cholesky factor (
H\to N^{2}/2
) and sampling (
S
matrix-vector products).

\mathcal{C}_{\text{VGLR-FC}}=L\left(\underbrace{2DH+HN^{2}}_{\text{Network }\phi}+\underbrace{2SN^{2}}_{\text{Sampling}}\right)

(36)

Even with the quadratic term
N^{2}
, since
N\ll D
(40 vs 1536), the total FLOPs remain roughly
2\times
lower than the weight-space baseline.

•

VTSR:
The temperature prediction network adds approximately
2DH+2H
FLOPs (Backbone + Scalar Head). This is followed by
N
divisions to scale the logits.

\mathcal{C}_{\text{VTSR}}=L\times(2DH+2H+N)

(37)

Note that this analysis does not include the cost of averaging multiple post-softmax outputs. If considered strictly from a theoretical standpoint, VTSR is even more efficient as it does not require Monte Carlo sampling (
S=1
) during the forward pass logic itself.

Appendix H
Additional Related Works

In this section, we provide coverage of additional related works beyond the core discussion in the main text.

Bayesian Deep Learning.
A Bayesian approach to learning in deep neural networks has been investigated for more than three decades
(
40
)
. Traditionally, a Bayesian perspective is applied to the entire learnable parameter space of a deep neural network; while exact inference is intractable, many approximate inference strategies have been investigated including HMC
(
39
)
, Bayes by Backprop (BBB)
(
3
)
, Stochastic Weight Averaging - Gaussian (SWAG)
(
35
)
, NoisyAdam (NA)
(
58
)
, and Variational Online Gauss-Newton (VOGN)
(
42
)
. The quality of the inference can have substantial effects on the resulting predictive uncertainty. For example, while it has been shown that Monte Carlo Dropout (MCDropout)
(
19
)
can be viewed as an approximation of a Bayesian posterior, improvements in methodology aimed at more faithful inference can substantially improve predictive uncertainty
(
32
)
.

Uncertainty and Robustness.
There is a long line of literature connecting the notion of Bayesian inference and robustness. In adversarial robustness, the kind that provides the motivating example for this paper it has been established that exact inference should have positive effects on a neural networks robustness
