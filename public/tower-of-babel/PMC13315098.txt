Change in circulating irisin level and its association with lipid metabolism after exenatide treatment in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Yuting Gao, Xiaoyu Hong, Kun Yang, Yunyi Le, Rui Wei, Jin Yang, Tianpei Hong, Qing Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIrisin is a metabolism-related myokine associated with lipid metabolism and insulin resistance. However, its changes during glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist therapy remain unclear.MethodsThis exploratory analysis of a multicenter trial included 189 adults with T2DM inadequately controlled with metformin and/or insulin secretagogue. The participants received the GLP-1 receptor agonist exenatide as an add-on therapy for 16 weeks. Serum irisin and glycolipid metabolism were evaluated. Comparison was performed between two subgroups of the participants stratified by a median change from baseline in serum irisin level. Associations between change in irisin level and changes in metabolic parameters were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis and multivariable linear regression analysis.ResultsAfter the 16-week exenatide treatment, serum irisin level significantly declined from baseline (3.22 ± 0.53 ng/mL vs. 3.35 ± 0.64 ng/mL, P = 0.031). Change in irisin level was positively associated with change in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) level (r = 0.154, P = 0.035), and negatively associated with changes in triglyceride (TG) level (r = −0.159, P = 0.029) and TG/HDL-C ratio (r = −0.172, P = 0.018). These associations for TG level and TG/HDL-C ratio remained significant after multivariable adjustment. No significant association was observed between change in irisin level and glycemic control parameters.ConclusionsSerum irisin level declines during the exenatide treatment in patients with T2DM, and this reduction is associated with change in lipid metabolism but not glycemic control, suggesting a potential link between dynamic change of irisin and lipid metabolism during the exenatide treatment.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is commonly accompanied by insulin resistance and atherogenic dyslipidemia, both of which contribute substantially to cardiometabolic risk [1]. To better characterize these metabolic abnormalities, several lipid-related metabolic indices have been developed and increasingly applied in clinical studies. The triglyceride (TG)/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio and the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) are the commonly used parameters of atherogenic dyslipidemia and cardiometabolic risk, while the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and metabolic score for insulin resistance have been proposed as the practical surrogate markers of insulin resistance [2], [3]. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) not only improve glycemic control but also exert beneficial effects on body weight and lipid metabolism, making them important therapeutic agents in patients with T2DM [4]. Exenatide is an exendin-4-based GLP-1RA and has been demonstrated to be efficacious for glycemic control in patients with T2DM [5]. However, substantial inter-individual variability exists in response to GLP-1RA therapies, with only approximately 50% of the treated patients achieving the target glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level [6]. The therapeutic response in some individuals may be insufficient, leading to treatment discontinuation [7]. Currently, factors associated with interindividual metabolic responses to GLP-1RAs remain incompletely understood. Given the important roles of insulin resistance and lipid metabolism in T2DM, circulating irisin has emerged as a potential metabolic regulator of interest during GLP-1RA therapy.

Irisin, a myokine derived from the proteolytic cleavage of fibronectin type III domain-containing 5 (FNDC5), has been recognized as a key regulator of energy homeostasis and metabolic health [8]. Initially identified as an exercise-induced hormone, irisin promotes the browning of white adipose tissue, thereby enhancing thermogenesis and increasing energy expenditure. As a downstream effector of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), FNDC5/irisin modulates energy metabolism and mitochondrial biogenesis via the PPARα and PGC-1α signaling pathways, contributing to increased glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and improved insulin resistance [9]. Notably, irisin and GLP-1 share several physiological features. For example, the two hormones are upregulated in response to carbohydrate and lipid intake, have been shown to improve pancreatic β-cell survival and insulin secretion, and are responsive to metformin treatment [10], [11]. Based on these observations, potential interactions between dynamic change of irisin and metabolic responses during GLP-1RA therapy have attracted increasing interest.

It has been suggested that GLP-1RAs may influence circulating irisin level. A recent preclinical study reported that liraglutide upregulated irisin production and enhanced lipid metabolism via the AMP-activated protein kinase α (AMPKα)/acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1)/uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1)/PPARα pathway [12]. Additionally, a clinical study demonstrated that exenatide treatment significantly increased serum irisin concentration in obese patients with T2DM [13]. However, the available evidence is limited by small sample sizes and inconsistent study designs. Therefore, the association between dynamic change of irisin and glycolipid metabolism during GLP-1RA therapy remains unclear.

In the present study, we aimed to evaluate change in serum irisin level after exenatide treatment and to explore the associations of this change with blood glucose and lipid parameters in patients with T2DM.

Materials and methods

Study design overview

The present study was a post hoc exploratory analysis based on a previously completed randomized, multicenter, non-inferiority clinical trial [14]. The multicenter interventional trial was registered at www.chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR-IPR-15006558) and conducted in seven tertiary hospitals in China. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital, and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The original trial compared the efficacy and safety of a generic exenatide injection versus branded exenatide (Byetta®) in patients with T2DM inadequately controlled on stable background therapy with metformin and/or insulin secretagogue. In the original trial, eligible participants were recruited from seven sites and randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive generic or branded exenatide for 16 weeks. Randomization was stratified by study center and implemented using a computer-generated sequence, with double-blinding ensured for participants, investigators, laboratory personnel and statisticians. The primary endpoint of the parent trial was the change from baseline in HbA1c level at week 16, and sample size calculation was performed accordingly. Detailed descriptions of the trial design, randomization procedures, eligibility criteria and primary outcomes had been published previously [14]. The scientific value of this trial cohort had also been supported by our previous publications focusing on the related metabolic endpoints in the exenatide-treated patients with T2DM [15], [16].

Participants

Eligible participants were Chinese adults with T2DM aged 20–70 years, with an HbA1c level of 7.0–10.0% after receiving either monotherapy or combination therapy with metformin and insulin secretagogue. Participants underwent a 16-week exenatide treatment adding on their background therapy. Exenatide (branded exenatide from Lilly Pharma Fertigung and Distribution GmbH & Co. KG, Giessen, Germany; or generic exenatide from Qinghai Chenfei Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Qinghai, China) was administered subcutaneously, starting at 5 μg twice daily for the first 4 weeks, followed by 10 μg twice daily for the remaining 12 weeks unless intolerance necessitated continuation of the lower dose.

Between July 2011 and June 2013, 240 participants were randomly assigned to the interventions, of whom 203 completed the trial. After excluding 14 individuals due to missing serum irisin data at baseline and/or at the end of exenatide treatment, 189 participants were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). The research protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment in this study.

Measurements and calculations

Baseline and follow-up assessments included fasting venous blood collection for laboratory assays. Anthropometric parameters, including body weight, height and blood pressure, were recorded. Body mass index (BMI) = weight / height2 (kg/m2). Diabetes duration and antidiabetic medication therapy history were retrieved from medical records. Laboratory analyses included fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2-h postprandial plasma glucose (PPG), HbA1c, total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), HDL-C, TG and uric acid. Serum irisin was measured by using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit for human irisin (EK-067-29; Phoenix Pharmaceuticals, Inc., Karlsruhe, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. The reference ranges for blood biochemical parameters were provided by the Department of Laboratory Medicine at Peking University Third Hospital according to the national clinical laboratory procedures. The lipid and glycolipid metabolism indices were calculated as follows: Non-HDL-C = TC − HDL-C, unit: mmol/L; Remnant cholesterol = TC − LDL-C − HDL-C, unit: mmol/L; TC/HDL-C ratio = TC / HDL-C; LDL-C/HDL-C ratio = LDL-C / HDL-C; TG/HDL-C ratio = TG / HDL-C; Arteriosclerosis index = (TC − HDL-C) / HDL-C; AIP = log (TG/HDL-C ratio); Lipoprotein combine index = (TC × TG × LDL-C) / HDL-C, unit: mmol/L; TyG index = ln [TG (mmol/L) × FPG (mmol/L) / 2]; TyG-BMI index = ln [TG (mmol/L) × FPG (mmol/L) / 2] × BMI (kg/m2); Metabolic score for insulin resistance = ln [(2 × FPG (mmol/L) + TG (mmol/L)] × BMI (kg/m2) / ln [HDL-C (mmol/L)] [17], [18], [19].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using appropriate t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and analyzed using nonparametric tests. Categorical variables were reported as counts (percentages). For the exploratory subgroup analysis, participants were stratified by a median change from baseline in serum irisin level (−0.029 ng/mL), resulting in two groups with greater versus smaller reduction in the irisin level. Associations between change in irisin level and changes in metabolic parameters were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis, depending on the data distribution. To further evaluate whether the observed associations were independent of the potential confounding factors, a multivariable linear regression analysis was performed for the metabolic indices that showed significant associations with change in circulating irisin level in the univariable correlation analysis. Changes in these metabolic indices were separately included as dependent variables, whereas change in circulating irisin level was included as the independent variable. Models were adjusted for age, sex, baseline BMI, baseline HbA1c, antidiabetic background therapies (metformin and/or insulin secretagogue), and category of exenatide (branded or generic). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Changes in clinical parameters and serum irisin level in patients with T2DM after 16 weeks of exenatide treatment

In this trial cohort, 77 (40.7%) participants had hyperlipidemia, and 63 (33.3%) participants had hypertension. Regarding background therapies, 85 (45.0%) and 18 (9.52%) participants were receiving monotherapy with metformin and insulin secretagogue respectively, and 86 (45.5%) participants were on combination therapy with metformin and insulin secretagogue. As for the category of exenatide, 91 (48.1%) and 98 (51.9%) participants were treated with branded exenatide and generic exenatide, respectively.

Association between change in serum irisin level and change in lipid metabolism following the exenatide treatment

Compared to participants with serum irisin reduction < 0.029 ng/mL, those with the irisin reduction ≥ 0.029 ng/mL experienced a larger decrease in serum LDL-C level (P = 0.028), and a smaller decrease in serum TG level (P = 0.026), TG/HDL-C ratio (P = 0.019) and AIP (P = 0.036) from baseline after 16 weeks of exenatide treatment (Table 2). Consistently, correlation analysis revealed that change from baseline in serum irisin level was negatively associated with changes in serum TG level (r = −0.159, P = 0.029), TG/HDL-C ratio (r = −0.172, P = 0.018) and AIP (r = −0.149, P = 0.043), while it was positively associated with change in serum HDL-C level (r = 0.154, P = 0.035) at the end of the 16-week exenatide treatment (Fig. 1). In the subgroup analysis stratified by post-treatment lipid level (using the upper or lower limit of normal reference ranges in our laboratory as cutoff values: TC < 5.18 mmol/L, LDL-C < 3.4 mmol/L, TG < 1.7 mmol/L and HDL-C ≥ 1.04 mmol/L), participants achieving normal LDL-C level had a more significant reduction in serum irisin level versus those with elevated LDL-C level (−0.08 [−0.34, −0.07] ng/mL vs. 0.05 [−0.11, 0.20] ng/mL, P = 0.023). By contrast, no significant difference in change from baseline in serum irisin level among the subgroups stratified by the cutoffs of TC, HDL-C and TG (Fig. 2). In a multivariable linear regression analysis, change in circulating irisin level remained significantly associated with changes in TG levels (standardized β = −0.153, P = 0.038) and TG/HDL-C ratio (standardized β = −0.168, P = 0.022) after adjustment for the treatment-related variables and major clinical confounders (Supplementary Table 2). Association between change in serum irisin level and glycemic control after 16 weeks of exenatide treatment

No significant difference was observed between participants with serum irisin reduction < 0.029 ng/mL and those with the irisin reduction ≥ 0.029 ng/mL in terms of changes in FPG, 2-h PPG and HbA1c levels after exenatide treatment (Table 2). Furthermore, baseline irisin level and change from baseline in irisin level in participants who achieved the target level of HbA1c (either < 7% or < 6.5%) were not significantly different from those in participants whose HbA1c level did not achieve the target level at the end of exenatide treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results suggested that despite effective glycemic control with the GLP-1RA therapy, irisin might not be directly involved in modulating glucose homeostasis in this clinical context.

Discussion

In the present study, we observed significant decreases in body weight, BMI, FPG, 2-h PPG and HbA1c levels as well as a significant reduction in serum irisin level after 16 weeks of exenatide treatment in patients with T2DM. Participants with a larger irisin reduction had a more significant decrease in serum LDL-C level, and a less significant decline in serum TG level, TG/HDL-C ratio and AIP. Moreover, change from baseline in irisin level was negatively associated with changes in serum TG level, TG/HDL-C ratio and AIP, and positively associated with change in serum HDL-C level after exenatide treatment. Importantly, these associations for TG level and TG/HDL-C ratio remained significant after adjustment for the treatment-related variables and major clinical confounders in the multivariable regression analysis, implying the robustness of these associations. However, no significant association was observed between change in irisin level and improvement in glycemic control. These results suggested that alteration in circulating irisin level might be linked to changes in lipid metabolism but not glucose control in patients with T2DM after exenatide treatment.

The decline in irisin level may be partially attributed to weight loss, as previous studies have shown a positive association between irisin level and body weight or BMI in non-diabetic populations, including individuals with obesity and women with polycystic ovary syndrome [20], [21], [22]. Elevated irisin level in individuals with obesity has been indicated to reflect a compensatory mechanism against metabolic stress and it is possibly driven by an increased secretion from white adipose tissue [23], [24]. One possible explanation is that irisin secretion is upregulated in individuals with obesity as a compensatory mechanism to enhance energy expenditure and maintain glucose homeostasis. Additionally, obesity-related “irisin resistance” has been proposed but remains controversial, and it may contribute to persistently elevated irisin level as a compensatory response to metabolic dysfunction [25].

Interestingly, we showed distinct associations between alteration in irisin level and changes in different lipid components. Greater reduction in irisin level was associated with larger decrease in LDL-C level, whereas smaller reduction in irisin level was associated with more favorable changes in TG level, TG/HDL-C ratio and AIP. Rather than being contradictory, these findings might indicate the fundamental differences in lipid metabolic pathways [26]. LDL-C predominantly reflects hepatic cholesterol metabolism and LDL receptor-mediated clearance, whereas the TG-related indices (TG level, TG/HDL-C ratio and AIP) are more closely linked to insulin resistance, energy balance and lipolytic activity [27], [28]. Recent studies have shown that under metabolic stress condition, irisin may participate in adaptive metabolic responses, including regulation of adipose tissue function and systemic energy utilization, which are more closely related to TG-rich lipoprotein metabolism than to isolated cholesterol transport pathways [29].

Experimental evidence indicates that irisin upregulates the expression of browning-related genes in white adipocytes [9], activates beige adipocytes [30] and stimulates lipolysis via the cAMP/PKA-mediated hormone-sensitive lipase/perilipin pathway [31]. In mouse models, Fndc5 knockout show elevated LDL-C and reduced HDL-C, while Fndc5 overexpression reduces adipocyte size and hepatic fat [32]. In this study, greater decline in irisin level alongside larger reduction in LDL-C level was observed after exenatide treatment, which might reflect a reduction in a previously activated compensatory metabolic response during systemic metabolic improvement following the GLP-1RA therapy. In contrast, the inverse associations between reduction in irisin level and improvements in TG level, TG/HDL-C ratio and AIP suggested that relatively higher irisin level at post-treatment might be associated with the modulation of TG metabolism. Triglyceride-rich lipoproteins are highly sensitive to ongoing energy balance, lipolytic activity and hepatic very low-density lipoprotein output, all of which are potentially influenced by irisin. Patients who maintained higher irisin level after exenatide treatment might have persistent metabolic demands for TG turnover, consistent with previous reports in which irisin displayed the effects in stimulating basal lipolysis, promoting mitochondrial uncoupling and enhancing lipid utilization [33], [34], [35], [36]. Collectively, these observations suggest that dynamic change of irisin may be differentially associated with distinct lipid metabolic profiles under different metabolic conditions.

Preclinical studies showed that liraglutide stimulated FNDC5/irisin production in skeletal muscle, thereby promoting the browning of white adipose tissue via the AMPKα/ACC1/UCP1/PPARα pathway [12], and induced pancreatic FNDC5 expression, with its secreted isoform (sFNDC5) promoting lipolysis [35]. A small clinical study revealed that a 12-week exenatide treatment elevated serum irisin level in obese patients with T2DM, which was accompanied by improvement in glycemic control [13]. Moreover, longer-term GLP-1RA therapies, including 52-week exenatide [36] and one-year liraglutide or dulaglutide regimens [37], also sustained irisin elevation, with conferring cardiometabolic benefits. In contrast, our study observed a significant decline in serum irisin level following the 16-week exenatide treatment, despite comparable reductions in body weight and glycemic parameters. This discrepancy likely reflects a time-dependent, bi-phasic regulatory pattern during GLP-1RA therapy. Recent evidence indicates an initial compensatory surge in irisin level to counter acute metabolic stress, followed by an adaptive decline as insulin sensitivity improves [29]. Thus, our 16-week observation may specifically capture this subsequent adaptive phase. In addition, prior studies showing irisin increase often had small sample sizes, whereas our study involving 189 participants provided a more robust statistical power and a potentially more representative picture of irisin response to the GLP-1RA therapy.

Despite improvement in glycemic control after 16 weeks of exenatide treatment, no significant association between change in irisin concentration and change in HbA1c level was observed in this study. However, prior studies reported that circulating irisin level was lower in individuals with prediabetes or T2DM than in those with normal glucose tolerance [24], [38], [39]. Mechanistic studies have suggested that irisin may enhance glucose metabolism by upregulating glucose transporter type 4 and key glycolytic enzymes, thereby promoting glucose uptake and utilization [40], [41], [42]. Additionally, irisin can protect pancreatic β-cells from pyroptosis in T2DM mice by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome via the miR-19b-3p/SOCS3/STAT3 axis-mediated autophagy pathway [43]. In individuals with normal glucose tolerance, circulating irisin was positively associated with insulin level and β-cell function as indicated by homeostasis model assessment of β-cell function index [44]. By contrast, other studies reported no significant association between irisin concentration and fasting insulin or C-peptide level in patients with T2DM [45], highlighting the ongoing uncertainty regarding its regulatory effect on glucose metabolism. These inconsistencies suggest that the metabolic effects of irisin on glucose homeostasis may be context-dependent and may be influenced by disease stage and antidiabetic background therapies. In our study, the significant weight loss and improved metabolic status following the exenatide treatment might have affected serum irisin level independently of glucose-lowering effect. Alternatively, GLP-1RAs might potentially influence irisin secretion via a direct action on skeletal muscle or adipose tissue. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the complex relationship between irisin level and glucose homeostasis under the GLP-1RA therapy condition. Nevertheless, TG/HDL-C ratio and AIP are recognized as surrogate markers of insulin resistance and predictors of diabetes progression [46], and patients with smaller irisin reduction exhibited greater improvements in these indices in our study, supporting a potential link between dynamic change of irisin and insulin resistance-related lipid metabolism.

The strengths of our study include the multicenter recruitment of participants across China, thereby ensuring a diverse and representative sample, and a standardized treatment regimen during the trial to minimize confounding effects. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, based on the nature of post hoc exploratory analysis, our results cannot establish causality between irisin reduction and lipid metabolism improvement. Second, although circulating irisin was measured, assessment of tissue-specific expression in either skeletal muscle or adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle mass or body composition were not evaluated during the exenatide treatment. Third, participants generally exhibited mildly to moderately elevated lipid levels at baseline, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings regarding relationship between circulating irisin level and lipid metabolism. Fourth, lack of serum insulin and C-peptide measurements also precluded detailed assessment of insulin resistance and pancreatic β-cell function. Fifth, physical activity, a major physiological stimulus for irisin secretion, was not systematically assessed or controlled in the original trial, and dietary intake data were also unavailable, which might have confounded the change in circulating irisin level independently of treatment effects. Sixth, reliance on the pre-post comparison without a parallel untreated control group might have introduced time-related bias and regression to the mean, and thus the observed associations in our study should be interpreted with caution. In addition, although several associations reached statistical significance, the correlation coefficients were modest in magnitude, which might limit their immediate clinical applicability. Seventh, methodological heterogeneity among currently available ELISA-based irisin assays has been reported, which may partially contribute to inconsistencies across studies involving circulating irisin measurements [47]. Finally, the relatively short treatment duration limited our ability to evaluate long-term dynamic change of irisin and their potential relationship with sustained metabolic remodeling during GLP-1RA therapy. Nevertheless, our findings suggest a potential link between dynamic change of irisin and lipid metabolic alterations during the exenatide treatment. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the clinical and biological significance of these associations.

Conclusion

In patients with T2DM receiving exenatide treatment, circulating irisin level declined during the treatment period and were modestly associated with changes in lipid-related metabolic indices, particularly TG-related parameters. These findings suggest that dynamic change of irisin during GLP-1RA therapy may reflect aspects of lipid metabolic adaptation rather than glycemic control improvement. Given the exploratory nature of our study and the absence of a parallel control group, further prospective and mechanistic studies are required to clarify the biological significance and clinical relevance of change in circulating irisin during GLP-1RA treatment.
