Eosinophil-derived apoptotic extracellular vesicles accelerate steatotic liver repair after ischemia/reperfusion injury through mitochondria-associated metabolic reprogramming
Authors: Chang Xiong, Liubing Chen, Zilong Rao, Jinyan Guo, Jiaming Ji, Zhenyu Yang, Jinyu Liu, Chengpeng Han, Ziqing Hei, Ying Bai, Weifeng Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eosinophils have recently been recognized as hepatoprotective immune cells; however, cell-based therapies still face significant challenges. Apoptotic extracellular vesicles (ApoEVs) serve as critical cell-free alternatives capable of promoting tissue repair, yet exhibit marked functional heterogeneity dependent on their cell source. The therapeutic potential and mechanisms of eosinophil-derived ApoEVs (Eos-ApoEVs) in steatotic liver ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury remain elusive. Herein, treatment with Eos-ApoEVs effectively reversed oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R)-induced cellular dysfunction in hepatocytes and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs). Moreover, Eos-ApoEVs exerted potent immunomodulatory effects. Mechanistically, Eos-ApoEVs are enriched with functional mitochondria that fuse with damaged mitochondria in recipient cells, thereby reprogramming energy metabolism to promote hepatocyte proliferation, restore LSEC migration and tube-forming capacity, and facilitate anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization. To develop a clinically translatable therapeutic approach, we constructed a decellularized amniotic matrix (dAM)-based hydrogel patch (ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA), which enables sustained release of ApoEVs and exhibits high biocompatibility. In a mouse model of steatotic liver I/R injury, transplantation of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA onto the liver surface significantly increased anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization, attenuated inflammation, promoted hepatocyte proliferation and enhanced angiogenesis, thereby accelerating liver repair. Thus, surface transplantation of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA provides a clinically viable and potent therapeutic strategy for steatotic liver I/R injury.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04716-4.

Introduction

Liver transplantation remains the only definitive treatment for end-stage liver disease. However, the persistent global shortage of donor organs has compelled the transplantation community to increasingly adopt marginal grafts [1]. Driven by the increasing prevalence of obesity and metabolic syndrome, steatotic livers now constitute > 30% of the potential donor pool and represent the most practical option for expanding organ availability [2]. However, these grafts exhibit an increased susceptibility to ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury, resulting in delayed graft function and impaired recovery [2–4]. Therefore, developing effective strategies to mitigate I/R injury in steatotic livers is urgently required.

Eosinophils (Eos) were traditionally regarded as effector cells in allergy and parasitic immunity [5, 6]. However, their non-canonical roles in inflammation resolution and tissue repair are now being recognized. In murine paw inflammation models, Eos surround inflamed tissue and release interleukin-4 (IL-4) to accelerate resolution [7]. Following corneal injury, they generate pro-resolving lipid mediators that facilitate wound closure and are indispensable for skeletal muscle regeneration as well as for cardiac repair post-myocardial infarction [8–11]. Recent studies have extended this protective profile to hepatic I/R injury—Eos are rapidly recruited to the liver during the early damage phase to limit injury and subsequently interact with macrophages to drive hepatocyte proliferation during repair [11, 12]. Therefore, Eos constitute an endogenous dual-function mechanism that attenuates initial damage and fosters regeneration after hepatic I/R injury. However, their peak hepatic recruitment is transient, and although the adoptive transfer of Eos significantly ameliorates liver injury, the logistical barriers related to cell storage and the risk of degranulation-associated adverse effects limit its clinical translation.

Apoptotic extracellular vesicles (ApoEVs) are membrane vesicles that are actively released by apoptotic cells and carry parental cell proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and various organelles [13]. They play crucial roles in signal transduction, homeostasis, and tissue repair [14–16]. Compared with living cell-derived EVs, ApoEVs can be lyophilized for long-term storage, pose no tumorigenic risk, and are produced in higher yields through simpler manufacturing processes, making them specifically promising for clinical applications [17, 18]. However, ApoEVs exhibit significant functional heterogeneity based on their parental cell origin, making the selection of optimal cell sources for maximal repair efficacy an emerging focus in the field. In the present study, Eos were not selected on the assumption that they are universally superior to all other immune or stromal cell types in every hepatic injury context. Rather, we prioritized Eos because they are endogenous pro-resolving cells already implicated in hepatic I/R injury, where they participate in both injury limitation and repair orchestration. We reasoned that Eos-derived ApoEVs (Eos-ApoEVs) may preserve part of this context-relevant reparative program in a cell-free form and therefore represent a biologically matched therapeutic candidate for steatotic liver I/R injury.

To address the limitations of intravenous ApoEV administration—such as rapid clearance by circulating phagocytes and insufficient liver targeting—we encapsulated ApoEVs in a decellularized amniotic matrix (dAM) hydrogel to generate ApoEVs@dAM; subsequent stabilization with hyaluronic acid-dopamine (HA-DA) was performed to secure stable adhesion to the liver surface, producing the final ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA patch. The three-dimensional (3D) collagen network of the dAM hydrogel enables the sustained release of ApoEVs. Additionally, the amniotic membrane is rich in transforming growth factor-β, fibroblast growth factor-2, and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases that can synergistically enhance the pro-repair effects of ApoEVs [19]. Given the clinical use of amniotic membrane-derived biomaterials and their excellent biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, dAM hydrogel presents a potentially translatable platform for localized therapy [20].

In this study, the ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA patch was fabricated to sustainably deliver Eos-ApoEVs into liver tissue and restore the function of hepatocytes, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), and macrophages after I/R injury, thereby promoting hepatocyte proliferation, enhancing angiogenesis, and driving inflammation resolution to accelerate steatotic liver repair. Mechanistically, our data suggest that functional mitochondria within Eos-ApoEVs contribute to mitochondria-associated metabolic reprogramming in recipient cells, thereby restoring cellular function (Scheme 1). In summary, this study identifies Eos-ApoEVs as a reparative cell-free candidate for steatotic liver I/R injury and offers a clinically translatable, sustained-release delivery system for precision cell-free therapy.

Materials and methods

Isolation and in vitro differentiation of bone marrow cells into Eos and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Isolation of bone marrow cells. Femurs and tibias from 6- to 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were dissected and cleared of muscle. After rinsing the marrow cavity with PBS, the resulting suspension was centrifuged; the pellet was resuspended in lysing buffer, filtered and centrifuged again to yield bone marrow cells.

Induction and characterization of Eos. As previously described [21], conditioned medium was prepared by supplementing Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 100 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, USA), 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, USA), 2 mM glutamine (Gibco, USA), 25 mM HEPES (Thermo Fisher, USA), and 1× non-essential amino acids (Gibco, USA). The isolated bone marrow cells were resuspended in an appropriate amount of this conditioned medium. From day 0 to day 4, 100 ng/mL stem cell factor (SCF, Peprotech, USA) and 100 ng/mL Fms-related tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (FLT3L, Peprotech, USA) were added. On day 4 and day 8, the old medium was removed by centrifugation and replaced with fresh medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL IL-5 (Peprotech, USA). On day 12, cells were harvested for phenotypic validation and subsequent experimental procedures.

Induction and characterization of BMDMs. According to a previous report [12], the conditioned medium was prepared by supplementing Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 100 mM sodium pyruvate, 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol, and 10% L929 cell supernatant. The isolated bone marrow cells were cultured in this medium for 7 days and harvested on day 8 for phenotypic characterization and subsequent experiments.

Near-infrared fluorescence (NIRF)

DiR (Aladdin, China)-labeled Eos-ApoEVs were used for NIRF in vivo distribution imaging. Briefly, DiR-labeled ApoEVs were encapsulated in the hydrogel patch and applied topically to the liver surface immediately after reperfusion. NIRF was performed at 1, 3, and 7 days after surgery, with additional time points at 14 days in some animals. The Ani-View100 multimodal animal in vivo imaging system (BLT, China) was used to capture the distribution signals.

Extraction and identification of Eos-ApoEVs

Eos-ApoEVs were isolated using sequential centrifugation as previously described [22–24]. Briefly, Eos were treated with 0.5 µM staurosporine (STS, 50606ES03, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) for 12 h to induce apoptosis. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was further centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min to thoroughly remove cells and debris. After discarding the pellet, the ApoEV-enriched supernatant was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 min to pellet the ApoEVs. The pellet was resuspended in ice-cold PBS and subjected to another round of centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 30 min to remove protein contaminants. Finally, purified ApoEVs were resuspended in 100 µL of ice-cold PBS and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Eos-ApoEVs were characterized by Western blot analysis for Eos protein markers C-C chemokine receptor type 3 (CCR3) and eosinophil peroxidase (EPX) and an apoptosis marker (Cleaved caspase 3). Morphological assessment was performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and particle size distribution was determined via dynamic light scattering (DLS). Additionally, Eos-ApoEVs were labeled with annexin V and further examined by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry.

Cell culture

Human immortalized LSECs were purchased from Guangzhou Qunxian Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China), and alpha mouse liver 12 (AML12) cells, a murine hepatocyte cell line, were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China). Both cell lines of passages 3–8 were used to establish an in vitro oxygen–glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) model. LSECs were cultured in Endothelial Cell Medium (ECM) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% endothelial cell growth factor (ECGF), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. AML12 cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 0.1% penicillin–streptomycin as complete culture medium.

Labeling of Eos-ApoEVs and cellular uptake assay

According to the manufacturer’s protocol, Eos-ApoEVs were labeled with the membrane dye CM-DiI (40792ES72, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) for 15 min, followed by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 30 min. BMDMs, AML12 cells, and human immortalized LSECs were incubated with 10 µg/mL of CM-DiI-labeled ApoEVs for 1 h and 6 h at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator. After washing with PBS, the samples were analyzed by immunofluorescence and flow cytometry. For immunofluorescence, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with phalloidin (C2221S, Beyotime, China) and DAPI, and imaged by fluorescence microscopy.

In vitro OGD/R model

To establish the OGD/R model, cells were seeded into plates one day before the experiment. After reaching appropriate density, the culture medium was replaced with glucose-free DMEM, and the cells were transferred to a tri-gas incubator. Under oxygen–glucose deprivation conditions (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂, 37 °C), LSECs were cultured for 10 h and AML12 cells for 16 h in the absence of glucose. Subsequently, the medium was immediately replaced with their respective complete media, and the cells were returned to a conventional incubator (normoxic atmosphere with 5% CO₂) for 6 h of reperfusion. During reperfusion, 10 µg/mL Eos-ApoEVs were added according to the experimental groups.

Tube formation assay

A 24-well plate was coated with 200 µL of Matrix-Gel™ Basement Membrane Matrix (Beyotime, C3071) per well and incubated at 37 °C for 40 min to allow gel polymerization. LSECs, either under normal conditions or after OGD/R treatment, were detached using trypsin and resuspended in culture medium with or without 10 µg/mL Eos-ApoEVs according to experimental groupings. Then, 20,000 cells were seeded into each pre-coated well. After gentle mixing, the plate was transferred to a conventional incubator (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Tubule formation was observed and imaged under a microscope after 4 h of incubation. Quantification of tube networks was performed using Image J software.

Wound healing assay

A linear scratch was created in a confluent monolayer of LSECs (either normally cultured or subjected to OGD/R) using a 1 mL pipette tip. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated in complete medium or complete medium containing 10 µg/mL Eos-ApoEVs. Images of the scratch were captured at 0, 12, and 24 h. The migration rate (%) of cells was calculated as follows: Migration rate (%) = (A₀ – Aₙ)/A₀ × 100%, where A₀ represents the initial wound area and Aₙ represents the wound area at the measured time point. Image analysis was performed using Image J software.

Transwell migration assay

The migration assay was performed using Transwell chambers. Normally cultured or OGD/R-treated LSECs were resuspended in serum-free medium at a density of 1.0 × 10⁵ cells/mL. Then, 200 µL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with 500 µL of complete medium with or without 10 µg/mL Eos-ApoEVs according to the experimental groups. After incubation at 37 °C for 12 or 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed with a dry cotton swab. The migrated cells on the lower surface were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. The migrated cells were imaged under an optical microscope, and the number of cells per field was counted.

Western blot

Proteins were extracted from cells or Eos-ApoEVs by lysis. Concentrations were determined with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Samples were separated by SDS–PAGE and probed with the following primary antibodies: CCR3 (GB112453; Servicebio, Wuhan, China; 1:600), EPX (29755-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China; 1:2,000), Cleaved caspase-3 (25128-1-AP; Proteintech; 1:1,000), TOM20 (42406T; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; 1:1,000), COX IV (4850T; Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1,000), VDAC (A27273PM; ABclonal, Wuhan, China; 1:9,000), PDHA1 (ab168379; Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:1,000), Drp1 (A21968; ABclonal; 1:3,000), MFN1 (A21293; ABclonal; 1:1,000), MFN2 (A19678; ABclonal; 1:1,500), OPA1 (A9833; ABclonal; 1:3,000), and β-actin (ab8226; Abcam; 1:1,000). After incubation with secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Tanon 5200, Shanghai, China).

EdU

Treated AML12 cells were incubated with EdU for 4 h, followed by fixation, permeabilization, and blocking as described above. Staining was performed using the BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with AF488 (C0071S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and samples were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Cell cycle analysis

After treatment, AML12 cells were washed three times with PBS, trypsinized, and collected by centrifugation. The cell pellet was gently resuspended in 1 mL of ice-cold 70% ethanol and fixed overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with 500 µL of PI/RNase staining buffer at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences) and ModFit LT software (v.5.0).

Proteomics

After flash-freezing in liquid nitrogen, the samples were shipped to Acegen Technology for proteomic analysis. Protein quantification was first performed using the BCA assay. Aliquots of 15 µg protein per sample were separated by SDS-PAGE. Following Coomassie Blue staining, in-gel digestion was carried out using a filter-aided sample preparation protocol with trypsin. The resulting peptides were desalted and analyzed by LC–MS/MS on a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific). MS data were processed with DIA-NN, identifying 6758 proteins annotated against the UniProt database. A stringent false discovery rate (FDR) threshold was applied for qualitative analysis, with cutoffs set at Peptide FDR ≤ 0.01 and Protein FDR ≤ 0.01. Functional annotation of the identified proteins was performed using general databases, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses, with additional assessment of protein subcellular localization.

Mitochondrial labeling

For mitochondrial labeling in Eos, the CellLight® Mitochondria-GFP construct (C10600, Thermo Fisher), which contains the E1 α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase leader sequence fused with Tag GFP, was transfected at a concentration of 2 µL per 10⁴ cells and incubated for 16 h. In BMDMs, AML12 cells, and LSECs, mitochondria were stained using MitoTracker™ Red FM (A66444, Thermo Fisher).

Inhibition of mitochondrial function within Eos-ApoEVs

To determine whether mitochondria within Eos-ApoEVs play a critical therapeutic role, mitochondrial function in Eos-ApoEVs was inhibited as previously described [25, 26]. ApoEVs were exposed to 10 mM 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+, 36913-39−0, Aladdin, China) for 90 min, followed by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 30 min to collect MPP+-treated ApoEVs (MPP+ApoEVs) for subsequent experiments.

Seahorse analysis

Mitochondrial stress tests and glycolytic stress assays were performed using the Seahorse XFe96 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) to quantitatively measure the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) in BMDMs, AML12 cells, and LSECs. For mitochondrial stress tests, the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent, #103015-100) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were sequentially injected with 1.5 µM oligomycin, 1.5 µM FCCP, and 0.5 µM rotenone/antimycin A (inhibitors of mitochondrial complex I and III, respectively) to assess OCR. For glycolytic stress assays, the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit (Agilent, #103020-100) was employed. Cells were sequentially injected with 10 mM glucose, 1.0 µM oligomycin, and 50 mM 2-DG at designated time points to determine ECAR. All experiments were conducted using the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer, and data were analyzed with Wave Software and its Report Generator. Post-assay normalization was performed based on cell counts. OCR and ECAR values are expressed as pmol/min and mpH/min, respectively.

Immunofluorescence

For cells. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Following permeabilization and blocking, samples were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope. The following antibodies were used: CD68 (ab53444; Abcam; 1:150), Arg1 (DF6657; Affinity Biosciences; 1:100), iNOS (A3374; Abclone; 1:100), Alexa Fluor® 594 (ab150160; Abcam; 1:800), Alexa Fluor® 488 (ab150077; Abcam; 1:800).

For tissue sections. Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval. After permeabilization and blocking at room temperature, sections were incubated sequentially with primary and fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were captured using a confocal microscope. The following antibodies were employed: F4/80 (ab300421; Abcam; 1:200), CD86 (ab239075; Abcam; 1:150), CD206 (ab64693; Abcam; 1:150), IL-6 (GB11117; Servicebio; 1:300), IL-10 (GB11108; Servicebio; 1:300), PCNA (ab92552; Abcam; 1:200), Alb (ab207327; Abcam; 1:200), CD31 (ab222783; Abcam; 1:150), Alexa Fluor® 594 (ab150080; Abcam; 1:800), Alexa Fluor® 488 (ab150077; Abcam; 1:800). Double staining for F4/80 and CD86/CD206 was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions of the BeyoTSA™ Dual Label Three-Color Multiplex Fluorescence Staining Kit (P1342S; Beyotime).

Flow cytometry

Identification of Eos.Cells (1 × 10⁶) were resuspended in 100 µL PBS. Antibodies against CD45 (103116; Biolegend; USA), Ly6G (127616; Biolegend; USA), CD11b (101206; Biolegend; USA), and Siglec-F (155505; Biolegend; USA), along with BV510 Zombie Aqua viability dye (423101; Biolegend; USA), were added and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark. After washing and centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded; the cells were resuspended in 300 µL of PBS. Eos were identified as CD45⁺ Ly6G⁻CD11b+Siglec-F⁺ cells.

Polarization Assessment of BMDMs. BMDMs were treated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and 20 ng/mL IFN-γ for 24 h (the Control group was maintained under normal culture conditions). After removal of the supernatant, the ApoEVs-treated group was supplemented with fresh medium containing 10 µg/mL ApoEVs and cultured for an additional 24 h. Subsequent staining procedures followed the same protocol as described for Eos identification, using antibodies or dyes against F4/80 (123108; Biolegend; USA), CD11b (101235; Biolegend; USA), 7-AAD (420404; Biolegend; USA), CD163 (155307; Biolegend; USA), and CD86 (105012; Biolegend; USA). Samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences).

Preparation and characterization of dAM hydrogel

Placental amniotic membranes were obtained from healthy full-term cesarean deliveries, rinsed with sterile PBS, flash-frozen at −80 °C, and stored until use. The collection of tissue samples was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Jinhua Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital (2024KY095). For decellularization, the amniotic membrane was incubated in trypsin solution with shaking at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by sequential treatment with 3% Triton X-100 and 4% sodium deoxycholate, each for 1.5 h with agitation. After thorough rinsing, the decellularized tissue was fixed, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin for histological sectioning. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and DAPI staining were performed to evaluate decellularization efficiency.

For lyophilization, the decellularized membrane was frozen at −80 °C and lyophilized. The resulting material was mechanically milled into a powdered form. The powder was subsequently analyzed for residual DNA and sulfated glycosaminoglycans (sGAG) content. To form the hydrogel, 100 mg of the lyophilized powder was digested in 10 mL of 0.01 M HCl containing 0.1% (w/v) pepsin (P7012, Sigma-Aldrich) under continuous stirring for 8 h. The pH was first raised above 8 using 1 M NaOH to inactivate pepsin, then adjusted back to pH 7.0–7.2 using 1 M HCl. One-tenth volume of 10× PBS was added, and the solution was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min to induce gelation. The microstructure of the resulting hydrogel was observed by scanning electron microscope (SEM) and its rheological properties were assessed using a rotational rheometer.

Synthesis HA-DA patch

A total of 1 g of hyaluronic acid (HA, MW = 200 kDa) was dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water under continuous stirring. After complete dissolution, 1162 mg of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) and 862 mg of N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide (NHS) were added to the HA solution and stirred for 30 min. Subsequently, 1425 mg of dopamine hydrochloride was introduced into the reaction system. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 5.5 using HCl and NaOH, followed by a 24-h reaction at 37 °C under nitrogen protection to prevent dopamine oxidation.

The resulting solution was dialyzed using a dialysis membrane (MWCO = 8 kDa), first under acidic conditions (pH = 5.5) for 48 h, and then in deionized water for another 24 h. The purified sample was frozen at −80 °C and lyophilized using a vacuum freeze-dryer. The final HA-DA conjugate was stored at −20 °C for further use. The structure of HA-DA was confirmed by ¹H NMR spectroscopy.

For patch preparation, a 2% (w/v) HA-DA solution was cast onto a plate and lyophilized for 48 h to form an HA-DA patch.

Mouse model of hepatic I/R injury

According to a previously described method [12, 27], mice were fasted for 12 h and anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (40–60 mg/kg). After midline laparotomy, the Glisson pedicle to the left and median lobes was clamped for 90 min (ischemia confirmed by lobe blanching) and then released for reperfusion. Sham mice underwent identical surgery without clamping. Warm saline and a heating pad were used throughout; food and water were restored post-operatively.

Laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI)

Hepatic blood flow was evaluated using LSCI. After anesthesia, mice were fixed on the operating platform and the abdominal area was disinfected. A midline abdominal incision was made to gradually expose the xiphoid region, and retractors were used to fully expose the left and median liver lobes. The laser speckle imaging system (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) was connected to a computer for data acquisition. Appropriate image resolution, frame rate, and pseudo-color settings were selected according to experimental requirements. The region of interest (ROI) tool in the software was used for perfusion analysis, and hepatic blood flow signals were recorded over 10 s and averaged for quantification.

Animal experiments

All experimental procedures involving mice were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of South China Agricultural University (2024H050). Six-week-old male C57BL/6J specific pathogen-free mice were purchased from Sijiajingda Biotechnology Co., Ltd. To establish a mouse model of mild-to-moderate steatotic liver, animals were fed a Western diet for 12 weeks, consisting of high-fat/high-carbohydrate chow (42% fat, 0.1% cholesterol; Trophic Animal Feed High-tech Co., Ltd, China) and high-fructose/glucose drinking water (23.1 g/L D-fructose +18.9 g/L D-glucose) [28]. Mice were maintained in individually ventilated cage systems (5 mice/cage) under controlled conditions: temperature 25–27 °C, humidity 50–60%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. Steatosis induction was confirmed by monitoring body weight changes, liver histopathology, Oil Red O staining, and biochemical parameters.

Steatotic and normal liver mice were randomly divided into five groups: (1) Sham group (n = 6); (2) I/R group (n = 6); (3) HA-DA group (n = 6): HA-DA patch was topically applied to the liver surface after reperfusion; (4) dAM/HA-DA group: dAM/HA-DA patch was implanted on the liver after reperfusion; (5) ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA group (n = 6): ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA patch was implanted on the liver after reperfusion.

At 1, 3, and 7 days post-intervention, hepatic blood flow was assessed by LSCI in steatotic liver mice. Blood samples were collected for alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed into paraffin or frozen sections for further evaluation.

RNA-sequencing

Liver tissues were collected. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent following tissue homogenization. RNA libraries were constructed with the Acegen RNA Library Prep Kit (Acegen, Cat. No. AG2402). Poly(A)-tailed mRNA was enriched using oligo (dT) magnetic beads, fragmented into 250–300 bp pieces, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and purified. The cDNA then underwent end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification to generate the final sequencing library. Library quality was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform with a GeneMind PE150 strategy. Raw sequencing data were converted to FASTQ format, and low-quality reads were filtered out using Fastp (v0.23.1). Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (Mus musculus). Gene expression levels were quantified as counts and FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) using HTseq-count (v0.11.0). Differential expression analysis was performed with the DESeq2 package (v1.42) in R. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log₂FC| ≥ 1 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Functional analysis of DEGs was carried out via GO and KEGG annotation, followed by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the entire gene set.

Histological analysis

Mouse liver and other organ tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and embedded in paraffin. The tissues were then sectioned into 5-µm-thick slices. H&E staining was performed to evaluate histopathological changes.

Biochemical function analysis

Blood samples were collected from mice via retro-orbital bleeding. After standing at room temperature for 1 h, the samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant serum was carefully transferred into clean 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes using a micropipette and sent to the Clinical Laboratory of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University for biochemical analysis, including measurements of ALT, AST, total cholesterol (TC) and triglycerides (TG).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2) and are presented as mean ± SD. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Isolation, characterization, and cellular uptake of Eos-ApoEVs

In this study, we induced the differentiation of bone marrow cells into Eos and confirmed their identity (Fig. 1a-c). Then, ApoEVs were isolated following the experimental procedure illustrated in Fig. 1d. Flow cytometry confirmed that after 12 h of STS treatment, over 90% of Eos were Annexin V-positive apoptotic cells (Fig. 1e). Similarly, over 90% of the isolated ApoEVs were Annexin V-positive, indicating their apoptotic origin and the retention of parental cell properties (Fig. 1e). Western blot analysis demonstrated that live Eos, apoptotic Eos, and ApoEVs shared common markers, including caspase-3, β-actin, and Eos-specific markers CCR3 and EPX (Fig. 1f). Additionally, cleaved caspase-3, a specific marker of apoptosis, was detected in both the apoptotic Eos and ApoEVs (Fig. 1f). DLS revealed that most ApoEVs ranged between 190 and 900 nm in diameter (Fig. 1g). TEM revealed the spherical morphology of ApoEVs (Fig. 1h), and immunofluorescence staining confirmed the presence of Annexin V-labeled phosphatidylserine (Fig. 1i). The results demonstrated the successful isolation of Eos-ApoEVs. To assess cellular uptake, DiI-labeled ApoEVs were incubated with macrophages (BMDMs), hepatocytes (AML12 cells), and human immortalized LSECs for 1 and 6 h. Results indicated that all three cell types internalized ApoEVs, with uptake rates increasing as incubation time was extended (Fig. 1j, k and Fig. S1). These findings confirmed the successful induction of Eos apoptosis and specific characteristics of Eos-ApoEVs that can be effectively internalized by macrophages, hepatocytes, and LSECs.

Pro-proliferative, pro-angiogenic and immunomodulatoryeffects of Eos-ApoEVs in vitro

In liver I/R injury, extensive hepatocyte death and disruption of endothelial vessels create necrotic areas [28]. These necrotic cells or tissues release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that stimulate liver macrophages to polarize toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype and release large amounts of inflammatory cytokines, resulting in secondary damage. Therefore, facilitating hepatocyte proliferation, microvascular regeneration and inflammation resolution after I/R injury is essential for tissue repair and functional recovery. However, under steatotic conditions, the capacity for inflammation resolution and tissue repair is impaired, creating an urgent need for effective intervention.

To assess the ability of Eos-ApoEVs to facilitate hepatocyte proliferation and LSEC-mediated angiogenesis, AML12 cells and LSECs subjected to OGD/R were used to mimic I/R injury. EdU fluorescence staining and cell cycle analysis were performed using flow cytometry to assess cell proliferation. OGD/R-treated AML12 cells exhibited reduced proliferation, with a decreased number of EdU-positive cells and cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase (Fig. 2a-d). ApoEVs treatment significantly increased the number of EdU-positive cells and facilitated cell cycle progression from the G0/G1 phase to the S and G2/M phases, indicating enhanced proliferation (Fig. 2a-d). We next assessed the effects of ApoEVs on LSEC function, including tube formation, wound healing, and Transwell migration. The OGD/R-impaired LSECs exhibited reduced tube-forming ability that was significantly restored by ApoEV treatment (Fig. 2e, f). Similarly, wound healing and Transwell migration assays demonstrated that ApoEVs significantly enhanced the migratory capacity of LSECs suppressed by OGD/R (Fig. 2g, h and Fig. S2a). These results indicated that ApoEVs possess strong pro-proliferative and pro-angiogenic activity. Subsequently, to assess the anti-inflammatory capacity of Eos-ApoEVs, BMDMs were stimulated with LPS and IFN-γ for 24 h, followed by co-culture with ApoEVs for an additional 24 h. Immunofluorescence and flow cytometry were used to assess the macrophage phenotype. Compared with untreated cells, LPS/IFN-γ-stimulated BMDMs demonstrated the increased expression of pro-inflammatory markers—inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS, detected by immunofluorescence) and CD86 (detected by flow cytometry)—while anti-inflammatory markers, such as arginase-1 (Arg-1, immunofluorescence) and CD163 (flow cytometry) were significantly reduced (Fig. S3; Fig. 2i-k and Fig. S2b). Treatment with ApoEVs significantly reduced pro-inflammatory markers and upregulated anti-inflammatory markers, indicating their immunomodulatory function (Fig. 2i-k and Fig. S2b). In summary, these findings demonstrated that Eos-ApoEVs exert pro-proliferative, pro-angiogenic and immunomodulatory effects in vitro, indicating their potential to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes in vivo and facilitate repair after liver I/R injury.

Preparation and characterization of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA patch

Short half-life, poor tissue targeting, and rapid clearance remain major challenges in EV-based therapies [18, 29]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to harness the reparative potential of ApoEVs while overcoming their rapid clearance and limited targeting in vivo. In situ delivery systems based on hydrogel-loaded EVs play crucial roles in the regeneration and repair of primary organs, such as the brain, heart, liver, and kidneys [30]. These systems effectively address the issues of poor targeting and short organ retention time of EVs. In this study, we used a dAM hydrogel as a carrier for Eos-ApoEVs that enables sustained local release of ApoEVs and possesses recognized pro-repair capabilities. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, the amniotic membrane was extracted from the placenta and processed into the final ApoEVs@dAM composite. Figure 3b-f indicated successful decellularization of the amniotic membrane. Images showed that the decellularized amniotic membrane appears white (Fig. 3b). H&E staining revealed that compared to the fresh amniotic membrane, the decellularized amniotic membrane no longer exhibited blue-purple nuclear structures, retaining only pink extracellular matrix components (Fig. 3c). DAPI staining confirmed these findings (Fig. 3e). Additionally, DNA content analysis revealed that the decellularized amniotic membrane contained < 50 ng/mg of residual DNA, below the internationally accepted threshold for decellularized tissues (Fig. 3f). Masson’s trichrome staining and quantification of sGAG confirmed the preservation of collagen and sGAG—the primary bioactive components of the decellularized matrix (Fig. 3d, f). Next, we prepared dAM hydrogel and incorporated ApoEVs into it to generate ApoEVs@dAM. The gelation behavior, microstructure, and rheological properties of both dAM hydrogel and ApoEVs@dAM were assessed (Fig. 3g-i). Three-dimensional spatial distribution and reconstruction of DiI-labeled ApoEVs further confirmed their successful and uniform loading within the dAM hydrogel (Fig. 3j).

To ensure their firm adhesion to the liver surface, we fabricated a HA-DA patch (Fig. S4a), which exhibits excellent tissue-adhesive properties [31]. The lyophilized HA-DA patch was applied to cover the ApoEVs@dAM, with its edges rapidly absorbing water to transform into a hydrogel patch, which then oxidatively adhered to the liver, forming ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA composite (Fig. S4b). Additionally, Live/Dead staining confirmed the biocompatibility of dAM and HA-DA (Fig. S5).

ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA accelerates the repair of I/R injury in steatotic and normal livers

After establishing the steatotic-liver mouse model (Fig. S6), we subjected the mice to hepatic I/R injury and then evaluated hepatic retention of the ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA patch, a prerequisite for sustained ApoEV release during repair. Macroscopic images demonstrated that the patch remained stably attached to the liver surface for at least 1, 3, and 7 days post-transplantation, providing continuous ApoEV release throughout the first week after I/R injury (Fig. 4a). By day 14, the patch had almost completely disappeared, indicating efficient biodegradation (Fig. 4a). The NIRF of DiR-labeled ApoEVs confirmed their prolonged presence in the dAM hydrogel (Fig. 4b). Moreover, H&E staining of other major organs revealed no significant inflammation or tumorigenicity (Fig. S7).

Although hydrogels loaded with cells, drugs, or EVs implanted on the liver surface can ameliorate liver injury, direct evidence of the loaded agents (drugs, cellular secretions, and EVs) entering liver tissue is lacking [32, 33]. Using DiI-labeled ApoEVs, we detected ApoEVs within the liver tissue at 1, 3, and 7 days post-implantation, with the distribution extending beyond the immediate implantation site (Fig. 4c and Fig. S8a, b). Additionally, co-staining with markers for macrophages, hepatocytes, and endothelial cells revealed co-localization of DiI-labeled ApoEVs, indicating their uptake by all three cell types (Fig. 4d). These results demonstrated that ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA enables the sustained local release of ApoEVs in the liver for at least 7 days, followed by eventual biodegradation in vivo.

To assess the reparative effects of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA implantation on steatotic liver I/R injury, assessments were conducted at 1, 3, and 7 days post-implantation (Fig. 5a). Subsequently, we quantified the markers of inflammation, proliferation, and angiogenesis to confirm these findings. Immunofluorescence co-staining revealed that ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA significantly reduced the proportion of CD86⁺F4/80⁺ pro-inflammatory macrophages and increased CD206⁺F4/80⁺ anti-inflammatory/reparative macrophages in steatotic livers after I/R injury (Fig. 6a, e; Fig. S9a, e and S10a, e). This shift was accompanied by reduced IL-6 and increased IL-10 expression, indicating that the patch remodeled the macrophage phenotype and dampened inflammation (Fig. 6b, f; Fig. S9b, f and S10b, f). Proliferation assays demonstrated that both PCNA immunofluorescence and Ki67 immunohistochemistry were significantly increased following ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA treatment, indicating enhanced hepatocyte proliferation (Fig. 6c, g; Fig. S9c, g and S10c, g). Finally, CD31 staining confirmed enhanced neovascularization after I/R injury (Fig. 6d, h; Fig. S9d, h and S10d, h). Notably, dAM/HA-DA alone produced similar trends; however, its effects were substantially less pronounced, highlighting the dominant role of sustained ApoEV release (Fig. 6; Fig. S9 and S10).

To determine whether the therapeutic effect of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA was specific to the steatotic microenvironment or could be extended to non-steatotic liver injury, we further evaluated its efficacy in a normal liver I/R injury model. Consistent with the observations in steatotic livers, ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA significantly promoted liver repair at days 1 and 3 after reperfusion, as evidenced by reduced serum ALT and AST levels, attenuated histopathological injury, and increased hepatocyte proliferation, as indicated by PCNA staining (Fig. S11a-f). These findings indicate that the reparative effect of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA is not restricted to steatotic livers, but is also evident in normal liver I/R injury.

To further distinguish whether ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA mainly affected the initial injury phase or the subsequent repair phase, we assessed liver injury at 6 h after reperfusion, a time point corresponding to the peak phase of acute hepatocellular injury in this model [34, 35]. H&E staining, serum ALT and AST levels, and TUNEL staining showed no significant differences among the treatment groups at this early time point in the normal liver I/R injury model (Fig. S12a-f). Similarly, in the steatotic liver I/R injury model, serum ALT and AST levels at 6 h after reperfusion were comparable among groups (Fig. S12g). Together, these results suggested that ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA does not markedly attenuate the initial acute injury burst, but instead primarily accelerates the subsequent repair and regeneration process after liver I/R injury.

In summary, these findings indicate that delivery of ApoEVs through the dAM/HA-DA patch prolongs their hepatic retention, thereby fully leveraging their reparative capacity after I/R injury. While this strategy was developed in the clinically relevant context of steatotic liver I/R injury, its reparative efficacy was also observed in normal liver I/R injury. Moreover, the absence of significant protection at 6 h after reperfusion suggests that ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA primarily promotes post-injury repair and regeneration rather than attenuating the initial acute injury phase. Together, these results establish ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA as a promising therapeutic strategy for liver repair after I/R injury, particularly in vulnerable steatotic grafts.

Transcriptome analysis bolstering therapeutic effect of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA and dAM/HA-DA

To elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms underlying the protective effects of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA and dAM/HA-DA against steatotic liver I/R injury, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of liver samples harvested on day 3 (n = 9): three I/R livers (termed I/R), three dAM/HA-DA-treated livers (termed dAM), and three ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA-treated livers (termed ApoEVs@dAM). Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) revealed tight clustering within each group. The I/R and dAM samples were closer to each other, whereas ApoEVs@dAM formed distinct clouds (Fig. 7a). Pairwise differential expression analysis (|log₂FC| ≥ 1, P < 0.05) demonstrated that ApoEVs@dAM induced the largest transcriptomic shift, with predominantly upregulated genes (DEGs: I/R vs. ApoEVs@dAM, 2,221 and dAM vs. ApoEVs@dAM, 3,125), whereas only 408 DEGs were identified between I/R and dAM, highlighting the dominant transcriptional effect of ApoEVs within the composite patch (Fig. 7b). GO enrichment (biological processes [BP], molecular functions [MF], and cellular components [CC]; top 10 terms) demonstrated a significant over-representation of energy metabolism, cell cycle, inflammatory response, and redox processes in ApoEVs@dAM vs. I/R, a pattern largely recapitulated in ApoEVs@dAM vs. dAM (Fig. 7c). In contrast, dAM vs. I/R yielded only a handful of GO terms associated with inflammation control and proliferation, with no energy metabolism signatures (Fig. 7c), indicating that the significant protection conferred by ApoEVs@dAM was associated with the restoration of bioenergetic homeostasis. KEGG pathway analysis of energy metabolism, proliferation/repair, and inflammatory response confirmed significant enrichment of the cell cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, VEGF signaling (pro-angiogenic), and inflammatory cascades in ApoEVs@dAM, whereas dAM primarily affected inflammation- and repair-related pathways (Fig. 7 d). GSEA confirmed robust alterations in cell cycle, inflammatory responses, and tissue-repair signatures across all comparisons (Fig. 7e). These transcriptomic findings align with our in vitro and histological data, reinforcing that the therapeutic benefit of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA stems from the complementary but unequal contribution of ApoEVs and dAM, with ApoEVs playing a crucial role. Because the anti-inflammatory and pro-repair mechanisms of dAM have been extensively characterized [19, 36, 37], subsequent studies will specifically assess the mechanistic actions of ApoEVs within this composite system.

Functional mitochondria are enriched in Eos-ApoEVs and required for therapeutic efficacy

Considering the primary roles of ApoEV proteins in intracellular activity and intercellular communication [16], we sought to clarify how Eos-ApoEVs facilitate the repair of steatotic liver I/R injury. Therefore, we performed a four-dimensional (4D) label-free proteomic analysis to catalogue the ApoEV proteome and identified 6,758 proteins. GO enrichment analysis of all the detected proteins was performed across three categories—BP, MF, and CC. Figure 8a illustrates that the top 10 GO terms in each category were mitochondria-related: three BP terms related to mitochondrial energy generation and respiration, three CC terms describing mitochondrial compartments, and one MF term involving the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Similarly, KEGG enrichment analysis (top 10 pathways) identified two pathways associated with mitochondrial energy metabolism—metabolic pathways and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 8b). Therefore, Eos-ApoEVs harbor an abundance of mitochondria/energy-metabolism proteins. Subcellular localization analysis confirmed this, assigning 805 proteins (12.8%) to the mitochondria (Fig. 8c, d). To exclude interference from low-abundance proteins, we repeated the GO, KEGG, and subcellular localization analyses of the 1,000 most abundant proteins (Fig. S13). Mitochondrial respiration and energy metabolism pathways remained among the top hits (Fig. S13a, b), with 168 proteins (16.8%) localized in the mitochondria (Fig. S13c, d). These data indicate that Eos-ApoEVs were enriched in mitochondrial components, likely in functional mitochondria. To assess the presence of mitochondria in Eos-ApoEVs, we used multiple approaches. Western blotting revealed primary outer membrane (TOM20 and VDAC), inner membrane (COX IV), and matrix (PDHA1) mitochondrial proteins in Eos-ApoEVs (Fig. 8e). TEM revealed mitochondria within the vesicles (Fig. 8f). Additionally, we labeled Eos mitochondria with green fluorescent protein (GFP), induced apoptosis, and isolated the Eos-ApoEVs. Flow cytometry revealed that 41–63% of the Eos-ApoEVs were GFP⁺ (Fig. 8g). Finally, GFP-containing ApoEVs were co-cultured with MitoTracker Red-labeled BMDMs, AML12 cells, and LSECs. Immunofluorescence imaging demonstrated that GFP⁺ ApoEVs co-localized with recipient mitochondria (Fig. 8h). These results demonstrated that Eos-ApoEVs were enriched in functional mitochondria.

Eos-ApoEVs mediate mitochondrial transfer to facilitate mitochondrial fusion and metabolic reprogramming

Mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired energy metabolism are characteristic of I/R injury, directly causing cell death and functional loss [38, 39]. Under physiological conditions, cells rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to meet energy demands and maintain functional homeostasis. In contrast, pathological insults such as I/R injury trigger mitochondrial dysfunction, compelling a metabolic shift toward glycolysis that sustains cell survival but compromises essential cellular functions [40, 41]. Mitochondrial transplantation or intercellular transfer can induce mitochondrial fusion and metabolic reprogramming in recipient cells, thereby restoring their function and alleviating damage [40–42]. Our results demonstrated that OGD/R triggered mitochondrial fission in AML12 cells and LSECs, as evidenced by upregulation of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) and downregulation of the fusion proteins mitofusin 1 (MFN1), MFN2, and optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) (Fig. S14e, f and Fig. 9g, h). This imbalance in mitochondrial dynamics forced a shift in cellular energy metabolism from OXPHOS, reflected by decreased OCR, to glycolysis, reflected by increased ECAR (Fig. S14g, h and Fig. 9i, j). Notably, untreated ApoEVs reversed mitochondrial fission and restored OXPHOS, whereas the regulatory effects of MPP+ApoEVs were significantly attenuated or even abolished (Fig. S14e-h and Fig. 9g-j).

Additionally, macrophage phenotype and function are tightly coupled to their metabolic state: pro-inflammatory macrophages primarily rely on glycolysis, whereas anti-inflammatory macrophages depend on OXPHOS [43, 44]. We found that LPS/INF-γ treatment also induces mitochondrial fission, driving BMDM metabolism toward glycolysis (Fig. S16a-d). In contrast, functional mitochondrial transfer via Eos-ApoEVs promotes mitochondrial fusion and restores OXPHOS (Fig. S16a-d).

Collectively, these results indicated that Eos-ApoEVs reprogram cellular energy metabolism through fusion of functional mitochondria with damaged mitochondria in hepatocytes, LSECs and macrophages, thereby restoring their function.

Discussion

Exacerbated liver I/R injury and impaired repair are major complications in patients with steatotic liver undergoing partial hepatectomy, shock, or transplantation [2]. Therefore, developing novel therapies to accelerate repair after I/R injury in steatotic livers is of paramount clinical significance. In this study, we demonstrated that Eos-ApoEVs effectively facilitate recovery from I/R injury in steatotic mice. Mechanistically, our data suggest that Eos-ApoEVs mediate mitochondria-associated metabolic reprogramming, likely through the transfer of functional mitochondria, thereby inducing mitochondrial fusion and reprogramming the energy metabolism of hepatocytes, LSECs and macrophages from glycolysis to OXPHOS—collectively driving hepatocyte proliferation, LSEC migration and tube-forming capacity, and anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization. For therapeutic translation, we constructed a hydrogel patch by embedding Eos-ApoEVs into a dAM hydrogel, enabling the sustained release of ApoEVs on the liver surface. In addition to serving as a delivery platform, the dAM hydrogel exerted synergistic therapeutic effects, augmenting the reparative function of Eos-ApoEVs. These findings offer novel therapeutic insights into steatotic liver repair after I/R injury and establish the Eos-ApoEV-embedded dAM hydrogel patch as a potent therapeutic resource for treating I/R injury-related liver damage.

An important conceptual issue raised by this study is why Eos were selected as the parental source of ApoEVs. We would like to emphasize that our aim was not to establish Eos as the universally optimal source of reparative vesicles across all injury models. Rather, Eos were prioritized because they are endogenous immune cells with established pro-resolving and pro-repair roles in hepatic I/R injury. Unlike many stromal cell sources that are not physiologically linked to the acute inflammatory response in the liver, Eos are rapidly recruited during hepatic injury and participate in both injury limitation and tissue recovery. We therefore considered Eos-ApoEVs to be a biologically matched cell-free therapeutic candidate for this disease context.

ApoEVs, rich in parental signaling molecules and functional cargo, can be internalized by target cells to modulate diverse physiological and pathological processes, such as proliferation, apoptosis, and inflammation [17, 45]. Although previous studies have predominantly focused on the therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived ApoEVs in various disease models [46], emerging evidence indicates that immune cell-derived ApoEVs possess unique and irreplaceable regulatory functions [23, 47, 48]. This source-dependent functional heterogeneity provides a rationale for investigating Eos-ApoEVs in liver I/R injury, given the direct involvement of Eos in hepatic inflammation resolution and repair. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that Eos play indispensable roles in inflammation resolution and tissue repair [8–11]. In the liver, Eos modulate drug-induced liver injury, facilitate regeneration after partial hepatectomy, and ameliorate I/R injury [12, 21, 49, 50]. Notably, unlike resident immune cells, such as Kupffer cells, Eos are virtually absent from the healthy liver; however, upon I/R challenge, circulating Eos are rapidly recruited to the injured liver, where they attenuate tissue damage and facilitate repair [12, 21]. To date, the protective effects of Eos have been primarily attributed to the release of IL-4/13; however, additional and more significant regulatory mechanisms remain unclear [12, 21, 48–50]. Here, we demonstrated that Eos-ApoEVs regulate multiple cellular functions in vitro and promote liver repair after steatotic liver I/R injury in mice, suggesting that ApoEVs represent a newly identified pathway through which Eos exert hepatoprotective effects. Nevertheless, it remains to be determined whether Eos recruited to the liver after I/R injury undergo apoptosis and subsequently release ApoEVs that contribute to hepatic repair in vivo.

To elucidate the functions of Eos-ApoEVs, we generated and purified these vesicles from in vitro-differentiated and apoptotic Eos. Eos-ApoEVs were readily internalized by hepatocytes, LSECs, and macrophages. Following OGD/R or LPS challenge, all three cell types exhibited significant phenotypic and functional deterioration, whereas Eos-ApoEV treatment attenuated or reversed these adverse effects by promoting (i) hepatocyte proliferation, (ii) LSEC migration and tube formation, and (iii) anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization. To explore the underlying mechanism, we subjected Eos-ApoEVs to 4D label-free proteomic and bioinformatics analyses. The vesicle proteome was enriched for proteins involved in mitochondrial structure and function, indicating the presence of mitochondrial components or intact organelles. Functional assays confirmed that Eos-ApoEVs harbor respiration-competent mitochondria, and pharmacological inhibition of these mitochondria significantly blunted the therapeutic activity of the vesicles. Importantly, our pharmacological loss-of-function experiments provided functional evidence supporting the requirement of bioenergetically competent mitochondria for Eos-ApoEV-mediated repair. Specifically, pretreatment of Eos-ApoEVs with MPP+, a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor, markedly attenuated or abolished their ability to promote hepatocyte proliferation, restore LSEC migration and tube-forming capacity, induce anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization, enhance mitochondrial fusion, and re-establish OXPHOS-dominant metabolism. These findings indicate that the therapeutic activity of Eos-ApoEVs is not merely associated with the presence of mitochondrial components, but depends on bioenergetically competent mitochondria. Although MPP+ is mechanistically distinct from classical mitochondrial uncouplers such as CCCP or FCCP, both approaches impair mitochondrial bioenergetics through distinct mechanisms and can therefore serve as complementary strategies for interrogating the mitochondrial dependence of EV-mediated tissue repair [25, 26, 51]. Future studies using complementary uncoupler-based strategies may further clarify the respective contributions of mitochondrial membrane potential, electron transport, and oxidative phosphorylation to Eos-ApoEV-mediated organ repair. Notably, the mitochondrial cargo is not unique to Eos-ApoEVs. Previous studies have detected mitochondria in ApoEVs derived from MSCs, neutrophils, and Jurkat T cells [52–54]; however, mitochondrial presence does not invariably equate to functionality. For example, neutrophil-derived ApoEVs rich in mitochondria did not facilitate proliferation [53], possibly because of their lower organelle activity.

Currently, mitochondrial transfer or transplantation is a highly active research field that has significantly enhanced disease prognosis in disorders involving mitochondrial dysfunction [40, 55]. Evidence indicates that healthy mitochondria can be delivered into damaged host cells, where they replace or fuse with defective organelles, reduce reactive oxygen species generation, and enhance ATP production [40, 55]. During organ I/R injury, mitochondrial dysfunction impairs cellular function and triggers cell death, thereby directly compromising tissue repair. By modelling I/R injury in vitro, we identified that hepatocytes, LSECs and macrophages exhibited significant functional and phenotypic alterations, including suppression of hepatocyte proliferation, reduced migration and tube-forming capacity of LSECs, and a shift of macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. All three cell types exhibited increased expression of mitochondrial fission protein (Drp1), reduced the expression of fusion proteins (MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1), and a metabolic shift toward glycolysis, with significantly reduced ATP synthesis. Mitochondrial transfer restores OXPHOS, stimulates hepatocyte proliferation, rescues endothelial cell function and facilitates anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization—findings that align with our results [40, 56–60]. In summary, our data support the interpretation that Eos-ApoEVs promote mitochondria-associated metabolic reprogramming after I/R injury, thereby facilitating the repair of the steatotic liver.

Recently, EVs, including ApoEVs, have been recognized as effective, cell-free, and promising modalities for ameliorating liver diseases [61]. Unfortunately, their clinical translation is hampered by major obstacles, specifically the short half-life and low retention of EVs in vivo—resulting in inadequate therapeutic doses at the injured sites [32, 62]. Recent advances in repair-oriented biomaterial and nanomedicine research have highlighted the importance of localized delivery, organ targeting, and sustained bioactivity in improving therapeutic efficacy for organ injury [63, 64]. The emergence of hydrogel-based delivery systems can help overcome these limitations. The EV-in-hydrogel platform enables the sustained release of EVs at the lesion while offering excellent stability and high biocompatibility [30]. In the context of liver therapy, various hepatic tissue sheets have been exploited to load cells, drugs, or EVs for the treatment of fibrosis and related disorders [32, 33, 65]. Building on these advances, we selected a dAM hydrogel as the carrier and reinforced its adhesion to the liver surface using HA-DA. Notably, dAM hydrogel is not merely an in situ depot; it also possesses intrinsic reparative efficacy and an excellent safety profile, as evidenced by ongoing clinical trials on skin and corneal repair [66]. We anticipated that this composite can maximize the therapeutic benefits while maintaining superior safety. The results demonstrated that ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA remained on the liver surface for at least 7 days in vivo and was largely degraded by day 14. Notably, ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA significantly facilitated hepatic repair following I/R injury in steatotic mice. Additionally, Live/Dead cell staining and histological examination of the major abdominal organs confirmed the biosafety of this construct. These data support the therapeutic potential and translational relevance of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA for treating I/R-induced steatotic liver injury.

Notably, in addition to mitochondria, ApoEVs contain a plethora of bioactive molecules (proteins and nucleic acids). For example, human umbilical cord MSC ApoEVs loaded with miR-100-5p and let-7i-5p induced M2 macrophage polarization and chondrogenic differentiation [28]. T cell-derived ApoEVs expressing CD39 and CD73 ameliorated bone loss [24]. Ubiquitin-specific protease 5-enriched ApoEVs regulated nuclear pulposus cell apoptosis and DNA damage repair by preventing the proteasomal degradation of E2F transcription factor 1 [67]. In this study, although functional inhibition of mitochondria significantly attenuated the effects of Eos-ApoEVs, their regulatory capacity on macrophages was not completely abolished, indicating that other bioactive cargoes must be present in these vesicles, warranting further identification and functional validation.

A further limitation of the current study is that direct in vivo evidence for mitochondrial transfer and fusion from Eos-ApoEVs to recipient hepatic cells is still lacking. Our mechanistic interpretation is currently supported by multiple indirect but mutually consistent observations, including the mitochondrial enrichment of Eos-ApoEVs by proteomic and structural analyses, Mito-GFP-based co-localization with recipient mitochondria in vitro, the loss of therapeutic activity after mitochondrial inhibition with MPP+, in vivo uptake of ApoEVs by hepatocytes, macrophages, and LSECs, and transcriptomic enrichment of OXPHOS and energy metabolism pathways in treated livers. While these findings strongly support a mitochondria-related mechanism, future studies using in vivo mitochondrial tracing and higher-resolution imaging will be required to directly verify mitochondrial transfer and fusion in injured liver tissue. Another limitation is that the in vitro OGD/R model used in this study does not fully recapitulate the metabolic characteristics of steatotic liver disease. These assays were designed to provide a controlled cellular I/R injury-like injury model for evaluating the direct regulatory effects of Eos-ApoEVs and their mitochondrial mechanisms, whereas the steatotic liver I/R injury model was used to address the disease-specific pathological context in vivo. Future studies using steatotic or lipid-loaded cellular models may further clarify how fatty metabolic stress influences ApoEV uptake, mitochondrial transfer, and reparative efficacy. In addition to this mechanistic limitation, the temporal design of the present study also warrants consideration. The major inflammatory events of hepatic I/R injury occur within 6–12 h after reperfusion, whereas our primary evaluations were performed on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7 because these time points are more suitable for assessing tissue repair, regeneration, and functional recovery. Consistent with this focus, serum ALT and AST levels measured at 6 h after reperfusion did not differ significantly among groups in the steatotic liver I/R injury model, suggesting that ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA does not substantially alter the initial acute injury phase but instead exerts its therapeutic effects mainly during the subsequent repair process. Nevertheless, early inflammatory events within the first 6–12 h, such as neutrophil infiltration, cytokine release, and early necrotic changes, were not comprehensively characterized in this study and should be addressed in future work.

Conclusion

In summary, our study reveals that Eos-ApoEVs possess potent hepatic reparative properties. Mechanistically, our data suggest that functional mitochondria carried by these vesicles contribute to mitochondria-associated metabolic reprogramming in hepatocytes, LSECs, and macrophages, shifting cellular energy metabolism from glycolysis toward OXPHOS. This metabolic remodeling promotes hepatocyte proliferation, LSEC migration and tube formation, and anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization, thereby accelerating steatotic liver repair after I/R injury. Furthermore, we encapsulated Eos-ApoEVs in a dAM hydrogel to generate ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA patches, which enable localized and sustained ApoEV release, synergistically enhance therapeutic efficacy through the bioactive components of dAM, and exhibit high biocompatibility. Overall, this work highlights the therapeutic potential of Eos-ApoEVs for steatotic liver I/R injury and proposes surface transplantation of ApoEVs@dAM/HA-DA patches as a clinically translatable and potent strategy for promoting liver repair.
