Near-Optimal Four-Cycle Counting in Graph Streams
Sebastian Lüderssen, Stefan Neumann, Pan Peng

Abstract
We study four-cycle counting in arbitrary order graph streams. We present a 3-pass algorithm for ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximating the number of four-cycles using O ~ ​ ( m / T ) \widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T}) space, where m m is the number of edges and T T the number of four-cycles in the graph. This improves upon a 3-pass algorithm by Vorotnikova using space O ~ ​ ( m / T 1 / 3 ) \widetilde{O}(m/T^{1/3}) and matches a multi-pass lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( m / T ) \Omega(m/\sqrt{T}) by McGregor and Vorotnikova.

Introduction

Subgraph detection and counting in large graphs is a fundamental problem in computer science. Given an undirected graph
G
and a small pattern graph
H
(e.g., a triangle or a four-cycle), the goal is to detect or count the number of subgraphs (not necessarily induced) of
G
that are isomorphic to
H
. Such small pattern graphs, or motifs
[
29
]
, play a crucial role in understanding structural properties of networks. Subgraph counting has diverse applications in different areas, including social network analysis
[
32
]
, biological systems
[
15
]
, and database theory
[
5
]
.

This problem has been extensively studied in the
graph streaming model
, which is relevant for massive graphs where storing the entire graph in main memory is infeasible. In this model, the edges of the graph arrive sequentially in an arbitrary (potentially adversarial) order, and the algorithm has limited memory and may make a constant number of passes over the stream. Depending on whether a single pass or multiple passes are used, we refer to the corresponding settings as the
single-pass
or
multi-pass
streaming models. While single-pass algorithms are necessary in time-critical settings like network monitoring, multi-pass algorithms are highly effective in cases where the data is too large to fit into main memory but multiple passes over the data are feasible, such as in external memory or I/O-bound computations.

Formally, let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph with
n
vertices and
m
edges that is represented as a graph stream, and let
H
be a small pattern graph. Denote by

the number of subgraphs in
G
isomorphic to
H
. The objective of subgraph counting in the streaming model is to solve one of the following tasks using sublinear space and a small number of passes:

•

Detection:
Distinguish between the case where
G
contains no copy of
H
and the case where it contains at least
T
copies.

•

Counting:
Estimate
T
within a factor of
1\pm\varepsilon
.

In this paper, we focus on four-cycles, i.e., cycles of length four, which are the next simplest class of pattern graphs after triangles.
In bipartite graphs, four-cycles (also known as butterflies) are the simplest non-trivial motifs, playing a similar role to triangles in general graphs.

Our contributions.

In this work, we present an algorithm that matches the known space lower bound

There exists a

Even though our focus is on insertion-only streams (i.e., streams containing only edge

Technical contributions.

Unlike many prior works, our algorithm relies almost entirely on node sampling. It samples multiple sets of nodes using different probabilities, and then, during multiple passes, collects a subset of edges from their induced subgraphs. We then use the number of four-cycles with certain properties in the resulting subgraph to perform detection or to define our estimator for counting. This allows us to obtain a space usage of just
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
in expectation, but it comes at significant challenges for our algorithm analysis, especially in the variance analysis, due to the complex dependencies among four-cycles introduced by our sampling scheme.

To control the variance, we need to identify nodes (or edges and wedges, i.e., paths of length two) that participate in “many” four-cycles and exclude those four-cycles from our estimators. To this end, we enhance the commonly used concept of
heavy
nodes (edges or wedges).
The
heaviness
of a node
v
is the number of four-cycles in which
v
appears, and
v
is considered
heavy
if its heaviness is above a
heaviness threshold
that we set in our analysis. (The heaviness of an edge or wedge is defined analogously.)
In most previous works on four-cycle (or other subgraph) counting, this heaviness threshold was uniform across all nodes, making
being heavy
a property that depends only on the graph itself. In contrast, in our analysis, for each node, edge or wedge
and each possible way it might get sampled
, we must define an individual heaviness threshold to properly bound the variance of our estimator. That is, whether a node is heavy depends on its sampling probability.

Moreover, we observe that a straightforward definition of node heaviness cannot be efficiently estimated within our target space bounds. To overcome this, we introduce
a refined notion of heaviness
, which can be checked efficiently while remaining sufficiently accurate for our variance analysis. This refinement, however, introduces additional technical challenges. For example, it may cause certain four-cycles to be double-counted. We show that such overcounting affects only an
\varepsilon
-fraction of all four-cycles and therefore does not impact our final bounds.

We believe our techniques are of independent interest and, due to the generality
of our vertex sampling approach, they will also be applicable to other subgraph counting
problems in the streaming model. We present a more detailed overview of our
algorithm and our analysis in
Sections

3
and
4
.

Other related work.

For counting four-cycles using single-pass algorithms,

26

\widetilde{O}(m^{4}/T^{2})

We note that for single-pass algorithms for counting four-cycles there remains a gap between the upper and lower bounds.

In the more powerful adjacency list streaming model, where edges are

[

,

\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})

Further, we note that the previously mentioned multi-pass algorithm by
28

for counting four-cycles in the adjacency list streaming model looks superficially similar to ours.
They also use
\log(T)
pairs of sampling probabilities to count the number of
K_{2,\kappa}
-subgraphs (which we will later call onions) for specific sizes
\kappa

Additionally, while for this algorithm it is enough to just consider node heaviness, here we must take into account the heaviness of

For sketching algorithms in the streaming setting,
18
showed that even for bounded-degree graphs, any algorithm for counting four-cycles requires
\widetilde{\Omega}(m/\sqrt{T})
space. They also presented an upper bound of
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
for graphs of maximum degree
\widetilde{O}(T^{1/4})

Interestingly, their algorithm also samples nodes, but it samples all nodes with the same probability, which is substantially different from our algorithm.

12
studied algorithms with
predictions and considered four-cycle counting when they have access to an
oracle that estimates the heaviness of a given substructure; this is motivated
by the integration of machine learning techniques into streaming models.
Given such oracle access, they presented a single-pass algorithm with space usage
\widetilde{O}(T^{1/3}+m/T^{1/3})

presented a two-pass algorithm for counting four-cycles in low degeneracy graphs. Their edge-sampling based algorithm uses
O(m\kappa/\sqrt{T})
space on graphs of degeneracy

and additionally performs well in practice.
For bipartite graphs,
33
,
23
and
31

presented practical algorithms for four-cycle counting. They evaluated their algorithms empirically and showed that, in practice, even single-pass streaming algorithms can achieve highly accurate predictions.

We also note that
20
proposed a single-pass node-sampling algorithm for triangle counting, which, similar to our approach, samples nodes with different probabilities determined by their heaviness values. However, their assignment is performed only for nodes, which suffices for single-pass triangle counting. In contrast, our algorithm requires analogous assignments for edges and wedges, which must be carefully combined to effectively control the variance of our estimator. Moreover, unlike the single-pass setting, we employ multiple passes to collect and identify the relevant four-cycles.

Finally, we note that our sampling approach differs significantly from previous
node- or edge-sampling techniques that were used in triangle counting

[
30
,
16
]
, offering a new
path for managing variance and enabling accurate estimation with sublinear

Sampling Paradigms and Algorithmic Overview

In this section, we describe our algorithm and present the main challenges and intuition for designing it.

3.1
Previous Approaches Based on Edge Sampling

We start by briefly discussing the previous algorithms by

28
and by
35
, which were heavily based on edge sampling. In a nutshell, their algorithms works as follows: In the first stream pass, sample each edge with probability
p=\frac{1}{T^{1/3}}
to obtain a set of edges
S
. Then perform a second pass over the stream and, for each arriving edge
e=(u,v)
, count how many 3-paths in
S
start at
u
and end at
v
, and thus complete a four-cycle with
e
. At the end, return the number of such four-cycles after rescaling appropriately.

While this is a very natural algorithm, it is inherently limited to using space
\Omega(m/T^{1/3})
: When we sample edges uniformly at random from the stream with probability
p
, we must pick
p
such that for at least
\Omega(1)
four-cycles we sample a 3-path of their edges. For each four-cycle, this event happens with probability
4p^{3}
. Thus, to sample
\Omega(1)
four-cycles in expectation, we need to set
p\geq\frac{1}{T^{1/3}}
and thus the sampled edge set
S
contains
\Omega(m/T^{1/3})
edges in expectation.

The main work of the papers by
28
and by
35
then goes into the analysis of their oracles, which they need to classify edges and wedges as heavy or light. This is done to ensure that their estimator’s variance does not get too large.

As mentioned above, this algorithm is very natural and this is perhaps the reason why
35
conjectured that space
O(m/T^{1/3})
is optimal for counting four-cycles. Indeed, observe that it is not at all obvious how this algorithm should be improved using edge sampling.

3.2
Challenges for Node Sampling

As a consequence of the discussion above, our novel algorithms are almost entirely based on node sampling (we only use edge sampling for some of our heaviness oracles later). Specifically, we show that by storing an induced subgraph of size
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
, we can successfully detect
\Omega(1)
four-cycles with high probability (each of which is sampled with probability
\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{T})
), and this will also allow us to estimate the number of four-cycles.

To build some intuition for our node sampling approach, we start by discussing some basic sampling approaches and concrete hard instances that break them. For each instance, we will briefly argue that it contains
T
four-cycles and give a bound on the desired space usage
O(m/\sqrt{T})
.

Perhaps the easiest way to employ node sampling would be to sample a set of nodes
S
which contains each node uniformly at random with probability
p
. Then, during a stream pass, we store all edges of the induced subgraph
E[S]
, and after that pass finishes, we count the four-cycles in
E[S]
.

Hard instance 1: A single onion.

Unfortunately, the simple sampling approach above fails horribly if our input graph is given by a single onion of width
\kappa
(i.e.,
G=K_{2,\kappa}
). Note that if we set
\kappa=O(\sqrt{T})
then this induces
T=\binom{\kappa}{2}
four-cycles (since the two nodes on the one side of the bipartite graph form a four-cycle with all
\binom{\kappa}{2}
pairs of vertices on the other side). Further, as this graph has
m=O(\kappa)
edges, we can only use space
O(m/\sqrt{T})=O(1)
.

Now observe that the simple algorithm above only samples the two special nodes with probability
p^{2}
. Thus, to ensure that we even detect a four-cycle, we would need to set
p=\Omega(1)
, resulting in space usage
O(m)
, which is too much.

Hard instance 2: Multiple overlapping onions.
Next, let us generalize the instance from above to consist of multiple overlapping onions. Concretely, consider the complete bipartite graph
K_{\sqrt{T}/\kappa,\kappa}
. Note that this graph has
\Theta(T)
four-cycles since for each of the
\binom{\sqrt{T}/\kappa}{2}
node pairs on the left side of the graph we obtain
\binom{\kappa}{2}
four-cycles by picking two neighbors on the right side of the graph. Thus, the total number of four-cycles is given by
\binom{\sqrt{T}/\kappa}{2}\cdot\binom{\kappa}{2}=\Theta(T)
. Further note that this graph has
m=(\sqrt{T}/\kappa)\cdot\kappa=\sqrt{T}
edges and thus an algorithm achieving our desired space bound can only use space
O(m/\sqrt{T})=O(1)
. Finally, observe that this graph can be viewed as
\binom{\sqrt{T}/\kappa}{2}
overlapping onions of width
\kappa
by picking pairs of nodes on the left-hand side of the graph and keeping all right-hand side vertices.

Now, as before, the simple algorithm of sampling a node set
S
and storing
E[S]
cannot succeed in achieving our desired space usage.
However, suppose that we sample each vertex from the left-hand side with probability
p_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}/\kappa}
to obtain a node set
S_{1}
and each vertex from the right-hand side with probability
p_{2}=\frac{1}{\kappa}
to obtain a node set
S_{2}
. Now, during the stream pass, we only store the edges in
E[S_{1},S_{2}]
. Crucially, observe that we sample only
\Theta(1)
nodes in total. This ensures that we sample a four-cycle with constant probability, and further, we only need space
O(1)
, matching our desired space bound.

Of course, this approach has two obvious drawbacks: First, it requires knowledge about the two sides of the bipartite graph. Second, it also requires knowledge about the parameter
\kappa
to define our probabilities. However, we will see later that we can overcome these obstacles.

Hard instance 3: Heavy edge instances.

While above we sketched an approach if our input graph contains many large overlapping onions, we now consider the other extreme. That is,
we consider instances with heavy edges, i.e., edges that appear in many four-cycles. Concretely, consider a graph with a single edge
e^{*}=(u^{*},v^{*})
that is contained in all
T
four-cycles. Now, if we still want to store an induced subgraph
E[S_{1},S_{2}]
as in the previous example, then we have to ensure that (say)
S_{1}
contains
u^{*}
and
S_{2}
contains
v^{*}
with high probability, as otherwise we never store
e^{*}
and thus have no opportunity to detect any four-cycle. However, as we do not know
u^{*}
and

S_{1}
and
S_{2}
must include
all

Thus, in such a case, we adapt our algorithm from above as follows. We consider an algorithm which samples two node sets
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, where we set
p_{1}=1
and
p_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}
. Then, during a first stream pass, we store all edges in
E[R_{1},R_{2}]\cup E[R_{2}]
.
After that, during a second stream pass, we check for each stream edge

–
a
–
b

Coming back to the concrete example above, observe that

are indeed in

are in

with probability

\Omega(1)

Note that this approach is somewhat similar to those of

(b,u)
were collected during the first stream pass since they are contained in the sets
E[R_{1b,\kappa},R_{2a,\kappa}]
,
E[R_{2a,\kappa},R_{2b,\kappa}]
and
E[R_{2b,\kappa},R_{1a,\kappa}]
, respectively. If the algorithm identifies such a four-cycle
A
, it stores the edge
(u,v)
.

After both stream passes finished, the algorithm returns whether the set of all stored edges contains a four-cycle.
We note that our algorithm remains correct even if the edge order changes in different stream passes.

Intuition for the algorithm and its analysis.

To understand the intuition behind the algorithm, note that it combines and generalizes the two sampling approaches we discussed in
Section

3.2
in the second and third hard instances.
In a nutshell, we use the sets
S_{1,\kappa}
and
S_{2,\kappa}
to detect four-cycles which are part of large, potentially overlapping onions (corresponding to the second hard instance) and we use the other sets
R_{1a,\kappa}
,
R_{1b,\kappa}
,
R_{2a,\kappa}
and
R_{2b,\kappa}
to detect four-cycles which contain heavy edges (corresponding to the third hard instance).

The reason why we use different sets
S_{\cdot,\kappa}
and
R_{\cdot,\kappa}
is that we use these sets to detect (and later count) different types of four-cycle, and we want these estimators to be independent from each other.
We note that with the parameter choices above, we always have that
p_{1,\kappa}\geq p_{2,\kappa}
and thus it is instructive to think of the sets that were sampled with probability
p_{1,\kappa}
as substantially larger than those that were sampled with probability
p_{2,\kappa}
.

Now let us briefly argue why the algorithm achieves its guarantees
in expectation
.

First, consider any four-cycle
A=(a,b,c,d)
.
Note that the algorithm detects
A
if
a,c\in S_{1,\kappa}
and
b,d\in S_{2,\kappa}
since in this case all edges of
A
are in
\mathbb{E}\left[S_{1,\kappa},S_{2,\kappa}\right]
.
Thus,
A
is detected with probability at least
p_{1,\kappa}^{2}p_{2,\kappa}^{2}=\left(\frac{c_{1}^{2}\kappa}{\delta^{3}\sqrt{T}\kappa}\right)^{2}=\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{T})
.
Hence, after summing over all
T
four-cycles, we obtain that the algorithm detects
\Omega(1)
four-cycles in expectation.
Observe that for this simple expectation analysis, we do not even require the sets
R_{\cdot,\kappa}
or even different
\kappa
-values, but they will be crucial later to obtain our guarantees with high probability.

Second, let us bound the space usage. Note
that the sets
E[S_{1,\kappa},S_{2,\kappa}]
,
E[R_{1b,\kappa},R_{2a,\kappa}]
and
E[R_{2b,\kappa},R_{1a,\kappa}]
only contain edges for which one of its endpoints was sampled with probability
p_{1,\kappa}
and the other one was sampled with probability
p_{2,\kappa}
. Thus, each edge is contained in this set with probability
2p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa}=\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}})
, and therefore we obtain an
expected space usage of
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
.
Similarly, the set
E[R_{2a,\kappa},R_{2b,\kappa}]
contains each edge with probability
p_{2,\kappa}^{2}=\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}})\in\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{T^{1/2}})
since
\kappa\in[T^{1/4},2T^{1/2}]
. Thus, this set also contains at most
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
edges in expectation.

This shows that the algorithm achieves its guarantees for detection in expectation. We sketch the extension to a four-cycle counting algorithm in
Section

4.2
.

4
Proof Strategy and Technical Overview

In this section, we give an overview of our technical contributions and outline several proof ideas.

4.1
Detecting Four-Cycles Using Space
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
With High Probability

While above we already argued that we find
\Omega(1)
four-cycles in expectation, showing that this holds with high probability is substantially more difficult.

To bound the probability that our algorithm detects a four-cycle, we have to bound the variance of the random variable counting the number of four-cycles in the sample. As in previous works, the variance of this variable is high if a substructure appearing in many four-cycles is sampled with small probability, where a
substructure
is either a node, an edge or a wedge.
Thus, we would like to exclude all four-cycles containing substructures that appear in “too many” four-cycles.

Now, one of our key technical challenges for excluding four-cycles is the following. Given the algorithm above, each four-cycle
A
can be sampled in
\Theta(\log T)
different ways:
A
can be sampled for
\Theta(\log T)
values of
\kappa

\Theta(1)
many different ways to assign the nodes of
A
to the different sets we sampled. For our analysis, we will pick exactly one such way per four-cycle.

To select how a four-cycle should be sampled, we will intuitively consider two types of four-cycles, which correspond to the two sampling approaches that we are using simultaneously.
Here, we use the notion of

These two types of four-cycles are as follows:

and
b,d\in S_{2,\kappa}
, as in this case we collected all edges of
A
in
E[S_{1,\kappa},S_{2,\kappa}]
.
This case corresponds to the second hard instance in
Section

.
Crucially, observe that here the nodes
a,c\in S_{1,\kappa}
are
opposite
nodes in
A
.

2.

Next, consider a four-cycle
A=(a,b,c,d)
containing exactly one edge

with heaviness
t(e)\geq\sqrt{T}
.
Then we want to detect

if
a\in R_{1a,\kappa}
,
b\in R_{1b,\kappa}
,

and

.
This corresponds to the third hard instance in
Section

.
Crucially, observe that the nodes
a\in R_{1a,\kappa}
and
b\in R_{1b,\kappa}
are
adjacent

.

Note that above we did not consider the case of a four-cycle containing two or more heavy edges. This is on purpose since there are only a few such four-cycles and we can safely ignore them (as we will see below).

Next, given the two types of four-cycles and the sampling “rules” that we sketched, observe that they have the following in common: We want to sample both four-cycle types in such a way that they contain two nodes that were sampled with probability
p_{1,\kappa}
and two nodes that were sampled with probability
p_{2,\kappa}
.
The main difference between the two cases is whether the two nodes that were sampled with probability

are opposite nodes (the first case) or if they are adjacent (the second case).
This will be the basis for our definition of

below.

(a)

We highlight that the introduction of configurations, labeled substructures, and sampling-dependent heaviness thresholds forms a key conceptual contribution of this work. In particular, we assign different thresholds to substructures based on how they are sampled.

Sketch of the variance analysis.

Next, we provide a sketch of our variance analysis.
We aim to show that our algorithm detects a configuration from
\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
with high probability. As pairs of configurations sharing labeled substructures are not independent, we need to carefully bound the covariance of each such pair. Let
X_{c}
be the binary random variable indicating whether configuration
c
is sampled.

For simplification, consider a single sampled configuration
c=(A,\kappa,x,y)\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
and only one of its labeled substructures
(v,\kappa,\ell)
, where
v
is a node sampled into
S_{1,\kappa}
.
Consider all four-cycles that share exactly the node
v
with
A
. Each such four-cycle corresponds to exactly one configuration
b
that contains
(v,\kappa,\ell)
and we let
\mathcal{B}
denote the set of all such configurations
b
.

Crucially, as
(v,\kappa,\ell)
is light, there are at most
\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}=\widetilde{O}(Tp_{1,\kappa})
such configurations
b
and thus
|\mathcal{B}|=\widetilde{O}(Tp_{1,\kappa})
.
The probability that
b
is sampled, given that

is sampled, is
p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa}^{2}
. This allows us to bound the covariances as

\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\mathrm{Cov}\left[X_{c},X_{b}\right]\leq\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{b}\mid X_{c}=1\right]\mathbb{P}\left[X_{c}=1\right]=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa}^{2}\cdot p_{1,\kappa}^{2}p_{2,\kappa}^{2}=\widetilde{O}\left(Tp_{1,\kappa}^{4}p_{2,\kappa}^{4}\right)=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right),

where in the penultimate step we used that

by the argument above.

The same bound applies to all

\widetilde{O}(T)

\widetilde{O}(1)
.
This is sufficient to show that a four-cycle will be detected with constant probability, which can be boosted to high probability, by running our algorithm
\log(n)
times in parallel.

For our formal variance analysis, see

4.2
Extension to Counting

Next, we extend our detection algorithm to counting. Recall that we previously showed how to detect (i.e., find)
\Theta(1)
four-cycles. To extend this to the counting setting, we use the following approach: for each of the
\Theta(1)
detected four-cycles
A
, check whether
A
was sampled with its assigned configuration
(A,\kappa_{A},x_{A},y_{A})
, and then scale the counts appropriately (see
Algorithm

2
for the pseudocode of our approach). This enables us to filter out configurations that may lead to high variance while still obtaining a good estimate of the number of light four-cycles, which in turn serves as a good approximation of
T
.
However, efficiently verifying whether a sampled four-cycle conforms to its assigned configuration — within
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
space — presents us with significant technical challenges.

Challenge 1:

Suppose a four-cycle
A
was sampled with a configuration
c=(A,\kappa,x,y)
. To check whether
c=(A,\kappa_{A},x_{A},y_{A})
, i.e., whether
A
was sampled with its assigned configuration, we have to check (among others) that all labeled substructures
z
of
c
are light. Thus, we need to check
t(z)\leq\theta_{(z,w,\ell)}
for all
z\in c
. However, we find that this cannot be done using space
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
.

To resolve this issue, we introduce a notion of
refined heaviness

t(z,\kappa,\ell)
that replaces
t(z)
. Here, recall that
t(z)
counts all four-cycles containing the substructure
z
, which is completely independent of our sampling algorithm. In contrast,
t(z,w,\ell)
takes the sampling probabilities and labelings of substructures into account and ensures that we only count configurations towards
t(z,\kappa,\ell)
in which important substructures are light. For instance, for labeled nodes
(z,\kappa,\ell)
we define:

\displaystyle\begin{split}t(z,\kappa,\ell)=|\{c\in\mathfrak{C}_{\text{valid}}\mid(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c,\;t(w,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})&\leq s_{1}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})}\text{ for all wedges }(w,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})\in c,\\
\quad t(e,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})&\leq s_{2}\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})}\text{ for all edges }(e,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})\in c\}|,\end{split}

(1)

where
\mathfrak{C}_{\text{valid}}
is the set of so-called valid configurations (defined in
Section

6
) and
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are some random shifts required for technical reasons.

Now we define labeled substructures as
heavy
if
t(z,\kappa,\ell)>s\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
, where the heaviness threshold
\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
is the same as before and again
s
is some random shift for technical reasons. The main advantage of this definition is that it allows us
to check whether

t(z,\kappa,\ell)\leq s\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
using space just
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
.
However, we note that now we might have that
t(z,\kappa,\ell)\ll t(z)
since we only count four-cycles in the definition of
t(z,\kappa,\ell)
with certain good properties. Despite this relaxation, we show that at most
O(\varepsilon T)
four-cycles are lost and that the variance remains bounded.

Challenge 2:

Next, to check whether
c=(A,\kappa,x,y)
satisfies
c=(A,\kappa_{A},x_{A},y_{A})
, we also have to check that
\kappa=\kappa_{A}
. However, whether a substructure is heavy is no longer a monotone property with respect to the parameter
\kappa
, causing significant challenges.

To understand this, let us briefly reconsider our detection algorithm. There, a substructure is heavy if
t(z)>\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
(i.e., using our old heaviness notion
t(z)
). This is a very convenient definition because
t(z)
is fixed and only
\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
depends on
\kappa
. Since
\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
is a monotone function in
\kappa
, whether a substructure is heavy is a monotone property in
\kappa
. Now,
since we picked
\kappa_{A}
as the smallest
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
such that
A
has a light configuration,
this suggests the following natural approach to check whether
\kappa=\kappa_{A}
: If
(A,\kappa,x,y)
is light and
(A,\kappa/2,x,y)
is heavy, then return that
\kappa=\kappa_{A}
, otherwise return
\kappa\neq\kappa_{A}
.

Unfortunately, for our new notion of refined heaviness
t(z,\kappa,\ell)
, this approach no longer works. Now, a substructure is heavy if
t(z,\kappa,\ell)>\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
— where both sides depend on
\kappa
. Indeed, note that in
Equation

1
the configurations that we are counting heavily depend on the choice of
\kappa
. Now, we can construct examples (see
Figure

4
) where the property
t(z,\kappa,\ell)>\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
is no longer monotone in
\kappa
.
Thus, our simple approach for checking
\kappa=\kappa_{A}
from before (for
t(z)
) no longer works. Further, we also cannot check this property for all
\kappa^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
with
\kappa^{\prime}<\kappa
as this will require too much space if
\kappa^{\prime}\ll\kappa
.

We resolve these issues, by only considering configurations
c=(A,\kappa,x,y)
which are
light and locally minimial
, i.e., they satisfy that all
(A,\kappa^{\prime},x,y)
with
\varepsilon^{2}\kappa\leq\kappa^{\prime}<\kappa
are heavy and that
c
is light.
This can be checked efficiently within our desired space
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
since we only have to check
\kappa^{\prime}
-values in a small, local neighborhood around
\kappa
.
However, the problem is that with this definition some four-cycles may now have
multiple
light and locally minimal configurations, i.e., we introduce some double-counting.
Fortunately, we manage to show that this is only the case for at most
O(\varepsilon T)
four-cycles, which still allows us to obtain our desired approximation guarantees.

Challenge 3:

The third challenge arises from our refined heaviness definition
t(z,\kappa,\ell)
.
To decide whether
t(z,\kappa,\ell)\leq\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
we have to estimate
t(z,\kappa,\ell)
. However, in the estimation, we have to ensure that we only count configurations
c^{\prime}
such that certain labeled substructures in
c^{\prime}
are light.
For instance, if
z
is a node, then for each sampled configuration
c^{\prime}
we must ensure that its wedges and edges are light (as per
Equation

1
). The latter can only be done by again checking the heaviness/lightness of these substructures. Thus, we have to build multiple so-called
oracles
for checking the heaviness/lightness of configurations, nodes, edges and wedges that call each other. For instance, the node oracle will call the wedge oracle and the edge oracle; the edge oracle itself will also call another wedge oracle. However, we make sure that we only perform
\widetilde{O}(1)
oracle calls in total, which is crucial to obtain our space bound.

5
Four-Cycle Detection

In this section, we present an algorithm for the detection version of our problem, i.e., the algorithm decides whether a graph has
0
four-cycles or at least
T
four-cycles. It performs two passes over the stream and uses space
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
, and it is the basis for our counting algorithm below.
We summarize its guarantees in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

There exists a 2-pass algorithm which obtains a graph
G
as an arbitrary-order edge stream and correctly decides whether
G
has 0 or at least
T
four-cycles using
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
space with high probability.

In the following, we describe the detection algorithm in
Section

5.1
. In
Section

5.2
, we formalize the notions of configurations, their labelings, and labeled substructures. Next, in
Section

5.3
, we define the concept of heaviness for (labeled) substructures, configurations, and four-cycles, and show how to assign each light four-cycle to a unique configuration. We establish a lower bound on the number of light four-cycles in
Section

5.4
, and analyze the variance of the random variable counting four-cycles in
Section

5.5
. Finally, we complete the proof of
Theorem

5.1
in
Section

5.6
.

5.1
Algorithm Description

We start by defining our detection algorithm and we summarize its pseudocode in

.

As we described in

, the algorithm uses a set of parameters
\mathcal{K}=\{2^{k}T^{\frac{1}{4}}\;|\;k=0,\dots,\lceil\log(T^{\frac{1}{4}})\rceil)\}
, and
iterates over all
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
. Then for each
\kappa
it proceeds as follows.

Initially, the algorithm defines probabilities

\displaystyle p_{1,\kappa}=\frac{c_{1}\kappa}{\delta^{1.5}\sqrt{T}}\quad\text{ and }\quad p_{2,\kappa}=\frac{c_{1}}{\delta^{1.5}\kappa},

where
\delta=\frac{1}{2098\log(T)}
and

.

S_{2,\kappa}
,
R_{2a,\kappa}
and
R_{2b,\kappa}
by sampling each vertex independently and uniformly at random with probability
p_{2,\kappa}
.

In a first pass over the stream, the algorithm collects all edges in

, as well as all edges in

,
E[R_{2a,\kappa},R_{2b,\kappa}]
and
E[R_{2b,\kappa},R_{1a,\kappa}]
for all

.

In a second pass over the stream, upon arrival of an edge

such that
u\in R_{1a,\kappa}
,

,

and

for any

. Note that we can check whether this four-cycle exists since we currently see
(u,v)
in the stream and the edges
(v,a)
,
(a,b)
and
(b,u)
were collected during the first stream pass since they are contained in the sets
E[R_{1b,\kappa},R_{2a,\kappa}]
,
E[R_{2a,\kappa},R_{2b,\kappa}]
and
E[R_{2b,\kappa},R_{1a,\kappa}]
, respectively. If the algorithm identifies such a four-cycle
A
, it stores the edge
(u,v)
.

After both stream passes finished, the algorithm returns whether the set of all stored edges contains a four-cycle.

5.2
Configurations and Labeled Substructures

As we already discussed above, each four-cycle
A
can be detected by our algorithm in different ways: it can be sampled for each value
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
, and it can also be detected in multiple ways based on how its nodes were sampled into the sets
S_{1,\kappa}
,
S_{2,\kappa}
,
R_{1a,\kappa}
,
R_{1b,\kappa}
,
R_{2a,\kappa}
and
R_{2b,\kappa}
. For this reason, we now introduce
configurations
and
labeled substructures
, which let us reason about how the algorithm detects a four-cycle
A
.

Configurations.

We start by defining configurations.

Definition 5.2
.

Given a four-cycle
A\in\mathcal{T}
and
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
, a
configuration
is a tuple
(A,\kappa,x,y)\in\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{K}\times V^{2}
where
x,y\in V(A)
and
x<y
.

Note that there are
|\mathcal{T}|\times|\mathcal{K}|\times\binom{4}{2}=\Theta(T\log(T))
configurations in total.

Further, observe that the definition of a configuration is completely deterministic and currently completely independent from our sampling. To connect this definition to our sampling, we are interested in whether certain well-behaved configurations are
realized
by our random sample. Thus whether a configuration is realized is a random event and, in a nutshell, all realized configuration satisfy that either
x,y\in S_{1,\kappa}
or that
x\in R_{1a,\kappa}
and
y\in R_{1b,\kappa}
, i.e., they contain two nodes that were sampled with probability
p_{1,\kappa}
.

More specifically, consider a four-cycle
A
with node set
V(A)=\{x,y,u,v\}
and a configuration
c=(A,\kappa,x,y)
. If
x
and
y
are opposite nodes of
A
, then we say that
c
is
realized
if

\displaystyle x,y\in S_{1,\kappa}\quad\text{ and }\quad u,v\in S_{2,\kappa}.

(2)

Otherwise, if
x
and
y
are adjacent in
A
,
c
is
realized
if

\displaystyle x\in R_{1a,\kappa},\quad y\in R_{1b,\kappa},\quad u\in R_{2a,\kappa}\quad\text{ and }\quad v\in R_{2b,\kappa},

(3)

where
v
and
y
are the neighbors of
x
in
A
.

Observe that both of these cases are in line with what we discussed above: Realized configurations contain exactly two nodes that were sampled with probability
p_{1,\kappa}
(namely
x
and
y
) and two nodes that were sampled with probability
p_{2,\kappa}
(namely
u
and
v
).
We illustrate this in
Figure

2
.

Now observe that if a configuration
(A,\kappa,x,y)
is realized, then it is much more likely that another configuration
(A^{\prime},\kappa,x,y^{\prime})
, which also contains node
x
and uses the same
\kappa
-value, is also realized.
Thus, we say that two configurations
c
and
c^{\prime}
are
dependent
if the random events that
c
and
c^{\prime}
are realized are dependent.
To argue about this dependence more precisely, we will now have to define labeled substructures and when they are realized.

Labelings of Configurations and Labeled Substructures.

Now, before we define labeled substructures, we say that the
labeling
\ell_{c}
of a configuration

c=(A,\kappa,x,y)
is a mapping
\ell_{c}\colon V(A)\to\{S_{1},S_{2},R_{1a},R_{1b},R_{2a},R_{2b}\}
, which encodes in which sets the nodes
v\in V(A)
must be contained such that
c
is realized.
Note that here we omit the subscript
\kappa
of the sets on purpose, because we only want
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
, etc. to denote
labels
and not concrete random sets.
For instance, if
x
and
y
are opposite nodes in
A=(x,u,y,v)
, then the labeling
\ell_{c}
of
c=(A,\kappa,x,y)
is given by
\ell_{c}(x)=S_{1}
,
\ell_{c}(y)=S_{1}
,
\ell_{c}(u)=S_{2}
,
\ell_{c}(v)=S_{2}
.

Next, recall from the preliminaries that a
substructure
is a node, edge or wedge of a four-cycle. Further, we will briefly also consider a pair of opposite nodes of a four-cycle as a substructure, but we will see below that we tacitly ignore it. For convenience, given a substructure
z
, we denote its set of nodes by
V(z)
.

We will call such mappings
labelings
and we extend this idea to
labeled substructures
.

Definition 5.3
.

A
labeled substructure

(z,\kappa,\ell)
is a substructure
z
together with a value
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
and a labeling function
\ell:V(z)\rightarrow\{S_{1},S_{2},R_{1a},R_{1b},R_{2a},R_{2b}\}
.

Again, observe that the definition of a labeled substructure is completely deterministic, and we stress again that here
S_{1},S_{2},R_{1a},R_{1b},R_{2a},R_{2b}
are
labels
, rather than (random) sets.

Now we say that a labeled substructure
(z,\kappa,\ell)
is
realized
if each node
v\in V(z)
is sampled into the node set defined by
\kappa
and the labeling
\ell(v)
.

For instance, if
z
consists of a single node
v
, then the labeled substructure
(\{v\},\kappa,v\mapsto S_{1})
is realized if
v\in S_{1,\kappa}
. As another example, consider a labeled substructure
(e,\kappa,\ell_{e})
, where
e=(u,v)
and
\ell_{e}(u)=S_{1}
and
\ell_{e}(v)=S_{2}
. Then this labeled substructure is realized if
u\in S_{1,\kappa}
and
v\in S_{2,\kappa}
.

Now observe that whether a labeled substructure
(z,\kappa,\ell)
is realized is a random event and we denote the probability of this event by
p_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
. Note that

\displaystyle p_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}=p_{1,\kappa}^{i}p_{2,\kappa}^{j},

where
i=|\ell^{-1}(\{S_{1},R_{1a},R_{1b}\})|
is the number of nodes of
z
with a label of a set into which nodes are sampled with probability
p_{1,\kappa}
, and
j=|\ell^{-1}(\{S_{2},R_{2a},R_{2b}\})|
is the number of nodes with a label of a set into which nodes are sampled with probability
p_{2,\kappa}
.

Now we can connect configurations and labeled substructures as follows. Given a configuration
c=(A,\kappa^{\prime},x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and a labeled substructure
(z,\kappa,\ell)
we write
(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c
if
\kappa=\kappa^{\prime}
and
V(z)\subset V(A)
and
\ell=\ell_{c}\big|_{V(z)}
, i.e., when restricting the domain of the labeling
\ell_{c}
of the configuration
c
to the vertices in
z
then the two labelings must agree.
Observe that if
c
is realized, then any labeled substructure
(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c
is realized.

Note that each configuration
c
contains 14 labeled substructures: 4 nodes, 4 edges, 4 wedges, and 2 opposite node pairs, each labeled according to
\ell_{c}
.
We obtain the following observation, which characterizes when two configurations are dependent.

Observation 5.4
.

Two configurations
c
and
c^{\prime}
are dependent if and only if there exists a labeled substructure
(z,\kappa,\ell)
with
(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c
and
(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c^{\prime}
.

Next, we note that the thresholds are not set arbitrarily. They are all set such that they are in

.

For each labeled substructure
(z,\kappa,\ell)

\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}\leq{Tp_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}}/{c_{1}}
where

is a constant.

Using the substructure classification, we also define the heaviness for configurations and four-cycles:

•

A configuration is
heavy
if any of its labeled substructures is heavy, and otherwise it is
light
.

•

A four-cycle is
heavy
if all of its configurations are heavy, and otherwise it is
light
.

Note that a light four-cycle can have
1
to
6|\mathcal{K}|
light configurations.
Thus, in such cases, we will select one unique light configuration per four-cycle.

Wedges Dominate Opposite Node Pairs.

Next, we argue that
the heaviness constraints on opposite node pairs are redundant. In the following, this will allow us to only consider nodes, edges and wedges as substructures, since we show that a configuration is already light if its labeled nodes, edges and wedges are light.

First, observe that the heaviness of an opposite node pair
\{u,v\}
and a wedge
w
connecting it are closely related since
t(\{u,v\})=\operatorname{os}(u,v)=\binom{t(w)+1}{2}
.
Second, for any configuration
c
with light wedges, consider a labeled opposite node pair
(z,\kappa,\ell_{z})
with
V(z)=\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\}
and a wedge
(w,\kappa,\ell_{w})\in c
with endpoints
u^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime}
. As
(w,\kappa,\ell_{w})
is light we have

t(w)<\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell_{w})}
and thus

\displaystyle t(z)=\operatorname{os}(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})=\binom{t(w)+1}{2}\leq\binom{\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell_{w})}+1}{2}\leq\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell_{w})}^{2}.

Now note that if
p_{(z,\kappa,\ell_{z})}=p_{1,\kappa}^{2}
or
p_{(z,\kappa,\ell_{z})}=p_{2,\kappa}^{2}
we have
\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell_{z})}=\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell_{w})}^{2}
and thus the opposite node pair is light. If
p_{(z,\kappa,\ell_{z})}=p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa}
consider the unique wedge
(w,\kappa,\ell_{w})\in c
with endpoints
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}
and
p_{(w,\kappa,\ell_{w})}=p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa}^{2}
. For this wedge we have
\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell_{z})}^{2}\leq\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell_{z})}
and thus
z
is light.

We conclude that the lightness of wedges implies the lightness of opposite node pairs, and thus, for the rest of this section, we only consider nodes, edges and wedges as substructures.

Assigning Configurations to Four-Cycles.

We now proceed by
assigning
a unique configuration to each four-cycle. This is crucial to bound the variance of our estimator.
Below, in
Proposition

5.6
, we show that nearly all four-cycles have a light configuration and thereby prove that nearly all four-cycles are light.

Specifically, the
configuration assigned to a four-cycle

A
is the configuration
\sigma_{A}:=(A,\kappa_{A},x_{A},y_{A})
, where
\kappa_{A}
,
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
are set as described below.
We will prove that this configuration
\sigma_{A}
is light for almost all four-cycles (see
Proposition

5.6
).

Choice of
\kappa_{A}
:

Intuitively, we want to choose
\kappa_{A}
as the smallest
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
over all light configurations of
A
. However, for technical reasons, it will be convenient to work with the following definition, where we set

\kappa_{A}=\Bigg\lceil\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V(A)\\
e\in E(A)\\
w\in W(A)\end{subarray}}\left\{T^{\frac{1}{4}},\delta^{1.5}\frac{t(v)}{\sqrt{T}},\delta\sqrt{t(e)},\delta t(w)\right\}\Bigg\rceil_{\mathcal{K}},

(4)

where the maximum is taken over all nodes, edges or wedges in
A
and then rounded up to the next value in
\mathcal{K}
(by at most a factor of 2).

Indeed, for this definition we note that picking a smaller value for
\kappa
directly violates the heaviness threshold for the substructure attaining the maximum above. Thus, the smallest value of
\kappa
such that
A
is light is at least
\kappa_{A}
, but could potentially be higher. This implies that there might be four-cycles which have a light configuration, but for which the assigned configuration
\sigma_{A}
is not light. However, in
Proposition

5.6
we show that this is not a problem since with this choice of
\kappa_{A}
and the choice of
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
below, a vast majority of the configurations
\sigma_{A}
are light.

Choice of
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
:

To finish the description of
\sigma_{A}
, we assign the nodes
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
for light four-cycles
A
as follows:

•

If
A
contains a
\sqrt{T}/\delta^{2}
-heavy edge then
we assign
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
to be the endpoints of this edge, i.e., in this case
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
are adjacent.

•

Otherwise, we assign an opposite node pair of
A
based on the value
\kappa_{A}
(where ties are broken arbitrarily) and the substructure
z
attaining the maximum in
Equation

4
as follows:

–

If
\kappa_{A}=T^{1/4}
we assign the lexicographically smaller opposite node pair of
A
as
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
.

–

If
z
is a wedge, we assign its endpoints as
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
.

–

If
z
is a node, we assign
z
and its opposite node in
A
as
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
.

–

Note that in this case
z
cannot be an edge: Since for all edges in
A
, we have that
t(e)<\sqrt{T}/\delta^{2}
and thus we get that
\delta\sqrt{t(e)}<T^{1/4}
, implying that none of these edges can attain the maximum in
Equation

4
.

Observe that this choice of
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
is exactly corresponding to which sampling “rule” should be used such that the algorithm detects
A
: If the heaviest substructure in
A
is a heavy edge, then we want to pick
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
as adjacent nodes, and in all other cases we want to pick
x_{A}
and
y_{A}
as opposite nodes in
A
.

5.4
Bounding the Number of Light Four-Cycles

Based on the assignment rule
\sigma_{A}
for assigning unique light configurations to light four-cycles, we can now prove the following proposition, showing that the vast majority of four-cycles are light.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\delta\in(0,\min\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{1049\log(T)}\})
.
For at least
(1-1049\delta\log(T))T
four-cycles
the configuration
\sigma_{A}
is light.

We note that these excluded four-cycles are also all heavy four-cycles, but we do not need this and thus also do not prove it.

To prove
Proposition

5.6
we use the next three lemmas to exclude at most
(328+145+576)\delta\log(T)T
four-cycles.

We start by restating a known result by
28
, showing that only a few four-cycles contain two or more heavy edges.
Recall the definition of
\theta
-heavy nodes, edges and wedges from
Section

2
.

Lemma 5.7

(
28
)
.

At most
328\delta^{2}T
four-cycles contain two or more
\frac{\sqrt{T}}{4\delta^{2}}
-heavy edges.

Next, we show that only a few four-cycles contain a heavy edge and also a heavy wedge.

Lemma 5.8
.

At most
145\delta T
four-cycles contain at least one
\frac{\sqrt{T}}{4\delta^{2}}
-heavy edge and a
\frac{1}{\delta}
-heavy wedge.

Proof.

In this proof, we call four-cycles with the property stated in the lemma
bad
.

Now let
u
and
v
be endpoints of a
\frac{1}{\delta}
-wedge. This implies that
u
and
v
form an onion of size

\displaystyle\operatorname{os}(u,v)=\binom{\operatorname{ow}(u,v)}{2}\geq\binom{1/\delta}{2}>\frac{1}{4\delta^{2}},

where we used that
\delta\leq\frac{1}{2}
. This implies that, since each four-cycle is part of two onions, there are at most
8\delta^{2}T
onions of size at least
\frac{1}{4\delta^{2}}
, as otherwise there would be more than
T
four-cycles in total.

Next, let
h(u,v)
be the number of wedges between
u
and
v
which contain at least one
\frac{\sqrt{T}}{4\delta^{2}}
-heavy edge.
Then any bad four-cycle in the onion with endpoints
u
and
v
consists of one of these
h(u,v)
wedges and one other wedge connecting
u
and
v
.
Hence, the number of bad four-cycles in this onion is at most
h(u,v)\operatorname{ow}(u,v)
.

Thus, the total number of bad four-cycles in the entire graph is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
\operatorname{ow}(u,v)>\frac{1}{\delta}\end{subarray}}h(u,v)\operatorname{ow}(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
\operatorname{ow}(u,v)>\frac{1}{\delta}\\
h(u,v)\geq 2\end{subarray}}h(u,v)\operatorname{ow}(u,v)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
\operatorname{ow}(u,v)>\frac{1}{\delta}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{ow}(u,v).

Now let
D_{\kappa}
denote the number of onions of width
\kappa
. Note that our result above implies that
\sum_{\kappa\geq 1/\delta}D_{\kappa}\leq 8\delta^{2}T
.
Now we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
\operatorname{ow}(u,v)>\frac{1}{\delta}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{ow}(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\kappa=\frac{1}{\delta}}^{\sqrt{T}}D_{\kappa}\kappa

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{\kappa=\frac{1}{\delta}}^{\sqrt{T}}D_{\kappa}}\sqrt{\sum_{\kappa=\frac{1}{\delta}}^{\sqrt{T}}D_{\kappa}\kappa^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{8\delta^{2}T\cdot 8T}

\displaystyle\leq 8\delta T,

where we used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Further, we used the identity
\sum_{\kappa=1}^{\sqrt{T}}D_{\kappa}\binom{\kappa}{2}=2T
, where the factor of 2 stems from the fact that each four-cycle is part of two onions. Finally, we used that
\kappa^{2}\leq 4\binom{\kappa}{2}
. This bounds the second summand above.

Next, we bound the first summand and get that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
\operatorname{ow}(u,v)>\frac{1}{\delta}\\
h(u,v)\geq 2\end{subarray}}h(u,v)\operatorname{ow}(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
\operatorname{ow}(u,v)>\frac{1}{\delta}\\
h(u,v)\geq 2\end{subarray}}h(u,v)^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
\operatorname{ow}(u,v)>\frac{1}{\delta}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{ow}(u,v)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
\operatorname{ow}(u,v)>\frac{1}{\delta}\end{subarray}}4\binom{h(u,v)}{2}\right)\cdot 8T}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{8\cdot 328\delta^{2}T\cdot 8T}

\displaystyle\leq 145\delta T,

where we again used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the first step. In the second step we again used that
h(u,v)^{2}\leq 4\binom{h(u,v)}{2}
and that
\sum_{u,v}\operatorname{ow}(u,v)^{2}\leq\sum_{u,v}4\binom{\operatorname{ow}(u,v)}{2}\leq 8T
. Then in the final step we applied
Lemma

5.7
since

counts pairs of wedges from

to

which both contain a
\sqrt{T}/(4\delta^{2})
-heavy edge, and thus form a four-cycle containing two
\sqrt{T}/(4\delta^{2})
-heavy edges.
∎

Next, we argue the algorithm’s space bound.

S_{1,\kappa}

Counting

In this section, we prove the main result of our paper, which we restate from the introduction.

See
1.1

Before we describe our algorithm, we give a short overview of how our approach from
Section

5
has to be adapted to extend our algorithm to the approximate counting setting.

Recall that in
Section

5.3
we described configurations
\sigma_{A}
that we assigned to four-cycles
A
. Then the crucial part of our analysis was to show that the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}=\{\sigma_{A}\mid A\in\mathcal{T},\;\sigma_{A}\text{ is light}\}

satisfies
(1-\varepsilon)T\leq|\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}|\leq T
. The most difficult part was to show that
\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
contains configurations for at least
(1-\varepsilon)T
four-cycles (see
Proposition

5.6
), i.e., to satisfy the first inequality, whereas the second inequality was trivial.
Then, to show that our algorithm detects a four-cycle, we only needed to bound the probability that at least one configuration from
\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
is realized.
While the variance of the number of sampled four-cycles is generally high, we proved that the variance of the number of realized configurations from
\mathcal{C}_{\texttt{Detect}}
is small.

Thus, the natural approach for extending our detection algorithm to counting four-cycles is to estimate
|\mathcal{C}_{\texttt{Detect}}|
.
Indeed, we would immediately get a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-approximate counting algorithm (after adapting our analysis from
Section

5
), if we could decide the following question during the algorithm:

Given a realized configuration
c
, is
c\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
?

The main problem here is that to check whether
c\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
, we have to verify that the heaviness thresholds that we introduced in
Section

5.3
are satisfied for all labeled substructures
(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c
. However, this is not possible, as we can show that there exist graphs for which verifying
t(v)\leq\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}
for some labeled node
(v,\kappa,\ell)\in c
requires space
\omega(m/\sqrt{T})
. Thus, we have to abandon the idea of checking whether
c\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
.

To fix this, we refine our heaviness notion.
Concretely, now we define a new heaviness notion
t(z,\kappa,\ell)
that replaces the old heaviness notion
t(z)
in the definition of heavy and light substructures. This allows us to check whether substructures are heavy (i.e., whether
t(z,\kappa,\ell)\leq\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
) using oracles that only use space
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
.
Now we can define a new set of configurations
\mathcal{C}
for which we will be able to answer the following question using one additional stream pass:

Given a realized configuration
c
, is
c\in\mathcal{C}
?

Similar to
\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
, the new set
\mathcal{C}
still only contains light configurations, but now it satisfies the relaxed property
(1-\varepsilon)T\leq|\mathcal{C}|\leq(1+\varepsilon)T
.
The main obstacle will be to show that our new heaviness notion
t(z,\kappa,\ell)
satisfies all properties that we need to adopt our variance proof from
Section

5.5
. In particular, one of the technical issues we encounter is that a single four-cycle
A
might now have multiple configurations
c\in\mathcal{C}
(this is different from
\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
, where each four-cycle
A
had at most one configuration
\sigma_{A}\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
), and thus showing that
|\mathcal{C}|\leq(1+\varepsilon)T
will be non-trivial.

Formally, we define the

as follows, where

and

are the random shifts used in the algorithm and the ordering is picked such that we avoid circular definitions:

•

\displaystyle t(z,\kappa,\ell)=|\{c\in\mathfrak{C}\mid(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c,\;t(w,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})\leq s_{1}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})}\text{ for all wedges }(w,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})\in c\}|.

(2) The reason why we were able to set

(3) In the definition of

(Non-)Monotonicity.

Next, we discuss a drawback of our refined heaviness notion. For this, recall that in
Section

5.3
we defined assigned configurations
\sigma_{A}=(A,\kappa_{A},x_{A},y_{A})
. We used these assigned configurations to ensure that each four-cycle has at most one assigned configuration and to argue that
|\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}|\leq T
. There, we intuitively chose
\kappa_{A}
as the smallest
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
over all light configurations of
A
. Thus, if we wanted to build an oracle for checking whether
c=\sigma_{A}
for some configuration
c
of
A
, this suggests the following strategy: If
c=(A,\kappa,x,y)
then check if
(A,\kappa/2,x,y)
is light; if that is the case, then report that
c\neq\sigma_{A}
, otherwise report that
c=\sigma_{A}
. Note that for this approach, it is crucial that if we fix
A
,
x
and

, then whether a configuration is light is a monotone function with respect to the parameter
\kappa
. Note that the same holds for all substructures.

Now the main disadvantage of the refined heaviness definition is that the property of a substructure being heavy is not monotone in
\kappa
anymore:
Let
(v,\kappa,\ell)
be a labeled node. The property of
(v,\kappa,\ell)
being heavy is monotone in
\kappa
for the true heaviness since the node heaviness

is constant for different
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
, while the heaviness threshold
\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}
changes monotonically.
However, for the refined heaviness

we lose this nice property, as now not only the thresholds
\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}
but also the heaviness
t(v,\kappa,\ell)
itself can vary for different
\kappa
. In particular, the definition of
t(v,\kappa,\ell)
might include some configurations only for specific values of
\kappa
. An example is presented in
Figure

.

6.3
LLM Configurations

To deal with the drawback that the heaviness of substructures and configurations is not monotone with respect to our refined heaviness, we now introduce the notion of LLM configurations. This will allow us to carefully select the configurations that we count in
\mathcal{C}
and in
Lemma

6.9
.

Before we give our definition, we need some additional notation.
Let
A
be any four-cycle with node set
\{x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\}
.
Now, for any
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
, we identify the configuration
(A,\sqrt{T}/\kappa,x,y)
with the configuration
(A,\kappa,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. This is useful for the following reasons. Note that
(A,\sqrt{T}/\kappa,x,y)
is not a configuration according to
Definition

5.2
as
\sqrt{T}/\kappa\notin\mathcal{K}
since
\sqrt{T}/\kappa<T^{1/4}
. However, if we extend the definitions of the probabilities
p_{1,{\kappa^{\prime}}}
and
p_{2,{\kappa^{\prime}}}
to arbitrary values
\kappa^{\prime}
, we observe that

\displaystyle p_{1,\kappa}=p_{2,{\sqrt{T}/\kappa}}\quad\text{ and }\quad p_{2,\kappa}=p_{1,{\sqrt{T}/\kappa}}.

Thus a configuration
(A,\kappa,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
which samples
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
with probabilities
p_{1,\kappa}
and
x
and
y
with probability
p_{2,\kappa}
corresponds exactly to sampling
x
and
y
with
p_{1,{\sqrt{T}/\kappa}}
and
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
with
p_{2,{\sqrt{T}/\kappa}}
. Furthermore,
x
and
y
are adjacent in
A
if and only if
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
are adjacent in
A
. This shows that this is a natural extension of configurations to values
\sqrt{T}/\kappa\notin\mathcal{K}
.

Now recall that the detection assignment
\sigma_{A}
assigns each light four-cycle a light configuration with minimal
\kappa
. As we already discussed above for the non-monotonicity of the refined heaviness, we cannot follow this approach any longer. It is possible that some four-cycle
A
has configurations for which
(A,\kappa,x,y)
and
(A,\kappa^{\prime},x,y)
with
\kappa\neq\kappa^{\prime}
are both light. The issue now is that we cannot check whether
(A,\kappa^{\prime},x,y)
is light for all
\kappa^{\prime}<\kappa
because if
\kappa^{\prime}\ll\kappa
then the oracle for checking the heaviness threshold
\theta_{(x,\kappa^{\prime},\ell)}
will have a too large space usage and will break our desired space bound. Thus, if we know that
(A,\kappa,x,y)
is light, we can only check if
(A,\kappa^{\prime},x,y)
is light for
\kappa^{\prime}
-values that are within a factor of
\widetilde{O}(1)
of
\kappa
to guarantee that our oracles do not use too much space. This is the intuition for our next definition of locally minimal configurations, where we need to cover several corner cases.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
c=(A,\kappa,x,y)
be a light configuration. Then:

•

If
\kappa\geq T^{1/4}/\delta^{2}
,
c
is
locally minimal
if
(A,\kappa^{\prime},x,y)
is heavy for all
\kappa^{\prime}\in\{\delta^{2}\kappa,\dots,\kappa/2\}
.

•

If
\kappa=T^{1/4}
, then
c
is
locally minimal
if
(x,y)
is the lexicographically smallest node pair in the four-cycle.

•

If
T^{1/4}<\kappa<T^{1/4}/\delta^{2}
then we say that
c
is
locally minimal
if
(A,\kappa^{\prime},x,y)
is heavy for all
\kappa^{\prime}\in\{\max\{\delta^{2}\kappa,2\sqrt{T}/\kappa\},\dots,\kappa/2\}
and
either

(A,\sqrt{T}/\kappa,x,y):=(A,\kappa,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is heavy as well
or

(A,\sqrt{T}/\kappa,x,y)
is light and
(x,y)
is lexicographically smaller than
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.

Note that in the final case of the definition above, we used our extended definition of configurations, where we allow the value
\kappa
in the configuration to be less than
T^{1/4}
.

Next, we can define LLM configurations, which is the property that four-cycles must satisfy such that they are counted by
Algorithm

2
.

Definition 6.4
.

A configuration is
LLM
if it is light, locally minimal and valid. We let
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of all LLM configurations.

Note that whether a configuration is LLM is not a deterministic property, since the definition depends on our notion of refined heaviness, which in turn uses the random shifts from the algorithm.

Next, we state a useful observation about light and LLM configurations.

Observation 6.5
.

If a four-cycle has a light configuration, then it also has an LLM configuration

Note that this observation holds because for each light four-cycle
A
, we can pick the light configuration
(A,\kappa,x,y)
with the smallest
\kappa
-value (exactly as when we defined
\sigma_{A}
above). However, now there could also be more LLM configurations for
A
, i.e., light configurations with non-minimal
\kappa
-values. Fortunately, we show in
Proposition

6.7
that this cannot happen for too many four-cycles.

Oracle.

Given the definitions of LLM configurations and the set
\mathcal{C}
, we now consider the guarantees that

Algorithm

2
requires for the oracle
\texttt{IsLLM}(\cdot)
, which checks whether
c\in\mathcal{C}
for any given configuration
c
.
The proposition below summarizes the oracle’s guarantees, and we prove it in
Section

7
.

Proposition 6.6
.

There exists an oracle
\texttt{IsLLM}(\cdot)
for checking whether a configuration is LLM that, with probability at least
2/3
,
answers all
\widetilde{O}(1)
queries by
Algorithm

2
correctly and uses
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
space.

6.4
Bounding the Number of LLM Configurations

Next, we bound the number of LLM configurations, which will be crucial for the analysis of our estimator below. In the following proposition we show that
||\mathcal{C}|-T|\leq 1049\delta\log(T)^{3}T
.

Proposition 6.7
.

The number of LLM configurations is bounded by

(1-1049\delta\log(T))T\leq|\mathcal{C}|\leq(1+3\delta\log(T)^{3})T
.

Now we show that most four-cycles admit an LLM configuration, which proves the first inequality in
Proposition

6.7
.

Lemma 6.8
.

At most
1049\delta\log(T)T
four-cycles do not admit an LLM configuration.

Proof.

Recall that by
Proposition

5.6
there are at most
1049\delta\log(T)
four-cycles that have no configuration in
\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
.
Since any configuration in
\mathcal{C}_{\text{detect}}
is light with respect to the true heaviness
t(z)
, this also bounds the number of light four-cycles. Now observe that these configurations are also light with respect to the refined heaviness since
t(z,\kappa,\ell)\leq t(z)
for all substructures
(z,\kappa,\ell)
by
6.1
.
Thus, if a four-cycle admits a light configuration, it also admits an LLM configuration. This shows that at most
1049\log(T)\delta T
four-cycles do not admit an LLM configuration.
∎

Next, to prove the second inequality in
Proposition

6.7
, recall that some four-cycles might have multiple LLM configurations in
\mathcal{C}
because being heavy is not a monotone property with respect to the refined heaviness. Thus, we say that a four-cycle
A
is
non-monotone
if there is a LLM configuration
(A,\kappa,x,y)
and a light configuration
(A,\kappa^{\prime},x,y)
with
\kappa^{\prime}<\kappa
(and with equal
x,y
).

Now we state an intermediate result showing that there are few non-monotone four-cycles.

Lemma 6.9
.

There exist at most
3\delta\log(T)^{3}T
non-monotone four-cycles.

Proof.

We prove the lemma in three steps:
(1) We show that any non-monotone four-cycle admits a triple of labeled substructures that induce its non-monotonicity.
(2) We show that each triple
Z
induces at most
\delta\theta_{Z}
non-monotone four-cycles for some
\theta_{Z}
depending on the triple.
(3) We show that there are at most
\log(T)^{2}T/\theta_{Z}
triples for each
\theta_{Z}
.

Step 1:
Any non-monotone four-cycle admits three configurations
c_{1}=(A,\kappa_{1},x,y)
,
c_{2}=(A,\kappa_{2},x,y)
,
c_{3}=(A,\kappa_{3},x,y)
where
\kappa_{1}\leq\delta\kappa_{2}\leq\delta\kappa_{3}
and
c_{1},c_{3}
are light and
c_{2}
is heavy.
Let
(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)\in c_{2}
be the labeled substructure which is heavy in
c_{2}
.
We know

\displaystyle{t}(z,\kappa_{1},\ell)<\theta_{(z,\kappa_{1},\ell)},\quad{t}(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)>\theta_{(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)},\quad{t}(z,\kappa_{3},\ell)<\theta_{(z,\kappa_{3},\ell)}

and say the triple
((z,\kappa_{1},\ell),(z,\kappa_{2},\ell),(z,\kappa_{3},\ell))

induces
the non-monotonicity of four-cycle
A
.
We know
z
is either a node or a
R_{2a}
-
R_{2b}
-edge as all other substructures are heavy or light based on true heaviness
t(z)
, which is independent of
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3}
.

Step 2:
Now observe that a triple
((z,\kappa_{1},\ell),(z,\kappa_{2},\ell),(z,\kappa_{3},\ell))
induces at most
\min\{\theta_{(z,\kappa_{1},\ell)},\theta_{(z,\kappa_{3},\ell)}\}
non-monotone four-cycles:
By
6.2
, at most
t(z,\kappa_{1},\ell)
(resp.
t(z,\kappa_{3},\ell)
) light configurations contain
(z,\kappa_{1},\ell)
(resp.
(z,\kappa_{3},\ell)
). Thus the triple can induce the non-monotonicity of at most
\min\{t(z,\kappa_{1},\ell),t(z,\kappa_{3},\ell)\}\leq\min\{\theta_{(z,\kappa_{1},\ell)},\theta_{(z,\kappa_{3},\ell)}\}
four-cycles.

Crucially, we have
\min\{\theta_{(z,\kappa_{1},\ell)},\theta_{(z,\kappa_{3},\ell)}\}\leq\delta\theta_{(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)}
as
z
is either a node or a
R_{2a}
-
R_{2b}
-edge:
If
z
is a node and
\ell(v)\in\{S_{1},R_{1a},R_{1b}\}
, we have

\theta_{(z,\kappa_{1},\ell)}=\kappa_{1}\sqrt{T}/\delta^{1.5}\leq\delta\kappa_{2}\sqrt{T}/\delta^{1.5}=\delta\theta_{(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)}.

If
z
is a node and
\ell(v)\in\{S_{1},R_{1a},R_{1b}\}
, we have

\theta_{(z,\kappa_{3},\ell)}=T/(\delta^{1.5}\kappa_{3})\leq\delta T/(\delta^{1.5}\kappa_{2})=\delta\theta_{(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)},

and if
z
is an edge, we have

\theta_{(z,\kappa_{3},\ell)}=T/(\delta^{2}\kappa_{3}^{2})\leq\delta^{2}T/(\delta^{2}\kappa_{2}^{2})\leq\delta\theta_{(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)}.

Step 3:
Now we count the number of triples of substructures
((z,\kappa_{1},\ell),(z,\kappa_{2},\ell),(z,\kappa_{3},\ell))
such that
(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)
is heavy. Fix values
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3}\in\mathcal{K}
and fix some heaviness threshold
\theta\in\{\kappa_{2}\sqrt{T}/\delta^{1.5},T/(\delta^{1.5}\kappa_{2}),T/(\delta^{2}\kappa_{2}^{2})\}
. Observe that there are at most
4T/\theta
heavy substructures
(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)
with
\theta_{(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)}=\theta
.
Each induces at most
\delta\theta_{(z,\kappa_{2},\ell)}
non-monotone four-cycles and thus there are at most
\delta T
non-monotone four-cycles for this choice of
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3},\theta
. As there are at most
3\log(T)^{3}
choices for these parameters, this proves the lemma.
∎

Using
Lemma

6.9
we can now prove the second inequality of
Proposition

6.7
.

Lemma 6.10
.

At most
3\delta(\log^{3}T)T
four-cycles have more than one LLM configuration.

Proof.

We show that any four-cycle with more than one LLM configuration must also be non-monotone. Then the lemma immediately follows from
Lemma

6.9
.

In the proof, we write
\mathcal{C}_{\text{adj}}\subset\mathcal{C}
for the set of all LLM configurations
(A,\kappa,x,y)
in which
x
and
y
are adjacent and
\mathcal{C}_{\text{opp}}
for the set of all LLM configurations in which
x
and
y
are opposite nodes.

Let
A
be a four-cycle with two different LLM configurations
c=(A,\kappa,x,y)
and
c^{\prime}=(A,\kappa^{\prime},x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
with
\kappa\geq\kappa^{\prime}
.
Note that here we make no assumptions on
x
,
y
,
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
, i.e., it could be that
x=x^{\prime}
and
y=y^{\prime}
, but it could also be that
x=y^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}
.
We perform a case distinction based on the values of
\kappa
and
\kappa^{\prime}
and whether
c
and
c^{\prime}
are in
\mathcal{C}_{\text{adj}}
or
\mathcal{C}_{\text{opp}}
:

•

Suppose
\kappa=\kappa^{\prime}=T^{1/4}
. Then only one of
c
or
c^{\prime}
is LLM by definition.

•

Suppose
\kappa>\kappa^{\prime}=T^{1/4}
. Note that
c^{\prime}=(A,T^{1/4},x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
being light implies that
c^{\prime\prime}=(A,T^{1/4},x,y)
is light, as corresponding substructures in
c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}
have equal heaviness thresholds for
\kappa=T^{1/4}
.
Thus
A
is non-monotone due to
c
being LLM and
c^{\prime\prime}
being light.

•

Suppose
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}>T^{1/4}
. Now consider the following cases:

–

Suppose both
c,c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{adj}}
. Then both
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
are
\sqrt{T}/\delta^{2}
-heavy-edges as otherwise
c
or
c^{\prime}
are not valid and thus not locally minimal. Thus, we have

and

since a light four-cycle can only have one

-heavy edge. As

and
c^{\prime}
are LLM configurations with the same node pair

,
A
is non-monotone.

–

Suppose
c\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{opp}}
and
c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{adj}}
.
This cannot happen because
c^{\prime}
contains a
\sqrt{T}/\delta^{2}
-heavy edge
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
(since

), which would imply that

is not light.

–

Suppose
c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{opp}}
and
c\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{adj}}
. This cannot happen for the same reason as above (but we note that it is a different case since we assume that
\kappa\geq\kappa^{\prime}
).

–

Suppose
c,c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{opp}}
. If
x=x^{\prime}
and

, then this implies that
A
is non-monotone. Otherwise,

and we have
(A,\kappa^{\prime},x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=(A,\sqrt{T}/\kappa^{\prime},x,y)
.
Now if
\sqrt{T}/\kappa^{\prime}<\delta^{2}\kappa
, then
A
is non-monotone.
Otherwise, observe that we get the series of inequalities
\delta^{2}\kappa\leq\sqrt{T}/\kappa^{\prime}<T^{1/4}\leq\kappa
and we also have that
\sqrt{T}/\kappa\leq\sqrt{T}/\kappa^{\prime}
since we assume that
\kappa\geq\kappa^{\prime}
. Thus
\max\{\delta^{2}\kappa,\sqrt{T}/\kappa\}\leq\sqrt{T}/\kappa^{\prime}

implying that

is locally minimal only if
c^{\prime}
is heavy or not locally minimal.
Thus, only one of
c
and
c^{\prime}
can be LLM, contradicting the assumption that both of them are LLM.

∎

.

6.5
Variance Analysis and Proof of
Theorem

1.1

Now we analyze the variance of our estimator similar to
Section

5.5
, but this time using our notion of refined heaviness. Throughout the section, we condition on the fact that all oracle calls to the
\texttt{IsLLM}(\cdot)
procedure are answered correctly, which happens with probability at least
2/3
by
6.6
.

First, note that similar to
5.5
, we obtain that the sampling probabilities
p_{1,\kappa}
and
p_{2,\kappa}
, which we use for
Algorithm

2
, satisfy the following inequality.

Observation 6.11
.

For any labeled substructure
(z,\kappa,\ell)
, it holds that

\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}<\delta^{2}Tp_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}/c_{1}
.

Next, let
X_{c}
be the binary random variable indicating whether configuration
c\in\mathcal{C}
was realized.
Let
X=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}X_{c}
and as in the algorithm set
p=\frac{c^{2}}{\delta^{7}\sqrt{T}}
.

Now, as before in
Section

5.5
, we get that

\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=|\mathcal{C}|p^{2},

and

\operatorname{Var}\left[X\right]\leq\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\operatorname{Var}\left[X_{c}\right]+\sum_{c,d\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{Cov}\left[X_{c},X_{d}\right].

Further, we again have

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\operatorname{Var}\left[X_{c}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{20}\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]^{2},

since
\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]>20/\delta^{2}
.

Next, we bound the covariances analogously to
Section

5.5
, but using refined heaviness instead of true heaviness:

\displaystyle\sum_{c,d\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{Cov}\left[X_{c},X_{d}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}p^{2}\sum_{(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in\mathcal{C}\\
(z,\kappa,\ell)=c\cap d\end{subarray}}p^{2}/p_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}p^{2}\sum_{(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c}t(z,\kappa,\ell)p^{2}/p_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}p^{2}\sum_{(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c}s_{1}^{2}s_{2}s_{3}\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}p^{2}/p_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{C}|p^{4}\cdot 14s_{1}^{2}s_{2}s_{3}T/c

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{20}|\mathcal{C}|^{2}p^{4},

where the first step is analogous to
Section

5.5
. The second step follows from
6.2
. The third step follows from the fact that all

Heaviness Oracles.

In the rest of this section, we build the heaviness oracles which are used by
Algorithm

3
.
Given any labeled substructure
(z,\kappa,\ell)\in c
as a query, the oracle should decide whether
t(z,\kappa,\ell)>s\theta_{(z,\kappa,\ell)}
, where
s\in\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}
is the appropriate shift from
Section

6.2
. We denote the oracle’s answer, which is either
light
or

\operatorname{oracle}(z,\kappa,\ell)

All of our heaviness oracles work by sampling a small subgraph and estimating
t(z,\kappa,\ell)
by counting the number of configurations in the sample that count towards
t(z,\kappa,\ell)
. We do not sample a new subgraph for each oracle query; rather, we generate one sample to answer all oracle calls. Storing the samples allows us to compute the oracle values after all stream passes finish, which has the advantage that our oracle’s answers are consistent: The same query is answered identically each time.

To obtain the sample, we use two different strategies:
For edges
(e,\kappa,\ell)
with
p_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}=p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa}
we use edge-sampling based oracles.
For all other substructures we use node-sampling based oracles which sample nodes similarly to the main algorithm.

Next, recall that by definition of the refined heaviness for nodes, a configuration may or may not count towards the node heaviness depending on the heaviness of its edges and wedges. Thus, naturally, our node heaviness oracles will depend on the edge and wedge heaviness oracles and call them internally to ensure the node heaviness is estimated correctly.

We use the following standard variants of the Chernoff and Chebyshev bounds for our analysis.

Lemma 7.2

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[X\leq M_{L}/(1+\delta)]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{12B}\delta^{2}M_{L}\right).

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{V}^{2}}{c_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[q(v)\right]^{2}+10\varepsilon_{V}^{2}s_{3}\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}^{2}(p^{\prime}_{1,\kappa})^{2}(p^{\prime}_{2,\kappa})^{4}/c_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{V}^{2}}{c_{2}}M_{H}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_{V}^{2}}{c_{2}}M_{H}^{2}=\frac{\varepsilon_{V}^{2}}{100L}M_{H}^{2}

for large enough
c_{2}>200L
. By applying
Lemma

7.3
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[q(v)>p_{1,\kappa}^{\prime}(p_{2,\kappa}^{\prime})^{2}s_{3}\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[q(v)>\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon_{V}}M_{H}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon_{V}^{2}}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left[q(v)\right]}{M_{H}^{2}}\leq\frac{4}{100L}.

Taking a union bound over all up to
L
oracle calls shows that all oracle answers are correct with probability at least
\frac{96}{100}
, which proves the lemma.
∎

The accuracy guarantee for all other cases
\ell(v)\neq S_{1}
follows completely analogous to
Lemma

7.5
.
We get the following intermediate result by deriving a lemma similar to
Lemma

7.5
for all four possible labels
\ell(v)
and then using that all of these four lemmas are satisfied simultaneously with probability at least
(\frac{96}{100})^{4}>\frac{84}{100}
.

Lemma 7.6
.

With probability
84/100
the node oracle correctly classifies all nodes
(v,\kappa,\ell)
with
t(v,\kappa,\ell)\notin[(1-\varepsilon_{V})s_{3}\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)},(1+\varepsilon_{V})s_{3}\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}]
.

Shifting Thresholds.

The analysis above shows that the node oracle is accurate for nodes for
which the heaviness is not too close to the heaviness threshold.
Now we call a node
(v,\kappa,\ell)

bad
if
t(v,\kappa,\ell)\in[(1-\varepsilon_{V})s_{3}\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)},(1+\varepsilon_{V})s_{3}\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}]
and the oracle is called on
(v,\kappa,\ell)
.
Next, we show that by randomly selecting the shift
s_{3}
, with probability
99/100
, the oracle is not called on any bad nodes.

Recall that
Algorithm

2
randomly selects one of approximately
100L
different values for
s_{3}
to shift the heaviness thresholds by a small multiplicative factor. Here, recall that
L
was the upper bound on the number of queries we obtain from
Equation

6
and that
s_{3}\in\mathcal{S}_{3}:=\{(1+\frac{1}{200L})^{i}\mid 0\leq i<\log_{1+\frac{1}{200L}}(2)\}
.
We set the oracle accuracy
\varepsilon_{V}=1/(600L)
such that

(1+\varepsilon_{V})\left(1+\frac{1}{200L}\right)^{i}<(1-\varepsilon_{V})\left(1+\frac{1}{200L}\right)^{i+1}

implying that all the possible oracle uncertainty intervals
[(1-\varepsilon_{V})s\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)},(1+\varepsilon_{V})s\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}]
for
s\in\mathcal{S}_{3}
are disjoint.
As the node heaviness
t(v,\kappa,\ell)
is independent of
s_{3}
, any node can be bad for at most one shift.
Thus, at most
L
shifts admit a bad node and the probability of picking a shift admitting a bad node is at most

\frac{L}{|\mathcal{S}_{3}|}\leq\frac{L}{100L}\leq\frac{1}{100},

where we used
|\mathcal{S}|\geq\log_{1+\frac{1}{200L}}(2)\geq\ln(2)\cdot 200L\geq 100L
.

Thus, the node oracle correctly answers all queries with probability at least
\frac{83}{100}
by
Lemma

7.6
.

Space.

It remains to show the space complexity of the oracle.

As before, all node sets are stored implicitly using hash functions.
Note that
q^{\prime}:=p_{1,\kappa}^{\prime}p_{2,\kappa}^{\prime}=\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
and
q_{\delta^{2}}:=p_{1,\kappa}^{\prime}p_{2,\delta^{2}\kappa}=\widetilde{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
are independent of
\kappa
.
Each edge in the first pass is collected by the oracle with probability at most
\max\{q^{\prime},q_{\delta^{2}}\}
.
In the second pass, the oracle realizes at most
100T\log(T)q^{\prime}q_{\delta^{2}}\in\widetilde{O}(1)
configurations with probability
99/100
.
Thus, a Markov bound implies that the space usage of the node oracle is
\widetilde{O}(m\max\{q^{\prime},q_{\delta^{2}}\})=\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
with probability at least
1/100
.

Now we can prove
Lemma

7.4
.

Proof of
Lemma

7.4
.

With probability
99/100
, the node oracle is called at most
L
times. With probability
99/100
none of the
L
calls is for a bad node, i.e., a node
(v,\kappa,\ell)
with
t(v,\kappa,\ell)\in[(1-\varepsilon_{V})s_{3}\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)},(1+\varepsilon_{V})s_{3}\theta_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}]
. Thus, by
Lemma

7.6
, all oracle calls are correctly answered with probability
84/100
.

The statement follows from a union bound and the above space considerations.
∎

7.2
Edge Oracles

We now design edge oracles which, when queried on a labeled node
(e,\kappa,\ell)
during
Algorithm

3
or inside the node oracle, output

\operatorname{oracle}(e,\kappa,\ell)=\textsf{heavy}
if and only if
t(e,\kappa,\ell)>s_{2}\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}

with a large constant probability.

To implement the oracle, recall the definition of refined heaviness for edges:
If
p_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}=p_{2,\kappa}^{2}
, then we defined

\displaystyle t(e,\kappa,\ell)=|\{c\in\mathfrak{C}\mid(e,\kappa,\ell)\in c,\;t(y,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})\leq s_{1}\theta_{(y,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})}\text{ for all wedges }(y,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})\in c\}|,

and otherwise we set
t(e,\kappa,\ell)=t(e)
.

We next build two different oracles for these two different cases. In the case of
t(e,\kappa,\ell)=t(e)
, we can build a simple oracle that is based on edge sampling. Otherwise, we again resort to node sampling to estimate
t(e,\kappa,\ell)
.

To understand why we need two different types of edge oracles, let us consider edges
(e,\kappa,\ell)
with
t(e,\kappa,\ell)=t(e)
. Here, we use an edge-sampling based oracles to estimate the true heaviness
t(e)
. Concretely, if
p_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}=p_{1,\kappa}^{2}
, we sample a set of edges
Q
which contains each edge with probability
p_{2,\kappa}^{2}\log(n)
. Then in the next stream pass, we collect all edges with one endpoint incident to
e
and the other endpoint incident to a sampled edge in
Q
(see below for the details). This requires
\widetilde{O}(L^{\prime}mp_{2}^{2})=\widetilde{O}(mp_{2}^{2})
space. As all four-cycles containing
e
are sampled independently with this procedure, we get an accurate oracle that is easy to analyze via Chernoff bounds.
However, this edge sampling approach breaks for edges
(e,\kappa,\ell)
with
p(e,\kappa,\ell)=p_{2,\kappa}^{2}
as then we need to sample the edges in
Q
with probability
p_{1,\kappa}^{2}
, giving us a space usage
\widetilde{O}(mp_{1,\kappa}^{2})
which exceeds our budget of
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
for
\kappa\gg T^{1/4}
.
Thus, we instead use a node-sampling based oracle for this case, which works similarly to the node heaviness oracle.

Additionally, we need to define the oracle
\operatorname{oracle}_{\text{valid}}(e)
for an edge
e
which is used in
Algorithm

3
to check whether a configuration containing
e
is valid.
Note that this oracle only checks whether
t(e)\leq\sqrt{T}/\delta^{2}
and that
\sqrt{T}/\delta^{2}
is also the threshold for edges
(e^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})
with
p_{(e^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})}=p_{1,\kappa^{\prime}}p_{2,\kappa^{\prime}}
.
Thus, we can reuse the edge-sampling based oracle for this purpose.

As before, we observe that the number
L^{\prime}
of calls to the edge heaviness oracle is
L^{\prime}=\widetilde{O}(1)
:
The edge oracle is called at most
L
times during
Algorithm

3
, where
L=\widetilde{O}(1)
is as in
Equation

6
, and since
the edge oracle is called at most four times for each configuration realized by the node oracle.

Next, we prove the following guarantee for our edge heaviness oracle.

Lemma 7.7
.

Assuming access to perfect wedge heaviness oracles, there is an edge heaviness oracle which correctly answers all queries by
Algorithm

3
and the node oracles with probability
9/10
. The oracle requires
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
space.

Edge-Sampling Based Edge Oracles.

We first consider the case of edges
(e,\kappa,\ell)
with
p(e,\kappa,\ell)=p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa}
.
Let
Z
be the set of such labeled edges for which the oracle is called during
Algorithms

2
and
3
.
Note that
|Z|\leq L^{\prime}\in\widetilde{O}(1)
as we argued above.
Let
\varepsilon_{E}=\frac{1}{600L^{\prime}}
be the edge oracle accuracy.

The oracle works as follows. During the first stream pass, we sample an edge set
Q^{\text{edge}}
by sampling each edge with probability
p^{\prime}=\log(n)p_{1}p_{2}/\varepsilon_{E}^{2}
.
In the second stream pass, we do nothing, and in the third stream pass, we collect all edges connecting edges in
Q^{\text{edge}}
to edges in
Z
, i.e., all edges in
E[Q^{\text{edge}},Z]
.

Note that the reason why we only collect the edges of the induced subgraph in third stream pass is that we only know
Z
after the second stream pass finished, since some of the configurations yielding the set
Z
are only realized during Pass 2 of
Algorithm

2
and Pass 2 of the node oracle.

For each edge
e\in Z
, we then count the number
q(e)
of four-cycles formed using
e
and one disjoint edge in the sample
Q^{\text{edge}}
.
The heaviness oracle is then defined as

\operatorname{oracle}(e,\kappa,\ell)=\begin{cases}\textsf{light}&\text{ if }q(e)\leq p^{\prime}s_{2}\theta_{e},\\
\textsf{heavy}&\text{ if }q(e)>p^{\prime}s_{2}\theta_{e},\end{cases}

where
\theta_{e}=\frac{\sqrt{T}}{\delta^{2}}
is the heaviness threshold for
e
.

The oracle for edges
(e,\kappa,\ell)
with
p(e,\kappa,\ell)=p_{1,\kappa}^{2}
works analogously by sampling with
p_{2}^{2}\log(n)/\varepsilon_{E}^{2}
instead of
p^{\prime}
.
We now obtain the following guarantee.

Lemma 7.8
.

With high probability the edge oracle correctly classifies all edges
(e,\kappa,\ell)
with
p(e,\kappa,\ell)\in\{p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa},p_{1,\kappa}^{2}\}
and
t(e)\notin[(1-\varepsilon_{E})s_{2}\theta_{e},(1+\varepsilon_{E})s_{2}\theta_{w}]
.
The oracle’s space usage is
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
.

Proof.

We prove the statement for
p(e,\kappa,\ell)=p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa}
. The other case follows analogously.

Observe that
\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]=t(e)p^{\prime}
.
Let
Y_{f}\in\{0,1,2\}
,
f\in Q^{\text{edge}}
, be the random variable indicating the number of four-cycles detected by the oracle using
e
and
f
. Note that
Y_{f}
and
Y_{f^{\prime}}
are independent for any
f\neq f^{\prime}
.
We have
q(e)=\sum_{f}Y_{f}
and get a high accuracy oracle using Chernoff bounds and a case distinction.

Case 1:
t(e)>(1+\varepsilon_{E})s_{2}\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}
.
Then by
Lemma

7.3
(with
B=2
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[q(e)<p^{\prime}\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[q(e)\leq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon_{E}}\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{24}s_{2}\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}p^{\prime}\right)\leq n^{-2},

since
p^{\prime}\geq p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa}\log(n)/\varepsilon_{W}^{2}\geq 2\cdot 12\log(n)c_{1}/(s_{2}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}\varepsilon_{W}^{2})
for large enough
c_{1}
.

Case 2:
t(e)<(1-\varepsilon_{E})s_{2}\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}
.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[q(e)>p^{\prime}\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[q(e)\geq\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon_{E})}\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{24}s_{2}\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}p^{\prime}\right)\leq n^{-2}.

Finally, observe that the expected space required of the oracle is
O(|Z||Q^{edge}|)=O(|Z|mp^{\prime})=\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
.
∎

Node-Sampling Based Edge Oracle.

Next, we present our edge heaviness oracle for edges
(e,\kappa,\ell)
with
p_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}=p_{2,\kappa}^{2}
, for which we want to estimate the refined heaviness
t(e,\kappa,\ell)
. It is based on node sampling and detecting four-cycles containing
e
by sampling nodes and collecting edges (similar to the node oracle in
Section

7.1
).

The oracle receives the node sets
R_{2a,\kappa},R_{2b,\kappa}
and
R_{2a,\kappa}^{\prime},R_{2b,\kappa}^{\prime}
for all
\kappa
as input. Here,
R_{2a,\kappa}^{\prime},R_{2b,\kappa}^{\prime}
are node sets sampled by the node oracle.
After the second stream pass, it may be queried for any edge
(e,\kappa^{\prime},\ell)
such that
(e,\kappa,\ell)
in
E[R_{2a,\kappa},R_{2b,\kappa}]\cup E[R_{2a,\kappa}^{\prime},R_{2b,\kappa}^{\prime}]
any pair
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}
with
\delta^{2}\kappa^{\prime}\leq\kappa\leq\kappa^{\prime}
.
It is implemented as follows.

Before the first stream pass, the oracle samples each node with probability
p^{\prime}_{1,\kappa}=c_{3}p_{1,\kappa}/\varepsilon_{E}^{2}
, where
c_{3}=\widetilde{O}(1)
is set below, into sets
Q_{1a,\kappa}
and
Q_{1b,\kappa}
.
During the first stream pass, the oracle collects the edges
E[R_{2a,\kappa^{\prime}}\cup R_{2a,\kappa^{\prime}}^{\prime},Q_{1b,\kappa}]\cup E[R_{2b,\kappa^{\prime}}\cup R_{2b,\kappa^{\prime}}^{\prime},Q_{1a,\kappa}]
for all
\delta^{2}\kappa^{\prime}\leq\kappa\leq\kappa^{\prime}
.
During the second stream pass, the oracle collects the edges in
E[Q_{1a,\kappa},Q_{1b,\kappa}]
which complete a four-cycle with any edge
e\in E[R_{2a,\kappa^{\prime}},R_{2b,\kappa^{\prime}}]\cup E[R_{2a,\kappa^{\prime}}^{\prime},R_{2b,\kappa^{\prime}}^{\prime}]
and one edge from
E[Q_{1a,\kappa},R_{2b,\kappa^{\prime}}\cup R_{2b,\kappa^{\prime}}^{\prime}]
and one edge from
E[Q_{1b,\kappa},R_{2a,\kappa^{\prime}}\cup R_{2a,\kappa^{\prime}}^{\prime}]
for any pair
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}
with
\delta^{2}\kappa^{\prime}\leq\kappa\leq\kappa^{\prime}
.

Note that in the algorithm above it is crucial that
R_{2a,\kappa},R_{2a,\kappa}^{\prime}
and
R_{2b,\kappa},R_{2b,\kappa}^{\prime}
are already known before the first stream pass, and that after the first stream pass we also stored all edges in
E[R_{2a,\kappa},R_{2b,\kappa}]
and
E[R_{2a,\kappa}^{\prime},R_{2b,\kappa}^{\prime}]
in
Algorithm

2
and the node oracle.

To compute an answer to the query
(e,\kappa,\ell)
, the oracle considers all four-cycles
(u,v,a,b)
where
e=(u,v)
,
a\in Q_{1a,\kappa},b\in Q_{1b,\kappa}
, since each such four-cycle corresponds to a configuration
c=(A,\kappa,a,b)
with
(e,\kappa,\ell)\in c
.
For each such configuration
(A,\kappa,a,b)
realized by the oracle, we check whether
c
counts towards
(e,\kappa,\ell)
, that is, whether the wedges in
c
are light. For this, we assume access to a perfect wedge oracle.

Let
q(e)
be the number of four-cycles found that count towards
t(e,\kappa,\ell)
.
The heaviness oracle is then defined as

\operatorname{oracle}(e,\kappa,\ell)=\begin{cases}\textsf{light}&\text{ if }q(e)\leq(p_{1}^{\prime})^{2}s_{2}\theta_{e},\\
\textsf{heavy}&\text{ if }q(e)>(p_{1}^{\prime})^{2}s_{2}\theta_{e},\end{cases}

where
\theta_{e}=\frac{T}{\delta^{2}\kappa^{2}}
is the heaviness threshold for
e
.

Next, we summarize the guarantees for our oracle.

Lemma 7.9
.

With probability
96/100
, the edge oracle correctly classifies all edges
(e,\kappa,\ell)
with
p_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}=p_{2,\kappa}^{2}
and
t(e,\kappa,\ell)\notin[(1-\varepsilon_{E})s_{2}\theta_{e},(1+\varepsilon_{E})s_{2}\theta_{e}]
.
The oracle uses space
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
.

Proof.

Let
Y_{c}
be the binary random variable whether
c
was realized by the edge oracle.
Let
\mathfrak{C}_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}
denote the set of configurations containing
(e,\kappa,\ell)
.

We have
\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]=t(e,\kappa,\ell)(p_{1}^{\prime})^{2}
and

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left[q(e)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathfrak{C}_{(v,\kappa,\ell)}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Var}\left[Y_{c}^{2}\right]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c,d\in\mathfrak{C}_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}\\
V(c\cap d)=3\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{d}Y_{c}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]+t(e,\kappa,\ell)\cdot 2s_{1}\theta_{c\cap d}(p_{1}^{\prime})^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]+2s_{1}t(e,\kappa,\ell)\sqrt{T}/(\delta\kappa)(p_{1}^{\prime})^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{c_{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]^{2}+\frac{2s_{1}\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{c_{3}}t(e,\kappa,\ell)\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}(p_{1}^{\prime})^{4}

for large enough
c_{3}>200L^{\prime}
. Here, we used that
\theta_{c\cap d}=\sqrt{T}/(\delta\kappa)
and
\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}=T/(\delta^{2}\kappa^{2})
. Note that this argument is the same as
Equation

7
.

As before, we finish the proof by distinguishing two cases and applying a union bound.

Case 1:
t(e,\kappa,\ell)\geq(1+\varepsilon_{E})s_{2}\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}
.

Then

\operatorname{Var}\left[q(e)\right]\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{c_{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]^{2}+\frac{4\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{c_{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]^{2}=\frac{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{100L^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]^{2}

for large enough
c_{3}
. By applying
Lemma

7.3
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[q(e)<\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}s_{2}(p_{1}^{\prime})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle<\mathbb{P}\left[q(e)<\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon_{E})}t(e,\kappa,\ell)(p^{\prime}_{1})^{2}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[q(e)<\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon_{E})}\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left[q(e)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]^{2}}\leq\frac{4}{\delta^{2}}\frac{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{100L^{\prime}}=\frac{4}{100L^{\prime}}.

Case 2:
t(e,\kappa,\ell)\leq(1-\varepsilon_{E})\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}
.

Then
\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]\leq(1-\varepsilon_{E})s_{2}\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}(p_{1}^{\prime})^{2}=:M_{H}
, and

\operatorname{Var}\left[q(e)\right]\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{c_{3}}M_{H}^{2}+\frac{4\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{c_{3}}M_{H}^{2}=\frac{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{100L^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]^{2}

for large enough
c_{3}
. By applying Chebyshev’s inequality (
Lemma

7.3
) we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[q(e)\geq\theta_{(e,\kappa,\ell)}(p_{1}^{\prime})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[q(e)\geq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon_{E}}M_{H}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}\frac{\operatorname{Var}\left[q(e)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[q(e)\right]^{2}}\leq\frac{4}{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}\frac{\varepsilon_{E}^{2}}{100L^{\prime}}=\frac{4}{100}.

Taking a union bound over all up to
L^{\prime}
oracle calls shows that all oracle answers are correct with probability at least
96/100
.
∎

Shifting Thresholds.

Analogously to
Section

7.1
, we now show that with probability
99/100
none of the
L^{\prime}
edges
(e,\kappa,\ell)
for which the oracle is called fulfills

The algorithm randomly selects the shift

different values in

out of

Thus, by randomly selecting the shift

, with probability

Proof of

.

With probability

7.3
Wedge Oracles

We now show how to build a wedge heaviness oracle which, when queried on a labeled wedge
(w,\kappa,\ell)
during
Algorithm

3
or inside the node or edge oracle, outputs

\operatorname{oracle}(w,\kappa,\ell)=\textsf{heavy}
if and only if
t(w,\kappa,\ell)>s_{2}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}

with a high probability.
Recall that refined heaviness
t(w,\kappa,\ell)=t(w)
equals true heaviness for wedges. However, the threshold
\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}
depends on the labeling.

We use node sampling to build the wedge oracles. Intuitively, given a wedge
w
with heaviness threshold
\theta
, we sample nodes with probability
p>1/\theta
and count how many of them form a four-cycle with
w
by collecting the required edge sets.
Observe that for wedges
(w,\kappa,\ell)
with
p_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}=p_{1,\kappa}^{2}p_{2,\kappa}
we have
\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}=\kappa/\delta
and thus
p_{2,\kappa}>1/\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}
. Similarly,
p_{1,\kappa}>1/\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})}
for wedges
(w,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})
with
p_{(w,\kappa,\ell^{\prime})}=p_{1,\kappa}p_{2,\kappa}^{2}
.

We now state the oracle’s guarantees in the following lemma.

Lemma 7.10
.

There is a wedge heaviness oracle which correctly answers all queries by
Algorithm

3
and by the node and edge oracles with probability
98/100
. The oracle requires
\widetilde{O}(m/\sqrt{T})
space.

Let
L^{\prime\prime}
be the number of oracle calls made to the wedge heaviness oracle by
Algorithm

3
, the node oracles and the edge oracles together. We have
L^{\prime\prime}\in\widetilde{O}(1)
as only
\widetilde{O}(1)
configurations and four-cycles are sampled in each of these algorithms. Let
\varepsilon_{W}=\frac{1}{600L^{\prime\prime}}
denote the accuracy of our oracle.

The oracle receives all node sets sampled by
Algorithm

2
, the node oracles and the edge oracles as input. Additionally, it samples two node sets
Q_{1,\kappa}^{\text{wedge}}
and
Q^{\text{wedge}}_{2,\kappa}
with probabilities

p_{1,\kappa}^{\prime}=p_{1,\kappa}\log(n)/\varepsilon_{W}^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad p_{2,\kappa}^{\prime}=p_{2,\kappa}\log(n)/\varepsilon_{W}^{2},

respectively before the first stream pass.

Now let
V_{1,\kappa}
(resp.
V_{2,\kappa}

Let
(w,\kappa,\ell)

p_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}=p_{1,\kappa}^{2}p_{2,\kappa}

The oracle then counts the number

The heaviness oracle is then defined as

\operatorname{oracle}(w,\kappa,\ell)=\begin{cases}\textsf{light}&\text{ if }q(w)\leq p^{\prime}_{2}s_{1}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)},\\
\textsf{heavy}&\text{ if }q(w)>p^{\prime}_{2}s_{1}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)},\end{cases}

where
\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}=\frac{\kappa}{\delta}

With high probability the wedge oracle correctly classifies all wedges
(w,\kappa,\ell)

t(w)\notin[(1-\varepsilon_{W})s_{1}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)},(1+\varepsilon_{W})s_{1}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}]

Proof.

We prove the oracle accuracy using

(w,\kappa,\ell)

Observe that
\mathbb{E}\left[q(w)\right]=t(w)p_{2,\kappa}^{\prime}
as each node
v

w

p_{2,\kappa}
into
Q_{2,\kappa}^{\text{wedge}}
and if sampled, the edges between
v
and the endpoints of
w

To bound the variance,
first assume
t(w)>(1+\varepsilon_{W})\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[q(w)<p_{2,\kappa}^{\prime}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[q(w)\leq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon_{W}}\mathbb{E}\left[q(w)\right]\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon_{W}^{2}}{12}s_{1}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}p_{2,\kappa}^{\prime}\right)\leq n^{-4},

as
p_{2,\kappa}^{\prime}\geq p_{2,\kappa}\log(n)/\varepsilon_{W}^{2}\geq\log(n)c_{1}/(\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}\varepsilon_{W}^{2})\geq 4\cdot 12\log(n)/(s_{1}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}\varepsilon_{W}^{2})
for large enough
c_{1}

Now assume
t(w)<(1-\varepsilon_{W})\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[q(w)>p_{2}^{\prime}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[q(w)\geq\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon_{W})}\mathbb{E}\left[q(w)\right]\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon_{W}^{2}}{24}s_{1}\theta_{(w,\kappa,\ell)}p_{2}^{\prime}\right)\leq n^{-4},

where the last inequality again assumes large enough
c_{1}
.
∎

This shows that the described wedge oracle is accurate with high probability for wedges, for which the heaviness is not too close to the heaviness threshold.
