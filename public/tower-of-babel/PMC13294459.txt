mNGS-Identified Mycobacterium porcinum Infection in a Newly Diagnosed Person With HIV Presenting With Recurrent Suppurative Cervical Lymphadenitis
Authors: Min Wei, Zhuoran Xiao, Xinyi Du, Jiaojiao Cao, Shasha Wu, RongRong Zhang, Xiaofei Yang, Chao Fan, Jianqi Lian, Wenzhen Kang, Chunfu Wang, Chuantao Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough reports of human infection caused by Mycobacterium porcinum (M. porcinum) have gradually increased in recent years, cases occurring in people with HIV (PWH) remain rare, and the association between M. porcinum infection and suppurative cervical lymphadenitis in PWH has not been previously reported. In this case, metagenomic next-generation sequencing was used to rapidly identify M. porcinum from a pus specimen obtained from a newly diagnosed person with HIV presenting with suppurative cervical lymphadenitis as the initial manifestation. Recognition of these rare clinical features may improve understanding of non-tuberculous mycobacterial infections in PWH and their diverse clinical presentations.

Non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections in people with HIV-1 (PWH) have received increasing attention worldwide [1]. The most common NTM pathogens are members of the Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), including Mycobacterium avium and Mycobacterium intracellulare; however, infections caused by other NTM species are increasingly being reported [2, 3]. Mycobacterium porcinum (M. porcinum) was initially identified as a cause of submandibular lymphadenitis in swine and has subsequently been isolated from patients with wound infections, central catheter–related infections, and pulmonary infections [4]. The limited reported cases of M. porcinum infection in PWH have predominantly presented with pneumonia, with the organism isolated from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid [5, 6]. Herein, we report a rare case of M. porcinum infection identified by metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) in a newly diagnosed person with HIV presenting with recurrent suppurative cervical lymphadenitis.

CASE REPORT

A 34-year-old man had been in his usual state of health until 2 months before admission, when he developed a left cervical mass without associated fever. He was treated with a cephalosporin antibiotic at a local clinic (details unknown), and the mass reportedly decreased in size. Two weeks before admission, the mass enlarged again despite treatment with a combination of cephalosporin antibiotics and ornidazole for 10 days. Three days before admission, he developed fever, with a peak temperature of 38°C.

On the day of admission, blood samples were obtained for routine laboratory testing. HIV antibody testing was positive, and the plasma HIV RNA level was 2.09 × 104 copies/mL. Flow cytometry revealed a CD3+ T-lymphocyte count of 395 cells/μL and a CD4+ T-lymphocyte count of 10 cells/μL (reference range: 414–1123 cells/μL), indicating severe immunosuppression consistent with AIDS. The patient also presented with leukopenia (white blood cell count, 2.72 × 109/L) and neutropenia (neutrophil count, 1.74 × 109/L). Inflammatory markers were elevated, including interleukin-6 at 41.98 pg/mL and C-reactive protein at 14.42 mg/dL. Neck ultrasonography revealed left level II cervical lymphadenitis with liquefaction and involvement of the adjacent left sternocleidomastoid muscle (Figure 1). On the second day after admission, before a biopsy could be performed, the neck mass ruptured spontaneously. Pus samples were collected for culture and mNGS analysis.

Ultrasound examination of the lymph nodes revealed a cystic-solid echo in the left cervical region II area, approximately 3.3 cm × 4.8 cm × 5.7 cm in size. The boundary was not clear, the shape was irregular, and the internal echo distribution was uneven. CDFI showed linear blood flow signals within it.

Next-generation sequencing analysis of the lymph node pus sample generated a total of 1 146 407 sequencing reads. Sequencing quality metrics showed Q20 and Q30 values of 96.04% and 94.31%, respectively, indicating high sequencing accuracy. After removal of host sequences, 89.12% of the reads were identified as human-derived. The remaining non-human reads were aligned to the microbial reference database. On day 3, mNGS detected M. porcinum (2448 reads; 99% confidence), HIV-1 RNA (116 reads; 99% confidence), Torque teno virus (28 083 reads), and Epstein–Barr virus (26 reads). Acid-fast staining of the pus specimen was negative for acid-fast bacilli. The specimen was also inoculated onto Löwenstein–Jensen medium and into liquid mycobacterial culture medium and incubated aerobically at 37°C. No mycobacterial growth was observed during the incubation period. Chest computed tomography (CT) showed no obvious evidence of pulmonary infection. Combined with the clinical manifestations and imaging findings, the mNGS results strongly suggested that M. porcinum was the causative pathogen of the patient's recurrent suppurative cervical lymphadenitis.

The patient was started on treatment for M. porcinum with azithromycin (500 mg orally once daily), ethambutol (15 mg/kg orally once daily), and rifabutin (300 mg orally once daily). At the same time, percutaneous drainage was performed. Two weeks later, antiretroviral therapy (ART) with tenofovir, lamivudine, and dolutegravir was initiated. The patient was discharged on hospital day 8. After 1 month of antimycobacterial treatment, the neck mass had significantly decreased in size.

DISCUSSION

M. Porcinum was first isolated and named in 1983 from the submandibular lymph nodes of pigs. Although M. porcinum has been isolated from clinical specimens associated with wound infections, central catheter–related infections, and pneumonia [4], there have been no reports describing M. porcinum infection in people with PWH. Furthermore, to our knowledge, no previous case reports have described cervical lymphadenitis caused by M. porcinum in PWH. In the present case, the patient presented primarily with a subcutaneous neck mass and fever. Laboratory testing and mNGS analysis of the pus specimen confirmed the diagnosis of M. porcinum cervical lymphadenitis in a newly diagnosed person with HIV. Previous studies have shown that NTM infections are more common in PWH with CD4+ T-cell counts ≤50 cells/μL [6]. In this patient, the CD4+ T-cell count was only 10 cells/μL, indicating severe immunosuppression and a high risk of opportunistic infections.

The source of infection in this patient remains unclear. He denied any history of contact with pigs, exposure to slaughterhouses, or consumption of undercooked pork. Given that M. porcinum and other rapidly growing mycobacteria are widely distributed in environmental reservoirs such as water and soil, exposure to contaminated water or soil was considered the most likely source of infection. Additionally, minor disruption of the skin barrier, although not recalled by the patient, may have facilitated bacterial entry.

Traditional diagnostic methods, including acid-fast staining, mycobacterial culture, Xpert testing, and histopathological examination, are limited by long turnaround times and low diagnostic sensitivity, often resulting in misdiagnosis, missed diagnosis, and delays in appropriate treatment. In recent years, the widespread application of mNGS, with its advantages of broad pathogen coverage, rapid turnaround time, and high sensitivity, has significantly improved the diagnosis of difficult infections and uncommon pathogens such as NTM [7]. In this patient, mNGS analysis of the pus specimen detected M. porcinum, supporting the diagnosis of NTM-associated cervical lymphadenitis. The detection of M. porcinum by mNGS from a sterile-site specimen (lymph node pus), together with the compatible clinical presentation, supported its pathogenic role rather than colonization or contamination.

To date, M. porcinum infection in people with PWH remains rare, and no clinical consensus or treatment guidelines are currently available regarding optimal antimicrobial regimens or treatment duration. Since RGM may exhibit resistance to commonly used anti-tuberculosis agents, treatment should ideally be guided by antimicrobial susceptibility testing. However, mycobacterial culture of the pus specimen was negative in this case. Therefore, the treatment strategy was primarily based on recommended regimens for Mycobacterium fortuitum infection. Previous in vitro susceptibility studies have demonstrated susceptibility of M. porcinum to fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, carbapenems, amikacin, sulfamethoxazole, and clarithromycin. Combination therapy with at least 2 active agents for a minimum duration of 6–12 months has been recommended [8, 9]. In this patient, initial empiric treatment with cephalosporins resulted in transient reduction of the neck mass, suggesting possible susceptibility of M. porcinum to cephalosporins. However, monotherapy was insufficient for complete pathogen eradication. Following admission and definitive diagnosis, triple-drug antimycobacterial therapy consisting of azithromycin, ethambutol, and rifabutin was initiated, given the patient's profoundly low baseline CD4+ T-cell count. After 1 month of therapy, follow-up examination demonstrated marked regression of the cervical mass. The patient remains on oral anti-mycobacterial therapy and ART, with regular follow-up to evaluate treatment response and determine the appropriate duration of therapy.

CONCLUSION

This case highlights the value of mNGS in the rapid diagnosis of M. porcinum infection presenting as a neck mass with fever in an immunocompromised host. mNGS enables rapid detection of rare pathogens in diagnostically challenging infections, particularly in PWH. Given that different NTM species exhibit distinct antimicrobial resistance patterns and require different treatment strategies, accurate species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing are essential. This rare case expands current understanding of M. porcinum infection in PWH and provides valuable clinical insights into its diagnosis, management, and prognosis.

Metagenomic mNGS

mNGS was performed on the lymph node pus aspirate by a commercial diagnostic laboratory, Guangzhou Kingmed Diagnostics Group Co., Ltd. (Kingmed Diagnostics, Guangzhou, China), using the MetaCAP hybrid capture-based pathogen-targeted sequencing platform. Total nucleic acids were extracted using the Nucleic Acid Extraction or Purification Kit (KS121-WSWTQ-48; Guangzhou KingCreate Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). RNA was reverse-transcribed using the Reverse Transcription Reaction Kit (KS630-NZL-48; KingCreate). Sequencing libraries were constructed with the MetaCAP Library Construction Reaction Kit (KS130-CAPJK-48; KingCreate) and subsequently enriched by hybrid capture using the MetaCAP Hybridization Capture Reaction Kit (KS129-CAPBH-6; KingCreate). The enriched libraries were sequenced on the KM MiniSeqDx-CN platform with the KM MiniSeqDx-CN Sequencing Reagent Kit (KingCreate), according to the manufacturer's protocols. After quality control, low-quality and human-derived reads were removed by alignment to the human reference genome. The remaining reads were aligned against a curated microbial genome database containing bacterial, fungal, viral, and parasitic genomes for pathogen identification [10]. Pathogen identification was based on uniquely mapped reads and genome coverage analysis. A negative control was processed in parallel throughout the workflow to monitor potential contamination.
