Evaluation of the effectiveness of the membrane composed of biodegradable polycaprolactone and polyglycolic acid layers in guided bone regeneration
Authors: Batuhan Aydın, Serkan Polat, Süleyman Kaplan, Gamze Altun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a biodegradable membrane composed of polycaprolactone (PCL) and polyglycolic acid (PGA) for guided bone regeneration in bone defects.MethodsTwelve Wistar albino rats were initially included in the study. During follow-up, 3 rats were excluded. To maintain the planned sample size, these animals were replaced with 3 additional rats. Consequently, a total of 15 rats were used, and the final statistical analyses were based on 12 rats. Twenty-four standardized bilateral parietal bone defects were created and randomly allocated to four groups. Defects received either 3D-printed PCL membranes or PCL membranes coated on the inner surface with PGA (PCL/PGA). Control defects received PCL membranes alone. Group 1 received PCL/PGA with a human-derived freeze-dried bone graft, an equine xenograft, and autologous blood. Group 2 received PCL/PGA with autologous blood. Group 3 received PCL with autologous blood. Six rats were euthanized at 4 weeks and six at 8 weeks. Histopathological and stereological analyses were performed to assess newly formed bone volume.ResultsAt week 4, Group 2 showed significantly greater newly formed bone volume and total tissue volume than all other groups (p < 0.001). At week 8, newly formed bone volume in Group 2 remained significantly greater than in Groups 1 and 3 (p < 0.001) but did not differ from the control group (p > 0.05).ConclusionIn this GBR model, the PGA-coated PCL membrane enhanced new bone formation during the early phase of bone regeneration.Keywords: Bone regeneration, Polycaprolactone, Polyglycolic acid, 3D printing, Rats, Histology

Introduction

The concept of guided bone regeneration (GBR) is used for the repair of bone defects or for alveolar bone augmentation in dental implant therapy, employing a biodegradable or non-biodegradable membrane that acts as a barrier to prevent soft tissue invasion into the bone defect site and creates a ‘space’ to ‘guide’ the bone regeneration process [1, 2].

Membranes are a fundamental component of the GBR approach because they can serve as a barrier against epithelial and connective tissue cells and maintain the space at the defect site for bone regeneration [3]. Numerous barrier membranes have been developed for GBR applications. These can be classified as biodegradable and non-biodegradable membranes. The selection of a specific material is based on the biological properties of the membrane and the treatment requirements [4]. Vertical or non-contained defects generally require membranes with adequate mechanical stability, prolonged space-maintaining capacity, and sustained barrier function, whereas contained defects with intact bony walls may be managed with more rapidly resorbable membranes that primarily provide temporary epithelial exclusion and favorable clinical handling [5–7]. Non-biodegradable membranes generally include titanium meshes, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membranes, and titanium-reinforced PTFE membranes. Depending on its structural configuration, PTFE can be further classified as expanded PTFE (e-PTFE) or dense PTFE (d-PTFE) membranes [5, 6]. Non-biodegradable membranes have been associated with a higher risk of complications related to membrane exposure compared with biodegradable membranes [8, 9]. Although these membranes provide favorable space-maintaining capacity, metal pins or mini-screws may be required to prevent collapse and preserve the intended membrane morphology [10, 11]. Their main clinical limitations include the need for a second surgical procedure for removal, which increases patient morbidity, treatment duration, and cost, as well as excessive stiffness, which may contribute to soft-tissue dehiscence and membrane exposure [12, 13]. To overcome the limitations of non-biodegradable membranes, biodegradable membranes derived from synthetic and natural sources have been developed [14].

Biodegradable membranes undergo degradation within the body and reduce patient morbidity by eliminating the need for a second surgical procedure [15]. However, their regenerative performance depends on whether the duration of the barrier function, degradation rate, and mechanical stability are compatible with the defect morphology and the anticipated healing period. This is particularly important in vertical GBR, where barrier collapse may compromise the secluded space required for bone formation [7, 16].

Another category of membranes used in GBR consists of aliphatic polyesters, which are synthetic, biocompatible, and biodegradable polymers. Among the aliphatic polyester polymers commonly used as GBR membranes are poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) and the poly(α-hydroxy ester) family, including polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), and their copolymer, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA). Various membranes and other biomedical materials produced from these polymers have received FDA (Food and Drug Administration) approval for human use. By adjusting molecular weight, crystallinity, and copolymer composition, their degradation rates can be tailored to range from weeks to years [17–19].

PCL is a thermoplastic polymer with high formability that can be shaped into three-dimensional (3D) membrane structures conforming to the designed geometry and degrades into non-toxic products after implantation into the host [20]. Its low melting temperature enables facile processing with high dimensional resolution via 3D printing-based additive manufacturing techniques [21]. PCL is a polymer-based biomaterial that has been extensively investigated for its potential use in tissue engineering owing to its numerous advantageous material properties. One of these properties is its low melting temperature, which makes the material suitable for rapid prototyping technologies such as fused deposition modeling (FDM) [22].

The slow degradation rate of PCL provides prolonged support for maturing cells, thereby facilitating the formation of denser tissue [23]. The hydrophobicity of PCL, together with its poor wettability and limited cell adhesion, constitutes a major drawback that limits its applications in tissue engineering. To overcome this issue, one strategy is to blend PCL with other polymers, such as collagen, gelatin, and PGA [24, 25]. Functional groups may also be incorporated to make the polymer more hydrophilic, more cell-adhesive, or more biocompatible, thereby promoting favorable cellular responses [26, 27]. PGA is one of the most hydrophilic polymers among synthetic biodegradable polyesters. It undergoes biodegradation more rapidly than PCL and PLA. The hydrophilic nature of PGA facilitates early-stage cell attachment [28, 29].

PCL and PGA are synthetic biodegradable aliphatic polyesters that degrade mainly through hydrolytic cleavage of ester bonds, with possible contribution from enzymatic processes in vivo. PCL degrades relatively slowly and can yield 6-hydroxyhexanoic acid, whereas PGA degrades more rapidly, yielding glycolic acid, which is present mainly as glycolate under physiological conditions. These low molecular weight degradation products can be further metabolized through physiological pathways, including β-oxidation and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, ultimately yielding CO₂ and H₂O, or may be eliminated through renal and pulmonary routes [30, 31].

This study aimed to histopathologically and stereological-histomorphometrically evaluate the effectiveness of a biodegradable dome-shaped PCL/PGA membrane, fabricated using a 3D printing system to create a vertical space on the parietal bones of Wistar rats used as the experimental animal model, on new bone formation via the GBR approach, after reducing the hydrophobicity of the PCL framework—a major concern in biomedical applications—by coating its inner surface facing the parietal bone with a hydrophilic biodegradable PGA polymer. The primary outcome was bone formation, quantified by stereological analysis of newly formed bone volume within the membrane-covered defect area. Secondary outcomes included mature bone volume, immature bone volume, connective tissue volume, total tissue volume, histopathological analysis and membrane-related healing events.

Materials and methods

This study received ethical approval from the Local Ethics Committee for Animal Research of Bolu Abant İzzet Baysal University (decision no: 2024/20, dated 2 October 2024). This animal study was prepared and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines [32].

Experimental animals, study design, and group allocation

A total of 12 female Wistar albino rats, aged 2–4 months and weighing 200–250 g (mean body weight: 234 g), were included in the study. Experimental animals were procured from the Bolu Abant İzzet Baysal University Experimental Animals Application and Research Center, where their preoperative and postoperative care was also provided. The general health status of all animals was confirmed by a veterinarian at the same center. As the animals were obtained from this institutional facility, no private owner consent was required. The animals were housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, with ad libitum access to water and a standard laboratory diet. Environmental conditions were maintained at 19–21 °C and 45–55% relative humidity.

The study included 12 rats and 24 defect sites, created bilaterally in the right and left parietal bones. Four groups were established: three experimental groups and one control group (Fig. 1). Each defect site was assigned to a study group. Two parietal defect sites (right and left) were created in each animal, and the defects were randomly assigned to two groups within each of the four study groups.

Experimental groups, treatment components, and group allocation

Sample size calculation

Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). An a priori one-way ANOVA power analysis was conducted using a confidence level of 95% (1 − α), a statistical power of 80% (1 − β), and an effect size of f = 1.673. The minimum required sample size was calculated as 3 defects per group, corresponding to a total of 12 defects across the four groups at each time point, based on the most comparable outcome reported in a previously published study [33] and in line with the 3R principles for animal research.

Calvarial defects in the rats were randomly allocated to the experimental groups. In a computer-based system, the numbers 1–24 were assigned to the right and left parietal bones of the experimental animals, and the defect sites were then randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group). Randomization was performed using Python (v3.12.6) in Replit (https://replit.com/), an online integrated development environment. The two parietal defect sites created in each animal were assigned to two different experimental groups. To evaluate different stages of the bone regeneration process, the animals were also randomly and equally allocated to two euthanasia time points: postoperative week 4 and postoperative week 8 (Figs. 2 and 3). Each experimental group was represented by an equal number of defects at both time points (n = 3 per group).

Blinding and coding procedure

Before surgery, each animal and each defect site were assigned unique identification numbers with no group-identifying information. The code key linking identification numbers to group allocation was stored separately for blinding purposes. All registration and randomization procedures were performed using identification numbers only. Prior to histopathological and stereological assessment, all tissue specimens were re-coded using a second coding set and submitted in a blinded manner. Unblinding was performed only after all measurements had been completed.

Production of the membrane composed of polycaprolactone and polyglycolic acid layers

In this study, biodegradable and biocompatible polycaprolactone (PCL; Mn = 80,000 g/mol; Sigma-Aldrich, United Kingdom) and polyglycolic acid (PGA; inherent viscosity: 1.4 dL/g; MFI: 0.4–0.7 g/10 min at 190 °C; Sigma-Aldrich, United States) were used. A 3D model of the dome-shaped polycaprolactone membrane design (outer diameter: 6 mm, inner diameter: 4 mm, outer height: 4 mm, and inner height: 3 mm) was created using SOLIDWORKS (Dassault Systèmes, Windows 11). The prepared 3D model was converted into STL (stereolithography) format. The STL files were sliced using the open-source PrusaSlicer v2.9.3 (Prusa Research, Windows 11) to generate G-code. A G-code file was produced using the printing parameters defined in the slicing software. The generated G-code file was transferred to the Axo A1 Bioprinter (Axolotl Biosystems, Türkiye) via AxoSuite software (Axolotl Biosystems, Türkiye), which is used for device control and management of the printing process in the 3D printer system. The FDM (fused deposition modeling) method was used to fabricate the 3D PCL membranes. Fabrication was carried out in a laboratory environment under constant parameters. The printing parameters were set as follows: printing temperature, 180 °C; pressure, 18 psi; nozzle diameter, 300 μm; printing speed, 1 mm/s; layer thickness, 0.2 mm; and infill density, 100% (Fig. 4).

A, B, C 3D printing fabrication stages of the PCL membrane

The inner surfaces of 12 of the 24 fabricated 3D PCL membranes were coated with PGA. A melt-coating method was employed to coat the inner surface of PCL with PGA. Considering the melting (220–225 °C) and thermal degradation (~ 255 °C) temperatures of PGA, the heating temperature was not allowed to exceed 240 °C. To obtain a homogeneous volume on the PGA-coated inner surfaces, the PCL membrane inner surface was shaped using a 4-mm-diameter round diamond bur (Fig. 5).

A PCL + PGA membrane B PCL membrane

Subsequently, a 1.5-mm-diameter hole was created at the apex of each of the 24 dome-shaped PCL and PCL + PGA membranes using a bur. Sterilization of the fabricated membranes was performed in a gas hydrogen peroxide plasma sterilizer (LK/MJG 100, Laoken Medical, Chengdu, China) using a standard cycle (38 min, 45–55 °C) with 58–60% H2O2 vapor and a plasma phase.

Surgical procedure

In the experimental animals, general anesthesia was induced by intramuscular administration of 90 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride (Ketasol 10%, VetViva Richter GmbH, Austria) and 10 mg/kg xylazine hydrochloride (Xylazin Bio 2%, Bioveta, Czech Republic). The m. quadriceps femoris was selected as the injection site for anesthetic administration. The depth of anesthesia was assessed using skin pinch and toe pinch reflex tests. Absence of these reflexes was considered indicative of deep anesthesia. Muscle relaxation was evaluated by monitoring the tone of the jaw and skeletal muscles.

After anesthesia, the dorsal surface of the rats’ scalp was shaved, and a 10% povidone-iodine solution was then applied for antisepsis of the surgical field. The surgical field and operative site were isolated using sterile drapes. A skin incision approximately 2 cm in length was made along the midline of the calvarium. The subcutaneous tissues and periosteum were carefully dissected. After identification of the sagittal, coronal, and lambdoid sutures, the parietal bones were exposed.

To promote bleeding beneath the membranes, the sagittal suture was used as a reference, and two decortication sites were created with a round steel bur (approximately 1 mm in diameter), each containing three perforations. Decortication was performed under continuous sterile saline irrigation using a low-speed handpiece. The diameter of each decortication site was limited to 4 mm. The sagittal suture line was excluded from the defect design because of its intrinsic osteogenic activity and its proximity to the superior sagittal sinus. Care was taken to preserve the integrity of the dura mater throughout the surgical procedure. The membranes were fixed to cover the decortication sites created on both parietal bones of the rat calvaria using an n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate–containing tissue adhesive (Histoacryl, B. Braun, Germany).

In three of the four experimental groups —PCL/PGA + graft + blood group, PCL/PGA + blood group and PCL+blood group—following decortication of the parietal bones and fixation of the membranes to cover the defect sites using tissue adhesive, autologous blood collected from each animal’s tail was injected into the defect sites through the holes created at the apex of the membranes. Before blood collection, a 10% povidone-iodine solution was applied to the rats’ tails for antisepsis. Autologous blood was obtained using a tail-tip incision technique, in which an approximately 2 mm segment was excised from the distal tail tip. The exposed blood was directly aspirated using a sterile syringe of appropriate gauge. After blood collection, sterile gauze was applied to the site to achieve hemostasis. Following autologous blood injection, the holes at the apex of the membranes were sealed with a tissue adhesive to prevent soft-tissue invasion into the area intended for new bone formation.

In PCL-only group, decortication was performed to induce intraosseous bleeding, and the PCL membrane was subsequently fixed to the bone with a tissue adhesive. In PCL/PGA+graft+blood group, following decortication of the parietal bone, the membrane cavity (outer layer: PCL; inner layer: PGA-coated) was filled with a 1:1 mixture of a cortical–cancellous human-derived bone graft (FDBA Cortical Cancellous Powder, Stembio, Türkiye) and an equine-derived xenograft (Maggi Biotechnology, Italy) using a curette, and the membrane was subsequently fixed to the bone. In PCL/PGA+blood group, after decortication of the parietal bone, the PCL + PGA membrane, composed of an outer PCL layer and an inner PGA layer, was fixed to the bone with a tissue adhesive. In PCL+blood group, after decortication of the parietal bone, the PCL membrane was fixed to the bone with a tissue adhesive.

The scalp and subcutaneous tissues were primarily closed with simple interrupted 3 − 0 silk sutures to ensure complete coverage of the applied membrane materials (Fig. 6). Following suturing, the incision site was gently cleaned with sterile isotonic sodium chloride solution. Excess blood and clot were removed by gentle wiping with sterile gauze.

Postoperative care and monitoring

Postoperative antimicrobial treatment and analgesia were administered according to institutional veterinary recommendations. Enrofloxacin (Enrocure 10%, 100 mg/mL; Teknovet İlaç San. ve Tic. A.Ş., Türkiye) was administered intramuscularly at a dose of 10 mg/kg twice daily for 3 days, beginning after anesthesia and continuing during the postoperative period. For analgesia, metamizole sodium (Dolarjin, 500 mg/mL; Alivira, Türkiye) was administered intramuscularly at a dose of 10 mg/kg twice daily for 3 days. To reduce injection-site irritation, the injectable enrofloxacin and metamizole sodium solutions were diluted 1:10 with sterile isotonic sodium chloride solution (0.9% NaCl) prior to administration.

Postoperative healing was monitored throughout the experimental period. Spontaneous detachment of the membrane from the calvarial surface during healing was established as a predefined exclusion criterion. This criterion was applied only when the membrane had completely detached from the calvarial surface, because detachment compromised the three-dimensional space-maintaining architecture of the membrane, which was essential for evaluating vertical bone formation in this calvarial defect model. Consequently, these specimens no longer represented a valid membrane-covered defect model suitable for histopathological and stereological assessment. Partial membrane exposure, defined as soft-tissue exposure of the membrane without detachment from the calvarial surface, was not considered an exclusion criterion, as the membrane remained attached to the calvarial surface and the three-dimensional secluded space was preserved. Therefore, these specimens were retained in the analysis. Animals excluded because of complete membrane detachment were replaced according to the original randomized allocation scheme to maintain the predetermined number of analyzable histopathological and stereological specimens per group and healing time point. However, all excluded animals and membrane-related events were recorded and reported as part of the experimental flow.

Euthanasia of animals and specimen collection

The first group of rats (n = 6) was euthanized at postoperative week 4, the second group (n = 6) was euthanized at postoperative week 8 by intraperitoneal overdose of ketamine (450 mg/kg) and xylazine (45 mg/kg). Unresponsiveness was verified by the absence of corneal and pedal withdrawal reflexes. Subsequently, exsanguination via cardiac puncture was performed as an adjunctive physical method to ensure irreversible circulatory arrest, in accordance with the recommendations for the euthanasia of experimental animals [34]. Death was confirmed by the absence of spontaneous respiration, loss of corneal and palpebral reflexes, and the presence of fixed and dilated pupils [34]. Following euthanasia, a midline incision was made along the scalp. The calvarial region of each rat was carefully dissected. The calvarial bone and associated periosteal structures, extending anteroposteriorly from the frontal bone to the occipital bone and mediolaterally between the bilateral temporoparietal sutures, were dissected. The membrane materials were completely exposed (Fig. 7). The parietal bones containing the created defects and membranes were carefully cut using a steel round bur under normal saline irrigation. The cut bone tissue was separated from the calvarium and removed in two pieces using a periosteal elevator, taking advantage of the lower resistance of the sagittal suture. The parietal bone tissues dissected after euthanasia were placed in separate containers labeled with the animal identification number and defect/side information and immersed in 4% formaldehyde solution for fixation.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was bone formation, assessed by stereological analysis of newly formed bone volume within the membrane-covered defect area. Secondary outcomes included mature bone volume, immature bone volume, connective tissue volume, total tissue volume, histopathological analysis, and membrane-related healing events, including partial membrane exposure and complete membrane detachment.

Histopathological analysis

At 4 and 8 weeks postoperatively, the animals were euthanized, and calvarial bone specimens containing the defect area were harvested and fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 10 days. Following fixation, the tissues were washed overnight under running tap water and subsequently decalcified in a rapid decalcification solution (Bes Lab., Türkiye; Lot No. 052024.004). After the decalcification procedure, the membranes were released from the tissues and removed en bloc (Fig. 8).

Removal of the membranes after decalcification and before routine histological tissue processing

The decalcified tissues were subjected to routine tissue processing procedures in a tissue processor (Thermo Shandon Citadel 2000, USA). After tissue processing, the tissues were embedded in L-shaped molds, with the samples blinded.

Serial Sect. (5 μm thick) were obtained from paraffin-embedded tissues using a microtome (Leica RM2245, Leica, Nussloch, Germany) in accordance with the systematic random sampling rule and were kept in a laboratory oven overnight for deparaffinization. The section sampling fraction in sagittal orientation obtained using the systematic random sampling rule is 1/30. An average of 30 sections were obtained from each specimen for stereological analysis. The sections were then deparaffinized in xylene and subsequently passed through a graded alcohol series, followed by triple staining (Masson’s trichrome) for histopathological and stereological analyses. After staining, the slide was fixed with acetic acid, then dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, followed by clearing in xylene. After the stained sections were coverslipped with Entellan, images were captured from serial sections at different magnifications (×4, ×10, ×20, and ×40) using a camera-attached light microscope (Olympus BX43, Center Valley, PA, USA) and the computer-assisted cellSens Entry microscopy software (Olympus, Center Valley, PA, USA).

Stereological analysis

During histopathological evaluation, images obtained from serial sections of the tissue at different magnifications were examined, whereas volumetric analysis was performed on the serial images acquired at ×10 magnification using the Cavalieri principle [35] and a point-counting grid in the ImageJ software program (Image Processing and Analysis in Java, NIH, USA) (Fig. 9). Using an appropriate number of sections and an appropriately distributed number of points, mature bone (lamellar bone), immature bone (woven bone), connective tissue, and total tissue volumes were estimated. Volume fractions of parameters were calculated as the proportion of each parameter relative to the total volume. The area represented by a single point on the point-counting grid was calculated as 20,000 μm². In all stereological evaluations, a coefficient of error (CE) of < 5% and a coefficient of variation (CV) of < 20% were taken into consideration. Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using jamovi 2.3.28 (The jamovi project, Sydney, Australia). Statistical analyses were conducted according to the predefined outcome hierarchy. The primary outcome was newly formed bone volume. Secondary outcomes included mature bone volume, immature bone volume, connective tissue volume, and total tissue volume. For each outcome, treatment groups were compared at each healing time point (4 and 8 weeks). In addition, the main effect of group was evaluated irrespective of healing time, and the main effect of healing time was evaluated irrespective of treatment group. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because the quantitative variables did not show normal distribution across groups and time, group comparisons were performed using robust ANOVA implemented in the Walrus package. Pairwise comparisons were performed using Holm-adjusted robust t-tests. Descriptive statistics were presented as median (minimum–maximum). The significance level was set at α = 0.05, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical findings

During the healing period, complete spontaneous membrane detachment from the calvarial surface occurred at both defect sites in three rats and met the predefined exclusion criterion. These included R1c, involving PCL/PGA + graft + blood and PCL/PGA + blood; R1d, involving PCL/PGA + graft + blood and PCL + blood; and R2d, involving PCL-only Control and PCL/PGA + blood. Accordingly, both defect sites from these animals were excluded from the histopathological and stereological analyses, and the animals were replaced according to the original randomized allocation scheme. Partial membrane exposure without detachment from the calvarial surface was observed at both defect sites in two rats, R2b and R2f. These included R2b, involving PCL/PGA + graft + blood and PCL + blood, and R2f, involving PCL/PGA + blood and PCL/PGA + graft + blood. Because the membranes remained attached to the calvarial surface, these specimens were retained in the histopathological and stereological analyses (Fig. 10). After replacement according to the original randomized allocation scheme, the final histopathological and stereological analyses included three analyzable defect sites per treatment group at each healing time point, corresponding to six sites per group overall (Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

GBR is widely and predictably used in implant surgery to reconstruct volume in cases of horizontal and vertical alveolar ridge deficiencies through membrane–graft combinations; to close and fill peri-implant fenestration-type wall defects with bone; to limit post-extraction resorption through socket preservation; and to stabilize graft material and protect it from soft tissue invasion in posterior maxillary vertical bone deficiencies, such as those requiring maxillary sinus floor elevation [37–39]. Previous studies have indicated that horizontal regeneration is more predictable than vertical regeneration [40, 41]. As emphasized by Roccuzzo et al. [42], considers vertical bone regeneration a clinical challenge because the volume to be augmented is not surrounded by bony walls, which makes adequate recruitment of osteogenic cells more difficult.

Ezirganli et al. [43] used a dome-like titanium membrane covered with a Teflon cap to evaluate vertical bone augmentation of different materials in a male New Zealand rabbit calvarial defect model. Murata et al. [44] used a PLCL (polylactic acid/caprolactone) bilayer barrier membrane in a calvarial defect model in female Wistar rats to achieve vertical bone regeneration. Kim et al. [45] used 3D-printed nylon in a calvarial defect model of male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats to achieve volume control in vertical bone regeneration.

In this study, 3D dome-shaped PCL and PCL + PGA membrane materials fabricated using the FDM method were used in a female Wistar rat calvarial defect model to achieve vertical bone regeneration.

In vertical bone augmentation, insufficient soft tissue coverage may result in membrane exposure, and subsequent contamination may increase the risks of infection and graft failure [41, 46]. Yamaguchi et al. [47] reported in a prospective human study that the PLA/PCL membrane used in maxillary sinus augmentation did not cause infection when exposure was observed. Considering clinical protocols indicating that titanium mesh membrane exposure is manageable (e.g., local antiseptic care, mechanical plaque control, and follow-up) [48, 49], and studies showing that [50, 51], in the absence of clinical signs of infection, mesh exposure does not compromise the intended bone gain, it is considered that including such cases in the study rather than excluding them would enhance the external validity of the findings. To our knowledge, no clinical or experimental data are currently available on changes in bone gain following exposure of PCL membranes.

In the present study, membrane-related healing events were distinguished as complete membrane detachment or partial membrane exposure. Complete detachment from the calvarial surface was considered a predefined exclusion criterion because it disrupted the three-dimensional space-maintaining architecture required for vertical bone formation, whereas partial exposure without detachment was retained because the membranes remained fixed to the calvarial surface. Garcia et al. [52] reported that membrane exposure after GBR significantly compromises bone augmentation outcomes, while Cucchi et al. [53] demonstrated that membrane exposure, vertical bone gain, and volumetric stability are clinically relevant parameters in vertical augmentation procedures using reinforced PTFE or titanium meshes. Because the number of membrane-related events was limited, a formal exposure-by-treatment interaction analysis was not considered sufficiently robust; therefore, these events were reported descriptively by treatment group and healing time point. The higher number of membrane-related events observed in the PCL/PGA + graft + blood group may have contributed to the lower newly formed bone volume in this group compared with PCL/PGA + blood, although this interpretation should remain cautious. In addition, the acidic decalcification step used during histological processing may have demineralized and/or partially removed calcium-containing graft particles, potentially affecting the stereological evaluation of newly formed bone volume.

The hydrophobic nature of PCL may impose in vivo limitations, such as reduced cell attachment, cellular affinity, and proliferation [54, 55]. Comparative in vivo studies reporting direct head-to-head results between PCL (as a standalone group) and other synthetic polymers within the same experimental model, particularly regarding GBR or new bone formation, are limited in the literature. Chen et al. [56] reported that a 3D-printed PCL scaffold with a large pore size provided an architectural framework for new bone formation in critical-size calvarial defects in male Sprague–Dawley rats, without the use of bone grafts or barrier membranes. Sun and colleagues [57] reported that PCL maintained its structural integrity for two years in a subcutaneous implantation model in female Wistar rats. Hwang and colleagues [58] reported that, in two young adult cases undergoing cranioplasty with patient-specific implants, imaging at long-term follow-up (8 and 20 months) demonstrated ossification within the defect regions when a PCL/β-TCP material was used.

In the present study, despite concerns about PCL’s hydrophobic nature in bone regeneration, its long-term structural stability suggests that it may be used without hydrophilic surface modifications. However, further studies with longer experimental endpoints are needed to improve the generalizability of these findings.

Surface wettability is one of the most important parameters influencing the biological response to an implanted material. Wettability affects protein adsorption, platelet adhesion/activation, blood coagulation, and both cell and bacterial adhesion [59, 60]. To overcome these limitations, various surface modification strategies have been investigated to enhance the hydrophilicity of PCL [61]. Various researchers have used combinations of metals [62, 63], ceramics [64, 65], and polymers [66, 67] with PCL to improve its properties. Matsumoto et al. [68] reported that in experimental mandibular defect models in beagle dogs, macroporous biodegradable PLGC membrane did not significantly increase the amount of new bone in defect areas compared to Ti-PTFE, but facilitated the regeneration of mature bone. Jiangfeng et al. [69] produced MBG/PGA-PCL composite scaffolds by obtaining a 3D porous scaffold structure using a solution casting particle removal method with PGA-PCL copolymer. Jonnalagadda et al. [70] produced PCL/PGA mixed fibers using the electrospinning technique in an in vitro study and reported that as the PGA content increased, hydrophilicity and water retention increased, along with an accelerated hydrolytic degradation.

In the present study, the inner surface of the hydrophobic PCL membrane facing the bone defect was modified with hydrophilic biodegradable PGA via melt coating address concerns regarding cell attachment and proliferation in in vivo applications. This method was preferred because it does not leave cytotoxic solvent residues, prevents solvent-related deterioration of the PCL membrane’s structural stability, and provides a rapid, provides a rapid and cost-effective manufacturing approach.

An in vitro study by Magazzini et al. [71] reported that adding PGA to PCL or PLLA mixtures increased wettability by reducing the water contact angle. Seo et al. [72], in a study comparing PGA and silk scaffolds, reported that in the in vitro phase, the initial cell adhesion (in the first 12 h) of mesenchymal stem cells was superior in PGA mesh scaffolds compared to silk scaffolds, but that cell densities reached similar levels in the proliferation phase (on day 21). Ceonzo et al. [73] reported that PGA hydrolysis products triggered acute inflammation by stimulating the complement system in in vitro human serum and in an in vivo mouse intraperitoneal model. No in vivo or in vitro studies have been found in the literature that quantitatively describe the time-pH levels of acidic degradation products of PGA in bone tissue.

In the present study, membrane modification achieved by coating the inner surface of PCL with the hydrophilic PGA biopolymer appears to have enhanced bone regeneration at week 4, as evidenced by the statistically significantly higher new bone volume observed in PCL/PGA + blood compared with the other groups. This effect may be attributable to improved early cell–material interaction and cell adhesion provided by the hydrophilic surface [69, 74]. At week 8, PCL/PGA + blood showed greater newly formed bone volume than PCL/PGA + graft + blood and PCL + blood but did not differ significantly from the PCL-only. This finding suggests that PCL/PGA + blood supported bone regeneration; however, the absence of a significant difference from PCL-only indicates that the later-stage contribution of the PGA coating may have become less pronounced over time. Importantly, PCL/PGA + blood also demonstrated superior overall bone formation compared with PCL + blood. Because PCL/PGA + blood and PCL + blood shared the same blood-filled defect model and differed mainly by the presence of the PGA coating, the superior overall bone formation observed in PCL/PGA + blood supports a possible contributory role of PGA in enhancing the regenerative response. The attenuation of the early advantage at week 8 may reflect time-dependent changes associated with PGA degradation rather than complete loss of the early biological effect. PGA degrades predominantly through hydrolysis, and the resulting acidic degradation products may contribute to a transiently acidic local microenvironment that can influence tissue response and bone formation [75, 76]. Nevertheless, the long-term effect of PGA coating should be interpreted cautiously, and further studies including additional time points and degradation analyses are needed to clarify its temporal contribution to vertical bone formation.

In histopathological examinations, the cartilage islands observed in PCL/PGA + blood group at week 4 may represent a transient endochondral-like response during early defect healing. Cartilage tissue was also observed in PCL/PGA + graft + blood group at week 8. Over time, this cartilage matrix may be progressively replaced by bone tissue through remodeling [77]. Likewise, the abundant spaces observed in the week 8 sections, containing bone matrix, may reflect ongoing remodeling of the regenerated bone tissue.

The translational relevance of the present rat calvarial findings should be interpreted in relation to human vertical GBR studies. Rocchietta et al. [78] reported differences in revascularization and remodeling between autogenous block and particulate grafts combined with GBR, while Cucchi et al. [79] and Dellavia et al. [80] demonstrated that titanium mesh-supported approaches can maintain the augmented space and support the formation of vital bone suitable for implant placement. In addition, the clinical report by Ivanovski et al. [81] provides relevant human proof-of-concept evidence for scaffold-guided alveolar bone regeneration using a PCL-based construct. In this context, the early increase in newly formed bone volume observed in the PCL/PGA + blood group may indicate a favorable regenerative response supported by the hydrophilic PGA-coated surface and the space-maintaining PCL framework. However, because the present study was conducted in a rat calvarial model and did not include micro-CT assessment, these findings should be interpreted as preclinical histopathological and stereological evidence rather than direct clinical proof.

In the present study, the non-mineralized tissue component beneath the dome-shaped membranes was quantified stereologically as connective tissue volume. Regardless of healing time, connective tissue volume differed significantly among the groups, with a significant pairwise difference only between PCL/PGA + blood and PCL + blood. In GBR, such non-mineralized connective tissue may correspond, at least in part, to the pseudo-periosteum layer described adjacent to regenerated bone; Cucchi et al. [82] proposed a pseudo-periosteum classification based on the thickness and regularity of this tissue after GBR using titanium-reinforced d-PTFE membranes or titanium meshes plus cross-linked collagen membranes. The higher connective tissue volume in the PCL/PGA + blood group may therefore reflect persistence of a pseudo-periosteum-like tissue component within the protected space. In addition, PGA undergoes hydrolytic degradation, and degraded PGA or glycolide degradation products have been associated with complement activation and local inflammatory responses; therefore, degradation-related tissue reactions may have contributed to this finding [73].

In this study 4-week and 8-week healing periods were selected to capture different stages of bone regeneration in the rat calvarial defect model. Because rats have a higher metabolic rate and osteogenic potential than humans, bone healing progresses more rapidly in this model, and 4- and 8-week intervals are commonly used to represent early and later stage regenerative responses, respectively [83, 84]. In the present study, newly formed bone volume and mature bone volume were significantly higher at 8 weeks than at 4 weeks, whereas connective tissue volume was significantly lower, irrespective of treatment group. These findings indicate a time dependent progression from early regenerative tissue formation toward more mature bone formation.

This study was conducted exclusively in female Wistar rats, and estrous cycle staging was not performed. Previous studies have reported that data obtained from female rodents are not more variable than those from males, and that monitoring of the estrous cycle is not mandatory for most experimental traits [32, 85, 86].

Bone decortication is considered an adjunctive technique for enhancing bone regeneration. Previous studies have shown that decortication stimulates angiogenesis in connective tissue and accelerates osteogenesis by promoting rapid revascularization in the grafted area [87, 88]. However, it has also been reported that decortication may not always result in favorable outcomes. Studies in the literature have suggested that this procedure may cause additional trauma at the surgical site, that increasing the number of decortication perforations may not provide an additional benefit in new bone formation, and that disruption of periosteal and cortical integrity may lead to a limited degree of bone volume loss at the donor site [89, 90]. However, these potential losses have been reported to be compensated for by increased angiogenesis and osteoblastic activity in the early phase, thereby resulting in a net positive effect on overall bone formation [88, 91].

In the present study, three decortication sites, each approximately 1 mm in diameter, were created in each parietal bone. This number was intentionally limited to account for anatomical boundaries. Therefore, the use of three decortication perforations per parietal bone in this study is an appropriate approach, as it may support regenerative processes while remaining within the anatomical limits of the rat parietal bone and providing experimental standardization compatible with the diameter of the 3D membranes used.

In the present study, in the three experimental groups other than the PCL-only group, fresh autologous blood obtained from the tail tip through an approximately 2-mm distal tail-tip incision was injected into the defect area through the opening created in the membranes. In the PCL-only group, although autologous blood injection was not performed, an opening was similarly created at the apex of the membrane, as in the other membrane groups, for experimental standardization. In mice and rats, excision of more than 5 mm of tail tip tissue is not recommended; the procedure should be performed only under appropriate hemostatic conditions and avoided in older animals. In addition, repeated incisions into the tail vasculature are recommended to be avoided [92, 93].

Application of autologous blood to the defect sites reflects clinical practice in humans, in which graft materials are mixed with patient-derived autologous blood or blood-derived products such as PRF (platelet-rich fibrin) and PRP (platelet-rich plasma) are used [94]. In one study, blood was shown to infiltrate from the surface into the inner structure of 3D nanoparticle-containing PCL scaffolds, forming stable clots, and this clot formation was associated with an increase in vivo bone formation [95]. In a study by Rezuc et al. [96] reported on rabbit tibia defects and found that PRF alone or in combination with grafts did not show superiority in bone formation.

In the present study, it was found that autologous blood application in GBR does not appear superior to decortication for long-term bone regeneration and new bone formation. To improve the generalizability of these findings, further experimental and clinical studies with larger sample sizes, additional experimental groups, and longer end-point durations are needed.

The dome-shaped PCL/PGA membrane tested in the present study should be interpreted within the context of vertical GBR, in which mechanical stability and space-maintaining capacity are essential to prevent membrane collapse and preserve the regenerative space required for vertical bone formation [97]. This principle is consistent with the clinical use of titanium meshes in vertical bone augmentation, where structural rigidity is intentionally used to maintain the augmented volume during healing [75]. In the present prototype membrane, PGA was applied as a thin coating layer, whereas PCL constituted the three-dimensional structural framework. Importantly, the thickness, porosity, geometry, and mechanical stiffness of 3D-printed PCL membranes can be modified according to defect morphology and clinical requirements. Therefore, the present construct should be regarded as a preclinical prototype for evaluating vertical bone formation rather than as a final clinically optimized membrane, and further studies should focus on improving flexibility, handling properties, degradation behavior, and clinical adaptability before translation to clinical use.

Conclusion

Considering the limitations of this study, the PCL/PGA + blood group supported new bone formation in a rat calvarial GBR model. This effect was most pronounced at 4 weeks, indicating an early regenerative advantage when the defect-facing surface of PCL was coated with PGA in a blood-filled defect model. Although PCL/PGA + blood did not differ significantly from PCL-only at 8 weeks, its superiority over PCL + blood at the 8-week and overall evaluations supports a possible contributory role of PGA in the regenerative response. Although the bilateral calvarial defect design reduced inter-animal variability and animal use, defect-level observations may share a common host environment within each rat; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as defect-level histological and stereological outcomes. The findings of this study are specific to a female rat calvarial defect model; therefore, further validation in larger animal models and controlled clinical studies is needed to assess efficacy, optimize the approach, and support clinical translation.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides histopathological and stereological evidence supporting the efficacy of PCL + PGA membrane in guided bone regeneration (GBR), several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the rat calvarial defect is a well-established model in regenerative research, interspecies biological differences may limit the direct extrapolation of these findings to human clinical settings. Second, although the planned sample size was maintained, the final analysis was based on a limited number of animals, which should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the results. Future studies are needed to further characterize the degradation kinetics, hydrophilicity, cell adhesion, mechanical performance, and biocompatibility of the fabricated PCL/PGA membrane system under standardized in vitro and in vivo conditions. Assessment of local pH changes and the time-dependent accumulation of acidic degradation products may help clarify the long-term biological safety of the PGA coating and its potential influence on the bone regeneration microenvironment. Although stereological analysis provided quantitative tissue-level data, the absence of micro-CT evaluation remains a limitation of the present study.
