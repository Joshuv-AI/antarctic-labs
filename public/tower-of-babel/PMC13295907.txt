Microbiota matters: a cross-sectional study protocol for mapping gut microbiome diversity in Malaysian major depressive disorder cases
Authors: Zuraifah Asrah Mohamad, Sophia Karen Bakon, Nur Fatihah Sholehah Zakaria, Nur Faizzatul Akma Mas’ud, Mohammad Zabri Johari, Nur Hasnah Maamor, Norni Abdullah, Norliza Chemi, Nor Asiah Muhamad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMental health is now recognised as a major global concern affecting people from diverse backgrounds. There is growing evidence that the gut microbiota plays a crucial role by producing metabolites that significantly influence a person’s mood and behaviour. Despite its importance, there is a significant gap in the profiling and understanding of the gut microbiota’s influence on mental health among Malaysians. Therefore, this study aims to determine gut microbiome profiles among patients with major depressive disorders (MDD) of different treatment groups attending psychiatric clinics in the state hospitals and compare them to healthy individuals in the community of Klang Valley, Malaysia.Methods and analysisThis cross-sectional study will be carried out in Klang Valley, Malaysia. Eligibility of the patients will be assessed by the psychiatrists prior to recruitment of patients. Patients with MDD will be categorised into monotherapy and polypharmacy while healthy individuals will be used as a comparison group. Demographic data will be recorded. Stool samples will be subjected to DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene-sequencing analysis to determine the microbial compositions of the gut microbiome.Ethics and disseminationThis study will be conducted following the procedure set by the National Medical Research Register Malaysia. The Medical Research Ethics Committee (MREC), Ministry of Health Malaysia, has ratified this study and granted ethical approval to conduct this study (NMRR ID-22-00893-JVW). All the participants will be given an information sheet and will sign a consent form to participate. Participants have the right to withdraw from the study at any time without an explanation. All information gathered will be used for research purposes only and treated as confidential. The results will be submitted to peer-reviewed journals for publication as well as presented at national and international conferences. Informing policy makers at all levels is a crucial aspect of the dissemination and will be done from local to international levels.Trial registration numberThis study was registered under National Medical Research Register Malaysia (NMRR ID-22-00893-JVW), a mandatory procedure of the Ministry of Health Malaysia before the start of a research.

Introduction

Mental health is an important global issue throughout every stage of life, from childhood to adulthood. According to the WHO (2018), mental health is defined as ‘a state of well-being in which an individual realises their own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively, and is able to contribute to their community’. Individuals with poor mental health, including chronic stress and depression, are at higher risk of developing physical illness, such as heart disease, diabetes, stroke, pain, osteoporosis and Alzheimer’s disease. Therefore, mental health, together with physical health components, is deemed equally important for overall health and well-being.

The number of people suffering from common mental illnesses is rising, especially among lower levels of household income, which is associated with increased risk for mental disorders.1 On a global scale, the WHO estimated over 300 million people to suffer from depression, equivalent to 4.4% of the world’s population.2 In Asian countries, major depressive disorders (MDD) showed a prevalence of 20% in Thailand, followed by Taiwan at 19.9%, Korea at 19.4%, Malaysia at 17.5% and China at 16.5%. Similarly, anxiety is also prevalent in Asia with a prediction that 20% of adolescents will experience anxiety episodes in any given year in the future.3

In Malaysia, the National Health Morbidity Survey (NHMS 2015) reported that the prevalence of mental health problems in Malaysia affects 29.2% and 12.1% of the adult and children populations, respectively.4 The National Mental Health Strategic Plan 2020–2025 has outlined the objective ‘to promote mental health well-being, prevent mental disorders, provide care, enhance recovery, and reduce the mortality, morbidity and disability for persons with mental health problems’. Mental health is also ranked third in the Health Research Priorities for the 12th Malaysia Plan (12MP-HRP) 2021–2025.

Various common factors can contribute to mental illnesses such as experiences of trauma or abuse, relationship problems, chronic medical conditions and issues caused by alcohol and drug use.57 MDD is a common mental disease which may also occur together with generalised anxiety disorder (GAD; figure 1).8

The human gut harbours a bacterial community termed ‘microbiota’ which plays a crucial role in the gut–brain axis affecting the human via the neuroimmune and neuroendocrine pathway.9 10 Research on the gut microbiota composition has been highlighted as a potential therapeutic target for treating mental illness in psychiatry. Research showed that psychological stress interacts not only directly with the brain and mood but also with the microbiome-gut-brain axis (MGBA) mechanisms thought to contribute to changes in mood with alteration of the gut microbiota.11 Emerging evidence suggests that alterations in gut microbiota composition may influence mood and behaviour through multiple interconnected pathways within the MGBA. Proposed mechanisms include immune-mediated effects, whereby dysbiosis promotes low-grade systemic inflammation that may affect neuroinflammatory processes implicated in depression. In addition, gut microorganisms are involved in the synthesis and modulation of neuroactive compounds, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), serotonin precursors and gamma-aminobutyric acid, which may influence central nervous system function. Microbiota-related regulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and bidirectional signalling via the vagus nerve have also been proposed as pathways linking gut microbial profiles to stress responsivity and mood regulation. The MGBA is influenced by a number of elements, including the immune system’s chemokines and cytokines, metabolic pathways and products and the central nervous system’s stress hormones and neurotransmitters. These elements can all be metabolised by different bacterial species in the gut.12 However, stress and emotions can also affect the release of stomach acid, bile and mucus that can change the microbiome’s habitat and its composition. Gut microbes produce metabolically active chemicals like SCFA that can influence gene expression, neurotransmitter signalling and metabolism by crossing the epithelial cell layer. As a result, changes in the microbiome composition can also have an impact on our emotions and stress responses and vice versa13 (figure 2). While causal relationships cannot be inferred from the present study, these mechanisms provide a biological framework for interpreting observed associations.

Communication between the brain, gut and microbes is crucial. A balanced gut microbiota is vital for normal gut function and plays a key role in transmitting signals along the brain–gut axis, promoting overall health in individuals.

A review by Huang et al showed that microbial diversity and taxonomic composition of individuals with mood disorders significantly changed compared with healthy individuals. Pro-inflammatory genera involved in lipid metabolism were increased in patients with depressive episodes. The abundances of Actinobacteria and Enterobacteriaceae were increased and Faecalibacterium was decreased in patients with either bipolar or MDD.10 In a study of 23 patients with MDD and 21 with GAD by Dong et al, there were significant differences in microbial compositions compared with healthy participants, with unique gut microbiota signatures associated with MDD and GAD.14

In another study, Chen et al found altered faecal microbiota in MDD patients with significant differences in Bacteroides, Proteobacteria, Firmicutes and Actinobacteria.15 A similar result was reported by Jiang et al, who found an increase in Bacteroides, Proteobacteria, Firmicutes and Actinobacteria among 46 patients with depression (MDD) compared with healthy controls (HCs).16 An altered gut microbiota profile was also reported in patients with GAD with increased Bacteroides, Ruminococcus and Fusobacterium, and decreased microbial richness and diversity.17 A scoping review analysis of the gut microbiome and behavioural and mental health studies among children and young adults (in the Netherlands, USA, China, Japan, Australia, Germany, Finland, New Zealand and Taiwan) showed that the presence of Bifidobacterium in the human gut to be associated with better mental health.18

Additionally, several intervention studies revealed that probiotic consumption of Bifidobacterium19 and Lactobacillus20 can relieve stress and decrease anxiety symptoms among study participants by modulating the neuroactive potential of the gut microbiota. There have been both animal and human studies that linked the effect of probiotic supplement on mood. Administration of probiotics such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus to mice with certain chronic diseases such as gastrointestinal inflammation,21 immunodeficiency,22 liver and brain inflammation,23 or stresses such as maternal separation24 and restraint stress25 demonstrated positive effects on mood. These effects resulted in a reduction across three categories: anxiety, symptoms of depression and anxiety-like behaviour. In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, men and women with MDD showed reduction in symptoms following 8 weeks of taking Lactobacillus capsules.26

The gut microbiome composition of Malaysia’s diverse population exhibits significant variation across different ethnic groups. This variation can be used to assess disease susceptibility and aid in the development of targeted medical treatments for specific groups. Therefore, understanding the profiles of gut microbiota inhabiting the human gut is crucial before considering any alterations of the microbiome as potential therapeutic targets for improving mental health. In Malaysia, studies of gut microbiota among different ethnicities of Malay, Chinese, Indian and Jakun have shown microbiota variation across ethnicity and diet among 214 healthy Malaysian adults.27 In another study conducted among pre-adolescent Malays, Chinese and Orang Asli in Northern Malaysia, Chong et al reported the highest bacterial diversity was detected in the economically-deprived indigenous children while the lowest diversity was recorded from the relatively wealthy Chinese children.28 However, to our knowledge, human gut microbiota in relation to mental illness and treatment groups has not been reported in Malaysia and there are no standard gut microbiota profiles available. Hence, reference is to be made to HCs from other countries (online supplemental table 1). Thus, exploring the link between gut microbiota and mental illness in a diverse country like Malaysia is crucial.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This is a cross-sectional observational study designed to investigate the association between the gut microbiome and MDD. The study will be conducted in the Klang Valley, Malaysia, and is scheduled to commence in March 2026, with participant recruitment expected to be completed by August 2026. Final data collection and analyses are planned to conclude by December 2026.

Participants with MDD will be recruited from three hospitals located within the Klang Valley, while control participants will be recruited from the general community in the same geographical region. The selection of study hospitals is based on regional population density and logistical considerations, including proximity between clinical sites and laboratory facilities required for microbiome analysis. Laboratory-based methodologies will be used for biological sample processing and analysis (figure 3). As this is an observational study, clinical trial registration is not applicable.

Sample size calculation

Building on a similar study conducted by Zhang et al in China, we recalculated using PS software (Vanderbilt University, Nashville, Tennessee, USA), setting the prevalence for Pasteurellales in the gut microbiome at 20% with 80% power. The calculations indicated a requirement of 52 participants for each group. For statistical robustness, an additional 25% of the participants was incorporated into each group, resulting in a total minimum sample size of 65 participants per group. The additional sample size enhances estimate precision, increases the study’s power to detect meaningful effects, and reduces type I (false positive) and type II (false negative) errors, making the findings more generalisable and less prone to random variation or sampling bias. In short, the study aims to recruit a total of 195 individuals, with (1) MDD group comprising 130 samples (divided into two groups, namely, monotherapy (n=65) and polypharmacy (n=65)), and (2) a separate HC group consisting of 65 participants for comprehensive comparative analysis. We are planning a study of independent cases and controls with 1 control per case. Prior data indicated that the probability of exposure among controls is 0.3. If the true probability of exposure among cases is 0.07, we will need to study 52 case patients and 52 control patients to be able to reject the null hypothesis that the exposure rates for case and controls are equal with probability (power) 0.8. The type I error probability associated with test of null hypothesis is 0.05. We will use a continuity-corrected χ2 statistic or Fisher’s exact test to evaluate this null hypothesis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients will involve solely as research participants and will not be involved in the study design, conduct, outcome selection or dissemination planning. Study outcomes will not be informed by patient or public priorities. In the recruitment phase, the study participants will receive a detailed explanation of the research process during clinic visits, and their written consent will be obtained. All participants are given an information sheet and they sign a consent form to participate. Participants have the right to withdraw from the study at any time without an explanation. All information gathered will be used for research purposes only and treated as confidential.

Participants and recruitment

Participants with MDD will be recruited during routine outpatient clinic visits, while HC participants will be recruited from the general community, including hospital populations and research institutions, provided they meet the study eligibility criteria. All participants will receive a detailed explanation of the study procedures during recruitment, and written informed consent will be obtained prior to enrolment. The HC group will be prospectively recruited from the hospital community and surrounding population during the study period. The HC group participants must meet the age requirement of 18 years old or older and not diagnosed with psychiatric diseases. Eligible individuals will be screened using the same exclusion criteria as the intervention group, except for the absence of the target condition. Recruitment will be conducted consecutively through advertisements. As a potential limitation, the faecal samples collection and availability may be affected by unstable mood; therefore, the study team will conduct follow-up telephone contact with patients subject to their consent. Where appropriate and with the participant’s permission, a family member, guardian or housemate will be identified as a secondary contact person solely to support follow-up related to study procedures.

On enrolment, the clinical and demographic data such as age, ethnicity, gender, body mass index (BMI), occupation (current/history), income, family size, medical history of chronic diseases and other mental illnesses will be collected. For patients with MDD, the severity of depressive symptoms and anxiety levels will be assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a validated 9-item self-report measure with established reliability and validity that has also been validated in Asian populations, including primary care settings and among Malay-speaking participants in Malaysia.29 30 While we initially considered including dietary intake, this was not done in the current baseline study for several reasons:

The one-time participation for each participant will span 2–3 days, covering recruitment, consent acquisition, data and faecal sample collection. Faecal samples will be collected fresh in a sterile container, kept in a cool box, transported at 4°C within 48 hours and immediately processed for culturable bacteria at the same district facility. Microbial integrity is preserved under these conditions, so stabilising agents were not needed. The samples can be stored at −20°C for periods ranging from a few months to up to a few years.31

DNA extraction

DNA extraction from the faecal samples will be performed using the Canvax Higherpurity Soil DNA isolation kit (Canvax Reagents, S.L.U., Boecillo Valladolid City, Spain) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with some modifications. Culturable bacteria will be kept for DNA extraction using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and subjected to PCR and bacterial identification.

Sample analysis

Culturable isolates obtained from each participant will undergo isolation and identification. The microbial compositions of the gut microbiome for all participants will be determined through 16S rRNA gene-sequencing analysis, conducted using Illumina Miseq (Illumina, Inc., San Deigo, California, USA) according to the standard protocol. Samples from each group (MDD and HC) will be pooled for subsequent functional profiles and gene pathway analysis. Orthogonal partial least-square discriminant analysis will be used to identify the differential genera responsible for discriminating between MDD patients and HCs. A co-occurrence network will be constructed using the identified differential genera to reflect microbial changes in HCs, mono and polypharmacy MDD patients. Genera with potential as biomarkers for diagnosing MDD will undergo stepwise logistic-regression analysis based on Akaike’s information criterion rule to analyse shared differential genera in monotherapy and polypharmacy MDD patients. Paired-end 16S rRNA gene sequencing reads were merged and quality filtered to remove low-quality sequences, ambiguous bases and chimaeras, retaining sequences longer than 200 bp. High-quality reads were clustered into operational taxonomic units at 97% sequence similarity using VSEARCH (University of Oslo/Oslo University Hospital, Norway; CIRAD, Montpellier, France; University of Kaiserslautern, Germany; University of Warwick/University of Glasgow, UK), and taxonomic assignment was performed against the SILVA 138 reference database using the Ribosomal Database Program Bayesian classifier (Michigan State University, Michigan, USA). Alpha diversity metrics, including Shannon and Chao1 indices, were calculated following rarefaction to assess microbial richness and evenness, and rarefaction and rank–abundance curves were generated. Beta diversity was evaluated using weighted and unweighted UniFrac and Bray–Curtis distance metrics, with community differences visualised by principal component analysis, principal coordinates analysis, and non-metric multidimensional scaling. Hierarchical clustering was performed using the unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean. Group differences in microbial composition were assessed using analysis of similarities (ANOSIM), permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA/Adonis), Metastats, Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) and non-parametric tests, including Wilcoxon rank-sum analysis. Associations between microbial communities and participant groups were explored using heatmaps, redundancy analysis, canonical correspondence analysis and network analysis to characterise group-specific microbial interactions and community structure.

Statistical analysis

For demographic data, to evaluate group differences between MDD and the HC, the χ2 test will be used for categorical variables, while the independent-samples t-test will be used for normally distributed continuous variables. Alternatively, the Mann-Whitney U test will be used for non-normally distributed continuous variables. The comparison between MDD group with their respective treatments and HC at the phylum, class, order, family, genus and species will be performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The partial correlations test (adjusted for sex, BMI and smoking status) will be performed to study the association between the abundance of bacteria and the total PHQ-9 score. Linear regression analysis will be used to evaluate the predominant microbial taxa influencing depression severity at both genus and species levels. The statistical analysis will be performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.22 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA), and the differences will be considered statistically significant when p value <0.05.

Ethics and dissemination

This study is conducted following the Procedure of National Medical Research Register Malaysia. The Medical Research Ethics Committee (MREC), Ministry of Health Malaysia, ratified this study and granted ethical approval to conduct this study (approval number: NMRR ID-22-00893-JVW). The MREC operates in accordance with The International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use and Malaysia Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. All participants are given an information sheet and sign a consent form to participate. Participants have the right to withdraw from the study at any time without an explanation. All information gathered will be used for research purposes only and treated as confidential.

Data will be stored securely by the National Institutes of Health, Ministry of Health for 5 years and distributed after approval from the Director General of Health, Malaysia, adhering to standard protocols and in accordance with relevant laws and regulations. When sharing survey findings through publication or presentation at national and international level, the identities of respondents will be kept confidential. Only bacterial cultures will be preserved for potential future purposes and will not be classified as biospecimens. The datasets produced in this study are not publicly accessible but can be obtained from the corresponding author on reasonable request.
