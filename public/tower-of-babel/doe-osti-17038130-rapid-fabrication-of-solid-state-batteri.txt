 Rapid Fabrication of Solid-State Batteries by Cold Spray Deposition
 Alexander Baker
 24ERD014

Project Overview
Solid-state batteries are attractive for advanced energy storage, particularly in the transportation
sector, due to their superior capacity and improved safety. Years of materials science research
have yielded high-capacity cathode and electrolyte materials, but their potential remains
unrealized due to fabrication challenges stemming from high processing costs and poor
interfacial stability. New production routes are essential to meet climate and energy security
needs. We propose to use cold spray deposition, a coating technique that entrains micron-scale
particles in supersonic gas to direct impacts on substrates, producing dense deposits through
particle deformation and interlocking. Cold spray is widely used for metal coatings and on-site
repairs but has not been deployed for functional materials or oxides. Nevertheless, it is well-
suited for battery fabrication due to its extremely high rate of deposition, ability to bond
dissimilar materials through the high kinetic energy of impact, and capability to coat non-planar
surfaces. This project demonstrated that cold spray deposition is a viable technique to fabricate
thin layers of cathodes (based on LiNi0.6Mn0.2Co0.2O2, NMC 622) and solid-state electrolyte
(based on Li3InCl6, LIC), as well as composite cathodes combining the two materials. We
demonstrated the first solvent-free processing route for these materials, thereby removing a
notable operational challenge for large-scale manufacturing, and confirmed that electrochemical
properties are preserved through the deposition process, with cold-sprayed cathode layers
displaying similar performance characteristics to samples made by conventional tape-casting
Accompanying this, we worked with partners at UC Davis to develop a reliably DEM model for
particle impact and adhesion, accurately capturing the roles of polymer binders and surface
roughness in particle impact and adhesion. This work represents a significant expansion of the
capabilities of cold-spray deposition, and an attractive new material-agnostic fabrication
pathway for solid-state energy storage.

Mission Impact
This project proved out the technical feasibility of cold spray deposition to manufacture solid-
state batteries, providing a rapid fabrication route for thin multilayer energy storage. The ability
to coat complex shapes or bond dissimilar materials could allow more ready integration of
batteries into existing architectures, addressing DOE’s energy and environmental security
missions. Beyond the specifics of deposition of solid-state batteries, the cold spray capabilities
developed here have been deployed in support of multiple lab and DOE efforts. We have shown
successful coating of iron oxide, yttrium oxide, and salts, for applications in stable anodes, molten
metal containment, and thermal storage. The capabilities developed in this project have had
direct utility to other LLNL programs, and will be available for partners across the lab. this project
supported the conversion of one post-doc to staff member, as well as providing career
development opportunities for multiple early-career staff, including training two new staff
members in cold spray deposition methods. Further, we supported the doctoral research of a
student at UC Davis, who graduated in the final month of the project.
