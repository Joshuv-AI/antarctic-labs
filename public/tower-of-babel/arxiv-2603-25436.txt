Real-Time Wiener Deconvolution for feature reconstruction in JUNO
X. Yan

Abstract
In particle physics, experiments generate substantial amounts of data that can be difficult to process without preliminary scaling. To avoid losing potentially crucial data, experimental collaborations are studying novel techniques for real-time data processing to extract features for further physics analysis. A common approach, especially in neutrino physics, is to use FPGAs for data acquisition and pre-processing. This paper presents an advanced Real-Time Wiener deconvolution algorithm designed to leverage the processing capabilities of the FPGA integrated into the readout boards of the Jiangmen Underground Neutrino Observatory (JUNO). The goal is to enable real-time reconstruction of the signal generated by photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) when neutrino interactions are detected. By exploiting online reconstruction of the signal generated by PMTs, we expect to improve the detection of low-energy depositions, such as those produced by transient astrophysical phenomena. These depositions are usually not saved because of the significant background that affects the low end of the energy spectrum, which would result in a large trigger rate, hence a large amount of data required for storage. This paper presents the features of the algorithm, including its ability to manage high-throughput data streams with minimal latency, adaptability, and resilience in discerning the characteristics of input data. Performance is evaluated on a JUNO electronic board. This study further demonstrates the potential of FPGA-based solutions for neutrino physics.

In experimental particle physics, there is a growing trend toward collecting and analyzing large volumes of data. This is particularly relevant at the intensity frontier, where large detector volumes are often required to compensate for low cross-sections and low fluxes. Neutrino experiments based on technology are a prime example, where large detection volumes correspond to a high count of readout channels. Beyond handling I/O, resources can perform signal pre-processing and filtering. This enables the extraction of relevant features from the output signal (hereafter referred to as the

Jiangmen Underground Neutrino Observatory

The
[
5
]
is a 20 kton multipurpose underground liquid scintillator detector designed to study neutrino interactions by detecting scintillation and Cherenkov light. It employs a
dual
photodetection system consisting of 17,612 20-inch and 25,600 3-inch . The operate within a wide dynamic range, spanning from a single photoelectron to several hundred within a
1~\mu s
readout window. Each group of three connects to a readout board known as a , which comprises six units and an AMD Kintex 7 . As the use a different readout strategy, they are excluded from this study; consequently, the term will hereafter be used exclusively to refer to the .

Each connects to two amplifiers with distinct gains, producing two output signals per . Each signal is sampled at 1 GS/s and digitized with a resolution of 14 bits using a dedicated . Every waveform sample is stored as two 8-bit words. The digital output is parallelized into eight data streams at 125 MHz via a to align with the clock. Consequently, for a given , the processes eight waveform samples per clock cycle—each sample being 16 bits wide—resulting in a total bus width of 128 bits. The primary tasks of the are: (i) to receive digitized data from the , to pack them, and to transmit them to the ; and (ii) to generate trigger primitives when waveforms exceed a defined threshold. These primitives are subsequently evaluated by the Back-End electronics to issue a global trigger, which initiates the readout of all active channels. A more comprehensive description of operations and JUNO electronics is provided in ref.
[
14
]
.

Since is responsible for waveform acquisition, trigger generation, and basic data processing, a large fraction of its resources have already been allocated. The goal of this study is to improve trigger efficiency, hit identification, and total charge reconstruction using the limited resources that remain available. The baseline operation of the electronics yields two distinct data streams: (i) , representing a stream of fully digitized signals segmented into readout windows—each window comprising 1008 samples—and recorded only upon event validation by the ; and (ii) , a trigger-less stream of Time and Charge pairs for each hit, which are continuously reconstructed and saved.
The readout system stores the full WF only when the issues a
global trigger
, which means that the number of trigger primitives, namely PMTs firing, is above a predefined threshold.
Storing all —each worth 2 kB—for all events would be infeasible, because the trigger rate induced by cosmic muons is roughly 4 Hz, and storing roughly 17,000 per event yields 33.2 MB/event, i.e., approximately 11 TB per day.
In contrast, serve as a compression of . Whenever a is detected, its charge and timestamp are calculated (currently through the algorithm, described later in this section) and sent directly to the . This allows continuous recording of single activity—even in the absence of a globally validated event—without exceeding bandwidth limits.
The method defines a hit whenever the amplitude of the waveform exceeds a threshold and computes its charge as the integral of all samples exceeding the said threshold. Each hit yields a pair, where the timestamp is defined as the time the threshold was exceeded.
The algorithm is able to disentangle two photoelectrons close in time only if the waveform goes back below the threshold between the first and second hit. Consequently, if a group of hits is too close in time, i.e., if the time distance between two hits is shorter than the width of the pulse generated by a single hit (roughly 40 ns in this study), the pileup can cause the algorithm to merge them into a single TQ pair, thereby degrading the reconstruction performance. This is ’s main limitation, which directly affects the energy evaluation of any interaction taking place in the detector. The reconstruction algorithm presented in this paper is intended to address primarily the limitation mentioned above.

Real-Time Wiener Deconvolution

When the output signal of a exceeds a threshold, we define the resulting charge as a
hit
. The objective of this study is to reconstruct this hit charge in real-time on an . An effective approach is to employ Wiener Deconvolution
[
17
]
, a method based on the Wiener filter (or optimum filter) designed to maximize the of a signal when both signal and noise characteristics are known.

To minimize the resources required for this task, we utilize a implementation of Wiener Deconvolution, hereafter referred to as . The adoption of filters is driven by the need for a low-latency algorithm with minimal resource consumption. Due to limitations on local storage and Flash sampling frequencies ranging from
10^{8}
to
10^{9}
S/s, the must process data with a latency of the order of
100
ns. Furthermore, neutrino experiments generally employ a vast array of to achieve the necessary photo-coverage, which requires a correspondingly large number of . This scale forces a trade-off between cost and computational power; consequently, the majority of resources are allocated to data acquisition, leaving a limited budget for advanced signal processing.

The implementation of comprises four steps: (i) Estimation of the template; (ii) Characterization of the noise; (iii) Computation of the filter frequency response; and (iv) Derivation of the filters.

4.1
Single photoelectron template estimation

The first and most critical step is to obtain a robust characterization of the acquisition system. In this context,
system
refers to all elements that influence the signal of interest, including the processing apparatus and the acquisition instruments. For complex detectors such as , this presents a challenge, as each exhibits a unique response. Moreover, different types are often combined; for instance, the Central Detector utilizes approximately 75% from NNVT and 25% Dynode from Hamamatsu

W(f)=\frac{SNR(f)}{SNR(f)+1},\quad\text{where}\quad SNR(f)=\frac{SPSD(f)}{NPSD(f)}.

(4.1)

The Wiener filter is approximated with a Type I filter
[

]
using the firls method from the Python library scipy.signal
[

4.3.2
Deconvolution filter

The Deconvolution filter is computed as the reciprocal of the template’s frequency amplitude spectrum

D(f)=\frac{1}{H(f)},

(4.2)

It is converted into a Type III filter using the remez method from scipy.signal, an implementation of the Remez exchange algorithm

5
Real-Time Wiener Deconvolution implementation in JUNO

In this section, we present the implementation of the algorithm within the , optimized to enhance the reconstruction of for .
The signal processing pipeline operates sequentially: first, the waveform baseline is evaluated. Next, an over-threshold block identifies hit candidates and subtracts the baseline. The algorithm is then applied to the baseline-subtracted signal, followed by a peak finder that reconstructs the final pairs.
This section focuses on the high-level functionality of these blocks, while
Appendix B
provides a detailed description of the specific implementation.

5.1
Baseline Tracker

A positive voltage supply was selected for the because the photocathode is in contact with the water; consequently, when a photon strikes the , the resulting waveform shows a negative spike relative to the baseline, and a small fluctuation with opposite polarity called
overshoot

[
13
]
. To fully capture the whole signal, namely to accomodate both polarities in the 14-bit range of the Flash , an offset is applied during waveform digitization.
A direct consequence of this configuration is the necessity to characterize the baseline of the Flash output
to monitor its evolution and account for baseline drift caused by both the power supply and aging.

The Baseline Tracker is designed to follow baseline variations while ignoring fast transients caused by . As shown in
Figure 6
, at the beginning of the acquisition, the baseline is initialized to the average of the amplitude
a_{i}
of the first eight waveform samples. As each subsequent packet of eight samples arrives from the , its average
\langle a\rangle
is calculated. The algorithm then determines whether this value is consistent with a baseline fluctuation or indicates the presence of a . If
\langle a\rangle
falls within the interval around the previous baseline
b
defined by the red dotted lines in
Figure 6

b
and
\langle a\rangle
. The delta variation parameter
\Delta b
, which defines this interval, can be tuned to track the baseline accurately; currently it is set to 3 counts, which matches the expected average noise level of a channel
[
12
]
. To prevent the baseline from latching onto an incorrect value due to poor initialization (e.g., if the first eight samples coincide with a hit) or other anomalies, the baseline is reset every 1000 samples to the average of the current eight samples.

Once the baseline has been found, it is subtracted from each waveform sample so that when there is no activity the sample amplitude fluctuates around zero. At the same time we invert the waveform polarity, in order to have the PE-induced spikes to be positive.

5.2
Over-threshold Check

Each waveform sample is compared to a threshold. If a sample exceeds this threshold, a trigger primitive is generated, and the algorithm transitions to the charge reconstruction state. The algorithm continues to reconstruct the charge as long as the samples remain above the threshold, after which it returns to an idle state. The trigger signals generated by this block are also utilized by the to identify events that require the storage of .

Subsequently, after both filters are applied, the precise peak is located within the time window indicated by the coarse identification. This process ensures that a search is initiated whenever the waveform exceeds the threshold, but a charge value is output only if the detected peak resembles a . RTL block diagram of the TQ_reco entity. The raw ADC data is processed in parallel paths for baseline estimation and threshold checking, followed by filtering and peak extraction. Shift registers ensure data synchronization across the pipeline.

The implementation of the reconstruction logic is hosted within the top-level VHDL entity TQ_reco. This module orchestrates the data path, manages the synchronization between pipelined stages, and interfaces with the system’s buffering resources via FIFO memory.

B.1
Top-Level Architecture (TQ_reco)

The TQ_reco entity instantiates four primary sub-modules corresponding to the algorithmic stages:

•

baseline_finder: Estimates the signal baseline.

•

over_check: Performs dynamic thresholding and baseline subtraction.

•

filter_wrapper: Wraps the Wiener and Deconvolution filters.

•

peak: Extracts the charge and fine timing.

The module operates on a 128-bit wide data bus (ADC_raw_data), processing eight 16-bit samples per clock cycle. To ensure temporal consistency across the pipelined stages, shift registers (sr_hit, sr_stop, sr_baseline) are used to delay control signals and metadata, aligning them with the processing latency of the filters and peak finder. The module also implements a Packing Output Logic to generate 128-bit packets, containing information about the input channel and the characteristics of the hit.

B.2
Baseline and Thresholding

The baseline_finder module calculates a moving average of the input signal. It utilizes an accumulator (sum) to compute the average of the incoming 128-bit data frame. The logic includes a stability check: the average is updated only if the current signal variation remains within a specified delta (baseline_d) of the previous baseline, ensuring that pulses do not skew the baseline estimation.

The over_check entity receives this baseline and performs two critical functions:

•

Thresholding:
It calculates a dynamic threshold (baseline - thrd_value_rel) and generates a bit-mask (overthrd) identifying which of the 8 samples in the current frame are active.

•

Conditioning:
It subtracts the baseline from the raw samples to produce ADC_check, which is passed to the filtering stage.

B.3
Real-Time Waveform Deconvolution (filter)

The filter wrapper encapsulates the core signal processing engines: wiener_filter and deconv_filter. These are implemented as parallel systolic filters to handle the high data throughput: their implementation is handled using the Vivado LogiCORE
TM
IP FIR Compiler
[
4
]
.

•

Data Path:
The data flows sequentially from the Wiener filter (denoising) to the Deconvolution filter (ballistic deficit correction). Bit resizing logic is applied between stages to manage bit-growth and maintain the 16-bit sample width.

•

Configuration:
A finite state machine (FSM) manages the reloading of filter coefficients. The FSM transitions through idle, load, and config states, allowing dynamic reconfiguration of the taps via an internal configuration FIFO (fir_config_fifo) without halting the rest of the system.

B.4
Peak Finding(peak)

The peak entity identifies the maximum amplitude (charge) and its precise timing within the processed waveform.

•

Pipeline:
A multi-stage pipeline (peak_find1 to peak_find3) compares neighboring samples to isolate the local maximum.

•

Pattern Matching:
To filter out false positives, the identify process analyzes the vicinity of the maximum. It confirms a valid peak only if the samples strictly increase before and decrease after the maximum, effectively applying a shape constraint.

•

Output Generation:
When a peak is validated, the module outputs the charge and a fine-grained hit_time (sample index within the frame).

B.5
Output Logic

Finally, the TQ_reco top-level calculates the absolute timestamp start_time_int by combining the coarse system timestamp (time_cnt), the pipeline delays, and the fine hit_time. This information, together with charge and baseline values, is packed into a 128-bit pair and written to the output FIFO.
