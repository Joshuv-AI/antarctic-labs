On topological frustration and graphene magnonics


Abstract
The graph-theoretic topological frustration is a peculiar situation on a finite piece of the honeycomb lattice that prevents a full pairwise coupling of the lattice sites via nearest neighbor links, even when the total number of sites is an even number. This type of frustration is inherent for organic molecules that are classified as concealed non-Kekulean hydrocarbons, representing peculiar diradicals. Here we show that this topological frustration persists in 2D systems based on honeycomb lattice. Such systems exhibit fully flat electronic energy bands located at the Fermi level. Therefore, 2D ultimately flat bands can be systematically and predictably constructed for graphene monolayer nanomeshes. These systems are prone to antiferromagnetic ordering and hybrid spin-wave excitations mixing weak ferromagnetic and strong antiferromagnetic features, which could pave the way towards low-power, compact, and ultrafast organic spintronics with near room-temperature operation.

2.1
Graph theory preliminaries and settings

The abstract graph theory deals with objects and the connections between them
[
97
]
. The objects are called vertices and are depicted as points. These points can represent atomic sites in a lattice or a molecule. The connections between vertices are called edges, which are normally represented by lines, physically standing for chemical bonds. Then a graph

and

for a maximum matching

[

Although abstract graph theory can be mapped onto real geometrical structures, in general, it does not care about geometry. For example, it does not care about edge crossings at all. Therefore, the same graph can be presented by an infinite variety of pictorial representations. Such details, however, become more important in the topological graph theory that considers graphs as embedding into some spaces, for instance, into some orientable surfaces

As one moves from the plane to other orientable surfaces, it becomes difficult to track the validity of even obvious statements let alone a more complex one such as existence of topological frustration.

In a less technical way, we have seen that graph theory provides a comprehensive formalism and a
reliable set of theorems
for tracking and handling topological frustration in bipartite graphs, including those composed of the integer number of hexagons of a honeycomb lattice.
Specifically, topological frustration boils down to a collection of sites that form an obstruction for the lattice site pairwise coupling. This collection of sites stays invariant if the piece of lattice in question that forms the nanostructure is extended further into the lattice, keeping the even-odd parity balance with respect to structural components surrounding the obstruction. This freedom allows one to extend nearly any finite hydrocarbon into a two dimensional graphene nanomesh predicting a fully flat band.

2.3
Comparison with other systems

In principle, flat bands are achievable with topological frustration of class I. Such systems can also contain an even number of sites in the system, but for the bipartite lattice, this means a sublattice imbalance
N_{A}-N_{B}\neq 0

[
154
,
155
,
100
]
. From the graph theory point of view, this is a less peculiar case because the vertices of the each independent subset of any bipartite graph are disjoint by definition, which makes the absence of the perfect matching obvious. In terms of condensed matter theory, the number of flat bands in this case is equal to the imbalance between the sublattices
[
99
,
17
,
112
]
. According to Ovchinnikov’s rule
[
101
]
and Lieb’s theorem
[
78
]
, such systems must be ferromagnetic with the non-zero spin ground state
S=|N_{A}-N_{B}|/2
.
In this respect, it is worth noting that the topologically frustrated systems of class II, for which
N_{A}=N_{B}
, ensure the antiferromagnetic ground state. This may be more appealing in some cases. For example, the standard phase diagram for unconventional superconductors features an antiferromagnetic region (separating two superconducting domes)
[
77
,
15
]
. Thus, topologically frustrated systems with antiferromagnetic ground state may have a link to such superconductivity.

In general, the flat band is not a necessary condition for magnetism. Magnetic orderings can arise even in systems without topological frustration of either class, i.e. I or class II. From a chemistry point of view, Clar’s aromatic sextet can play a key role
[
73
,
170
]
.
From physical viewpoint, the level of modeling does matter. For example, magnetically ordered states have been reported for 2D triangulene lattices without sublattice imbalance,
N_{A}=N_{B}

[
17
]
. Although in nearest-neighbor approximation such systems can be formally attributed to class II topological frustration (even though they do not consist of full hexagons only, i.e. do not comply with the above-adopted definition of a benzenoid graph), with interactions between the 3rd nearest neighbors, they correspond to more general graphs that host perfect matchings. Physically, relatively large molecular units connected by single covalent bonds into
covalent organic frameworks
represent a case of sparsely connected system which leads to energy bands that are nearly dispersionless as energy levels. Introducing longer range interactions (or chemical modifications) increases the coupling between the molecular units, adding to the band dispersion turning such systems into Kagome lattices
[
56
,
168
,
164
]
. However, more densely connected molecular units form
nanomeshes
, which have dispersive bands even in the nearest neighbors approximation. Ensuring full flatness in the latter case is non-trivial and this is where topological frustration is handy as in our topologically frustrated GNM. When a flat band is featured in the system, magnetization is ensured and it persists to the limit of
U\rightarrow 0
as in the case of Fig.
3
(e). The 3rd order nearest neighbors can also play their role, but their relative contribution is less significant for densely connected units.

Thus, the general criteria for the topological frustration of class II are useful as guidance for densely connected systems, where nearest-neighbor interactions are dominant. These criteria provide a tool for designing materials with tailored properties. However, given recent interest in 2D carbon allotropes
[
35
,
83
]
, it could also be appealing to investigate whether topological frustration (perhaps in some generalized form) can be inherent for periodic boundary conditions rather than embedded in GNMs as in our case, and whether flat bands can arise in those cases, if any.

3
Conclusion

In summary, it has been demonstrated that graph-theoretic topological frustration can persist on a torus. This gives rise not only to zero-energy states in toroidal structures such as nanotori but also to flat energy bands at the Fermi level in 1D nanotubes and 2D nanomeshes. The latter exhibits emergent metal-free quantum magnetism. Thus, topological frustration provides a tool for designing and systematically studying strongly correlated exotic phenomena in monolayer graphene avoiding twistronics with bi-and tri-layers or multilayer stackings in few-layer systems. Since electronic flat bands are expected to result in an elevated superconductivity transition temperature
[

,
151
,
71
,
72
,
110
,
64
,
152
,
109
,
96
]
, the superconducting phase diagram shall be a subject of future work for such topologically frustrated systems. A totally different but equally appealing can be a research line on the topological frustration in quantum spin liquids and dimerized magnets, i.e. in systems, where each site (atom) of the initial lattice carries an intrinsic spin.
