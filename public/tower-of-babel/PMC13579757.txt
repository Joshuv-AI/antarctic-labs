Modeling the impact of war, interventions, and funding on malaria transmission dynamics in Sudan
Authors: Mohamed Salah Alhaj, Farai Nyabadza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria poses a significant public health risk worldwide, and the infection results from the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito. Many malaria-endemic regions face instability, often driven by wars, heightening the risk of infectious disease transmission. Malaria funding and targeted prevention are essential for reducing and eventually eradicating transmission. This article focuses on developing a compartmental mathematical model to study malaria infection dynamics, incorporating the effects of malaria prevention, funding, and war on health systems. We evaluate the model’s basic reproduction number and identify equilibrium points. Our model analysis provides a forward bifurcation, indicating that malaria can be controlled if , and we show cases of backward bifurcation when the threshold . We fit our model to the collected data and perform a sensitivity analysis of and numerical simulations. Our results demonstrate that increased funding and effective prevention reduce malaria transmission, whereas war leads to higher transmission rates. Introduction

Malaria remains one of the most significant global threats to human health, with transmission occurring through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes [1]. Although the disease is widespread, sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate burden of global malaria cases and deaths. Compounding this challenge, many malaria-endemic regions are experiencing ongoing armed conflict, which severely undermines healthcare infrastructure and disrupts malaria control efforts [2].

Sustainable funding is a critical pillar for the continuity of malaria prevention programs, relying heavily on both national governments and international donors. However, warfare often leads to a reduction or complete cessation of such funding, thereby impeding intervention strategies. For instance, the High Burden to High Impact initiative was halted in Sudan due to the prevailing conflict [2].

Another major obstacle to malaria control is the presence of asymptomatic carriers—individuals who carry the parasite without exhibiting clinical symptoms but are still capable of transmitting the infection to mosquitoes [3]. These carriers complicate detection and surveillance efforts, posing a persistent challenge to eradication programs. Also, the multifaceted impacts of warfare on infectious diseases, including malaria, are well-documented. These impacts include increased transmission rates, disruption of control programs, and the creation of environments conducive to disease spread in conflict zones [4, 5]. The prevailing conflict in Sudan has severely compromised public health delivery, thereby exacerbating the transmission of infectious pathogens, including malaria [6].

Sudan continues to face a significant malaria burden, as evidenced by the three million-plus cases recorded in 2023 [2]. This endemicity is driven largely by Plasmodium falciparum, and to a lesser extent, P. vivax [7, 8]. Although the infection cycle is primarily seasonal, researchers have noted its presence in urban centers and irrigated agricultural zones [7, 9]. Specifically, the Sennar State, Gezira, and Shendi areas have been the focus of recent epidemiological investigations [8–10]. To combat the disease, various control interventions have been implemented, including insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying (IRS), larval source management (LSM), environmental management (EM), and artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) [7, 9].

A substantial body of literature has employed compartmental mathematical models to investigate malaria transmission dynamics [11–14]. These models have been instrumental in evaluating the effectiveness of various prevention strategies [12–14], and in assessing the role of asymptomatic carriers in sustaining transmission [15, 16]. Furthermore, recent studies have introduced compartments to represent awareness and control interventions [17–19]. A key insight from these models is that malaria transmission can be effectively controlled when the basic reproduction number, , is reduced below unity. Conversely, prevalence increases when see for instance [11, 14, 18, 19].

Chapwanya et al. [20] explored the impact of warfare on Ebola virus dynamics in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), using a model that distinguishes between conflict and non-conflict zones. Inspired by this approach, our study incorporates additional compartments to quantify the effects of warfare on healthcare infrastructure, malaria control interventions, and funding levels. The primary aim of this study is to construct a mathematical model that captures the interactions between warfare, malaria funding, and control interventions, and to evaluate their collective impact on malaria transmission. Specifically, the study seeks to quantify how conflict disrupts malaria control efforts, assess the influence of funding fluctuations on program sustainability, and to identify strategies to mitigate malaria resurgence in conflict-affected regions.

Model formulation

We formulate a mathematical model by modifying the models found in [15, 18, 19], by introducing the influence of warfare, malaria prevention, and malaria funding. In the model, the human population is subdivided into the following compartments: for susceptible humans, for asymptomatic malaria infected humans, for symptomatic malaria infected humans, and for recovered humans at time t. Meanwhile, the vector population is subdivided into the compartments: for susceptible vectors and for infected vectors at time t. Further, we consider the level of malaria prevention and the level of malaria funding using the compartments c and f, respectively. The compartment c depends on malaria control interventions, as shown in [17, 18], which target the reduction of both malaria incidence and vector density. The compartment f shows the level of malaria funding from different sources, such as governments and donors [2]. The influence of the level of malaria interventions is considered through the expression in the forces of infection functions.

Moreover, malaria prevention that focuses on reducing the vector population is given by the term γ c(t). In this model, the interventions, specifically insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spray (IRS), and larval source management (LSM), are designed to minimize human-vector contact and lower the overall density of the malaria vector population. Additionally, we can incorporate media such as TV and radio to increase awareness of malaria infection. Furthermore, the influence driven by the allocated funding for malaria control strategies is considered in our model.

Based on the method shown in [19], we introduced the compartment w to represent the level of warfare’s influence on the healthcare system. The impact of warfare is modeled by defining a set of functions for warfare influencing the human treatment by the inability to access healthcare facilities, shortage, and cut to medical supply chains, and armed attacks on healthcare facilities and workers [6], , represents warfare influence on malaria prevention such as the stop of the High Burden to High Impact (HBHI) program and a shortage in malaria prevention. is for warfare influence in malaria funding, such as the change of malaria funding from the HBHI program to another response [2].

In our model, we considered the direct and indirect impact of warfare on the healthcare system and malaria funding, which could affect the entire population under study, as recorded malaria transmission cases in Al-Gadaraf State and its conflict-free areas [21]. However, ignoring the influence of the displaced population poses a limitation in our model, since they are moved to an environment that enhances vector-borne diseases, including malaria [22]. Therefore, we could suggest another model to consider the influence of the displaced population as a separate population, or as a factor increasing malaria prevalence by a compartment representing the displacement of the populations.

We have limitations related to the WHO’s data and the fminsearch algorithm. The WHO’s data has many restrictions, such as being annual-based data, which neglects the seasonal pattern of malaria, and the data only accounts for symptomatic malaria cases and ignores asymptomatic malaria infections, which occur in the malaria-endemic regions. Furthermore, the data depends on the healthcare system capacity and reports, meaning many malaria cases could be unreported and thus introduce uncertainty. The fminsearch algorithm has estimation bias because it depends on the initial values of the parameters, as it is based on Nelder-Mead’s simplex. It converges to local minima, resulting in single parameter values, and ignores the uncertainty of the parameters.

The simulations highlight a critical dichotomy: effective malaria prevention and adequate funding are essential to suppress malaria transmission, whereas the impact of warfare cripples health systems and amplifies the risk of disease spread. Moreover, the stability of the model’s (1) endemic state is established.

Consequently, effective management of malaria in conflict-affected regions necessitates three approaches: maintaining robust funding for control interventions, ensuring a uniform distribution of malaria prevention, and implementing measures to shield health systems from the destabilizing effects of warfare.

Malaria prevention and funding play a critical role in controlling malaria dynamics, since an increase in malaria funding results in the allocation of malaria prevention to the target population. Warfare impacts malaria prevention and funding as part of the healthcare system. To mitigate the impact of warfare on malaria dynamics, we recommend the following for policymakers: launching mobile health units and telemedicine services, decentralizing malaria interventions and medical supply chains, involving the local community in following malaria cases, applying the low-level malaria treatment and interventions, moving the main center location for malaria treatment and prevention to mini-centers, which are far away from armed conflict, and securing new sources for malaria funding. Furthermore, the strategies applied to control the impact of warfare led to an increase in the provision of malaria prevention and treatment. Finally, utilizing media channels such as television and radio is crucial for educating the population about malaria transmission.

Discussion and conclusion

Sudan is a malaria-endemic country where ongoing armed conflict has significantly increased the malaria burden. This paper presents a modified SIRS-SI mathematical model designed to analyze how warfare, prevention, and funding affect transmission dynamics. We include specific state variables and time-dependent functions to capture these influences, ensuring that all model solutions remain positive and bounded. Following the derivation of the basic reproduction number and an assessment of equilibrium stability, we show that the system exhibits a forward bifurcation. Notably, our analysis also accounts for the occurrence of backward bifurcations, which have critical implications for disease control.

The numerical analysis of the model involves fitting the model to data, together with a sensitivity analysis of the basic reproduction number, , and numerical simulations. We examined the impact of the uncertainty of malaria reporting, and we found that it is critical to avoid underreporting of malaria cases for effective control strategies. The sensitivity results for indicate that reducing human–vector contact, sustaining malaria control interventions, ensuring adequate malaria funding, and limiting the effects of warfare are essential for mitigating malaria transmission. Numerical simulations were carried out to examine the influence of the key parameters associated with warfare, malaria prevention, and malaria funding, namely ξ, ϕ, and η, on the various model compartments. These findings are complemented by a phase portrait illustrating the stability of the endemic equilibrium. Overall, the results suggest that effectively reducing malaria prevalence during wartime requires consistent malaria prevention measures and funding, along with minimizing the disruptive impact of warfare on the healthcare system. In addition, recommendations are proposed to alleviate the effects of armed conflict on healthcare infrastructure and malaria dynamics. A scenario for controlling malaria infection when is provided.

To conclude, our study evaluates how warfare, prevention strategies, and funding levels shape malaria spread. By exploring these key drivers, we identify effective ways to manage malaria outbreaks and propose solutions to mitigate the negative impact of armed conflict on the healthcare system. Moving forward, this study paves the way for optimal control modeling to quantify the financial demands of malaria prevention amidst warfare. By highlighting the necessity of prevention and public education, our research supports the evidence presented by [18, 19]. Furthermore, regarding the challenges posed by conflict, we offer detailed recommendations consistent with [20] that prioritize rapid recovery and systemic responses to healthcare infrastructure collapse. Recommendations to mitigate the impacts of warfare on malaria dynamics are provided and include maintaining malaria prevention and funding, using mobile clinics and telemedicine, decentralizing medical supply chains, and strengthening local community engagement to track malaria cases and provide treatments.

Our results show that it is possible to control malaria dynamics when there is no migration of infected humans, since it was not considered in this study, and we expected it would hinder the ability to control malaria. While the influence of funding increases in response to symptomatic malaria cases only, the underreporting of malaria cases leads to ineffective control measures.

While our model provides valuable insights into malaria transmission dynamics under conflict, the assumption of homogeneous mixing of the human population presents a limitation of our model. Our model did not consider the age structure of the human population or the role of the displaced humans during armed conflict. Future model refinements could incorporate spatial dynamics to distinguish transmission in refugee camps, urban centers, and rural areas. Finally, extending the model to incorporate optimal control theory, as suggested in our discussion, would enable the quantitative evaluation of cost-effective intervention strategies under resource constraints typical of conflict settings.
