Persistent increased risk of renal replacement therapy following COVID-19: a 2-year follow-up study in Japan using propensity score matching and inverse probability censoring weighting
Authors: Daisuke Miyamori, Shingo Fukuma, Kotaro Ikeda, Daisuke Haratake, Shuhei Yoshida, Masanori Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis retrospective cohort study investigated the long-term risk of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) following COVID-19 using a nationwide database of Japanese medical claims.MethodsPropensity score matching was utilised to form a cohort of individuals with COVID-19 and a non-infected control group using data from the National Claims Database encompassing six prefectures in Japan. The primary outcome measured was the initiation of renal replacement therapy (dialysis or kidney transplantation) after the index month of the study period. Cox proportional hazards models incorporating inverse probability of censoring weighting were employed to estimate HRs for the association between COVID-19 and ESKD.ResultsA total of 3 073 150 pairs were matched in this study. During follow-up, COVID-19 was associated with a significantly increased instantaneous risk of the composite ESKD outcome (HR 2.79, 95% CI 2.56 to 3.04). The risk was increased for haemodialysis initiation (HR 2.77, 95% CI 2.54 to 3.02) and peritoneal dialysis (HR 5.16, 95% CI 1.93 to 13.75), whereas the estimate for kidney transplantation was imprecise (HR 5.20, 95% CI 0.62 to 43.27). Subgroup analyses showed broadly consistent associations across age, sex, hypertension, diabetes and COVID-19 severity.ConclusionThese findings suggest that COVID-19 may have sustained adverse effects on kidney outcomes, supporting close post-acute renal monitoring and early risk stratification in high-risk patients.

Introduction

While initial attention regarding COVID-19 focused on its respiratory symptoms, it has become clear that after infection, the disease can cause long-term complications affecting multiple organs, including the cardiovascular and nervous systems.1 There are also concerns about the impact on kidney function, and a link between COVID-19 and the onset of chronic kidney disease (CKD) has been suggested.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) frequently occurs in severe COVID-19, with reports indicating that AKI developed in 32%–57% of hospitalised patients. Furthermore, patients who develop AKI during the acute phase have a significantly increased risk of subsequent progression to CKD.2 However, there is an ongoing debate about whether patients who did not develop AKI during their initial infection may also have an increased risk of declining kidney function after COVID-19. For example, a large matched cohort study in the USA reported that the incidence of newly developed advanced CKD within 12 months after COVID-19 was approximately two times as high as that among patients with influenza. Furthermore, studies focusing on kidney failure (renal replacement therapy (RRT)) showed that patients with severe COVID-19 who required hospitalisation had a notably higher risk of progressing to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) than individuals without infection, whereas mild COVID-19 not requiring hospitalisation did not increase the long-term risk of worsening kidney function.3

However, evidence is still limited as to whether contracting COVID-19 independently increases the risk of developing ESKD, and data are particularly lacking in Asian populations. Japan records a very large number of patients undergoing dialysis, with approximately 347 000 patients on long-term dialysis (2781 per million population) as of the end of 2022.4 If COVID-19 increases the risk of developing ESKD, its impact on the healthcare system would be considerable. However, randomising COVID-19 status is ethically and practically difficult; therefore, this study used propensity score matching (PSM) with inverse probability censoring weighting (IPCW) to validate the estimated effect sizes. This method allows the simulation of a hypothetical randomised controlled trial using observational study data, enabling a more rigorous assessment of the causal relationship between COVID-19 and the risk of developing ESKD. Therefore, in this study, we investigated whether COVID-19 increases the risk of ESKD (initiation of RRT via dialysis or kidney transplantation) using a nationwide Japanese medical claims database.

Methods

Data source and study design

This retrospective observational study (retrospective cohort study) used a nationwide Japanese medical claims database. Data from six prefectures, Okayama, Hiroshima, Hyogo, Osaka, Kyoto and Tottori, were used. These prefectures have total population of 25 million, accounting for approximately 20% of Japan’s population. In Japan’s universal health insurance system, all data from the use of medical institutions are included in the database. The database includes medical information for patients of all ages who received care between January 2015 and December 2022. It consists of anonymised medical fee statement information covering patients’ basic demographics, diagnostic names (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, codes), prescribed medications, procedures and surgeries.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was the progression to ESKD. The occurrence of ESKD was determined by confirming the initiation of RRT after the index month. Haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis and kidney transplantation were defined as primary outcomes, and the occurrence of any of these was defined as a composite outcome. Specifically, outcome events were identified using reimbursement procedure codes restricted to those specifically applied at the initiation of chronic maintenance dialysis or kidney transplantation. Additional surrogate outcomes included vascular access insertion, plasma exchange therapy, new disease code registration and new prescriptions for relevant medications. (The list of codes used for dialysis initiation and kidney transplantation is provided in online supplemental table 1).

Study population, group composition and design

Figure 1 shows the process of extracting research participants. Two groups were established: a COVID-19 infected group (exposed group) and a non-infected group (control group). The exposed group consisted of patients who visited a medical institution between January 2020 and December 2022 and received their first reimbursement for the diagnosis and treatment of COVID-19. The control group was defined as patients who, in the same calendar month as their matched cases, had no record of COVID-19 up to that point and were thus considered non-infected in the index month. Each control was assigned the same index month as their matched case to ensure the exact temporal alignment of the data. Patients who had reached ESKD at baseline and had already undergone maintenance dialysis or kidney transplantation before the index month were excluded. Furthermore, only patients with at least 1 year of continuous insurance coverage prior to the index date were included to ensure an adequate observation history for the covariate assessment.

Process of extracting participants for this study. From the group of patients registered as of January 2020, excluding those already using renal replacement therapy, PSM was performed between patients who contracted COVID-19 each month and those who had not at that time. From the remaining group, matching between the newly infected and non-infected groups was repeated for each subsequent month until November 2022. Follow-up was conducted until December 2022, or the 24-month study period. Among the non-infected group, those who contracted COVID-19 during the observation period were censored, and the follow-up period was weighted using inverse probability of censoring weighting method. CCI, Charlson Comorbidity Index; ESRD, end-stage renal disease; PSM, propensity score matching.

Propensity score matching

To address potential confounding, 1:1 PSM was performed between the exposed and control groups. Propensity scores representing the probability of COVID-19 were estimated using logistic regression. Covariates included baseline characteristics (age and sex), medical history (presence of major chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease and CKD as well as the Charlson comorbidity index), medication history (prescriptions for chronic disease medications, defined as four or more records within the year prior to the index date; detailed drug codes are provided in online supplemental table 2), frequency of outpatient visits outside regular hours, prefecture of the most frequently visited medical institution and number of consultations. Matching was conducted using the nearest-neighbour method with a calliper width of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardised mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD of <0.1 considered indicative of an adequate balance.

Inverse probability of censoring weighting

To carry out unbiased estimation, we defined follow-up from the index month and censored observations at the earliest of the following: occurrence of the composite renal outcome, subsequent COVID-19 infection in the control group, end of follow-up (December 2022) or a 24-month period. To address potential informative censoring, particularly when control patients subsequently contract COVID-19, stabilised IPCW was applied. The censoring process was modelled using flexible parametric survival models (stpm2), with a denominator model incorporating baseline covariates (COVID-19 status, sex, age category, Charlson comorbidity index, comorbidities and healthcare utilisation) and a numerator model that included only the COVID-19 status. The stabilised weights were derived as the ratio of the numerator to the denominator of the survival probabilities.58 To reduce the influence of extreme weights, truncation was applied at the 2nd and 98th percentiles.

Statistical analysis

Survival analysis was performed to compare the risk of ESKD onset between the matched COVID-19 and non-infected groups. Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed and compared using the log-rank test. The primary analysis used Cox proportional hazards regression models weighted by the standardised IPCW, yielding HRs and 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption for the unweighted Cox model was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals and a global proportional hazards test. Given evidence suggesting potential non-proportionality of hazards, period-specific HRs were also estimated by splitting follow-up time into 0–6, 6–12 and 12–24 months and fitting an IPCW-weighted Cox model including an interaction term between COVID-19 status and follow-up period.

Furthermore, several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of our results. First, analyses were repeated using IPCW weights, trimming the extreme weights at the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles, and without IPCW weights. Second, to reduce reverse causation, a landmark analysis that excluded the first 2 months after the index date was conducted. Third, negative control outcomes unrelated to kidney disease pathways were examined, including cataract extraction, as an auxiliary negative control.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study.

The proportional hazards test based on scaled Schoenfeld residuals suggested that the association between COVID-19 and the composite ESKD outcome varied over time (p=0.0045). Visual inspection of the scaled Schoenfeld residual plot also suggested that the effect of COVID-19 was strongest during the early follow-up period and gradually attenuated over time (online supplemental figure 1). Therefore, additional period-specific analyses were conducted. The association between COVID-19 and the composite ESKD outcome was the strongest during the first 6 months of follow-up, and the interaction between COVID-19 status and follow-up period was statistically significant (p<0.001) (online supplemental table 5). These findings are clinically plausible, as the impact of COVID-19 on kidney outcomes may be most pronounced during the early post-acute phase.

Discussion

This study showed that a history of COVID-19 was associated with an approximately 2.8-fold higher instantaneous risk of ESKD requiring RRT during follow-up. This finding suggests that kidney impairment after COVID-19 may be sustained and have clinically significant consequences. The association was observed for haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis and was directionally consistent across major clinical subgroups, including patients managed without hospitalisation.

In earlier studies, the risk of AKI in patients with COVID-19 was reported to be approximately 10%, and the potential for future progression to CKD had been noted.9 10 In a matched cohort study investigating the risk of progression to ESKD within 6 months after infection, the risk was 7.7 times higher among hospitalised patients, whereas for non-hospitalised patients, it was 0.85 times, indicating that the increased risk of ESKD progression was not significant.3 However, in that study, 70% of the participants were white, and subgroup analyses suggested that the risk might be higher for people of colour than for white individuals. Another matched cohort study using a database of 140 000 pairs also showed that the risk of CKD 1 year after contracting COVID-19 was approximately doubled. In that study, the risk increase among younger individuals was not significant (HR 1.53, 0.76 to 3.07). In the present study, we focused on an Asian-dominant population; while hospitalised patients exhibited a higher risk, even non-hospitalised patients showed an increased risk of developing ESKD. Moreover, the risk increased in both age groups. This elevated risk persisted for up to 2 years. This result emphasises the importance of close renal function monitoring and early intervention during the initial stages of infection.

Chronic inflammation is believed to accelerate underlying nephropathy, leading to irreversible decline,11 and persistent inflammation following COVID-19 has been suggested as a contributing factor. In addition, the decrease in oxygen supply due to glomerular damage, vasoconstriction and interstitial fibrosis associated with hypoxaemia after COVID-19 can trigger tubular apoptosis and interstitial fibrosis, creating a vicious cycle that may promote ESKD.12 In patients who are highly dependent on medical care, the increased risk of vascular disorders and metabolic abnormalities can lead to a higher risk of underlying conditions.13 Kidney injury after COVID-19 develops through direct viral kidney injury,14 inflammatory damage,15 renal ischaemia due to endothelial damage16 and chronic inflammation after the acute phase and renin–angiotensin system imbalance.17 SARS-CoV-2 invades the proximal tubular epithelium and podocytes via ACE2 receptors and transmembrane protease serine 2 (TMPRSS2), causing cellular destruction. Autopsy cases have shown proximal tubular necrosis, vacuolar degeneration and virus-like particles, leading to acute tubular necrosis and proteinuria.14 In severe cases, excessive inflammatory cytokines activate neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages, inducing neutrophil extracellular traps that trigger glomerular and tubulointerstitial inflammation, causing tubular endothelial injury and microthrombosis.15 SARS-CoV-2 infects vascular endothelial cells, promoting vasoconstriction and thrombosis, which leads to ischaemic lesions and renal infarction.16 This risk is increased in patients with pre-existing arteriosclerosis. Residual viral antigens cause sustained inflammation in the renal tissue. Activation of the interleukin-6/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 and transforming growth factor beta/Smad pathways leads to tubulointerstitial fibrosis and vascular stenosis, contributing to a decline in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).17 These mechanisms combine to cause persistent renal dysfunction from acute to chronic phase.

This study has several strengths. First, with over 3.07 million matched pairs using a nationwide claims database, it boasts one of the largest sample sizes for an observational study, enabling highly accurate evaluation of the occurrence of ESKD, a rare outcome. Second, PSM and IPCW were used by employing 1:1 PSM and IPCW with detailed covariates, including age, sex, underlying diseases and medication history and minimised confounding factors other than the COVID-19 status. Third, by rigorously defining the outcome as the initiation of RRT based on reimbursement codes, which is considered a hard endpoint, we greatly reduced the risk of misclassification bias. Furthermore, by extending the follow-up period to a maximum of 2 years, systematically conducting analyses for the entire period and performing multiple subgroup and sensitivity analyses, we verified the consistency and robustness of the results. The consistency across weighted and unweighted analyses suggests that the observed association was not primarily driven by the IPCW model specification or informative censoring adjustment. Additionally, the focus on an Asian population primarily consisting of Japanese individuals is noteworthy, as it supplements the predominantly Western evidence base and provides highly generalisable findings that account for ethnic differences and variations in healthcare systems. Owing to these design and analytical strengths, our results overcome the limitations of previous studies on the risk of kidney function deterioration after COVID-19, offering more reliable clinical and public health implications.

This study also has several limitations. First, as a retrospective observational study based on claims data, known confounders were minimised through PSM. However, clinical indices and lifestyle factors such as smoking history, body mass index, blood pressure, eGFR, proteinuria, vaccination status and COVID-19 variant types could not be directly adjusted for; therefore, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Second, because the database lacks detailed information on the date and cause of death, analyses such as the Fine–Grey model that incorporates death as a competing risk could not be performed, and censoring due to death may have resulted in an underestimation of the incidence rate of ESKD. Third, while defining outcomes using reimbursement codes for dialysis initiation and kidney transplantation ensures high specificity, there remains a risk of misclassification bias for events not captured in the claims data. Fourth, the follow-up period was up to 2 years; thus, long-term (>3–5 years) renal function decline or the impact of fluctuating post-COVID-19 sequelae could not be fully evaluated. Fifth, the study population mainly comprised individuals enrolled in Japan’s national health insurance in Western Japan; therefore, the findings may have limited external validity for other countries, ethnicities or healthcare systems. Lastly, at the time of analysis, the spread of oral antiviral drugs, neutralising antibody medications and booster vaccination rates were inconsistent, making it impossible to assess the temporal effects of treatment interventions. In light of these limitations, future efforts should strengthen the evidence base by utilising prospective longitudinal cohorts, multi-institutional data linkage, including laboratory values and analytical methods that consider competing risks.

Conclusion

A history of COVID-19 was associated with a higher risk of ESKD requiring initiation of dialysis or kidney transplantation (HR 2.79, 95% CI 2.56 to 3.04). The association was observed across major demographic and clinical subgroups, supporting the need for post-acute renal monitoring and early intervention in high-risk populations. Prospective studies with laboratory data, vaccination information, variant periods and competing-risk ascertainment are needed to clarify mechanisms and identify effective preventive strategies.
