Executive Summary
Large loads, such as data centers, can impact the reliability of power systems due to their increasing power demand,
fast load dynamics, and high concentration of power electronic converters. Accurate electromagnetic transient
(EMT) modeling of data center equipment is essential for evaluating system stability and ensuring reliable grid inte-
gration. This report serves as the user manual for the accompanying EMT model library developed by the National
Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) for the EMT modeling of data center loads. The library includes detailed EMT
models of major electrical equipment found in data centers. The models can be used to conduct EMT studies of
different data center architectures for reliable grid interconnection and operation and to develop solutions to miti-
gate their impacts on grid reliability. The EMT library can be used for modeling both grid-connected and off-grid
data center facilities. The intended audience of this user manual includes utilities, system operators, consultants,
equipment manufacturers, and researchers who develop EMT models and perform EMT studies for large data center
loads.
Figure ES-1 shows the EMT model library components: a 5.5-kW power supply unit (PSU), a 2.5-MW uninter-
ruptible power supply (UPS), a 260-MW gas turbine generator, a 500-kW motor load, and a 33-kW information
technology (IT) server rack.

The primary technical contribution of this work is the EMT model of an IT rack, which can be used to represent the
compute load in data centers. It is developed based on a reference design of a PSU from Texas Instruments. Unlike
conventional power system equipment, the electrical behavior of the compute load is generally not well understood
within the power systems community, and publicly available dynamic models are limited. Accordingly, significant
effort has been devoted to the development of the IT rack model so that it accurately captures the dynamic behavior
of commercial power supply products while remaining suitable for large-scale EMT simulations. The IT rack model
can serve as a representative compute load for stability studies involving data centers.
The EMT model library also includes generic models for a UPS, a gas turbine generator, and a motor load (to repre-
sent cooling load); however, using vendor-specific models for these components is recommended whenever they are
available. In particular, the UPS is the primary grid-interface power electronic equipment in data centers; its archi-
tecture closely resembles that of power converters used in inverter-based resources (IBRs). Consequently, vendor-
specific EMT models are often available. Many modern UPS systems employ the same inverter or power conversion
system platforms used in battery energy storage systems and photovoltaic power plants, allowing corresponding
black-box EMT models to be obtained directly from original equipment manufacturers.

The EMT model library described in this user manual can be used for investigating converter-driven stability
problems in data center loads—including control interactions among data centers, nearby IBRs, and synchronous
generators—as well as weak-grid stability issues. The library is also suitable for studying the impacts of AI-driven
load fluctuations from data centers on the reliability of both the utility grid and behind-the-meter or off-grid gen-
eration. The library models do not implement equipment-specific fault ride-through characteristics, and therefore
they should not be used directly for fault ride-through studies without incorporating the appropriate ride-through
functionality.
Future editions of this user manual will include application examples and case studies demonstrating the use of the
EMT model library for representative grid integration studies of large data center loads.

1 Introduction
Power systems in many regions in the United States are experiencing unprecedented demand growth driven primarily
from the growth of large data centers. For example, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), the Texas
power system operator, anticipates large load additions of more than 125 GW over the next 5 years, with approxi-
mately 95 GW of load additions from data centers for the development of artificial intelligence (AI) and cryptocur-
rencies; this is a seismic change for a power system with a current peak demand of approximately 90 GW (ERCOT
2025a). Similar levels of demand growth are occurring or are projected in a few other regions in North America.
Data centers have large concentrations of different types of power electronic converters to process power—from
high-voltage three-phase AC point of interconnection down to sub-volt DC for supplying to GPUs and CPUs in the
IT) racks. The high concentration of power converters in data centers can pose similar grid reliability challenges as
experienced with inverter-based resources (IBRs). Additionally, data centers can create grid reliability impacts from
fast and repetitive load fluctuations, particularly when they are used to train AI algorithms. Electromagnetic transient
(EMT) models of data centers that can (1) accurately represent data center transient and dynamic behavior and (2)
simulate data centers at the speed and time steps generally used in power system studies are necessary to evaluate
various grid reliability challenges and to develop mitigation solutions.
This report serves as the user manual for the accompanying EMT model library developed by the National Laboratory
of the Rockies (NLR) for the EMT modeling of data center loads. The rest of this section summarizes grid reliabil-
ity challenges from data centers and the typical architecture of a grid-connected data center showing the flow of
electrical power from the grid to servers within IT racks. The subsequent sections describe the EMT models of the
individual components included in the library and a few use cases.

1.1 Grid Reliability Challenges From Data Centers
Four categories of grid reliability challenges from data centers are currently being evaluated by power system plan-
ners and operators: transient stability, dynamic stability, torsional oscillations, and power quality. They are summa-
rized in the following:
 • Transient stability:
 – Data center loads in general are very sensitive to voltage disturbances. They follow narrow tolerance
 limits on the variation in the grid voltage magnitude from ITIC/CBEMA limits or IEEE Std. 449-1995
 for triggering transfer of the load to a backup. The sudden loss of large loads during a grid disturbance
 can be detrimental to grid stability, as described in two recent incident reports from the North American
 Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC), respectively, on a sudden load reduction of 1,500 MW in the
 Eastern Interconnection following a grid disturbance and a series of load reduction events in the ERCOT
 territory (North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) 2025b, 2025c).
 – To mitigate reliability risks from large load reductions, system operators are recommending new ride-
 through requirements for data center loads so that they stay connected to the grid during a disturbance
 and minimize the size and duration of the load reductions (e.g., ERCOT NOGRR 282: Large Electronic
 Load Ride-Through Requirements, ERCOT 2025c).
 • Dynamic stability:
 – High concentrations of power electronic converters with fast controls in data centers make them prone to
 converter-driven stability problems, including oscillations, weak-grid instability, and control interactions
 with nearby generators (both rotating synchronous generators and stationary IBRs).
 – The converter-driven stability problems can impact the reliability of both the data centers and the grid.
 Oscillations have been reported locally at a few data centers (Sun, Xu, et al. 2022; Sun, Mihret, et
 al. 2022; ERCOT 2025b) as well as in a broader region involving numerous data centers (Mishra et
 al. 2025).
 – Similar to IBR-driven oscillations (Energy Systems Integration Group (ESIG) Stability Task Force 2024),
 it is easier to analyze the root cause of oscillations involving one or a few nearby data centers, but it

 is difficult to identify the root cause of systemwide oscillations involving numerous data centers; such
 analysis would require impedance-based stability analysis at several data centers (Shah, Lu, and Modi

 • Torsional oscillations:

 – Data centers can induce subsynchronous torsional oscillations in the shafts of nearby rotating genera-
 tors by repetitively exciting the torsional modes of the turbine generator systems via highly fluctuating
 active power load demand. This is sometimes exhibited by data centers that are used for AI applications
 (Choukse et al. 2025). Some studies have shown that these torques can reach very high levels (ERCOT
 and Electranix Corporation 2025). Such findings have raised concerns in the industry that some turbine
 generator systems located near data centers might experience fatigue-induced shaft damage and catas-
 trophic failures.
 – Data centers have rich dynamics because of high concentrations of power electronics converters, which
 can adversely interact with the electromechanical dynamics of the nearby rotating generators and lead to
 subsynchronous torsional interactions.

 – Several unknown factors make estimating fatigue-induced damage in turbine generator shaft systems
 extremely difficult. First, the level of cyclic or repetitive torque a turbine shaft can endure without accu-
 mulating any fatigue damage, which can be called the “cyclic endurance limit,” is generally not known
 or supplied by the turbine manufacturer. Second, the active power load profile of a data center, which
 has energy content at various frequencies, can change over time depending on the end-use application.
 Finally, the relative electrical distance or impedance between the compute or IT load in data centers and
 a nearby rotating generator with respect to the impedance from the grid also influences the amount of
 load fluctuations that are absorbed by the generator that result in cyclic torques. Because of all these
 unknowns, system operators are imposing conservative limits on large data center load profiles. For ex-
 ample, ERCOT is considering imposing the maximum limit of 10 MW for repetitive peak-to-peak active
 power variations within any 5 seconds from large data center loads (Electric Reliability Council of Texas
             (ERCOT) 2026).
 • Power quality:
 – Rapid, large, and repetitive load fluctuations from data centers can create power quality issues, including
 harmonics and flicker.

1.2 Data Center Power Systems: From Grid to Information Technology Racks
Figure 1 shows the typical architecture of a data center power delivery system from the point of interconnection
with the grid to the compute load in the IT server racks. As shown, an AC uninterruptible power supply (UPS) is
generally used to supply reliable power to the loads during a grid disturbance for a short duration until the backup
power source (e.g., diesel generator) is ready to start supplying the load. The AC UPS is usually located in an indoor
environment on the low-voltage side of the power delivery system near the IT racks, but outdoor medium-voltage
UPS are being considered in the industry to reduce costs and improve compliance with evolving grid interconnection
requirements. The AC UPS power output is distributed to several IT racks using an array of switchgear, cables, and
power management equipment, which are collectively referred to as the power distribution unit. Sometimes power
distribution units also contain a step-down transformer if the server racks require a lower-voltage input than supplied
by the AC UPS.

Note that Figure 1 does not show non-compute loads, including cooling and building loads, for clarity. The size
of the non-compute load can range from 10% to 50% of the compute load, and its transient behavior should be
properly represented in an EMT model that is used to perform grid reliability studies for data centers. In other words,
the transient behavior of non-compute loads is important for evaluating the fault ride-through performance of the
entire data center. On the other hand, the non-compute loads do not contain high concentrations of power electronic
converters, so their role can be neglected in the evaluation of small-signal or converter-driven stability problems with
some loss of accuracy in the analysis results.

 – In double-conversion mode, the power from the grid flows through back-to-back AC-DC-AC power
 converters in the UPS, as shown in Figure 2(a). This mode completely isolates the dynamics of the
 compute load from the grid; hence, the dynamics of the compute load can be ignored in the EMT model
 of a data center grid reliability studies when the AC UPS is operated in double-conversion mode.
 – In many data centers, the AC UPS operates in double-conversion mode only for a short duration to charge
 the UPS battery and bring its state of charge to a predetermined threshold following a discharge event
 (e.g., the discharge event might have happened because of a grid disturbance). For such data centers, it is
 important to properly model the dynamics of the compute load in their EMT models.
 – Some advanced UPS currently being deployed always operate in double-conversion mode and contain
 long-duration battery energy storage, ranging up to several hours, to isolate the grid from AI load fluctua-
 tions and enable data centers to support grid reliability (NLR 2026).
 • Eco mode:
 – In some data centers, the AC UPS operates in so-called eco mode during normal operation to achieve
 higher efficiency. In eco mode, the power from the grid flows through the AC UPS via a static bidi-
 rectional semiconductor switch, as shown Figure 2(b), instead of the back-to-back AC-DC-AC power
 converters, to avoid losses in the power converters.
 – For data centers with AC UPS operating in eco mode, the EMT model must consider the dynamics of
 power converters in the compute load to be able to capture converter-driven stability issues. On the other
 hand, the dynamics of the power converters in the AC UPS can be ignored in the EMT model for this
 operation mode. In other words, from the grid perspective, the AC UPS operating in eco mode only
 controls the transition of the compute load to the backup generation during a grid disturbance; hence, the
 AC UPS in eco mode can be modeled simply using the ride-through curve it follows for transitioning the
 compute load to the backup generation.
 • Single-conversion mode:
 – Some AC UPS can operate in single-conversion mode. As shown in 2(c), in single-conversion mode,
 the power from the grid flows through the AC UPS via the static switch, similar to eco mode; however,
 unlike eco mode, in single-conversion mode, the load-side converter of the AC UPS remains online to
 ensure power quality in the load-side voltage by mitigating harmonics from the grid. In other words, in
 single-conversion mode, the AC UPS operates similar to an active power filter. Single-conversion mode
 of the AC UPS is not commonly used, and it is not discussed further in this report.

1.3 Data Center Power Systems: IT Racks
Figure 3 shows the typical architecture of the electrical power distribution within an IT server rack. A typical modern
server rack in AI data centers is supplied by a three-phase low-voltage ac (LVAC) connection. It contains power
shelves to convert the input LVAC power to low-voltage dc (LVDC) output, typically at 12 Vdc or 48 Vdc. There can
be more than one power shelf in an IT rack depending on the total power demand from the compute load within the
rack. Each power shelf contains several power supply units (PSUs), sometimes referred to simply as power supplies.
A PSU is a power electronic converter that accepts the single-phase LVAC input (e.g., single-phase 277 Vac) and
generates the LVDC output (e.g., 48 Vdc) to supply to a vertical DC bus bar located at the back of the server rack. A
typical power shelf has six PSUs distributed among three phases on the input side with two PSUs per phase. The six
PSUs are connected in either a star or delta configuration on the input LVAC side, as shown in Figure 3. All the PSUs
in a shelf supply to a common DC bus bar; hence, they are all connected in parallel on the output LVDC side. The
compute load in a server rack is located in other shelves, which are commonly referred to as server blades, and each
server blade draws power from the LVDC bus bars located at the back of the rack. For server racks using 48-Vdc bus
bars, each server includes a power converter, called an intermediate bus converter (IBC), that steps down the DC-bus
voltage to 12 Vdc. Another type of power electronic converter, called a voltage regulator, (VR) are used to further
step down the voltage to a single volt level to supply to the GPUs and CPUs on the motherboard, as shown in Figure
3. Figure 4 shows a picture of an IT server rack with several power shelves, a picture of two power shelves with six
PSUs each, and two pictures PSUs rated at 3.6 kW and 5.5 kW, respectively.

1.4 Considerations for EMT Modeling of Data Centers
1.4.1   AC UPS
AC UPS use back-to-back AC-DC-AC power converters with the front-end power converter very similar to the two-
level voltage source converters used in grid-connected inverters in IBRs. In fact, modern UPS that integrate longer-
duration battery energy storage to provide various grid services use the same inverter or power conversion system
(PCS) platforms used in battery energy storage systems and photovoltaic power plants, allowing corresponding black-
box EMT models to be obtained directly from original equipment manufacturers (OEMs). Hence, EMT modeling
of AC UPS can leverage existing know-how and best practices from the modeling of IBRs. Moreover, because the
AC UPS is the front-end multimegawatt power electronic equipment in data centers that interfaces with the grid, and
its dynamics can significantly vary from one product to another, it is recommended to use vendor-specific black-box
EMT models of UPS in the EMT modeling of data centers.

1.4.2 IT Racks or Compute Load
PSUs used in power shelves are the front-end power electronic converters of an IT rack that interface with the power
distribution network of a data center. The downstream power electronic converters in an IT rack, including IBCs
and VRs do not interact with the power distribution network of the data center; hence, IBCs and VRs can be ignored
in the EMT modeling of data centers when performing grid stability studies. Moreover, each PSU contains a front-
end single-phase power factor correction (PFC) converter that converts the input single-phase LVAC voltage to an
intermediate DC voltage that is typically approximately 400 to 450 Vdc across a bulk capacitor; this intermediate
voltage is commonly referred to as the bulk capacitor or bulk capacitor voltage. Another DC-DC converter in the
PSU further steps down the bulk capacitor voltage to the IT rack bus bar voltage. Because this step-down DC-DC
converter does not directly interface with the power distribution network of the data center, its dynamics can be
ignored in the EMT modeling of a PSU. To summarize, the EMT modeling of an IT rack needs to focus only on the
PSUs, and the EMT modeling of PSUs needs to consider only the front-end single-phase PFC rectifier.

1.4.3 Neutral Wire and Grounding Circuits
The neutral wire circuit of an IT rack influences its zero-sequence input impedance response; the latter can adversely
interact with the zero-sequence impedance of the power delivery system of the data center and create instability.
Additionally, the neutral circuit of an IT rack can influence its positive- and negative-sequence input impedances
through frequency coupling effects and play a role in converter-driven stability problems; hence, it is important to
correctly capture the neutral circuit of an IT rack in the EMT modeling of data centers.

The PSUs in a power shelf are connected in either a star of delta configuration, as shown in Figure 3. When the

it is important to properly represent the neutral wire circuit of the IT rack in the EMT model to accurately evaluate
the risks of converter-driven instabilities involving zero-sequence dynamics (Sun, Mihret, et al. 2022). There are two
possible configurations in data centers for connecting the neutral wire of an IT rack (formed via the star connection
of PSUs in power shelves in the rack) to the power distribution network of the data center:
 • UPS with output transformer:
 – Traditionally, in the past, the AC UPS in data centers had output transformers to supply the power distri-
 bution unit. In this case, the neutral wire of the IT racks was connected to the neutral of the UPS output
 transformer.

 • Transformer-less UPS:
 – In modern transformer-less AC UPS, the neutral wire of the IT racks is usually directly connected to the
 neutral of the pad-mounted medium-voltage transformer that supplies the UPS (see Figure 1).
The common-mode or the zero-sequence impedance of the data center power distribution network seen by the IT
racks depends on the winding and grounding configurations of the transformers between the grid POI and the IT
racks. These configurations must be captured in the EMT models of a data center to evaluate converter-driven stabil-
ity problems.

1.5 Report Outline
This report describes the EMT models of different components of a data center power system as developed by
NLR in the accompanying EMT model library. The EMT model library can be used to study the impacts of grid-
connected data centers on grid reliability. The EMT models can also be used to study the stability of off-grid data
centers supplied by off-grid generation. This report also presents a few use cases to familiarize users with the various
library components.

The EMT library consists of EMT models of an IT rack, UPS, cooling load, and a gas turbine generator. The IT rack
model is based on a detailed model of a 5.5-kW PSU; the latter can be simulated with three different levels of fidelity
using simulation modes including switching mode, switch-averaged mode, and double-averaged mode. The IT rack
model can be supplied with a load profile representing the compute load profile at the DC bus bars of the IT rack to
evaluate the impact of the load profile on the grid.

The rest of this report is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the EMT model of a 5.5-kW PSU with 277-Vac
single-phase input; the EMT model can be simulated using switching mode, switch-averaged mode, or double-
averaged mode. Section 3 describes the EMT model of a 33-kW IT rack with six 5.5-kW PSUs; the IT rack model
is developed based on the 5.5-kW PSU model. Section 4 describes the EMT model of a 2.5-MW, 480-Vac UPS that
can be operated in either double-conversion mode or eco mode. Section 5 describes the EMT model of a 260-MW,
13.2-kV gas turbine generator to represent behind-the-meter and off-grid gas generation, which is sometimes used in
modern large data centers. Section 6 presents a couple of use cases to demonstrate the application of the EMT model
library to simulate grid-connected and off-grid data centers when conducting EMT studies.

2 EMT Modeling of Power Supply Unit
PSUs form the basic building blocks of the electrical power distribution in IT racks. As noted in the previous sec-
tion, the EMT modeling of an IT rack needs to consider only the front-end single-phase PFC rectifiers in PSUs; the
downstream step-down DC-DC converters in PSUs can be ignored. In the past, single-boost and dual-boost PFC
rectifier topologies were commonly used in data center applications (Sun, Xu, et al. 2022); however, modern PSUs
generally employ bridgeless PFC topologies. Specifically, single-phase totem-pole bridgeless PFC converters with
wide-bandgap devices, such as gallium-nitride FETs and silicon-carbide MOSFETs, are used in modern data centers
(Yin 2022). These wide-bandgap devices can achieve very high switching frequencies, e.g., up to a few hundreds of
kilohertz, while still maintaining high efficiencies, thereby reducing the size of the magnetic components used in the
PSU. We focused on the single-phase totem-pole bridgeless PFC converter topology for the development of the EMT
model PSU because of its widespread use in modern PSU products.

2.1 Reference Design for EMT Modeling
We leveraged a reference design from Texas Instruments of a 3.6-kW single-phase totem-pole bridegeless PFC for
the development of the EMT model PSU in data centers (Texas Instruments (TI) 2023). In addition to the test report
for this reference design, we used the publicly available PLECS model of the reference design to compare some
responses of the developed EMT model on the PSCAD platform.

2.2.1 Operation During Positive Half Cycle
During the positive half cycle of the input voltage, switch S4 in the slow leg is kept ON, and switch S3 is kept OFF.
Switches S1 and S2 in the fast leg continue to alternately turn ON and OFF at very high switching frequencies
depending on the duty cycle, d, generated by the control system. Because one main objective of the PFC converter is
to ensure that the input current, 𝑖 𝑎 , is in phase with the input voltage 𝑣 𝑎 , it can be assumed that 𝑖 𝑎 is positive during
the positive half cycle of the input voltage; So, when S1 is ON and S2 is OFF, the current, 𝑖 𝑎 , flows through S1,
discharges into the bulk capacitor, 𝐶, and returns to the input voltage source, 𝑣 𝑎 , through S4. Yet, when S1 is OFF
and S2 is ON, the current, 𝑖 𝑎 , flows through S2 and returns to the input voltage source, 𝑣 𝑎 , without discharging into
the bulk capacitor, 𝐶. In other words, the input voltage source, 𝑣 𝑎 , charges the inductor by increasing the magnitude
of the current, 𝑖 𝑎 , flowing through it when S1 is OFF and S2 is ON, whereas the inductor discharges into the bulk
capacitor, 𝐶, and the magnitude of the inductor current, 𝑖 𝑎 , reduces when S1 is ON and S2 is OFF. This is because
the bulk capacitor voltage, 𝑣 𝑑 , is always greater than the input voltage, 𝑣 𝑎 .
The duty cycle of switches S1 and S2 is regulated by the control system, shown in Figure 5(b), such that the inductor
current, 𝑖 𝑎 , remains in phase with the input voltage, 𝑣 𝑎 , achieving unity power factor, and the magnitude of the input
current, 𝑖 𝑎 , is regulated such that the bulk capacitor voltage, 𝑣 𝑑 , converges to its reference value, 𝑣 𝑑,𝑟 𝑒 𝑓 . Because
the input current, 𝑖 𝑎 , increases when S2 is ON (and S1 is OFF) and decreases when S2 is OFF (and S1 is ON), the
control system output, 𝑑, in 5(b) is used as the duty cycle for modulating switch S2, and switch S1 is modulated with
an inverted switching function with respect to S2.

2.2.2 Operation During Negative Half Cycle
The operation of the PFC converter during the negative half cycle of the input voltage, 𝑣 𝑎 , is the mirrored version of
its operation during the positive half cycle. During the negative half cycle, switch S3 in the slow leg is kept ON, and
switch S4 is kept OFF. Moreover, switches S1 and S2 in the fast leg continue to alternately turn ON and OFF at very
high switching frequencies depending on the duty cycle, d, generated by the control system. But because the input
current, 𝑖 𝑎 , is negative during the negative half cycle of the input voltage, 𝑣 𝑎 , the magnitude of the input current,

2.7.2 Switching Mode
In switching mode, the EMT model simulates the actual switching behavior of the semiconductor switches in the fast
and slow legs of the PFC converter. Additionally, all control functions—including the current control, bulk capacitor
voltage control, and PWM—are fully simulated as they are. The switching frequency of the fast leg in this mode
is kept at 50 kHz by default, whereas the slow leg switches at the fundamental frequency of the input voltage. The
switching frequency of the fast leg can be changed by changing the frequency of the triangular carrier signal used in
the PWM generator shown in Figure 6. The simulation time step for this operation mode should not be kept higher

than 0.1 𝜇s for a 50-kHz switching frequency for the fast leg. The simulation time step needs to be further reduced if
the switching frequency of the fast leg is further increased.

The following observation can be made from the responses shown in Figure 12:
 • The bulk capacitor voltage controller is able to return the bulk capacitor voltage, 𝑣 𝑑 , to its reference, 450 V,
 within approximately 50–100 ms following the load step change; this corresponds well with the designed bulk

 • The ripple content in the input current, 𝑖 𝑎 , stays reasonably low based on the sizing of the bulk inductor, 𝐿.
 • The duty cycle, 𝑑, varies according to the input voltage, 𝑣 𝑎 , based on the following relationship derived from

 should always be higher than the peak of the input AC voltage, 𝑣 𝑎 , by a sufficient margin. The default values
 of 𝑉𝑎𝑐,𝑟 𝑚𝑠 and 𝑉𝑑 of 277 Vac and 450 Vdc, respectively, used in the EMT model meet this requirement. They

 with three-phase 480-Vac input.

 • The average value of signal 𝑝, the output of the bulk capacitor voltage controller, increases from 2 kW to 5.5

2.7.3 Switch-Averaged Mode
In switch-averaged mode, the EMT model ignores the switching dynamics by replacing the semiconductor switch
bridge using a controllable voltage source on the ac-side and a controllable current source on the dc-side. The
controllable voltage source on the ac-side is placed after the boost inductor, and it implements the boost inductor
equation in (2.2). The controllable current source on the dc-side supplies to the bulk capacitor, and it implements
the bulk capacitor equation in (2.2). In addition to disabling the semiconductor switch bridge in the EMT model,
switch-averaged mode disables the PWM generator shown in Figure 6.
Because the switching dynamics in switch-averaged simulation mode are eliminated, the EMT model can be simu-
lated with a higher time step of up to approximately 10 𝜇s. Because the maximum allowable simulation time step for
switch-averaged mode is two orders of magnitude higher than that of switching mode, the total simulation time of the
EMT model using switch-averaged mode is approximately two orders of magnitude smaller than what is required us-
ing switching mode. Figure 13 shows the simulated response of the EMT model using switch-averaged mode for the
same load step event. It shows that the responses of all the variables using switch-averaged operation mode are the
same as those obtained using switching mode except the switching ripples are not captured in simulations performed
using switch-averaged mode.

2.7.4 Double-Averaged Mode
It is not feasible to increase the simulation time step above 10 𝜇s using switch-averaged mode even though it does not
simulate the fast switching dynamics. This is because the current control bandwidth of the PSUs ranges from a few
kilohertz to a couple of tens of kilohertz. Using a simulation time step higher than 10 𝜇s can introduce a significant
phase lag within the current control loop, which can either alter its performance or make it unstable. The EMT
models used in grid reliability studies generally employ several tens of microseconds as the time steps. Enforcing
simulation time steps lower than approximately 50 𝜇s for data center interconnection studies can substantially slow
large simulation studies. We addressed this problem by developing the double-averaged simulation mode for the
developed EMT model of a 5.5-kW PSU.
Double-averaged simulation mode of the EMT model eliminates the dynamics of the current control loop of the PFC
converter in addition to the switching dynamics. This is achieved based on the double-averaged model developed
in Section 2.5. In double-averaged simulation mode, the following components of the EMT model are disabled: the
switching bridge, the PWM generator, the boost inductor, and the current control loop of the control system, which
is shown within a dashed box in Figure 5(b); they are replaced by a controllable current source on the input side that
controls the PFC input current, 𝑖 𝑎 , according to Eq. (2.10) and a controllable current source on the output side that
controls the current entering the bulk capacitor, 𝑖 𝑑 , according to Eq. (2.12).
Because the EMT model ignores both the switching dynamics and the dynamics of the current control loop in
double-averaged simulation mode, the EMT model can be simulated with a significantly higher time step of up to
500 𝜇s. This significantly reduces the simulation time, and more importantly, this makes the EMT model of the
PSU compatible with typical simulation time step values when running EMT studies for evaluate grid reliability.
Figure 14 shows the response of the EMT model obtained using double-averaged simulation mode for the same load
step event as previously discussed. It shows that the response of all the variables using double-averaged simulation
mode is the same as that obtained using switching mode except that the switching ripples are not captured by double-
averaged mode. Moreover, double-averaged mode does not capture the response of the duty cycle, 𝑑, and the refer-
ence and measurement of the rectified input current, 𝑖 𝑎𝑟 ,𝑟 𝑒 𝑓 and 𝑖 𝑎𝑟 , respectively. This is because double-averaged
mode eliminates the dynamics of the current control loop of the PFC converter.

2.7.5   Summary
Table 3 compares the simulation speeds of the EMT model of a 5.5-kW PSU described in this chapter for the three
simulation modes. Clearly, double-averaged mode not only enables the use of high simulation steps for data center
models that are generally used in grid reliability studies but also significantly reduces the simulation time. It can
be assumed that the current control dynamics of the front-end PFC converter do not impact the response of a PSU
up to approximately one-tenth of the current control bandwidth. With the 5-kHz bandwidth of the current control
loop design in the EMT model described in this report, it can be assumed that the PSU model can be used in double-
averaged simulation mode to evaluate its behavior up to 500 Hz. This limit is acceptable for evaluating grid-level
interactions from IT racks. In other words, double-averaged simulation mode can be used for EMT models of PSUs
to study grid reliability impacts from large data center loads.

4.1 Control Design
This section explains the control logic of the rectifier and the inverter of the UPS system used in its EMT model.
Because both the rectifier and the inverter use the same size of line reactors, their inner-loop current controllers,
𝐻𝑖 (𝑠), are identical, and they are designed for a current control bandwidth of 500 Hz. Users will need to redesign the
parameters of 𝐻𝑖 (𝑠) if they change the size of the line reactors. The bandwidths of the outer-loop controls needs to
be sufficiently slow, and they are set to 20 Hz.

4.1.1 Rectifier Controls

5 EMT Modeling of Gas Turbine Generator
Gas turbine generators are frequently used to supply reliable electrical power to large data center loads. As behind-
the-meter generators in grid-connected data centers, gas turbine generators provide reliable electrical power during
normal operation and during grid outages, preventing service interruptions. Gas turbine generators are also increas-
ingly deployed as off-grid power sources for large data centers facing growing electricity demand, grid congestion,
and lengthy utility interconnection timelines. There is abundant literature on the modeling of gas turbine generators;
however, there are few generic EMT-PSCAD models of gas turbine generators for grid reliability studies of data cen-
ters. This section describes a generic PSCAD model of a 260-MW gas turbine generator that is included in the EMT
model library released with this user manual.

6 Example Use Cases
This section presents a few example use cases based on the EMT model library presented in this report to enable the
user to perform grid stability studies for data centers. The use cases are intended to enable the user to quickly start
access various components from the EMT model library. The use cases do not represent any specific data center.
An EMT model of a specific data center can be developed using the EMT model library presented in this report by
considering the architecture as well as the operation modes of the electrical power equipment of the data center.
To properly run the data center EMT model library, the user needs to load the library files including “NLR_DC_-
Library.lib” and the “master (Master Library).lib.” By default, the simulation time step is set to 10 𝜇s, and the IT
rack model is simulated using switch-averaged operation mode for the PSU model. As mentioned in Section 2, which
describes the PSU model, the simulation time step should be kept at 0.1 𝜇s when full switching mode is used for the
PSU models. It is recommended that the simulation time step is not kept higher than 100 𝜇s when double-averaged
mode is used for the PSU models. The user needs to enable/disable layers in the library file NLR_DC_Library.lib, as
described in the PSU model, to select one of the three operating modes for the PSU model.

6.1.1 Modeling of a 2.5-MW Data Center Room
The IT racks in a data center building are generally housed in separate rooms, with each room served by an indepen-
dent medium-voltage transformer and an AC UPS. In the following, we describe the modeling of such data center
rooms with compute, cooling, and building loads.
The compute load in the data center room is represented by using two 33-kW IT rack models, each scaled to repre-
sent 25 IT racks using a three-phase current scale with the gain of 25. In other words, each of the two IT rack models
represents the compute load of 25×33 = 825 kW, giving the total compute load of 1,650 kW. The user can change the
gain of the current scaler to represent different numbers of 33-kW IT racks or use multiple IT rack models. The sec-
ond approach reduces any inaccuracies associated with the aggregation of the IT rack models, but it can significantly
increase the simulation time if a large of number of IT rack models are used in the simulated system. The impedance
of cables between the IT rack and the AC UPS is selected based on the information provided in (Lin et al. 2023).

The cooling load in the data center room is represented by a compressor, which is simulated using one induction
motor load rated at 0.4 MW.
The building load, including lighting and site operation, in the data center room is aggregated as one 100-kW resis-
tive load.

Based on this discussion, the data center room has a total 2.15-MW load (compute load: 1,650 kW, cooling load:
100 kW, and building load: 100 kW), which can represent one data center room or a pod in a typical data center
used for AI applications. Typically, the compute load makes up approximately 65%–85%, the cooling load makes
up approximately 10%–20%, and the static load makes up approximately 5%-15% of the total load (ERCOT 2025b).
The user can adjust the load share and size based on the specific data center design.

This 2.15-MW load is supplied by a 2.5-MW AC UPS, which together represent a typical data center room. The AC
UPS can be operated in either double-conversion mode or eco mode. It is supplied by a pad-mounted distribution
transformer that steps down the three-phase 13.2-kV supply from the utility grid to the 480-Vac supply required by
the UPS.

6.1.2   200-MW Data Center
A three-phase current scaler is used at the 13.2-kV input side of the AC UPS with a gain of 80 to represent a data
center with 200-MW (= 80 × 2.5) capacity. The gain can be changed to represent a different capacity of the data
center.

6.1.3 Model Initialization During Simulations
The data center model performs initialization during the first 5 seconds. Specifically, each 5.5-kW PSU initially
starts the simulation with 0.1-kW load. At 1 s (this can be changed in the PSU model), the PSU load starts following
its load reference, which can be a constant load or any desired AI load profile. We included an example AI load
profile with file name "ai_load.txt". The cooling load, which is represented by an induction motor load, initially
starts the motor at a constant speed (locked speed); at 0.2 s, the motor control is switched to a torque control mode to
represent the dynamics of a typical compressor.

6.1.4 AC UPS in Double-Conversion Mode
Figures 29 and 30 show a snapshot of the simulated response of the 200-MW data center when the IT rack is simu-
lated using switching and switch-averaged simulation modes, respectively. In both figures, the left column shows the
active power response at different measurement locations in the 200-MW data center. The top plot shows the power
consumed by an IT rack; the middle plot shows the output of the AC UPS supplying the IT racks, which combines

6.1.5 AC UPS in Eco Mode
Figures 31 and 32 show the simulated response of the 200-MW data center when the AC UPS is operated in eco

rectifier is disconnected. This is different from double-conversion mode of the AC UPS because the load fluctuations
from the compute load are directly reflected to the grid.

7 Summary
This user manual presented an NLR EMT model library for data centers to support EMT simulation studies that
evaluate grid reliability impacts from large data center loads. The library provides a representative IT rack model
together with generic models for an AC UPS, a gas turbine generator, and a motor load, enabling the development of
realistic EMT models for both grid-connected and off-grid data centers.
The EMT models included in the library are intended to support studies of converter-driven stability problems,
weak-grid operation, and the interactions between data centers, IBRs, and conventional generation. Where available,
vendor-specific models should be used in place of the generic component models to improve modeling fidelity.
The current version of the library is intended for small-signal and transient stability analysis; it does not include
equipment-specific fault-ride-through characteristics, which must be added to the component model to study the fault
ride-through behavior of a data center.
Future releases of the EMT model library and this user manual will expand the available models. It will also include
representative application examples and case studies demonstrating their use in practical grid integration studies of
large data centers.
