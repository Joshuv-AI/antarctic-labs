Why Does Adaptive Zeroth-Order Optimization Work?
Haishan Ye, Luo Luo

Abstract
Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization is popular in real-world applications that accessing the gradient information is expensive or unavailable. Recently, adaptive ZO methods that normalize gradient estimators by the empirical standard deviation of function values have achieved strong practical performance, particularly in fine-tuning the large language model. However, the theoretical understanding of such strategy remains limited. In this work, we show that the empirical standard deviation is, with high probability, closely proportional to the norm of the (stochastic) gradient. Based on this insight, we analyze adaptive ZO methods under the generalized ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -smoothness condition with respect to the matrix norm. We establish explicit convergence rates and query complexity bounds for both deterministic and stochastic settings, demonstrating that adaptive ZO methods achieve the faster convergence and the improved query efficiency compared to the vanilla ZO methods with fixed-step.

Introduction

Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization has become a fundamental pillar of modern machine learning.
It is widely used in a variety of applications where gradient information is unavailable or prohibitively expensive to obtain, including black-box adversarial attacks
(
Ilyas et al., 2018
;
Chen et al., 2017
)
, reinforcement learning
(
Chrabaszcz et al., 2018
;
Salimans et al., 2017
;
Mania et al., 2018
)
, memory-efficient large language model (LLM) fine-tuning
(
Malladi et al., 2023
;
Dang et al., 2025
)
, etc.
The vanilla ZO algorithm estimates the gradient using a batch of function value queries. Specifically, the gradient estimator is constructed as

\mathbf{g}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{f(\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{s}_{i})-f(\bm{x})}{\alpha}\cdot\bm{s}_{i},

where
f(\bm{x})
is the objective function,
\ell
is the batch size,
\alpha>0
is the smoothing parameter, and
\bm{s}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{d}/\ell)
with
\bm{I}_{d}
being the
d\times d
identity matrix.

When minimizing a stochastic objective, where only noisy function evaluations
f(\bm{x};\;\xi)
are accessible, the corresponding stochastic gradient estimator is given by

\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{f(\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{s}_{i};\;\xi)-f(\bm{x};\;\xi)}{\alpha}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}.

Using either estimator, the vanilla ZO algorithm
(
Nesterov & Spokoiny, 2017
;
Ghadimi & Lan, 2013
)
updates the iterate according to

\bm{x}_{t+1}=\bm{x}_{t}-\eta\mathbf{g}(\bm{x}_{t})\qquad\mbox{ or }\qquad\bm{x}_{t+1}=\bm{x}_{t}-\eta\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\bm{x}_{t}),

where
0<\eta
denotes the step size.
This vanilla ZO scheme has been successfully applied to black-box adversarial attacks
(
Ilyas et al., 2018
)
, and memory-efficient LLM tuning
(
Malladi et al., 2023
)
.

To further improve the performance of vanilla ZO methods, a popular enhancement is to adaptively control the step size by normalizing the estimated gradient using the standard deviation
\sigma_{t}
of the
\ell
sampled function values (see Eq. (
2.5
) and Eq. (
2.7
)). The resulting update rule takes the form

\bm{x}_{t+1}=\bm{x}_{t}-\eta\cdot\frac{\mathbf{g}(\bm{x}_{t})}{\sigma_{t}}\qquad\mbox{ or }\qquad\bm{x}_{t+1}=\bm{x}_{t}-\eta\cdot\frac{\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\bm{x}_{t})}{\sigma_{t}}.

(1.1)

The intuition behind this normalization is that a small standard deviation
\sigma_{t}
indicates that the current iterate
\bm{x}_{t}
lies in a relatively flat region of the landscape, and therefore a larger effective step size is desirable.

Empirically, this adaptive ZO strategy achieves substantial improvements over the vanilla ZO method, particularly in large-scale language model applications. For instance,
Dang et al., 2025
apply the adaptive ZO update in Eq. (
1.1
) to LLM fine-tuning and demonstrate significantly higher query efficiency than MeZO
(
Malladi et al., 2023
)
, which adopts a fixed-step vanilla ZO method. More recently, adaptive ZO methods have also shown promising performance in reinforcement learning from human feedback
(
Qiu et al., 2025
)
.

Despite these empirical successes, a rigorous theoretical understanding of why adaptive ZO methods outperform vanilla ZO methods remains largely unexplored. In this paper, we aim to bridge this gap by providing a principled theoretical explanation for the advantages of adaptive ZO methods. We summarize our main contribution as follows.

•

First, we show that the standard deviation
\sigma_{t}
is, with high probability, nearly proportional to the norm of the (stochastic) gradient. This result formally justifies the intuition that a small
\sigma_{t}
corresponds to a flat landscape around the current iterate.

•

Second, we analyze the convergence rate and query complexity of adaptive ZO methods for generalized
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions with respect to the
\bm{H}
-norm, where
\bm{H}
is a
d\times d
positive semi-definite matrix, rather than the standard Euclidean norm.
The notion of original
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness was first introduced by
Zhang et al., 2020b
to demonstrate the superiority of gradient clipping in real-world applications.
In this work, we further generalize this setting to accommodate the
\bm{H}
-norm smoothness.

•

Finally, we establish explicit convergence rates and query complexity bounds for adaptive ZO methods in both deterministic and stochastic settings on the generalized
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions.
Our results demonstrate that adaptive ZO methods can
converge arbitrarily faster than fixed-step vanilla ZO methods
. Consequently, the query complexity of adaptive ZO methods can be
arbitrarily smaller than that of fixed-step vanilla ZO methods
. A detailed discussion of these improvements is provided in Remark
3
and Remark
6
.

Related Works

Adaptive Zeroth-Order Method.

The adaptive ZO method rediscovered independently times and times.

Salimans et al., 2017
use a similar normalization as the one in Eq. (
1.1
) and call it as the virtual batch normalization of the neural network policies.

Mania et al., 2018
improve the work of
Salimans et al., 2017
and add second-order information to achieve faster convergence rate.

Lyu & Tsang, 2019
propose an algorithm called INGO which is similar to the adaptive ZO method but with second-order information.
Furthermore,
Lyu & Tsang, 2019
also show that INGO will converge a stationary point even if the objective function is nonconvex.
Recently,
Dang et al., 2025
propose FZOO which is also a kind of adaptive ZO method but with Rademacher random vectors as the search directions
instead of the Gaussian vectors.

Dang et al., 2025
first realize that the standard deviation
\sigma_{t}
is related to the (stochastic) gradient norm and show that
\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t}^{2}\right]
is almost proportional to the square of (stochastic) gradient norm.
However, the property of
\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t}^{2}\right]
can
not
directly derive that
\sigma_{t}
should be proportional to the (stochastic) gradient norm.
Furthermore,
Dang et al., 2025
only show that FZOO will converge to a stationary point but
without
a sound theory to show FZOO will outperform vanilla ZO method.

Relaxed Smoothness.

The empirical observations in training neural networks indicate that the relaxed smoothness can better characterize the objective function than the standard smoothness
(
Zhang et al., 2020b
;
Crawshaw et al., 2022
;
Cooper, 2022
)
.
The (stochastic) first-order methods for optimization problems under relaxed smoothness conditions have been extensively studied in recent years
(
Chen et al., 2023
;
Vankov et al., 2025
;
Li et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2023
;
Gaash et al., 2025
;
Gorbunov et al., 2024
;
Koloskova et al., 2023
;
Chezhegov et al., 2025
;
Tovmasyan et al., 2025
;
Crawshaw & Liu, 2025
;
Lobanov et al., 2024
;
Tyurin, 2025
;
Reisizadeh et al., 2025
;
Zhang et al., 2020a
;
Yu et al., 2025
;
Khirirat et al., 2024b
;
Khirirat et al., 2024a
;
Jiang et al., 2025
;
Borodich & Kovalev, 2025
)
.
Notably, incorporating the step of normalization or clipping into stochastic gradient descent (SGD) can efficiently achieve approximate stationary points of relaxed smooth functions, which cloud be arbitrarily faster than the vanilla SGD with the fixed stepsize when the initialization is poor
(
Zhang et al., 2020b
)
.
Additionally,
Xie et al., 2024
introduce the trust-region methods to further improve the convergence by additional assuming the second-order smoothness condition.
Recently,
Lobanov & Gasnikov, 2025
apply the uniform smoothing to establish the zeroth-order variants of clipped and normalized gradient descent, achieving the linear convergence rates for convex objectives with sufficient large batch sizes.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we first formalization the notations and our problem settings, then introduce the background of zeroth-order optimization.

2.1
Notations

We introduce notations used throughout this paper.
A symmetric matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is called positive semi-definite if it holds that
\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}\geq 0
for any non-zero vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We let
\bm{A}=\bm{U}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{U}^{\top}
be the eigenvalue decomposition of positive definite matrix
\bm{A}
, where
\bm{U}
is an orthonormal matrix which contains the eigenvectors of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{d})
is a diagonal matrix with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{d}>0
.
This paper uses
\mathrm{tr}(\bm{A})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{ii}
to denote the trace of the matrix
\bm{A}
.
Given two vectors
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we use
\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}y_{i}
to denote their inner product.
Given symmetric positive semi-definite matrix
\bm{A}
, we let
\|\bm{A}\|_{F}\triangleq(\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^{2})^{1/2}=(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2})^{1/2}

be its Frobenius norm and

\|\bm{A}\|\triangleq\lambda_{1}
be its the spectral norm.
For given vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define its Euclidean norm and
\bm{A}
-norm as
\left\|\bm{x}\right\|\triangleq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}}
and

\|\bm{x}\|_{\bm{A}}\triangleq\sqrt{\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}}
, respectively.

2.2
Problem Settings

First, we consider the following deterministic minimization problem

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x}),

(2.1)

where the ZO algorithms can access the function value
f(\bm{x})
for given
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We also study the stochastic formulation

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[f(\bm{x};\xi)\right],

(2.2)

where the ZO algorithms can draw the random variable
\xi
to access the stochastic function value evaluation for given
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Next, we impose assumptions for above optimization problems.

Assumption 1
.

We suppose the differentiable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth with respect to a positive semi-definite matrix
\bm{H}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
, i.e., if for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|_{\bm{H}}\leq 1/{(L_{1}\sqrt{\left\|\bm{H}\right\|})}
holds that

\Big|f(\bm{y})-f(\bm{x})-\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle\Big|\leq\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}.

(2.3)

Assumption 2
.

We suppose the objective
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is lower bounded, i.e., it holds
f^{*}=\inf_{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(\bm{x})>-\infty
.

Assumption 3

(
Zhang et al., 2020b
)
.

We suppose the stochastic gradient
\nabla f(\bm{x};\xi)
in formulation (
2.2
) has bounded deviation, i.e., it holds
\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x};\;\xi)-\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|\leq\nu
for all
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where
\nu>0
is a constant.

Note that Assumption
1
is a generalization of
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth introduced by
Zhang et al., 2020a
, since we use the
\bm{H}
-norm to describe the distance in this condition.
Clearly, Assumption
1
will reduce to standard
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth by taking
\bm{H}
be the identity matrix.
In fact, Assumption
1
is important to the analysis of the adaptive ZO method, and it is helpful to show the adaptive ZO method can achieve the much faster convergence rate than the vanilla ZO method.

2.3
Oblivious Random Sketching Matrix

In this subsection, we introduce the notion of oblivious random sketching matrix.
Note that the recent work
(
Ye et al., 2025
)
showed that the search directions of ZO methods drawn from random sketching matrices can help ZO methods achieve weak dimension dependent query complexity (refer to Remark
2
and Remark
5
).

Definition 1

(Sketching in Matrix Product)
.

Given a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, parameters
\gamma\in(0,1]
,
k\geq 1
, and
\delta>0
, we say a matrix
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\ell}
, independent of
\bm{A}
, is an oblivious
(\gamma,k,\delta)
-random sketching matrix for matrix product if the following holds with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\left\|\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}\bm{A}-\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}\right\|\leq\gamma\left(\left\|\bm{A}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\left\|\bm{A}\right\|_{F}^{2}}{k}\right).

(2.4)

•

Gaussian sketching matrix:
The most classical sketching matrix is the Gaussian sketching matrix
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times d}
, whose entries are i.i.d. from the univariate Gaussian distribution of mean 0 and variance
1/\ell
.
Owing to the well-known concentration properties
(
Woodruff et al., 2014
)
, Gaussian random matrices are very attractive.
It is only requires
\ell=\Omega\left((k+\log({1}/{\delta}))\gamma^{-2}\right)
to achieve the
(\gamma,k,\delta)
-approximate matrix product property defined in Definition
1

(
Cohen et al., 2016
)
.

•

Rademacher Sketching:
As a discrete alternative, the Rademacher matrix uses i.i.d. entries from
\{\pm 1/\sqrt{\ell}\}
. It shares nearly identical sample complexity bounds with Gaussian matrices but is more efficient in terms of random bit consumption. Recent work has also demonstrated its efficacy in accelerating zeroth-order optimization for fine-tuning large language models
(
Dang et al., 2025
)
.

•

SRHT:
Structured sketching via SRHT, formulated as
\bm{S}=\bm{P}\bm{H}_{d}\bm{D}
, aims to bridge the gap between statistical efficiency and computational speed
(
Woodruff et al., 2014
)
.
Matrix
\bm{H}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the Walsh-Hadamard matrix with
+1
and
-1
entries.
\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries sampled uniformly from
\{+1,-1\}
, and
bP\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times d}
is the uniform sampling matrix. While the required
\ell
is slightly higher than Gaussian matrices by logarithmic factors, the fast Walsh-Hadamard transform allows for
\bm{S}\bm{A}
to be computed in
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}\log\ell)
(for
d\times d
matrices), offering a significant speedup for dense data.

•

Sparse Embedding:
These matrices optimize for sparsity by maintaining only
s\ll\ell
non-zero entries per column. Based on
Cohen, 2016
, a sketch size of
\ell=\Omega(k\log(k/\delta)\gamma^{-2})
and sparsity
s=\mathcal{O}(\log(k/\delta)\gamma^{-1})
are sufficient for the
(\gamma,k,\delta)
-property. This construction is particularly effective for high-dimensional sparse data where preserving sparsity is crucial.

3.1
Events

Assume the objective function
f(\bm{x})
satisfies Assumption
1
and
\bm{S}_{t}
is an oblivious
\left(\frac{1}{4},k,\delta\right)
-random sketching matrix just as defined in Definition

with
4\leq k
and
0<\delta<1

Then, we will define the following events

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t,1}:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}|\bm{s}_{i}^{\top}\bm{H}\bm{s}_{i}|^{2}\leq\zeta_{1}\mid\bm{s}_{i}\mbox{ is the }i\mbox{-th column of }\bm{S}_{t}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t,2}:=\left\{\left(\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq\nabla^{\top}f(\bm{x}_{t})\bm{S}_{t}\bm{S}_{t}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\leq\left(\frac{5}{4}+\frac{1}{k}\right)\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\right\},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t,4}:=\left\{\frac{1}{\ell}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}),\bm{s}_{i}\right\rangle\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\right\},

Lemma 1
.

\bm{S}_{t}

Lemma 2
.

Letting
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times d}
is an oblivious
\left({1}/{4},k,\delta\right)
-random sketching matrix follows Definition
1
with
k\geq 4

Proof.

By Eq. (

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{\top}f(\bm{x}_{t})\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})-\nabla^{\top}f(\bm{x}_{t})\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}+\frac{\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}}{k}\right).

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{\top}f(\bm{x}_{t})\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|-\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{\top}f(\bm{x}_{t})\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})-\nabla^{\top}f(\bm{x}_{t})\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}-\left\|\nabla^{\top}f(\bm{x}_{t})\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{\top}f(\bm{x}_{t})\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})-\nabla^{\top}f(\bm{x}_{t})\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|.

\displaystyle\left(\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\nabla^{\top}f(\bm{x}_{t})\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|\leq\left(\frac{5}{4}+\frac{1}{k}\right)\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}.

\displaystyle Q=T\cdot\ell=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\zeta_{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\ell\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{k\left\|\bm{H}\right\|}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{H})}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\delta}\right),

where the last equality is because of
\ell=\mathcal{O}\left(k\log\frac{T}{\delta}\right)
.
∎

Remark 1
.

Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
show Algorithm
1
can achieve an
\mathcal{O}\left({\left\|\bm{H}\right\|}/{\varepsilon^{2}}+{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{H})}/(k\varepsilon^{2})\right)
iteration complexity.
If we increase
k
which means increasing the batch size
\ell
, Algorithm
1
can find a
\varepsilon
-stationary point with less steps.
However Eq. (
3.25
) implies that increasing the batch size
\ell
will not reduce the query complexity at all.

Remark 2
.

The query complexity in Eq. (
3.25
) achieves a weak dimension dependency.
To make this clear, we can reformulate Eq. (
3.25
) as

\displaystyle Q=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{k\left\|\bm{H}\right\|}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{H})}{\left\|\bm{H}\right\|}\cdot\frac{\left\|\bm{H}\right\|}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\delta}\right).

In many real applications, we can choose proper matrix
\bm{H}
such that
{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{H})}/{\left\|\bm{H}\right\|}\ll d
.
Thus, Algorithm
1
can inherently achieve weak dimension dependency even in our extended
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth condition in Assumption
1
.
Our work extend the weak dimension dependency of zeroth-order algorithms with sketching matrices which holds for classical smooth functions to the extended
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions.

Remark 3
.

Recall that even by taking
\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\bm{x}_{t})=\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})
and Assumption
1
with
\bm{H}=\bm{I}
, finding an
\varepsilon
-stationary point via the fixed stepsize update

\bm{x}_{t+1}=\bm{x}_{t}-\eta\hat{\mathbf{g}}(\bm{x}_{t})

with
\eta_{t}>0

requires the iteration numbers that depends on

M=\sup\{||\nabla f(\bm{x})||\mid\bm{x}\text{~such that~}f(\bm{x})\leq f(\bm{x}_{0})\}

(
Zhang et al., 2020b
, Theorem 4)
.
In contrast, our Corollary
2
shows that
the iteration numbers required by Algorithm
1
only depends on
\left\|\bm{H}\right\|
and
\mathrm{tr}(\bm{H})
,
which implies the adaptive zeroth-order method can converge arbitrary faster than algorithm with fixed stepsize for the poor initialization.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we establish a theoretical explanation for the effectiveness of adaptive zeroth-order optimization methods. By characterizing the relationship between function value variability and gradient magnitude, we show that the empirical standard deviation of sampled function values serves as a reliable proxy for the norm of the (stochastic) gradient with high probability. This result provides a rigorous justification for standard deviation normalization as a principled adaptive step-size control mechanism in ZO optimization.

Building on this insight, we analyze adaptive ZO methods under a generalized
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness condition defined with respect to a positive semi-definite matrix norm. This setting goes beyond classical smoothness assumptions and captures anisotropic geometries commonly observed in modern deep neural networks, including large language models. Our analysis yields explicit convergence rates and query complexity bounds for both deterministic and stochastic objectives.

A central implication of our results is that adaptive ZO methods can achieve strictly faster convergence and significantly reduced query complexity compared to fixed-step vanilla ZO methods, by automatically adjusting to the local geometry of the optimization landscape. These findings bridge the gap between empirical practice and theoretical understanding of adaptive ZO algorithms. Looking forward, this framework opens avenues for analyzing more sophisticated adaptive mechanisms and for extending ZO optimization theory to broader classes of non-smooth and large-scale learning problems.

Lemma 25

(Azuma-Hoeffding Inequality)
.

Let
E_{1},\dots,E_{T}
be a martingale difference sequence with a uniform bound
|E_{i}|\leq B
for all
i
.
Then,

\Pr\left(\frac{1}{s}\sum_{t=1}^{s}E_{t}>\zeta\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2B}\cdot s\right).

(A.1)

Lemma 26
.

If
\bm{S}
is the Gaussian sketching matrix, that is, its entries are independently identically distribution drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,1/\ell)
with
\ell\geq 4
, then with a probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}
with

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}=\exp\left(-\left(\frac{\ell}{8}-\sqrt{\frac{\ell-2}{8}}\right)\right),

such that
\frac{1}{\ell}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{s}_{i}\right\rangle\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}
.

Proof.

First, if
S
is the Gaussian sketching matrix, that is, its entries are independently identically distribution drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,1/\ell)
.
Then by the property of Gaussian distribution, we can obtain that
\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{s}_{i}\right\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}/\ell)
.
Since
\bm{s}_{i}
’s are independent, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{s}_{i}\right\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}).

Thus,
(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{s}_{i}\right\rangle)^{2}
follows a
\chi^{2}(1)
distribution scaled with
\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}

Then, by Lemma
27
, the probability that

larger than

is no larger than

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[q_{1}\geq 1+2\sqrt{\tau}+2\tau=\frac{\ell}{4}\right]\leq\exp(-\tau),

with
\sqrt{\tau}=\sqrt{\frac{\ell-2}{8}}-\frac{1}{2}
which implies
\tau=\frac{\ell}{8}-\sqrt{\frac{\ell-2}{8}}
.
∎

Appendix B
The Connection between Assumption
1
and Related Work

Recall that the original relaxed smoothness condition
(
Zhang et al., 2020b
)
says the differentiable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
holds

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{2}f(\bm{x})\right\|\leq L_{0}+L_{1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|

(B.1)

for all
\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Later
Zhang et al., 2020a
proved (
B.1
) implies it holds

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})-\nabla f(\bm{y})\right\|\leq(AL_{0}+BL_{1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|)\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|.

(B.2)

and

\displaystyle f(\bm{y})\leq f(\bm{x})+\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\rangle+\frac{AL_{0}+BL_{1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|^{2}

(B.3)

for all
\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|\leq c/L_{1}
, where
A,B,c
are some constants. In fact we can strengthen (
B.3
) as follows

\displaystyle|f(\bm{y})-f(\bm{x})-\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla f(\theta\bm{y}+(1-\theta)\bm{x})-\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{x}-\bm{y}\rangle\,{\rm d}\theta\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\nabla f(\theta\bm{y}+(1-\theta)\bm{x})-\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(AL_{0}+BL_{1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|)\left\|\theta\bm{y}-\theta\bm{x}\right\|\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\theta(AL_{0}+BL_{1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|)\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}\,{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{AL_{0}+BL_{1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|^{2}.

It is natural to generalize the last term on above equation to the
\bm{H}
-norm, leading to the inequality

|f(\bm{y})-f(\bm{x})-\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle|\leq\frac{AL_{0}+BL_{1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|_{\bm{H}}^{2},

which is our Assumption
1
if we ignore the difference in constants.
