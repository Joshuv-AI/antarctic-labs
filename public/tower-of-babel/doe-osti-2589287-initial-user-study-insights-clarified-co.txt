1.0 Report Overview
The HPDF User Experience (UX) team conducted eight semi-structured interviews with twelve
individuals leading data and computing infrastructure work at DOE Office of Science (SC) user
facilities or projects. This report presents key takeaways synthesizing community perspectives
and needs, along with recommendations for the project. These insights should be considered as
conceptual design work continues, early partnerships begin, workflow readiness activities
evaluate and enhance key scientific tools, and early access systems are made available to the
Office of Science (SC) community. Vigilance in meeting community requirements and ensuring
workflow readiness will be needed to ensure HPDF’s success.

1.1 Interview Approach
Interviews were designed to update community members on the HPDF team’s progress as of
April 2025 and gather feedback on technical concepts and plans. A complete description of the
interview approach and protocol questions, and questions that arose from participants that were
answered can be found in Appendix A. In sum, each interview:

 1.​ Shared updated overview of the project’s anticipated scope
 2.​ Gathered feedback on community requirements
 3.​ Discussed perspectives and needs regarding concepts core to HPDF, including
 Availability, Reliability, and Resilience
 4.​ Presented the conceptual software architecture diagram and gathered reactions
 5.​ Discussed the state of AI/ML in their community and expectations

1.2 Key Findings & Recommendations
Below are summarized takeaways and recommendations for each focal section of the interview.
Additional details on topics can be found in the later detailed findings and discussion
sub-sections.

1.2.1 Clarified & Prioritized Community Requirements & Definitions
We have revised and clarified the original fifteen HPDF [1] (see Appendix A for the slide
presented to participants) requirements resulting in eight specific requirements which cover the
spectrum of community needs. Definitions were updated, requirements renamed, and some
requirements were merged. Two are overarching needs that must be persistently attended to,
four are key to HPDF Project work, and two should be focused on as HPDF prepares for facility
operations.

1.2.2 Value Propositions
Feedback from the user study clarified opportunities for HPDF to support work across the data
lifecycle across five key value propositions:

 1.​ Provide a stable, long-term home for (meta)data across the Office of Science. The
 data deluge and desire to leverage historic data for new analyses requires that users
 have access to dependable infrastructure for storage and use across the data lifecycle.
 Preserving and adding metadata within this infrastructure that describes the data sets is
 a high priority need.
 2.​ Enable data discovery and use through improved federation, breaking down silos.
 Participants look forward to the opportunity to leverage data from across facilities,
 projects, and communities. Users need to be able to search a centralized or federated
 catalog populated with high quality metadata.
 3.​ Provide reliable, resilient, and highly available infrastructure and services. Users
 need a system with cloud-like services specialized for science that ensures they can
 dependably accomplish their work when expected.
 4.​ Reduce effort and complexity for teams delivering services and end users building
 workflows. Common examples of opportunities to reduce the complexity of data work
 and services management include: automated data movement and resource
 optimization, containers, agentic AI, natural language workflow development, and
 automatic failover. Reducing the effort needed to engage with systems and services will
 allow communities to scale their infrastructure thus helping to accelerate science.
 5.​ Provide resources for enabling increased data stewardship within and among
 DOE communities. Research communities have ongoing efforts to develop shared
 metadata schemas, software stacks, and data types. HPDF should support this by
 enabling common infrastructure and supporting training without enforcing specific
 policies.

1.2.3 Perspectives on Core HPDF Concepts: Availability, Reliability, and
Resilience
Interviews inquired about participants' perspectives on the concepts of availability, reliability,
and resilience which are core to HPDF’s mission. Discussions also attempted to unpack each
community’s expectations and needs around these concepts when able.
​
Participants' ability to distinguish and define availability, reliability, and resilience was varied.
Interviewees generally combined or mixed concepts together, often rethinking the definition for
one when describing another. A synthesis of participants’ definitions and discussion yields the
following definitions:

 ●​ Availability — ability to fully leverage allotted resources to search for, access, and use
 data at any time when they need to do their scientific work.
 ●​ Reliability — near synonym for availability with strong emphasis on trust and
 transparency to end users.
 ●​ Resiliency — plans are in place to recover from errors and ensure continued service.

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 6

Participants emphasized that HPDF should evaluate and convey availability, reliability, and
resilience according to users’ experiences, not solely traditional facility metrics. For example,
participants need HPDF to account for lag when reporting on availability in time-sensitive
workloads. Users perceive long waits as rendering resources unavailable.

While participants did not clearly separate the three terms, they do require predictability from
HPDF and for data to be highly available. Interviewee’s mental model for HPDF’s availability,
reliability, and resilience consequently were most often compared to levels of service offered by
commercial cloud providers rather than high performance computing (HPC) facilities.
Interviewees caveated this explicitly by recognizing that HPDF will have fewer resources and
must make tradeoffs in its conceptual design and eventual operations. We expect that a strong
incentive for communities to leverage HPDF over standard commercial cloud services is the
anticipated value derived from partnerships and co-design efforts between HPDF and SC
facilities and projects, along with streamlined customization for scientific workflows across the
data lifecycle. Conceptual design will be aided by keeping the following points in mind:

 ●​ Metadata and associated catalogs must still be fully available in situations where facility
 services are degraded or down.
 ●​ Communities are likely willing to accept degraded performance to sustain access,
 especially if automated failover enables seamless experience.
 ●​ Enabling graceful failover and clearly communicating service expectations will make
 HPDF services feel highly available, reliable, and resilient to end users.
 ●​ Developing and evaluating approaches or solutions to managing tradeoffs between
 availability, reliability, and resilience should be a key activity in early partnership work.

Continued conceptual design, early partnerships, and workflow readiness activities can address
these expectations by:

 ●​ Defining how HPDF may or may not offer availability, reliability, and resilience
 guarantees for scientific communities.
 ○​ Define circumstances where guarantees require use of specific software or
 hardware designs.
 ○​ Define whether such guarantees are potentially available to all teams developing
 science infrastructure for end users, including Spokes that do not bring additional
 hardware, or just those building Spokes that specifically provide additional
 hardware and/or services support.
 ●​ Defining capabilities for reliably delivering data services to end users.
 ○​ Identify software and hardware needed to enable seamless degradation or
 failover modes.
 ○​ Identify needed capabilities with early partners and evaluate using early access
 systems.

1.2.4 Software Architecture and Design
Participants were interested in and excited by the conceptual design details that were presented
and discussed (see Appendix A). The conceptual software architecture was understandable and
sensible to participants. They appreciated the opportunity to see new details from the team and
give their feedback at this early stage. Interviewees understood that HPDF aims to offer a
platform as a service. Overall, interviewees were satisfied that HPDF plans generally align well
with their communities’ needs and directions.

Participants were interested in the project's general timeline and planned scope. They asked
about the prioritization of requirements and components in the conceptual software architecture
diagram. They responded positively to the clarification that significant portions of the software
architecture are meant to be purchased or adopted, rather than built purely by the HPDF team.
They were also eager to know when HPDF resources would become available to their
communities. The opportunity to take part in early partnerships to co-design solutions and
access HPDF resources as soon as possible was valued. Participants were also eager to hear
timelines for early access systems and opportunities to access any early HPDF resources.

Interviewees endorsed the approach of empowering communities to integrate their own tools
within the facilities resources via Scientific Community Zones. Participants appreciated that this
notion will enable isolated areas where community developed and maintained solutions can be
hosted. HPDF can expect that research communities will wish to integrate their own established
data portals and systems with HPDF infrastructure. Participants were excited about the
possibility of developing new shared data portals to increase access to DOE data generated by
their users. In addition, interviewees wanted access to resources and services in the HPDF
Services Zone without being required to open support tickets to obtain access to individual
services. Collaboration with early partners to design and test documentation and the early
access system will ensure research communities are able to leverage the full HPDF stack
without difficulty.

Participants were uncertain about the scale of compute and data resources their communities
would demand in future, especially with respect to enabling AI/ML work. Given that interviewees
emphasised the need to reduce complexity for facility users, priority might be given to tooling
and services like AI agents that accelerate and simplify workflow integration and execution.

1.2.5 Perspectives on AI/ML Needs and Opportunities
The final topic that interviews explored was participants' perspectives on AI/ML in their facility or
project. These discussions underscored scientific communities’ demand for these fast evolving
technologies but did not always delve into specific use cases or well defined needs. Participants
generally hope to utilize AI/ML resources for addressing scientific questions in more depth,
moving beyond straightforward completion of everyday tasks. Early partnerships activities will
be important opportunities to inquire into AI/ML needs to keep up with evolving expectations and
ensure HPDF can meet them.

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 8

Participants mentioned many possible types of AI/ML work, and overall interviewees described
a desire to reduce human effort through the use of AI tooling. Specific activities mentioned
include: dataset augmentation, automated curation, agents for supporting data discovery, RAG
models for conversing with publications, reasoning with multiple lines of evidence, automated
documentation, experimental steering and real-time analysis, and ML Operations support. The
ML Operations need specifically referred to the tooling to support user’s ML work from model
development and training to deployment, retraining, and publication. Additionally, participants
believe that AI agents will be a key element to future work. In particular, advanced data portals
to help communities advance data access and use will likely benefit by supporting AI agents.

Making data AI ready was also discussed as a necessary input to enabling a future where
scientific work of all kinds is facilitated through AI/ML features. During conversations study
participants understood AI ready data to be findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable
(FAIR) and also able to be replicated where desired, be designed for performance, and be
validated with domain expertise. Specific tools, practices, or services for doing this work were
not discussed and should be explored during ongoing conceptual design and early partnership
activities.

Interviewees noted that one opportunity for HPDF is to provide a streamlined, more usable “on
ramp” to help scientists use AI/ML in their work. This type of on ramp was envisioned as
providing a well designed architecture, appropriate tooling, training resources, and curated
(meta)data. Notably, while (meta)data curation and stewardship is foundational to the AI/ML
work that research communities seek to engage in, participants emphasized that they did not
want HPDF to impose specific formats, standards, and data policies—only to empower the
communities to conduct their own stewardship work.

Multiple participants emphasized that there are never enough computing resources to satisfy
user demand for the GPUs important to AI/ML work, though participants had difficulty predicting
what scale that demand may be in the coming years. Given these observations, HPDF can
expect that compute hardware (both CPU and accelerators) will be consumed no matter how
much is provided. In light of participants’ uncertainty about their needs, further capacity planning
will require additional acquisition of quantitative historical data on resource usage and agility as
community needs and the AI space evolve.

2.1 Clarified & Prioritized Community Requirements
Interviews were designed to gather feedback on the HPDF Project’s initial characterization of
community requirements. The HPDF Project’s core requirements initially emerged from the
mission need, funding opportunity announcement, and SC reports and documents (e.g., IRI
Architecture Blueprint Activity Report [2], ESnet Requirements Review reports [3]). Through a
meta-analysis of these resources, an initial set of fifteen requirements were identified. The
present study explored what participants thought of these requirements, how they would
prioritize them for their community’s work, and discussed needs and expectations not covered
by the fifteen listed requirements.

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 10

These insights have allowed us to validate and refine the community requirements. As
presented in the summary table in the Overview, the fifteen requirements have been merged to
create eight. The definitions are more detailed to emphasize key points. Requirement titles now
better reflect key concepts that the team needs to address in its work.

In response to project needs and participant inquiries, the requirements were prioritized to
indicate when they need most attention. Two are overarching needs that must be persistently
attended to, four are key to HPDF Project work, and two are most relevant as HPDF scales up
for facility operations. Consistent engagement with users across project and operations work will
be needed to respond to emergent needs, prepare for services and workflow readiness, and
evaluate HPDF’s success.

Below, the eight revised community requirements are presented along with context and
examples from the previous meta-analysis and present user study.

2.1.1. Store significant volumes of heterogeneous data
Overarching need: Users require support for collection & storage of 100s PB of data now &
exabytes in future. Must store raw, processed, reduced, & finalized data products along with
metadata. Scope includes heterogeneous types & sources of data.

Reiterating the needs expressed in the published reports synthesized in the earlier
meta-analysis, participants emphasized their growing need for data storage. Communities vary
in their dataset sizes but a common concern is the data deluge brought on by technological
upgrades—both simulation and observation data volumes are growing. HPDF has the
opportunity to alleviate these concerns through delivery of long-term storage resources.

Study participants were seeking to supplement the data storage they have already. Consistent
with the trends captured in published reports (summarized in the tables from the meta-analysis
report appendices [1]), participants described rapidly growing storage needs. One participant
from Basic Energy Sciences indicated their facility currently has 4.5 PB of tomography data on
tape and 1.5 PB on spinning disk. After a facility upgrade is completed, they estimate capturing
1 PB raw data daily, necessitating rapid deletion in favor of smaller, reconstructed data. Some
communities may find long-term storage difficult to obtain and sustain, particularly for older
experimental or observational data that is less frequently used. Long-term archival storage in
HPDF is likely to need to be in the exabytes; readily accessible storage needs are in the high
hundreds of petabytes, with the ability to grow to multiple exabytes of storage over a five to ten
year timeframe. This depends on how community needs grow and the degree to which
significant swaths are housed directly in HPDF systems. However, some participants also noted
that their data can be regenerated; in cases such as these, long-term storage is not necessary.

Datasets may integrate data from multiple sources. These data may be experimental,
observational, or simulation and modeling data. Data may be raw, processed, reduced, or
finalized data products and metadata must be included as well. Variance in data types and sizes
exist within communities—not just between them. One study participant summarized this

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 11

challenge: “Our data formats are not uniform by any stretch.” Many participants expressed the
need to conduct multimodal analyses, underscoring the importance of this requirement to
support heterogenous data types.

As data move through their lifecycle, provenance information and metadata must be tracked for
appropriate (re)use of the core data products. Study participants considered the capture and
storage of such data vital to their communities’ ability to interpret data in the future and to
leverage AI/ML capabilities (see AI/ML). Storing and providing access to these (meta)data will
help ensure HPDF is ready to support AI/ML workflows.

Volumes of data stored will increase over time; the HPDF facility will need to provide exabytes of
storage over time as the facility grows. Work during the project’s conceptual design efforts
should continue to engage in capacity planning to more clearly define needed ratios of different
types of storage (e.g., high performance ready access versus archival). HPDF can leverage
existing DOE data at ASCR facilities to further inform capacity planning.

2.1.2. Provide compute needed to support scientific workflows across the
data lifecycle
Overarching need: Community has a significant need for on-demand, customizable, & reliably
available computing environments for running workflows across the data lifecycle. Workflows
include interactive, time-sensitive, long-term campaign, & AI/ML inference & agents.

Compute allocations of all kinds are highly valued and demand often exceeds availability. HPDF
can support reliable execution of data-intensive work by increasing available compute resources
and enabling construction of heterogeneous computational environments. One light source
participant described the limitations they face sharing on-demand compute. This individual
emphasizes the need for more resources as a result.

 “So right now, 10% is kind of marked for this on demand computing. And so that's four of
 the 44 petaflops, so it's basically four racks. So 50 nodes, or 56 nodes are available for
 that. But depending on what beam lines we run, we could use one beam line that would
 just suck up all of that. So we have some beam lines where it actually takes 50 nodes,
 real-time, all the time to keep up with data generation. And so that's great for that one
 beamline, but there's 71 others that are now starved for resources.”

Other interviewees noted that today their time-sensitive and AI/ML workflows end up at risk
when compute nodes (particularly GPU) at their existing compute facilities are unexpectedly
unavailable. They perceived this unavailability of resources as a risk to accomplishing their
scientific work, illustrating a need for more reliably available on-demand compute resources
across facilities. Processes for compute allocations can be another challenging aspect to on
demand compute needs. Today allocations are typically awarded on an annual basis, an
approach that can make it difficult to meet emergent needs.

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 12

Across research communities, patterns and models for computing use vary. HPDF must provide
compute that can support interactive, time-sensitive, long-term campaign, and AI/ML workflows.
Workflows may involve moving data between instruments, edge devices, local compute
resources, HPC facilities, or the cloud. Data may be locally hosted or ingested from multiple
sources. Work and data movement may be scheduled, triggered by events, or conducted ad
hoc. Users may wish to do interactive work in a Jupyter notebook, execute Slurm jobs at a
facility, or to stream data in real-time. HPDF users will expect support for Python ecosystems
and libraries like PyTorch. AI/ML services are desired to manage real-time streaming and
support curation, especially in disciplines where data volumes are increasing, driving the need
for GPUs and other accelerators.

Participants in the study wanted to be able to customize their compute environments. They
wished for visibility into which resources are available at a given time and the ability to specify
which are acceptable for executing a given job. HPDF users should also be able to construct,
modify, monitor, and move workflows through a web interface, command line, or API.

Portability and isolation were highly important to participants. Docker containers and Kubernetes
orchestration were frequently mentioned as means of ensuring these requirements. Some
participants had prior experiences where they had limited means of accessing the Kubernetes
layer and hoped that HPDF would improve on that, offering better control over their environment
in addition to ensuring their resources were “cordoned off from everything.”

2.1.3. Provide high access & reliability
HPDF Project focus: Users require predictability & expect HPDF to sustain access to metadata,
data, & services during localized outages. Degraded performance is likely to be acceptable to
most stakeholders, except in time-sensitive situations.

Interviewees indicated that HPDF must offer reliable access to data and services. Study
participants stated that they would not evaluate HPDF according to conventional facility metrics
for reliability and availability, but that they would judge based on their (or their end users’) ability
to accomplish scientific work. Participants requested a system that makes resource availability
predictable—users should be able to accurately anticipate which resources will be available and
when. Offering clearly defined service level agreements, recovery time objectives, and recovery
point objectives were discussed as means to provide reliability and build user trust. The ability to
receive advanced warning of maintenance periods were also considered assets for reliability.
Importantly, participants need metadata to remain accessible during any outages.

Participants’ mental models for HPDF were based on what they have come to expect from
commercial cloud providers. They would prefer sustained access to data and resources, even if
that meant degraded performance. When errors or outages occur, participants want HPDF to
ensure their workflows and systems fail gracefully. In such cases, users would be transitioned to
appropriate resources automatically if they’re available. Otherwise clear information on
degraded performance and availability would be communicated. In addition, participants hope
HPDF will provide the resources to either automate this failover or automatically migrate the

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 13

work without direct user intervention. Ideally, data will be mirrored rapidly when put in HPDF to
support reliability, but this will vary. For example, allowing users and communities to configure
their reliability needs within the HPDF system is one way to ensure satisfaction. This should be
a co-design activity to prepare for workflow readiness during the HPDF project.

As time scales for given data workflows decrease, the tolerance for downtime does as well.
Time-sensitive workloads, common among light source user facilities, need greater reliability
than those that can be carried out over a longer period of time. In time-sensitive instances,
access, reliability, and low latency are paramount. To reduce latency, data processing and
analysis will ideally be done close to the data collection source. Eventually, Spokes will be
essential to meeting some of these needs.

2.1.4. Provide tools & services for data catalogs & portals to support AI
workflows
HPDF Project focus: Simplify the implementation, deployment, maintenance of data community
catalogs & portals. Facilitate cross-catalog search & provide services for effective discovery,
processing, analysis, curation, & stewardship of data for AI workflows across DOE SC.

Data portals and catalogs are key tools and services through which HPDF can support easy
access and discovery of scientific data, connecting previously siloed research communities and
creating opportunities for novel inquiry. Portals and catalogs are essential to enabling data
submission, search, querying, browsing, and access through download or transfer. They also
enable seamless movement of data to, or access from, interactive analysis environments like
Jupyter. Participants in the user study expect portals to support them throughout the data
lifecycle, from discovery through analysis and stewardship. They repeatedly emphasized the
value that a shared data portal could offer—enhancing their community’s ability to discover data
and standardize across facilities or projects. Moreover, participants hoped that by using HPDF
throughout the research lifecycle and using it to capture metadata about their workflows, HPDF
could give users “real information about the what and the why of the science,” supporting
reproducibility and further inquiry.

The July 2024 HPDF-IRI meeting with participants from an array of disciplines emphasized the
need to simplify the creation and maintenance of data catalogs and portals. The user study
further underscored the need for catalog and portal tooling, as participants described the ability
to integrate (i.e., federate, see Availability / Reliability / Resilience below) data sources and
access them in a single portal as a key step in breaking down barriers between disciplines.
HPDF has the opportunity to provide a cross-facility or disciplinary catalog of scientific data,
enabling new scientific inquiry. One participant from a user facility described a use case for a
general data portal, likely as part of a Scientific Community Zone, leveraging an HPDF catalog
that helps bring together data from across different facilities.

 “I fully expect that when HPDF comes online, we will have some sort of data portal, data
 catalog. We will register our data with HPDF and so will the other [facilities]. And we will
 be able to search across those data sets. And we will be able to do multi modal analysis.

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 14

 So be able to say, ‘Show me data from similar … that I've taken at all the [facilities] with
 this sample.’ And then be able to submit a job to analyze that data, you know, using
 some analysis technique from multiple light sources.”

This participant’s perspective is representative of others’ in this study—HPDF users will wish to
interact with their own and others’ data without concern for where the data is physically stored.

Study participants also hoped and expected that AI would be integrated into the HPDF portal to
enhance their ability to discover, analyze, and steward data. AI was viewed as a way to scale up
human ability to document metadata, harmonize data sets, discover relevant data, and
construct workflows. Natural language search and workflow specification were common
examples of how AI could be implemented. One participant stressed AI as a requirement for
ensuring HPDF is perceived as a modern facility:

 “...I think that’s where everything is going: Describe your workflow with natural language,
 and then use your existing data to help fine tune or train models. And a data portal has
 to be able to do this in the future, otherwise it will look really old school.”

Participants expected that well designed portals would empower less technical users to
leverage capabilities that would be otherwise out of reach to them and to reduce the amount of
technical work that project and facility staff must engage in.

Finally, some participants described the capabilities of the existing portals they are developing
and maintaining. Communities with existing portals may wish to maintain these while federating
their data with a catalog in HPDF. These established portals are useful for understanding
expectations for HPDF’s offerings. To provide the same functionality, HPDF-enabled data
portals will need to be able to connect proposals, publications, and workflows along with data
and catalogs.

2.1.5. Provide tools & services to enable community data stewardship &
archiving
HPDF Project focus: Must provide tools & services for communities to enact & enforce
stewardship policies they develop. Communities must be able to ensure data & metadata
cataloged in HPDF are reliably accessible & citable.

Stewardship and archiving were frequent topics of conversation throughout interviews, although
stewardship work managed by HPDF itself was discussed with hesitant sentiments. Noting that
scientific communities vary in their willingness to share data and their interpretations of key
stewardship metrics like FAIRness, participants were hesitant to assign HPDF the role of
enforcing data stewardship. One study participant put it bluntly, “Harmonizing the data itself is a
big lift across the facilities. But that's not the help that we would request from you.” Participants
understood stewardship as enacting policies regarding data storage and curation. They see this
as a community specific activity with many community-based cultural nuances. Participants
noted that standards development and curation policies are a challenge that must be overcome

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 15

in part through culture change. Greater valuation of sharing, for instance, was noted as needed
in some communities before substantial curation efforts can succeed. HPDF imposing specific
policies or cataloging requirements would not be welcomed by SC communities. Participants felt
that HPDF should offer tooling for curation that could help accelerate standardization within
communities by serving as what one participant described as a “neutral third party” who can
help drive alignment towards best practices or tools, when it is a facility.

HPDF can offer software services and tools for stewardship across the data lifecycle, while
allowing communities to manage specific formats, standards, and data policies that might be
specific to their community. HPDF can support researchers by making it easier to track
provenance across scientific processes and decisions underlying the resulting data products.
This will be essential to generating useful metadata and is one aspect of making data AI ready
(see Perspectives on AI/ML).

In addition, HPDF systems must be capable of associating and enforcing licenses and
permissions around datasets. This was less of a focus during interviews, but the previous
meta-analysis highlighted its importance [1]. Communities and their projects must be able to
shift between enforcing privacy and allowing limited or public access to their data. Projects with
licensing and rights management needs must be able to use HPDF to securely share data
among permitted individuals and organizations and, later, to adjust who has such access. Users
must be made aware what their permissions are with respect to a data set. Relatedly, to support
citation, HPDF must provide unique identifiers like DOIs for datasets via OSTI.

2.1.6. Provide seamless user experience & standard APIs
HPDF Project focus: Deliver usable, seamless experiences for varied users working across data
lifecycle. Consistent & usable APIs are required to support automation & facilitate interaction
across HPDF.

HPDF’s user communities will require experiences that are consistent, relevant, productive, and
joyful to enable their scientific work. The HPDF Mission Need emphasizes a broad need for
usability and productivity to ensure scientists can accomplish their work without barriers.

Interviewees requested HPDF provide a seamless experience across facilities for users and
data. Regardless of where data are or computation is executed (ASCR facilities, commercial
cloud, CERN, etc.), users must be able to move, access, update, analyze, and archive data with
ease. One study participant shared a popular vision of seamlessness, describing how they want
to work with data at multiple facilities but see one view of these federated systems so that the
interface is “the same thing” regardless of which resources they interact with. This seamless
federation was understood as a way to support data discovery. System abstractions should
allow users to work with their data without constantly requiring that they consider where their
data or other needed resources are located. Standard HPDF APIs should enable deeper
investigation as needed when issues arise or performance needs to be enhanced. Participants
noted that both object store and POSIX models are expected for data access by end users.

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 16

APIs were very frequently raised as a preferred mechanism for data management and other
tasks. APIs were a high priority need to support automation, a key aspect of usability for the
researchers in this study. Automated data movement, resource monitoring and optimization,
error recovery, and access management were considered vital to reducing human effort,
minimizing bottlenecks, and ensuring smooth data collection.

Participants also raised the likely importance of incorporating AI agents to assist with data
discovery and work in general to deliver a modern user experience. One emphasized that they
increasingly believe a modern user experience will be delivered via agentic AI and data portals,
or other tools, without it will offer an antiquated experience. However, most participants
emphasized that they were less concerned with radically new interfaces, placing higher
importance on ensuring seamless interaction with data and compute resources across facilities.
Even without AI agents, well designed interfaces can empower less technically savvy users and
automation can reduce the need for support staff intervention. HPDF can reduce complexity for
communities and end users with seamless interfaces and thoughtful system designs.

2.1.7. Develop user support resources & practices
Operations focus: Establish & test ticketing systems, documentation, training materials &
programs, & user engagement programs.

Participants varied widely in their expectations for what user support might look like and how
critical it would be. Rather than desiring 24/7 support, many participants were more concerned
that there would be adequate information available to architect their own community
applications and services, an unsurprising finding given interviewees were often tasked with
developing infrastructure themselves. Some participants indicated a preference for more
autonomy and access deep into the HPDF stack rather than needing to involve HPDF staff
primarily via a support ticket system. Other participants noted that they do not offer round the
clock support to their own users and would not expect that of HPDF. These assertions suggest
that HPDF should prioritize developing and testing documentation and training for teams
building infrastructure for communities. More extensive 24/7 support models may be required
during HPDF operations when time-sensitive workflows become more common (e.g., real-time
data streaming via Spokes).

Given the present study focused on the needs of possible early partners, further inquiry is
needed to better understand the support needs of end users. Based on discussions with
participants about their AI/ML needs, AI operations may be a particularly valuable topic to
address in user training activities (see Perspectives on AI/ML). Because the project’s early
partnerships will be direct engagements between HPDF staff and other projects and facilities,
this is an opportunity to establish best practices and identify documentation and support
infrastructure necessary for workflow readiness. During operations, attention should be given to
refining these resources and scaling up their accessibility.

2.1.8. Ensure IRI & ASCR Ecosystem interoperation
Operations focus: Interoperation is required with existing ASCR facilities, via IRI, to enable fluid
access to distributed data & compute resources. Users require federated identity & access
management across facilities.

Participants regularly noted the need to make use of multiple data and compute resources at
once. Participants were concerned that without resources like HPDF they would have to turn to
expensive commercial cloud providers. By ensuring interoperation with other ASCR facilities,
HPDF will accelerate research activities. Participants often assumed that the IRI program would
enable the desired multi-facility workflows. One study participant imagined such a scenario
where HPDF and IRI could support them:

 “...Even better would be if you had something that was sort of more globally resource
 aware within, within all the ASCR compute infrastructure, right? So, you know, basically,
 like you utilize IRI to ship your job wherever it needs to go.”

Finally, an important aspect of scientific collaboration is managing access permissions and
security. Via IRI and ASCR Ecosystem work, HPDF should support improved federated identity
and access management experiences. Remote, real time collaboration should be unhampered
by authentication issues. Resolving such frictions is a priority for supporting science teams; one
participant from the BES community stated this is “the absolute number one” priority, but this
was identified as absolutely essential across disciplines.

2.2 Feedback on Core HPDF Concepts: Availability, Reliability,
and Resilience
Interviews addressed participants' perspectives on three concepts core to the HPDF mission:
availability, reliability, and resiliency of resources. Conversation covered expectations for
HPDF’s offerings and examples of how these concepts are applied when participants build and
sustain their own data platforms or services for end users.

2.2.1. Characterizing Availability / Reliability / Resilience
When asked about the terms availability, reliability, and resiliency, interviewees treated them as
entangled concepts that were difficult to tease apart. On multiple occasions, participants
attempted to distinguish and define the terms only to later reconsider those distinctions.

Overall, participants characterized availability as the ability to fully leverage allotted resources to
search for, access, and use data at any time when they need to do their scientific work.
Participants were primarily concerned that a task can be carried out as defined, regardless of
computing location. Graceful failover that involves little interaction would protect the availability
HPDF users need. Participants also requested the ability to see which resources are available
at a given time.

Participants used reliability as a near synonym for availability. Reliability also brought about
connotations of trust and transparency. Informative error messages were noted by some study

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 18

participants as a way to provide reliability. Well defined service level agreements, recovery time
objectives, and recovery point objectives were also offered as examples of how to be reliable. A
reliable HPDF would be predictable and set expectations clearly.

Resilience was described as both resource redundancy and proactive planning and engineering
existing for cases of failure. Resilience implies solutions are engineered into a system in
advance, not crafted ad hoc in response to live issues. Participants building data infrastructure
want redundancy in resources such that data access and services can be maintained even if
one physical site goes down. Pre-established failover plans should be established and rapidly
able to be acted upon. Participants emphasized a need for systems that are able to ensure
graceful failover with little intervention or attention needed from developers so that end users
are not interrupted and developers’ work is reduced. Participants also perceived other users and
communities as possible threats to their own system’s resilience and sought assurance that
each community zone would be isolated.

Participants frequently framed their expectations for HPDF’s availability, reliability, or resiliency
against the well defined offerings of commercial services like AWS and Google Cloud, while
acknowledging that these commercial entities have far more resources available to offer such
levels of service. Interviewees also indicated that HPC systems provide a different form of
“production level” availability, reliability, or resilience than what they require from a facility
focused on supporting the complete data lifecycle like HPDF.

2.2.2 User Expectations for Metrics Assessing HPDF Availability, Reliability,
and Resiliency
Participants stressed that it will be important for HPDF to establish metrics that reflect the user
experience and which employ language in a way that aligns with their mental models. Absent
this effort, interviews suggested HPDF users may feel frustrated, confused, or misled.

There was consensus that metrics that HPDF uses to capture and report resource availability,
reliability, and resiliency should be based on how users perceive the delivery of services and
tools on an everyday basis. This is in contrast to metrics that study participants understood to
represent meaningful data to HPC facilities but which are less effective at characterizing end
users’ experiences, especially when working with data services. One participant made this point
with respect to latency. In their experience a facility’s metric might assert that resources are
available to end users, but in practice there could be such high latency that users cannot
leverage them to do work:

 “And then you get into the latency point of availability. So a [user] could say, ‘Is [the
 system] available all the time?’ I would say we have only had less than two hours of
 downtime, but they could say, ‘Actually there was like, once or twice where I checked
 and [the system] felt slow, so that means it's not available.’”

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 19

Similarly, enabling automated and graceful failover when a resource becomes unusable (e.g., due
to maintenance or high latency) will be essential to ensuring a reliable, resilient experience for
end users.

2.2.3 Supporting Conscious Tradeoffs
Individuals emphasized their desire for dependable and high performance access to data and
services—cloudlike near 99.99% uptime would be ideal but is recognized as not entirely feasible
given project limitations. In most cases (e.g., delivery of data services), participants would prefer
sustained access to data and resources even if performance is degraded from the optimum.
Sustained but slower access is preferred over none. Providing high availability, reliability, and
resilience was seen as particularly important for newer users who may perceive one failure as
indicative that a facility is not the resource for them.

Downtime or degraded availability is likely to be less of an imposition in some scenarios.
Machine learning training was described as likely to be tolerant of downtime. Data analysis
during data collection (e.g., between shots in fusion energy) would not be since scientific staff
may only have fifteen minutes to do that work and make a decision.

As time scales decrease, tolerance for downtime and latency decreases when doing essential
scientific work. Time-sensitive data streaming and processing workloads require higher
availability and reliability with low latency than work that can be carried out over longer periods
of time.

2.3 Feedback on Conceptual Software Architecture
Another focus of the interviews was presenting the draft of the software architecture and
conceptual design to gather community reactions and feedback. Participants were walked
through the high level diagram (Figure 1) then examined the more detailed software stack
(Figure 2) to elicit questions and feedback on this design.

Overall, participants were able to make sense of the architecture diagram. They could
understand that HPDF aims to offer a platform as a service and endorsed the approach of
empowering communities to integrate their own tools with HPDF. Feedback on the architecture
diagrams included interest in these newly available details about HPDF, satisfaction that the
diagram seemed comprehensive, appreciation for the idea of combining multiple data sources,
and satisfaction that the plans seemed to align well with their communities’ futures.

Participants were curious about the planned scope along with the project’s general timeline.
Participants inquired about how requirements and components shown in the software diagram
would be prioritized and when they could expect to use HPDF resources. Verbal clarification
that significant swaths of the software architecture are meant to be purchased or adopted, not
built by the HPDF team as part of the project, were well received. Early partnership activities will
be key to co-designing resources as communities' needs evolve and they continue building
varied resources. This will help ensure that development of HPDF software and services
co-evolve with changing technologies and science efforts.

2.4 Perspectives on AI/ML Needs and Opportunities
The final topic that interviews explored was participants' perspectives on AI/ML in their facility or
project. This topic often emerged during earlier parts of the discussion and was later revisited to
further explore data readiness and AI use cases.

2.4.1 Challenge of rapidly evolving ecosystem and demands
Discussions around AI/ML underscored scientific communities’ demand for these fast evolving
technologies. Participants imagined many applications for AI/ML, including dataset
augmentation, automated curation, agents for supporting data discovery, RAG models for
conversing with publications, automated documentation, experimental steering and real-time
analysis, and ML ops support. Multiple participants emphasized that there are never enough
computing resources to satisfy user demand, though participants had difficulty predicting what
that demand may be in the coming years. A key expectation was that users will consume as
many GPU and other AI oriented computing resources that HPDF can likely offer. This is likely
to be a strong driver for GPUs and other accelerators, particularly in eventual Spokes who host
hardware.

In addition to uncertainty around compute resource needs, constant shifts in state of the art
tools and practices have left study participants unclear about the best AI/ML opportunities for
their community to pursue. Further inquiry is needed as researchers determine their needs and
new tools become available so that HPDF can support new workflows and meet evolving
demands.

2.4.2 AI ready data
Facilities and projects recognize that their significant stores of data provide great value.
Ensuring that this data is AI ready is intertwined with aims of making data findable, accessible,
interoperable, and reusable (FAIR). Participants perceived data FAIRness as necessary but not
sufficient for AI readiness. Data must also replicate where desired, be designed for
performance, and be validated with domain expertise. Accessibility in this case requires the
capacity for high-throughput downloading, integration of federated data sources, and the ability
to automate data work, thus HPDF infrastructure would play a vital role in ensuring AI
readiness. One participant further asserted that AI ready data would be situated in a knowledge
graph to enable reasoning. Multiple participants expressed that capturing data and model
provenance would facilitate AI/ML work.

2.4.3 Model training & usage
Participants were eager to develop large models. Prediction was a common use for AI/ML
among participants, but they sought to shift towards addressing more meaningful scientific
questions and reasoning based on multiple lines of evidence. Given the lack of precedent, it is
as yet still unclear how AI agents and tooling can enable more dynamic collaboration between
distributed scientific teams and their growing datasets. Additionally, facilitating development and

 Initial User Study Insights​​ ​ ​ ​ 23

stewardship of model registries emerged as a possible area for HPDF to provide value to
communities that are building and sharing resources.

2.4.4 AI operations support
AI/ML operations emerged as an interesting but not clearly scoped opportunity for HPDF.
Ideally, participants expressed AI/ML operations support would facilitate the full lifecycle from
model development and training to deployment, retraining, and publication. Given the rapidly
evolving nature of this ecosystem, study participants expressed some disorientation and wished
for support in choosing and using common AI/ML tools and software stacks so that they align
with other SC user facilities. “We don’t know enough about what we’re doing,” said one person.
The HPDF Project can offer standard tools to make scientific AI work easier, and as there is a
move towards operations, trainings can be offered to help scale up AI adoption.

2.4.5 Evolving to AI-enabled interaction models
AI agents are perceived as a future mechanism for engaging with data and systems. For
example, AI agents could leverage a community’s AI ready data in HPDF to automatically, with
minimal to no ongoing human interaction, find and integrate additional data sources in response
to a query posed by a researcher. Such agents would shift the interaction model from classical
portals and search interfaces to a more conversational setup where agents attempt to anticipate
or interpret scientific user’s needs. Designing and supporting effective agents that are reliable
scientific partners will be a major challenge if trust is to be built among end users. Participants
were eager to pursue agentic AI so that complex tasks can be accomplished more efficiently,
enabling less computationally savvy users to easily advance their scientific techniques.
