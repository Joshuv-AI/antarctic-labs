Amino acid infusion and acute kidney injury after aortic surgery: a multicenter observational study with target trial emulation
Authors: Junjie Wang, Jian Hou, Tao Yan, Jun Li, Weize Zhang, Chenye Wu, Hao Lai, Chunsheng Wang, Kai Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a common complication after cardiac surgery and is associated with increased mortality. Amino acids may improve renal perfusion, but their effect in patients undergoing aortic surgery remains unclear. We used the Aortic Surgery Database of the Shanghai Hospital Development Center to emulate a target trial. Patients were classified to one of two exposure strategies: receiving a daily amino acid dose of > 21 g during the first 2 postoperative days, or not receiving amino acid therapy above this threshold. Overlap weighting was used to balance covariates and emulate randomized assignment. The primary outcome was the incidence of AKI. Secondary outcomes included AKI stage, renal replacement therapy, hospital stay, in-hospital mortality, and 30-day mortality. Overall, 1,816 patients were included in the study. The incidence of AKI and 30-day mortality was 37.8% (n = 686) and 1.6% (n = 29), respectively. No significant association was observed between low-dose postoperative amino acid exposure and the incidence of AKI (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.79–1.17, p = 0.70) or 30-day mortality (OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 0.68–3.50, p = 0.30). In subgroup analysis, amino acid exposure was associated with a lower risk of AKI in patients aged ≥ 65 years (OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.53–0.95, p = 0.02). The subgroup finding in older patients should be considered exploratory and requires confirmation in future studies.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is one of the most common complications following cardiac surgery, with an overall incidence of approximately 30%–40%. It is an independent predictor of increased mortality [1,2]. Renal replacement therapy (RRT) is frequently required in cases of severe AKI; however, this intervention carries an increased risk of bleeding and infection [3]. Despite these implications, there are currently no definitive and effective preventive strategies for cardiac surgery-associated AKI (CSA-AKI).

The causes of CSA-AKI are complex and involve multiple processes, including inflammation, hypoperfusion, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and oxidative stress [2]. Renal hypoperfusion is a major contributor to CSA-AKI and can occur intra- and postoperatively. During cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), factors such as low flow, decreased perfusion pressure, hypothermia, and non-pulsatile perfusion patterns significantly impair renal perfusion. In animal experiments, CPB has been shown to reduce renal blood flow by more than 50%, leading to renal medullary hypoxia [4]. During the early postoperative period, low cardiac output has been recognized as a common trigger of AKI.

Previous studies have demonstrated that amino acid intake is an effective strategy for dilating renal vasculature and improving the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) [5–7]. As metabolic substrates, acute amino acid intake stimulates proximal tubule reabsorption and suppresses tubuloglomerular feedback activation, leading to afferent arteriolar vasodilation and an increase in GFR [5]. Amino acids administered via a gastric tube or intravenous infusion can mimic the renal effects of a high-protein diet, increasing renal blood flow and GFR by 25%–40% [5].

Although randomized controlled trials have provided evidence supporting the protective effect of amino acids on renal function after cardiac surgery, most studies have focused on patients undergoing valve surgery or coronary artery bypass grafting, with limited inclusion of patients undergoing aortic surgery (<15%) [8,9]. Hypothermic circulatory arrest (HCA), commonly used during aortic surgery, is a significant risk factor for postoperative renal insufficiency [10]. The temperature and duration of HCA have been shown to significantly impact the incidence of postoperative AKI [11,12]. Therefore, heterogeneity exists among patients undergoing different types of cardiac surgery, raising questions about the applicability of previous findings regarding the protective effect of amino acids on renal function in this specific patient group.

In this context, we conducted a real-world study in patients undergoing aortic surgery to investigate the effects of early postoperative amino acid exposure on renal function using a target trial emulation approach.

Patients and methods

Data source and ethics statement

The data for this study were obtained from the Aortic Surgery Database of the Shanghai Hospital Development Center (SHDC-ASD). The SHDC-ASD integrates de-identified electronic medical records of patients who have undergone aortic surgery at major cardiac centers in Shanghai. These centers perform the vast majority of cardiac surgeries in Shanghai. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (approval number: B2020-158). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board because this was a retrospective study based on de-identified data obtained from an existing database.

Study design

In this study, we conducted a retrospective observational analysis designed to emulate a hypothetical randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating whether early postoperative low-dose amino acid exposure is associated with renal outcomes in patients undergoing open aortic surgery. The study was structured according to the framework of target trial emulation, with prespecified protocol components including eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, treatment assignment, start of follow-up, outcomes, censoring rules, and the causal estimand (see Table S1) [13]. The study design framework and timeline are shown in Figure 1. T0 was defined as the date of surgery and served as the common time point for eligibility assessment, treatment assignment, and the start of follow-up. The primary analysis was designed to estimate the observational analogue of the intention-to-treat (ITT) effect of assignment at T0 to one of the two postoperative treatment strategies. By aligning these key design elements, target trial emulation can help reduce avoidable biases in observational studies, including selection bias and immortal time bias, thereby improving causal interpretation of treatment effects using real-world data [14].

Patient eligibility and exclusion criteria

We conducted a comprehensive review of the database to identify patients who underwent open aortic surgery between December 2020 and May 2024. Patients treated with endovascular procedures were not included. To minimize potential confounding, patients who underwent concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting were also excluded. The simplified Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation was used to calculate the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [15]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: age < 18 years, eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, dissection or aneurysm involving the renal artery, concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting, endovascular intervention surgery, and missing main data. After applying these criteria, 1,816 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 2).

Flow chart of participant selection, exclusions, and cohort inclusion.

Treatment assignment

Postoperative amino acid administration was identified based on the order name and corresponding administration time in the medication records. The specifications of the amino acid formulations used in the cohort are provided in Table S2. Because this was a retrospective real-world study, postoperative amino acid administration was determined by routine clinical decision-making rather than a standardized study protocol, making it difficult to harmonize daily dosing with the protocolized high-dose renal-protection regimens used in previous RCTs (i.e., 2 g/kg/d) [8]. We therefore prespecified a pragmatic exposure threshold of >21 g/d. This threshold was chosen because it closely corresponded to the amino acid content of several commonly used standard infusion units in routine practice (approximately 21 g per infusion unit), thereby allowing us to identify patients who received clinically meaningful amino acid exposure rather than only trivial or incidental exposure. According to the framework of target trial emulation, patients in the ‘amino acid group’ were classified as receiving a daily amino acid dose of >21 g within the first 2 days after T0, whereas those in the ‘control group’ were classified as not receiving amino acid therapy above the prespecified threshold during the same period. This 2-day exposure window was broadly consistent with the optimal infusion duration suggested by available data from the PROTECTION trial [16].

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of postoperative AKI within the first week after aortic surgery. Secondary outcomes included AKI stage, the use of RRT during hospitalization, the duration of hospital stay post-T0, in-hospital mortality, and 30-day all-cause mortality. The occurrence and stage of AKI were determined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria [2]. In the primary analysis, follow-up began at T0 and ceased at the earliest of (1) occurrence of the outcome of interest, (2) the last available database record, or (3) the end of the study period. The baseline serum creatinine level was defined as the last laboratory value obtained before T0. If multiple serum creatinine values were recorded on the same day, the maximum value was used to determine AKI occurrence and stage.

Statistical analysis

To ensure covariate balance between the two patient groups and to approximate random assignment at T0, we applied overlap weighting (OW) to each patient. Compared with inverse probability weighting (IPW) and propensity score matching (PSM), OW retains the maximum sample size while mitigating the effects of extreme weights [17]. Specifically, we constructed a multivariable logistic regression model with amino acid exposure as the dependent variable and all potential confounders as covariates. The propensity score model included the following prespecified baseline covariates: age, sex, smoking status, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, arrhythmia, congenital heart disease, previous cardiac surgery, previous stroke or transient ischemic attack, NYHA class, white blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelet count, serum creatinine, urea nitrogen, serum albumin, left ventricular ejection fraction, cardiopulmonary bypass time, deep hypothermic circulatory arrest time, deep hypothermic circulatory arrest temperature, and surgery type. The probability of each patient receiving an amino acid infusion (i.e., propensity score [PS]) was calculated based on the model. Patients in the amino acid group were weighted using 1 – PS, whereas patients in the control group were weighted using PS. The distribution of propensity scores before and after OW is illustrated in Figure S1. The balance of baseline covariates was assessed by calculating standardized mean differences (SMDs) between groups after weighting, with an SMD < 0.1 considered acceptable. The primary analysis was designed to estimate the observational analogue of the ITT effect. Accordingly, patients were analyzed according to their initially assigned treatment strategy, regardless of subsequent treatment deviation, crossover, or discontinuation [18].

We calculated descriptive statistics for the weighted and unweighted baseline characteristics of the patients. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were described as means and standard deviations. A logistic regression model was applied to the weighted cohort to evaluate the effects of amino acid exposure on the outcome events. Independent t-tests were conducted for continuous outcomes, such as the length of hospital stay. We set the significance level at 0.05 and conducted two-tailed statistical tests. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Subgroup analysis

To further validate the generalizability of the results and minimize potential bias, we stratified patients into subgroups according to age (<65 vs. ≥65 years), sex (male vs. female), NYHA class (I–II vs. III–IV), eGFR levels (<90 vs. ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2), and CPB time (<120 vs. ≥120 min). We then performed subgroup analyses following the same workflow as the primary analysis.

Sensitivity analysis

The group-defining strategy according to the ITT principle may reduce or dilute estimated treatment effects, as it tends to be more conservative. In the sensitivity analysis, the group-defining strategy was changed to the per-protocol (PP) approach to further evaluate the robustness of the results. The PP approach aims to reflect treatment effects under ideal circumstances by including only participants who completed the intervention as planned. Patients who deviated from the intervention plan midway were excluded. Compared to the ITT principle, the PP approach is more likely to detect the impact of intervention on outcome events [18].

In the unweighted amino acid group, the mean age was 54.68 ± 13.86 years; 712 (71.9%) patients were male, 402 (40.6%) had NYHA class III–IV, and 629 (63.5%) patients had a history of hypertension. A total of 29% (n = 532) of the enrolled patients underwent the David procedure, while 25.8% (n = 468) underwent the Bentall procedure. Although the two groups had similar distributions for the above variables, they were imbalanced with respect to white blood cell count, hemoglobin, serum creatinine, LVEF, CPB time, and DHCA time, with SMDs exceeding 0.1. However, OW effectively balanced the patient characteristics between the two groups (SMD < 0.1).

Primary and secondary outcomes

In the unweighted population, AKI was diagnosed in 388 patients in the amino acid group, compared with 298 in the control group (Table 2). The incidence of AKI in the control group was slightly higher than that in the amino acid group (38.1% vs. 37.6%). The stage of AKI was mainly stage 1 in 263 (25.5%) and 216 (27.6%) patients in the amino acid and the control groups, respectively. Stage 3 AKI occurred in 66 (6.4%) patients in the amino acid group and 36 (4.6%) patients in the control group. After OW to analyze the effect of amino acids on postoperative renal function, amino acid supplementation was not associated with a reduced risk of AKI events (odds ratio [OR] = 0.96, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.79–1.17, p = 0.70).

In the unweighted population, the postoperative hospital stay was 8.88 ± 6.64 days in the amino acid group and 8.06 ± 5.94 days in the control group. No statistically significant difference was found between the two groups (p = 0.31). A lower proportion of patients in the control group received RRT, but the difference was not statistically significant (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.42–2.02, p = 0.84). Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

Several findings were observed in the subgroup analysis (Figure 3). First, compared with younger patients, those aged 65 years and older had a higher incidence of AKI (47.6% vs. 33.5%). However, low-dose amino acid exposure was associated with a lower incidence of AKI in patients aged 65 years or older (OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.53–0.95, p = 0.02). In addition, the incidence of AKI exhibited clear sex differences, with males having a significantly higher incidence than females (41.6% vs. 28.0%). Further analysis revealed that CPB time exceeding 120 min and an eGFR of less than 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 were important factors influencing the occurrence of postoperative AKI. However, in these high-risk subgroups, amino acid exposure was not significantly associated with AKI risk, which was consistent with the main analysis.

Discussion

In this multicenter real-world study, we used target trial emulation to evaluate the association between early postoperative low-dose amino acid exposure and renal outcomes in patients undergoing open aortic surgery. After OW to balance baseline variables, amino acid exposure was not associated with the incidence or severity of AKI. There was no statistically significant difference in early mortality between the two groups. However, in patients aged 65 years or older, amino acid exposure was associated with a lower incidence of AKI; this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the subgroup analysis was exploratory.

Many previous studies have analyzed the effects of amino acids on renal function, including multicenter RCTs and cohort studies. However, their conclusions have been inconsistent [8,9,19–21]. This variability may be due to differences in AKI definitions, variations in treatment strategies, or heterogeneity in study populations. Unlike the PROTECTION trial, we found no potential association between low-dose amino acid exposure and CSA-AKI after aortic surgery, possibly because the practice-based low-dose exposure in our study differed substantially from the protocolized high-dose regimens used in prior randomized trials [8]. As shown in Table S2, the common concentration of the compound amino acids is 5%–10%. According to the treatment standard (2 g/kg/d) for patients in the amino acid group in the PROTECTION trial, a 50-kg patient would need to receive at least an additional 1,000 mL of solution per day. This higher-dose strategy may be more difficult to implement in routine postoperative practice after aortic surgery, where fluid management is often clinically constrained. In a study by Doig et al. patients in the amino acid group received significantly larger fluid volumes despite increased urine output [19]. Fluid overload in the early postoperative period is associated with high mortality and low cardiac output syndrome [22]. Of note, in the study by Pu et al. participants were patients who were at a higher risk of developing AKI, with an eGFR of under 90 mL/min/1.73 m2. Patients receiving amino acids experienced a lower incidence of AKI than the control group (9.0% vs. 20.0%), though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.3) [9]. In contrast, our study included a more diverse patient population, which helped mitigate the potential influence of specific patient characteristics on outcomes. In our subgroup analyses, amino acid therapy did not reduce AKI risk across different renal function levels. However, patients with poorer preoperative renal function were more likely to develop AKI (41.8% vs. 31.8%). Currently, most studies determine AKI occurrence based on serum creatinine changes from baseline. However, postoperative fluid resuscitation strategies can significantly influence serum creatinine levels [23,24]. In previous studies, most patients developed AKI on the first postoperative day [8,9], but the actual onset may have occurred earlier [24]. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of AKI based on urine output, serum creatinine, and fluid balance may provide a more accurate evaluation of its occurrence.

In this study, we observed a possible association between amino acid supplementation and lower AKI risk among older patients, even at relatively low doses. Older individuals have higher protein requirements than younger adults, particularly during periods of physiological stress induced by cardiac surgery. Despite these increased needs, the vast majority of older patients fail to meet the recommended protein intake targets during the critical early postoperative period [25]. Different age groups respond differently to amino acid intake. Compared with younger adults, older adults are more vulnerable to insufficient amino acid consumption, which has been associated with an increased risk of mortality [26]. Amino acid supplementation has been shown to significantly improve physical function and promote protein anabolism in older patients undergoing cardiac surgery [27]. Additionally, supplementation with glycine and N-acetylcysteine in older adults can mitigate oxidative stress, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [28]. Furthermore, amino acids exert direct vasodilatory effects on the kidneys, either by serving as metabolic substrates or by coupling with oxidative metabolism [5,6]. These mechanisms may interact with each other and influence renal function in older patients; however, further investigation is required.

Our study has several strengths. First, we conducted this research within the framework of ‘target trial emulation’ [13]. Target trial emulation is one of the most powerful methods for drawing causal inferences about interventions in observational studies, as it prevents common biases such as selection and immortal time bias [14]. Ensuring consistency in the three critical time points—eligibility assessment, randomized treatment allocation, and the commencement of follow-up is essential for accurately emulating a randomized trial [29]. For instance, in the study by Faeq et al. the protein intervention was initiated before cardiac surgery, thereby failing to account for intraoperative mortality, which introduced immortal time bias [21]. However, in our study, these important time points were scrutinized and precisely defined. Second, we employed OW to balance covariates across groups. While IPW and PSM are widely used approaches for adjusting confounding factors in observational studies, both methods have limitations. PSM often results in excluding a subset of participants, whereas IPW is influenced by extreme propensity scores [30]. Compared with IPW, OW provides better variance control and greater precision. If applied properly, OW achieves a statistical balance comparable to that of randomization [17,31]. Finally, this was a multicenter observational study with a sufficiently large sample size, which enhances the reliability of causal inferences. Furthermore, we verified the robustness of our findings by conducting subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations primarily related to the nature of observational research. First, although we used OW to balance measured baseline covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Some clinically important factors—such as nutritional status, fluid tolerance, vasopressor use, and center-level variables—were unavailable in the database, which may have influenced both postoperative amino acid administration and AKI risk. Therefore, despite careful adjustment for measured baseline characteristics, confounding by indication may still have affected the estimated associations. Second, compared with other RCTs [8,9], the intervention dose used in our study was relatively lower. Before the publication of the PROTECTION trial, high-dose intravenous amino acid administration was not routine practice in most clinical settings. Therefore, in this real-world cohort, we used a pragmatic exposure threshold based on commonly used amino acid formulations in clinical practice. Whether higher-dose amino acid administration would provide additional benefit in patients undergoing open aortic surgery requires further investigation. In addition, postoperative AKI was evaluated solely based on the serum creatinine component of the KDIGO criteria, without incorporation of urine output criteria. Such a serum creatinine–only assessment may miss oliguria-based AKI and may be affected by perioperative volume shifts, which may have led to underestimation of the true incidence of AKI [24]. Finally, unlike previous RCTs, we did not have information on patients’ diets after surgery, which may have affected our judgment of group assignments [8,9]. However, since patients generally receive standard care after surgery, this potential factor is unlikely to have significantly influenced our conclusions.

Conclusion

In this multicenter observational study with a target trial emulation framework, we found no association between low-dose postoperative amino acid exposure and the incidence of AKI or short-term mortality in patients who underwent aortic surgery in the primary analysis. However, in subgroup analyses, amino acid supplementation was associated with a lower risk of AKI among patients aged 65 years or older. This association should be interpreted as exploratory and requires further confirmation.
