Combined Heart-Lung Transplant for Pulmonary Artery Aneurysm Secondary to Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension
Authors: Ajay P Kerai, A Cozette Killian, Charles W Hoopes, Eric Orozco-Hernandez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary artery aneurysms (PAAs) are rare occurring in approximately 1 in 14,000 cases. While PAA is frequently associated with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), the optimal management strategy remains undefined due to the rarity of the disease.Case SummaryWe report a case of a 58-year-old female with longstanding idiopathic PAH who underwent combined heart-lung transplantation to treat a rapidly expanding 8.95-cm PAA with associated cor pulmonale and severe pulmonic valve regurgitation. Graft function remained excellent; however, an ischemic stroke in the postoperative period led to significant morbidity.DiscussionDespite the known association of PAA with PAH, specific treatment guidelines are lacking. Delay in intervention can lead to poor surgical candidacy and adverse outcomes.Take-Home MessagesPAA may progress despite stable PAH hemodynamics. Combined heart-lung transplantation can be a viable option for select patients with severe PAH, giant PAA, cor pulmonale, and severe pulmonic valve regurgitation.Key words: combined heart-lung transplant, pulmonary artery aneurysm, pulmonary hypertension

History of Presentation

A 58-year-old female with idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) on triple pulmonary vasodilator therapy, ambrisentan, sildenafil, and intravenous treprostinil was admitted for a planned treprostinil pump exchange. She reported worsening New York Heart Association Class III symptoms and dyspnea for which she was admitted after the pump exchange. On examination, vital signs were stable (blood pressure 126/73 mm Hg, heart rate 74, respiratory rate 14, SpO2 93% on 2L nasal cannula), loud P2 was heard, and there was no jugular venous distention or peripheral edema.

Past Medical History

At age 29, she underwent mechanical mitral valve replacement for severe mitral regurgitation and was also diagnosed with PAH (pulmonary artery (PA) systolic pressure 63 mm Hg, mean PA pressure in the 40s, preserved cardiac index). She was managed with escalating combination PAH triple therapy.

In 2015, she was first found to have a main pulmonary artery aneurysm (PAA) measuring 6.65 cm (Figures 1 and 2). In 2016, she developed recurrent syncope and worsening dyspnea, with imaging showing moderate right ventricular (RV) dilation with moderate RV systolic failure and moderate to severe pulmonic valve regurgitation (PVR). She was subsequently listed as United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) status 4 for heart-lung transplantation and then later delisted in 2019 after clinical improvement.

Investigations

On admission, computed tomography angiography demonstrated further enlargement of the PAA to 8.95 cm (Figure 4), up from a prior size of 8.3 cm. Main PAA size was measured outer-wall to outer-wall at the level of PA bifurcation, perpendicular to vessel's long axis, taking the maximum diameter of the aneurysmal segment.1 Echocardiography showed moderately dilated RV with moderately reduced RV systolic function and severe PVR (Figure 5).

Management

Given progressive aneurysm enlargement, worsening RV dysfunction, and declining functional status, she was upgraded to UNOS status 2 by exception. She required high-flow nasal cannula oxygen (35 L/min, FiO2 100% with ambulation; 4L nocturnally) while continuing ambrisentan 10 mg daily, sildenafil 40 mg 3 times daily, and intravenous treprostinil 66 ng/kg/min.

After 164 days of inpatient waitlist management, suitable donor organs became available.

She underwent a combined heart-lung transplantation with cardiopulmonary bypass time of 255 minutes and a clamp time of 174 minutes. The PA was densely adherent to the ascending aorta, and severe anterior mediastinal calcifications necessitated delayed sternal closure on postoperative day (POD) 2. After weaning from sedation following a delayed sternal closure, she exhibited severe neurological deficits with a National Institute of Health stroke scale score of 20 and a last known normal prior to surgery. A noncontrast computed tomography head revealed multiple acute infarcts in the right cerebral hemisphere with a 3-mm leftward midline shift and suspected infarcts in the left hemisphere. Magnetic resonance imaging was consistent with extensive diffusion restriction in the right hemisphere compared to the left hemisphere concerning for diffuse hypoxic ischemic injury with mild hemorrhagic transformation and 4-mm leftward shift suggestive of subfalcine herniation. In the postoperative period, no arrhythmias were detected, and embolic workup was not pursued as the injury was attributed to intraoperative hypoperfusion. Thrombolysis or surgical intervention was not indicated, and the patient was not on therapeutic anticoagulation or antiplatelet agents.

She required tracheostomy and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube placement for prolonged airway and nutritional support. The tracheostomy was decannulated on POD 33, and she was discharged to a skilled nursing facility on POD 75. At discharge, graft function was excellent, with normal lung parenchyma and biventricular function. Neurologic deficits included cognitive impairment, left-upper-extremity weakness, spasticity requiring botulinum toxin injections, and expressive aphasia.

Outcome and Follow-Up

From 30 to 90 days, graft function remained excellent, with normal pulmonary function tests, imaging, bronchoscopies with biopsies, echocardiograms, and negative donor-specific antibodies, showing no evidence of primary graft dysfunction. At POD 90, the patient remained percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy–dependent, demonstrated bilateral toe movement, had mild word-finding difficulty but could converse and answer questions, and required total assistance for bed mobility.

Discussion

PAA is a rare entity with prevalence estimated at 1 in 14,000 cases.2 Managing PAA in the context of PAH is challenging due to the lack of standardized guidelines and the unpredictable correlation between hemodynamic severity and aneurysm progression.3 Mortality risk increases with aneurysms >6 cm or those associated with severe pulmonary hypertension.2

Although surgical thresholds, such as the 5.5-cm cutoff for surgical consideration, are often extrapolated from aortic aneurysm data, PAA represents a distinct entity with less-robust evidence where surgical indications are largely derived from expert opinion, case reports, and institutional experience.4,5 In patients with severe PAH and progressive aneurysm, surgical intervention is often the only viable long-term option.4

A key decision in this case was the choice of heart-lung transplantation over bilateral lung transplantation (BLTx). This was based on 3 major considerations. 1) likelihood of irreversible RV dysfunction. The patient had a decades-long history of idiopathic PAH with progressive cor pulmonale, RV dilation, and RV systolic dysfunction. As a result, there was significant concern that the RV would not recover adequately following BLTx, placing her at high risk of persistent or refractory RV failure postoperatively. 2) Despite maximal PAH therapy, patient's pulmonary vascular resistance remained markedly elevated (7.6-8.2 Wood units), indicating a substantial fixed component. This degree of residual pulmonary vascular resistance increases the risk of postoperative RV failure with BLTx, particularly in the setting of longstanding PAH and severe PVR. 3) Prior sternotomy, mechanical mitral valve, severe calcification, and a large aneurysm adherent to the aorta created substantial operative complexity. This created a hostile operative field with increased technical complexity and surgical risk. En bloc heart-lung transplantation allowed for a single, controlled operation addressing the aneurysmal PA, prosthetic valve, and mediastinal adhesions, while avoiding staged or reconstructive approaches that may carry higher cumulative morbidity. Additional considerations included the long-term implications of immunosuppression in a patient with a prosthetic valve and the potential need for future valvular reintervention, which would further complicate management after isolated BLTx.

The postoperative neurologic complication was likely multifactorial. The patient underwent a prolonged and technically complex reoperative sternotomy, including extensive dissection of a giant PAA densely adherent to the ascending aorta, in the setting of prior cardiac surgery and mediastinal calcification. These factors contributed to extended cardiopulmonary bypass duration and likely periods of reduced cerebral perfusion, supporting a primary mechanism of global hypoxic–ischemic injury. Additional contributors such as microembolic phenomena related to cardiopulmonary bypass, aortic manipulation, and reoperative dissection cannot be excluded. However, the absence of a large-vessel occlusion and the imaging pattern—bilateral, watershed-predominant injury—favor a hemodynamic mechanism rather than a focal embolic event. Importantly, while larger aneurysms have been associated with increased operative complexity and overall morbidity, they should not be interpreted as independent predictors of perioperative stroke. For example, Kreibich et al. noted increased postoperative morbidity with larger PAAs.1 Literature suggests varying surgical thresholds. Metras et al. advocated for early surgical intervention to prevent fatal outcomes such as rupture or right-heart failure, while Seguchi et al. recommended surgery for PAAs >6 cm.6,7 In this context, aneurysm size is more appropriately viewed as a marker of surgical difficulty and physiologic vulnerability rather than a direct causal factor in cerebrovascular complications.

Outside the United States, a similar case was described in the United Kingdom in 2020 by Eadington et al. of a 51-year-old patient with PAH who underwent a combined heart-lung transplant for a giant 12-cm PAA, severe PVR, and RV dysfunction.8 Schwarz et al. in Austria described 7 patients with PAH who underwent main pulmonary trunk and bilateral lung transplant for severe PAA (mean 70.4 mm) as a lung-only strategy.9

This case highlights the importance of early consideration for surgical intervention in rapidly expanding PAAs and underscores the need for further studies to establish standardized management guidelines for this rare but life-threatening condition.

Conclusions

This case highlights the complexity of managing PAH complicated by a rapidly enlarging giant PAA, cor pulmonale, and severe PVR. Despite long-term medical optimization and stable hemodynamics, her PAA progressed, ultimately necessitating combined heart-lung transplantation. While graft outcomes were excellent, significant postoperative neurologic morbidity occurred. Early recognition and timely surgical referral are critical, and standardized guidelines are needed to optimize management of this rare but high-risk condition.

Take-Home Messages

•Pulmonary artery aneurysm can progress despite seemingly stable pulmonary arterial hypertension hemodynamics, and rapid enlargement or associated right ventricular and valvular dysfunction should prompt timely surgical evaluation with close imaging surveillance and individualized risk assessment.

Pulmonary artery aneurysm can progress despite seemingly stable pulmonary arterial hypertension hemodynamics, and rapid enlargement or associated right ventricular and valvular dysfunction should prompt timely surgical evaluation with close imaging surveillance and individualized risk assessment.

•Combined heart-lung transplantation is a viable and definitive option for carefully selected patients with severe pulmonary arterial hypertension, giant pulmonary artery aneurysm, right ventricular dysfunction, and severe pulmonic valve regurgitation, especially when multiple surgical indications are present.
