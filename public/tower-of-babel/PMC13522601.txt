The metal mixture inflammatory index has been associated with an increased prevalence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and higher all-cause mortality
Authors: Zixing Yan, Xinyun Xie, Ziming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common respiratory condition with increasing prevalence and mortality worldwide. The Metal Mixture Inflammation Index (MMII) is a novel metric designed to quantify the potential impact of mixed heavy metal exposure on systemic low-grade inflammation. The weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) has emerged as a new anthropometric indicator of obesity. This study aimed to investigate the association between MMII and COPD, assess the mediating role of WWI, and evaluate the relationship between MMII and both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with COPD. A study was conducted using data from the 2005–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Logistic regression, subgroup analysis, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were employed to examine the association between MMII and COPD prevalence. Mediation analysis was performed to explore the potential mediating effect of WWI on the relationship between MMII and COPD. Additionally, Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the associations between MMII and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with and without COPD. A total of 11,577 participants were included, comprising 550 individuals with COPD and 11,027 without COPD. After adjustment for known confounders in multivariable logistic regression models, each unit increase in MMII and WWI was associated with a 5.85-fold (OR = 5.85, 95% CI: 3.41–10.03) and 1.30-fold (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.06–1.59) increase in the odds of COPD, respectively. Similar results were observed when MMII and WWI were categorized into tertiles, with a significant dose-response trend (P for trend < 0.05). RCS models revealed a linear positive association between both MMII and WWI with COPD prevalence. Mediation analysis indicated that WWI exerted significant direct and indirect effects, accounting for 2.40% of the association between MMII and COPD. Moreover, higher MMII levels were associated with increased all-cause mortality in the general population (HR = 2.41, P < 0.001), COPD population (HR = 2.62, P = 0.033), and non-COPD population (HR = 2.04, P < 0.001). No significant association was found between MMII and cardiovascular mortality. MMII was positively and linearly associated with COPD prevalence. Elevated MMII levels were independently associated with increased all-cause mortality among the overall, COPD, and non-COPD populations, but no significant relationship was observed with cardiovascular mortality. Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common chronic respiratory disorder characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms (such as cough and sputum production) and progressive, irreversible airflow limitation1. It is estimated to affect approximately 300 million individuals worldwide and is responsible for around 3 million deaths annually2, making it the third leading cause of death globally3. Although COPD remains incurable, its growing prevalence has positioned it as a major contributor to the global burden of disease. The condition is more prevalent in low- and middle-income countries, where exposure to both indoor and outdoor air pollution is often higher4. It is widely accepted that chronic airway inflammation and oxidative stress are key pathological mechanisms underlying COPD. Inhalation of harmful gases or particulate matter can induce inflammation in the lung parenchyma and airways. The release of inflammatory mediators contributes to tissue destruction, leading to emphysema and impairment of airway defense and repair mechanisms. This process results in an imbalance between oxidative and antioxidative responses, ultimately causing small airway fibrosis and progressive, irreversible airflow obstruction5. In addition to airway inflammation, COPD has also been associated with systemic inflammation, potentially linked to impaired lung function. However, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

With ongoing industrialization, heavy metal–induced environmental pollution has become a growing concern. Heavy metals—defined as elements with a density greater than 5 g/cm³6, including mercury, cadmium, cobalt, molybdenum, lead, and tungsten7—can enter the human body via ingestion, inhalation, or dermal absorption8. These metals have been shown to trigger inflammatory responses, disrupt the balance between oxidants and antioxidants, and induce oxidative stress9,10. This imbalance promotes the production of inflammatory mediators, activates inflammatory pathways, and affects cytokine metabolism and expression, ultimately altering the activity of inflammatory enzymes11, which may contribute to the development of respiratory diseases such as COPD. Recent studies have once again demonstrated a significant association between the concentrations of cadmium and lead in the blood and the risk of COPD (doi: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1494336). Higher levels of blood cadmium and lead are significantly associated with the risk of COPD (OR was 1.27, 95% CI: 1.13–1.44 and 1.69, CI: 1.13–1.50; p < 0.001). The Metal Mixture Inflammation Index (MMII), developed by Wang et al.7 using the reduced rank regression (RRR) model, is a novel index designed to quantify the potential systemic inflammatory response induced by mixed metal exposures. It provides new insights into the mechanisms through which heavy metals may contribute to disease pathogenesis. However, limited research has explored the relationship between MMII and both the prevalence and prognosis of COPD. Investigating this association may offer new directions for COPD prevention and promote respiratory health.

Obesity has been strongly associated with inflammatory respiratory diseases such as COPD. The weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), proposed by Park et al.12, was developed to overcome the limitations of body mass index (BMI) by more accurately reflecting body fat and muscle mass. Higher WWI has been significantly associated with an increased risk of respiratory symptoms—such as cough, wheezing, and dyspnea—and COPD itself13. Furthermore, several studies have indicated a positive association between heavy metal exposure and obesity14–16. Based on this evidence, it is hypothesized that WWI may mediate the relationship between MMII and COPD prevalence. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the associations between MMII, COPD prevalence, and its prognosis using data from the 2005–2018 cycles of NHANES.

Methods

Study participants

Data from seven consecutive NHANES cycles (2005–2018) were included in this study. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), is a cross-sectional, population-based survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population. All NHANES protocols were approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants; therefore, additional institutional review board approval was not required. Detailed methodological information is available at www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.

A total of 70,190 participants were interviewed across the 14-year study period. Participants were excluded if they were under 20 years old or pregnant (n = 31,152), had missing MMII data (n = 26,881), or lacked complete data on COPD and WWI (n = 580). The final analytic sample included 11,577 participants (Fig. 1).

A flow diagram of eligible participant selection in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. MMII, Metal Mixture Inflammatory Index; COPD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; WWI, weight-adjusted waist index.

Diagnosis of COPD

The diagnosis of COPD was defined based on previously published criteria17: (1) In individuals without a prior COPD diagnosis, a preliminary diagnosis was established if the post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC ratio was less than 0.7 with a negative bronchodilator response. (2) Self-reported medical questionnaire data were also used to determine COPD status, including: (a) Whether a physician or other health professional had ever diagnosed the participant with COPD. (b) Whether the participant had ever been told they had chronic bronchitis or emphysema. (c) Participants aged ≥ 40 years with a smoking history and chronic bronchitis who reported using (long-term use) inhaled corticosteroids, selective phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors, mast cell stabilizers, or leukotriene modifiers18.

Definition of MMII and WWI

The Metal Mixture Inflammatory Index (MMII) was constructed following the approach proposed by Wang et al.7 to quantify the inflammatory potential of mixed heavy metal exposure. Six urinary heavy metals (mercury, cadmium, cobalt, molybdenum, lead, and tungsten) were included as exposure variables, and two systemic inflammatory biomarkers, C-reactive protein (CRP) and white blood cell (WBC) count, were selected as response variables. Reduced rank regression (RRR) was applied to derive linear combinations of metal exposures that maximized the explained variance in CRP and WBC levels. The first RRR-derived factor, representing the metal exposure pattern most strongly associated with low-grade systemic inflammation, was retained for further analysis. Subsequently, stepwise linear regression (entry criterion P = 0.10) was performed to identify the metals that contributed most significantly to this inflammatory exposure pattern. Metals retained in the final model were weighted according to their regression coefficients. A higher MMII score reflects a stronger pro-inflammatory metal exposure profile and greater systemic inflammatory burden.

The weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) was calculated as waist circumference (in centimeters) divided by the square root of body weight (in kilograms), as proposed by Park et al. 12.

Mortality outcomes collection

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s National Death Index (NDI) database (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality-public.htm) provided the mortality statistics. On December 31, 2019, the most recent follow-up was carried out. The International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10) codes were used to determine the causes of death. This study’s main findings center on mortality from cardiovascular and all-cause diseases (encompassing codes I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I25, I26-I51, and I60-I69)19.

Covariables

Based on previous research17, several covariates were selected to control for potential confounding. These included age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, poverty-income ratio (PIR), hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Detailed definitions and categorizations of covariates are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1). All statistical analyses were performed in accordance with NHANES analytic guidelines. Appropriate examination sample weights, strata, and primary sampling units (PSUs) were applied in all analyses to account for the complex survey design and to ensure nationally representative estimates. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Continuous variables were presented as weighted mean ± standard error (SE), while categorical variables were expressed as weighted frequencies and percentages. Group differences were assessed using weighted t-tests for continuous variables and weighted chi-square tests for categorical variables.

To examine the association between MMII and COPD prevalence, three logistic regression models were constructed: Model 1: Unadjusted; Model 2: Adjusted for age, sex, education level, marital status, PIR, and race/ethnicity; Model 3: Further adjusted for hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia based on Model 2. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to explore potential linear or non-linear relationships between MMII, WWI, and COPD prevalence. Subgroup analyses were also conducted to examine the consistency of associations across strata.

Mediation analysis was performed to evaluate the indirect, direct, and total effects of WWI on the relationship between MMII and COPD prevalence. The proportion mediated was calculated as: (Indirect effect) / (Indirect effect + Direct effect) × 100%. All mediation analyses were conducted using the “mediation” package in R20. A nonparametric bootstrap procedure with 5,000 resampling iterations was applied to estimate the average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), and total effect. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals were obtained from the bootstrap distributions. An indirect effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval did not include zero. Weighted multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate associations between MMII and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among individuals with and without COPD. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were employed to assess differences in survival outcomes across MMII tertiles. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics

A total of 11,577 participants were included in the final analysis, with equal distribution of sex (50% male and 50% female). Most participants were in the 20–40 and 41–60 age groups, each accounting for 38% of the total sample. Participants were categorized into two groups based on COPD status, with a weighted COPD prevalence of 4.5%. Significant differences were observed between the COPD and non-COPD groups in terms of age, race, education level, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia (P < 0.05). Notably, participants with COPD had higher levels of both MMII and WWI compared to those without COPD. Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Mediation effect

The previous analyses justified the conduct of mediation analysis. As shown in Table 3, after adjusting for covariates, MMII was significantly associated with WWI (β = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.11–0.24, P < 0.001). After full adjustment, a significant mediation effect of WWI was observed (Fig. 4). The indirect effect of WWI was estimated at 3.01 × 10⁻³ (2.00 × 10− 4 − 6.04 × 10− 3, P = 0.024), and the direct effect was 1.20 × 10⁻¹ (7.88 × 10− 2 − 1.82 × 10− 1, P < 0.001). The proportion of the total effect of MMII on COPD mediated by WWI was 2.40% (P = 0.024).

Association of MMII with mortality

As presented in Table 4, weighted Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the associations between MMII and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in the total population, as well as in COPD and non-COPD subgroups. Higher MMII levels were significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality in the overall population (HR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.71–3.42), in participants with COPD (HR = 2.62, 95% CI: 1.08–6.33), and in those without COPD (HR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.42–2.93) (P < 0.05 for all). All models showed significant linear trends (P for trend < 0.05). However, no significant associations were observed between MMII and cardiovascular mortality in any of the groups (P > 0.05).

Discussion

This study aimed to clarify the association between MMII and COPD, while also exploring the mediating role of WWI. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first investigation to report a direct relationship between MMII and COPD using data from seven NHANES survey cycles (2005–2018). The findings revealed a significant positive association between MMII and COPD prevalence, as well as between WWI and COPD. Mediation analysis further indicated that WWI partially mediated the association between MMII and COPD. Additionally, MMII was positively associated with all-cause mortality in the general population, in individuals with COPD, and in those without COPD, but no significant association with cardiovascular mortality was observed.

Smoking remains the primary risk factor for COPD. Other environmental exposures, including air pollutants and heavy metals, also contribute to increased disease risk. Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress play critical roles in the onset and progression of COPD by damaging lung parenchyma and peripheral airways, leading to persistent respiratory symptoms and irreversible airflow limitation21. The chronic inflammation characteristic of COPD involves the accumulation of inflammatory cells—primarily macrophages, lymphocytes, and neutrophils—within the pulmonary vasculature, airways, and lung tissue. Once these inflammatory mediators “spill over” into systemic circulation, a widespread inflammatory response may be triggered, contributing to conditions such as skeletal muscle wasting and cachexia. Additionally, systemic inflammation may worsen comorbidities, including ischemic heart disease, heart failure, osteoporosis, normocytic anemia, lung cancer, depression, and diabetes22. Prolonged inflammation may weaken the airway’s antioxidant defenses, resulting in oxidative imbalance23. Excessive oxidative agents, especially superoxide dismutase and catalase, can accelerate the degradation of collagen and elastin, impairing the integrity of airway cellular barriers and promoting COPD progression24,25.

The MMII, developed by Wang et al.7, is an innovative index designed to comprehensively evaluate the systemic inflammatory burden resulting from combined exposure to multiple heavy metals and its potential link to mortality. The MMII integrates exposure data for nine metals, with six (mercury, cadmium, cobalt, molybdenum, lead, and tungsten) ultimately included. Notably, lead, cadmium, and cobalt are among the most abundant metals in smokers’ blood26. Cadmium, a toxic non-essential metal, has been linked to reduced FEV1/FVC ratios27. It disrupts tight junction proteins in airway epithelia, weakens the extracellular matrix by degrading collagen and elastin, and compromises barrier function—ultimately contributing to emphysema28,29. Lead (Pb) exerts toxicity across all biological systems, accelerating red blood cell lysis30, inducing tissue hypoxia, and impairing DNA repair, apoptosis, methylation, and cell differentiation31. These effects have been directly linked to the worsening of COPD symptoms32. Mercury, another highly toxic metal, primarily affects the respiratory system in its vapor form33. In animal models, mercury exposure resulted in oxidative stress, immune stimulation, and significant damage to alveolar and type II epithelial cells, as well as elastic fibers in the lungs, contributing to the development and progression of COPD34. Although molybdenum is an essential cofactor for enzymes such as xanthine oxidase and sulfite oxidase, high exposure levels may have toxic effects. Elevated xanthine oxidase activity has been linked to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and cardiovascular events, likely through oxidative stress pathways35. Since oxidative stress is a known pathological hallmark of COPD, high molybdenum levels may contribute to airway injury and disease onset. While data on tungsten toxicity are limited, the MMII developed by Wang et al. has been associated with increased all-cause mortality7, consistent with our findings that MMII is linked to higher mortality in both COPD and non-COPD populations, but not to cardiovascular mortality.

Obesity has been shown to induce metabolic dysfunction and chronic inflammation, which may in turn contribute to airway inflammation, impaired lung function, and ultimately COPD36. While Body Mass Index (BMI) is commonly used to assess obesity, its limitations have led to the development of WWI, which captures visceral adiposity more accurately by incorporating waist circumference and body weight. Prior studies have reported a significant positive association between WWI and the prevalence of emphysema37 and COPD13, consistent with our findings that WWI partially mediates the relationship between MMII and COPD. Although the mediation proportion of WWI in the association between MMII and COPD was relatively modest (2.4%), this finding should be interpreted within the context of COPD as a multifactorial disease. COPD development involves complex interactions among environmental exposures, systemic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and body composition. Therefore, it is biologically plausible that no single mediator would account for a large proportion of the total effect. The observed mediation suggests that altered body fat distribution represents one contributory pathway linking metal-related inflammatory burden to COPD risk. Importantly, even small mediation effects may have public health relevance at the population level. Considering the widespread exposure to environmental metals and the high prevalence of both central adiposity and COPD, a modest but statistically significant indirect effect could translate into a meaningful attributable burden in the general population. These findings provide mechanistic insight and support further investigation into metabolic pathways as potential targets for intervention.

This study offers several strengths: (1) It is the first to systematically explore the relationships between MMII, WWI, and COPD, demonstrating that MMII is positively and linearly associated with COPD prevalence, with WWI acting as a partial mediator. This suggests that metabolic inflammation may play a role in the pathway linking metal exposure to COPD. (2) MMII was positively associated with all-cause mortality in the general population, and among individuals with and without COPD, supporting its potential as a prognostic biomarker for COPD with clinical relevance. (3) The use of nationally representative NHANES data enhances the generalizability of our findings to the U.S. population. (4) Comprehensive adjustment for a wide range of sociodemographic and clinical covariates reduces residual confounding and strengthens the robustness of the findings. (5) Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were conducted to validate the stability and consistency of the main results, further supporting the scientific rigor of the study.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of the present study precludes inference of causal relationships between MMII, WWI, and COPD. Although mediation analyses were performed to explore potential pathways, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution. Prospective cohort studies with repeated exposure measurements and, where feasible, randomized controlled trials are needed to validate the proposed causal pathways and establish temporal relationships. In addition, due to the cross-sectional design, temporal relationships between MMII, WWI, and COPD cannot be established, precluding causal inference. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted as correlational. Reverse causality is also possible; for example, COPD-related systemic inflammation, altered renal function, medication use, or behavioral changes may influence metal metabolism or urinary excretion patterns, thereby affecting MMII levels. Second, although multiple covariates were included in the analyses to minimize confounding, unmeasured or residual confounding (e.g., occupational heavy metal exposure history (such as miners, metallurgical workers), smoking intensity and cumulative smoking amount, long-term indoor and outdoor air pollution exposure levels, and undiagnosed chronic inflammatory diseases) cannot be fully ruled out. These factors may influence both metal exposure and COPD risk, potentially affecting the observed associations. Third, several variables in NHANES are based on self-reported data, including disease history and lifestyle factors, which may introduce recall bias or misclassification. Although standardized questionnaires and trained interviewers were used to reduce reporting errors, residual bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Additionally, heavy metal exposure levels in NHANES are measured at a single time point, limiting the ability to assess long-term cumulative exposure effects, which may underestimate the true impact of chronic exposure. The NHANES database only obtains heavy metal concentrations through a single urine test, which cannot reflect the long-term cumulative heavy metal exposure levels of participants, and may underestimate the true impact of chronic exposure on COPD incidence and mortality; at the same time, MMII only includes 6 types of heavy metals (mercury, cadmium, cobalt, molybdenum, lead, and tungsten), and does not cover other heavy metals that may be related to COPD (such as arsenic, chromium, etc.), which may result in incomplete exposure measurement. In addition, the present analysis was based on NHANES data collected between 2005 and 2018. Although these data are nationally representative for the corresponding survey cycles, changes in environmental exposures, lifestyle factors, and healthcare practices over time may limit the generalizability of our findings to the current population. Therefore, while the present study benefits from a large, nationally representative sample, further longitudinal and contemporary studies are warranted to confirm the robustness and clinical utility of our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this nationally representative study based on NHANES data is the first to demonstrate a significant positive association between MMII and both COPD prevalence and all-cause mortality. Additionally, WWI was identified as a partial mediator in the relationship between MMII and COPD. This underscores the importance of early public health interventions and long-term environmental exposure monitoring. Future longitudinal studies are needed to establish causality and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms.
