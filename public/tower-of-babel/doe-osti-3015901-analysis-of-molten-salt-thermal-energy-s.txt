Executive Summary
Molten Salt Thermal energy storage (TES) is essential for commercial concentrating solar power (CSP)
plants, enhancing their capacity factor and dispatchability to provide a stable source of power to the grid
and ultimately reduce electricity costs. Molten salt TES is becoming more important for other
technologies including nuclear power, thermal-electrical storage, and concentrating solar thermal (CST)
for industrial processes. While molten salt TES tanks have been successfully deployed in lower-
temperature parabolic-trough plants, the current state-of-the-art central receiver systems operating at
temperatures up to 565°C have experienced multiple costly failures. These failures are associated with
the immaturity of the technology and problems in tank design, fabrication, commissioning and,
sometimes, aggressive operation. A primary contributing factor is the absence of a dedicated technical
standard for high-temperature molten nitrate TES tanks; designers must currently rely on existing codes
like API 650 and ASME Section II, which are insufficient because they fail to account for the unique
demands of high temperatures, thermal cycling, and transient conditions.

In March 2024, the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), formerly NREL, released the Failure
Analysis for Molten Salt Thermal Energy Tanks for In-Service CSP Plants report (NREL/TP-5700-
89036) that summarized the failure mechanism and factors contributing to these failures. This report
presented detailed information on the molten salt tank model formulation and validation and the
employment of the model to evaluate the temperature, stress, and deformation distribution in a
representative tank design for typical plant operation conditions. The report also presented a set
recommendations for future work to improve tank’s reliability including the consideration of new base
materials and welding fillers, salt distribution ring optimization, feasibility analysis of post weld heat
treatment, reevaluation and enhancement of welding procedure specification, and implementation of
safety operation protocols.

This report, part of the DOE SETO-funded project, agreement DE-EE0038475—'Failure analysis of
molten salt tanks for in-service CSP plants,' investigates the efficacy of the new CSP industry
hypothesis to use smaller TES tanks with thicker floors to reduce failure susceptibility and begin to
examine the effects of preheating during commissioning on the initial stress state and distortions of the
tank floor. It was found that while residual stress and distortion from welding fabrication are
independent of the tank diameter, a significant reduction in stress during operation was achieved with
the smaller, thicker-floored design, thereby providing evidence to support this new CSP plant
hypothesis.

The preheating analysis results, in turn, are still in an early stage and require further research to provide
an accurate quantification of the effect of this process on residual stress and distortion in the tank floor.

Resolving failures in molten salt TES tanks is crucial not only for CSP but also for other applications
considering these technologies. Despite the deficiencies of early implementations, molten salt TES
remains one of the most mature, scalable, and cost-effective TES technologies. Addressing current
failures by perfecting tank design and fabrication is fundamental to unlocking its full potential beyond
CSP, extending its application to nuclear, superhot geothermal, and industrial process heat.

 1 Introduction
 1.1 Background
 Current solar tower CSP plants use a two-tank system with molten salt for heat storage (TES). The hot
 salt tanks are usually built with stainless steel (SS) 347H, following design and material
 recommendations from the American Petroleum Institute’s (API) 650 [1] and the American Society of
 Mechanical Engineers’ (ASME) Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC), Section II [2]. Multiple
 failures in molten salt hot tanks in solar tower CSP plants [3], including Crescent Dunes in the United
 States, Gemasolar in Spain, Noor III in Morocco, and Cerro Dominador in Chile, have resulted in major
 financial losses and reduced trust in the technology. During the first phase of the project funded by the
 Department of Energy (DOE) – Solar Energy Technologies Office (SETO), agreement 38475—'Failure
 analysis of molten salt tanks for in-service CSP plants'—the primary causes for these failures were
 analyzed. It was found that these failures were mainly associated with a flawed tank design and
 fabrication of the tank floor, leading to high stress, stress relaxation cracking (SRC), buckling, low cycle
 fatigue (LCF), and creep [4]. Tank designers and constructors currently rely on existing standards like
 API 650 supplemented by ASME BPVC; however, this approach has proven to have limitations for the
 high temperatures, thermal cycling, and transient conditions unique to the CSP application. New
 standards like the ASME TES-1-2023 – ‘Safety Standard for Thermal Energy Storage Systems: Molten
 Salts’ [5] have been developed. This standard establishes the safety-related criteria and high-level
 requirements for the design and construction of molten salt systems; however, it does not provide
 prescriptive, step-by-step technical guidelines on how the specific construction, welding, and fabrication
 procedures should be executed, often deferring those execution details to established industry practice.

 The first project report for the DOE SETO-funded project, agreement DE-EE0038475, published in
 March 2024 [4], outlined a comprehensive path for future research, suggesting several alternatives
 regarding tank design, fabrication, operation, and commissioning. Two of these alternatives—tank size
 and the preheating process—were initially explored within the project, and their results are presented in
 this current report. Motivated by CSP industry trends toward multiple, smaller tanks (typically below 30
 meters in diameter), this analysis explored the impact of tank size. This approach represents a departure
 from the ~41-meter diameter commercial tanks employed at plants such as Crescent Dunes and Noor III.
 The study specifically considered tanks with varying diameters and floor thicknesses and their effect on
 stress distribution in the tank floor. In addition to tank size, industry evidence suggested that the
 preheating process could strongly influence both residual stress relief and the susceptibility to floor
 buckling [6]. Therefore, the project also focused on analyzing the stress distribution and deformation of
 the tank floor during a typical preheating process and provides a general description of tank preheating.

 1.2 Factors Affecting Tank Integrity
 Drawing on extensive research and experience with commercial Concentrating Solar Power
 (CSP) plants, the project team identified four primary failure modes in SS 347H molten salt hot tanks:
 Low Cycle Fatigue (LCF), Stress Relaxation Cracking (SRC), creep, and buckling, Figure 1. These
 failures result from a dangerous confluence of several major factors related to design, fabrication, and
 operation. Key design and fabrication deficiencies include a floor design prone to buckling, defects in
 weldments (like lack of fusion), the selection of the SRC-susceptible E347 filler material, and non-optimal
 welding procedures that introduce high residual stresses in the tank floor. Operational stressors exacerbate
 these flaws, specifically insufficient salt mixing creating large thermal gradients, non-uniform friction
 between the distorted floor and foundation, challenging transient conditions, and aggressive operation at

 1

low inventory levels. Based on this analysis, failures in SS 347H tanks are typically a combination of these
failure behaviors driven by multiple design, fabrication, and operational deficiencies.

1.3 Project Description and Approach
The first phase of the project consisted of Tasks 1 through 5 (see Figure 2), whose primary
goal was understanding the mechanical failure mechanisms in commercial-scale molten
salt TES tanks. The second phase of the project focused on analyzing the impact of tank
size and the tank preheating process (Tasks 6 and 7) on the stress and deformation
distributions in the tank, particularly in the tank floor.
This project was divided into seven tasks and seven milestones. A detailed diagram showing the tasks,
activities, and milestones is presented in Figure 2. Milestones 1 through 5, which were associated with
Tasks 1 to 5, were described in the previous project report [4]. The project team developed a
representative molten salt hot tank design based on information received from multiple CSP plants, see
Figure 3. This design considered high-level dimensions characteristic of tanks with a capacity of 100
MW or larger. Although the representative design applies to multiple plants, it does not fully correspond
to any particular plant, ensuring the confidentiality of the proprietary information received. After the
representative tank geometry was created in Solid Edge and transferred to ANSYS, the resulting model
was optimized and validated using historical operational data. Simulations were conducted using NLR’s
Eagle and Kestrel high-performance computers (HPC). Since the proprietary data could not be used
directly, the team developed a unique dataset of representative operational cases. This involved

 2

extracting, scaling, and reorganizing transient events—such as rapid temperature changes—collected
from multiple days and plants, allowing the team to simulate typical commercial operating conditions.

 Figure 2. Diagram of the technical scope summary of the project.

The complete hot tank model is an integrated simulation system built from four sequential sub-models to
analyze the thermal and structural performance of TES tanks. The process begins with the sparger ring
computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model, which uses the incoming salt flow conditions (from the
downcomer) to predict the flow rates exiting the sparger orifices. These rates then serve as boundary
conditions for the CFD molten salt inventory model, implemented in ANSYS, which simulates the salt
mixing process within the tank and determines the resulting temperature and pressure distributions
throughout the salt and tank at every timestep. The thermal data is then transferred to the tank
mechanical (structural) model to calculate the associated thermal stress induced in the tank's floor, shell,
and roof. A separate floor welding fabrication model (using Abaqus) was also developed to calculate the
initial residual stress and distortion caused by the construction process, ensuring these initial conditions
are imported into the structural model to provide a realistic starting state for the final stress analysis. A
diagram for the hot tank model, describing the integration of all sub-models, is presented in Figure 4.

1.4 Lessons Learned from Task 1-5
This report presents the results and analysis for Task 6 (Impact of Tank Size) and Task 7 (Tank
Preheating Process) conducted under the project agreement DE-EE0038475. The results and analysis for
Tasks 1 to 5 for this project were presented in the report NREL/TP-5700-89036 published in March
2024 [4]. Some of the main lessons learned from Tasks 1 to 5 are summarized as follows:

- Failures in molten salt tanks – The integrity of molten salt TES tanks in CSP plants is compromised
 by mechanisms such as stress relaxation cracking, buckling, low-cycle fatigue, and creep, all
 influenced by design, material quality, and operating conditions. The results isolate creep as the
 primary driver of failure, predicting that combined stresses will cause tank floor failure after few
 years of operation.

- Tank design – Tanks are usually designed following guidelines from API 650 and ASME BPVC
 Section II standards. However, important differences among tank designs have been observed,
 specifically in the salt distribution ring, piping network, and foundation, highlighting the need for a
 standard dedicated to molten salt TES tanks. Design features could be modified to reduce stress and
 deformation during operation. For instance, the sparger ring design could be improved to enhance
 mixing between salt inflow and inventory, and the floor thickness could be increased to reduce high
 stress during operation.

- Tank fabrication – The tank welding fabrication process for molten salt TES tanks leads to high
 residual stress and distortion, which strongly influence operational stress. High residual stress could
 lead to stress relaxation cracking during operation, while significant distortion in the tank floor could
 facilitate the formation of buckles. Welding processes should focus on producing tank floors with
 lower residual stress and distortion.

- Operation conditions – One of the most critical tank operating conditions that lead to high stress
 during operation occurs when a temperature difference between the salt inflow and a low inventory
 salt arises. This is exacerbated by high inflow mass flow rates. This operating condition is common
 at the tank's daily start, and the adverse effects could be minimized by making operational decisions
 such as maintaining a safe inventory level at the end of operation and regulating salt mass flow rates
 at low inventory levels.

2 Tank Size Analysis
2.1 Geometry Generation and Model Setup
The existing validated molten salt tank model was modified in the ANSYS Design Modeler environment
to enable flexible tank geometry development. This allowed for modifying the diameter and floor
thickness while maintaining the proportions of the sparger ring and foundation. Within the geometry
tool, a first tank design was generated by considering a radial section of the tank, composed of a cylinder
segment and a sub-dome roof. Three shell-type elements were used to represent the tank geometry: the
floor, wall, and roof. A circular pattern operation was then implemented to obtain the final simulation
geometry. Dividing the tank into pieces corresponding to different levels facilitates the generation of a
structured hexahedral mesh.

The geometry generation used a function in which the tank diameter is a variable that can be adjusted to
represent tanks with diameters ranging from 20 m to 42 m. Figure 5 presents three tank geometries with
a constant height of 12 m and diameters of 20 m, 30 m, and 40 m, respectively. Figure 6 shows an
example mesh generation for a tank with a 22 m diameter.

 a) b) c)
 Figure 5. Tank geometries with different diameters. a) 20 m, b) 30 m, and c) 40 m.

For the CFD simulation, the model included molten salt and air inside the tank. The inlet flow orifices
were set as injection inlets, parametrized according to the discrete-phase modeling to enable variation
for each tank diameter without affecting the mesh generation. A natural convection condition was
applied to the external surfaces of the tank wall and roof. The heat transfer coefficient and free-stream
temperature were set to 15 W/m²K and 300 K, respectively. Shell conduction boundary conditions were
applied to the tank wall, with two layers: one for the Stainless Steel (SS) 347H case and the other for the
thermal insulation. Shell conduction was also activated for the tank floor with five layers corresponding
to the stainless-steel floor, sand, steel slip plate, clay, and concrete. The mass flow outlet boundary
condition was adopted by an area at the bottom of the tank’s lateral wall. For the initialization process, a
steady-state condition simulation with a pseudo-transient solution was executed to determine the initial
values for the salt inflow, inlet temperature, outlet mass flow rate, inventory level, and initial
temperature.

2.3 Tank Floor Fabrication Modeling
The tank floor fabrication simulation for the representative tank design (39.6 m diameter) was presented
in a previous project report [4]. That analysis established a thermo-elastic-plastic finite element analysis
(FEA) shell element model with a simplified one-pass weld to analyze residual stress and distortion in
the floor. This FE shell element model is now adopted to simulate the welding-induced stress and
distortions for a 24 m and 32 m diameter tank floors.

2.3.1 Welding Model for the 24 m Diameter Tank Floor
The 24 m diameter hot tank floor was modeled as a welding assembly of thin, rectangular 347H stainless
steel plates. The shell element model was built and implemented in the Abaqus software. Half of the
tank floor was modeled by leveraging the tank's symmetry. Figure 14 presents both the tank floor layout
and the dimensions of the rectangular plates used for the assembly.

 Figure 14. Layout of the hot tank floor used in the 24 m diameter tank floor fabrication model.

The previous project report [4] compared two models with different mesh sizes to establish a meshing
strategy that balances accuracy with a reasonable element count. The strategy employing a minimum
size of 8*20mm2 proved efficient and accurate for the large tank floor models. To maintain a similar
calculation time to that of the 39.6 m diameter tank floor, the mesh size and gradient were reduced to
adapt to the 24 m tank diameter while keeping a similar calculation time. Figure 15 presents the mesh
model, which consists of 76,293 elements and 74,525 nodes. This meshing uses a mixture of four-node
linear quadrilateral and three-node linear triangular element types for both heat transfer (defined as
DS4/DS3) and non-linear elastic-plastic calculations (S4R/S3R).

Although the floor in a shell element model lacks geometric thickness, this parameter is factored in via
section integration points. In this case, a shell thickness of 7 mm was used, considering five thickness
integration points and employing the Simpson thickness integration rule. Similar to the solid model, this
shell element model utilized a Goldak double ellipsoidal heat source with simplified one-pass welding
[4,15].

2.3.3 Residual Stress and Distortion Comparison
In arc welding processes, longitudinal residual stresses are typically higher than transverse residual
stresses. This stress increases with longer welding passes and higher heat inputs and is highly dependent
on the constraint conditions. The peak residual stress gradually saturates once the weld length exceeds
critical values. Consequently, the residual stress comparison across the three floors was only focused on
the longitudinal residual stresses among the short and long plate edges.

Figure 27 presents the constraint conditions set up for weld models of the three tank floors. The overall
pattern of the contour maps of longitudinal stresses in the three floors is similar, comparing Figure 20
and Figure 25 in this report with Figure 91 in the previous report [4]. It is observed that the peak values
of longitudinal stresses on long-edge welds, S22 in Figure 20b and Figure 25b, increase from 641 MPa
in the 24 m diameter floor, to 686 MPa in the 32 m floor, and 687 MPa in the 39.6 m diameter floors [4].
The highest tensile residual stresses generally occur at a few point locations, such as highly constrained
locations or intersections of short-edge and long-edge welds. Within the majority of long-edge weld
seams, it was observed that the peak longitudinal residual stress slightly increased with increasing weld
pass length, e.g., up to 590 MPa in the 24 m tank, 670 MPa in the 32 m tank, and saturating at 678 MPa
in the 39.6 m tanks. Note that the long-edge welds originate from the central line (y = 0), as
schematically illustrated in Figure 16b, and progress along the y-axis in either the positive or negative
direction simultaneously. Therefore, the long-edge weld pass length increases with increasing floor size.
The observed stress trend agrees with the general trend reported in the literature. In comparison, for the
majority of the short-edge welds, the difference in peak longitudinal residual stress varied only within 25

3 Tank Preheating Analysis
3.1 Preheating Model Setup
Preheating is part of the molten salt tank commissioning process required to prevent thermal shock and
excessive thermal stress when molten salt at high temperatures comes into contact with the tank
structure. Tank preheating will condition the tank structure, foundation, pipes, pumps, and valves to a
temperature above the molten salt freezing point, avoiding salt solidification and potential damage to
tank pipes or components.

This section presents a preheating analysis based on a typical tank preheating procedure and empirical
temperature data from a molten salt hot tank during preheating. This analysis and results are still in an
early stage and are considered preliminary. Despite this fact, the results obtained are valuable for
understanding the impact of preheating on the stress and distortion of the tank floor. Further research is
required, however, to provide a more accurate quantification of these effects.

The preheating analysis was conducted by adapting the mechanical portion of the molten salt tank model
and using real temperature data to interpolate thermal profiles at every time step for the tank floor, walls,
and roof. The thermal profile was interpolated at each time step and used as a boundary condition in the
model. The model was configured in ANSYS Workbench, as shown in the flow diagram in Figure 30.
Initially, the tank geometry was imported into a static structural analysis to calculate the initial mesh
deformation. The deformed mesh is linked to a transient thermal analysis that incorporates preheating
data and interpolates temperature measurements across the entire tank mesh. Finally, the deformed mesh
and the interpolated preheating conditions are input into a transient structural analysis. This final
analysis also integrates the residual stress and plastic strains resulting from the floor welding fabrication
model.

 Figure 30. Flow Diagram of the Preheating Model Implementation in ANSYS Workbench.
Nonlinear static and transient structural analyses were conducted on the tank, using a structured mesh of
shell elements, as illustrated in Figure 31. A fixed support was applied to the floor's central node. The
friction coefficient at the interface between the steel floor and the sand was defined as 0.5. Since the
sand's behavior beyond its initial frictional resistance was not of interest, it was represented by a fixed
support.

One of the main challenges during the preheating analysis was the limited number of temperature data
points. Only a few temperature profiles were measured in some locations of the tank wall and floor
(spacing between sensors was 3 m in the wall and 6 m in the floor). This dataset associates each
measured temperature with its corresponding normalized x, y, and z coordinates within the 39.6 m
diameter tank. In this initial model, only one such dataset is used, representing the temperature
distribution at the start of preheating on the first day. In the Transient Thermal analysis, the imported
preheating temperature dataset was used to interpolate temperature values across all nodes of the heating
tank grid. Three interpolation techniques—Triangulation, Distance-Based Average (DBA), and
Kriging—were assessed for this purpose, with their results visualized in the isometric views of Figure 32
[16-18].

Comparing the three interpolation methods against the input data, Triangulation and Distance-Based
Average (the latter one used in this analysis) produce results that may not be sufficiently accurate to
represent the actual temperature distribution in a real tank, but they preserve the temperature values at
the measured nodes and use a simple, conservative interpolation for the remaining areas. Kriging
interpolation generates a temperature distribution with greater contour variability, which may not
accurately represent the actual preheating.

The preheating profile to be used in the model was smoothed to facilitate convergence. The preheating
analysis consisted of evaluating the first 27 days of preheating, followed by a slow tank fill starting on
day 27 that continued for some hours until day 28, when the tank reached a 1 m salt inventory level.

The commissioning procedure is considered critical to maintaining the integrity of the hot tank, as it can

3.2 Tank Commissioning Process
The commissioning process for a commercial two-tank nitrate salt storage system is complex and can
take several days or weeks. The general process is summarized as follows:

 • Preheat to at least 20°C above the solidus temperature of the storage medium with the air
 preheating system.

 • Containment test with cold molten salt from the cold tank: This entails filling the tank to the
 maximum operating level (at a lower temperature than typical operations, thus higher fluid
 density) and holding it for some hours.

 • After the containment test, the level is reduced to the level appropriate for the electrical heating
 system. Most commercial projects are equipped with embedded heaters or a recirculating
 electrical heater.

 • The tank is heated from the preheating temperature to the maximum heating system temperature
 at a constant fluid level. The maximum heating system temperature is assumed to be 400°C.

 • Heating to temperatures higher than the heating system allows is achieved with the Receiver
 assembly.

Multiple procedures may exist for heating with the receiver assembly depending on the specific
operation and internal tank temperature measurements.

Based on observed operational data, the largest thermal gradients occur during the air preheating and
initial salt fill steps; in addition, the tank is susceptible to buckling during these steps due to the low (or
zero) inventory level. For these reasons, the first two steps of the commissioning process are considered
the most critical for analysis.

3.2.1 Air Preheating Process
The air preheating step is performed with a temporary propane- or natural gas-fired hot-air blower. Air
is introduced into the tank through a nozzle(s) on the roof and exits the tank through a nozzle that
extends from the roof into the tank. It is desirable to use specialized nozzles for the air inlet and outlets
to control the preheating airflow. The air inlet temperature is controlled based on skin-point temperature
measurements within the tank and the air inlet temperature. Depending on the tank geometry, different
control strategies may be appropriate. Based on the parametric studies performed under this award
[4,19], temperature differences of 40°C or more can cause permanent damage to the floor; therefore, the
floor's temperature is estimated to be a maximum ∆𝑇𝑇 of 35°C.

Based on available operational data, the air inlet temperature profile was estimated as a function of skin-
point temperature measurements and assumed controller parameters; however, in the real process, many
other factors could cause the control system to deviate from this profile. The estimated inlet conditions
and the temperature values used to calculate them are presented in Figure 33. In the dataset referenced
for the pre-heating process, no major transient events occur once the initial thermal gradients have been
established. Therefore, it appears the air pre-heating could have been accomplished with minimal
deviations from this ideal profile.

3.2.2 Initial Tank Fill
During the initial filling, salt is transferred from the cold tank to the hot tank. The goal is to perform the
initial fill at a relatively constant temperature while minimizing thermal gradients in the tank; however,
there is no mechanism to control thermal gradients once salt begins flowing into the tank. Heat losses
and the salt inlet temperature will drive the temperature gradient in the tank. To help with this process,
the salt inlet temperature can be stabilized with an in-line heater, if available; otherwise, the inlet
temperature will be determined by the cold tank temperature and the heat losses through the piping.
Depending on the floor design, controlling the initial temperature gradient in the floor could be a critical
step for preserving structural integrity; therefore, flowing the fluid through an electrical heater could, in
some cases, help improve the thermal profile of the initial fill process. In addition, the air preheating
system may continue to operate, provided the pressure limitations of the tank are always respected; the
air preheating system can significantly reduce heat losses during the initial salt fill, but as the salt
approaches operational levels, the tank is generally not designed to have a pressurized headspace, and
great care must be taken by the operator of the filling and air heating systems.

tank floor is defined as the average temperature of the central portion of the floor to the outer radial

4 Final Remarks
4.1 Conclusions and Lessons Learned
The behavior of commercial-scale molten salt thermal energy storage (TES) tanks for central receiver
concentrating solar power (CSP) plants was initially modeled and analyzed as part of the DOE SETO-
funded project, agreement DE-EE0038475—'Failure analysis of molten salt tanks for in-service CSP
plants' Tasks 1 through 5. This foundational work involved formulating and validating a physics-based
model in a Multiphysics simulation environment using typical plant operation conditions [4]. This report
presents the analysis and findings from two subsequent tasks focusing on tank size and preheating. Key
conclusions and lessons learned from this final phase of work are summarized below:

 - Results indicate that operational stress increases with tank diameter, supporting the engineering
 preference for fabricating smaller-diameter tanks to achieve lower stress levels. In addition to the
 tank diameter, additional benefits in terms of stress reduction can be achieved by using thicker
 floors as was presented in the previous report [4].

 - The thermo-elastic-plastic FE method with shell elements, used to investigate two tank floors
 with diameters of 24 m and 32 m led to a peak von Mises stress of 596 MPa upon completion of
 all welds. This stress value is similar to the peak von Mises stress found for the 39.6 m-diameter
 tank floor in the previous report [4], which demonstrates that the maximum residual stresses,
 which are developed on the floor welds, are independent of floor diameter. The residual stresses
 of the tank floor strongly depend on the heat input, constraint design, and weld pass length. The
 highest residual stresses were observed in highly constrained point locations and interactions of
 plate edges.

 - Under similar constraint conditions, the maximum normal displacement increases with
 increasing tank diameter, rising from 114.5 mm in the 24 m diameter floor to 130.8 mm in the
 39.6 m diameter tank floor. With less constraint on the outer edges of the floor, the 32 m
 diameter tank floor exhibits a greater maximum displacement of 158.5 mm. For distortion
 control in welding, both diameter size and the weld plate layout or constraint design play
 significant roles. Introducing more constraints can effectively disrupt the distortion pattern and
 reduce the extent of distortion. In general, tanks with large diameters and thin floors may
 potentially experience buckling instability with severe distortions.

 - Preliminary results from preheating analysis show that the initial residual stress relaxes during
 the first days and then increases once the salt starts entering the tank. As expected, stresses
 during preheating are larger in the tank floor when compared to the wall due to the higher
 constraints of the tank floor, which makes it a critical component suitable for improvement to
 reduce susceptibility to tank failures.

Finally, solving failures in molten salt Thermal Energy Storage (TES) tanks is fundamental for CSP,
nuclear, industrial thermal process, and thermal-electrical energy storage technologies. In addition to the
recommendations provided in the previous report [4], some recommendations for future work to
advance toward a solution to molten salt tank failures include: 1) tank size analysis focused on the
relationship between tank diameter and floor thickness, considering initial stress and distortion in the
tank floor after welding fabrication; 2) preheating optimization to reduce residual stress while avoiding

 35

excessive floor distortion; and 3) improving welding process specifications and tank welding fabrication
strategies to reduce residual stress while controlling distortion in the tank floor.
