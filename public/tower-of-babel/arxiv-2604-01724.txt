Complete synchronization in networks of Sakaguchi-Kuramoto oscillators with bi-harmonic coupling
Subhasish Chowdhury, Sangita Dutta, Pitambar Khanra, Swarup Kumar Laha, Prosenjit Kundu

Abstract
In heterogeneous networks of coupled oscillators, phase frustration typically prevents the emergence of complete synchronization in the Sakaguchi-Kuramoto (SK) model. In this study, we propose an analytical framework to overcome this barrier and induce complete synchronization in oscillators governed by phase-frustrated bi-harmonic coupling. We derive a general set of natural frequencies correlated with the network’s degree heterogeneity, along with the parameters involved in the bi-harmonic coupling function that lead to complete synchronization ( r = 1 ) (r=1) in the presence of the harmonic coupling terms ( K 1 , K 2 ≠ 0 ) (K_{1},K_{2}\neq 0) . On top of that, we found hysteresis in the synchronization transition in the case of scale-free networks, indicating a first-order (discontinuous) phase transition, whereas Erdős–Rényi networks exhibit a second-order (continuous) synchronization transition. Furthermore, we use mean-field approximation to determine the critical coupling strength for the synchronization transition in the absence of first-harmonic coupling ( K 1 = 0 ) (K_{1}=0) . Here, the obtained optimal natural frequencies scale linearly with the node degree, and the critical coupling strength for the onset of synchronization is derived analytically from the self-consistent equations. In this specific regime, we observe distinct dynamical disparities: the second harmonic drives an explosive first order (or second order) transition for the second order parameter ( r 2 ) (r_{2}) , while the first order parameter ( r 1 ) (r_{1}) remains suppressed in the forward direction but emerges during the backward transition. These findings remain robust with higher-order harmonic coupling schemes, as well as across a diverse range of synthetic and empirical networks, including scale-free, Erdős–Rényi, Zachary Karate Club and C. elegans neural network, demonstrating their general applicability.

Introduction

Dynamical units often exhibit diverse collective phenomena when connected through complex network topologies. One of the most prominent emergent behaviors is synchronization
Pikovsky et al. 2001
;
Arenas et al. 2008
, a phenomenon observed and tested in various real-world fields. For instance, coherent behavior emerges in coupled cellular systems and power distribution grids, while both desired and undesired coherent bursts are common features in populations of neuronal activity
Strogatz 2004
;
Pikovsky et al. 2001
;
Arenas et al. 2008
;
Motter et al. 2013
;
Belykh et al. 2005
;
Dorogovtsev et al. 2008
. Characterizing the statistical scaling of various complex network topologies has advanced significantly in the last few decades
Dorogovtsev et al. 2008
;
Albert and Barabási 2002
;
Cohen and Havlin 2010
;
Kundu et al. 2020
;
Jalan et al. 2017
;
Bandyopadhyay and Jalan 2007
. Consequently, research has increasingly focused on uncovering the interplay between the structural heterogeneity of a network and the oscillatory or steady-state dynamics of its individual nodes
Barzel and Barabási 2013
. In addition, the interlink between the complex structure of a network and the macroscopic patterns emerging from node dynamics, such as synchronization, is investigated with a profound analytical description for different cases, ranging from networks of chaotic dynamics
Arenas et al. 2008
;
Ichinomiya 2004
;
Restrepo et al. 2005
to networks of higher-order interactions
Jain and Jalan 2025
;
Sharma et al. 2024
;
Dutta et al. 2024
.

The pioneering works of Winfree
Winfree 1967
and Kuramoto
Kuramoto 1984
established the theoretical foundation for these studies. The Kuramoto model, in particular, has become a central and extensively investigated paradigm for understanding collective synchronization in oscillator networks
Rodrigues et al. 2016
;
Acebrón et al. 2005
. In this framework, oscillators with distinct natural frequencies interact through pairwise couplings. Depending on the organization of coupling strengths or frequencies, these systems can display various forms of collective behavior, including continuous or abrupt (discontinuous) synchronization transitions
Kuramoto 1984
;
Gómez-Gardenes et al. 2011
;
Gómez-Gardenes et al. 2007
;
Leyva et al. 2012
;
Coutinho et al. 2013a
;
Ichinomiya 2004
. An important generalization of this framework is the Sakaguchi-Kuramoto model, which incorporates a phase-lag parameter into the interaction term
Sakaguchi and Kuramoto 1986
. This phase lag accounts for interaction delays common in natural and engineered systems. However, the introduction of even a small phase shift significantly influences stability, often completely suppressing abrupt synchronization transitions
Lohe 2015
;
Kundu et al. 2018
;
Kundu et al. 2020
;
Kundu et al. 2017
;
Skardal et al. 2015
. This leaves a critical open question: can a specific configuration of intrinsic parameters force a frustrated, bi-harmonic system into a state of complete synchrony?

While synchronization under single-harmonic coupling has been extensively investigated
Kuramoto and Tsuzuki 1975
;
Strogatz 2000
;
Kundu et al. 2018
;
Kundu et al. 2017
, recent years have seen a surge of interest in bi-harmonic Kuramoto models, which offer a richer framework for complex dynamics
Komarov and Pikovsky 2013
;
Komarov and Pikovsky 2014
;
Li et al. 2014
;
Wang et al. 2017
;
Eydam and Wolfrum 2017
;
Lohe 2015
. These higher-order coupling terms are not merely theoretical abstractions, but are intrinsic to several physical systems. A classical example is the Huygens’ clock setup, where the horizontal motion of the supporting beam induces a first harmonic interaction, while vertical vibrations contribute a second harmonic component
Czolczynski et al. 2013
;
Komarov and Pikovsky 2014
. Similarly, in Josephson junctions, phase dynamics evolving within a tilted double-well potential naturally enhance second harmonic contributions
Goldobin et al. 2011
;
Goldobin et al. 2013
. On the other hand, second harmonic terms have also been identified in the coupling functions of globally coupled electrochemical oscillators
Kiss et al. 2005
;
Kiss et al. 2006
.

Despite the theoretical richness of the Sakaguchi-Kuramoto model with bi-harmonic coupling, several fundamental aspects remain underexplored. Notably, complete synchronization is generally reported to be unattainable in these phase-frustrated networks, regardless of coupling strength. Furthermore, the specific role of the second harmonic term and the potential for synchronization in the absence of the first harmonic remain insufficiently understood.

In this paper, we propose a scheme to bridge these gaps by deriving a specific set of optimal natural frequencies that allows the system to achieve complete synchronization, even in the presence of phase frustration. Unlike previous works, we analytically derive this frequency set based on the network’s degree heterogeneity and the specific phase-frustrated coupling function.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we introduce the mathematical model. In Section
3
, we perform the analytical derivation to find the optimal frequency set. We also mathematically explore the effect of multiplicative noise in the natural frequency set on synchronization. Further, we employ a mean-field approach to analytically determine the critical coupling strength, specifically examining the regime where the first harmonic is absent
(K_{1}=0)
. In Section
4
, we present numerical results that demonstrate the system reaches complete synchronization. We also highlight a dynamical disparity where the first order parameter exhibits non-reversible transition pathways in the absence of direct first-harmonic coupling. Finally, in the Appendix, we demonstrate the generalization of our framework and extend the analysis to third-order harmonic coupling.

Mathematical Model

We consider here a system of
N
coupled oscillators (nodes) with bi-harmonics, whose phases follow the general dynamics

Kuramoto 2003
;
Sakaguchi and Kuramoto 1986

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{i}+{K_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}{F}(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}-\alpha_{ij}^{(1)})

(1)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{K_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}{F}(2\theta_{j}-2\theta_{i}-\alpha_{ij}^{(2)})

where,
\omega_{i}
denotes the natural frequency of node
i
,
A=(A_{ij})_{N\times N}
is an adjacency matrix that determines the underlying interaction network,
and the parameters

K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are the coupling strengths for the first and second harmonic functions, respectively.
The functional forms of the coupling are represented by

F(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}-\alpha^{(1)}_{ij})
and

F(2\theta_{j}-2\theta_{i}-\alpha^{(2)}_{ij})
,
which are
2\pi
-periodic functions. The distributed phase lags

\alpha^{(1)}_{ij}
and
\alpha^{(2)}_{ij}
characterize the presence
of frustration in the respective coupling interactions.

We would like to mention here

F(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}-\alpha^{(1)}_{ij})=\sin(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}-\alpha)
and
F(2\theta_{j}-2\theta_{i}-\alpha^{(2)}_{ij})=\sin(2\theta_{j}-2\theta_{i}-\beta)
(when all
\alpha_{ij}^{(1)}=\alpha
and
\alpha_{ij}^{(2)}=\beta
for all
i,j
),
Eq. (
1
) represents the Sakaguchi-Kuramoto model with a bi-harmonic coupling function, and in the limit

\alpha\rightarrow 0
and
\beta\rightarrow 0

converges to the classic Kuramoto dynamics with a bi-harmonic coupling function.
To quantify synchronization in the coupled network, we use the
Kuramoto order parameters

r_{m}=\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{im\theta_{j}}\right|,\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

The quantity
r_{m}\approx 0
corresponds to the incoherent state,
whereas
r_{m}>0
indicates the presence of phase coherence. Complete synchronization, defined by

\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=\cdots=\theta_{N}
, yields
r_{m}=1
.
Synchronization depends on the interplay between the spread of
natural frequencies
\omega_{i}
and the coupling strength

K

Kuramoto and Tsuzuki 1975
. Thus, coherence can be achieved
either by reducing the frequency dispersion (e.g., drawing

\omega_{i}
from a narrow normal distribution or from a homogeneous distribution) or by increasing
the coupling strengths
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
until
r=1
.

The difficulty arises because, in the presence of phase frustration

(\alpha,\beta>0)
, complete synchronization cannot be achieved.
This remains true even when the coupling strengths
K_{1}
and
K_{2}

are taken to be very large or when the natural frequencies
\omega_{i}

are highly homogeneous. Indeed, a small amount of lag is sufficient in order to prohibit
the system from reaching complete synchrony.
Contrast to all previous works, we show here that by strategically assigning all nodes with a specific set of natural frequencies,the system Eq. (
1
) can be able to drive itself into complete synchronization (
r\sim 1
) with even finite coupling strength.
We derive such a specific set of
optimal frequency sequences
,

\bm{\omega}=\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, based on network structure,
A_{ij}
,
and the activity of the phase frustrated coupling function
(F)
.

3
Analytical Derivation

3.1
Optimal frequency for complete synchronization

To obtain

\bm{\omega}
,
we analyze Eq. (
1
) as it approaches synchronization
Skardal et al. 2014b
;
Skardal and Arenas 2014
;
Skardal et al. 2014a
, that is, in the limit

|\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}|\rightarrow 0
.
In this limit, the coupling functions can be approximated by

F(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}-\alpha_{ij}^{(1)})\approx F(-\alpha_{ij}^{(1)})+F^{\prime}(-\alpha_{ij}^{(1)})(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})+\dots
,

F(2\theta_{j}-2\theta_{i}-\alpha_{ij}^{(2)})\approx F(-\alpha_{ij}^{(2)})+2F^{\prime}(-\alpha_{ij}^{(2)})(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i})+\dots
,
where

F^{\prime}(\alpha)=\left.\frac{dF(\theta,\alpha)}{d\theta}\right|_{\theta=0}
. This allows us to write Eq. (
1
) in its linearized form as

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\omega_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}\Big[K_{1}F(-\alpha_{ij}^{(1)})+K_{2}F(-\alpha_{ij}^{(2)})\Big]

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Bigg[\delta_{ij}\Bigg(\sum_{l=1}^{N}A_{il}\Big[K_{1}F^{\prime}(-\alpha_{il}^{(1)})+2K_{2}F^{\prime}(-\alpha_{il}^{(2)})\Big]\Bigg)

\displaystyle\ -A_{ij}\Big[K_{1}F^{\prime}(-\alpha_{ij}^{(1)})+2K_{2}F^{\prime}(-\alpha_{ij}^{(2)})\Big]\Bigg]\theta_{j}

(2)

or in vector form,

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bm{\theta}}}{dt}={\bm{\omega}}+{\bm{\tilde{d}}}-\tilde{\bm{L}}{\bm{\theta}}

(3)

Here
\tilde{\bm{L}}
and
{\bm{\tilde{d}}}
are the Laplacian matrix and degree vector, respectively, which can be written as

\tilde{d}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}\big[K_{1}F(-\alpha_{ij}^{(1)})+K_{2}F(-\alpha_{ij}^{(2)})\big]

and

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\sum_{l=1}^{N}A_{il}\Big[K_{1}F^{\prime}(-\alpha_{il}^{(1)})+2K_{2}F^{\prime}(-\alpha_{il}^{(2)})\Big]\Bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-A_{ij}\Big[K_{1}F^{\prime}(-\alpha_{ij}^{(1)})+2K_{2}F^{\prime}(-\alpha_{ij}^{(2)})\Big]

The system will reach a synchronized state if, for some choice of the natural frequencies

\omega_{i}
,
Eq. (
3
) gets a solution where all phases,

\theta_{i}
,
change according to some global frequency

\Omega

as

\theta_{i}(t)=\Omega t
,
independent of
i
.
So, steady state form, in the rotating frame

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{L}}{\bm{\theta^{*}}}=({{\bm{\omega}}+{\bm{\tilde{d}}}-\bm{{\Omega}}})

(4)

Here, vector

{\bm{\theta^{*}}}

is the steady state solutions of the full network in the rotating frame. Finally, the steady states look like

{\bm{\theta^{*}}}=\bm{\tilde{L^{\dagger}}}{({\bm{\omega}}+{\bm{\tilde{d}}}-{\bm{\Omega}})}

(5)

Here,
\bm{\tilde{L}^{\dagger}}
denotes the pseudo-inverse of the Laplacian operator
Ben-Israel and Greville 2003
.
Since the Laplacian matrix is symmetric and positive semi-definite, its eigenvectors
\bm{v}_{i}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and the corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
are real and non-negative, ordered as

0=\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}.

(6)

Using the spectral decomposition
Sarkar and Jalan 2018
of the Laplacian, the pseudo-inverse operator can be written as

\bm{\tilde{L}^{\dagger}}=\sum_{j=2}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{-1}\,\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{T},

(7)

Under the small-phase approximation
\lVert\bm{\theta}^{*}\rVert\ll 1
, the
m
-th order parameter can be approximated as

r_{m}\approx 1-\frac{\lVert m\bm{\theta}^{*}\rVert^{2}}{2N},

(8)

which quantifies the deviation from complete synchronization in terms of the squared Euclidean norm of the steady-state phase vector.

Now,

\displaystyle\|m\bm{\theta}^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle m\bm{\theta}^{*},\,m\bm{\theta}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=m^{2}\Big\langle\bm{\tilde{L}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\omega}+\bm{\tilde{d}}),\,\bm{\tilde{L}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\omega}+\bm{\tilde{d}})\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=m^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{-2}\,\big\langle v_{j},\,(\bm{\omega}+\bm{\tilde{d}})\big\rangle^{2}.

Therefore,

r_{m}=1-\frac{m^{2}}{2N}\sum_{j=2}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{-2}\big\langle v_{j},\,(\bm{\omega}+\bm{\tilde{d}})\big\rangle^{2}=1-\frac{m^{2}}{2}\,J(\bm{\omega},\bm{L}),

(9)

where
J(\bm{\omega},\bm{L})
denotes the synchrony alignment function (SAF),

J(\bm{\omega},\bm{L})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=2}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{-2}\big\langle v_{j},\,(\bm{\omega}+\bm{\tilde{d}})\big\rangle^{2}.

For complete synchronization state, we have
J(\bm{\omega},\bm{L})\to 0
, Eq. (
9
) gives
r_{m}=1

(So,
{r_{m}}
does not depend on
m
in the complete synchronization state)
. Now, we can find out
optimal frequency sequence
,

{\bm{\omega}}=\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
by taking,

\begin{split}J(\bm{\omega},\bm{L})=0&\;\implies\;\bm{\omega}+\bm{\tilde{d}}=0\\
&\;\implies\;\bm{\omega}=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}\Big[\,K_{1}F(-\alpha_{ij}^{(1)})\\
&\hskip 46.00012pt+\,K_{2}F(-\alpha_{ij}^{(2)})\,\Big].\end{split}

(10)

In our case,

\bm{\omega}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}[K_{1}Sin(\alpha)+K_{2}Sin(\beta)]

(11)

In this rotating frame, it is obvious that the stability of these fixed points (
{\bm{\theta^{*}}}
)
will ensure the stability of the synchrony in the heterogeneous network of phase frustrated oscillators. These fixed points depend on several components (see Eq.
5
): coupling strength
(K)
, the desired frequency set (
\bm{\omega}=\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
), and degree vector (
{\bm{\tilde{d}}}
) of the interacting network.
Here we observe that the analytically derived frequency set depends on the degree distribution of the underlying network.
Such degree-frequency correlations are known to induce abrupt or explosive synchronization transitions in some scale-free networks

;
Coutinho et al. 2013b

3.2
Synchronization under the Presence of Noise

Examine the response of synchronization to variations in the optimal frequency set (
11
) by adding Gaussian noise
\bm{\delta\omega}

such that
{\omega_{i}}\rightarrow\omega_{i}+\delta\omega_{i}

\delta\omega_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma{\omega_{i}})

, acting as a multiplicative perturbation of
\omega_{i}
. This noise can decrease the level of synchronization from a complete state (
r=1
) to a partial state (

) or desynchronized state (
r=0
). For this measurement, let us consider a function of
r
,
\eta=1-r_{m},
where
\quad 0\leq\eta\leq 1
and
r_{m}\approx 1-{\lVert{\bm{m\theta}}\rVert}^{2}/{2N}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=1-r_{m}

\displaystyle\approx 1-\left(1-\frac{\lVert\bm{m\theta}\rVert^{2}}{2N}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}}{2}{Var}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)

(12)

After adding noise
\bm{\delta\omega}
, from (
5
) we get

\displaystyle{{\theta_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}{\tilde{L_{ij}^{\dagger}}}{({{\omega_{j}}}+{\delta\omega_{j}}+{{\tilde{d_{j}}}}-{{\Omega}})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}{\tilde{L_{ij}^{\dagger}}}({\delta\omega_{j}})

Now,

\displaystyle\frac{m^{2}}{2}{Var}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}}{2}\sum_{j}\bigl(\tilde{L}_{ij}^{\dagger}\bigr)^{2}\,{Var}\bigl(\delta\omega_{j}\bigr)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{m^{2}}{2}\sum_{j}\bigl(\tilde{L}_{ij}^{\dagger}\bigr)^{2}\,\omega_{j}^{2}\,\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=Z_{i}\,\sigma^{2};\text{where},\,Z_{i}=\frac{m^{2}}{2}\sum_{j}\bigl(\tilde{L}_{ij}^{\dagger}\bigr)^{2}\,\omega_{j}^{2}.

(13)

Therefore, from (
12
) and (

),

\displaystyle\eta\sim\sigma^{2}

(14)

The scaling relation

indicates that the loss of

limit, the behavior follows the theoretical prediction in

increases, the natural frequencies

3.3
Mean field Analysis

In the absence of the first harmonic coupling strength, i.e., when
K_{1}=0
, the interaction between the oscillators is governed solely by the second harmonic term.
Under this condition, the SK model with bi-harmonic coupling reduces to a form that includes only the second harmonic contribution, as given by the following equation

\frac{d\theta_{i}}{dt}=\omega_{i}+{K_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}\sin(2\theta_{j}-2\theta_{i}-\beta)

(15)

Here, the natural frequency
\omega_{i}
is selected from the optimal frequency set that allows the system to achieve complete synchronization at the specific coupling strength
K_{2}=K_{2}^{(p)}
, as defined by Eq. (
11
)

\omega_{i}=K_{2}^{(p)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}\sin\beta=a\,q_{i}

(16)

where
a=K^{opt}_{2}\sin\beta
and
q_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}
denotes the degree of the
i
-th node.

To analyze the dynamics, we apply a mean-field framework to the above Eq. (
15
), following the procedure introduced in
Ichinomiya 2004
;
Komarov and Pikovsky 2013
. Let
\rho(q,\theta,t)
represent the density of the nodes of degree
q
having phase
\theta
at time
t
. This density is normalized over the phase variable such that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho(q,\theta,t)\,d\theta=1.

(17)

Assuming that the network does not exhibit degree–degree correlations, the probability that a randomly selected edge is connected to a node of degree
q
with phase
\theta
at time
t
is given by

\frac{qP(q)\,\rho(q,\theta,t)}{\int qP(q)\,dq},

where
P(q)
is the degree distribution of the network.

In the continuum limit, Eq. (
15
) can be expressed as

\frac{d\theta}{dt}=\omega+\frac{K_{2}q}{\langle q\rangle}\int dq^{\prime}\int q^{\prime}P(q^{\prime})\rho(q^{\prime},\theta^{\prime},t)\sin(2\theta^{\prime}-2\theta-\beta)\,d\theta^{\prime},

(18)

where the quantity
\langle q\rangle=\int qP(q)\,dq
represents the mean degree of the network.To ensure the conservation of oscillators in Eq. (
15
), the density function
\rho
must satisfy the continuity equation

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}(\rho v)=0,

(19)

where
v
is taken from the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
).

To quantify the macroscopic dynamics of the oscillator population, we consider the order parameter
r_{2}
in the thermodynamic limit, following the formulation presented in
Ichinomiya 2004

r_{2}e^{i\psi}=\frac{1}{\langle q\rangle}\int dq{{}^{\prime}}\int q^{{}^{\prime}}P(q{{}^{\prime}})\rho(q{{}^{\prime}},\theta{{}^{\prime}},t)e^{i2\theta^{\prime}}d\theta^{\prime},

(20)

where
\psi
denotes the mean phase of the oscillators, and the order parameter
r_{2}
lies in the interval
0\leq r_{2}\leq 1
.

Using the order parameter Eq. (
20
) , Eq. (
18
) can be rewritten as

\frac{d\theta}{dt}=\omega+K_{2}r_{2}qsin(\psi-2\theta-\beta)

(21)

To derive the self-consistent equations, we fix the global phase as
\psi(t)=\Omega t
, where
\Omega
denotes the collective angular velocity, and introduce a new phase variable
\chi
defined by

\chi(t)=2\theta(t)-\psi(t)+\beta.

In this rotating frame, Eq. (
21
) can be rewritten as

\frac{d\chi}{dt}=2aq-\Omega-2qK_{2}r_{2}sin(\chi)

(22)

Then continuity Eq. (
19
) can be written in the form

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(q,\chi,t)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\chi}\!\left[v_{\chi}\,\rho(q,\chi,t)\right]=0,\text{where},v_{\chi}=\frac{d\chi}{dt}

(23)

In the steady state,
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(q,\chi,t)=0

as follows

\rho(q,\chi)=\begin{cases}\delta\left(\chi-\arcsin\left(\frac{2aq-\Omega}{2qK_{2}r_{2}}\right)\right),&\left|\frac{2aq-\Omega}{2qK_{2}r_{2}}\right|\leq 1,\\
\frac{A(q)}{2aq-\Omega-2qK_{2}r_{2}\sin(\chi)},&\left|\frac{2aq-\Omega}{2qK_{2}r_{2}}\right|>1,\end{cases}

(24)

where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function and
A(q)
is the normalization constant given by
A(q)=\frac{\sqrt{(2aq-\Omega)^{2}-(2qK_{2}r_{2})}}{2\pi}.
The first solution represents the synchronous state, while the second solution is associated with the desynchronous state. Consequently, the order parameter
r_{2}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle r_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\langle q\rangle}\int d\chi\Biggl[\,\int_{q_{\min}}^{\infty}dq\,qP(q)\rho(q,\chi)e^{i(\chi-\beta)}

\displaystyle\qquad\times H\left(1-\left|\frac{2aq-\Omega}{2qK_{2}r_{2}}\right|\right)

where

\displaystyle r_{2}^{\text{lock}}

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\frac{\cos\beta}{\langle q\rangle}\int_{q_{\min}}^{\infty}dq\,qP(q)\sqrt{1-\!\left(\frac{2aq-\Omega}{2qK_{2}r_{2}}\right)^{2}}+\frac{\sin\beta}{\langle q\rangle}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{q_{\min}}^{\infty}dq\,qP(q)\left(\frac{2aq-\Omega}{2qK_{2}r_{2}}\right)\Bigg]H\!\left(1-\left|\frac{2aq-\Omega}{2qK_{2}r_{2}}\right|\right)

\displaystyle-i\Bigg[\frac{\sin\beta}{\langle q\rangle}\int_{q_{\min}}^{\infty}dq\,qP(q)\sqrt{1-\!\left(\frac{2aq-\Omega}{2qK_{2}r_{2}}\right)^{2}}-\frac{\cos\beta}{\langle q\rangle}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{q_{\min}}^{\infty}dq\,qP(q)\left(\frac{2aq-\Omega}{2qK_{2}r_{2}}\right)\Bigg]H\!\left(1-\left|\frac{2aq-\Omega}{2qK_{2}r_{2}}\right|\right)

(27)

5
Conclusions

We have analytically shown that a degree–frequency correlated optimal frequency distribution can obtain complete synchronization in Sakaguchi–Kuramoto oscillators with bi-harmonic (higher-order harmonic) coupling on complex networks. Using numerical simulations we show that the transitions to synchronization is first order for SF networks and continuous for ER and the real networks used in this study. Numerical simulations on scale-free and real-world networks reveal that the lower harmonic order parameter (
r_{i}
) shows the de-synchronization state with higher-harmonic coupling strength (
K_{j}
with
i<j
) in forward simulations, whereas the backward simulations remain in the synchronized state and reach complete synchronization at the desired coupling strength. For the case of same harmonic order parameter and coupling strength (
i=j
) it shows finite hysteresis characteristic of first-order transitions.

Analytical predictions for the synchronization order parameter, critical coupling strengths agree remarkably well with simulation results, confirming the validity of our theoretical framework. The extension to higher harmonic and real-world topologies demonstrates that the mechanism is robust and scalable, providing a generalizable approach for controlling synchronization in complex network. The addition of multiplicative noise in natural frequency shows that the created frequency is robust towards the added noise.
