Biomechanical Analysis of Mandibular Implant-Supported Overdentures With Different Retention Materials
Authors: Jing Li, Weijun Zhang, Zhe Qu, Qi Chen, Jiafei Wang, Jing Xu, Xiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study is to evaluate the biomechanical effects of different modulus of elasticity retentive materials (titanium [Ti]; polyetheretherketone [PEEK] and polyetherketoneketone [PEKK]) on mandibular implant-supported overdentures.MethodsA finite element model of a mandibular 2-implant overdenture was constructed. Attachment housings were simulated in Ti, PEEK or PEKK, paired with retentive inserts of nylon, PEEK or PEKK. Three occlusal loading conditions were simulated: a 100 N vertical load on the left first molar (centric occlusion), a 100 N oblique load on the same site (lateral occlusion), and a 100 N vertical load on the bilateral mandibular central incisors (50 N per tooth, protrusive occlusion). The stress distribution across all structures of the implant-supported overdenture and the denture displacement were analysed.Results(1) The Ti-housing/PEKK-insert configuration produced the lowest denture base stress and displacement but the highest stress on implants, abutments, and peri-implant bone, (2) Under all 3 loading conditions, bone tissue stress predominantly concentrated in the cortical bone near the implant cervix; implant stress mainly focused on its internal region connected to the abutment; denture stress primarily accumulated at the contact area between the tissue-facing surface of the denture base and the attachment housing, (3) In intergroup comparisons, oblique loading conditions showed the highest stress values for the implant, abutment, peri-implant bone tissue and denture base.ConclusionNylon, PEEK and PEKK are all viable retentive insert materials. Nylon is preferable for reducing stress on implants and bone, whereas PEEK or PEKK inserts better enhance prosthetic stability and reduce denture base fracture risk.Key words: Mandibular edentulism, Implant-supported overdentures, Finite element analysis, Polyetheretherketone, Polyetherketonketone

Introduction

The number of edentulous patients is increasing as the population ages.1 Traditional complete dentures were the main repair technique for edentulous jaws prior to the development of implant technology. Compared to maxillary complete dentures, edentulous mandibular patients have worse retention and stability due to the restricted retention region and considerable tissue movement. The alveolar ridge that remains after tooth loss keeps resorbing. About 4 times as quickly as the maxillary ridge, the mandibular alveolar ridge resorbs.2 The use of traditional complete dentures is considerably complicated by severe alveolar ridge loss. Patients who wear mandibular complete dentures often express dissatisfaction over their incapacity to carry out daily tasks and poor retention. Implant-supported overdentures represent one approach to addressing these issues, wherein the suprastructure overlays both the abutments and the mucosa, connects to the abutments via attachments, and derives its support and retention either solely from the implants or through a combination of implants and underlying soft tissue. Implant-supported overdentures have been shown to increase masticatory efficiency, decrease the rate of residual alveolar ridge resorption and improve the retention of complete dentures.3, 4, 5, 6 Mandibular 2-implant overdentures were the recommended treatment for patients with mandibular edentulism, according to the 2002 McGill Consensus7 and the 2009 York Consensus.8 Four-implant mandibular overdentures did not increase patient satisfaction, according to a 10-year randomised clinical investigation.9 There was no discernible difference in marginal bone resorption between patients using mandibular overdentures supported by 2 or 4 implants. This suggests that mandibular 2-implant overdentures is a viable therapeutic option with long-term stability.

The main types of attachments used in implant-supported overdentures are as follows: bar-clip, ball socket, stud, magnetic, telescopic and milled bar. Among these, the Locator attachment, a type of stud attachment, is currently the most widely used in clinical practice. According to a study by Kronstrom et al,10 the Locator attachment was the most frequently used system among 116 prosthodontists from 33 countries. The Locator attachment consists of 2 components: the attachment abutment, which is secured to the implant, and a nylon retentive insert and Ti housing, which are fixed to the tissue surface of the overdenture. Because the Locator attachment is self-retaining, removal and insertion are made simple. It allows for up to 40° deviation between implants, requires very little implant parallelism, and for retention force adjustment using various coloured inserts. The Locator attachment shows better clinical results in terms of peri-implant health, cost-effectiveness and long-term maintenance when compared to sleeve crowns and bar attachments. However, loss of retention as a result of attachment component deterioration is the most frequent mechanical issue with the Locator attachment,11, 12, 13 which raises maintenance costs and patient frequency. Many manufacturers are changing attachment designs and enhancing surface qualities to lower maintenance needs in order to improve patient experience.

The Novaloc attachment (made by Straumann, Basel, Switzerland) and the CM loc abutment (made by Cendres + Métaux, Biel, Switzerland) are 2 novel attachment systems. While visually resembling the Locator attachment, they differ primarily in the surface coating of the implant abutment. The key distinction lies in their retention components: the Novaloc system utilises a PEEK retentive insert and offers a choice of titanium or PEEK housings, whereas the CM loc system employs a PEKK) retentive insert. Wichmann et al14 evaluated the performance of the inserts of the Locator R-Tx, Novaloc and CM loc attachment systems over 5000 to 30,000 insertion-removal cycles. PEEK inserts and PEKK inserts demonstrated significantly superior retention strength compared to nylon inserts. This finding is further supported by research by Arnold15 and Choi.16 As new polymeric materials, PEEK and PEKK are members of the polyaryletherketone (PAEK) family, a type of linear aromatic polyetherketones. The sole difference between PEEK and PEKK is the ratio of ether to ketone groups; both have aromatic rings in their structures. Along with exceptional resistance to chemical corrosion, high temperatures and biocompatibility, PEEK demonstrates outstanding mechanical qualities in flexural, tensile and compressive strength. As a result, it has a great deal of promise for use in the dental industry, including implants, abutments, crowns, bridges, framework structures and related components.17 Because the elastic modulus of PEEK is similar to that of dentin, some researchers have attempted to use it for post-and-core restoration, with the prerequisite that the abutment tooth has a sufficient ferrule height.18 Similarly, PEKK has also begun to be widely used in the dental field. A study has shown that compared with zirconia implant bridges, PEKK bridges may be more helpful in protecting peri-implant bone in high-risk patients.19 Because PEKK has more ketone groups, its polymer chains are notably stronger, giving it better mechanical and physical qualities. Its compressive strength is 80% more than PEEK's.20,21 The potential advantages of PEEK and PEKK for implant-supported overdentures with self-retaining attachment systems should be investigated due to their notable mechanical advantages over nylon. However, studies comparing the biomechanical performance of these 3 materials in overdentures supported by implants have not yet been conducted.

Dental implants, in contrast to natural teeth, merge directly with the surrounding bone and do not have the periodontal ligament's ability to buffer stress. The success rate of implant-supported restorations can be impacted by problems such peri-implant bone resorption, implant fracture, and abutment fracture caused by uneven stress distribution. Therefore, accurate assessment of peri-implant bone condition is a critical factor for the long-term success of implant-supported restorations. Variables including loading conditions, number and distribution of implants, implant geometry, bone quality, attachment type and prosthetic materials all significantly influence the biomechanical behaviour of the implant, the surrounding bone interface, and the suprastructure.22, 23, 24, 25, 26 Finite element analysis (FEA) is a numerical method for analysing and simulating physical phenomena. This approach discretises a constructed model into a finite number of elements, assigns specific structural properties and materials to each, and connects them via nodes. By applying boundary conditions, FEA enables the visualisation of the location, magnitude and direction of applied forces, offering advantages such as reproducibility, time efficiency and cost-effectiveness.27, 28, 29 This approach, which can replicate intricate clinical situations, is currently frequently used in the dental industry. It is a trustworthy method for researching peri-implant bone stresses and implant components.30

In order to assess the biomechanical impacts of various material combinations for housings and retentive inserts on mandibular 2-implant overdentures under various loading forces, this study will use FEA. According to the null hypothesis, the biomechanics of 2-implant overdentures under various stress scenarios are unaffected by alternative retention materials.

Materials and methods

Modelling of implant-supported overdentures

Following approval by the Ethics Committee of Stomatological Hospital of Dalian University (Dalian Stomatological Hospital) (Ethical Review No. DLKQLL20240204), DICOM-format CBCT data obtained 5 months postoperatively from an adult patient with edentulous mandible and restored with a mandibular 2-implant overdenture were imported into Mimics Medical 19.0 software to reconstruct a 3-dimensional mandibular bone model. Using the offset and precision surface functions in the reverse engineering software Geomagic Wrap 19.0, a trabecular bone model was generated by inwardly offsetting the cortical bone surface by 2 mm, while a mucoperiosteal model was created by outwardly thickening it by 2 mm.22,31 The patient's mandibular complete denture was scanned with an intraoral scanner (brand: Carestream CS3600) to obtain data in STL format. Based on manufacturer specifications and measurements taken with a vernier caliper, a Straumann SLActive bone-level tapered implant (Model 021.5312, 4.1 × 12 mm) and the Novaloc attachment system components were selected. These included the Novaloc abutment (Model 022.0055, diameter 3.8 mm, gingival height 5 mm), the retentive insert, and the housing (Model 210.601-STM, heights 2.3 mm and 1.4 mm, respectively). Two-dimensional outline drawings of the implant and attachment were drafted in AutoCAD and saved in .dwg format. To simplify the modelling process, the implant was designed as a solid internal structure; the abutment and its internal central screw were modelled as an integrated unit; and surface features such as the implant's longitudinal grooves and the abutment's central screw access hole were omitted. Finally, solid 3-dimensional models of the implant and attachment were generated in SolidWorks 2017 based on their respective CAD dimensional drawings.

Lastly, SolidWorks 2017 was used to import and assemble the geometric models. A mandibular 2-implant overdenture model was created by placing a single implant in each of the mandible's bilateral lateral incisor regions (Figure 1). Tetrahedral meshing of the model with 10 nodes per element produced a total of 321,923 elements and 514,315 nodes.

Grouping experiments

By changing the materials of the retentive inserts and housing, groups were created. Ti, PEEK or PEKK may be used for the housing, and nylon, PEEK or PEKK could be used for the retentive inserts. A full factorial combination would result in 3 × 3 = 9 groups. However, from a manufacturing perspective, and given that PEEK and PEKK exhibit only minor differences in key mechanical properties, the 2 cross-combinations – PEEK insert/PEKK housing and PEKK insert/PEEK housing – were expected to yield redundant data. Moreover, in practical production, using the same material for both the insert and housing (eg, PEEK/PEEK or PEKK/PEKK) simplifies material management, reduces production costs and ensures process stability. Material properties and model assumptions

It is assumed that all of the materials used in this experiment are continuous, homogeneous and isotropic linear elastic materials. In order to prevent any sliding under loading conditions, the implant is adjusted to have 100% bonded contact with the mandible. In order to prevent separation or relative slippage between the implant and abutment, they are modelled as a hard bonded contact. A sliding friction coefficient of 0.334 is used to simulate sliding friction between the overdenture and mucosa.23 Mandibular movement is restricted by applying rigid constraints to the area from the mandibular angle to the posterior edge of the mandibular ramus to the condylar apex.

Results

Stress distribution in bone tissue, abutments and implants

The stress distribution patterns in implants, abutments and bone tissue were comparable among various attachment retention material groups under the same loading circumstances. The implant cervix, specifically the implant-abutment junction, was where the majority of implant stress was centred (Figure 2). The intersection of the implant neck and cortical bone was where bone tissue tension was most concentrated (Figure 3). All stress values reported below refer to von Mises equivalent stress. As shown in Tables S2-S4, under all 3 loading conditions (F1, F2, F3), the TK group (Ti housing/PEKK insert) consistently exhibited the highest stresses in the implant, abutment, and bone tissue, while the lowest stresses were observed in the EN group (under F1 and F3) and KN/TN group (under F2). Specifically, for implant stress, the TK group showed the highest values (F1: 30.86 MPa, F2: 58.63 MPa, F3: 60.83 MPa); the EN group showed the lowest values under F1 (23.60 MPa) and F3 (50.02 MPa); and the KN group showed the lowest value under F2 (55.94 MPa). For abutment stress, the TK group showed the highest values (F1: 45.85 MPa, F2: 57.00 MPa, F3: 75.45 MPa); the EN group showed the lowest values under F1 (30.46 MPa) and F3 (57.58 MPa); and the TN group showed the lowest value under F2 (54.04 MPa). For bone tissue stress, the TK group showed the highest values (F1: 29.14 MPa, F2: 59.70 MPa, F3: 60.91 MPa); the EN group showed the lowest values under F1 (22.73 MPa) and F3 (49.55 MPa); and the KN group showed the lowest value under F2 (57.32 MPa).Within the same model, the lowest equivalent stress was observed under the F1 loading condition (Figure 4A-C).

Discussion

Given that different retentive materials influence the biomechanical behaviour of 2-implant overdentures under varying loading conditions, the null hypothesis was rejected in this study. The retentive inserts used in this experiment were made of nylon, PEEK and PEKK, all of which had elastic qualities. Resilient attachment can lessen the load on implants and surrounding bone as compared to rigid attachment.36 According to experimental findings, the model using a nylon retentive insert transfers less stress to the abutment, implant and surrounding bone tissue than models using PEEK or PEKK retentive inserts when the housing material is the same but the retentive insert material is different. This could lessen the likelihood of issues like implant breakage and abutment loosening. This effect is probably caused by nylon's higher deformation tendency and lower elastic modulus, which allow it to absorb more energy while imparting less stress to nearby structures.37 In contrast to models with PEEK or PEKK retentive inserts, models with nylon retentive inserts showed noticeably higher stress concentration zones and maximum displacements in the denture base under anterior loading circumstances. This could be because nylon is more prone to significant deformation than PEEK and PEKK. When nylon retentive inserts are severely compressed under loading circumstances, their thickness decreases, increasing displacement. This implies that utilising PEEK or PEKK retentive inserts improves denture stability and retention by lowering the chance of denture base fracture when compared to nylon retentive inserts. There were no discernible differences when the retentive inserts materials were the same but the housings material was different. This indicates that, under the conditions of this study, retentive inserts have a greater impact on stress distribution than housings. The mechanism behind this finding – which may involve differences in volume, material properties, contact geometry, or load-transfer pathways – remains unknown and requires further investigation. The maximal equivalent stress in the mandible under all loading circumstances in this investigation stayed below the cortical bone's compressive strength limit of 140 to 170 MPa.39 Similarly, the maximal equivalent stress in the implant and abutment did not above the titanium alloy's yield strength limit of 900 MPa.40 Thus, all of the materials assessed in this study are appropriate for use as attachment-retained structures from a biomechanical standpoint. Furthermore, when the material of the retentive insert is identical, a higher elastic modulus of the housing results in a greater peak Von Mises stress in the retentive insert. This indicates that selecting a housing with a lower elastic modulus is more conducive to improving stress distribution in the retentive insert, thereby reducing the frequency of replacement. In conclusion, the combination of Ti and nylon for the housing and retentive insert was most detrimental to the biomechanical environment of the denture base and retentive insert, whereas the combinations of PEEK with PEEK and PEKK with PEKK produced better outcomes.

There are 2 sorts of load types in finite element analysis: dynamic and static. Static loading is used in the majority of current finite element analysis research on implant-supported overdentures. A study reported a maximum occlusal force of 119.84 ± 26.47 N in patients rehabilitated with maxillary conventional dentures and mandibular 2-implant-supported ball-attachment overdentures.41 Avukat et al42 similarly measured occlusal forces in mandibular complete overdentures retained by 2 implants using an occlusal force-testing device, with recorded forces ranging from 50 to 172 N (mean 94.3 N). Therefore, the present study employed a static loading magnitude of 100 N under 3 distinct loading conditions, simulating posterior central occlusion, lateral occlusion and anterior protrusive occlusion, respectively. Several finite element studies have indicated that oblique loading induces greater stress,40,43,44 a finding that is consistent with the results of the present study. In this experiment, the peak Von Mises stress values in the implant, abutment, surrounding bone tissue, and overdenture were all higher under unilateral posterior oblique loading than under unilateral posterior vertical loading. Unlike natural teeth, which are suspended in the alveolar bone by the periodontal ligament, implants are directly integrated with the alveolar bone through osseointegration. This structure prevents effective cushioning of nonaxial occlusal forces, thereby increasing the risk of implant failure. Therefore, occlusal design for implant-supported overdentures should prioritise reducing oblique forces to minimise complications. This can be achieved through measures such as decreasing cusp inclination and narrowing the buccolingual width of posterior teeth. When the opposing arch is restored with a conventional complete denture, bilateral balanced occlusion is typically adopted. However, under conventional tooth arrangement, contacts at the buccal cusps of artificial teeth impose off-axis loading on the implants, which is detrimental to their long-term stability.45 In cases of significant maxillary resorption, a lingualised occlusion design – characterised by prominent maxillary lingual cusps, reduced buccal cusps and broad mandibular central fossae – allows the maxillary lingual cusps to move freely within the mandibular fossae. This occlusion pattern reduces unfavourable lateral forces and directs the implant load more vertically toward the central fossa of the denture. For severely resorbed maxillae, another effective design is crossbite occlusion, which further promotes predominantly vertical loading on the implants.46 In this experiment, anterior vertical loading resulted in greater strains on the implant, abutment and mandible than posterior vertical loading. Free-end loading at the first molar as opposed to nonfree-end loading at the central incisor could cause this. Nonfree-end loading in implant-supported overdentures reduces mucosal stress while increasing stress at the implant-bone contact. This suggests that prioritising posterior chewing and avoiding anterior biting may be more favourable for load distribution in implant-supported overdentures. However, these findings are derived from biomechanical simulation and require further clinical validation.

The selection of 2 implants placed at the bilateral lateral incisor positions in the present study was based on the following clinical and mechanical considerations. Regarding the number of implants, mandibular implant-supported overdentures are commonly retained by 2 to 4 implants. However, with respect to marginal bone resorption, patient satisfaction and prosthetic maintenance requirements, a retrospective cohort study by Mahgoli et al47 found no significant differences between overdentures supported by 2 or 4 implants. Similarly, Mumcu et al48 observed no significant difference in patient satisfaction scores across groups with different numbers of implants. Therefore, 2 implants were selected for the present study. As for their placement, the lateral incisor region was prioritised over the canine region. First, a 5-year retrospective radiographic evaluation by Mourad et al49 demonstrated that the lateral incisor site exhibited less peri-implant bone loss and more favourable stress distribution compared with the canine site. Second, this region possesses a higher proportion of trabecular bone and thicker cortical bone, which are conducive to achieving favourable primary implant stability and stress transmission.50,51 Third, from a mechanical perspective, placing implants in the lateral incisor region effectively shortens the anterior cantilever length, thereby reducing the tilting moment and ‘seesaw effect’ on the overdenture and consequently lowering the risk of posterior residual ridge resorption.23 However, it should be acknowledged that this specific configuration represents only one of several clinically adopted protocols. In clinical practice, the selection of implant number and position involves a multifactorial decision-making process that must balance surgical morbidity, prosthodontic accessibility, economic cost, and individual patient anatomy. The present findings should therefore be interpreted within the context of the specific configuration modelled, and extrapolation to other implant positions or numbers should be made with appropriate caution, pending further validation, as these clinical considerations extend beyond the scope of this finite element analysis.

Implant stresses were localised at the implant cervix in this study, specifically at the internal junction of the implant and abutment. As the implant's root side approached, stress levels dropped. According to this research, strengthening this susceptible region should be given top priority in implant design in order to prevent implant breakage. Similarly, the cortical bone around the implant cervix was the main site of bone tissue stress in this experiment. This is explained by the ‘composite beam analysis’ principle, which states that the maximum stress happens at the initial point of contact between 2 materials with differing elastic moduli (such as bone and implant) when they come into contact and sustain a load.43 The implant cervix serves as this point of contact in the experimental setup. The results of this investigation are in line with clinical observations that early peri-implant marginal bone resorption starts from the cortical bone. This could happen because cortical bone has a much larger density and elastic modulus than cancellous bone,52 which increases the amount of stress that reaches the cortical bone.

Denture base fracture is a frequent consequence of implant-supported overdenture restorations. Mandibular overdenture bases supported by 1 or 2 implants had fracture rates of 21.4% and 9.3%, respectively. With an incidence rate of around 70%, the fracture zone for 2-implant overdenture bases most commonly occurred in the area above the implants.53 The attachment components above the implant abutments taking up space inside the denture base are the main culprit, according to analysis. Denture rotation, deformation, and stress concentration may result from the abutment acting as a fulcrum due to the reduction in base thickness in this area. Because of their smaller cross-sectional area, mandibular implant-supported overdentures are more likely to fracture than maxillary implant-supported overdentures.54 The interface between the attachment housing and the denture base tissue surface was the main site of stress in all of the model dentures in this study. This result is consistent with Pisani's study.55 It indicates that steps must be taken to prevent base fracture in this area, such as strengthening the base material or adding reinforcing devices like metal mesh inside the base.30

Adequate retention is necessary for implant-supported overdentures to operate, although it steadily deteriorates over time. Denture retention is influenced by attachment type, wear of attachment components, implant angle and oral environment, according to numerous studies.56,57 The CM loc attachment system with PEKK retentive inserts and the Novaloc system with PEEK retentive inserts show less retention loss during chewing and insertion/removal than the Locator attachment system with nylon inserts, according to studies by Choi16 and Friedrichsen.58 Additionally, nylon insets have poor dimensional stability due to their excessive water absorption. Loss of retention may result from cracking caused by immersion in water at 60 °C.59 PEEK and PEKK, on the other hand, exhibit exceptional resistance to thermal hydrolysis and continue to function well in high-temperature and high-humidity environments. According to a clinical investigation by de Souza RF et al,60 patients' perceptions of overdenture stability are impacted by the ‘clicking sound’ made when they insert dentures. The ‘clicking sound’ during wear is more noticeable because PEEK is tougher than nylon, which improves patient satisfaction with the NOVALOC attachment system. When employed as attachment components, PEEK and PEKK show better physical-mechanical qualities and retention forces than conventional nylon materials, according to all of these investigations.

In this study, implant osseointegration was set at 100% and all structures were considered to be homogeneous and isotropic, which is not consistent with clinical reality. In addition, the mandibular geometry was derived from a single-patient CBCT model, which may not represent general anatomical variation; future studies should incorporate diverse anatomical models for further validation. Only static loading conditions were applied, whereas clinical mastication involves dynamic and cyclic loading. Dynamic loading could better simulate real-world masticatory function; however, it is computationally intensive, time-consuming and resource-demanding. Furthermore, due to experimental constraints, neuromuscular mechanisms that regulate mandibular movement could not be replicated. No matter how carefully the model is built, in vitro research will never be able to accurately duplicate real-world clinical situations. These experimental results should be assessed in future longitudinal clinical research using suitable approaches. This would help clinicians choose the best attachment systems and allow manufacturers to improve attachment component designs.

Conclusions

The following conclusions are made in light of the finite element analysis's findings:

1.The stresses and displacements on the parts of the implant-supported overdenture and its supporting structure are influenced by variations in the elastic modulus of the retention material and load conditions.
