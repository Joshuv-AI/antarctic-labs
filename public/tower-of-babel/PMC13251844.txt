A conserved biphasic venous remodeling program governs inferior vena cava formation in zebrafish
Authors: Giuseppina Lambiase, Gal Perlmoter, Yaara Tevet, Pallavi Chakraborty, Amitai Shen, Noga Moshe, Calanit Raanan, Rudra N. Das, Karina Yaniv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Inferior Vena Cava (IVC), the largest vein in humans, develops through replacement of the embryonic cardinal veins (CVs), a complex process prone to congenital anomalies, with major clinical implications. Yet, the mechanisms underlying IVC formation remain unclear. Here, we identify the zebrafish IVC, which emerges during metamorphosis and replaces the embryonic CVs, following a biphasic sequence similar to that described in mammals. Using longitudinal imaging and clonal lineage tracing, we demonstrate that the IVC originates exclusively from preexisting venous endothelial cells. Unexpectedly, genetic and pharmacological perturbations reveal that IVC formation proceeds independently of Vegfc/Vegfr3 signaling and instead involves Vegfa-dependent venous sprouting. Last, we demonstrate that the transition from CV to IVC marks a shift from a multifunctional embryonic vein to a specialized adult conduit adapted for high-volume blood transport. Overall, we identify a venous morphogenesis program akin to the mammalian IVC formation, providing a foundation for investigating venous anomalies.

INTRODUCTION

The cardiovascular system emerges as one of the earliest organs in the embryo, supporting its rapid growth and survival by supplying oxygen and nutrients and removing waste products (1, 2). In mammals, including humans, two major venous conduits return deoxygenated blood to the right atrium of the heart. The superior vena cava (SVC) drains the upper body, while the inferior vena cava (IVC) collects blood from the lower extremities and the abdominopelvic region. The IVC does not arise during early embryonic stages; instead, the embryonic cardinal veins (CVs) function transiently before undergoing extensive remodeling and eventually being replaced by the newly formed IVC.

Around the fourth week of human gestation, a primitive vascular plexus composed of angioblast clusters gives rise to three major sets of veins: the vitelline veins draining the yolk sac, the umbilical veins draining the placenta, and the CVs draining blood from the embryo’s body (3). Between gestational weeks 6th and 8th, the CVs undergo a complex process of fusion and regression to establish the foundation of the definitive adult venous system, including the SVC, IVC, and other major veins. This intricate process involves selective regression, anastomosis, and replacement of specific segments of the CVs and their derivatives (3, 4). Disruptions to any of these steps can result in congenital venous anomalies, such as IVC duplication (due to the persistence of right and left supra-CVs), transposition (left-sided IVC), and IVC agenesis (5–7), which, although rare and mostly asymptomatic, often complicate surgical procedures and are associated with increased risk of venous thrombosis and venous stasis (8). Therefore, understanding the mechanisms underlying IVC formation is crucial for deciphering the etiology of these malformations and improving their diagnosis and treatment. While this biphasic development of IVC has been documented more than a century ago (4, 9), the underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. It is unclear why this process occurs and what physiological demands drive the replacement of embryonic CVs.

Different animal models have been used to investigate IVC development, including dogs, pigs, sheep, bats, and avians (10, 11). Nevertheless, the limited experimental accessibility and the precision of cell tracing in these systems have led to at least five different theories describing the origins and mechanisms of IVC formation (5, 11). All the proposed models share common elements, such as the early bilaterally symmetric configuration of the embryonic CVs and the anastomosis of the sub-CV and vitelline veins, which gives rise to the cranial-most portion of IVC. However, the main discrepancy among these models lies in the origin of the caudal part of the IVC (11).

Over the past decades, the zebrafish has emerged as a valuable model for studying vascular development (12). Same as humans, zebrafish have a CV during embryonic stages, which serves as the primary venous vessel carrying deoxygenated blood from the body to the heart (13, 14). However, the fate of this system at later stages remains unknown. In this study, we identify the zebrafish IVC and demonstrate that it forms during metamorphosis (15) and replaces the embryonic CV as the primary vein in the trunk. Using high-resolution longitudinal imaging and endothelial cell (EC)–specific clonal lineage tracing, we demonstrate that the IVC originates exclusively from venous ECs and show that the process is regulated by vascular endothelial growth factor A (Vegfa). Unexpectedly, perturbations in the venous-specific Vegfc/Vegfd/Vegfr3 (Flt4) signaling axis have no effect on IVC formation. Functional analyses reveal that while the embryonic CV displays clear regional compartmentalization (16–19) and serves multiple roles, including macromolecular scavenging (20, 21) and hematopoietic support (22), the adult IVC is a streamlined, high-volume conduit. This functional divergence suggests that replacing the CV with the IVC is not merely a structural change but represents a key vessel specialization event during the organism’s transition from embryonic to adult stages.

Overall, our findings reveal the existence of a well-established IVC in adult zebrafish, which develops through the replacement of the CV, following a biphasic process similar to that described in mammals. Moreover, this work highlights zebrafish as a valuable experimental model for understanding similar processes in human IVC formation and for addressing associated congenital anomalies.

RESULTS

The axial venous system undergoes extensive reorganization during zebrafish metamorphosis

The anatomical arrangement of the great vessels in the trunk of the zebrafish larva [~5 to 7 days postfertilization (dpf)] is well established (23). The three large vessels—dorsal aorta (DA), cardinal vein (CV), and thoracic duct (TD)—run horizontally spanning the whole trunk (Fig. 1, A and B, and fig. S1, A and B). Unexpectedly, while imaging the posterior trunk of 26-dpf/~9-mm Tg(kdrl:EGFP;lyve1b:dsRed) fish (24), we noticed the presence of an additional large vessel running between the DA and the CV (Fig. 1, C and D, and fig. S1C, magenta dashed line and magenta arrowheads) and encircled by the TD [Fig. 1D, (yellow dashed line) and fig. S1, C and D]. Given its anatomical location, which resembles the typical position of the IVC in other vertebrates, we hypothesized that this vessel might represent the zebrafish equivalent of the mammalian IVC.

The hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of sagittal sections from whole fish at 23 dpf/~8 mm further confirmed the presence of three axial blood-filled vessels spanning the animal’s trunk (Fig. 1E). Confocal images revealed that the new vessel is labeled by the blood EC-specific transgene kdrl:EGFP (Fig. 1C and fig. S1C) and the venous/lymphatic-specific reporters lyve1b:dsRed (Fig. 1D and fig. S1C, magenta dashed lines) and mrc1a:EGFP (fig. S1D, magenta dashed lines) (24, 25), as opposed to the DA that displays only kdrl:EGFP fluorescence (fig. S1C, white dashed lines), and the TD that is marked only by the lyve1b (Fig. 1D, yellow dashed line) and mrc1a (fig. S1D, yellow dashed line) transgenes. Concomitant to the appearance of the new axial vessel, we observed a marked reduction in the caliber of the CV. At early stages (13 dpf/5 mm), the nascent vessel is narrower than the CV. However, as the animal grows, its caliber progressively increases, while the CV decreases in size (Fig. 1F).

To further confirm that the newly formed trunk vessel functions as a vein, we assessed blood flow directionality through time-lapse live imaging of Tg(gata1a:dsRed) (26) using confocal and light-sheet microscopy. We found that at 18 dpf/~5 mm and 21 dpf/~6 mm, the DA carries blood from the heart to the trunk, whereas the CV returns blood to the heart (Fig. 1, G and H, and movie S1). Later, at 23 dpf/~7.5 mm, once the new vessel becomes lumenized, it carries blood toward the heart together with the CV, indicating its venous nature (Fig. 1I and movie S2). As the IVC continues to expand, an increasing fraction of blood flow is routed through it, with a corresponding reduction in flow through the progressively thinned CV (Fig. 1J and movie S2). Eventually, by ~9 to 10 mm, flow is barely observed in the CV (movies S2 and S3).

We then set out to define the sequence of events leading to the establishment of the IVC. We began by imaging the caudal trunk of Tg(kdrl:EGFP;lyve1b:dsRed) animals at 12 dpf/~4 mm, where we detected the DA, CV, and TD (Fig. 1, K and L, and fig. S1, E and F). Later, at 14 dpf/~5 mm, we observed sprouts arising from the CV and extending dorsally (Fig. 1, M and N, and fig. S1, G and H, white arrowhead). By 17 dpf/6 mm, the sprouts reach the level of the DA, where they bifurcate (Fig. 1, O and P and coalesce with adjacent sprouts to generate a new lumenized vessel that expands (Fig. 1, Q and R) and is fully functional by ~26 dpf/~9 mm (Fig. 1, S and T, and movie S3). To determine whether the juvenile IVC persists through adulthood, we examined histological sections of adult zebrafish. We detected a major vein running ventrally and parallel to the DA (fig. S1I, magenta dashed lines, magenta arrowhead), whereas no remnants of the CV were detected at this stage (fig. S1I, light blue asterisks). Together, our results demonstrate that the embryonic CV gradually regresses and is progressively replaced by a juvenile-born vessel that develops ventrally to the DA and persists until adulthood, taking over the function of bringing deoxygenated blood back to the heart. The notable similarity between this process and the one described during mammalian IVC development (3, 4) led us to conclude that the newly formed vessel represents the zebrafish equivalent to the mammalian IVC.

Cellular origins of the IVC

To investigate the ontogeny of the IVC, we used our previously established EC-specific brainbow-based clonal analysis tool, flibow (24), in combination with Tg(kdrl:CreERT2) (27), to specifically label blood ECs. We first asked whether the major axial vessels contribute to the formation of the IVC. Treating 1.5-, 2-, 2.5-, and 3-dpf Tg(kdrl:CreERT2;flibow) embryos with 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) resulted in effective mosaic labeling of the CV and its derivatives and also labeled the DA, although with lower efficiency (fig. S2, A to E, and table S1). This pattern allowed us to distinguish potential contributions from these vessels. Following 4-OHT administration, embryos were raised under normal conditions and screened between 5 and 10 dpf (i.e., before the initiation of IVC formation) for the presence of sparse and distinguishable labeled EC clones in the CV, venous ISVs (vISVs), and DA. The selected animals were then imaged at multiple time points for robust and reliable tracing of the identified clones (fig. S2A). The sparse labeling of ECs enabled the precise tracking of the dynamic behaviors of individual ECs and their clones during IVC formation. We found that, of all analyzed larvae containing labeled CV-ECs by 5 to 10 dpf (n = 28), 93% showed clonally related progeny in the IVC (table S1). For instance, in fish #1 (Fig. 2A), we identified two distinctly labeled ECs at 14 dpf/~5 mm—one located on the dorsal side (Fig. 2A, yellow EC, magenta arrowhead) and one on the ventral side (Fig. 2A, cyan EC, white arrowhead) of the CV. Imaging of the same animal at 21 dpf/~6.2 mm and 29 dpf/~9 mm (Fig. 2, B and C, magenta arrowhead) revealed that only the dorsal EC generated clonally related progeny, which contributed to the IVC. In contrast, ECs located on the ventral CV did not contribute to IVC formation and either remained stable throughout the duration of the experiment or sprouted ventrally to generate other vessels (Fig. 2, A to C, white arrowhead). In a cohort of 30 fish specifically analyzed for the dorsal versus ventral position of labeled ECs in the CV, ~63% of dorsal ECs contributed to the IVC, whereas only ~29% of ventral ECs did (fig. S2F). To check whether the observed percentages reflect a true difference in contribution to IVC formation rather than being a consequence of CV-EC polarization as described in the early embryo (19), we quantified the number of ECs in the dorsal and ventral CVs of Tg(kdrl:nls-mCherry;kdrl:GFP) fish at 14 dpf/5 mm (the onset of IVC formation). We divided the CV into dorsal and ventral halves and counted EC nuclei in each half. Our analyses revealed that the ventral and dorsal halves contain a comparable number of ECs at this stage (fig. S2, G and H), validating the differential contribution of dorsal versus ventral ECs to IVC. These data are in line with previous reports demonstrating the angiogenic properties of dorsal CV-ECs (28–30), while highlighting the more progenitor nature of ECs located in the ventral wall of the CV (17, 18).

Further lineage tracing analyses revealed that vISVs-ECs also contribute to IVC formation (fig. S2, I to K, fish #2, white arrowhead). While imaging these fish, we frequently noticed loop-like structures emerging from the CV, which later contributed ECs to the nascent IVC (Fig. 2, D to F, fish #3, magenta arrowheads). To better characterize these structures, we quantified the number of sprouts and loops along the CV between 4 and 6 mm, the window when IVC formation begins, and found that loops occurred more frequently than isolated sprouts (fig. S2L). Longitudinal imaging of five individual fish indicated that most loops form when two adjacent CV-sprouts connect, rather than representing a fundamentally distinct sprouting mode. This can be seen, for instance, in fig. S2 (M and N), where two labeled CV-ECs (fig. S2M, magenta and green) each send a sprout that later fuses to form a loop (fig. S2N).

Unlike the CV, we never found clones derived from the DA populating the IVC (fish #4; Fig. 2, G to I, blue arrowhead), indicating that this major vein is established exclusively from preexisting CV-ECs and their derivatives, the vISVs (fig. S2, I to K). These results were further confirmed through an unbiased lineage tracing strategy using Tg(hsp70l:CreERT2) animals, where CreERT2 is ubiquitously expressed, while the brainbow construct is specifically expressed in ECs upon heat and 4-OHT activation (24). In animals treated at 3 dpf, only venous-derived ECs were found in the IVC (fish #5; Fig. 2, J to L, magenta arrowheads) with no contribution from DA-ECs (fish #6; Fig. 2, M to O, light blue arrowhead).

Molecular mechanisms underlying IVC formation

Since venous sprouting in the zebrafish embryo is known to be controlled by Vegfc/Vegfd signaling via the Vegfr3/Flt4 receptor (28, 30, 31), we first interrogated the involvement of this pathway in IVC formation. Given that homozygous vegfc mutants are embryonic lethal (32), we examined heterozygous animals (26, 32). As seen in Fig. 3 (A to C), 26 dpf/10 mm fish displayed both a fully established IVC and a thin CV, which were indistinguishable from those of their wild-type (WT) siblings. Similarly, we found that IVC formation proceeded normally, with no significant differences in IVC caliber, in both Tg(kdrl:GFP;vegfd−/−) fish (26, 31) (Fig. 3, D to F, magenta dashed lines) and flt4 mutants (24, 28, 30) (Fig. 3, G to I, magenta dashed lines), which survive through adulthood. Since flt4 mutants completely lack trunk lymphatics (24, 30), our data also indicate that neighboring lymphatic vessels are not required for proper IVC formation. Together, these results indicate that IVC formation is independent of Vegfc/Vegfd/Flt4 signaling.

We then assessed the involvement of Vegfa signaling in IVC formation by analyzing vegfaa and vegfab mutants. Previous studies have established that vegfaa−/− embryos exhibit a complete loss of the DA and expansion of the CV and die around 3 dpf (33, 34), whereas vegfab mutants exhibit somewhat milder vascular defects and survive into adulthood (34). Accordingly, double homozygous mutants (vegfaa−/−;vegfab−/−) are embryonically lethal (33), while all other allelic combinations are viable through adult stages. We focused our analyses primarily on vegfaa+/−;vegfab−/− fish, which have only one WT allele. During the initial sprouting phase, between 5 and 6 mm, vegfaa+/−;vegfab−/− fish showed a visible reduction in the number of sprouts emerging from the CV and ISVs as compared to WT siblings (Fig. 3, J to L, and fig. S3, A to D, white arrowheads). At later stages (i.e., ~8 to 10 mm), these animals show a truncated and abnormal IVC, with several missing segments (Fig. 3, M and N). To better characterize the phenotype, we used a major shunt connecting the CV and the IVC in both WT and mutant animals as a landmark to divide the trunk into rostral and caudal regions (Fig. 3, O to Q). This shunt was consistently present in both groups at the analyzed juvenile stages, but in vegfaa+/−;vegfab−/− mutants, it was slightly shifted caudally (Fig. 3R). It remains perfused up to ~9 to 10 mm, which is the latest stage at which blood flow could be reliably tracked in vivo in this region using wide-field microscopy (movies S4 and S5). We found that vegfaa+/−;vegfab−/− fish exhibit a lumenized, albeit slightly narrower, IVC in the rostral trunk [Fig. 3, N and Q, magenta arrowhead, quantified in (S)], accompanied by a normally regressing CV [Fig. 3, N and Q, light blue arrowheads, quantified in (T)]. In contrast, the caudal trunk showed a thinner and fragmented IVC [Fig. 3, N and Q, yellow arrowheads, magenta asterisks, quantified in (U) and (V)], and a persistent, larger CV [Fig. 3, N and Q, blue arrowhead, quantified in (W)]. These results hint at a putative synchronized mechanism, wherein CV regression is intrinsically linked to IVC formation, with the CV maintaining its structure when IVC development is compromised.

The analysis of most other vegfaa/vegfab mutant combinations, i.e., vegfaa+/−; vegfab +/− (fig. S3, E and F) and vegfaa+/−;vegfab+/+ (fig. S3G) at 14 dpf/5 mm did not show significant alterations in the number of CV-sprouts (fig. S3, H and I) or the resulting IVC at 25 dpf/~10 mm (fig. S3, J to L). Only vegfaa+/+;vegfab−/− animals exhibited a significant reduction of CV-sprouts at 14 dpf/5 mm (fig. S3, M and N); however, this decrease ultimately did not impair IVC formation (fig. S3O), suggesting a compensation mechanism, likely through the functional Vegfaa paralogue.

While imaging vegfaa+/−;vegfab−/− mutants, we observed differences in blood flow in the trunk region. This was verified by wide-field imaging at 7 to 9 mm, which showed reduced flow velocity in mutants as compared to WT siblings, whereas the direction of flow remained unchanged (Fig. 3X and movies S6 and S7).

To distinguish direct effects of Vegfa signaling on CV sprouting from secondary effects of altered hemodynamics, we treated WT juveniles with SU5416, a broad VEGF receptor inhibitor, shown to inhibit venous and arterial sprouting in zebrafish (35–37). Overnight treatment for three consecutive days starting at 6.5 mm led to a significant reduction of CV-derived sprouts and impaired IVC formation (Fig. 3, Y and Z), closely phenocopying vegfaa+/−;vegafb−/− animals (Fig. 3, L and N). At the onset of treatment, the trunk vasculature and blood flow are normal in these WT fish, indicating that the observed defects arise primarily from acute Vegfr inhibition rather than preexisting vascular or flow abnormalities. Hence, we conclude that in the mutants, altered flow is not sufficient to account for the primary sprouting defects, which we therefore attribute mainly to reduced Vegf signaling. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that the hemodynamic changes in vegfaa+/−;vegafb−/− animals might influence later aspects of IVC growth and lumen expansion.

Last, we attempted to define the cellular sources of Vegfa by taking advantage of the Tg(vegfaa:GFP) reporter (38). At 14 dpf/4.2 mm, before the onset of CV sprouting, we detected vegfaa-driven GFP expression in the neighboring muscle fibers (fig. S3P, light-blue arrowheads) and in what appeared to be pericytes surrounding the DA (fig. S3P, white arrowheads). We further confirmed this expression pattern by imaging this line in combination with TgBAC(pdgfrb:Gal4FF);UAS:NTR_mCherry) (39), which indeed depicted clear colocalization of both signals (fig. S3, Q and R, white arrowheads). To test whether pericyte-derived Vegfaa is sufficient to control IVC formation, we ablated pericytes in TgBAC(pdgfrb:Gal4FF);UAS:NTR_mCherry) fish at 15 dpf/5 mm using metronidazole (MTZ) (40). As seen in fig. S3 (S to U), although no pericytes were detected in these animals at 26 dpf/9 mm (fig. S3T), the IVC formed normally (fig. S3, T and U), indicating that pericyte depletion per se does not prevent IVC development. These results are in line with the expression of Vegfaa in surrounding muscle fibers, which may compensate for the absence of pericytes.

Together, these data demonstrate that Vegfa signaling is required for IVC formation, and although the allelic combination (vegfaa+/−;vegfab−/−) does allow for blood vessels to develop in the early fish embryo, structures such as the IVC, which form during metamorphosis, are particularly sensitive to VegfA alterations.

Because IVC formation proceeds normally in vegfc/vegfd and flt4 mutants, yet it is strongly impaired when Vegf receptors are acutely inhibited with SU5416 (Fig. 3, Y and Z), we reasoned that Vegfa must act through receptors other than Vegfr3 during this process. Various reports have highlighted the involvement of Vegfr1/Flt1 in certain settings of venous sprouting (41–45). We therefore wondered whether a similar mechanism could account for IVC formation in response to Vegfa signaling. At 15 dpf/~5 mm, we detected clear expression of the Tg(flt1BAC:YFP) reporter (46) not only in the DA but also in ECs sprouting from the CV (Fig. 4, A and B, white arrowheads). In addition, flt1:YFP expression was also evident in the newly formed IVC at 22 dpf/~8 mm (Fig. 4, C and D, magenta dashed line), whereas the CV was not labeled by this transgene at any stage. In zebrafish, Vegfa/Flt1 has been implicated together with Dll4/Notch signaling in controlling EC sprouting behavior and limiting the number of tip cells in several vascular beds (43, 45, 47, 48). Therefore, we examined whether the Notch signaling pathway is also involved in IVC formation. First, we assessed dll4 expression in CV-sprouts and nascent IVC endothelium by imaging Tg(dll4:Gal4;UAS:Kaede) fish (48, 49). As seen in Fig. 4, E and F (white arrowheads), we detected dll4 expression in the initial CV-sprouts and in the IVC at 18 dpf/~8 mm (Fig. 4, G and H, magenta dashed lines). To investigate whether Notch signaling plays a role in IVC formation, we treated juvenile fish with the γ-secretase inhibitor LY411575, which blocks canonical Notch signaling downstream of Dll4 (43). Early intermittent treatment starting at 5 mm (onset of CV sprouting) led to a significant reduction in CV-sprouts that normally contribute to IVC formation in 6-mm fish (Fig. 4, I to K). Since prolonged exposure to the inhibitor caused systemic toxicity and lethality, we performed a second series of experiments starting at 6.5 mm, i.e., after initial stages of IVC formation. We found that although systemic toxicity persisted, the IVC caliber at 29 dpf (7.5 mm) was not significantly different from that of control animals (Fig. 4, L to Q), indicating that Notch signaling is particularly important for the initial phase of sprouting from the CV, whereas later remodeling steps appear largely Notch independent. Together, our genetic and pharmacological data support the requirement for Vegfa and Notch signaling during the initial sprouting of CV-ECs to generate the IVC. Vegf pathway inhibition (vegfaa;vegfab mutants and SU5416 treatment) and inhibition of γ-secretase–dependent Notch activation (LY411575 treatment) both significantly reduce the number of CV-sprouts and/or IVC segments within a defined temporal window.

CV and IVC have distinct functional and molecular properties

Having characterized how the IVC forms from CV-sprouts, we next investigated the physiological significance of this replacement and asked whether these two vessels serve different functions. In zebrafish, the embryonic CV is not only a conduit for deoxygenated blood but also a multifunctional vessel containing regionally specialized EC populations. For instance, the ventral floor houses specialized angioblasts that generate arterial, venous, and lymphatic EC types (17, 18). Likewise, dorsal CV-ECs are highly “angiogenic” and sprout in response to Vegfa/Vegfc signals (19, 28–30). The caudal segment of the CV forms the CHT, which serves as a niche for hematopoietic stem cell expansion (22, 50), and a scavenger-like EC type with high endocytic capacity has also been described in this vessel (21). Because the IVC endothelium arises from the CV, we asked whether these diverse functions are maintained in the IVC.

To address this, we first focused on the scavenger function described for CV-ECs (21). We used fluorescently labeled substrates as uptake tracers to compare endocytic activity in the CV and IVC. As seen in Fig. 5A (yellow arrowheads), intravascular injection of rhodamine-dextran (10 kDa) at 14 dpf/5 mm revealed robust uptake by CV-ECs, particularly in the caudal region. In contrast, at 22 dpf/~10 mm, neither the newly formed IVC nor the residual CV showed detectable scavenging activity (Fig. 5B). Thus, despite being largely derived from CV-endothelium, IVC-ECs do not retain the scavenger function characteristic of CV-ECs, consistent with a shift in EC specialization during venous reorganization and replacement. Large-sized tracers, such as Qdot705 (Fig. 5, C and D) or dextran (500 kDa) (Fig. 5, E and F), were not internalized by ECs of either vessel at early or late stages.

Next, we evaluated the contribution of receptor-mediated endocytosis to the scavenging properties of CV-ECs and whether this changes as the IVC forms. To this end, we performed intravascular injection of transferrin (Trf-488) (51), an iron-binding protein that binds the transferrin receptor and is internalized via receptor-mediated endocytosis. As shown in Fig. 5G, Trf-488 was rapidly internalized by CV-ECs (yellow arrowheads) and by TD-Lymphatic ECs, at both 14 dpf/~4.5 mm and 25 dpf/~8.5 mm (Fig. 5H, light blue dashed lines, yellow and red arrowheads), but not by IVC-ECs at comparable stages (Fig. 5H, magenta dashed lines), indicating that IVC-ECs have largely lost receptor-mediated scavenger activity.

In addition to their specialized scavenging function, embryonic ECs in the CHT have been reported to act as a niche supporting the expansion and differentiation of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) (22). To examine whether this property is retained in the IVC, we imaged Tg(kdrl:BFP;lyve1b:dsRed;runx1:GFP) animals. As expected, runx1+ HSPCs were readily detected within EC pockets in the CV-CHT at 4 dpf/~3.5 mm (Fig. 5, I and J, light blue arrowheads, and movie S7). In contrast, at 28 dpf/9.6 mm, the IVC did not harbor runx1+ cells (Fig. 5, K and L, and movie S8). Together, our findings reveal that the embryonic CV serves multiple specialized functions that are not shared by the IVC, underscoring the functional divergence between these two veins.

Next, we sought to determine whether CV and IVC ECs exhibit distinct molecular signatures. To this end, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on kdrl+;lyve1b+ ECs isolated from the whole trunk of 1.5-month-old (~14 mm) Tg(kdrl:GFP;lyve1b:dsRed) animals. In this transgenic combination, double-positive kdrl+;lyve1b+ cells correspond to venous ECs, whereas arterial ECs (kdrl+;lyve1b−) and most lymphatic ECs (kdrl−;lyve1b+) are excluded. The final dataset comprised 643 cells. MARS-seq analysis (24) identified seven distinct clusters (Fig. 5M and table S2), four of which (EC1 to EC4) expressed canonical EC makers such as fli1a, kdrl, and pecam1 (Fig. 5N and table S2). Three clusters were enriched for marker genes characteristic of mesenchymal cells (pdgfrb and postnb), macrophages (mpeg1.1 and marco), and red blood cells (hemgn and hbaa2), respectively (Fig. 5N).

At early developmental stages, the CV and vISVs express venous and sinusoidal markers such as lyve1b, mrc1a, and dab2 (18, 30, 50). We examined their expression in our clusters EC1 to EC4 and found them to be specifically up-regulated in EC1 (Fig. 5O), indicating the presence of these signatures in a subset of the juvenile trunk vasculature.

Within the kdrl+;lyve1b+ venous compartment, clusters EC3 and EC4 represented ECs that have largely down-regulated sinusoidal/CV markers (lyve1b and mrc1a) and instead express vwf, plvap, and cav1 (Fig. 5O), consistent with a transition from sinusoidal, niche-like CV endothelium to a more specialized, nonsinusoidal large vein endothelium, as seen in the IVC. Notably, cluster EC4 alone showed strong up-regulation of cav1 (Caveolin-1) (Fig. 5, O and P), a core caveolae protein. Caveolae are mostly absent from “leaky” sinusoidal endothelium but are abundant in many continuous and large-vessel endothelial beds, where they contribute to mechanotransduction and barrier regulation (52).

To validate these data, we examined TgBAC(cav1:cav1-EGFP)pd1096 animals (53). At 14 dpf/5 mm, Cav1 protein expression, driven by the cav1 promoter, was detected in the DA but not in the CV (Fig. 5Q). By 22 dpf/9 mm, however, strong Cav1 expression was detected in the IVC, while the CV remained largely negative (Fig. 5R), indicating that cav1 is specifically up-regulated in the IVC endothelium. Further studies will be required to elucidate the functional implications of Cav1 differential expression. Together, these results support the notion that cluster 4 most likely corresponds to IVC-ECs, highlighting a molecular signature that distinguishes the IVC from the sinusoidal CV. Moreover, these findings provide molecular support for the changes in EC properties and vessel functionality as the IVC emerges from CV and its derivatives.

DISCUSSION

The IVC is the primary venous conduit for returning deoxygenated blood from the lower body to the heart in mammals. Unlike the other major vessels, such as the dorsal aorta and the thoracic duct, which emerge during early embryogenesis and mature into their final form, the IVC forms later, coinciding with the regression of the early embryonic CVs. This complex ontogeny is associated with congenital IVC anomalies reported in ~0.3 to 10% of the population (54).

This biphasic development of the mammalian venous system has been recognized for over a century; yet, the lack of suitable animal models has hindered the study of its cellular and molecular underpinnings. Here, we show that the zebrafish trunk venous system develops through a remarkably similar sequence of events, in which an embryonic CV is later replaced by a new axial vein, indicating that this process is highly conserved and potentially has important physiological implications.

Zebrafish have long served as a major model for vascular biology studies, yet most studies have focused on embryonic and early larval stages. Tissue growth and remodeling, however, continue throughout life, and postembryonic stages often reveal highly interesting processes (24). Our work highlights a series of important vascular reorganization events that occur during metamorphosis, i.e., the larva-to-juvenile transition, providing an experimentally tractable system to investigate IVC development and function.

The technical advantages of the zebrafish system, including the amenability for longitudinal imaging, precise lineage tracing, and genetic and pharmacological perturbations, allowed us to define both the cellular origins of the IVC and signaling pathways involved.

The cellular origin is an important determinant of vessel properties (24); however, tracing progenitors during vertebrate post-embryonic development is technically challenging and requires specialized approaches (55). In the absence of robust lineage tracing models enabling the study of the IVC, the literature has been confounded by several reports, often conflicting, from humans and large mammals (5, 11).

In this work, we use a multicolor EC-specific lineage tracing tool (“flibow”) in conjunction with longitudinal live imaging to track the origins of the IVC. Our data reveal that the zebrafish IVC arises exclusively from venous sources, namely, the CV and CV-derived ISVs, with no detectable contributions from arterial or lymphatic endothelium. These results indicate that IVC assembly does not require major switches in EC fate but instead relies on a venous-to-venous remodeling program.

Unexpectedly, however, we find that this post-embryonic venous sprouting is not controlled by the Vegfc/Vegfd/Flt4 signaling pathway that is essential for embryonic venous and lymphatic sprouting (19, 30). Instead, our data highlight a pivotal role for Vegfa signaling (56) and pinpoint the specific requirement of the two zebrafish vegfa paralogs, vegfaa and vegfab. Through analysis of compound mutants, we show that a single functional vegfaa allele is sufficient to support early vascular development, but not adequate to sustain proper IVC formation in the absence of vegfab. Vegfab homozygous mutants alone however, do not display an obvious IVC phenotype. This suggests that vegfab plays an important compensatory role under reduced vegfaa dosage, consistent with previous evidence for transcriptional up-regulation of vegfab in vegfaa mutants (57).

Since vegfaa+/−;vegfab−/− mutants exhibit both impaired CV sprouting and decreased flow velocity at the time that coincides with IVC formation, it was important to distinguish direct Vegfa signaling requirements from secondary effects of altered hemodynamics. The acute pharmacological inhibition of Vegf receptors in WT juvenile fish led to a similar reduction in CV-derived sprouts and defective IVC formation, supporting a direct requirement for Vegf signaling in initiating CV-sprouting toward the IVC. At the same time, the decreased flow observed in vegfaa+/−;vegfab−/− mutants may modulate later aspects of IVC growth and lumen expansion. The possible contribution of hemodynamics in post-embryonic venous development requires further investigation.

The expression of dll4 in CV-sprouts and nascent IVC endothelium pointed toward a potential role for Notch signaling in this process. The pharmacological inhibition of Notch using the γ-secretase inhibitor LY411575 indeed resulted in reduced CV sprouting when applied at the onset of IVC formation. After the initial sprouting had occurred, inhibiting Notch activity had little effect on IVC caliber, indicating the requirement of Notch to be in the early stages of IVC formation, i.e., during CV-sprouting.

Although the process of CV-to-IVC replacement has been anatomically described in mammals, its functional significance remains unclear. The process is conserved across orders and is specific to the venous system, emphasizing its importance. Our results point to a functional divergence between the CV and IVC. The embryonic CV is a multifunctional vessel that fulfills diverse physiological roles, including harboring vascular progenitors (18), scavenging of circulating macromolecules (21), and supporting hematopoietic stem cell expansion (22). The mature IVC, on the other hand, appears specialized for high-volume blood transport, without displaying indicators of other functions. Our assays confirm that IVC-ECs do not retain the sinusoidal-like features of their CV progenitors (21), which favor exchange and niche functions but are not optimal for efficient bulk flow. The transition from CV to IVC is driven not only by anatomical rearrangement but also reflects a functional adaptation in which a multifunctional embryonic vein is remodeled into a streamlined conduit optimized to meet the increased hemodynamic demands of the growing organism.

Our single-cell transcriptomic and reporter analyses further support this divergence. Within the kdrl+;lyve1b+ venous compartment, we identify a cluster of ECs that down-regulate sinusoidal CV markers such as lyve1b and mrc1a and instead express vwf, plvap, and cav1. Cav1, a core caveolae component, is largely absent from sinusoidal endothelium but enriched in many continuous and large vessels, where caveolae contribute to mechanotransduction and barrier regulation. In line with this, cav1 expression is low in the embryonic CV but becomes strongly up-regulated in the IVC endothelium, marking a molecular transition from sinusoidal CV-like cells to a nonsinusoidal, large vein phenotype.

Overall, our results reveal a previously unrecognized program of IVC formation during zebrafish metamorphosis, characterized by the regression of the CV and its replacement by the IVC. This process is driven exclusively by venous ECs with no contribution from arterial or lymphatic lineages. We further demonstrate that IVC formation critically depends on Vegfaa signaling, whereas Vegfc/Vegfd and Flt4, key regulators of embryonic venous and lymphatic development, are not involved in this process. Functionally, this transition reflects a shift from the embryonic cardiovascular system’s diverse roles to the IVC’s specialization in efficient, high-volume blood transport. Together, these findings reveal a conserved biphasic developmental sequence and anatomic organization with a mechanistically distinct program of venous morphogenesis, establishing zebrafish as a powerful model for studying IVC development and its associated anomalies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Zebrafish husbandry and strains

Zebrafish were raised using standard methods and handled in accordance with the guidelines of the Weizmann Institute Animal Care and Use Committee, protocols 06430722-2, 00570121-2, 06560722-1 (24). Zebrafish lines used in this study are as follows: Tg(fli1a:EGFP)y1, Tg(lyve1b:dsRed)nz101, Tg(kdrl:EGFP)s843, Tg(kdrl:tagBFP)mu293, Tg(mrc1a:EGFP)y251, Tg(gata1a:dsRed)sd2, Tg(flibow), Tg(hsp70l:CreERT2), Tg(flt4um203/um203), Tg(fli1ep:DsRedEx)um13 and were previously described (24), Tg(kdrl:Cre-ERT2)fb13 (27), Tg(vegfaa)bns1, Tg(vegfab)bns92 (33), Tg(vegfd)bns257 (26), Tg(vegfc)um18 (26), Tg(kdrl:nls-mCherry)y173 (26), TgBAC(flt1:YFP)hu4624 (58), Tg(vegfaa:EGFPpd260) (38), Tg(dll4:gal4)mu106 (48), Tg(UAS:Kaede) (49), TgBAC(pdgfrb:Gal4FF) (39), Tg(UAS:NTR-mCherry) (40), Tg(runx1:eGFP)y509 (59), and TgBAC(cav1:cav1-EGFP)pd1096 (53). For imaging, we used either a casper (roy−/−; nacre−/−) (24), or embryos that were treated for 5 days with 0.003% N-phenylthiourea (PTU) (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO) to inhibit melanin pigment formation.

Lineage tracing analyses

For lineage tracing experiments, we used the previously generated Tg(flibow) line (24) in combination with two drivers, Tg(kdrl:CreERT2) and Tg(hsp70l:CreERT2). To specifically label blood ECs, Tg(flibow) animals were crossed with Tg(kdrl:CreERT2), and a calibration experiment was performed by treating embryos with 5 μM 4-OHT at 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 dpf for 24 hours in the dark. Animals were first screened and analyzed at 10 to 14 dpf to assess the distribution of labeled clones across vessel types. Based on these results, the subsequent characterization of IVC origins was performed using a single 24-hour induction with 5 μM 4-OHT at 3 dpf in the dark. After treatment, embryos were rinsed three times in fresh E3 medium and returned to system water. Animals with labeled cells were screened at 5 to 10 dpf, and selected individuals were imaged at the appropriate IVC developmental stages and maintained in fresh water until the experimental endpoint. To confirm the data obtained using Tg(kdrl:CreERT2; flibow), Tg(flibow) animals were crossed with Tg(hsp70l:CreERT2). Progeny were preheated at 36°C for 15 min to activate the hsp70l promoter, followed by a 45-min combined heat (36°C) and 5 μM 4-OHT treatment. After treatment, embryos were rinsed three times in fresh E3 medium and returned to system water. Larvae were screened for labeled cells at 5 to 10 dpf, and selected individuals were imaged at the appropriate IVC developmental stages and maintained in fresh water until the experimental endpoint.

Pharmacological treatments

For LY-411575 (Sigma-Aldrich, SML0506) treatment, 17-dpf/5-mm fish were incubated overnight with LY-411575 (Sigma-Aldrich, SML0506) at a final concentration of 50 μM for three consecutive nights. Each treatment consisted of a 16-hour incubation at 28.5°C in the dark in 12-well plates, followed by daily washes and routine feeding. A 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution was used as a control (60). To inhibit Vegfr, we used SU5416. Animals were treated at 14 dpf/~5 mm for three consecutive days, sequentially, with overnight treatment, daily washes, and feeding.

NTR-MTZ–mediated cell ablation

Nitroreductase-MTZ (NTR-MTZ)–mediated cell ablation was modified from (61). MTZ (Sigma-Aldrich, M1547) was diluted in E3 medium to a final 10 mM concentration, and 15 dpf/5 mm Tg(pdgfrb:GAL4FF;UAS:NTR-mCherry) larvae were soaked in it for 24 hours in the dark. Following washing and recovery, the animals were imaged at the stage where the formation of the IVC is completed.

Microinjections and uptake assays

Microangiography was performed on anesthetized juvenile animals using microinjection glass capillaries, as previously described (23). The following compounds were injected: Qtracker705 (Invitrogen, Q21061MP), tetramethylrhodamine dextran 10 kDa (Thermo Fisher Scientific, D7139), fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) dextran (500,000 molecular weight) (Sigma-Aldrich, 46947), and transferrin (Invitrogen, T13342). Injected animals were allowed to recover in fresh water for 5 min, following which they were anesthetized and processed for confocal microscopy.

Histology sample preparation

Samples from 8 mm/~20 dpf and 1.5-year-old zebrafish were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4°C. Tissue processing was performed using a Leica ASP300S processor with an automatic protocol. The dehydration process began with 70% ethanol for 45 min, followed by three steps of 96% ethanol for 30 min each and two 100% ethanol for 30 min each. Subsequently, the samples were washed with ethanol and Medite X-TRA-solv xylene substitute for 45 min, followed by two additional washes with Medite X-TRA-solv for 60 min each. Last, the wax was changed three times at 57°C for 60 min each before embedding the samples in Leica Paraplast paraffin.

H&E staining protocol

H&E staining was performed using an automatic stainer (Giotto, Diapath). Samples were initially cleared using Medite X-TRA-solv xylene substitute in three 5-min washes, followed by rehydration, with sequential 5-min incubations in 100, 96, and 70% ethanol. After brief rinses in water and distilled water (1 min each) slides were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, followed by a sequence of washes: Tween (2 min), acidified 70% ethanol (10 s), two tween washes (3 min each), distilled water (1 min), and 70% ethanol (3 min). Slides were then stained with eosin. Dehydration was completed with two 2-min washes in 96% ethanol and two 3-min washes in 100% ethanol. The protocol concluded with two 3-min clearing steps using Medite X-TRA-solv xylene substitute.

Confocal microscopy

Confocal imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM880 upright confocal microscope equipped with a water-immersed W-Plan Apochromat 20× numerical aperture 1.0 objective lens. Euthanized animals were mounted in 1.5% (w/v) low–melting point agarose. For reiterative imaging, a custom-built imaging chamber was used (24). Z-stacks were acquired at 2- to 3-μm intervals. Larger fields of view were obtained through tile scanning, and images were stitched using Imaris Stitcher 9.3 or Zeiss Zen software. Tg(flibow) confocal imaging and lambda stack acquisition were performed as previously described (24).

Time-lapse imaging

Time-lapse recordings of circulating erythrocytes were acquired using a Light Sheet Z.1 microscope (Zeiss Ltd.) equipped with a 10×/0.1 lens and dual pco.edge scientific complementary metal-oxide semiconductor cameras. Larvae were anesthetized, embedded in 1.5% low–melting point agarose, and mounted in glass capillaries. Subsequently, animals were suspended in egg water using the plunger. Images were captured more than 1000 frames within a single Z-stack. Analysis was conducted using Zen Blue and ARIVIS software.

Blood flow velocity quantification

Blood flow velocity in WT and vegfaa+/−;vegfab−/− animals was quantified using optical flow analysis. Videos were acquired at 99.67 frames per second (fps) using an Olympus IX83 inverted microscope equipped with a high-speed charge-coupled device camera and spatially calibrated at 0.324 μm/pixel. Blood vessels were segmented using temporal intensity variance across 100 to 300 frames, with manual verification and refinement when needed.

Flow velocities were computed using the Farnebäck optical flow algorithm (62) implemented in OpenCV (63) following methods validated for zebrafish blood flow analysis (64). The resulting displacement vectors (dx, dy) at each pixel location were converted to velocity vectors (μm/s) using the frame rate (99.67 fps) and spatial calibration (0.324 μm/pixel). Velocity vectors were extracted at 20-pixel intervals within vessel masks.

Image processing

Confocal images were processed offline using the Fiji version of ImageJ (NIH). The images shown in this study are single views, two-dimensional reconstructions of collected z-series stacks. Colocalization thresholds were set manually. Tg(flibow) images were processed using Imaris 9.3 (Bitplane). Each of the 11 channels was given RGB (Red, Green, and Blue) values corresponding to the wavelength collected. We selected six channels, excluding those that were redundant or displayed a poor signal-to-noise ratio, as previously described (24). The H&E images were processed using CaseViewer.

Library preparation, sequencing, and preprocessing of single-cell RNA sequencing data

Plate-based single-cell RNA sequencing libraries were processed using the MARS-seq protocol, as previously described (65). Sequence ready library was sequenced using Illumina NextSeq 500 to reach ∼50,000 reads per cell. We sequenced 669 cells in total. GRCz10 was used for genome alignment. Data preprocessing was carried out following cross-platform single-cell data preprocessing (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-34681-z). Practically, the protocol is padding the R2 reads with “AAAAAAAAA” (awk ‘NR%4==0 {print “IIIIIIIII” substr($0,0,15) “II”}; NR%4==2 {print “AAAAAAAAA” substr($0,0,15) “AA”}; NR%2==1 {print}’). Swapping R1 and R2 filenames and then executing standard Cell Ranger with --r1-length = 26 --chemistry = SC3Pv2Cells. We used the Seurat R package (Seurat ver. 5.0.0) for downstream analysis: Cells with more than 1800 nFeatures (genes) were removed as suspected doublets; cells with less than 100 nFeatures (genes) were removed for low coverage or empty wells. Cells with more than 8% mitochondrial gene content were excluded for low quality. Overall, 643 cells passed quality filters for downstream analysis. Mitochondrial (^mt-) and ribosomal (^rp[sl]|^RP[SL], zgc:158463) genes were removed for downstream analysis.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis was performed using the basic Seurat pipeline. Briefly, the data were normalized using the default parameters. The 2000 most variable genes were detected. Data were scaled, and principal components analysis was performed. The first 25 principal components (PCs) were selected for downstream analysis. Clustering was performed with a resolution of 0.6. Differentially expressed genes were detected using the FindAllMarkers() function and filtered by adjusted P value < 0.05 and average log2 fold change > 0.25.

Statistical analyses

Comparison of two samples was done using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test assuming equal variances from at least three independent experiments unless stated otherwise. Statistical significance for three or more samples was calculated via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, unless stated otherwise. All data are reported as mean values ± SEM and were analyzed using Prism 6 software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).
