Unlocking the potential of bronchoalveolar lavage: a promising supplement therapy for moderate-to-severe allergic asthma
Authors: Minghui Yu, Lu Zhang, Zhigang Pang, Minna Liu, Rou Liu, Qi Yao, Kai Yin, Xiaoxue Chen, Lei Li, Huanping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBronchoalveolar lavage holds potential therapeutic value for moderate-to-severe asthma that is poorly controlled with standard nebulizer therapy. To evaluate the efficacy and safety of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) as an adjunctive therapy in patients with moderate-to-severe allergic asthma inadequately controlled by standard inhaled corticosteroids and long-acting beta₂-agonists therapy.MethodsIn this retrospective study conducted between July 2021 and December 2024, eligible patients were allocated to a control group receiving standard high-pressure nebulizer therapy or a study group receiving additional BAL. Outcomes included changes in asthma symptom scores, pulmonary function, fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) levels, adverse events, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid pathogen distribution.ResultsThe study group demonstrated significantly greater improvement in subjective symptom scores and small airway function parameters compared to controls (P < 0.05). This superiority was particularly pronounced in a predefined subgroup with more severe impairment (FEV1% pred < 50%, FVC% pred < 80%, FEV1/FVC < 65%). No significant differences were observed in FeNO levels or overall adverse event incidence (P > 0.05). BALF analysis in 72 study patients identified 43 distinct pathogens (detection rate: 39.8%), predominantly bacterial species (72.1%).ConclusionAdjunctive BAL significantly improved lung function, especially in small airways, without increasing adverse reactions, demonstrating a favorable safety profile in patients with uncontrolled allergic asthma, supports BAL’s potential as a therapeutic procedure.Keywords: Bronchoscopy, Alveolar lavage, Moderate-to-severe allergic asthma, Pulmonary function, Pathogens

Introduction

Asthma represents a significant global health challenge, affecting approximately 300 million individuals worldwide and imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems [1]. In China, the prevalence of asthma has been rising, reportedly reaching an overall rate of 2.20% as of 2023, with projections suggesting a potential increase to 9.76% by 2050. A trend influenced by factors such as industrialization and lifestyle changes [2]. While the majority of asthma patients can achieve adequate symptom control through standardized stepwise treatment protocols—primarily involving inhaled corticosteroids combined with long-acting beta₂-agonists —a considerable number of individuals with moderate-to-severe disease continues to experience suboptimal control despite receiving high-to-moderate doses of these cornerstone therapies [3]. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) has established important roles in both the diagnosis and management of various respiratory conditions. It has been widely applied in the treatment of various diseases such as severe pulmonary infections, pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, and pneumoconiosis[4–8]. Given the pathophysiological features of moderate-to-severe allergic asthma, BAL has garnered increasing interest as a potential therapeutic intervention for this difficult-to-treat patient population. The therapeutic mechanism is thought to involve the physical clearance of inflammatory mediators, pathogens, and inspissated mucus plugs from the small airways and alveolar spaces. This intervention can lead to improvements in ventilation, alleviation of respiratory symptoms, and enhanced lung function [9–14]. Therefore, this study was designed to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of bronchoalveolar lavage as a therapeutic intervention in this specific patient population. We aim to provide high-quality evidence regarding its potential to improve clinical outcomes, thereby exploring its therapeutic value and offering insights for future clinical management strategies.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study enrolled 357 patients with moderate to severe allergic asthma who exhibited a suboptimal response to conventional inhaled corticosteroids combined with long-acting beta₂-agonists therapy at our hospital between July 2021 and December 2024. Inclusion Criteria: (1). Patients were required to have a physician-confirmed diagnosis of moderate-to-severe allergic asthma, with the severity classification fulfilling the criteria outlined in both “the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Bronchial Allergic Asthma (2020 Edition)” and “the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Allergic Asthma (2019 Edition)” [3, 15].(2) Eligible patients had a documented history of suboptimal asthma control despite regular use of medium to high dose inhaled corticosteroids combined with long-acting beta₂-agonists. Furthermore, all enrolled patients also demonstrated an inadequate clinical response to a subsequent 5-day inpatient nebulization regimen. This standardized regimen consisted of budesonide suspension (1.5 mg), terbutaline sulfate (2.5 mg), and ipratropium bromide (0.25 mg), administered via nebulization three times daily. (3) Patients allocated to the study group were required to meet the indications of the “Guidelines for Diagnostic Flexible Bronchoscopy in Adults (2019 Edition)” [16] and were deemed medically fit to tolerate the BAL procedure. Exclusion Criteria: (1). Patients who were unable to undergo fiberoptic BAL, such as those with congenital heart conditions, heart failure, coagulation issues, anesthesia allergies, or mental illnesses that prevented them from cooperating. (2). Patients were excluded if clinical, laboratory, or imaging evidence suggested an acute respiratory tract infection: (1) the presence of fever (oral temperature > 37.3 °C), (2) peripheral blood leukocyte count > 10 × 10⁹/L or < 4 × 10⁹/L, or (3) chest computed tomography (CT) imaging demonstrating new or progressive pulmonary infiltrates consistent with an infectious process. (3).There were no clinical or general demographic data to back up the study. This study was approved by our Hospital (YXLL-2025-045).

Research methods

Data collection

Medical records of patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were systematically reviewed to extract the following data: (1). General Demographic and Clinical Characteristics: included gender, age, body weight, documented history of respiratory diseases, and smoking history. (2). Clinical examinations: Pulmonary Function Parameters, including: Forced Expiratory Volume in One Second percent predicted (FEV1% pred), Forced Vital Capacity percent predicted (FVC% pred), Ratio of FEV1 to FVC (FEV1/FVC), Maximum Expiratory Flow at 75% of Forced Vital Capacity (MEF75% pred), Maximum Expiratory Flow at 50% of Forced Vital Capacity (MEF50% pred), Maximum Expiratory Flow at 25% of Forced Vital Capacity (MEF25% pred). Exhaled nitric oxide measurement. Improvement rate=(Lung function value after 4 weeks of treatment - Lung function value before treatment)/Lung function value before treatment×100%. (3). Subjective symptom scoring: ①Asthma Control Test: Assessed according to the scoring criteria in the “Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Bronchial Allergic Asthma (2020 Edition)” to evaluate the degree of symptom control [3]. ②Mini Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire: Assessed allergic asthma patients’ quality of life across four domains—physical symptoms, activity limitations, emotional functioning, and environmental triggers [17], which consisting of 15 questions, higher scores indicate better allergic asthma control. ③Rescue Medication Score: It primarily used to assess the frequency and dependence on rescue medications during acute allergic asthma episodes. A score of 0 indicates no rescue medication use; 1 indicates use ≤ 3 times per week; 2 indicates use ≥ 4 times per week. Improvement = Post-treatment score - Pre-treatment score. (4). Adverse Reactions: Record the incidence of adverse reactions in both groups post-treatment for safety comparison. Follow-up data including clinical examination results and subjective evaluations collected 4 weeks after treatment.

Patient screening and selection followed a multi-step process: (1) Initial identification of potential participants from the hospital’s electronic medical record system using diagnostic codes for moderate-to-severe asthma; (2) Manual chart review by two independent researchers to confirm eligibility against all inclusion criteria; (3) Application of exclusion criteria through structured data extraction; (4) Final exclusion of cases with incomplete follow-up data or missing key variables required for the analysis.

Grouping method

This study enrolled patients with moderate to severe allergic asthma that was refractory to conventional high-dose inhaled corticosteroids combined with long-acting beta₂-agonists therapy. Participants were stratified into two groups according to the treatment methods. The control group: After five days of nebulization treatment, the patients indicated that their symptoms had not improved considerably, but they insisted on continuing this therapy. The study group: Patients who did not respond well to nebulization therapy were also treated with bronchoalveolar lavage. BAL was performed under moderate sedation. A flexible bronchoscope was advanced under direct visualization and gently wedged into a subsegmental bronchus, preferentially in the middle lobe or lingula. Topical airway anesthesia was achieved with 2% lidocaine. Sterile, warmed 0.9% saline was instilled in three sequential aliquots. Each aliquot ranged from 20 to 60 mL (total instilled volume: 60–180 mL) and was immediately and gently aspirated after each instillation using controlled wall suction. Particular care was taken under bronchoscopic vision to avoid significant airway collapse during aspiration, especially when addressing visible mucus plugs. The pooled fluid was processed for subsequent analysis.

Statistical methods

For statistical analysis, SPSS 25.0 software was used. Measures that were not normally distributed were expressed as median (interquartile spacing) [M(P25, P75)], whereas those that were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (±S). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test; categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. For the primary and secondary outcomes, between-group differences are reported with 95% confidence intervals. Given the exploratory nature of this study, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied; p-values for secondary endpoints should therefore be interpreted with caution. Missing data for Fractional exhaled Nitric Oxide (FeNO) were handled by complete-case analysis, and this limitation is explicitly acknowledged. The subgroup analyses (e.g., by baseline FEV1% predicted < 50%, FVC% predicted < 80%, FEV1/FVC ratio < 65%) were exploratory and conducted post-hoc, based on initial observations in the overall cohort. They were not pre-specified in the original study protocol. As these analyses involved multiple comparisons without adjustment, the associated p-values should be interpreted with caution due to an increased risk of Type I error.

Results

General information

The analysis included 357 patients with moderate-to-severe allergic asthma, who were categorized into a study group (n = 181, 50.7%) and a control group (n = 176, 49.3%). The baseline characteristics of the entire study population are presented in Table 1 (Comparison of baseline data between the control group and the study group). The mean age and weight for the cohort were 47.1 ± 13.6 years and 65.6 ± 10.8 kg, respectively. The population consisted of 170 (47.6%) males and 187 (52.8%) females. Furthermore, 97 patients (27.2%) reported a smoking history, and 120 (33.6%) had a history of respiratory diseases. Importantly, the two groups were well-balanced at baseline, as no significant differences were detected in age, weight, gender distribution, smoking history, or history of respiratory diseases (P > 0.05), supporting the validity of subsequent intergroup comparisons.

Subgroup efficacy comparison

This study compared the efficacy of the study groups versus the control groups in improving FEV₁% pred, FVC% pred, and the FEV₁/FVC. The results indicated that the improvements in these three pulmonary function parameters were not statistically significant between the two groups (P > 0.05). To account for potential underlying heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses to evaluate whether the treatment response differed between the study and control groups across subgroups defined by specific large-airway function parameters baseline, with the aim of identifying patient subgroups that may derive greater benefit from the intervention.

To assess the treatment response in patients with more severely impaired lung function at baseline, we performed a subgroup analysis that specifically included subjects meeting the following criteria: FEV₁% pred < 50%, FVC% pred < 80%, and FEV₁/FVC < 65%. The baseline characteristics were well-balanced among the resulting subgroups. Following therapeutic bronchoalveolar lavage, the subgroups of patients with these specific functional impairments demonstrated significantly greater improvements in FEV₁% pred, FVC% pred, and the FEV₁/FVC compared to the control group receiving conventional care, and these differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05). (Figure 4: Comparison of fractional exhaled nitric oxide, subgroup efficacy and safety between the control group and the study group).

Safety comparison

The incidence of adverse reactions was 11.36% (20/176) in the control group and 13.26% (24/181) in the study group. Common adverse reactions observed in both groups included hoarseness, palpitations, hand tremors, nausea/vomiting, and worsening cough. Additionally, two cases of blood-streaked sputum (1.1%) were reported specifically in the study group. Although the incidence of adverse reactions was marginally higher in the study group compared to the control group, the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). These findings suggest that while providing symptomatic improvement, alveolar lavage maintains a relatively safe therapeutic profile, further supporting its feasibility and safety in clinical practice. (Figure 4: Comparison of fractional exhaled nitric oxide, subgroup efficacy and safety between the control group and the study group)

Pathogen distribution

In the study group, 72 patients (72/181, 39.8%) underwent high-throughput sequencing of their alveolar lavage fluid, and 43 distinct types were found. Of these, 95 cases (95/137, 69.3%) were bacterial, 32 cases (32/137, 23.4%) were viral, and 10 cases (10/137, 7.3%) were fungal, including several pathogens that are difficult to identify with conventional culture methods. Antibiotic use

Upon further clinical evaluation, antibiotic therapy was used to patients with positive pathogen findings on high-throughput sequencing. Subsequent analysis revealed that the improvement in pulmonary function was comparable between the treated and untreated groups, with no statistically significant difference observed (P > 0.05).

Discussion

Mechanism of action and safety of airway lavage fluid

In patients with moderate-to-severe allergic asthma, airway remodeling represents a pivotal pathological hallmark. Current evidence indicates that glucocorticoids exhibit limited efficacy in reversing established airway remodeling [18], and glucocorticoid sensitivity may further decline with disease progression [19]. Concurrently, the accumulation of airway secretions significantly impedes the recovery of pulmonary function. Excessive mucus production leads to its retention in small airways [20], resulting in the formation of gelatinous mucus plugs that cause persistent airway obstruction [21]. This pathological mucus is typically highly viscous, rendering it resistant to clearance even after standard nebulization therapy. Moreover, allergic asthma is associated with impaired ciliary function in the airway epithelium, which compromises the mucociliary clearance capacity [22]. Consequently, the physiological transport of mucus toward the pharynx is disrupted, prolonging mucus retention and exacerbating the formation of obstructive plugs [23].

BAL facilitates the rapid removal of airway secretions through irrigation with fluid and subsequent negative pressure suction. When combined with nebulized medications aimed at controlling airway inflammation and dilating bronchioles, this technique can produce enhanced therapeutic outcomes. The findings of this study indicate that alveolar lavage significantly improves small airway function in patients. Furthermore, the procedure aids in clearing inflammatory factors and allergens from the airways, which may help delay the progression of airway inflammation. Supportively, multiple domestic and international studies have established that bacterial colonization contributes not only to the pathogenesis of allergic asthma but also to an increase in disease severity [24]. BAL can successfully restrict pathogen colonization by directly eliminating certain pathogens, altering the local microenvironment, and increasing the local action of medications, resulting in a supplemental therapeutic benefit.

BAL is not currently included in standard domestic and international guidelines for the management of allergic asthma. However, findings from this study indicate its potential therapeutic value. Due to the airway hyperresponsiveness in asthma patients, bronchoscopy may trigger acute attacks—yet in this study, the procedure demonstrated satisfactory safety characteristics. Under the guidance of operators possessing over five years of experience, and with the support of continuous patient monitoring and comprehensive resuscitation protocols, all 181 enrolled patients successfully underwent and tolerated the BAL procedure. Beyond the transient discomfort commonly associated with nebulized inhalation, no severe adverse events—such as pneumothorax, tracheal perforation, atelectasis, or major hemorrhage—were recorded. Only two cases of minor and self-limiting blood-tinged sputum were reported post-operatively. Importantly, both objective pulmonary function parameters and subjective symptom scores showed significant improvement following the intervention. In summary, bronchoalveolar lavage may be considered represent a safe, feasible, and potentially effective adjunctive intervention for patients with allergic asthma who exhibit a suboptimal response to conventional inhaled therapies.

BALF high-throughput pathogen detection

In this study, pathogen high-throughput sequencing of BALF was completed for 72 patients (72/181, 39.8%) in the research study. Results demonstrated that among the bacteria detected by next-generation sequencing (NGS) in BALF, 31 species exhibited the highest detection rate (31/43, 72.1%). The most prevalent species were Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus aureus. This finding is consistent with numerous domestic and international studies, further supporting the role of specific bacteria as key pathogens in exacerbating chronic airway inflammation. Furthermore, NGS demonstrated the capability to detect pathogens that are difficult to culture using conventional methods, as it is not constrained by their growth requirements [25]. Owing to fastidious growth requirements or the inability to proliferate on standard culture media, these pathogens are frequently undetected by traditional methods, potentially leading to misdiagnosis or underdiagnosis, such as Tropheryma whipplei, Mycobacterium tortuosum, Pneumocystis jirovecii, Moraxella catarrhalis, and Ureaplasma species. Bacterial infection and colonization are implicated not only in the pathogenesis of allergic asthma but also in the exacerbation of disease severity [26]. Specific airway microbiota, such as Streptococcus, Haemophilus, and Moraxellaspecies, are associated with an increased risk of allergic asthma [27, 28]. BAL may physically eliminate pathogens that trigger local allergic reactions within the airways, thereby creating favorable conditions for the restoration of lung function. A limitation of the present study is the absence of long-term pulmonary function follow-up data. Future investigations should incorporate extended observation periods to systematically compare differences in patient outcomes. Additionally, the potential association between declining lung function and airway pathogen colonization or infection warrants further exploration.

Study limitations and results analysis

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the most important limitation of this study is its non-randomized, retrospective design. Participant allocation to the BAL or control group was not random but was based on a combination of clinical judgment and patient preference after detailed consultation. This method of treatment assignment inherently introduces a high risk of selection and indication bias. Consequently, patients who underwent BAL may have systematically differed from those in the control group in ways not captured or fully adjusted for by the reported baseline characteristics (e.g., disease severity perception, engagement with specialized care, or specific symptomatic profiles). These unmeasured confounding factors fundamentally limit the strength of causal inference that can be drawn regarding the efficacy of BAL. While we employed statistical adjustments where possible, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, not as evidence of causal therapeutic effect. The findings underscore the necessity for a prospective, randomized controlled trial to conclusively determine the efficacy and safety of therapeutic BAL in this patient population.

And the retrospective design constrained our data collection, resulting in the absence of several potentially important clinical variables. We lacked systematic data on inflammatory phenotypes (e.g., blood eosinophil counts), detailed history of exacerbations (frequency, severity), exposure to systemic corticosteroids, and the use of biologic therapies. The absence of this information limits the clinical characterization of our cohort, may obscure relevant effect modifiers, and restricts the generalizability of our findings. For instance, we cannot determine if the observed response to BAL varied by inflammatory endotype or concurrent use of advanced therapies. Future prospective studies must incorporate these critical parameters to allow for more precise patient stratification and outcome interpretation.

While this study observed post-intervention improvements in specific small airway indices (MEF75% perd, MEF50% perd, MEF25% perd) in the BAL group relative to controls, it is crucial to interpret this finding within its clinical context. The absence of a concurrent, significant between-group difference in core spirometric parameters—namely FEV1% pred, FVC% pred, and the FEV1/FVC ratio—suggests that the overall impact of a single BAL procedure on global ventilatory function, as measured by standard clinical metrics, may be limited. The physiological significance of isolated changes in small airway parameters, which can exhibit greater variability, remains uncertain. Therefore, these results should not be construed as evidence for a substantial or transformative effect of BAL on major asthma-related airflow limitation. Several mechanistic and methodological factors may explain this discrepancy. The intervention’s primary mechanism is likely the physical displacement of mucus plugs, which might preferentially benefit more distal, small airways. In contrast, a lack of effect on FEV1 could indicate that large airway physiology, potentially influenced by fixed architectural remodeling or sustained hyperreactivity, is not meaningfully altered by mechanical clearance alone. Furthermore, the short follow-up period may be insufficient to capture subsequent changes in larger airway function. The exploratory nature of these findings, alongside limitations in sample size and study design, necessitates that they be considered hypothesis-generating. They underscore the need for cautious interpretation and highlight that the clinical relevance of BAL therapy cannot be established from these data alone. Future prospective studies with longer follow-up and patient-centered outcomes (e.g., exacerbation reduction) are essential to determine if improvements in small airway mechanics translate to meaningful clinical benefit.

Furthermore, the subgroup analyses presented are exploratory and were conducted post-hoc. The thresholds used (e.g., FEV1% < 50%) were not pre-defined, and the analyses were not adjusted for multiple testing. Therefore, the signals suggesting a greater response to BAL in patients with more severely impaired baseline lung function are purely hypothesis-generating. These observations are susceptible to both chance findings and residual confounding, and they require prospective validation in appropriately designed studies before any clinical implications can be considered.

Another finding of this study is the absence of a significant between-group difference in the reduction of Fractional exhaled Nitric Oxide (FeNO) levels, despite observed improvements in small airway function in the BAL group. This dissociation is critical for interpreting the mechanism of action. While FeNO is a recognized biomarker of type 2 (T2) airway inflammation, the lack of a differential reduction suggests that the therapeutic benefit of a single BAL procedure may not be primarily mediated through the suppression of this specific inflammatory pathway. Several explanations for this discrepancy warrant consideration. First, the intervention’s principal effect may be mechanical, involving the physical clearance of mucus plugs, cellular debris, and inflammatory mediators from the airway lumen, thereby improving patency and airflow without directly altering the underlying inflammatory endotype as measured by a systemic biomarker. Second, BAL could potentially influence non-T2 or neutrophilic inflammatory components not reflected by FeNO. Third, the compartmentalized nature of inflammation and mucus plugging may not be fully captured by a global exhaled biomarker, suggesting that local improvements might occur without a significant change in the overall exhaled signal. While technical variability and the limited sample size may also contribute to the null finding, the consistency of the physiological improvement alongside a stable biomarker profile directs focus towards a mechanical rather than a broad anti-inflammatory mechanism. This hypothesis requires direct testing in future studies incorporating a broader panel of local and systemic inflammatory markers [29].

The pathogen profiles identified via BALF NGS in this study describe the presence of microbial communities in the lower airways of this symptomatic cohort. It is crucial to emphasize that the detection of microbial nucleic acids by this highly sensitive technique does not equate to a diagnosis of clinically active infection, a state which was an exclusion criterion for this study. The findings more likely represent airway colonization or the presence of a dysbiotic microbiome. Consequently, these data should not be interpreted as supporting routine or targeted antibiotic therapy, as no causal relationship between the detected microorganisms and clinical worsening was established. The primary value of this exploratory analysis is to characterize the lower airway microenvironment in patients with poorly controlled, moderate-to-severe allergic asthma. The patterns observed generate the hypothesis that an altered airway microbiome or persistent colonizing flora may be a contributing factor to disease chronicity or symptomatic burden in a subset of patients, potentially via immune modulation or local irritant effects. Confirming the clinical relevance of these findings and any potential therapeutic implications requires future prospective studies that integrate quantitative culture, local host immune response profiling, and longitudinal clinical data with NGS results.

Another significant limitation of this study is the four-week follow-up period, which is notably short for evaluating an intervention in a chronic inflammatory airway disease. This timeframe precludes assessment of the durability of the observed changes in lung function and symptom scores, and it does not allow for the evaluation of patient-centered outcomes that are critical in asthma management, such as exacerbation frequency, hospitalization risk, or the reduction in oral corticosteroid use. Consequently, this study provides no evidence regarding the potential for BAL therapy to modify the long-term disease course or impact clinically significant events. The findings are restricted to describing short-term physiologic and symptomatic changes, and they should not be extrapolated to infer any sustained or disease-modifying effect. Future studies with extended follow-up periods of at least 6–12 months are essential to determine whether the immediate benefits of BAL translate into meaningful, long-term clinical advantages.

The use of nonparametric tests in this study, while appropriate given the skewed distribution of lung function data from patients with moderate-to-severe allergic asthma, may have resulted in a reduction in statistical power compared to parametric alternatives. This inherent characteristic of nonparametric tests means a larger sample size would be required to detect an effect of the same magnitude with equal probability. Future research may attempt to enhance test efficacy by increasing sample size or employing more sophisticated data transformation methods, thereby enabling more accurate assessment of treatment effects and differences.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this retrospective, observational study provides preliminary evidence that therapeutic bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was associated with short-term improvements in selected small airway parameters and patient-reported symptoms in individuals with moderate-to-severe allergic asthma, with a favorable safety profile. The concurrent use of BAL fluid for NGS analysis also offers a novel descriptive window into the lower airway microbiome of this population. However, the absence of a significant effect on core spirometric measures (FEV1) and inflammatory biomarkers (FeNO) suggests a potential mechanism limited to mechanical clearance rather than broad anti-inflammatory action.

It is crucial to emphasize that, given the study’s significant methodological limitations—primarily its non-randomized design, risk of selection bias, and short follow-up—these findings must be interpreted strictly as hypothesis-generating. They do not establish efficacy and should not be construed as supporting a change to current standard asthma management, for which BAL holds no established therapeutic role. The primary value of this work lies in justifying and informing the design of a prospective, randomized controlled trial, which is necessary to definitively assess the efficacy, safety, and potential mechanistic role of BAL as a supplemental intervention in severe asthma.
