Asymptotic behavior of the variance of BLUE for the mean of stationary processes
Mamikon S. Ginovyan

Abstract
In this paper, we survey results on the asymptotic behavior of the variance of the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) for the mean of stationary processes. This behavior is influenced by the regularity and memory structures of the observed models. The results show that the asymptotic behavior of the variance of the BLUE is determined solely by the behavior of the spectrum near the origin. For nondeterministic models, the variance of the BLUE exhibits hyperbolic behavior, similar to the power function, while for purely deterministic models, the variance decreases at an exponential rate. Specifically, a necessary condition for the variance of the BLUE to approach zero exponentially is that the spectral density of the model vanishes on a set of positive Lebesgue measure in any neighborhood of zero. We also present results on the asymptotic efficiency of various unbiased linear estimators in comparison to the BLUE.



Keywords.
Best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE); stationary processes; spectral density; deterministic and nondeterministic models; asymptotic efficiency; memory; singularity.

1
Introduction

1.1
The problem

Consider the following stochastic model:

X(t)=m+Y(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{Z}:=\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots\},

(1.1)

where
m
is the constant unknown mean of
X(t)
, and the noise
Y(t)

is assumed to be a zero-mean, wide-sense stationary process with a spectral
density function
f(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in{\Lambda}:=[-\pi,\pi]

and a covariance function
r(t)
:

r(t)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{it\lambda}f(\lambda)d\lambda,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{Z}.

(1.2)

The problem of interest is estimation of the unknown mean
m
by unbiased
linear estimators
\widehat{m}_{n}
, based on a random sample
\{X(t),t=0,1,\ldots,n\}
:

\widehat{m}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}X(k),\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}=1,

(1.3)

where the condition
\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}=1
is needed for unbiasedness of
\widehat{m}_{n}
.

Of particular interest is the
best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE)
,
denoted by

\widehat{m}_{BLU}:=\widehat{m}_{n,BLU}
, i.e., the estimator of the form (
1.3
),
where the weights
c_{k}
,
k=0,1,\ldots,n
, are chosen so that the variance

\mathrm{Var}\left(\widehat{m}_{n}\right)=E|\widehat{m}_{n}-m|^{2}=\sum_{j,k=0}^{n}c_{j}\bar{c}_{k}r(j-k)

(1.4)

is minimal under the condition
\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}=1
.

1.2
Existence and uniqueness of the BLUE

We assume that the process
Y(t)
is non-degenerate, that is,

E|Y(t)|^{2}=r(0)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\lambda)d\lambda>0,

which, in view of (
1.2
), implies that the covariance matrix

R_{n}=\{r(j-k);\,j,k=1,2,\cdots,n\}

(1.5)

of
\{Y(t),t=0,1,\ldots,n\}

is positive definite. Then, as is well known (see, e.g., Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
, Section 11.1), the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
of the unknown mean
m
exists and has a unique representation of the form (
1.3
).
Specifically, the row vector c of coefficients in
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
is given by

c=(c_{0}\cdots c_{n})=(\mathbf{1}^{T}R_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{1})^{-1}\mathbf{1}^{T}R_{n}^{-1},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{1}^{T}=(1,\ldots,1),

(1.6)

and the estimator
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
is given by

\widehat{m}_{BLU}=(\mathbf{1}^{T}R_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{1})^{-1}\mathbf{1}^{T}R_{n}^{-1}X^{T},\hskip 10.00002ptX^{T}=(X(0),\cdots,X(n)),

(1.7)

with

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=(\mathbf{1}^{T}R_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{1})^{-1}>0.

(1.8)

Although formulas (
1.6
) – (
1.8
) provide explicit expressions for
c
,
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
,
and
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
, their practical value is moderate. This is primarily because they involve the inverse of the covariance matrix
R_{n}
, which may not be known in practice. Moreover, even when it is known, inverting it for high dimensions can lead to cumbersome computational challenges. Therefore, adequate approximations are necessary.

1.3
Efficiency of estimators

Let
\widehat{m}
be an unbiased linear estimator of the unknown mean
m
of
a process
X(t)
possessing a spectral density
f
.
The efficiency of the estimator
\widehat{m}
is defined as

e(n,\widehat{m},f):=\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})}{Var_{f}(\widehat{m})},

(1.9)

where
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
is the BLUE for the unknown
m
.
The asymptotic efficiency of
\widehat{m}
is defined as

e(\infty,\widehat{m},f):=\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}}e(n,f).

(1.10)

Definition 1.1
.

An estimator
\widehat{m}
of
m
is said to be efficient (or optimal) if
e(n,\widehat{m},f)=1
.
It is termed asymptotically efficient if
e(\infty,\widehat{m},f)=1
.

In this paper, we provide a survey of both established and recent findings related to the following two problems:

(a)

Describe the asymptotic behavior of the variance

\mathrm{Var}\left(\widehat{m}_{n,BLU}\right)
as
n\to\infty
for stationary models with various regularity and memory structures.

(b)

Analyze the asymptotic efficiency of various unbiased linear estimators relative to the BLUE.

The asymptotic behavior of the variance of BLUE is influenced by the
regularity and memory structure of the observed models.
The results indicate that this asymptotic behavior
is determined solely by the behavior of the spectral density near the origin.
For nondeterministic models, the variance of the BLUE exhibits
hyperbolic behavior (like a power). In contrast, for purely deterministic models, the variance decreases at an exponential rate.
Specifically, a necessary condition for the variance of the BLUE to approach zero
exponentially is that the spectral density of the model vanishes on a set of positive
Lebesgue measure in any neighborhood of zero.

Analyzing the asymptotic efficiency of various unbiased linear estimators relative to the BLUE for nondeterministic models, we observe that when the model has short memory
and a piecewise continuous spectral density without discontinuities at
\lambda=0
, the least squares estimator is asymptotically efficient.
However, if the spectral densities do not meet these conditions, the loss of efficiency can be significant.

1.4
A brief history

The question of the asymptotic behavior of the variance of BLUE and the asymptotic efficiency
of the least squares estimator
\widehat{m}_{LS}
, relative to the best linear unbiased
estimator
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
for short-memory nondeterministic models, traces back to the papers
by Grenander
[

28

29

[

[

Grenander demonstrated that if the model exhibits short memory

Since then, various authors have explored these questions for models with different memory structures. Vitale
[
61
]
and Grenander
[
30

analyzed certain classes of anti-persistent models.
In particular, it was shown that if the spectral density
f(\lambda)
is positive

\lambda=0
, then the asymptotic efficiency of
the least squares estimator is zero.

The asymptotic behavior of the variance of BLUE and the asymptotic efficiency
of the least squares estimator
\widehat{m}_{LS}
, relative to the best linear unbiased
estimator
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
for long-memory nondeterministic models, have been
considered in the papers by Adenstedt
[
1
]
, Adenstedt and Eisenberg
[
2
]
,
Beran
[
7
]
, Beran and Künsch
[
9
]
, Samarov and Taqqu
[
52
]
, and
Yajima
[
62
]
.
In these papers, for certain classes of spectral densities satisfying the condition

f(\lambda)\sim\lambda^{\nu}L(\lambda)
(
\nu>-1
) as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, where
L(\lambda)
is a slowly varying function at the origin with
0<L(0)<\infty
,
it was shown that the variance of BLUE decreases hyperbolically. Specifically,

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})\sim n^{-\nu-1}\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\to\infty.

These classes include ARFIMA
(p,d,q)
models with parameter
d<{1}/{2}
and fractional Gaussian noise with parameter
0<H<1
.

Estimating the mean for processes characterized by the spectral function,
applicable to both discrete and continuous time models, has been discussed by Adenstedt and Eisenberg
[
2
]
and Grenander
[
30
]
.
Parzen
[
46
]
provides a general Hilbert space approach to linear estimation problems (see also Adenstedt and Eisenberg
[
2
]
).

The concept of pseudo-best estimators for general regression models
was introduced by Yu. Rozanov (see Ibragimov and Rozanov
[
36
]
, Section 7.3). Pseudo-best estimators of the mean for anti-persistent and long memory deterministic models were discussed in the papers by
Kholevo
[
37
]
, Rasulov
[
49
]
, and Rasulov and Kholevo
[
50
]
.

The asymptotic behavior of the variance of the BLUE for deterministic (singular) models
has been studied by Babayan and Ginovyan
[
3
,
4
]
. They established the necessary and sufficient conditions for the variance of the BLUE to decrease exponentially. Specifically, it was demonstrated that a key condition for the variance of the BLUE to approach zero exponentially is that the spectral density of the model must vanish on a set of positive Lebesgue measure in any neighborhood of zero.

2.1
Second-order (wide-sense) stationary processes

Let
\{X(t),\ t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
be a centered real-valued second-order
stationary process defined on a probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
with covariance function
r(t)
. This means that

{\mathbb{E}}[X(t)]=0,\hskip 10.00002ptr(t)={\mathbb{E}}[X(t+s)X(s)],\hskip 10.00002pts,t\in\mathbb{Z},

where
{\mathbb{E}}[\cdot]
denotes for the expectation operator with respect
to the measure
\mathbb{P}
.

By the Herglotz theorem (see, e.g., Brockwell and Davis
[
12
]
,
p. 117-118),
there exists a finite measure
\mu
on
\Lambda
such that
the covariance function
r(t)

has the following
spectral representation
:

r(t)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{it\lambda}d\mu(\lambda),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{Z}.

(2.1)

The measure
\mu
in (
2.1
) is called the
spectral measure
of the
process
X(t)
.
The function
F(\lambda):=\mu\{(-\infty,\lambda]\}
is referred to as the
spectral function

of the process
X(t)
.
If
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure,
then the function
f(\lambda):=d\mu(\lambda)/d\lambda
is called the
spectral density
of
X(t)
.
We assume that
X(t)
is a
non-degenerate
process, meaning that

{\rm Var}[X(0)]:={\mathbb{E}}|X(0)|^{2}=r(0)>0
.
Without loss of generality, we can take
r(0)=1
.
To avoid trivial cases, we also assume that the spectral measure

\mu
is
non-trivial
, meaning that
\mu
has infinite support.

Notice that if the spectral density
f(\lambda)
exists, then

f(\lambda)\geq 0
,
f(\lambda)\in L^{1}(\Lambda)
, and equation (
2.1
) becomes

r(t)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{it\lambda}f(\lambda)d\lambda,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{Z}.

(2.2)

Thus, the covariance function
r(t)
and the spectral
function
F(\lambda)
(or the spectral density function
f(\lambda)
)
provide equivalent specifications of the second-order properties for a stationary process

\{X(t),\ t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The parametrization of the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
using the formula

establishes a bijection between

\mathbb{T}
and the interval
[-\pi,\pi)
.
Through this bijection, the measure
\mu
on
\Lambda
generates a corresponding measure on the unit circle

\mathbb{T}
, which we also denote by
\mu
.
Therefore, depending on the context, the measure
\mu
may be supported either on
\Lambda
or on
\mathbb{T}
, so we will identify integrals over both
\Lambda
and
\mathbb{T}
.

We utilize the standard Lebesgue decomposition of the measure
\mu
:

d\mu(\lambda)=d\mu_{a}(\lambda)+d\mu_{s}(\lambda)=f(\lambda)d\lambda+d\mu_{s}(\lambda),

(2.3)

where
\mu_{a}
is the absolutely continuous part of
\mu

(with respect to the Lebesgue measure)
and
\mu_{s}
is the singular part of
\mu
, which is the sum
of the discrete (pure point) and continuous singular components of
\mu
.
A similar decomposition holds for the spectral function

:

dF(\lambda)=f(\lambda)d\lambda+dF_{s}(\lambda).

(2.4)

By the well-known Cramér theorem (see, e.g., Cramér and Leadbetter
[
14
]
), for any stationary process
\{X(t),\,t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with spectral

there exists an orthogonal stochastic measure
Z=Z(B)
,

B\in\mathfrak{B}(\Lambda)
, such that for every

the process

has the following

X(t)=\int_{\Lambda}e^{it\lambda}dZ(\lambda),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{Z}.

(2.5)

Moreover,
{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z(B)|^{2}\right]=\mu(B)
for every
B\in\mathfrak{B}(\Lambda)
.
Here,

denotes the Borel

.
For the definition and properties of orthogonal stochastic measures and the stochastic integral in (

), we refer to Cramér and Leadbetter
[

]
, Ginovyan
[

]
, and Ibragimov and Linnik

]

We will consider stationary processes that possess spectral density

]
, Doob

]

]

Theorem 2.1
.

The following assertions hold.

(a)

The spectral function

of a stationary process
\{X(t),\,t\in\mathbb{Z}\}

is absolutely continuous (with respect to the Lebesgue measure), meaning that
F(\lambda)=\int_{-\pi}^{\lambda}f(x)dx

if and only if it can be represented as an infinite moving average:

where

(2.8)

(c)

2.3
Dependence (memory) structure of the model

Depending on the memory (dependence) structure, we will distinguish
the following types of stationary models:

(a) short memory (or short-range dependent),

(b) long memory (or long-range dependent),

(c) intermediate memory (or anti-persistent).

The memory structure of a stationary process is essentially a
measure of the dependence among all the variables in the process,
considering the effects of all correlations simultaneously.
Traditionally, memory structure has been defined in the time domain
in terms of the decay rates of autocorrelations or, in the
frequency domain, in terms of the rates of explosion of low-frequency spectra
(see, e.g., Beran et al.
[
8
]
, Ginovyan
[
22
]
, and references therein).

Note that the definitions of memory in both the time and frequency domains are generally not equivalent (see Proposition
9.9
for details).

It is convenient to characterize the memory structure in terms
of the spectral density function.

2.3.1
Short memory models

A stationary process
\{X(t),\,t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with a spectral
density function
f(\lambda)
is referred to as a
short memory
process if the spectral density
f(\lambda)
is bounded away from both zero and infinity. Specifically, there exist constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that

0<C_{1}\leq f(\lambda)\leq C_{2}<\infty.

A typical example of a short memory model is the stationary
Autoregressive Moving Average (ARMA)
(p,q)
process
X(t)
,
defined as a stationary solution to the following difference equation:

\psi_{p}(B)X(t)=\theta_{q}(B)\varepsilon(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{Z},

where
\psi_{p}
and
\theta_{q}
are polynomials of degrees
p
and
q
, respectively, with no zeros on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
,

B
is the backshift operator defined by
BX(t)=X(t-1)
,
and
\{\varepsilon(t),t\in\mathbb{Z}\}\sim
WN(0,1) is standard white noise.
The spectral density
f(\lambda)
is a rational function
(see, e.g., Brockwell and Davis
[
12
]
, Section 3.1):

f(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{|\theta_{q}(e^{i\lambda})|^{2}}{|\psi_{p}(e^{i\lambda})|^{2}}.

(2.9)

2.3.2
Long-memory and anti-persistent models

A
long-memory
model is defined as a
stationary process with
unbounded
spectral density,
while an
anti-persistent
model is a stationary
process with
vanishing
spectral density at some fixed
points (see, e.g., Beran et al.
[
8
]
, Brockwell and Davis
[
12
]
, Ginovyan
[
22
]
, and references therein).

A typical model that exhibits long memory and intermediate
memory (i.e., is anti-persistent) is the Autoregressive Fractionally Integrated Moving Average (ARFIMA)
(p,d,q)
process
X(t)
.
This process is defined as a stationary solution to the following
difference equation (see, e.g., Brockwell and Davis
[
12
]
, Section 13.2):

\psi_{p}(B)(1-B)^{d}X(t)=\theta_{q}(B)\varepsilon(t),\hskip 10.00002ptd<1/2,

where
B
is the backshift operator,
\varepsilon(t)

is standard white noise, and
\psi_{p}
and
\theta_{q}
are
polynomials of
degrees
p
and
q
, respectively.
The spectral density
f_{X}(\lambda)
of
X(t)
is given by

f_{X}(\lambda)=|1-e^{-i\lambda}|^{-2d}f(\lambda)=(2\sin(\lambda/2))^{-2d}f_{ARMA}(\lambda),\hskip 10.00002ptd<1/2,

(2.10)

where
f_{ARMA}(\lambda)
is the spectral density of an ARMA
(p,q)
process, as given by equation (
2.9
). The condition
d<1/2
ensures that

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\lambda)d\lambda<\infty
,
implying that the process
X(t)
is well-defined because

{\mathbb{E}}|X(t)|^{2}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\lambda)d\lambda.

By (
2.10
), we have

f(\lambda)\thicksim(2\pi)^{-1}|\lambda|^{-2d}\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\to 0.

(2.11)

Observe that for
0<d<1/2
the model
X(t)
specified by the spectral density (
2.10
) exhibits long memory, indicating that
f(\lambda)
blows up at
\lambda=0
like a power function, which is characteristic of long memory models.
For
d<0
, the model
X(t)
exhibits intermediate memory, where the spectral density in (
2.10
)
vanishes at
\lambda=0
. For
d=0
, the model
X(t)
displays short memory.
For
d\geq 1/2
, the function
f_{X}(\lambda)
in (
2.10
) is not integrable,
and therefore cannot represent the spectral density of a stationary process.

Another important discrete time long memory model is the fractional
Gaussian noise (fGn), characterized by the
Hurst index

H
, where
0<H\leq 1
.
The fGn is a discrete time centered Gaussian stationary process
X(k)
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and it has a
spectral density function:

f(\lambda)=c\,|1-e^{-i\lambda}|^{2}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}|\lambda+2\pi k|^{-(2H+1)},\hskip 10.00002pt-\pi\leq\lambda\leq\pi,

(2.12)

where
c
is a positive constant.

It follows from (
2.12
) that

f(\lambda)\thicksim|\lambda|^{1-2H}\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\to 0,

(2.13)

which indicates that
f(\lambda)
blows up if
H>1/2
and approaches zero if
H<1/2
.
Furthermore, by comparing (
2.11
) and (
2.13
), we observe that
the spectral density of fGn exhibits the same
behavior at the origin as ARFIMA
(0,d,0)
, with the memory parameter
d=H-1/2.

Thus, the fGn
\{X(k),k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
exhibits long memory when
1/2<H<1
and is anti-persistent when
0<H<1/2
. The variables
X(k)
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, are independent when
H=1/2
. For more details, we refer to Ginovyan
[
22
]
, and Samorodnisky and Taqqu
[
52
]
.

Another notable example of a process that exhibits long memory or anti-persistence is the Fisher-Hartwig model, also referred to as the Jacobian model.
We define a Fisher–Hartwig model as a stationary process
X(t)
whose spectral density
f(\lambda)
has the following form:

f(\lambda)=f_{1}(\lambda)\prod_{k=1}^{m}|e^{i\lambda}-e^{i\lambda_{k}}|^{2\alpha_{k}},\hskip 10.00002pt-\pi\leq\lambda\leq\pi,

(2.14)

where
f_{1}(\lambda)
is the spectral density of a short memory process,
the points
\lambda_{k}\in[-\pi,\pi]
are distinct, and the exponents
\alpha_{k}>-1/2
,
k=1,\ldots,m
.

Note that for
k=1,\alpha_{1}=-d
, and
f_{1}(\lambda)=f_{ARMA}(\lambda)
,
the Fisher–Hartwig model becomes ARFIMA
(p,d,q)
model.

2.4
Deterministic and nondeterministic processes

In this section, we present Kolmogorov’s isometric isomorphism theorem and provide frequency-domain characterizations of deterministic (singular), nondeterministic, and purely nondeterministic (regular) processes.

2.4.1
Kolmogorov’s isometric isomorphism theorem

Given a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, we define the
L^{2}
-space

L^{2}(\mathbb{P}):=L^{2}(\mathbb{P},\Lambda)
of real-valued random variables
\xi=\xi(\omega)
with

\mathbb{E}[\xi]=0
:

L^{2}(\mathbb{P}):=\left\{\xi:\,||\xi||^{2}:=\int_{\Omega}|\xi(\omega)|^{2}dP(\omega)<\infty\right\}.

(2.15)

Then
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
becomes a Hilbert space with the following
inner product: for
\xi,\eta\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
,

(\xi,\eta)=\mathbb{E}[\xi\eta]=\int_{\Omega}\xi(\omega){\eta(\omega)}dP(\omega).

(2.16)

For
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
-\infty\leq a\leq b\leq\infty,

we define the space
H_{a}^{b}(X)
as the closed linear
subspace of the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
spanned by the random
variables
X(t)=X(t,\omega)
,
t\in[a,b]
,
\omega\in\Omega
:

H_{a}^{b}(X):=\overline{sp}\{X(t),\,\,a\leq t\leq b\}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}.

(2.17)

Note that the space
H_{a}^{b}(X)
consists of
all finite linear combinations of the form
\sum_{k=a}^{b}c_{k}X(k)
,
as well as, their
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-limits.

The space
H(X):=H_{-\infty}^{\infty}(X)
is referred to as the
Hilbert space generated by the process

X(t)
, or the
time-domain
of
X(t)
.

Let
\mu
be the spectral measure of the process
\{X(t),\,t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
Consider the weighted
L^{2}
-space
L^{2}(\mu):=L^{2}(\mu,\Lambda)
of complex-valued functions

\varphi(\lambda),\,\lambda\in\Lambda
, defined by

L^{2}(\mu):=\left\{\varphi(\lambda):\,||\varphi||^{2}_{\mu}:=\int_{\Lambda}|\varphi(\lambda)|^{2}d\mu(\lambda)<\infty\right\}.

(2.18)

Then
L^{2}(\mu)
becomes a Hilbert space with the following
inner product: for
\varphi,\psi\in L^{2}(\mu)
,

(\varphi,\psi)_{\mu}=\int_{\Lambda}\varphi(\lambda)\overline{\psi}(\lambda)d\mu(\lambda).

(2.19)

The Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mu,\Lambda)
is referred to as the
frequency-domain
of the process
X(t)
.

Theorem 2.2

(Kolmogorov’s isometric isomorphism theorem)
.

For any stationary process
X(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, with spectral
measure
\mu
, there exists a unique isometric isomorphism
V
between
the time-domain
H(X)
and the frequency-domain
L^{2}(\mu)

such that
V[X(t)]=e^{it\lambda}
for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}.

According to Theorem
2.2
, any linear problem in the time-domain
H(X)
can be translated into a problem in the frequency-domain
L^{2}(\mu)
, and vice versa. This fact allows for the study of stationary processes using analytic methods.

For more information about Hilbert spaces related to stationary processes, see Section
9.1
.

2.4.2
Deterministic (singular), nondeterministic and purely nondeterministic (regular) processes. Characterizations

We start by stating the linear prediction problem.
Let
X(t),

t\in\mathbb{Z}:=\{0,\pm 1,\ldots\}
, be a centered discrete-time
second-order stationary process with spectral measure
\mu
.
Suppose we observe a finite realization of the process
X(t)
:

\{X(t),\,\,-n\leq t\leq-1\},

n\in\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}.

We aim to make a one-step ahead prediction, specifically to predict the
unobserved random variable
X(0)
using the
linear predictor

defined as
Y=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}X(-k).

The coefficients

c_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,n
, are chosen so as to minimize

the mean-squared error
:
\mathbb{E}\left|X(0)-Y\right|^{2},

where
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation operator.
If such minimizing constants
\widehat{c}_{k}:=\widehat{c}_{k,n}

can be found, then the random variable

\widehat{X}_{n}(0):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{c}_{k}X(-k)

is referred to as
the best linear one-step ahead predictor
of
X(0)

based on the observed finite past:
X(-n),\ldots,X(-1)
.
The minimum mean-squared error:

\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,\mu):=\mathbb{E}\left|X(0)-\widehat{X}_{n}(0)\right|^{2}\geq 0

(2.20)

is called
the best linear one-step ahead prediction error
.

Observe that
\sigma_{n+1}^{2}(0,\mu)\leq\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,\mu)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
and hence the limit of
\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,\mu)
as
n\to\infty
exists.
We denote
\sigma^{2}(0,\mu):=\sigma_{\infty}^{2}(0,\mu)

as the prediction error based on the entire infinite past:
\{X(t)
,
t\leq-1\}
.

From a prediction perspective, it is natural to distinguish the class of
processes for which we have
error-free prediction
based on the entire infinite past, i.e.,

\sigma^{2}(0,\mu)=0
. Such processes are termed
deterministic
or
singular
.
Processes for which
\sigma^{2}(0,\mu)>0
are referred to as
nondeterministic
.

Observe that the time-domain
H(X)
of any non-degenerate
stationary process
\{X(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
can be
represented as the orthogonal sum:

H(X)=H_{1}(X)\oplus H_{-\infty}(X),

where
H_{-\infty}(X)
is the remote past of
X(t)
, defined by
H_{-\infty}(X):=\cap_{t}H_{-\infty}^{t}(X)
,
and
H_{1}(X)
is the orthogonal complement of
H_{-\infty}(X)
.

Thus, we can provide the following geometric definitions for deterministic (singular), nondeterministic, and purely nondeterministic (regular) processes.

Definition 2.1
.

A stationary process
\{X(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is called

•

deterministic or singular if
H_{-\infty}(X)=H(X)
, that is,

H_{-\infty}^{t}(X)=H_{-\infty}^{s}(X)
for all
t,s\in\mathbb{Z}
;

•

nondeterministic if the remote past
H_{-\infty}(X)
is a proper subspace of
H(X)
, that is,
H_{-\infty}(X)\subset H(X)
;

•

purely nondeterministic (PND) or regular if
H_{-\infty}(X)=\{0\}
,
that is, the remote past
H_{-\infty}(X)
is the trivial subspace consisting of the singleton zero.

Remark 2.2
.

Every purely nondeterministic process
X(t)

is nondeterministic, but the converse is generally not true.
An example of such a process is given by
X(t)=\varepsilon(t)+\xi
,
where
\{\varepsilon(t),\,t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is a
{\rm WN}(0,\sigma^{2})

(a centered white noise with variance
\sigma^{2}
), and

\xi
is a random variable such that
Var(\xi)=\sigma^{2}_{0}
and

(\varepsilon(t),\xi)=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}

(see Pourahmadi
[
47
]
, p. 163).
Note that the only process
X(t)
that is both
deterministic and purely nondeterministic is the
degenerate process. Assuming that
X(t)
is a non-degenerate
process, we exclude this trivial case.

The following result provides spectral characterizations of deterministic, nondeterministic, and purely nondeterministic
processes (see, e.g., Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
,
p. 44, and Ibragimov and Rozanov
[
36
]
, pp. 35–36).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
X(t)
be a non-degenerate stationary process with spectral
measure
\mu
of the form (
2.3
). The following assertions hold.

(a)

(Kolmogorov-Szegő’s Theorem)
.
The following relations hold.

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,\mu)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,f)=\sigma^{2}(0,f)=2\pi G(f),

(2.21)

where
G(f):=G(0,f)
is the geometric mean of
f
, namely

G(f):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\ln f(\lambda)\,d\lambda\right\}&\mbox{if \, $\ln f\in{L}^{1}(\mathbb{T})$}\\
0,&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

(2.22)

(b)

H_{-\infty}^{0}(\mu_{s})=H(\mu_{s})\Leftrightarrow\sigma^{2}(0,\mu)=0\Leftrightarrow X(t)\,\,is\,\,deterministic,

(c)

(Kolmogorov-Szegő’s alternative)
.
Either

\displaystyle H_{-\infty}^{0}(\mu_{a})=H(\mu_{a})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\ln f(\lambda)\,d\lambda=-\infty\Leftrightarrow\sigma^{2}(0,f)=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow X(t)\,\,is\,\,deterministic,

or else

\displaystyle H_{-\infty}^{0}(\mu_{a})\neq H(\mu_{a})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\ln f(\lambda)\,d\lambda>-\infty\Leftrightarrow\sigma^{2}(0,f)>0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow X(t)\,\,is\,\,nondeterministic.

(d)

The process
X(t)
is regular (PND) if and only if
it is nondeterministic and
\mu_{s}\equiv 0
.

Remark 2.3
.

The second equality in (
2.21
) was proved by Szegő
[
57
]
, while the first equality was established by Kolmogorov
[
38
,
39
]

(see also Hoffman
[
34
]
, p. 49).
It is remarkable that the relation in (
2.21
) is independent of the singular part
\mu_{s}
.

2.4.3
Non-determinism. The Szegő Condition

Definition 2.2

(Szegő’s Condition)
.

The spectral measure
\mu
of the form (
2.3
) is said to satisfy the Szegő condition if

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\ln f(\lambda)\,d\lambda>-\infty

(2.23)

where
f
is the density of the absolutely continuous part of
\mu
. By convention, if
f(\lambda)=0
on a set of positive Lebesgue measure, the integral in (
2.23
) is considered to equal
-\infty
.

Note that the condition
\ln f\in{L}^{1}(\Lambda)
in (
2.22
) is equivalent to the Szegő condition (
2.23
).
This equivalence follows because
-\infty\leq\ln f(\lambda)\leq f(\lambda)
.
The Szegő condition (
2.23
) is also referred to as the
non-determinism condition
.

If the spectral measure
\mu
of the process
X(t)
is absolutely continuous with spectral density
f(\lambda)
, the singularity and regularity of
X(t)
depend on whether
f(\lambda)
approaches zero. For example, a short memory process
X(t)
is regular.
If
f(\lambda)
vanishes on some interval

I\subset[-\pi,\pi]
, then the Szegő condition (
2.23
) is violated, resulting in
X(t)
being singular. In this case, we will describe the process as purely deterministic.
Singularity can also occur if
f(\lambda)
vanishes at a single point; the spectral density can produce either nondeterministic or deterministic behavior depending on the flatness at zero.
Furthermore, note that the Szegő condition relates
to the nature of the singularities (zeros and poles) of the spectral density
f
, and does not depend on the differential properties of
f
.
The following example illustrates this point (see also Pourahmadi
[
47
]
, p. 68).

Example 2.1

(Flat-zero spectral model)
.

Consider the stationary process
X(t)
with spectral density

f_{a}(\lambda):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp\left\{-|\lambda|^{-a}\right\}&\mbox{if \, $\lambda\neq 0$}\\
0&\mbox{if \, $\lambda=0$,}\end{array}\hskip 10.00002pta>0.\right.

(2.24)

We first show that the function
f_{a}(\lambda)
is infinitely differentiable (
f_{a}(\lambda)\in C^{\infty}([-\pi,\pi])
)
with all derivatives vanishing at
\lambda=0
:

f_{a}^{(k)}(0)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall k\geq 0.

This follows using the formula for derivative of the function
|\lambda|
:

|\lambda|^{\prime}=\left(\sqrt{\lambda^{2}}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{\lambda}{|\lambda|}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-1&\mbox{if \, $\lambda<0$}\\
0&\mbox{is not defined if \, $\lambda=0$}\\
1&\mbox{if \, $\lambda>0$},\end{array}\right.

(2.25)

and the following relation:

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{b}e^{-ct}=0\hskip 10.00002pt(b\in\mathbb{R},\,\,c>0).

(2.26)

Using (
2.25
) and (
2.26
), a direct computation shows that

f_{a}^{(k)}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}P_{k}\big(|\lambda|^{-a}\big)\,|\lambda|^{-k}\,e^{-|\lambda|^{-a}},&\mbox{if \, $\lambda\neq 0$},\\
0,&\mbox{if \, $\lambda=0$,}\end{array}\right.

where
P_{k}
is a polynomial depending on
a
and
k
.
In addition, we have

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}f_{a}^{(k)}(\lambda)=0.

Thus,
f_{a}\in C^{\infty}([-\pi,\pi])
.
Next, the Szegő integral is:

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\log f_{a}(\lambda)\,d\lambda=-2\int_{0}^{\pi}\lambda^{-a}\,d\lambda.

•

For
0<a<1
,
\int_{0}^{\pi}\lambda^{-a}d\lambda<\infty
, so Szegő’s condition (
2.23
) is satisfied. The asymptotic one-step prediction error
\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,f)>0
, indicating that the process
X(t)
is
purely nondeterministic
.

•

For
a\geq 1
,
\int_{0}^{\pi}\lambda^{-a}d\lambda=\infty
, which means Szegő’s condition (
2.23
) fails,
\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,f)=0
, and the process
X(t)
is
deterministic
.

Remark 2.4
.

In the case where the measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous, represented as
d\mu=f(\lambda)d\lambda
(with
\mu_{s}=0
), we can differentiate between light and strong determinism. We will refer to the process
X(t)
as light deterministic if the spectral density
f(\lambda)
is positive everywhere except for a finite or countable number of points, which results in a violation of the Szegő condition (
2.23
).
In contrast, if
f(\lambda)
vanishes over a set of positive Lebesgue measure, the corresponding process
X(t)
is termed strong deterministic (or purely deterministic). An example of a light deterministic process is the flat-zero spectral model discussed in Example
2.1
for
a\geq 1
. Another important example
is the process characterized by the Pollaczek-Szegő spectral density:

f_{a}(\lambda):=\frac{e^{(2\lambda-\pi)\varphi(\lambda)}}{\cosh\left(\pi\varphi(\lambda)\right)},\hskip 10.00002ptf_{a}(-\lambda)=f_{a}(\lambda),\hskip 10.00002pt0\leq\lambda\leq\pi,

(2.27)

where
\varphi(\lambda)=(a/2)\cot\lambda
and
a
is a positive parameter.
Observe that for the function
f_{a}
in (
2.27
), we have the following asymptotic
relation (for details, see Babayan and Ginovyan
[
5
]
,
and Szegő
[
59
]
:

f_{a}(\lambda)\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2e^{a}\exp\left\{-{a\pi}/{|\lambda|}\right\}&\mbox{as $\lambda\to 0$},\\
2\exp\left\{-{a\pi}/{(\pi-|\lambda|)}\right\}&\mbox{as $\lambda\to\pm\pi$}.\end{array}\right.

(2.28)

Thus, the function
f_{a}
in (
2.27
) has a very high order of contact
with zero at the points
\lambda=0,\pm\pi
, due to which the Szegő condition (
2.23
)
is violated, indicating that the process with spectral
density
f_{a}
is light deterministic.

Remark 2.5

(Pure point spectrum)
.

The spectral measure
\mu
is
pure point
if

\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}\,\delta_{\lambda_{k}},\hskip 10.00002ptw_{k}>0,\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}<\infty.

From the spectral representation theorem applied to atomic
measures we have (see (
2.5
)):

X(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}Z_{k}e^{it\lambda_{k}},

(2.29)

where
\{Z_{k}\}
are uncorrelated random variables with

\mathbb{E}|Z_{k}|^{2}=w_{k}
.
Since there is no absolutely continuous part, Kolmogorov’s criterion gives

\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,\mu)=0
, implying that if the spectral measure is purely atomic, then the process
X(t)
is deterministic.

Remark 2.6

(Pure singular continuous spectrum)
.

The spectral measure
\mu
is
pure singular continuous
if it is continuous, assigns zero mass to points, and is singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure. If
\mu
is purely singular continuous, then the process
X(t)
is deterministic.

Remark 2.7
.

If the Szegő condition (
2.23
) is satisfied, but the spectral measure
\mu
is either discontinuous or continuous with a non-vanishing singular part (
\mu_{s}\neq 0
), then the process
X(t)
is neither singular nor regular.

3
Szegő’s extremum problem on the unit circle

3.1
Setting of the problem

We denote by
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
the set of monic polynomials
q_{n}(z)=c_{0}z^{n}+c_{1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+c_{n-1}z+c_{n}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
of degree

n\in\mathbb{N}
with leading coefficient
c_{0}=1
.
For a fixed
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
, we denote by
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\xi)
the set of polynomials
q_{n}(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
, that have a degree of at most
n
and satisfy the condition
q_{n}(\xi)=1
. Specifically,

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\xi):=\{q_{n}(z)=c_{0}z^{n}+\cdots+c_{n-1}z+c_{n};\,\,{\rm deg}\ q_{n}\leq n,\,q_{n}(\xi)=1\}.

(3.1)

Let
\mu
be a nontrivial measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
with the standard Lebesgue decomposition (
2.3
).
Define the
n^{th}

Christoffel function
associated with a measure
\mu
by

\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu):=\min_{q_{n}(z)\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\xi)}||q_{n}||^{2}_{\mu},

(3.2)

where
||q_{n}||_{\mu}
denotes the norm of the polynomial
q_{n}
in the space
L^{2}(\mu)
:

||q_{n}||^{2}_{\mu}:=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|q_{n}(e^{i\lambda})|^{2}d\mu(\lambda).

(3.3)

Evidently the sequence
\{\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu),\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is non-increasing.

The generalized Szegő extremum problem can be stated as follows:

(a)

Find the polynomial
q_{n}(e^{i\lambda})
of degree at most
n

in
e^{i\lambda}=z
that solves the minimum problem in (
3.2
).

(b)

Describe the asymptotic behavior of
\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)
as
n\to\infty
.

Definition 3.1
.

The polynomial
p_{n}(z):=p_{n}(z,\mu)
that solves the minimum problem in (
3.2
)
is referred to as the optimal polynomial (of degree
n
) for the measure
\mu
. Therefore,

\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)=\min_{q_{n}(z)\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\xi)}||q_{n}||^{2}_{\mu}=||p_{n}||^{2}_{\mu},\hskip 10.00002pt\xi\in\mathbb{C}.

(3.4)

The extremum problem mentioned above was solved by G. Szegő, who demonstrated that the optimal polynomial

p_{n}(z)
exists, is unique, and can be expressed in terms of the

orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
associated with the measure
\mu
.
He also described the asymptotic behavior of the function
\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)
as
n\to\infty
for some classes.

To present Szegő’s solution,
we will first review some key concepts and results from the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
.

3.2
Orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle (OPUC) and Szegő’s generalized theorem

Let
\mu
be a nontrivial measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
with the standard Lebesgue decomposition (
2.3
).
The system of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
associated with the measure
\mu
:

\{\varphi_{n}(z)=\varphi_{n}(z;\mu),\hskip 10.00002ptz=e^{i\lambda},\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}

is uniquely determined by the following two conditions:

(i)

\varphi_{n}(z)=\kappa_{n}z^{n}+\cdots+l_{n}

is a polynomial of degree
n
, in which the coefficient

\kappa_{n}
is positive;

(ii)

(\varphi_{k},\varphi_{j})_{\mu}=\delta_{kj}
for arbitrary
k,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, where

\delta_{kj}
is the Kronecker delta.

Define the monic (
\Phi_{n}(z)
) and the reversed (
\Phi^{*}_{n}(z)
) polynomials:

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(z):=\Phi_{n}(z,\mu)=\kappa^{-1}_{n}\varphi_{n}(z)=z^{n}+\cdots+l_{n}\kappa_{n}^{-1},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}_{n}(z):=\Phi^{*}_{n}(z,\mu)=z^{n}\overline{p_{n}(1/\overline{z})}=\overline{l}_{n}\kappa_{n}^{-1}z^{n}+\cdots+1.

(3.6)

Then, we have

||\Phi_{n}||_{\mu}^{2}=||\Phi^{*}_{n}||_{\mu}^{2}=\kappa_{n}^{-2}=\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,\mu),

(3.7)

where
\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,\mu)
represents the prediction error, as defined in equation (
2.20
).

For a nontrivial measure
\mu
on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}

of the form (
2.3
) and for a fixed
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
, we consider the
Szegő kernel

S_{n}(z,\xi):=S_{n}(\xi,z,\mu)
defined by (see, e.g., Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
, p. 39):

S_{n}(\xi,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\overline{\varphi_{k}(\xi)}\varphi_{k}(z).

(3.8)

Observe that

For every

(h(\cdot),S_{n}(\cdot,\xi))_{\mu}=h(\xi).

(3.9)

For the definition and properties of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, see Section

.

Example 3.1

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

).
We assume that the Szegő condition (

The following assertions hold.

(a)

(b)

For almost all

(3.11)

(c)

(3.12)

(d)

(e)

(3.15)

, pp. 280, 297, and 298).

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f(\lambda)
be a positive weight function on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
,
which satisfies the Lipschitz-Dini condition:

|f(\lambda+\delta)-f(\lambda)|<C|\ln\delta|^{-1-\alpha},

(3.16)

where
C
and
\alpha
are fixed positive numbers. Then we have, for
|z|=1

\varphi_{n}(z)=\frac{z^{n}}{\overline{D}(f,1/z)}+\varepsilon_{n}(z),

(3.17)

where
\varepsilon_{n}(z)=o(1)
as
n\to\infty
uniformly for
|z|=1
.

The next proposition provides local sufficient conditions for (

).

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
f(\lambda)
be a Riemann integrable function, and let it have the form:

f(\lambda)=h(\lambda)\left|\prod_{k=1}^{m}(z-z_{k})^{\beta_{k}}\right|,\hskip 10.00002ptz=e^{i\lambda},

(3.18)

where
0<c\leq h(\lambda)\leq C
and
z_{k}=e^{i\lambda_{k}}
are distinct points on the unit circle,
\beta_{k}>0
,

.
Assume that

is differentiable at the fixed point
\lambda=a
,

\xi=e^{ia}\neq z_{k}
,

, and let the following be bounded near

\frac{f(\lambda)-f(a)-f^{\prime}(a)(\lambda-a)}{(\lambda-a)^{2}}.

Then the relation (

) holds for

in the less informative form:

\varphi_{n}(\xi)=\frac{\xi^{n}}{\overline{D}(f,1/\xi)}+\varepsilon_{n},

(3.19)

where
\varepsilon_{n}(z)=o(1)
as

.

The next proposition provides an estimate of the orthogonal polynomials corresponding

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
f(\lambda)
be a weight function on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}

f(\lambda)=|e^{i\lambda}-e^{i\lambda_{0}}|^{2\alpha}g(\lambda),\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha>0,

where

.
Then, for the orthogonal polynomials
\varphi_{n}
corresponding to

, the following estimate holds:

|\varphi_{n}(e^{i\lambda},f)|\simeq n^{\alpha}\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.21)

3.3
Solution of Szegő’s extremum problem

The following proposition presents the solution to part (a) of Szegő’s extremum problem (see, e.g.,
Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
, Section 2.2, Nikishin and Sorokin
[
44
]
, Section 3.6, and Simon
[
54
]
, p. 124).

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\mu
be a nontrivial measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}

of the form (
2.3
). Let the set of polynomials
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\xi)
and the kernel
S_{n}(z,\xi)

be defined as in (
3.1
) and (
3.8
), respectively. The polynomial

p_{n}(z)=\frac{S_{n}(z,\xi)}{S_{n}(\xi,\xi)}

(3.22)

is the unique solution to the extremum problem in (
3.2
), and the minimum is
equal to
S_{n}^{-1}(\xi,\xi)
.
Specifically, the following relation holds:

\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu):=\min_{q_{n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\xi)}||q_{n}||^{2}_{\mu}=||p_{n}||^{2}_{\mu}=\frac{1}{S_{n}(\xi,\xi)}.

(3.23)

Proof.

Expand any trial polynomial
q_{n}(z)
by formula:

q_{n}(z)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}\varphi_{k}(z).

(3.24)

Then the normalization condition
q_{n}(\xi)=1
says

\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}\varphi_{k}(\xi)=1,

(3.25)

while

||q_{n}||^{2}_{\mu}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}|c_{k}|^{2}.

(3.26)

By (
3.26
) and the Schwarz inequality we obtain

S_{n}(\xi,\xi)\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}|c_{k}|^{2}\geq 1.

(3.27)

On the other hand, for the the choice

c_{k}=\frac{\overline{\varphi_{k}(\xi)}}{S_{n}(\xi,\xi)}

(3.28)

that is, for
q_{n}
given by the right side of (
3.22
), we have

||q_{n}||^{2}_{\mu}=\frac{S_{n}(\xi,\xi)}{S^{2}_{n}(\xi,\xi)}=\frac{1}{S_{n}(\xi,\xi)},

(3.29)

and the result follows.
∎

One immediate useful consequence of Proposition
3.5
is the following:

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
be nontrivial measures on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}

such that
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}
, which means that
\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}
is a nonnegative measure. Then

S_{n}(z,z,\mu_{2})\leq S_{n}(z,z,\mu_{1}).

(3.30)

For
\theta,\lambda\in\Lambda
and
r\in[0,1)
, define the real Poisson kernel:

P_{r}(\theta,\lambda):=\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-2r\cos(\lambda-\theta)+r^{2}}

(3.31)

In the interior of the unit circle in the
\xi
-plane there is defined a
function
G(\xi,f)
(cf. (
2.22
)):

G(\xi,f):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\ln f(\lambda)P_{r}(\theta,\lambda)\,d\lambda\right\}&\mbox{if \, $\ln f\in{L}^{1}(\mathbb{T})$}\\
0,&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

(3.32)

where
\xi=re^{i\theta}
;
|r|<1
.

The next result, known as Szegő’s generalized theorem,
provides the solution to part (b) of Szegő’s extremum problem
in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
.
The proof can be found in Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
,
Section 3.2 (see also Simon
[
54
]
, Section 2.3).

Proposition 3.7

(Szegő’s generalized theorem)
.

Let
\mu
be a nontrivial measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
of the form (
2.3
). Then for any fixed
\xi\in\mathbb{D}:=\{z:|z|<1\}
with
\xi=re^{i\theta}
the following asymptotic relation holds:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)=\lambda(\xi,\mu)=2\pi G(\xi,f)(1-|\xi|^{2}),

(3.33)

where
\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)
is the
n^{th}
Christoffel function defined by (
3.2
).
In the case when
\ln f\notin{L}^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, the limit in (
3.33
) must be replaced by zero.

Remark 3.1
.

The quantity
G(\xi,f)
in equation (
3.32
) is a mean value of
f(\lambda)
that depends on
\xi
and represents a generalization of the geometric mean
G(f)
, defined as in (
2.22
). It reduces to this geometric mean when
\xi=0
.
In this special case, Proposition
3.7
reduces to the Kolmogorov-Szegő Theorem
(see Theorem
2.3
, part (a)).

Remark 3.2
.

Taking into account the equality (see Simon
[
53
]
, p. 118):

-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}P_{r}(\theta,\lambda)\ln P_{r}(\theta,\lambda)\,d\lambda=\ln(1-r^{2})<0,

(3.34)

the relation (
3.33
) can be written in the following form (see Simon
[
53
]
, p. 139):

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)=\lambda(\xi,\mu)=2\pi G(\xi,f/P_{r}).

(3.35)

Remark 3.3
.

Note that the relation (
3.33
) holds already for a finite value of
n

\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)=2\pi G(\xi,f)(1-|\xi|^{2})

(3.36)

if and only if

f(\lambda)=\frac{1}{|1-\overline{\xi}z|^{2}}\frac{1}{\phi(\lambda)},\hskip 10.00002ptz=e^{i\lambda},

(3.37)

where
\phi(\lambda)
denotes a positive trigonometric polynomial of order
n
.

Theorem
3.7
provides the asymptotic behavior of the Christoffel function
\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)
inside the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
.
However, when estimating the unknown mean of a stationary process, we set
|\xi|=1
, which requires us to examine the asymptotic behavior of
\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)
on the boundary of the unit disk.

Note that on the boundary of the unit disk, Proposition
3.7
only gives the trivial result
\lambda(\xi,\mu)=0
for almost every
\xi
with
|\xi|=1
. More precisely, it was proven in Máté et al.

\lambda_{n}(e^{i\lambda},\mu)=\mu(\{\lambda\})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for every}\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\in[-\pi,\pi).

(3.38)

Therefore, in view of (

\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)
rather than
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}(\xi,\mu)

The following result was established by Máté et al.
[
41
]

Proposition 3.8

(Máté’s theorem)
.

Let
\mu
be a nontrivial measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
of the form (
2.3
). Assume that
\mu
satisfies the Szegő condition (
2.23
). Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}(n+1)\lambda_{n}(e^{i\lambda},\mu)=2\pi f(\lambda)

holds for almost every
\lambda\in[-\pi,\pi)
.

It is important to note that the limiting relation (
3.39
) was originally derived by Szegő under more restrictive conditions. Specifically, the measure
\mu
must be absolutely continuous, and
f=\mu^{\prime}>0
needs to be twice continuously differentiable
(see Szegő
[
60
]
, Theorem I’, p. 461).

The proof of the following result can be found in Golinskii
[
23
]
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\mu
be a nontrivial measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
that satisfies the Szegő condition (
2.23
). Assume that
\mu
is absolutely continuous and the weight
f(\lambda)
is continuous and
f(\lambda)\geq c>0
. Then the following relation holds

\lim_{n\to\infty}(n+1)\lambda_{n}(e^{i\lambda_{0}},\mu)=2\pi f(\lambda_{0}),\hskip 10.00002ptz_{0}=e^{i\lambda_{0}}.

(3.40)

Remark 3.4
.

In Geronimus
[
20
]
, it was proved that the asymptotic formula (

) holds for almost every
\lambda\in[-\pi,\pi]
under the weaker condition that

almost everywhere on
[-\pi,\pi]
. Additionally, it was observed that this condition is satisfied if the measure is from the Szegő class. Furthermore, it is also satisfied for the Pollaczek-Szegő weight function (see equation (
2.27
)),
for which the Szegő condition (
2.23
) is violated.
Unfortunately, as noted by Nevai
[

[
43
]
, pp. 27–28.

4
Formulas and properties of the variance of BLUE

In this section, we present formulas and properties of the variance of the BLUE.
We first utilize Kolmogorov’s isometric isomorphism between the time and frequency domains,
V:X(t)\leftrightarrow e^{it\lambda}
, to reformulate the problem of finding the BLUE in the frequency domain. Then, we apply Szegő’s results to derive a convenient formula for the variance
\mathrm{Var}\left(\widehat{m}_{n,BLU}\right)

in terms of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle with respect to the
spectral density
f(\lambda)
.

To begin, using equations (
1.2
) - (
1.4
), we can write

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}\left(\widehat{m}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=0}^{n}c_{j}\bar{c}_{k}r(j-k)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left|\sum_{\nu=0}^{n}c_{\nu}e^{i\nu\lambda}\right|^{2}f(\lambda)d\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left|\sum_{\nu=0}^{n}c_{\nu}e^{i(n-\nu)\lambda}\right|^{2}f(\lambda)d\lambda=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|q_{n}(e^{i\lambda}|^{2}f(\lambda)d\lambda,

(4.1)

where
q_{n}(z)=c_{0}z^{n}+c_{1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+c_{n-1}z+c_{n}
.

Thus, the problem of finding
\widehat{m}_{n,BLU}
reduces to the
special case of Szegő’s extremum problem on the unit circle where

\xi=1
. Specifically (see Section
3
),

||q_{n}||^{2}_{f}:=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|q_{n}(e^{i\lambda})|^{2}f(\lambda)d\lambda={\rm min},\hskip 10.00002ptq_{n}(z)\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1).

(4.2)

The polynomial
p_{n}(z):=p_{n}(z,f)
, which solves the minimum problem in (
4.2
)
is referred to as the optimal polynomial (of degree
n
) for
f(\lambda)
within the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
. The minimum itself, which represents
the variance of
\widehat{m}_{n,BLU}
, is denoted by
\sigma_{n}^{2}(1,f)
.
Therefore, for the variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}(1,f)
of the BLUE we have the following formula in terms of the optimal polynomial
p_{n}(z)
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}(1,f):=\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=\min_{q_{n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)}||q_{n}||^{2}_{f}=||p_{n}||^{2}_{f}.

(4.3)

Note that since the optimal
p_{n}(z)
is determined by
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
(and vice versa), it is unique.

For simplicity, we will use the following notation:

\widehat{m}_{f}
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f):=\sigma_{n}^{2}(1,f)
will denote the BLUE
and the variance of the BLUE calculated with respect to the spectral density
f(\lambda)
, respectively.
The optimal polynomials for
f(\lambda)
will be denoted by
p_{n}(z)
.

Applying Proposition
3.5
with
\xi=1
, we find that
the optimal polynomial
p_{n}(z,f)
for determining the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{n,BLU}
is given by the following formula:

p_{n}(z,f)=\frac{S_{n}(z,1)}{S_{n}(1,1)}=\frac{\sum\limits_{v=0}^{n}\overline{\varphi_{v}(1)}\varphi_{v}(z)}{\sum\limits_{v=0}^{n}|\varphi_{v}(1)|^{2}}.

Thus, for the variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
of the BLUE, we have the following formula
in terms of Szegő’s kernel and the orthogonal polynomials:

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)=\frac{1}{S_{n}(1,1)}=\left(\sum\limits_{v=0}^{n}|\varphi_{v}(1)|^{2}\right)^{-1}.

(4.4)

Next, by comparing equations (
3.2
) and (
4.3
), we observe that

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
coincides with the
n^{th}
Christoffel function at the point
\xi=1
. Specifically, we have the following formula for the variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
in terms of the Christoffel function:

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)=\lambda_{n}(1,f).

(4.5)

Denote by
B_{n}(f)
the truncated Toeplitz matrix generated by the spectral density
f(\lambda)
:

B_{n}(f)=\|\hat{f}(k-j)\|,\,\,k,j=1,\ldots,n,

(4.6)

where

\hat{f}(k-j)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{i(k-j)\lambda}f(\lambda)\,d\lambda,

(4.7)

are the Fourier coefficients of the function
f(\lambda)
.

By comparing equations (
1.2
) and (
4.7
), we observe that
\hat{f}(k-j)=r(k-j)

for
k,j=1,\ldots,n
, which implies that
B_{n}(f)=R_{n}
, where
R_{n}
is the covariance matrix of the observed process.
Thus, in view of equation (
1.8
), the variance
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
of
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
can be expressed in terms of Toeplitz matrices as follows:

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)=\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=(\mathbf{1}^{T}B_{n}^{-1}(f)\mathbf{1})^{-1},

(4.8)

where
\mathbf{1}^{T}=(1,\ldots,1)
.

The next proposition presents two important properties of the variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For the sequence
\{\sigma_{n}^{2}(f),\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
the following assertions hold:

(a)

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
is non-increasing
in
n
:
\sigma_{n+1}^{2}(f)\leq\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
.

(b)

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
is a non-decreasing functional of
f(\lambda)
, that is,

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)\leq\sigma_{n}^{2}(g)
when

f(\lambda)\leq g(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

Proof.

Assertion (a) follows from the obvious embedding
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)\subset\mathcal{Q}_{n+1}(1)
. Assertion (b) is derived from the definition of an optimal polynomial and the fact that the square norm
\|\psi\|_{f}^{2}
is non-decreasing in
f
for a fixed function
\psi
. Indeed, let
p_{n}(z,f)
and
p_{n}(z,g)
denote the optimal polynomials corresponding to the spectral densities
f
and
g
, respectively (see equation (
4.3
)). We have

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)=||p_{n}(f)||^{2}_{f}\leq||p_{n}(g)||^{2}_{f}\leq||p_{n}(g)||^{2}_{g}=\sigma_{n}^{2}(g),

and the result follows. Note that assertion (b) can also be deduced from Proposition
3.6
and equation (
4.4
).
∎

Remark 4.1
.

Formula (
4.3
) highlights a similarity between
the estimation problem discussed here and the best linear mean-squared prediction problem for stationary processes.
Specifically, the best linear mean-squared one-step ahead prediction error variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}(0,f)
, based on a history of length
n
(see equation (
2.20
)),
has representations similar to (
4.3
) and (
4.4
).
The key difference is that, in this case, the minimum is taken over the set

\mathcal{Q}_{n}
of monic polynomials of degree
n
, rather than
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
.
For the definitions of the sets
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
, see Section
3.1
. For more information regarding the prediction problem, we refer to the papers by Babayan and Ginovyan
[
5
]
and Babayan et al.
[
6
]
.

Asymptotic behavior of the variance of the BLUE for nondeterministic models

5.1
Asymptotic behavior of
\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
for short-memory models

The first results regarding the asymptotic behavior of the variance of the BLUE for short-memory models can be traced back to the classical works of U. Grenander
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
(see also Grenander and Rosenblatt
[
31
]
, and Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
, Sections 11.1-11.3). He demonstrated that the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
has an asymptotic variance of

2\pi f(0)/n
, provided that
f(\lambda)
is positive and continuous. Specifically, the following results were established.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that the conditions of Proposition
3.2
(or Proposition
3.3
) are satisfied. Then, for the variance of
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
, we have:

\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})\sim 2\pi f(0)/n\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(5.1)

Proof.

By equation (
4.4
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=\left(\sum\limits_{v=0}^{n}|\varphi_{v}(1)|^{2}\right)^{-1}.

(5.2)

Under the assumptions of the theorem, we have (see equations (
3.17
) and (
3.19
)):

\varphi_{n}(1)=\frac{1}{\overline{D}(f,1)}+\varepsilon_{n},\hskip 10.00002pt\varepsilon_{n}=o(1)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(5.3)

From equations (
5.2
) and (
5.3
), and Proposition
3.1
(b), the result follows. ∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Assume that the spectral density
f(\lambda)
is positive and continuous.
Then, for the variance of
\widehat{m}_{BLU}

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f):=\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})\sim 2\pi f(0)/n\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(5.4)

Proof.

Consider a
p

with the spectral density

(see equation (

f(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot|\psi_{p}(z)|^{-2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\left|\sum_{k=0}^{p}b_{k}z^{k})\right|^{-2},\hskip 10.00002ptz=e^{i\lambda},

(5.5)

where the polynomial
\psi_{p}(z)
has all its zeros in
|z|<1
and
b_{p}>0
.
In this case, for the orthogonal polynomials
\varphi_{\nu}(z)
, we have (see, e.g., Grenander and Szegő
[

\varphi_{m+p}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}b_{k}z^{k+m},\hskip 10.00002ptm=0,1,\ldots.

(5.6)

Therefore,

\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}|\varphi_{k}(1)|^{2}=f^{-1}(0)+o(1)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(5.7)

Now let
f(\lambda)
be an arbitrary positive and continuous spectral density.
Given an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, we can find two polynomials
p_{1}(z)
and
p_{2}(z)
that satisfy

\displaystyle f_{1}(\lambda):=|p_{1}(z)|^{-2}\leq f(\lambda)\leq|p_{2}(z)|^{-2}:=f_{2}(\lambda),

\displaystyle f_{2}(\lambda)-f_{1}(\lambda)<\varepsilon,\hskip 10.00002ptz=e^{i\lambda}.

Since, by Proposition
4.1
(b),
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
is a non-decreasing functional of
f(\lambda)
, the variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
lies within the interval

(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})
, where

\alpha_{n}\sim 2\pi f_{1}(0)/n,\,\,\beta_{n}\sim 2\pi f_{2}(0)/n\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(5.8)

Since
f_{1}(0)-f(0)
and
f_{2}(0)-f(0)
can be made arbitrarily small by choosing

\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we find that

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)\sim 2\pi f(0)/n\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty,

(5.9)

and the result follows. ∎

5.3
Asymptotic behavior of
\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
for long-memory and antipersistent models

In the following, we use the notation
\sigma^{2}_{n}(f):=\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
. Additionally, we denote
\widehat{m}_{f}
as the BLUE of
m
calculated with respect to the spectral density
f(\lambda)
, and
p_{n}(z)
will represent the optimal polynomials for
f(\lambda)
.
We will focus on spectral densities for which the variance
\sigma^{2}_{n}(f)
does not decrease too rapidly.
Specifically, we assume that the sequence
\{\sigma_{n}^{2}(f),n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is weakly varying (see Section
9.4
).
Note that if
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
is weakly varying, then for any
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
, we have (see Proposition
9.7
(a)):

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma^{2}_{n+\nu}(f)/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)=1.

(5.26)

We also define several classes of functions that will be considered below. All these functions are assumed to be Lebesgue measurable on

[-\pi,\pi].

Definition 5.1
.

Let a function
g(\lambda)
be defined on
[-\pi,\pi]
. We say that this function belongs to the class:

L_{0}
: if
g(\lambda)
is nonnegative, integrable over
[-\pi,\pi]
, and continuous at
\lambda=0
;

L_{0}^{+}
: if
g(\lambda)\in L_{0}
and has a positive lower bound;

B_{0}
: if
g(\lambda)
is nonnegative, bounded, and continuous at
\lambda=0
;

B_{0}^{+}
: if
g(\lambda)\in B_{0}
and has a positive lower bound.

Furthermore, suppose that
g(\lambda)
has the form:

g(\lambda)=h(\lambda)|\lambda-\lambda_{1}|^{\alpha(1)}|\lambda-\lambda_{2}|^{\alpha(2)}\cdots|\lambda-\lambda_{r}|^{\alpha(r)},

(5.27)

where
r
is a natural number,
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{r}
are constants in
[-\pi,\pi]
, and
\alpha(1),

\alpha(2),

\cdots,

\alpha(r)
are non-negative constants.

Definition 5.2
.

Let a function
g(\lambda)
be defined as in equation (
5.27
).
We say that this function belongs to the class
ZL_{0}^{+}
or
ZB_{0}^{+}

depending on whether
h(\lambda)\in L_{0}^{+}
or
h(\lambda)\in B_{0}^{+}
, respectively.

To connect the large-sample behavior of the variance
\sigma^{2}_{n}(f)
with the behavior of the spectral density near the origin,
we examine the ratio
\sigma_{n}^{2}(fg)/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
, where
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
satisfies condition (
5.26
) and
g(\lambda)

is a suitably ”nice” function.
Essentially, we are now considering the spectral density
of the process
Y(t)
to be
f(\lambda)g(\lambda)
instead of just
f(\lambda)
.

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
f(\lambda)
be a spectral density such that the sequence
\{\sigma_{n}^{2}(f),n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is weakly varying, and let
g(\lambda)
belong to the class
ZB_{0}^{+}
. Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}^{2}(fg)/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)=g(0).

(5.28)

Moreover, if
g(0)>0
, then the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{f}
, calculated under the assumption that the spectral density is
f(\lambda)
, is asymptotically efficient with respect to
f(\lambda)g(\lambda)
in the sense that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}^{2}(fg)/\mathrm{Var}_{fg}(\widehat{m}_{f})=1.

(5.29)

Before proceeding with the proof, we first establish some preliminary results.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
t(\lambda)
is a nonnegative trigonometric polynomial, then

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}^{2}(ft)/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)\geq t(0).

(5.30)

Proof.

The result is trivial if
t(0)=0;
therefore we assume that
t(0)>0
. By the Féjer-Riesz representation theorem (see Proposition
9.6
(c)), there is a polynomial
p(z)
of the same degree, say
\nu
, as
t(\lambda)
for which
t(\lambda)=|p(e^{i\lambda})|^{2}
. Letting
q_{0}(z),q_{1}(z),\cdots
be the optimal polynomials for
f(\lambda)t(\lambda)
, we define the polynomial
r(z):=p(z)q_{n}(z)/p(1)
of degree
n+\nu
, and note that
r(1)=1
. Therefore, we have

t(0)\sigma_{n+\nu}^{2}(f)\leq t(0)\|r\|_{f}^{2}=\|q_{n}\|_{ft}^{2}=\sigma_{n}^{2}(ft).

(5.31)

The proof is completed by dividing the extreme inequality by
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
, taking the
\liminf
, and employing equation (
5.26
).
∎

Define the kernel

K_{n}(\lambda)=|p_{n}(e^{i\lambda})|^{2}f(\lambda)/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f).

(5.32)

In the next lemma we show that the kernel
K_{n}(\lambda)
is an approximate identity (see Section
9.2
).

Lemma 5.2
.

The kernel
K_{n}(\lambda)
defined by (
5.32
) is an approximate identity.

Proof.

Observe first that
K_{n}(\lambda)
is nonnegative and satisfies
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}K_{n}(\lambda)d\lambda=1
.
Now we show that for any
0<\delta\leq\pi
the kernel
K_{n}(\lambda)
satisfies the relation

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\delta\leq|\lambda|\leq\pi}K_{n}(\lambda)d\lambda=0.

(5.33)

Define a function
g(\lambda)
as follows:
g(\lambda)=2
for
0\leq|\lambda|<\delta
and
g(\lambda)=1
for
\delta\leq|\lambda|\leq\pi
. With this definition, we have

\int_{\delta\leq|\lambda|\leq\pi}K_{n}(\lambda)d\lambda=2-\|p_{n}\|^{2}_{fg}/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f).

(5.34)

Therefore, to prove (
5.33
), it suffices to show that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|p_{n}\|^{2}_{fg}/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)=2.

(5.35)

To this end, we select a nonnegative trigonometric polynomial
t(\lambda)
that satisfies the conditions:
t(\lambda)\leq g(\lambda)
and
t(0)=2
.
Then, by Proposition
4.1
(b), we have

\|p_{n}\|^{2}_{fg}\geq\sigma^{2}_{n}(fg)\geq\sigma_{n}^{2}(ft).

Next, by dividing by
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
, taking the
\liminf
, and using Lemma
5.1
, we obtain

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|p_{n}\|^{2}_{fg}/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)\geq t(0)=2.

On the other hand, according to the definition of function
g
, we have

\|p_{n}\|^{2}_{fg}/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)\leq 2.

Thus, the relation (
5.35
) is established, and the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

If
g(\lambda)
is in the class
B_{0}
, then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{Var}_{fg}(\widehat{m}_{f})/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)=g(0).

(5.36)

Proof.

With
K_{n}(\lambda)
defined as in (
5.32
), the relation
(
5.36
) is equivalent to the following:

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}K_{n}(\lambda)g(\lambda)d\lambda\rightarrow g(0)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

This relation follows from Lemma
5.2
and Proposition
9.5
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

If
g(\lambda)
is in the class
B_{0}
, then

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}^{2}(fg)/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)\leq g(0).

(5.37)

Proof.

The result is immediate from Lemma
5.3
, since
\sigma_{n}^{2}(fg)\leq\mathrm{Var}_{fg}(\widehat{m}_{f}).

∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
g(\lambda)
be in the class
ZL_{0}^{+}
. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
there exists a trigonometric polynomial
t(\lambda)
with the following properties:
0\leq t(\lambda)\leq g(\lambda)
and
t(0)\geq g(0)-\epsilon
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the result for each factor on the right side of equation (
5.27
), since the product of the ”approximants” will provide an approximant for
g(\lambda)
in the required sense. We consider the individual factors.

(a)

Let
g(\lambda)\in L_{0}^{+}
. For
\epsilon<g(0)
, we choose
0<\delta\leq\pi
such that
g(\lambda)\geq g(0)-\epsilon
for
|\lambda|\leq\delta
. Then we can use

t(\lambda):=[g(0)-\epsilon][(1+\cos\lambda)/2]^{N},

where the integer
N
is chosen so large that

[(1+\cos\delta)/2]^{N}\leq\inf g(\lambda)/[g(0)-\epsilon].

For
\epsilon\geq g(0)
, we use
t(\lambda)\equiv 0.

(b)

Let
g(\lambda)=|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|^{2\nu}
, where
\nu
is a positive integer. The result follows from part (a) by expressing
g(\lambda)
as the product of a positive and continuous function and the trigonometric polynomial
[1-\cos(\lambda-\lambda_{0})]^{\nu}
.

(c)

Let
g(\lambda)=|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|^{\alpha},
where
\alpha>0
is not an even integer.
If
\lambda_{0}=0
, we can use
t(\lambda)\equiv 0
. Therefore, we assume that

\lambda_{0}\neq 0
. For any integer
\nu>\alpha/2
and constant
0<\delta<|\lambda_{0}|
, the function

h(\lambda)=[\max\{\delta,|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|\}]^{-2\nu+\alpha}

is positive and continuous. Thus, the function
g_{1}(\lambda)=h(\lambda)|\lambda-\lambda_{0}|^{2\nu}
can be approximated according to parts (a) and (b). Since
g_{1}(\lambda)\leq g(\lambda)
and
g_{1}(0)=g(0)
, the approximant for
g_{1}(\lambda)
also serves for
g(\lambda)
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
5.12
.

Any function
g(\lambda)
in
ZB_{0}^{+}

ZL_{0}^{+}
and in
B_{0}

5.37

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}^{2}(fg)/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)\geq g(0).

(5.38)

For any
\epsilon>0,
choosing a trigonometric polynomial
t(\lambda)
with the properties as in Lemma
5.5

4.1

\sigma_{n}^{2}(fg)\geq\sigma_{n}^{2}(ft).

Hence, with use of Lemma

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}^{2}(ft)/\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)\geq t(0)\geq g(0)-\epsilon.

Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0,
we obtain the desired relation (
5.38
).

The second assertion of the theorem, specifically the asymptotic relation in (
5.29
), follows directly from relation (
5.28
) and Lemma
5.3
.

Theorem
5.12
is proved.
∎

5.4
Asymptotic behavior of
\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
for the class
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g)

Theorem
5.12
allows us to determine the rate at which
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
decreases and to identify asymptotically efficient estimators for a broad range of spectral densities, particularly those characterized by a zero (or infinity) of fixed finite order at the origin.
As representatives of such spectral densities consider the following class (cf. (
5.15
)):

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g):=\left\{f(\lambda):=f_{\alpha}(\lambda)g(\lambda),\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha>-1/2,\,\,\lambda\in\Lambda\right\},

(5.39)

where
g(\lambda)
is the spectral density of a short-memory process, and

f_{\alpha}(\lambda)=(2\pi)^{-1}|1-e^{i\lambda}|^{2\alpha},

(5.40)

Here
\alpha
(not necessarily an integer) is a constant, with
\alpha>-{1}/{2}
for integrability.

Note that this class is highly important from an applications standpoint.
For instance, the ARFIMA(
p,-\alpha,q
) model, defined by equation (
2.10
),
and the fractional Gaussian noise with Hurst index
H
, defined by equation (
2.12
), both belong to this class.

The problem is in obtaining the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{f}
for the unknown mean

m
and in determining the asymptotic behavior of the variance

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f):=\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{f})
,
calculated with respect to the spectral density

f(\lambda)\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g)
.

We begin by analyzing the special case where
g(\lambda)\equiv 1
,
specifically the ARFIMA(
0,-\alpha,0
) model.
In this case, by (
5.40
), we have

f(\lambda)=f_{\alpha}(\lambda)=2^{2\alpha-1}\pi^{-1}(\sin^{2}\lambda/2)^{\alpha},\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\in[-\pi,\pi].

(5.41)

Note that as
\lambda\rightarrow 0,

f_{\alpha}(\lambda)\sim{2\pi}^{-1}|\lambda|^{2\alpha},

which diverges when
-{1}/{2}<\alpha<0
. This indicates
that the ARFIMA(
0,-\alpha,0
) model exhibits long-range dependence
(see Section
2.3
).

The covariance function
r_{\alpha}(t)
corresponding to the spectral density (
5.41
) is
given by the following formula (see Adenstedt
[
1
]
):

r_{\alpha}(k)=(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(2\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+k+1)\Gamma(\alpha-k+1)},\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,\pm 1,\cdots,

(5.42)

with the convention
1/\Gamma(\alpha)=0
when
\alpha
is a non-positive integer. Here
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the Gamma function.

Note that as k
\rightarrow

\infty
,

r_{\alpha}(k)\sim C_{\alpha}k^{-2\alpha-1},

(5.43)

where

C_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\pi}\Gamma(2\alpha+1)(-\sin\pi\alpha).

(5.44)

Theorem 5.1 of Adenstedt
[
1
]
shows that the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{\alpha}:=\widehat{m}_{f_{\alpha}}
of the
unknown mean
m
, based on the sample
\{X(t),t=0,1,\ldots,n\}
, calculated with respect to the spectral density (
5.41
), is given by

\widehat{m}_{\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}(n,\alpha)X(k),

(5.45)

where

c_{k}(n,\alpha)\\
={n\choose k}\frac{B(\alpha+k+1,\alpha+n-k+1)}{B(\alpha+1,\alpha+1)},\hskip 10.00002ptk=0,1,\cdots,n,

(5.46)

and the variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f_{\alpha})
is given by

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f_{\alpha})=\mathrm{Var}_{f_{\alpha}}(\widehat{m}_{\alpha})=B(n+1,2\alpha+1)/B(\alpha+1,\alpha+1),

(5.47)

where
B(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Beta function.

A straightforward application of Stirling’s formula for the gamma function yields, from (
5.47
), that

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f_{\alpha})\sim n^{-2\alpha-1}\Gamma(2\alpha+1)/B(\alpha+1,\alpha+1)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(5.48)

We are now ready to describe the asymptotic behavior of
\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
within the class
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g)
for the general short memory factor
g(\lambda)
. From equation (
5.48
), we conclude that the sequence
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f_{\alpha})
is weakly varying (see Proposition
9.7
). This allows us to apply Theorem
5.12
to establish the desired result.

Theorem 5.13
.

Let the model
X(t)
be specified by equation (
1.1
), and let the noise
\{Y_{t}\}
have spectral density function
f(\lambda)=f_{\alpha}(\lambda)g(\lambda)
, where
\alpha>-{1}/{2}
and
g(\lambda)\in ZB_{0}^{+}
(see Definition
5.2
) with
g(0)>0
. Then

\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})\sim n^{-2\alpha-1}\Gamma(2\alpha+1)g(0)/B(\alpha+1,\alpha+1)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(5.49)

Moreover, the estimator
\widehat{m}_{f_{\alpha}}
given by (
5.45
) and (
5.46
) is asymptotically efficient
in the class
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g)
. Specifically, we have

\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})}{\mathrm{Var}_{f_{\alpha}}(\widehat{m}_{\alpha})}\rightarrow 1\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(5.50)

For
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=1
the theorem gives slightly strengthened versions of Theorems
5.1
and
5.10
,
respectively (see also Grenander
[
29
]
and Vitale
[
61
]
). Additionally, the theorem established a conjecture of Vitale
[
61
]
for integral
\alpha
(see also Section
5.2
).

The optimal polynomials for
f_{\alpha}(\lambda)
.

We denote by
C_{n}^{(a)}(x)
the Gegenbauer (or ultraspherical) polynomials, i.e., the polynomials orthogonal on
[-1,1]
with respect to the weight function
(1-x^{2})^{a-1/2}
and with the standardization
C_{n}^{(a)}(1)=\Gamma(n+2a)/[n!\Gamma(2a)]
for
a\neq 0
. For the properties of the polynomials
C_{n}^{(a)}(x)

we refer to Erdélyi et al.
[
18
]
, Vol. 2., Sect. 10.9.

The next result shows that the optimal polynomials for
f_{\alpha}(\lambda)
can be represented in terms of
C_{n}^{(a)}(x)

(see Adenstedt
[
1
]
).

Lemma 5.6
.

The optimal polynomials

p_{n,\alpha}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}(n,\alpha)z^{k}

(5.51)

for
f_{\alpha}(\lambda)
are given by the following formula:

p_{n,\alpha}(e^{2i\theta})=e^{in\theta}C_{n}^{(\alpha+1)}(\cos\theta)/C_{n}^{(\alpha+1)}(1).

(5.52)

Proof.

In terms of the coefficients (
5.46
), it is known that

C_{n}^{(\alpha+1)}(\cos\theta)/C_{n}^{(\alpha+1)}(1)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}(n,\alpha)\cos(n-2k)\theta.

(5.53)

The right side can be clearly expressed as
e^{-in\theta}p_{n,\alpha}(e^{2i\theta})
, because the coefficients
c_{k}(n,\alpha)
are real and satisfy the relation
c_{k}(n,\alpha)=c_{n-k}(n,\alpha)
.
∎

The next property of the optimal polynomials

p_{n,\alpha}(z)
allows us to strengthen Theorem
5.13

(see Adenstedt
[
1
]
).

Lemma 5.7
.

For
\delta>0,n^{\alpha+1}\max_{\delta\leq|\lambda|\leq\pi}|p_{n,\alpha}(e^{i\lambda})|
is bounded uniformly in n.

Theorem 5.14
.

The conclusions of Theorem
5.13
remain valid if the condition therein that
g(\lambda)\in ZB_{0}^{+}
is replaced by the weaker condition that
g(\lambda)\in ZL_{0}^{+}
.

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem
5.12
, the boundedness of the function
g(\lambda)
is relevant only through Lemmas
5.3
and
5.4
. These lemmas would still hold for an unbounded
g(\lambda)
(in
L_{0}
) if condition (
5.33
) could be replaced by the stronger requirement that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\max_{\delta\leq|\lambda|\leq\pi}K_{n}(\lambda)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\delta>0,

(5.54)

where the kernel

is defined by equation (

).
It is straightforward to verify that, based on equations (

), (

∎

Estimating the zero-order.
When using the estimator
\widehat{m}_{\alpha}
from Theorem
5.13
for a spectral density that approaches zero at the origin, there is a risk of inaccurately estimating the true order
\alpha
of the zero.
In some cases, we may either overestimate or underestimate the zero-order. Below, we discuss these scenarios.

Overestimating the zero-order.
Assume that the true spectral density is
f_{\alpha}(\lambda)
but we use the estimator
\widehat{m}_{\alpha+\beta}
having coefficients
c_{k}(n,\alpha+\beta)
, given by equation (
5.46
), where
\beta
is restricted to the nonnegative integers (the case of the non-integral
\beta
remains open).
We define the
asymptotic efficiency
(if exists) of the estimator
\widehat{m}_{\alpha+\beta}
relative to
\widehat{m}_{\alpha}

for the spectral density
f_{\alpha}(\lambda)
by (cf. (
1.9
)
and (
1.10
)):

e(\alpha,\beta)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}^{2}(f_{\alpha})/\mathrm{Var}_{f_{\alpha}}(\widehat{m}_{\alpha+\beta}).

(5.55)

The next theorem provides an expression for the asymptotic efficiency
e(\alpha,\beta)
.

Theorem 5.15
.

For
\alpha>-{1}/{2}
and
\beta\geq 0
an integer, the following equation holds:

e(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(2\alpha+2)\Gamma^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)\Gamma(2\alpha+4\beta+2)}{{2\beta\choose\beta}\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\alpha+2\beta+1)\Gamma^{2}(2\alpha+2\beta+2)}.

(5.56)

The efficiency of
\widehat{m}_{\alpha+\beta}
for a more general spectral density
f_{\alpha}(\lambda)g(\lambda)
may be treated by
using an integral kernel argument presented in Section
9.2
. Moreover, in this general case, the estimator
\widehat{m}_{\alpha+\beta}
has the asymptotic efficiency
e(\alpha,\beta)
as in (
5.56
).
Specifically, we have the following result (see Adenstedt
[
1
]
).

Theorem 5.16
.

Let the noise
\{Y_{t}\}
have spectral density
f(\lambda)=f_{\alpha}(\lambda)g(\lambda)
, where
g(\lambda)\in ZL_{0}^{+}
,
g(0)>0
, and
\alpha>-{1}/{2}
. Then for any integer
\beta\geq 0
,

\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{\alpha+\beta})\sim g(0)\sigma_{n}^{2}(f_{\alpha})/e(\alpha,\beta)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(5.57)

and the estimator
\widehat{m}_{\alpha+\beta}
has the asymptotic efficiency
e(\alpha,\beta)
as in (
5.56
).

Remark 5.2
.

Since
e(\alpha,\beta)>0
, we see that overestimation still yields an estimator whose variance decreases at the optimal rate. From equation (
5.56
) we observe: (a)
e(\alpha,\beta)
decreases to
1/{2\beta\choose\beta}
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, and
(b)
e(\alpha,\beta)\rightarrow 1
as
\alpha\rightarrow-{1}/{2}
. Additionally, we note that
e(0,1)={5}/{6}
, which was previously stated in Theorem
5.11
(see also Vitale
[
61
]
).

Underestimating the zero-order.
We now consider the case when the estimator
\widehat{m}_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is an integer, is used, but the true spectral density has a higher-order zero at the origin. In this scenario, which is the opposite of the one discussed above, we have the following theorem
(see Adenstedt
[
1
]
).

Theorem 5.17
.

Let
\alpha
be a nonnegative integer and let the true spectral density be
f(\lambda)=f_{\alpha+1}(\lambda)g(\lambda)
, where
g(\lambda)\in L_{0}
. Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{2\alpha+2}\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\widehat{m}_{\alpha})=[(2\alpha+1)!/\alpha!]^{2}\pi^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(\lambda)d\lambda.

(5.58)

Thus, if
f(\lambda)=f_{\alpha+\beta+1}(\lambda)g(\lambda)
with
\beta\geq 0,g(\lambda)\in ZL_{0}^{+}
and
g(0)>0
, we can conclude (using Theorem
5.14
) that the efficiency of
\widehat{m}_{\alpha}
is
O(n^{-2\beta-1})
. Consequently, the estimator
\widehat{m}_{\alpha}
is far from efficient.
Note that the relation (
5.58
)
was previously obtained by Vitale
[
61
]
) for
\alpha=0

(see Theorem
5.8
).

Remark 5.3
.

The types of ”multipliers” of the function
g(\lambda)
allowed in Theorems
5.12
and
5.13
, as defined in equation (
5.27
), may appear overly complicated. However, we want to emphasize that only the continuity and behavior of the spectral density near
\lambda=0
are important. Discontinuities or isolated zeros that occur away from the origin are not relevant.

6
Efficiency of the least squares estimator for long-memory models

In this section, we utilize the results from Sections
5.3
and
5.4
to examine the efficiency of the least squares estimator (LSE)
\bar{X}_{n}
when the observed process

X(t)
has a spectral density of the form
\lambda^{2\alpha}L(\lambda)
as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, where
L(\lambda)
is slowly varying function at the origin with
0<L(0)<\infty
.
Recall that a function
L(\lambda)
is slowly varying at 0 if, for all
\alpha>0
, the limit of
L(\lambda\alpha)/L(\lambda)
approaches 1 as
\lambda\to 0
(see Section
9.5
).

The efficiency and asymptotic efficiency of the least squares estimator
\bar{X}_{n}
are defined as follows (see equations (
1.9
) and (
1.10
)):

e(n,f,\bar{X}_{n}):=\frac{Var_{f}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})}{\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\bar{X}_{n})},

(6.1)

and

e(\infty,f,\bar{X}_{n}):=\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}}e(n,f,\bar{X}_{n})),

(6.2)

where
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
is the BLUE for
m
.
The estimator
\bar{X}_{n}
is considered efficient or asymptotically efficient if
e(n,f,\bar{X}_{n})=1
or
e(f,f,\bar{X}_{n})=1
, respectively.

6.1
Efficiency of the least squares estimator for the ARFIMA(
0,-\alpha,0
) model

Using formulas (
5.42
) and (
5.45
) – (
5.47
), a straightforward algebraic manipulation yielded the following result, which is useful for numerical evaluations.
For details, see Samarov and Taqqu
[
52
]
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let the spectral density
f_{\alpha}(\lambda)
be as defined in equation (
5.41
) with
\alpha>-{1}/{2}
. The efficiency
e(n,f_{\alpha},\bar{X}_{n})
of
\bar{X}_{n}
is given by the following expression:

\begin{split}e(n,f_{\alpha},\bar{X}_{n})&=\bigg\{\bigg(1+\frac{2\alpha}{2}\bigg)\cdots\bigg(1+\frac{2\alpha}{n}\bigg)\\
&\times\bigg[1-2\alpha\bigg(\frac{1-1/n}{1+\alpha}+\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}\bigg(1-\frac{k}{n}\bigg)\frac{(k-1-\alpha)\cdots(1-\alpha)}{(k+\alpha)\cdots(1+\alpha)}\bigg)\bigg]\bigg\}^{-1}.\end{split}

6.2
Efficiency of the least squares estimator for the class
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g)

The next theorem provides sufficient conditions for asymptotic efficiency of the LSE
\bar{X}_{n}
in the class
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g)
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let the noise
\{Y_{t}\}
have spectral density
f(\lambda)=f_{\alpha}(\lambda)g(\lambda)
,
where
g(\lambda)\in ZL_{0}^{+}
(see Definition
5.2
) is slowly varying at 0, and
g(0)>0
. Let
r_{\alpha}(k)
and
R_{\alpha}(k)
be the covariance functions corresponding to the spectral densities
f_{\alpha}(\lambda)
and
f(\lambda)

R_{\alpha}(k)\sim r_{\alpha}(k)g(0)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptk\rightarrow\infty.

(6.3)

The following assertions hold.

(i)

If
\alpha\in(-{1}/{2},{1}/{2})\setminus\{0\}

e(\infty,f,\bar{X}_{n})=\frac{\pi\alpha(1-2\alpha)}{B(\alpha+1,\alpha+1)\sin\pi\alpha}.

(6.4)

(ii)

If
\alpha\geq{1}/{2}
, then

e(\infty,f,\bar{X}_{n})=0.

Remark 6.1
.

(1) Note that
lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}e(\infty,f,\bar{X}_{n})=1
. If
\alpha=0
and
g(\lambda)
is piecewise continuous, continuous at 0 and
0<g(\lambda)<\infty

is asymptotically efficient, that is,

5.6

(2) Part (ii) of Theorem
6.2

5.2
).

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

Since
g(0)>0
,

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{f})\sim n^{-2\alpha-1}\frac{\Gamma(2\alpha+1)}{B(\alpha+1,\alpha+1)}g(0)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty.

(6.5)

On the other hand, we have

\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\bar{X}_{n})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}R_{\alpha}(j-k)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bigg\{R_{\alpha}(0)+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}A_{\alpha}(j)\bigg\},

If

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-j}^{j}R_{\alpha}(k)-\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}R_{\alpha}(k)

Thus for
-{1}/{2}<\alpha<{1}/{2}

\alpha\neq 0

\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\bar{X}_{n})=n^{-2\alpha-1}\frac{-C_{\alpha}}{\alpha(1-2\alpha)}g(0)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\rightarrow\infty

X(t)=me^{i\lambda_{0}t}+Y(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{Z},

where
\lambda_{0}
is a fixed constant in
[\pi,\pi]
.

Indeed, by defining
\widetilde{X}(t):=X(t)e^{-i\lambda_{0}t}
and
\widetilde{Y}(t):=Y(t)e^{-i\lambda_{0}t}
, this new model reduces to the original:
\widetilde{X}(t)=m+\widetilde{Y}(t)
. If
Y(t)
has a spectral density
f(\lambda)
, then
\widetilde{Y}(t)
has a spectral density given by
\widetilde{f}(\lambda)=f(\lambda+\lambda_{0})
, where the definition of
f(\lambda)
is extended outside the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
by periodicity.
In this new model, the behavior of
f(\lambda)
near
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
becomes particularly significant.

7
Extensions

In this section, we present extensions of the results discussed in the previous sections for the model
X(t)=m+Y(t)
, where
t\in\mathbb{Z}
(see (
1.1
)). We specifically focus on estimating the mean for continuous time processes and for models characterized by their spectral functions. Furthermore, we explore the estimation procedure within a Hilbert space framework, introduce the concept of pseudo-best estimators, and address their efficiency.

7.1
Estimating the mean for processes specified by the spectral function

We revisit the model specified by equation (
1.1
), defined as
X(t)=m+Y(t)
for
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
Y(t)
is a zero-mean, stationary process. In Sections
5
and
6
, we assumed that the model’s spectrum is absolutely continuous, characterized by the spectral density function
f(\lambda)
.

But what happens when the spectrum also includes a singular component?

Specifically, we examine the case in which the model is described by the spectral function
F(\lambda)
, which contains both an absolutely continuous component

f(\lambda)
and a singular part. This can be expressed as (see equation (
2.4
)):

dF(\lambda)=f(\lambda)d\lambda+dF_{s}(\lambda),\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\in[-\pi,\pi],

(7.1)

where
dF_{s}(\lambda)
is singular with respect to
d\lambda
.

We are particularly interested in the asymptotic behavior of the variance of the BLUE and the efficiency of the LSE
\widehat{m}_{LS}=\bar{X}_{n}
relative to the BLUE based on the sample
\{X(t),t=0,1,\ldots,n\}
.

The following theorem demonstrates that the efficiency of the LSE relative to the BLUE, even in this broader context, is strongly influenced by the behavior of the spectral function at the origin (see Adenstedt and Eisenberg
[
2
]
).

Theorem 7.1
.

Let the noise process
Y(t)
have spectral function
F(\lambda)

given by equation (
7.1
). The following assertions hold.

(a)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{LS})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=2\pi dF(0)
.

(b)

Assume also that
f(\lambda)
is positive and continuous at
\lambda=0
, that
F_{s}(\lambda)-F_{s}(-\lambda)
is constant in

0<|\lambda|<\delta
for some
\delta>0
, and that

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{|p(\lambda)|^{2}}{f(\lambda)}d\lambda<\infty

(7.2)

for some trigonometric polynomial
p(\lambda)
. Then as
n\to\infty
,

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{LS})-dF(0)\sim\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})-dF(0)\sim 2\pi f(0)/n.

(7.3)

Note that the proof of Theorem
7.1
presented in Adenstedt and Eisenberg
[
2
]
does not require knowledge of the covariance function of
Y(t)
.

Remark 7.1
.

The condition (
7.2
) essentially states that the function
1/f(\lambda)
is integrable, but allows the spectral density

f(\lambda)
to have polynomial zeros that can be removed by
|p(\lambda)|^{2}
.
It can be shown that (
7.2
) implies that the model process
X(t)
is nondeterministic.

7.2
Estimating the mean of continuous time processes

In this section, we explore the estimation of the unknown mean for continuous time processes. Specifically, we will now consider the following model:

X(t)=m+Y(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{R},

(7.4)

where
m
is the constant unknown mean of
X(t)
, and the noise
Y(t)
is assumed to be a centered mean-square continuous stationary process. Additionally, we assume that
Y(t)
has an absolutely continuous
spectrum and is nondeterministic. In this context,
Y(t)
has a spectral density
f(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, satisfying the following condition:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\ln f(\lambda)}{1+\lambda^{2}}d\lambda>-\infty.

(7.5)

Then,
Y(t)
can be represented as a linear process with a time-invariant filter
g(t)
(see, e.g., Gihman and Skorokhod
[
21
]
, Section 4.7):

Y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}g(t-s)d\xi(s),\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(s)|^{2}ds<\infty,

(7.6)

where
\{\xi(s),s\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a real-valued orthogonal increment process satisfying

{\mathbb{E}}|d\,\xi(s)|^{2}=\sigma^{2}ds
.

The problem of interest is the estimation of the unknown mean
m
using unbiased linear estimators
\widehat{m}_{T}
, based on a random sample

\{X(t),\,0\leq t\leq T\},

(7.7)

and the efficiency of the LSE relative to the BLUE.

The next theorem provides a formula for BLUE of the unknown mean
m

for a class of nondeterministic processes (see Grenander
[
30
]
, p. 189, and Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
, Section 11.4).

Theorem 7.2
.

Let the noise process
Y(t)
be a nondeterministic linear process with a time-invariant filter
g(t)
and spectral density
f(\lambda)

satisfying the following conditions:

(a)

The spectral density
f(\lambda)
is positive at
\lambda=0
, i.e.,
f(0)>0
.

(b)

The filter
g(t)
is such that
g(t)=(1+t^{2})^{-1}\cdot O(1)
as
t\to\infty
.

Then, there exists a constant
\gamma
such that

\widehat{m}_{BLU}=\gamma\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{X}(t)dt,

(7.8)

where
\widehat{X}(t)
is the projection of
X(t)
onto the space

L^{2}_{X}(0,T)
.

In the continuous time case, the LSE
\widehat{m}_{LS}

of
m
is given by

\widehat{m}_{LS}=\bar{X}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}X(t)dt.

(7.9)

The next theorem shows that the LSE is asymptotically efficient relative to BLUE (see Grenander
[
30
]
, p. 248, and Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
, Section 11.4).

Theorem 7.3
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
7.2
, the LSE
\widehat{m}_{LS}
of
m
defined as in (
7.9
) is asymptotically efficient relative to BLUE
m_{BLU}
of
m
. Specifically, the following relations hold as
T\to\infty
:

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{LS})\sim\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})\sim 2\pi f(0)/T.

(7.10)

Remark 7.2
.

The results of Theorems
7.2
and
7.3
remain valid if condition (b) in
7.2
is replaced by the following (see Grenander
[
30
]
, p. 248):

\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{3}|g(u)|^{2}du<\infty.

(7.11)

Now, assume that the noise
Y(t)
in (
7.4
) is specified by the spectral function
F(\lambda)
of the form (
7.1
) with

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

The following theorem is a continuous analogue of Theorem
7.1
(see Adenstedt and Eisenberg
[
2
]
).

Theorem 7.4
.

Let the noise process
Y(t)
have spectral function
F(\lambda)

given by equation (
7.1
) with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
The following assertions hold.

(a)

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{LS})=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=dF(0)
.

(b)

Assume in addition that
f
is positive and continuous at
\lambda=0
, that
F_{s}(\lambda)-F_{s}(-\lambda)
is constant in

0<|\lambda|<\delta
for some
\delta>0
, and that

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{|p(\lambda)|^{2}}{(1+\lambda^{2})^{n}f(\lambda)}d\lambda<\infty

(7.12)

for some positive integer
n
and function
p(\lambda)
of the form:

p(\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}\exp(-it_{k}\lambda).

Then, as
T\to\infty
,

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{LS})-dF(0)\sim\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})-dF(0)\sim 2\pi f(0)/T.

(7.13)

Remark 7.3
.

The condition (
7.12
) requires that the spectral density
f(\lambda)
does not decrease too rapidly as
|\lambda|
approaches infinity. It also allows for the presence of zeros in
f(\lambda)
that can be removed by
|p(\lambda)|^{2}
. Additionally, it can be shown that (
7.12
) implies that the model process
X(t)
is nondeterministic.

7.3
Estimating the mean within a Hilbert space framework

In this section, we will examine the estimation problem within a Hilbert space framework, without imposing restrictions on the index set or the set of observations.
Specifically, we consider the model:

X(t)=m\beta(t)+Y(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{U},

(7.14)

where
\mathbb{U}
is an arbitrary index set,
m
is the unknown regression constant,
\beta(t)
is a known regression term, and the noise
Y(t)

is a zero-mean, second-order process with a covariance function
R(t,s)=\mathbb{E}[Y(t)Y(s)]
,
t,s\in\mathbb{U}
.

The problem of interest is finding the BLUE for the unknown regression constant

m
based on a random sample
\{X(t),t\in\mathbb{S}\}
, where
\mathbb{S}

is a fixed subset of
\mathbb{U}
.

Note that the previous sections discussed the cases where
\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathbb{S}=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
or
[0,T]
, with
\beta(t)\equiv 1
.

Denote by
\mathbb{P}_{m}
the probability measure on the sample path space
\Omega

for a process that is equal in law to
m\beta(t)+Y(t)
. Then, for
\omega\in\Omega
,
we regard
X(t,\omega)=Y(t,\omega)=\omega(t)
as the evaluation
of the sample path at point
t
. The processes
Y(t)
and
X(t)
differ only in that
Y(t)

is treated as an element of the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
, whereas
X(t)
is considered
as an element of the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{m})
. This approach allows us to focus on a single set of sample paths.

An estimator
\widehat{m}(\omega)

for
m
is now a function of the sample path. It is unbiased if

\int_{\Omega}\widehat{m}(\omega)d\mathbb{P}_{m}=m
, and it is linear if it lies within the
subspace
L^{2}_{\mathbb{S}}(X)
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{m})
, which is spanned by the family

\{X(t):t\in\mathbb{S}\}
. (For the definition of the space
L^{2}_{\mathbb{S}}(X)
, we refer to Section
9.1
.)

We first present an extension of Proposition
3.5
, which provides the solution
of Szegő’s extremum problem in an abstract Hilbert space setting
(see, e.g., Simon
[
53
]
, p. 17).

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space equipped with an inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
and a norm
\|\cdot\|
.
Let
\varphi\in H
be a non-zero vector. Then, the following holds:

\min\{||\psi||^{2}:\,\,(\psi,\varphi)\}=1\}=\frac{1}{||\varphi||^{2}}.

(7.15)

The vector
\psi
that achieves the minimum in (
7.15
) is given by
\psi=\varphi/||\varphi||^{2}
.
If
\psi
is restricted to lie in a closed subspace
H_{1}
, and
P
is the projection onto
H_{1}
, then the minimum becomes
1/{||P\varphi||^{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{0}=\varphi/||\varphi||^{2}
. If
(\psi,\varphi)=1
, then we have

(\psi-\psi_{0},\varphi)=0
. Consequently, it follows that
||\psi||^{2}=||\psi_{0}||^{2}+||\psi-\psi_{0}||^{2}
. Thus, the minimum occurs at
\psi=\psi_{0}
, sine
||\psi_{0}||^{2}=1/{||\varphi||^{2}}
, thereby proving the result.

For the case of subspace, make
H_{1}
the Hilbert space and note that for
\psi\in H_{1}
,
we have
(\psi,\varphi)=(\psi,P\varphi)=1
, and
P\varphi\in H_{1}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Using Proposition
7.1
, we can now state the following result regarding the BLUE for the unknown parameter
m
in the model described in (
7.14
) (see also Adenstedt and Eisenberg
[
2
]
).

Theorem 7.5
.

Let the model
X(t)
be defined as in equation (
7.14
) with the unknown parameter
m
.
Then the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
for
m
, based on observations
\{X(t),t\in\mathbb{S}\}
, satisfies the following relationships:

\widehat{m}_{BLU}=\frac{\varphi(\omega)}{||\varphi||^{2}},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=\frac{1}{||\varphi||^{2}},

(7.16)

where
\varphi
is the solution of the equation

\mathbb{E}[Y(t)\varphi]=\beta(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{S},\,\,\varphi\in H_{\mathbb{S}}(Y).

(7.17)

If the equation in (
7.17
) has no solution, then
\widehat{m}_{BLU}=m
, indicating that
m
is perfectly estimable.

Remark 7.4
.

Theorem
7.5
can be used to show that the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
is also the maximum likelihood estimator when the process
Y(t)
is Gaussian (see Adenstedt and Eisenberg
[
2
]
for details)
.

Calculating the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
, or equivalently solving equation (
7.17
), is often a challenging problem in most practical situations.
Therefore, appropriate approximations are necessary.
We outline a procedure that, in the spirit of the Cramér-Rao inequality,
provides a lower bound for the variance
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
.

Denote by
L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(Y)
the Hilbert space spanned by the family
\{Y(t),t\in\mathbb{U}\}
. Note that
L^{2}_{\mathbb{S}}(X)
is a subspace of
L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(Y)
.
While solving the equation (
7.17
) may be challenging, it may be easier to construct a function
\tilde{\varphi}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(Y)
that satisfies the equation:

\mathbb{E}[Y(t)\tilde{\varphi}]=\beta(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{S}.

(7.18)

It is important to note that such a function
\tilde{\varphi}
exists if and only if
m
is not perfectly estimable from observations over
\mathbb{S}
. Furthermore,
\tilde{\varphi}
is not unique.

If
P
is the projection operator onto
L^{2}_{\mathbb{S}}(Y)
and
\tilde{\varphi}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{U}}(Y)
satisfies equation (
7.18
), then we have

\mathbb{E}[Y(t)P\tilde{\varphi}]=\mathbb{E}[Y(t)\tilde{\varphi}]=\beta(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{S}.

(7.19)

This implies that
\varphi=P\tilde{\varphi}
is the solution to equation (
7.17
).

Furthermore, taking into account that
||\varphi||=||P\tilde{\varphi}||\leq||\tilde{\varphi}||
,
we can deduce the following result.

Theorem 7.6
.

If
\tilde{\varphi}
is in
H_{\mathbb{U}}(Y)
and satisfies equation (
7.18
), then

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})\geq{1}/{||\tilde{\varphi}||^{2}}.

(7.20)

If no such
\tilde{\varphi}
exists, then
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=0
.

Remark 7.5
.

Using the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) technique and the projection theorem in abstract Hilbert spaces (see Section
9.1
), Parzen
[
46
]
provided a general solution to the problem of minimum variance unbiased linear estimation for the following general model:

X(t)=m(t)+Y(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{U},

(7.21)

where
\mathbb{U}
is an arbitrary index set,
Y(t)

is a zero-mean, second-order process with a known covariance function
R(t,s)=\mathbb{E}[Y(t)Y(s)]
,
t,s\in\mathbb{U}
, and
the unknown mean-value function
m(t):=\mathbb{E}[X(t)]
.

Specifically, let
H(R)
be the RKHS generated by the
covariance function
R(t,s)
(see Section
9.1
).
We assume that the unknown mean function
m(t)
belongs to a known class
M
, which is a subset of
H(R)
.
A random variable
(h,X)_{R}\in L^{2}_{\mathbb{S}}(X)
, with
h\in H(R)
, is said to be an unbiased linear estimator for
m(t)
if the following condition holds:

\mathbb{E}[(h,X)_{R}]=(h,m)_{R}=m(t)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptm\in M.

(7.22)

In Parzen
[
46
]
, it was proven
that the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{BLU}(t)
for
m(t)
is given by the following formula:

\widehat{m}_{BLU}(t)=(P_{\bar{M}}R(\cdot,t),X)_{R},

(7.23)

where
\bar{M}
represents the smallest Hilbert subspace of
H(R)
that contains
M
, and
P_{\bar{M}}R(\cdot,t)
is the projection of
R(\cdot,t)
onto the space
\bar{M}
.

In particular, for the model described by equation (
7.14
), when
m(t)
is expressed as
m(t)=m\beta(t)
, where
\beta(t)
is a known function and
m
is a constant to be estimated based on a random sample
\{X(t),t\in[0,T]\}
, the following formulas for the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
of
m
and for the variance of the BLUE were derived:

\widehat{m}_{BLU}=\frac{(\beta,X)_{R}}{||\beta||_{R}^{2}},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=\frac{1}{||\beta||_{R}^{2}},

(7.24)

where
(\beta,X)_{R}
is the random variable from
L^{2}_{\mathbb{S}}(X)
corresponding to
\beta\in H(R)
, and
||\cdot||_{R}
is the the norm of the RKHS
H(R)
(for details, see Parzen
[
46
]
, pp. 459–462).

Remark 7.6
.

Consider the model defined by equation (
7.14
) with
\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{R}
(continuous time), observed for
t\in[0,T]
. We assume that the process
Y(t)
is mean-square continuous and that the regression function
\beta(t)
lies within the range of the covariance function
R(t,s)=\mathbb{E}[Y(t)Y(s)]
. Thus, for some function
a(s)
, where
s\in[0,T]
, we have

\beta(t)=\int_{0}^{T}R(t,s)a(s)ds,\hskip 10.00002pt0\leq t\leq T.

(7.25)

This relation yields

\mathbb{E}\left[Y(t)\int_{0}^{T}a(s)Y(s)ds\right]=\beta(t),\hskip 10.00002pt0\leq t\leq T.

(7.26)

Therefore, the function

\psi_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}a(s)Y(s)ds

(7.27)

characterizes the BLUE based on the observations
\{X(t),\,0\leq t\leq T\}
.
Observe that

||\psi_{T}||^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}a(t)\beta(t)dt.

(7.28)

Then, for the BLUE of the unknown regression constant
m
and its variance we have the following formulas:

\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{BLU}=\int_{0}^{T}a(s)X(t)dt\left[\int_{0}^{T}a(t)\beta(t)dt\right]^{-1},

(7.29)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=\left[\int_{0}^{T}a(t)\beta(t)dt\right]^{-1}.

(7.30)

These results can be compared to Parzen’s estimator, shown in equation (
7.24
), where we have
a(t)=\beta(t)
.

7.4
Pseudo-best estimators

The concept of pseudo-best estimators for the general model of the form:

X(t)=m(t)+Y(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{U},

(7.31)

where
m(t)
is an unknown deterministic function and the noise
Y(t)
is a centered Gaussian stationary process with a spectral
function
F(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in{\Lambda}
,
was introduced by Yu. Rozanov (see Ibragimov and Rozanov
[
36
]
, Section 7.3).

We are focusing on the special case where
m(t)=m
is constant,
and
F(\lambda)
is absolutely continuous
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure) with a spectral density
f(\lambda)
.

The concept of pseudo-best estimators is based
on the following observation (see Ibragimov and Rozanov
[
36
]
, Section 7.3, and Grenander and Rosenblatt
[
32
]
, Section 2.1).

Let
F(\lambda)
and
G(\lambda)
be two spectral functions, and let
L^{2}(F)
and
L^{2}(G)
represent the corresponding weighted
L^{2}
-spaces (see Section
2.4
for definitions). For an index set
I
, we denote by
H_{I}(F)
and
H_{I}(G)
the closed subspaces of
L^{2}(F)
and
L^{2}(G)
, respectively,
spanned by the family of functions
\{e^{ik\lambda}:k\in I\}
.
Specifically, we define:

H_{I}(F):=\overline{sp}\{e^{ik\lambda},\,k\in I\}_{L^{2}(F)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptH_{I}(G):=\overline{sp}\{e^{ik\lambda},\,k\in I\}_{L^{2}(G)}.

Observe that if
G(\lambda)\leq F(\lambda)
, then
L^{2}(F)\subseteq L^{2}(G)
and
H_{I}(F)\subseteq H_{I}(G)
.

Let the function
h(\lambda)
be an element of both
L^{2}(F)
and
L^{2}(G)
. Consider the errors of approximation:

\sigma^{2}_{F}:=\inf_{t\in H_{I}(F)}||h-t||_{L^{2}(F)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma^{2}_{G}:=\inf_{t\in H_{I}(G)}||h-t||_{L^{2}(G)}.

Then, we have

\sigma^{2}_{G}\leq\sigma^{2}_{F},

since the function that minimizes the error of approximation in
L^{2}(F)
produces an error that cannot be smaller than the error obtained when it is used as an approximation in the space
L^{2}(G)
.

We revisit the model
X(t)=m+Y(t)
, where
t\in\mathbb{U}
with
\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{R}
, and
Y(t)
is a centered stationary process that has a spectral density
f(\lambda)
.

Definition 7.1
.

A spectral density
\hat{f}
that satisfies the inequality
f(\lambda)\leq c\hat{f}(\lambda)
for
some positive
constant
c
is referred to as a pseudo-spectral density.
A pseudo-best estimator, corresponding to a pseudo-spectral density
\hat{f}
, is defined as the best linear estimator with respect to
\hat{f}
. It is also referred to as an
\hat{f}
-estimator.

Note that for the least squares estimators,
\hat{f}
= constant; for the BLUE
\hat{f}=f
; and for
estimators corresponding to spectral densities from the class
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g)

(see equation (
5.39
)),
\hat{f}=f_{\alpha}:=(2\pi)^{-1}|1-e^{i\lambda}|^{2\alpha}
.

Let
\sigma^{2}(\hat{f},f)
denote the variance of the
\hat{f}
-estimator for
m
, and let
\sigma^{2}(f):=\sigma^{2}(f,f)
represent the variance of the BLUE

\hat{m}_{BLU}
.

Pseudo-best estimators for regression coefficients
for discrete time models with spectral densities that exhibit polynomial-type zeros were examined in the paper by
Rasulov
[
49
]
. Specifically, for the class of spectral densities of the form:

f(\lambda)=(2\pi)^{-1}\prod_{k=1}^{n}|e^{i\lambda_{k}}-e^{i\lambda}|^{2q_{k}}g(\lambda)=(2\pi)^{-1}|P(e^{i\lambda})|^{2}g(\lambda),

(7.32)

where
q_{k}
are non-negative integers and
g(\lambda)
is a continuous positive
function, it was shown that the pseudo-best estimators corresponding to the pseudo-spectral density

\hat{f}(\lambda)=(2\pi)^{-1}|P(e^{i\lambda})|^{2}

are asymptotically efficient relative to the BLUE.

The asymptotic efficiency of pseudo-best estimators for continuous time stationary models was established in the paper by Rasulov and Kholevo
[
50
]
.

Consider the continuous time model as defined in (
7.4
), where the noise process
Y(t)
has a spectral density given by:

f(\lambda)=(2\pi)^{-1}|\lambda|^{2\alpha}g(\lambda)=f_{\alpha}(\lambda)g(\lambda),\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha>-1/2,

(7.33)

where the function
g(\lambda)
is bounded, positive and continuous for
\lambda=0
but may have
a finite number of power-type zeros at other points.

It is important to note that the class of spectral densities of the form given in (
7.33
) serves as the continuous counterpart to the class
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g)
, as defined in equation (
5.39
).

The following theorem describes the asymptotic behavior of the variance of the pseudo-best
f_{\alpha}
-estimator for an unknown mean
m
in the model defined by equation (
7.4
).

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
f(\lambda)
be defined as in equation (
7.33
), with
g(\lambda)
satisfying the conditions stated therein. Then the following relation holds:

\lim_{T\to\infty}T^{2\alpha+1}\sigma^{2}_{T}(f,f_{\alpha})=c(\alpha)g(0),

(7.34)

where the constant
c(\alpha)
is given by the following formula:

c(\alpha)=(2\alpha+1)\Gamma^{2}(2\alpha+1)\Gamma^{-2}(\alpha+1).

(7.35)

We define the class
\mathcal{G}
as the set of functions
g(\lambda)
that satisfy the following condition uniformly in
\lambda
:

|P(i\lambda)Q^{-1}(i\lambda)|^{2}g^{-1}(\lambda)\leq c<\infty,

where
Q(z)
is a polynomial with all its zeros in the left half-plane, and
P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}\exp\{zt_{k}\}
, with
t_{k}
,

k=0,\ldots,n
, being non-negative numbers, and
P(0)\neq 0
.

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
f(\lambda)
be defined as in equation (
7.33
), with
g(\lambda)
satisfying the conditions stated therein. If, in addition,
g(\lambda)\in\mathcal{G}
,
then the following relation holds:

\lim_{T\to\infty}T^{2\alpha+1}\sigma^{2}_{T}(f)=c(\alpha)g(0),

(7.36)

where the constant
c(\alpha)
is defined as in (
7.35
).
Moreover, the
f_{\alpha}
-estimator is asymptotically efficient:

\lim_{T\to\infty}{\sigma^{2}_{T}(f)}[\sigma^{2}_{T}(f_{\alpha},f)]^{-1}=1.

(7.37)

The condition in Theorem
7.8
that
g(\lambda)
be uniformly bounded can be relaxed to allow
g(\lambda)
to have integrable singularities. In this case, Theorem
7.8
extends Theorem
5.13
to continuous time models.

For
\alpha=0
, Theorem
7.8
implies the asymptotic efficiency of the LSE for
m
with significantly weaker restrictions on the spectral density than those typically assumed in such problems (see Theorem
7.3
and Remark
7.2
).
Specifically, the spectral density
f(\lambda)
may exhibit power-type zeros.

Remark 7.7
.

The results of Theorems
7.7
and
7.8
regarding
the model
X(t)=m+Y(t)
(
t\in\mathbb{R}
) with a spectral density as in
(
7.33
) can be easily extended to the following simple regression model:

X(t)=me^{i\lambda_{0}t}+Y(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{R},

where
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is a fixed constant (see Remark
6.5
for the discrete-time case).

8
Asymptotic behavior of the variance of BLUE for deterministic models

Summarizing the results presented in Sections
5
-
7
, regarding the asymptotic behavior of the BLUE for
the unknown mean
m
in the model:

X(t)=m+Y(t),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{Z}:=\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots\},

where the noise
Y(t)
is a centered stationary process with a spectral density function
f(\lambda)
,
we conclude that the variance

\sigma^{2}_{n}(f)
of the BLUE depends asymptotically only on the behavior of
the spectral density
f
near the origin.
Furthermore, for certain classes of nondeterministic models with spectral
densities that satisfy the condition:

f(\lambda)\simeq|\lambda|^{\nu}\hskip 10.00002pt(\nu>-1)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\rightarrow 0,

the variance
\sigma^{2}_{n}(f)
decreases hyperbolically (like a power):

\sigma^{2}_{n}(f)\simeq n^{-\nu-1}\hskip 10.00002pt(\nu>-1)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptn\to\infty.

The following question arises naturally:
Is it possible to increase the order of zero of the spectral density
f
at the origin to achieve exponential decay of the variance
\sigma^{2}_{n}(f)
as
n\to\infty
?

In this section, we address the question from a negative perspective. Specifically, we show that if the spectral density

f(\lambda)
vanishes only at the origin, it is impossible to achieve exponential
decay of
\sigma^{2}_{n}(f)
, regardless of the order of the zero of

f(\lambda)
at the origin.
Moreover, to achieve exponential decay of the variance

\sigma^{2}_{n}(f)
as
n\to\infty
, the underlying process
X(t)
must be purely deterministic. This means that the spectral density

f(\lambda)
should vanish on a set of positive Lebesgue measure in any vicinity of zero.

It is worth mentioning that a similar problem in the prediction theory of stationary processes has been considered in the classical paper by Rosenblatt
[
51
]

(see also the survey paper by Babayan and Ginovyan
[
4
]
).
The key difference is that in the prediction problem, the asymptotic behavior of the prediction error variance is determined
by the spectral density
f(\lambda)
over the entire interval

[-\pi,\pi]
, rather than just at the origin (see also Remark
4.1
).
Additionally, in Rosenblatt’s work
[
51
]
,
the technique of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle was used to establish the exponential decay of the prediction error variance.
However, this approach is not applicable in the case of ”BLUE variance.” Instead, we adopt the method developed by Babayan et al.
[
10
]
, which employs tools from complex analysis and measure theory.

8.1
Setting and results

Let
E_{f}
denote the spectrum of the process
X(t)
:

E_{f}:=\{e^{i\lambda},\,\,f(\lambda)>0\},

(8.1)

and let
\widetilde{\tau}_{f}:=\widetilde{\tau}(E_{f})
represent the generalized Chebyshev constant of the set
E_{f}
(for the definition of
\widetilde{\tau}(E_{f})
, see Section
8.2
).

The main results of this section are presented in the following theorems.

Theorem 8.1
.

If the spectrum
E_{f}
of the process
X(t)

is either an arc of the unit circle or the unit circle itself, then the sequence

\{\sqrt[n]{\sigma_{n}(f)},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
converges to a limit

\widetilde{\tau}(E_{f})\leq 1
. Specifically, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\sigma_{n}(f)}=\widetilde{\tau}(E_{f})\leq 1.

(8.2)

Theorem 8.2
.

The following assertions hold:

(a)

If the spectral density
f(\lambda)
is positive almost
everywhere in some neighborhood of zero, then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\sigma_{n}(f)}=1.

(b)

If the spectral density
f(\lambda)
vanishes almost
everywhere for
|\lambda|<\alpha
, where
0<\alpha<\pi,
then
\sigma_{n}(f)
decreases at least exponentially. More precisely, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\sigma_{n}(f)}\leqslant\cos({\alpha}/{2}).

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
8.2
(a)
we have the following result.

Corollary 8.1
.

A necessary condition for the variance
\sigma^{2}_{n}(f)
to decrease to zero
exponentially as
n\to\infty
is that the spectral density
f(\lambda)

vanishes on a set of positive Lebesgue measure in any neighborhood of zero.

Remark 8.1
.

Corollary
8.1
demonstrates that if the spectral density
f(\lambda)
only vanishes at the origin, it is impossible to achieve exponential decay of
\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)
, regardless of how high the order of the zero of
f(\lambda)
is at that point. In this context, it is important to describe the asymptotic behavior of

\sigma^{2}_{n}(f)
for the flat-zero and Pollaczek-Szegő models (see Example
2.1
and Remark
2.4
).

Remark 8.2
.

It follows from relation (
8.2
) that the question of the exponential decay of
\sigma_{n}(f)
as
n\to\infty
does not depend on the specific form of
f(\lambda)
but is determined solely by the value of the generalized Chebyshev constant
\widetilde{\tau}(E_{f})
.
Denoting
\gamma_{n}:=\sigma_{n}(f)/\widetilde{\tau}_{f}^{n}
, from (
8.2
) we infer that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{\gamma_{n}}=1
and

\sigma_{n}(f)=\widetilde{\tau}_{f}^{n}\cdot\gamma_{n}.

(8.3)

Thus, when
\widetilde{\tau}_{f}<1
, the error
\sigma_{n}(f)
can be decomposed into a product of two factors: one,
\widetilde{\tau}_{f}^{n}
, is a geometric progression, and the other,
\gamma_{n}
, is an exponentially neutral sequence (see Definition
9.4
).

8.2
Preliminaries

To prove Theorems
8.1
and
8.2
, we will need some auxiliary results involving Chebyshev polynomials and the Chebyshev constant for the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
.

First, we recall the definitions of the Chebyshev polynomial and the Chebyshev constant for the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
of monic polynomials. For the definitions of the classes
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
, see Section
3.1
.

Let
F
be a bounded closed (compact)
set in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. We define

m_{n}(F):=\inf_{q_{n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\max_{z\in F}|q_{n}(z)|.

It is well-known (see, e.g., Goluzin
[
26
]
, Section 7.1) that there exists a unique monic polynomial
T_{n}(z,F)
from the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
,
called the
Chebyshev polynomial
of
F
of order
n
, such that

m_{n}(F)=\max_{z\in F}|T_{n}(z,F)|.

(8.4)

Fekete
[
19
]
(see also Goluzin
[
26
]
, Section 7.1) proved that
\lim_{n\to\infty}(m_{n}(F))^{1/n}
exists.
This limit, denoted by
\tau(F)
, is called the
Chebyshev constant

for the set
F
. Thus,

\tau(F):=\lim_{n\to\infty}(m_{n}(F))^{1/n}.

(8.5)

The Chebyshev constant
\tau(F)
is a metric characteristic of the
compact set
F
.
One of the fundamental results of geometric complex analysis is the
classical theorem by Fekete and Szegő, which states that for any compact set
F
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, the Chebyshev constant, the transfinite diameter, and the capacity of
F
coincide, even though they are defined from very different perspectives.
Specifically, the Chebyshev constant of
F
characterizes the minimal uniform deviation of a monic polynomial on
F
, the transfinite diameter of the set
F
characterizes its asymptotic size, and the capacity of
F

describes the asymptotic behavior of the Green function at infinity
(for details, see, e.g., Fekete
[
19
]
, Goluzin
[
26
]
, Chapter 7, and Szegő
[
58
]
, Chapter 16).

The next lemma is analogous to the assertion regarding the existence of Chebyshev polynomials for the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
F
be an arbitrary infinite bounded closed set in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
.
Then, in the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
, there exists a polynomial

\widetilde{T}_{n}(z):=\widetilde{T}_{n}(z,F)
with the least maximum modulus on
F
:

\max_{z\in F}|\widetilde{T}_{n}(z)|=\min_{q_{n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)}\max_{z\in F}|q_{n}(z)|.

(8.6)

Proof.

Denote

\widetilde{m}_{n}(F):=\inf_{q_{n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)}\max_{z\in F}|q_{n}(z)|.

(8.7)

For a fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\{q_{n,k}(z),\,k\in\mathbb{N}\}

be the sequence of polynomials from the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
,
whose maxima of moduli on
F
tend to
\widetilde{m}_{n}(F)
as
k\to\infty
.
We fix
n+1
points
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n+1}\in F
and represent the polynomials

q_{n,k}(z)
according to Lagrange’s formula as follows:

q_{n,k}(z)=\sum_{\nu=1}^{n+1}P_{n}(z,{\mathbb{z}}_{\nu})\,q_{n,k}(z_{\nu}),

where
{\bf z}_{\nu}:=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{\nu-1},z_{\nu+1},\ldots z_{n+1})
, and

P_{n}(z,{\bf z}_{\nu}):=\frac{(z-z_{1})(z-z_{2})\cdots(z-z_{\nu-1})(z-z_{\nu+1})\cdots(z-z_{n+1})}{(z_{\nu}-z_{1})(z_{\nu}-z_{2})\cdots(z_{\nu}-z_{\nu-1})(z_{\nu}-z_{\nu+1})\cdots(z_{\nu}-z_{n+1})}.

It follows from this representation that the polynomials
q_{n,k}(z)
are uniformly bounded in modulus with respect to
k
on any arbitrary bounded closed set of
\mathbb{C}
.
Hence, based on the known condensation principle (see, e.g., Goluzin
[
26
]
,
Sections 1.2 and 7.1), the sequence
\{q_{n,k}(z),\,k\in\mathbb{N}\}
contains a
subsequence of polynomials that converges uniformly on every bounded set of
\mathbb{C}
.
Additionally, the coefficients of this subsequence also converge. Therefore, the limiting function

\widetilde{T}_{n}(z):=\widetilde{T}_{n}(z,F)
is a polynomial from the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
,
and satisfies the condition (
8.6
). Thus, we have

\max_{z\in F}|\widetilde{T}_{n}(z,F)|=\widetilde{m}_{n}(F)=\min_{q_{n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)}\max_{z\in F}|q_{n}(z)|.

(8.8)

Lemma
8.1
is proved.
∎

Remark 8.3
.

The polynomial
\widetilde{T}_{n}(z,F)
serves as an analog of the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{n}(z,F)
, which has the least maximum modulus on the set
F
within the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
.
Consequently, we refer to
\widetilde{T}_{n}(z,F)
as the
Chebyshev polynomial for the set
F
with respect to the point

\xi=1
,
or simply as the
the generalized Chebyshev polynomial
.

The next lemma is an analogue, within the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
, of the assertion regarding the existence of the Chebyshev constant for any bounded closed set
F
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}

(see, e.g., Goluzin
[
26
]
, Section 7.1).

Lemma 8.2
.

For any bounded closed set
F
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, the sequence

\{\widetilde{\tau}_{n}(F):=\sqrt[n]{\widetilde{m}_{n}(F)},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\},

where
\widetilde{m}_{n}(F)
is defined as in (
8.7
), converges to some finite limit

\widetilde{\tau}(F)
. Specifically, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\widetilde{m}_{n}(F)}=\widetilde{\tau}(F)<\infty.

Proof.

Taking into account that the set
F
is bounded, we have

R=R_{F}:=\max_{z\in F}|z|<\infty.

Hence, observing that the polynomial
q_{n}(z)=z^{n}
belongs to the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
,
in view of (
8.8
), we conclude that the sequence

\{\widetilde{\tau}_{n}(F),\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is bounded:

\widetilde{\tau}_{n}(F)=\sqrt[n]{\widetilde{m}_{n}(F)}\leq\sqrt[n]{\max_{z\in F}|z^{n}|}=R<\infty.

(8.9)

Define

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{\tau}_{n}(F)=a\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{\tau}_{n}(F)=b,

and observe that
a\leqslant b
.
Thus, to complete the proof, we need to show that
b\leqslant a
.
To this end, for a given
\varepsilon>0
, we choose
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}

such that
\widetilde{\tau}_{n_{0}}(F)<a+\varepsilon
. Then, we have

|\widetilde{T}_{n_{0}}(z)|<(a+\varepsilon)^{n_{0}},\hskip 10.00002ptz\in F.

Next, observe that for any
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
the polynomial
s_{n}(z):=z^{r}[\widetilde{T}_{n_{0}}(z)]^{q}
of degree
n=n_{0}q+r

satisfies the conditions:

s_{n}(z)\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm and}\hskip 10.00002pt|s_{n}(z)|<R^{r}(a+\varepsilon)^{n_{0}q},\hskip 10.00002ptz\in F.

(8.10)

Therefore, in view of (
8.8
) and (
8.10
), we have

\widetilde{m}_{n}(F)\leq\max_{z\in F}|s_{n}(z)|\leqslant R^{r}(a+\varepsilon)^{{n_{0}q}},

and

\widetilde{\tau}_{n}(F)\leqslant R^{\frac{r}{n}}(a+\varepsilon)^{\frac{n_{0}q}{n}}.

(8.11)

Now, let the subsequence
\{\widetilde{\tau}_{n_{\nu}}(F),\,n_{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}\}

converge to
b
as
\nu\rightarrow\infty
. We apply the inequality (
8.11
) for

n_{\nu}:=n_{0}q_{\nu}+r_{\nu}
with
0\leq r_{\nu}<n_{0}
to obtain

\widetilde{\tau}_{n_{\nu}}(F)\leqslant R^{\frac{r_{\nu}}{n_{\nu}}}(a+\varepsilon)^{\frac{n_{0}q_{\nu}}{n_{\nu}}}.

(8.12)

Finally, by letting
\nu
tend to infinity and applying inequality (
8.12
), we find that
b\leqslant a+\varepsilon
. Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, we conclude that
b\leqslant a
.

Thus, Lemma
8.2
is proven.
∎

Remark 8.4
.

1. The quantity
\widetilde{\tau}(F)
is a metric characteristic of a closed set

F
, similar to the Chebyshev constant
\tau(F)
(see Goluzin
[
26
]
, Section 7.1).
Therefore, we refer to
\widetilde{\tau}(F)
as the
Chebyshev constant of the
set
F
in the class
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
, or simply as the
generalized Chebyshev constant of
F
.

2. It follows from (
8.9
) that
\widetilde{\tau}(F)\leq R
. In particular,
for the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
we have

\widetilde{\tau}(\mathbb{T})\leq 1.

(8.13)

3. Using (
8.8
), it is straightforward to verify that
\widetilde{\tau}(F)

is a non-decreasing set function:

\widetilde{\tau}(F_{1})\leq\widetilde{\tau}(F_{2})\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm if}\hskip 10.00002ptF_{1}\subset F_{2}.

(8.14)

Additionally, from (

) and (

\widetilde{\tau}(F)\leq 1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for any }\hskip 10.00002ptF\subset\mathbb{T}.

(8.15)

, then we have

), for any set

that contains

, it follows that

\widetilde{\tau}(F)=1.

In particular, for the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
and any of its arcs

we have

\widetilde{\tau}(\mathbb{T})=\widetilde{\tau}(\Gamma_{\alpha})=1.

(8.16)

Note that for the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
, the Chebyshev constant
\tau(\mathbb{T})

[

\widetilde{\tau}(\mathbb{T})=\tau(\mathbb{T})=1.

The following result by Mazurkievicz
[
42
]
is crucial for proving Theorem
8.1
.
First, we introduce the key concepts and definitions.
A
continuum
is defined as a continuous, rectifiable curve in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
.
A
linear set

G
in
\mathbb{C}
is defined as any subset of a continuum.
The
linear measure

\mu(G)
of a linear set
G
is defined as the Lebesgue measure generated by the length of an arc of the continuum.

Proposition 8.1

(The Mazurkievicz Theorem)
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta=\delta(\epsilon)>0

such that for any continuum
\Gamma
of diameter
d

and any closed subset
F\subset\Gamma
, the following inequality holds:

M_{n}:=\max_{x\in\Gamma}|q_{n}(z)|\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{n}\max_{z\in F}|q_{n}(z)|,

provided that
\mu(\Gamma\setminus F)<d\delta
, where
q_{n}(z)
is an arbitrary
polynomial of degree
n
, and
\mu(e)
denotes the linear measure of a set
e
.

8.3
Proof of Theorems
8.1
and
8.2

Proof of Theorem
8.1
.

Based on the definition of the optimal polynomial
p_{n}(z,f)
(see Definition
3.1
), along with formulas (
4.3
) and (
8.8
), we can write

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)\leq\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|\widetilde{T}_{n}(e^{i\lambda},E_{f})|^{2}f(\lambda)d\lambda\leq\widetilde{m}_{n}^{2}(E_{f})\cdot\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\lambda)d\lambda.

Hence, by Lemma
8.2
and (
8.15
), we get

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\sigma_{n}(f)}\leqslant\widetilde{\tau}(E_{f})\leqslant 1.

(8.17)

Now we proceed to prove the inequality:

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\sigma_{n}(f)}\geqslant\widetilde{\tau}(E_{f}).

(8.18)

To this end, we consider a sequence of subsets
G_{n}
of
E_{f}
, defined by

G_{n}:=\{z=e^{i\lambda}\in E_{f}:\,|p_{n}(z,f)|>\sqrt{\sigma_{n}(f)\cdot\widetilde{m}_{n}(E_{f})}\},

(8.19)

and we denote by
\mu_{f}
a measure on the unit circle defined as follows:

\mu_{f}(G)=\int_{W^{-1}(G)}f(\lambda)d\lambda,\hskip 10.00002ptG\subset E_{f},

where
W^{-1}(G):=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\,e^{i\lambda}\in G\}.

It is clear that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\mu_{f}(G_{n})}\leqslant 1.

Next, for a given sufficiently small number
\rho
(
0<\rho<1
),
we can express the set of natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
in the form

\mathbb{N}=J_{1}\cup J_{2}
, where

\displaystyle J_{1}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\mu_{f}(G_{n})>(1-\rho)^{n}\},

\displaystyle J_{2}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\mu_{f}(G_{n})\leq(1-\rho)^{n}\}.

It is clear that at least one of the sets
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
is infinite.
We can assume, without loss of generality, that both
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
are infinite sets.
For each
n\in J_{1}
, we have

\sigma_{n}^{2}(f)=\int_{E_{f}}|p_{n}(z,f)|^{2}d\mu_{f}\geqslant\int_{G_{n}}|p_{n}(z,f)|^{2}d\mu_{f}>\sigma_{n}(f)\widetilde{m}_{n}(E_{f})\mu_{f}(G_{n}),

implying that

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\sigma_{n}(f)}\geq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\widetilde{m}_{n}(E_{f})}\cdot\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\mu_{f}(G_{n})}\geq\widetilde{\tau}(E_{f})(1-\rho).

(8.20)

For each
n\in J_{2}
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{f}(G_{n})=0.

(8.21)

Since
f(\lambda)
is positive on the set

W^{-1}(E_{f}):=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\,e^{i\lambda}\in E_{f}\}
,
the linear measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous
with respect to the measure
\mu_{f}
.
Taking into account that the measure
\mu
is also finite, we obtain from equation (
8.21
) that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu(G_{n})=0,\hskip 10.00002ptn\in J_{2}.

(8.22)

For
n\in J_{2}
, we define the sets
E_{n}:=E_{f}\backslash G_{n}
and use
(
8.19
) to obtain

|p_{n}(z,f)|\leqslant\sqrt{\sigma_{n}(f)\cdot\widetilde{m}_{n}(E_{f})},\hskip 10.00002ptz\in E_{n}.

(8.23)

Let
\varepsilon>0
be an arbitrary number satisfying

(1+\varepsilon)^{-2}\geq(1-\rho),

(8.24)

and let
\delta:=\delta(E_{f},\varepsilon)
be chosen according to the Mazurkievicz theorem (see Proposition
8.1
).
Then, in view of (
8.22
), for sufficiently large
n\in J_{2}
, we have

\mu(E_{f}\backslash E_{n})=\mu(G_{n})<\delta(E_{f},\varepsilon).

Therefore, based on inequality (
8.23
) and Proposition
8.1
, we can write

\widetilde{m}_{n}(E_{f})=\max_{z\in E_{f}}|\widetilde{T}_{n}(z,E_{f})|\leqslant\max_{z\in E_{f}}|p_{n}(z,f)|

\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{n}\max_{z\in E_{n}}|p_{n}(z,f)|\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)^{n}\sqrt{\sigma_{n}(f)\cdot\widetilde{m}_{n}(E_{f})},\hskip 10.00002ptn\in J_{2},

implying that

\sigma_{n}(f)\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)^{-2n}\widetilde{m}_{n}(E_{f}),\hskip 10.00002ptn\in J_{2}.

Letting
n
tend to infinity and using the inequality (
8.24
), we obtain

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\sigma_{n}(f)}\geqslant\widetilde{\tau}(E_{f})(1-\rho).

(8.25)

By combining (
8.20
) and (
8.25
) and taking into account
the arbitrariness of
\rho
, we derive the desired inequality (
8.18
).
The combination of (
8.17
) and (
8.18
) leads to (
8.2
),
thereby completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

Proof of Theorem
8.2
.

Both assertions (a) and (b) of the theorem are derived from Theorem
8.1
.
To prove assertion (a), we first note that the spectral density
f(\lambda)
is positive almost everywhere in a neighborhood of zero. Therefore, we can assume that
E_{f}\supset\Gamma_{\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha<\pi
, where

\Gamma_{\alpha}=\{e^{i\lambda},\,\,|\lambda|\leq\alpha\}
.

Let
f_{\alpha}(\lambda)
denote the contraction of
f(\lambda)
on the set
W^{-1}(\Gamma_{\alpha})
. Specifically,

f_{\alpha}(\lambda):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(\lambda)&\mbox{if \, $|\lambda|\leq\alpha$}\\
0&\mbox{if \, $|\lambda|>\alpha$}.\end{array}\right.

(8.26)

Taking into account the obvious inequality
f(\lambda)\geq f_{\alpha}(\lambda)

and Proposition
4.1
, we conclude that
\sigma_{n}(f)\geq\sigma_{n}(f_{\alpha})
.
Thus, in light of Theorem
8.1
and equation (
8.16
), we obtain

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{\sigma_{n}(f)}\geq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}(f_{\alpha})=\widetilde{\tau}(\Gamma_{\alpha})=1.

(8.27)

By combining relations (
8.17
) and (
8.27
), we complete the proof
of assertion (a) of the theorem.

To prove assertion (b) of the theorem, we can assume without loss of generality that
E_{f}=\Gamma^{\prime}_{\alpha}=\{e^{i\lambda},\,\alpha\leq|\lambda|\leq\pi\}
. Thus, the relation in equation (
8.2
) holds.
Therefore, to complete the proof, it remains to show that

\widetilde{\tau}(E_{f})=\widetilde{\tau}(\Gamma^{\prime}_{\alpha})\leqslant\cos({\alpha}/{2}).

(8.28)

To this end, consider the polynomial of degree
n
:

q_{n}(z)=\left[{(z+1)}/{2}\right]^{n},

and observe that

q_{n}(z)\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(1)
and
|q_{n}(e^{i\lambda})|=\left(\cos({\lambda}/{2})\right)^{n}.

Next, according to the definition of the polynomial

\widetilde{T}_{n}(z)
, with
E_{f}=\Gamma^{\prime}_{\alpha}
, we have

\widetilde{m}_{n}(E_{f})=\max_{z\in E_{f}}|\widetilde{T}_{n}(z,E_{f})|\leqslant\max_{z\in E_{f}}|q_{n}(z)|=\max_{|\lambda|\geq\alpha}|q_{n}(e^{i\lambda})|=\left(\cos({\alpha}/{2})\right)^{n}.

By taking the
n
-th root and passing to the limit as
n\to\infty
, we obtain (
8.28
). The combination of (
8.2
) and (
8.28
) completes the proof of assertion (b) of the theorem. Thus, Theorem
8.2
is proven.

In this section, we present several known concepts, results, and tools that are used
to state and prove the results presented in Sections
2
-
8
.

9.1
Hilbert spaces associated with second-order processes

9.1.1
Hilbert space spanned by a family of elements.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space with a norm
\|\cdot\|_{H}
and an inner
product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{H}
. Let
{\bf T}
be an index set, and
\{u(t),t\in{\bf T}\}

be a family of elements of
H
. We define the linear manifold
spanned
by the family
\{u(t),t\in{\bf T}\}
, denoted by

L:={\rm sp}\{u(t),\,t\in{\bf T}\}=L(u(t),\,t\in{\bf T}),

as the set consisting of all elements
u(t)\in H
that can be represented
as a linear combination:

u=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}u(t_{k}),\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\mathbb{N}

(9.1)

for some real constants
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in{\bf T}
.

We define the
Hilbert space spanned by the family

\{u(t),t\in{\bf T}\}

as the closure of
L
in
H
:

\overline{L}=\overline{L}(u(t),\,t\in{\bf T})=\overline{sp}\{u(t),\,t\in{\bf T}\}_{H}.

(9.2)

Note that the space
\overline{L}
consists of all finite linear combinations of the form given in (
9.1
), along with their limits in the norm of
H
.

Generally,
\overline{L}
is a subspace of
H
, and it is possible for
\overline{L}
to be equal to
H
. In this case, we say that the family
\{u(t),t\in{\bf T}\}

spans

H
.

Note that the family
\{u(t),\,t\in{\bf T}\}
spans
H
if and only if the only vector in
H
that satisfies the condition

(g,u(t))_{H}=0
for every
t\in{\bf T}
is
g=0
(see, e.g., Parzen
[
45
]
).

Proposition 9.1

(The Basic Congruence Theorem)
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be two abstract Hilbert spaces with inner products

(\cdot,\cdot)_{1}
and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{2}
, respectively.
Let
\{\varphi(t),\,t\in{\bf T}\}
and
\{\psi(t),\,t\in{\bf T}\}
be families of vectors that span
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, respectively.
Suppose that, for every
s,t\in{\bf T}
,

(\varphi(s),\varphi(t))_{1}=(\psi(s),\psi(t))_{2}.

(9.3)

Then, the spaces
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are congruent (isometrically isomorphic),
allowing us to define a congruence
V
between
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
with the property that

V[\varphi(t)]=\psi(t)\,\mbox{ for all }\,t\in{\bf T}.

(9.4)

Proposition 9.2

(The Projection Theorem)
.

Let
H
be an abstract Hilbert space equipped with a norm
||\cdot||
and an
inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
.
Let
M
be a Hilbert subspace of
H
, and let
h\in H
and
h^{*}\in M
. A necessary and sufficient condition that
h^{*}
to be the unique
vector in
M
that satisfies the following condition:

||h^{*}-h||=\min_{g\in M}||g-h||

(9.5)

is that

(h^{*},g)=(h,g)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for every}\hskip 10.00002ptg\in M.

The vector
h^{*}
that satisfies the minimization condition (
9.5
)
is referred to as the projection of
h\in H
onto
M
, and is denoted by
P_{M}h
.

9.1.2
Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS’s)

Let
H
be a class of functions defined on a set
{\bf T}
, forming a Hilbert
space with a norm
\|\cdot\|
and an inner
product
(\cdot,\cdot)
, and let
K(t,s)
be a function defined on

{\bf T}\times{\bf T}
.

Definition 9.1
.

The function
K(t,s)
,
t,s\in{\bf T}
, is called a reproducing kernel (r.k.)
of the space
H
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

For every
s\in{\bf T}
,
K(t,s)
as a function of
t
belongs to
H
; i.e.,
K(\cdot,s)\in H
.

(b)

The reproducing property holds: for every
s\in{\bf T}
and every

f\in H
,

(f(\cdot),K(\cdot,t))=f(t).

(9.6)

The Hilbert space
H
with reproducing kernel
K(t,s)
is called a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
and is often denoted by
H(K)
. The space
H(K)
is uniquely determined by conditions (a) and (b).

From the formula (
9.6
) with
f(\cdot)=K(\cdot,t)
, we derive the following important equality:

(K(\cdot,t),K(\cdot,s))=K(s,t).

(9.7)

Proposition 9.3

(The Moore-Aronszajn Theorem)
.

Let
{\bf T}
be a given set, and let
K(t,s)
be a symmetric non-negative definite kernel defined on
{\bf T}\times{\bf T}
. Then, there exists a unique Hilbert space
H:=H(K)
of functions defined on
{\bf T}
for which
K(t,s)
serves as a reproducing kernel. Specifically, we have

a)

the system
\{K(\cdot,t),\,t\in{\bf T}\}
spans the entire space
H
:

H(K)=\overline{sp}\{K(\cdot,t),\,t\in{\bf T}\}_{H};

b)

the inner product in
H
satisfies the condition:

(f(\cdot),K(\cdot,t))_{H}=f(t)\mbox{ for any }f\in H.

9.1.3
RKHS representation of
L^{2}
-processes

The purpose of the Hilbert space representation of an
L^{2}
-process is to identify specific Hilbert spaces of functions that are congruent
(isometrically isomorphic) to the Hilbert space generated by that process.

Recall the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
of all centered random variables
\xi=\xi(\omega)
defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
(see Section
2.4
).
Given an index set
\bf T
and a centered
L^{2}
-process
\{X(t),t\in{\bf T}\}

with covariance function
R(t,s),\ t,s\in{\bf T}
, let
L^{2}_{\bf T}(X)
denote the Hilbert space spanned by the family
\{X(t),t\in{\bf T}\}
:

L^{2}_{\bf T}(X):=\overline{sp}\{X(t),\ t\in{\bf T}\}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}.

(9.8)

Note that the space
L^{2}_{\bf T}(X)
consists of all finite linear combinations of the form

\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}X(t_{k})
,
t_{k}\in{\bf T}
, as well as their
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-limits.

Definition 9.2
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space with inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{H}
.
A family of vectors
\{f(t),\ t\in{\bf T}\}
in the Hilbert space
H

is said to be a representation of the process
\{X(t),\ t\in{\bf T}\}
if
for every
s,t\in{\bf T}
, the following holds:

(f(s),f(t))_{H}=(X(s),X(t))_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}=E[X(s)X(t)]=R(s,t).

(9.9)

In other words, the family of functions
\{f(t),t\in\mathbf{T}\}
represents
the
L^{2}
-process
\{X(t),t\in\mathbf{T}\}
if the Hilbert spaces

L^{2}_{\bf T}(X)
and

\overline{L}(f,{\bf T}):=\overline{sp}\{f(t),\ t\in{\bf T}\}_{H}

(9.10)

are congruent. The Hilbert space
\overline{L}(f,{\bf T})
is also referred to as a representation of the process
\{X(t),\ t\in{\bf T}\}
.

Since the covariance function
R(t,s)
is a symmetric, non-negative definite kernel,
we can apply the Moore-Aronszajn theorem (see Proposition
9.3
), to state
the following result, which shows that an
L^{2}
-process
\{X(t),t\in\mathbf{T}\}
can be represented by the RKHS generated by its covariance function.

Proposition 9.4
.

Let
\{X(t),t\in{\bf T}\}
be a centered
L^{2}
-process with a covariance
function
R(t,s)
,
t,s\in{\bf T}
. The following assertions hold.

(a)

There exists a unique RKHS, denoted by
H(R)
,
for which
R(t,s)
is the reproducing kernel.

(b)

The space
H(R)
consists of functions
f(\cdot)
of the form: for some
Y\in L^{2}_{\bf T}(X)
,

f(t)=\mathbb{E}[Y\cdot X(t)]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptt\in{\bf T}.

(c)

The inner product in the space
H(R)
is defined by

(f,f)_{R}=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}].

(d)

The space
H(R)
is spanned by the family
\{R(\cdot,s),\ s\in{\bf T}\}
.

(e)

The Hilbert spaces
L^{2}_{\bf T}(X)
and
H(R)
are isometrically isomorphic. In this context,
(f,Y)_{R}
denotes the random variable in
L^{2}_{\bf T}(X)
that corresponds to the function
f\in H(R)
.

9.2
Fejér-type singular integrals

Let
T
be a parameter that ranges over some set
{\bf T}
, which is
either an interval
(a,b)
with
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
or the set

\mathbb{N}
.
A set of functions
\{K_{T}(\lambda),\,T\in{\bf T}\}
is called a

kernel
if
K_{T}(\lambda)\in L^{1}(\Lambda)
for any
T\in{\bf T}
,
where
\Lambda=\mathbb{T}
or
\Lambda=\mathbb{R}
, and

\int_{\Lambda}K_{T}(\lambda)\,d\lambda=1.

(9.11)

A sequence of kernels
\{K_{T}(\lambda),\,T\in{\bf T}\}
is called an
approximate identity
if, with some constant
C>0
, it satisfies the following conditions
(see, e.g., Butzer and Nessel
[
13
]
, pp. 31, 121):

\displaystyle\sup_{T}\int_{{\Lambda}}K_{T}(\lambda)\,d\lambda=C<\infty\hskip 10.00002ptT\in{\bf T};

(9.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{|\lambda|\geq\delta}K_{T}(\lambda)d\lambda=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for any}\hskip 10.00002pt\delta>0.

(9.13)

Apart from (
9.13
) sometimes we use the following stronger condition:

\lim_{T\to\infty}\sup_{|\lambda|\geq\delta}K_{T}(\lambda)\,d\lambda=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\delta>0.

(9.14)

An example of the approximate identity is the classical Fejér kernel
F_{T}(\lambda)
defined for both the real line (
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
) and the periodic case (
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
), given by

F_{T}(\lambda):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2\pi T}\cdot\left[\frac{\sin(T\lambda/2)}{\sin(\lambda/2)}\right]^{2}&\mbox{ for $\lambda\in\mathbb{T},\,T\in\mathbb{N}$},\\
\\
\frac{1}{2\pi T}\cdot\left[\frac{\sin(T\lambda/2)}{\lambda/2}\right]^{2}&\mbox{ for $\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\,T\in\mathbb{R}^{+}$}.\end{array}\right.

(9.15)

Let
\psi(\lambda)\in L^{1}(\Lambda)
and
K_{T}(\lambda)
be a kernel.
Then an expression of the form

\psi_{T}(\lambda)=\psi_{T}(\psi;\lambda):=\int_{\Lambda}K_{T}(x)\psi(\lambda-x)dx

(9.16)

is called a
singular integral
(or
convolution integral
) corresponding to the function
\psi(\lambda)
.

If the dependence of the kernel
K_{T}(\lambda)
on the parameter
T
takes the special form
K_{T}(\lambda)=TK(T\lambda)
, it defines an approximate identity.
Kernels of this type, along with the corresponding singular integrals, are known as
Fejér-type kernels
and
Fejér-type singular integrals
, respectively.

Note that if
K_{T}(\lambda)=F_{T}(\lambda)
is the Fejér kernel, then
\psi_{T}(\lambda)
, as defined in equation (
9.16
), is an entire analytic function of exponential type
T
when
\Lambda=\mathbb{R}
, and a trigonometric polynomial of degree
T
when
\Lambda=\mathbb{T}
.

In the following proposition, we present an important and well-known property of the singular integral (
9.16
)
(see, e.g., Butzer and Nessel
[
13
]
, pp. 32 and 121, and Simon
[
55
]
, p. 139).

Proposition 9.5
.

If the kernel
K_{T}(\lambda)
in the integral (
9.16
) is an approximate identity and
\psi(\lambda)\in\mathbb{C}(\Lambda)
, then

\lim_{T\to\infty}\psi_{T}(\lambda)=\psi(\lambda).

(9.17)

9.3
Some properties of the geometric mean and trigonometric polynomials.

Recall that a trigonometric polynomial
t(\lambda)
of degree
\nu
is a
function of the form:

t(\lambda)=a_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\nu}(a_{k}\cos k\lambda+b_{k}\sin k\lambda)=\sum_{k=-\nu}^{\nu}c_{k}e^{ik\lambda},\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

where
a_{0},a_{k},b_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
,
c_{0}=a_{0}
,
c_{k}=1/2(a_{k}-ib_{k})
,

c_{-k}=\overline{c}_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,\nu
.

For a non-negative function
h
, we denote the geometric mean of
h
by
G(h)
(see formula (
2.22
)). In the following proposition, we list some properties of the geometric mean
G(h)
and trigonometric polynomials (see Babayan et al.
[
10
]
).

Proposition 9.6
.

The following assertions hold.

(a)

Let
c>0
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\geq 0
and
g\geq 0
. Then

G(c)=c;\hskip 10.00002ptG(fg)=G(f)G(g);\hskip 10.00002ptG(f^{\alpha})=G^{\alpha}(f)\,\,(G(f)>0).

(9.18)

(b)

G(f)
is a non-decreasing functional of
f
: if
0\leq f(\lambda)\leq g(\lambda)
,
then
0\leq G(f)\leq G(g)
.
In particular, if
0\leq f(\lambda)\leq 1
, then
0\leq G(f)\leq 1
.

(c)

(Fejér-Riesz theorem, see. e.g., Grenander and Szegő, Sec. 1.12)
. Let
t(\lambda)
be a non-negative trigonometric polynomial of degree
\nu
. Then
there exists an algebraic polynomial
s_{\nu}(z)

(z\in\mathbb{C})

of the same degree
\nu
, such that
s_{\nu}(z)\neq 0
for
|z|<1
, and

t(\lambda)=|s_{\nu}(e^{i\lambda})|^{2}.

(9.19)

Under the additional condition
s_{\nu}(0)>0
the polynomial
s_{\nu}(z)

is determined uniquely.

(d)

Let
t(\lambda)
and
s_{\nu}(z)
be as in assertion (c). Then

G(t)=|s_{\nu}(0)|^{2}>0
.

9.4
Weakly varying sequences

We introduce the concept of weakly varying sequences and
outline some of their properties (see Babayan et al.
[
10
]
).

Definition 9.3
.

A sequence of non-zero numbers
\{a_{n},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is said
to be weakly varying if

\lim_{n\to\infty}{a_{n+1}}/{a_{n}}=1.

For example, the sequence
\{n^{\alpha},\,\alpha\in\mathbb{R},n\in\mathbb{N}\}

is weakly varying (it is weakly decreasing for
\alpha<0

and weakly increasing for
\alpha>0
). In contrast, the geometric progression

\{q^{n},\,0<q<1,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is not weakly varying.

In the following proposition, we outline some simple properties of weakly varying sequences, which can be easily verified.

Proposition 9.7
.

The following assertions hold.

(a)

If
a_{n}
is a weakly varying sequence, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}{a_{n+\nu}}/{a_{n}}=1
for any
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
.

(b)

If
a_{n}
is such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=a\neq 0
,
then
a_{n}
is a weakly varying sequence.

(c)

If
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are weakly varying sequences, then
ca_{n}

(c\neq 0),

a_{n}^{\alpha}\,(\alpha\in\mathbb{R},a_{n}>0)
,
a_{n}b_{n}
and
a_{n}/b_{n}
are also
weakly varying sequences.

(d)

If
a_{n}
is a weakly varying sequence,
and
b_{n}
is a sequence of non-zero numbers such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}{b_{n}}/{a_{n}}=c\neq 0,

then
b_{n}
is also a weakly varying sequence.

We will need the following definition, which characterizes
the rate of variation of a sequence of non-negative numbers compared with a geometric progression
(see Babayan et al.
[
10
]
and Simon
[
53
]
, p. 91).

Definition 9.4
.

(a) A sequence
\{a_{n}\geq 0,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is called exponentially neutral if

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{a_{n}}=1.

(b) A sequence
\{b_{n}\geq 0,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is called exponentially decreasing if

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[n]{b_{n}}<1.

For instance, the sequence
\{a_{n}=n^{\alpha},\,\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is exponentially neutral because

\log\sqrt[n]{n^{\alpha}}=({\alpha}/{n})\log{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
.
The geometric progression
\{b_{n}=q^{n},\,0<q<1,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}

is exponentially decreasing because

\sqrt[n]{b_{n}}=q^{n/n}=q<1
.
The sequence
\{b_{n}=n^{\alpha}q^{n},\,\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\,0<q<1,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is also exponentially decreasing because

\sqrt[n]{b_{n}}=n^{\alpha/n}q\rightarrow q<1.

It can be easily shown that a sequence
\{b_{n}\geq 0,\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}

is exponentially decreasing if and only if there exists a number
q
(
0<q<1
) such that
b_{n}=O(q^{n})
as
n\to\infty
.

It is well-known that for an arbitrary sequence of positive numbers
a_{n}
,
the convergence
a_{n+1}/a_{n}\to a
implies the convergence

\sqrt[n]{a_{n}}\to a
. Therefore, if
a_{n}

is a weakly varying sequence, it is exponentially neutral.
However, the converse is not generally true (see Remark 3.7 in Babayan et al.
[
10
]
).

9.5
Slowly varying functions

We introduce the concept of slowly varying functions and briefly discuss their properties.
For a comprehensive treatment of this subject, see the monographs by Bingham et al.
[
10
]
and Zygmund
[
63
]
.

Definition 9.5
.

A measurable function
u:(c,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

(c\geq 0)
is called slowly varying at zero (in Karamata’s sense) if it is positive for
x
large enough, and for any
t>0

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\frac{u(xt)}{u(x)}=1.

Definition 9.6
.

A measurable function
u(x)
is called slowly varying at 0 in Zygmund’s sense if for
x
large enough, it is positive, and for any
\delta>0
, there exists a finite number
x_{0}(\delta)>0
such that
x^{\delta}u(1/x)
monotonically increases, and
x^{-\delta}u(1/x)
monotonically decreases for
x\geq x_{0}(\delta)
.

It is known that the class of functions that are slowly varying in the Zygmund sense is a subclass of those that are slowly varying in the Karamata sense.

Note that a measurable function
u(x)
is called
slowly varying at infinity
if the function
\tilde{u}(x):=u(1/x)
is slowly varying at zero.

For the next proposition see Zygmund
[
63
]
(Sect. V, Theorem 2.24).

Proposition 9.8
.

Let
0<\beta<1
and
g(\lambda)
be a function of bounded variation on
any interval
(\varepsilon,\pi)
, which is slowly varying at 0.
Then, for the coefficients
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
of the function
\lambda^{-\beta}g(\lambda)

(0<\lambda\leq\pi)
, the following relations hold:

\displaystyle a_{n}\sim 2\pi^{-1}n^{\beta-1}g(1/n)\Gamma(1-\beta)\sin(\pi\beta/2),

\displaystyle b_{n}\sim 2\pi^{-1}n^{\beta-1}g(1/n)\Gamma(1-\beta)\cos(\pi\beta/2).

2.3

(see Beran et al.
[
8

Proposition 9.9

\{X(t),\,t\in\mathbb{Z}\}

(9.20)

(9.21)

with

L_{f}(\lambda)=L_{r}(1/\lambda)\pi^{-1}\Gamma(2d)\sin(\pi/2-\pi d).

(9.22)

b)

f(\lambda)

(9.23)

where

(a,\pi)
for any
a>0
, then

r(t)\sim L_{r}(t)|t|^{2d-1}\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm as}\hskip 10.00002ptt\to\infty,

9.6
Approximation of the inverse of covariance matrix

We revisit the model
X(t)=m+Y(t)

(t\in\mathbb{Z})
,
where
m
is the constant unknown mean of
X(t)
that needs to be estimated, and the noise
Y(t)

is a centered, stationary process with a spectral
density
f(\lambda)
and a covariance function
r(t)
.

By equation (
1.8
) for the variance
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
of the BLUE
\widehat{m}_{BLU}
we have

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})=(\mathbf{1}^{T}R_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{1})^{-1},

(9.26)

where
R_{n}
is the covariance matrix and
\mathbf{1}^{T}=(1,\ldots,1)
.

Thus, to calculate
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{m}_{BLU})
, we need the explicit form
of the inverse
R_{n}^{-1}
of the covariance matrix
R_{n}
. This is a challenging problem that can be solved for only a few simple models. Consequently, an approximation of
the inverse
R_{n}^{-1}
of the covariance matrix
R_{n}
is required.

Observe first that the covariance matrix
R_{n}
is a Toeplitz matrix generated by the spectral density
f(\lambda)
.
Specifically, we have (see equations (
1.5
) and (
2.2
)):

R_{n}=B_{n}(f)=\|r(k-j)\|,\,\,k,j=1,\ldots,n,

(9.27)

where

r(k-j)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{i(k-j)\lambda}f(\lambda)\,d\lambda.

(9.28)

For large values of
n
, we anticipate that the Toeplitz matrix
B_{n}(1/f)
will serve as an approximation for the inverse of the matrix
B_{n}(f)
, denoted as
B_{n}^{-1}(f)
. It is important to note that the inverse of a Toeplitz matrix is generally not a Toeplitz matrix itself.
This expectation is based on the following consideration (see, e.g., Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
, Section 8.1).

Let
g_{i}\in L^{2}(\Lambda)
,
i=1,2
. Consider the infinite Toeplitz matrices

B(g_{i})
(
i=1,2
) generated by the functions
g_{i}
. Specifically,

B(g_{i})=\|\widehat{g}_{i}(k-j)\|,\,\,k,j\in\mathbb{Z},\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,

(9.29)

where
\widehat{g}_{i}
are the Fourier coefficients of functions
g_{i}
,
i=1,2
:

\widehat{g}_{i}(k)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{ik\lambda}g_{i}(\lambda)\,d\lambda,\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,\hskip 10.00002ptk\in\mathbb{Z}.

(9.30)

Then, we can form the product
B(g_{1})B(g_{2})
and use Parseval’s identity to obtain

\sum_{t=-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{g}_{1}(k-t)\widehat{g}_{2}(t-j)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{-i(k-j)\lambda}g_{1}(\lambda)g_{2}(\lambda)\,d\lambda,

(9.31)

which shows that the product
B(g_{1})B(g_{2})
is an infinite Toeplitz matrix generated by the product function
g:=g_{1}g_{2}
. Thus, we have

B(g_{1})B(g_{2})=B(g_{1}g_{2}).

(9.32)

If the inverse of
g_{1}
is quadratically integrable, then
B(g_{1})
has an inverse generated by the function
1/g_{1}
. Specifically, we have
B^{-1}(g_{1})=B(1/g_{1})
.

Now, let
B_{n}(g_{1})
and
B_{n}(g_{2})
be the finite (truncated) Toeplitz matrices
generated by the functions
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, respectively.
The relation in (
9.32
) does not hold for the matrices
B_{n}(g_{1})
and
B_{n}(g_{2})
.
In other words, the product of truncated Toeplitz matrices is not necessarily a Toeplitz matrix.

Thus, it is necessary to find an analogous form of (
9.32
).
To establish a relation similar to (
9.32
), Szegő proposed approximating
the trace of the product of Toeplitz matrices by the trace of a single Toeplitz
matrix generated by the product of their respective generating functions.
This approach is known as the trace approximation problem (see, e.g., Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
, Section 8.1).

More specifically,
let
\mathcal{H}=\{g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{m}\}
be a collection of integrable
real symmetric functions defined on
\Lambda
. Define

S_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\hbox{tr}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}B_{n}(h_{i})\right]\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm and}\hskip 10.00002ptM:=\frac{1}{n}\hbox{tr}\left[B_{n}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}g_{i}(\lambda)\right)\right],

and let

\Delta_{n}:=S_{n}-M.

The trace approximation problem involves identifying sufficient conditions that ensure

\Delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and estimating the rate of convergence of the approximation error
\Delta_{n}
to zero as
n\to\infty
.

For a comprehensive treatment of the trace approximation problem, we refer to
Chapter 4 in Ginovyan
[
22
]
and Chapter 8 in Grenander and Szegő
[
33
]
. Additionally, see the papers by Dahlhaus
[
15
,
16
]
.

Below, we discuss the idea that the Toeplitz matrix
B_{n}(1/f)
can approximate the inverse
B_{n}^{-1}(f)
in a non-rigorous manner.

Denote the entries of the matrix
B_{n}(1/f)
by
a(k-j)
,
k,j=1,\ldots,n
, which are the Fourier coefficients of the function
f^{-1}/(2\pi)
:

a(k-j)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{i(k-j)\lambda}\frac{1}{f(\lambda)}\,d\lambda.

(9.33)

For large
n
, by using Parseval’s identity, we can write

\displaystyle c_{k,j}

\displaystyle=\left(B_{n}(1/f)B_{n}(f)\right)_{k,j}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}a(k-t)r(t-j)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{t=-\infty}^{\infty}a(k-t)r(t-j)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{-i(k-j)\lambda}\widehat{a}(\lambda)\overline{\widehat{r}(\lambda)}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{-i(k-j)\lambda}{f(\lambda)}\frac{1}{f(\lambda)}\,d\lambda=\delta_{k,j},

where
\delta_{k,j}
is Kronecker’s delta, and
\approx
indicates an approximation for large
n
.
This leads to the following approximation of the quadratic form in (
9.26
):

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}^{T}[B_{n}(f)]^{-1}\mathbf{1}\approx\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}^{T}B_{n}(1/f)\mathbf{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)F_{n}(t)\,dt,

(9.34)

where
\mathbf{1}^{T}=(1,\ldots,1)
and
F_{n}(t)
is the Fejér kernel (see (
9.15
)).
If
1/f
is continuous at
0
, then by Proposition
9.5
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}^{\top}B_{n}(1/f)\mathbf{1}\longrightarrow 1/f(0).

(9.35)

Results on the inversion of Toeplitz matrices with generating functions
from the class
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(g)
, where
\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}

and
g
is a sufficiently smooth and positive factor, were obtained
by Bleher
[
11
]
and in a series of
papers by Rambour and Seghier (see
[
48
]
and references therein).
