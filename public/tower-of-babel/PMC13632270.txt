Multiscale characterization and distribution of the thyroid hormone transporter MCT8 in the adult and aged human brain

Jonas Rotter, Toni Kirmann, Jana Brendler, Larissa Anthofer, Nina Maria Wilpert, Stefan Hallermann, Heike Biebermann, Ingo Bechmann

Abstract

The local availability of thyroid hormones (TH) depends on TH transporters such as monocarboxylate transporter 8 (MCT8, encoded by SLC16A2). Mutations of SLC16A2 cause Allan-Herndon-Dudley syndrome (AHDS), a disorder characterized by severe neurodevelopmental and motor deficits. While MCT8’s importance in foetal brain development is well-established, its precise topographical, cellular, and subcellular distribution in the adult human brain has remained largely uncharacterized, limiting our understanding of which structures depend on MCT8-mediated TH transport throughout life and whether neurodegenerative processes such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) affect local transporter availability. In this study, we systematically mapped MCT8 expression across eight functionally distinct brain regions from 14 aged human body donors. Our multiscale approach combined immunostaining, automated whole-slide image analysis of over 13 million cells, multiplex RNAscope in situ hybridization, stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy, and single-nucleus RNA sequencing data analysis. We additionally applied standardized Braak staging to investigate whether AD progression correlates with alterations in MCT8 expression. We demonstrate robust MCT8 expression across brain barriers, glial cells, and in distinct neuronal populations. RNAscope confirmed a strong concordance between SLC16A2 mRNA presence and MCT8 immunoreactivity. STED and confocal microscopy localized MCT8 to plasma membranes, perinuclear regions, and axonal/dendritic compartments, indicating roles in both trans- and intracellular TH transport. MCT8 expression was preserved across Braak stages, with no significant difference between donors with varying degrees of neurofibrillary pathology. Our findings establish a comprehensive cellular and regional framework for MCT8-mediated transport in the human brain, mapping its specific anatomical distribution across regions crucial for motor control and cognitive processing. Stable MCT8 expression across Braak stages suggests that cerebral TH dysregulation in AD likely reflects altered hormone metabolism rather than transporter loss.

Introduction

Thyroid hormones (TH) have a crucial impact on the development and maintenance of physiological functions in the human brain. To enter various target cells in the brain, the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and cellular plasma membranes need to be crossed [], requiring TH transporters such as the monocarboxylate transporter 8 (MCT8), the organic anion transporting polypeptide 1C1 (OATP1C1) and multiple others [, ].

Among the different TH transporters, MCT8 is of particular importance due to its high specificity for TH []. During human development, MCT8 provides the principal route for TH delivery to the foetus, as it regulates TH transport across the placenta and ensures adequate TH supply to the developing brain parenchyma [, , ]. Within the foetal brain, this is reflected by widespread expression of MCT8 at the BBB, in leptomeningeal cells, the hippocampus, and the brainstem []. The importance of foetal MCT8-mediated TH transport is illustrated by MCT8 deficiency, also known as Allan-Herndon-Dudley Syndrome (AHDS) [, , ]. In AHDS, mutations in the MCT8 encoding gene SLC16A2 cause severe global developmental delay and movement disorders in affected patients due to local cerebral TH deprivation [, , ]. The severe clinical manifestations of AHDS emphasize the critical need for proper TH availability in early neurodevelopment, consistent with the severe neurodevelopmental deficits observed in conditions that limit overall foetal TH availability, such as congenital hypothyroidism and maternal iodine deficiency [, , , ].

Throughout life, TH continues to affect neurogenesis [, ], myelination [], and brain metabolism []. Dysfunction in its adequate supply leads to neuropsychiatric disorders, reduced adult neurogenesis, and may be associated with dementia, memory loss and impaired attention [, ]. Corresponding to these diverse effects, MCT8 expression in the adult human brain has been described at brain barriers, including blood vessels and choroid plexus cells [, , ]. Its expression has been extensively studied in the hypothalamus, where it is present in both glial and neuronal cells in the arcuate, supraoptic, infundibular and paraventricular nuclei [, ]. Moreover, MCT8 has been reported in tanycytes [], a type of specialized nutrient-sensing ependymal cells which send long processes deep into hypothalamic nuclei [, , ]. Recently, work by Wang and colleagues described the expression of MCT8 in pericytes, pyramidal neurons, subtypes of GABAergic interneurons, astrocytes and (myelinated) neurofilaments in the adult human motor cortex, basal ganglia and thalamus, while noting that microglia express OATP1C1, but not MCT8 [, ]. Highlighting the functional relevance of MCT8 for adult human cognition, common polymorphisms in SLC16A2 have been associated with inter-individual variation in cognitive domains such as attention, alertness, and planning in healthy adults []. However, the actual role of MCT8 for maintaining adult and aged brain TH homeostasis and its transcriptional regulation remains unknown []. Although essential for understanding MCT8 in normal physiology as well as in aging and disease, a comprehensive characterization across brain regions and cell types is still missing.

Given this link to adult cognition, MCT8 is of particular interest in the context of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). AD is a multifaceted neurodegenerative disorder and the most prevalent form of dementia that mainly affects the brain, causing a progressive decline in memory, language, visuospatial skills, and overall cognitive function []. Epidemiological studies have linked abnormal TH levels to AD, although individual studies failed to draw a conclusive answer in a definite direction: some report lower TSH and TH levels in AD patients [, , ], others higher levels [], and others no association []. Meta-analytic evidence points toward hypothyroidism as the more consistent finding, with a significantly higher prevalence of hypothyroidism in AD patients than in controls []. Whether this reflects a risk factor or a consequence of AD remains to be resolved in longitudinal studies. Mechanistic studies point to candidate pathways, including T3-dependent regulation of AD-related genes and TH-dependent modulation of microglial immune signalling, though their relevance to human AD pathophysiology remains to be established [, ]. Data on how MCT8 expression affects AD progression or vice versa are sparse. In a streptozotocin-induced sporadic AD rat model, TH supplementation improved cognitive impairment and attenuated glial neuroinflammation, accompanied by increased MCT8 expression, suggesting that MCT8 may be conditionally upregulated in response to elevated T3 [].

In this study, we addressed the knowledge gap of MCT8 expression across the human brain. By combining immunolabelling, RNAscope, automated image analysis, STED microscopy, and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data analysis, we comprehensively characterized the regional, cellular, and subcellular distribution of MCT8. We further assessed whether MCT8 expression changes with neurofibrillary pathology by analysing donors across Braak stages.

MCT8-expressing cells were present in all analysed regions, with distinct regional patterns in neuronal and non-neuronal cell populations and the highest neuronal proportions in the motor cortex and medulla oblongata. Detailed analyses across cortical and subcortical brain regions further identified region-specific expression patterns. Together, these findings provide the first comprehensive anatomical map of MCT8 expression in the adult and aged human brain, as well as an anatomical framework that may help explain the motor and cognitive impairments observed in individuals with MCT8 deficiency.

Materials & methods

Human tissue

All tissue samples used in this study were obtained from body donations received by the Institute of Anatomy in Leipzig. Use of these samples was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Leipzig (129–21 ek), in accordance with the Saxonian Death and Funeral Act of 1994, third section, paragraph 18, item 8. All donors provided written informed consent. After post-mortem examination and approval for further use, fresh tissue was obtained from different organs, including brain, thyroid gland and liver. Irrespective of gender, a total of 14 individuals with an age range of 64 to 101 years (mean 88.0 ± 9.0; median 88.0) and a post-mortem interval (interval between death and tissue retrieval) of 13–36 h were examined (Table ). We dissected brain samples representing complementary functional systems: motor function (primary motor cortex/precentral gyrus and cerebellum), sensory processing (occipital cortex), higher-order association and executive function (frontal and temporal cortex), learning and memory (hippocampus and entorhinal cortex), and homeostatic and autonomic regulation (hypothalamus and medulla oblongata). These regions also show varying susceptibility to neurodegenerative pathology, enabling assessment of MCT8 expression across both phylogenetically conserved and cortical structures.

Depending on the downstream application, tissue samples were processed either as fresh-frozen or paraffin-embedded tissue blocks. For standard immunolabelling, fresh tissue was placed into cryomolds (Tissue-Tek Cryomold), covered in OCT (Tissue-Tek O.C.T.), frozen on dry ice-chilled isopentane (ROTH), and wrapped in aluminium foil for storage in air-sealed containers at − 80 °C. Fresh-frozen blocks were cryosectioned at 12 μm thickness (Leica CM3050 S). The sections were then mounted on Superfrost Plus slides (Epredia), dried for 5 min at room temperature, and stored at − 80 °C until further use.

For RNAscope in situ hybridization and immunolabelling comparison, tissue was fixed with 4% phosphate-buffered formaldehyde (Roti-Histofix) for 24 h, washed with tap water and then further processed for paraffin embedding (dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin). Paraffin blocks were sectioned on a microtome at 12 μm thickness (Microm HM 400), mounted on Superfrost Plus slides (Epredia), dried overnight at 37 °C, and stored at room temperature.

For optimal MCT8 staining performance, various methods were tested, including different antigen retrieval protocols (citrate buffer, pH 6.0; TRIS buffer, pH 9.0), fixation types (100% ethanol, 4% phosphate-buffered formaldehyde) and embedding techniques (sucrose-protected cryopreserved, paraffin, polyethyleneglycole, OCT-fresh-frozen). We found that the epitope accessibility of MCT8 was best preserved when using fresh-frozen or shortly formaldehyde-fixed (≤ 24 h) tissue, as previously described [].

Antibodies and immunolabelling

For fresh-frozen tissue immunolabelling, the sections were placed in a − 20 °C freezer for 30 min and subsequently for 15 min in a 4 °C refrigerator for a slow equilibration process. The sections were fixed with 100% ethanol for 20 s and washed in cuvettes containing 0.02 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4. To prevent non-specific binding, normal donkey serum (NDS) at a concentration of 1:20 in PBS containing 0.3% Triton (PBS-T) was applied for 30 min, matching the host species of the secondary antibody. After a further washing step, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies (Table ) in 0.03% PBS/Triton and 0.5% NDS.

The next day, several washing steps were followed by incubation with the secondary antibody (Table ) for 60 min at room temperature, DAPI staining and treatment with TrueBlack (Biotium TrueBlack, Cat#23,007) for 15 s to block autofluorescence. Finally, the sections were coverslipped with Dako Glycergel mounting medium (Dako, Cat#C0563).

To compare staining performance and investigate potential topological or conformational differences in subcellular MCT8 labelling (e.g. due to suspected N-terminal capping/oligomerization []), we evaluated four MCT8 antibodies targeting distinct epitopes. These included three polyclonal antibodies—one N-terminal (Sigma-Aldrich Cat#HPA003353, RRID: AB_1079343), one C-terminal (Novus Cat#NBP2-57,308, RRID: AB_3342370) and a middle region antibody (Antibodies online Cat#ABIN1387834, RRID: AB_3750275) – as well as one monoclonal antibody directed against the N-terminus (Abcam Cat#ab302706, RRID: AB_3750276) (Table ). The polyclonal N-terminal and C-terminal antibodies had been validated previously []. We reconfirmed their specificity alongside the two additional antibodies using wild-type and MCT8 knockout mouse tissue collected during our previous study [] (Fig. ). The comparative staining performance of the four antibodies in human tissue is reported in the Results (Fig. ). Based on this comparison, the polyclonal N-terminal antibody was used for all regional and cellular MCT8 distribution analyses, including the automated whole-slide quantification. For the RNAscope and STED microscopy experiments, we used both the N-terminal and the middle region antibody, as the latter showed the most intense intracellular signal (Fig. c, d). The peak-to-peak quantification was based on the middle-region antibody alone. Additionally, double- and triple-stainings were performed using cell-type- or hallmark-specific antibodies to investigate various study-specific questions (Table ). Omission controls, in which the primary antibody was omitted while all other steps were kept unchanged, were run in parallel for every primary antibody to check for unspecific staining. Antibodies that did not yield interpretable signal in fresh-frozen tissue are listed separately in Table . Each antibody combination used for cellular characterisation was applied to sections from at least four body donors and two brain regions and yielded comparable and reproducible results.

RNAscope

RNAscope analyses were performed using the Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 (ACDbio, Cat#323,100) following the manufacturer’s standard protocol. Tissue samples were fixed in 4% buffered formaldehyde for 24 h and embedded in paraffin. Slides were baked at 60 °C for 1 h, deparaffinized, and subjected to antigen retrieval in retrieval buffer in a lightly boiling water bath for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating slides in hydrogen peroxide. Protease Plus (ACDbio, Cat#322,381) treatment was applied, followed by PBS washes. Hybridization was carried out by incubating sections with 1:50 diluted probe buffer (Table ) for 2 h at 40 °C in a HybEZTM II manual hybridization oven (ACDbio, Cat#321,710). Amplification and washing steps were performed in alternating order three times, followed by application of the tyramide signal amplification (TSA) fluorophores (1:750, Table , Opal 570/690).

Subsequent IF staining was performed by blocking non-specific binding with normal goat serum (NGS, 1:20 in PBS-T) for 2 h at room temperature, and incubating sections overnight with primary antibodies diluted in PBS-T containing 1% NGS. After several PBS-T washes, secondary antibodies diluted in PBS-T with 1% NGS were applied for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using DAPI for 12 min, and autofluorescence was quenched with True Black for 15 s. Finally, sections were coverslipped with Dako Fluorescence Mounting Medium (Dako, Cat#S3023).

Stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy

Super-resolution imaging was performed using an Expert Line Abberior Confocal and STED microscope equipped with an Olympus UPlanXApo 60× oil objective (numerical aperture of 1.42). The microscope system included a pulsed high-power STED laser module operating at a wavelength of 775 nm, with a laser power of 3 W and a repetition rate of 40 MHz and 3 pulsed fluorescence excitation laser modules for wavelengths of 485, 561, and 640 nm. MCT8 and Lamin B1 (labelled with Abberior StarOrange and StarRed, respectively; see Supplementary Table ) were sequentially imaged in two-dimensional STED mode with a 20 nm pixel size. Nuclei were stained with NucSpot® Live 488 (Supplementary Table ) and imaged in confocal mode. All channels were captured with a dwell time of 5 μs/pixel and a two-fold line accumulation, with system settings kept strictly constant across all experiments.

To quantify spatial relationships, fluorescence intensity line profiles across the nuclear lamina were extracted from the STED images using ImageJ (Fiji). For each nucleus (10 nuclei in total across two biological samples), multiple line profiles (three to five) were drawn across the MCT8 and Lamin B1 signals. Peak positions for each channel were determined by fitting a Gaussian curve to the intensity profiles, enabling the calculation of the peak-to-peak distance between MCT8 and Lamin B1. Multiple measurements per nucleus were averaged to obtain a single representative value per nucleus. Because this analysis was designed to visually map subcellular spatial relationships rather than perform statistical testing, the data are reported descriptively. We present the distribution of all individual measurements alongside the nucleus-level means.

Braak staging

For each brain donor (Table ), neuropathological staging of neurofibrillary degeneration was performed according to Braak and Braak [, ]. Hyperphosphorylated tau in neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) was detected by immunostaining using the AT8 antibody (Table ). Donors were categorized as Control (Braak stages 0-I, minimal or absent NFTs confined to the transentorhinal cortex), Braak Low (stages II, NFTs in the entorhinal cortex), Braak Medium (stages III-IV, NFTs in limbic areas, including the hippocampus), and Braak High (stages V-VI, NFTs in neocortical areas) (Table ). For the primary comparison, Control and Braak Low donors were combined into a "Control" group and Braak Medium and High donors into an "AD" group. Amyloid-β deposition was not staged in this cohort. The "AD" group designation therefore reflects the extent of neurofibrillary degeneration and does not constitute a neuropathological diagnosis of AD according to current consensus criteria.

Microscopy, semi-quantification and statistical analysis

Confocal images were either captured with an Olympus FV1000 laser scanning microscope and Fluoview software (Olympus; pixel size ranging from 0.10 μm to 0.62 μm/pixel) or an Olympus BX51 epifluorescence microscope connected to an Olympus XM10 digital camera and CellSens software (pixel size ranging from 0.16 μm to 0.64 μm/pixel).

For the quantification analyses, a Zeiss AxioScan slide scanner in combination with Zeiss ZEN Software 3.7 was used to create high resolution full slide scans at 20× magnification (pixel size: 0.346 μm/pixel). Scans were then imported to QuPath 0.4.3 and processed using a custom written script (see Availability of data and material). We used StarDist (version 0.4) for nuclei recognition and subsequently performed fluorescence signal thresholding by calculating the mean fluorescence intensity for each given cell and adding four standard deviation values to distinguish MCT8 signals from background. Depending on the colocalization of DAPI, NeuN, and MCT8 signals, a total of 13,664,502 cells were classified in a total of 78 slides. To map regional MCT8 hotspots, cell coordinates were converted into grey-scale density maps in QuPath using a 500-pixel Gaussian kernel downsampled to 20 μm resolution (see visual workflow in Fig. ). In FIJI (version 1.54), we applied a standard “Fire” Lookup Table to the density maps and merged them with low-resolution images of the original scans to colocalize morphological hallmarks with MCT8 expression hotspots. The raw cell classification data was exported for further processing using a Python (version 3.9) script calculating the number of classified and detected cells in each slide. We normalized these numbers by calculating the percentage of MCT8-positive cells within their respective target cell populations (total, neuronal, or non-neuronal). Where more than one section of a region was available from the same donor, the corresponding values were averaged so that each donor contributed a single value per region. Statistical comparisons were therefore performed on donor-level values. Regional differences were assessed separately for each of the three target populations using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test, with p-values adjusted for all 28 pairwise comparisons within each analysis. Differences in MCT8 expression between the consolidated Control and AD groups were assessed using Mann–Whitney U tests. Differences across the four Braak subgroups were analysed separately for each cell class using two-way ANOVA with Braak subgroup and brain region as factors. Statistical analyses and graph creation were performed in GraphPad Prism (version 11.0.0, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Information on the distribution of the raw whole-slide scan images can be found in the Data availability statement.

Gene expression analysis

To investigate SLC16A2 expression across human brain regions and cell types, we analysed publicly available snRNA-seq data from the human whole-brain transcriptomic cell type atlas [], accessed through the Allen Brain Cell (ABC) Atlas (dataset WHB-10Xv3, release manifest 20260415). The dataset comprises over 3 million single-nucleus transcriptomes sampled across around 100 anatomical dissections of the adult human brain from three neurotypical donors, with cell-type, neurotransmitter, and anatomical annotations provided in the atlas metadata []. Analyses were performed in Python using abc_atlas_access for data retrieval, together with Scanpy (v1.11.1), NumPy (v2.2.5), Matplotlib (version 3.9.4) and Seaborn (v0.13.2).

Expression was quantified from the raw (unnormalized) count matrices. A nucleus was classified as SLC16A2-positive if it carried more than one raw SLC16A2 UMI (i.e. ≥2 counts, to exclude background signal). Proportions of positive nuclei were computed (i) for each anatomical region x cell type, (ii) across major neuronal and non-neuronal cell classes, and (iii) across neurotransmitter-defined neuronal classes from the atlas annotation (each class reflects the primary neurotransmitter, with co-release combinations assigned to the primary class in the following order: histaminergic > dopaminergic > cholinergic > serotonergic > glycinergic > GABAergic > glutamatergic). Cellular proportions of SLC16A2-positive nuclei were calculated across anatomical regions, cell types, and neurotransmitter classes, excluding groups with <20 nuclei. Results

In this study, we characterized the spatial distribution patterns of MCT8 expression in adult and aged human brain tissue using a multi-scale analytical approach spanning four organizational levels: (1) cellular microenvironmental context, (2) cell type-specific distribution, (3) cytoarchitectural organization, and (4) regional comparative analysis across brain areas. To achieve this, we performed systematic immunohistochemical analysis of 78 brain tissue samples obtained from 14 individuals (Table ), representing eight anatomically and functionally distinct brain regions.

MCT8 antibody staining performance

To assess antibody specificity, all four MCT8 antibodies were validated in MCT8-knockout tissue, in which none produced a specific signal (Fig. ). In human brain tissue, the staining performance varied significantly by antibody. The C-terminal antibody produced only weak signals (Fig. a). In contrast, the polyclonal N-terminal gave the strongest and most consistent staining, showing both membranous and perinuclear signals (Fig. b). Antibodies targeting the middle region primarily labelled the perinuclear compartment but also showed immunoreactivity in ramified cells (Fig. c, d). The monoclonal N-terminal antibody showed robust signals with cellular staining profiles similar to its polyclonal counterpart (Fig. e). Specificity was further supported by SLC16A2 mRNA to protein colocalization (Fig. c). We therefore selected the polyclonal N-terminal antibody as the primary antibody of this study. The assignment of antibodies to the individual experiments is given in the Methods.

Consistent with previous studies, we found abundant expression of MCT8 at brain barriers, with strong MCT8 immunoreactivity (MCT8-ir) in endothelial cells, perivascular cells, astrocytes, choroid plexus epithelial cells and tanycytes lining the third ventricle (Fig. ). To separate neuronal from non-neuronal MCT8 signals and to place them in their anatomical context, we first assessed the spatial relationship of MCT8-ir structures within the cellular microenvironment using MCT8/NeuN double stainings (Fig. a). MCT8-ir outlined blood vessels and was occasionally present in ramified cells of the adjacent parenchyma. Some of the processes of these cells approached the vascular surface. NeuN-positive neuronal somata were scattered between the MCT8-ir vessels at variable distance, sometimes directly adjacent to them. However, we did not observe MCT8-ir processes directly connecting neuronal somata with the vascular surface. Processes contacting blood vessels instead originated from NeuN-negative ramified cells (Fig. a, right image, upper white arrow).

To assess MCT8 expression in cerebral blood vessels, we performed double-stainings using MCT8 and collagen IV (COL4) antibodies. MCT8-ir outlined the vascular profile along its entire length (Fig. b, left). Along these vessels, the COL4-immunoreactive basement membrane formed local bulges enclosing individual cell bodies, outlining them towards both the endothelium and the surrounding parenchyma. This position within the vascular basement membrane is characteristic of pericytes [, ]. However, since none of the pericyte-selective markers we tested yielded interpretable staining in our fresh-frozen material (Table ), we refer to these cells as perivascular cells in a pericyte-like position. Part of these perivascular cells displayed MCT8-ir surrounding the soma, whereas others showed no detectable MCT8 signals (Fig. b, enlarged details).

In addition to the rather homogenous, almost cylindrical signals that allowed for blood vessel identification, we found distinct cellular processes showing MCT8-ir contacting the outer surface of the blood vessels. GFAP-MCT8 double stainings identified these processes as astrocytic (Fig. c). Within the grey matter, only a limited number of GFAP-expressing astrocytes showed MCT8-ir, typically at vascular contacts. In contrast, MCT8-ir astrocytes were more frequently located within the subcortical and juxtacortical white matter, where they were situated amidst axonal structures.

In the choroid plexus, we observed strong MCT8 IF signals at the epithelial cell membrane facing the ventricle (apical surface) (Fig. d). Furthermore, we detected less intense signals at the inner side of the villus, which we morphologically identified as MCT8-positive stromal capillaries.

Along the wall of the third ventricle, Vimentin-positive cells with basal processes extending into the hypothalamic parenchyma showed MCT8-ir (Fig. e). Their Vimentin immunoreactivity and radial morphology identified these cells as tanycytes. They were located in the ventricular wall, and a substantial proportion of them co-expressed GFAP along Vimentin (Fig. e, left) – a combination of location and marker profile reported for α-tanycytes []. We therefore classified these cells cautiously as α-tanycytes. As the median eminence itself was not represented in the analysed sections, β-tanycytes were not sampled and no statement can be made regarding MCT8 expression in this subtype. At higher magnification, MCT8 signals were present in the tanycyte somata and at their apical surface facing the ventricle and continued into the basal processes (Fig. e, right) but diminished within a few hundred micrometres as the processes extended into the parenchyma.

To assess MCT8 expression in oligodendrocytes, we performed co-IF stainings for MCT8 and 2',3'-cyclic nucleotide 3'-phosphodiesterase (CNPase), a marker of differentiating and mature oligodendrocytes []. We observed only sparse colocalization of MCT8 signals with CNPase-positive cell bodies and proximal processes (Fig. f). Most CNPase-positive somata lacked detectable MCT8-ir and prominent MCT8-positive somata were typically CNPase-negative. We did not observe MCT8-ir in cells with microglial morphology. As the microglia-specific antibodies available to us did not perform adequately in the fresh-frozen tissue (Table ), we addressed this cell type separately by RNAscope instead (Fig. ). Both cell types are quantified in the single-nucleus transcriptomic dataset presented below (Fig. ).

MCT8 expression in neurons

MCT8-ir was consistently observed in cells with neuronal morphology. In addition, long, fibre-like structures in the juxtacortical and subcortical white matter exhibited MCT8-ir. To confirm their neuronal identity, we performed double stainings with several neuron-specific antibodies (neuronal nuclei (NeuN), microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2), neurofilament heavy chain (NF-H)) and subsequently employed markers of neurochemically defined neuronal populations, including glutamate decarboxylase 67 (GAD67), tyrosine hydroxylase (THy) and vesicular glutamate transporter 1 (VGLUT1). A systematic analysis of populations defined by neurotransmitters is provided by the snRNA-seq analysis presented below (Fig. ).

Double staining for MCT8 and NeuN confirmed neuronal MCT8 expression across all examined brain regions and revealed two major patterns of neuronal MCT8-ir (Fig. a). The first pattern was observed in large neurons with prominent membranous MCT8 signals encircling the cell soma and extending into both apical and basal dendrites. These neurons were predominantly found in the hippocampus, temporal cortex, and motor cortex, whereas fewer were observed in the frontal and occipital cortex. The second pattern was associated with smaller neurons exhibiting less extensive MCT8 immunosignals. Some of these smaller neurons appeared solitary, while others were located among larger pyramidal neurons (see also Figs. and ). Both types of neurons occasionally displayed intracellular signals with varying patterns, including granular staining within the cell soma that extended into the axonal compartment and a limited perinuclear staining pattern (Fig. a). Notably, the frequency of cells exhibiting perinuclear signals varied depending on the antibody used. Morphologically, MCT8-ir neurons varied in size, shape, and signal intensity throughout all examined cortical regions (see Methods). Among the cortical layers, layer V stood out with a row of neurons with triangular-shaped cell bodies, whereas layers II-VI contained occasional MCT8-expressing neurons with pyramidal and round cell bodies. We performed additional immunostainings using the axonal marker NF-H and the somatodendritic marker MAP2 to assess MCT8-ir in axonal and dendritic neuronal compartments and to examine the long, fibre-like MCT8-ir structures observed in the juxtacortical and subcortical white matter. The NF-H-immunoreactive structures occasionally colocalized with MCT8 signals, suggesting MCT8 expression at the cell membrane and axons of large projecting neurons (Fig. b, NF-H). Similarly, we observed occasional colocalization of MAP2 and MCT8 signals, suggesting MCT8 expression within or on dendrites (Fig. b, MAP2). The immunosignals often exhibited a punctate appearance and were primarily located on the outer surface of the examined dendrites and axons (Fig. b).

To further characterize the observed MCT8-expressing neurons, we combined MCT8 with markers of defined neuronal populations. Co-staining for GAD67, an established marker of GABAergic neurons, identified occasional MCT8-ir cells that were simultaneously NeuN- and GAD67-positive, indicating that MCT8 is present in a subset of GABAergic neurons (Fig. c). We further observed MCT8-ir in neurons that were immunoreactive for THy (Fig. d). THy-expressing cells in the human cerebral cortex represent a distinct population of local circuit interneurons, approximately half of which contain GABA []. Because THy expression alone does not confirm complete catecholamine synthesis, we refer to these neurons as THy-immunoreactive alone. A catecholaminergic or even dopaminergic assignment would require additional markers []. Finally, we examined the relationship between MCT8-expressing neurons and glutamatergic terminals. VGLUT1-immunoreactive puncta were observed in close perisomatic apposition to MCT8-ir neurons (Fig. e). Consecutive optical sections of a z-stack confirmed that individual puncta remained in direct contact with, but separate from, the MCT8-positive somatic membrane (Fig. e, z-stack). Because VGLUT1 is confined to presynaptic terminals and not to the somata they contact [], these images document apposition of glutamatergic terminals to MCT8-expressing neurons rather than co-expression of the two proteins. These findings suggest that MCT8-expressing neurons receive glutamatergic synaptic input.

Descriptively, values were lowest in the cerebellum and highest in the medulla oblongata and the motor cortex/precentral gyrus. Analysing the neuronal and non-neuronal compartments separately, however, revealed distinct regional patterns. While the absolute number of neurons was considerably smaller here, the ratio of MCT8-positive to all neurons was among the highest of all examined regions (Fig. d).

In the cerebellum, we primarily found MCT8 signals in blood vessels of the molecular and granular layer and the cerebellar white matter (Fig. g). Occasional ramified cells with astrocyte-like morphology were observed in the granular layer and white matter, whereas only very few neurons were MCT8-positive (Fig. g, left and right detail panels).

The hypothalamus exhibited the most complex and heterogeneous MCT8 expression pattern among all investigated brain regions (Fig. h). While non-neuronal MCT8-expressing cells were concentrated at the surface facing the third ventricle, we also observed some MCT8-ir processes extending deep into the tissue (see also Fig. e). Discussion

Multiple studies have established the pivotal role of MCT8 in enabling early human brain development [, ]. However, while some evidence indicates its continued presence in adult motor components [, ], comprehensive data on its exact expression sites and mechanisms in the adult and aged human brain remain scarce. This raised the question motivating our study: does MCT8 expression persist across distinct regions in the adult and aged brain? Our data indicate that MCT8 forms a continuous anatomical route for TH transport from the brain barriers to individual parenchymal cells and, within these cells, from the plasma membrane potentially towards the nucleus. We also provide an anatomical framework that may help in understanding the motor, cognitive, and autonomic features of MCT8 deficiency.

MCT8 expression at the brain barriers

MCT8 is expressed at brain barriers, which are a major site of restriction for TH entry in MCT8 deficiency experimental models [, ]. Whether they represent the principal limitation remains open: barrier-restricted deletion in mice does not fully reproduce the global knockout phenotype [], human MCT8-deficient cerebral organoids show impaired T3 uptake and altered induction of TH-responsive genes [], and OPCs require MCT8 at their own membrane []. Conversely, deletion restricted to murine GABAergic or glutamatergic neurons leaves hippocampus-dependent functions intact []. TH transporter expression at the brain barriers differs between rodents and humans [, ], limiting extrapolation from rodent models in either direction. In the human brain, our findings confirm that MCT8 is consistently expressed across all major brain barrier compartments, supporting their central role in cerebral TH transport.

Regional MCT8 expression in the human brain

Recent findings by T. Wang and Y. Wang showed MCT8 expression in neurons of the motor cortex, the motor thalamus, and basal ganglia – brain regions which mediate voluntary motor function and are key regions affected by MCT8 deficiency [, ]. Y. Wang et al. suggested a key role of MCT8 in maintaining TH levels in these cells, underlining the importance of proper MCT8 function in the adult human brain and potentially explaining the loss of voluntary motor function in MCT8 deficient patients []. In the motor cortex, we likewise observed large, layer V neurons with triangular somata and prominent apical dendrites among the most intensely MCT8-ir cells. Their laminar position and morphology are consistent with pyramidal projection neurons, although we did not apply a pyramidal-neuron-specific marker. This pronounced expression suggests particularly high TH transport demands in these neurons. Consistent with the involvement of descending motor pathways, we also found one of the highest proportions of MCT8-expressing neurons in the medulla oblongata, a region critical for both motor output and autonomic regulation [].

The clinical phenotype of MCT8 deficiency – including spasticity and childhood-onset parkinsonism, which manifest in characteristic features such as hypokinesia, dystonia and axial hypotonia – may reflect impaired TH signalling in the cortical and basal ganglia motor circuits where MCT8 expression is observed [, ]. The additional presence of bulbar symptoms and dysautonomia such as elevated resting heart rate and poor weight gain is consistent with the MCT8 expression we identified in the medulla oblongata [, , ]. In contrast, respiratory function and conjugated eye gaze are less severely affected, suggesting selective vulnerability of specific brainstem neurons to the loss of MCT8-mediated TH transport [, ]. The widespread MCT8 expression across motor cortex, basal ganglia, and brainstem regions is thus consistent with the multisystem neurological phenotype of MCT8 deficiency, underscoring the critical role of adequate TH transport for proper motor control and autonomic regulation throughout the central nervous system. These correspondences are anatomical correlates rather than explanations as our cohort is aged, whereas the phenotype of MCT8 deficiency originates in development.

MCT8 expression in the hippocampus

A notable finding was the pronounced MCT8 expression in hippocampal granule and mossy cell-like neurons. While the exact function of these cells remains incompletely understood, they appear to be involved in neuroplasticity, spatial and associative learning, and pattern separation – processes vital for episodic memory formation [, , ]. If MCT8 maintains optimal levels of TH in these cells, its expression could indirectly enhance synaptic plasticity and support cognitive functions such as learning and memory [].

Granule cells of the DG undergo adult neurogenesis, primarily in the subgranular zone []. The pronounced MCT8 expression we observed in these cells suggests a potential link to TH-dependent neurogenesis, as TH promote the proliferation and differentiation of neural stem cells []. While the extent and persistence of neurogenesis in the adult and aged human brain remains a subject of intense debate [], impaired neurogenesis has been proposed as a candidate mechanism underlying cognitive decline, psychiatric disorders [, ] and AD []. Therefore, altered TH transport in the DG could theoretically contribute to cognitive impairment through this pathway. However, independent of neurogenesis, in mossy cells of the CA4 region, MCT8 may facilitate increased TH availability, supporting granule cell modulation and hippocampal output during memory processing. Additionally, TH-mediated regulation of glutamate receptor expression may further influence synaptic transmission and neuroplasticity [].

Enhanced MCT8 expression was also observed in CA3, the subiculum, and the entorhinal and transentorhinal cortices – regions critically involved in episodic memory, spatial navigation, and cortico-hippocampal information integration [, ]. CA3 contributes to pattern completion via its recurrent circuitry, while the subiculum acts as a major hippocampal output relay [, ]. The entorhinal and transentorhinal cortices facilitate bidirectional communication between the hippocampus and neocortex, particularly during memory consolidation [, ]. The enriched presence of MCT8 in these areas suggests that TH transport via MCT8 may play a key role in supporting the metabolic and plasticity-related demands of these circuits, thereby contributing to the regulation of higher cognitive functions. Given the essential role of TH in neurodevelopment and synaptic function, impaired MCT8-mediated transport in these regions may contribute to memory deficits and disrupted hippocampal connectivity, as observed in patients with MCT8 deficiency [, ].

MCT8 expression in AD affected patients

Despite the possible role of MCT8 in preserving and supporting cognition and memory function, our observation of consistent MCT8 expression levels in both AD and non-affected individuals suggests that MCT8 availability is not significantly altered by AD pathology. However, previous studies report altered TH metabolism in AD, potentially involving impaired local conversion (via deiodinases) and glial dysfunction [, ]. Subclinical thyroid dysfunction, which increases in prevalence with age [], may additionally contribute to the altered TH levels reported in AD []. Moreover, alterations in the brain’s metabolic milieu or immunological alterations in AD may further modulate TH responsiveness or signalling. Notably, a recent study in an AD rat model showed that T3 supplementation restores cognitive function, with enhanced MCT8 expression facilitating hormone entry into the brain, possibly aided by improved cerebral blood flow []. These findings suggest that TH signalling deficits or dysregulation in AD likely results from altered hormone levels rather than MCT8 expression or insufficiency. While our data indicate that MCT8 expression is preserved across Braak stages and is therefore unlikely to drive TH dysregulation in AD, this does not exclude the converse possibility: individuals with constitutionally reduced MCT8 function, such as MCT8 mutation carriers, could in principle have lowered cerebral TH availability that increases vulnerability to, or accelerates the course of, age-related cognitive decline. This remains a hypothesis, as no direct clinical evidence currently links MCT8 mutation status to AD risk.

Intracellular MCT8 expression

The concordance we observed between MCT8-ir and SLC16A2 probe signals in hippocampal and postcentral gyrus cells is in line with previous observations of mRNA-protein correlation [] and supports interpreting the observed intracellular signals as genuine, rather than as an antibody artefact. Previously, MCT8 was thought to exclusively reside at the cellular plasma membrane [, ]. More recently, Salas-Lucia et al. showed MCT8 to be expressed not only at the cellular plasma membrane, but also in endosomes, non-degrading lysosomes, the Golgi apparatus and in the nuclear membrane using electron microscopy in murine tissue []. These observations are consistent with our findings in human tissue, although our results were acquired with an imaging technique of lower resolution. Nonetheless, the mean MCT8-Lamin B1 peak-to-peak distance of 30.7 nm measured by STED microscopy supports a localization of MCT8 at or near the nuclear lamina. Salas-Lucia et al. further suggested that MCT8 contributes not only to TH uptake, but also to intracellular TH distribution and transport to and into the nucleus []. These findings may contribute to explaining the etiopathogenesis of the AHDS and challenges in medication trials to date []. We speculate that MCT8 may similarly facilitate intracellular TH transport in humans, ensuring TH delivery to and into the nucleus.

MCT8 expression in oligodendrocytes and microglia

Wang et al. recently reported MCT8-ir fibres surrounded by MBP signal []. As MBP primarily labels the compact myelin sheath rather than the main oligodendrocyte cell body [, ], this indicates an association of MCT8 with myelinated fibres rather than expression in oligodendrocyte cell bodies. Our own MCT8/CNPase co-staining faces a similar limitation: CNPase localizes to the somata and non-compact myelin of differentiating and mature oligodendrocytes, but not OPCs [], and is itself a myelin constituent, so the sparse co-labelling we observed cannot reliably assign MCT8 to the oligodendrocyte soma rather than to the surrounding sheath. Whether mature oligodendrocytes express MCT8 protein therefore remains unresolved by either approach. At the transcript level, SLC16A2 was enriched in OPCs relative to mature oligodendrocytes, in line with the cell-autonomous requirement for MCT8 in OPCs [], while remaining detectable at low levels in the mature population. Direct spatial transcriptomic validation of SLC16A2 in human oligodendrocytes via multiplex RNAscope represents a valuable direction for future study.

The clinical relevance of MCT8 or SLC16A2 expression in the oligodendroglial lineage remains debatable. Although conventional MRI in individual case reports has suggested an apparent normalization of myelination with age [, , ], post-mortem and histological studies show persistent myelin deficits, including reduced myelin staining and a shift toward small-calibre axons [, ]. This indicates that the hypomyelination does not truly resolve. Whether these deficits contribute directly to the permanent neurological phenotype of MCT8 deficiency or represent one manifestation of a broader central hypothyroid state affecting multiple cell populations, remains unresolved [, ].

Microglia, by contrast, showed no detectable SLC16A2 by either RNAscope or snRNA-seq analysis. This aligns with prior reports that human microglia express OATP1C1 rather than MCT8 []. Consequently, microglial TH uptake in the adult human brain seems to operate independently of MCT8, an important fact, given the role of TH signalling in microglial immune function [].

Limitations of this study

A primary limitation of this study is the relatively narrow age range of our post-mortem cohort (64 to 101 years). The expression of TH-handling proteins, plausibly including MCT8, is dynamic across development [, ], and murine data indicate an age-related decline in neuronal MCT8 expression []. Our immunohistochemical findings therefore mainly characterize MCT8 in the aged human brain and should not be extrapolated to younger adults, children, or the developmental period. Consequently, while our data provide a strong anatomical framework, inferences regarding the developmental origins of the MCT8 deficiency phenotype are necessarily indirect. Furthermore, due to the nature of post-mortem material, our work is restricted to descriptive spatial analysis of MCT8 distribution and cannot capture precise functional roles or expression dynamics. Our Braak-stratified comparison is similarly limited by small subgroup sizes, which permitted detection of only large effect sizes. Moreover, donors were staged for neurofibrillary pathology only, meaning that cases in the Braak III–IV range may represent primary age-related tauopathy rather than AD [].

While our multiscale approach encompassed a broad range of functionally distinct brain regions, it did not include the subventricular zone (SVZ). Given the SVZ’s critical role as a lifelong neurogliogenic niche, investigating local MCT8 distribution and expression in this region represents an important possibility for future research. Relatedly, our Braak-stratified analysis was performed at region level and does not resolve effects restricted to individual hippocampal subfields, including the DG as a second neurogliogenic niche.

Finally, several cell-type-selective antibodies failed to yield interpretable signals in the used fresh-frozen tissue and were therefore omitted (Table ). Additionally, using automated analysis of IF stainings can introduce variability through potential over- or under-detection of labelled cells. To mitigate this issue, we conducted repeated, partially supervised analyses; however, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of inaccurate cell detection. To minimize any further technical or interindividual variability, all samples were processed using standardized protocols upon arrival in our institute.

Synopsis of the physiological MCT8 expression model

Our data suggest that:(i)MCT8 is expressed in a variety of regions in the adult and aged human brain.(ii)MCT8 expression ensures adequate TH supply via a transport cascade from the BBB to individual neurons.(iii)MCT8 contributes to transmembrane and intracellular transport of TH.(iv)Physiological MCT8 expression in the human brain might be essential for maintaining normal cognitive performance, motor coordination, and autonomic regulation.

Overall, we present the most comprehensive analysis of MCT8 expression across the human brain to date, demonstrating its widespread distribution (Fig. ). The observed expression pattern is consistent with previous findings and provides novel insights into brain regions where MCT8 expression has not been described before. The broad distribution of MCT8 in the human brain suggests a critical role not only in neurodevelopment, but also in maintaining TH homeostasis in the mature brain. By providing an anatomical framework, our findings may also help to explain some of the functional impairments observed in patients with MCT8 deficiency. Notably, we did not detect alterations in MCT8 expression under pathophysiological conditions.
