Project Overview & Executive Summary
This project demonstrated a heavy-duty engine converted from using diesel fuel to a flex-fuel engine that
can use any blend of gasoline and ethanol. The combustion system is an innovative concept that allows
very low cetane fuels to be used in a conventional mixing-controlled combustion (MCC) strategy without
auxiliary heating, changes to the basic engine structure, or advanced controls. This allows the engine to
maintain the operational characteristics of a diesel engine, such as excellent snap torque response, high
torque at low speed, high efficiency, knock-free operation, and combustion robustness. The concept uses
an actively fueled prechamber and high-pressure direct injector, both utilizing the same fuel. Ethanol is an
extremely successful, domestic renewable fuel that is widely available as gasoline/ethanol blends. Ethanol
has been unable to be used in heavy-duty engines due to its low cetane number and the fact that heavy-duty
applications struggle to be converted to spark ignition engines. The proposed concept delivered a 10% to
50% reduction in life cycle CO2 emissions compared to diesel when operating on E15 and E100,
respectively. The project was conducted in 3 budget periods:

Budget Period 1: Development of Prototype Active Prechamber Igniter
The effort focused on proof-of-concept single-cylinder experiments with an offset prechamber design.
Iterative Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) modeling and prototype design work developed an
integrated active prechamber igniter for single- and multi-cylinder testing. Lastly, rapid compression
machine experiments measured ignition delay and soot formation that allowed the CFD models to be
validated for gasoline/ethanol fuel blends. CFD simulations of the rapid compression machine experiments
were conducted to validate the chemical kinetic mechanism and soot models that were used throughout the
project to simulate gasoline/ethanol fuel blend combustion.

Budget Period 2: Single-Cylinder Prechamber Enabled Mixing Controlled Combustion (PC-MCC)
Testing & Operating Strategy Development
The effort focused on testing the prototype prechamber ignition system on a heavy-duty single-cylinder
engine. The operating strategy for the flex-fuel prechamber mixing-controlled combustion (PC-MCC)
engine was developed to cover the entire operating range of the engine. Additionally, the robustness of the
combustion process was validated and what hardware is required for production viability was determined.
The prototype PC-MCC hardware performed as expected on the single-cylinder research engine. The
engine was able to operate as flex-fuel, running on any blend of gasoline and ethanol, while delivering
diesel engine performance characteristics and efficiency. The PC-MCC operating strategy was developed
experimentally, which consisted of a single main injection with direct PC ignition assistance at low engine
loads. As the engine load increased and prechamber spark ignition became problematic due to high gas
pressure, a pilot injection strategy in the main chamber was implemented with advanced prechamber spark
timing. This operating strategy allowed full coverage of the baseline diesel engine operating space.

Budget Period 3: Multi-Cylinder PC-MCC Validation & Techno-economic Analysis
A flex-fuel PC-MCC engine based on a John Deere 9.0L platform was constructed and tested. The heavy-
duty multi-cylinder engine used the operating strategy developed on the single-cylinder engine. The multi-
cylinder PC-MCC engine operating on E98 showed diesel-like combustion characteristics, similar or lower
engine-out NOx emissions, but elevated UHC and CO emissions compared to the diesel baseline. Lastly, a
techno-economic analysis of the technology was conducted to assess total cost of ownership, identify first
adopter, and determine potential market penetration. For the targeted agricultural market in the midwestern
US, where E98 can be purchased from fuel distributors, an E98 PC-MCC engine that can deliver diesel
engine efficiency with a ~25% reduction in engine out NOx, can deliver a TCO savings of ~$45,000.

Technical Accomplishments by Task

Task 1 – Optical RCM Experiments & Modeling
Task #1 focused on collecting experimental data and conducting CFD simulations with real
gasoline/ethanol fuel blends. The experimental data collected were ignition delay and soot volume fraction
in fuel-rich homogeneous mixtures using an optical rapid compression machine (RCM). The experimental
apparatus is shown in Figure 1. The RCM used pneumatic actuation to drive a cam at high velocity. The
cam moves a roller-follower that rapidly compresses the charge in the reaction cylinder. The fuel/oxidizer
mixture to be tested in premixed in the reaction cylinder, resulting in a homogeneous chemical reaction.
The optical RCM has line-of-sight access in the reaction cylinder head and a HeNe laser is used to collect
line-of-sight integrated laser extinction data to determine soot volume fraction. The Beer-Lambert law and
assumptions about the optical properties of the soot formed are used to determine the soot volume fraction.
Details on these calculations were included in the quarterly project reports and technical publications but
are not included here for brevity.

Figure 1: (Left) Schematic of the optical RCM drive system. (Right) Schematic of the laser extinction diagnostic
 for measuring soot volume fraction in the burned gases.

The experiments focused on the use of the full range of gasoline/ethanol blends, from E10 to E100, as the
flex-fuel technology aims to allow the heavy-duty off-road vehicle to operate on any blend of gasoline and
ethanol. The experimental fuels are splash blended by volume, with E10 denoting 10% by volume fuel
grade ethanol and 90% by volume gasoline. The gasoline used in the RCM experiments is a commercially
available Blendstock for Oxygenate Blending (BOB). It was purchased from Gage Products and has an
anti-knock index (AKI) of 85. Thus, the E10 fuel has the approximate fuel reactivity of regular grade 87
AKI gasoline. The octane number of the fuel blends will obviously increase with ethanol content.

Figure 2 shows an example dataset from the RCM experiments. The unattenuated laser transmittance, I0, is
acquired just prior to the rapid compression process. In this experiment, the mixture is compressed to ~20
bar and then the piston stops at top dead center (TDC), which is marked by time zero. The mixture has an
ignition delay (ID) and then autoignites, going to high pressure and temperature. Shortly after ignition, the
fuel-rich mixture may form soot, depending on the equivalence ratio of the mixture. In this example, soot
was formed, as indicted by the decrease in laser transmittance. The amount of laser beam attenuation
reaches a near steady-state value in the burn gas, suggesting that the soot volume fraction in the burned gas
is essentially constant. The amount of laser attenuation due to soot particles is represented by 𝐼 ,̅ which is
acquired as the average in a ~100 ms window, to determine the steady-state soot volume fraction in the
burned gas. The equation in the inset of the figure shows how the soot volume fraction is computed.

In the current investigation, the RCM was operated with an air stream that contained 21% O2 and the balance
N2 and Ar. For these experiments, the compressed gas pressure (Pc) was fixed at 20 bar for all fuel blends
and the compressed gas temperature (Tc) was varied to keep ignition delay in a window of ~25 to 75 ms
after top dead center. The compressed gas temperature was controlled with the amount of Argon in the air
mixture. Figure 3 shows ignition delay data for E10 and E100 fuel under these conditions in the RCM. In
this narrow temperature range, the ignition delay as a function of temperature is relatively linear.

 Figure 3: RCM ignition delay data as a function of compressed temperature at a compressed pressure of 20 bar
 for (left) E10 with a fuel/air equivalence ratio of 1.88 and (righ) E100 with a fuel/air equivalence ratio of 2.51.

Figure 4 shows the resulting steady-state soot volume fraction in the burned gas of the RCM as a function
of equivalence ratio for 4 different gasoline/ethanol fuel blends – E10, E30, E85, and E100. The fuels do
NOT form any measurable soot until an equivalence ratio of ~1.85, which is the critical equivalence ratio

for E10 at these conditions. As the ethanol content in the fuel blend is increased, the critical equivalence
ratio for forming soot increases, which is expected due to the oxygenated nature of ethanol, which reduces
its soot propensity. The critical equivalence ratio for E100 to form soot at these conditions is ~2.4, which
is significantly higher than E10.

 Figure 4: (Left) Steady-state soot volume fraction in the burned gas of the RCM as a function of fuel/air

The primary goal of the optical RCM experiments was to collect fundamental soot formation data for
gasoline/ethanol fuel blends, such that our CFD modeling approach could be validated for designing PC-
MCC engines. The selection of a chemical mechanism, fuel surrogate model and soot model for accurate
simulation the ignition and soot formation characteristics of gasoline/ethanol fuel blends was conducted.

 a. The CFD modeling used the reduced chemical kinetics mechanism developed by Kalvakala et.
 al., which was previously used for modeling soot emissions in GCI engines.
 b. An appropriate fuel surrogate model based on a Toluene Primary Reference fuel has been
 developed and chosen that accurately captures the ignition characteristics of the real fuel blends.
 c. A final selection of the Particulate Size Mimic (PSM) model has been chosen as the soot model
 as it can accurately capture the soot formation characteristics of the different fuel blends at the
 tested equivalence ratios as well as providing detailed information about the soot formation.
A CFD model of the RCM apparatus was constructed and used to simulate the RCM experiments to validate
the chemical kinetic mechanism, the fuel surrogate model, and the soot formation/oxidation model under
homogeneous, fuel-rich conditions. The test cases that were simulated from the RCM are in Table 4. The
equivalence range for the CFD models was extended to capture the soot formation trends of the different
fuel blends more accurately. The CFD results of the soot model compared to the experiments are shown in
Figure 5. For experimental cases, soot volume fraction numbers above 7.5 ppm have a higher level of
uncertainty based on the signal-to-noise ratio analysis and are labeled as such. The simpler Hiroyasu soot
model, which is traditionally used for mixing-controlled combustion with diesel fuel, falls short in
predicting soot at the higher equivalence ratios throughout the different gasoline/ethanol fuel blends. On
the other hand, the PSM model does a very good job of accurately predicting the soot yield for the different
fuel blends at all equivalence ratios.

Task 2 – Offset Prechamber Testing & Surface Temperature Measurements
The primary objective of Task #2 was two-fold: 1) collect proof-of-concept test data early in the project to
demonstrate the PC-MCC can deliver a diesel-like combustion process with gasoline/ethanol fuel blends
under a wide range of conditions and 2) develop and manufacture a sensor that is capable of fast heat flux
measurements in the prechamber and other combustion chamber surfaces.

The experimental work for this study will be conducted in a fully instrumented test cell featuring the
Cummins ISB engine, modified for single-cylinder operation. The 6.7L ISB is a six-cylinder diesel engine
typically found in medium-duty applications, such as pickup trucks, buses, recreational vehicles, and other
vocational vehicles. For the offset prechamber setup, a pre-chamber housing containing an AVL spark plug
pressure transducer, and fuel supply line has been placed next to the DI fuel injection system. The AVL
spark plug ZI22 has an integrated pressure sensor to measure pressure in the Prechamber. Figure 6 (i) shows
the CAD model of the prechamber setup with the DI fuel injector. The pre-chamber holes are aligned in
such a way that the prechamber jets exit into the main combustion chamber and ignite the fuel spray from
the main fuel injector. The spray holes can be seen in Figure 6 (ii). The prechamber housing is placed in
one of the two exhaust ports of cylinder #6. This exhaust port therefore had to be welded shut to prevent
coolant and oil from entering the exhaust as shown in Figure 7.

In addition to the proof-of-concept PC-MCC engine experiments, Task #2 focused on the development and
manufacturing a sensor that is capable of fast heat flux measurements in the prechamber and other
combustion chamber surfaces. The behavior of two surface temperature sensors under motored engine
operation were assessed in this project. The sensors were constructed using (a) lithography, which is the
process by which integrated circuits are manufactured, and (b) aerosol jet printing, a more forgiving method
that can only provide larger feature sizes. Both sensors were found to produce high quality temperature data
that easily enabled the determination of heat flux using a very straightforward inversion method. The benefit
of the aerosol jet method is that it is more forgiving with respect to the surface quality and would be the
method that should be pursued for measurements on complex surfaces like a prechamber.

The objective of this project was to measure thermal loading on a prechamber. Measurements of the
prechamber cap surface were presented in several quarterly reports, and these measurements were quasi-
steady, which meant that cycle-resolved heat flux could not be recovered. In order to obtain heat flux to the
prechamber body; the first step is to develop a sensor capable of measuring surface temperature and
transmitting that signal out of the engine chamber, i.e., through a pressure boundary. The two sensor designs
are shown in Figure 9 and share a common aluminum body with a 12.5 µm Parylene HT coating. This
coating and its thickness were specifically chosen to increase the temperature swing seen at the surface as
a means of improving signal-to-noise ratio. The sensing material for the two sensors, which acts as a
resistance thermometer, is nickel for the lithographic sensor, and was just 0.09 µm thick, while it was silver
and approximately 1 µm thick for the aerosol jet print (AJP) sensor. The lithographic sensor was
manufactured in a clean room and required a very flat and highly polished surface. The AJP sensor was
manufactured using hardware similar to an inkjet printer and was more tolerant of surface imperfections;
the drawback is that the feature size achievable was significantly larger than for the lithographic sensor.
Figure 10 shows the sensing elements for the two sensors. The line widths are 10 and 25 µm for the
lithographic and AJP sensors, respectively, which leads to a longer sensing element for the lithographic

Page 12/61 Final Scientific/Technical Report DE-EE0009872

sensor. Additionally, the thinner layer increases resistance. At room temperature, the lithographic sensor
had a resistance of 8230 Ω as compared to 164 Ω for the AJP sensor. Data were acquired by putting a
known current through the sensor and using the measured voltage drop to determine resistance; a direct
calibration provided temperature as a function of resistance.

Measurements were performed in a Kohler LH775 four-stroke utility engine that was available at the Engine
Research Center under motored operation allow sensor evaluation with reduced stress (thermal and
mechanical) on the sensor. A sample result of the sensor performance is shown in Figure 11, where the
upper panel shows the cycle-resolved surface temperature history and the lower panel shows the heat flux
that was derived from the temperature measurements. The first observation is that the signal-to-noise ratio
of the temperature measurements is very high, and individual cycle analysis is feasible. In both panels, the
black curve represents the ensemble-average temperature (and heat flux) and the grey band reflects the 95%
coincidence window about the mean. Individual cycles are also shown, and these cycles were chosen based
on their temperature history. For example, the curve labeled ‘Highest’ represents the cycle with the highest

intra-cycle temperature. It can be seen that the cycle-to-cycle variability in the surface temperature is quite
high, and when one considers the very low variability observed during the gas exchange period it is clear
that this is not influenced by random noise effects. Therefore, these measurements will allow a significant
investigation of the turbulence-driven variations in heat flux.

 Figure 11 Lithographic temperature and heat flux data for 1800 rpm and 70% of the WOT airflow.

The results from these measurements confirm well-known properties of heat flux in engines, see Figure 12.
The peak heat flux (shown as the swing in the figure to account for the negative heat flux that can occur
during parts of the cycle) increases with engine speed due to the increase in turbulence intensity, and also
increases with throttle position (air flow) via the density. The location of the peak heat flux is relatively
insensitive to engine operating conditions (recall this is motored) because it is driven by the compressed
gas temperature, which does not vary appreciably with speed or load. Finally, the performance of the two
sensor manufacturing methods is shown in Figure 13 using the heat flux swing as the measure. There is
excellent agreement between the two sensors, which practically means that the simpler AJP method can
provide sufficient data at likely lower cost.

Task 3 – Engine CFD Modeling
The primary focus of Task #3 was to conduct engine CFD modeling of the CAT C9.3B single-cylinder
engine to design the prototype hardware and operating strategy for flex-fuel PC-MCC. Figure 14 shows a
high-level overview of the CFD modeling approach that was used in this project. CONVERGE CFD
software was used and the boundary conditions were obtained from engine experiments.

  Figure 14: Overview of the CAT C9.3B CFD modeling approach for MCCI and PC-MCC flex-fuel operation.

In this work, a center mounted prechamber (CMPC) and side mounted prechamber (SMPC) were
considered. As detailed in the quarterly reports, the CMPC had excellent ignition assistance of all DI jets
and under optimal conditions delivered superior performance. However, the CMPC was very sensitive to
PC spark timing as the PC jet flames are traveling away from the DI injector, which is undesirable. Also,
the CMPC proved challenging to package in the CAT C9.3B cylinder head. For these reasons, the SMPC
design was selected as the final design.

Figure 15 shows images of the CMPC and SMPC, an overview of the SMPC design variables considered,
and the ultimate final SMPC design. The CFD simulations indicated that there is a preference to large
orifice diameters and small prechamber volumes. Large orifice diameters are needed to ensure deep
penetration of the PC jet flames towards the DI injector. A large orifice diameter increases jet momentum
and rate of penetration. A small prechamber volume is desirable because it minimizes the losses associated
with the prechamber, such as heat transfer and pumping. The CFD simulations also indicated that number
of prechamber orifices has little consequence on igniter performance – if the orifices are targeted
appropriately to the direct injected fuel. Thus, 2 to 3 prechamber orifices are preferred, as they are easier to
manufacture. The final design is shown in Figure 15. A large amount of detail on the CFD simulations that
guided these decisions can be found in the project quarterly reports and technical publications.

The other major accomplishment from the engine CFD simulations was developing the PC-MCC operating
strategy – in particular, how to operate the prechamber regarding fueling level and spark timing. At a high
speed, low load engine operating condition (C10), where ignition assistance is needed the most, the
prechamber fueling and prechamber spark timing were varied to understand their impact on main chamber
ignition assistance and combustion. The general operating strategy is highlighted in Figure 16 for E10 and
pure ethanol E100.

Figure 16: PC-MCC operation strategy for C10 condition. Solid lines denote main chamber direct injection and
 dashed lines denote PC injection. PC spark timing window is from -4 to -12 °ATDC.

Figure 17 shows the simulation results for E10 and E100 over a PC fueling and PC spark timing sweep. As
shown, when the PC is operated fuel lean (φ = 0.7), the ignition assistance is poor and very sensitive to PC
spark timing. As the PC fueling is increased, eventually reaching a fuel-rich condition (φ = 1.3) at spark
timing, the main chamber ignition assistance is substantially improved, the combustion strategy is far less
sensitive to spark timing, and replicates the diesel combustion process. This is desirable as the goal of flex-
fuel PC-MCC is to replicate diesel combustion with gasoline/ethanol fuel blends ranging from E10 to E100.

  Figure 17: Simulation results of E10 (left) and E100 (right) PC-MCC spark timing sweeps (-4 to -12 °ATDC)
with varying prechamber equivalence ratio (𝝓). Diesel baseline is provided in black dot-dashed line for reference.

Further CFD simulations demonstrated the primary reason that fuel-rich PC operation yields the most
favorable ignition assistance is because the PC jets are rich with incomplete combustion products. In the

Page 18/61 Final Scientific/Technical Report DE-EE0009872

oxygen rich main chamber environment, the PC jets “burn as the penetrate”, maintaining a high
temperature, making them superior ignition aids for the DI fuel. The operating strategy of fuel-rich PC
operation with PC spark timing nearly equal to DI start of injection timing was used in both the single-
cylinder and multi-cylinder engine experiments, particularly at low engine loads.

As the engine load was increased, a pilot+main injection strategy was utilized in the main chamber. At high

As the engine load increases, the intake pressure and in-cylinder pressures are elevated. Achieving a reliable
spark in with high gas densities in the prechamber is challenging. As shown in Figure 32, if the single main
injection with direct ignition assistance strategy was maintained, it was observed that the pre chamber
combustion process could be erratic resulting in poor prechamber combustion and poor main chamber
ignition assistance.

Page 30/61 Final Scientific/Technical Report DE-EE0009872

Figure 32: Example of erratic PC-MCC operation at higher load (9 bar BMEP) with direct ignition assistance of

It was discovered that the primary reason for erratic prechamber combustion at higher engine loads, was
unreliable spark ignition for prechamber gas pressures above approximately 50 to 60 bar at spark timing.
By tailoring the operating strategy such that the pre chamber gas pressure is 50 bar or less at spark timing,
robust and stable prechamber combustion could be recovered. This can be done a variety of ways, two of
which are shown are Figure 33.

First, the intake pressure can be lowered, such that the prechamber pressure at spark timing is less than ~50
bar, ensuring that the gas density is low enough to achieve a reliable spark. This technique was successful,
but results in a low air/fuel ratio, and at high load this can lead to high exhaust temperatures and ultimately
will severely limit peak engine power output. This is illustrated in Figure 33 on the left, and was not chosen
to be pursued in this project as it will ultimately limit power density, and thus a better solution is needed.

Another approach, which is preferred and the path forward for this project, is to advance the prechamber
spark timing, where the prechamber gas pressures are lower and a reliable spark can be achieved. However,
the challenge with this approach alone is that the prechamber jet flames are ejected too early, and mix out
before the main injection event, resulting poor ignition assistance of the main injection of E98. However,
if a main chamber pilot injection is added near the prechamber spark timing, the prechamber jet flames
serve to ignite the pilot injection. The pilot injection burns, locally raising the temperature, and serving as
an ignition assistant for the main injection. This is illustrated in Figure 33 on the right.

Page 31/61 Final Scientific/Technical Report DE-EE0009872

 Figure 33: Example of stable and robust PC-MCC operation at higher load (9 bar BMEP) with (left) direct
 ignition assistance of the main injection and (right) pilot + main injection strategy.

CFD modeling was used to highlight the pilot + main injection strategy with an early PC spark timing. The
model was also used to illustrate how simply advancing the PC spark timing is not sufficient, and the
addition of the pilot injection is critical to the success of this strategy.

The modeling results are shown in Figure 34. On the left in Figure 34, the red traces show CFD modeling
with an advanced prechamber spark timing, but with no pilot injection added. The direct injected ethanol
from the main injection does ignite eventually, but the ignition delay is very long, which is undesirable. By
adding a pilot injection, which is shown with the blue traces, the main injection ignition delay is
significantly shortened and results in a much more favorable mixing-controlled combustion process.

On the right in Figure 34, in-cylinder visualization is used from the CFD modeling to highlight how the
prechamber jet flames interact with the pilot injection to serve as a robust ignition aid for the main injection
of ethanol. The visualization also illustrates how rapidly the prechamber jet flames mix-out to low
temperatures, resulting in poor ignition assistance for the main injection only case that features no pilot
injection.

To improve the excessive PC variability, the PC fueling level needed to be reduced, however the MC
combustion quality would rapidly begin to deteriorate in doing so. In other words, the engine needed the

accompanied by additional latent heat of vaporization losses that amplify the charge cooling of the local
gas temperature, thus exacerbating the low ignitability of the ethanol fuel. This subtle difference might
explain the observed dependence on even richer conditions in the PC for stable MCE operation. It was
determined lowering the rail pressure to slow the injection rate helped improve the ignition performance.

There is a notable discrepancy in the PC and MC pressure agreement during compression, MC diffusion
combustion, and expansion. This discrepancy was also observed at light and mid-load conditions but at less
severity than what is observed at full load. During compression, the PC pressure is elevated of the cylinder
pressure and is expected to trail the MC slightly. During diffusion combustion and expansion, the
discrepancy is amplified with the PC pressure reading significantly higher than expected. A similar
phenomenon was observed at mid to full load conditions on the SCE, but to a lesser extent. These results
may suggest questionable pressure quality in either of the transducers due to improper calibration or thermal
shock effects. Without further investigation into the pressure data quality, the validity of indicated
efficiency calculations are in question. As such, discussion on thermal efficiency for the MCE is limited to
brake efficiency while the SCE is limited to gross efficiency as the frictional characteristics of a single-
cylinder engine are not representative of a brake measurement.

To start the comparisons of ignition technologies that enable MCC of ethanol, PC-MCC and MCCI ignition
strategies are assessed at a light load, high engine speed condition of 2 bar BMEP at 2200 rpm on the single-
cylinder test engine. These conditions are especially challenging to achieve MCC of ethanol due to the
limited mixing time and low in-cylinder compressed gas temperature and pressure. As such, ethanol
requires a high degree of thermal ignition assistance to facilitate stable combustion. The ignition strategies
assessed are as follows:

 • PC-MCC: the prechamber combustion timing is coupled closely to the direct injection
 timing, resulting in a jet-induced ignition. This is commonly referred to as a “direct ignition
 assistance” methodology as discussed previously.

 • MCCI: the intake temperature is increased artificially with a laboratory recirculation heater
 to simulate means of intake heating on a production engine, such as uncooled exhaust gas
 recirculation (EGR) or use a grid heater in the intake.
 o MCCI operation with and without a pilot injection is considered here to evaluate the
 impact on the intake heating requirement.

Figure 51 illustrates the in-cylinder data for all ignition strategies considered. PC-MCC has been previously
shown to operate well under light load conditions with no other ignition enhancements due to the robustness
of the jet-induced ignition process. The PC combustion ejects hot turbulent jets that impinge the direct
injected ethanol, resulting in a short ignition delay that promptly transitions to MCC. Under MCCI
operation with a single injection, an intake temperature of 150°C is required to achieve combustion, while
stable combustion (COV of IMEP < 5%) was only possible with an intake temperature with 160°C or above.
Despite the increase in intake temperature, and therefore compressed gas temperature, the ignition delay is
still considerably longer than that of PC-MCC, resulting in combustion process that has a high premixed
fraction. The introduction of a small pilot injection does not alter the combustion process significantly;

however it does reduce the minimum intake requirement to achieve combustion from 150°C to 95°C and
stable combustion achieved around 105°C.

Figure 52 illustrates the engine performance and emissions metrics of interest for all ignition strategies
considered. PC-MCC exhibits heightened gross thermal efficiency and reduced NOx emissions relative to

For all cases, no soot was detected in the exhaust, highlighting ethanol’s low sooting propensity in a
diffusion flame. The PC-MCC combustion efficiency was recorded at ~98%, indicating there are
combustion inefficiencies present in either the prechamber or main chamber combustion processes. It is
thought that the majority of the inefficiencies reside on the far side of the main combustion chamber from
the PC as these sprays are likely last to ignite and may experience some overmixing as a result. Other
sources could be from fuel impingement on the piston or PC walls. The combustion efficiencies of MCCI
were recorded to be roughly 99% and above, as the high in-cylinder temperature ensures a higher
conversion efficiency. Based on these results, it is clear that utilization of a PC as an ignition assistant
device rather than artificially increasing the intake temperature can provide simultaneous improvements in
light load thermal efficiency and engine out NOx while maintaining conventional diesel engine boundary
conditions but has some difficulty with complete fuel oxidation. An oxidation catalyst is still expected to
handle the engine out unburned emissions as performed on production diesel engines today.

At initial glance, the operational trends of the MCE in terms of performance and emissions were consistent
with the SCE findings. The largest discrepancy in the SCE and MCE testing results was the level of PC
combustion variability that lead to unacceptable levels of MC instability in severe cases. The excessive PC
variability was attributed to the engine’s dependence on excessively rich PC conditions for “stable”
operation. In other words, the engine was dependent on the increased jet exothermicity produced by rich
PC combustion, however in cases where the fueling was exceedingly rich the corresponding PC variability
overshadowed the merits of rich PC operation and lead to a deterioration in ignition performance. The
dependence on rich operation might be explained by the addition of higher steady flow injectors to the MCE

Page 54/61 Final Scientific/Technical Report DE-EE0009872

in contrast to the nearly halved steady flow of the SCE in which the igniter was originally designed around.
The increase in steady flow is accompanied by additional latent heat of vaporization losses that amplify the
charge cooling of the local gas temperature, thus exacerbating the low ignitability of the ethanol fuel.

Further research work on this platform should consider revisiting the steady flow of the injectors used. The
original decisioning on the increased flow was to account for the differences in energy delivery rate between
diesel fuel and ethanol at matched volumetric flowrate. Revisiting the steady flow rate would address the
observed dependence on rich operation and also allow for adequate comparisons of any benefits or
drawbacks associated with increased steady flow rate. Future work should also place an impetus on
improving the mixture preparation in the PC. The available injection pressure in the PC was limited to 80
bar, for the multi-cylinder testing, which is roughly half of the nominal operating pressure in the SCE
experiments. The lower available pressure limits the fuel mass addition rate into the PC, subsequently
forcing the injection process to end late with start of injection limited to just after intake valve closure,
likely leading to stratified mixtures that can also induce undesirable burn variability.

Task 8 – Techno-Economic Analysis of PC-MCC Concept
For any new engine or powertrain technology to enter the marketplace, it must be attractive economically
to the end-use customer. By conducting a techno-economic analysis and predicting the flex-fuel PC-MCC
total cost of ownership (TCO), the technology can be assessed for customer attractiveness. Comparisons
are made between the current state-of-the-art diesel engine technology and the flex-fuel prechamber MCC
engine technology to identify the fluid (fuel and DEF) and emissions control cost reduction payback period.
The assessment is based on a 9.0L off-road engine application.

To conduct the analysis, engine performance and emissions information is needed for the diesel and PC-
MCC engine technologies. The diesel engine performance and emissions data were taken from the
production John Deere 9.0L engine calibration and the PC-MCC data was taken from the single-cylinder
and multi-cylinder engine testing campaigns in this project.

As discussed in this report, the single-cylinder PC-MCC engine delivered equal or higher indicated thermal
efficiency compared to conventional diesel combustion. The PC-MCC engine was able to deliver ~20% to
30% lower engine-out NOx emissions when operating on E98 fuel compared to diesel combustion. The
John Deere 9.0L multi-cylinder engine was tested on E98 fuel and a summary of the findings was discussed
in the previous section, Task #7. Regarding brake thermal efficiency (BTE), the findings were very similar
to the efficiency trends from the single-cylinder engine. At low engine loads, the PC-MCC efficiency is
higher than diesel. As the engine load is increased, the PC-MCC efficiency is essentially equal to that of
diesel combustion. The single-cylinder testing showed a significant reduction in the engine out NOx
emissions for PC-MCC operation. These results could not be replicated on the multi-cylinder engine. The
production diesel calibration shown in the figure below uses exhaust gas recirculation to reduce NOx
emissions. However, during PC-MCC testing with E98 fuel, no exhaust gas recirculation was used, thus
the multi cylinder data is not a fair comparison in terms of NOx emissions. For the TCO analysis, based on
the single-cylinder test data that used no exhaust gas recirculation for PC-MCC and diesel, which is a fair
comparison, it will be assumed that E98 PC-MCC can deliver a 25% reduction in engine-out NOx.

Based on the SCE and MCE engine test data, a preliminary TCO analysis of an offroad vehicle with a 9.0L
engine can be performed. The focus of the analysis was to assess the economic viability of the flex-fuel
PC-MCC concept as compared to a state-of-the-art diesel engine. Table 7 shows the assumptions and the
source of data for the TCO analysis. It was assumed, based on the SCE and MCE engine data from this
project, that the diesel and ethanol PC-MCC engines will be able to produce the same power and torque,
while achieving the same cycle average BTE of 40%. The engine-out NOx emissions for ethanol PC-MCC
were assumed to be 25% lower than that of diesel, which will result in lower DEF consumption. The
additional initial cost of the engine by adding six prechamber igniters was provided by Mahle Powertrain
USA (now Dumarey USA), which was estimated to be $1,350 per engine. An additional $500 of initial cost
was assumed per engine for the prechamber fuel supply and electrical connections. The aftertreatment
system was assumed to stay in production form and no changes were planned for the fuel tank size. Any
additional cost of product development, emissions certification, vehicle maintenance, and product
marketing were not considered in the analysis.

This preliminary and simple TCO analysis suggests that heavy-duty ethanol engines present an economic
challenge with the current fuel prices for E85 at on road pumps in the United States. However, for a targeted
agricultural market in the midwestern United States, an E98 PC-MCC engine that can deliver diesel engine

also showed that these results are highly dependent on the cost of the fuel and the incremental cost increase
of the engine technology is inconsequential. This is expected in the heavy-duty vehicle industry, for which
fuel expense is a major contributor to TCO. Regarding GHG emissions, the E85 and E98 PC-MCC engines
have a lifetime reduction in CO2 compared to diesel of 285 tons and 358 tons, respectively.
