Consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages and all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality: insights from nationwide prospective cohort studies and Global Burden of Disease study 2021
Authors: Xiaolu Tan, Kaiyi Chi, Chunran Lai, Cong Li, Qinyi Li, Minjing Zhuang, Jiahui Cao, Qiaowei Wu, Wei Sun, Xianwen Shang, Yijun Hu, Zhuoting Zhu, Lei Liu, Xiayin Zhang, Honghua Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) consumption has become an emerging global public health concern. The relationship between SSBs consumption and mortality is not yet fully clarified. This study aims to quantify the dose-response relationships between SSBs consumption and mortality risks in US and UK cohorts, while also reviewing up-to-date estimates of SSB-related burden from 1990 to 2021 at a national level.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide prospective cohort study and systematic analysis. The study included 13,438 participants from US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) (1999–2000 and 2017–2018), and 63,436 participants from UK Biobank (2006–2010). For the systematic analysis using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 data, mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for the US and UK (1990–2021) were analyzed to generate estimates.ResultsIn the US population, during a 9.17-year cohort follow-up, 1,929 deaths were identified among 13,438 participants, while in the UK population, during a 12.41-year cohort follow-up, 3,466 deaths were identified among 63,436 participants. A significant dose-response association was found between higher SSBs consumption and increased all-cause mortality (both P for trend < 0.001), as well as mortality due to cardiovascular disease and chronic lower respiratory diseases. In GBD, SSBs-related mortality and DALYs rates were higher in older populations (55 + years) in both countries, consistent with the results in US NHANES and UK Biobank. Ischemic heart disease and type 2 diabetes mellitus were leading contributors to these burdens.ConclusionsThis study establishes a robust relationship between SSBs consumption and all-cause mortality, mortality caused by cardiovascular disease and chronic lower respiratory diseases. In line with GBD findings, SSBs consumption, especially among elderly populations, should be considered a crucial public health concern.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13098-026-02215-y.

Introduction

Humans display a preference for sugar intake. Dietary sugars manifest in various forms in daily life, including solid or liquid, naturally present or added to food products. Free sugars refer to monosaccharides and disaccharides added to foods and beverages by manufacturers, cooks, or consumers, as well as sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juices, and fruit juice concentrates. In contrast, added sugars specifically refer to those introduced during food manufacturing or preparation, excluding naturally occurring sugars in whole fruits and vegetables [1]. Current evidence supports the direct or indirect effects of high sugar intake on a number of non-communicable diseases, including diabetes mellitus (DM), cardiovascular diseases, and certain types of cancer, such as colorectal cancer [2, 3]. Sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), a major source of added sugars, typically contain water sweetened with various forms of sugar, including sucrose, high-fructose corn syrup, or other simple sugars [4]. Research suggests that the worldwide consumption of sugary drinks has surged, with per capita consumption of soft drinks rising from 9.5 L in 1997 to 11.4 L in 2010 [5]. Although World Health Organization guideline recommends reducing free sugar intake throughout life to less than 10% of total energy intake, with an additional suggestion to aim for below 5% (approximately 25 g per day) for extra health benefits [6], there are still no specific guidelines for SSBs consumption.

Recent findings indicate that SSBs consumption is associated with obesity [7] and type-2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [8], cardiovascular diseases [9], and even mortality from specific causes such as cardiovascular disease and stroke [10]. However, few large-scale prospective cohort studies have comprehensively estimated the association between SSBs exposure and mortality risk, focusing mainly on specific countries and diseases [11]. To date, no study has quantified the reduction in total SSBs intake required to lower mortality risk using dose–response analyses in both the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the UK Biobank.

Understanding the disease burden of SSBs intake is important for the provision of adequate healthcare resources in different regions. There are several studies that analyzed the burden of disease attributable to high SSBs globally [12–14]. At the regional level, only 3 studies evaluate the burden of SSBs in China [5, 15], and Brazil [16], both of which are countries of middle socio-demographic index. Higher-income countries such as the US and the UK are particularly cases where SSBs intake levels and trends were upward in the 20th century [17]. However, few studies have specifically described the burden of SSBs in the US and UK. Also, the causes and burden for SSB-contributed mortality in these two countries are unclear, which might limit the establishment of effective measures to deal with the problem at the national level.

Therefore, this study integrates large-scale cohort analyses with global burden data to comprehensively assess the mortality risks and disease burden attributable to SSBs consumption in the UK and US. The insights generated will help fill critical knowledge gaps and provide actionable evidence to guide national efforts in reducing SSB-related health harms and improving population health outcomes.

Methods

Study populations

US NHANES is a survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC’s) National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) to assess the health and nutrition status of the civilian population in the USA. The survey utilizes a multi-stage probability sampling design and examines a nationally representative sample of about 5,000 individuals annually since 1999 [18]. Besides, appropriate sampling weights were applied to obtain nationally representative estimates [19]. US NHANES was approved by the US NHANES institutional review board and National Center for Health Statistics Research ethics review board. All participants in this survey provided written informed consent. In US NHANES, we included 101,316 participants surveyed from 1999 to 2018 and removed individuals: (i) No valid SSB measurements at baseline; (ii) Age less than 40 years old; (iii) Loss to follow up; (iv) Reported with cancer at baseline, resulting in a final analytic sample of 13,438 participants (Figure S1).

The UK Biobank is a large-scale, population-based prospective cohort, consisting of half a million UK participants aged 40–69 years when recruited in 2006–2010. At one of 22 assessment centers located throughout England, Scotland, and Wales, participants underwent a touchscreen questionnaire and verbal interview, as well as physical measurements, and provided biological samples [20]. The UK Biobank received ethics approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (reference no. 16/NW/0274). All participants provided written informed consent for the study. This study was conducted under UK Biobank application number 86091. Participants were excluded only if all available assessments were missing. We accessed the UK Biobank cohort consisting of 502,365 individuals. Then, we excluded individuals: (i) No valid SSBs measurements at baseline or in subsequent assessments; (ii) Loss to follow up; (iii) Reported with cancer at baseline. Finally, we retained 63,436 participants in UK Biobank for subsequent analysis (Figure S1).

In Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database, data sources for SSBs burdens were obtained for individuals aged 25 years and above, at 5-year intervals, from 1990 to 2021 by the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) query tool https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/ according to sex, age groups, the US and UK. This study is compliant with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) statement. The University of Washington institutional review board committee approved GBD 2021, and informed consent was waived because of the use of deidentified data (http://www.healthdata.org/gbd/about/protocol).

Assessment of SSBs

SSBs referred to beverages that contain added sugar, including carbonated and non-carbonated soft drinks, fruit drinks, iced tea, and energy and vitamin water drinks that are typically low in nutritional quality with essentially similar metabolic effects [21, 22]. A diet high in SSBs is defined as any consumption (in grams per day) of beverages containing 50 or more kilocalories per 226.8-gram serving.

US NHANES utilized a two-day 24-hour dietary recall questionnaire to gather detailed information on food items and their components. The primary recall was administered by trained investigators at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC), followed by a telephone-based follow-up recall 3 to 10 days later. Each type of SSBs was categorized according to food codes specified by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) [23]. The UK Biobank study employed the Oxford WebQ, a web-based 24-hour dietary online assessment tool specifically designed for large population studies. SSBs measurements at baseline was defined as the dietary assessment period from April 2009 to June 2012, which included one in-person Oxford WebQ assessment at recruitment (April 2009–September 2010) and four subsequent online Oxford WebQ cycles (Cycle 1: February–April 2011; Cycle 2: June–September 2011; Cycle 3: October–December 2011; Cycle 4: April–June 2012). For participants with more than one dietary assessment during this period, we calculated the mean SSBs intake across all available assessments, as repeated measures have been shown to improve data quality [24]. They were asked to report the number of units (such as glasses, cans, cartons or 250 ml) of beverages consumed on the previous day. Response options ranged from 0 to more than 6 units, including choices of 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and more than 6 units [25]. The GBD 2021 study utilizes the 24-hour recall as the gold standard for measuring food intake to ensure data comparability when assessing mean intake at the population level. In this context, the intake of SSBs is measured continuously rather than categorically [26].

Ascertainment of mortality

Mortality data of the US NHANES was identified by the National Death Index public-use linked mortality files, with a follow-up period extending until December 31, 2019 [27]. Data on mortality status and length of follow-up were available for nearly all participants, with only 39 missing data on follow-up time. For the UK Biobank, date and cause of death were obtained from death certificates held within the National Health Service Information Centre (England and Wales) and the National Health Service Central Register Scotland (Scotland). Detailed information about the linkage procedure is available from https://content.digital.nhs.uk/services. Person-time was calculated from baseline to the study outcomes or the end of follow-up (19 December 2022).

The outcomes of both studies were all-cause and cause-specific mortality based on the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Health Problems, version 10 (ICD-10). The cause of death of participants was defined as the primary or secondary cause of death recorded in the mortality data. Six cause-specific mortality outcomes were assessed in our study:heart disease (I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51), chronic lower respiratory disease (J40-J47), cerebrovascular disease (I60–I69), DM (E10 –E14), influenza or pneumonia (J09–J18), and nephritis/nephrosis (N00–N07, N17–N19, N25–N27) [28].

For GBD database, the methods used in this study having been extensively peer-reviewed, with previous research detailing these methodologies and notable differences from preceding years [26, 29, 30]. Six outcomes attributed to the consumption of SSBs, including ischemic heart diseases (I20–I21.6, I21.9–I25.9, Z82.4–Z82.49), T2DM (E11–E11.1, E11.3–E11.9), tuberculosis (A15–A19), stroke(I64), chronic kidney diseases (N18.1–18.5, N18.9), lower extremity peripheral arterial disease (I70.2–I70.8, I73–I73.9) were identified based on the ICD-10. ICD-10 codes are used solely to define disease outcomes and to map them to the standardized GBD cause hierarchy and are not used to determine the relationship between sugar-sweetened beverage intake and tuberculosis. Cause-specific mortality was estimated primarily with the GBD cause of death ensemble modelling tool, CODEm.

Assessment of covariates

Covariates were selected before analysis using a directed acyclic graph based on prior literature and subject-matter knowledge [31]. Possible confounding factors included socio-demographic factors (including age, sex, ethnicity), lifestyle factors (including smoking, and intake of meat) and physical condition (including body mass index (BMI), HbA1c, and physical activity levels), and disease status (DM, hypertension, and hyperlipemia) (Figure S6). In both the US NHANES and UK Biobank databases, these variables were adjusted for, with details provided in the Table S1 and Table S2.

Statistical analyses

Variables of baseline characteristics are shown as n (%) if categorical, mean (SD) if normally distributed, and median (interquartile range) if nonnormally distributed. The distribution of baseline characteristics by categories of SSBs intake was compared using a chi-square test, ANOVA, and a Kruskal-Wallis test, respectively. Additionally, in US NHANES we used weighted chi-square tests for categorical variables and weighted ANOVA for continuous variables.

Cox proportional hazards models were applied to examine the association of SSBs intake with all-cause and cause-specific mortality risk. The results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption of the Cox models was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. The results indicated no significant violations of the PH assumption (all P > 0.05). We then employed the restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with three knots to explore the nonlinear associations of SSBs intake and the all-cause mortality, using the median values of SSBs intake as references. Two multivariable-adjusted models were constructed to account for potential confounding: model 1, adjustment for age and sex; model 2, additional adjustment for ethnicity, smoking status, BMI, HbA1c level, DM, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and intake of meat. For subgroup analysis, the association between SSBs intake and all-cause mortality was assessed with stratification by baseline age (<55 or ≥ 55 years) and sex, and their interactions were tested for statistical significance (Fig. 1). Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding participants who died within the first 2 years after recruitment to reduce potential reverse causation.

Within our investigation, mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributable to high SSB intake were collated from the US and UK datasets spanning 1990 to 2021 and presented as both absolute numbers and age-standardized rates (ASRs). All rates are reported per 100,000 population. In the GBD framework, exposure prevalence and relative risks are estimated as age–sex–location–year–specific parameters using population-level data within each calendar year, rather than from repeated individual visits. The GBD world population standard was used to calculate ASRs. We calculated DALY as the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs). YLLs were estimated from deaths and standard life expectancy, and YLDs from disease prevalence and disability weights derived from population surveys. To attribute DALYs to high SSBs intake, we applied the comparative risk assessment framework of the GBD study: population attributable fractions were calculated from exposure distributions, relative risks, and the theoretical minimum risk level (zero intake), and then multiplied by total DALYs for each outcome. For all estimates, 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) are reported. Uncertainty was propagated through each step of the estimation process, with UIs representing the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the distribution of 1000 draws at each step. The trends of the disease burden of high SSBs over the past 32 years were quantified by the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC). The EAPC is a common metric for assessing trends in ASRs over time. If both the EAPC and its 95% CI lower limit were > 0, it is considered that the ASR is exhibiting an increasing trend. Conversely, a decreasing trend is signified if both are less than 0. Otherwise, the ASR is regarded as stable. The data analysis was conducted using the R program (version 4.3.1) and RStudio. The main R packages used in this study included tidyverse, survival, rms, ggplot2 and related dependencies. In this study, we considered two-sided P values < 0.05 to be significant.

Results

Population characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. Of the 13,438 US NHANES participants (mean age 57.44 years, 50.25% women) from the US, the mean SSBs intake was 6.51 L/week (SD = 7.98). This cohort had a higher prevalence of non-white participants (58.17%) compared to white participants (41.83%). Additionally, 52.72% did not meet the physical activity recommendations. In contrast, of the 63,436 UK Biobank participants (mean age 55.03 years, 50.85% women) from the UK, the mean SSBs intake was significantly lower at 2.60 L/week (SD = 1.75). This cohort had a significantly higher proportion of white participants (94.27%) compared to non-white participants (5.73%). Furthermore, a larger percentage (81.24%) met the physical activity recommendations.

Association of sugar-sweetened beverages with mortality

In US population from NHANES study, 1,929 all-cause deaths (143.55 deaths per 1,000 population) were recorded over a median follow-up period of 9.17 years (interquartile range: 5.17–12.75). After adjusting for covariates, each 1 L/week increase in SSBs consumption was significantly associated with a 1% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.012, 95% CI = 1.006–1.018, P < 0.001). Similarly, during the median follow-up of 12.41 years (interquartile range: 11.81–13.19) in the UK Biobank study, 3,466 all-cause deaths (54.64 deaths per 1,000 population) were documented. After adjusting for potential confounders, each 1 L/week increase of SSBs consumption was significantly associated with a 5% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.05, 95%CI = 1.03–1.07, P < 0.001) (Table 2). It is also noteworthy that the consumption of SSBs exhibited a dose-response relationship with all-cause mortality in both US NHANES and UK Biobank study (P for trend = 0.004 and 0.001, respectively, Table 2). Furthermore, RCS analyses did not suggest any nonlinear relations between SSBs consumption and all-cause mortality (Pnon-linear = 0.22 for US NHANES and Pnon-linear = 0.11 for UK Biobank, Figure S2).

Figure S3 illustrates the outcomes of the cause-specific analysis according to fully adjusted model. Cause-specific mortality analysis revealed a significant positive association between SSBs intake and increased mortality from both cardiovascular disease and chronic lower respiratory diseases in the fully adjusted models for both the US NHANES (HR = 1.013, 95%CI = 1.001–1.026, P = 0.039; HR = 1.02, 95%CI = 1.01–1.03, P < 0.001, respectively) and UK Biobank (HR = 1.12, 95%CI = 1.06–1.17, P < 0.001; HR = 1.15, 95%CI = 1.02–1.30, P = 0.022, respectively) studies.

For subgroup analyses, the association between SSB intake and all-cause mortality was significant among participants aged over 55 and among women in both the US NHANES and UK Biobank studies. Consistent results across age and sex-specific stratification showed no significant interaction (Table S3).

In sensitivity analyses, the results were not materially changed when deaths occurred within the first 2 years after recruitment were excluded. For NHANES, the HR was 1.012 (95%CI = 1.006–1.019, P < 0.001). For the UK Biobank, the HR was 1.04 (95%CI = 1.02–1.07, P < 0.001). In addition to the primary models, further adjustments were made for income, education, general health status, total energy intake, and diet quality score. The associations between SSB intake and mortality remained materially unchanged after these additional adjustments (Table S4).

Disease burden of sugar-sweetened beverages in 2021

Globally, the number of deaths attributable to SSBs increased by 170.33% from 28.03 thousand (95%UI = 11.48, 43.22) in 1990 to 75.73 thousand (95%UI = 34.28, 117.27) in 2021. During the same period, there was a dramatic increase of DALY number from 988.55 thousand (95%UI = 429.79, 1543.49) to 3607.90 thousand (95%UI = 1703.48, 5566.16) in 2021.

In the US, there was a significant rise in deaths due to SSBs, with numbers increasing from 4.88 thousand (95%UI = 1.89, 7.86) in 1990 to 9.97 thousand (95%UI 4.48, 15.19) in 2021. The age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR) showed a slight decline from 1.52 per 100,000 (95%UI = 0.58, 2.45) in 1990 to 1.73 per 100,000 (95%UI = 0.77, 2.63) in 2021, with an EAPC of −0.41% (95%UI = -0.93, 0.10). In contrast, the UK saw a decrease in deaths attributed to SSBs, declining from 0.64 thousand (95%UI = 0.21, 1.08) in 1990 to 0.54 thousand (95%UI = 0.20, 0.92) in 2021. The ASDR in the UK also decreased from 0.7 per 100,000 (95%UI = 0.23, 1.20) in 1990 to 0.38 per 100,000 (95%UI = 0.14, 0.65) in 2021, with an EAPC of −2.17% (95%UI = -2.43, 1.92). (Table 3)

Discussion

Using regional population data analyses from the US NHANES and UK Biobank, this study observed the dose-response relationships between SSBs intake and all-cause mortality, establishing a robust dose-response association in both US and UK cohorts. A comprehensive assessment of national temporal trends and variation in disease burden attributable to high SSBs intake was conducted using GBD 2021 database. We demonstrated the causes of death and DALYs attributable to high SSBs intake between 1990 and 2021, as measured by age-standardized rates.

Findings from both databanks emphasize the universal health risks posed by SSBs, especially in the elderly. This aligns with the national trend observed in the GBD study 2021, which intuitively explains why the disease burden was higher in the elderly than in the middle-aged and young. Both studies showed a dose-response relationship between SSBs consumption and all-cause mortality, with higher consumption linked to increased risk. Additionally, both the US NHANES and UK Biobank data show a significant positive association between SSBs intake and cardiovascular disease-related mortality as well as chronic lower respiratory disease mortality. This is consistent with the GBD study findings, where ischemic heart disease was also a major contributor to the disease burden.

As differences in dietary patterns, healthcare access, and socioeconomic status might influence the disparities, a deeper understanding could further illuminate the complex interplay of biological, socioeconomic, and environmental factors, helping to tailor more effective public health interventions. This study systematically estimated the burden associated with high SSBs over the last 32 years. Based on analysis from the GBD 2021, there is an increasing trend of the ASR of DALYs due to high SSBs intake from 1990 to 2021 in the US and UK. However, while the mortality rate in the UK has decreased, the mortality rate in the US continues to rise. The differences may result from a different population characteristic where US population showed a much higher portion of those consumed SSBs > 2 L/week. Public health policies and the general awareness regarding the risks of SSBs consumption also show differences between regions [17]. The UK and certain cities and state in US have implemented various measures to reduce SSBs consumption, including the Soft Drinks Industry Levy (commonly known as the “sugar tax”), which places a tax on high-sugar drinks, whereas there is no nationwide tax on SSBs in the US [32, 33].

Prior research has linked SSBs intake to diverse adverse outcomes. Using a prospective cohort study, Ying Sun et al. demonstrated that consumption of SSBs and artificially sweetened beverages (ASBs) at > 2 L/week was associated with an increased risk for atrial fibrillation (AF) and the association between SSBs and AF risk persisted after adjustment for genetic susceptibility to AF [34]. Higher consumption of SSB has been associated with risk of hyperandrogenism in women [35], incident heart failure [36], and dementia [37]. Recently most studies focused on a specific health outcome related to SSBs intake, while few have examined its contribution to the overall disease burden across multiple causes. Using data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey and the UK Biobank, we found that SSBs intake was positively associated with cardiovascular and chronic lower respiratory disease mortality, while DM showed no association between SSBs intake and mortality. Despite this, higher consumption of ASBs and SSBs was linearly associated with an increased risk of microvascular complications and incident cardiovascular disease in adults with T2DM [38, 39]. Using GBD 2021, specific causes of burden contributed to high SSBs intake were analyzed on a national level, where ischemic heart disease, T2DM, tuberculosis, stroke, chronic kidney disease, and lower extremity peripheral arterial disease(LEAD) were included. Among these causes, T2DM, ischemic heart disease and chronic kidney disease are main cause between SSBs intake and disease burdens including mortality and DALYs. The inconsistency in T2DM and respiratory disease may be due to differences in disease prevalence, biological factors and healthcare infrastructure access. Chronic respiratory diseases prevalence with highest prevalence among both males and females in high-income regions, although improved diagnosis cannot be ruled out [40]. High income countries including the US and UK have experienced significant increases in obesity rates over the past few decades [41]. Obesity is a major risk factor for lower respiratory diseases and has been associated with impaired immune function and reduced lung capacity [42, 43]. Additionally, access to advanced healthcare services is more available in high-income regions, facilitating better management and control of DM among their populations, which also prevents complications, such as diabetic nephropathy [44]. Consequently, deaths and DALYs attributed to DM and DM-related nephropathy in US and UK are relatively not as significant as in lower-income areas.

Given the burden of disease caused by SSBs, a comprehensive, population-based multi-component policy approach transforming the food system is needed in countries. Several studies have shown that higher prices can deter consumers and reduce disease burdens, for instance, the introduction of a 20% ad valorem or tiered SSBs tax in Germany, based on scientific recommendations and taxes implemented in countries like Mexico or the UK, could help to reduce the national burden of cardiometabolic non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and save a substantial amount of healthcare and productivity costs [32]. Clear, prominent labelling of sugar content and health risks, together with restrictions on SSBs marketing and advertising, particularly those directed at children, can further support informed choices and reduce consumption [45]. Additionally, educating healthcare providers about the risks associated with SSBs and encouraging them to counsel patients on reducing intake as part of routine clinical care can play a role in promoting healthier behaviors and improving patient outcomes [46].

Strengths and limitations of this study

Our study utilized both the US NHANES and UK Biobank databases, enhancing statistical power and generalizability. Its prospective design allows for establishing a temporal relationship between SSBs consumption and mortality. By analyzing the dose-response relationship, the study provides a detailed understanding of how varying levels of SSBs intake impact mortality risk, while also examining the specific causes of mortality and the correlation with age demographics. Additionally, we comprehensively assessed the disease burden in the US and UK attributed to SSBs intake and the temporal trends over 32 years based on the GBD study 2021. The use of multiple data sources, supplemented by the GBD study data, offers a comprehensive view of the impact of SSBs across different populations and time periods.

However, our study had several limitations. Firstly, there is also evidence of a “healthy volunteer” selection bias in the UK Biobank [47]. However, the sufficiently large numbers of individuals, different levels of exposures, high internal validity, and long-term follow up still provide valid scientific inferences to produce generalizable associations of exposures with mortality. Second, in the US NHANES and the UK Biobank, SSBs intake was self-reported using 24-hour recalls or food-frequency questionnaires, which introduces recall error, social desirability bias, and systematic underreporting. Misclassification (e.g., SSBs vs. artificially sweetened beverages, sugar added to drinks) and portion-size estimation errors may also occur. These measurement errors are likely non-differential and would bias estimates toward the null. As an observational study, it cannot definitively establish causality despite adjustments for confounders. Third, our results are subject to the methodological defects of the GBD 2021. Where data were not available, the results depended on the out-of-sample predictive validity of the modelling efforts; even when data were available, the preferred case definition or measurement method might not have been used (e.g., large variations still exist in the definition of T2DM across the data sources). Several techniques to reduce bias and inaccuracy were used in the GBD Study 2021, but bias cannot be fully ruled out. Finally, an important methodological consideration is the difference in age inclusion criteria between these datasets. The GBD analysis included participants aged ≥ 25 years, while NHANES and UK Biobank were restricted to participants aged ≥ 40 years. This broader age range in GBD may introduce variability in exposure and risk, as younger adults often have higher SSBs intake but lower incidence of chronic disease and mortality. As a result, estimates of SSB-attributable burden in GBD may appear lower in younger age groups, potentially reducing overall comparability with older cohorts. Although we used age-standardized rates to mitigate this issue, differences in the underlying age structure and risk dynamics remain a potential source of heterogeneity that should be considered when interpreting cross-dataset comparisons. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the relationship between SSBs consumption and mortality, emphasizes the need for targeted public health interventions to reduce SSBs intake and mitigate its impact on mortality and highlighting important public health implications.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified a strong dose-response relationship between increasing SSBs consumption and significantly higher all-cause mortality, especially pronounced among participants aged over 55. SSBs intake is a growing global health problem, particularly among middle-aged and elderly populations. The varying disease burden with different age, sex and causes underscores the need for targeted public health measures to address this escalating issue. Implementing policies to reduce SSBs consumption, and promote healthier alternatives is crucial to mitigating the long-term health impacts associated with SSBs.
