Microbiome-host interactions in different pathomic subtypes of early-stage lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Tianshuai He, Zhu Ai, Qijia Han, Fang Chen, Hao Wang, Minyi Wu, Qiuxing Chen, Shulin Li, Lei Wang, Zhihe Yang, Peng Wang, Zhang Wang, Zhiming Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicrobiome-host interactions in different pathological subtypes of the early-stage lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remain poorly understood.MethodsWe conducted histopathological and imaging analysis on a cohort of 106 Chinese patients with LUAD. Further deep analysis of the lung tissue microbiome, serum metabolome, and host transcriptome was performed. Using correlation analysis, microbial genetic information, and established metabolite-human gene pairs, we integrated multi-omics data to explore potential associations between the microbiome, microbial metabolites, and host gene expression.FindingsWe identified distinct groups based on malignancy severity. Massilia, Sphingomonas, Staphylococcus, and Brevundimonas may influence host gene expression through their metabolites, affecting the progression of LUAD. We used Mendelian Randomization(MR) and found suggestive evidence that the levels of key metabolites (serotonin, uric acid) are negatively associated with LUAD risk.InterpretationsThis study demonstrates that pathological images of early-stage LUAD cells can reflect potential clinical differences, and some microbial-metabolite-gene potential associations have been found in early-stage LUAD, which may affect the development of early-stage lung adenocarcinoma.FundingThis research was supported by grants from the National Natural Science Foundation of China (No. 82171931), the Guangdong Basic and Applied Basic Research Foundation Enterprise Joint Fund (2025). Introduction

Globally, lung cancer has the highest incidence and mortality rate out of all cancers [1]. Adenocarcinoma of the lung, a type of non-small cell lung cancer, constitutes 40% of all lung cancer cases. Metastasis mechanisms in lung adenocarcinoma have been extensively studied [2–4], but the mechanisms involved in the early stages of lung adenocarcinoma development remain unclear [5–9].

The diagnosis and subtyping of early-stage lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) rely on pathological examinations, making tumour histopathology crucial. Depending on the cellular morphology, LUAD can be classified into five histological subtypes: lepidic, acinar, papillary, solid, or micropapillary. Typically, lung adenocarcinomas consist of cells exhibiting different histological subtypes, a phenomenon known as intra-tumoral heterogeneity. Solid and micropapillary growth patterns tend to have a poor prognosis. Previous studies have demonstrated by pathomics that atypical epithelial cells gradually increase and lymphocytes decrease as early lung adenocarcinoma progresses [10]. Many studies have explored predictive models analysing pathological image data for lung adenocarcinoma progression and prognosis [11–13], yet the clinical significance of histopathological data remains somewhat limited [14]. Many studies indicate that respiratory microbiota interact with host diseases in various ways, and microbes can influence disease progression through metabolic processes [15, 16]. Numerous studies have explored the interaction of lung microbiota with lung adenocarcinoma. Jin et al. found that lung microbiota can induce inflammation associated with lung adenocarcinoma by activating pulmonary γδ T cells [17]. Chen et al. discovered that butyrate derived from microbiota within lung cancer promotes cancer metastasis.[18]. In order to explore the heterogeneity of lung adenocarcinoma cells by mining pathological information that is difficult to detect with the naked eye, we extracted the pathomics characteristics of cancer cells and grouped the individuals by clustering the pathomics data. We found that different histopathological groups of lung adenocarcinoma have distinct microbiome characteristics, suggesting that there may be associations between the microbiome and lung histopathology [19]. We further explored whether histological characteristics could be linked to LUAD clinical features and how microbiome-host interaction may be implicated in different pathological subgroups.

Methods

Patients

This study utilized patient samples collected from Guangzhou Medical University Panyu Central Hospital between January 2018 and December 2020. Patients included in the study underwent preoperative Computed Tomography (CT) scans of the lungs followed by pulmonary lobectomy, segmentectomy, or wedge resection by thoracic surgery. Patients had lung nodules confirmed by intraoperative frozen section pathology or postoperative paraffin pathology examination. Participation in the study was conditional upon obtaining informed consent from the patients. The study was approved by the ethic committee of Panyu Central Hospital Affiliated to Guangzhou Medical University (PYRC-2021–006).

Exclusion criteria for this study were the absence of rapid frozen section or paraffin pathology diagnostic information, pathological diagnosis of nodules not falling within the four types of lung adenocarcinoma (AAH, AIS, MIA, IAC), metastases from other cancers (such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma, colorectal carcinoma, and breast carcinoma), poor-quality CT images with significant respiratory or motion artefacts, prior interventions such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy before surgery, or poor-quality histopathological haematoxylin and eosin (HE) images that were not readable.

A total of 106 patients were included in this study. Lung tissues from these patients underwent 16S rRNA gene sequencing and analysis; host transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted on lung tissues from 98 patients, and serum non-targeted metabolomics sequencing and analysis were performed on serum samples from 60 patients.

Clinical information

H&E pathology slides from all patients were obtained from the Pathology Department of our hospital and digitized using a digital scanner to create whole-slide images (WSI) at a magnification of 40x. Initially, WSIs were exported in KFB format and later converted to the commonly used SVS format for ease of further processing in digital pathology image analysis. Tumour regions within all WSIs were delineated by two pathologists (F.C. and H.W.), each with over 10 years of experience in pulmonary pathology, using Qupath software [20]. Further image segmentation was performed to divide these into tiles of size 1024 × 1024 pixels. From these segments, 50 random tiles were selected. CellProfiler (Version: 2.2.1, https://cellprofiler.org/) was utilized to extract cell features [21]; The modules chosen for analysis included Unmix Colors, Identify Primary Objects, Object Intensity Distribution, Object Intensity, Texture, and Object Size Shape. [13] Given the central role of nuclear morphometry in characterizing malignant phenotypes and the high spatial similarity between nuclear and cytoplasmic regions, we focused on nuclear texture for clustering analysis rather than cytoplasmic features. This selection strategy prioritizes the core pathological changes underlying tumor aggressiveness.To determine the optimal number of clusters for pathomic feature stratification, we performed two complementary statistical analyses: the elbow method and silhouette analysis.

Clinical epidemiological information was obtained from medical records, including gender, age, history of malignant tumours and family history of cancer, Additionally, two radiologists specializing in chest imaging (Z.M.X. with 20 years of experience and Z.A. with 10 years of experience) provided expert knowledge for CT diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma. Without knowing the histopathological diagnosis, they recorded 11 radiological features independently, including nodule location, solid component of the nodule, nodule morphology, margin, lobulation sign, spiculation sign, cavitation sign, air bronchogram sign, vascular sign, pleural indentation sign, and maximum diameter. They reached a consensus through discussion. We compared the differences between the two pathological subgroups using chi-square tests.

Sample collection and multi-omics sequencing

Sample collection

After pre-cooling with DEPC-treated water in a 4 °C refrigerator, disposable medical tweezers and a disposable medical bent plate were used to remove any remaining bloodstains from the samples. The cleaned samples were placed into new cryogenic tubes labelled with sequential numbers and sample information. Samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 30 s, then placed into a cryogenic storage box and stored at −80 °C.

Blood sample collection

Sterile vacuum blood collection tubes were used to obtain 5 mL of venous blood from lung cancer patients in the morning before surgery,, following an 8-h overnight fast at room temperature. After allowing the blood to stand and centrifuging to separate the serum, it was labelled and stored at −80 °C.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from the tumor samples using Qiagen DNA Mini kit. The V4 hypervariable region of 16S rRNA gene was PCR amplified with forward primer sequence GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA and reverse primer sequence GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT. Purified PCR products were pooled and sequenced using Illumina NovaSeq platform. To monitor potential reagent contamination, blank negative controls (containing all extraction and PCR reagents without lung tissue) were processed in parallel with patient samples. No visible PCR bands were detected in blank controls, though sequencing was not performed on these negative controls.Subsequently, library quality control was conducted, followed by sequencing of raw data. A total of 15,212,430 raw reads were obtained, all meeting data quality standards. Post-sequencing, barcode sequences were removed [22]. The demultiplexed sequencing reads were denoised to generate amplicon sequence variants using DADA2 [23]. A custom Naïve Bayes classified was trained on the Greengene 13_8 99% OTUs using V4 hypervariable region of 16S rRNA gene sequences obtained with 515 F and 806R primers and applied to assign taxonomy for the ASVs. Rarefaction was performed to the minimum sequencing depth of all samples.

Untargeted serum metabolomics sequencing

Serum samples were subjected to non-targeted metabolomic mass spectrometry analysis using Xevo G2-XS QTOF (Waters, UK). Progenesis QI (v.2.2; Waters, UK) was employed to import and extract peaks from the post-sequencing raw data. MetaX software (v.1.4.16) was used for data preprocessing, [24] which included KNN (K-nearest neighbours) imputation to fill missing values in extracted data, removal of low-quality ions with missing values exceeding 50% in QC samples or exceeding 80% in actual samples, and signal correction using the QC-RSC (Quality Control-based Robust LOESS Signal Correction) method. Metabolite identification was performed by comparison with databases such as KEGG, HMDB, and LipidMaps.According to Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines, this approach corresponds to Level 2 (putatively annotated compounds).

RNA Sequencing

RNA was extracted from tumor tissue using Qiagen RNease Mini kit. Upon qualifying RNA samples, mRNA was enriched and fragmented into short fragments by ultrasound. Using mRNA as a template, cDNA synthesis was initiated with primers, followed by synthesis of second-strand cDNA to form double-stranded cDNA. Purification was performed, followed by the addition of poly(A) tails and sequencing adapters, and PCR amplification to construct the library. Initial quantification was then conducted, and the library was diluted to 1 ng/μl. Insert fragments were checked to confirm their expected size. Once confirmed, precise quantification ensured library quality. After passing library quality control, an Illumina high-throughput sequencing platform (HiSeq) was used for paired-end sequencing. Upon obtaining raw sequencing reads, filtering and quality assessment were performed to obtain clean reads for analysis. To ensure subsequent analyses were not affected by ribosomal RNA, sequences that did not align with ribosomal RNA sequences in the Rfam database were retained for further analysis. The unmapped reads from the previous step were aligned against the reference genome using Hisat2 (v.2.1.0) [25]. Gene expression levels were estimated by counting the sequencing reads mapped to genomic regions or gene exons. We employed RNA-Seq by expectation maximization (RSEM, v1.3.1) to compute the read count for each sample.

Statistical analysis of multi-omics data

For the obtained amplicon sequence variants, we performed statistical analysis at the genus level using R (v.4.3.2). Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to calculate significantly differentially abundant taxa between groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using the vegan (v.2.6–6.1) and ape (v.5.8) packages. Spearman’s correlation analysis was utilized to correlate data between groups.

We performed log2 transformation and z-score normalization on the peak areas of metabolites and gene expressions. Differential analysis was conducted using the DESeq2(v.1.42.1) package in R [26]. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA,v.1.72–5) was used for dimensionality reduction [27]. Metabolite WGCNA was performed with the following key parameters: soft-thresholding power = 5 (Supplementary Fig. 5), minModuleSize = 10, and mergeCutHeight = 0 (no module merging). And gene WGCNA was performed with the following key parameters: soft-thresholding power = 6 (Supplementary Fig. 6), minModuleSize = 10, and mergeCutHeight = 0. The number of genes and metabolites in each co-expression module are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to correlate data between groups without FDR correction. Metabolomics data were analysed for enrichment using the online metabolomics data analysis tool MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (www.metaboanalyst.ca) [28]. For transcriptomics data, gene enrichment analysis was conducted using org.Hs.eg.db (v.3.18.0) and clusterProfiler (v.4.10.1) in R [29].

For the pathological characteristics of the cells, we first added the absolute value of the minimum feature plus one to all features before performing log2 transformation and z-score normalization for standardization.

Following Yan et al.'s research, we conducted biological functional analysis in the order microbiota-metabolites-genes to explore the relationship between microbiota and the host [16]. The relationship between enzyme classification (EC) numbers and microbial communities was analysed using PICRUSt2(v.2.5.2) [30], followed by biological association analysis between microbiota-metabolites and metabolites-genes using MetaCyc (v.23.1) [31] and the STITCH (v.5.0) database [32].

Two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was conducted to explore the causal association between microbial-associated metabolites and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) risk. The analysis strictly followed the three core assumptions of MR: selected genetic variants (instrumental variables, IVs) were significantly associated with target metabolites (relevance assumption), independent of common confounders (independence assumption), and only affected LUAD risk through the metabolites without direct pleiotropy (exclusion restriction assumption). IVs were selected based on public GWAS summary statistics, and sensitivity analyses (Cochran’s Q test, MR-Egger regression, leave-one-out analysis) were performed to ensure result robustness.

External dataset validation

We retrieved GWAS IDs for exposure factors (metabolites) and outcomes (lung adenocarcinoma) from the IEU Open GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). Analysis was conducted using the R package TwoSampleMR (v.0.6.4). The metabolite dataset included serotonin (met-a-358), uric acid (ebi-a-GCST90018977), xanthine (met-a-363), adenosine deaminase levels (ebi-a-GCST90000438), isoleucine (ebi-a-GCST90092843, met-c-873), sulfate (ebi-a-GCST90026009) and cholate(met-a-438). The lung adenocarcinoma dataset used was finn-b-C3_NSCLC_ADENO_EXALLC, which specifically excludes other cancers. To enrich for SNP data, we applied a threshold of 5 × 10–8 for uric acid and 5 × 10–5 for other metabolites, different thresholds were used to obtain an appropriate number of SNPs.. SNP clumping parameters were set at r2 = 0·001 and kb = 10,000. The mean F-statistics for instrumental variable strength are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Results

Pathomics subtypes and imaging differences

A total of 106 patients with LUAD were examined in this study (Supplementary Fig. 1). Using CellProfiler, we extracted features of pathological cell nuclei (Fig. 1a). We validated the differences in histopathological subgroups (adenocarcinoma in situ, minimally invasive adenocarcinoma, and invasive adenocarcinoma) within the histopathological data (Fig. 1b–c). There was no significant difference in PCA, and the Adonis test (R2 = 0 043, p = 0 007) and ANOSIM test (R = 0 027, p = 0 164) confirmed that the difference between groups was less than the difference within the group. The cell features extracted by CellProfiler broadly include five categories: area shape, intensity, location, radial distribution, and texture (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To determine the optimal value of k, we first used the elbow method to calculate the total within-cluster sum of squares (WSS) for K values ranging from 1 to 10. A clear inflection point (elbow) was observed at K = 2(Supplementary Fig. 4)., indicating that this is the optimal number that balances clustering compactness and interpretability. Next, silhouette analysis was conducted to evaluate the clustering quality for K = 2. The results showed that all 220 samples had positive silhouette coefficients, with an average silhouette width of 0.22. The average silhouette widths of the two clusters were 0.17 (n = 121) and 0.29 (n = 99), respectively, confirming the robustness and significance of the two pathological subtypes (PHG/PLG). Based on these results, K = 2 was chosen for subsequent multi-omics integrative analysis.We employed hierarchical clustering to classify the extracted cell features into two groups: pathological high-risk (PHG) or low-risk (PLG) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To validate whether this classification holds clinical significance, we analysed the clinical information and lung nodule CT results of these samples (Table 1). In both pathomics subtypes, lobulation sign (p = 0·02), solid nodule component (SNC, p = 0·08), and pleural indentation sign (p = 0·14) showed some differences, suggesting that PHG may indicate a higher malignant potential compared with PLG.

Pathomics subtype-associated lung microbiome

At the genus level, Acinetobacter, Enhydrobacter, Bacillus, Pseudomonas and Sphingomonas were among the most abundant taxa in the pulmonary microbiota in patients with LUDA (Fig. 2c). There were no significant differences in microbial α-diversity between different pathological subtypes (Fig. 2d). Additionally, PHG and PLG did not have significant microbiome separation (Fig. 2a). We assessed the differences in relative abundances between the two groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a threshold of p < 0·05 and an average relative abundance greater than 0·001. Five differential genus-level taxa were identified, Lysobacter, Oceanobacillus, Sphingobacterium, Staphylococcus, and Xanthomonadaceae, all of which were enriched in PHGs in comparison to PLGs (Fig. 2b).

The significant associations (p < 0·05, r > 0·3) between the top 20 taxa and imaging features, metabolome modules, and transcriptome modules were analysed (Supplementary Fig. 3). Lobation sign was correlated with Lactobacillus in PHG, while Massilia was correlated with PLG; additionally, pleura was associated with Lactobacillus in PLG.

Pathomics subtype-associated serum metabolome

A partial separation between PHG and PLG was observed in the metabolome (Fig. 3a). After annotation, 3,300 metabolites were identified. By applying a threshold of |logFC|> 1 and p < 0·05, we detected 60 differential metabolites between different pathological subtypes (Fig. 3b). Further enrichment analysis suggested that these metabolites were enriched in pyrimidine metabolism (uridine triphosphate, cytidine monophosphate and uracil) and amino acid metabolism (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, and histidine) pathways (Fig. 3c). We performed a WGCNA which clustered all metabolites clustered into 55 co-abundance metabolite (MetaB) modules. Correlation analysis between the top 20 genera (average relative abundance) and the metabolite modules revealed that in PHG, Anoxybacillus and Acinetobacter had the most significant associations with metabolite modules, whereas Proteiniclasticum and Paracoccus were most significantly associated in PLG (Fig. 3d).

Pathomics subtype-associated lung transcriptome

No significant separation between PHG and PLG was observed in the transcriptome in principle component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 4a). A total of 218 differentially expressed genes were identified out of over 22,000 total human genes (Fig. 4b). The differential genes were mainly enriched to legionellosis and longevity regulating pathway (Supplementary Fig. 5). Using WGCNA, all genes were binned into 157 co-abundance host transcriptome (HostT) modules. Nine of the modules were significantly different, with T027, T019, T125, and T154 enriched ubiquitin mediated proteolysis-related genes, while T019, T026, T028, T032, T124, T125, and T154 enriched bacterial infection-related genes (Supplementary Table 2). Among all co-abundance modules, 34 modules were found containing non-small cell lung cancer-related genes. We assessed the association of these 34 modules with the metabolomic modules and with imaging features (lobulation sign, SNC, and pleural sign, Fig. 4c). Compared with PLG, PHG had a greater number of transcriptomic modules significantly associated with image features, but fewer modules were significantly associated with metabolites.

Pathomics subtype-associated multi-omic links

We next sought to identify potential microbiome-metabolite-host interaction links associated with specific pathomic subtypes using multi-omics. Specifically, if a microbial taxon encodes genes involved in metabolite reactions within a MetaB module, we considered there to be a biological link between the microorganisms and the module. If the metabolites within a MetaB module were known to interact with human genes in the HostT module, then these modules were considered linked. We identified 23 links in PHG and 299 links in PLG (Supplementary Fig. 7). In PHG, the links involved Anoxybacillus, Brevundimonas, Pseudomonas and Sphingomonas, which were linked to metabolites in M035 and M052. In PLG, the links involved Massilia, Sphingomonas and Staphylococcus, which were linked to metabolite in modules M035, M044, and M028 (Fig. 5a–b).

We performed enrichment analysis on the frequently observed metabolite modules (Fig. 5c). M035 was enriched in pathways related to valine, leucine, isoleucine, and primary bile acid biosynthesis. M052 was enriched in purine metabolism, specifically including 2-oxo-4-hydroxy-4-carboxy-5-ureidoimidazoline and uric acid. M028 was primarily enriched in tryptophan metabolism, including L-tryptophan, serotonin, and 6-hydroxytryptamine. M044 was mainly enriched in purine (xanthine and adenosine) and sphingolipid (sphingosine 1-phosphate) metabolism.

In PLG, isoleucine was a key metabolite within M035 linked to Staphylococcus. Members of Staphylococcus encodes enzyme branched-chain amino acid aminotransferase (EC: 6.1.1.5 and EC: 2.6.1.42) involved in isoleucine catabolism, implying that the low Staphylococcus abundance in PLG may have contributed to the accumulation of branched-chain amino acids in the valine/isoleucine biosynthesis pathway (M00019), including isoleucine. Isoleucine was further linked to PSMB6, PSMB7, SLC7A, and UBB genes in host transcriptome; among these, PSMB6, PSMB7, and SLC7A showed slight upregulation (logFC > 0·2), while UBB did not exhibit significant differential expression.

In PLG, L-tryptophan and serotonin were key metabolites in M028 linked to Massilia. Biological associations suggested that Massilia may influence tryptophan synthesis through tryptophan synthase (EC: 4.2.1.20). In the tryptophan metabolism pathway (map00380), serotonin is a downstream product of L-tryptophan, implying that the low abundance of Massilia in PLG may have affected the accumulation of tryptophan and serotonin. Furthermore, tryptophan was associated with SLC7A5 and SLC7A8 in host transcriptome (logFC = 0·42 and 0·27, respectively), while serotonin was associated with C3 and ADRA1D (logFC = −0·58 and 0·42, respectively).

In PLG, the key metabolites of M044 were adenosine and xanthine associated with Sphingomonas. Sphingomonas encodes adenosine deaminase (EC: 3.5.4.4) that metabolizes adenine ribonucleotides in purine metabolism (map00230), leading to the consumption of adenosine. Adenosine was associated with TNF, LSS, DRD5, PTGER3, and ADM2 in host transcriptome; among these, TNF and LSS were downregulated in PLG (logFC = −1·01 and −0·68, respectively), while DRD5, PTGER3, and ADM2 were upregulated (logFC = 0·95, 0·78, and 0·69, respectively).

In PHG, the key metabolite in M052 linking to Brevundimonas was uric acid. Brevundimonas could influence the synthesis of uric acid through xanthine dehydrogenase (EC: 1.17.1.4), and uric acid was linked to the TNF gene in host transcriptome (logFC = −1·01).

In PHG, the key metabolite in M009 linking to Pseudomonas was sulfate. Pseudomonas could influence the synthesis of sulfate through Choline-sulfatase (EC: 3.1.6.6), and sulfate was linked to the PLK3 and LDLR gene in host transcriptome (logFC = −0·185, −0·181).

In PHG, the key metabolite in M001 linking to Massilia was cholic acid. Massilia could influence the synthesis of cholic acid. through 7alpha-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (EC: 1.1.1.159), and cholic acid was linked to the SCTR and GCGR gene in host transcriptome (logFC = −0·558, −0·357).

Key metabolite MR

To assess potential links between the identified microbial-associated metabolites and LUAD, we conducted a Mendelian randomization study on the selected key metabolites, which provided suggestive evidence that serotonin exhibited a significant inverse effect on the occurrence of lung adenocarcinoma (OR = 0·388, 95% CI = 0·170–0·883, p = 0·024). Uric acid also showed a potential protective association with lung adenocarcinoma (OR = 0·609, 95% CI = 0·394–0·912, p = 0·026). Isoleucine, adenosine deaminase, xanthine, sulfate and cholic acid did not have a significant impact on lung adenocarcinoma development (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Heterogeneity analysis, horizontal pleiotropy analysis, and leave-one-out analysis were performed specifically for the associations between serotonin and uric acid with lung adenocarcinoma to rigorously assess the reliability of the MR analysis results. Cochran’s Q test was used to assess the heterogeneity of the selected SNPs, resulting in no heterogeneity between uric acid, serotonin, and LUAD (p > 0·05). The results of the horizontal pleiotropy test indicate that, in this specific MR analysis, no significant horizontal pleiotropy was found for the SNPs related to either serotonin or uric acid in relation to LUAD (Supplementary Table 3). The leave-one-out analysis confirmed the stability of the MR results, suggesting the associations between serotonin, uric acid, and LUAD were not SNP-specific but instead collectively driven (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Discussion

In this study, we identified potential links of microbiome-metabolite-host interactions pertaining to two pathomic subtypes of LUAD. We used CellProfiler to digitize cellular images of LUAD. To avoid excessive internal redundancy in the obtained pathological features, we selected the mean of identified primary objects.

Due to the lack of interpretability of pathomics data, we performed hierarchical clustering of pathomics and combined with clinical data to reveal the heterogeneity of pathology in early lung adenocarcinoma. We observed imaging differences between the two pathological subtypes; compared with PLG, PHG exhibited more lobation and pleural indentation, and SNC was significantly higher. In patients diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma, this typically indicates that PHG has a higher degree of malignancy compared with PLG.

In the subsequent multi-omics differential analysis, we identified several differentially abundant microorganisms in the microbiome, including Lysobacter, Oceanobacillus, Sphingobacterium, Staphylococcus, Xanthomonadaceae (shown in some studies to differ between lung cancer and control mice [17]), and Staphylococcus aureus (common pathogen in pulmonary infections, which are a frequent complication of lung cancer [33]). In the differential analysis of the metabolome, differential metabolites were primarily concentrated in pyrimidine metabolism and the synthesis of several amino acids. Many amino acids, including tryptophan, are considered to play a significant role in the development of lung adenocarcinoma [34].

Subsequently, we conducted both statistical and biological association analyses between microbe-metabolite and metabolite-transcriptome modules. Many studies have demonstrated that pulmonary microorganisms interact with the host through the secretion of metabolites [15, 16, 35]. We established links between microorganisms, metabolite modules, and transcriptome modules using metabolite modules as intermediaries. After screening, we identified six such links. In the microbiota, Massilia was found to have a higher abundance in lung cancer patients with TP53 mutations [36]. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a common bacterium for lung infections, and the relationship between Pseudomonas and lung cancer is unclear. S. aureus and Staphylococcus hominis are associated with smoking [37]. There have been limited studies on the relationships of Sphingomonas and Brevundimonas with lung cancer. In both PHG and PLG, Massilia, Pseudomonas and Sphingomonas are positively correlated with SCN, lobation and pleura.

Studies have reported a positive correlation between isoleucine and squamous cell carcinoma, but reliable evidence on its role in lung adenocarcinoma is still lacking [38]. Adenosine is a precursor of xanthine; in metabolite profiling of lung cancer tumour tissues and serum samples, xanthine has been found to be highly expressed, [39] and in our study, xanthine has a positive correlation with SCN and lobation, which also indicates a positive relationship between xanthine and tumor malignancy. Serum tryptophan concentration in lung adenocarcinoma is significantly lower than in benign conditions, which corroborates findings from our study [40]. Of note, serotonin exhibits distinct spatially heterogeneous functions between peripheral circulation and intratumoral microenvironment. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that peripheral platelet-derived serotonin tends to facilitate lung adenocarcinoma progression and suppress systemic anti-tumor immunity, whereas intratumoral serotonin mediates complicated bidirectional immune and proliferative regulation in tumor lesionsPMC. Thus, the suggestive protective association revealed by MR analysis merely reflects systemic metabolic features, and cannot fully represent the precise regulatory roles of serotonin in local tumor microenvironment. [41, 42] The correlation between serotonin and imaging features is also not entirely consistent. These conflicting results suggest that the specific mechanisms involved require further exploration. Previous studies indicated that uric acid plays a protective role in lung adenocarcinoma [43], though, in our study, uric acid levels were consistent with most metabolic profiles of lung adenocarcinoma, showing no significant differences and no significant correlation with clinical imaging features. The role of sulfate and cholate in lung cancer has not been clarified.

The microbial metabolite-associated host genes are closely associated with the development of lung cancer. Variations in the PSMB gene family were not significant, though overexpression of SLC7A5 is considered to promote NSCLC growth [40, 44], which conflicts somewhat with our findings (High expression of SLC7A5 in PHG). Research on ADRA1D in lung cancer is limited; in other cancers, ADRA1D is often considered to inhibit cancer cell invasion and proliferation [45]. This contradicts the expression of ADRA1D in our different groups, but is consistent with the negative correlation between ADRA1D and image features. suggests that the function of ADRA1D may be tissue-specific and complex. TNF is generally considered to be highly expressed in tumour tissues, consistent with existing research. ADM2 is regarded as a potential immune-related gene associated with a favourable prognosis in patients with lung adenocarcinoma [46]; PTGER3 inhibits breast cancer through ferroptosis [47], and activation of DRD5 inhibits tumour growth by autophagic cell death [48]. LDLR are key mediators of cholesterol uptake and have been identified as novel metabolic targets that limit the progression of a variety of cancers and EGFR-mutant lung cancer cell lines are highly dependent on LDLR. [49] C3 facilitates immunosuppressive microenvironment formation and tumor immune escape in lung adenocarcinoma [50]. PLK3 functions as a core tumor suppressor that inhibits lung epithelial malignant transformation and oncogenic signaling cascades. [51] This also confirms our study that LDLR and SCN were significantly negatively correlated in PHG and PLG. Genes, including LSS, GCGR and SCTR have not been reported in lung cancer. These findings not only enrich our understanding of cancer treatment targets but also indirectly support the potential similar roles of specific genes in lung cancer as observed in our study; however, direct evidence in LUAD models still needs further validation.

Our study also had several limitations. More than half of the patients lacked complete smoking status data; therefore, we were unable to perform intergroup comparisons or statistical adjustments for smoking, a recognized confounding factor of airway microbiome and circulating metabolome characteristics. Our lung tissue microbiome analysis has inherent limitations related to low-biomass samples. Although blank PCR-level negative controls showed no visible amplification, we did not sequence these controls, and we cannot exclude reagent-derived contamination. Notably, several differential genera enriched in PHG (Lysobacter, Oceanobacillus, Sphingobacterium) are common known contaminants, and these taxa require further validation using metagenomic or culturomic methods to confirm their true presence in lung tissue. We did not conduct shotgun metagenomic sequencing, which would have provided more detailed information on microbial taxonomy and functions; however, we used PICRUSt2 to establish relationships between enzymes and microbiota (although there are differences between PICRUSt2 and actual metagenomic data) [52], and linked microbiota with metabolites through MetaCyc. And serum metabolomics only reflects systemic metabolic changes and cannot accurately represent microbe-host interaction events specific to the tumor microenvironment [53]. Therefore, the associations between the lung tumor microbiome and tumor-related metabolites revealed in this study need to be further validated through tissue-level multi-omics analysis. Overall, the relatively small sample size, especially for metabolomic analysis, may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the multi-omics integration results. And transcriptomic PCA failed to distinguish PHG and PLG clearly, which indicated weak overall transcriptional differences and may introduce noise bias into WGCNA module-trait associations.Notably, our bulk RNA-seq profiling was performed without cellular composition deconvolution; thus, the observed transcriptional differences may partly reflect variations in tissue cellular proportions rather than genuine gene expression changes. Future studies incorporating computational cell type deconvolution or single-cell transcriptomics will help clarify cell-type-specific expression patterns. In large-scale correlation analyses, FDR correction was not performed in order to retain more results, which may carry the risk of false positives. Therefore, all identified associations should be considered preliminary and require validation in larger independent cohorts.In addition, the MR analysis had inherent limitations: we used GWAS summary statistics from European ancestry populations, which may not fully match the genetic architecture of our study cohort, potentially limiting the generalizability of the suggestive causal associations identified between metabolites and lung adenocarcinoma. Therefore, MR results should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive causal evidence.All of our analyses are preliminary computational results and need to be validated through cell or animal experiments. In terms of experimental validation, existing studies have mainly relied on the size of lung nodules and pathological characteristics to model lung adenocarcinoma, but specific standardized parameters and methods for early-stage cancer have not yet been established,and all conclusions require subsequent in vitro experimental validation. [54, 55].

In summary, we annotated the classification of pathomics data of early lung adenocarcinoma through clinical imaging images. Then, multi-omics analysis was used to establish the microbial-metabolite-host gene links in different pathomic subgroups. MR analysis further validated the link between microbial-derived metabolites and lung adenocarcinoma, providing insights into possible microbiome-host interactions during early lung adenocarcinoma.
