Synergistic effects of pigments, morphology, and physio-biochemical parameters reveal petal coloration differences in eight rose cultivars
Authors: Xianglu Jia, Xiaoyu Gao, Hongying Liao, Tian Xu, Lurong Pan, Li Gong, Ting Xu, Quanguang Yang, Tinghui Yin, Shu Li, Miaoqin Wei, Weichao Teng, Zilong Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As a key ornamental plant, the core value of rose (Rosa spp.) resides in its petals. Variations in petal development and physio-biochemical parameters among cultivars exhibiting divergent floral coloration could significantly influence their horticultural performance and commercial viability. To address this, we systematically analyzed eight common rose cultivars from Nanning Botanical Garden, measuring petal color parameters, pigment content, morphological structure, antioxidant capacity, nutrient and mineral element content. Our objectives were to: (1) characterize coloration features, (2) identify key drivers of color variation, and (3) identify superior cultivar. Our study revealed that: Petal coloration is primarily regulated by pigment content (chlorophyll, carotenoids, anthocyanins, and flavonoids), enabling classification into deep-toned and light-toned groups. Morphological and physio-biochemical parameters collectively drive color variation: deep-toned cultivars (R4, Rosa ‘Golden Scepter’; R5, Rosa ‘Brass Band’; R7, Rosa ‘Sheherazad’; R8, Rosa ‘Hiogi’) are synergistically regulated by petal thickness and width ratio (TWR), phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) content, malondialdehyde (MDA) content, superoxide dismutase (SOD) content, and phosphorus (P), boron (B), and calcium (Ca) content, whereas light-toned cultivars (R1, Rosa ‘Icecap’; R2, Rosa ‘Scepter’d Isle’; R6, Rosa ‘Pink Fan’) are synergistically regulated by soluble sugar (SS), catalase (CAT) content, nitrogen (N), and zinc (Zn). The blue hue (R3, Rosa ‘Shinoburedo’) is specifically regulated by elevated pH and Ca. Membership function analysis identifies R4 as the top-performing cultivar, demonstrating superior landscape applicability and comprehensive development potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58202-w.

Introduction

Petal coloration is regulated not only by external factors such as temperature, humidity, and light, but also by a complex interplay of internal factors including pigments, pH, pigment enzyme content, morphological structure, nutrients, antioxidant capacity, and mineral elements1–3. Chlorophyll (Chl), anthocyanins (AC), flavonoids (Fla), and carotenoids (Car) are key pigments responsible for flower coloration4. Although elevated Chl content confers green pigmentation, its concentration remains generally low in non-green petals5. Car and Fla collectively contribute to yellow or orange coloration6. The chromatic expression of AC is influenced by vacuolar pH, while intramolecular co-pigmentation can shift their spectral properties, leading to variations between red and blue hues. For instance, in Hydrangea macrophylla, petals appear red at pH 3.3 and blue at pH 4.17. Similarly, in Ipomoea purpurea, petals transition from purplish-red to blue as vacuolar pH increases from 6.6 to 7.78. Changes in pigment content are characterized by color parameters (L*, a*, b*, C*, h*)9,10. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), the rate-limiting enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway, is crucial for AC synthesis, and its content shows a significant positive correlation with AC levels11. By scavenging reactive oxygen species (e.g., O₂⁻, H₂O₂) and reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) content, the antioxidant system maintains cellular physiology and functional stability, thereby ensuring color stability12. Nutrients within petals, such as total carbon (C), non-structural carbohydrates (NSC; including starch (St) and soluble sugars (SS)), and soluble protein (SP), serve as key metabolic reserves. These provide energy and structural foundations for petal development and morphogenesis13,14. Among these nutrients, dynamic NSC levels directly influence AC synthesis and color intensity by supplying UDP-glucose substrate and modulating vacuolar pH7,15. During Rosa damascena flower opening, NSC content, as well as Chl and Car, peaks before declining16. Mineral element content frequently correlates significantly with color parameters and pigment levels. This correlation stems from minerals functioning as essential components that modulate coloration through multiple mechanisms: chelation effects, pH buffering, enzymatic activation, and redox regulation. Specific mechanisms include altering vacuolar pH, affecting pigment metabolic enzyme content, and stabilizing AC structure17–20. Furthermore, petal morphological structure modulates color expression physically. The microstructure of epidermal cells regulates coloration by controlling light absorption and scattering. Higher petal thickness and width ratio (TWR) characteristic of conical epidermal cells intensifies red-tone reflection through enhanced light diffraction thereby deepening coloration. Conversely, lower TWR associated with flat cells induces diffuse light scattering resulting in lighter visual appearance21.

Roses (Rosa spp., Rosaceae) exhibit exceptional adaptability, vigorous growth, striking floral diversity, high ornamental value, versatile horticultural applications and are cultivated globally22,23. Although extensive research exists on landscape use24, cultivation practices25, germplasm evaluation26, metabolite analysis27, fragrance profiles28, bioactive compounds29, functional component development30,31, and genetic regulation32, these studies typically address singular dimensions. The pronounced color variation among rose cultivars under identical conditions stems from the complex interplay of internal factors, encompassing multidimensional indicators like morpho-structural and physio-biochemical parameters. However, how these factors collectively determine final coloration remains poorly understood. Few studies comprehensively integrate pigments, morphology, and physio-biochemical parameters to unravel their synergistic effects on rose color development. Consequently, we hypothesize that rose flower coloration (characterized by specific color parameters and pigment content) is fundamentally regulated by the aforementioned parameters, including pH, pigment enzyme content, morphological structure, antioxidant capacity, and nutritional/mineral status.

This study evaluates the hypothesis using eight multicolored rose cultivars from Nanning Botanical Garden. We systematically measured multidimensional indicators and applied multivariate statistical analyses (correlation, principal component, redundancy, and membership function analyses) to: characterize color features, identify key drivers of color variation, and identify superior cultivar based on integrated morphological structural and physio-biochemical parameters. This approach elucidates complex regulatory mechanisms beyond single-factor limitations while meeting the garden’s practical needs for landscape development and industrial advancement by identifying superior cultivar with dual horticultural-commercial potential, thus supporting scientific rose applications.

Materials and methods

Study site and species

Situated at the Plant Germplasm Resource Nursery (22° 47′ 50.14″ N, 108° 24′ 16.60″ E) within Nanning Botanical Garden, Guangxi, South China, the experimental site lies within a subtropical monsoon climate zone. Characterized by warm conditions (mean annual temperature: 21.8 °C), abundant sunshine (mean annual duration: 1480.4 h), and high precipitation (mean annual rainfall: 1453.4 mm), this typical urban green space covers 25.11 km2 of flat terrain with well-developed transport infrastructure and marked urbanization features.

Eight rose cultivars with distinct flower colors were selected from the Plant Germplasm Resource Nursery at Nanning Botanical Garden: Rosa ‘Icecap’ (White, R1), Rosa ‘Scepter’d Isle’ (Pinkish white, R2), Rosa 'Shinoburedo' (Pale blue, R3), Rosa ‘Golden Scepter’ (Golden yellow, R4), Rosa ‘Brass Band’ (Orange-yellow, R5), Rosa ‘Pink Fan’ (Pink, R6), Rosa ‘Sheherazad’ (Purple, R7) and Rosa ‘Hiogi’ (Deep red, R8). Using a randomized block design, we established three experimental plots as block units, each containing six uniformly growing plants per cultivar. During peak bloom (April–May 2025), fully opened flowers were collected. Middle-layer petals were selected, and their central regions were retained for analysis after excising the basal and marginal zones with a sharp scalpel on ice. All samples were processed within 30 min of harvest to ensure freshness and minimize tissue aging. Within each block, same-cultivar petal samples were uniformly pooled to generate composite samples, yielding three biological replicates per cultivar. Samples underwent immediate transport to the laboratory and were allocated to four groups for measuring different types of indicators: Group 1 for instant analysis of color parameters and pigment content; Group 2 for vacuum infiltration with FAA fixative prior to paraffin sectioning; Group 3 for oven-drying to constant weight following enzyme inactivation at 100 °C for 30 min and subsequent drying at 70 °C for 10 h before total carbon and mineral element assays; Group 4 for storage at − 80 °C pending determination of antioxidant physiological indices and nutrient content. All analyses employed three biological replicates with three technical replicates each. Determination of petal color parameters, pigments content and pH

Color Measurement: Flower colors were characterized using the Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage (CIELAB) color space coordinates33. Petal reflectance was measured with a spectrophotocolorimeter (DS-700E, Hangzhou CHNSpec Technology Co., Ltd., China) under C/2° illuminant conditions. The measured parameters included L* (lightness), a* (red-green axis), b* (yellow-blue axis), C* (chroma), and h* (hue angle)34.

Pigment Extraction and Quantification: A 0.2 g sample of fresh petal tissue from the central region of each flower was finely chopped and placed in a centrifuge tube. 5 mL of 80% (v/v) acetone were added, and the tube was stored protected from light at 4 °C for over 24 h, until the petals became colorless. The supernatant was obtained by filtration. Chlorophyll (Chl) and carotenoid (Car) contents were quantified spectrophotometrically using a full-wavelength microplate reader (Varioskan Flash3001, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Germany), following established methods35,36. Anthocyanin (AC) and flavonoid (Fla) contents were both determined using commercial assay kits (Shanghai Win–win Biotechnology Inc., China).

pH Measurement: 1 g of finely chopped fresh petal tissue from the central region was ground. Immediately after adding 8 mL of deionized water, the pH of the petal homogenate was measured using a pH meter (PHS-3E, Shanghai Leici Scientific Instrument Co., LTD, China)37.

Determination of petal pigment enzyme content, antioxidant enzyme content and malondialdehyde content

Pigment Enzyme content: Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) content was determined using a commercial assay kit (Shanghai Win–win Biotechnology Inc., China).

Antioxidant Enzyme Extraction and Assay: Crude enzyme extracts for antioxidant enzyme analysis were prepared as follows: 0.3 g of fresh petal tissue from the central region was homogenized in a mortar pre-cooled with liquid nitrogen. An appropriate amount of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and quartz sand was added, followed by 3 mL of ice-cold 0.05 mol L⁻1 phosphate buffer (pH 7.0, containing Na₂HPO₄ and KH₂PO₄). The homogenate was centrifuged at 11,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (crude enzyme extract) was carefully collected, and the pellet was discarded. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) content was assayed using the NBT photoreduction method, peroxidase (POD) using the guaiacol method, catalase (CAT) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) using UV spectrophotometric methods (H₂O₂ decomposition for CAT and AsA oxidation for APX), following established protocols38–40.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) Extraction and Content Measurement: For MDA extraction, 1 g of fresh petal tissue from the central region was homogenized with quartz sand and 2 mL of 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA). An additional 8 mL of 10% TCA was gradually added, and the homogenate was centrifuged at 4000×g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant served as the MDA extract. MDA content was determined following the method of Idalgo et al.41.

Determination of petal unstructured carbohydrates, carbon and soluble protein content

Starch (St) and soluble sugar (SS) contents were determined according to Zhou et al.42. Briefly, 0.2 g of petal tissue from the central region was ground in liquid nitrogen. The homogenate was extracted with 80% (v/v) ethanol and centrifuged. The resulting supernatant (SS extract) was used to quantify SS using the anthrone method43. The remaining pellet was gelatinized and then extracted overnight with HClO₄. After centrifugation, the supernatant (starch extract) was collected to determine St content using the anthrone method43. Non-structural carbohydrate (NSC) content was calculated as NSC = St + SS.

Soluble protein (SP) content was determined using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 method32. Total carbon (C) content in petals was measured with a Total Organic Carbon (TOC) analyzer (Analytik Jena AG, Germany)44.

Determination of petal mineral element content

Add 0.5 g of ground dried petal mid-region sample to a digestion tube, followed by 5 mL of a mixed acid solution of HNO₃ and HClO₄ (5:1, v/v). Digest the mixture on a digestion furnace for 3–4 h until a clear and transparent solution is obtained. After cooling, filter the digestate and dilute to a final volume of 50 mL. Elemental contents of phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), zinc (Zn), boron (B), iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), and sulfur (S) were determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Germany)45. Nitrogen (N) content was measured using an AA3 Continuous Flow Analyzer (Model G-157-96; Bran-Luebbe Inc., Germany)46.

Determination of petal morphological structure parameters

Temporary water-mounted slides of the petal epidermis were prepared, and the epidermal cells were observed under a light microscope at 10× magnification (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Petals were anatomically examined using paraffin sectioning47–49. Central petal tissues were fixed in FAA solution (formaldehyde: glacial acetic acid: 70% ethanol = 5:5:90, v/v) for ≥ 24 h, followed by dehydration through an ethanol series (70–100%), clearing in xylene, paraffin infiltration and embedding at 65 °C. Sections of approximately 4 μm thickness were prepared using a microtome. After deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through a graded ethanol series, sections were stained with safranin O, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Digital images were acquired with a slide scanner (P250 FLASH Ⅲ, 3DHISTECH, Hungary) and analyzed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to measure: Petal thickness (PT), Upper epidermal thickness (UET), Lower epidermal thickness (LET), Parenchyma tissue thickness (PTT), Upper epidermal cell width (UEW), and Petal thickness and width ratio (TWR).

Qualification of petal morphological structure, nutrient content, antioxidant ability and mineral element content

The petal morphological structure index (PMSI) was developed based on Huang et al.50 with minor modifications: addition of Eq. (3) for negative indicators. Comprehensive membership function evaluation for qualification of eight rose cultivars

Following the method of Zhang et al.51 with minor modifications, the qualification data were standardized to scale phenotypic trait function values between 0 and 1 using the formula:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{R}}\left( {X_{i} } \right) = \left( {X_{i} - X_{min} } \right)/\left( {X_{max} - X_{min} } \right)$$\end{document}where Xi is the measured value of a parameter, Xmin and Xmax denote the minimum and maximum values of that parameter across the eight rose cultivars, respectively, and Ri is the membership degree of the parameter. The sum of membership degrees for all qualification indices was calculated as the total membership degree. The eight rose cultivars were then ranked based on their total membership degrees, with higher values indicating better comprehensive performance.

Data processing

Data are presented as means ± standard deviations (STD) (n = 3). Statistical significance for all data (including those in the supplementary materials) was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD test (P < 0.05) using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The 3D coordinate plot, bar charts, correlation heatmaps, and principal component analysis (PCA) plots were generated using Origin 2019b (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). Redundancy Analysis (RDA) and associated graphical representations were generated using Canoco 5.0 (Microcomputer Power, Ithaca, NY, USA) software.

The PCA plot reveals associations among color parameters, pigment contents and rose cultivars. As shown in Fig. 4, Principal Component 1 (PC1) and Principal Component 2 (PC2) collectively explained 73.2% of the total variance. Cultivars R1, R2, and R3 showed close vector alignment with L* and Chl at acute angles, indicating that petal coloration in these cultivars is primarily regulated by L* and Chl. Similarly, R4 and R5 aligned with Car and b* vectors, suggesting dominant roles of Car and b* in their coloration. R8 exhibited alignment with C*, Fla, a*, and AC, demonstrating their collective influence on red petal development. Discussion

Coloration characteristics of petals in eight rose cultivars

Floral coloration represents a paramount ornamental trait in plants. Quantitative color parameters provide precise characterization of petal pigmentation, enabling systematic flower color analysis52–54. This study observed progressive L* value reduction alongside a* value elevation during petal darkening, revealing that light-toned cultivars (R1, R2, R3, R6) exhibit high brightness with low redness, while deep-toned cultivars (R4, R5, R7, R8) display low brightness with high redness. Elevated b* values specifically imparted yellowish hues to R4 and R5, consistent with established literature55. Floral coloration further correlates with ecological functionality: vivid pigmentation enhances diurnal pollinator attraction and pollination efficiency, whereas reduced nocturnal visibility aids herbivore deterrence56. Light-colored petals attract nocturnal pollinators by enhancing visibility in low-light conditions, while pollinator preferences concurrently drive color variation57–59. Beyond landscape aesthetics, observed variations in rose petal coloration may influence ecological dynamics. Research investigating connections between petal pigmentation and animal interactions, specifically pollinators such as bees and butterflies alongside herbivorous insects including beetles and aphids, holds critical implications for biodiversity conservation in botanical gardens and interspecies interaction studies, requiring further examination.

Petal coloration and chromatic variation in roses are principally regulated by four key pigments: Chl, Car, AC and Fla34,60. Our integrated correlation and principal component analyses confirmed significant interdependencies between pigment profiles and colorimetric parameters. Deep-toned cultivars (R4, R5, R7, R8) exhibited elevated AC and Fla concentrations coupled with reduced Chl content, while light-toned cultivars (R1, R2, R3, R6) displayed increased Chl accumulation alongside diminished Fla levels. These pigment distribution patterns demonstrate that varietal color differentiation is primarily governed by pigment composition, consistent with findings by Wang et al.52 and Yeo et al.61. Beyond their chromatic functions, these pigments confer photoprotective advantages and enhance environmental adaptability62. Deeper pigmentation provides thermal benefits in cooler climates through efficient heat absorption, whereas lighter pigmentation mitigates photodamage via enhanced solar reflectance63,64. Collectively, the synergistic interaction of these pigments modulates colorimetric parameters and orchestrates petal coloration across rose cultivars.

Petal morphological structures and physio-biochemical parameters drive coloration development in eight rose cultivars

Petal coloration is integrally regulated by multiple intrinsic factors1,3. Our study revealed extensive significant correlations among chromatic parameters, pigment content, pH, PAL content, morphological features, nutrient content, antioxidant properties, and mineral element content, demonstrating their synergistic involvement in color development. Redundancy analysis identified TWR, pH, PAL, SS, MDA, SOD, CAT, N, P, Zn, B, and Ca as primary drivers of chromatic variation across eight rose cultivars. These morphological structural and physio-biochemical parameters collectively modulate coloration, with pH exhibiting the highest explanatory power (49.3%, P = 0.002), establishing it as the core regulatory determinant. Substantial evidence indicates pH governs petal coloration through three principal mechanisms: (1) altering AC conformation to shift color equilibrium, yielding red hues in acidic environments and blue/purple tones under alkaline conditions; (2) modifying pigment coordination sites to influence co-pigmentation effects; and (3) regulating formation, stability, and configuration of metal-pigment chelates65,68. Specifically, calcium stabilizes the quinoidal AC structure through coordination with phenolic hydroxyl groups (3'-OH and 4’-OH), enhancing blue pigmentation69. Consistent with these mechanisms, elevated pH and Ca levels directly induced bluish pigmentation in cultivar R3.

It is often observed that TWR exhibits a significant correlation with color depth. For instance, in Catharanthus roseus, TWR shows a highly significant negative correlation with L* but a positive correlation with a*, resulting in darker coloration70. Similarly, a study of 107 Dendrobium hybrids found that AC-rich cultivars displayed a positive correlation between the TWR and a*, enhancing red coloration and saturation, consistent with the findings of this study71. However, in Morning Glory, an increase in TWR accompanies a rise in pH (from 6.6 to 7.7), shifting AC coloration from red to blue72. This contrasts with our observation that cultivars with higher TWR exhibited redder petal color and lower pH. This phenomenon may be attributed to high TWR increasing pigment distribution density (e.g., elevating AC concentration within vacuoles), indirectly intensifying color. Kay et al. surveyed 201 angiosperms and found that higher petal TWR is prevalent in high-light environments73. This is because high TWR both enhances light absorption efficiency by refracting incident light towards pigment-rich regions at the cell base (e.g., AC-containing vacuoles or carotenoid-containing plastids), reducing energy loss from light scattering, and minimizes direct exposure of excess light energy to the plasma membrane. This promotes efficient light utilization over accumulation, thereby lowering the risk of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and cell membrane damage. Our study also observed concurrent increases in SOD and MDA with TWR. This likely stems from energy absorption by petals raising temperature, promoting superoxide anion (O₂⁻) generation and MDA accumulation, which subsequently triggers SOD content74.

Although this study identified a highly significant positive correlation between P and Fla, and a significant negative correlation between P and Chl, this may relate to P regulating the MYB-bHLH-WDR (MBW) transcription complex. This complex coordinately activates Fla biosynthesis genes (e.g., CHI, F3’H) and Chl degradation genes (e.g., PAO)75. In Gerbera hybrida, Ca content showed a significant negative correlation with b* value. High Ca treatment reduced Fla content, further revealing that Ca alters Fla metabolism by inhibiting FLS gene expression and activating DFR gene expression, consequently influencing flower color19. In the present study, cultivars R4, R5, and R8 exhibited higher Fla content and b* values, potentially associated with their relatively lower Ca content. PAL content typically shows a significant positive correlation with AC accumulation. This enzyme provides precursors for AC synthesis by catalyzing the conversion of phenylalanine to cinnamic acid, and its content is co-regulated by light and temperature, aligning with our results76. In this study, TWR, PAL, MDA, SOD, P, B, and Ca acted synergistically to drive the formation of deep-toned petals (R4, R5, R7, and R8).

Under sunlight exposure, accumulated AC can act as sugar buffers and provide indirect photoprotection, compensating for the decline in other metabolic processes like St and protein synthesis77. Additionally, to cope with light stress and natural senescence, petals consume non-structural carbohydrates (NSC; St and SS) to maintain coloration and longevity. The mechanisms include:(1) St degradation into SS provides a sustained carbon source for AC synthesis; (2) SS indirectly stabilizes AC conformation by maintaining vacuolar pH; (3) SS is converted into flavonoids via the phenylpropanoid pathway; (4) SS serves as an energy reserve to delay senescence; (5) SS acts as an osmolyte to lower cellular water potential, mitigating light-induced membrane damage; and (6) NADPH generated from SS breakdown supplies reducing power to the ascorbate–glutathione (ASA-GSH) cycle, scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and preventing AC oxidation/fading78–81.

In this study, AC showed a significant positive correlation with MDA and SS, but a significant negative correlation with St. This indicates that petals can promote AC synthesis in response to excessive light stress while MDA accumulates, thereby protecting normal metabolism. A significant positive correlation was observed between MDA and a*, while a highly significant negative correlation occurred between MDA and L*. This suggests darker petals may experience greater stress due to factors like temperature rise or water loss during light absorption82,83. Conversely, lighter petals may reduce stress levels by enhancing light scattering to mitigate negative light effects. Redundancy analysis revealed that the arrow directions for CAT and MDA were opposite and formed an obtuse angle, indicating that increased CAT content reduces MDA content. This occurs because CAT catalyzes the decomposition of H₂O₂ into H₂O and O₂, decreasing ROS accumulation and subsequently lowering MDA content, thus stabilizing petal coloration84.

N regulates pH via H⁺-ATPase activity, whereby decreased activity under nitrogen starvation elevates pH, promoting a shift toward blue-purple coloration85. Furthermore, N coordinately regulates positive (MYB transcription factors) and negative (LBD proteins) modulators of the flavonoid pathway in grapevines. Low N increases the transcription levels of genes such as chalcone synthase (CHS), flavonoid 3′,5′-hydroxylase (F3′5′H), dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR), and leucoanthocyanidin dioxygenase (LDOX), thereby promoting AC synthesis86. In this study, N exhibited significant negative correlations with a*, AC, and Fla, consistent with the above findings. The significant negative correlation between Zn and Fla observed here may arise from Zn forming chelate complexes with Fla molecules, affecting their biosynthesis or stability, and inhibiting the content of Fla synthase87. In this study, SS, CAT, N, and Zn acted synergistically to drive the formation of light-toned petals (R1, R2, and R6).

In summary, our results demonstrate that petal morphological structure and physio-biochemical parameters collectively drive color variation across the eight rose cultivars, with TWR, pH, PAL, SS, MDA, SOD, CAT, N, P, Zn, B, and Ca serving as key drivers.

Comprehensive evaluation of eight rose cultivars

Roses (Rosa spp.) possess high ornamental value in landscaping, strong adaptability, and rich nutritional content, demonstrating significant potential for applications in food, cosmetics, and related industries88–92. With its exceptional horticultural traits and versatile applications, the rose has emerged as a globally significant ornamental cash crop, propelling advancements in modern urban landscaping alongside economic and cultural development93. This study selected eight representative rose cultivars widely used at Nanning Botanical Garden. Though exhibiting diverse floral colors, all effectively enhance landscape aesthetics. By comparing their coloration differences and investigating underlying drivers, we aimed to elucidate color characteristics and provide insights for landscape applications and cultivation management.

Radar chart analysis (Fig. 8) revealed distinct variations among cultivars in petal morphological structure and physio-biochemical parameters: R4 showed superior nutrient content, antioxidant capacity, and mineral element levels; R5 and R7 showed enhanced antioxidant capabilities; R6 showed optimal morphological structure and nutrient content. To identify the best-performing cultivar for foundational planting and utilization, comprehensive evaluation using membership function scores (Table 4) indicated R4 as the top performer, followed by R6 and R5, with R1 being the weakest.

This study has the following limitations: Firstly, analysis was limited to full bloom stage; future work should include other flowering stages to track dynamic trait variations. Secondly, only eight cultivars from Nanning Botanical Garden were examined, meaning findings primarily explain coloration mechanisms in these specific cultivars. Future studies should include a larger number of cultivars to broaden the generalizability of the conclusions. Consequently, screening additional Nanning-adapted cultivars remains essential to enrich landscape diversity, advance horticultural applications, and promote industrial development.

Conclusion

Significant variations exist among the eight rose cultivars in color parameters, pigment content, morphological structure and physio-biochemical parameters, directly resulting in differential ornamental value and developmental potential. Overall, petal coloration is primarily regulated by pigment content (Chl, Car, AC, and Fla). Deep-toned cultivars (R4, R5, R7, R8) exhibit higher AC and Fla content with lower Chl levels, characterized by low lightness (diminished L*) and high red-green axis values (enhanced a*). Conversely, light-toned cultivars (R1, R2, R3, R6) display higher Chl content and lower Fla levels, showing high lightness (enhanced L*) and low red-green axis values (diminished a*). Petal morphological structure and physio-biochemical parameters collectively drive color variation across these cultivars: TWR, PAL, MDA, SOD, P, B, and Ca synergistically drive deep-tone formation (R4, R5, R7, R8); SS, CAT, N, and Zn coordinately regulate light-tone formation (R1, R2, R6); while elevated pH and Ca levels specifically induce a bluish tone in R3. Comprehensive evaluation identifies R4 (Rosa ‘Golden Scepter’) as superior in nutrient content, mineral element content, and antioxidant capacity, demonstrating dual utility for foundational landscape planting and integrated development applications.
