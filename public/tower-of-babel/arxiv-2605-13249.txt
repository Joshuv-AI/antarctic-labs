Acoustic Chirality
Alex J. Vernon, Konstantin Y. Bliokh

Abstract
We reveal a previously unknown continuous symmetry and conservation law in the equations of linear isotropic elasticity, which describe the chirality of elastic waves. We show that the integral chirality is determined by the population imbalance between right- and left-handed transverse phonons, whereas the local chirality density generally involves both transverse and longitudinal wave components. We also introduce the related concepts of acoustic helicity and “false chirality”. The theory is illustrated with simple interference fields exhibiting distinct distributions of chirality, spin angular momentum, and false chirality. Our results establish chirality as a fundamental property of elastic waves and provide a general theoretical framework for chiral acoustic phenomena.

Acoustic duality and chirality.—

The acoustic wave field in an isotropic homogeneous elastic medium can be characterized by the local displacement of the medium particles,
\mathbfcal{R}({\bf r},t)
, and the corresponding velocity field
\mathbfcal{V}({\bf r},t)=\partial\mathbfcal{R}/\partial t
.
Linear elasticity shares similarities with Maxwell’s electromagnetism
3
;
10
, where the
\mathbfcal{R}
and
\mathbfcal{V}
fields can be associated with the magnetic vector-potential and electric field, respectively.
To further develop this analogy, we introduce an additional vector field
\mathbfcal{F}=c_{t}\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbfcal{R}
(analogous to the magnetic field, with a minus sign) and a scalar field
\mathcal{G}=c_{l}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbfcal{R}
, which characterizes longitudinal sound modes.
Here,
c_{t}=\sqrt{\mu/\rho}
and
c_{l}=\sqrt{(\lambda+2\mu)/\rho}
are the velocities of the transverse shear and longitudinal compression modes, respectively, where
\lambda
and
\mu
are the Lamé coefficients and
\rho
is the mass density of the medium.

Using the three fields
\mathbfcal{V}
,
\mathbfcal{F}
, and
\mathcal{G}
(which all share the same velocity dimension), the linear-elasticity wave equation
\partial^{2}\mathbfcal{R}/\partial t^{2}=c_{l}^{2}\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbfcal{R})-c_{t}^{2}\bm{\nabla}\times(\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbfcal{R})

31
;
10
can be recast as the following system of first-order equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbfcal{V}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-c_{t}\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbfcal{F}+c_{l}\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{G}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbfcal{F}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=c_{t}\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbfcal{V}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=c_{l}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbfcal{V}\,.

(1)

The field
\mathbfcal{F}
is purely transverse,
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbfcal{F}=0
, whereas the velocity field can be decomposed using the Helmholtz decomposition into transverse (divergence-free) and longitudinal (curl-free) components:

\mathbfcal{V}=\mathbfcal{V}_{t}+\mathbfcal{V}_{l}=-c_{t}\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbfcal{M}+c_{l}\bm{\nabla}\mathcal{K}\,,

(2)

where
\mathbfcal{M}
and
\mathcal{K}
are the vector and scalar potentials of the transverse and longitudinal velocity fields
\mathbfcal{V}_{t}
and
\mathbfcal{V}_{l}
, respectively. (Note that
\mathbfcal{M}
is analogous to the electric vector-potential in electromagnetism

;
1
;

;

;

Using the decomposition (

The latter describes longitudinal compression waves and is similar to the sound-wave equations in fluids (where
\mathcal{G}
parallels the pressure field).
From the similarity of the transverse subsystem to Maxwell’s equations, it immediately follows that Eqs. (
Acoustic Chirality

\displaystyle\to\mathbfcal{F}\cos\theta-\mathbfcal{V}_{t}\sin\theta\,.

Equations (
Acoustic Chirality

According to Noether’s theorem, the symmetry transformation (

\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{U}=0\,,

\displaystyle C

(5)

\displaystyle=\underbracket{\frac{\rho}{2}\left[\mathbfcal{V}_{t}\cdot(\bm{\nabla}\!\times\!\mathbfcal{V}_{t})+\mathbfcal{F}\cdot(\bm{\nabla}\!\times\!\mathbfcal{F})\right]}_{C_{t}}+\underbracket{\frac{\rho}{2}\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\mathbfcal{V}_{t}\!\times\!\mathbfcal{V}_{l})}_{C_{m}}

\displaystyle\bar{C}

\displaystyle=\underbracket{\frac{\rho\omega}{2c_{t}}{\rm Im}\!\left({\bf V}_{t}^{*}\cdot{\bf F}\right)}_{\bar{C}_{t}}+\underbracket{\frac{\rho\omega}{4c_{t}}{\rm Im}\!\left({\bf V}_{l}^{*}\cdot{\bf F}\right)}_{\bar{C}_{m}}\,,

\displaystyle\bar{\bf U}

\displaystyle=\bar{\bf U}_{t}=\frac{\rho\omega}{4}{\rm Im}\!\left({\bf V}_{t}^{*}\!\times{\bf V}_{t}+{\bf F}^{*}\!\times{\bf F}\right)\,.

{\bf k}

{\bf F}=-(c_{t}/\omega){\bf k}\times{\bf V}_{t}

\bar{C}=\bar{C}_{t}=\omega{\bf k}\cdot\bar{\bf S}

\bar{\bf U}=\omega^{2}\bar{\bf S}

Importantly, Eqs. (

describe local acoustic chirality in a much more general case of arbitrarily structured waves, where the plane-wave approximation is no longer applicable (see examples below).

Integral chirality and phonons.—

We now examine the integral chirality of a localized acoustic wave field:
\langle C\rangle=\int C\,d^{3}{\bf r}
.
The mixed part of the chirality density (
5
) vanishes upon integration over all space, so that the integral chirality is determined entirely by the transverse modes:

\langle C\rangle=\int C_{t}\,d^{3}{\bf r}
.

To express the integral chirality in terms of phonon amplitudes and numbers, we represent the transverse velocity field as a Fourier integral over plane waves with circular polarizations (helicities)
\sigma=\pm 1
:

\mathbfcal{V}_{t}=\sum_{\sigma}\int\!\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega}{2\rho}}\!\left[a^{\sigma}({\bf k})\mathbf{\hat{e}}^{\sigma}({\bf k})e^{i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}-i\omega t}+{\rm c.c.}\right].

(10)

Here,
a^{\sigma}
are the amplitudes of right- and left-handed phonons, while
\mathbf{\hat{e}}^{\sigma}
are the corresponding unit polarization vectors.
One can show that the integral energy of the transverse field is
\langle E\rangle=\int\hbar\omega(|a^{+}|^{2}+|a^{-}|^{2})\,d^{3}{\bf k}
, so that
|a^{\pm}|^{2}
can be regarded as the densities of right- and left-handed phonons in
{\bf k}
-space.

Using the representation (
10
), the integral acoustic chirality can be calculated in a manner entirely analogous to electromagnetism

;
1
;
52
;
9
;
12

\langle C\rangle=\int\hbar\omega k(|a^{+}|^{2}-|a^{-}|^{2})\,d^{3}{\bf k}\,.

(11)

For a quasi-monochromatic field with a narrow frequency spectrum centered around
\omega_{0}
, Eq. (
11
) reduces to
\langle C\rangle\simeq\hbar(\omega^{2}_{0}/c_{t})(N^{+}-N^{-})
, where
N^{\pm}
are the total numbers of right- and left-handed phonons.

Acoustic helicity.—

It is known from electromagnetism that chirality and helicity are closely related quantities

;
11
;
1
;
52
;
9

12

For both photons and phonons, the helicity of a plane wave is given by the projection of its spin onto the wavevector direction, while the integral helicity is determined by the difference between the numbers of the right- and left-handed particles:

\bar{C}_{H}\!=\bar{\bf S}\cdot\frac{\bf k}{k}\,,~\langle C_{H}\rangle\!=\!\int\!\hbar(|a^{+}|^{2}-|a^{-}|^{2})\,d^{3}{\bf k}=\hbar(N^{+}-N^{-}).

(12)

Hence, in the monochromatic limit, the acoustic chirality quantities described by Eqs. (
4
)–(
6
), (
8
), (
9
), and (
11

\omega^{2}/c_{t}
.

To properly introduce the acoustic helicity density and its flow, satisfying a continuity equation, we return to the Helmholtz decomposition of the velocity field, Eq. (
2

Assuming that
\mathbfcal{M}
is a purely transverse field,
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbfcal{M}=0
, we find from Eqs. (
Acoustic Chirality
) that
\partial\mathbfcal{M}/\partial t=\mathbfcal{F}
and
\partial\mathcal{K}/\partial t=\mathcal{G}
.
Since
\mathbfcal{V}=\partial\mathbfcal{R}/\partial t
, it follows that the fields
\mathbfcal{R}
,
\mathbfcal{M}
,
\mathcal{K}
satisfy the same equations of motion (
Acoustic Chirality
) as the fields
\mathbfcal{V}
,
\mathbfcal{F}
,
\mathcal{G}
. Accordingly, they obey the same dual symmetry (
Acoustic Chirality
) and conservation law (
4
)–(
6
) under the substitution

\left\{\mathbfcal{V},~\mathbfcal{F},~\mathcal{G}\right\}\to\left\{\mathbfcal{R},~\mathbfcal{M},~\mathcal{K}\right\}\,.

Applying this substitution and defining the corresponding densities similar to Eqs. (
5
), (
6

8

Notably, unlike the chirality density

is gauge-dependent because the vector-potential
\mathbfcal{M}

\langle C_{H}\rangle

False chirality.—

Alongside the proper
P
-odd and
T
-even chirality density, one can also introduce a “false chirality” (using the terminology of L. D. Barron
6
;
5
) characterized by a
P
-odd and
T
-odd scalar. In light-matter interactions, such a quantity describes the nonreciprocal magnetoelectric effect, i.e., the magnetic (electric) response of matter induced by an external electric (magnetic) field
8
.
Optical media exhibiting this type of response are also known as “Tellegen media”
48
;
51
;
22
;
26
;
55
.

For the elasticity equations (
Acoustic Chirality
), we construct a false-chirality density
D
, satisfying the conservation law
\partial D/\partial t+\bm{\nabla}\cdot{\bf W}=0
and expressed through acoustic spin density (

\displaystyle D=\dfrac{\rho}{2}\left[\mathbfcal{V}\cdot(\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbfcal{R})-\mathbfcal{R}\cdot(\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbfcal{V})\right]=\dfrac{1}{2}\bm{\nabla}\cdot{\bf S}\,,

(14)

\displaystyle{\bf W}=\dfrac{\rho}{2}\,\mathbfcal{R}\times\left(c_{t}\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbfcal{F}-c_{l}\bm{\nabla}G\right)=-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial{\bf S}}{\partial t}\,.

(15)

The corresponding time-averaged monochromatic quantities are [cf. Eqs. (
8
) and (
9

\displaystyle\bar{D}=\frac{\rho}{2c_{t}}{\rm Re}({\bf V}^{*}\cdot{\bf F})\,,\quad\bar{\bf W}={\bf 0}\,.

The false-chirality density

is entirely similar to the corresponding electromagnetic quantities: the magnetoelectric energy density

It vanishes for a plane wave but is generally nonzero in spatially inhomogeneous fields.

Examples.—

We now present simple examples of non-plane-wave acoustic fields possessing local chirality, spin, and false chirality, which clearly illustrate the distinctions between these quantities.
The simplest field that cannot be described by a single plane wave is a standing wave formed by the interference of two counter-propagating plane waves with the same frequency
\omega
and amplitude
A
. Such fields can naturally arise as eigenmodes of finite solid bodies.

Consider the interference of transverse waves propagating along the
\pm z
directions with circular polarizations. The corresponding complex wave fields
{\bf V}_{t}
and
{\bf F}
are:

\displaystyle{\bf V}_{t}

\displaystyle=A\left(\hat{\bf e}^{\sigma_{1}}e^{ikz}+\hat{\bf e}^{\sigma_{2}}e^{-ikz}\right),

\displaystyle{\bf F}

\displaystyle=A\left(i\sigma_{1}\hat{\bf e}^{\sigma_{1}}e^{ikz}+i\sigma_{2}\hat{\bf e}^{\sigma_{2}}e^{-ikz}\right).

(17)

Here,
k=\omega/c_{t}
and
\hat{\bf e}^{\sigma_{1,2}}=(\hat{\bf x}\pm i\sigma_{1,2}\hat{\bf y})/\sqrt{2}
are the circular-polarization unit vectors of the two waves with helicities
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
.

\displaystyle\bar{E}

\displaystyle=\rho A^{2}\,,~~\bar{\bf P}={\bf 0}\,,~~\bar{\bf S}=\frac{\rho A^{2}}{2\omega}(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})[1+\cos(2kz)]\hat{\bf z}\,,

\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}

Let us consider another example, which highlights the
mixed
chirality contribution. We examine the interference between an

-propagating longitudinal plane wave and a

-propagating,

-polarized transverse plane wave of the same frequency
\omega
.
The corresponding wave fields in our formalism are:

\displaystyle{\bf V}

\displaystyle=A\hat{\bf x}e^{ik_{l}x}+B\hat{\bf z}e^{ik_{t}y}={\bf V}_{l}+{\bf V}_{t}\,,

\displaystyle{\bf F}

\displaystyle=-B\hat{\bf x}e^{ik_{t}y}\,,\quad G=-Ae^{ik_{l}x}\,.

(19)

Here,
A
and
B
are the amplitudes of the two waves, whereas
k_{l}=\omega/c_{l}
and
k_{t}=\omega/c_{t}
are their wavenumbers.
The time-averaged energy, momentum, spin, chirality, and false-chirality densities of the field (
Acoustic Chirality
) read:

\displaystyle\bar{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{2}\!\left(A^{2}+B^{2}\right),~~\bar{\bf P}=\frac{\rho}{2\omega}\!\left(A^{2}k_{l}\,\hat{\bf x}+B^{2}k_{t}\,\hat{\bf y}\right),

\displaystyle\bar{\bf S}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho AB}{\omega}\sin(k_l x - k_t y)\,\hat{\bf y},~~\bar{C}=\frac{\rho ABk_{t}}{4}\sin(k_l x - k_t y),

\displaystyle\bar{D}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rho AB}{2c_{t}}\cos(k_l x - k_t y)\,.

(20)

Here, the chirality density has a purely mixed origin,
\bar{C}=\bar{C}_{m}
, arising from the interplay between the transverse and longitudinal wave components. The regions of positive and negative chirality alternate throughout the
(x,y)
plane, so that the spatially-averaged chirality vanishes,
\langle\bar{C}\rangle=0
, in agreement with Eq. (

).
Nonetheless, the mixed chirality density can manifest itself in local interactions, e.g., with electron or optical fields.
Note also that the regions of maximal chirality,
k_{l}x-k_{t}y=\pi/2+n\pi
,

, coincide with the regions of maximal spin density, while the false chirality reaches its extrema in the chirality- and spin-free regions,
k_{l}x-k_{t}y=n\pi
.

Conclusion.—

To summarize, we have uncovered a new conservation law and a continuous symmetry of the linear isotropic elasticity equations, which describe the chirality of elastic waves. Several aspects of acoustic chirality closely parallel the well-established concepts of electromagnetic chirality and the dual (electric-magnetic) symmetry of Maxwell’s equations.
In particular, acoustic chirality involves not only the usual displacement (or velocity) field, but also a second field proportional to the curl of the displacement, which serves as an acoustic analogue of the magnetic field.
An important distinction from electromagnetism is the presence of longitudinal wave modes. Although the integral acoustic chirality is determined solely by the population imbalance between right- and left-handed transverse phonons, the local chirality density has a mixed contribution involving longitudinal acoustic fields. In addition, we have introduced and analyzed the related concepts of acoustic helicity and “false chirality”.

We have illustrated the general theory with simple examples of acoustic interference fields exhibiting chirality, spin angular momentum, and false chirality. These examples clearly demonstrate the fundamental differences between these quantities and may be relevant to observable acoustic phenomena.
