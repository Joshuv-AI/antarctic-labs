Evaluation of the effect of obesity on oral health and halitosis in children
Authors: Fatma Dilara Baysan, Meryem Uzamiş Tekçi̇çek, Zehra Buyuktuncer, Sevilay Karahan, Melek Dilek Turgut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveObesity has become one of the leading health concerns in the world. There has been limited data on the effect of obesity on oral health in children and no research has specifically examined the impact of obesity on halitosis in this population. The aim of this study was to evaluate the potential effects of obesity on oral health parameters including dental caries, periodontal health, and halitosis in children.Materials and methodsA total of 295 children aged 10–13 years were included in the study. Height, body weight, and waist circumference were measured, and body mass index (BMI), z-scores, and percentile values were calculated using the WHO AnthroPlus 2007 program. Participants were classified into obese and non-obese groups. The obese group consisted of overweight/obese children, whereas the non-obese group comprised underweight/normal weight children. Oral health status, including the presence of halitosis, was assessed. In addition, a questionnaire was administered to the parents. The collected data were statistically analyzed using the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, Mann–Whitney U test, and multivariable logistic regression analysis (p < 0.05).ResultsThe obese group consisted of 111 children (37.6%), whereas the non-obese group comprised 184 children (62.4%). No statistically significant differences were observed between the obese and non-obese groups regarding DMFT/DMFS, dmft/dmfs, ICDAS II, plaque index, or gingival index scores. However, a statistically significant difference was found in the prevalence of halitosis between the groups. The frequency of halitosis was significantly higher in the obese group (24.3%) than in the non-obese group (14.7%) (p = 0.038). Furthermore, the association between tongue coating and halitosis was statistically significant in the non-obese group (p = 0.049), but not in the obese group (p = 0.790). Multivariable logistic regression analysis also identified overweight and obesity as potential risk factors for halitosis (OR = 1.847, 95% CI: 1.006–3.389).ConclusionsIn addition to the well-known causes of halitosis, overweight and obesity may also be considered potential risk factors in children. Parents should be informed that overweight and obesity may be associated with bad breath, which can lead to embarrassment and anxiety in children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08893-4.

Introduction

Obesity has been considered as one of the most important health problems affecting not only adults but also children and adolescents. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, the prevalence of childhood obesity doubled from 1980 to 2015 in both developed and developing countries [1]. The growing problem has led to an increase in studies investigating the effects of obesity on both general and oral health in all over the world. It is known that obesity, which has a multifactorial and complex etiology, has negative effects on many systems such as endocrine, cardiovascular, respiratory, digestive and immune systems [2]. Regarding oral health, obesity has also been considered as one of the important risk factors for dental caries and periodontal health [3].

In the literature, there have been studies reporting a positive relationship between obesity and periodontal disease in both adults and children [4]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2017, it was reported that childhood obesity is associated with bleeding on probing, visible plaque index, subgingival calculus, probing depth and decreased salivary flow [5]. Although the exact reason for the decrease in salivary flow rate is not known, it is thought that proinflammatory cytokines released from adipose tissue cause a low level of chronic inflammation in the salivary glands [6]. In addition to the decreased flow, changes in the composition may also trigger periodontal disease. Increase in salivary bacterial population, sialic acid, protein concentration and TNF-α levels along with decrease in phosphorus and peroxidase activity were reported in obese individuals [7]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2019, it was reported that the increased salivary TNF-α level may be a helpful criterion for the diagnosis of obesity [7]. Moreover, it was also found that there was an increase in salivary immunoglobulin A (IgA) levels in obese children [6]. These alterations in periodontal status and salivary composition may also affect the oral microbial environment and contribute to the production of volatile sulfur compounds, thereby increasing the risk of halitosis [8].

Halitosis, also known as bad breath, is defined as an unpleasant breath odor originating from the mouth or outside the mouth. Causes of bad breath may include dental problems, dry mouth, infections, medications, or systemic diseases [9]. Halitosis is mainly classified as true or pseudo halitosis. While true halitosis is categorized as physiological (temporary) and pathological, pathological halitosis can be oral or extraoral, depending on the source of the odor. Physiological halitosis can occur due to temporary conditions such as dry mouth, hunger, smoking, certain foods and stress. Pseudo halitosis refers to the perception of bad breath without clinically detectable odor [10].

In the evaluation of halitosis, organoleptic evaluation, gas chromatography and sulfur monitoring are used. Organoleptic evaluation is a subjective method in which the physician scores the bad breath perceived on the patient [11]. In the sulfur monitoring method, measurement is made with a halimeter device that detects the amount of volatile sulfur compounds (VSC) in ppb (parts per billion) units. The method is easy to use, inexpensive, rapid, portable and reproducible. However, the compounds other than VSC cannot be detected with the device [12].

Regarding the association between halitosis and oral health in children; studies evaluated the effect of tongue coating, plaque index, dental caries and mouth breathing [8, 13, 14]. On the effect of obesity on oral health in children, caries and/or periodontal health were investigated [15]. The association between obesity and halitosis, however, has been carried out in limited studies concerning adults [16, 17]. To the best of our knowledge there has been no study on the effect of obesity on halitosis in children. Therefore, the aim of the present study is to investigate the effect of obesity on oral health and halitosis in children. The null hypothesis was formed as ‘There is no difference in terms of halitosis between obese children and non-obese children’.

Materials and methods

In the present study, considering that there will be at least 20% difference in the prevalence of halitosis between obese and normal weight children, the minimum patient number required was calculated as 282, at 80% strength and 5% type I error levels. According to the studies investigating the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children in Turkey, the rate of overweight ranged from 9.2% to 22.4%, while the prevalence of obesity ranged from 1.6% to 10.3% [18–20]. According to these studies, the combined prevalence of overweight and obesity was determined as 32.7%. Therefore, in the present study, it was decided that at least 32.7% of the planned sample size would consist of overweight and obese individuals, with the remaining participants being of normal weight. The study protocol was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Hacettepe University (Decision No: GO 18/477 − 17). The study was conducted in the Department of Pediatric Dentistry at Hacettepe University, Faculty of Dentistry and lasted for 16 months. A total of 295 children aged 10–13 years referred to the Department of Pediatric Dentistry were included in the study. This age range was selected to correspond to the early adolescence period. Adolescence is characterized by rapid growth and development, increased caloric needs, and lifestyle changes such as reduced physical activity and unhealthy dietary habits, alongside social, environmental, and psychological influences that may contribute to obesity. Therefore, the lower age limit was set at 10 years, corresponding to the onset of adolescence according to the World Health Organization, and the upper age limit was set at 13 years, based on the upper age of patients attending this department.

Written informed consents were obtained allowing data collection and the clinical procedure, after explaining the study protocol to the parent and child in detail. Inclusion criteria for the study were defined as being healthy and 10–13 years of age who were willing to participate. Before starting the study, a pilot study was conducted to determine the exclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria for the patients included: (a) systemic diseases, upper respiratory tract (tonsillitis, sinusitis), or gastrointestinal disease; (b) regular medication usage; (c) using antibiotic for the last month; (d) mouth breathing; (e) current orthodontic treatment; (f) dental caries cavities, prominent tongue coating, acute gingivitis and periodontitis or oral lesions. These exclusion criteria were applied to minimize potential confounding factors that could influence halitosis and to improve the internal validity of the study.

Data collection

A questionnaire consisted of two parts was designed to collect information about the parents and their children. The questionnaire was administered in person to the parents of children participating in the study at the dental clinic. The first part focused on information of the parents, including their age, educational status, occupation, income, smoking habits, body weight and height, presence of any chronic diseases, oral hygiene habits and oral health status. In the second part, the information of the children, including their age, number of siblings, birth weight, oral hygiene and oral health, eating habits, mouth breathing and sleep apnea, were recorded. The questionnaire was developed for this study and presented as an attachment. The questionnaire was used as a descriptive data collection tool and did not aim to generate a score. Therefore, formal validation analyses were not performed. It was developed based on an extensive review of the literature and evaluated by a multidisciplinary team consisting of a pediatric dentist, a dietitian, and a statistician, ensuring content relevance, clarity, and suitability of the items.

Extraoral examination

Anthropometric measurements including body weight, height and waist circumference were taken. The body weight and height of the children were measured by a stadiometer (Seca, model: 7671321009, Germany) without shoes and thick clothes. During the measurement, it was ensured that the child was in an upright position and his/her feet were parallel to each other. Body weight was recorded in kilograms and height in meters. All anthropometric measurements were performed by one calibrated pediatric dentist (F.D.B.) who conducted the intraoral examinations using standardized procedures, following prior calibration on a subset of children to ensure consistency.

The waist circumference of the children was measured using a 150 cm long tape in the middle of the distance between the lowest rib and the iliac bone prominence. Items such as loose and thick clothes and belts that prevented the measurement were removed. The child was asked to stand in an upright position, hands and arms at both sides, feet close to each other. During the measurement, the child was asked to breathe normally, not to hold his/her breath and not to squeeze himself/herself, and the tape measure was not kept loose or applied excessive pressure. Measurements were made to the nearest millimeter (with an accuracy of 0.1 cm) and the detected value was recorded in cm.

After taking the measurements, body mass index (BMI), z-score and percentile values for the children were calculated with the WHO ANTRO Plus 2007 program. Using the growth reference values published by WHO for children and adolescents in the 5–19 age group in 2007, children were divided into two groups. Intraoral examination

Oral health status of the children was evaluated using DMFT/DMFS, dmft/dmfs and ICDAS II indices, plaque index, gingival index and tongue coating by one calibrated pediatric dentist (F.D.B.). Prior to the study, examiner calibration was conducted through repeated assessments of a subset of children under standardized conditions. For the plaque index assessments, the labial (buccal), palatal (lingual), mesial, and distal surfaces of the teeth were scored using a periodontal probe according to the index developed by Silness and Löe. For the gingival index assessments, the labial (buccal), palatal (lingual), mesial, and distal surfaces of the teeth were scored using the periodontal probe according to the index developed by Löe and Silness.

The Winkel Tongue Coating Index (WTCI) was used to evaluate tongue coating. According to the index, the dorsum of the tongue was divided into six regions (three anterior, three posterior) and scored as follows: 0: No coating, 1: Mild tongue coating, 2: Severe tongue coating [21]. The total WTCI scores of the children were calculated and children with a total WTCI score of less than 6 were included in the study.

In the evaluation of halitosis, a portable “Halimeter” (Interscan Corp. Chatsworth, US & Canada) device was used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to measure the VSC in the mouth. The children were asked to refrain from eating, drinking, brushing, tongue cleaning and chewing for 2 h before the evaluation. Halimeter measurements were performed prior to periodontal examination in order to avoid any potential influence of mechanical stimulation on salivary flow and volatile sulfur compound levels. In the measurement, the children were asked to breathe only through the nose and keep their mouth closed for 3 min. This time is necessary for the formation of VSC in sufficient concentration. At the end of the 3-minute period, a disposable pipette attached to the halimeter device was advanced 2.5–5.0 cm into the child’s mouth and placed on the posterior dorsal part of the tongue. While the device was measuring, the child continued to breathe through the nose with his lips closed. The largest value obtained from the measurement is expressed numerically in ppb on the screen. The measurement was repeated three times and the average of the three measurements was recorded as the amount of VSC in the child’s breath. According to the manufacturer’s recommendations, VSC values of 150 ppb or higher were considered halitosis [12].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 (SPSS Inc. Released. SPSS for Windows, Version 23.0) package program. Number, percentage, mean standard deviation, median, minimum and maximum values were used as descriptive statistics. Chi-square, Fisher’s exact, and Mann-Whitney U tests were used to evaluate whether there was a statistically significant difference between categorical variables. Spearman correlation coefficient was used to determine whether there is a linear relationship between two numerical measurements, and if so, to assess the direction and severity of the relation. The factors associated with halitosis were identified using multivariable logistic regression analysis. Variables associated with halitosis in univariate analyses were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. In addition, plaque index and gingival index were included a priori as clinically relevant confounding variables based on previous evidence indicating a strong association between periodontal health and halitosis. Other potential confounding variables, including snack consumption and oral hygiene habits (tooth brushing frequency, flossing, and mouthwash use), were also evaluated in univariate analyses; however, no statistically significant associations were found between these variables and halitosis, and therefore they were not included in the final model. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The children were also categorized as “halitosis group” and “no halitosis group” according to the presence or absence of bad breath. No halitosis group comprised 241 (81.7%) children, while halitosis group comprised 54 children (18.3%).

With respect to gender, the obesity was more common in boys compared to girls (p = 0.018). However, there was no statistically significant difference in halitosis between boys and girls (p = 0.975).

Most of the obese children (41.1%) were 12 years old. Nevertheless, there was no statistically significant relationship between age and BMI (p = 0.801). The rate of halitosis was 15.6% in the 10 and 11 years, 21.1% in the 12 years and 19.3% in the 13 years old children. There was no statistically significant relationship between age and halitosis (p = 0.769).

In comparison of the BMI of the parents (calculated according to their self-reported data) with the BMI of their children revealed a positive relationship (p < 0.001). It was found that 46% of the obese parents had also obese children.

a. Comparison of the BMI groups in terms of chronic disease, birth characteristics, and oral hygiene habits

One hundred fourteen parents, (38.6%) reported chronic diseases in their families being 41.2% of them endocrine disorders such as diabetes and hypothyroidism. Twenty-seven children (57.4%) with a family history of endocrine disease were obese. The presence of endocrine disease in the family showed a significant difference between obese and non-obese groups (p = 0.010). Among the children with a family history of diabetes and hypothyroidism 52.5% and 85.7% were obese, respectively. The relationship was statistically significant for obesity and family history of hypothyroidism (p = 0.013).

In regard to the comparison of the obese and non-obese groups with the children’s birth characteristics, significant difference was found for the birth weight (p < 0.001). No significant difference was found between obese and non-obese groups for the time of birth or mode of delivery (p = 0.383, p = 1.000).

The most common site for tongue coating was the middle posterior region (P2 area), and the least common site was the anterior right region (A1 area). In 204 children (69.2%) mild tongue coating (score 1) was observed in the P2 area and in 7 children (2.4%) in the A1 area.

The distribution of the children for total Winkel index scores of 0,1,2,3,4 and 5 were 84 (28.5%) 136 (46.1%), 31 (10.5%), 21 (7.1%), 22 (7.5%), and 1 (0.3%), respectively.

The halitosis group had higher tongue coating than no halitosis group (p = 0.004). No statistically significant relationship was found in obese and non-obese groups with respect to the total Winkel index score or the presence of tongue coating (p = 0.439 and p = 0.872, respectively).

f. Comparison of waist circumference with DMFT/DMFS, dmft/dmfs, ICDAS II, plaque index, and gingival index

No significant associations were found between waist circumference and DMFT/DMFS (p = 0.812 and p = 0.562, respectively) or plaque index (p = 0.206). In contrast, significant inverse associations were observed with dmft/dmfs (p < 0.001) and gingival index (p = 0.044), while ICDAS II scores increased significantly with increasing waist circumference (p = 0.035).

g. Comparison of the BMI groups for eating habits with DMFT/DMFS, dmft/dmfs, ICDAS II, plaque index, and gingival index

Thirty-nine (32.8%) children who skipped meals and 72 (40.9%) children who did not skip meals were obese. However, there was no statistically significant difference between the obese and non-obese groups in terms of meal skipping (p = 0.157). In addition, there was no difference between the obese and non-obese groups in terms of the frequency of snack consumption (p = 0.599).

The most common consumed snacks were biscuits, cakes, chocolate, wafers and crackers. It was found that boys consumed more chocolate, wafers, chips, hamburgers, and acidic-sugary drinks than girls (p = 0.019, p = 0.009, p = 0.013, p = 0.002, respectively). 43.9% of the children who consumed crackers were obese. The cracker consumption during snacks was found to be statistically higher in the obese group (p = 0.044).

h. Parent’s perception on children’s obesity and halitosis

In the study, only 6 parents (2%) reported that their children were diagnosed with obesity. The parents’ perceptions on their children’s physical appearance and halitosis were asked. The results revealed a significant relationship (p < 0.001). Almost all of the parents who reported that their children were overweight or obese, had overweight or obese children. The positive relationship between parents’ perception on halitosis and the presence of halitosis in the child was also statistically significant (p = 0.010).

Discussion

The fact of being a common health problem has led to an increase in studies involving the effects of obesity on both general and oral health in children. In these studies, the overweight and obesity rate were reported as 9.6%- 30.9% and 3.7% − 18.3%, respectively [19–21]. In the present study, 3.1% of the patients were underweight, 59.3% were normal, 22% were overweight, 13.9% were obese and 1.7% were morbidly obese, in accordance with the literature.

It has been reported that overweight and obesity are important risk factors effecting the oral health in children [3]. In the studies, the effect on dental caries, periodontal health and saliva were evaluated [3, 22]. Regarding the effect on halitosis, there has been a limited number of studies involved adults and adolescents [16, 17, 23]. To the best of our knowledge, no study has been concerned on children. In the aforementioned studies, contradictory results on the effect of obesityhave been reported which may have been related to the different age groups and the methodology used [16, 17, 23].

In the present study, significant results were found regarding obesity-related risk factors. Overweight and obesity were observed more frequently in boys than in girls, in accordance with the literature [24]. It would be reasonable to attribute the result to their higher carbohydrate-containing and high-calorie foods and drinks (chocolate, wafers, chips, hamburgers, acidic-sugary drinks) consumption during snacks.

In a study involved primary school students in Turkey, it was reported that the BMI’s of the parents of overweight-obese students were statistically significantly higher than the parents of the normal weight children [25]. Our results also confirmed this finding supporting the view that obesity in the family increases the likelihood of obesity in the child [26].

Regarding birth characteristics, it has been suggested that high birth weight increases the risk of obesity in children. In parallel, overweight-obesity was more common in children with high birth weight, in the present study. Although the exact mechanism is unknown, the reason may be related to the mother being diabetic or obese in the prenatal period or the weight she gained during pregnancy [27].

In terms of systemic diseases in the family, hypothyroidism was found to be related to the overweight and obesity of the children. This result showed that endocrine diseases in the family should be regarded as a risk factor for the development of overweight and obesity in the children.

The relationship between dental caries and obesity is contradictory [28]. In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, it has been reported that obese children have more dental caries than normal weight children [29]. It has also been suggested that obesity and dental caries is associated with common risk factors such as high sugar diet, low socioeconomic level, and some social and environmental factors [28].

In a study investigating the relationship between caries and obesity in 280 children aged 7–12 in China, ICDAS-II and DMFT/dmft indices were used for caries detection. No correlation was found between obesity and dental caries in the logistic regression model, and it was concluded that obesity did not directly lead to dental caries [30]. In the present study, no statistically significant relationship was found between DMFT, DMFS, dmft, dmfs, ICDAS II indices and the groups. Nevertheless, this result may not reflect the direct relationship between caries and obesity, since patients with caries cavitation were not included in the study due to the potential risk of halitosis. As a result, the findings may not fully represent the relationship between caries experience and obesity in the general pediatric population.

Obesity is also considered a risk factor for periodontal disease [5, 6]. Modeer et al., reported higher plaque index values in obese adolescents and children [22]. Similar conclusion cannot be made in our study as patients with gingivitis and periodontitis were not included. Plaque and gingival index were evaluated to assess gingival health, and no statistically significant difference was observed between the groups, as expected.

In addition to dental caries and periodontal health, oral hygiene habits have also been investigated in obese children. In a study conducted on 180 children between the ages of 6–14, it has been found that the frequency of tooth brushing is higher in non-obese children [31]. In the present study, the relationship between tooth brushing frequency and overweight/obesity was not statistically significant.

Tooth brushing is a daily personal care procedure that is recommended for everyone to prevent dental caries as well as to prevent bad breath. In a study conducted on children with an average age of 12, the prevalence of halitosis was 37.6% [32]. In a study conducted in Turkey, which included 628 healthy children between the ages of 7 and 11, the incidence of halitosis was reported as 14.5% [33]. In the present study, the prevalence of halitosis was found to be 18.3%. The rate may be regarded as lower than expected since the factors that may cause halitosis were excluded in order to evaluate the absolute effect of obesity on halitosis.

Regarding the relationship between halitosis and gender, there have been contradictory results. The prevalence of halitosis was found to be higher in male Korean adolescents [17]. Motta et al. found no significant difference in the 3–14 age group, and Patil et al. in the 7–15 age group [34, 35]. Similar to these studies, no significant difference was found between both genders in terms of halitosis, in the present study. The significant difference for halitosis was found between the BMI groups indicating that children in the obese group had more bad breath than non-obese children. This result may be attributed to the effects of obesity on saliva. Fadel et al. found that there was an increase in immunoglobulin a (IgA) levels in saliva and a decrease in salivary flow rate in obese children [6]. Although the etiology of the decrease in salivary flow rate is not known exactly, it is predicted that pro-inflammatory cytokines released from adipose tissue cause low-level chronic inflammation in the salivary glands [22]. In another study, decrease in the amount of phosphate and peroxidase activity in saliva, and increase in sialic acid and protein concentrations were found in the obese children. The decrease in the amount of phosphate and peroxidase activity in the saliva, and the high sialic acid and protein concentration, facilitate the adhesion of plaque bacteria to the tooth, causing dental caries and periodontal disease [22, 36]. Bacterial species such as Proteobacteria phylum, Campylobacter rectus and Neisseria mucosa were found to be more abundant in saliva of obese adolescents. In the same study, the number of subgingival bacterial cells was found to be associated with obesity [37]. Additionally, obesity poses a risk for inflammatory diseases. CRP is a protein specific to systemic inflammation. CRP concentration in serum and saliva is higher in obese children than in children of normal weight [38].

In our preliminary study, it was observed that almost none of the pediatric patients who applied to our faculty had the habit of tongue brushing and the number of patients without tongue coating was extremely low. For this reason, it was decided to include those with no or mild tongue coating (scores 0 and 1 according to WTCI). WTCI is an index that uses subjective and visual criteria. In a study in which tongue coating was determined by the autofluorescence method, it was reported that score 1 in WTCI does not always indicate tongue coating, and such a mistake may occur due to hyperkeratosis of the filiform papillae on the tongue [39]. Lundgren et al. examined the reproducibility of WTCI. They reported that reproducibility was better by not using score 1 and using only scores of 0 (no coating) and 2 (presence of severe coating) [21].

In the present study, halitosis was more common in obese children than in non-obese children. A significant association was observed between the total WTCI score and halitosis. Tongue coating was considered a potential confounding factor in the evaluation of the relationship between halitosis and BMI groups. However, no significant association was found between the total WTCI score and BMI groups. While the presence of tongue coating was significantly associated with halitosis in the non-obese group, this association was not observed in the obese group. After adjusting for potential confounding factors (tongue coating, dmfs, plaque index, and gingival index) in the multivariable logistic regression model, BMI remained significantly associated with halitosis. These findings suggest that overweight and obesity may be associated with halitosis beyond the effect of oral hygiene–related factors, including tongue coating.

Sleep apnea caused by obesity can cause dry mouth and eventually a risk for halitosis [40]. In the present study, only 1 (0.3%) parent reported that his child was diagnosed with sleep apnea 4 years ago but had not receive treatment. Therefore, in our study, statistical analysis could not be performed for the relationship between the presence of sleep apnea and halitosis and BMI.

It has been indicated that abdominal fat is one of the main reasons for the increased risk of death. Waist circumference is a specific measurement of upper body fat and has been used to identify overweight and obese children who are at risk of developing metabolic complications [41]. There have been few studies including waist circumference measurement in children [18, 42]. In the present study, in addition to height and weight assessment, waist circumference was also measured. Hatipoğlu et al., developed age and gender specific reference values for waist circumference in Turkish children between the ages of 7–17. They reported that waist circumference measurement increased with age and the waist circumference percentage values of boys at all ages were higher than girls [43]. Parallel results were also obtained in the present study. In a study investigating the relationship between anthropometric measurements and dental caries in children aged 5–9 Turkey; an inverse relationship between waist circumference and DMFT was reported [18]. In another study in the 6–17 age group, BMI and waist circumference values were found to be significantly higher in children with a DMFT score of 0 than in children with a DMFT score > 1 [42]. In the present study, although the DMFT/DMFS index increased as waist circumference increased, the relationship was not significant. However, the inverse relationship between waist circumference and dmft/dmfs index was statistically significant. Additionally, as the waist circumference of the patients increased, the ICDAS II index also increased. However, these results were considered as weak correlation due to the low correlation coefficient. In addition, as there was no significant relationship between BMI and DMFT/DMFS, dmft/dmfs and ICDAS II indices, it would be reasonable to ignore some significant relationships around waist circumference, due to weak correlation.

In the present study, the null hypothesis was rejected, and the findings suggest that overweight and obesity may be associated with halitosis in children, independent of tongue coating and overall oral health status. The strict exclusion criteria applied, including systemic diseases, recent antibiotic use, upper respiratory tract and gastrointestinal conditions, orthodontic treatment, and various oral pathologies, were necessary to minimize potential confounding factors. However, these criteria may have introduced selection bias by resulting in a relatively healthy study population, which could limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader pediatric population.

One of the limitations of this study is that systemic conditions were assessed based on parental self-report without laboratory confirmation. Therefore, undiagnosed metabolic abnormalities, such as impaired glucose metabolism or elevated HbA1c levels, which may be associated with obesity and potentially influence halitosis, cannot be excluded. This may have introduced a degree of bias in the assessment of systemic conditions. Another limitation is the inability to determine the causative mechanisms underlying the association between obesity and halitosis, which may be related to changes in salivary composition. Considering that previous studies suggest obesity may reduce salivary flow rate, salivary flow rate was not measured in the present study and considered as another limitation. Therefore, further studies investigating these potential mechanisms and including more diverse populations are warranted.

Conclusion

Within the limits of the present study, no statistically significant associations were observed between overweight and obesity, and oral health parameters, including DMFT/DMFS, dmft/dmfs, ICDAS II, plaque index, and gingival index, possibly due to the exclusion of children with carious lesions and periodontal problems. Nevertheless, multivariable logistic regression analysis indicated that overweight and obesity were independently associated with halitosis, suggesting that these conditions should be considered additional risk factors for halitosis in children. In cases where the etiology of halitosis is unclear, pediatric dentists may take overweight and obesity into account as potential contributing factors.
