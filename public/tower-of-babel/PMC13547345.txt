A two-stage deep-learning model using MobileNetV2 and U-Net for CT-based muscle volume assessment in total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Tetsuya Tachibana, Hiroki Katagiri, Toru Esaki, Tomoaki Kawashima, Takahisa Mizuno, Takahisa Ogawa, Ryusuke Saito, Tetsuya Jinno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preoperative muscle strength is an important predictor of functional recovery after total hip arthroplasty (THA); however, accurate assessment is difficult in patients with severe hip pain. In this study, we developed a two-stage deep-learning framework that combines slice-level classification and U-shaped convolutional neural network–based segmentation to automatically quantify periarticular and thigh muscle volumes from preoperative computed tomography (CT) images. The model was trained using CT scans from 107 patients undergoing primary THA and was validated against manual segmentation. The model was subsequently applied to an independent cohort of 58 patients to examine the associations between preoperative muscle volume indices and postoperative clinical outcomes. Automated segmentation demonstrated high agreement with manual measurements, with absolute volume errors of 1–10% and Dice coefficients exceeding 0.89 across all muscle groups. In the clinical cohort, the preoperative muscle volumes were significantly lower on the affected side than on the unaffected side. Greater preoperative volumes of the gluteus maximus, quadriceps, and hamstrings were significantly associated with postoperative hip abductor strength and Timed Up and Go test performance, whereas gluteus medius/minimus volumes showed weaker associations. These findings support the feasibility of automated CT-based muscle volumetry for objective functional assessment in THA.

Introduction

In total hip arthroplasty (THA), the preoperative muscle strength of the periarticular hip musculature, particularly the gluteus medius and quadriceps, is an important predictor of postoperative functional recovery1. However, in patients with severe hip pain, the accurate assessment of muscle strength is often difficult because of pain-related inhibition. In this context, the objective evaluation of preoperative muscle mass has gained increasing attention as an alternative approach for assessing postoperative functional potential2. Muscle mass has been traditionally evaluated using cross-sectional area measurements on selected slices from computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or indirectly estimated by analyzing the body composition of major regions by using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry3,4. However, these methods provide only indirect estimates of muscle volume. Accurate volumetric assessment requires the manual segmentation of muscle tissue across all CT or MRI slices, which is time consuming and labor intensive, thus limiting its feasibility in routine clinical practice.

Recent advances in automated muscle segmentation using CT or MRI have demonstrated the potential of deep learning–based approaches for quantitative muscle analysis5,6. Nevertheless, MRI-based methods are often constrained by limited imaging coverage, which makes comprehensive muscle evaluation challenging. By contrast, CT-based segmentation remains technically demanding because of the low contrast between adjacent muscle groups. Despite these challenges, CT is routinely performed for preoperative planning in patients undergoing THA. Overcoming these technical limitations could provide clinically valuable information without an additional imaging burden. Therefore, we applied a convolutional neural network (CNN)–based approach to achieve automated muscle segmentation in CT images. In particular, the U-shaped CNN (U-Net) architecture proposed by Ronneberger et al. has been widely adopted for medical image segmentation and has demonstrated high performance in various applications, including automated meniscus segmentation on knee MRI7,8.

In this study, we used preoperative hip CT images from patients undergoing THA to automatically segment the gluteus medius/minimus, gluteus maximus, quadriceps, and hamstrings and to quantitatively measure muscle volumes. Furthermore, we examined the relationships between muscle volume measurements, patient-related factors, and functional assessment parameters such as hip abductor strength and gait performance. We aimed (1) to evaluate the accuracy of a CNN-based model for the automated segmentation and volumetric measurement of periarticular hip and thigh muscles by using preoperative CT images in patients undergoing THA and (2) to investigate the associations between preoperative muscle volume measurements, patient characteristics, and functional performance indicators.

Methods

Study design and patient cohorts

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Dokkyo Medical University Saitama Medical Center, Saitama, Japan (approval no. 24016). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Because the study was retrospective in nature and the data were anonymized, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB of Dokkyo Medical University Saitama Medical Center. This study consisted of two distinct phases: (1) development and validation of an automated deep-learning segmentation model for hip muscles and (2) clinical application of the model to evaluate the relationship between muscle volume around the hip and functional outcomes (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1Overview of the study design and workflow.

Overview of the study design and workflow.

For Phase 1 (development and validation of an automated segmentation model for hip muscles), a total of 117 consecutive patients who underwent primary THA at Dokkyo Medical University Saitama Medica Center between March 2023 and October 2024 were included in this study to construct and validate the deep learning model for the automated segmentation for hip muscles. Patients without preoperative CT scans or those with prior contralateral THA were excluded from the study. Four patients were excluded because of insufficient CT data, and six were excluded because of previous contralateral THA, thus resulting in a final cohort of 107 patients. Among these 107 patients, 99 were used for training, and 8 were allocated as an independent test set for the segmentation model evaluation.

For Phase 2 (after model development), the final trained segmentation model was applied to an independent cohort of 105 consecutive patients who underwent primary unilateral THA between January 2022 and March 2023. Patients without appropriate preoperative CT scans, those with a history of hip surgery, or those who did not undergo a 3-month postoperative rehabilitation assessment were excluded. In addition, patients with bilateral symptomatic hip osteoarthritis were excluded to allow valid comparisons between the affected and unaffected sides. As a result, 2 patients were excluded because of insufficient CT data, 3 because of previous pelvic osteotomy, 30 because of the lack of postoperative rehabilitation assessment, and 12 because of bilateral symptomatic hip osteoarthritis, thus yielding a final cohort of 58 patients.

Input dataset and ground-truth mask generation

In all patients, whole-leg CT was performed in the supine position within 3 months before surgery and covered the region from the iliac wing to the distal ankle. The scanning parameters were 120 kV, 200 mAs, 512 × 512 matrix, and 1.0-mm slice thickness. Each case contained approximately 900–1100 axial slices (total: ≈100,000 slices). Ground-truth masks were manually generated by two board-certified orthopedic surgeons (TT and MF) by using Synapse software (version 4.6, FUJIFILM Corporation, Tokyo, Japan; https://www.fujifilm.com/) in Phase 1. TT (> 200 THA cases) delineated the gluteus maximus and gluteus medius/minimus, and MF (> 100 total knee arthroplasty cases) delineated the quadriceps and hamstrings, based on their respective surgical expertise. All annotations were subsequently reviewed and confirmed by TT. For each slice, all muscle groups were delineated separately on both sides. The original CT images and their corresponding binary masks were used as paired inputs for training. All CT images were resized from 512 pixels × 512 pixels to 224 pixels × 224 pixels to match the input requirements of the MobileNetV2 encoder, which is a lightweight CNN architecture designed to reduce computational cost and memory usage while maintaining high feature extraction performance9. Among the 107 patients included in the study, data from 99 patients (approximately 99,000 axial slices) were used for training, and data from the remaining 8 patients (8,000 axial slices) were reserved as an independent test set for the final model evaluation.

Pre-processing and two-stage training strategy of deep learning

Given that slices containing target muscles were far fewer than background-only slices, a two-stage pipeline was used for the deep learning in this study.

Classification stage

A slice-level classifier was trained to detect whether each CT slice contained any muscle region of interest. The model used an ImageNet-pretrained MobileNetV2 encoder, followed by a GlobalAveragePooling2D layer, a Dropout layer (rate = 0.5), and a sigmoid-activated dense layer for binary prediction. The classifier was trained using binary cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 1 × 10 −4) Only slices predicted as positive were forwarded to the segmentation stage, thus effectively removing the majority of background slices.

Segmentation stage

To further reduce class imbalance, only 1/100 of the background slices were randomly retained, whereas all slices containing muscle labels were preserved. Data augmentation consisted of ± 20° rotation, and horizontal or vertical flipping was excluded to maintain anatomical laterality and clinical validity.

The segmentation network adopted a U-Net architecture using MobileNetV2 as the encoder (Fig. 2)9. MobileNetV2 comprises of inverted residual bottleneck blocks consisting of (1) a 1 × 1 expansion convolution, (2) a 3 × 3 depthwise convolution, and (3) a 1 × 1 projection convolution. The encoder reduced the spatial resolution by five times. The skip connections extracted from the expansion-ReLU layers were concatenated with the corresponding decoder features. The decoder used UpSampling2D layers, followed by convolution and batch-normalization layers, to progressively restore the spatial resolution. The final output layer was a 1 × 1 convolution without activation, producing two-channel logits for subsequent softmax computations. Slices that were identified as positive during the classification stage were used to train the segmentation model. The loss function was a combined Dice + Categorical Cross-Entropy loss10. Both the classification and segmentation models were trained with the Adam optimizer by using a batch size of 2 and 50 epochs. All training was performed using Python (version 3.6; https://www.python.org/), using TensorFlow (version 1.14; https://www.tensorflow.org/) with Keras (version 2.3.1; https://keras.io/) on an RTX 4060 Ti (16 GB) graphics processing unit (NVIDIA Corporation, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Muscle volume calculation and model evaluation

Following segmentation, the predicted muscle masks were converted into muscle volumes by summing the number of voxels belonging to each muscle and multiplying them by the voxel volume (pixel width × pixel height × slice thickness). The volumes of the gluteus maximus, gluteus medius/minimus, quadriceps, and hamstrings were calculated separately. Model performance was evaluated using an independent test set of 8 patients (approximately 8000 slices). For each patient, the segmentation model was applied to all axial slices, and the predicted volumes were compared with the manually measured volumes. The accuracy assessment included (1) the Dice coefficient calculated from segmentation outputs across all slices containing the target region and (2) the absolute error in muscle volume between automated predictions and manual measurements.

Clinical application of the trained model and comparison with clinical parameters

The final trained segmentation model was applied to an independent cohort of 58 patients in Phase 2. For each patient, preoperative whole-leg CT scans were processed using the trained model to automatically extract the gluteus maximus, gluteus medius/minimus, quadriceps, and hamstring muscles. The volume of each muscle was calculated automatically. To adjust for the effects of body size, muscle volumes were normalized by height squared and expressed as muscle volume indices11. Clinical parameters were obtained from electronic medical records. The patient-related factors included age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). All patients underwent THA via a minimally invasive anterolateral approach (modified Watson–Jones approach in the supine position) using cementless cup and cementless stem implants. A standardized clinical pathway was implemented in all patients, including unrestricted weight-bearing, gait exercise, stair climbing, ADL training, and standardized postoperative pain management. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the modified Harris Hip Score and the Forgotten Joint Score at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively. Functional assessments were performed preoperatively and 3 months postoperatively during rehabilitation. These assessments included hip abductor muscle strength measured using a handheld dynamometer, the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, and the 30-second Chair Stand Test (CS-30). Univariate correlation analyses were performed to evaluate the relationships between the preoperative muscle volume indices and postoperative clinical parameters. Functional assessments (hip abductor strength, TUG test, and CS-30) were analyzed at 3 months postoperatively, whereas patient-reported outcomes (modified Harris Hip Score and Forgotten Joint Score) were analyzed at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year. Analyses were conducted separately at each postoperative time point.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA 16 software (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous variables are presented as means ± SD, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The normality of the distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and histograms. For paired comparisons, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was used for unpaired comparisons. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test. Correlations between variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

BMI was positively correlated with muscle volume indices, particularly for the gluteus medius/minimus and gluteus maximus on the unaffected side and for the hamstrings on both the affected and unaffected sides. Hip abductor strength demonstrated consistent positive correlations with the muscle volume indices. Postoperative hip abductor strength demonstrated weaker correlations with the gluteus medius/minimus but showed stronger positive correlations with the gluteus maximus, quadriceps, and hamstrings on both the affected and unaffected sides.

Functional performance measures were significantly associated with muscle volume indices. The postoperative TUG test results demonstrated stronger moderately negative correlations with the gluteus maximus on the affected side, quadriceps on both sides, and hamstrings on the unaffected side; therefore, patients with greater muscle volume indices in these muscle groups had better postoperative functional performance. The correlations between CS-30 and muscle volume indices were not significant. Furthermore, no significant correlations were observed between the muscle volume indices and clinical outcome scores, including the modified Harris Hip Score and the Forgotten Joint Score, at any postoperative time point.

Discussion

This study yielded two main findings. First, we developed a highly accurate model for automated muscle segmentation and volume estimation by using a two-stage deep-learning framework that integrates classification and segmentation. The model demonstrated high spatial agreement with manual segmentation and low volume estimation errors across multiple periarticular hip muscle groups, thereby supporting its robustness and clinical applicability. Second, in patients undergoing THA, postoperative walking ability and hip abductor strength were more strongly associated with the preoperative volumes of the gluteus maximus, quadriceps, and hamstrings than with those of the gluteus medius and minimus, as shown by the automatically derived muscle volume indices. These findings suggest that in addition to the traditionally emphasized hip abductors, the thigh musculature may play an important role in early postoperative gait recovery after THA. This study demonstrates that deep learning–based muscle volume analysis using routine CT images is a clinically useful tool for the objective and large-scale assessment of periarticular and thigh muscles and may contribute to individualized risk stratification and rehabilitation planning based on postoperative functional outcomes.

Studies on automated muscle segmentation have often been limited to small datasets12 or restricted to selected image slices13. By contrast, the present study utilized a large dataset comprising whole-leg CT scans from approximately 100 patients, thereby enhancing model robustness. The Dice coefficient achieved by our inference model (> 0.89) was comparable to or higher than those reported in previous studies12,14. Furthermore, the two-stage deep-learning framework enabled segmentation to be performed only on slices containing the target muscles, thus improving computational efficiency while reducing false-positive predictions. In addition, the use of MobileNetV2 substantially reduced model complexity, thus allowing for accurate high-resolution segmentation within practical computational constraints.

Cross-sectional area measurements on CT images have been widely used to assess preoperative muscle mass in patients undergoing THA, and studies have reported associations between cross-sectional areas and postoperative functional outcomes2,4,15. However, cross-sectional areas provide only regional estimates of lean mass and lack sufficient spatial resolution for evaluating individual muscles. By contrast, CT-based volumetric analysis enables direct and muscle-specific quantification. The findings of the present study extend previous cross-sectional area–based studies by demonstrating clinically meaningful associations between postoperative hip abductor strength and walking performance and the volumes of relatively large muscle groups, including the gluteus maximus, quadriceps, and hamstrings. Furthermore, prior automated muscle segmentation studies have been limited by single-slice approaches or lack of functional outcome correlations in THA cohorts, and the present study addresses these limitations. AI-assisted CT workflows have also been shown to add clinical value in THA preoperative planning16,17, and our findings suggest that extracting muscle volume information from routine preoperative CT may offer an additional application of this concept to support postoperative rehabilitation planning.

In this study, postoperative hip abductor strength and walking performance were more strongly associated with the preoperative volumes of the gluteus maximus, quadriceps, and hamstrings than with those of the gluteus medius and minimus. Although pelvic stabilization by the gluteus medius and minimus has traditionally been emphasized in THA rehabilitation2, early postoperative gait may depend more heavily on the gluteus maximus and thigh muscles. Given that these muscles provide knee joint stability and lower-limb control18, this functional support may be particularly beneficial following THA.

Although Ham et al. reported a significant association between the preoperative gluteal muscle volume and postoperative clinical outcomes11, no significant association was observed between the preoperative muscle volume and postoperative patient satisfaction in the present study. Patient satisfaction after THA is influenced by multiple factors including pain relief, expectations, and psychological status, which may attenuate the effect of physical capacity alone19. Furthermore, although CS-30 showed significant postoperative improvement, it did not significantly correlate with any preoperative muscle volume in the present study. This finding suggests that the substantial improvements in joint stability and pain relief provided by THA itself may obscure the differences attributable to preoperative muscle volume.

This study has a few limitations. First, this study had a retrospective, single-center design, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. External validation using datasets acquired from different institutions or imaging protocols was not feasible in the current study, and the scalability of the proposed model across heterogeneous clinical settings remains to be established in future multicenter research. Second, although a relatively large dataset was used for model development and validation, the clinical cohort size was modest. Furthermore, model validation was performed using a holdout test set rather than cross validation, possibly limiting the robustness of the generalizability assessment. In addition, the correlation analyses were exploratory and univariate in nature, without correction for multiple comparisons; findings should therefore be interpreted with caution and confirmed in larger prospective studies. Third, muscle quality parameters, including CT-based attenuation values (Hounsfield units) as a surrogate for intramuscular fat infiltration, were not assessed in this study. Iwasa et al. reported that muscle volume and density capture complementary aspects of muscle health and may independently contribute to postoperative functional recovery20. However, accurate muscle density quantification requires phantom-based calibration to correct for scanner- and protocol-dependent variability in Hounsfield units, which limits its generalisability in routine clinical settings. The coefficient of variation for muscle density measured as raw Hounsfield units across different scanners has been reported to be as high as 15%, highlighting the need for standardised calibration methods21. Incorporating both volumetric and density-based assessments in future prospective studies would provide a more comprehensive characterisation of preoperative muscle status and its relationship to functional recovery after THA. Fourth, the postoperative muscle volumes were not assessed. Repeated postoperative CT examinations would increase radiation exposure and are not ethically justifiable for research purposes. More importantly, our aim in this study was to evaluate the predictive value of preoperative muscle volume for postoperative functional recovery. Finally, comparisons with alternative deep-learning architectures were not performed. Although the segmentation accuracy was high, future studies could explore whether other network architectures could further optimize performance.

In conclusion, a two-stage deep-learning framework enabled the accurate, fully automated segmentation and volumetric quantification of the periarticular hip and thigh muscles from the routine preoperative CT images of patients undergoing THA. The model demonstrated high agreement with manual measurements and proved robust for large-scale clinical applications. Clinically, greater preoperative volumes of the gluteus maximus, quadriceps, and hamstrings were more strongly associated with postoperative hip abductor strength and walking performance than those of the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus. These findings suggest that the preoperative status of the gluteus maximus and thigh musculature may be relevant to early functional recovery after THA, in addition to the traditionally emphasized hip abductors. Automated CT-based muscle volume analysis may be an objective and practical tool for preoperative functional risk stratification and individualized rehabilitation planning.
