Moving beyond anatomy: the future of rectal cancer management is biologically-informed, response-adapted, and patient-centered
Authors: Krishan R. Jethwa, J. Joshua Smith, Hanna Sanoff, Nina N. Sanford, Martijn P.W. Intven, Hyun Kim, Alexandra E. Hotca-Cho, Karyn A. Goodman, Prajnan Das, Mary Feng, Theodore S. Hong, Ane Appelt, Cihan Gani, Emmanouil Fokas, Paul B. Romesser, William A. Hall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The purpose of this correspondence is to expand upon the recent review article by Noiret et al. addressing management of locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) and proposing anatomically guided treatment strategies. While tumor location influences surgical complexity and functional outcomes, we caution against an overly anatomy centric framework that may oversimplify treatment selection and underemphasize patient preferences and biological determinants of response. Current evidence does not support the assertion that chemotherapy followed by surgery provides superior long-term functional outcomes compared with total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) with organ preservation (OP). Contemporary management of LARC includes multiple curative-intent strategies, each associated with distinct tradeoffs in oncologic control, toxicity, and quality of life. In particular, modern radiotherapy techniques and OP strategies have demonstrated favorable response rates, functional outcomes, and quality of life compared with radical surgery. We argue that future treatment paradigms should move beyond anatomy alone and prioritize biologic risk stratification, response adaptive treatment, and incorporation of multi-omic biomarkers. Ultimately, treatment decisions should integrate patient preferences, oncologic risk, functional outcomes, and evolving biologic understanding to optimize individualized care for patients with LARC.

We read with interest the review by Noiret et al., which summarizes historical practice and contemporary complexities in managing locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) [1]. They propose anatomically-guided management, including total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) with organ preservation (OP) for patients achieving clinical complete response (cCR), particularly for low-rectal tumors. For mid-rectal tumors (>1 cm from the anorectal ring), they propose chemotherapy followed by total mesorectal excision (TME) or TNT with OP. However, as noted in Figure 1, the authors suggest that chemotherapy followed by surgery yields “improved long-term functional outcomes” compared to TNT with OP, a claim not supported by evidence. We agree with the premise that LARC is a heterogeneous disease. However, we have concerns about how the proposed framework defines and applies these distinctions and does not prioritize patient goals.

While anatomic considerations clearly influence surgical complexity, local control, and functional outcomes, the anatomy represents only one dimension of treatment selection. Acknowleding that some series suggest acceptable functional outcomes after coloanal anastamosis in select patients, the bulk of literature suggests that functional outcomes are strongly influenced by anastomotic height, with lower tumors and coloanal anastomoses consistently associated with higher rates of persistent bowel dysfunction. However, anatomical considerations exist along a continuum and a threshold as narrowly defined by the authors risks shifting many clinically distal tumors into a “mid-rectal” category, even when surgery may still carry substantial functional consequences.

There are multiple curative-intent strategies for LARC each carrying risks of morbidity and long-term functional impairment and no clear preferential hierarchy of a universally applied “best” option [2], [3], [4], [5]. Replacement of radiotherapy with systemic therapy results in similar oncologic outcomes but higher neoadjuvant toxicity and only modest reduction in long-term sequelae [2], [3]. PROSPECT demonstrated higher rates of acute patient-reported symptom burden and ≥ grade 3 toxicity with neoadjuvant FOLFOX, while chemoradiation was associated with more diarrhea. Long-term, there were no differences in quality of life (QoL) or bowel/bladder outcomes compared with chemoradiation, but chemoradiation was associated with poorer sexual function. Furthermore, extending oxaliplatin-based systemic therapy is associated with neuropathy [6]. Thus, chemotherapy-first strategies shift toxicity rather than clearly reducing treatment burden. However, the additive value of systemic therapy upon chemoradiation also has been questioned, especially in non-high risk or N0 disease, and thus the spectrum of toxicity may shift in either direction if considering options like intensification of systemic therapy to replace chemoradiation or utilizing chemoradiation to allow de-intensification of systemic therapy [6], [7].

In regard to the characterization of radiotherapy, modern radiotherapy techniques—including improved conformality, image guidance, adaptive therapy, and dose intensification—have improved response and tolerability. Brachytherapy or external beam boost approaches report cCR rates of 80–90% and OP rates of 70–80%, with favorable functional outcomes [8]. Data also suggest a generally better functional and QoL outcome with OP, when compared with surgery, especially in the context of modern radiotherapy delivery. For example, the STAR-TREC trial comparing upfront TME versus radiation-based OP demonstrated comparable disease-free survival (excluding local regrowth) and improved quality of life with OP (71% posterior probability at 2 years per EORTC QLQ-C30) [9]. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that OP was associated with improvements in several general patient-reported QoL domains and also better bowel function when compared to radical surgery [10]. These findings have supported a shift toward OP as a central objective in clinical trials and as a standard of care clinical practice.

Within this context the limitations of an anatomy-centric approach warrant discussion. The assumption that OP for mid-rectal tumors cannot achieve excellent functional outcomes is not supported by current evidence and future trials will inform this endpoint. While consideration of tumor location is critical, emphasis on anatomy is overly restrictive, not aligned with patient preferences, and underemphasizes biological-determinants of response. While tools such as circulating tumor DNA are not yet ready to guide routine clinical decisions, they may help characterize recurrence risk in ways that anatomy alone cannot. Future efforts should focus on biologic risk-stratification integrating multi-omic response-based biomarkers that guide patient selection and balance treatment intensity with oncologic risk and QoL.

In summary, while tumor location remains relevant, it should not be the primary determinant of treatment strategy. Decisions in modern practice are driven by how patients and clinicians weigh tradeoffs between oncologic risk and functional outcomes integrating patient preference, anatomic factors, biological response, and a goal of mitigating treatment-related toxicity. Future paradigm shifts necessitate incorporation of multi-omic response-based biomarkers to guide patient selection. Ultimately, our treatment recommendations need to be guided by the voice of the patients, reflecting their preferences and perspectives.
