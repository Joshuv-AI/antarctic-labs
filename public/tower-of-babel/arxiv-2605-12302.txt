A sharp degree bound in the real Jacobian conjecture
Francisco Braun ∗ , Filipe Fernandes † , Janusz Gwoździewicz ‡ , and Bruna Oréfice-Okamoto ∗

Abstract
. Let F = ( p , q ) : ℝ 2 → ℝ 2 F=(p,q):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2} be a polynomial map with nowhere zero Jacobian determinant. A long-standing problem is to determine the largest integer k k such that the condition deg ⁡ p ≤ k \deg p\leq k guarantees the global injectivity of F F . Although several partial results have been obtained over the past 30 30 years, the sharp degree bound has remained unknown. In this paper, we prove that F F is injective whenever deg ⁡ p = 6 \deg p=6 . On the other hand, we construct a non-injective polynomial map with nowhere vanishing Jacobian determinant for which deg ⁡ p = 7 \deg p=7 . Combined with the previously known injectivity results for deg ⁡ p ≤ 5 \deg p\leq 5 , our results completely settle the problem and establish the optimal degree bound. More precisely, we show that 7 7 is the minimal degree for which non-injective examples can occur.

Introduction

Let
F=(p,q):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a smooth map such that

(1)

JF(x,y)\neq 0\ \forall(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
JF(x,y)
stands for the Jacobian determinant of
F
at
(x,y)
.
By the Inverse Function Theorem,
F
is a local diffeomorphism but not necessarily a global one.

If we assume in addition that
F
is a polynomial map, the
real Jacobian conjecture
states that (
1
) implies that
F
is a global diffeomorphism.
This conjecture is not true: in 1994 Pinchuk
[
21
]
constructed a class of non-injective polynomial maps
F=(p,q)
satisfying (
1
).
In this class
p
is a fixed polynomial of degree
10
and
q
varies, but the smallest degree it can have is
25
, see
[
8
]
for more details.

On the other hand, if
p
has degree
1
, then we can assume
p(x,y)=x
and
q_{y}(x,y)\neq 0
, so that
y\mapsto q(x,y)
is injective for each
x
and then the real Jacobian conjecture holds for
F
.
Let
k
be the positive integer such that the real Jacobian conjecture is true with the additional assumption that the degree of
p
is less than
k
and such that there exists a counterexample with
\deg p=k
.
Then
2\leq k\leq 10
.

Since the appearance of the Pinchuk counterexample, researchers have been trying to determine the exact value of
k
.
It is not difficult to conclude, by extending a little the above reasoning that
k>2
.

In
[
15
]
it was proved that the real Jacobian conjecture holds if both
\deg p<4
and
\deg q<4
.
Subsequently, in
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
, it was concluded that the conjecture holds by assuming that
\deg p<6
, see also
[
12
]
.

On the other hand, in
[
11
]
it was constructed a counterexample to the real Jacobian conjecture with
\deg p=10
and
\deg q=15
.
Then in
[
2
]
, a counterexample with
\deg p=9
and
\deg q=15
appeared.

So far it follows then that
6\leq k\leq 9
.
In this work, we show that
k=7
.
Indeed, Corollary
5.2
shows that
k\geq 7
, and Corollary
6.5
shows that
k\leq 7
.

Our results were obtained by combining tools from algebraic geometry and dynamical systems.
Indeed, in Section
1
we recall general key facts in the context of the global injectivity, briefly explaining our strategy.
In Section
2
we recall the tools from algebraic geometry we will employ in our paper.
It is here that we will introduce the function
N_{S}(c)
, which counts the number of branches at infinity of the curve
p(x,y)=c
associated with the outer edge
S
of the Newton polygon of the polynomial
p
.
When
p
is a submersion, the sum of the functions
N_{S}(c)
, considering all the outer edges, is
N(c)
, which is precisely the number of connected components of the fiber
p^{-1}(c)
.
In this section, Lemma
2.4
, with results on
N_{S}
, will be the main one.
We finish the section by defining the Euler integral of
N_{S}
and recalling Sȩkalski’s theorem relating the Euler integral with the index of the gradient of
p
at infinity.

In Section
3
we recall the Poincaré compactification of polynomial vector fields as well as some key concepts on dynamical systems we will need.
Then we particularize to the Hamiltonian vector field
\nabla p^{\perp}
associated with a polynomial function
p
.
The concept of hyperbolic sectors at infinity of
\nabla p^{\perp}
will be recalled.
It is the main subject of Theorem
3.1
, a result from
[
4
]
, that gives conditions for the non-existence of Jacobian mates for polynomial submersions.

In Section
4
we put together the techniques of the previous sections to construct the main tools we will use in our proof.
In particular, we prove Theorem
4.1
, that precisely describes the function
N_{S}(c)
when
S
has only one interior lattice point.
Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
will relate the existence of Jacobian mates with some properties on edges of the Newton polygon of
p
.

In Section
5
we prove that
k\leq 7
by applying the results of the previous sections.

Then we end the paper in Section
6
presenting a non-injective polynomial map
(p,q):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose Jacobian determinant is nowhere zero such that
\deg p=7
.

1.
Candidates for
p

1.1.
Connectedness of fibers and bifurcation set

It is well known that a polynomial map
F
satisfying (
1
) is injective if and only if the fibers of
p
are connected.
Indeed, (
1
) ensures the monotonicity of
q
along the connected components of
p^{-1}(c)
, what proves the “only if” part.
The “if” part follows because injective polynomial maps of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are automatically onto, see for instance
[
1
]
, so the fibers of
p
are connected.
Then for
F
satisfying (
1
), the injectivity of
F
is equivalent to
p
being a
locally trivial fibration
, that is, the
bifurcation set of

p
, that we will denote by
B(p)
, is empty, see more details, for instance, in
[
6
,
23
]
.

1.2.
Highest homogeneous part

For a polynomial function
p
, we will denote by
p_{+}
its homogeneous part of highest degree.
From
[
4
, Lemma 4.2]
, as well as from
[
9
, Theorem 2.2]
and
[
7
, Proposition 4]
, it follows that if a polynomial submersion has a disconnected fiber then
p_{+}
must have linear factors of multiplicity at least
2
.
Therefore, since we want to prove the injectivity of maps
F
satisfying (
1
), we only need to be concerned with polynomial submersions
p
such that

(2)

p_{+}(x,y)=(ax+by)^{2}r(x,y),

where
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
r(x,y)
is a homogeneous polynomial.

1.3.
Jacobian mates

We say that a polynomial submersion
p:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
has a
Jacobian mate
when there exists a polynomial submersion
q:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
such that the map
(p,q)
satisfies (
1
).
In this case,
q
is a
Jacobian mate of
p
.

Our strategy is to consider polynomial submersions of degree
6
with disconnected fibers or, equivalently, such that
B(p)\neq\emptyset
, and to prove that they have no Jacobian mates.

1.4.
Quadratic-like

It was proved in
[
3
]
that quadratic-like polynomial submersions – those of degree less than
3
in
x
or
y
– either have all fibers connected or have no Jacobian mates.
Therefore, we only need to be concerned with the polynomial submersions having degree at least
3
in both
x
and in
y
.

2.
Newton polygon for counting connected components of fibers: the branches at infinity technique

2.1.
Branches at infinity

Throughout this paper, any mention to
infinity
, or to the
line at infinity
, will mean the border of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
.
As usually, we shall represent
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
by the closed unit disc identifying antipodal points on its boundary.
In this model, the boundary of the disc corresponds to the line at infinity.
For any polynomial
p(x,y)
of degree
d
, by its
homogenization
we mean the polynomial
P(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=z_{3}^{d}p(z_{1}/z_{3},z_{2}/z_{3})
.

We will say that an infinity point
[a:b:0]
is contained in the algebraic curve
p(x,y)=0
when
P(a,b,0)=0
.
This is equivalent to the real linear factor
ay-bx
being a divisor of
p_{+}(x,y)
.

2.2.
Milnor at infinity

It is well known, see Milnor
[
20
, Lemma 3.3]
, that for a non-isolated point
z
of a given real algebraic curve
p(x,y)=0
there exists a neighborhood
U
of
z
such that
p^{-1}(0)\cap U
is the union of finitely many
branches
intersecting only at
z
.
Each branch is homeomorphic to an interval
(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
under an analytic parametrization
s(t)=\left(s_{1}(t),s_{2}(t)\right)
with
s(0)=z
.
The images
s(-\epsilon,0)
and
s(0,\epsilon)
are the
half-branches
of the branch.
In this case, we say that each of these branches or half-branches is
at
z
.

For a
branch at infinity
of an algebraic curve we will mean a branch of the curve at a point in the line of infinity.

By considering the homogeneous coordinates and their inverses at the chart of
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
(see also Section
3.1
below), neighborhood of a point in the line of infinity contained in an algebraic curve
p(x,y)=0
, we can “convert” to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
the parametrization of any branch at infinity not entirely contained in the coordinate axes at infinity to a Laurent series.
To be more precise, let
s(t)
be the above parametrization of a branch of an algebraic curve at infinity with
s_{1}(t)s_{2}(t)\not\equiv 0
.
Deleting the point at infinity, we can put this branch back to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and parametrize it as
\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t))
,
0<|t|\leq\epsilon
, where

(3)

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=At^{-\alpha}+\mbox{ terms of higher degrees, }

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=Bt^{-\beta}+\mbox{ terms of higher degrees, }

for suitable
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
,
AB\neq 0
, and
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, with
\alpha>0
or
\beta>0
.
It is easy to conclude that this
branch

\gamma
is at
[A:B:0]
if
\alpha=\beta
, at
[1:0:0]
if
\alpha>\beta
, and at
[0:1:0]
if
\alpha<\beta
.

2.3.
Newton polygon

For a given polynomial
p(x,y)=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}x^{i}y^{j}
, we define its
support
as the subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
given by
\{(i,j)\ |\ a_{ij}\neq 0\}
, and its
Newton polygon
, denoted by
NP(p)
, as the convex hull of its support in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Up to a linear change of variables, we can always assume that
NP(p)
is
convenient
, that is, that it touches both the
x
and
y
axes away from the origin.
In this case we also say that
p
is convenient
.
Up to adding a constant, we may assume that a convenient Newton polygon has positive area.
In this case, for each edge
S
of a convenient
NP(p)
, we define the vector
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, with coprime coordinates, that is orthogonal to
S
and points outward
NP(p)
as the
outer normal vector of
S
.

Any edge such that its outer normal vector has at least one positive coordinate is said to be an
outer edge
.

For a face
S
of
NP(p)
we define the polynomial
p_{S}(x,y)=\sum_{(i,j)\in S}a_{i,j}x^{i}y^{j}
, that can be written as, see
[
19
]
,

(4)

p_{S}(x,y)=\delta x^{r}y^{s}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(y^{\xi_{1}}-c_{i}x^{\xi_{2}})^{\nu_{i}},

for suitable
r,s,\nu_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
.
The
c_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
, are pairwise distinct and non-zero.

Definition 2.1
.

The polynomial
p(x,y)
is
nondegenerate on the edge
S
of
NP(p)
if
\nu_{i}=1
whenever
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, for all
i=1,\ldots,k
.
Besides,
p(x,y)
is
nondegenerate with respect to
NP(p)
, or simply
nondegenerate
, when
NP(p)
is convenient and
p(x,y)
is nondegenerate on each outer edges of
NP(p)
.

Sometimes we will extend the definition of Newton polygon for forms
p(x,y)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}a_{ij}x^{i}y^{j}
in the natural way.

2.4.
Branches associated with outer edges of
NP(p)

Definition 2.2
.

Let
p(x,y)
be a polynomial.
We say that a

\gamma

at infinity is associated with the outer edge

Remark 1
.

If

Indeed, let
\gamma

Then

\displaystyle 0=t^{d}\sum_{k=\ell}^{d}p_{k}\big(x(t),y(t)\big)

\displaystyle=t^{d}\sum_{k=\ell}^{d}p_{k}\Big(t^{-\alpha}(A+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}t^{j}),t^{-\beta}(B+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}t^{j})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=\ell}^{d}t^{d-k}p_{k}\Big(A+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}t^{j},B+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{j}t^{j}\Big),

for suitable

Letting

Since

Note that outer edges of a convenient Newton polygon do not change when

Definition 2.3

Let

We define

Remark 2

(1)

It is clear that for a convenient

(2)

Also, for a polynomial submersion

So, in this case, the function

3.
Global dynamics of Hamiltonian vector field for eliminating Jacobian mates

3.1.
Poincaré compactification

Any planar polynomial vector field
\mathcal{X}
can be compactified by means of the
Poincaré compactification of
\mathcal{X}
, giving rise to a vector field
p(\mathcal{X})
in
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
, in such a way that the invariant flow on the line at infinity corresponds to the behavior of
\mathcal{X}
at infinity, and the flow on the interior of the disc is the transformed flow up to multiplication by a suitable positive function.

There is an appealing geometric construction of this compactification, summarized as follows.
We first identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the affine tangent plane
\pi
of the bi-dimensional unit sphere
\mathbb{S}_{2}
in the north pole.
Each straight line non parallel to
\pi
through the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
cuts
\pi
in one point and
\mathbb{S}_{2}
in two points.
This gives two analytic diffeomorphisms
f^{+}
and
f^{-}
from
\pi
onto the north and south hemispheres of
\mathbb{S}_{2}
, respectively.
Through
f^{+}
and
f^{-}
we get two copies of
\mathcal{X}
in
\mathbb{S}_{2}
, one in the north and one in the south hemisphere, respectively.
It turns out that by multiplying these copies by

, where
m
is the degree of
\mathcal{X}
and
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
are the coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we get a polynomial vector field in the entire
\mathbb{S}_{2}

Poincaré compactification of
\mathcal{X}
, whose restrictions to each of the hemisphere is topologically equivalent to
\mathcal{X}
.
By construction, the equator of
\mathbb{S}_{2}
is invariant by the flow.
The points at the equator are said the
infinite
points of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and the singular points of the Poincaré compactification of
\mathcal{X}
at the equator are said the
infinite singular points of
\mathcal{X}
.
It is clear that
\mathcal{X}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
together with its infinite points is fully qualitatively understood in the unit disc, our model for
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
: simply project the closure of the north hemisphere of
\mathbb{S}_{2}
onto the disc.
Since the antipodal points of
\mathbb{S}_{2}
correspond to the same point in the original plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, in order to study the Poincaré compactification of
\mathcal{X}
we only need the local canonical charts

, where
U_{i}=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{S}_{2}\ |\ x_{i}>0\}
, with
\phi_{i}(x)=(x_{j_{1}}/x_{i},x_{j_{2}}/x_{i})
,

,
i=1,2,3
.
It is simple to see that both composite applications

and
\phi_{i}\circ f^{-}(x_{1},x_{2})
give the expression
(x_{j}/x_{i},1/x_{i})
,

,

.
So if in the original plane we want to study the infinite point given by a direction

.
For more details on the Poincaré compactification, we address the reader to

Next lemma follows by the rules of differentiation.

Lemma 6.3

J\Big(p,\frac{1}{m^{2}}\Big)=\frac{2(2w-1)}{m^{2}},\quad J(p,w)=-mp.
