Self-reported menstrual-related physical and psychological symptoms and health-seeking behaviour among rural school-going adolescent girls in Bangladesh
Authors: Abdul Basit, Kanij Fatema, Mohammad Ohid Ullah, Md. Assraf Seddiky, Mahfuzul Mithun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMenstrual Hygiene Management (MHM) is recognized globally as a public health concern; however, it remains a neglected issue in Bangladesh, particularly in rural communities. This study examined the self-reported prevalence and perceived severity of menstruation-related physical and psychological symptoms, their association with menstrual irregularity, and health-seeking behaviour among rural school-going adolescent girls.MethodsA cross-sectional mixed-methods study was conducted among 474 grade 9–10 school-going girls in rural Sylhet division, Bangladesh, between September and November, 2024. Participants reported menstrual cycle characteristics and self-rated the severity of their common physical and psychological symptoms as mild, moderate, or severe. Menstrual cycle regularity was assessed through self-report. Symptom prevalence and severity distributions were summarised descriptively by cycle regularity group. Chi-square tests examined associations between menstrual irregularity and symptom severity. Qualitative in-depth interviews (n = 29) were conducted with purposively selected participants to contextualise quantitative patterns within broader sociocultural and structural determinants.ResultsOf 474 participants, 111 (23.4%) reported irregular menstrual cycles. Abdominal pain or cramps (77.2%), fatigue or weakness (69.4%), and joint or muscle pain (48.5%) were the most prevalent physical symptoms. Irritability (73.4%), mood swings (64.1%), and anxiety (58.6%) were the most common psychological symptoms. Menstrual irregularity was significantly associated with higher symptom severity for both physical (χ²(2) = 157.81, p < 0.001) and psychological (χ²(2) = 100.85, p < 0.001) outcomes; 49.5% of irregular-cycle girls reported severe physical symptoms versus 3.9% of regular-cycle girls, and 27.0% reported severe psychological symptoms versus 3.0%. Qualitative findings revealed that these high symptom burdens were systematically normalised through cultural beliefs, intergenerational transmission of avoidance strategies, and stigma-mediated communication failures, resulting in very low rates of formal health-care seeking irrespective of symptom severity.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that menstrual symptom burden, both physical and psychological, is substantial among rural adolescent girls in Bangladesh and is disproportionately severe among those with irregular cycles. These findings highlight the importance of integrating symptom recognition, stigma reduction, and accessible care pathways into health initiatives for adolescents. Given the cross-sectional and self-reported design, the results should be interpreted as exploratory but indicative of an important area for targeted public health attention in rural contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04569-x.

Introduction

Menstrual hygiene management (MHM) refers to practices and resources that enable women and girls to manage their menstruation safely, hygienically, and with dignity [1] It incorporates access to menstrual materials, clean water, and safe spaces for changing, and proper disposal systems [2–4]. Globally, MHM is considered a serious public health issue that affects various aspects of women’s lives, including health, education, and social equality, especially in low-income countries [5, 6]. In many such settings, girls face persistent barriers to managing menstruation safely, including limited resources, inadequate facilities, and strong social stigma [7–9]. These constraints shape adolescents’ everyday experiences of menstruation and influence their interpretation and response to menstruation-related symptoms.

Menstrual health encompasses several interrelated but distinct dimensions. MHM reflects environmental and behavioral conditions [10], whereas menstrual cycle characteristics, such as regularity, represent biological patterns. Separately, girls may experience a range of physical and psychological symptoms during menstruation that affect their comfort, daily functioning, and school participation [11, 12]. Physically, adolescents commonly report abdominal and back pain, excessive bleeding or anaemia, headaches and migraines, nausea and digestive problems, breast pain and swelling, fatigue [13, 14]. Adolescents may also describe emotional and psychological symptoms during menstruation, including irritability, low mood, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, and sleep disturbances, particularly when cycles are irregular [15]. Menstrual irregularity, a common issue among women, disrupts the normal cycle of 21–35 days, lasting 2–7 days. It includes cycle changes, excessive or abnormal bleeding, painful periods, or prolonged absence of menstruation, leading to serious health effects [16]. Previous studies suggest that irregular cycles may be associated with increased physical discomfort and greater reporting of emotional or mood-related symptoms [17, 18]. At the same time, psychological stress may influence cycle regularity, suggesting a potentially bidirectional relationship [19, 20]. However, much of the existing evidence comes from adult populations, and less is known about how adolescents experience the combined burden of physical and psychological symptoms in relation to menstrual irregularity. When these symptoms remain unrecognised or unaddressed, as is common in rural low-income settings adolescents frequently rely on traditional practices rather than formal care, creating a cycle of unmet health need that this study seeks to document and explain.

Despite growing recognition of the health impacts of menstrual irregularities, evidence on how these physical and psychological health symptoms are experienced simultaneously, particularly among adolescents, and how they shape patterns of health-conscious behavior is still limited. Much of the existing literature has focused on disparities in MHM practices or access to menstrual products, often ignoring the combined burden of physical and psychological distress associated with menstrual irregularities and how adolescents respond to these challenges. This gap is particularly evident in low-income rural contexts, where structural barriers and social norms strongly influence menstrual experiences and care awareness. In Bangladesh, menstrual health is still a neglected public health issue, especially in rural areas where deep-rooted cultural taboos, stigma, and silence surrounding menstruation remain [21–23]. Access to modern menstrual products is unequal, with evidence suggesting that only a small number of women rely on commercially produced sanitary pads, with most using traditional and often unhygienic materials, which increases the risk of health complications [24]. Studies from Bangladesh and comparable settings suggest that menstrual irregularities are common and are often associated with physical discomfort, such as anxiety, depression, and lower abdominal pain [25–27]. However, health-seeking behaviors for menstruation-related problems are extremely low. Menstruation is often considered a private or shameful issue, discouraging open discussion and timely care seeking [28, 29]. Among unmarried adolescent girls, medical awareness of menstrual and reproductive health concerns is particularly limited, with many relying on self-care, traditional remedies, or informal providers rather than formal health care [30, 31]. These challenges are exacerbated by poverty, limited mobility, limited adolescent-friendly healthcare, and entrenched gender norms in rural settings [32–34]. Despite these documented challenges, limited research has examined how rural school-going adolescents simultaneously experience menstrual-related physical and psychological symptoms and how these experiences influence decisions about seeking care.

Accordingly, this study aims to: (i) examine the physical and mental health symptoms experienced by rural school-going adolescents during menstruation; (ii) assess the association between menstrual irregularities and the severity of these symptoms; and (iii) explore menstrual health-seeking behavior within a rural Bangladeshi context. By examining adolescents’ symptom experiences alongside health-seeking practices, this study provides context-specific evidence that may inform school-based menstrual health education and adolescent-friendly service strategies in rural Bangladesh. Specifically, this study addresses three research questions:

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

This study employed a sequential explanatory cross-sectional mixed-methods design, in which quantitative data collection and preliminary analysis preceded and informed purposive qualitative sampling. Qualitative interviews were subsequently conducted to contextualise and expand upon patterns identified in the quantitative phase. The study was conducted in rural secondary schools in the Sylhet Division, Bangladesh. Although the sample was drawn from one of the eight divisions, these schools were run under the country’s national curriculum and uniform school management framework. According to the Survey on Children’s Education in Bangladesh 2021 [35], the net enrolment rates of girls and boys at lower secondary and higher secondary levels in the Sylhet Division (52.6% and 41.2%, respectively) are within the national rural average (59.1% and 48.7%, respectively), reflecting the general educational trend in rural Bangladesh [36], especially the relatively high participation of girls. In addition, the National Hygiene Survey 2018 [37] showed that almost all rural schools in the country lack access to drinking water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) facilities, which is also observed in Sylhet. However, the findings are not intended to be nationally representative. Due to the cross-sectional design, the analyses identified associations rather than causal relationships between menstrual irregularity and symptom severity. Studying these issues in rural settings is important because limited health service access, strong social norms, and menstrual stigma may shape both symptom experiences and care-seeking practices.

Study setting and sampling strategy

The study population consisted of adolescent girls enrolled in grades 9 and 10 (approximately 14–17 years old) in government-registered secondary schools. A multistage sampling strategy was used. Four upazilas (Sylhet Sadar, Jaintapur, Bishwanath, and Dakshin Surma) within the Sylhet district were randomly selected. Eighteen secondary schools were then randomly chosen from official school lists. Within each selected school, eligible students were identified from the school records and selected using the random number generation function in IBM SPSS (version 26). A total of 474 participants were included. If a selected student was absent or refused to participate, no replacement was included to maintain randomness. The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for estimating proportions, assuming a 95% confidence level, 4% margin of error, and an estimated prevalence of menstrual-related symptoms from prior literature. The calculation assumes simple random sampling. School-level clustering was not incorporated into the sample size calculation, which may have modestly underestimated the required sample size. Because the sampling frame included only school-enrolled adolescents, girls who had dropped out or never attended school were not represented, potentially limiting the generalizability to more socioeconomically vulnerable populations.

For the qualitative phase, a purposive sampling was employed following the preliminary analysis of quantitative data. Participants were selected from among survey respondents representing variations in self-reported symptom severity to ensure maximum variation in experiences. A total of 29 semi-structured interviews were conducted. Data saturation was reached after 27 interviews; two additional interviews were conducted to confirm thematic stability and ensure that no new themes emerged. Only participants who completed the quantitative survey and consented to the follow-up interviews were eligible for qualitative participation. This sequential sampling approach allowed qualitative findings to contextualize and expand upon patterns observed in the quantitative data.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were female students enrolled in grades 9 or 10, experienced menarche, and provided written parental consent and personal assent to participate in the study. Adolescents who had not yet attained menarche were excluded because the study focused specifically on menstrual experiences. Additionally, participants with diagnosed chronic medical conditions known to affect menstrual function were excluded to minimize potential confounding between underlying medical disorders and self-reported menstrual-related symptoms. While this exclusion criterion was intended to improve internal validity, it may have influenced the prevalence estimates of menstrual irregularity and symptom burden, and is acknowledged as a limitation.

Data collection instruments and study variables

Menstrual irregularity

Menstrual cycle regularity was assessed through self-reporting. Participants were asked about the timing and predictability of their menstrual cycles during the previous six months. Cycles shorter than 21 days, longer than 35 days, or described as unpredictable were categorized as irregular. This measure reflects self-reported perception and has not been clinically verified.

Physical symptoms

Participants reported the presence of common menstrual-related physical symptoms (e.g., abdominal pain, breast tenderness, bloating, and gastrointestinal discomfort) using a structured checklist developed from prior adolescent menstrual health literature.

Psychological symptoms

Participants reported menstruation-related psychological experiences, such as irritability, sadness, anxiety, and difficulty concentrating. These items reflect subjective emotional experiences during menstruation and do not represent clinical diagnoses of depression, anxiety, or stress disorders. The absence of validated instruments (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7) is acknowledged as a key methodological limitation. Findings regarding psychological symptoms should therefore be interpreted as descriptive self-reports of perceived experiences during menstruation rather than clinical assessments, and future research should employ validated adolescent-appropriate tools to allow comparability across studies.

Symptom severity

For each reported symptom, participants rated their perceived severity on a three-point scale: 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, and 3 = severe. No composite symptom burden index was constructed. Severity was analysed as a distributional outcome, that is the proportion of participants rating their symptoms as mild, moderate, or severe, within each symptom domain separately.

Data collection procedures and quality assurance

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire. The instrument was developed based on national and international literature and was pre-tested with 20 students outside the study area to assess clarity and contextual relevance. Minor wording adjustments were made accordingly. Trained female data collectors fluent in Bengali and the local Sylheti dialect conducted interviews to ensure cultural appropriateness and participant comfort. Participants were assured that there were no right or wrong answers and were encouraged to respond based on their personal experiences. For the qualitative phase, semi-structured interviews were conducted with purposively selected participants representing the higher and lower ends of self-reported symptom severity. The interview guide (see Supplementary File 1 for the interview guide) explored health-seeking behavior, perceived barriers to care, sources of support, and social norms surrounding menstruation. All interviews were conducted in a separate room within the school premises to ensure privacy and confidentiality. In a few cases, when requested by parents, interviews were conducted at participants’ homes following the same protocol. The interviews were audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Data were entered and cleaned using IBM SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were calculated to summarise symptom prevalence and severity distributions across the full sample and by menstrual cycle regularity group. Associations between menstrual irregularity and self-reported symptom severity were examined using cross-tabulations and Pearson’s chi-square tests. Given the large sample size, chi-square statistics may be sensitive to sample size effects and should be interpreted alongside the magnitude of observed distributional differences. Regression-based multivariate models were not conducted; accordingly, potential confounding variables (including age, nutritional status, body mass index, socioeconomic status, and workload) were not adjusted for, and all reported associations should be interpreted as unadjusted and exploratory.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis. An initial code framework was developed after repeated reading of transcripts. Codes were generated inductively from participants’ narratives and grouped into broader thematic categories. To enhance analytical credibility, transcripts were reviewed multiple times to ensure consistency of interpretation across cases. Themes were refined through comparison of patterns across participants rather than relying solely on frequency of statements. Reflexive notes were maintained during analysis to minimize researcher bias in interpreting culturally sensitive narratives. The results from both phases were combined using a joint display matrix (see Supplementary File 2) to illustrate convergence and divergence between quantitative trends and qualitative descriptions. The integration of qualitative and quantitative findings allowed for a comprehensive interpretation of the challenges adolescent girls face and the pathways they follow when seeking support.

Ethical considerations

Institutional approval was obtained from the participating schools. Written parental consent and participant assent were obtained prior to participation. Participants were informed of voluntary participation, confidentiality safeguards, and their right to withdraw without penalty. Each participant was assigned a unique identification code. Audio recordings were stored securely and deleted after transcription. The transcripts used pseudonyms to maintain confidentiality. This study adhered to the national research guidelines and international standards for research involving minors.

Findings

Physical and psychological symptoms faced by menstruating adolescents

Physical symptoms

Table 1 presents the prevalence of twelve self-reported physical symptoms across the full sample and stratified by menstrual cycle regularity. The three most reported physical symptoms overall were abdominal pain or cramps (77.2%), fatigue or weakness (69.4%), and joint or muscle pain (48.5%). Back pain (47.7%), headaches (45.8%), and dizziness or fainting (43.7%) were also frequently reported. Less prevalent but notable symptoms included heavy menstrual bleeding (31.2%), bloating or swelling (27.4%), and breast tenderness (20.0%). Diarrhea or constipation was the least commonly reported symptom (8.2%).

Prevalence of self-reported physical symptoms during menstruation by cycle regularity (N = 474)

When stratified by cycle regularity, adolescents with irregular cycles reported a higher prevalence of back pain (55.0% vs. 45.5%), fatigue (73.9% vs. 68.0%), diarrhea or constipation (12.6% vs. 6.9%), and bloating (31.5% vs. 26.2%) compared to those with regular cycles. Interestingly, abdominal pain or cramps was more prevalent among girls with regular cycles (81.0%) than irregular ones (64.9%), suggesting that this symptom is common across both groups rather than being specifically associated with cycle irregularity. The mean number of physical symptoms reported per participant was 4.56 (SD = 2.17) overall, with a slightly higher mean in the irregular group (4.76, SD = 2.21) compared to the regular group (4.50, SD = 2.16).

While these prevalence figures document the extent of physical burden, qualitative accounts illuminate how these symptoms were experienced and interpreted in everyday life. The high prevalence of fatigue and abdominal pain in both groups was accompanied by a cultural tendency to normalise such discomfort rather than treat it as a medical concern. Most participants described managing physical symptoms through traditional or home-based practices rather than seeking formal care. One participant explained:If I have lower abdominal pain, I take a bath with hot water or chew garlic. I have seen this since childhood.

If I have lower abdominal pain, I take a bath with hot water or chew garlic. I have seen this since childhood.

This account reflects the intergenerational transmission of home-management strategies for menstrual pain. Formal medical consultation was considered unnecessary unless symptoms became markedly severe, which helps explain why physical symptom severity across the full sample skews predominantly toward mild (40.5%) and moderate (44.9%) categories despite a high symptom count burden. The routine reliance on traditional remedies functionally substituted for care-seeking, keeping severe cases particularly concentrated in the irregular group (49.5%) without appropriate medical attention.

With respect to overall perceived severity, the majority of all participants rated their physical symptoms as moderate (44.9%), followed by mild (40.5%) and severe (14.6%). However, this overall pattern conceals a sharp contrast by cycle regularity: among girls with irregular cycles, nearly half (49.5%) rated their physical symptom burden as severe, compared to only 3.9% of those with regular cycles, a pattern examined further in the association analysis below.

Psychological symptoms

Table 2 presents the prevalence of thirteen self-reported psychological symptoms. Overall, the psychological symptom burden was substantial. The most commonly reported symptoms were irritability (73.4%), mood swings (64.1%), anxiety (58.6%), and anger or frustration (57.8%). Social withdrawal (49.2%) and feelings of shame or embarrassment (47.9%) were also reported by nearly half of all participants, highlighting the prominent psychosocial dimension of menstrual experience in this population. Nervousness (44.7%), stress (41.1%), difficulty concentrating (36.5%), and depression or sadness (36.1%) were reported by approximately one-third to two-fifths of participants. Sleep disturbances, low self-esteem, and lack of motivation were less prevalent but still reported by 18 to 35% of participants.

Prevalence of self-reported psychological symptoms during menstruation by cycle regularity (N = 474)

Stratification by cycle regularity revealed a consistent pattern of higher symptom prevalence among girls with irregular cycles across most psychological domains. Depression or sadness showed the largest absolute difference: 53.2% of irregular-cycle girls reported this symptom compared to 30.9% of regular-cycle girls. Social withdrawal (58.6% vs. 46.3%), feelings of shame or embarrassment (55.9% vs. 45.5%), and stress (49.5% vs. 38.6%) also showed notably higher prevalence in the irregular group. Notably, sleep disturbances were slightly more common in the regular-cycle group (35.3%) than in the irregular group (33.3%), suggesting that this symptom may be influenced by factors beyond cycle regularity alone. The mean number of psychological symptoms per participant was 5.91 (SD = 2.74) overall, with the irregular group reporting a meaningfully higher burden (6.66, SD = 2.67) compared to the regular group (5.67, SD = 2.72).

These quantitative patterns, particularly the high prevalence of shame, irritability, and social withdrawal, are directly contextualised by participants’ qualitative accounts. The data show that many of the most frequently reported psychological symptoms were simultaneously normalised and suppressed, creating a paradox in which widespread emotional distress remained largely unaddressed. Participants consistently described a cultural expectation to endure emotional changes during menstruation without disclosing them:I don’t tell anyone when I feel sad or angry or cry. These are normal things during menstruation, why should I tell anyone!

I don’t tell anyone when I feel sad or angry or cry. These are normal things during menstruation, why should I tell anyone!

This statement reflects more than simple stigma; it reveals the internalised normalisation of psychological distress. Emotional changes including the irritability (73.4%) and mood swings (64.1%) that ranked highest in Table 2, were reframed by participants as expected biological inevitabilities rather than health concerns warranting attention. This normalisation directly suppresses help-seeking behaviour and helps explain why, despite the high prevalence of psychological symptoms, overall mental severity across the sample remained predominantly mild (52.1%).

Communication barriers compounded this normalisation. The high prevalence of social withdrawal (49.2%) and shame or embarrassment (47.9%) observed quantitatively corresponds to a structural silencing of menstrual discourse within households, particularly in the presence of male family members:It feels wrong to talk about menstruation in front of the men in the house.

It feels wrong to talk about menstruation in front of the men in the house.

This indicates that restrictions on menstrual communication operate at both interpersonal and structural levels. Even within female family networks, open discussion was constrained. The result is that psychological distress associated with menstruation is doubly invisible: it is widespread in frequency (as Table 2 demonstrates) yet rarely acknowledged, disclosed, or treated as a legitimate health concern.

Regarding overall perceived psychological severity, most participants rated their burden as mild (52.1%), with moderate (39.2%) and severe (8.6%) less commonly reported. As with physical severity, this overall distribution masks a pronounced group difference: 27.0% of girls with irregular cycles reported severe psychological symptoms, versus only 3.0% in the regular group.

Association between menstrual characteristics and symptom severity

Chi-square tests were conducted to examine the association between menstrual cycle regularity and the severity of both physical and psychological health symptoms. Table 3 presents these results. The analyses revealed statistically significant associations for both domains. Among adolescents with irregular menstrual cycles (n = 111), nearly half (49.5%) experienced severe physical health symptoms, compared to only 3.9% of those with regular cycles (n = 363); χ² (2) = 157.814, p < 0.001. Mild physical symptoms were far more prevalent in the regular group (50.1%) than in the irregular group (9.0%). A similar pattern was observed for psychological symptoms: 27.0% of irregular-cycle girls reported severe mental health symptoms versus 3.0% of regular-cycle girls; χ² (2) = 100.847, p < 0.001. The majority of regular-cycle girls (62.8%) reported mild psychological symptoms, whereas moderate and severe categories dominated among irregular-cycle girls (55.9% and 27.0%, respectively).

The magnitude of this association, a 45-percentage-point gap in severe physical symptom prevalence and a 24-percentage-point gap in severe psychological symptoms between the two groups is striking. Yet the qualitative findings reveal that even these elevated severity levels did not routinely translate into formal care-seeking. Girls in the irregular group, who bore the greatest symptom severity, described the same patterns of normalisation that constrained help-seeking among their peers. A recurring attitude captured this dynamic:

This statement is particularly telling when considered alongside the severity data: it was expressed by a participant carrying a considerable symptom burden, yet formal care was categorically dismissed. The phrase “every girl has menstruation” encapsulates how the universality of the experience was used to delegitimise individual suffering, transforming a health concern into a universal female condition requiring only endurance. This pattern means that the statistically severe burden observed in the irregular group was systematically filtered through a cultural lens of normalisation before any care-seeking decision was made.

Menstrual health seeking behavior among rural adolescent schoolgirls

Qualitative findings revealed that adolescent schoolgirls were very unlikely to seek formal health care for menstrual symptoms, irrespective of severity. Health-seeking behaviour was shaped by a convergence of belief systems, communication barriers, family dynamics, and institutional failures, each of which is examined in turn below.

A notable feature of the qualitative data was the way in which menstruation-related physical symptoms were sometimes attributed to environmental or superstition-based causes rather than physiological factors. These explanatory frameworks influenced how girls understood their symptoms and whether they considered formal care appropriate:We flush clothes in the bathroom or sometimes bury them underground. If animals pull them out, then girls will have problems.

We flush clothes in the bathroom or sometimes bury them underground. If animals pull them out, then girls will have problems.

Such accounts demonstrate how culturally embedded beliefs about menstrual cloth disposal shape girls’ causal understanding of physical discomfort. When symptoms occurred, some participants suspected improper disposal practices rather than physiological processes as the underlying cause. These belief systems are not merely incidental; they constitute an alternative explanatory framework that reduces perceived need for medical consultation and reflects the depth of cultural taboo surrounding menstruation in this context.

When physical symptoms intensified sufficiently to prompt action, participants typically turned to local pharmacies rather than trained medical providers. However, even these encounters were mediated by stigma, leading girls to conceal the menstrual origin of their complaints:I was going through several abdominal pains. That is why I went to the local pharmacy to bring some medicine. But I cannot share the actual problem with the shopkeeper. Instead, I said, brother, my stomach hurts, and my body is itching. Give me something for this.

This account illustrates a dual barrier: the stigma that prevents direct disclosure of menstrual problems, and the resulting reliance on unregulated, informally trained providers. In rural settings, many pharmacies operate without formal licensing or adequate clinical training, meaning that symptomatic treatment is dispensed without proper diagnosis. The concealment of the true complaint compounds this problem, as the pharmacist cannot respond appropriately to a condition that has not been disclosed. This dynamic helps explain why even the most severely symptomatic girls predominantly those with irregular cycles remained without adequate clinical management.

Family attitudes were a critical determinant of care-seeking behaviour. The majority of participants described household environments in which menstrual health was not openly discussed and seeking care was discouraged:I feel ashamed to tell my father, and he asks what happened then I do not know how to tell him. My mother says learn to tolerate it, do not think too much about these things.

This reflects diminished prioritisation of menstrual health within households, with mothers reinforcing the same normalisation and endurance norms that shaped their daughters’ responses. The inter-generational transmission of these attitudes perpetuates a cycle in which menstrual symptoms are neither disclosed to parents nor treated as medical concerns. However, not all household environments reproduced this pattern. An exceptional case emerged in the qualitative data:I had a lot of stomach pain during my period and even had a fever. Then my father himself took me to the hospital.

It was subsequently established that both this participant’s parents were educated and in formal employment. This exceptional case underscores how parental education and socioeconomic position function as enabling factors for adolescent girls’ healthcare access, and, conversely, how their absence constitutes a structural barrier in households with lower educational attainment. The contrast between this experience and the majority pattern demonstrates that health-seeking behaviour is not simply a product of individual attitudes but is shaped by the family’s social and educational capital.

Participants consistently reported that neither schools nor community health workers had provided them with any information about menstrual health. This absence of institutional support reinforced the cultural silence surrounding menstruation:No one talks about these things in school; it is considered shameful to even talk about it. No one from the health center ever comes to our village about these things.

This suggests that formal health education and outreach have not penetrated the communities in which these girls live. The school which represents the primary institutional point of contact for this population failed to provide accurate menstrual health information, leaving girls dependent on family members and peers whose knowledge was itself shaped by stigma and tradition. Community health workers, who might provide a more accessible first point of care, were similarly absent from participants’ accounts. This institutional vacuum means that the primary determinants of menstrual health knowledge and behaviour remain cultural norms and intergenerational transmission rather than evidence-based health education.Taken together, the quantitative and qualitative findings converge to explain a coherent pattern: a high and widespread burden of physical and psychological menstrual symptoms with irregular-cycle girls carrying a disproportionately severe load exists within a sociocultural and structural environment that systematically suppresses recognition of these symptoms as health concerns and constrains the pathways to care. The quantitative data document the scale of the problem and the qualitative data explain the mechanisms that sustain it. Normalisation of distress, intergenerational transmission of avoidance strategies, superstition-based symptom attribution, stigma-mediated communication failures at pharmacies, family-level gatekeeping of care, and absence of institutional support together constitute a layered system in which menstrual health burden remains both high and unaddressed. The exceptional case of parental-facilitated hospital access demonstrates that this system is not immutable, but its disruption appears to depend on individual family circumstances, particularly parental education, rather than on any systematic health infrastructure. Discussion

This exploratory, cross-sectional study examined self-reported physical and psychological symptoms associated with menstruation among rural school-going adolescents in Bangladesh, assessed their unadjusted association with menstrual irregularity, and explored health-seeking behaviour in this context; findings should be interpreted in light of the methodological constraints described in the Limitations section. The findings reveal three principal insights including the presence of the burden of both physical and psychological symptoms is substantial with abdominal pain (77.2%), fatigue (69.4%), irritability (73.4%) and mood swings (64.1%); menstrual irregularity is strongly associated with markedly higher symptoms severity, yet this heightened burden was not accompanied by formal care-seeking; a layered system of cultural normalization, stigma, family dynamics and institutional failure systematically suppresses appropriate MHM.

The prevalence of physical symptoms observed in this study aligns with regional estimates from low and middle income countries. Armour, Parry [38] reported that dysmenorrhea affects 71–85% adolescents globally, with abdominal cramps being the dominant complaint. Another Bangladeshi study in Jashore found 80% of adolescents reporting lower abdominal pain during menstruation [39]. Besides, A meta-analysis of dysmenorrhea among Indian students similarly documents abdominal pain as the dominant menstrual complaint across South Asian school populations [40]. Similarly, studies from India and Nepal have documented fatigue and back pain as common accompaniments [41–43]. In contrast to these comparable prevalence rates, the present study extends prior literature by stratifying symptoms by cycle regularity. Girls with irregular cycles reported meaningfully higher prevalence of back pain (55.0% vs. 45.5%), diarrhoea or constipation (12.6% vs. 6.9%), and bloating (31.5% vs. 26.2%), suggesting that irregular cycles are associated with a broader and more heterogeneous physical symptom profile.

The psychological symptoms burden was also equally striking. A Bangladeshi cross sectional study found anxiety in 24.6% and depression in 25.5% of school going adolescents [27]. The substantially higher rates in the present study for comparable symptoms particularly for the irregular group (depression or sadness: 53.2%) likely reflect both genuine population differences and methodological variation. A 2024 Psychopathology study using cross-sectional and prospective designs found that irregular-cycle women consistently reported higher depressive symptom severity even after controlling for stress, providing evidence from a more methodologically rigorous design that corroborates the directional pattern observed here [44]. The high prevalence of shame or embarrassment (47.9%) and social withdrawal (49.2%) is consistent with a systematic review of menstrual health literacy across LMICs, which documented that shame, stigma, and self-exclusion are pervasive psychological consequences of menstruation across diverse low-income settings [45]. Importantly, the high prevalence of psychological symptoms (mean 5.91 symptoms per participant) but predominantly mild-to-moderate perceived severity (52.1% mild) reveals a normalisation paradox. Participants internalised distress as an inevitable female experience rather than a health concern. This finding resonates with qualitative work from Ethiopia and rural Pakistan, where adolescent girls similarly described menstruation as a “curse” or “punishment” requiring silent endurance [46, 47]. A distinctive contribution of the present study is in quantifying this disconnect: despite 36.1% reporting depressive symptoms and 58.6% anxiety, formal help-seeking was virtually absent.

The association between menstrual irregularity and symptom severity was statistically significant and large in magnitude. Among irregular-cycle girls, 49.5% rated physical symptom severity as severe versus 3.9% among regular-cycle girls (χ² (2) = 157.81, p < 0.001), and 27.0% rated psychological severity as severe versus 3.0% (χ² (2) = 100.85, p < 0.001). These gaps substantially exceed those reported in comparable LMIC studies. A Korean population-based study of 808 adolescent girls found that the risk of irregular cycles increased with the number of mental health problems [48], while a community-based cross-sectional study among rural adolescents in India documented that girls with irregular cycles had significantly higher rates of depression (27.3%) and anxiety (37%) [49]. The present finding of 27.0% severe psychological symptoms among irregular-cycle girls is thus broadly in range but distinguishes itself by documenting severity, not merely presence of the association. A 2024 systematic review on psychological stress and menstrual irregularity reviewed 41 studies and found that 88.5% reported positive associations, supporting a bidirectional relationship between psychological health and cycle regularity [50]. The cross-sectional design of the present study cannot establish the direction of this association; both possibilities that irregularity exacerbates symptom perception, and that psychological distress disrupts cycle regularity remain plausible and require longitudinal investigation.

The finding that participants overwhelmingly avoided formal care, even when severely symptomatic, is consistent with prior LMIC research but the mechanisms identified here extend current understanding. Holmes, Curry [45] concluded that adolescents in both low- and high-income settings tend to normalise menstrual pain and seek alternative treatments rather than formal care, unless pain is extreme. A study in Odisha, India, found that approximately 60% of women experiencing menstrual symptoms did not seek any external care, with shyness and stigma cited as primary reasons [51]. This study corroborates this pattern in rural Bangladesh but adds a mechanistic layer not previously described: girls who visited pharmacies deliberately misrepresented their symptoms to conceal their menstrual origin, effectively masking the true complaint from an already undertrained provider. This masked-disclosure dynamic constitutes a qualitatively distinct barrier, one that product-distribution or general education interventions cannot address without specifically targeting menstrual communication skills. A Lancet Global Health analysis across 44 LMICs further documented that menstrual-related absenteeism from school, work, and social activities is widespread, driven by both symptom burden and cultural restrictions [52]. The exceptional case in the present study, where an educated father facilitated hospital access, illustrates how parental education can disrupt the intergenerational cycle of avoidance, consistent with evidence from LMICs that family socioeconomic and educational capital moderates girls’ health-seeking behaviour [53]. The complete absence of school-based menstrual health education and community health outreach reported by participants reflects a systemic institutional failure documented globally [54, 55] and underscores that behaviour change cannot be achieved through individual-level interventions alone. Besides, superstition-based attribution of symptoms to improper menstrual cloth disposal represents a novel finding. This belief system shifts causality away from physiological processes, thereby reducing perceived appropriateness of medical consultation. Similar alternative explanatory frameworks have been described for reproductive health symptoms in sub-Saharan Africa [56] but not previously documented for menstrual health in South Asia.

Limitations

Several limitations must be considered. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; all associations reflect co-occurrence. All outcomes were self-reported, including cycle regularity (not clinically verified) and symptom severity (three-point non-validated scale). Psychological items were non-validated, limiting comparability with studies using PHQ-9 or GAD-7 and precluding clinical interpretation. Key confounders, nutritional status, BMI, anaemia, academic workload were not measured; all associations are unadjusted. School-level clustering was not accounted for in chi-square analyses, potentially inflating statistical precision; future research should employ multilevel modelling or cluster-robust variance estimation to address this limitation appropriately. Findings are therefore exploratory, context-specific, and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Conclusion

This study provides exploratory evidence that self-reported menstrual symptom severity both physical and psychological is substantial among rural adolescent girls in Bangladesh and is disproportionately severe among those with irregular cycles. The most prevalent physical symptoms included abdominal pain, fatigue, and joint or muscle pain, while irritability, mood swings, and anxiety dominated the psychological domain. Menstrual irregularity was consistently associated with greater severity of both physical and psychological symptoms, suggesting that cycle regularity may function as an important indicator of menstrual health vulnerability in this population. Qualitative findings further revealed normalisation of menstrual discomfort, limited formal care-seeking, reliance on traditional practices, and perceived gaps in school-based and community-level support.

These findings indicate that menstrual health should be approached not solely as a reproductive concern but as an integrated adolescent health issue requiring context-specific attention. School-based health education initiatives may benefit from incorporating structured guidance on symptom recognition, stigma reduction, and appropriate care pathways. Simple screening approaches using self-reported symptom severity could assist in identifying adolescents experiencing heightened distress, particularly those who report irregular cycles. Strengthening engagement among schools, families, and community health workers may improve access to accurate information and early support. These recommendations warrant further investigation through longitudinal, multi-site research employing validated measurement instruments and adjusted analytical models before programmatic application.
