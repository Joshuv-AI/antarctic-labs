Solving Chance Constrained Programs via a Penalty based Difference of Convex Approach
Zhiping Li, Nan Jiang, Rujun Jiang

Abstract
We develop two penalty based difference of convex (DC) algorithms for solving chance constrained programs. First, leveraging a rank-based DC decomposition of the chance constraint, we propose a proximal penalty based DC algorithm in the primal space that does not require a feasible initialization. Second, to improve numerical stability in the general nonlinear settings, we derive an equivalent lifted formulation with complementary constraints and show that, after minimizing primal variables, the penalized lifted problem admits a tractable DC structure in the dual space over a simple polyhedron. We then develop a penalty based DC algorithm in the lifted space with a finite termination guarantee. We establish exact penalty and stationarity guarantees under mild constraint qualifications and identify the relationship of the local minimizers between the two formulations. Numerical experiments demonstrate the efficiency and effectiveness of our proposed methods compared with state-of-the-art benchmarks.

Introduction

As a powerful tool for addressing uncertainty in decision-making, the chance constrained program (CCP) has been extensively studied in the literature. It has been widely applied across fields such as finance, energy systems, and supply chain management
[
2
,
32
]
. In general, a chance constrained program can be written as follows:

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x}):\Pr\{g(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\xi})\leq 0\}\geq 1-\alpha\right\},

(1)

where
\tilde{\xi}
is a random vector supported on
\Xi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a prescribed violation risk level. The feasible region
\mathcal{X}
is a deterministic set contained in an open set
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the functions
f:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
,
g:\mathcal{U}\times\Xi\to\mathbb{R}
are real-valued functions. We use
\tilde{\xi}
to denote the random vector and
\xi
as its realization. The goal of Problem (
1
) is to minimize the objective function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
subject to the requirement that the constraint
g(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\xi})\leq 0
be satisfied with probability at least
(1-\alpha)
.

Problem (
1
) is generally difficult to solve, especially when the distribution information of the random variable
\tilde{\xi}
is unknown
[
1
]
. First, verifying whether a candidate solution satisfies the chance constraint can be computationally challenging. Moreover, the feasible region of a CCP is generally nonconvex even when
\mathcal{X}
is convex, implying that finding an optimal solution with a provable guarantee can be elusive.

Given the challenges mentioned above, there are two major strategies to address the CCP. The first strategy uses tractable conservative approximations, where the CCP is formulated as a convex optimization problem that can be efficiently solved and yields a feasible solution to the original CCP
[
43
,
3
,
15
]
. The second approach is the Sample Average Approximation (SAA), also sometimes known as the scenario approach, and has been extensively studied in
[
2
,
38
,
40
]
. It is a popular method to approximately solve Problem (
1
), especially when the distribution of
\tilde{\xi}
is unknown, and we can only access a sample of
S
i.i.d. realizations
\{\xi^{s}\}^{S}_{s=1}
.

In this paper, we consider the SAA approximation of Problem (
1
) as follows:

v^{*}=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x}):\frac{1}{S}\sum^{S}_{s=1}\mathbb{I}\{g(\boldsymbol{x},\xi^{s})\leq 0\}\geq 1-\alpha\right\},

(2)

where the indicator function
\mathbb{I}\{g(\boldsymbol{x},\xi^{s})\leq 0\}=1
if
g(\boldsymbol{x},\xi^{s})\leq 0
and
0
otherwise. Moreover, we focus on the convex setting and make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

The feasible region
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact, convex, and has a nonempty relative interior.

Assumption 2
.

The function
f:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable and convex. The function
g:\mathcal{U}\times\Xi\to\mathbb{R}
takes the form
g(\boldsymbol{x},\xi)=\max_{i\in[I]}h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x},\xi)
, where
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Xi\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable and convex in
\boldsymbol{x}
for every
\xi\in\Xi
and
i\in[I]
. Moreover,
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open set that contains
\mathcal{X}
.

Assumptions
1
and
2
are common in the existing literature
[
50
,
30
,
52
,
4
]
and cover several common settings, e.g., the objective function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
is linear, and the feasible region
\mathcal{X}
is a polyhedron
[
37
]
. Note that Assumptions
1
and
2
imply that
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})
is bounded on
\mathcal{X}
and thus
f
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{X}
. That is, there exists
L_{f}>0
such that

|f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})|\leq L_{f}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}.

Problem (
2
) is known as a single chance constrained program if
I=1
and a joint chance constrained program otherwise.
Other assumptions will be introduced if needed. Given a sample of
S
realizations
\{\xi^{s}\}^{S}_{s=1}
, we use
g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
to denote
g(\boldsymbol{x},\xi^{s})
and use
h_{i,s}(\boldsymbol{x})
to denote
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{x},\xi^{s})
for all
s\in[S]
and
i\in[I]
in the rest of the paper.

1.1
Relevant Literature

Chance Constrained Programs.

Chance constrained programming has a long history as a framework for incorporating uncertainty directly into decision models. Since its early development
[
11
,
12
]
, it has become a central tool in applications where decisions must remain feasible with high probability. In energy systems, for instance, chance constrained programs provide a mechanism for coping with operational randomness such as renewable generation variability, fluctuating demand profiles, and unexpected network contingencies
[
16
,
42
]
. Such models enable system operators to enforce reliability targets while acknowledging the stochastic nature of real-world power systems. Similar ideas appear in financial decision-making, where probabilistic constraints are used to balance portfolio performance against the risk generated by volatile market conditions
[
19
]
. By controlling the likelihood of unacceptable losses, these models support investment strategies that remain robust in uncertain environments. In logistics and supply chain management
[
20
,
22
]
, chance constraints play a comparable role: they allow planners to ensure service, routing, or inventory requirements are satisfied with prescribed confidence levels despite uncertain demand or transportation disruptions. We refer interested readers to
[
2
,
32
]
for a comprehensive review.

A central difficulty in working with chance constrained programs (
1
) is that the feasible region is typically nonconvex, which limits the applicability of standard convex optimization tools. To address this issue, a commonly used strategy is the sample average approximation (SAA), in which the probabilistic constraint is replaced with a finite set of scenarios. This yields a deterministic mixed-integer formulation that can be solved by modern mixed-integer optimization solvers. While this provides a viable computational framework, its practical applicability is generally limited to instances of moderate scale
[
38
,
44
]
. Another line of research focuses on constructing convex inner approximations of the original chance constraint
[
40
]
. These methods replace the probabilistic requirement with a tractable approximation that can be handled using standard convex optimization techniques. A commonly used example is the conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) reformulation, which provides a convex upper bound on the probability of violation. While this approximation preserves feasibility with respect to the chance constraint, it can be conservative and generally does not recover the optimal objective value of the original problem.
More recent developments aim to mitigate the conservatism of single-shot convex approximations by employing iterative refinement procedures
[
30
,
31
]
. The ALSO-X method is a representative example of this direction. When the deterministic feasible region
\mathcal{X}
is convex, it has been shown that ALSO-X can yield solutions that improve upon those obtained from the CVaR approximation, due to its iterative updates.

Building on ALSO-X, an enhanced variant, ALSO-X+, has been proposed to improve solution quality further. In numerical experiments, ALSO-X+ has been observed to achieve better objective values than ALSO-X and CVaR approximation; see, for example,
[
54
,
51
,
46
]
. At the same time, these works also document that the computational time required by ALSO-X+ is substantially higher than that of ALSO-X and the CVaR approximation, especially on larger instances. A further limitation is that the theoretical properties of the solutions produced by ALSO-X+ are not fully understood. In particular, current analyses in
[
30
]
do not establish convergence to a stationary point of the underlying chance constrained problem. Hence, the quality of the limiting solutions lacks a rigorous optimality guarantee.

Difference of Convex (DC) Program.

DC programs are optimization problems that aim to minimize a DC function subject to DC constraints. DC programs play an important role in nonconvex optimization and have been extensively studied for decades
[
28
,
47
]
. One of the most important methods for solving DC programs is the DC algorithm (DCA)
[
27
,
36
]
, which solves a sequence of convex subproblems by replacing the concave part with its first-order approximation at the current iterate. Recently,
[
50
]
proposed a DCA to solve the SAA of chance constrained programs, with subproblems that are easy to implement and are amenable to the off-the-shelf solvers. Another popular method for solving the DC program is exact penalty
[
35
,
23
,
29
]
, which penalizes the DC constraint in the objective function and solves a sequence of DC programs using DCA with increasing penalty parameters. Compared with the DCA, the exact penalty method does not require an initial feasible solution. It allows constraints to be violated in the initial updates, which can sometimes lead to a lower objective value
[
35
]
.

1.2
Summary of Contributions

In this paper, we study the penalty based DC algorithms for solving chance constrained programs. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

(i)

Building on the rank-based DC decomposition of the SAA chance constraint proposed in
[
50
]
, we first propose a penalty based DC method in the primal space. The method does not require an initial feasible solution and can alleviate over-conservatism caused by infeasible subproblems. This algorithm improves both solution quality and running time in the polyhedral setting, where both
\mathcal{X}
and
g
are polyhedral, but exhibits pronounced oscillations in the nonlinear setting.

(ii)

To mitigate the aforementioned oscillations that may arise in the primal space under nonlinear settings, we derive an equivalent lifted formulation with complementary constraints, inspired by the Toland duality framework for DC programs
[
48
]
. This leads to a tractable DC structure over a simple polyhedron and yields a proximal DC algorithm in the lifted space. The subproblem admits finite termination, is amenable to effective warm starts, and exhibits substantially improved numerical stability.

(iii)

We establish an exact penalty for both the primal and lifted formulations under mild constraint qualification assumptions, including global exactness, local exactness for the primal formulation, and equivalence between strongly stationary points of the lifted formulation and stationary points of the penalized counterpart. We further analyze the relationship between lifted and primal local minimizers, identify the possibility of spurious lifted local minimizers, and provide a sufficient condition under which such spurious solutions are ruled out.

(iv)

Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed methods substantially reduce over-conservatism. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, the algorithm in the lifted space remains computationally efficient and outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in both solution quality and runtime.

Organization.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we develop the algorithmic framework: we first review the DC decomposition of the chance constraint and the exact penalty framework in the primal space. Then we derive an equivalent lifted formulation with complementary constraints and the algorithm in the lifted space. Section
3
establishes the exact penalty and stationarity guarantees for both formulations, including global and local results. Section
4
investigates the relationship between local minimizers of the lifted and primal formulations, characterizes when spurious lifted local minimizers may arise, and provides conditions ensuring that lifted local optimality implies primal local optimality. Section
5
reports numerical experiments on real and synthetic instances and compares against state-of-the-art baselines. In Section
6
, we conclude the paper and provide some future directions.

2
Algorithm Frameworks

In this section, we present the overall algorithmic foundations of our penalty based approach for solving chance constrained program (
2
). We first review the DC reformulation of the SAA chance constraint in the primal space and discuss why the exact penalty in the primal space can be fragile in practice, especially in the nonlinear settings. Motivated by these limitations and inspired by the general Toland duality of DC programs
[
48
]
, we propose an alternative exact penalty approach and then derive an equivalent lifted formulation, which is a mathematical program with complementarity constraints (MPCC), and show that the resulting value-function representation yields a tractable DC structure. This leads to a polyhedral DC program with a finite convergence guarantee and is amenable to effective warm starts.

2.1
Algorithm in the Primal Space

To obtain a tractable reformulation of Problem (
2
),
[
50
]
recently proposed a difference of convex based reformulation of Problem (
2
) along with a difference of convex based algorithm (DCA). The algorithm is easy to implement, with subproblems that can be solved directly by state-of-the-art solvers, and is especially efficient in the polyhedral setting. We begin this section by briefly reviewing its key elements. Let
m:=\lfloor\alpha S\rfloor
and let
g_{(1)}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\cdots\leq g_{(S)}(\boldsymbol{x})
denote the nondecreasing rearrangement of the vector
(g_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\dots,g_{S}(\boldsymbol{x}))
. Define

G_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{s=S-m}^{S}g_{(s)}(\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{s=S-m+1}^{S}g_{(s)}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(8)

The following equivalence is standard, and we include it for completeness.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
50
, lemma 1]

For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, the sample based chance constraint

\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathbb{I}\{g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0\}\;\geq\;S-m

(9)

holds if and only if
G_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})-G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0
.

By Proposition
2.1
, Problem (
2
) is equivalent to

v^{*}=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x}):\boldsymbol{x}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}\right\},

(10)

where the feasible region
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
of (
10
) is defined as follows:

\hat{\mathcal{X}}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}:\phi(\boldsymbol{x}):=G_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})-G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0\}.

We next summarize several properties of the functions
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
established in
[
50
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

[
50

Under Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
, both
G_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})
defined in (
8
) are convex and continuous on
\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, let
\mathcal{M}_{G_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x})
denote the active index set of
G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})
at
\boldsymbol{x}
by

\mathcal{M}_{G_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x})\;:=\;\Bigl\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{m})\subseteq[S]:\ \sum_{t=1}^{m}g_{s_{t}}(\boldsymbol{x})=G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\Bigr\}.

Then for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, the subdifferential of
G_{2}
admits the explicit representation

\partial G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\;=\;\mathrm{conv}\left\{\bigcup_{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{m})\in\mathcal{M}_{G_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x})}\ \sum_{t=1}^{m}\partial g_{s_{t}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\},

where for all
s\in[S]

\partial g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\;=\;\mathrm{conv}\Bigl\{\nabla h_{s,i}(\boldsymbol{x}):\ i\in\mathcal{M}_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\Bigr\},

and
\mathcal{M}_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
denotes the active index set of

\boldsymbol{x}
by

\mathcal{M}_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\;:=\;\left\{i\in[I]:\ h_{s,i}(\boldsymbol{x})=g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\}.

We make the following remarks on Proposition
2.2
.

(i)

The sample based chance constraint (
9
) is equivalent to an empirical value-at-risk (VaR) constraint.
The requirement that at least
(S-m)
scenarios satisfy
g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0
holds if and only if

g_{(S-m)}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0,

i.e., the empirical
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of
\{g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\}_{s=1}^{S}
is nonpositive.

(ii)

Similarly, both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
can be viewed as the empirical Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)
[
43
]
up to a scaling factor, and each admits an equivalent minimization formulation based on strong duality of linear programs, which will be useful in the following section:

G_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})=\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{(m+1)\alpha+\sum^{S}_{s=1}[g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})-\alpha]_{+}\right\},\quad G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{m\beta+\sum^{S}_{s=1}[g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})-\beta]_{+}\right\}.

(iii)

Under Assumption
1
–
2
,
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are convex and continuous on the compact set
\mathcal{X}
. Therefore,
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
and their difference
\phi(\boldsymbol{x})
are Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{X}
and their Clarke subdifferentials are well-defined
[
17
, section 2.1]
.

The authors in
[
50
]
proposed a difference of convex based algorithm (DCA) for solving Problem (
10
). At the
k
th iteration, let
\boldsymbol{x}^{k}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}
. We pick a subgradient
\boldsymbol{n}^{k}\in\partial G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})
, linearize the concave part in the constraint, and solve the following subproblem as the update:

\boldsymbol{x}^{k+1}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\{f(\boldsymbol{x}):G_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})-G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})-\langle\boldsymbol{n}^{k},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\leq 0\}.

(11)

We note that the above DCA scheme has several intrinsic limitations despite its strong numerical performance:

(i)

DCA can be viewed as a conservative approximation of Problem (
2
). The affine majorization of the concave part can sometimes be overly conservative, so the subproblem (
11
) may become infeasible. A similar problem may also arise in other conservative approximation techniques, e.g., the CVaR approximation
[
43
]
.

(ii)

The DCA requires an initial feasible point (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}
) to linearize the constraint, and finding such a point can be computationally demanding.

(iii)

Problem (
10
) is a polyhedral DC program when
h_{i,s}
are affine for all
i\in[I]
and
s\in[S]
. While DCA can be computationally efficient in the polyhedral setting due to the finite convergence guarantee, it often performs poorly with nonlinear constraints due to pronounced oscillations and slow progress (see
[
50
]
and the numerical experiments in Section
5
for details).

To address the first two limitations, we propose an exact penalty based approach for the DC program (
10
), which has been extensively studied in the literature
[
35
,
33
]
and does not require an initial feasible solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}
. Let
\sigma>0
be the penalty parameter, and we consider the following penalized problem:

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\ \bigl\{f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma[\phi(\boldsymbol{x})]_{+}\bigr\}.

(12)

It is well-known (
[
28
, proposition 2.1]
) that the
\ell_{1}
exact penalty of a DC function still admits an explicit DC decomposition. Therefore, Problem (
12
) admits a DC decomposition as follows:

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in X}\quad f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma\max\{G_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\}-\sigma G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(13)

For a fixed
\sigma>0
, we may also adopt a proximal DC algorithm to solve Problem (
13
). Similar to the DCA, for the
k
th iteration, we can pick a subgradient
\boldsymbol{n}^{k}\in\partial G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})
by Proposition
2.2
. We then need to solve the following subproblem:

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in X}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma\max\{G_{1}(x),G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\}-\sigma\langle\boldsymbol{n}^{k},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\right\}.

(14)

We remark that the subproblem (
14
) is feasible under the mild assumption that
\mathcal{X}
is nonempty. To stabilize the iterates, we can also add a proximal term with
\rho>0
. By introducing an auxiliary epigraph variable
t\in\mathbb{R}
and leveraging the dual representation of CVaR, we can obtain an equivalent formulation as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\ t,\ \eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ \boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{S},\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{S}}

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma t-\sigma\langle\boldsymbol{n}^{k},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{k}\|^{2}

(15)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle t\ \geq\ (m+1)\eta_{1}+\sum_{s=1}^{S}u_{s},\quad u_{s}\ \geq\ g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})-\eta_{1},\quad\forall s\in[S],

\displaystyle t\ \geq\ m\,\eta_{2}+\sum_{s=1}^{S}v_{s},\quad v_{s}\ \geq\ g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})-\eta_{2},\quad\forall s\in[S].

We now present the full penalty framework for Problem (
13
) in Algorithm
1
, which is a double-loop algorithm. The inner loop solves the DC program (
13
) via update (
14
) to a critical point, and the outer loop updates the parameter
\sigma
by enlarging it with a constant factor
\beta>0
. Following
[
41
, Chapter 17]
, we initialize the algorithm with a small penalty
\sigma^{0}
and then increase
\sigma
progressively, so that the violation of the chance constraint (equivalently,
[\phi(\boldsymbol{x})]_{+}
) is gradually driven to zero.

Similar to the standard analysis of DC program
[
28
]
, the sequence generated by Algorithm

subsequently converges to a critical point of Problem (
13

H(\boldsymbol{x})=\max\{G_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\}
. We say
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
is a critical point of Problem (
13
) if there exists
\boldsymbol{n}^{*}\in\partial G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}),\boldsymbol{h}^{*}\in\partial H(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})
such that the following condition holds:

\boldsymbol{0}\in\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\ +\ \sigma\,\boldsymbol{h}^{*}\ -\ \sigma\,\boldsymbol{n}^{*}\ +\ \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}).

We conclude this subsection with some remarks on Algorithm
1
.

(i)

Algorithm
1
admits effective warm starts. Specifically, the outer penalty loop can reuse the output solution of the previous penalty level as the initialization for the next penalty level, which often accelerates convergence in practice. In addition, when
\sigma
is small, and constraint enforcement is mild, it is usually unnecessary to solve the proximal DC subproblem with fixed-
\sigma
to high precision. Instead, one may adopt a loose inner stopping tolerance at early penalty stages and gradually tighten it as
\sigma
increases, thereby reducing the overall runtime.

(ii)

In our experiments, we set
\rho
to a small constant (
10^{-4}-10^{-3}
) to improve numerical stability and mitigate oscillations, which are observed in the numerical experiments when
\rho=0
.

(iii)

The primal-space framework extends directly to the case where the objective itself admits a DC decomposition, namely
f(\boldsymbol{x})=f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})-f_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})
with
f_{1},f_{2}
being convex. In this case, the penalized objective can be written as

\bigl(f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma H(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)\ -\ \bigl(f_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr),

which is still a DC function on
\mathcal{X}
. A similar DCA algorithm can also be applied by solving a sequence of subproblems where the concave part
(f_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma G_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}))
is linearized using first-order approximation at the current iterate.

Similar to the DCA algorithm in
[
50
]
, Algorithm
1
is particularly efficient when applied to a polyhedral DC program, which has the property of finite convergence under the mild assumption
[
47
, theorem 6]
. A sufficient condition for such property is that
\mathcal{X}
is a polyhedron and
f
,
h_{i,s}
are affine for all
i,s
. In the numerical experiments (see Section
5
), we observe that Algorithm
1
yields a better solution with a lower objective value than the DCA algorithm in the polyhedral setting. The running time of both algorithms is comparable in the setting with a linear objective function. At the same time, Algorithm
1
exhibits substantial speedup over the DCA algorithm when the objective function is quadratic.

In contrast, when the constraints are nonlinear, Algorithm
1
may exhibit pronounced oscillations, similar to the DCA algorithm, and require substantially more iterations and runtime to solve a single subproblem, degrading its practical performance. Moreover, Algorithm
1
fails to return a feasible solution within a reasonable runtime even though we use a larger initial penalty
\sigma^{0}
and a more aggressive growth factor
\beta
. This phenomenon motivates us to propose an alternative exact penalty approach with better numerical stability in the general setting.

2.2
Equivalent Formulation in the Lifted Space

Motivated by the limitations discussed above, we next consider equivalent formulations of Problem (
2
), which will serve as the basis for our subsequent algorithmic development. Observe that the chance constraint (
9
) admits an equivalent reformulation
[
55
]
as follows:

\sum^{S}_{s=1}\mathbb{I}\{g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0\}\geq S-m\Longleftrightarrow\sum^{S}_{s=1}\mathbb{I}\{[g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})]_{+}>0\}\leq m.

Introducing an auxiliary variable
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}_{+}
, we obtain an equivalent reformulation of Problem (
2
) with a cardinality constraint:

v^{*}=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{y}\geq\boldsymbol{0}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x}):\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{0}\leq m,\;g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\;\forall s\in[S]\right\},

where the cardinality constraint
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{0}\leq m
enforces that the number of violated scenarios cannot exceed
m
.

It is well-known in the literature that the
\ell_{0}
-norm admits a DC decomposition
[
18
,
23
]
, that is

\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{0}\leq m\Longleftrightarrow\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{1}-\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{(m)}=0,

where
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{(m)}
denotes the Ky-Fan norm of
\boldsymbol{y}
. In
[
23
]
, the authors considered a penalty approach based on the DC decomposition of the cardinality constraint. In our setting, since
\boldsymbol{y}\geq\boldsymbol{0}
, the penalized problem can be simplified as follows:

v^{*}=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{y}\geq\boldsymbol{0}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma(\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\mathrm{T}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y})):\;g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\;\forall s\in[S]\right\},

(16)

where
\mathrm{T}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y})=\max_{\boldsymbol{u}}\{\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle:\boldsymbol{0}\leq\boldsymbol{u}\leq\boldsymbol{1},\;\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}\leq m\}
denotes the sum of the
m
-largest elements of
\boldsymbol{y}
. To obtain a simplified reformulation, define the convex polyhedron

\mathcal{C}\ :=\ \Bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}:\ 0\leq z_{s}\leq 1\ \forall s\in[S],\ \boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}\geq S-m\Bigr\}.

We can now obtain an equivalent reformulation of Problem (
16
) using the support function
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
of the polyhedron
\mathcal{C}
as follows:

v^{*}=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{y}\geq\boldsymbol{0}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x})-\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(-\sigma\boldsymbol{y}):\;g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\;\forall s\in[S]\right\},

(17)

where
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{y})=\max_{\boldsymbol{z}}\{\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}:\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}\}
.

A key observation is that Problem (
17
) is a polyhedral DC program since the nonsmooth concave part in the objective function
-\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(-\sigma\boldsymbol{y})
is polyhedral. Moreover, its Fenchel conjugate takes a simple form: the indicator function of the same polyhedron composed with a linear mapping. Therefore, a natural idea is to lift this problem into the dual space using the Toland duality
[
48
,
28
,
5
]
for DC programs, which can transfer the nonsmoothness in the objective function to simple polyhedral constraints. Compared with the primal problem (
17
), the dual problem takes the form of minimizing a concave function on a polyhedron, for which the standard DC algorithm guarantees finite convergence. This simple structure can potentially improve numerical stability regardless of the nonlinearity of the constraints and objective. We formally state this in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
–
2
hold.
Consider Problem (
17
) in the unconstrained form

\min_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{S}}\Bigl\{\zeta(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})-\eta(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\Bigr\},

where

\displaystyle\zeta(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=f(\boldsymbol{x})+\delta_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\delta_{\mathbb{R}^{S}_{+}}(\boldsymbol{y})+\delta_{\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):\,g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\ \forall s\in[S]\}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),\quad\eta(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(-\sigma\boldsymbol{y}).

Let
(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{S}
denote the dual variables associated with
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
. Then the Fenchel conjugates of
\eta
and
\zeta
satisfy:

(i)

\eta^{*}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\delta_{\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}(\boldsymbol{p})+\inf_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}}\left\{\delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{z}):-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{q}\right\}.

(ii)

\zeta^{*}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\ \boldsymbol{y}\geq\boldsymbol{0}\\
g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\ \forall s\in[S]\end{subarray}}\Bigl\{\langle\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle-f(\boldsymbol{x})\Bigr\}.

Consequently, by parameterizing the dual variable of the support function directly by
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}
, the Toland dual of Problem (
17
) can be written as

\min_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}}\ \Bigl(-\zeta^{*}(\boldsymbol{0},-\sigma\boldsymbol{z})\Bigr)=\min_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}}\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\ \boldsymbol{y}\geq\boldsymbol{0}\\
g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\ \forall s\in[S]\end{subarray}}\Bigl\{f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}\Bigr\}.

(18)

Proof.

We derive the two Fenchel conjugates separately. The Toland dual problem (
18
) can then be derived via straightforward calculation.

(i): Computing
\eta^{*}
.

Since
\eta
does not depend on
\boldsymbol{x}
, its conjugate separates as

\eta^{*}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle+\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}}\Bigl\{\langle\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle-\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(-\sigma\boldsymbol{y})\Bigr\}.

The first term equals
\delta_{\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}(\boldsymbol{p})
.
For the second term, write
h(\boldsymbol{u})=\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{u})
, so that

\eta(\boldsymbol{y})=(h\circ A)(\boldsymbol{y}),\qquad A:=-\sigma I.

Since
h
is the support function of
\mathcal{C}
, we have

h^{*}(\boldsymbol{z})=\delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{z}).

By the conjugacy rule for composition with a linear mapping,

(h\circ A)^{*}(\boldsymbol{q})=\inf_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}}\Bigl\{h^{*}(\boldsymbol{z}):A^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{q}\Bigr\}.

Because
A^{\top}=-\sigma I
, it follows that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}}\Bigl\{\langle\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle-\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(-\sigma\boldsymbol{y})\Bigr\}=\inf_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}}\left\{\delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{z}):-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{q}\right\}.

Therefore

\eta^{*}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\delta_{\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}(\boldsymbol{p})+\inf_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}}\left\{\delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{z}):-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{q}\right\},

which proves part (i).

(ii): Computing
\zeta^{*}
.

By definition,

\zeta^{*}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\sup_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{S}}\Bigl\{\langle\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle-\zeta(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\Bigr\}.

Substituting the definition of
\zeta
yields

\zeta^{*}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\ \boldsymbol{y}\geq\boldsymbol{0}\\
g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\ \forall s\in[S]\end{subarray}}\Bigl\{\langle\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle-f(\boldsymbol{x})\Bigr\},

which proves part (ii).
∎

We provide an alternative approach to deriving the same formulation (
18
) based on integer programming techniques, as discussed in the literature. Starting from (
2
), we introduce a binary vector
\boldsymbol{z}\in\{0,1\}^{S}
to represent the indicator functions.
To obtain a lifted (higher-dimensional) reformulation, we can introduce another nonnegative vector
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}_{+}
to indicate constraint violation instead of using the big-M formulation. A similar approach has been proposed in
[
30
]
. To make the paper self-contained, we state the result formally in the following proposition without proof.

Proposition 2.4
.

[
30
, proposition 1]

Problem (
2
) can be viewed as the following equivalent formulation:

v^{*}=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}_{+},\boldsymbol{z}\in[0,1]^{S}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x}):\sum^{S}_{s=1}z_{s}\geq S-m,\;V(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})=0,\;g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\;\forall s\in[S]\right\},

(19)

where
V(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})=\sum_{s=1}^{S}y_{s}z_{s}
.

We remark that the variable
\boldsymbol{z}
in formulation (
19
) can be either continuous or discrete. From this perspective, Problem (
19
) can be viewed as a special case of a mathematical program with complementarity constraint (MPCC)
[
34
,
45
]
. In
[
30
]
, the authors proposed an algorithm named ALSO-X+ to solve Problem (
19
) using alternating minimization with an iterative procedure. The algorithm can outperform the CVaR approximation
[
43
]
under Assumptions
1
and
2
, and exhibits applicability in various settings. However, this algorithm lacks a stationarity guarantee and is inefficient due to the iterative procedure.

Motivated by these limitations, we instead study the following penalized formulation of Problem (
19
), which is equivalent to the Toland dual problem (
18
):

v^{*}_{\sigma}=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}_{+},\boldsymbol{z}\in[0,1]^{S}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma V(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}):\sum^{S}_{s=1}z_{s}\geq S-m,\;g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\;\forall s\in[S]\right\},

(20)

where
\sigma>0
is the penalty parameter. Note that Problem (
20
) is the
\ell_{1}
exact penalty formulation
[
24
]
of (
19
). In particular, the term
V(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})
can be used directly as an exact penalty function without any positive-part truncation since
y_{s}\geq 0
and
z_{s}\geq 0
imply
y_{s}z_{s}\geq 0
for all
s\in[S]
.

With this equivalence in hand, we take (
20
) as the starting point of our analysis since the penalty term only appears once in the objective function. In particular, we propose an algorithmic framework that simultaneously addresses the limitations of both Algorithm
1
and ALSO-X+.

2.3
Algorithm in the Lifted Space

In this subsection, we develop an alternative exact penalty framework for solving the lifted MPCC formulation (
19
) through its penalized counterpart (
20
). For any fixed penalty parameter
\sigma>0
, the penalized model (
20
) is still nonconvex because of the bilinear term
\sum_{s=1}^{S}y_{s}z_{s}
appearing in the objective. Nevertheless, from Proposition
2.3
, we observe that once the remaining variables
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
are minimized out, this nonconvexity induces a concave minimization structure in the dual variable
\boldsymbol{z}
.

Before proceeding, we introduce several pieces of notation used throughout the remainder of this paper.
Using the scenario-wise representation
g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})=\max_{i\in[I]}h_{s,i}(\boldsymbol{x})
, we write the feasible region of Problem (
20
) as

\Omega_{0}\ :=\ \Bigl\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{S}\times\mathcal{C}:\ h_{s,i}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\ \forall s\in[S],\ \forall i\in[I]\Bigr\}.

The objective function of Problem (
20
) is denoted by

F_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})=f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma V(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}).

Similarly, the feasible region of Problem (
19
) is

\Omega\ :=\ \Bigl\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})\in\Omega_{0}:\ y_{s}z_{s}=0,\ \forall s\in[S]\Bigr\}.

We next isolate the dependence on
\boldsymbol{z}
by introducing the following value function.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{S}\times\mathbb{R}_{++}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by

\Psi(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma):=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}_{+}}\left\{-f(\boldsymbol{x})-\sigma\sum_{s=1}^{S}y_{s}z_{s}:\ g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\ \forall s\in[S]\right\}.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, the function
\Psi(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma)
is convex in
\boldsymbol{z}
for every fixed
\sigma>0
.

Proof.

For each fixed feasible pair
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
,
-F_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})
is affine in
\boldsymbol{z}
.
By definition,
\Psi(\cdot,\sigma)
is the pointwise supremum of
-F_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})
on
\Omega_{0}
. Thus
\Psi(\cdot,\sigma)
is convex.
∎

We remark here that the convexity of
\Psi
in
\boldsymbol{z}
does not depend on the convexity of the function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
or of
\mathcal{X}
as long as the maximum is attained. Therefore, the following analysis can be naturally extended to the discrete setting, e.g., when
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}^{d}
. With this notation, we can rewrite the penalized problem (
20
) as an optimization problem in
\boldsymbol{z}
only. Indeed, for any fixed
\boldsymbol{z}
, the inner minimization over
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
in (
20
) equals
-\Psi(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma)
, and hence Problem (
20
) is equivalent to the following DC program

\min_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}}\ -\;\Psi(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma).

(21)

To implement a proximal DC method for (
21
), we need a computable subgradient of
\Psi(\cdot,\sigma)
to linearize the concave part. This can be done by Danskin’s theorem, where we can restrict the variable
\boldsymbol{y}
to a compact set under Assumptions
1
and
2
without loss of generality.

Proposition 2.6
.

[
7
, proposition B.25]

For any
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\sigma>0
, let
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma),\boldsymbol{y}^{*}(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma))
be an optimal solution to the following subproblem:

(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma),\boldsymbol{y}^{*}(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma))\in\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}_{+}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma\sum_{s=1}^{S}y_{s}z_{s}:g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq y_{s},\ \forall s\in[S]\right\}.

(22)

Define

\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma):=-\sigma\bigl(y_{1}^{*}(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma),\dots,y_{S}^{*}(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma)\bigr)^{\top}.

Then
\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma)\in\partial_{\boldsymbol{z}}\Psi(\boldsymbol{z},\sigma)
.

We now apply a DC algorithm to solve the DC program (
21
) with fixed
\sigma>0
. Given the current iterate
\boldsymbol{z}^{k}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\sigma>0
, we first choose a subgradient
\boldsymbol{n}^{k}\in\partial_{\boldsymbol{z}}\Psi(\boldsymbol{z}^{k},\sigma)
,
which can be computed via Proposition
2.6
. Linearizing the concave term
-\Psi(\cdot,\sigma)
at
\boldsymbol{z}^{k}
and adding a proximal regularizer yields the convex subproblem

\boldsymbol{z}^{k+1}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{-\langle\boldsymbol{n}^{k},\boldsymbol{z}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{z}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{z}^{k}-\frac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{n}^{k}),

where
\rho>0
is a proximal parameter to enhance numerical stability and avoid potential oscillation. Hence, each proximal DC update in the
\boldsymbol{z}
-space is reduced to a projection onto the simple polyhedron
\mathcal{C}
. This projection can be computed efficiently via sorting and scales well for large
S
.

The complete implementation of the penalty method is summarized in Algorithm
2
. Similar to Algorithm
1
, we start with a relatively small penalty parameter
\sigma^{0}
and increase it geometrically. The penalty update continues until the violation of the chance constraint becomes sufficiently small, and further increasing
\sigma
will have little effect. Moreover, since the objective function in (
20
) is differentiable, the sequence generated by Algorithm
2
converges subsequentially to a stationary point of Problem (
20
), which can be defined using the standard first-order stationarity condition. Let
\boldsymbol{w}:=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})
. We say
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\Omega_{0}

3
Exact Penalty Frameworks

In this section, we aim to develop an exact penalty framework for both algorithms proposed in Section
2
. To this end, we provide two complementary theoretical guarantees. First, under a constraint qualification, we show that the proposed penalty for both the primal and lifted formulation is globally exact, in the sense that all global minimizers of the original problem can be recovered by solving the penalized problem with a sufficiently large penalty parameter. Second, we focus on the local exactness. For the primal formulation, we prove the local exactness of the penalty and provide sufficient conditions to verify local optimality. For the lifted problem, instead of pursuing local exactness in terms of local minimizers, which typically requires strong regularity conditions (e.g., strict complementary slackness), we establish an equivalence between strongly stationary points of the original and the stationary points of the penalized formulation under a MPCC-tailored constraint qualification. This equivalence implies local optimality in the lifted space and serves as a theoretical guarantee for the developed algorithm.

3.3
Equivalence between (Strongly) Stationary Points of the Lifted Formulation

In this section, we investigate the local connection between the bilinear model Problem (
19
) and its smooth penalized counterpart Problem (
20
).
A well-known subtlety in MPCC is that even when the penalty parameter is sufficiently large, the set of local minimizers of an MPCC generally does not admit a one-to-one correspondence with that of its penalty counterpart
[
45
,
21
,
29
]
.

Nevertheless, any accumulation point of the sequence generated by Algorithm
2
is a stationary point of Problem (
20
). Therefore, instead of seeking a direct equivalence between local minimizers, we adopt a more practical perspective by relating stationary points of the smooth penalized problem (
20
) to a suitable notion of generalized stationarity for Problem (
19
).

We now introduce the definition of strong stationarity for Problem (
19
). Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}=(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
be given. Define the index sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{y}(\bar{\boldsymbol{w}})

\displaystyle:=\{s\in[S]:\bar{y}_{s}=0,\bar{z}_{s}>0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{z}(\bar{\boldsymbol{w}})

\displaystyle:=\{s\in[S]:\bar{z}_{s}=0,\bar{y}_{s}>0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}(\bar{\boldsymbol{w}})

\displaystyle:=\{s\in[S]:\bar{y}_{s}=\bar{z}_{s}=0\}.

Using these index sets, we define the
relaxed program
at
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}
by fixing the active-side variables to maintain the same complementarity pattern.

\min_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega_{0}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{x}):y_{s}=0,\forall s\in\mathcal{I}_{y}(\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}),\;z_{s}=0,\forall s\in\mathcal{I}_{z}(\bar{\boldsymbol{w}})\right\}.

(28)

Definition 3.6

(Strong stationarity
[
29
]
)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, a point
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\Omega
is called a
strongly stationary point
of Problem (
19
) if it is an optimal solution to the convex relaxed program (
28
).

Denote the feasible region of Problem (
28
) by

\Omega_{\mathrm{re}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{w}})\ :=\ \Bigl\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})\in\Omega_{0}:\ y_{s}=0\ \forall s\in\mathcal{I}_{y}(\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}),\ z_{s}=0\ \forall s\in\mathcal{I}_{z}(\bar{\boldsymbol{w}})\Bigr\}.

Since (
28
) is a convex program,
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}
solves it if and only if

\boldsymbol{0}\ \in\ \nabla f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\times\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\times\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\ +\ \mathcal{N}_{\Omega_{\mathrm{re}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{w}})}(\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}).

(29)

The next theorem shows that every strongly stationary point is a local minimizer of Problem (
19
), which is known in the MPCC literature.

Theorem 3.7
.

[
29
, proposition 1]

Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}=(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
be a strongly stationary point
of Problem (
19
). Then
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}
is a local minimizer of Problem (
19
).

We next establish the connection between stationary points of the penalized problem (
20
) and strongly stationary points of (
19
). For this purpose, we adopt a standard constraint qualification tailored to MPCC models.

Definition 3.8

(MPCC-MFCQ
[
26
,
29
]
)
.

Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\Omega
be given. We say that
MPCC-MFCQ holds at
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}
if the classical MFCQ holds at
\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}
for the convex program (
28
).

Finally with this constraint qualification at hand, we are able to establish the equivalence between the stationary points of the two problems.

Theorem 3.9
.

[
29
, proposition 6]

Let
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
be given. Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.

(a)

If
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
is a stationary point for Problem (
20
) for some
\sigma>0
and is feasible for Problem (
19
), then it is a strongly stationary point of Problem (
19
).

(b)

If
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
is a strongly stationary point for Problem (
19
) and the MPCC-MFCQ holds, then it is a stationary point of Problem (
20
) for all
\sigma
sufficiently large.

4
Identifying Spurious Local Minimizers

In the previous section, we have established the local exact penalty for the primal formulation and the fact that strong stationarity for the lifted formulation (
19
) implies local optimality in the lifted space. A natural question is whether such a local minimizer of the lifted formulation (
19
) also yields a meaningful local optimality guarantee for the primal formulation (
10
) in the
\boldsymbol{x}
-space. More precisely, given a local minimizer
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})
of (
19
), whether the projection
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a local minimizer of the DC formulation (
10
) remains unclear.

Our aim in this section is to answer this question. We show that such an implication from the
\boldsymbol{x}
-space to the lifted space always holds: every local minimizer

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
of (
10
) can be lifted to a local minimizer of (
19
). However, the reverse does not hold in general: (
19
) may admit spurious local minimizers whose
\boldsymbol{x}
-components are not locally optimal for (
10
). We then identify a mild regularity condition under which the reverse statement holds.

Define
\psi_{s}(\boldsymbol{x}):=[g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})]_{+}
for all
s\in[S]
and the set-valued mapping

Z(\boldsymbol{x})=\arg\min\left\{\sum^{S}_{s=1}\psi_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})z_{s}:\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}\right\}.

Since
\mathcal{C}
is nonempty, compact, and convex and
(\psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\ldots,\psi_{S}(\boldsymbol{x}))
is continuous in
\boldsymbol{x}
,
Berge’s maximum theorem
[
6
]
implies that
Z(\boldsymbol{x})
is upper semicontinuous. We first show that every local minimum of the
DC formulation (
10
) yields a local minimum of the lifted formulation (
19
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}
be a local minimizer of Problem (
10
). Then there exists

\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}_{+}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathcal{C}
such that

(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
is a local minimizer of Problem (
19
).

Proof.

Since
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}
, Proposition
2.1
implies that there exists an index set
\bar{J}\subseteq[S]
with
|\bar{J}|=S-m
such that
g_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq 0
for all
s\in\bar{J}
.
Define
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathcal{C}
by
\bar{z}_{s}=1
if
s\in\bar{J}
and
\bar{z}_{s}:=0
otherwise.
Define
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}_{+}
by
\bar{y}_{s}=0
if
s\in\bar{J}
and
\bar{y}_{s}:=[g_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})]_{+}
otherwise.
Then
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}\geq 0
,
g_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq\bar{y}_{s}
for all
s
, and
\sum_{s=1}^{S}\bar{y}_{s}\bar{z}_{s}=0
, hence

(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be such that
f(\boldsymbol{x})\geq f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}\cap B(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\varepsilon)
.
Take any
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})\in\Omega
with

\|(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})-(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\|<\varepsilon
. Therefore, we have
f(\boldsymbol{x})\geq f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
for every
\|(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})-(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\|<\varepsilon
and
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})\in\Omega
since Problem (
10
) and (
19
) have the same objective function.
∎

However, the inverse of Theorem
4.1
does not hold without additional assumptions. This can be demonstrated using the following one-dimensional example.

Example 4.2
.

Consider the one-dimensional setting with
S=2
,
m=1
,
\mathcal{X}=[-1,+\infty)
. Let
f(x)=x
, the sample based constraints
g_{1}(x)=[x]_{+}
and
g_{2}(x)=[-x]_{+}
. Since
g_{s}(x)\geq 0
, we have

G_{1}(x)-G_{2}(x)=\min\{[x]_{+},[-x]_{+}\}=0,\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

Therefore, the DC formulation (
10
) reduces to
\min\{x:x\geq-1\}
, whose unique global minimizer is
x=-1
.

We now show that
x=0
can nevertheless appear as the
\boldsymbol{x}
-component of a local minimizer of the lifted formulation, which can be written as follows

\min_{x,\boldsymbol{y}\geq\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{x:g_{1}(x)\leq y_{1},g_{2}(x)\leq y_{2},\;y_{1}z_{1}+y_{2}z_{2}=0\right\}.

Take
\bar{x}=0
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}=(0,0)
, and
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}=(1,1)
. Then
(\bar{x},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
since
g_{1}(0)=g_{2}(0)=0
,
\sum_{s=1}^{2}\bar{z}_{s}=2\geq S-m=1
, and
\sum_{s=1}^{2}\bar{y}_{s}\bar{z}_{s}=0
. Moreover, there exists
r\in(0,1/4)
such that for any feasible
(x,\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})\in\Omega
with
\|(x,\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})-(\bar{x},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\|<r
, we have
z_{1}>1/2
and
z_{2}>1/2
, which together with
\boldsymbol{y}\geq 0
and
\sum_{s=1}^{2}y_{s}z_{s}=0
implies
y_{1}=y_{2}=0
. Hence feasibility forces
g_{1}(x)\leq 0
and
g_{2}(x)\leq 0
, i.e.,
[x]_{+}=[-x]_{+}=0
, so necessarily
x=0
. Consequently, every feasible point in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
(\bar{x},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})
has the same objective value
f(x)=0
, showing that
(\bar{x},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})
is a local minimizer of (
19
) while
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=0
is not a
local minimizer of (
10
).

In the above example,
\mathcal{C}=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}:z_{1}+z_{2}\geq S-m=1\}
and one can verify that

Z(x)=\begin{cases}\{(1,0)\},&x<0,\\
\mathcal{C},&x=0,\\
\{(0,1)\},&x>0.\end{cases}

Example
4.2
arises because, at
\bar{x}=0
, the set-valued mapping
Z(\boldsymbol{x})
contains isolated elements. In particular, for any
sequence
x^{n}\downarrow 0
we have
Z(x^{n})=\{(0,1)\}
. Similarly, for any sequence
x^{n}\uparrow 0
we have
Z(x^{n})=\{(1,0)\}
and thus no choice
\boldsymbol{z}^{n}\in Z(x^{n})
can converge to
(1,1)
. This is precisely the pathology that allows
(x,\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})=(0,(0,0),(1,1))
to be a local minimizer of (
19
) even though
0
is not a local minimizer of the DC formulation (
10
). We next introduce an assumption that can eliminate such spurious isolated local minimizers.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
be a local minimizer of Problem (
19
). Suppose that
Z(\boldsymbol{x})
is lower semicontinuous at
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Then
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a local minimizer of Problem (
10
).

Proof.

Define
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
by
\tilde{y}_{s}=\psi_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
for all
s\in[S]
.
We first show that
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
and is also a local minimizer of Problem (
19
).

Since
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
, we have

g_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq\bar{y}_{s}
,
\bar{y}_{s}\geq 0
, and
\bar{y}_{s}\bar{z}_{s}=0
for all
s\in[S]
.
Hence
\tilde{y}_{s}=[g_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})]_{+}\leq\bar{y}_{s}
, so
g_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq\tilde{y}_{s}
and

\tilde{y}_{s}\geq 0
for all
s
. Since
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}\leq\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}\geq\boldsymbol{0}
, and

\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}=0
, we get
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}=0
. Thus

(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
.

Suppose, to the contrary, that
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})
is not a local minimizer of
Problem (
19
). Then there exists a sequence

\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\boldsymbol{y}^{k},\boldsymbol{z}^{k})\}\subset\Omega
such that

(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\boldsymbol{y}^{k},\boldsymbol{z}^{k})\to(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})
and

f(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})<f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
for all
k
.

Partition
[S]
into

I^{+}:=\{s:\bar{z}_{s}>0\},\qquad I^{0,+}_{>}:=\{s:\bar{z}_{s}=0,\ \bar{y}_{s}>\psi_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\},

I^{0,+}_{=}:=\{s:\bar{z}_{s}=0,\ \bar{y}_{s}=\psi_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})>0\},\qquad I^{0,0}:=\{s:\bar{z}_{s}=0,\ \bar{y}_{s}=0\}.

For each
k
, define
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
by

\hat{y}_{s}^{k}:=\begin{cases}0,&s\in I^{+},\\[2.84526pt]
\bar{y}_{s},&s\in I^{0,+}_{>},\\[2.84526pt]
[g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})]_{+},&s\in I^{0,+}_{=}\cup I^{0,0}.\end{cases}

Then
(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{k},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
for all sufficiently large
k
:
if
s\in I^{+}
, then
\bar{z}_{s}>0
, so
z_{s}^{k}>0
for all sufficiently large
k
; since

(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\boldsymbol{y}^{k},\boldsymbol{z}^{k})\in\Omega
,
y_{s}^{k}\geq 0
,
z_{s}^{k}\geq 0
, and

\sum_{t=1}^{S}y_{t}^{k}z_{t}^{k}=0
, hence
y_{s}^{k}z_{s}^{k}=0
and therefore
y_{s}^{k}=0
.
Thus
g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})\leq y_{s}^{k}=0=\hat{y}_{s}^{k}
.
If
s\in I^{0,+}_{>}
, then
\bar{y}_{s}>\psi_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})=[g_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})]_{+}\geq g_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
,
so by continuity of
g_{s}
,
g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})<\bar{y}_{s}=\hat{y}_{s}^{k}
for all sufficiently large
k
.
If
s\in I^{0,+}_{=}\cup I^{0,0}
, then by definition

\hat{y}_{s}^{k}=[g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})]_{+}\geq g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})
and
\hat{y}_{s}^{k}\geq 0
.
In all cases, since
\bar{z}_{s}=0
for
s\notin I^{+}
, we have
\hat{y}_{s}^{k}\bar{z}_{s}=0
for every
s
.

Moreover,
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{k}\to\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}
: for
s\in I^{+}
, both are
0
; for
s\in I^{0,+}_{>}
,

\hat{y}_{s}^{k}=\bar{y}_{s}
; and for
s\in I^{0,+}_{=}\cup I^{0,0}
,

\hat{y}_{s}^{k}=[g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})]_{+}\to[g_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})]_{+}=\psi_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})=\bar{y}_{s}.

Thus
(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{k},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\to(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})
.
Since the objective of Problem (
19
) depends only on
\boldsymbol{x}
, we have

f(\boldsymbol{x}^{k})=f(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{k},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})<f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
for all
k
, contradicting the local
minimality of
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})
. Therefore,

(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})
is a local minimizer of Problem (
19
).

Now let
r>0
be such that

f(\boldsymbol{x})\geq f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\quad\forall\,(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})\in\Omega\text{ with }\|(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})-(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\|<r.

Since
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\in\Omega
, we have

\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\sum_{s=1}^{S}\tilde{y}_{s}\bar{z}_{s}=0
. As

\tilde{y}_{s}=\psi_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\geq 0
for all
s
, it follows that

\sum_{s=1}^{S}\psi_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\bar{z}_{s}=0
, so
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}
attains the minimum of

\min\Bigl\{\sum_{s=1}^{S}\psi_{s}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})z_{s}:\ \boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}\Bigr\}.

Hence
\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\in Z(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
.

Fix
\varepsilon:=r/3
. Since
Z
is lower semicontinuous at
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
and

\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\in Z(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
, there exists
\rho_{1}>0
such that for every

\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
with
\|\boldsymbol{x}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\|<\rho_{1}
, one can choose

\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{x})\in Z(\boldsymbol{x})
satisfying
\|\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{x})-\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\|<\varepsilon
.
Since
\boldsymbol{\psi}
is continuous, there exists
\rho_{2}>0
such that

\|\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{\psi}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\|<\varepsilon
whenever

\|\boldsymbol{x}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\|<\rho_{2}
.

Set
\rho:=\min\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\varepsilon\}
and take any

\boldsymbol{x}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}\cap B(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\rho)
. Let
\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})
and choose

\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{x})\in Z(\boldsymbol{x})
as above. Since
\boldsymbol{x}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}
, the optimal value of

\min\Bigl\{\sum_{s=1}^{S}\psi_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})z_{s}:\ \boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{C}\Bigr\}

is
0
, so
\sum_{s=1}^{S}y_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})z_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
. Also,

g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq[g_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})]_{+}=y_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
y_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq 0
for all
s
, hence

(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{x}))\in\Omega
.

Furthermore,

\|(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{x}))-(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{x}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\|+\|\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{x})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}\|+\|\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{x})-\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\|<\varepsilon+\varepsilon+\varepsilon=r.

Therefore, by the local minimality of
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})
,

f(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{x}))\geq f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})=f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}).

Since
\boldsymbol{x}\in\hat{\mathcal{X}}\cap B(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\rho)
was arbitrary,
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a local minimizer of
Problem (
10
).
∎

A simple sufficient condition for the lower semicontinuity of
Z(\boldsymbol{x})
at
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is that it is a singleton, which has been proved in
[
9
]
. In our setting, a standard sufficient condition ensuring that
Z(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
is a singleton is that the threshold exhibits a strict gap, namely

\psi_{(S-m)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})=0<\psi_{(S-m+1)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}),

which implies that the set of
(S-m)
smallest components of
\{\psi_{1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\}^{S}_{s=1}
is uniquely determined and thus the set-valued mapping
Z(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
is a singleton.

Finally, we emphasize that the lower semicontinuity of
Z
is only a sufficient condition: spurious local minima may occur at isolated lifted points, but not every isolated lifted local minimizer is spurious in the sense of having an
\boldsymbol{x}
-component that fails to be locally optimal for the DC formulation.

Example 4.4
.

Let us revisit Example
4.2
but replace the objective by
f(x)=x^{2}
. Since
\phi(x)\equiv 0
on
\mathcal{X}
,
the DC formulation (
10
) becomes
\min\{x^{2}:x\geq-1\}
, so
x=0
is a global, hence local minimizer. On the other hand, the lifted point
(\bar{x},\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})=(0,(0,0),(1,1))
remains feasible for (
19
) and is again an isolated local minimizer in the lifted space by the same neighborhood argument as in Example
4.2
. In this case, however, the lifted local minimizer is not spurious: its
\boldsymbol{x}
-component
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=0
is also a local minimizer of the primal formulation, even though
Z(\cdot)
is still not lower semicontinuous at
0
.

5.1
A VaR-constrained Portfolio Optimization Problem

In this section, we consider a Value-at-Risk (VaR) constrained mean–variance portfolio selection model studied in
[
4
,
50
]
. Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denote the estimated mean vector and covariance matrix of returns for
n
risky assets, and let
\gamma>0
be a risk-aversion parameter. Let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
denote the portfolio weight vector. The problem can be formulated as follows:

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\gamma\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}:\Pr\{\tilde{\xi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\geq R\}\geq 1-\alpha,\sum^{n}_{i=1}x_{i}=1,0\leq x_{i}\leq u_{i},\;i\in[n]\right\}.

The problem can be interpreted as follows: the objective minimizes a quadratic variance penalty minus expected return, subject to a chance constraint requiring that the realized portfolio return
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}
exceeds a prespecified target
R
with probability at least
1-\alpha
. In addition, we impose the budget constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1
and box constraints
0\leq x_{i}\leq u_{i}
for each
i\in[n]
to avoid overconcentration.

To construct test instances from real data, we use a dataset of 2523 daily returns of 435 stocks in the S&P 500 index over March 2006 to March 2016, which can be downloaded from
https://github.com/INFORMSJoC/2024.0648
. Specifically, we consider four problem sizes
n\in\{100,200,300,400\}
and set the sample size to
S=3n
. For each pair
(n,S)
, we run the algorithms on five independent instances and report the average performance metrics over these five instances. For the PenDC-L method, we set
\sigma_{0}=5\cdot 10^{-3}
,
\beta=4.0
, and
\rho=10^{-4}
. For the PenDC-P method, we set
\sigma_{0}=3\cdot 10^{-3}
,
\beta=1.5
, and
\rho=0
.

A Probabilistic Resource Planning Problem

We next consider a chance constrained linear resource planning problem, which has been studied in
[
37
,
4
]
. The model concerns shipping goods from
n
suppliers to
m
customers at minimum total transportation cost, where customer demands are uncertain. Specifically, the demand of customer
j\in[m]
is modeled by a random variable
\xi_{j}
, while each supplier
i\in[n]
is subject to a deterministic capacity limit
\theta_{i}>0
. Let
c_{ij}\geq 0
denote the unit shipping cost from supplier
i
to customer
j
, and let
x_{ij}\geq 0
be the amount shipped along arc
(i,j)
. The shipment plan is decided in advance of demand realization, and feasibility is enforced in a probabilistic sense by requiring that all customer demands are met simultaneously with probability at least
(1-\alpha)
. The resulting formulation is

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{ij}x_{ij}:\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}\geq\xi_{j},\ \forall j\in[m]\right\}\geq 1-\alpha,\\
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{ij}\leq\theta_{i},\ \forall i\in[n],\quad x_{ij}\geq 0,\quad\forall i\in[n],\;j\in[m]\end{array}\right\}.

We use the public dataset provided at
http://homepages.cae.wisc.edu/~luedtkej/
. Throughout, we let
(n,m)\in\{(40,100),(40,200)\}
and consider three sample sizes
S\in\{1000,2000,3000\}
. For both the PenDC-L and PenDC-P methods, we set
\sigma_{0}=5
,
\beta=4.5
, and
\rho=10^{-3}
.

5.3
Linear Objective with Joint Quadratic Chance Constraint

We further test our algorithm on a problem with a linear objective and a joint nonlinear (convex) chance constraint, which has been used as a benchmark in
[
27
,
50
]
. The decision variable is
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
and the objective is to minimize the negative sum of components. The feasibility requirement is imposed through a joint quadratic chance constraint of the form

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}:\Pr\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{ij}^{2}x_{i}^{2}\leq\theta,\ \forall j\in[m]\right\}\ \geq\ 1-\alpha\right\},

where the random coefficients
\{\xi_{ij}\}_{i\in[d],\,j\in[m]}
follow a dependent Gaussian structure.

In particular, for each
j
, the vector
(\xi_{1j},\ldots,\xi_{dj})
is multivariate normal with
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{ij}]=j/d
and
\mathrm{Var}(\xi_{ij})=1
, and the within-
j
correlations satisfy
\mathrm{Cov}(\xi_{ij},\xi_{i^{\prime}j})=0.5
for
i\neq i^{\prime}
. Across different indices
j\neq j^{\prime}
, the corresponding random variables are independent, i.e.,
\mathrm{Cov}(\xi_{ij},\xi_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})=0
. In the numerical study, we fix
d=20
,
m=20
, and
\theta=100
, and we vary the sample size
S
. Specifically, we consider
S\in\{500,1000,2000\}
. For the PenDC-P method, we let
\sigma^{0}=4\cdot 10^{-3}
,
\beta=15
and
\rho=0
. For the PenDC-L method, we let
\sigma^{0}=8\cdot 10^{-5}
,
\beta=10
and
\rho=10^{-3}
. For the first two outer iterations of the two penalty methods, we terminate the inner loop after 1 and 2 iterations, respectively, as a warm start. We also set a time limit of 600 seconds for the DCA method.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed penalty based DC algorithms for the SAA approximation of chance constrained programs in a convex setting. Using a rank-based DC representation of the empirical chance constraint, we proposed a primal-space method that avoids feasible initialization and a numerically more stable lifted formulation with a finite termination guarantee. We established exact penalty and stationarity guarantees for both formulations under mild constraint qualification assumptions. Numerical studies demonstrated the efficiency of the proposed methods. Future works include extending the method to distributionally robust chance constrained programs (DRCCPs) under Wasserstein ambiguity sets
[
53
,
14
]
, and chance constrained programs with linear conic inequality constraints
[
49
]
.

Proof.

We start by recalling the standard representation of the Clarke directional derivative
(see, e.g.,
[
17
, Proposition 2.1.2]
):

g^{\circ}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{d})\;=\;\max_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})}\ \langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{d}\rangle.

(31)

Step 1: GMFCQ
\Longrightarrow

0\notin\partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
.

Assume GMFCQ holds at
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
, i.e., there exists
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
such that

g^{\circ}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{d})<0
. By (
31
), this implies

\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{d}\rangle<0
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
.
If
0\in\partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
, then there exist

\boldsymbol{v}\in\partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
and
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
with
\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{z}=0
.
Since
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
and
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
, we have
\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{d}\rangle\leq 0
and hence

\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{d}\rangle=-\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{d}\rangle\geq 0
, a contradiction.
Therefore
0\notin\partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
.

Step 2:
0\notin\partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})

\Longrightarrow
metric regularity.

Define

\ker\!\bigl([\partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}),I_{d}]\bigr):=\bigl\{(\lambda,\boldsymbol{z})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ 0\in\lambda\,\partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\boldsymbol{z}\bigr\}.

Since
g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})=0
, we have
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}(g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}))=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}(0)=\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
A direct verification shows that

0\ \notin\ \partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\ker\!\bigl([\partial g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}),I_{d}]\bigr)\ \cap\ \bigl(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}(g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}))\times\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\bigr)\;=\;\{(0,\boldsymbol{0})\},

which is the regularity condition
[
10
, Eq. (2.8)]
(see also
[
8
, Eq. (33)]
).
Applying
[
10
, Thm. 2.4]
(see also
[
8

) to the set-valued mapping

\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{x})=g(\boldsymbol{x})+\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we conclude that
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{G}^{-1}(0)

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}

Finally, metric regularity at

which is exactly (

).

f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})+\sigma[g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})]_{+}\leq f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma[g(\boldsymbol{x})]_{+},\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}\cap B(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},r).

For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{F}\cap B(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},r)
, we have
[g(\boldsymbol{x})]_{+}=0
and thus

f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq f(\boldsymbol{x})
. Hence
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a local minimizer of (
3
).

(ii)
Assume that
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a local minimizer of (
3
).
Let
\kappa,\varepsilon
be the constants in the error bound (
5
).
Fix any
\sigma>L_{f}\kappa
.
We show that
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
locally minimizes (
6
).

Take any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}\cap B(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\varepsilon)
and choose
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{F}
such that

\|\boldsymbol{x}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\|=\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{F})
.
By Lipschitzness of
f
and the error bound,

f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq f(\boldsymbol{x})+L_{f}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}\|=f(\boldsymbol{x})+L_{f}\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{F})\leq f(\boldsymbol{x})+L_{f}\kappa[g(\boldsymbol{x})]_{+}.

Moreover, since
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a local minimizer of (
3
), we have
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})
for all
\boldsymbol{x}
sufficiently close to
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
Therefore, for such
\boldsymbol{x}
,

f(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq f(\boldsymbol{x})+L_{f}\kappa[g(\boldsymbol{x})]_{+}\leq f(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma[g(\boldsymbol{x})]_{+}.

Since
[g(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})]_{+}=0
, this shows that
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a local minimizer of (
6
).
Taking
\bar{\sigma}:=L_{f}\kappa
completes the proof.
∎
