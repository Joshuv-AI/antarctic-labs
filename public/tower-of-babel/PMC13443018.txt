Continuum of Irreversible and Potentially Reversible Traumatic Brain Injury Sequelae: Axonal Injury and Hydrocephalus
Authors: Pablo Alfredo Vargas-Ardila, Manuela Rondón, María Camila Bonilla-Lersundy, Andrés Felipe Vargas-Ardila, Leonardo Andrés Chacón-Zambrano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is a neurodegenerative tauopathy linked to repetitive head trauma and definitively diagnosed postmortem. Its proposed clinical correlate in living patients is traumatic encephalopathy syndrome (TES). Within this spectrum, diffuse axonal injury (DAI) and post-traumatic hydrocephalus (PTH) represent interacting mechanisms contributing to long-term neurocognitive decline after traumatic brain injury (TBI). While DAI reflects largely irreversible axonal damage, PTH constitutes a potentially reversible disturbance of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics. Their coexistence and pathophysiological interplay remain underrecognized. Understanding this continuum is essential to guide therapeutic strategies targeting both structural injury and CSF circulation abnormalities. A narrative review of PubMed-indexed literature (1980–2025) was conducted, focusing on pathophysiological correlations, neuroimaging features, and management strategies for CTE/TES, DAI, and PTH. Case series, clinical trials, and review articles addressing post-traumatic CSF disturbances were prioritized. A total of 100 records were identified (85 via database search, 15 through citation tracking). After screening, 32 were excluded, and 62 articles were included following full-text review. No duplicates were identified. Studies not relevant to pathophysiology, diagnosis, or management were excluded. Persistent ventriculomegaly after TBI is frequently misinterpreted as cerebral atrophy rather than hydrocephalus, leading to missed opportunities for surgical intervention. Advanced neuroimaging improves differentiation between DAI-related atrophy and treatable PTH. Selected patients demonstrate meaningful functional recovery following CSF diversion procedures, including programmable shunts and endoscopic third ventriculostomy. This continuum highlights the importance of recognizing reversible components of post-traumatic injury. Early identification and appropriate management of PTH may significantly improve functional outcomes in patients with chronic TBI.Keywords: Diffuse axonal injury, Post-traumatic hydrocephalus, Neurorehabilitation, Chronic traumatic encephalopathy, Tauopathy, Brain injuries

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) remains one of the leading causes of acquired neurological disability among adults worldwide. Although improvements in intensive care have increased survival rates, a substantial proportion of patients develop chronic neurological deterioration that challenges long-term recovery. This spectrum encompasses entities ranging from diffuse axonal injury (DAI), a largely irreversible structural lesion, to post-traumatic hydrocephalus (PTH), a potentially reversible disorder. Within this spectrum, it is important to distinguish between chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), a neuropathological diagnosis confirmed exclusively at autopsy by perivascular hyperphosphorylated tau aggregates associated with repetitive head impacts, and traumatic encephalopathy syndrome (TES), its proposed clinical counterpart in living patients, defined by progressive cognitive impairment and/or neurobehavioral dysregulation following repeated head trauma.31,43) We use the term chronic post-traumatic encephalopathy (CPTE) as a pragmatic umbrella to describe chronic neurological deterioration after TBI, encompassing both irreversible neurodegeneration (e.g., DAI) and potentially reversible processes such as PTH. This construct is distinct from CTE, a neuropathological diagnosis. The ability to distinguish between these entities is crucial, as it determines whether the patient might benefit from surgical treatment or if the deficit results from irreversible axonal damage.28,49)

DAI arises from rotational or acceleration–deceleration forces that shear axons within the deep white matter, corpus callosum, and brainstem. This leads to widespread neuronal disconnection and progressive neurodegeneration. Advanced imaging techniques such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) have demonstrated persistent reductions in fractional anisotropy (FA) correlating with long-term cognitive and functional deficits.35) In contrast, PTH develops due to impaired cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) absorption following subarachnoid or intraventricular hemorrhage, leading to progressive ventriculomegaly and decreased intracranial compliance. Unlike DAI, neurological symptoms in PTH can improve significantly once CSF dynamics are restored through shunting.8,47)

The challenge arises from the frequent coexistence of both processes. Many patients fall within an overlapping zone where the clinical presentation, magnetic gait, apathy, and urinary incontinence, can be attributed to either DAI or communicating hydrocephalus. This diagnostic ambiguity often results in under-recognition of treatable hydrocephalus or, conversely, unnecessary surgical procedures in patients without CSF dynamic disturbance.28,49) Because standardized diagnostic criteria are lacking, clinicians increasingly rely on complementary tools. Quantitative neuroimaging can reveal ventriculomegaly disproportionate to cortical atrophy, while intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring and CSF dynamic tests, such as the tap test or extended lumbar drainage, help determine physiological reversibility. Moreover, emerging neuronal and glial biomarkers, including neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), offer promise in quantifying axonal injury burden and predicting long-term neurodegeneration after TBI, potentially informing patient selection for rehabilitative and surgical strategies.51)

Within this framework, CPTE should be conceptualized not as a binary diagnosis but as a pathophysiological continuum. This review integrates current evidence on the underlying mechanisms, key clinical and radiological features, and modern diagnostic approaches. Finally, it proposes a practical diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm designed to guide clinical decision-making and optimize patient selection for CSF diversion, aiming ultimately to improve neurological outcomes in individuals suffering from chronic deterioration after TBI.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A narrative review of PubMed-indexed literature published between 1980 and 2025 was conducted. The search was performed using the following term combinations: “diffuse axonal injury” AND “hydrocephalus”; “post-traumatic hydrocephalus” AND “traumatic brain injury”; “post-traumatic encephalopathy” AND “hydrocephalus”; “cerebrospinal fluid dynamics” AND “traumatic brain injury”; “neurorehabilitation” AND “diffuse axonal injury”; and “ventriculoperitoneal shunt” AND “traumatic brain injury.” Filters applied included human subjects and adult population (19+ years). A total of 100 records were identified: 85 from database searches and 15 from citation searching. No duplicates were identified. After screening, 32 records were excluded as irrelevant. From database searches, 53 reports were sought for retrieval, all successfully retrieved and assessed for eligibility, with no further exclusions. From citation searching, 9 reports were sought for retrieval; 6 were not retrieved, and all 9 assessed for eligibility were included with no further exclusions. A total of 62 studies were included in the final review. Articles were excluded when not relevant to the pathophysiology, diagnosis, or management of CPTE. Case series, clinical trials, and reviews addressing post-traumatic CSF derangements were prioritized. DISCUSSION

Pathophysiology: the lesional continuum of DAI and PTH

Chronic neurological deterioration after TBI rarely results from a single mechanism. Instead, it represents a continuum of interrelated lesions ranging from irreversible DAI to potentially reversible PTH. Understanding where each patient lies along this spectrum is essential, as it determines whether the deficit may respond to CSF diversion or reflects permanent axonal loss.

DAI: mechanical damage and long-term structural loss

DAI constitutes the irreversible foundation of many chronic post-traumatic deficits. Rapid acceleration-deceleration and rotational forces stretch and shear axons within the deep white matter, corpus callosum, brainstem, and gray-white junctions. This mechanical insult triggers calcium influx, mitochondrial dysfunction, cytoskeletal breakdown, and impaired axonal transport, ultimately leading to Wallerian degeneration and progressive disconnection.32)

A longitudinal cohort of 143 patients with mild, moderate, and severe TBI demonstrated progressive whole-brain volume loss for up to five years post-injury, independent of initial severity. Serum NfL and GFAP concentrations correlated strongly with rates of atrophy, linking structural degeneration to axonal biomarker burden.51) Similarly, elevated serum NfL levels during the subacute and chronic phases have been associated with decreased gray and white matter volume and reduced FA on DTI, confirming persistent axonal pathology.52)

Secondary degeneration: neuroinflammation, cell death, and delayed deterioration

After the mechanical insult, secondary injury mechanisms, such as neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, continue to perpetuate cellular damage for weeks to months. Activated microglia and astrocytes release cytokines and free radicals, contributing to demyelination, oligodendrocyte loss, and gliosis. These processes explain why some patients deteriorate cognitively or behaviorally long after the initial trauma, even when conventional imaging appears stable.32)

A diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study found persistently increased extracellular free-water fraction in white matter up to six months after TBI, suggesting ongoing interstitial edema, microstructural disruption, and possible glymphatic clearance impairment. Higher free-water content correlated with worse neuropsychological outcomes during the subacute phase.20)

Disturbed CSF dynamics and PTH: mechanisms and clinical implications

PTH represents the potentially reversible end of the post-traumatic spectrum. It results from impaired CSF circulation or absorption due to meningeal fibrosis, arachnoid granulation obstruction, intraventricular hemorrhage, or inflammatory ependymal injury.

Experimental models have shown that hemorrhage into the choroid plexus, iron deposition, and ependymal ciliary damage can lead to progressive ventricular enlargement even contralateral to the primary lesion, indicating a global CSF absorption deficit rather than mere ex-vacuo atrophy.61)

In clinical series, incidence rates of PTH after TBI vary widely (0.07%–29%), depending on injury severity, surgical interventions, age, and case definitions.49) A multicenter cohort of 2,882 patients followed for a median of 4.6 years found that 2.1% developed PTH, typically diagnosed approximately 102 days after discharge. Independent predictors included older age, midline shift >5 mm, traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, external ventricular drainage, and decompressive craniectomy. PTH independently predicted long-term disability.40) These findings underscore the importance of surveillance during the months following injury, as delayed hydrocephalus is treatable and can significantly alter prognosis.

Risk modifiers: surgical interventions, timing, and radiological predictors

Certain factors increase susceptibility to PTH or amplify its clinical impact. Decompressive craniectomy markedly raises PTH risk; interhemispheric hygromas and delayed cranioplasty (>3 months) correlate with greater need for VPS (FIGURE 2).45) In patients undergoing craniectomy for acute subdural hematoma, hematoma thickness, midline shift, and intraventricular hemorrhage are independent predictors of postoperative hydrocephalus.33) Absence of the ambient cistern, prolonged coma (>2 months), and elevated plasma fibrinogen levels have been associated with poorer outcomes once PTH develops.55) Radiologically, progressive ventricular enlargement, interhemispheric hygromas, or persistent midline shift after initial recovery may indicate evolving CSF circulation failure.

Mixed phenotypes: interaction between irreversible and reversible injury

Many patients exhibit overlapping features of DAI and PTH, a mixed phenotype in which reduced cerebral reserve amplifies the impact of CSF pressure alterations. In such cases, even mild ventricular enlargement can precipitate cognitive or gait deterioration because damaged white matter cannot buffer ICP fluctuations. Clinical reports suggest that some patients with chronic TBI and ventriculomegaly improve after CSF diversion on conventional MRI and computed ttomography (CT) follow-up, implying that a reversible hydrocephalic component can coexist with axonal injury.34)

Biomarkers and physiologic correlates: toward defining reversibility

Biomarkers are emerging as potential tools to distinguish reversible hydrocephalic deterioration from irreversible axonal degeneration. Elevated serum NfL and GFAP predict accelerated brain atrophy and poorer long-term outcomes in chronic TBI.52) In idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH), CSF NfL concentrations decrease after successful shunting, correlating with clinical improvement.55) Persistent elevation of serum GFAP, NfL, and tau several years after TBI has been linked to ongoing neurobehavioral dysfunction, suggesting continuing neurodegeneration.13) NfL shows potential as a biomarker of axonal injury burden, but its role in distinguishing reversible from irreversible pathology remains investigational.

Clinical presentation

Overlapping and progressive symptoms

The chronic neurological manifestations following TBI often blur the distinction between irreversible DAI and treatable PTH. Both conditions may present months after the initial insult with overlapping features, cognitive decline, gait disturbance, and urinary incontinence, a triad reminiscent of NPH, yet with a post-traumatic substrate.50)

Typical symptoms include slowing of psychomotor performance, executive dysfunction, apathy, and impaired attention, reflecting frontal-subcortical disconnection. Gait disturbances are often described as "magnetic" or "hesitant," resulting from periventricular compression of corticospinal and frontopontine tracts. In contrast, patients with predominant DAI tend to exhibit spasticity, dystonia, or diffuse motor slowing without true gait freezing. Urinary incontinence appearing late in the clinical course is relatively specific for hydrocephalic involvement rather than pure axonal injury.49,50)

A key clinical clue lies in temporal evolution: while DAI-related deficits tend to plateau or slowly improve, PTH may manifest as secondary deterioration after an initial period of stability, a “second hit” phenomenon suggestive of evolving CSF circulation failure.2,34,40)

Diagnosis

Structural neuroimaging: CT and MRI markers

1) CT

On CT, ventriculomegaly disproportionate to cortical atrophy is the hallmark of PTH. The Evans' index (>0.3) and callosal angle (<90°) are quantitative indicators of possible hydrocephalus, though not pathognomonic. The periventricular low-density halo reflects transependymal CSF migration and raised intraventricular pressure.14)

Conversely, DAI typically presents with symmetrical cortical thinning, generalized sulcal widening, and absent periventricular edema, consistent with global atrophy rather than pressure-related changes. Focal encephalomalacia, cavitary lesions, and corpus callosum atrophy are also common sequelae of severe DAI.56,57)

2) MRI

MRI provides superior characterization of both axonal and CSF-related pathologies. T2/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences (FIGURE 3) delineate periventricular hyperintensities from transependymal edema. Susceptibility-weighted imaging highlights microhemorrhages within white matter tracts, a sensitive marker of DAI.16,41) Three-dimensional volumetric MRI allows quantitative assessment of ventricular volume and cortical thickness.5) DTI demonstrates reduced FA and increased mean diffusivity in affected tracts, correlating with neurocognitive outcomes.21,48)

Recent studies suggest that ventricular enlargement out of proportion to DTI-derived axonal loss may indicate a reversible hydrocephalic component rather than irreversible atrophy. Integration of morphometric MRI with CSF dynamic testing is therefore recommended.

Advanced neuroimaging and quantitative tools

Beyond conventional MRI, several advanced techniques provide deeper insight into the interplay between DAI and PTH, offering structural, microstructural, and functional perspectives that help distinguish irreversible axonal degeneration from potentially reversible CSF dynamics.

DKI

DKI expands upon DTI by capturing the non-Gaussian diffusion behavior of water molecules, providing greater sensitivity to subtle microstructural changes in white matter tracts and periventricular regions. Studies have shown that DKI detects abnormalities even when conventional MRI and DTI appear normal, reflecting axonal beading, demyelination, and glial reactivity in mild TBI.30) This enhanced sensitivity makes DKI particularly valuable for characterizing the microstructural burden of DAI in periventricular areas where overlapping hydrocephalic changes may coexist.

Volumetric and morphometric analysis

Automated segmentation platforms such as FreeSurfer and FSL enable quantitative assessment of global and regional brain volumes, cortical thickness, and ventricular size. Longitudinal volumetric studies have demonstrated that automated brain morphometry can objectively track progressive atrophy in TBI, aiding differentiation between chronic degenerative loss and pressure-related ventricular enlargement.18) Volumetric measurements have also been applied in hydrocephalus research, showing that cortical thinning patterns and ventricular-to-brain ratios can help identify patients with potentially reversible CSF accumulation.50)

PC-MRI

PC-MRI enables noninvasive quantification of CSF flow dynamics by measuring velocity and direction of flow through the aqueduct of Sylvius. Increased aqueductal flow pulsatility, bidirectional flow, or elevated stroke volume are often observed in patients with treatable hydrocephalus.1) In post-traumatic settings, these parameters may reveal subtle CSF flow disturbances masked by concurrent parenchymal atrophy, thereby guiding shunt candidacy.

Resting-state functional MRI

Functional MRI performed at rest provides information on intrinsic connectivity across major neural networks. Disruptions within the default mode, frontoparietal, and dorsal attention networks have been consistently reported in patients with mild-to-severe TBI, correlating with cognitive and behavioral outcomes.25) These connectivity deficits tend to be more extensive in DAI-dominant phenotypes, whereas partially preserved network synchrony may indicate residual compensatory potential in hydrocephalus-dominant cases.

Although not yet standard in clinical workflows, these advanced neuroimaging techniques form a research framework for predicting reversibility, stratifying patients, and refining surgical selection criteria in complex post-traumatic presentations.

CSF dynamics and physiologic testing

Dynamic testing remains the cornerstone of differentiating hydrocephalic from non-hydrocephalic deterioration, particularly in patients with chronic TBI presenting with overlapping features of DAI and post-traumatic ventriculomegaly. In this group, conventional MRI findings may not reliably distinguish progressive axonal loss from reversible disturbances in CSF absorption.37,42) Physiologic evaluation provides complementary data that can identify patients who might benefit from CSF diversion despite ambiguous imaging.10,37)

High-volume lumbar CSF drainage

Although most evidence derives from idiopathic and secondary NPH, the same physiologic principles apply to post-traumatic cases with communicating CSF pathways.42) Lumbar CSF drainage, followed by quantitative gait and cognitive assessment over 24–72 hours, can transiently normalize intracranial compliance and reveal reversible components of ventriculomegaly.10) In severe TBI, lumbar CSF drainage has been used to reduce intracranial hypertension and improve cerebral perfusion pressure, supporting the physiologic rationale for CSF volume manipulation.3,53) Although formal tap-test criteria are not yet standardized for TBI, selected reports suggest that symptomatic improvement after lumbar drainage may predict shunt responsiveness in chronic post-traumatic states.10,34)

Continuous ICP monitoring

ICP monitoring remains an essential diagnostic tool in moderate-to-severe TBI and provides physiologic insight beyond mean pressure values. Analysis of ICP waveforms can detect “B waves” or plateau waves reflecting decreased intracranial compliance and impaired CSF buffering capacity. These oscillatory patterns often precede clinical deterioration even when average ICP remains within normal limits.22) Such findings may indicate a dynamic or “functional” hydrocephalus component rather than irreversible axonal degeneration. Continuous ICP monitoring has also been used to assess cerebral autoregulation and pressure-volume compensation, both frequently altered after DAI, contributing to delayed neurological decline.44)

CSF outflow resistance (Rout)

In the chronic phase of TBI, infusion testing allows direct quantification of CSF outflow resistance, providing a physiologic biomarker of absorptive dysfunction. Elevated Rout values (>6 mmHg·min/mL) or opening ICP higher than 15 mmHg have been associated with favorable outcomes after shunting in patients with post-traumatic ventriculomegaly.10,37) These findings demonstrate that even years after trauma, a subset of patients retains impaired CSF resorption despite normalized ICP, suggesting a potentially reversible hydrocephalic mechanism superimposed on diffuse white matter injury.

Together, these physiologic tests provide an evidence-based framework for identifying functional CSF absorption failure in patients with ambiguous post-traumatic neuroimaging. When interpreted alongside clinical and radiological findings, they allow differentiation between progressive DAI-related atrophy and treatable hydrocephalus, refining surgical selection and improving long-term outcomes.

Integrating biomarkers and imaging

Recent evidence supports a multimodal diagnostic approach combining biomarkers, neuroimaging, and CSF dynamics. Persistent elevation of serum NfL and GFAP indicates ongoing axonal and astroglial injury, whereas normalization post-shunt suggests reversibility and functional recovery.13) NfL is a key biomarker of axonal degeneration: elevated CSF NfL levels are associated with irreversible white matter injury and neurodegeneration, as observed in subcortical ischemic vascular disease and other neurodegenerative conditions. In contrast, patients with idiopathic NPH typically exhibit lower NfL concentrations, reflecting less axonal damage and a greater likelihood of reversibility with shunt surgery.4,9,29) Thus, NfL serves as a discriminant between irreversible axonal degeneration and reversible hydrocephalic states.

Additional biomarkers with diagnostic utility include Aβ1-42, Aβ1-40, total tau (t-tau), phosphorylated tau (p-tau181), soluble amyloid precursor protein, leucine-rich alpha-2-glycoprotein-1, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1. These have demonstrated value for differential diagnosis between NPH and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease, as well as for predicting response to shunt surgery.4,9,29) Low preoperative NfL and soluble amyloid precursor protein derivatives are associated with better prognosis after shunting, suggesting less irreversible axonal damage.

Neuroimaging biomarkers complement this biochemical profile: indices such as ventricular volume, the frontal-temporal horn ratio, the modified Evans index, and the coronal posterior callosal angle are highly predictive of hydrocephalus and allow distinction from cerebral atrophy and irreversible degeneration.4,9,12,17,29) Taken together, lower NfL and tau levels, preserved amyloid profiles, and characteristic neuroimaging findings support a diagnosis of reversible hydrocephalus and predict favorable surgical outcomes, whereas elevated NfL and tau, abnormal amyloid profiles, and imaging evidence of atrophy point toward irreversible axonal degeneration and poor prognosis.4,9,29)

MANAGEMENT AND THERAPEUTIC DECISION MAKING

Early neurocritical management of DAI

Even when a clear hydrocephalic component is not yet established, proper management of DAI in the acute and subacute periods is foundational to optimizing long-term outcomes. Core principles follow established TBI guidelines: maintaining cerebral perfusion, limiting secondary injury, and controlling ICP. The Brain Trauma Foundation recommends tiered therapies, sedation, osmotherapy, hyperventilation (when needed), decompressive craniectomy (FIGURE 4) for refractory elevated ICP, while carefully monitoring for complications.6,60,62)

In patients with DAI, surgical interventions are rarely directed at the axonal injury itself. Instead, management focuses on minimizing cerebral edema, avoiding hypotension or hypoxia, and ensuring appropriate neuromonitoring.27,60) In patients where imaging and biomarkers suggest predominance of axonal injury, neuroprotective and pharmacologic strategies are the foundation. The SB623 stem cell therapy has shown, in a randomized, sham-controlled trial, significant motor improvement at 24 weeks versus control in chronic motor deficits after TBI, with maintained safety.46)

Pharmacologic agents such as amantadine have been meta-analyzed: an updated meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (426 patients) found that amantadine significantly improved Glasgow Coma Scale and Mini-Mental State Examination scores compared to placebo at some early time points post-injury, though evidence is mixed and long-term effects are less certain.15) Another systematic review found that functional recovery improvements with amantadine are more consistent when treatment begins early (within the first few weeks) but less so in chronic phases without other supportive interventions.19)

Early rehabilitation planning should begin as soon as the patient is medically stable, with a multidisciplinary team ready to intervene. Prolonged rehabilitation, including physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, and cognitive retraining, is the backbone of functional recovery in DAI.

Chronic management and neurorehabilitation

Once the patient passes the acute injury phase, the emphasis shifts to maximizing neuroplasticity and functional recovery. Neurological rehabilitation programs aim to harness residual networks, promote compensatory strategies, and restore as much independence as possible. Therapy intensity, frequency, and tailoring to patient-specific deficits are key.36)

In the chronic phase, clinicians must vigilantly monitor for secondary decline that may indicate evolving pathologies, such as hydrocephalus, superimposed on the DAI substrate. Any new gait slowing, urinary incontinence, or cognitive regression should raise suspicion of a treatable CSF-related component, rather than attributing all decline to fixed axonal damage.

Management of PTH

When a hydrocephalic component is suspected in a patient with chronic TBI/DAI, the therapeutic aim is to identify those who may benefit from CSF diversion while avoiding unnecessary surgery in those without true absorptive dysfunction.

Indications and timing

Clinical features (worsening gait, cognitive decline, urinary symptoms) combined with neuroimaging evidence of ventriculomegaly disproportionate to atrophy trigger evaluation. Some retrospective series show better outcomes when VPS placement is performed earlier, before irreversible parenchymal loss ensues.23,34)

In cohorts of patients undergoing neurosurgery for TBI, the incidence of surgically treated PTH ranges from 2%–3%.23) In shunt-dependent PTH series, longer-term functional outcomes remain somewhat uncertain, but many patients show at least partial improvement after diversion.58)

Surgical options

• VPS: the most commonly used diversion technique. Valve selection (fixed, programmable) and anti-siphon mechanisms should be carefully considered in patients with coexisting brain atrophy to minimize overdrainage.51)

• Lumboperitoneal shunt or lumbar drainage: in selected communicating forms, lumbar drainage trials may help identify responders.10,24,54)

• Endoscopic third ventriculostomy: less commonly used in post-traumatic settings but may be considered in appropriate anatomy.26,51)

• Cranioplasty timing: In patients who underwent decompressive craniectomy, early cranioplasty (ideally before 3 months) has been associated with lower rates of subsequent hydrocephalus, possibly by restoring normal CSF pulsatility and intracranial compliance.26)

Monitoring and revision

After shunt placement, regular clinical and imaging follow-up is essential. Some patients experience shunt failure, overdrainage, infections, or underdrainage requiring revision. In post-traumatic populations, clinical improvement may be delayed, and setting patient expectations accordingly is important.51)

Surveillance for secondary PTH

Clinicians should maintain active surveillance for PTH development throughout the post-injury period. The highest-risk window is within the first 0–3 months after TBI, with the first 50 days representing the peak risk period7); the median diagnosis occurs at 102 days from hospital discharge, and notably, 60% of cases are diagnosed after discharge, underscoring the need for outpatient follow-up.40,47) Patients warranting closer surveillance include those with decompressive craniectomy, traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, external ventricular drainage, midline shift >5 mm, and older age.40,59) Clinically, new or worsening gait disturbance, cognitive decline, urinary incontinence, or failure to improve as expected should prompt imaging evaluation. CT remains the primary surveillance modality, while CSF dynamics testing can help distinguish true hydrocephalus from post-traumatic atrophy in ambiguous cases.42) In patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness, active surveillance is particularly important, as classic signs of hydrocephalus may be masked by the underlying neurological condition.2)

PROGNOSIS AND FUNCTIONAL OUTCOMES

The long-term outcome of CPTE depends on the relative contributions of DAI and PTH. While DAI reflects irreversible structural damage, PTH may offer partial reversibility if recognized early.

Prognosis after DAI

Functional recovery after DAI is highly variable. In a recent multicenter study of moderate and severe TBI, approximately one-third of patients with MRI-confirmed DAI achieved favorable outcomes (GOSE 6–8) among younger patients (<40 years), while deeper axonal lesions strongly predicted long-term disability.39) Similar findings were reported, where higher DAI grades correlated with poorer prognosis at 3 months post-injury.27) Pediatric studies also demonstrate gradual recovery over time, reflecting neuroplastic potential even after severe DAI.38) Nevertheless, cognitive and behavioral deficits frequently persist, and secondary degeneration can limit long-term improvement.

Prognosis in PTH and mixed phenotypes

When a hydrocephalic component is present, outcomes tend to be more favorable if CSF diversion is achieved before irreversible atrophy develops. In a prospective cohort of 402 severe TBI patients, PTH occurred in approximately 10%, and shunted patients showed functional gains despite frequent revisions.11) A proposed diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm integrating these principles is presented in FIGURE 5.

In mixed DAI–PTH cases, CSF diversion may yield modest but clinically meaningful recovery in gait or cognition, suggesting that pressure-related dysfunction can coexist with axonal injury. The extent of improvement depends largely on injury burden, age, and timing of intervention.

Predictors of outcome

Poor outcomes are associated with older age, higher DAI grade (especially brainstem or callosal lesions), and delayed recognition of PTH. Conversely, younger age, early shunting, and absence of secondary insults (hypoxia, hypotension) predict better functional trajectories.27,39) Overall, prognosis in long-term TBI sequelae is shaped by a dynamic balance between fixed axonal loss and potentially reversible CSF dynamics. Long-term, multidisciplinary management, including shunt evaluation, rehabilitation, and neurocognitive support, remains essential.

LIMITATIONS

This review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a narrative review, it is subject to selection bias in the choice of studies included, and no formal quality assessment or meta-analytic synthesis was performed. Second, the heterogeneity of the included literature, spanning case series, retrospective cohorts, and narrative reviews with variable definitions of PTH and DAI, limits the generalizability of conclusions. Third, the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria for PTH across studies makes direct comparisons difficult. Fourth, most included studies are observational in design, representing low-to-moderate levels of evidence; randomized controlled trials specifically addressing CSF diversion in the DAI-PTH continuum are lacking. Fifth, the search was limited to PubMed-indexed literature in English, potentially excluding relevant studies published in other languages or indexed in other databases. Finally, given the rapid evolution of neuroimaging and biomarker research in this field, some findings may require updating as new evidence emerges.

CONCLUSION

The sequelae of TBI represent a pathophysiological spectrum in which irreversible DAI and potentially reversible PTH often coexist. Distinguishing the dominant mechanism is essential (TABLE 2): DAI-driven decline requires rehabilitation and neuroprotective strategies, whereas hydrocephalic physiology may respond to CSF diversion.

A multimodal approach combining diffusion and volumetric MRI, CSF dynamics tests, ICP waveform analysis, and serum biomarkers (NfL, GFAP), improves diagnostic accuracy and guides individualized therapeutic decisions.10,11,21,37,42,51) Because this continuum is dynamic, repeated reassessment offers the best chance to identify reversible components and avoid futile interventions.
