The links between direct gaze and social cognition – implications for impression accuracy and joint attention
Authors: Clara D C Claveau, Lauren Human, Francesca Capozzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman eyes are hypothesized to play a critical role in social cognition, but this hypothesis has yet to be tested concretely in naturalistic settings. This study investigated how direct gaze (i.e., looking someone in their eyes) during live interactions contributes to impression formation and subsequent social cognition processes. Participants were invited to interact with confederates acting as impression targets (e.g., four different people, one at a time), rated each target’s personality after the interaction, and then completed a gaze-cuing procedure as a measure of joint attention. Our research questions were (1) whether direct gaze occurrence varies as a function of eyes’ visibility, (2) whether such variations meaningfully relate to impression accuracy, and (3) whether they additionally modulate subsequent joint attention. Results show that participants mainly looked at the targets’ eyes during the interaction, particularly when their eyes were visible. Additionally, direct gaze showed limited abilities to predict impression accuracy but preliminary links with joint attention. Integrating theories and methods from both social cognitive psychology and personality psychology, this research shows the concrete importance of the social information conveyed by eyes in our everyday lives.Keywords: Cognitive ethology, Eye contact, Impression formation, Social attentionSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Eyes are said to be a window to someone’s soul – but are they? Looking someone in the eyes is thought to facilitate several social processes, including first impression formation and subsequent social cognition1. However, with some exceptions, most available research on social attention (e.g., how we look at others) has been conducted using stimuli displayed on a computer, such as pictures or videos of people, rather than live social interactions2. Thus, it remains unclear whether the purported benefits of social attention reflect real-life social processes3. This study aims to investigate how social attention during live interactions contributes to social cognition, broadly defined as the range of abilities that allow humans to perceive, process, store, and apply information about other people and social situations4. In this paper, we specifically focus on two aspects of social cognition: impression formation (i.e., the ability to understand others’ personalities) and joint attention (i.e., the ability to coordinate attention with another by following their gaze cues).

Social attention refers to the allocation of attention cued by social stimuli1; it includes attending to where someone is looking to achieve common reference and joint attention (called attentional orienting) and looking at someone scanning their face, clothing, or presentation (called attentional engagement). Direct gaze (looking someone in the eyes) is an example of attentional engagement. Although the term direct gaze has different uses across domains (e.g5, we use it as in the recent naturalistic interaction research on live dyadic interactions, where it generally describes eye-directed behaviors from both actors (thus including the behavior of looking into an interaction partner’s eyes6–8; see also9. Research using computer-based stimuli where participants view videos of people answering personal questions found that increased attentional engagement (i.e., looking at someone more) benefits impression formation accuracy (i.e., understanding someone’s personality)10. Accuracy is defined as the extent to which impressions correspond to realistic criteria for what the person being perceived (i.e., the target) is generally like, such as the target’s self- and close-other personality reports11.

Additionally, human eyes may play a critical role in social cognition as they spontaneously attract human attention and may facilitate decoding another person’s emotional and mental states12. However, research using more naturalistic approaches found that direct gaze in live interactions between strangers is less frequent than one would predict based on computerized investigations3. This may be because gaze in real life not only acquires information but also communicates it to others; when others can see where one is looking, direct gaze is modulated by social conventions, familiarity, and social goals13–15. Thus, it is unclear to what extent attentional engagement and direct gaze occur in everyday interactions and facilitate, in turn, accurate impression formation.

How might attentional engagement and direct gaze benefit accurate impression formation? According to the Realistic Accuracy Model RAM16, accurate impression formation can occur when the impression targets make (a) relevant cues, (b) available to the perceiver, and the perceiver successfully (c) detects those cues and (d) utilizes them. Notably, each stage must be met for accuracy to occur, making the model multiplicative rather than additive. As such, if a target’s eyes provide especially relevant cues to the target’s personality, then that information must be both available to and detected by perceivers. In the present study, we will test both pathways by (1) manipulating the availability of cues by making targets’ eyes visible or not, and (2) measuring detection by assessing perceivers’ attentional engagement and direct gaze. In this context, we define cue availability and detection from an attentional perspective. Specifically, cue availability refers to the presence of a given cue (e.g., eyes’ visibility), and cue detection is defined as (overt) attentional engagement on that cue (e.g., looking at the target’s eyes).

Previous research has found initial support that cue detection, indexed by overall attentional engagement (to the eyes and other areas of a target person), promotes impression accuracy using video-recorded targets10. However, cue detection may not require overt attentional engagement, especially in live interactions. For example, an observer may be peripherally aware of available social cues yet not engage with them overtly for contextual reasons, as described above. Note that this is not an issue when using computer-based studies, where the relationship between cue availability and attentional engagement is not modulated by the observer’s need to hide or downplay their gaze behavior17. Thus, live interactions provide a unique opportunity to investigate the effects of gaze availability and detection on impression formation. For example, it is possible that gaze availability (i.e., whether a target’s eyes are visible or hidden) modulates not only the occurrence of direct gaze but also its contribution to impression accuracy.

Additionally, naturalistic research investigating the long-term effects of attention has revealed that attentional engagement may facilitate other social cognitive processes in addition to impression formation. For example, while joint attention is a multifaceted phenomenon, foundational literature in this domain operationalizes gaze-cued attention orienting as one of its basic, measurable mechanisms, which is often approximated using the gaze-cuing procedure1,15,18. This procedure, described in detail below, is a computerized task measuring response times to gazed-at vs. non-gazed-at targets, under the assumption that responses to gazed-at targets are facilitated because participants spontaneously follow the face’s gaze cues and attend to the same objects the face is looking at. In this sense, it is used to measure joint attention (which does not require that the gazers are aware of their common attentional state)1 but not more complex forms of social attention that require mutual knowledge (e.g., shared attention)18. Previous research has shown that preferential attention to someone during a social interaction predicts attentional prioritization and increased joint attention, as measured via gaze cuing, with that same person in subsequent encounters19. However, the effects of direct gaze availability and detection on these processes remain largely unknown. Importantly, investigating such effects would allow testing whether the purported benefits of direct gaze on social cognition extend beyond impression formation.

If the eyes are truly a window to others’ souls, then direct gaze during a live social interaction should concretely benefit social processes, including first impression accuracy and subsequent social cognition. To test this hypothesis, participants were invited to interact with confederates acting as impression targets (e.g., four different people, one at a time), rated each target’s personality after the interaction, and then completed a gaze-cuing procedure as a measure of joint attention. The experimental setting and study procedure are depicted in Fig. 1. This study aims to test the concrete importance of the social information conveyed by eyes and direct gaze in our everyday lives. We further articulated this overarching goal in three specific research questions: (1) whether direct gaze occurrence during live interactions varies as a function of eyes’ visibility, (2) whether such variations meaningfully relate to impression accuracy, and (3) whether they additionally modulate subsequent joint attention.

Results

Q1. Does direct gaze vary as a function of eyes’ visibility?

To answer this question, we compared direct gaze and non-social gaze (i.e., time spent looking elsewhere, not at the target) as a function of the targets’ eye visibility. Considering the social goal of the present interaction, we hypothesized to observe more direct vs. non-social gaze overall; we formulated this hypothesis specifically in contrast to previous research in naturalistic settings, which reported minimal eye contact in naturalistic settings where social interest was virtually absent (e.g., a waiting room)14. However, eye visibility may additionally modulate direct gaze, indicating a role for cue availability in social gaze behavior beyond the mere knowledge of being watched20.

In line with these hypotheses, as shown in Fig. 2A, participants mainly looked at the targets’ eyes (M = 0.70, CI = 0.70, 0.73) or in general at their face (M = 0.81, CI = 0.80, 0.84), showing limited non-social gaze overall (M = 0.16, CI = 0.15, 1.8), and even more limited body-directed gaze (M = 0.01, CI = 0.00, 0.01). Consistently, the ANOVA comparing direct gaze and non-social gaze as a function of eye-visibility yielded no main effect of eye-visibility (p =.081) but a main effect of gaze behavior, whereby direct gaze was significantly higher than non-social gaze, F(1, 67) = 483.358, p <.001, ηp2 = 0.878. A significant interaction between gaze behavior and eye-visibility indicated that direct gaze increased (visible: M = 0.74, CI = 0.71, 0.76; hidden: M = 0.69, CI = 0.66, 0.72) and non-social gaze decreased (visible: M = 0.15, CI = 0.13, 0.18; hidden: M = 0.18, CI = 0.15, 0.21) when the targets’ eyes were visible (vs. hidden), F(1, 67) = 22.598, p <.001, ηp2 = 0.252.

Panel A shows participants’ attentional engagement toward the target (social gaze) or elsewhere (non-social gaze, that is, the time spent looking elsewhere, not at the target). Panel B shows the association between participants’ locus of gaze, expressed as the proportion of time (from 0 to 1) participants spent looking at the target’s eyes or elsewhere, and (log-transformed) gaze-cuing magnitudes reflecting joint attention.

Q2. Does direct gaze relate to impression accuracy?

This question can be broken down into the following: Q2.1) Does eye visibility increase impression accuracy (testing cue availability)? Q2.2) Do direct gaze and attentional engagement influence impression accuracy (testing cue detection)? Q2.3) Do eye visibility and direct gaze/attentional engagement interact to predict greater impression accuracy (testing the multiplicative nature of RAM)?

To assess the accuracy, we used a componential profile agreement approach, which examines the agreement between participants’ ratings of the targets and an accuracy validation measure, here a composite of target self- and close other ratings16. Specifically, we used the Social Accuracy Model SAM21,22; to measure both distinctive accuracy, which refers to understanding a target’s unique profile of traits, and normativity, reflecting the extent to which an observer is rating a target in line with what the typical person is like, such as more kind than hostile, as most people tend to be. Of note, the normative profile tends to be highly socially desirable23, enabling it to also be used as an indicator of positive personality impressions (see the Analyses section for specific modeling details).

We use the general term accuracy and the more specific term distinctive accuracy interchangeably. Although our primary research questions related to (distinctive) accuracy, the model also allows a simultaneous test of the implications for impression normativity. For example, impression normativity may be higher overall when target eyes are visible and with higher direct gaze and general attentional engagement24, because perceivers may tend to view targets whose eyes they can see or who they look at more in a more positive, normative light.

Preliminary accuracy analyses

We first examined average levels of impression accuracy and normativity and whether they differ as a function of the targets’ eye visibility to determine the role of cue availability. On average, perceivers rated targets with both distinctive accuracy (b = 0.26, z = 4.706, p <.001) and normativity (b = 0.77, z = 12.233, p <.001).

Q2.1) Does eye visibility increase impression accuracy (testing cue availability)?

Eye visibility showed a slight positive, though non-significant, association with both distinctive accuracy (b = 0.041, z = 1.391, p =.164) and normativity (b = 0.043, z = 1.485, p =.138). This suggests that the extent to which the targets’ eyes were visible did not reliably improve perceivers’ ability to form accurate impressions, whether in terms of capturing the target’s unique personality profile or aligning with socially typical traits.

Q2.2) Do direct gaze and overall attentional engagement influence impression accuracy (testing cue detection)?

We ran parallel models to examine whether direct gaze (i.e., eye-directed gaze) and/or overall attentional engagement (i.e., face-directed gaze and body-directed gaze) are associated with accuracy and normativity to determine the role of cue detection. Specifically, attentional engagement and direct gaze were included as predictors of the distinctive accuracy and normativity slopes in separate models. Replicating past literature10, we hypothesized a positive interaction between the distinctive accuracy and attentional behavior. Additionally, if eyes are truly the window to others’ souls, we should expect a positive relationship between accuracy and direct gaze.

First, we found that direct gaze was not significantly associated with either distinctive accuracy (b = 0.020, z = 1.324, p =.186) or normativity (b = 0.012, z = 0.444, p =.657). Similarly, overall attentional engagement (i.e., face-directed and body-directed gaze) was not significantly associated with either distinctive accuracy (b = 0.017, z = 1.118, p =.264) or normativity (b = −0.010, z = −0.368, p =.713). Thus, greater looking time at targets did not predict greater accuracy, which is inconsistent with previous literature10.

Q2.3) Do eye visibility and direct gaze/attentional engagement interact to predict greater impression accuracy (testing the multiplicative nature of RAM)?

As pre-registered, we next examined three-way interactions with the targets’ eye-visibility condition to determine whether attentional engagement and direct gaze are especially beneficial to accuracy when targets’ eyes are visible (including attentional engagement and targets’ eye-visibility as moderators of the distinctive accuracy criterion predicting impressions. In line with the multiplicative nature of the RAM, we expected a positive interaction between eye visibility and attentional behavior, as paying more attention should be more beneficial when more cues are available25.

First, the interaction between direct gaze and eye visibility was not significant for either distinctive accuracy (b = 0.010, z = 0.337, p =.736) or normative accuracy (b = − 0.009, z = − 0.290, p =.772), indicating that any effects of direct gaze on the accuracy of impression judgment were not dependent on eye visibility. Similarly, the three-way interaction, including eye visibility and attentional engagement, was also not significant for either distinctive accuracy (b = 0.032, z = 1.062, p =.288) or normative accuracy (b = − 0.013, z = − 0.449, p =.654). Contrary to expectations, these results suggest that greater attentional engagement did not translate into more accurate personality judgments when the eyes were visible.

Q3. Does direct gaze modulate subsequent joint attention?

We first examined participants’ response times as a function of eye visibility (i.e., whether the targets’ eyes were visible or hidden during the interaction). Given the robustness of joint attention, we did not expect significant differences as a function of face stimulus or cue-target interval26, although it was possible that joint attention magnitudes would be greater in response to gaze cues of the targets whose eyes were visible (vs. hidden) due to increased perceived social value or intentionality27–29. We then turned to the main question, testing the links between direct gaze and subsequent joint attention; we hypothesized that direct gaze during the interaction would predict greater subsequent joint attention magnitudes19.

Preliminary joint attention analyses

ANOVA testing of accuracy and response time (RT) yielded results largely in line with the joint attention literature. The accuracy analysis showed that participants were slightly more accurate in valid (M = 0.97, CI = 0.96, 0.98) than in invalid (M = 0.96, CI = 0.95, 0.97) trials, F(1, 63) = 7.813, p =.007, ηp2 = 0.110. Additionally, an interaction between cue-target interval and eye visibility, F(1, 63) = 3.994, p =.050, ηp2 = 0.060, indicated a small trend toward higher accuracy when the eyes were visible (M = 0.97, CI = 0.96, 0.98) than hidden M = 0.96, CI = 0.95, 0.96) at long (p =.055) but not short cue-target interval (p =.353). The RT analysis showed faster responses in valid (M = 567 ms, CI = 545, 589) than in invalid (M = 575 ms, CI = 553, 597) trials, F(1, 63) = 15.353, p <.001, ηp2 = 0.196, and with the long (M = 557 ms, CI = 535, 579) than with the short (M = 585 ms, CI = 563, 603) cue-target interval, F(1, 63) = 15.353, p <.001, ηp2 = 0.196. Furthermore, an interaction between validity and cue-target interval, F(1, 63) = 5.347, p =.024, ηp2 = 0.078, indicated that the difference between valid (M = 550 ms, CI = 528, 573) and invalid (M = 564 ms, CI = 541, 586) trials was significant at long (p <.001) but not short (p =.250) cue-target interval. These results indicate a typical foreperiod effect (faster responses at longer cue-target intervals) and a typical joint attention effect, particularly when the cue-target interval was longer15,26,30.

Direct gaze and joint attention

This link was analyzed using a multilevel analysis with direct gaze and non-social gaze as predictors of joint attention, and eye visibility as moderators. As shown in Fig. 2B, the analysis yielded a main effect of direct gaze, F(1, 64) = 3.931, p =.049, β = 0.233, indicating that increased direct gaze during the interaction predicted greater subsequent joint attention, and of non-social gaze, F(1, 64) = 6.373, p =.012, β = 0.292, indicating that looking elsewhere (not at the target) was also beneficial to subsequent joint attention. Importantly, this analysis tests the effect of a variable (e.g., direct gaze) while controlling for the other (e.g., non-social gaze); these main effects thus indicate that the effects of direct gaze on joint attention emerged once accounting for non-social gaze, and vice versa for non-social gaze once taking into account direct gaze, reflecting a linked rather than independent relationship. Indeed, neither the main effect of direct gaze, F(1, 64) = 0.036, p =.917, β = 0.007), or non-social gaze, F(1, 64) = 2.105, p =.150, β = 0.098, was significantly related to joint attention when tested alone.

When we explored how these effects applied to overall attentional engagement, the results additionally revealed an interaction between body-directed gaze and eye visibility, F(1, 64) = 5.227, p =.023, β = 0.167, whereby looking at the targets’ bodies was detrimental to subsequent joint attention when the eyes were visible, F(1, 64) = 8.258, p =.005, β = − 0.306, but not when they were hidden (p =.583). No other effect or interaction was significant (ps > 0.056). Overall, these data indicate that both direct gaze and non-social gaze behavior were beneficial to subsequent joint attention and that looking at the targets’ bodies when their eyes were visible was detrimental to subsequent joint attention. However, these data should be interpreted with caution, as the same analyses yielded no significant effects when repeated without the logarithmic function (p >.185), potentially reflecting the disruptive effects of extreme values.

Discussion

In this study, we tested the concrete benefits of direct gaze on subsequent social cognition by examining three research questions: (1) whether direct gaze occurrence varies as a function of eyes’ visibility, (2) whether such variations meaningfully relate to impression accuracy, and (3) whether they additionally modulate subsequent joint attention. Results showed (1) that participants mainly looked at the targets’ eyes during the interaction, particularly when their eyes were visible, (2) that their impression accuracy was reliable but not significantly linked to direct gaze during the interaction, and (3) that direct gaze during the interaction showed a preliminary association with subsequent joint attention.

The analysis of gaze behavior during the interaction revealed a predominant tendency for participants to look at the targets’ eyes throughout the interactions, regardless of the eyes’ visibility (i.e., whether the target wore transparent or opaque glasses). This is a novel and important finding that contributes to clarifying current conflicting findings in the literature on social gaze behavior. While laboratory research using computerized stimuli typically indicates a spontaneous attentional bias toward the eyes31,32, real-world investigations have often found the opposite pattern3,7,14. For instance, in settings like a waiting room, the physical presence of another often decreases direct gaze14, likely to avoid unwarranted social interaction15. In contrast to these real-world investigations, our participants maintained high levels of direct gaze, yielding a pattern of results similar to that observed in laboratory investigations10. These results suggest that social gaze behavior may be contextually modulated. Specifically, unlike a waiting room scenario, our participants’ task was to “get to know” the target; since eyes are perceived as a rich source of social information, maximizing direct gaze may have served a strategic function to gather personality cues12. Additionally, research on conversational dynamics indicates that gaze serves as a turn-taking signal: people tend to avert gaze while speaking (to hold the floor or plan speech) but maintain direct gaze while listening (to monitor the speaker and invite further communication)6,33. Thus, our results appear to reflect the communicative functions of direct gaze and lend support to the notion that these functions vary contextually1,15.

Interestingly, eye visibility modulated the overall prevalence of direct gaze during the interactions. When the targets’ eyes were hidden, participants engaged in more non-social gaze and less direct gaze, and vice versa when the eyes were visible. This shift may reflect an adaptive response to the availability of social information. Since the eye region is a rich source of social cues12 and contributes to holistic face processing34, blocking this region might reduce the incentive to look and redirect attention elsewhere, leading to increased non-social gaze35. Such a decrease in direct gaze when eyes are hidden may stem from a disruption in social signaling. Clear eye contact typically induces high autonomic arousal because it involves both sending and receiving signals, thereby enhancing the potential for social interaction36. Hindered gaze signals may affect such arousal effect, potentially dampening the perceiver’s social interest. This interpretation aligns with prior research showing that observers look less at partners whose eyes are occluded (e.g., when wearing sunglasses37–39; and suggests that eye accessibility contributes to detecting and establishing communicative intentions20,40. Future work could explore how these gaze dynamics shift in fully reciprocal interactions, particularly where the relational power inherent in asking questions is balanced between dyad members.

In terms of impression accuracy, perceivers were able to form accurate impressions of the targets’ personalities overall. This distinct ability to judge others correctly, observed regardless of eye visibility or direct gaze duration, underscores the fundamental evolutionary advantage of accurate personality perception41. However, while our first set of analyses confirmed the prevalence of direct gaze, our subsequent analyses did not reveal that greater attentional engagement with the eyes (or with the overall person) translates into greater impression accuracy. This non-significant relationship between gaze to the target and accuracy does not align with previous research using video stimuli, which found that greater attentional engagement predicted more accurate personality judgments10. We can speculate that this divergence may stem from the differences between computerized and real-world social perception. While in video-based studies, gaze serves only the function of information gathering (i.e., cue detection), in live interactions, gaze serves the dual function of monitoring the partner while simultaneously signaling one’s own intent1,3. In our live paradigm, it is possible that direct gaze was driven less by the need to encode personality cues and more by the social necessity of managing the interaction (e.g., turn-taking6;, thereby diluting the direct link between looking time and accuracy. Additionally, these results might also indicate that covert cue detection (i.e., the ability of attention to gather information without eye movements) is useful to overall cue detection and impression formation in natural social interactions42. Further research is needed to identify the contextual factors that modulate decoupling between overt and covert visual cue detection and their integration with other sensory modalities contributing to impression accuracy43.

Finally, our results suggested a link between direct gaze during social interactions and subsequent joint attention. Specifically, the more a participant looked at a target’s eyes during the interaction, the more their attention was sensitive to the target’s social cues in the subsequent joint attention task. Interestingly, non-social gaze (i.e., looking away from the target) was also positively associated with joint attention. These two main effects were not independent of each other, suggesting that enhanced joint attention may not simply be linked to increased direct gaze, but to a balanced gaze pattern characterized by increased direct gaze and strategic disengagement from the target. In other words, the most effective social gaze behavior may involve an appropriate alternation between direct and non-social gaze, potentially mitigating the inherently intense or confrontational aspects of sustained eye contact44.

These results are overall in line with previous literature showing that joint attention is stronger with an individual with whom we have had previous social interactions19,45,46. For example, group research indicates that group members tend to look more at the group member they perceive as the group leader than at other group members, and to preferentially engage in joint attention with them in subsequent encounters19 Additionally, such previous studies showed that the links between attentional engagement and subsequent joint attention were associated with the perceived social relevance of the group leader’s social cues rather than the mere increase in attentional engagement47. Thus, it is tempting to interpret the present results as indicative of the impact of the communicative function of direct gaze on subsequent social coordination. Relatedly, the exploratory analyses also showed that looking at the target’s body when their eyes were visible was detrimental to subsequent joint attention. This result is intriguing because it once again suggests that eyes may be a primary source of social information that should be prioritized when available to facilitate subsequent social communication12.

One could ask why direct gaze supported direct joint attention but not impression accuracy. Speculatively, we can suggest the hypothesis that impression accuracy is a more complex social process that does benefit from the communicative function of direct gaze but does not depend on it. In other words, an accurate impression can result from the integration of multiple processes, cues, and targets’ characteristics and does not strictly depend on any of these elements taken in isolation (e.g48,49,. In contrast, joint attention, a more basic building block of social communication, may be more linked to the communicative engagement expressed through direct gaze, thereby promoting further nonverbal social coordination in subsequent encounters15,19,47. Further research is needed to deepen the understanding of the links between direct gaze, nonverbal communication, and subsequent social processes in a variety of real-world, complex social settings.

Taken together, these results support the importance of the eyes’ communicative function and its effect on subsequent social cognition. However, this study has some limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, the laboratory context may have diminished the importance of detecting visual cues during the impression-formation phase. In real life, acquiring subtle and detailed information about a target can be critical, especially in situations where personality decisions are highly consequential (e.g., in a hiring context, a clinical setting). Future research could address these issues by manipulating motivational and contextual information during the impression-formation task, including systematic covariations between participants’ and targets’ characteristics50. For example, in our study, the match between participants’ and targets’ gender was allowed to vary randomly because it was not a primary research question; however, the effects of gender and perceived similarities across social groups promise to be an intriguing area for future research on social perception (e.g51; see also our exploratory analyses below).

Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that the links between direct gaze and joint attention are promising but preliminary, as they seem to be affected by individual variability and extreme values. Further research may attempt to replicate these findings in a more powerful study that controls for individual variables affecting social cognition (e.g., extraversion, social adjustment48. Additionally, our analysis of gaze behavior relied on the manual coding of first-person video recordings. While this methodology has been successfully employed in prior research to study naturalistic social attention e.g19, and the inter-rater reliability achieved in the current study was satisfactory for the main variables of interest, it is still possible that a more precise measurement could strengthen the results observed in this study. Future studies using wearable and even dual eye-tracking systems will provide a more precise assessment of the links between social cognitive processes and specific social gaze-behavior (e.g., eye-directed vs. mouth-directed gaze, direct gaze vs. eye-contact behavior, etc.7;.

To conclude, our research indicates that direct gaze is frequent in social first encounters when social interest and communicative intentionality are high, and that it has potentially differential effects on subsequent social cognition. While its effects on the accuracy of impression judgment are limited, direct gaze appears to be linked to subsequent joint attention, though these findings remain preliminary. The results quantify, for the first time, the effects of direct gaze on social cognition and highlight the importance of real-life investigations to uncover the complex links between implicit socio-cognitive processes and everyday social behavior.

Methods

Transparency and openness

We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions, all manipulations, and all measures in the study. All study materials, anonymized grouped data, and R and SPSS analysis scripts are publicly available at this link: FORMA - OSF LINK. All authors declare no conflicts of interest. No artificial intelligence-assisted technologies were used in this research or the creation of this article. This research has received ethical approval from the host institution (UQAM | Université du Québec à Montréal) ethics board before data collection (approval #2025–7045). All methods were carried out in accordance with institutional and national regulations; all participants provided informed consent before participating in the study and confirmed their consent to participate and to the use of their anonymized data during the debriefing phase after the study. The hypotheses, methods, and analyses have been pre-registered on AsPredicted.org prior to data collection (#191576, https://aspredicted.org/nxkn-5mtd.pdf). Any deviations from the preregistration are appropriately declared.

Participants

Sixty-eight participants aged between 18 and 35 (46 self-identifying as female, 18 self-identifying as male, 3 self-identifying as non-binary, 1 preferred not to answer) participated in this study in exchange for monetary compensation (i.e., $20 for an expected participation of about one hour). This sample size was calculated a priori, setting significance levels at α = 0.05 and power at 1 - β = 0.80, and using medium expected effects for within-subjects designs (dz = 0.4) or correlational measures (r=.35) reported in previous literature linking social attention with subsequent socio-cognitive processes as reference values7,19. To ensure transparency on sampling practices, we collected additional demographic information (e.g., biological sex, country of origin, skin tone) following our previously established methods for transparent sampling52. Exclusion criteria

Participants were to be excluded (and replaced) if they knew any of the impression targets or if their first-person video recordings were corrupted or otherwise unusable for measuring gaze behavior. Additionally, and following previous literature, participants were to be excluded from the impression formation analyses if they select the same value for over 75% of their questionnaire responses and from the joint attention analyses if their joint attention performance shows a proportion of errors (i.e., incorrect letter categorization) and/or outliers (i.e., responses below 200ms or above 1500ms) above 20%53.

Design, procedure, stimuli, and apparatus

The study is a within-subject design measuring individual attentional behavior during the interaction, impression accuracy, and subsequent joint attention performance as a function of targets’ eye visibility during the interaction (see Fig. 1). Before data collection, a pool of 17 individuals was recruited as research assistants to act as impression targets in the interaction phase and as visual stimuli in the joint attention task.

Target selection

Seventeen individuals aged between 19 and 31 (12 self-identifying as women, 5 as men; 62.6% of interactions featured female targets and 37.4% featured male targets) were recruited at UQAM as research assistants and invited to be available to participate in this study as impression targets. To this effect, they were asked to rate their own personality using the 30-item Big Five Inventory-2-S54; they were also asked to have two close others complete the measure about the target to serve as accuracy criteria (see below for further details). The targets’ mean scores (reported in Table 2) tended to be relatively socially desirable (e.g., the conscientiousness mean is 5.22 on a 1–7 scale), in line with the typical finding that average self-reports are positive23. However, there was still sufficient range, with scores ranging from below the midpoint (3.89) on conscientiousness to close to the upper limit (6.28). Moreover, there was generally minimal skew in the distributions (< 0.53, > − 0.47).

Additionally, three pictures of each target’s face were taken in the same room (e.g., under the same lighting conditions) and with no glasses or other eye-covering: one with a straight-ahead gaze, one with the gaze completely shifted to the left, and one with the gaze completely shifted to the right. The photograph with the straight gaze was not altered. These pictures were used as visual stimuli in the joint attention task. This protocol is well-established by previous literature using naturalistic stimuli e.g19.

The live interaction phase

During the live interaction, participants were instructed to ask each target one or two questions about them (e.g., “What are two or three things you like to do in your leisure time?”; “What are you passionate about?”; Fig. 1A). The match between participants’ and targets’ sex or gender was not a pre-registered variable of interest and was allowed to vary randomly (see below for additional analyses).

During the interaction (lasting about 2 min), the target wore recording devices to capture a first-person view of the interaction (OhO Camera Sunglasses, HD 1080 32GB). These devices are akin to “spyglasses”; they look and feel like typical eyeglasses but hold a concealed camera on the bridge to record first-person videos. The scene was additionally recorded with a scene-view camera. The recordings were used for later coding of attentional behaviors. To manipulate cue availability during the live interaction, half the targets wore recording glasses with dark lenses (preventing the participant from seeing the target’s eyes), and the other half of the targets wore transparent lenses (perceptually similar but not obstructing eye visibility). The order of targets’ eye visibility and the association between targets and dark vs. transparent lenses was counterbalanced across participants. This manipulation allowed us to test the impact of actual (the targets’ eyes are visible) vs. expected (the targets’ eyes are hidden) cue availability, as research shows that the mere knowledge of being watched modulates direct gaze behavior1 over and above cue availability55. To justify the dark vs. transparent glasses, participants were told the cover story that we combined two investigations in one and that half of the participants (i.e., the targets) were involved in a different study investigating basic perception in social contexts.

Personality ratings

After each interaction, participants rated the personality of the target with whom they had just interacted using the 30-item Big Five Inventory-2-S54. Additional questions about the interaction partner were assessed25, including the item “was engaging and attention-getting,” which we examined in exploratory analyses for consistency with past work10.

Joint attention task

After completing all personality ratings, participants were brought to another experimental room to complete a computerized joint attention task; to maintain coherence with the cover story, participants were told that we were interested in the effects of familiarity on attention. In this task, shown in Fig. 1B, a stimulus face (varying in size from 8 to 10 cm in width and from 10 to 13 cm in height) was presented on the screen, showing a straight gaze at first and then shifting their gaze to the left or the right; 200ms (short cue-target interval) or 700 ms (long cue-target interval) after the gaze shift, a target response (e.g., the letter H or N, 2 × 3 cm) appeared in the direction of the gaze shift or the opposite direction. Participants were instructed to ignore the gaze shift and identify the target quickly and accurately by pressing one of two adjacent keyboard keys marked in yellow and blue (key response-target identity assignment was counterbalanced across participants). Past research has shown that, despite the instructions to ignore the gaze shift, participants typically respond faster and more accurately to gazed-at (vs. gazed-away) targets as they spontaneously follow the stimulus face’s gaze direction15,26,30. Critically, in this study, pictures of the faces of the same targets involved in the live interaction were used as stimuli19. This manipulation allowed us to quantify the impact of direct gaze availability and detection on joint attention behavior.

Independent of whether their eyes were visible or not visible during the interaction phase, each target’s gaze shift provided 50% valid (i.e., the target letter appearing in the direction of the gaze shift) and 50% invalid (i.e., the target letter appearing in the direction opposite to the gaze shift) gaze cues for the response target; the use of long or short cue-target intervals was counterbalanced as in past research (50% of trials each). The stimulus sequence was presented on a 27-in 4 K monitor with a color background matching the face stimulus background (i.e., light grey) and at an approximate viewing distance of 60 cm. E-prime 3.0 (Psychology Software Tools) was used to control stimulus presentation and data collection.

Finally, after the debriefing, participants completed a formal suspicion check. They were asked a dichotomous (Yes/No) question to assess whether they believed the cover story: ‘Before the debriefing, did you think that the people you interacted with were not actual participants?’ (see below for additional analyses).

Measurements

As detailed below, participants’ attentional gaze behavior was extracted from the video recordings of the live interactions. Impression, distinctive accuracy, and normativity were calculated from the personality ratings using the Social Accuracy Model SAM21. Joint attention responses and magnitudes were calculated using response time (RT) and error proportions during the computerized joint attention task1,15,30.

Gaze behavior during the live interaction

In line with previous literature, the main dependent variables we aimed at extracting were direct gaze (the proportion of time a participant spent looking at the target’s eyes), face-directed gaze (proportion of time a participant spent looking at the target’s face), body-directed gaze (proportion of time a participant spent looking at the target’s body), and non-social gaze (proportion of time a participant spent looking elsewhere19. The reason to calculate face-directed gaze in addition to direct gaze is that relevant literature indicates possible similarities between direct gaze toward the eyes (what is commonly called eye contact) and more broadly toward the face7,37. Body-directed gaze was calculated to allow for (exploratory) analysis of overall attentional engagement to the person, in line with previous literature using video-stimuli, indicating that looking at the overall person may benefit impression accuracy10,56.

To measure gaze behavior, we adapted the methodology used in previous research in naturalistic settings e.g19. Before data collection, we piloted the methodology by asking four coders (including the two who coded the final data) to perform multiple video tests, ranging from testing veridical eye-directed and mouth-directed gaze (i.e., by instructing the interaction partners on where to look and then testing coders’ ability to code correctly) to natural interactions more similar to the final data.

After data collection, two independent naïve coders watched the first-person videos (without audio) and were asked to rate where the participant was looking during each interaction. Specifically, they were instructed to press one of four preassigned keyboard keys corresponding to the “upper face (eyes)”, “lower face (mouth)”, “body”, and “none (not looking at the interaction partner)”, respectively, to identify where the participant was looking at any time. Additionally, to strengthen measurement precision, we included the default key “missing” when the interaction partner was not visible in the recorded scene (e.g., because the target moved their head; 1.12% of the data. The time between successive key presses was used to arrive at the length of each instance of looking behavior. Mangold INTERACT software (Mangold International) was used to collect and summarize the coders’ data.

Before calculating the dependent variables of interest, we assessed the measurement precision using inter-rater reliability. All target variables reached satisfactory inter-rater agreement (ICC >.70) – direct gaze: ICC =.774, p <.001; body-directed gaze:.759, p <.001; non-social gaze: ICC =.984, p <.001 – except for mouth-directed gaze (ICC =.470, p =.694). However, this did not significantly affect the overall reliability of face-directed gaze (calculated as the sum between eye-directed and mouth-directed gaze; ICC =.984, p <.001), which was one of our main pre-registered variables, and mouth-directed gaze was not included in subsequent analyses. We thus created the target variables by averaging the raw time data across the two coders and normalizing them to the video’s duration (i.e., dwell times expressed as proportions ranging from 0 to 1)”.

Impression accuracy

As mentioned above, we used the SAM to examine distinctive accuracy, which refers to understanding a target’s unique profile of traits after partialling out normativity22. Normativity refers to the extent to which observer ratings correspond to the average self-report (which is obtained by averaging the responses of the full sample of participants). As the normative profile tends to be highly socially desirable23, it is often used as an indicator of positive personality impressions. Although it is possible to statistically disentangle normativity from social desirability57,58, because of the high degree of overlap between the normative and socially desirable profiles (generally over r =.80), including both in the model can create interpretational issues. As such, greater normativity reflects seeing a target as more in line with an average, socially desirable personality profile. Simultaneously, distinctive accuracy controls for normativity, therefore capturing observers’ ability to understand how a target’s profile of traits is different from the average (see the Analyses section for specific modeling details).

Joint attention performance

Response times and errors were extracted by the joint attention task as a function of the targets’ gaze cue validity during the task (valid, invalid), cue-target interval (short, long), and targets’ eye visibility during the interaction (visible, hidden). Next, as in previous research, the magnitude of the joint attention effect was quantified by subtracting the response time in valid trials from the response time in invalid trials45,53.

Analyses

As articulated above, our main research questions were (1) whether direct gaze time varies as a function of eyes’ visibility, (2) whether such variations meaningfully relate to impression accuracy, and (3) whether they additionally modulate subsequent joint attention. Acceptable significance was set at α = 0.05 for all analyses; we report all statistics with appropriate effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the mean where appropriate.

Analyses Q1. Does direct gaze vary as a function of eyes’ visibility?

To answer this question, we compared direct gaze and non-social gaze as a function of the targets’ eye visibility. To this aim, we used a repeated measures ANOVA with gaze-behavior (2 levels: direct gaze, non-social gaze) and targets’ eye-visibility (2 levels: visible, hidden) as within-subject factors.

Analyses Q2. Does direct gaze relate to impression accuracy?

Following our pre-registration criteria, two interactions were excluded from the impression formation analyses due to excessively uniform responding (i.e., selecting the same response option for over 75% of questionnaire items), but no participants were completely excluded, so they were not replaced.

We first examined average levels of impression accuracy and normativity and whether they differ as a function of the targets’ eye visibility to determine the role of cue availability. As mentioned above, to assess accuracy, we used the Social Accuracy Model SAM21,22; multilevel modeling approach, where perceivers’ impressions of targets on each item are predicted by (1) target and close-other reports of target personality on each item (distinctive accuracy criterion) and (2) the average target self-report on each item (normativity criterion). In SAM, slopes and intercepts are allowed to vary randomly by perceiver and target. In line with prior research e.g48, dyadic random effects were not included due to convergence issues. In addition, we removed random intercepts and the perceiver distinctive accuracy random slope as these were estimated as 0 and the model did not converge with their inclusion. Analyses Q3. Does direct gaze modulate subsequent joint attention?

Following our pre-registered methods, four participants were excluded because their error rate exceeded 20% of trials. Thus, our final sample for the joint attention analyses was N = 64.

In line with standard practices in joint attention research, we first compared response time (RT) and accuracy (proportion of errors) using two separate repeated measures ANOVA with validity (2 levels: valid trials, invalid trials), cue-target interval (2 levels: short, long), and targets’ eye-visibility (2 levels: visible, hidden) as within-subject factors.

To test the links between direct gaze and joint attention, we used a multi-level analysis with direct gaze and non-social gaze as predictors, the interaction term of targets’ eye visibility (2 levels: visible, hidden) as a moderating variable, and joint attention magnitudes as the dependent variable. The multi-level analyses were performed directly on standardized values and integrated a logarithmic function to address any outliers or extreme values. Before running the multilevel analysis to link direct gaze, joint attention, and targets’ eye visibility, we checked for possible multicollinearity concerning direct gaze and non-social gaze. Although direct gaze and non-social gaze are not perfectly related (because the participants could look at other areas of the target, such as the rest of the face and their body), it is still possible that they are inversely proportional to each other because they are mutually exclusive. The analyses showed indeed that the two variables were negatively correlated, β = − 0.691, p<.001, with VIF = 0.311; however, these values go in the expected direction e.g., they indicate a negative relationship59; and are below VIF = 5, which is often considered the threshold to exclude multicollinearity concerns60.

Additional checks on cover story and gender match

In addition to our main analyses reported above, we also checked whether the participants’ beliefs about the cover story influenced the results. Participants’ responses during the debriefing indicated that 54.4% of the sample (n = 37) believed the cover story, while 45.6% (n = 31) expressed some level of suspicion about the targets’ true roles. However, further analyses revealed that the deception belief did not significantly moderate the primary results for normative or distinctive accuracy, nor did it moderate the specific effects of eye visibility, direct gaze, or attentional engagement on impression accuracy (all ps > 0.076); additionally, it did not significantly modulate the links between looking behaviors and joint attention (ps > 0.411).

Finally, for exploratory interests, we tested any effects related to gender match and found that it significantly moderated the effect of eye visibility on distinctive (b = −0.156, z = −2.670, p =.008) but not normative accuracy (b = −0.079, z = −1.293, p =.196), with eye visibility improving distinctive accuracy for mismatched pairs (n = 130) but not for matched pairs (n = 142), potentially indicating that eye visibility is helpful for accurate personality impressions when interacting with another gender. Gender match also moderated the effect of direct gaze (b = −0.093, z = −2.864, p =.004) and attentional engagement (b = −0.072, z = −2.233, p =.027) on normative accuracy, such that direct gaze and attentional engagement were positively associated with normative accuracy for gender matched dyads and negatively associated with normative accuracy for gender mismatched dyads; however, none of these simple slopes were statistically significant (ps > 0.099). Additionally, gender match had no significant effect on the links between looking behaviors and joint attention (ps > 0.514). However, it is important to keep in mind that even though these analyses are based on a large number of dyads, there were only five male targets, so we hesitate to draw strong conclusions about gender-related associations for such a small sample.
