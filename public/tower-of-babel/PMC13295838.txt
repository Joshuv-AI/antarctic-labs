Feasibility of prehospital telemedicine for paediatric emergencies: a cluster-randomised pilot trial in Karachi, Pakistan
Authors: Junaid Abdul Razzak, Sheza Hassan, Huba Atiq, Walid Farooqi, Kamran Idris, Irfan Habib, Saira Noushad, Shane Pathania, Brady Rippon, Zeeshan Noor Shaikh, Ali Kashan, Amyna Husain, Adil Haider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNearly half of all 4.8 million annual paediatric deaths worldwide are caused by acute illnesses, which are preventable with timely triage, emergency care and prehospital transport. We assessed the feasibility of using telemedicine to connect emergency medical technicians (EMTs) in ambulances with paediatric telemedicine physicians (TMPs) to improve patient care in a low-resource setting.MethodsWe conducted a pilot cluster-randomised trial in Karachi, Pakistan, with city’s primary emergency medical transport provider and a paediatric telemedicine facility. Ten ambulances were randomised equally to intervention (real-time audiovisual telemedicine support) and control arms. The intervention was piloted using 25 simulated cases, followed by low-acuity and high-acuity patients as classified by the Medical Priority Dispatch System. Primary outcomes included rates of refusal, call completion, safety (rated by EMTs and TMPs) and completion of the Paediatric Early Warning Score (PEWS) in ambulances and hospitals.FindingsOf 166 parents approached, 151 (91%) consented; 73 patients were assigned to the intervention and 78 to the control. The mean patient age was 2.5 years (SD 43 months; range 1 day–12 years). Ambulance dispatchers categorised 35.7% as low acuity and 64.3% as high acuity. Most cases (68%) were interhospital transfers. Calls were completed in 96% of cases; 87% of calls received favourable audio/video quality scores. All interactions were rated as very safe, safe or neutral; none was deemed unsafe. 91% of EMTs and TMPs rated the system as usable (System Usability Scale). PEWS was completed for 100% of patients in ambulances and 86% in the emergency department.InterpretationProviding real-time telemedicine support to EMTs in ambulances is feasible and safe in low-resource settings. These findings support further research into the clinical impact and scalability of prehospital telemedicine for paediatric emergencies.Trial registration numberThis project is a pilot trial for the Cluster Randomised Trial NCT07027813 titled ‘Feasibility and Efficacy of Ambulance-Based m-Health for Paediatric Emergencies (FEAMER) Trial (FEAMER)’ registered on 19 June 2025. The link to the clinicaltrials.gov is attached here: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07027813.

Introduction

Despite a significant decline, childhood mortality remains disproportionately high in low and middle-income countries (LMICs), which account for nearly 80% of all deaths.14 Most of these deaths result from time-sensitive illnesses such as sepsis, pneumonia, diarrhoea and injuries.4 Delays in reaching facilities equipped to provide acute and emergency care contribute significantly to this burden.5 Emergency medical services (EMS) can play a critical role in reducing mortality by enabling timely interventions and triage during transport; however, many LMICs lack formal EMS systems, and existing services often prioritise adult patients.6 7 In high-resource settings, telemedicine has improved ambulance-based care by reducing treatment times and enhancing diagnostic accuracy for conditions such as stroke and myocardial infarction.8 In contrast, evidence for the use of telemedicine in LMIC prehospital settings is limited, and no studies have evaluated its feasibility for paediatric emergencies during ambulance transport.911 The successful use of telemedicine in ambulances can help LMICs improve the quality of paediatric emergency care in a relatively short time horizon.12

Pakistan ranks third globally in under-5 mortality, with 1 in 12 children dying before their fifth birthday.13 14 Although formal EMS services in Pakistan began approximately 20 years ago, significant variability remains in training, resources and communication infrastructure.15 About 4.1% of patients in Pakistan arrive at emergency departments via ambulance or EMS, including paediatric cases, which make up roughly 10% of ED visits.16 17 The emergency rooms in Pakistan continue to be the entry points for paediatric care. Telemedicine has been successfully implemented in various hospital and outpatient settings in Pakistan, especially since the COVID-19 pandemic, yet its feasibility in prehospital paediatric emergency care remains unknown.18 19

To address this gap, we conducted a pilot cluster-randomised trial to assess the feasibility of implementing ambulance-based telemedicine (ABT) for paediatric emergency transport in Karachi, Pakistan. The cluster design was chosen to prevent contamination among emergency medical technicians (EMTs) within the same ambulance, reflect real-world operational conditions and ensure logistical and ethical feasibility. If feasible, this approach could inform scalable strategies to strengthen prehospital paediatric emergency care in other resource-limited settings.

Methodology

Trial design and setting

We conducted a prospective, parallel-group, cluster-randomised pilot trial in Karachi, Pakistan—a city of approximately 20 million people. Ambulances were randomised 1:1 to intervention (ABT) or control arms. The study was a collaboration between Child Life Foundation (CLF), a national paediatric tele-emergency service provider and Sindh Integrated Emergency and Health Services (SIEHS), the city’s largest formal EMS provider. No changes to trial methods were made after commencement.20 21

Study participants

Our study participants included three groups: children transported by SIEHS’s ambulances, EMTs and telemedicine physicians. Before enrolment, and after immediate on-scene care was provided following standard EMS protocols, EMTs obtained verbal and written (electronic) consent from guardians using a standardised script.

Children, 14 years or younger, transported by SIEHS ambulances with an expected transport duration of 20 min or more were included in the study. We excluded patients for whom guardians were unavailable to provide consent or refused to consent to participate. Similarly, EMTs and TMPs who received training on telemedicine and study procedures and consented to participate were included.

Randomisation and blinding

Ten ambulances were randomly assigned (five per arm) using a computer-generated sequence. The ambulance assignment was fixed for the duration of the study. Patients were enrolled into either arm based on the ambulance dispatched by proximity, minimising selection bias. Due to the nature of the intervention, EMTs could not be blinded to group allocation. Data analysts were not blinded due to the need for ongoing monitoring, but standardised criteria were used for outcome assessment to reduce bias.

Interventions

Telemedicine setup

The ambulances in the intervention arm were equipped with real-time, audio (bilateral), and video (unilateral) communication, enabling virtual interaction between EMTs and telemedicine physicians. The following equipment was installed in the ambulance: (1) voice-over-internet protocol phone, (2) Bluetooth-enabled headset, (3) touchscreen Samsung tablets for recording parental consent and data, (4) 4G internet device with a router, (5) a dome camera (HIKVISION) with 360° rotation and zoom-in features. For video security, existing data privacy standards at CLF were utilised, ensuring password-protected login, role-based access control, HTTPS (Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure), secure encryption and data transfer through a virtual private network.

Training

All EMTs, TMPs and SIEHS station supervisors received training on equipment use, consent, data collection, communication protocols, troubleshooting and research ethics. TMPs were familiarised with ambulance operations and camera orientation. Supervisors were trained in equipment maintenance.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the rate of successful call completion, defined as a telemedicine call-completed end-to-end with minimal to no disruption in audiovisual connectivity and successful implementation of treatment advised by the TMP to the EMT during ambulance transport. Secondary outcomes included (1) rate of consent refusals: the consent refused by the guardian during ambulance transport before intervention or within 72 hours of patient transfer to the hospital, (2) audiovisual quality: determined by the EMTs and TMPs during the telemedicine call based on the quality of call, (3) Liman audiovisual score of ≤3: 6-point Likert scale that demonstrated the quality of audio and video with a score ≤3 as good, (4) rate of safe interactions: determined by TMP and EMT for the safety in use of telemedicine for the patients during the transport, (5) System Usability Survey (SUS): a quick, 10-item questionnaire used to assess the perceived usability of telemedicine during ambulance transport. The success criteria for the feasibility phase are outlined as: (1) per cent of successful call completion (EMT reported): >70%, (2) refusal rate: <30%, (3) safety perception of very safe or safe: >95%, (4) audio quality score of ≤3 on the Liman Reliability Scale: >70% for audio, (5) visual quality score of ≤3 on the Liman Reliability Scale: >70% for video, (6) approval by DSMB (Data Safety and Monitoring Board) to go ahead with the next phase: approved. No changes to trial assessments or measurements were made after trial commencement.

Sample size

The sample size of 120 patients was chosen to balance feasibility and statistical power while ensuring adequate representation of emergency cases across the intervention and control arms. The trial did not have stopping criteria, considering the treatment poses no additional risks to participants.

Data collection

We recruited our first patient in the low acuity phase on 18 September 2023, and the last patient on 1 November 2023, at the end of the high acuity phase. The pilot trial was concluded on 6 November 2023. Data were collected on password-protected tablets using Qualtrics and uploaded to a password-protected institutional server. Data were collected at three primary locations: (1) in the ambulance during transport (via the EMT eCRF), (2) at the telemedicine centre, where TMPs provided remote guidance (via the TMP eCRF) and (3) at the hospital on patient arrival (via the hospital staff eCRF). The EMT forms contained a consent form and data collection tool to collect patient demographic information, clinical information required to calculate the Modified Paediatric Early Warning Score (PEWS), the treatment provided, perceptions of safety and the users’ experience of the quality of audio-visual (AV) signals using a tool previously reported by Liman et al Safety was rated on a 3-point scale: ‘safe/very safe’, ‘neutral’ and ‘unsafe’. Neutral indicated no perceived additional risk or benefit compared with standard care.

We selected the PEWS as a feasibility indicator because it is a validated tool for assessing paediatric clinical status and predicting deterioration. Successful completion of PEWS during transport demonstrates EMTs’ ability to perform structured assessments under telemedicine guidance, which is critical for evaluating operational feasibility and clinical safety in prehospital settings. The Telemedicine Physician reported patient information, perceptions of safety, and the quality of AV signals as well. The hospital staff form contained questions to determine PEWS on arrival at the hospital. Additionally, an online data collection tool was developed to gather the SUS.

Study phases

The feasibility testing was carried out in three distinct phases.

Phase 1 included testing on simulated patients, followed by low acuity and high acuity patient enrolment

(Online supplemental material–appendix A). Patients were categorised using the Medical Priority Dispatch System (MPDS), an internationally recognised triage protocol that classifies emergency calls into acuity levels (Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo) based on symptom severity and urgency. This system ensures standardised prioritisation for dispatch and is widely used in EMS operations globally.

Phase 2: Testing on stable patients

The enrolment was started with stable children categorised as alpha (non-serious, non-life-threatening) or bravo (serious, non-life-threatening, urgent) by the MPDS. The calls were transferred to the intervention or control ambulances based on their proximity and availability. Dispatchers from the Command-and-Control Centre (CnC) communicated with the EMTs and dispatched ambulances as per existing protocols. On arrival at the scene, EMTs initiated the emergency assessment and care. After the scene care, the patient and parents were seated in the ambulance, and one of the two EMTs in the ambulance obtained consent using a verbal consent script. For those who consented to participate, an electronic signature was obtained on the tablet, and a detailed written description of the study was provided as a handout. During this phase, we worked on identifying challenges and barriers to implementation, adjusting the process, resolving any IT-related issues, and worked on further training and strengthened the consent process.

Phase 3: Testing on all patients

The third phase included all patients and was initiated once the following outcomes were achieved during the phase-2: 1->70% successful completion of all initiated calls; 2-<30% participant refusal rate; 3->90% ability to successfully calculate PEWS by EMTs and ED triage nurses and 4- Safety perception of Very safe or Safe in>95% of cases. The research team reviewed each case regularly by scheduling daily case discussion for smooth data collection at both EMT and ED sites.

Data analysis

The descriptive analyses were used to present frequencies and 95% confidence intervals (where applicable) of 1) quality of the telemedicine call; 2) ABT reliability, safety, and usability; and 3) sociodemographic and health characteristics of the patient (age, gender, vital signs, chief complaint, assessment indicators, treatment provided and PEWS). χ2 or t-test statistics were applied to test for the distribution of characteristics. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed to account for any imbalance. This allowed for imbalanced variables in addition to pre-specified variables for adjustments to assess the robustness of the primary analysis. We also calculated and compared the difference in PEWS measured by the EMTs on initial evaluation and the one from the receiving hospitals.

Call completion and quality

Of the 73 ABT calls, EMTs reported 70 as completed calls, yielding a completion rate of 95.9%. When asked to assess the perceived safety of interactions, EMTs rated 80.8% (n=59/73) of calls as “very safe or safe” and 19.2% (n=14/73) as “neutral”. Similarly, TMPs rated 71.4% (n=50/70) of calls as “very safe or safe”, with 28.6% (n=20/70) being rated as “neutral”. Importantly, none of the calls were deemed “unsafe or very unsafe” by either EMTs or TMPs.

Audio quality was perceived as continuously, predominantly, or more comprehensible than not (Liman audio quality score of≤3) for 76.7% of calls (n=56/73) by EMTs and for 98.6% of calls (n=69/70) by TMPs. Clinical assessment and PEWS completion

Baseline assessment of vital signs, appearance, breathing pattern, and oxygen requirement was conducted in the ambulance during transport for all patients to calculate the first set of PEWS. For PEWS-1, Heart rate and Temperature were reported for 100% of the patients. Additionally, Oxygen Saturation and Respiratory Rate (RR) were reported for 99% of the patients. In contrast, systolic and diastolic blood pressures were calculated for 83% of the cases either due to “unavailability of smaller cuff” or due to “weak arm” in young patients.

Overall, PEWS-2 was calculated for 130/151 (86%) of the patients. Oxygen saturation, heart rate and temperature were documented for all 130 cases. Respiratory rate and Blood pressure were documented for 129 and 121 patients, respectively. (Table 2)

Discussion

This pilot study is one of the first to evaluate the feasibility of prehospital ABT for paediatric emergencies in a low-resource setting such as Pakistan. Our findings demonstrate that a cluster-randomised trial of mHealth interventions during ambulance transport is not only feasible but also safe and acceptable to both healthcare providers and families. The high rate of telemedicine call completion, low refusal rates, and positive safety assessments by EMTs and telemedicine physicians collectively support the potential for scaling up such interventions in similar contexts.

The FEAMER study was specifically designed for paediatric patients, with a consent and data collection process informed by prior acceptability research. The average age of enrolled patients was 2.5 years, illustrating the broad applicability of ABT across paediatric age groups. Our consent strategy, offering forms in the ambulance and a 72-hour follow-up call, was tailored to address the unique sensitivities of paediatric emergency research. This approach resulted in a combined refusal rate of less than 10%, highlighting strong parental acceptance of participating in a telemedicine study, even in high-stress scenarios. These findings align with previous research indicating that thoughtful consent processes can facilitate enrolment in paediatric emergency trials.2224

Establishing a reliable telemedicine platform for use in ambulances posed significant technical and operational challenges, particularly in the context of emergency care.25 We addressed these challenges through comprehensive training of EMTs and telemedicine physicians, simulated case testing, and the maintenance of a risk registry to promptly identify and resolve technical issues. Consistent with prior studies such as the TeleABT and Aachen projects, we found that robust internet connectivity is critical for maintaining AV quality during teleconsultations.2630 By equipping ambulances with 4G routers and commercially available AV equipment, we achieved high rates of call completion and AV quality, even in areas with variable broadband infrastructure. This approach supports the adaptability and scalability of ABT in other low-resource settings.

A key strength of our intervention was the use of commercially available, low-cost equipment, which enhances the potential for widespread adoption. This cost advantage is critical for scalability in resource-limited settings, and future studies should evaluate cost-effectiveness by considering potential savings from reduced morbidity and mortality through early detection and intervention. The adaptability of this mobile telemedicine system extends beyond paediatric emergencies and may benefit other time-sensitive conditions such as trauma, stroke and acute cardiovascular events.263133 While our results are promising, further research is needed to assess the feasibility in settings with less developed EMS infrastructure or lower technological capacity.

Unlike text-based mHealth interventions, real-time video telemedicine provides direct visual assessment, enabling earlier recognition of clinical deterioration and immediate guidance for life-saving procedures. This capability supports decision-making in the field, allowing EMTs to perform advanced interventions under specialist supervision. In contrast, text or audio-only systems lack the immediacy and contextual clarity required for high-acuity paediatric emergencies, limiting their effectiveness in time-critical scenarios.

The successful implementation of ABT depends not only on technical feasibility but also on user acceptance. Our study demonstrated high levels of satisfaction among both EMTs and telemedicine physicians, with 91% rating the system favourably on the SUS. The ability to maintain continuous, high-quality telemedicine calls for the duration of ambulance transport (often exceeding 20 min) was particularly valued by providers, echoing findings from the iTreat study and others.34

Safety is paramount in emergency interventions. Notably, none of the telemedicine interactions in our study was deemed unsafe by providers, and no adverse events were reported. These results underscore the reliability of ABT as an adjunct to standard EMS care in paediatric emergencies.35

Our study has several limitations. First, incomplete hospital PEWS data limited our ability to compare prehospital and emergency department assessments for all patients. Second, the study was conducted in a single urban centre with a relatively well-resourced EMS system, which may limit generalisability to rural or less developed settings. Third, the success of ABT is contingent on reliable technological infrastructure, which may not be universally available. Safety ratings were subjective and were assessed by EMTs and TMPs, potentially introducing bias. Future studies should include objective metrics (eg, adverse events) and an independent review of recorded calls.

A key limitation of this intervention is its reliance on stable 4G internet connectivity, which may pose challenges for scaling to rural and remote areas with limited network coverage. Additionally, the use of PEWS, a subjective tool, requires extensive training for accurate application, and observer bias is possible given that EMTs and TMPs were aware of the study aims. The high proportion of interfacility transfers in our sample may limit generalisability to primary scene emergencies, where operational challenges and safety considerations could differ. Moreover, although participants agreed to participate in a telemedicine study, the low refusal rate does not accurately reflect the high acceptance of telemedicine in the population. Addressing these limitations will be essential in future, larger scale studies.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings demonstrate that ABT is a feasible, safe and acceptable intervention for paediatric emergency care in a low-resource setting. High consent rates, successful call completion and positive provider feedback support the potential for broader implementation. Future research should focus on evaluating clinical outcomes, expanding to diverse geographic and resource settings and developing strategies to overcome technological and training barriers. Additionally, future iterations could consolidate the intervention into a smartphone-based platform, potentially integrating point-of-care ultrasound and AI-driven decision support to enhance early detection and intervention. Ultimately, ABT has the potential to strengthen prehospital emergency care and improve outcomes for children in LMICs and beyond.
