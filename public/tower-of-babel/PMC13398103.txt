Conditional activation of IL-12 through a Fibronectin-EDB dependent switch gate
Authors: Josephine D. Kahn, Kothai Parthiban, Peter Slavny, Aaron Arvey, Marc Presler, Jamie Nosbisch, Adrienne Rothschilds, Allan Capili, Teresa Barata, Joana Martins, Jiang Zhu, Robert Kamen, Robert Tepper, Ton N. Schumacher, Lioudmila Tchistiakova, Divya Mathur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPotent immune-activators, such as interleukin-12 (IL-12) have been challenging to develop for the treatment of solid tumors due to high systemic toxicity. To expand the therapeutic window achievable with IL-12, we engineered a novel and reversible antibody format, comprising a switch arm and targeting arm, that permits conditional activation of IL12 only in the presence of the pan-tumor matrix antigen Fibronectin-EDB (FN-EDB). The switch arm is formed by a dual specificity Fab that binds to tethered IL-12 or FN-EDB in a competitive manner. The FN-EDB targeting arm promotes the avidity‑driven unveiling of IL-12 that is tethered to the switch arm. We employed a quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) model to define binding parameters required for Switch-IL-12 activity and use a phage screening and rational library design process to generate switch binders with the desired binding profiles. We show that our in vitro functional data support FN-EDB dependent Switch-IL-12 activity and incorporate these data into our QSP model to further refine and expand the therapeutic index of Switch-IL-12.

Introduction

Cytokines are potent activators of the immune system and hold strong potential as anti-cancer therapies. Despite numerous preclinical and clinical studies highlighting the anti-tumor activity of cytokines, their clinical application has been limited by systemic toxicity, which restricts dosing to a narrow therapeutic window. Interleukin-12 (IL-12), a cytokine with strong immunostimulatory effects, is a prominent example of these challenges, where early trials were terminated due to severe immune-related toxicities despite signs of anti-tumor activity.1,2 Since then, numerous strategies have been pursued to increase the therapeutic index of IL-12 delivery, such as intra-tumoral administration, half-life extension, and tumor-selective release of protease-cleavable pro-drugs.3–8 However, these approaches have seen limited clinical success so far, highlighting the need for new technologies that allow for potent intratumoral IL-12 activity while limiting systemic exposure.

Recent advances in antibody engineering have enabled the development of dual antigen-binding fragments (Fabs) that can form building blocks for logic-gated biologicals.9–12 Dual target-binding Fabs that recognize their targets in a competitive fashion, here referred to as “switch arms,” enable conditional switching between antigen-bound states (Figure 1a). Such “switch arm” antibodies are being developed for tissue- or tumor-specific activation of cytokines (e.g., IL-2) and other immune-stimulatory agents (e.g., 4–1BB agonist). The majority of dual-binding Fab-based strategies have focused on cis-activation gates, such as PD-1-dependent IL-2 activation, in which binding to a first receptor on a target cell unveils the ligand for a second receptor on this cell. In contrast, trans-activation gates have to date been less explored. We hypothesized that dual-binding Fabs could be engineered to generate a trans-activation logic gate in which IL-12 activity is controlled by competitive binding of the switch arm to a tumor-specific antigen. Furthermore, the presence of a second, non-switch Fab (referred to as “targeting arm”) that binds the tumor-specific antigen with high affinity enables avidity-driven binding in the tumor. This creates a reversible ON/OFF switch in which IL-12 is available upon tumor antigen engagement and auto-inhibited in the absence of antigen in a reversible manner (Figure 1b). A critical component of this dual-binding Fab strategy is the identification of an appropriate tumor-associated antigen to serve as the gating element for IL-12 activation. Fibronectin Extra Domain B (FN-EDB) is an attractive candidate, as it is abundantly expressed in the tumor extracellular matrix, with levels reaching 100 nM, while largely absent from normal adult tissues.13,14 FN-EDB is enriched at sites of active angiogenesis in cancer and wound healing, placing it near the tumor vasculature, which serves as the site of entry for infiltrating T cells. In addition, since FN-EDB is deposited in the extracellular matrix and not internalized, it allows for sustained antibody retention within the tumor. Finally, antibodies against FN-EDB have been successfully used in other tumor-targeted biologics, confirming its potential as a localization element for cytokine delivery.15–19

Here, we report the generation of a dual-binding Fab that binds both IL-12 and FN-EDB in a competitive manner, enabling the conditional activation of IL-12 only in the tumor microenvironment. This is, to our knowledge, the first demonstration of a trans-activation logic gate that functions as a reversible switch, enabling FN-EDB–dependent IL-12 exposure. The plausibility of the drug concept to confer a therapeutic index in vivo was established using a quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) model. Next, we used a phage screening and rational library design strategy to identify Fabs that demonstrate FN-EDB-dependent IL-12 availability and activity in preclinical assays. Finally, we used QSP to model the in vivo activity of the switch-IL-12 molecules generated, revealing an improved therapeutic window compared to clinically tested molecules, and identified strategies for further optimization of these molecules. Together, these findings demonstrate the value of dual-binding Fabs to engineer molecular switches that enable tissue-specific activation of potent immunostimulatory cytokines, such as IL-12.

Materials and methods

Production and characterization of antigens

The single-chain IL-12 (scIL-12) constructs were generated by cloning DNA encoding the human p40 (UniProt#: P29460) and human p35 (UniProt#: P29459) subunits linked by a flexible (G4S)3 linker into an expression vector. Genes encoding human Fibronectin EDB (UniProt#: P02751), either as a single domain (Glu1266 - Thr1356; EDB), or flanked by surrounding type III domains (F7B89 Val1170-Gly1533;), as well as a control construct comprising the type III domains without EDB (F789) were cloned into an expression vector. Each of the encoded proteins contains a C-terminal AviTag followed by six histidine residues.

Antigens were transiently expressed in Expi293 cells according to the manufacturer’s recommendation. Antigens were purified by affinity chromatography (HisTrap HP IMAC column, Cat. No. 17,524,801) followed by size exclusion chromatography using HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 75 pg or Superdex 200 pg (GE Healthcare, Cat. no. 28–9893-36 S200 or 28–9893-34 S75) columns on an AKTA system.

Purity of protein samples was analyzed by size-exclusion high-performance liquid chromatography (SEC-HPLC) and sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Briefly, 10 µg of purified proteins was injected onto a Superdex 200 Increase 5/150 GL (Cat. no. 28–9909-45) column, equilibrated in 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) pH7.4, using an isocratic mode at a flow rate of 0.45 mL/min for 10 min at 25°C.

Biotinylation of Antigens: scIL-12, F7B89, F789 and EDB were biotinylated using an enzymatic protein biotinylation kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. CS0008) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein biotinylation was confirmed by the streptavidin pull-down SDS-PAGE assay.

Phage display selections to identify FN-EDB binders

Selection of an scFv binder panel against FN-EDB (F7B89) from IONTAS phage display libraries was performed as described previously.20 Briefly, FN-EDB binders were enriched through two to three rounds of solution-phase phage display selections. Phage antibodies were incubated with 100 nM biotinylated antigen in solution, and the resulting phage antibody-antigen complex was then captured using streptavidin- or neutravidin-coated paramagnetic beads (Dyson et al., 2011). Deselection against recombinant Fibronectin (F789) (1 µM) and DNA (500 µg/mL) was carried out in all rounds. To maximize the diversity within the final antibody panel, libraries containing Kappa or Lambda light chains were selected separately.

Phage display selections to introduce scIL-12 binding into anti-FN-EDB scFv scaffolds

Dual-specificity antibodies were selected from HCDR1 and HCDR2 randomized libraries of FN-EDB binders. HCDR1 and HCDR2 libraries were constructed as shown in Figure 2 and subjected to three rounds of selection, alternating between an scIL-12 and FN-EDB. Deselection was in all rounds carried out against DNA (500 µg/mL) for both antigens and against recombinant F789 (1 uM) only in case of FN-EDB. For scIL-12 selection, competitive elution with 1 µM of anti-IL-12/IL-23 antibody Ustekinumab was performed in parallel to trypsin-based elution, in order to enrich antibodies that compete with Ustekinumab for IL-12 binding.

Affinity maturation by phage display

Affinity maturation libraries to optimize dual binders were generated as described in Figure 2. Phage display selections were performed as described above, alternating between scIL-12 and FN-EDB between rounds, using decreasing antigen concentration and phage input in subsequent rounds. Selection outputs from the separate LCDR1/LCDR2 and HCDR3/LCDR3 libraries were recombined en masse using inverse PCR to maintain linkage of HCDR3 and LCDR3 variants in the final combinatorial libraries. Subsequently, stringent solution phase phage display selections were performed again using decreasing antigen concentration and phage input in subsequent rounds. To mitigate the potential for enrichment of polyreactive clones by sequential selection against two antigens with negatively charged surfaces, subtractive selection pressure using DNA (500 µg/mL) and recombinant F789 (1 µM) was maintained throughout.

Diversification strategy

Diversification of HCDR1 and HCDR2 across all five germlines listed in Table S1 was achieved through targeted mutagenesis of selected residues within each CDR. Libraries were generated using trimer oligonucleotides to introduce sequence diversity at positions predicted to contribute to IL-12 binding. The choice of substitution amino acids was based on natural occurrence. Cysteine and methionine were excluded. The number of amino acids used per substitution varied and ranged between 3 and 18 amino acids.

LCDR1, LCDR2, and LCDR3 were diversified using trimer oligonucleotide synthesis. The introduced amino acids were selected based on their natural distribution, with the wild-type amino acid retained at a frequency of 50%, while the remaining amino acids were distributed equally. HCDR3 libraries were generated using high-throughput oligonucleotide synthesis targeting 1–2-mer variants, whereby each variant contained one or two amino acid substitutions relative to the wild-type sequence.

Dissociation-enhanced lanthanide fluorescence immunoassay

The ability of IgG formatted antibodies to specifically bind human FN-EDB was tested using a dissociation-enhanced lanthanide fluorescence immunoassay (DELFIA). Briefly, NuncTM Maxisorp 96-well plates were coated with Streptavidin at 10 μg/ml in PBS (50 μL/well) and incubated at 4°C overnight. Control wells were coated with DNA (10 ug/ml) or insulin (10 ug/ml). Three wash steps were performed with PBS. Biotinylated FN-EDB (F7B89), EDB and Fibronectin (F789) were prepared in 3% Marvel-PBS at 4 µg/mL, and 50 µL/well was added to the streptavidin coated plates and incubated for 15 minutes. Plates were washed three times with PBS. Further blocking was performed with 200 µl 3% (w/v) dried milk (Marvel) in PBS for 1 hour. 50 μL of antibody at the indicated concentration was added per well and incubated for 1 hour. Wash steps were carried out with PBS containing Tween20. Antibody binding was detected using anti-IgG Europium. The DELFIA system and BMG Pherastar instrument were used for signal detection.

Surface plasmon resonance off rate screening

Antibodies from selected phage display outputs were reformatted to IgG and off-rates for human FN-7B89 and scIL-12 were determined by surface plasmon resonance (SPR), using either the Sierra SPR-32 Pro (Bruker) or the Biacore 8K+ (Cytiva). In brief, IgG-containing supernatants or purified antibodies were captured on a surface pre-coated with anti-Fc via amine coupling. Antigens were injected in solution followed by dissociation, and baseline levels were restored to pre-antibody capture levels using regeneration solution (10 mM glycine-HCL pH 1.5 for 30 sec). Dissociation rate (Kd) constants obtained from single-cycle kinetics were used to rank antibody clones. Data acquired on the Sierra SPR-32 Pro were analyzed using Bruker Analyser R3 (v3.4.5.1) and GraphPad Prism, while data from the Biacore 8K+ were analyzed using Biacore Insight Evaluation software.

Affinity determination by SPR

Kinetic measurements of antibodies were performed using a Biacore 8K+ instrument (Cytiva). IgG antibodies were captured via an anti-human Fc ligand immobilized on a CM5 Series S sensor chip by amine coupling. Recombinant F7B89 and scIL-12 were prepared as two-fold serial dilutions in 1× HBS-EP buffer (pH 7.4), with concentration ranges of 1,000–7.81 nM and 50–0.39 nM, respectively. Analytes were injected for 2 min at a flow rate of 35 µL/min, followed by a 5 min dissociation phase. Regeneration was performed using 10 mM glycine-HCl (pH 1.5) for 30 s. Data were analyzed using Biacore Insight Evaluation Software, and dissociation constants were determined using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model.

Rational design of high affinity dual-binding variants

Rational design of high affinity dual-binding variants was performed using machine learning models that were trained on multiple datasets. Briefly, output from libraries following HCDR and LCDR diversification (as described in Figure 2a) was shuffled and repanned. From the resulting pool, 927 clones were reformatted into IgG format and screened using high-throughput SPR. The ESM2 model21 was used to embed CDR sequences, and subsequent fine-tuning was performed by training on single-antigen SPR koff measurements and phage output next-generation sequencing. Validation of the fine-tuned model was then used to predict single-antigen affinity for held-out CDR sequences. Sequences were then generated and scored by the single-affinity models to identify those predicted to have high affinity to each antigen.

Production of IgG and switch molecules

Antibodies were expressed in ExpiCHO cells as human IgG1 incorporating the L234A, L235A, and P329G (LALAPG) mutations. Purification was carried out using a two-step process, consisting of Protein A affinity chromatography (HiTrap MabSelect™ SuRe™), followed by SEC performed on an ÄKTA system. Protein purity was evaluated using microfluidic capillary electrophoresis – SDS (µCE-SDS) and SEC-HPLC.

Switch molecules were expressed in ExpiCHO cells as human IgG1 incorporating the L234A, L235A, and G237A (LALAGA) mutations. Heavy-chain heterodimerization was achieved using knob-into-hole (KIH) technology. ScIL-12 was fused to the N-terminus of the variable heavy chain of the dual-binding (knob) arm via a Gly-Ser linker. Linker lengths ranging from 25 to 40 amino acids were tested for IL12 activity, with the final Switch molecules having a linker length of 40 amino acids. The construct was co-transfected with the corresponding light chain and the hole-arm construct encoding the VHH anti-FN-EDB binding arm22 fused to the Fc. Binding kinetics of VHH anti-FN-EDB were verified using a control molecule (FN-EDB VHH x Ustekinumab-scIL12, which functions as an Always-OFF control molecule where IL-12 is always masked due to the high affinity of Ustekinumab to IL-12), as shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Affinity purification was performed using Protein A chromatography (HiTrap MabSelect™ SuRe™) to isolate heterodimeric species. Purity following affinity capture was evaluated by µCE-SDS and SEC with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS) and further increased by preparative SEC. In cases where the desired purity was not achieved, an additional purification step using ion-exchange chromatography was implemented with a molecule-specific optimized gradient elution. Final protein purity was assessed by µCE-SDS and SEC-MALS. Intact mass spectrometry was performed to confirm protein identity.

Biochemical detection of FN-EDB dependent switch-IL-12 availability

50 µL/well AffiniPure Goat Anti-human IgG, Fcγ fragment (Jackson ImmunoResearch Code:109–005-098) or biotinylated FN-EDB in PBS at 10 µg/ml was coated overnight at 4°C. Biotinylated FN-EDB density on ELISA plates was determined by measuring the saturation point of Eu-Strepatividin based detection relative to a Eu-Streptavidin standard curve. Assuming 1:1 binding between Eu-Streptavidin and biotinylated FN-EDB (approximately 1 biotin per molecule), saturation signal for biotinylated FN-EDB was calculated to be 0.083 pmol/well (1.66 nM/well). After washing three times in PBS, blocking was performed using PBS +3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) + 0.02% Tween20 for 1 hour at room temperature (RT) (300 µL/well). After washing 3 times in PBS, 50 µL/well serially diluted Switch-IL-12 molecules (16.6 nM − 0.023 nM in PBS +3% BSA +0.02%Tween20) were incubated in the absence or presence of biotinylated Ustekinumab Fab (10 nM) for 2 hours at 37οC. Binding of biotinylated Ustekinumab Fab to Switch-IL-12 molecules immobilized via FN-EDB binding or Fc capture was detected by adding Eu-Streptavidin in 3% BSA in PBS +0.02% Tween20 for 20 minutes at room temperature (50 µL/well). As an additional control, surface-bound Switch-IL-12 molecules were measured by incubation with Biotinylated anti-Kappa (in PBS +3% BSA +0.02% Tween20) for 1 hour at room temperature (50 µL/well), followed by detection using Eu-Streptavidin in Blocking Buffer for 20 minutes at RT, 50 µL/well. Finally, DELFIA enhancement solution (Perkin Elmer 4001–0010) was added (50 ul/well) and signal was quantified using BMG Pherastar (Excitation 340 nm, Emission 615 nm). To note, solution-phase‑based methods were tested alongside plate-deposition during early assay optimization. However, given the absence of FN-EDB dependent unmasking using solution-phase FN-EDB, and the natural deposition of FN-EDB in the extracellular matrix rather than solution, all ELISA‑based experiments were performed with plate-deposited FN-EDB.

Generation of HEK-FN-EDB cells

Vectors were created to allow expression of either a human FN-EDB (accession #P02751, Val1170 – Gly1533) or human Fibronectin fragment on the surface of the HEK293 cells. The Fibronectin fragment (F789) contains the human Fibronectin domains 7, 8 and 9, whereas the FN-EDB fragment (F7B89) also contains the additional domain B (EDB), flanked at the N-terminus by domain 7 and at the C-terminus by domain 8 and 9. Vector-encoded FN-EDB and Fibronectin fragments contain a Myc tag and the PDGFR transmembrane domain, and were introduced into suspension adapted HEK293 cells by transfection. Cell pools with stable expression of the indicated transgenes were generated through puromycin selection. FN-EDB expression was confirmed by flow cytometry following staining with L19-IgG, and the resulting stable cell pool was subsequently used in cell-based assays.

IFN-γ release neutralization assay

The neutralizing activity of IgG molecules was assessed by measuring IFN-γ release from NK-92 cells. NK-92 cells were seeded at 50,000 cells/well in 96-well plates (Corning, Cat. No. 3799). Antibodies were titrated from 1000 nM to 0.2 nM using a 4-fold serial dilution and pre-complexed with 1 nM scIL-12 for 1 hour at 37°C. The pre-complexed samples were then added to the NK-92 cells and incubated for 24 h at 37°C. Cell culture supernatants were collected and IFN-γ concentrations were determined using a Human IFN-γ DuoSet ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Cat. No. DY285B) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a PHERAstar plate reader (BMG Labtech), and data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism.

Detection of FN-EDB dependent switch IL-12 activity in cell-based assays

50,000 NK-92 cells were seeded per well on 96-well plates (Corning, Cat #3799). Test antibodies were pre-incubated with FN-EDB or Fibronectin expressing HEK293 cells at 37°C for 2 hours and then added to the NK-92 cells. Co-cultures were incubated at 37°C for 24 h and IFN-γ levels in supernatants were then measured using Human IFN-gamma DuoSet ELISA (R&D Systems, Cat# DY285B). IFN-γ levels were normalized to the FN-EDB binding of the constitutively active IL-12 control molecule L19-IL-12 and plotted using GraphPad Prism.

QSP modeling

A QSP model was developed to assess the feasibility of achieving tumor-selective IL-12 signaling by Switch-IL-12 molecules in patients with FN-EDB expressing solid tumors. An overview of the model structure and parameterization is described below with additional details provided in Supplementary Methods. The model is composed of central, peripheral, and tumor compartments, representing systemic blood circulation, the interstitial volume of tissues, and an FN-EDB expressing solid tumor, respectively. The model describes the intravenous administration of the Switch-IL-12 molecule into the central compartment, distribution into and out of the peripheral and tumor compartments from the central compartment, and the first-order elimination of the molecule from all three compartments. IL-12 receptor is maintained at steady-state in all three compartments, while FN-EDB is only found in the tumor compartment.

The model describes the association (masking) and dissociation (unmasking) of the tethered IL-12 cytokine to the switch arm of the molecule, the binding and unbinding of the unmasked IL-12 cytokine to the IL-12 receptor, and the binding and unbinding of both the target and switch arms of the molecule to FN-EDB. The higher effective local concentration of the IL-12 cytokine due to the tether is accounted for in the masking rate, while the potential attenuation of the tethered IL-12 cytokine binding to an IL-12 receptor is accounted for in the IL-12 receptor association rate. The model assumes that having one arm of the Switch-IL-12 molecule bound to FN-EDB will increase the likelihood of FN-EDB binding by the second arm (avid binding), which is captured in the model using a multiplier on the forward binding rate for the second arm of the molecule binding FN-EDB. The switch arm displays competitive binding between FN-EDB and the tethered IL-12 cytokine in that the switch arm can be bound to FN-EDB or the IL-12 cytokine, but not to both.

A literature search was performed to inform the physiological human parameters, including compartment volumes, IL-12 receptor expression and turnover, and distribution and elimination rates typical of monoclonal antibodies. An FN-EDB concentration in the tumor compartment of 100 nM was assumed.23 Initial estimates for binding and masking rates were used for the preliminary human dose projections. These parameter values were later updated based on the measured binding rates for the Switch1 and Switch2 versions of the Switch-IL-12 molecule and benchmarking model simulations using an in vitro version of the model to the ELISA data as described in the results section on FN-EDB dependent IL-12 availability. These data-informed parameter values were then used for the final in vivo pharmacological modeling projections. The subsequent scenario analysis reports the dose achieving a maximum of 20% systemic IL-12 receptor occupancy and the dose achieving an average of 50% tumor IL-12 receptor occupancy based on 14-day simulations following a single dose. The 20% metric for maximum systemic IL-12 receptor occupancy was chosen based on estimated IL-12 receptor occupancy at maximum tolerable doses reported for human recombinant IL-12. The 50% metric for average IL-12 receptor occupancy in the tumor was chosen as a conservative target for defining the therapeutic index acknowledging full coverage of the IL-12 receptor by an agonist is not necessary for antitumor effects. Scenario (1) used the unmodified Switch2 parameterization, while scenarios (2) - (7) each modified select parameters relative to Switch2 to isolate the impact of individual design properties on the therapeutic index. The parameter changes were as follows: (1) no changes; (2) k_mask = 0; (3) k_mask = 0 and koff_IL12R multiplied by 100; (4) kon_fibro = 0 for the target arm; (5) kon_fibro = 0 for the switch arm; (6) k_mask multiplied by 10; and (7) kon_IL12R multiplied by 0.1.

Additionally, a sensitivity analysis exploring how masking and binding parameters impact the therapeutic index and IL-12 receptor occupancy in the central and tumor compartments was performed by varying a subset of the parameters 100-fold from their nominal values for the Switch2 molecule. The results of the analysis are shown in Supplementary Figure S2. The model was implemented and simulations were performed with Applied BioMath QSP Notebook version 1.21.0, and all analysis was performed using Python version 3.12.

Results

QSP modeling of Switch-IL-12 predicts binding parameters to enable a higher therapeutic window compared to untargeted IL-12-Fc

QSP modeling was used to define the binding affinity scenarios for a Switch-IL-12 molecule capable of achieving a positive therapeutic window when simulated in vivo. The model simulated dose responses for three different cases, which include an IL-12-Fc with free (unmasked) IL-126, and two switch arm molecules that differ in their affinity for IL-12 receptor (parameters shown in Table S2, selected based on QSP modeling predicted values considered feasible to broaden the therapeutic window compared to unmasked IL12-Fc). The model predicts that a switch arm with high affinity for IL-12 (Kd ~0.27 nM) and low affinity for FN-EDB (Kd 160 nM) that is combined with a targeting arm with high affinity to FN-EDB (0.5 nM), would enable decreased IL-12 receptor occupancy systemically and improved IL-12 receptor occupancy in the tumor compartment compared to an unmasked IL-12 Fc (Figure 1c). The model predicted that the therapeutic window could be further enhanced through 10-fold attenuation of IL-12 binding to the IL-12 receptor, resulting in a positive therapeutic window. This work derisked the concept, as either scenario would represent a substantial improvement to safety, while maintaining tumor targeting.

Selection of dual binders for IL-12 and FN-EDB through phage library screening

A panel of 17 binders to FN-EDB was selected from a naïve phage display library based on specific binding to two forms of FN-EDB antigen (F7B89 and the isolated EDB domain) over splice variants of Fibronectin that lack the EDB domain (F789) and charged control molecules (DNA and insulin). Sequence diversity and FN-EDB dissociation kinetics similar to the clinical anti-FN-EDB benchmark antibody (L19)24 were also considered during clone triage to create the panel of FN-EDB binders for further engineering (Figure 2a, top panel). To maximize the likelihood of success in the absence of a priori knowledge concerning which of the FN-EDB binders would accommodate the introduction of IL-12 binding, a combinatorial approach was taken in which groups of binding scaffolds were pooled to construct oligoclonal libraries. In brief, five libraries were created from the 17 FN-EDB binders according to germline usage (Table S1), and in each of these HCDR1 and HCDR2 were diversified to generate libraries with theoretical diversities of 5.2E + 8, 1.8E + 9, 1.6E + 9, 1.1E + 9, and 7.8E + 8, respectively (Figure 2a, purple panel). Sequential phage display selections against IL-12 and FN-EDB were then performed to enrich for retained binding to FN-EDB and de novo binding to IL-12. To mitigate the potential for enrichment of polyreactive antibodies arising from cross-panning on unrelated antigens with high negative surface charge, stringent “de-selection” against DNA was performed. Using this approach, 34 sequence-unique dual binding antibodies (“dual binders”) able to specifically recognize both FN-EDB and IL-12 were identified. These dual binders were screened for recognition of neutralizing epitopes on IL-12 using a competitive binding assay with the clinically approved IL-12 neutralizing antibody Ustekinumab.25,26 This screen identified the H01 antibody, which showed the basic features required for “switch arm” generation, i.e., specific and potentially competitive binding to both FN-EDB and a neutralizing epitope of IL-12 (Figure 2a, middle panel).

To generate binders with affinities within the desired ranges based on QSP simulations, it was necessary to substantially increase the binding affinity of the H01 antibody for both targets. To address the challenge of simultaneous optimization for binding to two unrelated antigens, a two-stage strategy was devised to achieve a comprehensive search of combinatorial sequence space (Figure 2a, green panel). Briefly, two separate libraries diversifying either LCDR1 and LCDR2 (diversity = 3E + 9) or HCDR3 and LCDR3 (diversity = 2.8E + 9) of H01 were constructed. As the full combinatorial diversity of 5.8E + 18 could not be explored outright, an initial low stringency “library focusing” phage display selection was performed to remove deleterious variants and the enriched output populations were recombined to create a final library of 1.7E + 10 cfu covering beneficial variant combinations across all four CDRs. Library construction was performed using inverse PCR to maintain the linkage of variants in HCDR3 and LCDR3 during cloning of enriched LCDR1/LCDR2 mutations (Supplementary Figure S3). Stringent phage display selections were then performed to obtain high affinity binders to IL-12 and FN-EDB (with continued de-selection against DNA and Fibronectin to limit enrichment of sequences with unwanted off-target binding and polyreactivity). Off-rate screening of phage output populations resulted in the identification of clone Switch1 (Figure 2a, lower panel). Switch1 exhibited > 1,000-fold improved binding affinity (KD) relative to the H01 parent on both target antigens (Figure 2b).

While the Switch1 variant achieved sufficient affinity to FN-EDB, an additional increase in scIL-12 affinity was required to meet QSP modeling predictions. To this end, off-rate and sequence data for 927 clones were used for rational design-based optimization of additional variants. Analysis of panning sequence data and off-rate kinetics identified 4 backbone variants, including Switch1, that had diverse sequence and dual-affinity patterns, providing independent starting points where additional affinity could be introduced through rationally guided mutations. Machine learning models were trained on off-rates and predicted structures of Fab-antigen complexes (see Methods). Using these machine learning models 48 variants were then generated that included additional mutations layered on top of the 4 backbone variants, which were synthesized into IgG format and screened by SPR.

Off-rate characterization of the 48 variants identified multiple hits that achieved significant affinity increases to either one or both target antigens. A variant of Switch1 that included a single additional mutation in HCDR2 achieved 9-fold higher affinity to IL-12 while retaining comparable binding to FN-EDB. The binding properties and amino acid changes incorporated from the original FN-EDB binding antibody scaffold (clone A03), up to the final switch arm candidate, Switch2, are shown in Figure 2b. Affinities of Switch2 were in line with the affinities predicted to confer a positive therapeutic window by QSP modeling (Figure 1c) and comparable to those of single binding benchmark antibodies Ustekinumab, a clinically approved IL-12 neutralizing antibody25,26 and L19, a clinically validated anti-FN-EDB antibody15 (Figure 2c).

Characterization of switch arm variant binding kinetics and IL-12 neutralization

Binding kinetics of antibody arms Switch1 and Switch2 to FN-EDB and scIL-12 were determined by SPR (Figure 3a). Switch1 and Switch2 demonstrated similar binding to FN-EDB, with KD values of 180 nM and 250 nM, respectively. Switch1 and Switch2 recognized scIL-12 with binding affinities (KD) of 2.91 nM and 0.33 nM, respectively (Figure 3a). Both antibodies were able to inhibit IL-12 induced IFN-γ release from NK-92 cells (Figure 3b). The difference in IL-12 binding affinity between Switch2 and Switch1 translated into enhanced potency of IL-12 neutralization for Switch2, which exhibited an IC50 that was similar to the clinically approved IL-12 neutralizing antibody Ustekinumab (Figure 3b). Demonstration of FN-EDB dependent IL-12 availability and IL12R signaling by Switch-IL-12 molecules

The ability of Switch-IL-12 molecules to unveil IL-12 in the presence of FN-EDB was evaluated in an ELISA assay, in which IL-12 availability was probed in the presence and absence of FN-EDB using a biotinylated anti-IL-12 Fab (Figure 4a). A positive control molecule for IL-12 availability (Always ON, Figure 4b) was generated by tethering IL-12 to the anti-FN-EDB binder, L19, that is unable to bind IL-12 and therefore unable to mask IL-12. A negative control (Always OFF) molecule was generated by tethering IL-12 to Ustekinumab, which has high affinity for IL-12, leading to IL-12 masking (Always OFF, Figure 4b). To mimic systemic circulation, where no FN-EDB is present, Switch molecules, or control molecules were captured on an ELISA plate via their Fc domains using plates coated with an anti-Fc capture antibody (Figure 4a). In this scenario, both Switch-IL-12 candidates demonstrated minimal IL-12 availability, similar to the Always OFF control (Figure 4c,d). Hence, in the absence of FN-EDB, Switch-IL-12 molecules adopt the intended “masked” conformation in which the IL-12 moiety is internally ligated and largely unavailable for intermolecular interaction. A FN-EDB positive tumor state was simulated by coating plates with FN-EDB protein. Under these FN-EDB positive conditions, Switch1 showed robust IL-12 availability similar to the Always ON control. These findings demonstrate that the transition of a Switch-IL-12 molecule into the FN-EDB bound state is concomitant with release of the IL-12 moiety from intramolecular inhibition (“unmasking”). The candidate containing the Switch2 switch arm demonstrated more modest IL-12 availability, which only became apparent at high molecule concentrations (Figure 4c,d). It is likely that the increased affinity of this variant for IL-12 creates a higher threshold for its release from the masked state, especially in this in vitro model where, due to the technical limitations of achieving high FN-EDB concentrations when plated in vitro, the FN-EDB concentration in the in vitro assays is approximately 100-fold lower than in human tumors.

The ability of Switch1 to unmask IL-12 in the presence of FN-EDB was further evaluated in a functional cell-based assay. In this assay, Switch1 only induced dose-dependent IFNγ release from IL-12 R-expressing NK-92 cells in the presence of FN-EDB (Figure 4e). IFNγ release from NK-92 cells was not detectable with Switch1 in the absence of FN-EDB when normalized to the L19-IL-12 (Always ON) control.

In vivo pharmacological modeling predictions

Human IL-12 does not cross-react with the murine IL-12 receptor, complicating the use of in vivo models to study the pharmacology of our Switch molecules. We therefore used a QSP approach to predict human in vivo exposure and IL-12 receptor occupancy of the Switch molecules. The Switch molecule binding parameters (Figure 2b) and ELISA data (Figure 4) were used to parameterize and update the previously generated model that was used for affinity predictions as described in Figure 1c. The resulting data-informed model was then used to predict IL-12 receptor occupancy in vivo. As shown in Figure 5b, the Switch2 molecule exhibits markedly lower systemic IL-12 receptor occupancy and enhanced IL-12 receptor occupancy within the tumor compared to an “unmasked” IL-12-Fc molecule (Figure 5a), a profile predicted to translate into an improved therapeutic window. Notably, these predictions closely recapitulate the behavior of the initial model shown in Figure 1c, which served as the basis for defining the desired binding parameters during antibody selection. The QSP model was further leveraged to identify strategies to increase the predicted therapeutic window. These include enhancing systemic IL-12 masking through increased switch arm affinity to IL-12 (Figure 5c), or attenuating IL-12 activity (Figure 5d). The Switch1 molecule also demonstrated an improved therapeutic window, albeit to a lesser extent (Figure 5e-g). An analysis of several scenarios isolating individual Switch2 design properties showed that IL-12 masking was necessary for a reasonable therapeutic window by reducing systemic IL-12 receptor binding, while avid FN-EDB binding by both the target and switch arms was able to preserve tumor IL-12 receptor binding at levels comparable to those achieved by an unmasked IL-12 cytokine (Figure 5h). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate the development of a conditional trans-activation logic gate using a dual-binding Fab that serves as a switch arm by binding to IL-12 and FN-EDB in a competitive manner. The presence of a second, non-switch targeting arm enables avidity-driven binding to FN-EDB, thereby creating a reversible ON/OFF switch. This mechanism has the potential to substantially improve the therapeutic window of IL-12 or other cargo molecules by maintaining the cargo in a masked state in systemic circulation while enabling localized cargo availability in the tumor extracellular matrix upon FN-EDB binding.

Sequential phage display selections were successful in identifying dual binders against IL-12 and FN-EDB, although initial hits exhibited low affinity. Given the complexity of simultaneously selecting for dual specificity and neutralization of IL-12, multiple high-diversity libraries were required. Rational library design and affinity maturation enabled the generation of dual binders with affinities in the range predicted to confer a positive therapeutic window as evaluated by scenario testing using the QSP model. The ELISA-based IL-12 availability assay and the functional IFN-γ release assay both demonstrated FN-EDB‑dependent exposure of IL-12, confirming that binding of Switch-IL-12 to FN-EDB leads to release of the IL-12 moiety from intramolecular inhibition and functional availability of IL-12. Switch1 showed better unmasking in the ELISA and IFN-γ release assay compared to Switch2, likely due to the lower affinity of Switch1 to IL-12, thereby resulting in weaker masking.

One limitation of this study is the lack of translatable murine in vivo models to directly evaluate Switch-IL-12 pharmacology. Human IL-12 does not cross-react with the murine IL-12 receptor, necessitating either the development of murine surrogate Switch molecules, which would require parallel antibody discovery and optimization efforts, or the use of humanized models, which do not fully recapitulate human IL-12 biology. We therefore used QSP modeling to translate our in vitro findings to an in vivo context. QSP modeling predicts that Switch-IL-12 molecules result in substantially lower systemic IL-12 activity compared with the unmasked Fc-IL-12 that is similar to molecules currently in clinical trials.4 This reduction in systemic IL-12 exposure leads to a marked shift in the therapeutic window, which would be expected to translate into improved tolerability and efficacy in clinical settings. This effect is most pronounced for Switch2, which exhibits higher affinity for IL-12 and closely matches the affinities predicted for the switch arm by our original QSP modeling (Figure 1c). In line with this, the QSP model predicts that increasing IL-12 affinity would further enhance the therapeutic window by strengthening systemic masking without impairing IL-12 release in the tumor, which is primarily driven by high local FN-EDB concentration. QSP modeling further indicates that attenuation of IL-12 activity represents an orthogonal strategy to increase the therapeutic window. Finally, the QSP model demonstrated that Switch design containing both a switch and targeting arm is the optimal format to increase the therapeutic window; IL-12 masking is necessary to reduce systemic IL-12 receptor binding, while anti-FN-EDB binding of both the targeting arm and switch arm allows for tumor IL-12 receptor at levels comparable to those achieved by unmasked IL-12.

The importance of high FN-EDB concentrations for effective unmasking likely explains the limited unmasking observed in vitro with Switch2, as technical constraints on FN-EDB plating resulted in in vitro FN-EDB concentrations that were approximately 100-fold lower than those reported in human tumors. In addition, the inability of in vitro models to fully capture the three-dimensional intratumoral presentation of FN-EDB likely leads to an underestimation of the FN-EDB‑dependent IL-12 release, resulting in conservative estimates of the therapeutic window in our QSP model. In future work, patient-derived tumor fragments or ex vivo tumoroid models that preserve the native ECM architecture along with FN-EDB concentrations similar to those observed in human tumors could be explored further to test IL-12 activity.27

Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates that switch molecules can be created with a predicted improvement in therapeutic window. Switch2 exhibits promising FN-EDB-dependent IL-12 activity and a predicted therapeutic window that is superior to that of IL-12 therapeutics that have been evaluated in the clinic. Further optimization of Switch2, either through increased IL-12 affinity of the switch arm or through attenuation of the IL-12 cargo, has the potential to further expand the therapeutic window and yield a best-in-class, tumor-specific IL-12 therapy. More broadly, this work establishes a unique strategy for engineering dual-binding switch arms that enable logic-gated control of potent cytokine therapies.
