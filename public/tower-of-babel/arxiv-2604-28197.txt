OmniRobotHome : A Multi-Camera Platform for Real-Time Multiadic Human-Robot Interaction
Junyoung Lee 1∗, Sookwan Han 1∗, Jeonghwan Kim 1∗, Inhee Lee 1∗, Mingi Choi 1, Jisoo Kim 1, Wonjung Woo 1, Hanbyul Joo 1,2

Abstract
Human-robot collaboration has been studied primarily in dyadic or sequential settings. However, real homes require multiadic collaboration, where multiple humans and robots share a workspace, acting concurrently on interleaved subtasks with tight spatial and temporal coupling. This regime remains underexplored because close-proximity interaction between humans, robots, and objects creates persistent occlusion and rapid state changes, making reliable real-time 3D tracking the central bottleneck. No existing platform provides the real-time, occlusion-robust, room-scale perception needed to make this regime experimentally tractable. We present OmniRobotHome , the first room-scale residential platform that unifies wide-area real-time 3D human and object perception with coordinated multi-robot actuation in a shared world frame. The system instruments a natural home environment with 48 hardware-synchronized RGB cameras for markerless, occlusion-robust tracking of multiple humans and objects, temporally aligned with two Franka arms that act on live scene state. Continuous capture within this consistent frame further supports long-horizon human behavior modeling from accumulated trajectories. The platform makes the multiadic collaboration regime experimentally tractable. We focus on two central problems: safety in shared human-robot environments and human-anticipatory robotic assistance, and show that real-time perception and accumulated behavior memory each yield measurable gains in both.



1
Introduction

Enabling robots to assist with or replace human labor in everyday living environments remains a fundamental objective in robotics. Unlike structured industrial workspaces or constrained tabletop scenarios, household environments are inherently unstructured and dynamic. Humans move freely, interact with diverse objects, and continuously reconfigure their surroundings. A system that observes, understands, and anticipates such behavior can enable far more intelligent robotic assistance. Yet practical home scenarios go beyond a single robot assisting a single person: many tasks require multiple robots and humans to share the same workspace, acting concurrently on interleaved subtasks with tight spatial and temporal coupling—a regime we refer to as multiadic collaboration. Studying this regime requires distributed real-time perception that spans the full living space, tightly coupled with robotic hardware so that assistance is conditioned on live human and object state. The central bottleneck is reliable multi-agent 3D tracking: close-proximity interaction between humans, robots, and objects creates persistent occlusion and rapid state changes, making real-time, occlusion-robust, room-scale perception the critical missing capability.

Without such a platform, the core research questions of this regime—how to maintain safety under concurrent operation, how to infer human intent for timely handovers, and how to coordinate simultaneous actions with shared dependencies—have remained beyond experimental reach.

We present
OmniRobotHome
, a room-scale physical AI testbed that removes this bottleneck and enables systematic study of multiadic human-robot collaboration in a natural home environment. The system continuously perceives human activity and object state across the full living space, and provides coordinated multi-robot assistance conditioned on the shared, real-time 3D scene state, through four key capabilities.

(1) Large sensing coverage in a unified world frame.
The system tracks human location and full-body pose across a wide living space as the person moves naturally, supporting analysis of trajectories, behavioral patterns, and activity intent. A single consistent world frame shared by all cameras and robot arms provides the spatial foundation for multi-agent coordination and safety policies.

(2) Real-time markerless multi-human and multi-object 3D perception.
Unlike conventional multi-camera systems designed for offline processing, our system performs real-time markerless multi-human tracking with 6D object pose estimation. This allows robot actions to be conditioned on live human and object state rather than replayed data, enabling reactive safety and timing-sensitive interaction.

(3) Multi-robot actuation.
Two robot arms are deployed within the environment, temporally aligned with the shared perception stream. This enables multiadic interaction scenarios where multiple robots act concurrently with task-coupled dependencies on the human and on each other, and broadens the available workspace.

(4) Human behavior memory.
Continuous markerless capture within a consistent world frame enables long-term accumulation of behavioral data from residents, supporting modeling of habitual routines. Accumulated trajectories become priors that collaboration policies consume directly, enabling anticipatory assistance that improves with observation time.

Together, these four capabilities make
OmniRobotHome
the first platform that simultaneously provides room-scale real-time human and object sensing in 3D with coordinated multi-arm robotic assistance in a unified residential environment. Building such a system introduces substantial engineering challenges in calibration, synchronization, networking, and distributed processing; this paper presents practical solutions to each. More importantly, the platform makes multiadic collaboration experimentally tractable, enabling controlled studies that isolate each capability’s contribution. We focus on two central problems: (1)
safety in shared human-robot environments
: we demonstrate that real-time sensing and predictive modeling of human motion significantly improve safety when humans and robots coexist; and (2)
human-anticipatory robotic assistance
: we evaluate whether behavioral observation enables intent inference for anticipatory assistance, from placement prediction to pose-conditioned handovers.

Our contributions are: (1) a room-scale residential platform with 48 hardware-synchronized cameras and two robot arms; (2) an end-to-end real-time sensing pipeline for perception, data acquisition, and human motion prediction; and (3) systematic human-robot interaction studies in multiadic residential environments demonstrating that wide-area sensing, real-time perception, and long-term behavioral modeling each contribute measurable gains to safety and assistive performance. All code and data will be publicly released.

3
System Design

3.1
Overview

Exploring multiadic human-robot collaboration requires real-time, occlusion-robust, room-scale 3D perception tightly coupled with robotic actuation.
OmniRobotHome
provides this through a synchronized sensing and control architecture (
Fig.

1
).
We instrument a 23.1 m
2
residential space with 48 hardware-synchronized RGB cameras operating at 30 Hz. The cameras are distributed across 12 edge nodes that run real-time person detection and 2D pose estimation in parallel. A central server triangulates 3D whole-body keypoints and estimates object 6D poses, producing a unified scene state in a shared world frame. This scene state is temporally aligned with two Franka Research 3 arms, enabling multi-robot actuation conditioned on live human and object state. Continuous capture within this consistent frame further accumulates behavioral data over time, providing trajectory priors that improve both safety responses and anticipatory assistance.

3.2
Hardware Configuration

Capture Space Setup.

Our capture space spans
23.1\,\mathrm{m}^{2}
and is instrumented with 48 RGB cameras to provide dense visual coverage of the entire space and reduce occlusions. Among those, we set 2 cameras placed as a stereo pair facing toward the tabletop for 6D object pose tracking.

Robot Setup.
Our system deploys two Franka Research 3 arms, each equipped with a parallel-jaw gripper at the end-effector.

Hardware Synchronization.

All RGB cameras are synchronized via an external hardware trigger. We extract timestamps from a reference camera to trigger the robot’s data reception, ensuring that all detection and reconstruction outputs are temporally aligned.

3.3
System Calibration

Placing 48 cameras and two robot arms in a shared world frame requires three calibration stages. First, each camera is intrinsically calibrated with ChArUco boards
[
12
]
using Zhang’s method
[
59
]
. Second, we recover extrinsic poses by solving PnP
[
14
,
37
]
across 16 distributed ChArUco boards, unifying all cameras into a single world frame via a bipartite pose graph, and refining jointly with bundle adjustment over board poses, camera poses, and optionally intrinsics. This custom pipeline outperforms COLMAP
[
41
]
in our setting because it avoids accuracy degradation in regions without calibration patterns. Third, each robot base is registered into the camera world frame via hand-eye calibration
[
45
]
, placing the full kinematic chain in the unified coordinate frame. Detailed formulations are provided in the supplementary material.

Multi-View Human Pose Estimation.

The 48 cameras generate approximately 13.5 GB/s of raw data. We handle this with a distributed architecture: each of 12 edge nodes runs TensorRT-optimized YOLO26

Object 6D Pose Estimation.

Our system uses only RGB cameras, as dedicated depth sensors lack the hardware synchronization support needed across 48 streams. We adopt a fully learned pipeline on a calibrated stereo pair: FoundationStereo
[
50

(TensorRT-optimized) provides dense metric depth, YOLO-E

4.1
Research Questions

We ground both problems in a shared kitchen organization scenario. Food items are distributed across a table and kitchen surfaces, and two robot arms must concurrently sort each item to its correct destination; fresh produce to the sink, packaged goods to shelves; while a human moves freely through the workspace. The two arms divide the workload across spatially separated regions, perceive scene state through the shared real-time 3D stream, and must cooperate with each other (e.g., handing off items between table and kitchen) and with the human throughout. This setup stresses all four system capabilities: room-scale coverage localizes the human and objects across the full space, real-time tracking feeds live 3D state to both arms, multi-robot actuation broadens the workspace to kitchen and table via cooperative handovers, and behavior memory provides priors on human routines.

Safety in shared workspaces.

The core question is whether real-time 3D human-state conditioning enables policies that are simultaneously safe and productive, and whether accumulated behavioral history can further improve safety by anticipating human motion before it occurs. Room-scale coverage ensures the human is always observed; real-time perception lets each arm react instantly; behavior memory enables preemptive avoidance. We replay recorded human trajectories in our custom simulation environment, comparing four safety policies of increasing sophistication and one augmented with behavior learning. We measure collision count and cycle time (
Table

1
), analyze behavior memory’s effect on anticipatory safety (
Fig.

4

Fig.

Human-anticipatory assistance.

The question is whether the robot can infer a human’s sorting intent from partial observation from our system and generalize to unseen categories. We frame this as a placement prediction task: given that the human has placed
k
of 12 objects (6 fruits to the sink, 6 packaged goods to the shelf), the robot must predict the correct destination for each remaining item. We compare a lookup baseline, which copies exact observed placements, against two LLM-based methods, a direct model (LLM) and one augmented with chain-of-thought reasoning (+ Reasoning), both receiving demonstrations as text via domain-neutral prompts without category hints. We measure correct placement ratio as a function of observed demonstration count (

Qualitative Demonstrations

Beyond the kitchen scenario, we demonstrate intention-aware transfer with increasing coupling between agents (

Intention-aware transfer.

The robot must infer which object a human needs and deliver it with timing conditioned on real-time pose. The pipeline proceeds in three stages: (1) the system detects the human in the workspace via multi-view 3D tracking; (2) a VLM infers the target object from the human’s 3D pose and surrounding scene context (objects and spatial configuration around the human); and (3) the robot executes a pick-handover-place sequence from the shelf to the human’s hand, with the handover target continuously updated from the human’s real-time 3D hand keypoints rather than committed to a fixed pose. The transfer initiates when short-horizon cues indicate readiness and delays when the human disengages. We demonstrate three scenarios (
Fig.

5
): (a) a human notices a dry plant and the robot delivers a watering kettle, (b) a human prepares to eat a hot dog and the robot hands over a mustard bottle, and (c) a human eating chips gestures for a drink and the robot delivers a soft drink. In all cases, the system successfully identifies the needed object from pose and scene cues and adapts the handover trajectory online as the human’s reach evolves.

4.4
System Ablations

We validate core system components quantitatively: calibration accuracy against a standard baseline, and the effect of camera count on pose coverage and reconstruction quality.

Calibration accuracy (
Table

3
).

We compare our custom ChArUco-based calibration pipeline against COLMAP, both evaluated with markers captured at multiple random positions in the capture space. Our pipeline reduces the mean reprojection error from 1.42 px to 1.21 px and the standard deviation from 1.09 px to 0.80 px, indicating both lower bias and tighter consistency across camera views. When we reproject every triangulated 3D corner back into its observing camera views, 48.9% of reprojections land within 1 px of their detected location (vs. 43.9% for COLMAP), and 86.8% within 2 px (vs. 77.3%).

5
Conclusion

We presented
OmniRobotHome
, a room-scale residential platform that makes multiadic human-robot collaboration experimentally tractable. The system instruments a 23.1 m
2
home environment with 48 hardware-synchronized RGB cameras for markerless, occlusion-robust 3D tracking of multiple humans and objects in a unified world frame, temporally aligned with two robot arms for multi-robot actuation conditioned on live scene state. Continuous capture within this consistent frame further accumulates behavioral trajectories that the system converts into priors for safer and more anticipatory assistance.

Controlled studies on two central problems isolate the contribution of each capability. For safety in shared workspaces, conditioning on real-time 3D human state breaks the safety-throughput tradeoff that static-radius policies cannot resolve, reducing collisions by 2.6 times at improved cycle time, and accumulated behavior memory further reduces collisions with no throughput penalty by anticipating human motion before it occurs. For human-anticipatory assistance, partial observation of a resident’s behavioral history already disambiguates intent: the system reaches full placement accuracy from half the demonstration sequence, while a copy baseline requires the complete sequence. Behavior memory reveals complementary dynamics across the two problems: safety benefits from a compact early observation window that captures dominant movement patterns, while intent prediction requires demonstrations spanning both object categories before converging. Qualitative demonstrations of intention-aware transfer extend the coupling further: the system infers the target object from real-time 3D human pose and scene context and adapts the handover trajectory online as the human’s reach evolves. System ablations confirm that the infrastructure supports these results, with calibration achieving 1.21 px mean reprojection error versus 1.42 px for COLMAP and 48 cameras providing over 80% volumetric coverage across the full room. Across both problems, the common enabling factor is continuous room-scale perception in a unified world frame: real-time 3D state drives reactive safety and timing-sensitive interaction, while accumulated behavioral priors improve anticipatory performance, together making the multiadic regime observable and actionable for the first time.

Limitations and future work.

Our evaluation uses a single instrumented room with two fixed-base arms and task-specific controllers; generalization across layouts, embodiments, and control interfaces remains open. Future work includes integrating mobile robots, expanding to diverse room configurations, and releasing synchronized multiadic sequences with calibrated 3D trajectories, object poses, robot states, and task annotations. Appendix 0.A
System Details

0.A.1
Hardware Details

Camera configuration.

The perception system comprises a synchronized network of 48 industrial RGB cameras (FLIR Blackfly S BFLY-PGE-31S4C-C) capturing
2048\times 1536
images at 30 Hz. We employ a hybrid lens configuration to balance environmental context with fine-grained detail: 24 wide-angle lenses (
f{=}3
mm) provide broad spatial coverage for human pose estimation, while 24 telephoto lenses (
f{=}6
mm) enable detailed capture—of which 8 are arranged as 4 stereo pairs for real-time 6D object pose estimation. Exposure is globally fixed at 2.5 ms to minimize motion blur while maintaining an adequate signal-to-noise ratio.

Synchronization and data acquisition.

Frame-level synchronization is enforced via a hardware trigger (10 Vpp square wave, 50% duty cycle) generated by a UNI-T UTG962 unit and distributed through a hierarchical chain of 8-port GPIO signal distributors.
Data acquisition is distributed across 12 capture nodes connected via GigE Vision interfaces, each managing four cameras. Ten nodes are dedicated to pose estimation (40 cameras), while two nodes each host two calibrated stereo pairs for 6D object pose estimation (8 cameras). A dedicated server aggregates all data streams in real-time. Each capture node is equipped with dual local SSDs (500 GB + 1 TB) for high-bandwidth buffering, and 15 LED panels (4500 lm each) provide uniform illumination throughout the workspace.

0.A.2
Extrinsic Calibration Pipeline

Placing 48 cameras in a shared world frame requires a robust extrinsic calibration pipeline.
A common choice is COLMAP
[
41
]
, but it requires pattern placement throughout the scene and its accuracy degrades when tracking objects in regions where patterns were missing during calibration.
We therefore customize an extrinsic calibration pipeline using multiple ChArUco boards as robust and precise targets, together with a custom bundle adjuster.

Per-board PnP initialization

We place 16 ChArUco boards in the capture space and solve the Perspective-N-Point (PnP)
[
14
,
37
]
problem for every camera-board pair, recovering a per-board-to-camera pose
T_{c\leftarrow b}\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
.

Bipartite graph unification

These independent solutions are unified into a single world frame by constructing a bipartite camera-board pose graph and traversing it with BFS from the most-connected camera, chaining
\mathrm{SE}(3)
transforms along minimum-error paths and additionally transforming all poses to Board 0 as the world origin.

Bundle adjustment

A custom bundle adjustment refines the result in two phases: an extrinsics-only pass that optimizes board and camera poses while holding intrinsics fixed, followed by a full joint optimization over board poses, camera poses, and optionally intrinsic parameters.
The optimization solves:

\underset{{T_{b},T_{c},(\mathbf{K}_{c})}}{\arg\min}\sum_{i}\left\lVert\Pi\left(T_{c},T_{b},\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\text{loc}}\right)-\mathbf{u}_{i}\right\rVert^{2}

(1)

where
T_{b}\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
is the board pose,
T_{c}\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
is the camera pose,
\mathbf{K}_{c}
denotes camera intrinsics (optional),
\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\text{loc}}
is the fixed 3D corner coordinate on the board,
\mathbf{u}_{i}
is the detected 2D pixel location, and
\Pi(\cdot)
is the full camera projection.
We optionally further refine the output with SIFT feature points using Ceres Solver with the default bundle-adjustment formulation from COLMAP.

0.A.3
Hand-Eye Calibration Pipeline

The handover precomputation in
Section

0.B.2
assumes that both
robots and the multi-camera perception system occupy a shared workspace.
Aligning robot kinematics with camera-defined world coordinates requires
accurate base-to-world transforms for each arm.
This section describes the automated pipeline that estimates these transforms,
registering each Franka FR3 into the multi-camera world frame.

We attach a ChArUco calibration board to the robot flange and estimate two
primary unknowns: the flange-to-board transform
X\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
and the
robot-base-to-world transform
Z\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
.
These satisfy the frame chain

T^{world}_{board,i}\,X=Z\,T^{base}_{flange,i},

(2)

where
T^{base}_{flange,i}
denotes forward kinematics at calibration sample
i

and
T^{world}_{board,i}
is the board pose estimated from synchronized multi-view
images at that sample.
We additionally register a fixed anchor board near each robot base,
enabling single-capture re-localization of the base pose without
repeating the full hand-eye procedure.

Data acquisition

Solving for
Z
and
X
requires paired observations of board pose and robot
joint state across configurations with sufficient rotational spread to
constrain both unknowns.
To maximize this diversity without manual intervention, the robot automatically
visits
N{=}20
perturbed configurations around a center pose
(R_{c},\mathbf{t}_{c})
.
Translation and rotation perturbations are drawn independently:

\Delta\mathbf{t}\sim\mathcal{U}(-r_{p},r_{p})^{3},\;r_{p}{=}0.07\,\text{m},\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}_{local}\sim\mathcal{U}(-r_{\theta},r_{\theta})^{3},\;r_{\theta}{=}30^{\circ},

(3)

and the perturbed pose is constructed by additive translation and
right-multiplied local-frame rotation:

\mathbf{t}_{pert}=\mathbf{t}_{c}+\Delta\mathbf{t},\quad R_{pert}=R_{c}\exp([\boldsymbol{\omega}_{local}]_{\times}).

(4)

The robot executes smooth min-jerk trajectories between poses at low velocity
to ensure settling before capture.
At each configuration, a hardware-synchronized multi-camera capture is
triggered and the joint angles
\mathbf{q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{7}
are recorded.
Camera intrinsics and extrinsics are treated as fixed throughout and are
loaded from a prior multi-camera calibration.

Multi-view board pose estimation

For each capture index
i
, we estimate the calibration board pose

T^{world}_{board,i}
by minimizing multi-view reprojection error across all
cameras that detected the board.

Detection.

ChArUco corners are detected in each camera image, yielding 2D–3D
correspondences

\left\{(\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{ij},\,\mathbf{p}^{B}_{j})\right\}_{j=1}^{N_{m}},

(5)

where
m
indexes cameras,
\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a
detected corner, and
\mathbf{p}^{B}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the known corner
position in the board frame.

Initialization.

For each camera with detections, we compute an initial board pose via PnP.
Because planar targets can produce ambiguous solutions, we validate each
candidate and apply a reflection correction when the board normal is
inconsistent with the camera viewing direction.
The initialization with the lowest cross-view reprojection error seeds
global optimization.

Multi-view refinement.

We refine the board pose by minimizing the multi-view reprojection residual:

\min_{T^{world}_{board,i}}\sum_{m}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{m}}\rho\!\left(\left\|\pi\!\left(T^{cam_{m}}_{world}\,T^{world}_{board,i}\,\mathbf{p}^{B}_{j}\right)-\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{ij}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right),

(6)

where
T^{cam_{m}}_{world}
is the fixed world-to-camera extrinsic,

\pi(\cdot)
applies perspective projection with radial-tangential distortion,
and
\rho(\cdot)
is a robust loss.
Optimization proceeds in two stages: a Cauchy loss for outlier rejection
followed by a Huber loss for precision refinement.

Hand-eye solve and anchor localization

With board poses
\{T^{world}_{board,i}\}
and forward kinematics

\{T^{base}_{flange,i}\}
computed from recorded joint angles via Pinocchio
with the FR3 URDF, we solve for
Z
and
X
through relative and absolute
constraints.

Relative constraints for
Z
.

For each pose pair
(i,j)
, we form relative-motion matrices

A_{ij}=T^{world}_{board,i}\!\left(T^{world}_{board,j}\right)^{-1},B_{ij}=T^{base}_{flange,i}\!\left(T^{base}_{flange,j}\right)^{-1},

(7)

and enforce the classical hand-eye constraint
A_{ij}\,Z=Z\,B_{ij}
.
We initialize
Z
with the closed-form Tsai-Lenz solver and refine by
robust nonlinear least squares over all pose pairs.

Absolute constraints for
X
.

With
Z
fixed, we recover
X
from the frame chain

T^{world}_{board,i}\,X=Z\,T^{base}_{flange,i}
, again via nonlinear least
squares with robust loss.

Anchor board localization.

In addition to the moving calibration board, we place a fixed anchor ChArUco
board in the workspace.
Its pose
T^{world}_{anchor}
is estimated by the same multi-view reprojection,
and we derive the robot-base pose in the anchor frame as

T^{anchor}_{base}=\left(T^{world}_{anchor}\right)^{-1}Z,

(8)

providing a stable reference for re-localizing the base without repeating
the full calibration.

0.A.4
Camera Count and Voxel Coverage Analysis

This section describes the camera-count analysis underlying
Fig.

6
in the
main paper.
We quantify how workspace coverage degrades as cameras are removed, providing
empirical justification for the 48-camera configuration.

Voxelization.

We reconstruct a reference point cloud of the workspace using COLMAP and
voxelize it at 1 cm resolution.
Each occupied voxel center is tested for visibility from each camera,
producing a binary
C\times V
frustum-visibility matrix
\mathbf{F}
, where

C=48
is the camera count and
V
is the number of occupied voxels.

Frustum visibility check.

A voxel
\mathbf{v}
is visible from camera
c
if it satisfies four
conditions:
(1) the world-to-camera transform places it in front of the camera
(
z>0
);
(2) its depth falls within the valid range
[d_{\min},d_{\max}]
;
(3) perspective projection maps it inside the image bounds; and
(4) no occlusion invalidates the line of sight (not modeled; we rely on the
high camera redundancy to compensate for furniture and robot-arm occlusions).

Coverage fraction.

For a camera subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{1,\dots,C\}
of size
k
, the
coverage at threshold
M
is the fraction of voxels visible from at least
M

cameras in
\mathcal{S}
:

\text{cov}(\mathcal{S},M)\;=\;\frac{1}{V}\sum_{j=1}^{V}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\sum_{c\in\mathcal{S}}F_{c,j}\;\geq\;M\right].

(9)

For each camera count
k
, we sample 25 random subsets and report the mean
coverage.
We evaluate
M\in\{1,2,4,6,10,15\}
, spanning single-view detection
through dense multi-view stereo requirements.

Farthest-point camera ordering.

To obtain a deterministic removal sequence that maximally preserves spatial
diversity, we apply a greedy farthest-point ordering.
Starting from the full set, the algorithm iteratively selects the camera whose
removal least reduces the minimum pairwise distance among the remaining
cameras:

c^{*}=\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{c\}\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\lVert\mathbf{t}_{i}-\mathbf{t}_{j}\rVert_{2},

(10)

where
\mathbf{t}_{i}
is the translation component of camera
i
.
This
O(N^{2})
procedure yields a progressive removal order used for the composite visualization (
Fig.

6
(h)) and the supplementary video; the coverage-fraction curves in
Fig.

6
(g) use random subset sampling as described above.

Joint visibility and triangulation error.

For 3D human pose estimation, a joint must be visible in at least two cameras
to be triangulated.
The triangulation error is bounded by
\epsilon\propto 1/\sin\theta
, where

\theta
is the angle between the two viewing rays.
Cameras that are nearly co-linear yield large
\theta^{-1}
and thus poor
depth estimates; the farthest-point ordering preserves wide baselines and
keeps
\theta
large as cameras are removed.
These results feed into
Fig.

6
, confirming that the 48-camera array provides
substantial redundancy while demonstrating graceful degradation down to
approximately 12–16 cameras for most tasks.

0.A.5
Multi-View Human Pose Estimation Pipeline

Our human detection pipeline operates on a distributed client-server architecture, where each capture node independently performs 2D pose estimation and transmits results to a central server for multi-view 3D triangulation.

2D Pose Estimation.

Each capture node processes frames from its designated cameras through a two-stage detection pipeline. First, a TensorRT-optimized YOLO26
[
40
]
detector performs batch inference on full-resolution frames (
2048\times 1536
) to localize persons with a confidence threshold of 0.5. To mitigate momentary occlusions, we employ a temporal carry-over mechanism that propagates the last valid bounding box for up to 3 frames with a confidence decay factor of 0.85. Detected person regions are cropped, affine-warped to
384\times 288
, and fed into an RTMPose
[
17
]
model (also TensorRT-accelerated). The model outputs 133 keypoints per person via SimCC
[
30
]
decoding, covering the body (17), feet (6), face (68), and hands (42). Finally, 2D keypoints are undistorted using calibrated lens parameters before transmission.

Data Aggregation.

Clients transmit keypoint data to the server via ZMQ PUSH/PULL sockets using msgpack serialization. The server tracks the liveness of each camera and promotes a frame for triangulation only when at least 60% of the active pose cameras (minimum 4) report data within a 60-frame window, ensuring sufficient multi-view coverage.

Cross-Camera Person Association.

When multiple persons are present, we adopt a two-stage association strategy inspired by
[
7
]
: the first stage tracks confirmed persons via projection matching, while the second stage initializes newly appearing persons through geometric consensus.

In Stage 1, for each tracked person
p
with known 3D joint positions
\mathbf{J}_{p}
, we project the four torso keypoints (hips and shoulders) into every camera view using the calibrated projection matrices.
To handle brief occlusions, we apply a velocity-corrected prediction
\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{p}=\mathbf{J}_{p}+\mathbf{v}_{p}\cdot n_{\text{miss}}
, where
\mathbf{v}_{p}
is an exponentially weighted moving average of per-frame displacement with momentum
\alpha=0.7
and displacement clamped at
0.5
m/frame, and
n_{\text{miss}}
counts consecutive unmatched frames. For each camera, we construct a cost matrix of Euclidean distances between the projected and detected 2D positions, using a depth-adaptive threshold
\tau=\tau_{\text{base}}\cdot(d_{\text{ref}}/d_{\text{cam}})
(
\tau_{\text{base}}{=}150
px,
d_{\text{ref}}{=}3.0
m) to compensate for perspective foreshortening. The Hungarian algorithm
[
27
]
is then applied per camera to obtain optimal assignments.

In Stage 2, unmatched 2D detections across cameras are considered as candidates for new person initialization. We extract the hip midpoint from each unmatched detection, enumerate all pairwise camera combinations, and triangulate via DLT. Each triangulated candidate is reprojected into all cameras, and we count inlier views whose reprojection error falls below 20 pixels. Candidates are greedily accepted in order of decreasing inlier count, requiring agreement from at least 3 cameras; accepting a candidate suppresses all 2D detections it claims, preventing duplicate initializations.

We manage track lifecycles with three mechanisms. First, a
confirmation gate
requires newly detected persons to be consistently matched for 3 consecutive frames before their keypoints enter the triangulation pool. Second, persons that receive no match for 60 frames are pruned from the active set. Third, a
re-identification cache
retains the last known 3D position of pruned persons for 90 frames (
{\sim}
3 s); if a newly initialized person appears within 1 m of a cached position, the original identity is restored, preserving continuity across temporary disappearances.

3D Triangulation.

Of the 133 detected keypoints, we triangulate only the 65 non-face keypoints (body, feet, and hands), as face landmarks are too fine-grained for room-scale 3D reconstruction.
For each confirmed person in a synchronized frame, the associated 2D keypoint observations from matched camera views are triangulated using the Direct Linear Transform (DLT). Each keypoint is solved independently by constructing an overdetermined system from the 2D observations and corresponding projection matrices, followed by an SVD-based solution. Triangulation is restricted to keypoints observed in at least two camera views with a confidence score exceeding 3.0, where the score is defined as
\sqrt{s_{x}\cdot s_{y}}
from the SimCC
[
30
]
logit maxima along each spatial axis. To suppress outlier detections, we enforce RANSAC-based rejection with a 3-view minimal sample set, running up to 30 iterations, and a reprojection error threshold of 2 pixels.

Temporal Smoothing.

Triangulated 3D joints are processed by per-joint One Euro Filters
[
4
]
(
f_{min}=1.0
Hz,
\beta=0.007
) to adaptively balance jitter reduction during stationarity and responsiveness during fast motion. To prevent drift during long occlusions, filter states are re-initialized if a joint remains undetected for more than 10 consecutive frames.

0.A.6
Object 6D Pose Estimation Pipeline

Our system uses only RGB cameras, as dedicated depth sensors lack the hardware synchronization support needed across 48 streams.
We adopt a fully learned pipeline built on a calibrated stereo pair from our existing RGB cameras.

Depth estimation.

We estimate dense metric depth with FoundationStereo
[
50
,
49
]
, optimized with TensorRT and run on downsampled images (
672\times 448
) for real-time throughput.
We capture a stereo pair
(I_{L},I_{R})
at
2048\times 1536
, downsample for TensorRT inference, and undistort and rectify the images to obtain
(I^{\prime}_{L},I^{\prime}_{R})
.

Segmentation and 6D tracking.

Given the estimated depth, YOLO-E
[
40
]
provides open-vocabulary segmentation masks that are fed, together with the depth map, into FoundationPose
[
51
]
for marker-free 6D tracking.
FoundationPose requires only a template mesh; for objects without pre-existing CAD models, we reconstruct meshes offline using MV-SAM3D
[
5
]
, a multi-view extension of SAM3D that leverages our dense camera array.

World-frame alignment.

As the reconstructed mesh and estimated pose are not inherently aligned to our pre-calibrated world frame, we further optimize the scale
s
, rotation
R
, and translation
t
via silhouette reprojection across multiple views.

Runtime performance.

The end-to-end tracking pipeline operates at approximately 6 Hz; while slower than our human pose estimation loop, manipulated objects typically exhibit lower velocities, making this rate sufficient for downstream robot planning.

0.B.2
Pick-Handover-Place Pipeline

At runtime, the Pick-Handover-Place (PHP) pipeline must translate precomputed robot-to-robot handover poses into coordinated dual-arm motion while reacting to perception updates, contact events, and human proximity.
This section describes the handover precomputation, execution architecture that bridges offline planning with real-time control, and the phase-specific controllers for force-adjust pick and compliant handover.

Handover Precomputation

The runtime policies in
Section

0.B.2
depend on category-specific handover poses computed offline.
This section details the procedure that produces those poses.
Given calibrated base-to-world transforms for both robots (
Section

0.A.3
), we search for a pair of joint configurations
(\mathbf{q}_{g},\mathbf{q}_{r})
that realize a stable handover geometry at a mutually reachable position, subject to object-category grasp constraints and kinematic quality criteria.

Candidate position sampling.

Handover candidates are sampled in the mutual workspace of both arms.
Let
\mathbf{b}_{g}^{xy}
and
\mathbf{b}_{r}^{xy}
denote the XY coordinates of each robot base in the world frame.
We place a nominal center at

\mathbf{c}^{xy}=\frac{\left(\mathbf{b}_{g}^{xy}+\mathbf{b}_{r}^{xy}\right)}{2},c^{z}=\max\left(\frac{b_{g}^{z}+b_{r}^{z}}{2}+0.45,\;0.4\right)

(11)

and sample a grid around
\mathbf{c}
with fixed extents.
A candidate
\mathbf{p}
is retained only if it lies within the reachable annulus of both robots:

\displaystyle d_{i}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{xy}-\mathbf{b}_{i}^{xy}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle 0.35\leq d_{g}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\leq 0.75\ \wedge\ 0.35\leq d_{r}(\mathbf{p})\leq 0.75.

(12)

Object-category handover constraints.

Each object class maps to a geometric constraint family that specifies the receiver TCP pose relative to the giver.
We define two families.

Spherical objects.

Both grippers target the same center region with opposite approach directions and perpendicular finger orientations to avoid collision.
Given giver pose
(\mathbf{p}_{g},R_{g})
, the receiver pose is

\mathbf{p}_{r}=\mathbf{p}_{g}+R_{g}\,\Delta\mathbf{p},\qquad R_{r}=R_{g}\,R_{z\_anti,x\_perp}

(13)

where
\Delta\mathbf{p}=[0.03,0,0.01]^{\top}
m and

R_{z\_anti,x\_perp}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

(14)

Constraint satisfaction is scored from the giver and receiver TCP axes:

\displaystyle s_{z}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1-\langle\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{g},\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{r}\rangle}{2},\quad s_{x}=1-\left|\langle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{g},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{r}\rangle\right|,

\displaystyle s_{tcp}

\displaystyle=0.6\,s_{z}+0.4\,s_{x}

(15)

Elongated objects.

The robots grasp at offset positions along the object length, with antiparallel
x
axes and parallel
y
axes.
The receiver pose is

\mathbf{p}_{r}=\mathbf{p}_{g}+R_{g}\,\Delta\mathbf{p},\qquad R_{r}=R_{g}\,R_{x\_anti,y\_par}

(16)

where
\Delta\mathbf{p}=[0.06,0,0]^{\top}
m and

R_{x\_anti,y\_par}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

(17)

The corresponding score is

\displaystyle s_{y}

\displaystyle=\left|\langle\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{g},\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{r}\rangle\right|,\quad s_{x}=\tfrac{1-\langle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{g},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{r}\rangle}{2},

\displaystyle s_{tcp}

\displaystyle=0.5\,s_{y}+0.5\,s_{x}

(18)

Orientation sampling and bimanual IK.

For each candidate position
\mathbf{p}
, we sample giver TCP orientations on a grid over yaw
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N_{\theta}}
and tilt
\{\phi_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N_{\phi}}
up to
\phi_{\max}
.
For each sampled giver pose
(\mathbf{p}_{g},R_{g})
, we compute the receiver pose
(\mathbf{p}_{r},R_{r})
via the object-category rule above, transform both targets into each robot’s base frame, and solve IK with multiple random restarts.
A candidate is valid if both IK calls succeed within position tolerance.

Multi-criteria scoring and selection.

Among valid bimanual solutions, we rank configurations by smoothness, kinematic quality, joint safety, and category-constraint satisfaction.
Let
w(\mathbf{q})
denote Yoshikawa manipulability,
m(\mathbf{q})
a joint-limit margin, and
c(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}_{pre})
continuity to a reference prepick configuration.
The total score is

S=\lambda_{c}\,\overline{c}+\lambda_{w}\,\overline{w}+\lambda_{m}\,\overline{m}+\lambda_{tcp}\,s_{tcp}+\lambda_{z}\,\overline{s_{z}^{down}}

(19)

where
\overline{\cdot}
averages across the two arms.
We assign
\lambda_{tcp}
the largest weight to prioritize collision-free gripper geometry.
The highest-scoring candidate is selected per object category.

Prehandover computation.

Given the selected handover configuration
\mathbf{q}_{handover}
, we compute a prehandover pose by retracting along the TCP approach axis.
Let
(\mathbf{p}_{h},R_{h})
denote the TCP pose at
\mathbf{q}_{handover}
and
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{h}
the TCP
z
axis.
The retracted position is

\mathbf{p}_{pre}=\mathbf{p}_{h}-\delta\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{h}

(20)

with
\delta=0.05
m for spherical and
\delta=0.08
m for elongated objects.
We solve IK for
(\mathbf{p}_{pre},R_{h})
with additional restarts to obtain
(\mathbf{q}_{pre},\mathbf{q}_{handover})
for each robot.

Execution architecture

Dynamic policy generation.

We generate runtime policies for each robot arm through template substitution.
Each template contains placeholder waypoints for the handover and prehandover phases.
At execution time, these are replaced with category-specific poses precomputed offline (
Section

0.B.2
), producing policies specialized to the detected object class.
This bridges offline optimization with real-time execution without performing workspace search or bimanual IK online.

Persistent daemon execution.

Each robot is controlled by a persistent C++ daemon that maintains robot and gripper connections across cycles.
A daemon supports policy reload without reconnecting, stepwise execution that advances one policy step on command, and synchronization signaling through interprocess markers.
This reduces cycle overhead and enables rapid switching across object categories.

Dual-arm synchronization.

PHP enforces two sync points:
Sync A
at prehandover, ensuring aligned approach timing, and
Sync B
at handover, initiating the gripper exchange.
At
Sync B
, the receiver closes first; after a fixed delay the giver opens, ensuring the object is secured before release.

Asynchronous detection scheduling.

We overlap perception and manipulation by triggering the next object detection at
Sync B
, while both robots are stationary and the grasp exchange is ongoing.
This timing reduces occlusion in camera views and hides perception latency behind manipulation time.
Detection runs asynchronously in the background, and the most recent 6D pose is consumed for the next pick.

Force-adjust pick

Even with 6D object pose tracking, estimation noise and calibration drift introduce residual positioning error at the grasp site.
Force-adjust pick addresses this by using wrench-based contact cues during descent to detect and correct lateral misalignment before closing the gripper.

Descent controller.

We execute a slow vertical descent with smooth ramp-in and ramp-out:

\displaystyle v_{z}(t)

\displaystyle=-v_{d}\cdot\min\!\left(1,\,\frac{t}{t_{\mathrm{acc}}}\right),

(ramp-in)

(21)

\displaystyle v_{z}(t)

\displaystyle=-v_{d}\cdot\max\!\left(0,\,1-\frac{t-t_{s}}{t_{\mathrm{dec}}}\right),

(ramp-out)

(22)

Descent terminates on contact, plane reach, or timeout.

Contact inference and correction.

Franka reports external wrench in the stiffness frame
K
(located at the end-effector flange by default); hence we shift the torque reference to the end-effector origin:

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{EE}^{base}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{K}^{base}-\mathbf{r}_{EE\rightarrow K}^{base}\times\mathbf{F}^{base}.

(23)

We subtract a stationary baseline and apply an EMA filter:

\mathbf{F}_{f}(t)=\alpha\left(\mathbf{F}(t)-\mathbf{F}_{base}\right)+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{F}_{f}(t-1).

(24)

Assuming dominant vertical contact force
F_{z}
, contact offset is estimated from
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}
:

y_{est}=-\frac{\tau_{x}}{|F_{z}|},\qquad x_{est}=-\frac{\tau_{y}}{|F_{z}|}.

(25)

We use
(x_{est},y_{est})
to detect off-center contact and apply a small lateral correction before continuing descent.
If contact is classified as left or right, the robot lifts slightly, steps laterally toward the center by a fixed amount, and repeats descent.
To suppress oscillation under noise, we apply hysteresis by requiring a stable classification over multiple control cycles before switching labels.
For large objects, we use an early-finish rule that grasps once the end-effector is near the pick plane and sustained normal force exceeds a threshold.

Compliant handover

To absorb residual misalignment at handover, the receiver enables compliance along its TCP
y
axis while holding the remaining directions stiff.

Hybrid impedance and admittance.

At the hardware level, we configure Cartesian impedance with low stiffness on
y
and high stiffness on
x
and
z
.
At the software level (1 kHz), we command Cartesian velocity:

v_{x}=K_{p}\,e_{pos,x}^{tool},\quad v_{z}=K_{p}\,e_{pos,z}^{tool},

(26)

where
\mathbf{e}_{pos}^{tool}=R_{goal}^{\top}(\mathbf{p}_{nom}^{base}-\mathbf{p}_{cur}^{base})
is the position error in the tool frame.
On
y

v_{y}=\begin{cases}-K_{adm}F_{y}&\text{if }|F_{y}|>F_{thresh},\\
K_{p}e_{pos,y}^{tool}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(27)

We clamp
v_{y}
and apply rate limiting:

\displaystyle\Delta v_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{clamp}\!\left(v_{new,i}-v_{prev,i},\;-a_{lim}\,dt,\;+a_{lim}\,dt\right),

(28)

\displaystyle v_{i}

\displaystyle=v_{prev,i}+\Delta v_{i}.

(29)

We track accumulated displacement along the compliant axis and exit compliance if it exceeds a bound.

Rate limiting.

All commanded Cartesian and joint velocities are rate-limited per axis at the servo loop rate, producing smooth acceleration profiles and reducing reflex triggers from sudden command jumps.

Defensive clearing and reflex handling.

Before each motion segment, the executor stops residual motion and clears pending error states.
Reflex errors are caught, reported to the orchestrator, and recovery proceeds only under bounded retries.

Human-avoid requests interrupt motion smoothly and move the robot to a predefined fallback configuration via lock-free atomic flags, allowing the servo loop to react immediately.

Safety Coexistence Policies

This section details the safety triggering policies evaluated in the main paper (
Table

).
All policies share our custom simulation environment described in

Note that we compute a single human body center from the tracked 3D skeleton:

\mathbf{h}_{\text{center}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{J}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathbf{h}_{j},\quad\mathcal{J}=\{0,\dots,22\}\text{ with valid joints}.

(30)

Non-aware (baseline).

This baseline never triggers avoidance:

\texttt{trigger}\leftarrow\text{False}.

(31)

Static cylinder (TCP-centric).

We trigger when the human center is within a fixed cylinder around the robot TCP.
Let
\mathbf{p}_{\text{tcp}}
be the TCP position,
r
the radius, and
H
the height tolerance:

\texttt{trigger}_{\text{static}}=\bigl\lVert\mathbf{h}_{\text{center}}^{xy}-\mathbf{p}_{\text{tcp}}^{xy}\bigr\rVert_{2}<r\;\wedge\;\bigl|h_{\text{center}}^{z}-p_{\text{tcp}}^{z}\bigr|\leq\frac{H}{2}.

(32)

We evaluate
r=0.5
m and
r=2.0
m, both with
H=2.0
m.

Dynamic cylinder (velocity-adaptive, handover-centric).

This baseline centers the region at the handover midpoint
\mathbf{C}
and adapts the radius using the human approach velocity.
Define the unit direction from handover to the human and the approach velocity:

\mathbf{d}=\frac{\mathbf{h}_{\text{center}}^{xy}-\mathbf{C}^{xy}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{h}_{\text{center}}^{xy}-\mathbf{C}^{xy}\right\rVert_{2}},\qquad v_{\text{app}}=-\dot{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{center}}^{xy}\cdot\mathbf{d},

(33)

where
v_{\text{app}}>0
indicates motion toward the handover region.
The radius is

R(t)=\mathrm{clamp}\!\left(r_{\text{base}}+k\,v_{\text{app}},\;r_{\min},\;r_{\max}\right),

(34)

\mathbf{C}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{C}+\max\!\left(0,\;R(t)-r_{\text{base}}\right)\mathbf{d}.

(35)

The trigger is

\texttt{trigger}_{\text{dyn}}=\bigl\lVert\mathbf{h}_{\text{center}}^{xy}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}(t)^{xy}\bigr\rVert_{2}<R(t),

(36)

m/(m/s),

0.B.4
Simulation Environment

We evaluate all safety policies in a custom 3D simulation that
replays recorded human trajectories alongside robot task execution, enabling
reproducible and high-throughput comparison under identical conditions.

Simulation architecture.

The simulator runs an internal physics loop at 1 000 Hz, matching the
Franka Research 3 control frequency, while the 3D visualization
[
57
]
is rendered at
60 Hz.
Human trajectories are replayed from 30 Hz skeleton recordings captured from
four subjects; each frame is linearly interpolated to the internal rate.
The robot simultaneously executes pipelined pick-handover-place task
trajectories, with the safety-trigger policy under test governing pause and
fallback decisions at every control step.

Collision geometry.

We approximate the robot tool-center-point (TCP) as a sphere with radius

r_{\text{tcp}}=0.08
m and the human body as a vertical cylinder with radius

r_{\text{h}}=0.3
m and height
H_{\text{h}}=1.8
m centered at the hip.
At each collision-check step, the signed distance between the sphere and
cylinder is computed analytically: the horizontal distance between
centers is reduced by both radii, and the vertical extent is clamped to the
cylinder height.
A collision is registered when the signed distance falls below zero.

QP-based collision avoidance controller.

In addition to the discrete trigger policies, the simulator implements a
continuous collision avoidance layer formulated as a quadratic program:

\min_{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}\;\lVert\dot{\mathbf{q}}-\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{ref}}\rVert^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{J}\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{n}\;\geq\;-\alpha\,h(\mathbf{q}),

(37)

where
\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{ref}}
is the nominal joint velocity,

\mathbf{J}
is the Jacobian at the TCP,

\mathbf{n}
is the unit normal from the TCP toward the human,

h(\mathbf{q})
is the control barrier function (CBF) encoding the safety
margin, and
\alpha
is the repulsion gain.
We set the safety margin to 0.05 m, the influence distance to 0.3 m, and

\alpha=2.0
.

Timing and metrics.

Collision checks are performed at 50 Hz (every 20 internal frames) to balance
accuracy and throughput.
We report four metrics for each policy: (1)
average cycle time
—wall-clock duration per
pick-handover-place cycle; (2)
human hits
—total
sphere-cylinder penetration events; (3)
triggers
—safety-trigger activations; and (4)
fallback time
—cumulative fallback duration per trial.

0.B.5
Behavior Learning and Memory

Learning mechanism

The
Dynamic + Behavior Learning
policy extends the dynamic cylinder baseline
(
Section

0.B.3
) by learning per-direction intrusion
statistics from trajectory history and modulating the safety cylinder
accordingly.
We describe the approach-episode formulation, bearing-binned memory, and the
resulting anticipatory modulation.

Approach episodes.

An
approach episode
begins when the human center enters a 2.0 m zone
around the handover region and ends when the human leaves (with a 1.2
\times

hysteresis factor to avoid flickering) or after a 30 s timeout.
Each episode is classified as an
intrusion
if the minimum
human–TCP distance during the episode falls below 0.3 m, and as a

passthrough
otherwise.

Bearing-binned memory.

The approach bearing is quantized into 12 bins of
30^{\circ}
each.
To reduce noise from boundary effects, each episode increments not only its
primary bin but also the two adjacent bins (
\pm 1
) with unit weight.
The per-bin intrusion ratio is

\rho_{b}\;=\;\frac{n_{b}^{\text{intr}}}{n_{b}^{\text{intr}}+n_{b}^{\text{pass}}},\quad b\in\{0,\dots,11\},

(38)

where
n_{b}^{\text{intr}}
and
n_{b}^{\text{pass}}
are the smoothed intrusion
and passthrough counts in bin
b
.
A bin is considered
active
only after accumulating at least 2 episodes;
the policy enters its learned mode after 3 total episodes across all bins
(warmup threshold).

Exponential forgetting.

To adapt to changing human behavior, all bin counters are decayed every 20
episodes by a factor of 0.95:

n_{b}^{\text{intr}}\leftarrow 0.95\cdot n_{b}^{\text{intr}},\qquad n_{b}^{\text{pass}}\leftarrow 0.95\cdot n_{b}^{\text{pass}}.

(39)

This exponential forgetting ensures that outdated patterns are gradually
discounted while recent observations dominate the statistics.

Velocity prediction.

An exponential moving average (EMA) with
\alpha=0.15
tracks the human
approach velocity.
We additionally extrapolate the trajectory over a 0.5 s horizon to obtain a
predicted velocity; the effective velocity used for cylinder sizing is

v_{\text{eff}}=\max(v_{\text{raw}},\,v_{\text{pred}})
, which guards
against curved approaches and sudden accelerations.

Anticipatory modulation.

High-intrusion bearings (
\rho_{b}
near 1) indicate directions from which the
human historically penetrates the safety zone.
For these directions the cylinder radius is boosted by up to
+80\%
above
the base dynamic radius, triggering earlier pauses.
Pass-through bearings (
\rho_{b}
near 0) retain the nominal radius without
shrinkage, preserving reactivity.
The full pipeline at each control step is:
raw velocity
\to
EMA + extrapolation
\to
bearing lookup
\to
modulation
gain
\to
cylinder radius.

As shown in
Fig.

4
(a) of the main paper, collision count decreases as memory grows, with
diminishing returns after approximately 20 episodes.

Collection and utilization

Per-bin approach-episode counters are persisted between sessions, so the
behavior memory grows monotonically.
The bearing-binned intrusion statistics feed the Dynamic + Behavior Learning
policy’s cylinder-radius modulation, while for the placement task
(
Section

0.B.7
) the accumulated demonstration history
serves as few-shot context for the VLM.
The top-down occupancy heatmaps in
Fig.

4
(c) project the hip center
(average of COCO-WholeBody joints 11 and 12) onto a ground-plane grid with
Gaussian smoothing; progressive snapshots at 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of
the recording duration show how spatial coverage evolves.

Memory freeze ablation.

To quantify how much memory contributes at different learning stages, we
freeze (stop updating) the behavior memory at fixed time points and continue
simulation with the frozen statistics.
We evaluate a 5-point freeze schedule (coarse) and a 48-point schedule
(fine-grained, 212 total simulations), producing the learning curves in

Fig.

4
(a,b).

0.B.6
Intention-Aware Transfer Pipeline

The preceding sections address robot-to-robot object transfer; this section
tackles the complementary problem of delivering an object to a human based on
inferred intention.
Because the multi-camera infrastructure provides both real-time 3D human body
tracking and 6D object pose estimation concurrently, the robot can reason
jointly about
what
the person needs and
where
to deliver it.
We describe a fully automated pipeline: once the user issues a trigger,
multi-view perception, intention inference, object selection, and physical
delivery proceed without further human intervention.

Upon receiving the trigger signal, the system executes the following sequence:
(1) multi-view image capture,
(2) 3D human body pose extraction via triangulation,
(3) vision-language model (VLM) based intention inference,
(4) object identification, and
(5) Pick-Handover-Place (PHP) execution with the handover target set to the
human’s hand.

Multi-view scene representation

Inferring human intention from a single viewpoint is inherently ambiguous:
occlusion, oblique perspectives, and limited spatial context can all lead to
incorrect readings of the person’s gaze, gesture, or surrounding objects.
To mitigate these issues, we select six camera views from the 48-camera array
and composite them into a single
2\!\times\!3
grid image.
The resulting mosaic provides comprehensive spatial coverage of the person’s
posture, gaze direction, and environmental context from multiple complementary
angles.
Among the six views, a single view specifically captures the tabletop
region where graspable candidate objects are arranged, allowing the VLM to
observe both the person’s ongoing activity and the available objects within a
single input frame.

3D human body keypoints

In addition to the visual grid, we supply the VLM with quantitative geometric
cues derived from 3D human body tracking.
Our multi-view system reconstructs 16 body keypoints—from pelvis to
wrists—in world coordinates via multi-view triangulation.
These keypoints compactly encode body pose, facing direction, hand extension,
and spatial location, complementing the qualitative visual information with
structured numerical input.

VLM-based chain-of-thought inference

We employ GPT-5.2 with high-effort reasoning to perform structured intention
inference.
The model receives three inputs: the six-view grid image, the 3D body
keypoints, and a predefined candidate list of 23 everyday items spanning food,
kitchenware, household tools, and personal belongings.
Each candidate has a corresponding 3D mesh generated through an automated
reconstruction pipeline using MV-SAM3D
[
5
]
.
Rather than directly predicting the target object, we instruct the model to
reason in five explicit steps:

(i)
identify which candidates are physically present on the tabletop
and thus reachable by the robot;

(ii)
describe the person’s current activity by examining all six
viewpoints holistically;

(iii)
infer the person’s underlying intention from gaze, gesture, body
pose, and situational context;

(iv)
rank the top five most plausible objects from the reachable subset;
and

(v)
select the single best object with contrastive reasoning explaining
why each runner-up is less suitable.
This chain-of-thought formulation grounds the selection in observable evidence
rather than generic priors, and the intermediate outputs—reachable list,
activity description, and intention analysis—provide interpretable
justification for the final decision.

Handover execution

Once the target object is identified, the PHP pipeline grasps it from the
tabletop using 6D pose estimation and delivers it to the human.
The handover target pose is continuously updated using the person’s real-time
3D hand keypoints from the multi-view tracking system.
Because full-body tracking runs concurrently, the delivery destination
naturally adapts as the person moves, enabling fluid handover without
predefined waypoints or static target positions.
The full VLM prompt is reproduced in
Section

0.B.8
.

Intent-Aware Placement Task

This section details the intent-aware placement prediction task reported in

Table

2
of the main paper.
The robot must infer the correct placement location (sink or shelf) for each
object after observing a variable number of human demonstrations.

Object set.

We use 12 everyday objects divided into two semantic categories:

fresh produce
(orange, apple, banana, lemon, pear, peach) whose
correct destination is the
sink
for washing, and

packaged goods
(pringles can, spam, mustard bottle, soup can, cereal
box, ketchup bottle) whose correct destination is the
shelf
for
storage.
The category boundary is never disclosed to any method; it must be discovered
from the demonstrations alone.

Demonstration sweep.

We sweep 13 demonstration percentages corresponding to

k\in\{0,1,\dots,12\}
demonstrated objects out of 12 total
(
p=\lfloor k/12\cdot 100\rfloor\%
).

Table

2
in the main paper reports the subset

p\in\{0,25,50,75,100\}\%
.
For each
p
, we sample 15 random
k
-object subsets (or enumerate all

\binom{12}{k}
if fewer than 15 exist), using a fixed seed of 42 for
reproducibility.
Each sampled subset serves as the demonstration set; the remaining

12-k
objects form the test set.

Metric.

The evaluation metric is the
success ratio
: the fraction of test
objects whose predicted placement matches the ground-truth destination,
averaged over all sampled combinations at a given
p
.

Lookup baseline.

This non-learned baseline maintains a lookup table mapping each observed
object category to its demonstrated placement.
For test objects whose category has been seen in the demonstrations, it
copies the observed placement; for unseen categories it resorts to a uniform
random guess.
Lookup is purely analytical and does not invoke any language model.

LLM baseline.

We query Claude Sonnet 4.5 (temperature
=0.0
) with a domain-neutral
prompt that enumerates the demonstrations as text and asks the model to
predict placements for the remaining objects.
The prompt explicitly instructs:
“Do NOT use prior knowledge about
these objects; base your answer solely on the provided examples.”

This tests the model’s ability to perform pattern extraction without
chain-of-thought scaffolding.

+ Reasoning baseline.

The same model and temperature are used, but the prompt is augmented with
a chain-of-thought instruction:
“First, figure out the underlying
pattern from the demonstrations. Then, apply the pattern to predict the
placement for each remaining object.”

The output is requested in a structured JSON format to enable automated
parsing.
No category names or semantic hints are provided.

Query volume.

Across all 13 percentages and 15 combinations each, the LLM and + Reasoning
baselines require approximately 1,170 LLM queries in total.
We use prompt caching to reduce latency and cost.
