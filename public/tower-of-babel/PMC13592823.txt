Clinical outcomes of patients bilaterally implanted with the light adjustable lens: results of a phase IV clinical registry
Authors: Julie M. Schallhorn, Alekya Rajanala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluates outcomes from a clinical registry of the light adjustable lens in patients with and without prior corneal refractive surgery.

Numerous advancements have been made in biometry and intraocular lens (IOL) power calculations resulting in increased accuracy of refractive targeting after cataract surgery. With these, an estimated 69% to 75% fall within 0.5 diopter (D) of intended target and 94% to 95% within 1 D of intended target.1,2 Results in patients who have undergone previous corneal refractive surgery (CRS) are worse, with 8% to 68% within 0.5 D of intended.3

Residual refractive error has a substantial negative impact on both visual acuity and patient-reported satisfaction with visual quality. This is true for both residual sphere and residual cylindrical refractive error, and both have an independent contribution to the deterioration of visual acuity and patient-reported satisfaction. For every 0.25 D increase in residual refractive sphere, patients have a significant decrease in uncorrected acuity and patient-reported satisfaction.4 A similar correlation holds for uncorrected cylinder.5

Several factors interfere with IOL power calculations and contribute to variations in postsurgical refractive outcomes, including prior CRS. According to a study of the University of Colorado Cataract Outcomes Registry (N = 3382), an estimated 8.3% of eyes undergoing cataract surgery have had previous CRS.6 Prior laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK), photorefractive keratectomy (PRK), or radial keratotomy (RK) induces challenges with IOL calculation through difficulties determining corneal refractive power and effective lens positioning.7

The light adjustable lens (LAL; Rxsight, Inc.) is a new IOL that allows noninvasive correction of residual refractive errors by enabling postoperative lens power adjustment using UV light. Up to 3 adjustments can be performed, followed by 2 lock-in light treatments to prevent any further lens power change, all performed through a light delivery device (LDD). Outcomes with the LAL have been reported in a small series of patients with prior CRS, with promising results successfully addressing the “refractive surprise” resulting from the difficulty of obtaining accurate biometry in this subset of patients.8,9

This is a study of LAL outcomes in eyes with prior CRS and those with no prior corneal surgical history.

METHODS

This was a prospective observational multicenter study of visual and refractive outcomes after bilateral implantation of the LAL. The study was approved by a centralized Institutional Review Board and site-specific boards, and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable HIPAA regulations. Signed informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

A total of 126 sites and investigators participated in the study. Inclusion criteria were successful bilateral implantation of the LAL with a postimplantation and preadjustment corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) ≥20/20−2, successful completion of LDD treatments, and lock-in. Participants were excluded from the study if they had visually significant eye disease (CDVA <20/20−2), a history of previous intraocular surgery aside from the surgical procedure required to implant the LAL, or missing pertinent data.

The study was conducted at one follow-up visit performed 1 to 3 months after the final lock-in light treatment. Data were collected between September 2021 and January 2025 using case report forms and a centralized database. Data collected and assessments performed at the study visit included demographics, ocular history, light treatment history, monocular and binocular uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) and uncorrected near visual acuity (UNVA) at best focus (participants holding a near card at preferred distance), subjective manifest refraction, and monocular CDVA (spectacle corrected). Participants' complaints and adverse events were reported to the manufacturer for documentation, assessment, and medical device reporting, if necessary.

Participants were subdivided into 2 groups: eyes with no prior CRS (No CRS) and eyes that had previously undergone CRS. For this study, CRS was defined as LASIK, laser-assisted subepithelial keratomileusis (LASEK), PRK, small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE), astigmatic keratotomy (AK), conductive keratoplasty (CK), or RK. The primary outcome measure was the proportion of eyes that achieved ≥20/20 UDVA. Other outcome measures included proportion of eyes that achieved manifest spherical equivalent (MSE) ≤0.5 D, proportion of eyes that achieved manifest cylinder ≤0.5 D, adjustment accuracy and prediction error, and binocular UDVA and UNVA at best focus. Descriptive statistics were used to present data. A 2-tailed, 2-sample t test was used to compare means between groups.

RESULTS

A total of 1101 participants (2202 eyes) were included for analysis. Of these, 12.3% of patients were targeted for bilateral emmetropia, 9.3% for bilateral near, and 78.4% for blended vision (defined as one eye targeted for distance vision and the fellow eye targeted for MRSE ≤−0.25 D). In total, 998 eyes targeted for emmetropia (No CRS = 720, CRS = 278) were included in the residual refractive error analysis; 2202 eyes (No CRS = 1616, CRS = 586) were included in the prediction error analysis, and 1101 participants (No CRS = 800, CRS = 301) were included in the binocular VA analysis.

LAL Adjustments

The mean number of weeks from surgery to first adjustment was 4.5 ± 2.9 in the No CRS group and 5.0 ± 5.5 in the CRS group. The mean number of weeks from surgery to final lock-in was 7.7 ± 4.7 in the No CRS group and 8.9 ± 6.7 in the CRS group. The range of adjustments achieved was −3.625 to +2.00 D in the No CRS group and −3.00 to +2.875 D in the CRS group. The mean number of adjustments before lock-in was 1.7 for the No CRS group and 1.8 for CRS group; 25.4% of the CRS group underwent 3 adjustments compared with 17.8% of the No CRS group.

A total of 67% of participants had a change in the target refraction during the adjustment period, with a mean change of −0.22 ± 0.45 (range −2.50 to +1.50) from the first adjustment target to final target (Figure 1).

Of the 966 eyes targeted for near vision, the median final target refraction chosen by the patient was −1.25 D (interquartile range 0.75 D, range −3.25 to −0.25).

The mean number of weeks from lock-in 2 to the data collection visit was 8.10 ± 6.03 (range: 1 to 59) in the No CRS group and 8.67 ± 5.58 (range: 1 to 76) in the CRS group.

Residual Refractive Error

A total of 998 eyes targeted for emmetropia (No CRS group = 720; CRS = 278) were included in the residual refractive error analysis. For patients who were targeted for bilateral emmetropia, 1 eye was randomly selected and included in the residual refraction and uncorrected distance visual acuity analysis.

The mean absolute residual MSE of all eyes targeted for emmetropia at follow-up was 0.21 ± 0.25 D (95% CI 0.19-0.22). The mean absolute residual MSE of eyes targeted for emmetropia at follow-up was 0.202 ± 0.238 D in the No CRS group and 0.217 ± 0.263 D in the CRS group. The difference was not statistically significant (P = .389). The proportion of eyes achieving MSE ≤0.5 D was 94.3% overall, 94.4% of No CRS, and 93.9% of CRS eyes (Figure 2A).

Of the 998 eyes targeted for emmetropia, the mean residual manifest cylinder of eyes targeted for emmetropia at follow-up was 0.198 ± 0.273 D in the No CRS group and 0.264 ± 0.356 D in the CRS group. The difference was statistically significant (P = .002). The proportion of eyes achieving residual manifest cylinder of ≤0.5 D was 92.0% overall, 93.6% in No CRS eyes and in 87.8% CRS eyes (Figure 2D).

Attempted vs achieved analysis demonstrated that there was a slight tendency toward undercorrection for both No CRS and CRS eyes, with low variance in each (Figure 2E and F).

The mean difference between target and light treatment-induced astigmatism (astigmatic error) was 0.23 ± 0.38 D. The mean correction index was 0.99, indicating that there is no difference in correction between with-the-rule, against-the rule, and oblique orientations (Supplementary Figure 2, available at http://links.lww.com/JRS/B673). Error of angle shows even distribution with no consistent rotational error (Supplementary Figure 3, available at http://links.lww.com/JRS/B674).

Monocular UDVA

Of the 998 eyes targeted for emmetropia, the mean UDVA was 0.008 ± 0.094 logMAR in the No CRS group and 0.017 ± 0.087 logMAR in the CRS group. The difference was not statistically significant (P = .132). The proportion of eyes which achieved the primary outcome of 20/20 or better UDVA was 81.2% overall, 82.2% of No CRS eyes, and 78.4% of CRS eyes. The proportion of eyes which achieved 20/25 or better UDVA was 95.1% overall, 95.4% of No CRS eyes, and 94.2% of CRS eyes (Figure 3A).

The mean gain in UDVA from prelight treatment to follow-up was 0.96 ± 1.50 lines, with 9.3% gaining 3 lines or more (Figure 3B).

Follow-up UDVA was within 1 line of CDVA in 97.2% of eyes and within 2 lines in 99.3% of eyes (Figure 3C).

Of the 966 eyes targeted for near, 19.3% achieved a monocular UDVA of 20/20 or better (No CRS = 19.2%, CRS = 19.4%), while 79.9% achieved 20/50 or better (No CRS = 80.5%, CRS = 78.1%; Figure 3D).

Binocular UDVA

In the No CRS group, 89% and 93% of participants targeted for blended vision and bilateral emmetropia achieved binocular UDVA of 20/20 or better. Ninety-eight percent and 99% of participants targeted for blended vision and emmetropia, respectively, achieved binocular UDVA of 20/25 or better.

In the CRS group, 87% of participants targeted for blended vision and 87% of those targeted for emmetropia achieved binocular UDVA of 20/20 or better. Ninety-seven percent and 96% of participants targeted for blended vision and emmetropia, respectively, achieved binocular UDVA of 20/25 or better (Table 2).

Safety

There were no device-related serious adverse events reported on this cohort.

DISCUSSION

Cataract surgery is effectively refractive surgery because it alters the refractive state of the eye. Refractive targeting matters to outcomes, with patients reporting increased satisfaction the closer they are to the intended target.4 Despite significant advances in IOL power calculation, biometry, and surgical technique, postoperative residual refractive errors are not uncommon, especially in patients who have undergone CRS.2,3

Options for the correction of residual refractive error, aside from spectacles or contact lens, involve additional surgery. Of the available procedures, laser vision correction is generally the most commonly performed.10,11 The incidence of postcataract surgery laser vision correction is not well-documented but one retrospective study reports a cohort where LASIK was indicated and performed in 20% of patients implanted with a multifocal IOL who reported not being satisfied with their vision.12 Although these procedures are generally safe, there are risks associated with them because there are with any surgical intervention.13,14

As an alternative, the LAL allows nonsurgical correction of residual refractive errors using a series of light treatments that modify the shape and power of the implanted lens. It also has a unique feature that allows patients to trial their target refraction after the cataract has been removed, even change their minds during the adjustment period. In patients electing blended vision, this ultimately gives them the option of balancing their preferred working distance with tolerance to anisometropia. In this study, 67% of participants modified their target refraction during the adjustment period, indicating that adjustability of near vision target is an important factor to patients.

This study demonstrated that after LAL implantation and adjustments, 94.3% of all participants achieved ≤0.5 D of residual MSE and 92.0% achieved ≤0.5 D of residual manifest cylinder. The mean residual MSE of eyes targeted for emmetropia at follow-up was 0.21 ± 0.25 D, and the mean manifest cylinder was 0.22 ± 0.30 D. This mirrored the results of a recently completed Postapproval Study (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT05202808) which showed a mean residual MSE of 0.210 ± 0.213 D and a mean residual manifest cylinder of 0.262 ± 0.275 D, with 93.1% achieving MSE ± 0.5 D and 92.5% achieving manifest cylinder ≤0.5 D.15 The proportion of eyes achieving ≤0.50 D of MSE and manifest cylinder was slightly lower for both parameters in eyes that had previously undergone CRS (MSE: 94.4% of No CRS and 93.9% of CRS eyes, manifest cylinder: 93.6% of No CRS and 87.8% of CRS eyes). This is better than any reported result using nonadjustable IOLs, and results were better compared with a previous LAL studies performed in eyes with prior CRS which showed that only 55% to 86% of eyes were within ± 0.50 D of target MSE and 31% to 74% of eyes had 20/20 or better UDVA.3,16–18

The predictability results for both No CRS and CRS patients are better than most reported outcomes. No CRS eyes have been reported to be within 0.5 D of intended between 70% and 80% of the time in numerous studies, using modern formulas and biometers.2,19 CRS eyes are reported to be within 0.5 D from 28% to 68% of the time.3 The fact that both No CRS and CRS eyes outperform these metrics in this large study with numerous different surgeons, biometers, and surgical techniques indicates that the adjustability of the LAL offers superior predictability in all forms of eyes and in the hands of different practitioners.

Of the 278 emmetropia eyes that had prior CRS, 241 had excimer-based surgery (LASIK, PRK, LASEK, or SMILE) and 37 had incision-based surgery (AK, CK, or RK). A subgroup analysis showed that excimer-based surgery had better outcomes compared with incision-based surgery. This is perhaps due to incision surgery being more prone to irregular or asymmetrical outcomes compared with laser surgery (Table 3).

Conventional cataract surgery uses various techniques for reducing astigmatism, including toric IOLs. A published report of 34 891 eyes from multiple sites showed that 67.8% (an odds ratio of 2.11:1) achieved ≤ 0.50 D of manifest cylinder. Comparing this with the LAL rate of 92.7% (an odds ratio of 12.7:1) results in an odds ratio of 4.7. Thus, an eye implanted with an LAL would theoretically be 6.0 times more likely to achieve a manifest cylinder of ≤0.50 D than an eye with a toric IOL.5 Possible factors accounting for this discrepancy with traditional toric IOLs include variability in preoperative keratometry measurements, posterior corneal astigmatism, surgically induced astigmatism, and IOL tilt and postoperative rotation.20 With LAL implantation, the astigmatic treatment is based on a refraction performed at least 3-week post-LAL implantation, which essentially already accounts for these factors.

Given the above, it is not surprising that this study did not identify large differences between No CRS and CRS eyes about achieved vs intended correction of MSE or manifest cylinder. Single-angle plots showed an astigmatic error of 0.23 D with a mean correction index of 0.99, which indicates that there is no difference in correction between with-the-rule, against-the rule, and oblique orientations.

The mean monocular UDVA of eyes targeted for emmetropia was 0.01 ± 0.09 logMAR, and 81.2% of all participants achieved UDVA of 20/20 (82.2% in the No CRS group and 78.4% in the CRS group).

For participants electing blended vision, the mean amount of anisometropia was 1.1 D. This is below the level of anisometropia that has been shown to interfere with binocular stereoacuity.21,22 Under binocular conditions, 88% of the No CRS group had 20/20 or better UDVA and 93% had J2 or better UNVA at best focus, while 86% of the CRS group had 20/20 or better UDVA and 90% had J2 or better uncorrected VA at best focus. Eighty-four percent of participants with blended vision had 20/20 UCVA and J2 or better at best focus. Overall, 87% of all participants achieved a binocular UDVA of 20/20 or better, and 92% achieved J2 or better UNVA. These results indicate that patients can get excellent and functional distance and near vision with only moderate induction of anisometropia.

Most of the patients (67%) changed their target during the adjustment period, most often for near. This indicates that patients are trialing the amount of anisometropia and balancing their near vision with their binocular vision demands. The flexibility in the LAL in allowing this is unique and allows patients to find the amount of near defocus optimal for their lifestyle while preserving stereoacuity. Significant differences in refractive power between the eyes, greater than 1.5 D, can induce loss of stereoacuity and in some cases, binocular fusion.20,22,23 With the ability to adjust refractive power with a high degree of accuracy after surgery, customization of the patient's refractive target to meet individual visual preferences can be fine-tuned to minimize anisometropia and address the need for distance, intermediate, and near vision.

CDVA was within 1 line in 99.0% of eyes before and after LDD light treatments. Approximately 1% of eyes lost 2 or more lines of CDVA. There are a number of potential reasons for this. With repeated acuity measurements, the compounded variability results in an increase in the standard deviation of the repeatability between 2 BCVA measurements. This results in a standard deviation of 70% of 1 line of acuity between 2 measurements. This is nearly identical to the observed difference in BCVA between prelight and postlight treatments.24,25 In addition, this study captured data before the performance of any additional procedures such as YAG capsulotomy. It is possible, and likely given the incidence of posterior capsular opacification in silicone IOLs26 that some patients in this study lost BCVA because of the development of posterior capsular opacification, which would be resolved with YAG capsulotomy.

This study leaves some questions to be addressed. First, it did not include a patient-reported outcomes measure, so we are unable to comment on patient satisfaction and dysphotopsias. In addition, refraction was performed by a large number of different practitioners in different offices. Although there may be some intersite and intrasite variability in refraction, there is consistency within individual patients because they underwent their study examination at the same practice where they underwent adjustments. Thus, the risk of bias in measuring the attempted vs achieved refractive correction is low. Selection of patients with prelight treatment CDVA ≥ 20/20-2 may artificially inflate postoperative outcomes and exclude patients with more complex or realistic cataract. This may explain why our outcomes are better than previous LAL studies on eyes with prior CRS. The amount of induced spherical aberration by LDD treatments was not measured and is beyond the scope of this study. Although spherical aberration may affect visual acuity, depth of focus, and patient satisfaction, we are unable to comment on induced spherical aberration and its effect on these results. Finally, the follow-up period is short, and we do not have follow-up on patients who went on to undergo YAG capsulotomy and thus are unable to report the visual acuity results or refractive stability in this cohort.

In this study, we found that in a real-world setting, the LAL offered outstanding refractive and visual outcomes in both patients without prior CRS and those who had undergone CRS. Postoperative UDVA, UNVA, residual sphere, and cylinder are improved compared with historical series using traditional IOLs with fixed power.
