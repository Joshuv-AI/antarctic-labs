A Unifying View of Attention Sinks: Two Algorithms, Two Solutions
Lukas Fesser, Mozes Jacobs, Thomas Fel, Andy Keller, Sham Kakade

Abstract
When attention concentrates on a single token, a sink , what is the model actually computing? Attention sinks are ubiquitous in softmax transformers, yet this shared visual signature can hide fundamentally different algorithms. We show that visually similar sink patterns can reflect two distinct mechanisms: ( i ) adaptive NOP * * * From the NOP instruction in assembly. On many architectures, NOP is encoded as all zeros (unmapped memory returns zeroes by default), so a jump into the void does nothing gracefully. , where a head suppresses its update by routing to a null token, and ( ii ) broadcast, where a sink aggregates and redistributes global information. Proposed interventions like gating or registers work because they implicitly target one or the other, revealing a duality between method and assumed mechanism: gating implicitly assumes NOP ; registers implicitly assume broadcast. Each mechanism leaves distinct traces ( NOP sinks exhibit negligible value norms; broadcast sinks induce low-rank outputs) which we formalize on synthetic tasks and use to derive practical diagnostics. Applied to pretrained vision transformers, these diagnostics reveal that both mechanisms exist at scale: sinks transition from CLS in early layers to patches in deeper layers, and concentrate in specialized heads. Strikingly, register tokens, designed for broadcast, are repurposed to also serve NOP , confirming that neither intervention alone suffices. Combining gating with registers yields complementary gains in stability and performance. † † † Code available at: https://github.com/KempnerInstitute/attention_sink Overall, we find that the same attention pattern can reflect two very different computations and effective intervention requires first asking what the model is actually computing.

•

We use this distinction to reinterpret sink mitigation, showing that gating and register tokens are complementary because they target different sink computations, and that combining them can yield stronger mitigation and improved downstream performance in settings where sink behavior matters.

Preliminaries and Related Work

2.1
Softmax Attention and Attention Sinks

We consider the attention mechanism of a Vision Transformer
[
13
]
, which operates on a sequence of
n
patch tokens with hidden representations
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Unlike autoregressive language models
[
27
,
5
,
36
]
, ViTs employ bidirectional attention where each token can attend to all positions in the sequence. For a single attention head, treating individual token representations as column vectors, queries, keys, and values are computed as

\bm{q}_{i}=\bm{W}_{Q}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}
,
\bm{k}_{i}=\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}
, and
\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{W}_{V}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}
, where
\bm{W}_{Q},\bm{W}_{K},\bm{W}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{h}}
are learned projection matrices, so that
\bm{q}_{i},\bm{k}_{i},\bm{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}
.
We collect these column vectors into row-stacked matrices
\bm{Q},\bm{K},\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{h}}
whose
i
-th rows are
\bm{q}_{i}^{\top},\bm{k}_{i}^{\top},\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}
, respectively.
The single-head attention output at position
i
is given by:

\bm{o}_{i}^{\mathrm{head}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{ij}\bm{v}_{j},~~\text{where}~~{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{ij}=\frac{\exp(\bm{q}_{i}^{\top}\bm{k}_{j}/\sqrt{d_{h}})}{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\exp(\bm{q}_{i}^{\top}\bm{k}_{l}/\sqrt{d_{h}})}.

(1)

Equivalently, in matrix form, the single-head output is

\bm{O}^{\mathrm{head}}=\mathbf{A}\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{h}}
,
whose
i
-th row is
(\bm{o}_{i}^{\mathrm{head}})^{\top}
.

The attention weights
{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{ij}\in(0,1)
are bounded, and the attention matrix for all tokens
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is row-stochastic (forming a probability distribution over all
n
positions for each query
i
), thereby enabling information exchange within a single layer.
We use the standard residual-stream convention:
\bm{o}_{i}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is added to
\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell+1}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell}+\bm{o}_{i}^{\ell}
, with other sublayers such as MLPs omitted here for clarity.

We now formally recall a central notion in our study: attention sinks. We define an attention sink (consistent with prior work
[
30
,
15
]
) as a position that attracts disproportionate attention mass across many queries, without committing to a specific underlying cause.

Definition 1

(
\epsilon
-Attention Sink
)
.

Let
\epsilon\in[0,1)
be a small scalar. A position

\epsilon

\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}

{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}\geq 1-\epsilon

\epsilon=0
corresponds to a

sink (all mass placed on
s

approximately
perfect sink (most mass on
s

sink strength
of a position
s
over a query set
\mathcal{I}

\mathrm{sink}(s;\mathcal{I})\;=\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is},

(2)

and we may refer to

This classic definition (see

[CLS]

Related Work

Attention sinks in LLMs.

Attention sinks have been studied extensively in autoregressive language models, where they often appear at early sequence positions or special tokens and can persist even when those positions are not semantically central
[
5

4

16

,

,
27

Attention sinks in ViTs.

In vision transformers, sink-like patterns have been observed around the
[CLS]

[
22
,
10
,
20
,
21

. Existing work interprets these phenomena from several complementary perspectives. Some papers emphasize sinks as artifacts tied to outliers, saturation, or structured approximations in softmax attention
[

,
7

[

Overall, the ViT literature suggests that sink-like attention is related both to optimization pathologies and to potentially useful global coordination mechanisms, but does not yet provide a practical framework for distinguishing or mitigating these regimes when visually similar sink patterns appear within the same pretrained ViT.

3
Two Hypotheses for Attention Sinks

The signature of an attention sink (a vertical stripe in the attention map) is computationally ambiguous. Does the model attend to these tokens because they contain crucial information, or precisely because they do not?
In this paper, we analyze two distinct computational regimes that can give rise to the same visual pattern
. The goal of this section is not to provide a general theory of attention sinks, but to derive stylized mechanistic intuition and operational diagnostics that will later be applied to pretrained ViTs. We formally define these two regimes below, before analyzing their distinct spectral and geometric signatures in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
. For simplicity, in the hypotheses below we use
\bm{v}_{s}
to denote the value contribution in the space being discussed: the single-head value in head-space analyses, and the output-projected value when referring to residual-stream updates.

Hypothesis 1

(
Adaptive
NOP
)
.

An attention head implements an adaptive
NOP
mechanism
[
33
,
5
]
if attention concentrates on a sink position
s
.
For tokens
i
attending predominantly to
s
, the output
\bm{o}_{i}
is negligible and the residual stream remains unchanged through the block, i.e.
\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell+1}\approx\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell}
.

Hypothesis 2

(
Broadcast
)
.

An attention head implements a broadcast mechanism
[
10
]
if attention from many positions concentrates on a sink position
s
whose value vector
\bm{v}_{s}
carries meaningful information.
For tokens
i
attending predominantly to
s
, the residual stream update is dominated by the broadcast’s value:
\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell+1}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell}\approx{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}\bm{v}_{s}
.
Consequently, the representations of tokens attending to
s
acquire a shared linear component aligned with
\bm{v}_{s}
.

While both mechanisms produce similar attention patterns, they imply fundamentally different roles for the sink token and consequently require different interventions. Hypothesis 2 posits that the sink is not a passive operation for discarded attention mass, but an active component of the computation. In the following sections, we dissect them individually. We begin with the Adaptive
NOP
hypothesis.

3.1
Hypothesis 1: Sink as Adaptive
NOP
.

The first hypothesis posits that sinks arise when attention heads learn to selectively suppress their contribution to the residual stream. In transformers, attention outputs are added to the residual stream via
\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell+1}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell}+\bm{o}_{i}^{\ell}
. When a head has no useful information to contribute for certain tokens, it must still produce an output due to the softmax normalization constraint
[
4
]
.
Crucially, this behavior is adaptive: for some tokens the head performs a meaningful operation, while for others it routes to the sink and contributes nothing
[
33
]
.

NOP
Signatures.

Under this hypothesis, extreme attention logits and outlier activation norms are byproducts of the model learning to implement a null operation. The sink position serves as a learned null token that absorbs attention when no information transfer is needed. We may ask: is a negligible value norm the only way to achieve
NOP
behavior?

Lemma 1

(
Uniqueness of
NOP
Solution
)
.

Assume a single attention head with sink position
s
and residual update
\bm{o}_{i}=\sum_{j}{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{ij}\bm{v}_{j}
. For tokens
i
routed predominantly to the sink, suppose the head implements an
\epsilon
-approximate
NOP
, i.e.
\|\bm{o}_{i}\|\leq\epsilon
. Then the no-op condition admits two solutions: the sink value
\bm{v}_{s}
can either (i) cancel out the residual contributions from other tokens, or (ii) have zero norm. As the sink becomes perfect (
\epsilon=0
), the cancellation mechanism vanishes, leaving
\|\bm{v}_{s}\|=0
as the unique solution.

Having established the uniqueness of the
NOP
solution, we now address the adaptive nature of this function – that is, it must not perform a
NOP
permanently, but only when triggered. We examine the linear case (given the linearity of
\bm{W}_{Q}
and
\bm{W}_{K}
) to understand how an attention head can function as a conditional gate. The attention probability
{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}
must saturate to
1
specifically when a “flag” feature is detected.
We show that this gating mechanism requires maximizing the query-key dot product, which can be achieved either through large activation norms at the sink position or through a dominant singular value in
\bm{W}_{Q}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}
aligned with the flag direction.

Lemma 2

(
Mechanisms for Adaptivity
)
.

Assume a linear query-key interaction with
\bm{q}_{i}=\bm{W}_{Q}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}
,
\bm{k}_{s}=\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}_{s}
, and interaction matrix
\bm{\Theta}=\bm{W}_{Q}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}
.
To implement a hard gate (
{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}=1
) for specific queries, the pre-softmax logit
S_{is}=\langle\bm{q}_{i},\bm{k}_{s}\rangle/\sqrt{d_{h}}
must significantly exceed competing logits. In this setting, the sink logit magnitude scales with
\|\bm{\Theta}\|_{2}\cdot\|\bm{x}_{s}\|
. Consequently, the model can implement gating via two regimes:
(i) Spectral Gating:
The interaction matrix
\bm{\Theta}
learns a large leading singular value (spectral spike) aligned with the flag direction.
(ii) Massive Activation:
The sink token
\bm{x}_{s}
learns a disproportionately large norm, acting as a “super-attractor” or flag variable.

See Appendix
B.1
for a constructive proof of both regimes. This duality could offer a theoretical explanation for the “massive activations” often observed in outlier tokens
[
10
]
: they might be the energetic cost of enforcing a reliable
NOP
gate without distorting the weights.

Toy Model of Adaptive
NOP
.

To isolate the mechanics of the adaptive
NOP
, we train a single-layer attention head on what could be summarized as a “conditional damping” task. The input is a sequence of tokens
\bm{x}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The objective depends on the alignment between the token and a fixed gate vector
\bm{v}^{\star}
. If
\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{v}^{\star}\rangle>0
, the target is damped:
\bm{y}_{i}=\gamma\cdot\bm{x}_{i}
(where
\gamma=0
in the default experiment, is the
NOP
factor). Otherwise, the target preserves the input:
\bm{y}_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}
.
The attention mechanism produces a convex combination of values (geometrically
\bm{o}\subseteq\mathrm{conv}(\bm{V})
), so outputting
\approx\bm{0}
for the positive-alignment tokens requires the head to use one of the two mechanisms explained in Lemma
2
(cancellation effect or null value vector). Conversely, to output
\bm{x}_{i}
when
\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{v}^{\star}\rangle<0

Summary.

The
NOP

||\bm{v}_{s}||\approx 0

propagate

Hypothesis 2: Sink for Broadcasting.

2

In bidirectional attention, any token can gather information from and distribute it to all other tokens. A broadcast sink exploits this by accumulating a global representation and making it available sequence-wide.

Broadcast Signatures.

Under this hypothesis, high attention mass toward the sink reflects a meaningful computational pattern: the sink acts as a communication hub that distributes global or aggregated features across spatially distant patches. This leads to two measurable consequences.
First, because the sink token’s value vector
\bm{v}_{s}
is added to many positions simultaneously, the resulting update matrix becomes low-rank.

(

Suppose a single sink position

\bm{O}\approx{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{s}\bm{v}_{s}^{\top}

The input is a sequence of random tokens

The objective requires every token in the sequence to update its representation by adding a rotated version of this source token:

[CLS]

[CLS]
as it saturates with semantic content.

Two Algorithms, Two Signatures.

As elucidated by the preceding targeted analysis, the two algorithmic hypotheses for the role of attention sinks yield distinct measurable signatures in real models. Adaptive no-op sinks are characterized by negligible value norms at the sink position and representations that remain approximately unchanged through the attention block. Broadcast sinks, in contrast, exhibit non-zero value norms, induce low-rank structure in the attention output matrix, and increase cosine similarity across token representations as they distribute shared information.
Taken together, these controlled analyses provide stylized support for the empirical story and yield operational diagnostics for distinguishing the two regimes. In the following section, we use these diagnostics to separate sink behavior in large-scale vision models and illuminate the role of attention sinks more broadly.

6
Discussion

This paper argues that attention sinks in vision transformers should not be treated as a single phenomenon: the same stripe-like attention pattern can implement different algorithms. We analyze two canonical mechanisms: Adaptive
NOP
, where a head routes attention to a sink to produce an approximately null residual update, and
Broadcast
, where a sink acts as a global workspace that aggregates and redistributes shared information. This framing yields simple diagnostics that connect attention maps to computation: sink value norms identify NOP-like suppression, while the rank structure of the attention-induced update identifies broadcast-like mixing. Minimal synthetic objectives isolate each mechanism, and applying these diagnostics to pretrained ViTs suggests that both occur in practice, with register-like tokens often shifting where sink behavior concentrates rather than uniformly eliminating it. Empirically, combining gating and registers eliminates
NOP
sinks, redirects broadcast sinks to registers, and improves dense prediction: ImageNet classification remains largely unchanged, while ADE20K and Pascal VOC 2012 linear probes improve across mitigation variants, with gating + registers performing best. This supports the view that sink mitigation primarily improves patch-level spatial representations rather than global classification accuracy.

Our analysis also has limitations and points to concrete next steps. First, although gated attention improves training dynamics in LLMs
[
27
]
, it remains unclear when analogous benefits arise in ViTs, especially because gated attention and registers do not remove broadcast sinks. Our LeJEPA results suggest that benefits may be clearest when localized patch representations matter, but future work should test whether they persist or grow with model, data, and training scale. Controlled studies should track update norms, sharpness, downstream spatial performance, and the emergence timing of sink-like heads under gating and registers. Second, if some sinks are useful broadcasters, can architecture support broadcasting without inducing sink behavior? More broadly, linking attention computations to the architectural constraints that encourage them may help redesign attention to preserve what helps learning while removing what hinders it.

{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}=\softmax(\bm{Q}\bm{K}^{\top}/\sqrt{d_{h}})\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}

We start by recalling our definition of a sink:

Definition 2

(
\epsilon
-Sink)
.

A position
s

\epsilon
-sink if
{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}\geq 1-\epsilon

is small.

B.1
Proof of Lemma
2
: Mechanisms of Adaptivity

Proof.

Consider a simplified attention mechanism with a single head. Let
\bm{x}_{i}
be a query token and
\bm{x}_{s}
be the dedicated sink token. The attention score (logit) for the sink is given by:

S_{is}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{h}}}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{W}_{Q}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}\bm{x}_{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{h}}}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Theta}\bm{x}_{s}.

(4)

We assume the gating decision is based on a specific feature direction
\bm{u}
(with
\|\bm{u}\|=1
).
We decompose the query as
\bm{x}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\bm{u}+\bm{x}_{\perp}
, where
\lambda_{i}
is the scalar activation of the feature triggering the
NOP
, and
\bm{x}_{\perp}\perp\bm{u}
.

To consistently route attention to the sink when the feature is active (
\lambda_{i}>0
), the model aligns the interaction matrix
\bm{\Theta}
and the sink token
\bm{x}_{s}
with this direction. We construct:

•

The interaction matrix as rank-1:
\bm{\Theta}=\sigma\bm{u}\bm{u}^{\top}
, where
\sigma=\|\bm{\Theta}\|_{2}
is the spectral norm.

•

The sink token as aligned with the flag:
\bm{x}_{s}=\gamma_{s}\bm{u}
, where
\gamma_{s}=\|\bm{x}_{s}\|
is the token norm.

Substituting these into the attention score equation:

S_{is}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{h}}}(\lambda_{i}\bm{u}+\bm{x}_{\perp})^{\top}(\sigma\bm{u}\bm{u}^{\top})(\gamma_{s}\bm{u})=\frac{\lambda_{i}\sigma\gamma_{s}}{\sqrt{d_{h}}}.

(5)

For the attention weight
{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}
to approach 1 (hard gating), the score
S_{is}
must exceed the scores of all other
N
tokens by a large margin
M
(where
M
depends on the desired sharpness of the softmax). That is:

\frac{\lambda_{i}\sigma\gamma_{s}}{\sqrt{d_{h}}}\gg\max_{j\neq s}S_{ij}\implies\sigma\gamma_{s}\gg\frac{M\sqrt{d_{h}}}{\lambda_{i}}.

(6)

This inequality
\sigma\gamma_{s}\gg C
reveals the two distinct optimization paths available to the model to satisfy the gating condition:
(i) Spectral Gating:
The model increases
\sigma
(the singular value of
\bm{W}_{Q}\bm{W}_{K}^{\top}
) while keeping the sink norm
\gamma_{s}
moderate.
(ii) Massive Activation:
The model increases
\gamma_{s}
(the norm of the sink token
\bm{x}_{s}
) while keeping the weight spectral norm
\sigma
moderate.
Both solutions are mathematically equivalent for producing the necessary logit
S_{is}
to trigger the sink.
∎

B.2
Adaptive No-Op

Lemma 5

(
Uniqueness of No-Op Solution
)
.

Let
s
be an
\epsilon
-sink and suppose the no-op constraint
\|\bm{o}_{i}\|\leq\delta
holds for all tokens
i
. Let
V_{\max}=\max_{j}\|\bm{v}_{j}\|
. When
\epsilon>0

\|\bm{v}_{s}\|\leq\delta+\epsilon\cdot V_{\max}

\|\bm{v}_{s}\|\leq\delta

Proof.

Let
s
be an
\epsilon
-sink. The attention output for any token
i
is:

\bm{o}_{i}={\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}\bm{v}_{s}+\sum_{j\neq s}{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{ij}\bm{v}_{j}=(1-\epsilon_{i})\bm{v}_{s}+\bm{r}_{i},

(7)

where
\epsilon_{i}=1-{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}\leq\epsilon
and the residual
\bm{r}_{i}=\sum_{j\neq s}{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{ij}\bm{v}_{j}

\|\bm{o}_{i}\|\leq\delta

\|\bm{v}_{s}\|\approx 0

\|\bm{o}_{i}\|\approx\|\bm{r}_{i}\|\leq\epsilon\cdot V_{\max}

\bm{v}_{s}\approx-\bm{r}_{i}/(1-\epsilon_{i})

\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell+1}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell}+\bm{o}_{i}
where
\bm{o}_{i}=\sum_{j}{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{ij}\bm{v}_{j}
. Let
s
be an
\epsilon
-broadcast sink.

We first bound the deviation from the broadcast approximation. For each position
i
:

\displaystyle\bm{o}_{i}

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}\bm{v}_{s}+\sum_{j\neq s}{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{ij}\bm{v}_{j}.

(13)

The residual term satisfies:

\left\|\sum_{j\neq s}{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{ij}\bm{v}_{j}\right\|\leq\sum_{j\neq s}{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{ij}\|\bm{v}_{j}\|\leq\epsilon\cdot\max_{j}\|\bm{v}_{j}\|.

(14)

Thus,
\bm{o}_{i}={\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}\bm{v}_{s}+\bm{r}_{i}
where
\|\bm{r}_{i}\|\leq\epsilon\cdot\max_{j}\|\bm{v}_{j}\|
.

The mean representation after the block is:

\bar{\bm{x}}^{\ell+1}=\bar{\bm{x}}^{\ell}+\bar{\bm{o}},

(15)

where
\bar{\bm{o}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\bm{o}_{i}=\bar{a}_{s}\bm{v}_{s}+\bar{\bm{r}}
, with
\bar{a}_{s}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}
and
\|\bar{\bm{r}}\|\leq\epsilon\cdot\max_{j}\|\bm{v}_{j}\|
.

The centered representation is:

\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell+1}-\bar{\bm{x}}^{\ell+1}=\left(\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell}-\bar{\bm{x}}^{\ell}\right)+({\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}-\bar{a}_{s})\bm{v}_{s}+(\bm{r}_{i}-\bar{\bm{r}}).

(16)

Let
\bm{d}_{i}^{\ell}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell}-\bar{\bm{x}}^{\ell}
and
\delta_{i}={\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}-\bar{a}_{s}
. The variance after the block is:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left\|\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell+1}-\bar{\bm{x}}^{\ell+1}\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left\|\bm{d}_{i}^{\ell}+\delta_{i}\bm{v}_{s}+(\bm{r}_{i}-\bar{\bm{r}})\right\|^{2}.

(17)

Expanding and using
\sum_{i}\delta_{i}=0
:

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left\|\bm{d}_{i}^{\ell}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}^{2}\|\bm{v}_{s}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\|\bm{r}_{i}-\bar{\bm{r}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\left\langle\bm{d}_{i}^{\ell},\bm{v}_{s}\right\rangle+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i}\left\langle\bm{d}_{i}^{\ell},\bm{r}_{i}-\bar{\bm{r}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\left\langle\bm{v}_{s},\bm{r}_{i}-\bar{\bm{r}}\right\rangle.

(18)

The second term equals
\mathbb{V}(\bm{a}_{s})\|\bm{v}_{s}\|^{2}
where
\mathbb{V}(\bm{a}_{s})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}^{2}
is the variance of attention weights to the sink. The remaining terms involving
\bm{r}_{i}
are bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\cdot\max_{j}\|\bm{v}_{j}\|)
.

For the cross-term
\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\langle\bm{d}_{i}^{\ell},\bm{v}_{s}\rangle
, by Cauchy-Schwarz:

\left|\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i}\delta_{i}\left\langle\bm{d}_{i}^{\ell},\bm{v}_{s}\right\rangle\right|\leq 2\sqrt{\mathbb{V}(\bm{a}_{s})}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left\|\bm{d}_{i}^{\ell}\right\|^{2}}\cdot\|\bm{v}_{s}\|.

(19)

Combining these bounds:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left\|\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell+1}-\bar{\bm{x}}^{\ell+1}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left\|\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell}-\bar{\bm{x}}^{\ell}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\bm{v}_{s}\|^{2}\cdot\mathbb{V}(\bm{a}_{s})\right)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(20)

When attention to the sink is uniform, i.e.,
{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{is}=c
for all
i
with
c\geq 1-\epsilon
, we have
\mathbb{V}(\bm{a}_{s})=0
and
\delta_{i}=0
for all
i
. In this case:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left\|\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell+1}-\bar{\bm{x}}^{\ell+1}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left\|\bm{x}_{i}^{\ell}-\bar{\bm{x}}^{\ell}\right\|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(21)

The variance is approximately preserved, but all tokens receive the same additive component
c\cdot\bm{v}_{s}
, shifting collectively along the direction
\bm{v}_{s}
.

Appendix C
Extended Literature Review

C.1
Gated Attention

We briefly describe the
gated attention
modification of softmax attention
[
27
]
used throughout this paper. Starting from the standard single-head formulation in Eq. (
1
), let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
denote the matrix whose
i
-th row is
\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}
, and let

\bm{Q}=\mathbf{X}\bm{W}_{Q},\quad\bm{K}=\mathbf{X}\bm{W}_{K},\quad\bm{V}=\mathbf{X}\bm{W}_{V},

(22)

\mathbf{S}=\frac{\bm{Q}\bm{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{h}}},\quad{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}=\softmax(\mathbf{S}),\quad\bm{O}={\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}\bm{V}.

(23)

Gated attention introduces an additional
gate
computed from a (typically pre-normalized) hidden-state signal and uses it to modulate one of the intermediate quantities in the attention block. In its generic (multiplicative) form, given an input
\mathbf{Y}
to be gated and a signal
\mathbf{X}
from which to compute gate scores, we define

g(\mathbf{Y};\mathbf{X})\;:=\;\mathbf{Y}\odot\sigma(\mathbf{X}\bm{W}_{\theta}),

(24)

where
\bm{W}_{\theta}
are learnable parameters,
\sigma(\cdot)
is an activation function (by default a sigmoid), and
\odot
denotes elementwise multiplication.

Default gated-softmax output.

Unless stated otherwise, we use
head-specific, multiplicative sigmoid gating
applied to the SDPA (softmax-attention) output. Concretely, for a single head we compute a gate matrix

\mathbf{G}\;=\;\sigma(\mathbf{X}\bm{W}_{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{h}},

(25)

and form the gated head output

\widetilde{\bm{O}}\;=\;\bm{O}\odot\mathbf{G}\;=\;({\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}\bm{V})\odot\sigma(\mathbf{X}\bm{W}_{\theta}),

(26)

i.e
\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{i}\;=\;\bm{o}_{i}\odot\sigma(\bm{x}_{i}\bm{W}_{\theta})
. This
\widetilde{\bm{o}}_{i}
then replaces
\bm{o}_{i}
in the residual update. One can analogously gate other locations (e.g.,
\bm{Q}
,
\bm{K}
,
\bm{V}
, or the concatenated multi-head output); when relevant, we will specify the gated quantity explicitly while keeping the same notation as above.

C.2
Register Tokens

We also consider the
register token
modification of a ViT
[
10
]
, which appends a small number of additional learnable tokens to the input sequence. Concretely, let
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the patch-token representations (and optionally a class token, treated as an additional special token in the same way). We introduce
r
register tokens with learnable embeddings
\bm{r}_{1},\dots,\bm{r}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that are independent of the input image and are appended to the sequence after patch embedding. The resulting extended sequence has length
\tilde{n}=n+r
and can be written as

\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\;=\;\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{1}^{\top}\\[-2.0pt]
\vdots\\[-2.0pt]
\bm{x}_{n}^{\top}\\[-2.0pt]
\bm{r}_{1}^{\top}\\[-2.0pt]
\vdots\\[-2.0pt]
\bm{r}_{r}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times d},

(27)

with
\tilde{n}=n+r
. Attention is then computed
without any other change
on
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
: for each head,

\tilde{\bm{Q}}=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\bm{W}_{Q},\quad\tilde{\bm{K}}=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\bm{W}_{K},\quad\tilde{\bm{V}}=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\bm{W}_{V},\quad

(28)

\tilde{\mathbf{S}}=\frac{\tilde{\bm{Q}}\tilde{\bm{K}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{h}}},\quad\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}}=\softmax(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}),\quad\tilde{\bm{O}}=\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}}\tilde{\bm{V}}.

(29)

The same residual update is applied to all
\tilde{n}
tokens.
At the end of the network, the register tokens are
discarded
: downstream representations use only the original tokens (e.g., patch tokens and optionally
[CLS]
), while registers serve purely as internal workspace during the forward pass.

C.3
Non-softmax Attention

1. Clipped-softmax attention.

Clipped-softmax
[
7
]
replaces the usual row-wise softmax with a stretched-and-clipped variant. First form standard softmax weights

\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{S})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\quad\tilde{a}_{ij}=\frac{e^{s_{ij}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{s_{ik}}}.

(30)

Given hyperparameters
\zeta\geq 1
,
\gamma\leq 0
, define the elementwise clipped-softmax

\operatorname{clipped\_softmax}(\mathbf{S};\zeta,\gamma):=\operatorname{clip}\big((\zeta-\gamma)\,\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}}+\gamma,\;0,1\big),

(31)

where
\operatorname{clip}(x,0,1)
truncates each entry to
[0,1]
. The attention matrix is

{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{clip}}=\operatorname{clipped\_softmax}(\mathbf{S};\zeta,\gamma),\quad\operatorname{Attn}_{\text{clip}}(\mathbf{X})={\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{clip}}\bm{V}.

(32)

Softmax outputs in
(0,1)
are stretched to
(\gamma,\zeta)
and then clipped back to
[0,1]
, so entries larger than
\tfrac{1-\gamma}{\zeta-\gamma}
become exactly
1
and entries smaller than
-\tfrac{\gamma}{\zeta-\gamma}
become exactly
0
. This allows exact zeros/ones with finite score ranges and kills gradients where clipping is active.

2. ReLU-attention with
1/n
sequence-length scaling.

ReLU-attention
[
41
]
uses a pointwise ReLU on scores and normalizes only by sequence length. For each pair
(i,j)
,

a_{ij}^{\text{ReLU}}=\frac{1}{n}\,\operatorname{ReLU}(s_{ij})=\frac{1}{n}\,\max\{s_{ij},0\},

(33)

so the attention matrix is

{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{ReLU}}=\frac{1}{n}\,\operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf{S})\quad\text{(elementwise)}.

(34)

The output is

\operatorname{Attn}_{\text{ReLU}}(\mathbf{X})={\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{ReLU}}\bm{V}=\frac{1}{n}\,\operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf{S})\,\bm{V}.

(35)

Rows of
{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{ReLU}}
are not normalized to sum to
1
; the factor
1/n
is chosen so that at (random) initialization
\mathbb{E}_{j}[a_{ij}^{\text{ReLU}}]=O(1/n)
, matching the scale of standard softmax attention without requiring a reduction over the sequence dimension.

3. General scaled point-wise attention family.

This family generalizes ReLU-attention by replacing softmax with a generic elementwise nonlinearity plus a length-dependent scaling
[
34
]
. For an activation
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and exponent
\alpha\in[0,1]
, define

a_{ij}^{(h,\alpha)}=n^{-\alpha}\,h(s_{ij}),\quad{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{h,\alpha}=n^{-\alpha}h(\mathbf{S})\;\;\text{(elementwise)}.

(36)

The attention output is

\operatorname{Attn}_{h,\alpha}(\mathbf{X})={\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{h,\alpha}\bm{V}=n^{-\alpha}h(\mathbf{S})\bm{V},

(37)

with
h
chosen from
\operatorname{ReLU}
,
\operatorname{ReLU}^{2}
,
\operatorname{GELU}
,
\operatorname{softplus}
,
identity
,
\operatorname{sigmoid}
. ReLU-attention is recovered as the special case
h=\operatorname{ReLU}
,
\alpha=1
. Empirically,
\alpha\approx 1
stabilizes training by keeping the typical magnitude of entries
a_{ij}^{(h,\alpha)}
on the order of
1/n
, again echoing the effective scaling provided by softmax.

4. Polynomial attention with
\sqrt{1/n}
scaling.

Polynomial attention
[
34
]
replaces the softmax by an elementwise polynomial of the scores, with a
\sqrt{1/n}
prefactor chosen to control the Frobenius norm of the attention matrix. Starting from the same score matrix
\mathbf{S}
, define for a degree-
p>0
power

{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{poly}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}\,\mathbf{S}^{\odot p},

(38)

where
\mathbf{S}^{\odot p}
denotes the elementwise power
(\mathbf{S}^{\odot p})_{ij}=s_{ij}^{p}
. The head output is

\operatorname{Attn}_{\text{poly}}(\mathbf{X})={\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{poly}}\bm{V}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}\,\mathbf{S}^{\odot p}\bm{V}.

(39)

In the experiments they focus on cubic activations, e.g.
\varphi(x)=\tfrac{1}{k}x^{3}
with
k\approx\sqrt{n}
(fixed or learned “dynamic scale”), which fits this template up to a constant factor. Under Gaussian assumptions on
\bm{Q},\bm{K}
, the
\sqrt{1/n}
scaling yields
\|{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{poly}}\|_{F}=O(\sqrt{n})
and similarly controlled Jacobians, mimicking softmax’s implicit Frobenius-norm regularization while dropping non-negativity, normalization, and sparsity.

5. Sigmoid self-attention.

Sigmoid attention
[
29
]
replaces row-wise softmax by an elementwise sigmoid with an additive bias that can depend on
n
. With the same
\mathbf{S}
, define

\sigma_{b}(u):=\bigl(1+e^{-(u+b)}\bigr)^{-1}

(40)

for a learnable or hand-chosen bias
b
(scalar or matrix). Then

{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{sig}}=\sigma_{b}(\mathbf{S})\quad\text{(elementwise)},\qquad a_{ij}^{\text{sig}}=\sigma\bigl(s_{ij}+b\bigr),

(41)

and

\operatorname{Attn}_{\text{sig}}(\mathbf{X})={\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{sig}}\bm{V}=\sigma_{b}(\mathbf{S})\,\bm{V}.

(42)

Rows of
{\color[rgb]{0.0313,0.5703,0.6992}\bm{A}}_{\text{sig}}
are not normalized and entries lie strictly in
(0,1)
. With appropriate normalization schemes and a suitable choice of
b
(often roughly
b\approx-\log n
at initialization), attention norms remain controlled, and SigmoidAttn-based transformers can act as universal approximators with different regularity properties compared to softmax attention, while remaining compatible with efficient kernels such as FLASH-SIGMOID.

C.4
Other Related Works

Attention sinks in LLMs.

In autoregressive LLMs, attention sinks often coincide with special tokens (e.g., beginning-of-sequence), high-frequency separators (newlines, punctuation), or early positions that become convenient aggregation points under causal masking
[
42
]
. Once established, sinks can reduce the model’s
effective
context utilization: instead of distributing attention to the most relevant evidence, a significant fraction of attention is repeatedly spent on a small set of sink positions, which can manifest as degraded long-context retrieval, increased copying of boilerplate, or brittle reasoning when relevant operands are far apart in the prompt
[
4
,
27
]
. This behavior is particularly salient in settings where the model must integrate multiple dispersed constraints—precisely the regime emphasized by our relational reasoning perspective—because sink formation can act like an implicit bottleneck that prioritizes globally “easy-to-attend” anchors over the specific relational evidence needed for correct composition.

Mitigation strategies beyond register tokens and gated attention.

Beyond architectural additions like register tokens and modifications like gated attention, sinks can be mitigated via (i)
normalization and scaling
interventions that prevent extreme QK logits (e.g., QK-normalization, per-head temperature control, or logit clipping) so that a small set of keys cannot dominate attention across many queries; (ii)
regularization objectives
that discourage overly peaky or position-collapsed attention patterns (e.g., entropy/coverage penalties, auxiliary losses that promote diversity across heads, or discouraging persistent mass on special-token keys); (iii)
data and curriculum
strategies, such as training on longer contexts with targeted supervision requiring evidence far from the prompt prefix (and augmentations that randomize separators/formatting), which reduces the incentive to overuse early “anchor” tokens; and (iv)
inference-time adjustments
, including attention temperature tuning, selective head masking/pruning for sink-heavy heads, or KV-cache compression policies that preserve high-utility keys while downweighting persistently over-attended anchors.
