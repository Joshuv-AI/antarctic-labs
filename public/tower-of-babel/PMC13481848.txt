Urodynamic and Anamnestic Predictors for Individual Dose Adjustment of Intravesical Oxybutynin in Neurogenic Detrusor Overactivity: Results From the UPSIDE Study
Authors: Arndt Van Ophoven, Severine Banek, Kristin Becker, Markus Heibel, Almut Hirsch, Margot Kieruj, Ines Kurze, Anja Lingnau, Barbara Ludwikowski, Tobias Luithle, Fabian Queissert, Wolfgang Rösch, Oliver Schindler, Regina Stredele, Thomas Michael Karl Völkl, Quentin Leidl, Michael Gedamke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionIntravesical oxybutynin (IVO) is a 2nd line treatment option for patients with neurogenic detrusor overactivity (NDO) who are not adequately managed by oral anticholinergics. The UPSIDE study aimed to identify urodynamic and anamnestic predictors for achieving a therapeutically satisfactory dose (TSD) of 1 mg/mL IVO and to evaluate treatment outcomes including continence, quality of life (QoL), adherence, and handling.MethodsUPSIDE was a prospective, multicentre, non‐interventional, real‐world study including paediatric (age ≥ 6) and adult patients with NDO due to spinal cord injury (SCI), spina bifida (SB), or multiple sclerosis (MS). Patients switched to IVO (VESOXX) were observed for at least 3 months. The primary endpoint was achievement of a TSD. Secondary endpoints included urodynamic changes, continence, spontaneous micturition, catheter volume, urinary tract infections (UTI), QoL (SF‐Qualiveen‐questionnaire), adherence, persistence, handling, pre‐ and concomitant therapies and safety.ResultsOf 54 patients enrolled across 14 study centres, 36 formed the full analysis set, and 26 (72%) were categorized with a TSD. Older age and reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were identified as predictors for TSD (p = 0.059; p = 0.011, respectively). A dose adaptation was necessary for almost every second patient (47.2%). Urodynamic parameters such as p det max and bladder compliance improved. Maximal catheter volume increased significantly from 304 to 409 mL (p = 0.012). Continence rates doubled (28% to 53%, p = 0.043), spontaneous micturition increased from 33% to 42% (observed exclusively in patients with MS), daily micturition frequency decreased statistically significant from 8.3 to 4.6 (p = 0.008, n = 12, n = 14 respectively). UTIs decreased from 42% to 25%. SF‐Qualiveen improved statistically significant by 25% (p = 0.03). 70% of patients rated handling of the prefilled syringe as easy or very easy. Adherence and persistence exceeded 80%. No therapy‐related serious adverse events were reported.ConclusionVESOXX was effective in improving urodynamic parameters, continence, QoL, reduction of UTI and handling experience in NDO patients, with high adherence and no therapy‐related serious adverse events. TSD prediction is associated with older age and reduced eGFR. UPSIDE provides clinically relevant guidance for individualized intravesical therapy; however, interpretation of pressure‐based safety targets should remain cautious within the context of this pragmatic real‐world study. Introduction

Neurogenic detrusor overactivity (NDO) often arises from neurological disorders such as spinal cord injury (SCI), spina bifida (SB), and multiple sclerosis (MS), leading to high intravesical pressure, difficulties or inability to empty the bladder due to detrusor sphincter dyssynergy and impaired continence [1]. Intravesical application of 1 mg/mL intravesical oxybutynin (IVO) provides a therapeutic alternative to oral anticholinergics and has reduced systemic side effects [1, 2]. Regarding dosing, there has never been a dosing study with IVO. Despite clinical experience, systematic criteria for dosing adjustments based on functional parameters are lacking. Therefore, dosing is usually based upon the treating expert's experience. The UPSIDE study addresses this gap by identifying predictors for a therapeutically satisfactory dose (TSD) and evaluating functional outcomes.

Materials and Methods

UPSIDE was a prospective, multicentre, non‐interventional real‐world study conducted at 14 sites across Germany between 11/2022 and 08/2024. The trial was registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS, DRKS00028775). Inclusion criteria encompassed male and female paediatrics (≥ 6 years) and adults with NDO who were not adequately treated with oral anticholinergics or botulinum toxin. Patients were followed from VESOXX 1 mg/mL IVO initiation (visit 1) through urodynamic reassessment at ≥3 months (visit 2) and optionally visit 3 (≥ 3 months from visit 2 and maximum total time of 15 months from visit 1). Data collection followed exclusively the clinical routine; thus, no additional medical examinations or visits were required.

Primary endpoint: Proportion of patients with “Therapeutically satisfactory dose after readjustment or switch to IVO at the time of the first control urodynamics after ≥3 months” (visit 2). A patient was considered with TSD at visit 2 if either one of the urodynamic endpoints was met. These criteria were selected to reflect routine clinical decision‐making at the time of study planning and should not be interpreted as a stand‐alone evidence‐based renal safety threshold, particularly in paediatric patients. Furthermore, patients stopping the therapy were considered not therapeutically satisfactory.

A sample size calculation was determined for the primary endpoint. An effect size of f 2 = 0.25 and a significance level α = 0.05 were assumed, and a power of 16% was calculated with 20 factors and a power of 23% with 15 factors. It was initially planned to enrol approximately 250 patients. Due to the limited number of recruited patients, statistical analysis was adapted from a multilinear regression model to a univariate analysis.

Secondary endpoints: changes in urodynamics (p det max, baseline p det max at end of filling phase, compliance, detrusor leak point pressure, maximum filling volume), continence status, spontaneous micturition, catheter volume, urinary tract infections (UTI) occurrence/severity, antibiotic usage, Quality of Life (QoL, SF‐Qualiveen scores) [3], adherence, persistence, and device handling. Furthermore, demographic data and frequency of usage were documented.

The study also aimed to evaluate the type and sequence of pre‐ and concomitant therapies, parameters of the patients' indication‐specific QoL and the tolerability profile of the treatment. Assessment of vesicoureteral reflux and structural abnormalities such as large diverticula was performed only as part of routine clinical diagnostics; no mandatory central imaging review or protocol‐mandated video‐urodynamic reassessment was implemented [4, 5].

Results

A total of 54 patients were enrolled across 14 centres in Germany, of whom 36 formed the full analysis set (FAS), and 26 patients were identified with a TSD for the primary endpoint. The majority of patients were adults (66.7%), with the remainder being paediatric participants (6–17 years). Underlying aetiologies included SCI (38.9%), SB (27.8%), and MS (30.6%) (Table 1). An analysis of visit three was omitted due to the very low number of patients documented (n = 5).

Primary Endpoint

At visit 2 (≥3 months post‐initiation), 72% (n = 26) of evaluable patients had reached a TSD of IVO. Univariate analysis identified older age as a positive predictor for achieving higher doses: for every 10‐year increment, an average increase of 20% in total daily dose was observed with borderline statistical significance (p = 0.059). Conversely, patients with reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m2) tended to reach dose stability at lower total daily doses. Renal function seemed to inversely influence required dosage, suggesting half of the standard dose for patients with eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 (p = 0.011).

The mean starting dose was 0.55 mg/kg body weight/day for paediatric patients (0.37−0.83, 6−17 years) and 0.36 mg/kg body weight for adults. 17 patients (47%) reported dose changes, with equal distribution over paediatrics and adults (41.6% vs. 50.0%). Total daily dose increased in six patients, while total daily dose decreased in six patients. Five patients underwent bidirectional dose adaptation. The reason for dose adaptations is listed in Table 2. In paediatric patients, four out of five underwent a dose increase, and only one paediatric patient had to decrease the dosage due to side effects. Mean dose at visit one was 0.57 mg/kg body weight/day in children.

Urodynamic Outcomes

Urodynamic assessment at visit 2 demonstrated improvements in all urodynamic parameters (Figure 1). Mean maximum detrusor pressure (p det max) decreased below 40 cm H2O compared to baseline, accompanied by an increase in bladder compliance (V1 vs. V2, 46.7 cm H2O, 39.0 cm H2O; 38.4 ml/cm H2O, 58.1 ml/cm H2O). The number of patients with TSD who achieved p det max ≤ 40 cm H2O increased from 13 (50.0%) patients at baseline to 17 (65.4%) patients at visit 2. Patients with no TSD did not show p det max ≤ 40 cm H2O (4 [40%] patients at baseline to 4 [40%] patients at visit 2).

Voiding Behaviour and Continence

Information regarding the person performing intermittent catheterization (IC) was available in 27 patients. Most patients (50.0%) applied the IC themselves. In 11.1% of cases, their parents performed the procedure. For the remaining patients, nurses either supported the procedure or performed the IC fully. Median daily (night and day) frequency of IC was 6.0 (2.0; 10.0, respectively) at baseline, 5.5 (2.0; 10.0, respectively) at visit 2; changes were not statistically significant. Maximum catheter volume per IC increased statistically significant from a median of 304 ml at baseline to 409 mL at visit 2 (p = 0.012), while mean catheter volume improved statistically significant from 208 ml to 250 ml (p = 0.002) (Figure 2). The proportion of patients reporting spontaneous micturition, increased from 33.3% at baseline to 41.7% at visit 2, all of them suffering from MS. More prominently, continence status improved substantially. The proportion of fully continent patients almost doubled from 27.8% to 52.8% (Figure 3), statistically significant (p = 0,043). The number of patients with partial or total incontinence declined correspondingly. Noteworthy, the number of continent patients was independent of TSD, as the continence rate increased in the non‐TSD group from 10% at baseline to 70% at visit 2. Micturition frequency per day also improved (V1 8.3; V2 4.6, p = 0.008), and mean micturition volume increased from 121.7 to 205.0 mL, the latter not reaching statistical significance. Post‐voiding residual volume decreased statistically significant from 33.3 to 8.3 mL by 75% (p < 0.001).

UTI

Both the frequency and the severity of UTIs declined during the observation period. At baseline, 41.7% of patients reported at least one UTI in the preceding 3 months; this proportion decreased to 25% at visit 2. In patients with botulinum toxin A (BTX) as preceding therapy, baseline infections were present in 83.3% (n = 5/6) and 50% (n = 3/3) of the patients at visit 2. Overall, the number of patients requiring antibiotic treatment decreased statistically significant from 11 to 6 (p = 0.006). Severity of UTI decreased statistically significant (p = 0.044). The proportion of asymptomatic infections increased, showing a shift toward less clinically significant events. Fever‐associated UTIs were rare at both timepoints. Similar improvements were seen in patients with BTX as preceding therapy.

QoL

Patient‐reported urinary‐specific QoL improved significantly over the course of treatment. The median score reduction corresponded to an 25% improvement from baseline (p = 0.03), with the greatest benefits observed in domains related to emotional impact (Fears, 24%) and daily activities (21%). Paediatric patients experienced a statistically significant 27%, improvement (p = 0.028).

During the study the manufacturer changed the device design from a two‐piece “Luer Lock plus cone adapter” to a “single piece” device. The improvements between baseline and visit 2 were larger in patients using “Single‐piece Syringe” compared to “Luer‐Lock cone adapter” (0.45 vs. 0.29; 19.7% vs. 14.1%), but not statistically significant.

Adherence, Persistence, and Handling

Treatment adherence at visit 2 was 75%, and partial adherence (partial adherence 20%−79% adherence) 5.6%, with a similarly high rate of persistence across all diagnostic subgroups. Adherence was best in paediatric patients (83.3%) compared to adult patients (70.8%). Most (88.9%) patients continued IVO treatment, in the paediatric group, even 100%. A total of 47.2% of patients required some form of dose or interval adjustment, with 19.4% of patients undergoing up‐titration. In paediatric patients, 80% of the dose adaptations were dose increases. Notably, 70% of users rated the handling of the IVO prefilled syringe and adapter system as “easy” or “very easy,”. Comparing the two different device designs (“Luer‐Lock cone adapter” vs. “Single‐piece Syringe”), the later was favoured (easy and very easy 69% vs. 75%).

Pre‐ and Concomitant Therapies

Prior medication was reported at baseline (n = 36, multiple responses were possible): oral anticholinergics/patches in 29 (80.6%) patients (oral oxybutynin in 9 of those patients), other therapies in 9 (25.0%) patients (mirabegron in 8 of those patients), BTX in 6 (16.7%) patients and IVO on prescription (other than 1 mg/ml) in 3 (8.3%) patients. Concomitant therapies with urological impact were used in 41.7% of the patients (Table 3).

Concomitant Diseases

The most frequently reported concomitant diseases were musculoskeletal system/connective tissue disorders in 12 (33.3%) patients followed by nervous system disorders in 10 (27.8%) patients, gastrointestinal disorders in 7 (19.4%) patients and other diseases in 9 (25.0%).

Safety

No therapy‐related serious adverse events (SAEs) were reported. Overall, 45.1% of adverse events (AEs) were considered related to treatment, the majority of which were mild or moderate in intensity. Importantly, 72.1% of all reported AEs resolved spontaneously during the study. The safety profile was consistent with previous experience with IVO, including the known benefit of avoiding first‐pass metabolism and associated systemic side effects (Table 4). Two out of 10 paediatric patients reported a lack of efficacy as an AE resulting in an increase of dosage.

Discussion

The current standard therapy for NDO involves controlled emptying of the bladder by IC in combination with anticholinergics, which can be administered orally or transdermally (1st line therapy) or intravesically via IC (2nd line therapy). The efficacy, safety, and tolerability of intravesical application (10 ml up to 4 times a day) of a 0.1% oxybutynin chloride solution compared to oral application (5 mg 3 times a day) was confirmed in a randomised study in adult NDO patients. After 28 days of use, a significant difference in bladder capacity was found in favour of intravesical use [1]. The effect of IVO is dose‐dependent. This was demonstrated in a prospective study of patients with neurogenic bladder disorders and NDO. The standard dose of IVO (0.3 mg/kg/day) was effective in 66% of patients, while 87% of patients could be successfully treated by up‐titrating the dose to 0.9 mg/kg body weight/day [6]. Recommended starting dose of IVO is 0.1 mg/kg body weight/day. Since these are the only available data on dose escalation, the aim of the UPSIDE study was to generate real‐world data of urodynamic and patient‐reported clinical parameters as potential predictors of therapeutically satisfactory (initial) dose regimens.

This observational study revealed that the dosage of IVO in the treatment of patients with NDO was shown to be based on individual judgement of the treating experts. Interestingly, the mean of weight‐adjusted starting dose was different between paediatric and adult patients. While paediatric patients received 0.55 mg/kg body weight/day was the mean starting dose for adults lower with 0.36 mg/kg body weight/day. Guidelines recommend dosing in dependence on urodynamics and side effects; dosages up to 0.9 mg/kg body weight are being described in the literature [7], real‐life data suggest 0.54 to 0.9 mg/kg body weight as frequently used dosage in paediatric patients [8]. The increased starting dose in paediatric patients might reflect the physician's ambition/concern to avoid damage of the upper renal tract even with the risk of AEs. However, the physicians' risk tolerance might have been diminished due to the full reversibility of those AEs. In this study paediatric patients reported exclusively lack of efficacy as AE, which resulted in an increased dosage. Elevated starting doses thus appear justified in retrospect.

Furthermore, this real‐life study showed an age‐dependent dosing with a 20% total daily dose increase for every 10‐year increment of age. However, age dependence missed statistical significance (p = 0.059), potentially due to insufficient statistical power, that is, because of low patient number in the subgroup of paediatrics. Yet, age‐dependent dosing may reflect both physiological differences and physicians' risk‐benefit assessment.

Impaired renal function (eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73) led to dose stability at significantly lower total daily doses. A dose‐response relationship for eGFR has not yet been described in literature. Initial low doses maybe result from precautionary measures. Oxybutynin is mainly degraded in the liver but excreted via the kidneys, therefore it maybe hypothesized that biochemical degradation of the active ingredient in combination with reduced kidney clearance results into a stronger and extended effect of IVO in patients with reduced eGFR.

In terms of TSD, the study confirms the clinical benefit of individualized IVO‐therapy in NDO as most patients (72%) reached a TSD, with significant increase of continence and QoL. Importantly, even patients not meeting formal TSD criteria experienced improved continence. AEs were the main reason for the assignment to the non‐TSD group.

All study patients had already received at least a first‐line therapy with oral oxybutynin, which failed due to lack of efficacy or AEs. Some even received third‐line therapy. Thus, 50% of the patients started with an already reduced p det max < 40 cm H2O. Nevertheless, p det max < 40 cm H2O improved to more than two thirds in the TSD group.

The applied detrusor pressure threshold of ≤40 cm H2O in UPSIDE reflects current guideline‐based clinical practice, where a low‐pressure system is typically defined within a range of <30–40 cm H2O [7]. However, this threshold should be interpreted cautiously, because even lower pressure thresholds may be desirable for upper urinary tract protection, especially in paediatric NLUTD. Tarcan et al. showed that a 40 cm H2O cut‐off was not sufficiently reliable for upper urinary tract protection in children with myelodysplasia, and a systematic review concluded that no single urodynamic threshold alone reliably predicts upper tract damage across neuro‐urological populations [4, 9]. However, recent consensus work by the ICI‐RS Think Tank 2 [10] emphasizes that the concept of a high‐risk bladder cannot be reduced to a single pressure cut‐off but rather represents a multifactorial construct involving bladder compliance, detrusor overactivity, and detrusor leak point pressure. While historical studies have linked detrusor leak point pressures above 40 cm H2O to upper urinary tract deterioration, no universally accepted threshold has been validated. In summary, the chosen threshold should be interpreted within the context of a pragmatic real‐world study design rather than a strict safety benchmark [9].

In the subgroup of MS patients, spontaneous micturition increased over time. Although this change did not reach statistical significance, it suggests a trend toward improved voiding autonomy in this subgroup. Spontaneous micturition should therefore not be interpreted as a general therapeutic goal across SCI, SB, and MS populations. The marked reduction in post‐void residual volume may reflect improved storage and voiding efficiency under treatment; however, as no mechanistic assessments were performed, this finding remains hypothesis‐generating.

UTIs are reported in connection with IVO, presumingly related to improper hygienically conduct of intermittent catheterisation [11, 12]. In this study, the high load of UTI at baseline, that is, within the interval of 3 month prior to study entrance, dropped drastically from over 40% to only 25% at visit 2. This decrease was consistent with a reduction in UTI severity and reflected by a reduction in the antibiotic treatment cycle, finally indicating a correlation with IVO treatment. A similar decrease in UTI amount was also observed in patients with a preceding BTX treatment, although this was on a much higher level of UTI, with UTI in over 80% of BTX patients prior to IVO reduced to 50% at visit 2. BTX is known for a frequent rate of UTI [13].

QoL is a common outcome parameter in interventional studies for NDO, for example, in studies with BTX treatment for the disease [14, 15]. The current UPSIDE study delt with urinary QoL outcome results for the very first‐time regarding IVO in NDO.

A statistically significant improvement in QoL resulting from IVO could be shown. To our point of view this is most likely the result from increased continence rates and reduced frequency in UTIs.

During the study period, two different device forms were used, numerically favouring the single‐piece syringe. However, because device design changed during the study and treatment was self‐administered, this aspect represents a relevant potential confounder for patient‐reported outcomes and handling assessments and should therefore not be interpreted causally [11, 12].

Treatment adherence and user‐friendliness of any treatment approach are key to real‐world effectiveness. Adherence to and compliance of IVO were rated excellent in the study and are in concordance with earlier results [16]. With a majority of patients rating the syringe and adapter system as easy or very easy to use, treatment integration into daily routine was feasible. During the study, the single‐piece syringe device became available as an alternative to the adapter system and appeared to be well accepted and preferred by patients (75% vs. 69%, respectively); nevertheless, this crisscrossing device change also limits direct comparability within the cohort.

A limitation of the UPSIDE study is its non‐interventional design. Thus, data retrieved are inherently of indicative character only, while simultaneously reflecting real‐world clinical practice and routine patient management.

Potential confounding by vesicoureteral reflux or bladder diverticula cannot be entirely excluded. However, only one patient presented with vesicoureteral reflux and one patient with a bladder diverticulum in the present cohort, suggesting a negligible impact on the overall findings.

Reduced number in personnel in clinics following the corona pandemic led to a reduced number of participating sites and to reduced capacities for patient inclusion into the trial. The number of finally recruited patients made an adaptation in the statistical analysis plan necessary and let to a univariate analysis, thus multifactorial effects could not be assessed. Nevertheless, this is the largest prospective trial conducted for IVO to date. The inhomogeneous demographic composition of study groups allowed insights into different patient groups while limiting at the same time the statistical power for each subgroup.

Preceding first (e.g., oral), second (e.g., IVO) and third‐line treatment (e.g., BTX) modalities occurred in 25%, leading to bias regarding urodynamic improvements and regarding interpretation of improvement data.

With BTX offering a relevant third‐line treatment option, IVO offers a less invasive and more flexible alternative, particularly relevant for paediatric and MS populations, with high rates of treatment persistence. Safety outcomes align with previous reports of favourable tolerability of IVO.

Conclusion

UPSIDE suggests age and eGFR as predictors for dose finding. Dose adjustments were necessary in almost every second patient. VESOXX showed a favorable short‐term effectiveness and tolerability profile in this real‐world cohort, improving continence and QoL in both pediatric and adult populations. The study provides actionable guidance for clinical dose titration, while pressure‐based safety targets should be interpreted with appropriate caution.
