Attention-guided temporal convolutional pseudo-velocity generation for underwater inertial/Doppler navigation
Authors: Caiqin Jia, Guoxiang Wang, Xingcheng Han, Ling Wang, Shengwen Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Doppler velocity log (DVL) outages can rapidly degrade loosely coupled inertial/Doppler navigation for autonomous underwater vehicles, especially in deep water or over complex seabed terrain. This study presents an attention-guided temporal convolutional method for generating pseudo-DVL velocity measurements during such outages. The model uses recent inertial measurement unit outputs and strapdown inertial navigation system (SINS)-derived attitude, position and velocity as sequential inputs, and learns the mapping to horizontal DVL velocity measurements from DVL-available periods. During DVL-unavailable intervals, the trained model predicts pseudo-velocity measurements at the original DVL update instants, which are then used in an extended Kalman filter measurement update. Causal dilated convolutions are used to extract temporal dependencies without future information, while the attention module weights motion segments that are more informative for velocity prediction. Simulations using 16 autonomous underwater vehicle trajectories show that the proposed method reduces velocity and trajectory errors compared with multilayer perceptron (MLP), temporal convolutional network (TCN) and gated recurrent unit with attention (GRU-Attention) baselines under continuous DVL outage. In a closed-loop turning task, the eastward and northward velocity root mean square error (RMSE) values are reduced by 14.69% and 14.74%, respectively, compared with GRU-Attention.

Introduction

Autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) are increasingly used for seabed mapping, marine resource exploration and long-duration underwater operations. Reliable navigation is difficult in these environments because Global Navigation Satellite System signals are unavailable underwater, while optical and vision-based methods are often affected by attenuation, turbidity and poor illumination. Acoustic positioning can provide an external reference, but its accuracy depends on propagation conditions, deployment geometry and environmental noise. For this reason, strapdown inertial navigation systems (SINSs) remain a central component of underwater navigation systems. They provide autonomous, high-rate motion information, but their velocity and position errors accumulate over time.

Doppler velocity logs (DVLs) are commonly integrated with SINSs to limit inertial drift. In a loosely coupled SINS/DVL system, the DVL velocity is compared with the SINS velocity, and the resulting residual is used as a measurement update in a Kalman-filter-based estimator. This structure is simple and effective when DVL measurements are available. In practice, however, DVL measurements may be interrupted or degraded in deep water, over complex seabed terrain, or when the bottom-lock condition is lost. Once the DVL velocity update is unavailable, the integrated system has to rely mainly on inertial propagation, and navigation errors can grow rapidly.

Several types of methods have been developed to improve navigation performance during DVL degradation or outage. Hardware-assisted approaches introduce additional sensors or acoustic positioning systems, such as ultra-short baseline positioning, to provide external constraints. These methods can improve robustness, but they also increase cost, system complexity and deployment requirements. Software-based approaches avoid additional hardware and focus on improving the estimation model or compensation mechanism. State-transformed and beam-fault-oriented filtering methods have been used to improve SINS/DVL navigation under abnormal measurement conditions1,2. Invariant-error and Lie-group-based formulations have also been introduced to improve estimation consistency under attitude changes and nonlinear motion3,4. In addition, adaptive and robust filtering methods can adjust process or measurement uncertainty under complex operating conditions5.

Data-driven compensation has recently become a useful alternative for DVL-denied navigation. Early hybrid and neural-network-based studies showed that missing or faulty DVL velocity information can be reconstructed or compensated from navigation-related measurements6,7. Deep-learning-based SINS/DVL methods further demonstrated the feasibility of maintaining integrated navigation accuracy during DVL malfunction or signal failure8,9. Experimental studies in DVL-denied environments also showed that learned velocity compensation can be useful for AUV navigation when real DVL measurements are unavailable10.

Recent work has extended this idea from velocity-level compensation to DVL beam-level modelling. Deep-learning approaches have been used to compensate partial DVL outages and estimate velocity under complete beam measurement outage scenarios11,12. Related studies such as BeamsNet and MissBeamNet further showed that data-driven models can enhance or recover DVL beam measurements for AUV navigation13,14. These studies suggest that learned models can provide useful information when DVL observations are incomplete or unreliable.

Other learning-based navigation methods have explored recurrent networks, Gaussian-process regression, integrated filtering and DVL velocity optimization. Attention-GRU and deep-learning-assisted SINS/DVL methods have been used to improve velocity compensation and integrated navigation performance15,16. Online transfer Gaussian process regression and broader deep-learning-assisted inertial navigation studies also support the use of learned motion features in navigation systems17,18. More recent studies have incorporated deep learning into ES-EKF-based navigation and DVL velocity optimization frameworks19,20. However, two issues remain. First, the temporal dependence within inertial-navigation sequences is not always fully represented, especially during turning, acceleration and deceleration. Second, many models treat different time steps in the input window in a relatively uniform way, although short manoeuvring segments may carry more useful information for velocity prediction.

Here we present an attention-guided temporal convolutional method for generating pseudo-DVL velocity measurements during DVL outage. The model uses recent inertial measurement unit outputs and SINS-derived attitude, position and velocity as sequential inputs. Causal dilated convolutions are used to extract temporal dependencies while preserving time causality, and an attention module is used to weight the time steps that are more informative for pseudo-velocity prediction. The generated horizontal pseudo-velocity measurements are then introduced into the extended Kalman filter at the original DVL update instants, allowing the loosely coupled SINS/DVL system to maintain measurement updates during DVL-unavailable intervals.

The main purpose of this study is to evaluate whether attention-guided temporal convolution can provide reliable pseudo-velocity measurements for continuous DVL outage compensation. The method is tested using 16 simulated AUV trajectories and compared with multilayer perceptron, temporal convolutional network and gated recurrent unit with attention baselines. The evaluation considers both velocity prediction errors and trajectory recovery errors. The study focuses on pseudo-velocity generation and its use in the filter update; online DVL validity detection and switching logic are not addressed here.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows. First, an attention-guided temporal convolutional pseudo-DVL velocity generation framework is developed for loosely coupled SINS/DVL navigation under continuous DVL outage. The framework uses recent IMU measurements and SINS-derived navigation states to generate horizontal pseudo-velocity measurements without requiring additional external sensors. Second, a causal TCN-Attention model is designed to extract short-term temporal motion features while preserving time causality. The temporal attention module allows the model to assign different weights to different time steps in the input window, which is especially useful during turning, acceleration and deceleration. Third, the generated pseudo-velocity measurements are incorporated into the EKF using the same horizontal velocity residual form as the real DVL update. This makes the proposed method compatible with the conventional loosely coupled SINS/DVL filter structure and enables a direct evaluation of its effect on both velocity prediction accuracy and trajectory recovery performance.

Methods

Overview of the pseudo-velocity-aided navigation framework

The proposed framework combines data-driven pseudo-velocity generation with a loosely coupled SINS/DVL integrated navigation filter. When DVL measurements are available, the DVL velocity is used as the supervised label for training the pseudo-velocity model. When DVL measurements are unavailable, the trained model predicts horizontal pseudo-DVL velocity measurements from recent inertial-navigation information. These pseudo-measurements are then introduced into the extended Kalman filter (EKF) at the original DVL update instants to constrain horizontal velocity errors and reduce the accumulation of position drift.

The navigation procedure contains two stages. In the offline training stage, DVL-available data are used to construct input–label pairs, where the input is a recent inertial-navigation sequence and the label is the corresponding horizontal DVL velocity. In the DVL-outage stage, the trained network parameters are fixed. The SINS mechanization continues to propagate attitude, velocity and position using IMU measurements, while the network produces pseudo-DVL horizontal velocity only at the original DVL update instants. These pseudo-measurements are then used in the EKF measurement update in place of the missing real DVL velocity. Therefore, the IMU measurements are used continuously for state propagation, whereas the real or pseudo-DVL velocity is used intermittently for measurement correction.

The input to the learning model consists of inertial measurement unit (IMU) angular velocity and specific force, together with SINS-derived attitude, horizontal position and horizontal velocity. The model outputs two-dimensional horizontal velocity measurements in the navigation frame. Temporal feature construction

At each DVL update instant, the learning model uses a short temporal window ending at the current time. The single-time-step input vector is defined as19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{x}_t= \left[ \omega _x,\omega _y,\omega _z, f_x,f_y,f_z, \phi ,\theta ,\psi , p_E,p_N, v_E,v_N \right] ^T , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _x,\omega _y,\omega _z$$\end{document} are the three-axis angular velocities measured by the IMU; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_x,f_y,f_z$$\end{document} are the three-axis specific forces; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi ,\theta ,\psi$$\end{document} are the attitude angles; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_E,p_N$$\end{document} are the horizontal position components; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_E,v_N$$\end{document} are the horizontal velocity components obtained from the SINS solution.

The temporal input sequence is constructed using a sliding window of length L:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{X}_t= \left[ \boldsymbol{x}_{t-L+1}, \boldsymbol{x}_{t-L+2}, \ldots , \boldsymbol{x}_{t} \right] \in \mathbb {R}^{L\times 13}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In this study, L=20. With an IMU update frequency of 200 Hz, this corresponds to a time span of 0.1 s. This window length was selected to capture short local dynamics while keeping the input compact for online inference.

The supervised label at the kth DVL update instant is the horizontal DVL velocity:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{y}_k = \boldsymbol{S}_v \boldsymbol{v}^{n}_{\textrm{DVL}}(t_k) = \left[ v_{\textrm{DVL},E}(t_k), v_{\textrm{DVL},N}(t_k) \right] ^T . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The corresponding input sample is22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{X}_k= \left[ \boldsymbol{x}(t_k-(L-1)\Delta t), \ldots , \boldsymbol{x}(t_k) \right] , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where Δ t=1/200 s is the IMU sampling interval. The IMU, SINS and DVL data were synchronized under a common time base, so each DVL velocity label corresponds to the inertial-navigation window ending at the same time.

Attention-guided temporal convolutional model

The pseudo-velocity model uses temporal convolution to encode the input sequence and an attention module to aggregate the encoded temporal features. This design follows the general idea that temporal convolution can extract local sequential dependencies, while attention can adaptively weight informative time steps in a temporal window21,22. Causal dilated convolution is used so that the output at time t depends only on the current and previous inputs.

For a one-dimensional input sequence, the causal dilated convolution can be written as23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y(t) = \sum _{i=0}^{K-1} f(i)\,x(t-d\,i), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where K is the kernel size, i is the kernel-tap index, f(i) is the convolution kernel coefficient corresponding to the ith tap, d is the dilation factor of the current convolutional layer, and x(t) and y(t) are the input and output of the convolution operation. The term x(t-d i) indicates that the output at time t only depends on the current and previous input samples. In this expression, d is a fixed dilation factor for a given convolutional layer rather than a function of i.

Let the temporal feature sequence produced by the TCN module be24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{H} = [\boldsymbol{h}_1,\boldsymbol{h}_2,\ldots ,\boldsymbol{h}_L], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{h}_t$$\end{document} is the hidden feature at the tth time step. The attention score for each time step is calculated as25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} e_t = \boldsymbol{u}_a^T \tanh \left( \boldsymbol{W}_a \boldsymbol{h}_t+\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{att}} \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{W}_a$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{u}_a$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{att}}$$\end{document} are trainable attention parameters. The notation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{b}_{\textrm{att}}$$\end{document} is used to distinguish the attention bias from the accelerometer bias \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{b}_a$$\end{document} in the SINS error-state model.

The normalized attention weight is26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \alpha _t = \frac{\exp (e_t)}{\sum _{j=1}^{L}\exp (e_j)} . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The context vector is obtained by weighted aggregation:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{c} = \sum _{t=1}^{L} \alpha _t \boldsymbol{h}_t . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The context vector is passed through a fully connected output layer to generate the horizontal pseudo-DVL velocity:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_k = [\hat{v}_{E,k},\hat{v}_{N,k}]^T . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The model is trained by minimizing the mean squared error between the predicted and reference DVL velocity:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L} = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{k=1}^{N} \left\| \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_k - \boldsymbol{y}_k \right\| _2^2 , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where N is the number of training samples.

The detailed layer-level configuration of the proposed TCN-Attention model is included in Table 2. The model first maps the 20× 13 input sequence into hidden temporal features using two causal dilated convolutional blocks. Each block contains a one-dimensional causal dilated convolution, ReLU activation, dropout and residual connection. The dilation factors of the two convolutional blocks are 1 and 2, respectively, which enlarges the temporal receptive field without using future information. The temporal attention module then calculates a normalized weight for each encoded time step and forms a context vector by weighted aggregation. Finally, a fully connected regression layer maps the context vector to the two-dimensional horizontal pseudo-DVL velocity.

Simulation dataset and sensor settings

The simulation dataset consisted of 16 AUV trajectories with different motion patterns. Ten trajectories were used for model training, two were used for validation and four were used for testing. The four test trajectories corresponded to nonlinear disturbed motion, closed-loop turning, steady straight-line motion and arc-turning motion. This split was kept the same for all compared learning-based models.

In the simulation, IMU measurements were generated by adding bias and Gaussian white noise to the reference angular velocity and specific force. DVL measurements were generated by adding random measurement noise to the reference velocity. The IMU, SINS and DVL data were synchronized under a common time base. The learning models generated pseudo-velocity measurements only at the original DVL update instants; at other IMU update instants, the navigation system performed inertial propagation only.

Pseudo-measurement update during DVL outage

During DVL-available periods, the measured DVL velocity was used to construct supervised labels for offline training. During DVL-unavailable periods, the trained network parameters were fixed, and only forward inference was performed. At each original DVL update instant, the predicted pseudo-velocity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_k$$\end{document} was used to replace the missing horizontal DVL velocity in the EKF measurement update.

Thus, the proposed method does not change the state vector or the measurement matrix of the loosely coupled SINS/DVL filter. The only change during DVL outage is the source of the horizontal velocity measurement: the real DVL velocity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{S}_v\boldsymbol{V}^{n}_{\textrm{DVL}}$$\end{document} is replaced by the network-generated pseudo-velocity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_k$$\end{document}. This design keeps the pseudo-measurement update consistent with the conventional DVL velocity update and avoids introducing additional filter states.

The pseudo-measurement vector was constructed as30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\boldsymbol{Z}}_k = \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_k - \boldsymbol{S}_v \boldsymbol{V}^{n}_{\textrm{SINS}} . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The same measurement matrix in equation (15) was then used for the EKF update. To ensure a fair comparison, the same fixed pseudo-measurement noise covariance was used for all learning-based auxiliary models. Adaptive estimation of pseudo-measurement uncertainty was not considered in this study.

Evaluation metrics

Velocity prediction accuracy was evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE). For a velocity component, these metrics are defined as31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \left| v_i-\hat{v}_i \right| , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \left( v_i-\hat{v}_i \right) ^2 }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{v}_i$$\end{document} are the reference and estimated velocity components, respectively.

Trajectory recovery performance was evaluated using absolute trajectory error (ATE), defined as33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{ATE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \sqrt{ \left( p_{E,i}-\hat{p}_{E,i} \right) ^2 + \left( p_{N,i}-\hat{p}_{N,i} \right) ^2 }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{E,i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{N,i}$$\end{document} are the reference eastward and northward position components, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{p}_{E,i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{p}_{N,i}$$\end{document} are the corresponding estimated position components.

Results

Continuous DVL outage evaluation

The proposed method was evaluated under a continuous DVL outage setting. During each test trajectory, real DVL velocity measurements were not provided to the filter. Instead, each learning-based model generated horizontal pseudo-velocity measurements at the original DVL update instants, and these measurements were used for the extended Kalman filter update. This setting was designed to test whether learned pseudo-measurements could maintain navigation performance when the loosely coupled SINS/DVL system lost external velocity observations for an extended period.

The simulated dataset contained 16 AUV trajectories, covering straight-line motion, curved motion, acceleration–deceleration and closed-loop manoeuvres. Ten trajectories were used for training, two for validation and four for testing. The four test trajectories are shown in Fig. 2. Task 1 represents nonlinear disturbed motion with steering and velocity changes. Task 2 is a closed-loop turning trajectory. Task 3 is a comparatively steady straight-line trajectory with small heading variation. Task 4 is an arc-turning trajectory with sustained lateral motion. These trajectories were selected to evaluate the models under both steady and manoeuvring conditions.Fig. 2Test trajectories used for continuous DVL outage evaluation. Velocity error performance

The proposed TCN-Attention model was compared with three learning-based baselines: multilayer perceptron (MLP), temporal convolutional network (TCN) and gated recurrent unit with attention (GRU-Attention). All four models used the same input features, temporal window, training and validation data, normalization parameters and two-dimensional horizontal velocity output. This comparison was used to assess the contribution of temporal convolution and attention-based temporal weighting to pseudo-velocity generation.

Figure 3 shows the eastward and northward velocity errors for the four test tasks. The MLP model reduced the velocity error to some extent, but its error curves were more sensitive to changes in motion state. The TCN model generally produced smaller errors than MLP, indicating that temporal information in the inertial-navigation sequence is useful for reconstructing DVL velocity. GRU-Attention showed better short-term response than MLP and TCN in several manoeuvring intervals.

The TCN-Attention model produced the lowest overall velocity errors among the compared learning-based methods. Its advantage was clearer in manoeuvring trajectories, especially in the closed-loop turning and arc-turning tasks. During turning and velocity-transition intervals, the TCN-Attention error curves remained smoother and showed smaller deviations than those of the other models. Discussion

The results show that learned pseudo-velocity measurements can reduce navigation drift when DVL velocity updates are unavailable for an extended period. Under the continuous outage setting, all learning-based methods improved the navigation solution compared with SINS-only propagation, but their performance differed across motion patterns. The TCN-Attention model produced the lowest velocity errors and the smallest trajectory errors, including ATE and horizontal position RMSE, in all four test tasks. This suggests that combining temporal convolution with attention-based weighting is useful for reconstructing DVL-like horizontal velocity observations from recent inertial-navigation information.

The improvement is closely related to the temporal structure of the problem. During DVL outage, the mapping from inertial measurements and SINS-derived states to the missing DVL velocity is not determined by a single time step. It depends on the recent motion history of the vehicle, especially when the vehicle is turning or changing speed. Temporal convolutional networks have been used in sequence prediction problems because they can model local temporal dependencies efficiently21. Attention mechanisms have also been used to emphasize more informative time steps in temporal prediction tasks22. In the present study, the temporal convolution module provides a compact way to extract local dependencies from the input window while preserving causality. The attention module then allows the model to weight different time steps unequally, which is useful because manoeuvring intervals do not contribute equally to velocity prediction. This may explain why the advantage of TCN-Attention was clearer in the closed-loop turning and arc-turning tasks than in the nearly straight trajectory.

The comparison with the baseline models also supports this interpretation. The MLP model uses the flattened input window but does not explicitly model the temporal order of the sequence. Its performance was therefore more sensitive to changes in motion state. The TCN baseline captured temporal information and reduced the error relative to MLP, but it did not include an explicit mechanism for emphasizing more informative time steps. GRU-Attention provided a recurrent temporal representation with attention, but in the present setting the TCN-Attention model showed lower velocity and trajectory errors. This difference may be related to the ability of causal dilated convolution to capture short local dynamics efficiently within the selected window.

The trajectory results are important because small velocity errors can accumulate into position drift during long outage intervals. The lower ATE values obtained by TCN-Attention indicate that the pseudo-velocity measurements were not only closer to the reference velocity, but also useful as filter observations for constraining the integrated navigation solution. This is consistent with neural-network-assisted integrated navigation studies in which learned motion features were used to support positioning or filtering under degraded measurement conditions23,24. The improvement was particularly clear in manoeuvring trajectories, where velocity prediction errors are more likely to affect the recovered path. These results support the use of learned pseudo-measurements as a software-based compensation strategy when additional external navigation sensors are unavailable.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the evaluation was based on simulated AUV trajectories. Although the simulated trajectories included different motion patterns and sensor noise settings, real underwater environments may introduce more complex disturbances, including acoustic dropouts, water-current effects, installation errors and unmodelled sensor behaviour. Second, the DVL outage intervals were predefined in the experiments. The study therefore does not address online DVL validity assessment, fault detection or switching logic between real and pseudo-DVL measurements. Third, the same fixed pseudo-measurement noise covariance was used for the learning-based auxiliary models to keep the comparison consistent. In practical deployment, the uncertainty of the generated pseudo-velocity measurements may vary with motion state, outage duration and input quality.

Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as a simulation-based validation of the proposed pseudo-velocity generation strategy rather than as a complete demonstration of field-ready underwater navigation performance. The method shows that attention-guided temporal convolution can provide useful pseudo-DVL velocity measurements under the tested conditions, but its practical performance will still depend on sensor calibration quality, vehicle dynamics, environmental disturbances, DVL outage patterns and the reliability of online DVL validity detection.

Future work should validate the method using lake-trial or sea-trial data and examine its robustness under more diverse sensor errors and environmental disturbances. Another useful direction is to estimate the uncertainty of the pseudo-velocity output online and use it to adapt the EKF measurement noise covariance. Intermittent DVL outage scenarios, DVL quality assessment and switching strategies should also be incorporated before practical deployment. In addition, further analysis of the attention weights may help clarify which motion segments contribute most to pseudo-velocity generation and may improve the interpretability of the model for underwater navigation applications.
