Weak Adversarial Neural Pushforward Method for the McKean–Vlasov / Mean-Field Fokker–Planck Equation
Andrew Qing He, Wei Cai

Abstract
We extend the Weak Adversarial Neural Pushforward Method (WANPM) [ 1 ] to the McKean–Vlasov mean-field Fokker–Planck equation, covering both the stationary and time-dependent cases. The key observation is that the mean-field nonlinearity — an expectation under the solution distribution — is naturally estimated by Monte Carlo sampling from the pushforward network, requiring no change to the architecture and only minor modifications to the training loop. For the quadratic (granular media) interaction kernel, the interaction term reduces to the batch sample mean, eliminating secondary sampling entirely. We also identify a dimension-dependent frequency initialization rule for the adversarial test functions, necessary to avoid spurious minimizers. Numerical experiments on linear McKean–Vlasov benchmarks in 2, 5, 20, and 100 dimensions confirm accurate recovery of the exact Gaussian stationary and transient distributions, with training times ranging from 27 seconds (2D) to 10 minutes (100D) on a single GPU.



Keywords:
McKean–Vlasov equation, mean-field Fokker–Planck, neural pushforward
map, weak adversarial network, plane-wave test functions, granular media, interacting
particle systems.

1
Introduction

The standard Fokker–Planck equation (FPE) governs the time evolution of the probability
density
\rho(t,\mathbf{x})
of a stochastic process whose drift and diffusion coefficients are
prescribed functions of
(t,\mathbf{x})
. A natural and physically important generalization arises
when these coefficients depend on the evolving distribution
\rho_{t}
itself, yielding the
McKean–Vlasov or mean-field Fokker–Planck equation:

\partial_{t}\rho(t,\mathbf{x})=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\bigl[b(\mathbf{x},\rho_{t})\,\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\bigr]+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\,\Delta\rho(t,\mathbf{x}),

(1)

with initial condition
\rho(0,\mathbf{x})=\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})
. This class of equations appears in
interacting particle systems and granular media
[
3
]
, Curie–Weiss spin
systems, stochastic flocking
[
4
]
, mathematical finance and mean-field
games
[
5
]
, and the mean-field analysis of overparameterized
neural networks
[
6
]
.

The nonlinearity of (
1
) — the unknown
\rho
appears both as the evolved
density and inside the convolution integral defining the drift — makes it significantly
harder to solve than the standard FPE. Particle methods exploit the propagation of chaos
phenomenon
[
2
]
: as
N\to\infty
, the empirical measure of
N

interacting particles converges to the solution of (
1
). However, particle
methods suffer from statistical noise, require a large number of particles for accuracy,
and do not naturally produce a functional representation of
\rho
queryable at arbitrary
points.

Neural network methods for solving Fokker–Planck equations have been developed in
recent works
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
. In

[
1
]
, the Weak Adversarial Neural Pushforward Method (WANPM) was
introduced, which learns a neural pushforward map
F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
that transforms
samples from a simple base distribution into samples from the solution distribution,
trained via an adversarial weak formulation with plane-wave test functions. WANPM avoids
the need for explicit density representations, invertible architectures, or score
estimation, and scales to high-dimensional problems.

The present paper extends WANPM to the McKean–Vlasov setting. For the quadratic
(granular media) kernel
W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
, the mean-field
nonlinearity reduces to a sample mean, requiring no secondary sampling. We develop both
the stationary and time-dependent formulations, analyze training subtleties that arise
from the self-referential structure of the equation, and demonstrate accuracy across
dimensions from 2 to 100.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the McKean–Vlasov
equation and the quadratic kernel simplification. Section
3
develops
WANPM for the stationary problem. Section
4
extends the method to the
time-dependent case, including a discussion of adversarial frequency initialization.
Section
5
presents numerical experiments, and Section
6

concludes.

2
The McKean–Vlasov Equation

2.1
Derivation from an Interacting Particle System

Consider
N
particles with positions
X_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
obeying

dX_{i}=b\!\left(X_{i},\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{j}(t)}\right)dt+\sigma\,dW_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

(2)

where
W_{i}
are independent standard Brownian motions and
\delta_{\mathbf{y}}
denotes the
Dirac measure at
\mathbf{y}
. By the propagation of chaos phenomenon
[
2
]
,
as
N\to\infty
the single-particle density satisfies the McKean–Vlasov equation (
1
).

2.2
The Aggregation–Diffusion Form

The most widely studied instance takes the drift as the superposition of a confinement
force and a pairwise interaction:

b(\mathbf{x},\rho_{t})=-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})-\nabla(W*\rho_{t})(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla V(\mathbf{x})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,\rho(t,\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}.

(3)

Substituting (
3
) into (
1
) yields the aggregation–diffusion
equation

\partial_{t}\rho=\nabla\cdot\bigl[(\nabla V)\rho\bigr]+\nabla\cdot\bigl[(\nabla W*\rho)\rho\bigr]+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\rho.

(4)

The unknown
\rho
appears both as the evolved density and inside the convolution; this
self-referential structure is the central mathematical difficulty.

2.3
Quadratic Interaction Kernel and Complexity Reduction

We focus on the quadratic (granular media) kernel

W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
, for which a key simplification holds.

Proposition 1

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,\rho_{t}(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}-m(t),\qquad m(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\rho_{t}}[\mathbf{y}].

(5)

Proof.

Direct computation:
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
, so

\int(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\rho_{t}(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}-m(t)
.
∎

With confinement
V(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\theta}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
and the quadratic kernel, the full
drift simplifies to

b(\mathbf{x},\rho_{t})=-\theta\mathbf{x}-(\mathbf{x}-m(t))=-(\theta+1)\mathbf{x}+m(t)=:-\lambda\mathbf{x}+m(t),

(6)

where
\lambda:=\theta+1
is the effective mean-reversion rate. The interaction integral
reduces to a single scalar
m(t)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]
, which is the sample mean of the primary
training batch during training — no secondary sampling is needed.

3
WANPM for the Stationary McKean–Vlasov Equation

3.1
The Stationary Problem

Setting
\partial_{t}\rho=0
in (
1
) yields

-\nabla\cdot\bigl[b(\mathbf{x},\rho)\,\rho(\mathbf{x})\bigr]+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta\rho(\mathbf{x})=0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}=1.

(7)

This is a nonlinear eigenvalue problem: the operator acting on
\rho
depends on
\rho

itself through the convolution. For the quadratic kernel with
V(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\theta}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
,
the drift (
6
) gives
b(\mathbf{x},\rho)=-\lambda\mathbf{x}+m^{*}
where

m^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathbf{x}]
. By the symmetry of
V
and
W
, the unique stationary solution is

\rho^{*}=\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\lambda}I)
with
m^{*}=0
, giving

b(\mathbf{x},\rho^{*})=-\lambda\mathbf{x}
.

3.2
Weak Formulation

To derive the weak form, multiply (
7
) by a smooth test function

f(\mathbf{x})
and integrate over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The right-hand side becomes

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla\cdot\!\left[\rho(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla\!\left(V(\mathbf{x})+(W*\rho)(\mathbf{x})+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\rho(\mathbf{x})\right)\right]d\mathbf{x}.

One integration by parts moves the divergence onto
\nabla f
:

=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\left[\rho(\mathbf{x})\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\!\left(V(\mathbf{x})+(W*\rho)(\mathbf{x})+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\rho(\mathbf{x})\right)\right]d\mathbf{x}.

Separating the nonlinear diffusion term
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\rho
from the remaining
drift and applying integration by parts once more to that term gives

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\!\Bigl(V(\mathbf{x})+(W*\rho)(\mathbf{x})\Bigr)\,d\mathbf{x}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}\,d\mathbf{x}.

Expanding the interaction term
(W*\rho)(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\rho(\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}

and collecting by role, the right-hand side decomposes as

-E_{V}-E_{W}+E_{D}=0,

(8)

where

\displaystyle E_{V}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial V(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\,d\mathbf{x},

(9)

\displaystyle E_{W}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\rho(\mathbf{y})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial W}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}\,d\mathbf{x},

(10)

\displaystyle E_{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}f(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}\,d\mathbf{x}.

(11)

Equation (
8
) is the weak stationarity condition that the pushforward
network is trained to satisfy.

Following
[
1
]
, we choose plane-wave test functions

f^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})=\sin(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b^{(k)})
, whose derivatives are all analytic:

\nabla f^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cos(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b^{(k)}),\qquad\Delta f^{(k)}(\mathbf{x})=-\|\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\|^{2}\sin(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b^{(k)}).

(12)

For the quadratic kernel, substituting (
6
) into
E_{V}+E_{W}
and using

\nabla W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
gives the combined drift contribution

(E_{V}+E_{W})\big|_{\text{quad}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(\mathbf{x})\,(-\lambda\mathbf{x}+m^{*})\cdot\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cos(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b^{(k)})\,d\mathbf{x},

(13)

where
m^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathbf{x}]
is the only quantity depending nonlinearly on
\rho
.

3.3
Neural Parametrization

We parametrize the stationary distribution
\rho^{*}
via a neural pushforward map

F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
d
is the dimension of the base distribution (not
necessarily equal to
n
). If
\mathbf{r}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{base}}
, then
\mathbf{x}=F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(\mathbf{r})
is a
sample from the learned distribution
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
. The pushforward parametrization
naturally enforces the normalization constraint. In the stationary setting,
F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}

takes only
\mathbf{r}
as input.

The
K
test functions have learnable parameters
\eta=\{\mathbf{w}^{(k)},b^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.

3.4
Loss Function

The training objective is the adversarial squared weak residual:

\mathcal{L}[\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\eta]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Bigl(-E_{V}^{(k,m)}-E_{W}^{(k,m)}+E_{D}^{(k,m)}\Bigr)\right]^{2},

(14)

where
\{\mathbf{x}^{(m)}=F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(\mathbf{r}^{(m)})\}_{m=1}^{M}
is a batch of pushed samples,
the terms
E_{V}^{(k,m)},E_{D}^{(k,m)}
are the per-sample contributions
to (
9
)–(
11
) evaluated via (
12
),
and the mean
m^{*}
is approximated by the batch mean

\hat{m}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(\mathbf{r}^{(m)}),\quad\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{base}},

(15)

computed from the same batch as the residual so that
\partial\hat{m}/\partial\boldsymbol{\vartheta}

contributes to the total gradient, enforcing the self-consistency constraint

m^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}[\mathbf{x}]
through the optimization dynamics.

3.5
Training Algorithm

The min-max optimization problem is

\min_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\max_{\eta}\,\mathcal{L}[\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\eta],

(16)

solved by alternating gradient descent on
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
and gradient ascent on
\eta
.

4
Extension to the Time-Dependent Problem

4.1
Weak Formulation

To derive the weak form for the time-dependent problem, multiply (
1
) by a
smooth test function
\psi(t,\mathbf{x})
and integrate over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The spatial integral of
the right-hand side proceeds identically to the stationary derivation of
Section
3.2
, yielding

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})\,\partial_{t}\rho\,d\mathbf{x}=-E_{V}(t)-E_{W}(t)+E_{D}(t),

where
E_{V}(t),E_{W}(t),E_{D}(t)
are the time-
t
versions of the
integrals (
9
)–(
11
) (with
f
replaced by
\psi(t,\cdot)
).
Integrating both sides over
t\in[0,T]
and applying integration by parts in time to
the left-hand side,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi\,\partial_{t}\rho\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(T,\mathbf{x})\rho(T,\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(0,\mathbf{x})\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}-\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{t}\psi\cdot\rho\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt,

the space-time weak form becomes

E_{T}-E_{0}-E_{t}+E_{V}+E_{W}-E_{D}=0,

(17)

where we use the plane-wave test functions

\psi^{(k)}(t,\mathbf{x})=\sin(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\kappa^{(k)}t+b^{(k)})
and define

\displaystyle E_{T}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(T,\mathbf{x})\,\rho(T,\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x},

(18)

\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(0,\mathbf{x})\,\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x},

(19)

\displaystyle E_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}(t,\mathbf{x})\,\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt,

(20)

\displaystyle E_{V}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_{i}}\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt,

(21)

\displaystyle E_{W}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\,\rho(t,\mathbf{y})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\psi(t,\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial W}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{y}\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt,

(22)

\displaystyle E_{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}\,d\mathbf{x}\,dt.

(23)

The derivatives of the plane-wave test function are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi^{(k)}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{(k)}\cos(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\kappa^{(k)}t+b^{(k)}),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi^{(k)}}{\partial x_{i}}

\displaystyle=w_{i}^{(k)}\cos(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\kappa^{(k)}t+b^{(k)}),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\psi^{(k)}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-(w_{i}^{(k)})^{2}\sin(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\kappa^{(k)}t+b^{(k)}).

(24)

The terms
E_{T},E_{0},E_{t},E_{V},E_{D}
have exactly the same structure as in the standard
FPE weak form of
[
1
]
; the sole new ingredient is
E_{W}
, which involves a
double spatial integral over
\rho(t,\cdot)\otimes\rho(t,\cdot)
.

The adversarial loss is the squared residual of (
17
) averaged over
K

test functions:

\mathcal{L}[\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\eta]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Bigl(\widehat{E}_{T}^{(k)}-\widehat{E}_{0}^{(k)}-\widehat{E}_{t}^{(k)}+\widehat{E}_{V}^{(k)}+\widehat{E}_{W}^{(k)}-\widehat{E}_{D}^{(k)}\Bigr)^{2},

(25)

where each
\widehat{E}
denotes the Monte Carlo estimator of the corresponding integral,
described below.

4.2
Pushforward Representation

We represent
\rho(t,\cdot)
via a time-parameterized pushforward map:

F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(t,\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\sqrt{t}\,\tilde{F}_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(t,\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{r}),

(26)

where
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
,
\mathbf{r}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{base}}
, and

\tilde{F}_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}:\mathbb{R}^{1+n+d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. At
t=0
,
F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(0,\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{x}_{0}
,
enforcing the initial condition exactly.

4.3
Monte Carlo Estimators

For the standard terms
E_{t},E_{V},E_{D}
, we draw
M
i.i.d. triples

(t^{(m)},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(m)},\mathbf{r}^{(m)})
with
t^{(m)}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)
,

\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(m)}\sim\rho_{0}
,
\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{base}}
, and let

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)}=F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(t^{(m)},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(m)},\mathbf{r}^{(m)})
. The terminal and initial terms
use dedicated batches of size
M_{T}
and
M_{0}
at fixed times
T
and
0
.

Estimating
E_{W}
.

The interaction term (
22
) involves a double integral over
\rho(t,\cdot)
:

E_{W}=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\rho_{t}}\!\bigl[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\psi(t,\boldsymbol{\xi})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\sim\rho_{t}}[\nabla W(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\eta})]\bigr]\,dt.

(27)

We introduce a dedicated batch of
M_{W}
triples

(t^{(m)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(m)})
, where
t^{(m)}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)
and

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(m)}
are two independent pushforward samples at time
t^{(m)}
.
The Monte Carlo estimator is

\widehat{E}_{W}^{(k)}=\frac{T}{M_{W}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{W}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\psi^{(k)}(t^{(m)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)})\cdot\nabla W(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)}-\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(m)}).

(28)

This estimator is unbiased for any
M_{W}\geq 1
.

Remark 1

(Choice of
M_{W}
)
.

In practice one may set
M_{W}=M
and reuse the time points
\{t^{(m)}\}
drawn for
the interior terms, with
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)}
being the same pushed sample. However, this sample
reuse does not appreciably reduce training cost:
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(m)}
still requires a full
forward and backward pass since it also depends on
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
. Setting
M_{W}
independently
gives more flexibility to trade cost against estimator variance.

Remark 2

(Quadratic kernel simplification)
.

For
W(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
, we have
\nabla W(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\eta})=\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\eta}
.
Since
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(m)}]=m(t^{(m)})
, one may replace
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(m)}
by the batch mean

\hat{m}(t^{(m)})
to reduce variance, recovering the formulation of the stationary case
and eliminating the secondary sample entirely. This is what is done in our experiments.

Remark 3

(Singular kernels)
.

When
W
is singular (e.g.
W(\mathbf{x})=-\log\|\mathbf{x}\|
for Keller–Segel),
\nabla W(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\eta})

is large when
\boldsymbol{\xi}\approx\boldsymbol{\eta}
. We adopt kernel mollification: replace
\nabla W(\mathbf{x})

by
\nabla W_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
evaluated at
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}

instead of
\|\mathbf{x}\|
. The bias introduced is
O(\varepsilon^{2})
rather than
O(\varepsilon^{-(n-1)})

because
\nabla W
is odd (
\nabla W(-\mathbf{z})=-\nabla W(\mathbf{z})
) and
\boldsymbol{\xi}-\boldsymbol{\eta}
has a
symmetric distribution around zero, causing near-field contributions to cancel in pairs.

4.4
Tensor-Product Time Sampling for the Mean-Field Drift

For the time-dependent McKean–Vlasov problem, accurate estimation of

m(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t}}[\mathbf{x}]
is essential because the drift depends on it. A naive approach
— drawing all
M
interior samples at
M
different random times — produces a
time-averaged constant
\hat{m}\approx\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}m(t)\,dt
, which biases the
drift and corrupts the learned mean evolution.

The correct approach uses a tensor-product sampling structure: draw
N_{T}
fixed
quadrature time nodes
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{T}}
on
(0,T]
and, at each
t_{i}
, draw

M_{\mathrm{per}}
independent base samples
(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(j)},\mathbf{r}^{(j)})_{j=1}^{M_{\mathrm{per}}}
.
Then

\hat{m}(t_{i})=\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{per}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\mathrm{per}}}F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}\!\left(t_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(j)},\mathbf{r}^{(j)}\right)

(29)

is a valid unbiased estimate of
m(t_{i})
, and the interior term is approximated by the
quadrature sum

\widehat{E}^{(k)}=\frac{T}{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{T}}\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{per}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\mathrm{per}}}\left[\frac{\partial\psi^{(k)}}{\partial t}+\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t_{i}}]\psi^{(k)}\right]\!\left(t_{i},F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}(t_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(j)},\mathbf{r}^{(j)})\right).

(30)

The total number of forward passes
N_{T}\times M_{\mathrm{per}}
matches the standard
WANPM interior batch size, so there is no additional computational cost.

4.5
Adversarial Frequency Initialization

The initialization scale of the frequency vectors
\mathbf{w}^{(k)}
in the adversarial test
functions is critical for avoiding spurious minimizers.

For the quadratic kernel, the two-point distribution
\rho_{\mathrm{two}}
has the same
mean and variance as the true stationary Gaussian, and satisfies the weak residual
approximately when test function frequencies are small. One can verify analytically
that the residual under
\rho_{\mathrm{two}}
at a plane-wave test function with scalar
frequency
w
and bias
b
is

R^{(k)}_{\mathrm{two}}=\sin(wa)\Bigl(\lambda aw\sin(b)-\tfrac{\sigma^{2}w^{2}}{2}\cos(b)\Bigr),

(31)

while the exact Gaussian gives
R^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Gaussian}}=0
for all
(w,b)
. For small

w
(specifically when
wa\ll 1
), the factor
\sin(wa)\approx wa
is small, making

|R^{(k)}_{\mathrm{two}}|
comparably small to
|R^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Gaussian}}|
. In this
regime, the two-point distribution achieves a lower loss than the exact Gaussian.

When frequencies are initialized at a sufficiently large scale, the two-point distribution
incurs a large residual while the Gaussian remains near zero, so the adversary correctly
penalizes the spurious solution.

Dimension-dependent frequency scaling.

Beyond this issue of spurious minimizers, there is a second and independent consideration:
the test function wavelength must match the spatial scale of the solution.

An
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2}I_{d\times d})
random vector has expected length

\mathcal{O}(\sigma_{w}\sqrt{d})
, so the plane-wave test function

\sin(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b^{(k)})
has effective wavelength
\mathcal{O}(\sigma_{w}\sqrt{d}/\|\mathbf{x}\|)

near the bulk of the distribution. To correctly probe the solution at the spatial scale

\mathcal{O}(\sigma/\sqrt{2\lambda})
, the frequency should satisfy

\sigma_{w}\cdot(\sigma/\sqrt{2\lambda})\approx 1
, giving
\sigma_{w}\approx\sqrt{2\lambda}/\sigma
.
Equivalently, the test function inner product
\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}
should be

\mathcal{O}(1)
for typical samples
\mathbf{x}
.

In higher dimensions, an additional correction arises: the dot product

\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}
with
\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2}I)
and
\mathbf{x}
at
the scale
\sigma/\sqrt{2\lambda}
has standard deviation

\sigma_{w}\cdot(\sigma/\sqrt{2\lambda})\cdot\sqrt{n}
. Requiring this to be

\mathcal{O}(1)
gives the scaling

\sigma_{w}\approx\sqrt{2\lambda}/(\sigma\sqrt{n}).

(32)

This is consistent with the empirically observed rule: for
d=2
,

\sigma_{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,2^{2})
works well, while for
d=20
, initializing

\sigma_{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.2^{2})
is needed.

6
Conclusion

We have developed the Weak Adversarial Neural Pushforward Method for the McKean–Vlasov
mean-field Fokker–Planck equation, covering both the stationary and time-dependent
settings. The central findings are as follows.

For the quadratic (granular media) interaction kernel, the mean-field nonlinearity
reduces to the sample mean of the primary training batch, requiring no secondary sampling
and no modification to the pushforward architecture. The same principle extends to
polynomial-degree and separable kernels, for which the interaction integrals reduce to
batch moments or scalar expectations.

The initialization scale of adversarial test function frequencies is critical. Small
initial frequencies allow the two-point distribution, which matches the exact Gaussian in
mean and variance, to achieve a lower loss than the true solution. The required scale
follows the dimension-dependent rule (
32
): for an
n
-dimensional
problem the scale should decrease as
1/\sqrt{n}
. The adversarial training is in
principle self-adaptive, but does not always converge to the correct frequency without a
good initialization.

For the time-dependent problem, a tensor-product sampling structure over time and space
is needed to produce valid per-time mean-field estimates without bias. Fixed quadrature
nodes in time, with independent Monte Carlo draws at each node, resolve this correctly at
no extra computational cost.

Numerical experiments on the linear McKean–Vlasov benchmark in 2, 5, 20, and 100
dimensions confirm accurate recovery of both the stationary and transient Gaussian
distributions, with per-component mean and variance errors at the
1
–
5\%
level and
training times from
27\,
s to
10\,
min on a single GPU.

Natural extensions include higher-dimensional stationary problems, separable and singular
interaction kernels (requiring mollification of
\nabla W
for the Keller–Segel case),
and granular-media-type kernels exhibiting phase transitions. The Keller–Segel kernel

W(\mathbf{x})=-\log\|\mathbf{x}\|
requires a secondary batch for the interaction integral, and the
singular-kernel estimator relies on the oddness cancellation analyzed in
Remark 3 of Section
4
.
