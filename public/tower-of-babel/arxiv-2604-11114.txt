Some universal inequalities for Dirichlet eigenvalues of the Laplacian on a Euclidean convex domain
Kei Funano

Abstract
. We establish two universal inequalities for Dirichlet eigenvalues of the Laplacian on a Euclidean convex domain.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
35P15, 53C23, 58J50

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We consider the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem for the Laplacian:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda u&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

It is well known that the spectrum consists of an infinite sequence of eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1}(\Omega)<\lambda_{2}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{3}(\Omega)\leq\dots\to\infty
. The asymptotic behavior of eigenvalues is described by the classical Weyl’s law (
[
18
]
, refer to
[
11
, Section 3.3]
):

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\sim 4\pi^{2}\left(\frac{k}{\omega_{n}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)}\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}\quad\text{as }k\to\infty,

where
\omega_{n}
denotes the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is therefore a natural question whether the ratio
\lambda_{k}(\Omega)/\lambda_{l}(\Omega)

can be bounded above and below by explicit multiples of
(k/l)^{2/n}
with constants.

Such inequalities are referred to as
universal inequalities
,
as they hold for all domains in a given class without dependence on the particular geometry of
\Omega
. The study of universal inequalities for Dirichlet eigenvalues was pioneered by
Payne, Pólya, and Weinberger
[
14
]
. After that many mathematicians contributed and now the study of universal inequalities has a rich history (refer to
[
1
]
and
[
11
, Section 5.4]
). A notable feature of these Dirichlet universal inequalities is that they hold
for
arbitrary
bounded domains, without any geometric restrictions. However, many of these universal inequalities focus on the ratio of consecutive eigenvalues or the sum of the first
k
eigenvalues. It is more challenging to establish ”ratios of arbitrary eigenvalues” that hold uniformly for a specific class of domains.

In this paper, we consider the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem on bounded convex domains
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and establish both upper and lower bounds
for
\lambda_{k}(\Omega)/\lambda_{l}(\Omega)
in terms of
(k/l)^{2/n}
.
Our main results are the following two theorems.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a constant
c_{n}>0
depending only on
n
which satisfies the following. Let
\Omega
be a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then for any
k\geq l
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\leq c_{n}\Big(\frac{k}{l}\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{l}(\Omega).

The constant
c_{n}
can be chosen as
c_{n}=12n^{3}(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}
, where
j_{\nu,1}
is the first positive zero of the Bessel function
J_{\nu}
of the first kind of order
\nu
.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist two constants
\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
depending only on
n
which satisfy the following. Let
\Omega
be a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
k\geq l
. If
\lambda_{l}(\Omega)>\alpha_{n}\lambda_{1}(\Omega)
then we have

\displaystyle\beta_{n}\Big(\frac{k}{l}\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{l}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{k}(\Omega).

The constants
\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}
can be taken as

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}=\frac{4^{2}}{\pi^{2}}(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}n^{3}\text{ and }\beta_{n}=\frac{1}{12n^{3}(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}}.

In Section
4
we compare Theorem
1.1
with preceeding known inequalities. To the best of the author’s knowledge Theorem
1.2
is the first result to give a lower bound of Dirichlet eigenvalues in terms of lower eigenvalues. Also without any condition, like
\lambda_{l}(\Omega)>\alpha_{n}\lambda_{1}(\Omega)
, Theorem
1.2
does not hold in general. Actually for each
k
consider
\Omega_{k}:=[0,k]\times[0,1]
. Then we have
\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{k})=\pi^{2}(1+\frac{1}{k^{2}})\sim\text{ const.}
and
\lambda_{k}(\Omega_{k})=2\pi^{2}
.

As a corollary of Theorem
1.1
we obtain the following upper bound for multiplicities of Dirichlet eigenvalues. For any natural number
k
the
m
ultiplicity of the
k
th eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(\Omega)
is denoted by
m_{k}(\Omega)
.

Corollary 1.3
.

For any bounded convex domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for any natural number
k
, if
\lambda_{k}(\Omega)>\alpha_{n}\lambda_{1}(\Omega)
then we have

\displaystyle m_{k}(\Omega)\leq 12^{\frac{n}{2}}n^{\frac{3}{2}n}(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{n}k.

Here
\alpha_{n}
is the same constant appeared in Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Preliminaries

The following lemma by Hatcher is useful to treat convex domains. He showed that bounded convex domains can be approximated by an orthotope in some sense by utilizing the John theorem which asserts that bounded convex domains can be approximated by ellipsoids (
[
9
, Theorem III]
).

Lemma 2.1

(Hatcher,
[
6
, Proposition 2.3]
)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Choosing the coordinate axes appropriately if necessary we can find a sequence
a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}
of positive real numbers such that

\displaystyle\Big[-\frac{a_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{a_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big]\times\cdots\times\Big[-\frac{a_{n}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{a_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big]\subseteq\Omega\subseteq[-a_{1}n,a_{1}n]\times\cdots[-a_{n}n,a_{n}n].

The above lemma together with domain monotonicity for Dirichlet eigenvalues suggest us that the proof of the main theorem reduces to the case where the domain is an orthotope. Domain monotonicity for Dirichlet eigenvalues asserts that larger domains have smaller eigenvalues (
[
11
, Theorem 3.2.1]
):

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}\subseteq\Omega_{2}\Rightarrow\lambda_{k}(\Omega_{2})\leq\lambda_{k}(\Omega_{1})\text{ for any }k.

For a bounded domain
\Omega
recall that the
inradius

\rho_{\Omega}
of
\Omega
is the radius of the largest ball contained inside
\Omega
. From domain monotonicity it is known the following upper bound for the
1
st Dirichlet eigenvalue in terms of the inradius.

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\leq\frac{(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}}{\operatorname{inrad}(\Omega)^{2}}.

When the domain is convex, Hersch
[
7
]
and Protter
[
16
]
proved the following reverse inequality:

Theorem 2.2

(Hersch-Protter,
[
5
, Proposition 2.55]
)
.

For a bounded convex domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4\operatorname{inrad}(\Omega)^{2}}.

We also use the following lower bound for Dirichlet eigenvalues in terms of volume due to Berezin
[
3
]
and Li-Yau
[
12
]
.

Theorem 2.3

(Berezin-Li-Yau,
[
11
, Corollary 3.3.22]
)
.

Let
\Omega
be a (not necessarily convex) bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then for any natural number
k
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\geq\frac{(2\pi)^{2}n}{n+2}\Big(\frac{k}{\omega_{n}\operatorname{vol}\Omega}\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}.

3.
Proof of the Main Theorems

In this section we prove the main theorems.
The following lemma is a key to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a constant
c_{n}>0
depending only on
n
which satisfies the following. For any orthotope
R
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any
k\geq l
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(R)\leq c_{n}\Big(\frac{k}{l}\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{l}(R).

The constant
c_{n}
can be chosen as
c_{n}=12(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Hatcher’s lemma (Lemma
2.1
), after choosing appropriate coordinate axes, we can find an orthotope
R
such that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}R\subseteq\Omega\subseteq nR
. Let the constant
c_{n}:=12(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}
. Applying Lemma
3.1
and using domain monotonicity for Dirichlet eigenvalues we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{k}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}R\Big)=n\lambda_{k}(R)\leq c_{n}n\Big(\frac{k}{l}\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{l}(R)=\

\displaystyle c_{n}n^{3}\Big(\frac{k}{l}\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{l}(nR)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle c_{n}n^{3}\Big(\frac{k}{l}\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{l}(\Omega).

This completes the proof of the theorem.

\Box

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Put
r:=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\big(\frac{l}{k}\big)^{\frac{1}{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{l}(R)}}
. Changing the coordinate axes if neccessary we may assume that
R=[-a_{1},a_{1}]\times[-a_{2},a_{2}]\times\cdots\times[-a_{n},a_{n}]
and
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}
.

Take a maximal
r
-separated points
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}
in
R
. The maximality gives
R\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}B(x_{i},r)
which shows

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}R\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}\operatorname{vol}B(x_{i},r)=k^{\prime}\omega_{n}r^{n}=\Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\Big)^{n}\cdot\frac{\omega_{n}k^{\prime}l}{k\lambda_{l}(R)^{\frac{n}{2}}}.

By virtue of the Berezin-Li-Yau inequality (Theorem
2.3
) we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}R\leq\Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\Big)^{n}\cdot\Big(\frac{2+n}{(2\pi)^{2}n}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}k^{\prime}}{k}\operatorname{vol}R,

which leads to

\displaystyle k\leq\Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\Big)^{n}\cdot\Big(\frac{2+n}{(2\pi)^{2}n}\Big)^{\frac{n}{2}}\omega_{n}^{2}k^{\prime}.

Since

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{(2\pi)^{2}n}{2+n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{n}^{-\frac{2}{n}}=2\Big(\frac{n}{2+n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma\Big(\frac{n}{2}+1\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}\geq 2\Big(\frac{n}{2+n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}},

we have
k\leq k^{\prime}
.

Claim 3.2
.

a_{1}\geq r/2

Let us admit the claim for a while. Put
A_{i}=B(x_{i},\frac{r}{2})\cap R
for
i=1,\cdots,k^{\prime}
. Then since
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}
is
r
-sparated these balls are disjoint each other. Thanks to the claim the inradius of
A_{i}
is at least
\frac{r}{4}
(Here we use that our domain
R
is an orthotope). As a consequence we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(R)\leq\lambda_{k^{\prime}}(R)\leq\max_{i}\lambda_{1}(A_{i})\leq\frac{(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}}{(\frac{r}{4})^{2}}=12(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}\Big(\frac{k}{l}\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{l}(R),

which concludes the lemma.

We now prove the claim. Note that
a_{1}=\operatorname{inrad}R
. Thus the Hersch-Protter inequality (Theorem
2.2
) gives

\displaystyle a_{1}\geq\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(R)}}\geq\frac{\pi l^{\frac{1}{n}}}{2k^{\frac{1}{n}}\sqrt{\lambda_{l}(\Omega)}}>\frac{r}{2},

which implies the claim. This completes the proof of the lemma.

\Box

Lemma 3.3
.

There exist two constants
c_{n}
and
d_{n}
depending only on
n
which satisfy the following. Let
R
be an orthotope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
k\geq l
. If
\lambda_{l}(R)>c_{n}\lambda_{1}(R)
then we have

\displaystyle d_{n}\Big(\frac{k}{l}\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{l}(R)\leq\lambda_{k}(R).

The constants
c_{n},d_{n}
can be taken as

(3.1)

\displaystyle c_{n}=\frac{4^{2}}{\pi^{2}}(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}\text{ and }d_{n}=\frac{1}{12(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}}.

As the proof of Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.2
follows from Lemma
3.3
together with
domain monotonicity and Hatcher’s lemma (Lemma
2.1
), so we omit the proof.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Suppose that
\lambda_{l}(R)>c_{n}\lambda_{1}(R)
and put
r:=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\big(\frac{k}{l}\big)^{\frac{1}{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}(R)}}
, where we take
c_{n}
as in (
3.1
). We may assume that
R=[-a_{1},a_{1}]\times[-a_{2},a_{2}]\times\cdots\times[-a_{n},a_{n}]
and
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}
.

Take a maximal
r
-separated points
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{l^{\prime}}
in
R
. Then as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
we have
l\leq l^{\prime}
.

Suppose that
a_{1}\leq\frac{r}{2}
. Putting
A_{i}:=B(x_{i},a_{1})\cap R
we see that
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{l^{\prime}}
is a family of disjoint subsets in
R
. Note that
\operatorname{inrad}A_{i}\geq\frac{a_{1}}{2}
. We thereby get

\displaystyle\lambda_{l}(R)\leq\lambda_{l^{\prime}}(R)\leq\max_{i}\lambda_{1}(A_{i})\leq\frac{(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}}{(\frac{a_{1}}{2})^{2}}.

Using the Hersch-Protter inequality (Theorem
2.2
) we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{l}(R)\leq\frac{4^{2}}{\pi^{2}}(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}\lambda_{1}(R)=c_{n}\lambda_{1}(R),

which contradicts to our assumption
\lambda_{l}(R)>c_{n}\lambda_{1}(R)
.

Therefore we obtain
a_{1}>\frac{r}{2}
. Putting
B_{i}=B(x_{i},\frac{r}{2})\cap R
we thus find that
\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}
is a family of disjoint subsets in
R
such that
\operatorname{inrad}B_{i}\geq\frac{r}{4}
. As a result we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{l}(R)\leq\lambda_{l^{\prime}}(R)\leq\max_{i}\lambda_{1}(B_{i})\leq\frac{(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}}{(\frac{r}{4})^{2}}=12(j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1})^{2}\Big(\frac{l}{k}\Big)^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{k}(R).

This completes the proof.

\Box

4.
Previous results

In this section we overview preceeding results related to main theorems.

For any bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, Payne-Pólya-Weinberger
[
14
]
(see also
[
11
, Theorem 5.4.1]
) proved the following universal inequality:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}(\Omega)-\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\leq\frac{1}{nk}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\text{ for any }k,

which leads to

(4.2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}(\Omega)\leq\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\text{ for any }k.

Later Hille-Protter
[
8
]
improved this inequality and then Yang
[
19
]
proved an even stronger inequality. From Yang’s inequality one can derive that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}(\Omega)\leq\Big(1+\frac{4}{n}\Big)\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(\Omega)\text{ for any }k,

which is known as Yang’s second inequality (
[
11
, 5.4.4]
). In particular this inequality derives that

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}(\Omega)\leq\Big(1+\frac{4}{n}\Big)\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\text{ for any }k.

Note that this inequality is weaker than (
4.1
) although (
4.3
) is not the consequence of (
4.1
).

For any bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
Levitin-Parnovski
[
10
, (4.14)]
proved that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i+k}(\Omega)\leq(4+n)\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\text{ for any }k.

This yields that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i+k}(\Omega)\leq\frac{4+n}{n-i+1}\lambda_{k}(\Omega)\text{ for any }k\text{ and for any }i\leq n.

Ashbaugh-Benguria
[
2
]
resolved the conjecture by Payne-Pólya-Weinberger concerning the ratio of the first and the second eigenvalue:

(4.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{2}(\Omega)}{\lambda_{1}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{\lambda_{2}(B(0,1))}{\lambda_{1}(B(0,1))}=\frac{j_{\frac{n}{2},1}^{2}}{j_{\frac{n}{2}-1,1}^{2}}.

They also showed that the equality in (
4.5
) occurs if and only if
\Omega
is a disk.

Cheng-Yang (
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
) proved that for any bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for any
n\geq 41
and
k\geq 41
,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}(\Omega)\leq k^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{1}(\Omega).

They also proved that for any general
n
and
k
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}(\Omega)\leq C_{0}(n,k)k^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{1}(\Omega),

where

\displaystyle C_{0}(n,k)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{j_{n/2,1}^{2}}{j_{n/2-1,1}^{2}},&\text{for }k=1\\
1+\dfrac{a(\min\{n,k-1\})}{n},&\text{for }k\geq 2\end{cases}

and
a(1)\leq 2.64
,
a(2)\leq 2.27
and
a(p)\leq 2.2-4\log(1+\frac{p-3}{50})
for
p\geq 3
is a
constant depending only on
p
. For
k\geq 2
this yields

(4.7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}(\Omega)\leq\

\displaystyle\Big(1+\max\Big\{\frac{2.64}{n},2.24\log\Big(1+\frac{n-3}{50}\Big)\Big\}\Big)k^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{1}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\Big(1+\max\Big\{\frac{2.64}{n},\frac{2.24}{50}\cdot\frac{n-3}{n}\Big\}\Big)k^{\frac{2}{n}}\lambda_{1}(\Omega).{}

Note that none of the above results make any assumptions about the domain wheares in our theorems (Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
) we assume that our domain is convex.
Since

\displaystyle\sqrt{(\nu+1)(\nu+5)}<j_{\nu,1}<\sqrt{2(\nu+1)(\nu+3)}

(
[
13
, (1)]
and
[
17
, p.486 (5)]
), we see that Theorem
1.1
is weaker than (
4.2
–
4.7
) in each case. The advantage of Theorem
1.1
is that it unifies these inequalities up to a constant factor under the convexity assumption. At present, the author does not know whether the assumption of convexity is necessary for Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.
