A bibasic double sum extension of a q q -binomial theorem arising out of subspace enumeration
Gaurav Bhatnagar, Amritanshu Prasad

Abstract
. We prove a conjecture that arose in the context of a subspace enumeration problem over finite fields. We prove, more generally, a bibasic, double-sum identity, which extends a q q -analogue of the (terminating) binomial theorem.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 05A19; Secondary 33D15, 33D65

1.
Introduction

We prove a conjecture that arose in a combinatorial context, while considering a subspace enumeration problem over finite fields. Our proof uses a
q
-analogue of the terminating binomial theorem, and leads to a double-sum identity which extends this
q
-binomial theorem. This conjecture belongs to a broader set of (conjectural) combinatorial identities.

We require the notation of
q
-rising factorials from Gasper and Rahman
[
4
]
.
The
q
-shifted factorials
, for
k
a non-negative integer, are defined as

\displaystyle{\left({a};q\right)_{k}}:=\prod\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(1-aq^{j}\big);

and for
|q|<1
,

\displaystyle{\left({a};q\right)_{\infty}}:=\prod\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\big(1-aq^{j}\big).

The parameter
q
is called the
base
. Our main result is bibasic—it has two independent bases
p
and
q
.

We use the notation
\boldsymbol{j}=(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{r})
and
{\left|\boldsymbol{j}\right|}
for
j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}+\cdots+j_{r}
.
The
q
-multinomial coefficients are defined as:

\begin{bmatrix}n\\
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},\dots,j_{r}\end{bmatrix}_{q}=\frac{(q;q)_{n}}{(q;q)_{j_{1}}(q;q)_{j_{2}}\cdots(q;q)_{j_{r}}{\left({q};q\right)_{n-{\left|\boldsymbol{j}\right|}}}}.

These are polynomials in
q
. As is common, we use the convention:

(1.1)

\frac{1}{{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}=0\text{ if $n<0$},

so the
q
-multinomial coefficient is
0
if
j_{r}<0
for any
r
, or, if
n<{\left|\boldsymbol{j}\right|}
.
When
r=1
, this is called the
q
-binomial coefficient.

The main objective of this paper is to prove the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1

(Prasad and Ram)
.

Let
q
be a prime power and
\omega
denote a primitive cube root of unity; let
\boldsymbol{j}=(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})
. Then:

(1.2)

\sum_{{\left|\boldsymbol{j}\right|}=3m}q^{\frac{1}{3}\big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}\binom{j_{i}}{2}-g(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})\big)}\begin{bmatrix}3m\\
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\end{bmatrix}_{q}\omega^{\,j_{2}+2j_{3}}=\delta_{m,0}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $m=0$};\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\\[6.0pt]
\text{where}\quad g(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})=\begin{cases}2j_{2}+j_{3},&\text{if }j_{2}+2j_{3}\equiv 2\pmod{3};\\
j_{2}+2j_{3},&\text{if }j_{2}+2j_{3}\equiv 0,1\pmod{3}.\end{cases}

Note that the restriction of
q
being a prime power can be removed, since the left hand side is a polynomial in
q
. It is easy to see that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}\binom{j_{i}}{2}-g(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})

is a non-negative integer divisible by
3
, so the power of
q
is a non-negative integer.
So this is a polynomial identity and
q
can be an arbitrary complex number. Alternatively, all the identities in this paper may be regarded as identities for formal power series with variable
q
.

Prasad and Ram arrived at this (unpublished) conjecture while studying an enumeration problem over finite fields posed by Bender, Coley, Robbins, and Rumsey
[
3
]
.
As a follow-up to their work
[
5
,
6
]
, they were seeking the coefficients of a
universal formula
of the form

\sum_{{\left|\boldsymbol{j}\right|}=3m}c^{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(q)Y^{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}=\sigma^{T}_{(m,m,m)},

for any matrix
T\in M_{3m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Here
\sigma^{T}_{(m,m,m)}
is the number of
m
-dimensional subspaces
W\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3m}
such that
W\oplus TW\oplus T^{2}W=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3m}
;
Y^{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}
is the number of flags
W_{1}\subset W_{2}\subset W_{3}
of subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3m}
such that
T(W_{i})\subset W_{i}
, with
\dim(W_{i}/W_{i-1})=j_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
; and
c^{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(q)
is a polynomial in
q
independent of the matrix
T
. The form of the universal formula was predicted by
[
3
]
. We give more details in §
2
.

We prove the following generalization of (
1.2
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
q
be a complex number,
\omega
denote a primitive cube root of unity; and let
\boldsymbol{j}=(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})
.
Then

(1.3)

\sum_{{\left|\boldsymbol{j}\right|}=3m}q^{\frac{1}{3}\big(j_{2}^{2}+j_{2}j_{3}+j_{3}^{2}-g(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})\big)}z^{j_{2}+j_{3}}\begin{bmatrix}3m\\
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\end{bmatrix}_{q}\omega^{\,j_{2}+2j_{3}}={\left({z};q\right)_{3m}},\\
\text{where,}\quad g(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})=\begin{cases}2j_{2}+j_{3},&\text{if }j_{2}+2j_{3}\equiv 2\pmod{3};\\
j_{2}+2j_{3},&\text{if }j_{2}+2j_{3}\equiv 0,1\pmod{3}.\end{cases}

Conjecture
1
follows. When
m=0
, both sides of (
1.3
) are
1
. This matches (
1.2
) for
m=0
. For
m>0
, take
z=q^{-m}
in (
1.3
). The right-hand side reduces to
0
; this shows (
1.2
) for
m>0
.

Alternatively, we can write (
1.3
) as:

(1.4)

\sum_{n=0}^{3m}q^{n^{2}}z^{n}\sum_{k=n}^{2n}q^{\frac{1}{3}({k^{2}}-g(k,n))-kn}\begin{bmatrix}3m\\
2n-k,k-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}\omega^{k}={\left({z};q\right)_{3m}},

where,

g(k,n)=\begin{cases}3n-k,&\text{if }k\equiv 2\pmod{3};\\
k,&\text{if }k\equiv 0,1\pmod{3}.\end{cases}

The proof yields an even more general, bibasic, result:

Theorem 3
.

Let
p
and
q
be complex numbers. Then

(1.5)

\sum_{n=0}^{3m}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}p^{\binom{n}{2}}z^{n}\begin{bmatrix}3m\\
n\end{bmatrix}_{p}\sum_{k=n}^{2n}q^{\frac{1}{3}({k^{2}}-g(k,n))-kn}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}\omega^{k}={\left({z};p\right)_{3m}},

where,

g(k,n)=\begin{cases}3n-k,&\text{if }k\equiv 2\pmod{3};\\
k,&\text{if }k\equiv 0,1\pmod{3}.\end{cases}

Conjecture
1.2
can be regarded as a
2
-dimensional sum extending a
q
-analogue of the binomial theorem.

An analogous
1
-dimensional case of the conjecture is the identity

(1.6)

\sum_{j=0}^{2m}(-1)^{j}q^{\binom{j}{2}-mj}\begin{bmatrix}2m\\
j\end{bmatrix}_{q}=\delta_{m,0},

which is obtained by taking
n=2m
and
z=q^{-m}
in the following
q
-analog of the (terminating) binomial theorem
[
4
, Ex. 1.2(vi)]
:

(1.7)

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
j\end{bmatrix}_{q}(-1)^{j}\,q^{\binom{j}{2}}z^{j}={\left({z};q\right)_{n}}.

Interestingly, the proof of the
2
-dimensional sum also relies upon the very same identity.

Taking
m\to\infty
in Theorem
3
, we obtain the following bibasic, double-summation identity.

Corollary 4
.

Let
|p|<1
and
g(k,n)
be as in (
1.5
). Then

(1.8)

\sum_{n,k\geq 0}p^{\binom{n}{2}}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}({k^{2}}-g(k,n))-kn}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}z^{n}\omega^{k}}{{\left({p};p\right)_{n}}{\left({q};q\right)_{k-n}}{\left({q};q\right)_{2n-k}}}={\left({z};p\right)_{\infty}}.

Note that in the sum on the left-hand side of (
1.8
), the index
k
ranges from
n
to
2n
.

When
p=q
in (
1.8
), we obtain

(1.9)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}z^{n}\sum_{k=n}^{2n}q^{\frac{1}{3}({k^{2}}-g(k,n))-kn}\frac{\omega^{k}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{k-n}}{\left({q};q\right)_{2n-k}}}={\left({z};q\right)_{\infty}}.

The outer sum (if we ignore the inner sum with index
k
) is what
Andrews
[
2
]
called a partial theta function.

Conjecture
1
is a special case of a broader combinatorial conjecture. In §
2
, we explain the origin of (
1.2
), as well as the broader conjecture,
and the link to a classic combinatorial formula—namely the Touchard–Riordan
[
8
,
9
]
formula. We give a proof of Theorems
2
and
3
in §
3
. Our proof is just the first step in understanding these combinatorial identities.

2.
Counting subspaces and Conjecture
1

In this section we explain the origins of Conjecture 1. This conjecture is a special case of a more general conjecture (Conjecture 5, below),
which arose from the work of Prasad and Ram
[
5
,
6
]
on an enumerative problem posed by Bender, Coley, Robbins and Rumsey
[
3
]
. A proof of Conjecture 5 would lead to an extension of the classical Touchard–Riordan formula.

Let
q
be a prime power, and let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denote a finite field with
q
elements.
Consider a matrix
T\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and a subspace
W
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
Let
W^{(0)}=\{0\}
, and for each
k\geq 1
, let

W^{(k)}=W+T(W)+\cdots+T^{k}(W).

Let
j_{i}=\dim W^{(i)}/W^{(i-1)}
for
i\geq 1
.
The sequence
(j_{1},j_{2},\dots)
is called the
dimension sequence
of
W
with respect to
T
.
It is not difficult to see that if
(j_{1},j_{2},\dots)
is the dimension sequence of a subspace
W\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with respect to a matrix
T\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, then
n\geq j_{1}\geq j_{2}\geq\cdots
and
j_{1}+j_{2}+\cdots\leq n
.
In other words, the dimension sequence of a subspace is a partition of some integer less than or equal to
n
.

Bender, Coley, Robbins and Rumsey
[
3
]
considered the following enumerative problem:

Problem
.

Let
T\in M_{n}(\mathbf{F}_{q})
be any matrix over the finite field
\mathbf{F}_{q}
with
q
elements.
Enumerate the number
\sigma^{T}_{(j_{1},j_{2},\dots)}
of subspaces
W
with dimension sequence
(j_{1},j_{2},\dots)
.

The solution to this problem depends on the matrix
T
.
If
(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{k})
is a sequence of non-negative integers such that
j_{1}+j_{2}+\cdots+j_{k}\leq n
, let

Y^{T}_{(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{k})}=\#\{W_{1}\subset W_{2}\subset\dotsb\subset W_{k}\mid T(W_{i})\subset W_{i},\;\dim(W_{i}/W_{i-1})=j_{i}\}.

The number
Y^{T}_{(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{k})}
remains unchanged under reordering of the sequence
(j_{1}
,
j_{2}
,
\dots
,
j_{k})
, so we may assume that

j_{1}\geq j_{2}\geq\dots\geq j_{k}

is a partition of some integer less than or equal to
n
.
Bender, Coley, Robbins and Rumsey
[
3
]
showed that there exists a
universal formula

(2.1)

\sigma^{T}_{(j_{1},j_{2},\dots)}=\sum_{(k_{1},k_{2},\dots)}c^{(k_{1},k_{2},\dots)}_{(j_{1},j_{2},\dots)}(q)Y^{T}_{(k_{1},k_{2},\dots)},

for all partitions
(j_{1},j_{2},\dots)
, where the coefficients
c^{(k_{1},k_{2},\dots)}_{(j_{1},j_{2},\dots)}
are polynomials in
q
independent of the matrix
T
.

The general problem of determining the coefficients
c^{(k_{1},k_{2},\dots)}_{(j_{1},j_{2},\dots)}
was solved by Ram
[
7
]
, where the solution is given in terms of symmetric functions. However, the work in this paper concerns more explicit formulas solving special cases of this problem.

For example, when
n=2m
,
\sigma^{T}_{(m,m)}
counts the number of subspaces
W\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2m}
of dimension
m
such that
W\oplus TW=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2m}
.
Prasad and Ram
[
5
]
obtained

(2.2)

\sigma^{T}_{(m,m)}=q^{\binom{m}{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{2m}(-1)^{j}q^{\binom{m-j+1}{2}}Y^{T}_{(j)},

giving a closed formula for the coefficients in (
2.1
) in the case of the partition
(m,m)
.
When
T=0
(or any scalar matrix),

Y^{T}_{(j)}=\begin{bmatrix}2m\\
j\end{bmatrix}_{q},

while
\sigma^{T}_{(m,m)}=\delta_{m,0}
(since
W=TW
for any subspace
W
). As a result, we obtain (
1.6
).

In seeking a formula analogous to (
2.2
) for
\sigma^{T}_{(m,m,m)}
, Prasad and Ram arrived at the following (unpublished) conjecture.

Conjecture 5

(Prasad and Ram)
.

For any
T\in M_{3m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
:

(2.3)

\sigma^{T}_{(m,m,m)}=q^{m^{2}-m}\sum_{{\left|\boldsymbol{j}\right|}=3m}q^{\frac{1}{3}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\binom{j_{i}}{2}-g(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})\right)}Y^{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\omega^{j_{2}+2j_{3}},

with
g(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})
as in (
1.2
).

Again, when
T
is a scalar matrix,

Y^{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}=\begin{bmatrix}3m\\
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\end{bmatrix}_{q},

and
\sigma^{T}_{(m,m,m)}=\delta_{m,0}
. This yields Conjecture
1
, the proof of which is the object of this paper, and given in §
3
.

By varying
T
, a plethora of new identities can be obtained from Conjecture
5
.
We illustrate one such possibility by giving an example of a combinatorial nature.

When
T\in M_{2m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
has distinct eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, Prasad and Ram
[
6
]
obtained a different formula for the left-hand side
\sigma^{T}_{(m,m)}
of
(
2.2
), in terms of chord diagrams:

(2.4)

\sigma^{T}_{(m,m)}=(q-1)^{m}\sum_{\sigma}q^{v(\sigma)}Y^{T}_{(2m)},

the sum being over all chord diagrams
\sigma
on
2m
nodes, and
v(\sigma)
being the number of crossings in
\sigma
.
Figure
1
is an example of a chord diagram on eight nodes with two crossings.

Combining (
2.2
) and (
2.4
) gives a new proof of the Touchard–Riordan formula
[
8
,
9
]
(see also
[
1
, pp. 337–344]
for an elegant exposition):

(2.5)

(q-1)^{m}\sum_{\sigma}q^{v(\sigma)}=\sum_{j=0}^{2m}(-1)^{j}q^{\binom{m-j+1}{2}}\binom{2m}{j}.

Similarly, when
T\in M_{3m}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
has distinct eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
\sigma^{T}_{(m,m,m)}
can be expressed in terms of set partitions of
[3m]
into
m
blocks of size
3
. For such a set partition, an interlacing of blocks
(a,b,c)
and
(x,y,z)
is either
a<x<b<y
or
b<y<c<z
.
The results of
[
6
]
show that

(2.6)

\sigma^{T}_{(m,m,m)}=(q-1)^{2m}\sum_{\pi}q^{\text{interlacings}(\pi)}Y^{T}_{(3m)},

the sum being over all set partitions
\pi
of
[3m]
into
m
blocks of size
3
, and
\text{interlacings}(\pi)
being the number of interlacings in
\pi
.
In this case,

Y^{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}=\binom{3m}{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}

is the ordinary multinomial coefficient, and so (
2.3
) implies the following extension of the Touchard–Riordan formula:

(2.7)

(q-1)^{2m}\sum_{\pi}q^{\text{interlacings}(\pi)}=q^{m^{2}-m}\sum_{{\left|\boldsymbol{j}\right|}=3m}q^{\frac{1}{3}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\binom{j_{i}}{2}-g(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})\right)}\binom{3m}{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}\omega^{j_{2}+2j_{3}},

with
g(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})
as in (
1.2
).
This is currently unproven.

3.
Proof of Theorems
2
and
3

In this section, we prove Theorem
2
, and then extend the proof to obtain Theorem
3
.

To begin, we first take
j_{1}=3m-j_{2}-j_{3}
in the sum on the left-hand side of (
1.3
). The sum becomes over
j_{2}+j_{3}\leq 3m
. The sum may then be written in the form

\sum_{j_{2}+j_{3}\leq 3m}(***)=\sum_{n=0}^{3m}\sum_{j_{2}+j_{3}=n}(***).

Next, we change indices in the inner sum to
k
. We see that:

\left\{\vbox{\halign{\hfil$#$&\hbox to7.7778pt{\hss$#$\hss}&\hfil$#$&\hfil$#$\hfil&$#$\hfil&\hbox to7.7778pt{\hss$#$\hss}&$#$\hfil\cr\vrule depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt,height=9.0ptj_{2}&pt{\hss$+&2j_{3}&{}={}&k&pt{\hss$&\cr j_{2}&pt{\hss$+&j_{3}&{}={}&&pt{\hss$&n\vrule height=0.0pt,width=0.0pt,depth=4.0pt\cr}}\right.\quad\iff\quad\left\{\vbox{\halign{\hfil$#$&\hbox to7.7778pt{\hss$#$\hss}&\hfil$#$&\hfil$#$\hfil&$#$\hfil&\hbox to7.7778pt{\hss$#$\hss}&$#$\hfil\cr\vrule depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt,height=9.0pt&pt{\hss$&j_{3}&{}={}&k&pt{\hss$-&n\cr j_{2}&pt{\hss$&&{}={}&-k&pt{\hss$+&2n\vrule height=0.0pt,width=0.0pt,depth=4.0pt\cr}}\right..}}}}}}}}

Note that
2j_{2}+j_{3}=3n-k
. Further
0\leq j_{3}\leq n
, so
n\leq k\leq 2n
.
We obtain:

(3.1)

\sum_{n=0}^{3m}\sum_{k=n}^{2n}q^{n^{2}}z^{n}q^{\frac{1}{3}({k^{2}}-g(k,n))-kn}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m-n}}{\left({q};q\right)_{2n-k}}{\left({q};q\right)_{k-n}}}\omega^{k},

where, now,

g(k,n)=\begin{cases}3n-k,&\text{if }k\equiv 2\pmod{3};\\
k,&\text{if }k\equiv 0,1\pmod{3}.\end{cases}

Note that the sums terminate naturally, due to (
1.1
).
So
n\leq 3m,k\leq 2n
, and
k\geq n
is implied by the terms; thus we may as well write the sum with an unrestricted index. We now break the sums into three parts, where
k\mapsto 3k,3k+1,\text{ and }3k-1
. We obtain three sums in the form:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A(n,k)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A(n,3k)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A(n,3k+1)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A(n,3k-1).

Eventually, we get that the left-hand side equals the following:

(3.2)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}z^{n}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m-n}}\,{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{3k^{2}-k-3kn}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{2n-3k}}\,{\left({q};q\right)_{3k-n}}}\\
+\omega\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}z^{n}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m-n}}\,{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{3k^{2}+k-3kn-n}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{2n-3k-1}}\,{\left({q};q\right)_{3k-n+1}}}\\
+\omega^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}z^{n}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m-n}}\,{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{3k^{2}-k-3kn}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{2n-3k+1}}\,{\left({q};q\right)_{3k-n-1}}}.

Next we consider the three inner sums on
k
.
Denote them by
S_{1}(n)
,
S_{2}(n)
and
S_{3}(n)
, respectively.
They can be written as:

(3.3a)

\displaystyle S_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{3k^{2}-k-3kn}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
3k-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}

(3.3b)

\displaystyle S_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=q^{-n}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{3k^{2}+k-3kn}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
3k+1-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}

(3.3c)

\displaystyle S_{3}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{3k^{2}-k-3kn}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
3k-1-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}.

Note that these sums run over
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. There are natural limits in all these sums, and they are actually finite sums. We will show the following:

Proposition 6
.

Let
S_{1}(n)
,
S_{2}(n)
and
S_{3}(n)
be as above. Then:

(i.)

S_{2}(n)=S_{3}(n)
.

(ii.)

S_{1}(n)-S_{3}(n)=(-1)^{n}q^{-\binom{n+1}{2}}.

Using Proposition
6
, we see that (
3.2
) reduces to:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}z^{n}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m-n}}\,{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}(S_{1}(n)+\omega S_{2}(n)+\omega^{2}S_{3}(n)\\
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}z^{n}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m-n}}\,{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}(-1)^{n}q^{-\binom{n+1}{2}}\\
+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}z^{n}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m-n}}\,{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}S_{3}(n)(1+\omega+\omega^{2}).

Since
1+\omega+\omega^{2}=0,
the second sum is
0
. In fact, the first sum can be factored. We have:

(3.4)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}z^{n}\frac{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m}}}{{\left({q};q\right)_{3m-n}}\,{\left({q};q\right)_{n}}}(-1)^{n}q^{-\binom{n+1}{2}}={\left({z};q\right)_{3m}}.

This is the
m\mapsto 3m
case of (
1.7
).

To complete the proof of (
1.3
), we still need to prove Proposition
6
.

Proof of Proposition
6
.

We first prove part (i). Let

F(n,k):=q^{3k^{2}+k-3nk-n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
3k+1-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}.

Note that
F(n,k)
is the summand of
S_{2}(n)
.
Consider
F(n,n-k)
:

F(n,n-k)=q^{3(n-k)^{2}+(n-k)-3n(n-k)-n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
3(n-k)+1-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}\\
=q^{3k^{2}-k-3nk}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
2n-3k+1\end{bmatrix}_{q}=q^{3k^{2}-k-3nk}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
3k-1-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}.

This is the summand of
S_{3}(n)
. Thus each term of
S_{2}(n)
corresponds to a term in
S_{3}(n)
and the two sums are the same; the symmetry shows that
S_{3}(n)
is obtained by reversing the sum
S_{2}(n)
. This proves (i).

For the proof of part (ii), consider

g(n):=S_{1}(n)-S_{3}(n).

We observe that
g(0)=1
and show that

g(n)=-q^{-n}g(n-1).

We require the identities
[
4
, eq. (I.45)]
:

\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
r\end{matrix}\right]_{q}=q^{r}\left[\begin{matrix}n-1\\
r\end{matrix}\right]_{q}+\left[\begin{matrix}n-1\\
r-1\end{matrix}\right]_{q}=\left[\begin{matrix}n-1\\
r\end{matrix}\right]_{q}+q^{\,n-r}\left[\begin{matrix}n-1\\
r-1\end{matrix}\right]_{q}.

We have:

\displaystyle g(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{3k^{2}-k-3nk}\bigg(\begin{bmatrix}n\\
3k-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}-\begin{bmatrix}n\\
3k-1-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{3k^{2}-k-3nk}\Bigg(\bigg(q^{3k-n}\begin{bmatrix}n-1\\
3k-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}+\begin{bmatrix}n-1\\
3k-n-1\end{bmatrix}_{q}\bigg)-

\displaystyle\hskip 108.405pt\bigg(\begin{bmatrix}n-1\\
3k-1-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}+q^{2n-3k+1}\begin{bmatrix}n-1\\
3k-2-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}\bigg)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{3k^{2}-k-3nk}\bigg(q^{3k-n}\begin{bmatrix}n-1\\
3k-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}-q^{2n-3k+1}\begin{bmatrix}n-1\\
3k-2-n\end{bmatrix}_{q}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{3k^{2}-k-3nk}\bigg(q^{3k-n}\begin{bmatrix}n-1\\
3k-1-(n-1)\end{bmatrix}_{q}-q^{2n-3k+1}\begin{bmatrix}n-1\\
3k-3-(n-1)\end{bmatrix}_{q}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{3k^{2}-k-3nk+3k-n}\begin{bmatrix}n-1\\
3k-1-(n-1)\end{bmatrix}_{q}

\displaystyle\hskip 108.405pt-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{3(k+1)^{2}-(k+1)-3n(k+1)+2n-3(k+1)+1}\begin{bmatrix}n-1\\
3k-(n-1)\end{bmatrix}_{q}

\displaystyle=-q^{-n}g(n-1).

In the last but one equality, we shifted the index of the second sum.

From
g(n)=-q^{-n}g(n-1)
, we obtain, by iteration:

g(n)=(-1)^{n}q^{-\binom{n+1}{2}}g(0)=(-1)^{n}q^{-\binom{n+1}{2}}.

∎
