Perceived social support and its impact on alcohol treatment outcomes: a qualitative study among women with HIV enrolled in a clinical alcohol pharmacotherapy trial
Authors: Nanyangwe Siuluta, Abigail Gracy, Sashaun Ranger, Christina E Parisi, Rebecca J Fisk-Hoffman, Isaac Payton, Robert L Cook, Shantrel S Canidate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMotivation to engage in treatment and maintain adherence is essential for people with unhealthy alcohol use. Social support may increase motivation to engage in treatment. Still, there is limited knowledge regarding social support providers and types of social support that facilitate or hinder alcohol treatment participation among women with HIV (WWH). This study explores social support among WWH in Florida: types, sources, and perceived impact on drinking outcomes.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 20 WWH (mean age 49, 85% Black) with a history of unhealthy drinking enrolled in an alcohol pharmacotherapy trial. Participants discussed their sources, types, and the importance of social support in trial participation. Transcripts were thematically analyzed using NVivo 12.0.ResultsThe most mentioned source of social support was social networks, including family and friends. Abstinent family members provided more support by discouraging drinking and motivating recovery. The most frequently mentioned types of social support for alcohol treatment were emotional, including non-judgmental listening, trustworthiness, and care, as well as informational, such as teaching WWH about new topics and engaging them in conversations. Women also emphasized the importance of encouragement and open discussions in alcohol treatment.ConclusionWWH highlighted that social support from their social networks benefited their alcohol treatment through engagement and positive social interactions. Encouraging support is essential. Thus, social support reinforces adherence to both alcohol use and HIV treatment, thereby improving outcomes across the HIV care continuum in underserved communities.Keywords: Social support, Women with HIV, Substance use, Clinical trial, Florida

Background

Women represent more than one quarter of adults diagnosed with HIV in the United States [1]. Unhealthy alcohol use (i.e., consuming more than seven standard drinks per week or exceeding three drinks per occasion) is prevalent among women with HIV (WWH), with 6% to 25% reporting current unhealthy drinking behaviors [2–4]. Longitudinal studies WWH demonstrate that unhealthy or escalating alcohol use undermines medication adherence, heightens the risk of viral non-suppression, accelerates disease progression, raises hospitalization rates, and ultimately worsens HIV-related clinical outcomes [5–9]. Therefore, reducing unhealthy alcohol use is essential for improving HIV outcomes and overall health. Further, motivation to engage in treatment and adherence to treatment are necessary for reducing unhealthy alcohol use among WWH.

Receiving social support during alcohol treatment is associated with motivation to participate in treatment and aids in abstaining from, as well as in recovering from, substance use disorders [10–12]. Perceived social support is the cognitive evaluation of feeling securely connected to others [13]. Social support involves verbal and nonverbal interactions that reduce uncertainty and enhance individuals’ perceptions of personal control in their experiences [14]. Studies and clinical findings indicate that women with substance use disorders often have limited social networks, more so among WWH [15]. However, there is limited knowledge regarding types and sources of social support that influence alcohol treatment participation [16].

The biopsychosocial framework posits that health and illness emerge from the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors across multiple levels of organization [17]. Guided by a biopsychosocial framework, social support among women with HIV may function as a contextual mechanism influencing alcohol treatment response by enhancing self-efficacy, reducing stress and stigma, and reinforcing adherence within pharmacotherapy-based clinical trials [18]. Even in the absence of formal psychosocial components, social support may operate alongside pharmacotherapy through existing social networks and supportive clinical interactions that promote engagement and sustained participation [18].

Commonly identified types of social support include emotional, informational, instrumental, and appraisal support. Moreover, social support can be either tangible or intangible, and its benefits include maintaining healthy behaviors, effective coping mechanisms, perceived control, and a sense of stability [19, 20].

Beyond substance use treatment, social support is a well-established determinant of successful chronic disease management, including HIV care [21]. Among women with HIV, social support has been consistently linked to improved antiretroviral therapy adherence, sustained engagement in HIV care, viral suppression, and overall quality of life [22]. For WWH who engage in unhealthy alcohol use, social support may therefore serve a dual function, facilitating alcohol reduction while simultaneously supporting adherence to HIV treatment and long-term disease management.

Studies report that individuals in treatment for substance use receive social support from various sources, including their social network support (i.e., spouse, family, and friends), spiritual leaders and church community, health professionals, and sponsors (e.g., Alcoholics Anonymous) [16]. However, women typically receive less social support from spouses and family for recovery compared to men [23].

Despite efforts by the National Institute of Health to increase the participation of women and other minoritized groups in clinical studies, research on alcohol treatment among WWH remains limited [24, 25]. This underrepresentation in alcohol treatment studies highlights the need to address minority groups’ unique challenges in alcohol treatment research. In their lifetime, 22% of men and 15% of women with substance use disorders who are younger than age 45 received treatment [4, 26, 27]. WWH specifically may be less likely to enter treatment due to limited awareness of treatment benefits or negative beliefs about treatment [28].

Moreover, there is limited knowledge regarding the clinical trial experiences of WWH who engage in alcohol-related research. Understanding treatment effectiveness includes gaining insights into women’s perspectives on support [29, 30]. Hence, this study aimed to explore the types and sources of social support received, as well as the perceived impact of social support on drinking outcomes, among a sample of WWH in the WHAT-IF? study. alcohol-treatment pharmacotherapy trial in Florida.

Methods

Aim, design, and setting of the study

This qualitative study was part of a double-blinded randomized clinical trial, the WHAT-IF? (Will Having Alcohol Treatment Improve My Functioning? ) study, designed to assess the efficacy of pharmacotherapy medication in reducing unhealthy alcohol use among WWH living in Miami, Florida. Further details regarding the study have been published elsewhere [31]. Briefly, women in the trial were eligible for the parent study if they were 18 years or older, provided documentation for HIV infection, and met criteria for unhealthy alcohol use (defined as consuming > 7 drinks per week or > 3 drinks in one sitting) for women [18]. Eligible WWH were randomized to receive treatment with naltrexone, 50 mg daily, or placebo for four months. WWH also completed several assessments during the study period at 2, 4, and 7 months. All participants met criteria for unhealthy alcohol use at baseline, and month 7 drinking status was intentionally reported to reflect their status at the time of data collection. The WHAT-IF? Study enrolled participants from 2013 to 2016. As a qualitative sub-study, this study did not independently measure alcohol use or medication adherence outcomes; month 7 drinking status from the parent trial is reported as a descriptive characteristic of the sample only.

Characteristics of participants and procedures

Between July 2015 and March 2016, 20 WWH who had varying degrees of success with alcohol cessation during the clinical trial were invited to complete semi-structured, in-depth interviews.

Interviews were conducted and analyzed iteratively until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new codes or themes related to types, sources, or perceived impacts of social support emerged in successive interviews [18, 32].

Using convenience sampling (i.e., contacting women who had completed the parent trial and consented to future contact) and theoretical sampling (i.e., purposively selecting participants to ensure variation across the biopsychosocial domains framing the interview guide), the Miami-based study coordinator recruited potential participants from the parent trial to take part in the interviews [18]. The senior author (S.S.C.), trained in qualitative research methods, conducted the interviews. The interviews were conducted privately at the University of Miami Clinical Research Building in Miami, FL, and were audio-recorded. Each interview lasted approximately 1 h. Participants were asked about the types of social support provided (before, during, and after study completion), their perceived sources of social support during these periods, and the perceived impact of social support on treatment outcomes. Table 1 presents the relevant interview guide questions. All participants received a $25 gift card. The semi-structured interview guide was informed by the biopsychosocial framework and adapted from prior qualitative work conducted within the WHAT-IF? trial, which used theory-driven questions to assess biological, psychological, and social determinants of alcohol use and treatment engagement among women with HIV [18].

Social support framework

Cutrona and Suhr developed a social support framework that operationalizes five general social support categories: (a) informational, (b) emotional, (c) esteem, (d) social network support, and (e) tangible support [33]. For this analysis, we focused on four types of social support: informational, emotional, esteem, and tangible. Emotional support refers to someone who is caring, trustworthy, friendly, and listens. Informational support is teaching someone about a topic or receiving information or guidance pertinent to the recovery process (e.g., referrals and recommendations for self-care). Esteem support provides information that validates an individual’s choices (e.g., a person’s decision to pursue sobriety). Tangible support provides participants with goods and services to aid their recovery. Although “social network support” is included in the Cutrona and Suhr typology, we operationalized it as a source of social support (family, friends, church). We examined types of support as emotional, informational, esteem, and tangible. As shown in Table 3, social networks (e.g., family, friends, church) were treated analytically as sources of emotional and informational support rather than as a standalone type of social support.

Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim and uploaded to NVivo 12.0 for analysis [34]. Thematic analysis was employed, with transcripts read multiple times to achieve a thorough understanding. Codes were generated in NVivo by the first and senior author, and data were extracted into Excel by the first author (N.S.). Quotes were color-coded based on a common theme or subtheme, defined by N.S. with input from S.S.C. Next, overarching themes were created by grouping the common subthemes. Furthermore, N.S. and S.S.C. met regularly to discuss and compare emerging codes and resolve disagreements. The first author compiled and condensed the results into a conclusive report. To enhance analytic rigor, coding discrepancies were resolved through iterative consensus discussions between N.S. and S.S.C., with the coding structure and themes refined throughout the analysis.

Ethics statement

Ethical approval for the parent study and this qualitative study was granted by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) at the University of Florida (Gainesville, FL) and the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine (Miami, FL), and it was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964. WWH in the parent study provided written informed consent before participating in the interviews.

Results

The mean age of the 20 interviewed WWH was 49 (SD: 7; range 36–59). The majority (85%) of women were Black/African American, half (55%) had less than a high school diploma, most (60%) were single, and the vast majority (95%) were unemployed. We conducted our analysis based on a coding framework to explore three main areas: (1) types of social support, (2) sources of social support, and (3) the perceived impact of social support on drinking outcomes. Key themes, subthemes, and associated definitions are presented in Table 3.

Types of social support

According to the Cutrona & Suhr framework, there are five general categories of social support: emotional, informational, esteem, tangible, and social network support. In this study, the most common categories of support reported by participants were emotional and informational, whereas tangible and esteem support were reported less frequently.

Emotional support

Among women with HIV receiving treatment for alcohol use, emotional support was seen as an essential source of encouragement that provided a sense of connection, validation, and motivation throughout the recovery process. Participants highlighted the value of being heard without judgment and having someone who listens with empathy when needed. This type of emotional support allowed them to express their feelings openly, which was seen as therapeutic and grounding.

“Giving me an ear. Sometimes, I just need to call and talk. I don’t need you to give me a response; just listen to what I have to say; that’s it. I have a cousin who is like that. I can call her at any time, even at two or three in the morning. If she sees it’s me, she’ll pick the phone up and say, Cuz, what’s wrong?’ and we’ll just talk.” (Age 53, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

Many participants emphasized the importance of having someone attentive and genuinely concerned about their well-being. Emotional support included receiving care from family members, especially children, who reminded them to take their study medication and offered companionship.

“Making sure I’m taking care of my health every day. Ensuring I’m on top of my medication. My son calls my daughters every morning. I’ve got two boys and two girls. I take mine at bedtime. They’ll call me once they get off work because they know I’ve been asleep. ‘Mom, did you take your medicine [study medication] last night?’ Then, they spend quality time with me.” (Age 51, non-Hispanic Black, did not reduce or quit drinking).

A few participants expressed that emotional support also came in the form of physical presence and intentional acts of kindness, which helped them combat isolation and depressive thoughts. For some, this consistent encouragement was instrumental in sustaining their efforts toward sobriety.

“Sometimes my friend would grab me before she went to work and say, ‘Here goes your breakfast. I want you to eat this before you do anything else.’ She called me throughout the day and said, ‘How’s everything going?’ or ‘What’s on your mind?’ to keep me from being depressed, to keep me motivated. She would say, ‘I’m going to pick you up at lunch; we’ll sit on the beach.’ Things like that helped me. It cleared my mind. If we all had somebody who could do that for us, I think a lot of us wouldn’t be drinking, except for those who have it hereditary. It’s hard for them to stop.” (Age 44, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

Informational support

In the context of WWH receiving treatment for alcohol use, informational support refers to receiving guidance, education, or resources that are directly relevant to their recovery process, such as referrals to services, strategies for managing alcohol use, and recommendations for self-care. Some participants reported that it involved not only being taught about health and recovery-related topics but also receiving actionable advice that empowered them to make informed decisions.

“They are not only showing me; they’re teaching me. They are guiding me, you know. They give me information when I need it, which is the key. So, just with that, I don’t need nobody to take me here or there. Just tell me where to go, what I can do, and allow me to do it.” (Age 45, Hispanic, quit drinking).

“They’re listening, they’re giving me good advice, suggestions. Me taking suggestions, that’s something I never really did before. I learned how to take suggestions and put them to good use.” (Age 50, non-Hispanic White, quit drinking).

Informational support also extended beyond practical advice to include honest, sometimes tough, feedback that was essential for their personal growth and sustained recovery.

“If I share something with her, she doesn’t make me feel worse than what I’m feeling. She is raw. ‘Leave them alone. They don’t mean you no good.’ She’s not cutting the cake with me and fixing it up where I’ll be okay to wallow in whatever I feel. She’s always there with the right words to say. Even if I don’t want to hear it, it’s what I need to hear at that present moment.” (Age 49, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

Sources of social support

Sources of social support included participants’ social networks, which consisted of family and close friends. While not listed as a consistent source of support among all participants, some women mentioned that the spiritual support from their church was instrumental in helping them reduce or quit their drinking.

Social network members are a source of social support (interpersonal networks)

Having a strong social network that provides sources of social support through interpersonal networks can contribute to the health and well-being of WWH who may also engage in unhealthy alcohol use. For many participants in this study, non-drinking family members were identified as a stronger support source, as they did not encourage continued drinking and encouraged the participant to change their lifestyle.

“My family, they don’t like when I am under the influence. As far as the support system, some of them still drink and never stop drinking. Then you got some of them that don’t drink. Those are the ones I look at as being supportive of me because they’re not doing anything to influence me. They don’t do it they selves.” (Age 36, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

Another participant discussed how a family member was concerned for their health after finding out that they engaged in unhealthy alcohol use. The family member would often discuss the negative risks associated with drinking while taking HIV medication in hopes of encouraging the participant to make better choices.

“My sisters say I don’t need to drink because my medication [HIV medication] I take don’t agree with alcohol.” (Age 44, non-Hispanic Black, did not reduce or quit drinking).

Other participants described receiving social support from many friends with whom they felt comfortable confiding over the years. This participant discussed how sharing their experiences living with HIV and recovering from alcohol use allowed their friends to provide the type of support they needed.

“Yeah, I have a lot of friends, and some of them know my past, some of them don’t. You know, like I told my friend girl the other day, I have an HIV momma, I have a recovery momma, I have a recovery daddy, you know, and all of them give me what I need. I go to those different people.” (Age 39, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

Among the minority of participants who were married, very few described receiving social support from their husbands. This participant described her husband as her primary source of support, emphasizing his constant presence and support across all aspects of her life.

“My husband is my support and my church, but mainly my husband because he’s there for me at all times. He supports me with everything I do and anything;…Mainly, my husband is my support. ”- (Age 52, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

Church members are a source of social support (faith-based source of social support)

Church members emerged as a valuable source of social support for WWH participating in alcohol treatment. For many participants, faith-based community ties, such as church congregations, offered a sense of structure, purpose, and emotional support. Christian friends and fellow congregants offered encouragement, accountability, and nonjudgmental companionship that reinforced their motivation to reduce or abstain from alcohol use.

“My church has become my support system. I wasn’t really into the church back then. I was not into it, so when my son got in trouble and all that happened, the guy I was renting from actually heard me. I was living in an efficiency which was connected to his house. He could hear me on his side and me crying out. The next day, he was getting ready to go to church and knocked on the door. He said, ‘You’re fixing to go to church with me.’” (Age 53, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

“I had a great support system. I think that’s what helped me. I used to go to church. I have a lot of Christian friends who were there to try to push me, especially those who knew I was in a study. They were there to motivate me.” (Age 44, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

These narratives highlight how faith-based communities can serve as alternative or complementary support systems for WWH in recovery. For some, the church environment provided both spiritual guidance and practical encouragement essential to sustaining recovery efforts.

Perceived impact of social support on drinking outcomes

WWH, participating in alcohol treatment, frequently reflected on how social support played a critical role in shaping recovery outcomes, specifically their self-reported ability to reduce or quit alcohol use. Participants emphasized the overall impact of having consistent, affirming, and recovery-oriented people in their lives. This presence helped reinforce their motivation, encouraged healthier behaviors, and provided accountability in moments of vulnerability. Themes of self-efficacy and positive encouragement emerged as key mechanisms through which this support contributed to successful treatment outcomes.

Self-efficacy

Some participants reported that social support strengthened their willpower and belief in their ability to change. Encouragement from others often reinforced their internal motivation to reduce or quit drinking, even when temptations or past habits resurfaced. While some acknowledged that the ultimate decision to stop drinking rested within themselves, support from others enhanced their determination and self-control.

“No, it’ll keep me, and then it’s mind over matter. It has to matter, you know. You have to want to do it. [A] person could lead you to hell, but if you go in, that’s you. You have to make the decision. Somebody could lead you to the water but drinking it’s up to you. So, you know, it’s willingness.” (Age 39, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

“I can only speak for me. I can’t speak for nobody else. Yeah. Being the person I am, it was just something that I knew I needed to do, whether I got support or not. I knew they were going to be supportive. It’s good to have people there for you and be in your corner. Whenever you need something, regardless of whatever it is. As long as you know that this is right. Now, as far as me going back out there smoking crack, I wouldn’t even want nobody to be in my corner for that. Knowing that drinking is going to hurt me, I wouldn’t want nobody to be in my corner for that. That’s just dumb.” (Age 54, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

Positive encouragement

Participants described positive encouragement as verbal affirmation, emotional upliftment, and recovery-oriented motivation provided by supportive individuals that reinforced their belief in their ability to reduce or stop drinking. Participants emphasized that positive reinforcement from supportive individuals helped them remain focused on their recovery goals and served as a motivator for behavior change. Women reported being encouraged to reduce or abstain from alcohol use.

“Encouraging you, leading you in the right directions, that’s what support is, helping people up when they’re down.” (Age 39, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

“Everyone needs someone to be there to support them, to encourage them, to let them know that they’re an over-comer. They can come out. They can be a better them. That’s very important in an individual’s life to know that someone is there to help lift them up when they’re at that point in their life.” (Age 49, non-Hispanic Black, quit drinking).

Discussion

The purpose of this qualitative study was to explore the types and sources of social support received, as well as the perceived impact of social support on drinking outcomes among a sample of WWH in the WHAT-IF? alcohol-treatment pharmacotherapy trial in Florida. Given the trial-based context, these findings primarily reflect social support mechanisms that may facilitate engagement and behavior change within pharmacotherapy-based alcohol treatment studies and should be interpreted within this clinical research setting.

Emotional and informational support were the most commonly reported types of social support. Additionally, participants most frequently identified their social networks as their primary source of support. Moreover, participants emphasized the importance of continuous encouragement to enable participation in alcohol treatment studies. Participants’ narratives indicate that social support for alcohol treatment often simultaneously supported HIV care, with overlapping support roles (e.g., medication reminders, discussions of alcohol - ART interactions, and shared caregiving figures) serving both conditions. This integrated support may be crucial for women with HIV, for whom maintaining separate support networks for HIV and alcohol use may not be feasible.

Findings revealed that emotional and informational support were the most mentioned types of social support for alcohol treatment [35]. Emotional support in our study involved listening without judgment, demonstrating trust, and showing care, which are key assets in addiction recovery [36]. Most participants reported having trusted family and friends available to listen and care when needed. Previous research highlights the importance of trust and care in the long-term effectiveness of substance use disorder treatment [37]. Emotional support enhances recovery and societal reintegration [10].

Informational support included teaching WWH about new topics and engaging them in meaningful conversations. Participants appreciated having someone provide care, understanding, and guidance, along with the necessary information to support their recovery process. Providing this information empowered them and encouraged independent coping. Other studies have reported that informational support helps women develop coping skills and understand the benefits of sobriety, aiding recovery [11]. Thus, informational support is essential in improving awareness of unhealthy alcohol use, helping prevent relapse, and maintaining long-term recovery.

Considering engagement in alcohol treatment, the most commonly mentioned source of social support was their social network, comprising interpersonal relationships with family and friends [38, 39]. Despite participants having family members who were not sober, sober family members were instrumental in offering support by discouraging drinking and motivating recovery. The positive results we observed in alcohol treatment (alcohol remission) are in line with research showing the benefits of family support [40, 41]. Additionally, a recent study in the general population suggests that friendships [42] and peer support [43] are more effective in alcohol treatment. Peer support aids in treatment retention [44] and contributes to favorable post-treatment outcomes [41], thereby supporting sustainable recovery.

While faith-based support emerged as an essential source of encouragement for some participants, it is important to note that religious support in this study was primarily described within the context of Christian church involvement. As such, these findings should not be interpreted as broadly representative of all religious or spiritual traditions. Prior research suggests that religious and spiritual support can positively influence substance use recovery through mechanisms such as meaning-making, accountability, and social integration; however, the form and impact of this support may vary substantially across faith traditions, cultural contexts, and individual belief systems [45, 46]. Similarly, although spouses and intimate partners are often identified as key sources of support in substance use treatment, spousal support was not frequently reported in this sample, reflecting the diversity of relationship structures and social circumstances among women with HIV. Consequently, we cannot draw broader conclusions regarding the role of spouses or non-Christian religious institutions in supporting alcohol treatment outcomes among women with HIV cannot be drawn from this study alone. Future research should intentionally include participants from diverse religious backgrounds, as well as women with varied partnership and family structures, to better understand how different forms of spiritual, relational, and cultural support influence treatment engagement, adherence, and long-term recovery outcomes [47].

Overall, WWH in this study emphasized the importance of social support in alcohol treatment, particularly continuous encouragement and open conversations. Participants reported that social support was critical because it helped women overcome challenges and improve their situations. Studies show that individuals with higher self-efficacy tend to have more social support [48]. Self-efficacy, confidence in one’s ability to achieve a desired outcome [49], not only increases the likelihood of recovery in people with substance-use disorders but can also be reinforced by strong social support [50]. Although participants across drinking outcomes described similar types (emotional and informational) and sources (family, friends, church) of social support, women who reported quitting drinking more often described support that was consistent, proactive, and explicitly aligned with recovery goals, including encouragement, accountability, and reinforcement of behavior change. In contrast, participants who did not reduce or quit drinking described support that was caring but less sustained or less clearly linked to alcohol behavior change, suggesting that the alignment and consistency of support may differentiate successful alcohol reduction from continued drinking.

This study had limitations. For the parent qualitative study, participants were recruited for interviews either at the time of the parent study completion or up to 2 years after. As such, the timing of interviews was not identical for all participants [18]. This variation in timing was not expected to materially affect the findings, as the interviews focused on participants’ retrospective experiences. We acknowledge that recall bias is a potential limitation, as some participants were interviewed up to two years after completing the parent study. Given the potential recall bias, the findings of this study are the women’s self-reported reasons for participating. Findings are most applicable to women with HIV who are engaged in pharmacotherapy-based alcohol treatment within a clinical trial and may not be generalizable to all WWH who engage in unhealthy drinking behaviors, particularly those not enrolled in treatment or participating in non-pharmacologic interventions. We did not analyze qualitative themes by randomized treatment assignment (naltrexone vs. placebo); therefore, potential differences in trial experiences by arm were not assessed. Given the demographic makeup of our sample, these findings might not be generalizable to all WWH who engage in unhealthy drinking behaviors. However, this study focused on social support within a pharmacotherapy alcohol treatment trial, and these findings may not apply to other types of alcohol treatment interventions (e.g., Cognitive Behavioral Therapy or Contingency Management). Additionally, social support was only briefly explored in interviews, despite being a complex issue, suggesting the need for further research to understand barriers and facilitators to alcohol treatment trial participation in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

WWH reported that social support from family, friends, and faith-based communities benefited their alcohol treatment through encouragement, emotional support, guidance, and motivation to reduce or abstain from alcohol use. Participants emphasized the importance of supportive relationships that reinforced self-efficacy, accountability, and recovery-oriented behavior change. Encouraging WWH to seek support from friends, family, healthcare providers, community organizations, and peer support networks may help strengthen alcohol reduction and recovery efforts. Integrating emotional, informational, and community-based support alongside alcohol and HIV care may also support adherence to treatment and improve HIV-related health outcomes among underserved communities.
