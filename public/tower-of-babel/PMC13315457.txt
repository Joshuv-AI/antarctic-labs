Background

Location of any manufacturing plant is a competitive advantage for the organization from a point of view of cost, logistics, supply chain, or proximity to customers [1,2,4]. Choosing a site involves looking at multiple factors at a time. Initially in site selection context Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) methods such as AHP, TOPSIS, Analytic Network Process (ANP), and Preference Ranking Organization Method for Enrichment Evaluation (PROMETHEE) have been used for evaluating alternatives [13,16,10,22] with Geographic Information System (GIS). These methods are valuable in stakeholder-driven contexts but are not considered for subjective weighting [21,60]. They have limited capacity to process high-dimensional, heterogeneous datasets for site selection [177]. Machine Learning (ML) methods have powerful predictive and pattern recognition capabilities.

In recent studies, it is shown that from offshore carbon storage [155] to urban park planning [158], machine learning is used in large-scale socio-economic and environmental datasets. Algorithms such as Random Forest, Deep Neural Network (DNN), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Support Vector Machine are effective in capturing complex, non-linear relationships [156,165]. In situations where decisions carry high stakes or require emergency planning, decision makers need not only transparent and explainable outputs but also outputs that help reduce potential bias [159,162]. Recent studies highlight that Hybrid MCDM-ML frameworks have emerged as a promising direction for site selection [179,184].

By combining the interpretability and systematic reasoning of MCDM with the predictive accuracy and adaptability of ML [181,182], these hybrid approaches (MCDM+ML) enhance the processes of weighting and ranking [180,183]. These approaches show insights from large datasets, remain reliable when conditions are unpredictable, and keep transparency for stakeholder involvement, while also making use of computational efficiency [185,187]. This integration shows a movement toward accountable, explainable, evidence-informed decision support [186]. Apparently, site selection can be understood as a multi-objective optimization problem under uncertainty, where decisions are guided by rationality and evidence-based reasoning [30,31]. In this review, the terms site selection, location selection, and siting are used interchangeably to describe the process of identifying and evaluating suitable locations for a specific facility, project, or activity. The terminology varies across studies and application domains, but the underlying concept remains the same.

This research directly supports sustainability transition theory and the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy) by enabling transparent, data-driven siting of renewable energy and other sustainable infrastructure [35,36,47]. It also contributes to optimization and energy systems planning by offering frameworks that integrate technical performance, cost efficiency, and environmental sustainability [91,94].

This review is among the first to systematically integrate MCDM, ML, and hybrid approaches across multiple domains, including energy generation, public services, logistics and infrastructure, retail and commercial facilities, water management, emergency and disaster management, and waste management over the period 2015–2025. Existing reviews are limited to specific application areas like energy systems or GIS-based site selection [48,49]. Its contributions are fourfold:

Previous versions of reviews have examined site selection methods that were mostly limited in scope, focusing either on single domains such as renewable energy (e.g., solar, wind, or hydropower) [132,138], on specific infrastructures such as dams or landfills [109], or on individual methodological families such as MCDM [134]. These reviews, though valuable, provide only a limited outlook of the methodological landscape.

In contrast, the present study offers the first systematic synthesis that integrates MCDM, machine learning, and hybrid frameworks across multiple domains, including energy, healthcare, waste management, disaster resilience, and commercial infrastructure. Using PRISMA and CIMO frameworks, this review not only maps methodological diversity but also highlights cross-domain trends, interpretability challenges, and emerging opportunities for integration. This placement differentiates our work from earlier reviews and focuses on its findings to both methodological innovation and practical decision support.

The rest of the paper follows the PRISMA and CIMO frameworks. It begins by presenting the main insights on MCDM, ML, and hybrid (MCDM+ML) methods used in site selection. The findings are then organized by domain and method category, after which the proposed conceptual framework is introduced. This is followed by the discussion and the final section on future research directions.

Method detail

This systematic review shows the CIMO logic framework (Context, Intervention, Mechanism, Outcome) to structure and analyse the literature on site selection. The framework ensures clarity and consistency by joining the research domain, the methods applied and their use, and the results achieved. The methodology follows established systematic review practices and incorporates elements of the PRISMA approach to ensure transparency and reliability.

Research questions

The review was guided by the following research questions (RQs):

RQ1. In which domains have site selection using MCDM and/or ML techniques been applied between 2015 and 2025? (Context)

RQ2. Which specific MCDM and ML methods have been used for site selection? (Intervention)

RQ3. What factors influence the selection of these methods for different application areas? (Mechanism)

RQ4. What are the commonly used criteria across different domains, and how do these methods enhance decision-making? (Outcome)

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted using major peer-reviewed publisher-hosted scholarly platforms, including ScienceDirect (Elsevier), IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink, Wiley Online Library, Taylor & Francis Online, MDPI, and Hindawi. These platforms provide indexed access to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings. The search focused on publications in English between 2015 and 2025. The initial database searches were conducted on the following dates: ScienceDirect (24 March 2025), IEEE Xplore (25 March 2025), SpringerLink (25 March 2025), Wiley Online Library (26 March 2025), Taylor & Francis Online (26 March 2025), MDPI (24 March 2025), and Hindawi (26 March 2025). As the review progressed, additional searches, screening activities, and reference checks were carried out across the selected databases before the final study selection was completed. Searches were performed primarily in the title and abstract fields across all databases to improve relevance and consistency. In some databases, author keywords or indexed terms were also included where these fields were part of the default platform search behaviour. No additional subject-specific filters or category restrictions were applied. This approach helped maintain consistency and comparability across databases while acknowledging minor differences in platform search behaviour. To identify relevant studies, combinations of keywords related to site selection, MCDM/MCDA techniques, and machine learning were used. A common Boolean search structure was developed and applied across all databases:

(“site selection” OR “location selection” OR siting) AND (“MCDM” OR “MCDA” OR “multi-criteria decision making” OR AHP OR TOPSIS OR BWM) AND (“machine learning” OR “decision support system” OR SVM OR “random forest”)

This structure was applied across all selected databases. In practice, small adjustments were required to match platform-specific rules such as query length limits, handling of Boolean operators, quotation marks, and field restrictions. Even with these adjustments, the overall keyword groups and search logic remained consistent. Platform-specific query formats and search fields are summarized in Table 2. The IEEE Xplore query was slightly simplified to better align with the platform's search functionality. However, the main concepts related to site selection, MCDM, and machine learning were still represented in the search. No important keyword groups were intentionally omitted, and the overall search strategy remained consistent across all databases.

•Peer-reviewed journal or conference papers between 2015 and 2025.

Quality appraisal and risk considerations

To ensure the quality of the review, we conducted a structured appraisal during the full-text eligibility stage. A checklist was made to evaluate both the technical and reporting standards of studies, which includes MCDM, ML, or hybrid MCDM+ML approaches to site selection. Each study was independently assessed by two corresponding authors using the predefined criteria, and any differences in judgment were resolved through discussion and consensus. To further assess consistency between authors, a random subset of records was independently reviewed by both authors, and the inter-rater reliability results are presented in Supplementary Table S1, which showed 95% agreement. Each study was evaluated using a checklist based on the following criteria: (i) methodological clarity- It includes a clear description of the modelling framework and criteria selection. (ii) Dataset transparency- It was examined by checking whether data sources and key characteristics, such as size or resolution, were reported. (iii) Validation Procedures-It covers such as Accuracy, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Area Under the Curve (AUC), Kappa, or Coefficient of Determination (R²), which were reviewed to determine how model performance was evaluated and whether relevant metrics were provided. (iv) Reproducibility-It was considered based on the level of detail given for algorithms and model parameters. Finally, (v) Contextual Relevance-It was used to ensure the study directly addressed a site selection problem. Each criterion was assessed qualitatively rather than scored numerically. Studies were expected to demonstrate adequate coverage across these appraisal domains to be considered methodologically sound. Papers lacking essential detail in any domain, such as unclear dataset description or missing validation information, were excluded during this stage. The quality appraisal was conducted using a qualitative checklist based on these five appraisal domains. No numerical scoring system was applied. The complete appraisal checklist is provided in Supplementary Table S4. Studies with substantial missing methodological information in one or more appraisal domains were excluded following discussion and consensus between the authors involved in the appraisal process.

During the quality appraisal stage, 6 studies were excluded after full-text review due to irrelevant scope (n = 1), methodological limitations (n = 2), and insufficient information (n = 3), as shown in the PRISMA flow diagram. The remaining 189 studies met the defined quality standards and were included for the final analysis. All the included studies provide clear descriptions of methodological frameworks and defined decision criteria. Variations in reporting dataset characteristics and validation procedures were observed. These differences reflect variations between MCDM, ML, and hybrid approaches rather than systematic bias.

Because the studies varied in their domains, datasets, and validation protocols, we did not apply a quantitative scoring system, a minimum threshold count, or a comparative risk-of-bias index. Instead, performance outcomes were interpreted within the context of each study to avoid overgeneralization. Overall confidence in the evidence was assessed qualitatively, based on the clarity of methods, validation procedures, reproducibility, and consistency of reporting across the included studies. A statistical test for reporting bias, such as publication bias analysis, was not conducted since this review did not include a quantitative meta-analysis. To minimize potential bias, the search covered multiple peer-reviewed databases using predefined strategies, and only peer-reviewed journal and conference publications were included.

A formal certainty-of-evidence framework was not applied, as the review focused on methodological mapping rather than effect comparison.

If certain details, such as dataset size or validation procedures, were not clearly stated in a study, this was noted as “not specified”. No missing information was assumed or calculated. Due to heterogeneity in domains, datasets, and evaluation metrics, quantitative meta-analysis was not appropriate, and no sensitivity testing was performed. Information regarding funding sources or author affiliations was not collected, since it was not directly relevant to the methodological focus of this review. As this review used a qualitative synthesis rather than meta-analysis, it presented performance metrics (e.g., Accuracy, RMSE, MAE, AUC, R², Kappa) used as the primary outcome measures. No statistical transformations or data conversions were performed. Extracted information was synthesized as reported in the original studies.

This review has some limitations. The search was limited to English-language publications and selected publisher-hosted databases between 2015 and 2025. Grey literature, theses, reports, and non-English studies were excluded. As a result, some relevant studies may not have been captured. In addition, excluding grey literature may have introduced some publication bias, as studies with positive or stronger results are generally more likely to appear in published sources than studies reporting negative or inconclusive findings. The performance metrics reported in the reviewed studies varied depending on the type of site selection problem and the modelling approach adopted. Studies focused on classification tasks typically reported metrics such as Accuracy, AUC, F1-score, and Kappa, whereas studies involving prediction or estimation commonly used RMSE, MAE, and R². Since the reviewed studies differed in their application domains, datasets, objectives, and validation strategies, direct comparison of performance results across studies was often not appropriate. Therefore, the reported metrics were presented and interpreted within the context of each study.

Synthesis approach

Using the CIMO logic, the studies were analysed by matching the context (problem area), the intervention (MCDM/ML methods), the mechanism (why and how the method was used), and the outcome (the effectiveness of the approach). This allowed for a clearer understanding of how MCDM and ML techniques are being applied in various domains and which approaches work best in which situations.

Key insights from ML and hybrid methods in site selection

Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) techniques are prominently used for site selection across various domains where expert judgment and structured evaluation frameworks are necessary. AHP, TOPSIS, PROMETHEE, and ELimination Et Choix Traduisant la REalité (ELECTRE) are among the most utilized methods, applied in contexts such as Energy generation [4,5,7,14,[17], [18], [19], [20],28,[34], [35], [36],38,42,43,[47], [48], [49],51,55,[61], [62], [63], [64], [65],69,72,80,82,83,87,89,91,94,95,97,99,100,102,[110], [111], [112], [113],120,122,123,128,132,134,137,138,141,142,149,150,[152], [153], [154]], Water management [[9], [10], [11], [12],[23], [24], [25],41,45,52,54,77,[107], [108], [109]] and public services [1,2,16,26,29,32,68,75,84,88,125,129,140,147], Waste management [22,31,37,40,44,53,57,58,66,70,71,73,76,[78], [79], [80], [81],86,90,98,103,105,106,114,121,124,127,131,135,139,144] Logistics and infrastructure [3,6,21,27,30,39,46,50,56,60,74,93,96,101,104,116,119,133,136,143,148,151] Retail, commercial & leisure [8,92,145], Emergency, safety and disaster [13,15,33,59,67,85,115,117,118,126,130,146]. The site selection process involves multiple interrelated factors to make optimal decisions. The main key factors include economic, environmental impacts, proximity to location, risk assessment and sustainability. Decision-makers need to balance these factors based on project needs and industry and stakeholders’ requirements. An analytical method that incorporates both the quantitative and qualitative aspects will be more objective. Through available data, analysis and other considerations, the best decision can be made for an organization's future stability. In the last decade, multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) methods have been extensively employed in different fields for selecting suitable sites. Classical techniques like AHP, Fuzzy AHP and ANP continue to find application in various industries such as public services [32,75], energy generation [47,149], logistics and infrastructure [151], retail, commercial & leisure [169], water management [77], emergency, safety and disaster [67] as well as waste management sectors [70]. Ranking-based techniques such as ELECTRE and its variants [12,109], TOPSIS with its fuzzy extensions [127,102,74], and PROMETHEE and its variants [30] have also gained popularity for site selection. Aggregation-based techniques such as Weighted Linear Combination (WLC) [21,69], and Delphi-AHP hybrid methods [119,60] have also been used. Most recently, the Best–Worst Method (BWM) [141,62], Weighted Multi-Objective Optimization on the basis of Ratio Analysis (Weighted MOORA) [133] and Type-2 fuzzy aggregation [90,28] were added for this purpose. A hybrid MCDM framework, as defined in Section 2.3, combining different techniques with GIS and fuzzy logic has been used [117,179,68,66]. These combinations show that MCDM techniques can be integrated to support site selection, especially when multiple interrelated criteria and spatial factors have equal importance in the context of site selection. All 189 included studies were carefully classified by application domain and methodological approach. However, more detailed analysis was devoted to ML and hybrid methods because these approaches are relatively recent and have received less consolidated attention in existing reviews.

Site selection using machine learning (ML) methods

Machine Learning (ML) has been integrated into site selection processes, mainly in domains having complex pattern recognition from large datasets. Techniques like Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, Artificial Neural Network (ANN) and deep learning models have been applied in energy generation, where several studies reported strong predictive performance under specific datasets and validation conditions. These studies (2015–2025) confirm that MCDM methods are already explored in the existing literature, including various methodological combinations. While this review acknowledges their breadth, the novel contribution of the present work lies in examining the use of machine learning (ML) and ML+MCDM hybrid methods. These methods are comparatively less explored yet relevant for handling complex site selection problems in several domains.

Energy generation [155,156,162,163,165,[175], [176], [177]], Waste management [168], Logistics and infrastructure [158,[170], [171], [172], [173],178] Retail, commercial & leisure [157,161,169,174], public services [166,167], Emergency, safety & disaster [159,160,164]. ML methods offer advanced data-processing capabilities for handling large and complex datasets. However, challenges remain such as limited labelled data, feature selection complexities, and the need for explainability. These techniques contribute to decision-making by uncovering hidden patterns and trends within complex datasets, particularly in real-world site selection applications. Machine learning has advanced site selection studies by helping researchers handle large and diverse datasets. In energy generation domains, machine and deep learning models have reported high predictive capability in several individual studies. For example, DNNs have improved offshore CO₂ storage siting [155]. Machine learning models such as XGBoost, combined with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, have enhanced wind farm site planning [162] and ensemble learning approaches have supported national-scale suitability map for wind farms [163]. Similarly, Random Forest and gradient boosting models have supported the siting of solar PV in arid areas [165], while Genetic Algorithm (GA)-based optimization has strengthened planning for energy storage and hybrid renewable systems [176]. In these energy-focused studies, ML methods were reported to support technical feasibility, cost efficiency, and sustainability assessment within the specific application contexts, datasets, and evaluation settings considered.

ML has also been applied in public services, Emergency, safety, and disaster domains. Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest were reported to support the siting of hospitals and elderly care facilities by addressing spatial imbalance and demographic pressures in several reviewed studies [159,166,170]. Emergency and evacuation domain problems and graph-based hybrids have been applied to the allocation of emergency shelters and evacuation centres, with reported reductions in travel distances [160,164] using ANNs and Variational Graph Autoencoder (VGAE). Urban Park planning has also used Decision Trees 158and k-means algorithm has supported business facility location analysis [171]. These examples illustrate how ML-based approaches have been applied to support planning and allocation decisions in public infrastructure, emergency site selection, and logistics-related applications. Several reviewed studies reported that ML methods supported accessibility and allocation of critical services. In studies [157,169] Random Forest was reported to perform better than regression-based models for retail demand analysis and consumer clustering. Ensemble learners and multimodal facilities have been applied to ATM deployment and location strategy analysis for businesses. Clustering and recommendation frameworks have also been used for ATM installation and business sitting in crowded urban markets [173]. Recent studies indicate that, in specific retail and commercial contexts, ML methods were associated with improved technical efficiency while also supporting competitiveness and customer-oriented services.

Hybrid ML approaches are used in neuro-fuzzy systems applied for hydropower siting [175] and Artificial Neural Network-Genetic Algorithm (ANN-GA) frameworks for cultural infrastructure [178]. Limited labelled data, complex feature selection, and the need for clear, explainable outputs are the challenges to building such a model that can be trusted by stakeholders and policymakers. For integrating such a framework into mainstream planning and policy for site selection, these issues are very important.

Machine learning methods are often used for site selection problems involving diverse datasets and nonlinear relationships. Random Forest, XGBoost, and deep neural networks are commonly applied algorithms in fields such as energy generation, water management, waste management, logistics, and Emergency planning. These methods have been reported to provide reliable predictions and scalability in several domain-specific studies. They have also been used to analyse complex spatial and socio-economic factors, when evaluating suitable sites. Site selection using hybrid (MCDM+ML) methods

Hybrid approaches represent a growing trend in site selection. These models combine evaluation approaches, such as AHP and TOPSIS, with ML algorithms like Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Gradient Boosting Regression (GBR), and ANN. This integration provides a framework for decision-making, like Energy generation [181,184,186,187] Waste management [179,180,183], public services [182], Emergency, safety & disaster [188], Water management [189], Logistic and infrastructure [185]. Hybrid (MCDM+ML) models have been applied in areas such as waste management, emergency safety, disaster, public services, and energy generation, etc. It involves combining multi-dimensional datasets with stakeholder contributions, allowing both data-driven and expert-based factors to be considered during site selection.

Starting with waste management, Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP)-ML-Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) models have been used for landfill siting in Saudi Arabia. In this paper, clustering, ensemble learning, and explainable AI tools like SHAP and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) were reported to improve both predictive robustness and transparency [179]. Hybrid AHP-RF models have been applied in India for sewage treatment plants and solid waste disposal. By combining expert weighting with ML classification, these studies developed suitability maps for urban and metropolitan cities [180,183]. These studies illustrate how the hybridization framework has been used to incorporate environmental considerations and stakeholder inputs within site selection processes. In the energy sector, ML+MCDM hybrids have been used for renewable energy storage and siting. For example, fuzzy TOPSIS, Additive Ratio Assessment (ARAS), Evaluation based on Distance from Average Solution (EDAS), and Multi-Objective Optimization by Ratio Analysis (MOORA) are used for rankings, and the hybrid method was used to address the complexity of problem [181]. AHP, MLP, and XAI frameworks have been applied to solar PV siting, integrating fuzzy evaluation, clustering, and SHAP interpretability [184]. Models such as AHP, Fuzzy Inference System (FIS), and Self-Attention Convolutional Long Short-Term Memory (SA-ConvLSTM) were reported to improve solar planning by producing more stable suitability maps than static GIS-MCDM approaches in that study [186]. SVM combined with MCDM methods was reported to help stakeholders handle complex datasets while considering multiple factors in site selection [187]. Hybrid AHP+MLP models have been applied to hospital site selection in public services and infrastructure [182]. For rural settlement planning in Emergency, disaster domain RF, AHP, and Entropy methods have been used [188]. The integration of AHP + ML + GIS/RS was reported to provide a useful hybrid framework for strategic site selection [185]. These examples illustrate the application of hybrid approaches across different domains where both stakeholder considerations and practical implementation aspects are important. Dam siting reviews [189] divide methods into GIS/RS, MCDM, and ML, and recommend combining them to improve predictive accuracy and decision quality. Overall, the reviewed studies indicate that MCDM+ML hybrid frameworks have been applied as practical and transparent approaches for site selection across different domains. In this review, a study was considered hybrid (MCDM+ML) when both a multi-criteria decision-making method and a machine learning algorithm were present and combined within the same analytical framework to support the site selection process. This typically involved using MCDM techniques for tasks such as criteria weighting or ranking, alongside ML algorithms for prediction, classification, or optimization within the same study. Studies that only compared MCDM and ML approaches without actually combining them in a single framework were not classified as hybrid.

Findings

This section shows the findings from this review based on the four research questions. This analysis combines the insights from the literature on site selection using MCDM, ML, and hybrid approaches, as defined in Section 2.3, from 2015 to 2025. Tables, charts, and figures are used to present the findings.

Among the 189 included studies, energy generation was the most frequently represented domain with 70 studies, followed by waste management with 34 studies, logistics and infrastructure with 29 studies, public services with 17 studies, emergency, safety and disaster with 16 studies, water management with 16 studies, and retail, commercial and leisure with 7 studies. These counts were derived from the domain classification process described in Section 2.7, where each study was assigned to one primary domain only.

RQ1. In which domains have site selection using MCDM and/or ML techniques been applied between 2015 and 2025? (Context)

Between 2015 and 2025, site selection research using MCDM and ML has covered seven domains, like Energy generation, Logistics and infrastructure, Emergency, safety and disaster, Water management, Waste management, Retail, commercial & leisure, and public services. The energy sector includes applications in solar PV, wind farms, hydropower, hydroelectric, nuclear plants, and energy storage systems. Studies such as [165,162,176] shows how ML and hybrid frameworks have been applied to balance technical, environmental, and economic key criteria in energy planning.

Beyond energy domain, applications are extended to public services including hospitals, libraries, and elderly care facilities. Common criteria include accessibility, population density, and equity considerations. In Retail and commercial siting, applications include restaurants, ATMs, and tobacco shops. This domain has become an active research area, where consumer behaviour data and geospatial analytics are used as key criteria. Waste management applications include landfills, sewage treatment plants, and disaster shelters. Several reviewed studies suggest that hybrid methods can help balance environmental risk with urban growth. Water management [45,77] has also explored site selection.

Finally, Emergency safety and disaster response, such as emergency operations site selection [67], safety zone site selection [85] and emergency shelter homes [164]. These studies show that the methods used in this domain can also be adapted for rapid response conditions.

In summary, we can say that use of MCDM and ML has grown from energy generation to waste management and also covers infrastructure planning to business competitive areas like logistics and infrastructure [74]. It is showing the capability of these hybrid methods in several domains. In this review, seven domains are classified as Energy generation, public services, Logistics and infrastructure, Retail and commercial & leisure, Water management, Emergency, safety and disaster, and lastly Waste management. RQ2. Which specific MCDM and ML methods have been used for site selection? (Intervention)

Across the studies reviewed, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) remains the most commonly used multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) technique. It is often combined with fuzzy logic (FAHP) to deal with uncertainty. Other MCDM methods include TOPSIS, PROMETHEE, ELECTRE, MOORA, and Weighted Linear Combination (WLC). These methods are used because they are relatively clear to interpret and can add expert judgment in a structured way. As a result, they are mostly applied in domains such as healthcare, waste management, and urban planning.

In machine learning (ML), tree-based algorithms such as Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM are used. Several studies reported that they perform well on geospatial datasets and can handle complex relationships in the data. Neural network models, including MLP, CNN, ConvLSTM, and SA-ConvLSTM, are often applied in domains such as energy generation and logistics and infrastructure, where nonlinear patterns and spatiotemporal forecasting are important. Support Vector Machines (SVM) and clustering methods such as K-means, DBSCAN, and SOM are also used in several studies. This is particularly for classification and exploratory analysis.

Hybrid frameworks that combine MCDM with ML have also been applied in site selection problems. Examples from the reviewed literature are AHP-RF for water management [180], FAHP-SOM-XAI for landfill siting [179], and WLC-FIS-SA-ConvLSTM for photovoltaic siting [186]. These combinations are useful because they combine structured expert-based methods and data-driven predictive modelling within the same decision workflow.

Tree-based models, particularly Random Forest, are the most frequently used approaches across the reviewed literature. Neural network models (ANN, MLP, and DNN) represent the second most commonly applied family, followed by clustering methods. RQ3. What factors influence the selection of these methods for different application areas? (Mechanism)

The choice of method is mostly affected by data availability, decision context, and specific needs. In data-rich, domains such as renewable energy, where large volume of climate and geospatial data is available, machine learning methods such as RF, XGBoost, and CNNs are used. They are used due to their predictive strength and ability to capture nonlinear relationships. On the other hand, in situations where stakeholder trust and interpretability are crucial, such as in healthcare, waste management, and disaster planning, multi-criteria decision-making methods like AHP and TOPSIS are more common.

In landfill siting, FAHP is used to give structured weighting, while machine learning is applied to refine and validate the predictions. Deep learning is used to forecast demand patterns, while fuzzy inference systems translate these outputs into practical suitability maps in urban PV siting. Additional factors also influence method choice. For example, regulatory frameworks want researchers to use transparent MCDM approaches in public policy settings. In contrast, when sufficient data and computational resources are available, researchers tend to adopt machine learning methods. In the studies where geographical criteria play an important role, often rely on MCDM methods due to their interpretability. On the other hand, national-level studies that involve large datasets tend to use machine learning methods due to their scalability and ability to handle complex data. Overall method selection shows a balance between data complexity, stakeholder requirements, and decision-making needs.

MCDM methods help to make choices by systematically structuring a decision problem and to balance competing goals and priorities. These methods also make the process clear and understandable to policymakers and stakeholders. ML methods are used to improve predictive accuracy, managing large datasets, and identifying hidden patterns. Hybrid methods (MCDM+ML) may reduce bias, increase robustness under uncertainty, and provide well-explained data-driven results. For example, SHAP and LIME explanations in landfill siting [179] demonstrate how explainable AI tools can support more informed decision -making.

The integration of MCDM and ML has changed site selection from a subjective, expert-based process to a systematic, evidence-based, and reproducible solution. This transformation has definitely improved the effectiveness of spatial planning decisions as site selection across different contexts. Although existing studies have explored various methods but still several gaps are present across domains. To address these gaps, this review proposes a framework based on three connected pillars: benchmarking, explainability, and validation.

Proposed conceptual framework: benchmarking-explainability-validation

In this review, we introduced a framework based on three components: benchmarking, explainability, and validation. These stages are supposed to be applied in sequential manner in a practical site selection workflow.

Benchmarking creates common grounds for geo-spatial repositories and criteria so that results across different domains (e.g. energy generation, logistics and infrastructure, and water management) can be easily compared. This stage involves collecting, cleaning, normalizing, and spatially aligning datasets before modelling begins in practice. Without benchmarking, hybrid models may lack transferability across different contexts.

Explainability enhances trust and accountability by making model outputs transparent and understandable to stakeholders. After benchmarking, the hybrid model is applied, and its outputs are interpreted. Decision makers need to understand why a site is selected, which is supported through explainability tools such as SHAP, LIME, along with MCDM-based weighting methods. Among the reviewed studies, SHAP was the most frequently reported explainability technique and was used in five studies [162,165,166,179,184]. LIME appeared less frequently and was reported in only one study, where it was used together with SHAP in landfill siting studies [179].

Validation ensures reliability, scalability and transferability, particularly in high-stakes applications such as hospital siting, energy plants, and business location planning. At this stage, model outputs are evaluated using appropriate performance metrics and robustness checks. Many existing site selection models are tested within a single region or context. In hybrid models, MCDM components should be tested for robustness under uncertainty, while ML components should be validated across regions to reduce overfitting.

Benchmarking was included because many studies used different datasets, criteria set, and validation methods, making direct comparison difficult. Explainability was included because only a few studies used tools such as SHAP and LIME, even though transparency is important in site selection decisions. Validation was included because many studies were tested only in a single region or context, with limited cross-regional or real-world validation.

These components together provide a pathway for developing a reliable, transparent, and adaptable decision model for site selection. Table 5 summarizes the implementation stages of BEV Framework.

Discussion

This review synthesis is based on 189 studies to find how site selection research has evolved and also to highlight the existing challenges. Traditional MCDM methods are preferred for their transparency and stakeholder legitimacy, while ML methods have gained popularity for handling large and complex datasets. Hybrid models, discussed earlier in Section 2.3, combine interpretability with predictive strength and are also capable of handling complex decisions. This development shows that method selection depends not only on data availability and governance needs, but also on the decision context and the nature of the criteria involved. The discussion is organized around three themes: Methodological evolution, Domain-specific adaptations, and Criteria convergence. These themes show how site selection research is moving towards more transferable, integrated, and stakeholder-oriented solutions.

Methodological evolution

Classical MCDM methods such as AHP, TOPSIS, and PROMETHEE remain widely used due to their transparency and acceptance among stakeholders. For large and heterogeneous datasets, ML techniques such as Random Forest, XGBoost, and neural networks are mostly applied for their predictive accuracy. A recent development is the emergence of hybrid frameworks (e.g., AHP+RF, FAHP+SOM+XAI, AHP+FIS+SA-ConvLSTM). These frameworks combine interpretability with predictive strength and support explainable, interdisciplinary decision support in the field of site selection.

The continuous use of AHP and TOPSIS reflects their simplicity and familiarity, but may also indicate limited adoption of newer methods in some contexts. In the case of large labelled datasets, ML methods provide better predictive accuracy, which sometimes can be a problem in data-scarce environment. Hybrid methods offer a balanced solution. However, their complexity may limit practical adoption unless supported by clear and repeatable workflows.

Fig. 8 shows the methodological growth in site selection research between 2015 and 2025. The earlier studies were dominated by classical MCDM approaches, which reached their peak around 2017. Machine learning methods were less explored until 2021. They were rapidly explored after that, which reflects the focus on using predictive accuracy and data-driven validation for site selection.

Methodological evolution.

Hybrid (MCDM+ML) approaches appear less frequently in the literature. These methods still represent an emerging and underexplored integrated framework. This evolution shows a shift from conventional decision-support to information-rich models. The hybrid approach offers a balance between transparency, reduced bias, and predictive capability within specific application contexts.

Domain-specific adaptations

Method selection is strongly influenced by application context. Energy planning often favors ML and hybrid approaches due to the availability of geospatial and climatic data. Healthcare and public services rely on MCDM methods to ensure transparency and policy alignment. Waste management and disaster planning studies also adopt hybrid approaches to balance uncertainty with transparency. In retail and commercial siting, clustering and predictive ML methods are used to capture consumer behavior and demand patterns. Strategic and security-sensitive domains give importance to robustness and integration of multiple criteria. These patterns confirm that method selection depends on data richness or availability, decision stakes, and governance requirements.

Fig. 9 shows the distribution of site selection across the seven domains discussed in this review. Energy generation is the most dominant domain and reflects the maturity of MCDM and hybrid approaches in balancing technical and environmental criteria. Public services, logistics and infrastructure follow, where method selection is influenced by transparency and governance requirements. Waste and water management appear moderately and often apply fuzzy or statistical hybrid methods to address uncertainty. Emergency, safety, & disaster related studies are fewer in number but remain highly significant. Retail, commercial & leisure are less explored and indicate an opportunity for ML-enhanced models.

Criteria convergence

Across the seven domains, the most common key criteria include environmental factors (slope, elevation, geology, hydrology), socio-economic factors (population density, land price, accessibility), infrastructure factors (roads, railways, substations), and sustainability/risk-related factors (hazard zones, biodiversity, regulations). The pattern suggests the possibility of developing more generalizable frameworks, domain-specific factors such as wind speed in energy applications and groundwater depth in waste management still require contextual customization.

However, many studies choose criteria based on data availability instead of what is actually required for a specific domain. This practice may reduce consistency across domains and introduce local biases into otherwise generalizable model.

Fig. 11 illustrates how different criteria converge across the seven domains. Environmental and infrastructure-related factors dominate energy and logistics applications. Socio-economic factors are more prominent in public services and retail, where community and market needs are central. Sustainability and risk criteria are more balanced, particularly in water and waste management. Gaps and challenges

Explainability in ML remains underutilized, despite the growing use of tools such as SHAP and LIME. Cross-domain validation remains limited, raising concerns about the transferability of many existing models. In addition, socio-political and behavioural factors are often overlooked, even though they strongly influence real-world siting decisions. Without standardized datasets, reproducible pipelines, and inclusive integration strategies, the full potential of hybrid frameworks may not be fully realized.

Positioning against prior reviews

Earlier existing reviews have provided valuable insights, but their scope has generally been limited. Most have focused on specific application domains, offering only a partial view of methodological developments across domains. This review contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it brings together MCDM, ML, and hybrid approaches across seven domains. This enables the identification of common criteria and methodological patterns across domains that have not been examined in earlier reviews. Second, it proposes a framework based on benchmarking, explainability, and validation. This helps organize the key methodological gaps into a research agenda. Third, this study highlights visual tools such as Sankey diagrams, radar charts, and Domain distribution tree maps. These visual representations improve the clarity and interpretability of the findings. Compared with previous reviews, this study shows that the site selection has progressed beyond isolated case studies. It also demonstrates the value of cross-domain frameworks for advancing site-selection research in both theory and practice.

Benchmarking and cross-regional validation

One of the main issues identified across the existing studies is the absence of systematic benchmarking and cross-regional validation. Most models rely on locally sourced datasets, making their results difficult to compare across studies and contexts. These problems show the relevance of the benchmarking, explainability, and validation (BEV) framework introduced earlier. The framework organizes current gaps within a clear research roadmap rather than proposing an entirely new method. The review shows how future methodological improvements can support more credible, transferable, and user-friendly decision-support systems using hybrid methods by positioning these limitations within this framework.

Benchmarking is preferable when studies rely on comparable datasets, evaluation metrics, and validation approaches. The reviewed literature shows considerable variation in how models are assessed. The validation methods used are simple holdout splits, cross-validation, spatial validation, and expert-based evaluation. Results were also compared with existing sites or historical data in some studies. Performance is reported using different metrics such as accuracy, RMSE, MAE, or AUC. Direct comparison across studies is actually difficult due to these differences. Validation practices also varied considerably across the reviewed ML and hybrid studies. Among the 35 studies summarized in Table 3, Table 4, holdout validation was the most commonly reported approach (10 studies), followed by spatial validation (8 studies). Mixed validation approaches, expert or rule-based validation, and cluster validation were reported in 4, 3, and 3 studies, respectively, while cross-validation was reported in 3 studies. Simulation-based validation appeared in 2 studies, whereas correlation-based validation and literature synthesis without explicit validation were each reported in 1 study. To avoid double counting, studies that used more than one validation approach were grouped under a mixed-validation category. The wide variation in validation practices highlights the lack of a common benchmarking framework and makes direct comparison of reported performance across studies difficult. This highlights the need for more consistent benchmarking and validation practices in future site selection research. This also supports the significance of the proposed framework for improving comparability and reliability across studies.

Practical implications

Making standardized criteria across domains can reduce repetitive efforts and contribute to the development of reusable frameworks. At the stage of implementation, the hybrid (ML+MCDM) methods can support infrastructure development decisions that are moretransparent, responsible, and effective for stakeholders. Finally, the importance of explainability and validation is highlighted by this review. Even robust models may face limited adoption if their outputs are not clearly interpreted or validated across different regions. Future site-selection tools should incorporate explainable AI and reproducible validation procedures to improve their credibility and wider adoption.

Conclusion

Summary of contributions

This review provides a comprehensive overview of site-selection studies that combine multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM), machine learning (ML), and hybrid methods, discussed earlier in Section 2.3. The 189 selected studies show the evolution of methods, identify common decision criteria, and highlight the increasing importance of hybrid frameworks for site selection.

Limitations

The review is limited by the databases searched and the different reporting standards used in the included studies. As noted in Section 2.7, restrictions related to language, publication type, and exclusion criteria may have led to the omission of some relevant studies.

Final remarks

This study shows how merging MCDM and ML as hybrid methods has influenced the site-selection research in recent years. The study highlights the growing need for hybrid, explainable, and data-rich frameworks to support scalable and transferable decision support. The study provides background for developing site-selection tools that are both scientifically sound and practically relevant. The following section of this paper outlines specific research directions for this agenda. For stakeholders and policymakers, this study proposes a framework that can provide sustainable, fair, and optimal site selection planning outcomes.
