Enhanced fragmentation of water dications upon high Rydberg states
Authors: Jiaqi Zhou, Lu Wu, Xintai Hao, Xiaorui Xue, Qingrui Zeng, Qibo Ma, Yongtao Zhao, Xiaokai Li, Lanhai He, Sizuo Luo, Chuncheng Wang, Dajun Ding, Xueguang Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ionization and fragmentation of water are fundamental processes across numerous scientific and technological fields, yet the relaxation pathways of water dications-particularly the rare D+ + O+ + D channel-remain poorly understood. Here, we report enhanced fragmentation pathways within this channel induced by electron-impact. Using multi-particle coincidence momentum spectroscopy supported by electron-capture-mediated molecular dissociation calculations, we identify a Rydberg state-controlled fragmentation mechanism and resolve its ultrafast relaxation dynamics. Furthermore, we demonstrate that this mechanism also occurs in ammonia and strong-field ionization experiments. In the laser-induced processes, electron-recollision with the cation leads to additional ionization plus excitation into dicationic Rydberg states on a sub-cycle (~2 fs) timescale, effectively freezing nuclear motion. Our findings reveal a general molecular fragmentation pathway governed by high Rydberg states, providing a molecular clock to probe electron-nuclear coupling and offering new insights into water radiolysis.

Introduction

The radiolysis of water involving ionization and excitation is a fundamental process in nature with profound implications across science and technology. It generates a cascade of reactive species, including hydroxyl radicals, hydrogen peroxide, protonated water clusters, and hydrated electrons1–4. These products are highly reactive and can affect the local environment through various biochemical reactions, which are of significant relevance to diverse fields such as radiobiological physics and chemistry, atmospheric and environmental science, plasma and nuclear technology5–8. In fact, the ionization and fragmentation of water have been the subject of numerous experimental and theoretical investigations induced by electron, ion, and photon radiations. These studies can link specific excited electronic states to distinct dissociation pathways, thereby elucidating the details of the processes. Recently, intense studies have been performed on the dynamics of water dication, as it is readily formed either through direct double ionization or via inner-shell ionization followed by the Auger process, which initiates ultrafast relaxation processes; see refs. 9–28. Nevertheless, the dynamics of the relaxation processes are still poorly understood, particularly when water dications are formed in excited states.

Due to the complex potential energy surfaces of the excited states mediated by non-adiabatic interactions22–24, the nuclear wave-packet can evolve across multiple surfaces via non-adiabatic transitions, thus opening new reaction pathways. More recent experiments have indicated that the excited dicationic states of (deuterated) water, primarily dissociating into D+ + D+ + O, can transition to the D+ + O+ + D channel through spin-orbit coupling and charge transfer22–24. However, the D+ + O+ + D channel was observed to be very rare, likely due to the low probability of such transitions occurring at the few- or sub-percent level22. In addition, high-energy and high-efficiency pathways were also observed in this channel, qualitatively attributed to the population of higher excited states13,16,25,26. Yet none of these studies identified the ionization mechanism, dissociation dynamics, and relaxation efficiency of the high-energy fragmentation pathway from these excited states.

In this work, we observe an enhancement of the D+ + O+ + D fragmentation yield appearing in the high kinetic energy release (KER) region. Our studies were performed using electron-impact (E0 = 200 and 80 eV), which is motivated by the fact of the abundant secondary electrons produced during water radiolysis and the significance of electron-driven processes in the radiation effect in gas and condensed matter5–7,29–31. Through fragment ions and electron coincident momentum spectroscopy (see “Methods” section)32–35, the three-body dissociation channels are measured in association with the projectile energy loss (Eloss) spectra, which enable us to elucidate the mechanistic and dynamical details of the ultrafast relaxation processes. We demonstrate that the high-KER fragmentation pathways originate from the highly-excited Rydberg states of water dications, which are formed by electron-impact double-ionization plus excitation processes. To further explore the interaction mechanism, we extend the study to ammonia and to strong-field ionization processes. In the laser-induced studies, the ionic Rydberg states are likely formed by processes such as frustrated tunneling ionization (FTI)36–43 and resonant multiphoton excitation (REMPE)44–47. By comparing the results between linearly and circularly polarized (CP) laser experiments, we identify a specific strong-field mechanism: electron recollision-induced double ionization plus excitation on a sub-cycle timescale. Furthermore, we develop an electron-capture-mediated molecular dissociation model that quantitatively links the measured KER and angular correlations to the Rydberg orbital radius and capture time. The combined results establish a unified picture of Rydberg-state-controlled fragmentation of molecules.

To identify the initial ionization states, we measured the projectile Eloss spectra associated with each fragmentation pathway. Pathway I shows an Eloss onset at about 41.5 ± 0.5 eV (Fig. 1d), which corresponds to the low excited states and agrees well with the photo-double ionization experiments22,50. Remarkably, pathways II and III exhibit substantially higher Eloss values: ~71.5 ± 0.5 eV and ~79.5 ± 0.5 eV, respectively (Fig. 1e, f). These energies correspond to water dications converging to Rydberg series, i.e., a tricationic core with one electron occupying a Rydberg orbital51,52. The total intensity of the D+ + O+ + D channel (pathways I-III combined) is approximately 1.4 times that of the D+ + OD+ channel (Table 1). Previous studies reported only 1.5% yield for this channel, primarily because pathways II and III are energetically inaccessible with 61 eV photon radiations22–24. In electron-impact, excited dicationic states can be populated by direct double ionization plus excitation on a sub-femtosecond timescale, ionizing two electrons while exciting a third into a Rydberg orbital53,54.

To quantify the Rydberg orbitals involved, we calculated transition energies of water dications using the CCSD/aug-cc-pVTZ method55 with the Gaussian package56. Our calculated energies for the 1b1 → 4a1 and 1b1 → 2b2 excitations are 21.3 eV and 22.5 eV, respectively, which are in reasonable agreement with previously reported values of 19.7 eV and 24.1 eV51. Comparing these with the ground state of water dications, we estimate that the high excitation energies of 32.5 ± 0.5 eV and 40.5 ± 0.5 eV (relative to the double ionization threshold at ~39 eV) correspond to valence electron transitions from 1b1 or 3a1 to the 3p and 4d Rydberg orbitals, respectively. Their average radii are estimated to be roughly 4.5 Å and 10.9 Å57,58, indicating that the Rydberg electron is weakly bound to the trivalent ion core. It should also be noted that high-efficiency dissociation channels from Rydberg states have been observed in other molecular systems59–62, yet their underlying dynamical mechanisms remain elusive.

Electron-impact drives electron-capture dissociation

To elucidate the role of the Rydberg electron in the relaxation dynamics, we analyze the three-body dissociation using the Dalitz plot63, which employs an energy-conservation transformation to the momentum correlation map for three fragments onto a circle of radius 1/3 inscribed in an equilateral triangle. To quantitatively understand this behavior, we developed a classical Rydberg electron capture model based on a two-step Coulomb explosion mechanism (illustrated in Fig. 3). The highly excited water dication is treated as a tricationic core \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${({{{{\rm{D}}}}}^{+}+{{{{\rm{O}}}}}^{+}+{{{{\rm{D}}}}}^{+})}^{{{{{\rm{e}}}}}_{{{{\rm{R}}}}}^{-}}$$\end{document}(D++O++D+)eR− with one weakly bound Rydberg electron (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{e}}}}}_{{{{\rm{R}}}}}^{-}$$\end{document}eR−). Initially, fragmentation is driven by the unscreened Coulomb repulsion among the three ions. When the interionic distances expand to the size of the Rydberg orbital, the Rydberg electron begins to screen the charges and is eventually captured by one of the D+ ions, forming neutral D. Subsequently, the system evolves as a two-body D+ + O+ Coulomb explosion plus a neutral D atom. The neutral D thus ceases to gain kinetic energy from Coulomb repulsion, resulting in its lower momentum compared to D+. During dissociation, the lighter D+ ions accelerate more rapidly than O+, approaching the Rydberg electron faster and preferentially capturing it to form a neutral D atom. This leads to high-lying excited states predominantly dissociating into D+ + O+ + D rather than D+ + D+ + O, thereby enhancing the cross-section of this channel. Dynamical calculations using our model (see Methods) reproduce the experimental momentum distributions of D and D+ and the KER spectrum for pathway II with good agreement (Fig. 2b, c), thereby validating the model. While the current framework captures the essential physics, we note that quantitative accuracy may be limited by polyatomic complexities such as autoionization lifetimes, spin-orbit interactions, and correlated electronic motions not explicitly included. Pathway III, appearing as a shoulder structure at KER ~ 32.5 ± 0.6 eV (Fig. 1a), provides further support for the proposed mechanism. This KER value matches that of the D+ + D+ + O+ Coulomb explosion channel64. The associated Eloss ~ 79.5 ± 0.5 eV (Fig. 1f) yields an excess energy of 40.5 ± 0.5 eV relative to the ground state dications, corresponding to the excitation energy of the 1b1 valence electron to the 4d Rydberg orbital. Notably, the energy difference between pathways II and III closely matches their KER difference, indicating that both pathways converge to the same dissociation limit. With an estimated average radius of about 10.9 Å for the 4d Rydberg orbital57, the Coulomb potential among the three cations becomes negligible at the point of electron capture. The KER for pathway III, therefore, approaches that of the D+ + D+ + O+ channel64. This interpretation is supported by the Dalitz plot in Fig. 2d, where an intensive island is distributed symmetrically about the central line perpendicular to the O+ side, indicating nearly equal momentum sharing between D and D+. The measured momentum distributions (Fig. 2e) show single-peak structures centered at about 65 ± 0.05 a.u. and 67 ± 0.05 a.u. for D and D+, respectively. This indicates that when the Rydberg electron occupies a highly diffuse orbital far from the ion core, it acts as a delayed mediator during dissociation. Our calculations accurately reproduce both the momentum distributions and KER spectrum for pathway III (Fig. 2e, f), further validating its general applicability to Rydberg molecule fragmentation dynamics. Importantly, the direct relationship between measured KER and Rydberg orbital radius (Fig. 2c, f) provides a valuable tool for probing Rydberg orbital dimensions from KER measurements.

To demonstrate the generality of this mechanism, we extended our investigation to ammonia (NH3). Moreover, the branching ratio of pathway I to pathway II is determined to be approximately 3.3:1. Strong-field laser-induced double-ionization plus excitation

The electron-impact results provide a unique signature for direct excitation of Rydberg states in water dications on sub-femtosecond timescales. In strong-field laser physics, electron recollision-where an electron liberated by tunneling ionization is driven back to its parent ion by the oscillating laser field-offers an analogous mechanism for populating such states. However, the D+ + O+ + D channel induced by strong-field ionization has rarely been studied66, and previous studies primarily focused on the D+ + D+ + O dissociation channel67–69. Comparing the results between electron-impact and laser experiments can deepen our understanding of the formation mechanism of Rydberg states.

We performed strong-field ionization experiments using both linearly polarized (LP) and CP femtosecond laser pulses to interact with D2O molecules, detecting the D+ + O+ + D channel via ion coincidence momentum spectroscopy (see “Methods” section)64,70. We now focus on the difference between LP and CP spectra at the high-KER region (dashed boxes in Fig. 5a, c), where the laser-induced electron-recollision has frozen the nuclear motions, thus enabling direct comparison with electron-impact. As shown in Fig. 5a, b, the comparison between LP and CP spectra shows an enhancement in the high-KER region of the LP result because the electron-recollision process is largely suppressed in the CP case. In the recollision process, an electron initially freed by tunneling must be driven back to its parent ion by the field, which strongly depends on the laser polarization. If the polarization is linear, the oscillating field first accelerates the free electron away from the core and then, upon reversal of the field, turns it back into the parent ion. For the CP light, it gets pulled by the field in constantly changing directions. The cusplike motion ensures that the electron never returns to the ion of its birth, thus suppressing the recollision72. This difference is more clearly seen in Fig. 5c, where the KER spectrum for the LP case shows a distinct hump structure in the high-KER region. Furthermore, we subtract the KER distribution of CP from that of LP after normalization at the dominant peaks. The resulting difference in the high-KER region is presented in Fig. 5d. Interestingly, this difference spectrum exhibits three peaks, with peak 3 located at ~23.2 ± 0.15 eV, which is consistent with the KER peak of pathway II in the electron-impact process. This result suggests that the high Rydberg states of water dications can also be populated under an LP laser field on an ultrafast timescale.

Based on the good agreement between electron-impact and LP laser experiments, we propose a mechanism for the formation of the dicationic Rydberg states in intense laser fields. As illustrated in Fig. 5e, the first electron is freed from a neutral water molecule by tunneling ionization, then it is accelerated and driven back to impact with the water cation within ~ 2 fs. According to ref. 73, the recolliding electron can gain energy of about 70 eV, sufficient to simultaneously ionize and excite the cation. In this scenario, the bond stretching or bending motion can be negligible during the 2 fs wavepacket propagation along the cationic state64. The molecular structure at the moment of recollision is therefore essentially the equilibrium geometry, yielding a KER (peak 3) comparable to electron-impact. If recollision only leads to single ionization or excitation74, the bond stretching or nuclear motion would occur, even within 2 fs, along the dissociative state of the dication64. This structural change can lead to the well-resolved decrease in the KER distribution, which is not the case of the present study. To test this assumption, we calculate the KER distribution for the water cations undergoing minimal structural reorganization (OD bond stretching 0.05 Å) within the ultrashort (2 fs) excitation window. This calculation yields a slightly lower KER distribution relative to peak 3 (Fig. 5d), which remains resolvable from our experiments.

The recollision-induced double ionization plus excitation mechanism is distinct from the well-understood FTI and REMPE processes. FTI involves electron capture at the end of the laser pulse, permitting substantial nuclear rearrangement (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{H}}}}}_{2}^{+}$$\end{document}H2+ bond elongation from 0.74 Å to 5.3 Å)75. REMPE does not involve recollision at all45,75–77. Furthermore, recollision-induced ionization plus excitation requires a hard electron-ion collision, generating strong correlated dynamics among three active electrons-a feature absent in FTI, where low-drift-momentum electrons are captured without violent collision36–43. The two additional peaks in the difference spectrum (Fig. 5d) provide further insight. Peak 1 at KER ~ 8 ± 0.2 eV corresponds to pathway I observed in electron-impact and single-photon experiments22–24. Peak 2 at KER ~ 14.5 ± 0.24 eV is attributed to the dissociative FTI mechanism. Details about these two peaks are provided in Supplementary Note 5.

Discussion

We have identified strongly enhanced fragmentation pathways of the water dication into D+ + O+ + D following excitation to high-lying Rydberg states by electron-impact. The high-KER pathways (II and III) exhibit fragmentation efficiencies more than 50 times greater than the previously observed pathway I22–24. Multi-particle coincidence momentum spectroscopy, combined with projectile energy-loss (Eloss) analysis, provides direct spectroscopic evidence that these pathways originate from a Rydberg state-controlled fragmentation mechanism. The same mechanism is observed in ammonia and in strong-field ionization of water, demonstrating its generality across excitation schemes and molecular systems.

During dissociation, a D+ ion can capture the Rydberg electron to form neutral D, with the Rydberg electron acting as both an active participant (pathway II) or a delayed mediator (pathway III). These dynamics are quantitatively reproduced by our electron-capture-mediated molecular dissociation model. Strong-field experiments further corroborate this interpretation: a pronounced enhancement at KER ~ 23 eV, identical to that observed in electron-impact, appears under LP pulses but is suppressed under circular polarization. This polarization dependence identifies laser-driven electron-recollision as the initiating step. We therefore establish recollision-induced double ionization plus excitation as an efficient route to populate high-lying Rydberg states on sub-cycle (~2 fs) timescales, with nuclear motion effectively frozen during excitation.

Our results define a unified framework in which fragmentation observables function as a femtosecond molecular clock. Discrete KER values encode the degree of Coulomb expansion prior to electron capture and thus reflect the Rydberg orbital radius, capture time, and instantaneous molecular geometry at the capture event (Fig. 6a, b). This enables time-resolved access to coupled electron-nuclear dynamics without external pump-probe timing. The angular distributions (Fig. 6c, d) provide complementary temporal information. Rapid capture (<10 fs) produces broadened angular patterns because early electron localization screens the D+ charge before substantial nuclear alignment occurs. In contrast, delayed capture permits extended Coulomb repulsion, aligning fragment momenta more closely with the original molecular axes and yielding narrower angular distributions. An analogous behavior is also observed in ammonia (see Supplementary Note 6). The high-KER fragmentation arising from Rydberg-mediated dissociation also has important implications for Coulomb explosion imaging (CEI). Transient electron capture introduces non-Coulombic screening that distorts reconstructed bond lengths and angles if pure Coulomb repulsion is assumed. For pathway II, the peak of momentum angle between D and D+ is located at about 107° (Fig. 6e), while this angle shifts to 110° in pathway III (Fig. 6f), yielding a derived ∠DOD angle of 98° that remains contracted relative to neutral water. Our quantitative comparison between CEI and the Rydberg electron capture model shows different consequences, where standard CEI produces two distorted structural solutions from pathways II (ROD = 1.32 Å, ∠DOD = 94°) and III (ROD = 1.00 Å, ∠DOD = 98°), our model correctly recovers the equilibrium geometry of the water molecule (ROD = 0.92 Å, ∠DOD = 106°). A similar trend is observed for ammonia, where the standard CEI returns a distorted structure (RNH = 1.2 Å, ∠HNH = 90∘), whereas our model yields RNH = 1.05 Å, ∠HNH = 108°, in agreement with the equilibrium geometry of NH3. These results demonstrate that transient electronic screening must be explicitly considered when extracting structural information from CEI, particularly for highly excited molecular ions.

The recollision mechanism identified here can play a role in enhancing multiple ionization78–82. At intensities above 1014 W cm−2 with 500–1000 nm LP light, the presently observed recollision-induced ionization-plus-excitation can be effectively activated82. This turns molecules into their own ultrafast electron guns and enables time-resolved study of electron-driven processes83. This ultrafast recollision mechanism induces correlated electron dynamics in the residual ion via rescattering of the returning electron, which ionizes one electron while exciting its correlated counterpart. The three-body collision among the recolliding electron and the two bound electrons reveals how dynamical screening, exchange interactions, and Coulomb repulsion collectively determine the branching ratio between ionization and excitation pathways.

Finally, our findings have direct implications for water radiolysis. Secondary electrons produced during radiolysis have energies predominantly in the 0–300 eV range30; those above ~70 eV, constituting approximately 15% of the secondary electron spectrum, can initiate the high-efficiency fragmentation mechanism reported here. The resulting energetic D+, O+, and D species can induce further reactions via charge transfer, hydrogen abstraction, excitation, and ionization in adjacent water and biomolecules84–87. These cascade reactions are expected to amplify the effects of water radiolysis, indicating that the high-efficiency, high-KER fragmentation pathways we have identified should be incorporated into models of radiation damage in biological environments.

Methods

Electron-impact experiments

The electron-impact experiments were conducted using a COLTRIMS reaction microscope32,33 coupled with a photoemission electron source34,35. The water molecules were produced by a supersonic gas expansion of water mixed with helium seed gas at room temperature. Ionization of the neutral water molecule was achieved using a well-focused pulsed electron beam (E0 = 80 eV, 200 eV) with a repetition rate of 40 kHz. The photoemission electron source was driven by a pulsed ultraviolet laser (0.5 ns pulse duration) that irradiated a tantalum photocathode. To detect ions and electrons, a homogeneous electric field of 1.5 V cm−1 and a magnetic field of 7 G were applied, directing them toward microchannel plate (MCP) detectors equipped with multi-hit delay-line position readouts (100-mm-diameter). Fragment ions, typically carrying higher momenta, required a higher electric field of 25 V cm−1 across the 5 cm detection region to ensure complete 4π collection. Our trajectory simulations confirmed this configuration can achieve complete 4π collection for D+ up to 32 eV, encompassing dissociation processes with KER up to 60 eV. This higher field was generated using a pulsed-field generator after the electron extraction. For the offline data analysis, the three-dimensional momentum vector of each detected electron and ion was reconstructed from the measured time-of-flight (TOF) and position. A −2200 V bias applied to the MCP detector front plate enabled efficient detection of low-energy D+ species. The near-identical kinetic energies of two D+ ions in the D+ + D+ + O+ channel demonstrate our detector’s capability to resolve ions with close energy. In addition to the pulsed extraction field measurements, we also performed the non-pulsed (constant) extraction field measurements. During the offline data analysis, we use the non-pulsed data to calibrate the pulsed extraction field results. This ensures the accuracy of the fragment ion momentum imaging. The measured KER uncertainty is determined by the detector resolution (~0.1 eV, arising from the timing resolution of ~5 ns and position resolution of ~0.1 mm) and the fitting uncertainty. For the double-ionization and fragmentation of D2O into D+ + O+ + D channel, we performed e−/D+/O+ triple-coincidence momentum measurements. The momentum and consequently kinetic energy of the neutral D fragment are determined by momentum conservation48. The experimental details on H2O are similar to the measurements of D2O. Moreover, to confirm that the high-KER pathways stem from the Rydberg states of water dications rather than the water trications, we performed an additional experiment with energy (E0 = 80 eV) below the triple-ionization threshold.

Strong-field laser experiments

The strong-field ionization experiments were conducted using laser pulses with a central wavelength of 800 nm and a pulse duration of 40 fs (full width at half maximum, FWHM), generated by a Ti:sapphire laser system operating at a 1 kHz repetition rate. The laser beam was focused onto the molecular beam using a mirror with a focal length of 75 mm. D2O molecules were introduced into the interaction region via supersonic expansion, ensuring a well-defined molecular density for the experiment. Ionic fragments produced by the laser pulses were collected in coincidence using the COLTRIMS technique32,33. The apparatus was configured with a TOF length of 7 cm, and a homogeneous electric field of 45 V cm−1 was applied for ion trajectory analysis64,70. This configuration enables complete 4π collection of D+ ions with kinetic energies ≤25.7 eV, covering dissociation processes with KER values up to 54 eV. The three-dimensional momentum vectors of the ionized fragments were reconstructed by combining the TOF information obtained from the spectrometer with position data recorded by a position-sensitive detector (80-mm-diameter). This allowed for the precise determination of the ion velocities and momenta. To investigate the influence of laser polarization, both linearly and CP laser pulses, each with identical peak electric field amplitudes, were employed. The estimated peak intensities for the linear and CP pulses were 3.0 × 1014 W cm−2 and 6.0 × 1014 W cm−2, respectively. In a CP laser field, the recoil momentum of the resulting ions is determined by the laser intensity88. We measured the TOF axial momentum P of He+ ions under circular polarization and derived the peak intensity (I) via the relation I = (P⋅ω)2, yielding I = 6.0 × 1014 W cm−2. For comparison, the equivalent peak intensity under linear polarization was 3.0 × 1014 W cm−2. Both beams have the same peak electric field, and the ionization rate ratio between linear and circular polarization was determined to be 1:2.9. Based on these parameters, the maximum recollision energy of the returning electron was estimated to be approximately 70 eV73.

Electron-capture-mediated molecular dissociation calculations

Our dynamical calculations of molecular fragmentation upon high Rydberg states were carried out with a classical Rydberg electron capture model. In this model, the water dication at high Rydberg states is generally considered as a tricationic core with a weakly bound electron, its fragmentation process is simulated using a direct two-step Coulomb explosion scheme. First, fragmentation is initiated by a three-body Coulomb explosion of D+ + O+ + D+, because the electric force between the ionic core and the distant electron is very weak in Rydberg state molecules. When the three ions have expanded to the size of the Rydberg orbital, the Rydberg electron starts to screen the charges and eventually captures with one of the D+ ions, forming a neutral D atom. Then, this neutral D atom ceases the three-body Coulomb repulsion, and its kinetic energy is no longer increasing due to the Coulomb potential. Whereas the remaining D+ and O+ pair continues to separate, driven by Coulomb potential, which is simulated up to the interfragment distance of 100 Å in our calculations. The kinetic energies of D+ and O+ were determined by summing their kinetic energies at the interfragment distance of 100 Å plus the remaining Coulomb potential (~0.15 eV). In this model, the momenta and consequently the kinetic energies of all three fragments are determined mainly by the initial configurations of the molecule and the radius of the Rydberg orbital. The initial configurations of water molecules were obtained using the equilibrium structure with a Gaussian distribution sampling of the O-D bond length and the ∠DOD angle for about 0.3 Å and 30∘ (FWHM), respectively. The Rydberg orbital radius was obtained from theoretical calculations57,58, in which the mean values for the radius of the 3p (pathway II) and 4d (pathway III) Rydberg orbitals of water are determined to be roughly 4.5 Å and 10.9 Å, respectively. Using this method, we have calculated the momentum distributions of D+ and D fragments and the KER spectra of both pathways II and III. The calculations show very good agreement with the measurements (see Fig. 2 of the main text), which confirms the validity of our model. We also notice that this method applies to the fragmentation of other molecules (e.g., NH3 in Fig. 4) at high Rydberg states.
