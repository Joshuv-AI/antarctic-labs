Statistical Proof as a Window into Human-AI Collaboration: Practical Insights and a Community Agenda
Xiaojing Sun, Huayu Tang, Buxin Su, Mateo Matijasevick, Chong Wu, Fei Xue, Bingxin Zhao

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly woven into expert cognitive work in daily research, yet we know little about how human expertise should adapt when an AI system can execute substantial technical reasoning on its own. Here we use statistical proof development, a demanding and structured form of expert reasoning, as a window into this broader question. Drawing on day-to-day proof problems, we find that current general-purpose LLMs occupy a useful but limited role: they can execute technical components given a precisely formulated problem and targeted guidance, but become unreliable when the problem is open-ended or requires a long reasoning chain with multiple interdependent steps. This execution-strategy gap is rooted in what makes research-level statistical proof distinctive: unlike pure mathematics, where problems arrive pre-formulated and solutions often demand entirely novel techniques, statistical proof requires first modeling a scientific question into a statistical framework with appropriate assumptions, and then identifying and adapting the right strategy from a repertoire of reusable domain-specific tools. Each step involves judgments that require deep expertise in both the statistical literature and the real-world context being modeled. For human-AI collaboration in such cognitive work, current AI assistance does not reduce the need for human expertise; it relocates that expertise to where human decision-making matters most, such as problem formulation and verification of AI-generated results, and may raise the bar for both, since human users must now make rapid decisions alongside AI tools. These findings yield practical suggestions for how statisticians can structure AI-assisted proof workflows in their daily research, and point to a broader community agenda for shared resources, better AI tools, and training the next generation of researchers. Using statistical proof as a window, our study carries implications for how experts structure human-AI collaboration in technical cognitive domains more broadly.

Introduction

Proof is the backbone of statistical research, where theory and real-world scientific problems are deeply intertwined. Through rigorous mathematical argument, statisticians justify new estimators and procedures used in data analysis, clarify the assumptions under which they are valid, and explain how performance depends on model structure, dataset features, and noise
(
43
)
. These arguments are often directly motivated by interdisciplinary applications such as in genomics, economics, and medicine, where the choice of assumptions needed to form a statistical framework and the interpretation of results must remain grounded in the underlying science.
Together, these features make statistical proof development a demanding form of expert cognition: it requires scientific judgment about how to formulate a real-world question as a tractable statistical problem, domain expertise to navigate a broad landscape of proof strategies, and the experience to know which strategy fits a new problem and context, capabilities that can take years to acquire even for experienced researchers.
For graduate students, acquiring this expertise is also challenging: they must learn to formulate real-world questions as precise statistical problems, identify appropriate proof strategies, and execute rigorous arguments, skills that are currently transmitted slowly and unevenly through textbooks, previous literature, and one-on-one guidance from their advisors. These bottlenecks slow both the development of new statistical methodology and the transmission of proof expertise to the next generation of statisticians.

Large language models (LLMs) are now reshaping how scientists conduct research across many disciplines.
Notably, LLM-based AI systems are increasingly capable of mathematical reasoning and proof assistance

.
The statistics community is no exception: researchers are increasingly asking whether AI can assist with proof development, the central intellectual task of statistical research
(

. From a practical standpoint, a key question is whether current general-purpose LLMs, which are now widely accessible to everyday researchers, can provide meaningful assistance with proof tasks in day-to-day statistical research. Progress on this question would have direct implications not only for how statisticians work, but also for how graduate students are trained, how proof expertise is acquired and shared, and how the community allocates its intellectual effort. Yet despite the growing presence of LLMs in statistical research workflows, their boundaries as proof assistants remain poorly understood: which tasks they can meaningfully assist with, and which remain out of reach?

These open questions in statistical proof provide a useful lens for a broader question, now facing many areas of expert work, about human-AI collaboration: as AI systems take on substantial technical reasoning in daily research, how should human expertise adapt? This question reaches well beyond statistics, into other fields where experts are beginning to delegate technical execution to AI while remaining responsible for the direction and outcome. Statistical proof is a demanding and highly structured form of expert cognition, which makes it a clear setting in which to study this question: it allows us to ask not just whether AI can help, but specifically where in the process AI assistance is reliable and where human judgment remains essential. The practical insights that emerge are not a narrow statistical finding; they speak to many expert domain in which technical work is increasingly being reshaped by human-AI collaboration.

Although mathematics is a related domain where AI proof assistance has been increasingly tested, the insights and progress in AI for mathematical reasoning do not transfer automatically to statistical proof. Statistical proof tasks differ from most mathematical proof tasks in at least two important respects that determine when and how AI assistance can be effective. First, statistical problems are inherently closer to real-world applications than most mathematical problems
(
43
)
. Assumptions in statistical proofs are rarely mathematically simple or clean; they must be chosen to be both tractable and interpretable, and consistent with the scientific problems being modeled, a judgment that has no direct analogue in pure mathematics, where problems typically arrive pre-formulated. Second, statistical proofs less commonly demand entirely novel proof approaches; instead, they predominantly build on a repertoire of reusable domain-specific strategies that need to be identified and adapted to each new problem and context. Effective AI assistance for statistical proof thus requires a different set of capabilities, priorities, and resources from those needed for mathematical reasoning

In this paper, we investigate when and how current general-purpose LLMs succeed and fail as proof assistants in day-to-day statistical research.
Through paired case studies drawn from active research problems across several areas of modern statistical research, we identify a consistent pattern: current general-purpose LLMs can assist with substantial components of statistical proof when the problem is precisely stated and supplemented with targeted guidance, but become unreliable when the problem is open-ended, requires a long reasoning chain, or demands simultaneous control of multiple technical components, settings in which the model must identify and adapt an appropriate strategy on its own. This execution-strategy gap is currently a primary determinant of whether AI assistance succeeds or fails on research-level statistical proof tasks. These observations indicate that the demands on human expertise do not shrink as AI takes on more of the execution; rather, they relocate to the points of greatest judgment, such as problem formulation and verification of AI-generated results, and may grow more exacting as experts must make rapid decisions alongside AI tools. These demands may also grow more exacting, as experts must now make rapid judgments alongside AI tools. Based on these observations, we discuss practical lessons for individual statisticians, covering problem formulation, proof decomposition, rapid verification, and the use of targeted guidance, and outline a broader community agenda: building shared repositories of reusable proof strategies, developing agentic AI and proof assistants, and training future statisticians to interact effectively with AI tools. As AI systems take on more of the technical execution across many expert domains, our practical insights from statistical proof provide an early and clear picture of what this shift demands of human expertise.

Study design and key findings

We evaluate AI assistance for statistical proof development through four paired case studies that together cover eight research-level proof problems. Within each pair, one problem illustrates a setting in which current general-purpose LLMs provide useful proof assistance, while the other illustrates a setting in which they become unreliable. Both problems are drawn from the same broader research context, enabling direct comparison and practical insights into when and how these tools can be used most effectively in statistical research.
Specifically, the four pairs cover high-dimensional inference, extreme value theory, transfer learning, and differential privacy, together reflecting the breadth of modern statistical research
(
42
;
38
;
24
;
11
;
28
)
.
Most proof problems arise from ongoing research projects without publicly available proofs. The only exception is the high-dimensional inference pair, where a related paper
42
is publicly available, yet the AI models arrived at correct results through proof strategies distinct from those in that paper, suggesting the model followed a genuinely different proof route rather than reproducing it.
An overview of the study design is shown in Figure
1
.

For each problem, we evaluated three widely used general-purpose LLMs: GPT-5.4 Thinking, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Claude Opus 4.6. The qualitative conclusions were remarkably consistent across all three models, suggesting that the patterns we identify reflect systematic properties of current general-purpose LLMs.
Unless stated otherwise, we focus our illustrations on the results of GPT-5.4 Thinking, with model-specific outputs for all three models reported in the supplementary material.
Our goal is not to rank or compare these models, but to extract practical insights for statisticians working with these tools today, and to outline a broader community agenda for advancing AI-assisted statistical proof development.

Our case studies yield the following practical insights into the capabilities and limitations of current general-purpose LLMs for statistical proof development.

•

Clarity and precision enable success. Current general-purpose LLMs can assist with substantial components of research-level statistical proof when the problem is stated clearly, the goal is explicit, the reasoning scope is kept at an appropriate scale, and the prompt is written in a precise and professional way.

•

Zero-shot prompting is often insufficient. Models frequently struggle when the problem depends on newly introduced context
(
14
)
, requires careful control of modeling assumptions, or demands selection of an appropriate proof strategy. In these cases, models often produce plausible-looking but ultimately incorrect or incomplete derivations.

•

Modest guidance can make a substantial difference. Concise proof-strategy hints or literature-based methodological references often enable the model to produce a correct and rigorous argument where zero-shot prompting had failed. The right guidance does not need to be extensive, a few targeted sentences are often enough to redirect the model toward a valid proof strategy.

•

Execution is more reliable than strategy selection. Current general-purpose LLMs perform considerably better at executing a suitable proof strategy than at identifying one on their own. This asymmetry, between what LLMs can execute and what they can identify, is the primary manifestation of the execution–strategy gap in practice.

•

Long reasoning chains and weak guidance lead to failure. AI reliability decreases substantially when the argument requires a long reasoning chain, simultaneous control of multiple technical components, or decision making under weak guidance. In these settings, models tend to introduce convenient but unjustified intermediate assumptions, shift the target of the proof, or produce notation-heavy derivations that obscure rather than illuminate the main argument.

Together, these observations suggest that current general-purpose LLMs can be meaningfully integrated into statistical proof development within a structured workflow that specifies the problem clearly and professionally, states assumptions explicitly, decomposes the proof into manageable problems, and introduces targeted guidance when necessary. They also point to a broader need in the statistics community for explicit and accessible resources that organize reusable proof strategies by research area, since shared repositories of proof strategies and literature-based templates could improve both human and AI-assisted proof development. For education, these findings suggest that training future statisticians should include not only mathematical theory but also effective interaction with AI tools: learning to formulate statistical problems precisely, specify context and assumptions, evaluate whether an AI-generated proof uses an appropriate strategy, provide corrective guidance when it does not, and distinguish a plausible-looking but inaccurate derivation from a rigorous one.

Beyond these specific lessons, our case studies point to more general insights about the role of human expertise in human-AI collaboration for statistical proof.

•

Defining a good proof task is a growing bottleneck. As AI lowers the technical barrier to executing a proof, the difficulty shifts to what AI currently cannot do: knowing what should be proved in a scientific context, why that problem is important, what assumptions are appropriate, and what result would be scientifically meaningful. This requires a chain of human decisions, from selecting a worthwhile research question, to formulating it as a precise statistical problem, to conveying that precision to the AI model in efficient human-AI conversations. Human researchers who can make these decisions fluently extract more value from AI assistance and direct it toward more meaningful research outputs.

•

Live human verification makes the workflow productive. In proof development, the realistic workflow is iterative, closer to an engaged human-AI conversation than to a one-shot prompt query. At every step, the human user must rapidly diagnose whether the AI-generated proof is valid. This diagnosis is often not a matter of mathematical correctness alone; it is about whether the proof answers the intended question, whether unjustified assumptions have been quietly introduced, and whether the result is sharp and scientifically useful. A good human expert can often tell quickly whether a generated proof is on the right track, has changed the target, or has produced a result that is mathematically valid but scientifically weak. This kind of rapid proof triage is what makes the iterative workflow actually work: an AI model can only be corrected by guidance if a human already knows what went wrong. Consistent with the first point, AI does not reduce the expertise statistical proof demands; it relocates and intensifies it.

The contributions of this paper are therefore fourfold. First, we provide a set of paired research-level case studies that characterize both productive and unreliable scenarios for general-purpose LLMs as proof assistants in statistical research. Second, we identify an execution-strategy gap that helps explain when AI assistance succeeds and fails, and show that this gap reflects a broader pattern: AI does not reduce the human expertise that statistical proof demands, but relocates it to where human decision-making matters most. Third, we extract practical insights for integrating AI tools into daily statistical research workflows. Fourth, we outline a broader community agenda and highlight the opportunities and challenges this shift in human-AI collaboration creates. We view this as an evolving effort and will continue to update our findings as additional proof problems, AI models, and tools become available.

3
When and how AI assistance succeeds

Despite coming from different areas of statistical research, the four successful cases reveal a consistent pattern: AI assistance works best when the proof task is precisely scoped, the modeling assumptions are made explicit, and the reasoning chain is kept at a manageable scale. In three of the four cases, zero-shot prompting, asking the model to solve the problem directly from the problem statement alone, without any additional domain-specific guidance, was insufficient. In those cases, however, relatively modest and targeted guidance, such as a short strategy hint, a key literature reference, or an important clarifying instruction, was enough to redirect the model toward a correct and rigorous argument. These cases illustrate that the barrier to effective AI assistance in statistical proof is often not mathematical difficulty alone, but the availability of the right proof strategy at the right moment.

3.1
Variance calculation in high-dimensional inference

Our first example comes from linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC), a widely used method for estimating trait heritability from genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics
(
6
)
.
The specific task is a technical problem in the high-dimensional asymptotic analysis of LDSC: computing the exact finite-sample variance of the oracle LDSC estimator, where “oracle” means that the linkage disequilibrium (LD) scores are treated as known. This problem is mathematically nontrivial but sufficiently self-contained, making it a natural task of whether AI can carry out a rigorous derivation when the statistical setting is stated clearly.
The prompt and problem setup are summarized in the following boxes, with full derivations provided in the supplementary material.

This example illustrates that zero-shot prompting can be sufficient for research-level statistical proof when the task is precisely scoped and the prompt is written with care.
Although zero-shot prompting ultimately succeeded here, the experience of getting there yields several practical lessons, and shows that human expertise plays a strong role during problem formulation. First, the problem must be stated precisely and the relevant assumptions made explicit. When the setup is underspecified, the model tends to introduce additional assumptions that are mathematically convenient but inconsistent with the intended statistical framework and scientific context.
Second, when the intended modeling framework differs from what dominates the relevant literature, the prompt must do more than state the correct assumption. It must also explicitly rule out the incorrect default.
Specifically, the critical assumption here was that the genetic effects are fixed rather than random. Because the LDSC literature is predominantly developed under a random-effects framework
(
6
;
21
)
, the model defaulted to that framework even when a fixed-effects setup was provided in the prompt, producing a derivation that appeared superficially reasonable but was answering the wrong question. The fixed-effects assumption is essential here because our goal is an exact finite-sample variance, which requires treating the genetic effects as deterministic rather than stochastic. Explicitly instructing the model to treat the genetic effects as fixed, and to avoid the random-effects framework, was necessary to obtain a correct derivation. This is a subtle but serious pitfall: for researchers less familiar with the distinction between fixed and random effects in this context, an AI-generated proof under the wrong framework may appear valid on the surface while being fundamentally misaligned with the intended problem.

3.2
Scaling invariance of angular tail dependence in copulas

Our second example comes from an ongoing project on copulas
(
32
)
and angular tail dependence, which is representative of a broad class of questions in extreme value theory
(
11
)
.
The task is to prove that a limit-based measure of lower-tail behavior
(
22
;
19
;
34
)
, designed to quantify dependence along different angular paths toward the tail, remains invariant with respect to a scaling parameter under suitable regularity conditions.
The main difficulty here is strategic rather than computational. The proof requires choosing an appropriate way to compare limits and identifying the regularity conditions needed to justify each step. This is the kind of judgment that current general-purpose LLMs struggle to exercise without guidance. Yet as we show below, a short and targeted proof-strategy hint is often enough to resolve it.

The problem setup is given in the following box.
The quantity
\kappa_{L}(\delta,\gamma;C)

\delta

\gamma

Establishing invariance with respect to
\gamma

Without any proof-strategy guidance, the model produced a lengthy and seemingly well-structured derivation. However, it adopted a proof technique rarely used for problems of this type and did not fully engage with the newly introduced research context, leading to several non-rigorous steps, most notably, failing to state the regularity conditions necessary to justify key arguments. This zero-shot attempt therefore did not constitute a valid proof. Notably, this failure was not algebraic: the model made no obvious calculation errors, but did not recognize which proof approach was appropriate for this statistical problem. The main limitation was thus strategic rather than computational.

The standard approach for establishing this type of invariance result is to bound the difference between the two quantities of interest by decomposing it using the triangle inequality and then invoking suitable regularity conditions to control each term
(
30
)
, an approach that statisticians working in extreme value theory would recognize as natural and well-suited to this problem. The model did not adopt this strategy, instead pursuing a less natural and ultimately non-rigorous route. This observation motivated us to supplement the original prompt with two concise proof-strategy hints pointing toward the standard approach, as shown in the box below.

\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}

(i) To prove equality of these two limits, consider to show that there exists

\varepsilon

\varepsilon>0

(ii) To use the triangle inequality by adding and subtracting terms inside the absolute value, bounding each term, and stating essential assumptions of the limits (such as type of the convergence) based on our research context and problem.

User:
“Give a full formal proof. Justify each step and state any assumptions you use”

With these two strategy hints, the model produced a correct and rigorous proof, decomposing the difference into three terms and bounding each by
\varepsilon/3
, with appropriate regularity conditions stated explicitly.
The same model that had produced a non-rigorous derivation under zero-shot prompting delivered a correct and complete proof once the right strategy was indicated, demonstrating that the bottleneck was not mathematical ability but strategy identification.

This example illustrates a clear instance of the execution–strategy gap: the bottleneck was not mathematical execution but strategy identification. A key reason the zero-shot attempt failed is that the model did not genuinely understand the research context. It processed the problem statement and background but could not situate the proof task within the broader landscape of extreme value theory, and therefore had no principled basis for selecting an appropriate proof strategy.
This is where human expertise becomes essential. A statistician familiar with the domain can recognize immediately which proof approach is natural for a problem of this type, and supplying that insight in a few targeted sentences can change the entire outcome. Conversely, without such guidance, the model may pursue a plausible-looking but ultimately invalid route, producing a derivation that appears well-structured on the surface but fails to meet the standards of statistical rigor.
This points to a practical workflow for statisticians: rather than asking the model to develop a proof from scratch, it may be more effective to first identify the appropriate proof strategy, drawing on domain expertise and familiarity with the statistical literature, and then provide that strategy as targeted guidance before asking the model to execute.

3.3
Bounding the prediction risk in transfer learning

Our third example comes from an ongoing project on transfer learning under a high-dimensional linear regression framework
(

;

The goal is to derive an asymptotic upper bound for the prediction risk of a transfer learning estimator that leverages information from a source site to improve prediction at a target site, while accounting for cross-site heterogeneity and feature mismatch. Deriving a sharp and informative bound, rather than a valid but loose one, is the central challenge, as an overly conservative bound provides little insight into how the estimator’s performance depends on the key problem parameters.

-DP curve and the full exact

-DP curve, and whether the converse implication holds.

(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP for reporting guarantees, and use

-DP to interpret distinguishability
(
2

4
When and how AI assistance fails

The four failure cases share a complementary pattern to the successful cases: current general-purpose LLMs break down most severely when the proof task is open-ended, the reasoning chain is long, or the problem requires simultaneous control of multiple technical components. In these settings, the execution–strategy gap widens: the model not only struggles to identify an appropriate proof strategy but also fails to sustain a coherent reasoning path long enough to reach a valid conclusion. Together, these cases reveal the current boundaries of general-purpose LLMs as proof assistants in statistical research, and point to the kinds of advances that would be needed to push those boundaries further.
They also sharpen the role of human expertise: as the model’s own derivations grow less reliable, the burden of recognizing where and why an argument has gone wrong falls more heavily on the human collaborator.

4.1

Our first failure case follows directly from the successful case in Section

4.4
Zero-concentrated differential privacy

Section
3.4
showed that the three standard privacy formalisms, Rényi-DP,
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, and
f
-DP, are equivalent under the centered-symmetry assumption when one retains the full exact privacy curve. We now ask a related but harder question: what happens when the full exact curve is replaced by a scalar summary?
Specifically, we consider zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP)
(
7
)
, and a mechanism is said to satisfy
\rho
-zCDP if, for every order
\alpha>1
, its Rényi divergence is bounded by

D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)\leq\rho\alpha

for every pair of neighboring output distributions
P
and
Q
.
Thus, zCDP can be viewed as a scalar summary of the full Rényi-DP curve: instead of looking at the entire function
\alpha\mapsto D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)
, it keeps a single parameter
\rho
that upper bounds the curve by a line.
This scalar summary is convenient, as the composition rule is simple and additive, but potentially lossy, since it replaces the full Rényi-DP curve with a single linear upper bound
\rho\alpha
. The question is: how lossy is it, and under what conditions?
In particular, we ask when zCDP is nearly tight, as it is known to be for Gaussian mechanisms, and when it loses substantial information relative to the full exact Rényi-DP curve, especially under non-Gaussian noise distributions and heterogeneous composition.

Unlike the problem in Section
3.4
, this question is inherently more open-ended and has no pre-determined solution path. There is no standard proof strategy to retrieve, no directly applicable theorem to invoke, and no existing literature that resolves the question. The problem asks the model not to execute a known argument but to navigate an open solution space and identify a promising direction, a fundamentally different and more challenging task.

The problem statement, research context, and formal proof instruction are shown in the boxes below. We asked the model to derive lower bounds for a gap metric that explicitly quantifies the optimality of zCDP. Specifically, the bound should reveal how this optimality varies with the number of compositions, the allocation of privacy parameters, and the underlying noise distribution.

5
Toward AI-assisted statistical proof

The case studies presented in this paper point to an actionable picture of where current general-purpose LLMs stand as proof assistants in statistical research. They are genuinely useful, capable of carrying out substantial and technically demanding derivations when the problem is precisely specified and the proof strategy is clear. But they are also systematically limited by the execution-strategy gap: the inability to identify, select, and adapt appropriate proof strategies on their own when facing a new problem and context. This gap is not merely a performance shortcoming; it reflects something deeper about what statistical proof requires that current general-purpose LLMs, trained broadly across many domains, are not yet equipped to provide. The gap, however, is only half the story. As Section
2
highlighted, the demands on human expertise persist on either side of it in the human-AI collaborative workflow: in formulating a good proof task, and in verifying whether what an AI model returns is valid, on-target, and sharp. At the level of individual research practice, they suggest concrete ways to structure AI-assisted proof workflows so that the model’s strengths are leveraged and human judgment is applied where it matters most. At the level of the broader statistical community, they point to a coordinated agenda: building shared resources that make proof strategies more accessible, developing more capable AI systems that can reason across longer and more complex arguments and support faster verification, and training a new generation of statisticians to work effectively in human-AI collaboration. In this section, we discuss each of these directions.

5.1
Practical lessons for individual statisticians

Our case studies show that the difference between a useful and an unhelpful proof generated by a general-purpose LLM often comes down to how the human-AI interaction is structured. Four practical lessons emerge consistently across the examples.

The first is precision in problem specification. A well-structured prompt makes the target quantity, notation, modeling assumptions, asymptotic regime, and any nonstandard conventions explicit from the outset. When the setup is underspecified, the model tends to fill in the gaps with mathematically convenient but scientifically inappropriate defaults, as illustrated in Section
3.1
, where an underspecified prompt led the model to adopt a random-effects framework inconsistent with the intended fixed-effects setup. Moreover, when the intended formulation departs from the default convention in the relevant literature, stating the correct assumption is often not enough, the prompt should also explicitly rule out the incorrect default. Negative instructions of this kind are an effective way to prevent the model from defaulting to a confident but wrong formulation.

The second lesson is the value of targeted guidance. When zero-shot prompting fails because the model cannot identify or retrieve the right proof strategy, providing concise strategy hints or key literature references can make a substantial difference, often transforming a non-rigorous or uninformative derivation into a correct and sharp one, as illustrated in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
. The guidance does not need to be extensive: a few targeted sentences pointing to the right approach, or two or three literature references indicating the relevant tools, are often sufficient. What matters is that the guidance resolves the strategy selection problem that the model cannot solve on its own.

The third lesson is proof decomposition. When the proof task is long, open-ended, or requires simultaneous control of multiple technical components, asking the model to produce a complete end-to-end proof in a single pass is unlikely to succeed, as illustrated in Sections
4.1
and
4.3
. A more effective approach is to decompose the proof into smaller and more tractable problems and work on each within a structured human-AI workflow. This decomposition strategy shifts the model’s role from end-to-end proof generator to targeted proof assistant, a role it is considerably better equipped to play in human-AI collaboration.

The fourth lesson is that there is no magic prompt and that iterative engagement is needed. Even for the small and tractable problems illustrated in our successful case studies, a single prompt attempt was rarely sufficient on its own; the prompt blocks used to present our case studies, including zero-shot attempts and targeted guidance, are a simplification chosen for clarity of exposition, not a description of how the underlying work actually proceeded. Arriving at each guided result typically took more exploration than the final presentation shows. For more complex and open-ended problems, the need for iteration is even greater. The realistic workflow is therefore closer to an engaged research conversation than to a single prompt query: specify the question, ask the model to propose a proof route, inspect that route, verify the AI-generated output at each step, and repair what fails. Treating prompt design as a one-time act risks the failure mode our case studies observed: a plausible-looking derivation accepted without the iterative scrutiny that catches when it has gone wrong.

In summary, these four lessons suggest a practical workflow for AI-assisted statistical proof: specify the problem precisely, provide targeted guidance when the strategy is not obvious, decompose complex arguments into manageable problems, and treat the whole process as iterative rather than a single exchange. This workflow does not replace human statistical expertise. It redefines the human role, redirecting expertise away from routine execution and toward the judgments and decision-making that current AI cannot reliably make on its own.

5.2
Building shared resources for proof strategies

The practical lessons in Section
5.1
all depend, to varying degrees, on the availability of the right proof strategy at the right moment. In the copula example of Section
3.2
, two short strategy hints transformed a failed derivation into a correct proof. In the transfer learning example of Section
3.3
, two literature references were enough to shift the model from a loose and uninformative bound to a sharp and meaningful one. In these cases, the critical resource was not computational power or mathematical ability, it was access to the right domain-specific proof strategy, supplied by a human expert who knew where to look.

This points to a broader opportunity for the statistics community. Statistical proof development has two distinctive components that set it apart from most mathematical proof: translating a real-world scientific question into a precise statistical framework with appropriate assumptions, and then identifying and adapting the right strategy from a repertoire of reusable domain-specific tools. Both components currently rely on knowledge that is rarely documented in a retrievable form. The modeling component covers decisions about which assumptions are scientifically appropriate, which quantities are meaningful, and which mathematical idealizations are justifiable. This knowledge is largely transmitted through domain-specific training and research experience, with little explicit guidance available for researchers working at the boundaries of their expertise. The strategy component covers knowing which proof technique applies to a given problem and how to adapt it to a new context. This knowledge is similarly scattered across textbooks, papers, and the tacit knowledge of experienced researchers, rarely organized in a way that makes it easy to find and reuse. Building shared, organized repositories that address both components could make this knowledge substantially more accessible across the community. Such repositories would document not only reusable proof strategies but also the modeling principles and assumption choices that characterize different research areas, indexed by proof goal, scientific context, and typical assumptions, with representative references for each entry.

Such repositories would serve multiple purposes simultaneously. For individual researchers, they would reduce the time spent searching for applicable proof techniques, particularly for researchers working at the boundaries of their expertise. For graduate students, they would provide a structured entry point into the proof strategies of a new field, complementing what is currently transmitted slowly and unevenly through textbooks and one-on-one advising. For AI systems, they could serve as a retrieval resource that addresses the strategy-selection bottleneck directly, providing the targeted guidance that our case studies show is often sufficient to unlock reliable AI performance. Recent community efforts such as

and StatProver

provide encouraging early examples of what such resources might look like, and point toward a more systematic and community-wide effort.

Building and maintaining such repositories will require collective effort from the statistics community, contributions from researchers across subfields, curation to ensure quality and consistency, and regular updates as new proof techniques emerge. We view this as an important and tractable community agenda item, one that would benefit both human and AI-assisted statistical proof development simultaneously.

5.3
Agentic AI systems and formal verification

The workflow insights and shared repositories described in Sections
5.1
and
5.2
can substantially improve the reliability of current general-purpose LLMs for well-scoped statistical proof tasks.
But our failure cases, such as Sections
4.1
and
4.4
, suggest that some limitations run deeper than what better prompting or richer resources can address. When the reasoning chain is long or when the problem is genuinely open-ended, current general-purpose LLMs struggle in ways that targeted guidance alone cannot resolve. These cases point to the need for more capable end-to-end AI architectures, such as agentic AI systems that can plan, maintain memory across steps, verify intermediate results, revise their own arguments, and draw on external tools and literature in a coordinated way.

Recent progress in agentic AI for mathematics provides an encouraging reference point. Systems such as Aletheia
(
17
)
and the AI co-mathematician
(
44
)
organize mathematical reasoning through iterative generation-verification-revision workflows, in which a generator proposes a candidate argument, a verifier checks for errors and logical gaps, and a reviser improves the argument based on the feedback. These systems also use web search and literature navigation to reduce fabricated citations and ground arguments in existing results. Applied to research-level mathematical problems, they have shown substantially stronger performance than single-pass LLM prompting on complex reasoning tasks
(
41
)
.
In addition, the use of formal proof assistants such as Lean
2
2

2

https://lean-lang.org/
can provide machine-checkable verification of mathematical arguments. Recent work has shown that LLMs can be trained to generate Lean-verifiable proofs for Olympiad-level problems, suggesting that formal verification could eventually serve as a rigorous and automated checking mechanism within agentic statistical proof workflows. This is one of the supports our case studies suggest are most needed: not a replacement for the rapid diagnostic judgment described in Section
2
, but a complementary check that can flag what even a careful human reviewer might miss in a long or notation-heavy derivation.
However, many of these advanced agentic AI systems and LLM-Lean integrations are not publicly available; they are developed and maintained by large research organizations with access to substantial computational resources, and are not yet accessible to everyday statistical researchers in the way that general-purpose LLMs are. Closing this accessibility gap is itself an important community challenge, and points to the value of open and community-driven development of agentic AI proof tools tailored to the needs of statistical research.

Notably, adapting these architectures to statistical proof development would require going beyond what current mathematical agentic AI systems provide. Statistical proofs are shaped by modeling assumptions tied to scientific application domains and often require careful domain-specific judgment about what constitutes a sharp and meaningful result.
A statistical proof AI system would therefore need to integrate several capabilities that current systems lack: a mechanism for navigating and applying domain-specific modeling conventions, access to organized repositories of statistical proof strategies of the kind described in Section
5.2
, tools for verifying not just mathematical correctness but also the scientific meaningfulness and sharpness of conclusions, and an exposition-revision stage that simplifies notation, removes inessential definitions, and ensures that the final argument is readable and verifiable by a human statistician.
Formal proof assistants such as Lean could play a valuable role in this architecture, providing a verification layer that catches logical gaps and unjustified steps of the kind observed in our failure cases. For higher-level assessment, such as whether a proof is asking and answering the right scientific question, human diagnostic judgment is unlikely to be easily replaced.

5.4
Training future statisticians for AI-assisted research

The findings of this study have direct implications for how the next generation of statisticians should be trained. Our case studies show that effective use of general-purpose LLMs in statistical proof development is not simply a matter of knowing how to operate the AI tools: it requires a set of intellectual skills that are distinct from, but deeply complementary to, the mathematical skills that current graduate training already emphasizes. The main educational goal is to train students to direct, constrain, and critically evaluate AI assistance, treating current general-purpose LLMs as proof assistants that are often useful for precisely formulated problems but unreliable for unsupervised end-to-end derivations. We suggest emphasizing five complementary skills that current curricula do not systematically address.

The first is precise problem formulation with AI interaction. Students should learn to identify important questions from a scientific context, formulate statistical problems with appropriate assumptions, and translate a research-level proof task into a prompt that conveys the problem precisely and unambiguously to the AI model. This skill is closely related to the broader statistical skill of problem formulation, but applied in a new context where the consequences of underspecification are immediate and sometimes difficult to detect. For example, the LDSC example in Section
3.1
illustrates both the importance of this skill and the subtle pitfalls that arise when it is not exercised carefully.
As Section
2
noted, this is also a skill whose importance will likely increase: as AI handles more of the execution, what distinguishes researchers is increasingly their ability to formulate the right question in the first place. From a technical perspective, learning structured prompting techniques can also help students formulate research problems more precisely with AI.
From a technical perspective, actively keeping up with fast-evolving structured prompting techniques
3
3

3

https://github.com/mattpocock/skills
can also sharpen students’ ability to formulate research problems with AI.
For example, rather than relying solely on the student to write a complete and precise prompt, a “grill me” routine asks the AI model itself to interrogate the student about the theorem statement, the assumptions, the target quantity, and the intended proof strategy, until the problem is well specified before any derivation begins.

The second is proof decomposition. Students should learn to identify which components of a proof are well-scoped enough for reliable AI assistance and which require human judgment, and to decompose complex arguments into tractable tasks accordingly. This skill is not only useful for AI-assisted proof development, but it is also a valuable approach in its own right, encouraging students to think carefully about the logical structure of a proof before attempting to execute it.

The third is critical evaluation of AI-generated proofs. Students should learn to read AI-generated derivations with the same critical eye they would apply to a proof from a textbook or a paper, checking for hidden assumptions, unjustified appeals to standard results, changes in the estimand, and notation that obscures rather than clarifies the argument. Our failure cases show that general-purpose LLMs can produce derivations that appear complete and rigorous on the surface but contain errors that are easy to miss and hard to detect without domain expertise. Actively learning structured prompting techniques can support this skill in practice. For example, a “zoom out” routine can ask the model to explain the proof’s overall architecture, the role of each assumption, and where the main difficulty lies, before technical details are generated, which helps a student check the shape of an argument before getting lost in its details. An “assumption audit” routine can ask the model to list every assumption used in the proof and classify each one as stated in the original problem, derived during the argument, newly introduced, or unnecessary, which makes hidden or unjustified assumptions more visible and checkable. Detecting these errors requires both mathematical maturity and familiarity with the specific failure modes that current general-purpose LLMs tend to exhibit.

The fourth is strategic use of guidance. Students should learn to recognize when a zero-shot prompt is unlikely to succeed. This is often the case when the problem requires strategy selection, careful control of modeling assumptions, or a long reasoning chain. In such situations, students should learn to provide targeted guidance that resolves the current bottleneck efficiently. This includes knowing how to identify relevant literature references, how to formulate concise proof-strategy hints, and how to calibrate the level of guidance needed for a given task. The copula and transfer learning examples in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
illustrate how modest but well-targeted guidance can transform an unreliable AI output into a useful and rigorous one.

The fifth is rapid diagnostic judgment. As Section
5.1
discussed, what separates a productive AI-assisted session from an unproductive one is often not whether an error eventually gets caught, but how quickly it is caught and corrected within the interactive workflow. A student who can tell within moments that a derivation has changed its target, smuggled in an unjustified assumption, or produced a result that is technically valid but scientifically weak can redirect the model immediately and keep a multi-round conversation moving toward a correct proof. A student who lacks this fluency may accept a flawed intermediate step, build further reasoning on top of it, and only discover the error much later, at far greater cost. This is, at its core, a form of human decision making rather than purely a technical skill: the student must decide whether to trust an output, redirect it, or dig deeper before proceeding, decisions that AI cannot make on the student’s behalf.

Put together, these five skills outline what a well-structured AI-assisted proof session might look like for future statisticians.
It begins with formulating the statistical question, sharpened where useful by a grill-me interrogation that forces precision before any derivation starts. The student then selects or proposes a proof strategy, supplying targeted guidance where zero-shot prompting is unlikely to succeed, and decomposing the argument into lemma-level problems where the reasoning chain is long or the components are numerous. As the model generates each piece of the proof, a zoom-out check confirms that the overall architecture is sound before the details are scrutinized, and an assumption audit surfaces anything stated, derived, added, or left unjustified along the way. Throughout, rapid diagnostic judgment operates continuously, catching a wrong target or a quietly weakened result early enough to redirect the conversation. The exposition is then refined once the argument itself is sound, simplifying notation and removing inessential definitions so that the final proof is as readable to a human reader as it is correct. None of these stages is mechanical on its own; each depends on the kind of judgment that AI assistance relocates to the human rather than removes.

These skills can be integrated into graduate training through dedicated courses on AI-assisted statistical proof. Such courses would be structured around hands-on exercises in which students practice the five skills described above: formulating precise proof tasks, decomposing complex arguments, evaluating AI-generated outputs critically, providing targeted guidance, and developing rapid diagnostic judgment. The goal is not to replace traditional proof training but to complement it, preparing future statisticians to work effectively at the interface of statistical expertise and AI assistance that will increasingly characterize statistical research in the years ahead. Since courses of this kind are new to most statistics departments, developing them will itself benefit from community-wide collaboration: sharing course materials, exercises, and assessment frameworks across departments, and revisiting them regularly as the capabilities of AI tools continue to evolve rapidly.

6
Limitations and future work

The practical insights of this paper should be interpreted in light of several limitations.
The first limitation is scope. Our findings are based on eight proof problems drawn from four areas of statistical research. While these problems were chosen to reflect the range of tasks that arise in day-to-day statistical research, and while the qualitative conclusions were consistent across three different models, eight problems cannot capture the full diversity of proof tasks in statistics. The execution-strategy gap we identify may manifest differently, or may be less pronounced, in other fields, proof types, or levels of technical difficulty. Future work can extend this investigation to a broader range of problems, including problems from areas such as Bayesian statistics, causal inference, and survival analysis, where proof strategies and modeling conventions differ substantially from those examined here.

A second limitation is the rapid pace of AI development. The findings reported here reflect the capabilities of GPT-5.4 Thinking, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Claude Opus 4.6 at the time of writing. Model capabilities are changing rapidly, and some of the limitations we observe may be partially addressed by future models. We view this study as a snapshot of the current state rather than a permanent characterization, and we plan to update our findings as new models and tools become available. This applies with particular force to our claim that the importance of problem formulation and rapid diagnostic judgment will grow as execution improves: this is a prediction about a dynamic, not a direct empirical finding from our case studies, and it deserves the same scrutiny and updating as any other forecast about a fast-moving technology.
The paired case study design introduced here, with matched success and failure cases drawn from the same research context, provides a reusable framework for ongoing evaluation as the landscape evolves.

Another limitation is that we focus exclusively on general-purpose LLMs accessible to everyday researchers. Domain-specific fine-tuned models and agentic AI architectures may perform substantially better on statistical proof tasks. As discussed in Section
5.3
, some early evidence from mathematical agentic AI suggests meaningful improvements on complex reasoning tasks. Evaluating these more capable but currently less accessible systems on statistical proof tasks is an important direction for future work, particularly after these systems become more publicly available.

Despite these limitations, we believe the findings reported here provide a useful and actionable starting point for the statistics community. The execution-strategy gap is a real and systematic phenomenon that shapes the reliability of current general-purpose LLMs on statistical proof tasks, and the practical lessons and community agenda outlined in this paper provide concrete directions for addressing it, both through better use of existing AI tools and through the development of more capable ones. More broadly, statistical proof serves as a window into what human-AI collaboration in expert cognitive work actually demands: structured, verifiable, and tied to real-world scientific questions, it makes visible patterns that are likely to recur across domains where experts delegate technical execution to AI while remaining responsible for its direction and outcome.
