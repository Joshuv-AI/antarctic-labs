Inhaled corticosteroids and risk of cardiovascular events in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a nationwide cohort study in Denmark
Authors: Mia Ritzau Ishøj Nielsen, Emma Borremose Wedervang, Anna Kubel Vognsen, Karen Hougaard Frost, Alexander Ryder Jordan, Sigrid Anna Aalberg Vikjord, Alexander G Mathioudakis, Jens-Ulrik Stæhr Jensen, Pradeesh Sivapalan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale Cardiovascular disease is a major comorbidity in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Previous studies evaluating the association between inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and cardiovascular events (CVEs) in COPD have yielded conflicting results and a potential dose–response relationship remains unclear.ObjectivesTo determine the association between ICS exposure and risk of CVE in COPD patients.MethodsIn this nationwide, retrospective register-based cohort study, we included patients with a specialist-verified COPD diagnosis between January 2008 and January 2022. ICS exposure was quantified as mean daily budesonide-equivalent dose redeemed in the year prior to index and categorised as none, low (>0 µg/day and <400 µg/day), moderate (400–800 µg/day) or high (>800 µg/day). We assessed CVE risk using adjusted cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models, with additional sensitivity analyses including a complete-case analysis, time-varying Cox regression, Fine-Gray competing risk model, and a negative control outcome analysis.ResultsAmong 82 327 patients, 8948 (10.9 %) experienced a CVE during a 1-year follow-up. In the primary analysis, low- and moderate-dose ICS was associated with a significantly reduced CVE rate compared with no ICS use. A modest decrease in CVE rate was observed for moderate-dose ICS largely across all sensitivity analyses.ConclusionICS use in COPD was not associated with an increased risk of CVE at any dose level. A modest association with reduction in CVE risk was confined to moderate-dose ICS. These findings support the cardiovascular safety of ICS and reinforce that ICS prescribing should be guided by exacerbation risk and symptom burden rather than cardiovascular concern.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) affects more than 400 million individuals worldwide and is currently the third leading cause of death globally.1 COPD is characterised by airway obstruction and emphysema, leading to progressive air trapping, shortness of breath and exertional dyspnoea.2 In addition, COPD is a systemic inflammatory disease with well-recognised cardiovascular implications, where chronic inflammation acts as a shared pathological driver of both obstructive lung disease and adverse cardiovascular events (CVEs),3 thus making cardiovascular disease one of the most prevalent and clinically significant comorbidities in this population.

Acute exacerbations of COPD are known to trigger systemic inflammatory spillover, substantially increasing the risk of CVE in the weeks following an acute episode.4 5 This inflammatory cascade, characterised by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, a prothrombotic state and endothelial dysfunction supports the hypothesis that reducing exacerbation frequency may yield secondary cardiovascular benefit.6 The potential protective cardiovascular effects of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) are therefore hypothesised to operate primarily through exacerbation prevention and attenuation of this inflammatory cascade.7

ICS have long been a fundamental component of COPD management, primarily by reducing exacerbation frequency in selected patients, yet their long-term use carries a well-documented burden of adverse effects including pneumonia, oral candidiasis, diabetes mellitus, osteoporosis and fractures.8–10 In light of this accumulating evidence, recent guidelines recommend restricting ICS use in COPD to selected patients likely to benefit the most from treatment, such as those with asthma features or frequent exacerbations or patients with high blood eosinophils.11 Beyond exacerbation prevention, ICS may exert more direct cardiovascular effects through systemic immunosuppressive and metabolic mechanisms that influence vascular inflammation and endothelial function.4 Whether these pathways confer a protective, neutral or harmful effect on cardiovascular risk in patients with COPD therefore remains uncertain. While some studies have suggested a potential protective association between ICS use and CVE in patients with COPD,12–16 others have reported no significant association.17–21 Notably, studies conducted in asthma populations have observed an increased risk of CVE associated with high-dose ICS use22 and although asthma and COPD differ fundamentally in their pathophysiology, these findings raise the possibility that dose-dependent cardiovascular risk may also exist in COPD and warrant further investigation. Any cardiovascular effect of ICS is likely to depend on dose and be modified by patient characteristics and inflammatory phenotype,23 24 yet few studies have examined this association.

The aim of this study is to address this knowledge gap by investigating a dose-dependent association between ICS use and CVE risk in a large nationwide COPD cohort. We further explore predefined subgroups to identify potential effect modification, thereby providing a foundation for more personalised ICS therapy.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We conducted a nationwide, register-based retrospective cohort study. We included all patients 40 years or older with a specialist-verified diagnosis of COPD. Patients were included on their first inpatient or outpatient contact in relation to their COPD (defined as the index date) between 1 January 2008 and 1 January 2022 and were followed until 1 year after the index date, with complete follow-up for all patients. The 1-year follow-up period was prespecified, as any potential cardiovascular effect of ICS was hypothesised to occur primarily during the early period after treatment initiation, when prevention of COPD exacerbations may reduce the short-term increase in cardiovascular risk associated with exacerbations. Patients with a history of registered CVE were excluded from the population.

Data sources

Data were obtained by linking Danish national health registries using each patient’s unique personal identification number, enabling complete follow-up. The following registers were used: The Danish Register of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (DrCOPD) includes all patients aged above 30 years with a specialist-verified diagnosis of COPD,25 and served as the primary source for patient identification. The Danish Civil Registration System provides individual-level data on all Danish residents, including civil registration number, sex, date of birth and vital status.26 The Danish National Patient Register contains records of all hospital admissions since 1977 and outpatient visits since 1995,27 and was used to identify CVE, comorbidities and exacerbations. The Danish National Database of Reimbursed Prescriptions captures all reimbursed prescriptions dispensed at hospital and community pharmacies nationwide since 1995,27 and was used to classify ICS exposure and to identify concomitant pharmacological treatments. The Danish Register of Causes of Death (1970–2022) provided data on date and cause of death,28 and was used to identify deaths due to CVE. Finally, the Danish National Laboratory Database contains nationwide laboratory data linked at the individual level,29 and was used to retrieve biochemical measurements relevant to cardiovascular risk.

Exposure and outcome

Prescriptions of ICS dispensed within the year preceding the index date were identified (online supplemental table E1 for ATC codes). All prescriptions were converted into budesonide-equivalent doses using established conversion factors30 (online supplemental table E2). The mean daily dose during the year prior to the index date was calculated and categorised into four groups: (1) no ICS use, (2) low-dose ICS (>0 µg/day and <400 µg/day), (3) moderate-dose ICS (400–800 µg/day) and (4) high-dose ICS (>800 µg/day).

The primary outcome was the first occurrence of a CVE, including CVE-related death. CVEs were defined using ICD-10 diagnostic codes, encompassing ischaemic heart disease (I20–I25), cardiac arrhythmias (I48), heart failure and cardiomyopathy (I50, I110, I130, I132, I420, I426–I429), cerebrovascular disease and transient ischaemic attack (I60–I69, G45) and coronary revascularisation procedures identified by surgical codes (online supplemental table E3). CVE-related death was defined as death attributed to ischaemic heart disease or cerebrovascular disease. Follow-up continued until the first occurrence of a CVE, death, end-of-study (31 December 2022) or 1 year after the index date, whichever came first.

Missing data

If baseline data were missing at the first outpatient visit, the earliest available subsequent outpatient measurement was used. Implausible values were excluded (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁) <5% or >165% predicted; body mass index (BMI) <6 or >75 kg/m²). Remaining missing data were handled using multiple imputation in the primary analysis.

Statistical analysis

The association between ICS dose and CVE risk was estimated using cause-specific Cox proportional hazards regression, with death treated as a competing risk and ICS exposure treated as a time-fixed variable. The model was adjusted for the following prespecified confounders: sex, age, index year, BMI and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) (myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease and cerebrovascular disease was removed from the CCI since it was part of the primary outcome), FEV1%, number of hospital-admitted exacerbations, smoking status, oral corticosteroid (OCS) use (converted to prednisolone-equivalent doses using previously published conversion rates31) (online supplemental table E4) and use of long-acting beta-2 agonists and long-acting muscarinic antagonists in the year prior to the index date. Variable categorisation is detailed in online supplemental table E5. Cumulative incidence of CVE and death was estimated across ICS dose groups using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model to account for the competing risk of death. We tested the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals. Subgroup analyses were performed on three predefined groups: age ≥65 (n=54 466) or <65 years (n=27 861), FEV1<30% (n=6445) versus ≥30% (n=36 622) and blood eosinophil count (BEC) subgroup (n=39 583) patients with occasionally high (≥0.3×109/L, n=17 676) or consistently low (<0.15×109/L, n=9115) BEC.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess robustness, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, a complete-case analysis was performed to evaluate the potential impact of the multiple imputation approach. Second, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to account for confounding by indication. The IPTW balance was assessed using standard mean differences (SMDs), targeting SMD values <0.1. Third, to account for potential changes in ICS exposure over time, a time-dependent Cox regression model was applied with exposure status updated at 90-day intervals from 90 days prior to the index date through 1 year of follow-up. Fourth, a negative control outcome analysis using inguinal hernia (ICD-10: K40) as the outcome was performed to assess the presence of unmeasured confounding. Missing variables were handled using multiple imputation. All analyses were performed in R (V.4.4.3) using packages: mice, ggplot2, survival and twang. Results are presented as HRs with 95% CIs and statistical significance was defined as a p value <0.05. All HRs are adjusted and cause specific unless otherwise specified.

Use of artificial intelligence

ChatGPT (V.4.5) and Claude (V.4.6) were used to rephrase and improve the language. Additionally, both were used for the development and refinement of R code used for statistical analyses.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Sensitivity analysis

Moderate-dose ICS stayed significant across several sensitivity analyses, including complete-case analysis and Fine-Gray model. However, after the IPTW adjustment, none of the ICS doses reached statistical significance. In the negative control outcome analysis, none of the ICS dose groups reached statistical significance, supporting the robustness of the primary findings (online supplemental table E9).

In time-dependent analyses, low-dose ICS was associated with an increased rate of CVE (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.37, p=0.0285) and moderate-dose ICS were associated with a decreased rate of CVE (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.79 to 0.97, p=0.0125) whereas high-dose ICS still remained insignificant (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.95 to 1.13, p=0.471) (online supplemental table E10).

Subgroup analysis

We found a significant effect modification by age and disease severity. Moderate-dose ICS was associated with a reduced rate of CVE in patients aged <65 years (n=27 861) but not in those aged ≥65 years (n=54 466). Similarly, an association was observed in patients with FEV1<30% (n=6445) but not in those with FEV1≥30% (n=36 622). Stratification by BEC, including patients with occasionally high (n=17 676) and consistently low (n=9115) eosinophil counts, yielded no significant associations. Full results of all subgroup analyses are presented in online supplemental table E11.

Discussion

This nationwide study found no evidence of an increased cardiovascular risk associated with ICS use at any dose level. In the primary analysis, low- and moderate-dose ICS were associated with modest reductions in CVE rates, although the protective association was most consistent and robust for moderate-dose ICS.

Across sensitivity analyses, the protective association was largely confined to moderate-dose ICS. This remained significant in complete-case and Fine-Gray analyses and was attenuated, but retained a similar effect direction, in the IPTW models.

In contrast, results for low-dose ICS were less consistent. Although the primary analysis suggested a modest reduction in CVE risk, the time-dependent Cox model showed an increased hazard. We consider this discrepancy most likely to reflect residual confounding by indication, whereby patients initiate ICS following a COPD exacerbation and consequently enter the low-dose exposure category. Because COPD exacerbations themselves are associated with a transient increase in cardiovascular risk, the elevated hazard observed in the time-dependent analysis is more plausibly explained by worsening disease severity than by a harmful effect of low-dose ICS. Importantly, no sensitivity analysis provided consistent evidence that ICS use increased cardiovascular risk. Furthermore, the complete-case analysis yielded effect estimates comparable to those obtained after multiple imputation for moderate-dose ICS, suggesting that missing baseline covariates did not influence the findings. Likewise, the absence of an association in the negative control analysis argues against substantial unmeasured confounding.

Our findings align with the majority of previous studies reporting no increased cardiovascular risk associated with ICS use in COPD,18 21 32 while also supporting prior suggestions of a modest protective effect.12 13 15 16 However, an important distinction from earlier studies is the observed time dependency of the association. In our data, violation of the proportional hazards assumption was confined to the early follow-up period (approximately 90–110 days), suggesting that the effect of ICS on CVE risk may be transient. This is further supported by the exploratory 3-year analysis, which showed neutral associations across all ICS dose groups (online supplemental table E8), suggesting that any potential cardiovascular effect of ICS may be limited to an early treatment period and diluted over longer follow-up. This may partly explain the neutral findings of previous studies with longer follow-up periods.17 19 Another key contribution of this study is the dose-stratified exposure assessment. While most previous studies have treated ICS as a binary exposure, our findings suggest a potential dose–response pattern, with a signal confined to moderate-dose ICS and no benefit observed at higher doses. This could reflect a balance between anti-inflammatory effects and systemic adverse effects at increasing doses, although the absence of a clear gradient limits causal interpretation.

Several mechanisms may explain the observed findings. ICS may reduce systemic inflammation indirectly through prevention of exacerbations, which are known triggers of acute CVEs. Additionally, ICS may exert more direct effects on vascular inflammation and endothelial function.6 However, the lack of a consistent association across all analyses, particularly the attenuation in IPTW models and conflicting findings for low-dose ICS in time-dependent analyses, underscores that these mechanisms remain speculative.

This study has several important strengths. The use of nationwide registries with complete follow-up enabled inclusion of a large, unselected COPD population with detailed clinical characterisation. COPD diagnoses were specialist-verified through the DrCOPD registry, reducing misclassification. Furthermore, the consistency of findings across multiple sensitivity analyses and the use of a negative control outcome strengthens the credibility of the results.

Several limitations should be considered. As an observational study, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Patients receiving ICS had more severe disease and higher comorbidity burden at baseline, which may influence both treatment allocation and cardiovascular risk. Although extensive adjustment and IPTW were applied, some degree of confounding by indication is likely to persist. Exposure was defined based on redeemed prescriptions and may not fully reflect actual adherence. However, the use of prescription registry data eliminates the risk of recall bias from patient-reported drug use. Additionally, adjustment for exacerbations may have introduced overadjustment bias if exacerbations act as intermediates in the causal pathway between ICS use and CVE.

In conclusion, ICS use in COPD was not associated with an increased risk of CVEs at any dose level, supporting its cardiovascular safety within current guideline recommendations. A modest reduction in CVE risk was observed for moderate-dose ICS, particularly in selected subgroups, although this finding was not consistent across all analytical approaches. These results reinforce that ICS therapy should remain guided by established indications, such as exacerbation risk and symptom burden, rather than anticipated cardiovascular benefits.
