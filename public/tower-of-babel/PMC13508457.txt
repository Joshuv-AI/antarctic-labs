Glutamate change in the anterior cingulate cortex after starting clozapine: evaluating 1H-MRS pilot data
Authors: Maxwell Seward, Esther Puiras, Candice E Crocker, Philip G Tibbo, Temi Toba Oluboka, Derek J Fisher, Kara Dempster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground30% of individuals with schizophrenia do not respond to first line dopamine-blocking antipsychotic medications and meet criteria for treatment resistant schizophrenia (TRS). Elevated glutamate levels in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) have been found to be associated with poor response to dopamine-blocking antipsychotics in early phase psychosis (EPP). However, the exact mechanisms through which clozapine is effective for TRS remain largely unclear and require further investigation.ObjectiveTo our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate glutamatergic changes after 6 months of clozapine treatment in patients with EPP. Background

Schizophrenia is a severe and persistent mental illness that presents heterogeneously in terms of symptoms, course, treatments and outcome [1]. It is hypothesized that excess dopamine transmission is involved in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia [2], and first line antipsychotic medications work primarily by inhibiting dopamine neurotransmission [3]. Unfortunately, nearly 30% of individuals will meet criteria for treatment resistant schizophrenia (TRS) due to lack of response to these medications [4]. Current criteria for TRS requires two failed trials of first line antipsychotics at adequate doses, for a period of six weeks or greater [4, 5]. Most individuals with TRS however are poor responders to first line treatments from their first episode of psychosis (FEP) [6, 7], suggesting that biomarkers of TRS may be characterized from the time of disease onset. In defining TRS by eventual treatment non-response, prolonged suffering and disability for affected individuals is caused by a treatment delay for clozapine use. Thus, there is an imperative need to increase understanding of the neurobiology which underlies TRS, in order to provide evidence-based treatment as early as possible in the illness course.

TRS may be characterized by neurotransmitter abnormalities that differ from treatment responders and in particular these signaling differences may not be related to dopamine [1, 8], rendering dopamine blocking medicines ineffective. Recent work has found that amphetamine induced dopamine release at FEP predicts response to dopamine blocking antipsychotic medications, such that higher dopamine release at baseline was associated with better treatment response [9]. Relatedly, it has been shown that treatment responders have significantly greater striatal dopamine synthesis than both healthy control subjects and TRS subjects, while TRS subjects have elevated glutamate in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), compared to HC and treatment responding subjects [10]. This aligns with suggestions that there may be two subtypes of schizophrenia based on this distinct neurobiology; a dopaminergic subtype and a glutamatergic subtype [10], and that the glutamatergic subtype is associated with treatment resistance [8]. Glutamate is a prominent excitatory neurotransmitter that has also been implicated in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia [8, 10]. Elevated glutamate concentrations are consistent with the n-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) glutamate receptor hypofunction model of schizophrenia, which posits that hypofunction of NMDA receptors on GABAergic interneurons leads to disinhibition of pyramidal neurons and increased cortical glutamate [11]. Increases in glutamate signalling are especially pronounced in frontal cortical areas [12] and associated with increases in positive and negative symptoms [12, 13].

The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) is a medial frontal brain region that is part of the salience network [14], a brain network responsible for switching between focus on self and task relevant, directed attention on external stimuli [15]. The salience network is hypothesized to be dysregulated in schizophrenia [15], thus an aberrant salience hypothesis of schizophrenia has been proposed [16]. In this model, dysregulation of the salience network leads to aberrant assignment of salience to elements of an individual’s perception [16], resulting in psychotic symptoms. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) acquired glutamate concentration in the ACC has been associated with dysconnection in core nodes of the salience network at the first episode of psychosis, directly linking glutamate function and cortical disinhibition in schizophrenia [17].

Prior research has also suggested an association between treatment resistance and elevated glutamate in the ACC in early phase psychosis (EPP) where elevated glutamate concentrations in the ACC were seen in those who failed to meet criteria for remission after at least one course of antipsychotic treatment [18]. Additional research has suggested that elevations in baseline ACC glutamate in antipsychotic naive EPP patients significantly predicted treatment nonresponse at six [19, 20], and twelve weeks [21] after commencing an antipsychotic. Together, these MRS findings support the involvement of a glutamatergic basis for TRS, and the ACC as a key region of interest.

Current consensus guidelines recommend clozapine for TRS [22]. Clozapine has a distinct pharmacological profile [23] and a recent network meta-analysis has reinforced clozapine as the most effective treatment for those who do not respond to first line antipsychotics [24]. Clozapine has been shown to improve quality of life and functioning in those with TRS [25] and is associated with other positive outcomes such as reduced risk of suicide [26], and significantly lower all cause mortality [27]. Despite clozapine’s superior efficacy, the therapeutic mechanisms by which clozapine acts on the brain are poorly understood. Growing evidence implicates the involvement of the NMDA receptor and glutamate system as mechanisms by which clozapine produces a therapeutic effect [28, 29] as clozapine and its major active metabolite N-desmethylclozapine (NMDC) can both activate NMDA glutamate receptors [30].

Despite the evidence supporting clozapine’s potential impact on the NMDA receptor and resultant glutamate neurotransmission, only one study to date has investigated changes in glutamate levels after switching to clozapine using MRS. McQueen and colleagues [31] found no significant change in ACC glutamate after only 12 weeks on clozapine. The study sample consisted of mostly chronic patients with treatment resistant schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder (mean duration of illness = 15 years, mean age = 39).

The present study

Given that a body of evidence implicates higher levels of glutamate in the ACC with TRS, and clozapine is the gold standard of treatment for TRS, further exploration of the impact of clozapine on glutamate levels is warranted. Pre-clinical evidence supports clozapine acting on the glutamate system via the NMDA receptor system, specifically in the mPFC (of which the ACC is a sub-region), however, prior 1 H-MRS investigations have failed to reveal an effect after 12 weeks. The use of clozapine earlier in the illness course has been associated with better response and outcomes [32–34], thus there is a need to identify clozapine eligibility earlier in the illness course, and to understand the mechanisms by which it works. Given the significant impact of illness duration on neurometabolite levels and clozapine response, there is an impetus to investigate this association in an early psychosis sample.

The present report is a pilot sub-study of a larger neuroimaging project [35], comparing ACC glutamate levels between treatment resistant and treatment responsive individuals with early phase psychosis (< 5 years of illness). 7 participants in the TRS group elected to start clozapine as per clinical guidelines for the treatment of schizophrenia [5] and were invited for 6 month follow up MRS scans. For the first time, we aim to investigate the effect of clozapine treatment on ACC glutamate levels in an EPP sample. We hypothesized that ACC glutamate would decrease from baseline to 6 month follow up. Additionally, it was expected that the degree of glutamate decrease would be associated with the degree of symptom reduction.

Methods

Participants

All individuals with EPP being followed by the Nova Scotia Early Psychosis Program (NSEPP) were invited to participate in a larger parent neuroimaging study. NSEPP is a specialized EPP clinic that provides care to individuals experiencing a first episode of psychosis in the Canadian province of Nova Scotia, who are then followed clinically for 5 years. 7 clozapine eligible participants from the parent study were started on clozapine independently of the study and agreed to participate in a baseline scan and a follow up scan 6 months after starting clozapine. The decision to switch to clozapine was made in collaboration between the treating psychiatrist and the patient following clinical guidelines [5]. This is a naturalistic observational study, and no specific protocol was in place regarding switching antipsychotic medication. NSEPP does not accept individuals for follow-up who meet criteria for a primary substance induced psychotic disorder (though does accept those with comorbid substance misuse) or a mood disorder with psychosis. Other exclusion criteria included having a history of head trauma leading to loss of consciousness for greater than 30 min, inability to provide informed consent, unstable other medical or neurological conditions, contraindications to undergoing MRI scans, and extrapyramidal symptoms resulting in movement disorders. This sub study was approved by the Research Ethics Board at the Nova Scotia Health Authority, and therefore complies with ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and later amendments.

Clinical measures

At baseline, demographic information and clinical measures were obtained. Psychotic symptoms were assessed at baseline using the Positive and Negative Symptom Scale (PANSS; [36]) and functioning at baseline was assessed with the Social and Occupational Functioning Assessment Scale (SOFAS; [37]). Overall illness severity was assessed at baseline, and at the time of the six month follow up scan, using the Clinical Global Impression - Schizophrenia (CGI-S; [38]. It was not possible to collect full PANSS and SOFAS at follow up due to constraints of the pilot study, therefore CGI-S was used as the only follow up clinical measure due to availability of routine clinical data. Duration of untreated psychosis (DUP) was estimated at baseline and defined as the number of weeks between the first positive symptoms of psychosis and accessing treatment [39].

MRI imaging / MRS spectrum

Participants underwent MRI scans at both baseline and 6 month follow up, both time points following an identical protocol. Imaging took place at the Biomedical Translational Imaging Center in Halifax, Canada using a 3 Tesla GE Healthcare Model MR750 whole body magnetic resonance imager with a 32 channel head coil. A 3D T1- weighted FSPGR sequence with a slice thickness of 1 mm isometric, was used to acquire brain images. A 2.0 × 2.0 × 2.0 (8cm3) 1H-MRS voxel was placed in the bilateral dorsal ACC (see Fig. 1). The posterior end of the voxel was set to coincide with the precentral gyrus and the caudal face of the voxel set to coincide with the most caudal location that is not a part of the corpus callosum. Following best practices for reliability of 1H-MRS measured human brain metabolites published by Mullins et al. [40], 1H-MRS acquisitions were performed using the Point Resolved Spectroscopy (PRESS) sequence (TE=40ms, TR= 2000ms, 128 averages) with a standard GE base shim. MRS data was fit with Linear Combination of Model (LCModel; [41]). The program analyzes the spectra (see Fig. 2) and produces a maximum likelihood estimate of metabolite concentrations as well as a % SD for each estimate [41]. The unsuppressed water spectrum was used for internal referencing of the glutamate concentration. The T1-weighted sequence was used for tissue type segmentation and glutamate concentration levels were adjusted for partial volumes of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), grey matter and white matter in the voxel of interest.

Quality criteria for spectrum profiles were set at a signal to noise ratio (SNR) of 12 within LCModel, a scanner full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the water peak less than 10hz, and a percent error in the estimation of 1H-MRS concentration levels lower than 18% SD around the mean value as reported by LCModel. To account for potential drift, we tested the 1H-MRS Spectroscopy phantom (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) monthly over the course of the study [42]. This testing allows us to evaluate quality and avoid potential mistakes or defects made by the machine [42].

Metabolite values were corrected for voxel tissue content using the formula published by McQueen et al. [43], Mcorr = M × (wm + 1.21 × gm + 1.55 × CSF) / (wm + gm). M represents the uncorrected metabolite, while gm, wm, and CSF represent the proportion of gray matter, white matter, and CSF in the voxel. This formula assumes that CSF water concentration is 55,556 mol/m3 and an LCModel default brain water concentration of 35,880 mol/m3 [44, 45].

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (v4.4.2; [46]). Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and clinical measures at baseline. Differences in spectral quality metrics were calculated using paired samples t tests between baseline and follow up scans. Changes in CGI scores and ACC glutamate over time were assessed using a paired sample t-test. Subsequently, a Spearman’s Rho correlation was used to examine the association between ACC glutamate change and CGI-S change. Based on findings from planned analyses, an exploratory analysis of individual differences was conducted, comparing the magnitude of glutamate change with baseline clinical measures.

Clinical impression at follow up

Follow up CGI-S were conducted based on review of participants’ medical charts 6 months from the time of their baseline scan (M = 3.85, SD = 0.89). Paired sample t -test revealed CGI significantly improved from baseline to 6 months, t (6) = 6.30, p < .001, 95% CI [1.31, 2.97].

MRS spectrum quality

Paired sample t-tests were used to compare FWHM, LCModel SNR, and % SD for Glutamate. Mean SNR, FWHM, and %SD did not significantly differ between baseline and follow-up, indicating that MRS data quality was consistent across timepoints (see table S1).

Glutamate change after starting clozapine

One case was excluded from repeated measures analysis due to a signal-to-noise ratio below the specified cut off of 12 at both time points. In the remaining cases, ACC glutamate was not found to be significantly different between baseline and follow-up t (6) = 0.49, p = .647, 95% CI [− 2.72, 4.00]. While not statistically significant, we did observe a decrease in average glutamate concentration in the ACC from baseline (M = 14.79, SD = 2.07) to 6 months after starting clozapine (M = 14.15, SD = 1.66). Cohen’s d for the change in average glutamate was d = 0.34, 95% CI [-0.81, 1.47]. See Fig. 3.

Relationship between glutamate change and symptom change

To examine the association between glutamate change and symptom change, we calculated change scores for Glutamate (6 M-Baseline;ΔGlu) and CGI (6 M-Baseline;ΔCGI). Subsequently, the relationship between change scores was compared using a Spearman’s Rho correlation. In this sample, the relationship between change in ACC glutamate and CGI was not significant (Rs <0.001, p > .999). With such high variability and a small sample size, we cannot meaningfully interpret this correlation, even using non parametric statistics. See Fig. 4.

Exploratory analysis of individual differences

We found that the group change in ACC glutamate was in the expected direction with a small effect, but not reaching statistical significance. When visualizing our data, we observed that some individuals had increases in ACC glutamate over time, contrary to our hypotheses (see Fig. 4). To further explore this observation we compared ΔGlu values with baseline demographic and clinical measures using a Spearman’s rho correlation procedure.

Glutamate change from baseline to 6 M was not significantly correlated with any baseline clinical measures or demographic factors (see table S2).

Discussion

A growing body of 1 H-MRS literature consistently links elevated glutamate in the ACC with treatment resistant schizophrenia [10, 18–21, 47]. However, only one prior study has investigated whether clozapine, the recommended treatment for TRS, is associated with glutamatergic changes in the ACC. To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal examination of ACC glutamate after clozapine treatment in an early phase psychosis sample, we investigated the impact of 6-months of clozapine treatment on ACC glutamate using 1 H-MRS. We hypothesized that there would be a significant reduction in ACC glutamate after 6 months of clozapine treatment, given ACC glutamate has been shown to be elevated in EPP patients who eventually do not respond to treatment [10, 18–21], and those glutamate elevations are not present when clozapine responders are compared with non responders [48]. We found a low - moderate effect on ACC glutamate over time (d = 0.34; see Fig. 3), though not statistically significant (see Fig. 4). Given the paucity of evidence surrounding mechanisms by which clozapine is effective for TRS, this finding may lend itself to the development of future research that investigates glutamatergic changes in the ACC after starting clozapine. The clinical significance of a small decrease in absolute glutamate remains uncertain, however one hypothesis could be a normalization of NMDAR function via clozapine in line with the findings of Hribkova and colleagues [49]. It is clear that larger studies are required to interpret the impact of clozapine on NMDAR function, and future studies may wish to combine MRS with other neuroimaging indices of NMDAR function such as the EEG/MEG mismatch negativity and underlying oscillatory activity [50], to provide additional context for interpretation of MRS findings.

Our findings are in line with those of McQueen and colleagues [31], who also found no significant change in ACC glutamate after only 12 weeks of clozapine treatment in a sample of individuals with chronic schizophrenia. Compared with findings by McQueen and colleagues [31], our finding indicates a stronger effect (d = 0.34 vs. d = − 0.13) for EPP vs. chronic schizophrenia, after a longer period of clozapine treatment, but this must be interpreted cautiously as a hypothesis generating finding due to our small sample size and the uncertainty reflected in our confidence intervals. This is plausible, however, in line with theoretical support that clozapine response would be more robust in EPP [32–34]. It has also been suggested that mechanisms may be easier to capture in the early phase of illness, due to cascading effects of multiple prior medication trials and relapses in chronic schizophrenia on neurotransmission [6]. Recent evidence also suggests that clinically meaningful response to clozapine may take up to 12 months for some individuals [51] therefore future studies may wish to utilize a longer follow-up period, or perform follow up scans at multiple time points.

Upon examination of figures published by McQueen and colleagues [31], it appears that in many cases, ACC glutamate decreased over time, while in a few cases there was a substantial increase. This is a trend that we saw in our data as well, with some cases showing an increase in glutamate while others display the hypothesized decrease (see Fig. 4). One interpretation that McQueen and colleagues [31] put forth regarding individual differences in ACC glutamate reductions is that significant reductions may only be seen in patients who have a marked reduction in illness severity after clozapine treatment. In our sample, we did not find that the correlation between ACC glutamate change and CGI reduction was significant (Rs < 0.001, p > .999; see Fig. 4). This finding is limited by the CGI being the only clinical measure available at follow-up. All participants in our study showed at least some improvement on clozapine based on the CGI; however it may be the case that measures that are more sensitive to specific symptoms, such as the PANSS, would be better suited to modeling the relationship between schizophrenia symptoms change and metabolite changes after starting clozapine. Those with a marked increase in ACC glutamate and minimal symptom reduction, could go on to eventually represent a clozapine resistant group - as higher levels of glutamate have been associated with worse symptoms [52], and may indicate treatment non response. Importantly, we feel that these results indicate potential trends and may help to generate hypotheses which can be further investigated by studies with a larger sample size.

Exploratory analysis of individual differences

Visual inspection of our data (see Fig. 4) revealed that 3 individuals displayed a decrease in glutamate while 3 displayed an increase. To further investigate this observation, we compared the magnitude of ACC Glu change with baseline demographic and clinical measures. We did not observe any significant associations. Prior research has found higher hippocampal glutamate and glutamine (Glx) levels, and reductions in hippocampal volume of antipsychotic naive individuals with FEP who had a DUP of 12 months or greater, compared to those with a shorter DUP [53]. Relatedly, DUP emerged as a relevant covariate in a larger 1H-MRS study that compared glutamatergic metabolites between treatment resistant and treatment responsive EPP patients [35]. We observed a non significant correlation between the DUP and glutamate change with a strong effect (Rs = − 0.64, p = .16); shorter DUP correlated with an increase in ACC glutamate, longer DUP corresponded to a greater decrease (see table S2). In light of our small sample, this non-significant finding is presented purely as hypothesis generating, as in the context of previous findings this may warrant further investigation. Future research with larger samples may consider the influence of DUP when evaluating brain response to clozapine, and other antipsychotic medications.

Limitations

The present study is not without limitations. Namely, our study has a relatively small sample size as referenced above. It may be the case that in a large early phase sample, the trend of a moderate decrease in glutamate could be statistically significant, and with the heterogeneity in our sample, we were not sufficiently powered to detect the true effect. Another limitation is that clinical data at our 6-month time point is limited to the CGI-S based on chart review as opposed to standardized reassessment, which may limit the sensitivity of the association between ACC Glu and this measure. Furthermore, if we had more detailed clinical data (e.g. a full PANSS at follow-up), we may be able to further investigate the change in glutamate from baseline to 6 months as it relates to treatment response, providing a more clinically relevant result. Additionally, we lack reliable information about substance use at both time points. McQueen and colleagues (2021) found alcohol use in the week before scanning had a significant impact on ACC glutamate, something we were not able to control for. Finally, as with many similar studies, it is difficult to guarantee that participants were taking their medication as prescribed. It is possible some individuals in the study were not adhering, or only partially adhering to their clozapine treatment. Finally, our pilot study lacks a control group to compare clozapine related glutamate change over time against no clozapine. This limits the interpretability of the present effect as a direct effect of clozapine.

Conclusion

Despite limitations, we have conducted a pilot investigation of ACC glutamate change after six months of clozapine treatment in an EPP sample, and found a non-significant change in Glu over time. To our knowledge, it is the second MRS investigation of longitudinal glutamatergic change after clozapine treatment, and the first in an EPP population. We hope that this pilot study may inform future groups, who aim to use similar methods to investigate clozapine action on ACC glutamate in EPP populations.
