Factors associated with COVID-19 preventive behaviors among patients with hypertension and type 2 diabetes in southern Iran: a structural equation modeling study
Authors: Seyed Ali Shamsnia, Narges Mobasheri, Mohammad Hossein Kaveh, Mahin Nazari, Masoud Karimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring the transitional phase of the COVID-19 pandemic—when vaccines were available but coverage remained low—individuals with chronic diseases such as hypertension and type 2 diabetes faced elevated risks, yet their adherence to nonpharmaceutical preventive behaviors was uneven, and the psychological determinants of this variability remained unclear. This study examined the HBM constructs as correlates of COVID-19 preventive behaviors among this high-risk population in southern Iran.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 338 patients (mean age = 43.83 ± 9.79 years) in Kavar, Iran, using a validated HBM-based questionnaire (CVR > 0.6, CVI > 0.79, Cronbach’s α = 0.65–0.92). Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using AMOS 24.ResultsSEM demonstrated good fit (χ2/df = 1.46, CFI = 0.995, RMSEA = 0.037). Perceived barriers exerted the strongest negative association with preventive behaviors (β = –0.311, p < 0.001). Perceived severity (β = 0.206) and cues to action (β = 0.203) were significant positive factors. Perceived benefits (β = 0.150) had a modest positive association, while perceived susceptibility and self-efficacy were nonsignificant.ConclusionAmong the studied diabetic and hypertensive patients, practical and psychological barriers— not lack of knowledge or risk awareness—were the primary determinants of behavior. The non-significance of perceived susceptibility and self-efficacy may suggest that due to the prolonged nature of the COVID-19 pandemic, behavior was more heavily influenced by persistent structural barriers than by individual beliefs or confidence. Public health interventions should therefore prioritize reducing access-related, financial, and sociocultural barriers (e.g., mask affordability, misinformation) rather than solely promoting risk perception. Background

Individuals with chronic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and hypertension face significantly increased risks of severe illness, hospitalization, and mortality from respiratory infections, as starkly demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic [1]. These high-risk populations not only require tailored health guidance but also face unique barriers to maintaining preventive behaviors, including limited mobility, greater dependence on healthcare services, and psychological stress [2].

The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated that vaccination alone is insufficient for controlling large-scale respiratory outbreaks. The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasized that controlling highly transmissible respiratory pathogens requires a multifaceted approach—combining vaccination with nonpharmaceutical interventions such as mask wearing, social distancing, and hand hygiene [3]. However, critical challenges exist in sustaining preventive behaviors over time. Initial high levels of compliance often decline as a pandemic progresses and are influenced by pandemic fatigue, misinformation, and varying perceptions of risk [4, 5]. These dynamics were particularly evident during transitional periods when vaccines became available, but vaccination rates remained low, and social restrictions were still partially in place [6, 7]. This study was conducted during a critical transitional phase of the COVID-19 pandemic in Kavar, southern Iran. This period was marked by the emergence of a new and severe wave of infections, with daily reported COVID-19 cases in Iran exceeding 20,000 in May and June 2021, and in some days surpassing 30,000 [8]. Despite the availability of vaccines, vaccination coverage remained low, with fewer than 3 million people vaccinated by the end of June 2021, representing approximately 3–4% of the population [8]. During this time, partial social restrictions remained in place in many regions, and adherence to health protocols, particularly among individuals with chronic conditions and in rural areas, decreased significantly. Studies indicated that compliance with mask-wearing and physical distancing dropped to below 60%, reflecting increasing pandemic fatigue and reduced adherence to public health guidelines [9]. The regional context — marked by strong familial ties, frequent social gatherings, and limited access to preventive resources [10]— created a high-risk environment where adherence to preventive behaviors among individuals with chronic conditions became an urgent public health concern.

To understand the factors associated with preventive health behaviors, public health research has often turned to frameworks such as the Health Belief Model (HBM), which has proven effective in exploring health-protective behaviors during epidemics. The HBM posits that individuals' actions are related to six key psychological constructs [11]: (1) perceived susceptibility—an individual's subjective assessment of their risk of contracting a specific disease (in this context, COVID-19); (2) perceived severity—beliefs about the seriousness of the health threat and its potential consequences, including complications and mortality; (3) perceived benefits—beliefs about the effectiveness of preventive actions (such as mask wearing, hand hygiene, social distancing, and staying at home) in reducing infection risk; (4) perceived barriers—obstacles, costs, or difficulties that hinder the adoption of recommended behaviors; (5) cues to action—internal or external stimuli (such as symptoms, media messages, or healthcare provider advice) that trigger health-related decisions and behavior change; and (6) self-efficacy—an individual's confidence in their ability to successfully perform the recommended preventive behaviors despite challenges. Operationally, preventive behaviors in this study refer to the frequency and consistency of engaging in four key practices: wearing face masks in public spaces, performing hand hygiene (washing hands with soap or using hand sanitizer), maintaining physical distance of at least 1–2 m from others, and limiting time spent outside the home to essential activities only.

Studies conducted in countries such as Iran, the Philippines, Romania, and Italy have consistently validated the HBM's effectiveness in explaining COVID-19 preventive behaviors [12–15]. Key constructs such as perceived benefits, barriers, severity, and self-efficacy significantly shape adherence to health guidelines [13–15]. Moreover, cues to action are associated with protective behaviors, particularly among rural Iranian youth [12]. Additionally, demographic factors, including age, sex, and education level, have been found to further shape preventive behaviors [13, 14]. However, these factors often vary across demographics and health conditions, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions.

Despite the extensive body of research on preventive behaviors during the COVID-19 pandemic, three critical research gaps remain that this study addresses. First, existing research has predominantly focused on general populations, with limited investigation of patients managing multiple chronic conditions (such as diabetes and hypertension) who face compounded vulnerabilities and unique behavioral barriers [13, 14]. Second, while individual HBM constructs have been examined, few studies have employed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to examine the complex interrelationships and structural pathways through which these constructs are associated with behavior—insights essential for understanding the statistical predictors underlying behavior and designing more effective interventions [15]. Third, research examining preventive behaviors during transitional pandemic phases—when vaccination is available but coverage remains low—is notably scarce.

This study addresses these gaps by exploring behavioral factors associated with adherence to key preventive practices among patients with type 2 diabetes and hypertension in Kavar, southern Iran. The study makes three key contributions: (1) it focuses exclusively on a high-risk population with chronic comorbidities; (2) it employs SEM to uncover not only direct associations but also the structural interrelationships between HBM constructs and behavior; and (3) it captures behavioral dynamics during a critical transitional phase of the pandemic.

The primary objective of this study is to examine the structural relationships between HBM constructs and COVID-19 preventive behaviors among patients with type 2 diabetes and hypertension. Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted between April 21, 2021, and June 22, 2021 on individuals aged between 30 and 60 years with diabetes and/or hypertension in Kavar city, located in Fars Province, southern Iran. The study population included patients with type 2 diabetes and/or hypertension who received primary health care at the Comprehensive Urban Health Center. A unified electronic patient registry containing a total of 5,334 records—comprising 1,812 patients with type 2 diabetes and 3,522 patients with hypertension—was used as the sampling frame. All eligible patients within these diagnostic categories were identified and randomly selected from the registry to ensure representativeness of the target population. Because there were no prior estimates of COVID-19 preventive behavior proportions in the target population of patients with hypertension and type 2 diabetes, we assumed the most conservative estimate of p = 0.50 (which maximizes sample size and is commonly used when prevalence is unknown [16, 17]) with a confidence level of o.05, and margin of error d = 0.05, and considering total population of patients of 5000. This yielded a sample size of 360. To account for potential nonresponse, we added 5% to this figure, resulting in a required sample size of approximately 378 respondents.

A systematic random sampling method was performed using the combined patient registry from the electronic health information system. The patient registry was numbered sequentially, and every 14th (5334/378 ≈ 14) patient was selected for inclusion in the study. Patients were contacted via phone calls by trained health staff, who explained the objectives of the study and invited them to participate. To ensure a diverse and representative sample, efforts were made to include patients from different socioeconomic and demographic backgrounds.

Selected patients were contacted via telephone by trained health staff, who explained the study objectives and invited them to participate. Among the sampled individuals, those who could not be reached due to incorrect or unavailable phone numbers or lack of response after repeated attempts at least three attempts at different times and days) were recorded as non-contact cases (5 patients). In addition, 13 of the selected individuals expressed their unwillingness to participate in the study, and ultimately 360 questionnaires were distributed among the participants.

The age range of 30–60 years was selected based on several considerations. This range aligns with Iran’s Package of Essential Noncommunicable Disease Interventions (IraPEN), implemented within the national primary healthcare system, in which routine screening, monitoring, and follow-up for diabetes and hypertension begin at age 30 and primarily target adults within this age bracket [18]. Individuals in this age group are typically engaged in active social and occupational roles, which increase their exposure to community settings and influence preventive behavior decisions during a pandemic. The upper limit of 60 years was chosen to minimize potential confounding from severe age-related comorbidities, polypharmacy, and cognitive or functional limitations that could affect both self-reported data accuracy and adherence to preventive measures [19].

The inclusion criteria were individuals aged 30 to 60 years who had diabetes, hypertension, or both for at least six months, were able to read and write, and signed the informed consent form. The exclusion criteria were lack of interest in participation or failure to complete the questionnaire. Among 360 administered questionnaires, 22 questionnaires were excluded from the study due to incomplete answers and finally 338 questionnaires entered the analysis (response rate: 93.88%).

The data collection tool was a researcher-developed questionnaire comprising three sections. The first section gathered demographic information, including age, gender, employment status, education level, and economic status. The second section assessed participants’ experiences with COVID-19. The third section, developed based on the HBM constructs, was initially drafted and subsequently refined by a panel of experts. A higher score in each domain indicates greater knowledge, perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, self-efficacy, cues to action, and preventive behavior, while a higher score in the barriers domain reflects greater perceived barriers. The complete questionnaire is provided as a supplementary file. To minimize self-reporting bias, responses were anonymized, and participants were provided with clear instructions to ensure accuracy and honesty.

The face and content validity of the questionnaire were rigorously established through a quantitative approach involving a panel of ten experts in health education and promotion. The Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) were calculated for each construct, with mean values exceeding the minimum thresholds of 0.62 and 0.80, respectively, based on Lawshe’s criteria [20] and Waltz and Basel instructions [21] (see Table 1).

Internal consistency was assessed via a pilot study of 30 participants who mirrored the demographic and health characteristics (diabetes and/or hypertension) of the main study population. For the HBM constructs, Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranged from 0.65 to 0.92, indicating acceptable to excellent internal consistency. For the knowledge section, which utilized dichotomous items, the Kuder-Richardson 20 (KR-20) formula was applied, yielding a coefficient of 0.71. External reliability was confirmed via the test–retest method with a two-week interval, resulting in correlation coefficients above 0.70 (p < 0.05) for all constructs. Furthermore, construct validity was supported by the factor loadings within the structural equation model, confirming that the items appropriately represented their respective latent variables.

Harman’s single-factor test using principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on all study variables. The first unrotated factor accounted for 29.33% of the total variance, substantially below the 50% threshold, indicating that common method bias does not pose a significant threat to validity [22].

Participants received detailed explanations about the study objectives, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before completing the questionnaire.

The collected data were processed and analyzed using SPSS version 24 and AMOS version 24. The normality of data distribution was examined and confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05). Independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc correction were conducted to examine differences in HBM constructs and preventive behaviors across demographic subgroups (sex, marital status, education level, employment status, and disease type). Pearson correlation analysis was used to investigate bivariate relationships between HBM constructs and COVID-19 preventive behaviors.

Multiple regression analysis was employed as a complementary analytic step to examine direct associations between Health Belief Model constructs and preventive behaviors in a transparent and familiar framework. Given the theory-testing objective of this study, the primary regression model focused on core Health Belief Model constructs to assess their explanatory relationships with preventive behaviors. Potential demographic and clinical variables, including vaccination status, were examined in preliminary analyses to assess their associations with the outcome. However, consistent with theory-driven modeling principles, these variables were not included in the final model to preserve theoretical parsimony and avoid over-adjustment, and unnecessary complexity, particularly for variables that may lie downstream of health beliefs. This approach is consistent with theory evaluation research, which prioritizes testing hypothesized theoretical pathways while minimizing unnecessary statistical control [23, 24]. Structural equation modeling was subsequently used as the primary theory-testing approach to evaluate the hypothesized relationships simultaneously while accounting for measurement error and intercorrelations among constructs. For the estimation of indirect effects in the structural equation model, we relied on maximum likelihood estimation without bootstrapping. The convergence of findings across these analytic approaches strengthens the robustness and interpretability of the theoretical conclusions.

Result

Among 360 administered questionnaires, 27 were excluded due to incomplete responses, yielding a final sample of 338 participants (response rate: 93.88%). Mean participant age was 43.83 ± 9.79 years. The sample comprised individuals with hypertension (45.30%), type 2 diabetes (32.80%), and both conditions (21.90%). A total of 161 participants (47.63%) reported a history of COVID-19 infection, of whom 29.74% required hospitalization.

A comparison of HBM construct scores across demographic subgroups revealed that men had significantly higher perceived susceptibility scores than women (p = 0.02, Cohen’s d = 0.26), while women reported significantly higher perceived benefits (p = 0.03, Cohen’s d = 0.25). Single individuals reported significantly higher perceived barriers than married individuals (p = 0.00, Cohen’s d = 0.30). With regard to preventive behaviors, women demonstrated significantly higher scores than men (p = 0.00, Cohen’s d = 0.24). No significant differences were observed in any HBM construct scores between individuals with diabetes and those with hypertension (Table 2).

As shown in Table 6, the direct effects analysis revealed that perceived barriers exerted the strongest negative influence on preventive behavior (β = –0.31, p < 0.001). Perceived severity demonstrated a significant positive direct effect (β = 0.13, p < 0.001), as did cues to action (β = 0.17, p < 0.001) and perceived benefits (β = 0.15, p < 0.001).

Path analysis revealed potential indirect pathways through which HBM constructs may influence behavior. Specifically, perceived severity showed a negative indirect pathway through perceived barriers (β = –0.074, P = 0.001), whereas cues to action demonstrated a positive indirect pathway through multiple mediators (perceived severity, perceived barriers, and perceived benefits) which was not statistically significant (β = 0.03, P = 0.210). These pathways suggest that perceived severity may heighten perceived barriers, thereby reducing preventive behaviors, while cues to action may enhance both perceived severity and benefits, ultimately promoting preventive behaviors.

Total effects (combining direct and indirect pathways) confirmed that perceived barriers had the strongest overall negative influence on behavior (β = –0.31), followed by perceived severity (β = 0.21, reflecting a positive direct and a smaller negative indirect effect) and cues to action (β = 0.20).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined associations between HBM constructs and COVID-19 preventive behaviors among patients with type 2 diabetes and hypertension in Kavar during a critical transitional pandemic phase. Perceived barriers emerged as the strongest negative correlate of preventive behaviors across both regression and SEM analyses, while perceived severity and cues to action showed significant positive associations. Perceived susceptibility and self-efficacy were not statistically significant.

The weak association between knowledge and behavior, despite adequate baseline knowledge, reflects the "knowledge-behavior gap" documented in health promotion literature [25, 26]. This indicates that information alone is insufficient when substantial structural or psychological barriers exist. Future experimental or longitudinal research is needed to establish causality and test whether barrier removal or enhanced information produces greater behavioral change.

Perceived barriers demonstrated the strongest negative association with preventive behaviors, capturing tangible barriers including physical discomfort from mask use, vaccine side-effect concerns, and social opposition to preventive measures. Prior research suggests that when barriers are immediate and personally experienced, they override perceived benefits [13, 27]. Our study setting in Kavar, a close-knit community with strong cultural and familial norms [10], likely created an environment where such barriers were particularly salient. This dominant role of perceived barriers in relation to adherence highlights the importance of addressing practical barriers in similar community settings.

Perceived severity showed the second strongest positive association with preventive behaviors. Interestingly, individuals without COVID-19 deaths in their social network reported higher severity and adherence; prior research suggests this pattern may reflect bereavement-related emotional responses [28, 29]. However, SEM revealed a negative indirect pathway through perceived barriers. This suggests that while heightened threat perception directly promotes preventive behavior, it simultaneously increases perceived barriers (such as vaccine side-effect concerns or mask discomfort), which in turn reduce adherence. This paradoxical effect—wherein fear intensifies awareness of preventive measures' drawbacks—aligns with 'dread risk' concepts [30, 31].

Cues to action (interpersonal and media communication from providers, family, and outlets) showed significant associations through direct and indirect pathways. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting that credible external prompts can serve as behavioral triggers [13, 32], highlighting the importance of coordinated communication among healthcare professionals, media, and community networks.

Perceived benefits showed a significant but modest association with preventive behaviors—the weakest among all statistically significant constructs. This finding is consistent with prior evidence that perceived benefits alone do not fully explain adherence patterns when individuals face substantial practical barriers or social pressures [33, 34].

A key finding of this study is the lack of statistically significant associations between perceived susceptibility, self-efficacy, and preventive behaviors in the SEM model. Although men reported higher levels of perceived susceptibility—likely reflecting greater occupational exposure to crowded environments—this heightened awareness was not associated with stronger behavioral adherence within the study sample. This observation is consistent with previous research showing that while personal risk perception is necessary, it is not sufficient to drive behavioral change when structural or psychological barriers remain unaddressed [25, 26, 35]. Among patients with chronic diseases, the continuous experience of vulnerability may become normalized over time, thereby diminishing its motivational impact. The absence of a statistically significant structural association between self-efficacy and preventive behaviors warrants further consideration. Although self-efficacy is a central construct within the HBM, prior research indicates that its behavioral relevance may be context-dependent and constrained by substantial practical challenges or social pressures [36, 37]. In the specific case of Kavar, the high magnitude of perceived barriers may reflect a situation where structural and cultural barriers may limit the behavioral relevance of individual confidence. However, we acknowledge that since our study did not directly measure the interaction between self-efficacy and these external pressures, this interpretation remains a hypothesis.

Limitations

This study encompasses several important limitations that warrant consideration.

First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, as the observed associations cannot establish directionality or temporal relationships between variables. This design also prevents adjustment for time-varying factors that could affect outcomes among chronically ill populations. Consequently, longitudinal investigations are essential to clarify whether identified barriers directly influence health behaviors or vice versa.

Second, the study was conducted at a single urban health center in southern Iran, which may limit generalizability. Although systematic sampling enhanced representativeness within the center's catchment area, the findings may not be applicable to rural populations, higher-income urban settings, or regions with substantially different healthcare infrastructure and accessibility patterns. Validation across diverse geographical and socioeconomic contexts is needed before these findings can be generalized.

Third, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential limitations including recall bias and social desirability bias. Although measures were implemented to minimize these biases—including ensuring anonymity, using validated instruments, and maintaining clarity in questionnaire items—the accuracy of responses may still be compromised by respondents' subjective interpretations or unconscious response patterns. Integration of objective measures in future research would strengthen the validity of findings.

Unmeasured confounding variables represent an additional limitation that may influence the observed associations. To address these methodological constraints, future research should prioritize longitudinal or interventional designs to capture temporal dynamics and establish causal pathways. Implementation studies evaluating targeted strategies—such as subsidized protective materials, peer-led educational interventions, mobile health reminders, or healthcare provider training—would provide evidence-based approaches to mitigate identified barriers and improve health outcomes in vulnerable populations.

Conclusion

Given the study’s cross-sectional design and the limitations noted above, we suggest that public health initiatives should adopt multicomponent strategies that simultaneously strengthen perceived severity, enhance cues to action, communicate perceived benefits, and reduce practical barriers. To achieve these goals, such initiatives should:

Reduce practical barriers by improving access to comfortable, culturally appropriate, and affordable protective equipment and vaccines;

Reduce practical barriers by improving access to comfortable, culturally appropriate, and affordable protective equipment and vaccines;

Leverage trusted interpersonal sources—particularly healthcare providers and family members—as credible communicators, tailored to local communication preferences;

Leverage trusted interpersonal sources—particularly healthcare providers and family members—as credible communicators, tailored to local communication preferences;

Design inclusive, context-sensitive programs addressing heightened barriers among single individuals and lower engagement among men, incorporating gender-specific and socioeconomic considerations.

Future epidemic preparedness initiatives should prioritize these integrated approaches addressing multiple Health Belief Model constructs.
