Teleophthalmology in the Korean Military: An 8-Year Review of Patients Admitted to the Military Telemedicine Team
Authors: Jung Suk Yoon, Seeun Kwon, Jong Ha Moon, Seoyeon Hwang, Kyung Joo Maeng, Mirang Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2015, the Republic of Korea Armed Forces implemented a telemedicine pilot program that included teleophthalmology services provided through booths in remote and medically underserved military units linked to the Medical Emergency Operation Center. This study aimed to evaluate the epidemiology, distribution, and injury patterns of eye diseases managed using this military telemedicine program.MethodsTeleophthalmology consultations conducted between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Diagnoses, causes of trauma, seasonal distributions, and evacuation trends were analyzed.ResultsAmong the 47,701 telemedicine patients, 3,167 were ophthalmology patients. The most common diagnoses were hordeolum/chalazion (39.3%) and viral conjunctivitis (20.3%). Ocular trauma and injury occurred in 384 cases, most of which were caused by task-related activities (47.1%), particularly welding and exposure to metal fragments. Seasonal variation was significant, with the highest trauma incidence observed in summer (P = 0.014). Evacuation was required in 19.4% of ophthalmology patients and 49.9% of trauma and injury cases.ConclusionThis was the first long-term analysis of a military teleophthalmology program in South Korea. The study findings demonstrate the effectiveness of telemedicine in remote military environments, and underscore the need for protective equipment and task-specific safety training. Further development of remote diagnostic tools and targeted preventive strategies is warranted to reduce injury-related evacuations and to support combat readiness.Keywords: Medical Emergency Operation Center, Military, Ophthalmology, Telemedicine, Teleophthalmology

INTRODUCTION

Over the past few years, technological advancements have facilitated the expansion of telemedicine across various medical specialties.1,2,3,4,5 Indeed, telemedicine has developed sufficiently to overcome geographical and temporal barriers to provide medical services ranging from simple information exchange to actual consultations and prescriptions. Technological advancements have further led to significant progress in teleophthalmology, where high-quality imaging is crucial to ensure accurate diagnosis and management. This has opened possibilities in areas that were previously inaccessible.1,4,6 Telemedicine is also an effective option for providing appropriate medical aid in medically underserved areas or remote regions where it is difficult for military medical officers to be stationed, thereby contributing to the efficient distribution of medical resources, and the maintaining of combat readiness within the military.7,8

Currently, telemedicine is not legally permitted in South Korea, except in cases where information and communication technology is used to support medical knowledge and information sharing among licensed healthcare professionals in separate locations, such as through interprofessional consultations or educational purposes. However, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic resulted in the development of telemedicine pilot programs and has gained permission for services currently being provided by private companies.

Before the launch of these pilot programs, the Armed Forces Medical Command (AFMC) of South Korea took the initiative to provide medical services by establishing telemedicine booths in remote locations that lacked military medical officers. This initiative, approved as a pilot program by the Ministry of Health and Welfare in 2015, was implemented across the Army, Navy, and Air Force. These telemedicine booths were installed in the border areas near North Korea, island regions, and mountainous air defense control sites. When a medical consultation request is made through a telemedicine booth, military medical officers from the Medical Emergency Operation Center (MEOC) use monitors and videoscope equipment to conduct consultations. These officers are assisted by on-site medics whose job is to help operate the devices and support patient interactions during sessions. The medical officer further documents medical records using an independent computer system (Republic of Korea Telemedicine System), and either prescribes medications or orders evacuations to a medical facility if deemed necessary.

Although prior studies have investigated ocular trauma and reported eye injuries within the Republic of Korea (ROK) military,9,10 none have utilized data collected through telemedicine platforms or provided comprehensive long-term analysis across the entire military teleophthalmology system. This study therefore aimed to characterize the epidemiology, distribution, and injury patterns of eye diseases among military personnel managed via telemedicine consultations, placing a particular focus on ocular trauma cases, seasonal variation, and evacuation trends. Additionally, this study introduced an ongoing military telemedicine pilot program and evaluated its outcomes, strengths, and limitations through a retrospective analysis of teleophthalmology cases over the 8-year duration of the program.

METHODS

This study analyzed data from a telemedicine pilot program conducted over an 8-year period from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2023. Among the 47,701 patients who received telemedicine services, 3,147 ophthalmology patients were included in the analysis. Patients were included if they presented with eye-related symptoms and received treatment. Telemedicine consultations were conducted by emergency medicine (EM) military medical officers at the MEOC. The date of consultation, season, sex, rank, diagnosis, cause of trauma, and injury were analyzed retrospectively. Seasons were defined as spring (March to May), summer (June to August), autumn (September to November), and winter (December to February). All diagnoses were verified by an ophthalmologist based on the documented details in the medical records.

The telemedicine pilot program, initiated by the AFMC under the Ministry of National Defense of the ROK, began with the establishment of 28 telemedicine booths between July and November 2015, marking the first phase of implementation. This was followed by the establishment of 12 booths from November 2015 to December 2016 (2nd phase), 23 booths from December 2016 to November 2017 (3rd phase), and 13 booths from November 2017 to February 2020 (4th phase). Since February 2020, the program has completed the 5th phase by setting up 13 additional booths, and telemedicine services are currently being provided to approximately 26,307 military personnel across 89 telemedicine booths. Additionally, further plans for the 6th phase and the establishment of telemedicine booths on naval ships are in progress with the aim of improving medical access in remote areas where military medical officers are not stationed.

Medical examinations were conducted at telemedicine booths installed in various regions nationwide; in most cases, they were conducted similar to outpatient consultations, in which patients were treated at the scheduled date and time. However, additional consultations were conducted in urgent situations. When a patient enters the telemedicine booth, an EM military medical officer at the MEOC conducts a real-time video consultation while a medic waiting in the booth assists the patient. Depending on the situation, the vital signs were measured, and if more detailed observations were necessary, procedures such as otoscopy, laryngoscopy, dermoscopy, and ophthalmoscopy were performed using a camera system installed in the booth (Dr. Camscope Pro; Sometech Inc., Seoul, Korea), with information transmitted in real time through the screen. The scope delivers a high-quality Full HD (1,920 × 1,080) output and provides up to 32 GB of storage for recording videos and photos. Once the consultation was complete, prescriptions were provided along with explanations, and an assistant medic prepared the medication on site according to the prescription. The telemedicine booth was stocked with 30 types of medications approved by the Ministry of Health and Welfare. If further treatment or hospital care was required, evacuations or arrangements were made for further care. All information, including real-time videos and consultations, was securely transmitted through the military intranet (Fig. 1).

The Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to compare the distribution of ophthalmology patients across different seasons. If a statistically significant difference was observed between seasons, Dunn’s post-hoc test was conducted to identify which specific pairs of seasons showed any significant differences in patient distribution. Statistical analyses were conducted using Python v3.13, and a P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the AFMC (AFMC 2020-10-082-001) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of the AFMC due to its retrospective nature, which made informed consent infeasible. The collected data were processed and securely stored to prevent personal identifying information from being exposed, and measures were taken to block access by anyone other than authorized personnel.

RESULTS

Throughout the study period, 47,701 patients visited telemedicine booths, of whom 3,167 (6.6%) were ophthalmology patients and 384 (0.8%) were diagnosed with ocular trauma or injury. Among the ophthalmology patients, 3,161 (99.8%) were male, reflecting the male-dominated demographics of the ROK military population. The mean patient age was 20.9 ± 2.0 years. Regarding laterality, 1,114 (36.1%) cases involved the right eye, 1,118 (35.3%) involved the left eye, and 666 (21.0%) were bilateral. Patients of various military ranks, from Private to Major, received care, with the highest proportion being Private First Class (1,474 patients; 46.5%), followed by Corporals (856 patients; 27.0%). While enlisted soldiers accounted for over 90% of all ophthalmology patients, only 7.6% were non-commissioned officers (NCOs), while 2.1% were commissioned officers. Due to the relatively small number of higher-ranking personnel, NCOs and officers were grouped for analytical clarity (Table 1).

The total number of patients treated through the telemedicine pilot program increased steadily over the years, with the highest number recorded in 2023 (7,668 patients; 16.1%) and the lowest in 2016 (2,178 patients; 4.6%) (Fig. 2). Among the ophthalmology patients, the highest annual count was observed in 2019 (545 patients; 17.2%). The number of ocular trauma and injury cases peaked in 2020 (67 patients; 17.4%); however, no increase was observed over time (Fig. 3). Seasonal distribution analysis revealed that the number of ophthalmology patients was the highest in spring (1,032 patients; 32.6%) and lowest in winter (585 patients; 18.5%). The difference in the number of patients across seasons was statistically significant (P = 0.013), with a particularly notable difference observed between spring and winter (P = 0.011). Among ophthalmology patients, the number of ocular trauma and injury cases was the highest in summer (121 patients; 31.6%) and lowest in winter (62 patients; 16.2%) (Fig. 4). The seasonal variation in ocular trauma and injury cases was also statistically significant (P = 0.014), with notable differences observed between spring and winter (P = 0.042), and summer and winter (P = 0.026).

Among the 3,167 ophthalmology patients, the most common diagnoses were hordeolum and chalazion (1,246 cases; 39.3%), followed by acute viral conjunctivitis (643 cases; 20.3%), and allergic conjunctivitis (563 cases; 17.8%). Six diagnoses consistent with the mechanisms of ocular trauma and injury (burn injury, corneal abrasion, eyelid laceration, foreign body, ocular contusion, and photokeratitis) were categorized and analyzed in the ocular trauma and injury groups. Among the 384 patients diagnosed with ocular trauma or injury, the most common diagnosis was the presence of a foreign body (143 cases; 37.2%), followed by corneal abrasion (89 cases; 23.2%), ocular contusion (81 cases; 21.1%), photokeratitis (45 cases; 11.7%), burn injury (16 cases; 4.2%), and eyelid laceration (10 cases; 2.6%) (Table 2).

Among the various causes of ocular trauma and injuries, incidents that occurred during task-related activities accounted for the largest proportion (181 cases; 47.1%), followed by daily military life (e.g., barracks or routine military duties) (114 cases; 29.7%), sports (53 cases; 13.8%), training (11 cases; 2.9%), and violence (2 cases; 0.5%) (Fig. 5). Among the task-related injuries, the most common direct cause was ultraviolet exposure from welding (45 cases; 24.9%), followed by injuries caused by metal (24 cases; 13.3%), stones (22 cases; 12.2%), chemical substances (22 cases; 12.2%), and wood (19 cases; 10.5%). Among the metal-related injuries, flying metal fragments from grinder use were the most common (14 cases; 58.3%). Among chemical-related injuries, petroleum splashes accounted for most cases (14 cases; 63.6%). When categorized by task, welding was the most frequent cause (45 cases; 24.9%), followed by weed trimming (32 cases; 17.7%). For ocular injuries that occurred during daily military life (excluding unknown causes), the most common cause was blunt trauma to the hands or body parts (16 cases; 14.0%), followed by chemical injuries (15 cases; 13.2%), and biological causes, such as insect exposure (11 cases; 9.6%). Among the chemical injuries in this category, bleach exposure accounted for most cases (4 cases; 26.7%). Among sports-related injuries, ball-related trauma was the most common (28 cases; 52.8%), followed by blunt trauma from body contact (10 cases; 18.9%) and injuries from exercise resistance bands (8 cases; 15.1%). During training, the most common cause of ocular trauma and injury was related to firearms or firearm components (3 cases; 27.3%).

Causes of ocular trauma and injury among military personnel consulted via telemedicine.

Of the 47,701 patients who received telemedicine consultations, 8,538 (17.9%) were evacuated for further care. Of the 3,167 ophthalmology patients, 614 (19.4%) were evacuated, and of the 384 patients with ocular trauma and injuries, 191 (49.9%) required evacuation. The overall evacuation rate has decreased since 2016. However, the evacuation rates for ophthalmology patients, particularly those with ocular trauma and injuries, remained relatively higher than the overall patient evacuation rate, and demonstrated an increasing pattern (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

Overall, this study aimed to assess the effectiveness and necessity of providing timely ophthalmic care to military personnel stationed in medically underserved and geographically isolated areas using data from ophthalmology consultations conducted through military telemedicine booths. Additionally, this study sought to reduce the burden on military medical officers at the MEOC by analyzing common ophthalmologic conditions and offering practical insights for consultation.

Supporting medical readiness and maintaining combat capability have long been the core missions of the AFMC.11 In pursuit of this goal, the Emergency Control Center was established in 2007, and later expanded into the MEOC on August 13, 2015, comprising teams for emergency response, teleconsultation, and referred patient management. The army also established a dedicated MEDical Evacuation helicopter unit (MEDION). The MEOC has since served as a real-time control tower for emergency evacuation, providing 24/7 medical guidance via telephone (1688-5119) and smartphone application (messages, voice, or video calls).12 Emergency physicians on standby coordinate with on-call specialists, and determine the most suitable evacuation method, route, and hospital—executing a seamless, one-stop evacuation process from the point of injury to definitive care.

Given the military’s structure, many units are located in remote regions with limited access to medical care. To address this issue, a telemedicine pilot program was launched in November 2015, and has since successfully completed five phases. The program continues to expand and aims to establish more booths on naval vessels, islands, and remote areas.

As all Korean men are required to serve in the military, the patient population in this study predominantly comprised males in their 20s, with 2,859 (90.8%) falling within this age group (Table 1). The overall number of telemedicine and ophthalmology patients increased until 2019 and subsequently declined from 2020. However, with the opening of additional telemedicine booths, the total number of patients began to rise again in 2021, while ophthalmology consultations showed a modest increase beginning in 2023. There is no precise answer to explain the peak in the number of patients in 2019; however, the subsequent gradual decrease may be explained by the reduced outdoor activity and work during the COVID-19 pandemic, the effects of quarantine and strict on-based controls, the impact of ongoing injury-prevention education, and the wider availability and use of protective equipment. Moreover, another study on ROK military patients by Yoon et al. reported a decline in infectious conjunctivitis cases following the outbreak, attributing this trend to improved hand hygiene and social distancing.13 Our findings similarly show a sharp drop in infectious conjunctivitis post-2019; the percentage of patients with this diagnosis dropped temporally (30.6% in 2019; 18.2% in 2020; 15.8% in 2021; 11.9% in 2022), while the percentage of allergic conjunctivitis cases remained relatively steady (21.8% in 2019; 17.7% in 2020; 17.3% in 2021; 18.6% in 2022)—supporting the notion that infection control measures may have been effectively implemented across military settings (Fig. 6).

Few studies have been conducted on ocular trauma among ROK military personnel. One prior study by Bae and Kim9 analyzing inpatients with ocular injuries at the Armed Forces Capital Hospital (AFCH) from January 1996 to June 2000 reported that trauma caused by soccer balls accounted for the largest proportion (34.5%) of ocular trauma cases. A later study by Lee et al.,10 covering ocular trauma admissions in the same institute from January 2001 to December 2010, found that sports-related injuries were the most common (39%), followed by task-related (23.4%), training-related (15.7%), and daily military life-related injuries (14.3%), with violence accounting for 4%. Unlike the prior studies, our study showed that task-related injuries were the most common cause of ocular trauma (47.1%), followed by daily military life (29.7%) and sports (13.8%), with violence remaining the least common cause, albeit at a lower rate of 0.5%. The decrease in violence-related injuries was an encouraging finding, as violence in the military has been a long-lasting concern.

Among sports-related injuries, ball-related trauma accounted for 52.8% of cases; however, the proportion of ball-related trauma to total ocular trauma and injuries was 7.2%. This was much lower than that reported in previous studies (Bae and Kim 34.5%,9 Lee et al. 30.5%10), possibly reflecting improved safety measures, or the inclusion of less severe outpatient-level cases in our dataset. Moreover, the unique constraints of Guard Post (GP) or General Out Post (GOP) units and naval vessels, where space is limited and recreational sports are restricted, may have contributed to this trend.

Prior studies have further suggested that, while protective equipment and safety education is rigorously enforced during military drills, such control may be inadequate during routine tasks or sports activities.10 Our results align with this observation, as the most frequent ocular injury mechanisms during tasks included ultraviolet exposure (24.9%), metal fragments (13.3%), stone (12.2%), chemical exposure (12.2%), and wood (10.5%), which mostly occurred in the absence of protective eyewear. Given that ocular trauma is one of the most preventable causes of visual impairment, the proper use of protective equipment is essential.14,15 In particular, education on eye protection is crucial for tasks such as welding, weed trimming, and handling chemical substances such as petroleum.

Seasonally, ocular trauma and injury cases were most frequent in summer, which differs from the findings of Lee et al.,10 which identified autumn as the peak season. However, both studies showed that the frequency of ocular trauma was the lowest in winter, likely due to reduced activity. This consistency indicates that spring, when outside activities begin to increase after winter, may be the ideal period for implementing safety education for service members.

While the overall evacuation rate among all telemedicine patients declined over time, the evacuation rate for ophthalmology patients remained relatively constant at an average of 19.4%. Notably, nearly half (average 49.9%) of all patients with ocular trauma and injuries were evacuated, and this proportion has increased in recent years. This trend may reflect the diagnostic challenges faced by non-ophthalmologists in evaluating ocular trauma or injuries. Because medical evacuations from isolated areas involve significant manpower resources, transportation, and financial burdens, providing reference materials for telemedicine providers, offering regular training on common ophthalmic conditions, and establishing a communication channel with ophthalmologists may help to preserve the assets associated with unnecessary evacuations.7

The United States military has actively employed teleophthalmology for nearly two decades to overcome the logistical challenges of delivering specialist eye care in austere and combat environments.7,16 The U.S. Army’s Ocular Teleconsultation Program, implemented from 2004 to 2018, facilitated over 650 asynchronous email-based consultations, between deployed providers and ophthalmologists, predominantly originating from combat zones such as Iraq and Afghanistan. Consequently, 22.7% of consults resulted in recommendations for evacuation, emphasizing the program's role in ensuring timely triage and appropriate management of serious cases, while avoiding unnecessary evacuations in many others.7 A more recent innovation involved beta testing a secure mobile teleophthalmology app, termed ‘FOXTROT’ (Forward Operating Base Expert Telemedicine Resource Utilizing Mobile Application for Trauma), in Afghanistan. This application allows point-of-care medics to transmit ocular consultations to an ophthalmologist stationed at a regional military hospital. The app achieved a high diagnostic concordance rate (86%), and prevented aeromedical evacuation in 14% of cases. Furthermore, 54% of patients were able to return to duty after teleconsultation, thereby demonstrating its operational value.17 These experiences indicate that robust, secure, and responsive teleophthalmology systems can enhance efficiency, accessibility, and quality of care in deployed settings. Such evidence further supports the potential for developing an ongoing telemedicine program for the ROK Armed Forces, particularly in geographically isolated units, or during military operations, where access to specialist care is limited.

This study had several limitations. Firstly, the findings may not be applicable to the general population, given that the study data consisted of military personnel stationed in remote and isolated areas. Second, the initial diagnoses were made by EM specialists rather than ophthalmologists, potentially affecting the diagnostic accuracy. To mitigate this, the diagnoses were reviewed and reclassified by an ophthalmologist during the analysis. Third, the absence of ophthalmic tests, such as visual acuity or intraocular pressure measurements limits the ability to assess the severity and prognosis of ocular trauma. Fourth, not all patients were subsequently evacuated to military hospitals, making follow-up observations difficult. Consequently, it was impossible to verify whether those who were accurately diagnosed were suitable candidates for evacuation. Further research on recent cases of ocular trauma and injuries in patients visiting the AFCH is needed to better understand the current trends in eye injuries in the military. Finally, most care in telemedicine booths is delivered following an outpatient appointment-based model. In cases where ocular trauma or injury appeared severe, units often bypassed the telemedicine booth, contacted the MEOC via the hotline directly, consulted an on-call ophthalmologist at the AFCH, and arranged evacuation. To optimize telemedicine programs, a broad campaign to raise awareness of telemedicine booths among military personnel is warranted. However, given the current status of the available immediate specialist hotlines, the best pathway for emergencies requires further consideration. Nonetheless, given the potential shortage of military medical officers, as well as the impracticality of 24/7 specialist coverage, the role of telemedicine booths should not be limited to routine care.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze data from ophthalmology consultations through the ROK military telemedicine program. This study is significant in that it compared the diagnoses and mechanisms of ocular trauma and injuries in remote and isolated units since 2016 with findings from previous studies, while also providing insights into the current state of medical evacuation on ocular injuries. Through this research, we hope to provide statistical data to support consultations on ophthalmic conditions for military medical officers at the MEOC, and contribute to the establishment of guidelines for adequate evacuation. Structured evacuation criteria will help to achieve a more consistent and generalized decision. Furthermore, by identifying the distribution of ophthalmic diseases and causes of ocular trauma and injuries in the military, this study may support the development of preventive measures. Such measures could then be shared with frontline units and incorporated into training and guidance programs to inform unit-level management protocols. Since initial visual acuity is an important predictor of prognosis in ocular trauma,18 equipping telemedicine booths with vision charts or basic vision testing tools and training medics in their use would help to better assess the condition and prognosis of injured patients. In constrained environments such as naval vessels, simplified virtual reality-based tools can be employed for visual acuity, visual field, and color vision testing. These tools could enhance remote consultations with ophthalmologists and provide greater reassurance to field personnel and medical staff. Given that cases with ophthalmological conditions consistently show a higher evacuation rate than others, accurately determining the necessity of evacuation and ensuring timely transfers could significantly improve the efficient use of limited resources.

Furthermore, considering the efficacy of telemedicine in providing care to geographically isolated military units, it may also serve as a viable solution for improving healthcare accessibility in civilian areas with limited medical resources or an absence of healthcare providers. However, telemedicine must be implemented within a framework that does not disrupt the existing healthcare delivery systems. Primary telemedicine services should first be led by local healthcare institutions with referral and collaborative care involving higher-level hospitals provided as needed. To support this model, appropriate reimbursement systems and inter-institutional teleconsultation protocols must be established.

In summary, based on the results of our analysis of the current telemedicine pilot program, teleconsultation appears to be a practical alternative in medically underserved and inaccessible areas. However, as other studies have noted, telemedicine should be used as an ancillary tool, not a replacement, for in-person ophthalmologic services.7 Ultimately, these combined efforts aim to strengthen readiness, reduce preventable injury, and prevent loss of combat capability.
