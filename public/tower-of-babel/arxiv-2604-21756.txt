Maximum principle and local stability for a class of coupled nonlinear thermo–reaction–phase systems
Gossrin Jean-Marc Bomisso, Ali Ouattara Kouma, Marie Esther Anassé

Abstract
We study a nonlinear coupled system of partial differential equations arising from thermo–reaction–phase models. The system combines a heat diffusion equation, temperature-dependent chemical reactions of Arrhenius type, and a phase variable, and is formulated as a strongly coupled parabolic problem with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions. We first establish a maximum principle ensuring the positivity of the temperature on a suitable time interval, as well as the invariance of the physically admissible domain. In particular, we prove that the internal variables remain in the interval [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . We then analyse the asymptotic behaviour of the system in the free regime, that is, in the absence of external forcing. By introducing a relative energy functional and exploiting the structure of the coupling terms, we obtain local asymptotic stability of a homogeneous stationary state. The model belongs to a broader class of coupled diffusion–reaction–phase systems.

Introduction

Heat-sensitive materials constitute a class of engineering materials whose physical and chemical properties evolve significantly, and sometimes irreversibly, in response to a thermal stimulus. Among the most emblematic phenomena in this class, thermochromism refers to the modification of the optical absorption spectrum of a material under the effect of heat, leading to a macroscopically observable colour change. This phenomenon results from a fine coupling between heat transport, molecular chemical transformations and phase reorganisation, making it both a rich object of study from the modelling perspective and a delicate one from the mathematical analysis point of view. From an applicative standpoint, such materials appear in sectors as varied as intelligent food packaging, technical textiles, safety paints, passive display devices, and thermal protection of electronic components. A remarkable synthesis of the physical and chemical mechanisms underlying thermochromism in polymers is provided by Seeboth et al.
[
18
]
, who distinguish in particular the mechanisms of thermal isomerisation, supramolecular reorganisation, and proton transfer. MacLaren and White
[
10
]
have established design rules for reversible thermochromic mixtures based on leuco dyes, whose decolouration-recolouration kinetics constitutes precisely the central mechanism of the model studied here. More generally, the notion of stimuli-responsive material has been highlighted by Stuart et al.
[
19
]
, who survey the emerging applications of polymers capable of modifying their structure in response to external stimuli such as temperature, pH, or light. This unifying framework shows that the thermal variable should no longer be regarded as a mere external parameter, but as a dynamic quantity coupled to the internal state variables of the material.

From this perspective, the mathematical description of these phenomena naturally leads to strongly coupled nonlinear systems of partial differential equations. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
(
N\geq 1
) be a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
of class
C^{1,1}
, and let
\mathcal{T}>0

\theta
(in kelvin), the active chemical fraction
c
representing the proportion of the coloured form of the material, and the macroscopic phase variable
\phi
describing the visibility state of the material. These three unknowns are governed by

\left\{\begin{aligned} \rho c_{p}\,\partial_{t}\theta-\nabla\cdot\bigl(k(\phi)\nabla\theta\bigr)&=H_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t)+\alpha L_{c}\,\partial_{t}c+\alpha L_{\phi}\,\partial_{t}\phi&&\text{in }Q_{\mathcal{T}},\\[4.0pt]
\partial_{t}c-d_{c}\,\Delta c&=-\beta K_{d}(\theta)\,c+\gamma K_{r}(\theta)\,(1-c)&&\text{in }Q_{\mathcal{T}},\\[4.0pt]
\tau_{\phi}\,\partial_{t}\phi-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\phi+F^{\prime}(\phi)&=\lambda(c-\phi)&&\text{in }Q_{\mathcal{T}},\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

complemented by the homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions

\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=0,\quad\frac{\partial c}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=0,\quad\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,\mathcal{T}),

\theta(\cdot,0)=\theta_{0},\quad c(\cdot,0)=c_{0},\quad\phi(\cdot,0)=\phi_{0}\quad\text{in }\Omega.

[

established fundamental results for the Cahn–Hilliard equation, while Evans, Soner and Souganidis
[

shed light on the connection between the Allen–Cahn equation and motion by mean curvature. The monograph of Miranville
[

provides a comprehensive overview of the analysis of Cahn–Hilliard and Allen–Cahn type systems. Added to this family of models is the central reference of the Caginalp model
[
3

, which couples temperature and order parameter via a thermal relaxation term; Colli and Sprekels
[

subsequently studied several variants of this framework by proving the existence of global weak solutions. The Penrose–Fife model
[

[

illuminate the difficulties related to nonlinear dependencies on

Finally, concerning the asymptotic behaviour, the general theory of dissipative dynamical systems developed by Temam

The present work stands at the confluence of these different streams, but distinguishes itself from them by a richer and more strongly coupled structure. Indeed, the system (
1.1
) introduces a chemical variable
c
subject to a reversible Arrhenius-type kinetics, with coexistence of decolouration (
K_{d}
) and recolouration (
K_{r}
) mechanisms, and couples this variable to an Allen–Cahn type phase dynamics via the term
\lambda(c-\phi)
. Moreover, the heat equation is affected by time-derivative coupling terms,
\alpha L_{c}\,\partial_{t}c
and
\alpha L_{\phi}\,\partial_{t}\phi
, which strengthens the nonlinearity of the problem and complicates the construction of energy estimates. To the best of our knowledge, this architecture does not appear in classical models of thermochromism. The first is the singularity of the Arrhenius rates near
\theta=0
, which requires guaranteeing strict positivity of the temperature even before the equations on
c
can be written. The second is the strongly non-symmetric nature of the coupling between the three equations, since each possesses its own structure: nonlinear diffusion for heat, semilinear reaction-diffusion for
c
, and an Allen–Cahn type equation for
\phi
. The third is the hierarchical interdependence of the a priori bounds: the positivity of
\theta
conditions the well-definedness of
K_{d}(\theta)
and
K_{r}(\theta)
, which in turn conditions the control of
c
, which itself appears in the control of
\phi
via the forcing term
\lambda(c-\phi)
. More recent contributions have also investigated multiphysics phase-field systems
involving thermal and reactive couplings. In this direction, Miranville et al. in
[
13
]
studied the asymptotic behaviour
of a coupled Cahn-Hilliard/Allen-Cahn system with temperature, establishing the
existence of exponential and finite-dimensional global attractors under both the
classical Fourier heat law and a type III heat conduction law. This framework was
further extended by Makki et al. (see
[
11
]
),
who incorporated singular potentials of logarithmic type into the same class of
thermally coupled phase-field systems, and derived additional regularity results
including a strict separation property from the pure phases. From a different
perspective, Ntsokongo in
[
15
]
analysed an Allen-Cahn type equation
nonlinearly coupled with a temperature equation and motivated by applications in
chemistry, proving well-posedness as well as the existence of finite-dimensional
global and exponential attractors.

The analysis carried out here rests on a two-step strategy. In the first step, we establish a maximum principle adapted to the system, which ensures the preservation of the physically admissible domain and, in particular, the positivity of the temperature on an appropriate time interval. This result is fundamental since it guarantees that the Arrhenius kinetic laws remain well-defined and bounded, while establishing, as a mathematical consequence, that
c
and
\phi
remain in the interval
[0,1]
for all time. In the second step, we study the local asymptotic stability of a homogeneous stationary state by means of a suitably chosen relative energy functional. The idea consists of linearising the system around this state, testing the perturbed equations by the unknowns themselves, and then combining the resulting estimates with Poincaré and Gronwall type inequalities in order to obtain exponential decay under appropriate coercivity assumptions. The model studied in the present work belongs
to this broader class of coupled diffusion–reaction–phase systems, but
distinguishes itself by the simultaneous presence of Arrhenius-type chemical
kinetics, a reversible reaction-diffusion equation for the active fraction, and
an Allen–Cahn phase dynamics, all three of which interact through the thermal
variable.

The organisation of the paper is as follows. Section
2
gathers the preliminary tools needed for the analysis, in particular the positive and negative parts of a measurable function, the Gronwall lemma, and the abstract parabolic maximum principle. Section
3
is devoted to the maximum principle for the system, establishing sequentially the positivity of
\theta
, then the invariance of
[0,1]
for
c
, and then for
\phi
. Section
4
develops the local asymptotic stability analysis. Finally, a general conclusion synthesises the main results obtained, puts their scope in perspective, and outlines directions for future research, notably with a view to a global analysis and possible numerical validations.

Definition 2.1

(
Sub-solution and super-solution

[
7
]
)
.

Let
f:Q_{\mathcal{T}}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function that is Lipschitz continuous with respect to its last variable. We say that
\underline{u}
(resp.
\overline{u}
) is a
sub-solution
(resp.
super-solution
) of the parabolic problem with Neumann conditions

\partial_{t}u-\nabla\cdot(a(x)\nabla u)=f(x,t,u),\quad\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\quad u(\cdot,0)=u_{0},

if
\underline{u}
(resp.
\overline{u}
) possesses the same regularity properties as a weak solution and satisfies

\partial_{t}\underline{u}-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla\underline{u})\leq f(x,t,\underline{u})\quad(\text{resp. }\partial_{t}\overline{u}-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla\overline{u})\geq f(x,t,\overline{u}))\quad\text{a.e. in }Q_{\mathcal{T}},

\frac{\partial\underline{u}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}\leq 0\quad\left(\text{resp. }\frac{\partial\overline{u}}{\partial\mathbf{n}}\geq 0\right)\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

\underline{u}(x,0)\leq u_{0}(x)\quad(\text{resp. }\overline{u}(x,0)\geq u_{0}(x)).

Definition 2.2

(
Positive and negative parts

[
21
]
)
.

For any measurable function

, we define

\left[u\right]^{+}(x)=\max(u(x),0),\qquad\left[u\right]^{-}(x)=\max(-u(x),0).

We have

u=\left[u\right]^{+}-\left[u\right]^{-},\;|u|=\left[u\right]^{+}+\left[u\right]^{-},\;\left[u\right]^{+}\cdot\left[u\right]^{-}=0.

If

, then
\left[u\right]^{+},\left[u\right]^{-}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
with

\nabla\left[u\right]^{+}=\mathbf{1}_{\{u>0\}}\nabla u,\qquad\nabla\left[u\right]^{-}=-\mathbf{1}_{\{u<0\}}\nabla u\quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega.

Lemma 2.3

(
Gronwall, integral form

[
7
]
)
.

Let
\psi:[0,\mathcal{T}]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be an absolutely continuous function satisfying

\psi(t)\leq a+b\int_{0}^{t}\psi(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

for almost every
t\in(0,\mathcal{T})
, with

. Then
\psi(t)\leq a\,e^{bt}
for all
t\in[0,\mathcal{T}]
.

Theorem 2.4

(

[
7
]
)
.

Let

be a weak solution of

, with

is a sub-solution with

. Likewise, if

is a super-solution with

3.1
Positivity of the temperature

Theorem 3.1

(
Maximum principle for

\theta
)
.

Under hypotheses
(H1)
-
(H6)
, any solution
\theta
of the system (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) satisfies, for almost every
(x,t)\in Q_{\mathcal{T}}
,

\theta(x,t)\geq\underline{\theta}(t)>0,

where
\underline{\theta}(t)=\theta_{*}-\dfrac{C_{0}}{\rho c_{p}}\,t
, and this holds for all

t\in[0,T_{0})
, with

T_{0}=\frac{\rho c_{p}\,\theta_{*}}{C_{0}}>0\qquad\bigl(\text{with the convention }T_{0}=+\infty\text{ if }C_{0}=0\bigr).

Proof.

We introduce the affine function

\underline{\theta}(t)=\theta_{*}-\frac{C_{0}}{\rho c_{p}}\,t,

which is strictly positive on
[0,T_{0})
. This function (independent of
x
) is a sub-solution of the first equation of the system. Indeed, since
\nabla\underline{\theta}=0
, the diffusion term vanishes. The time derivative is
\partial_{t}\underline{\theta}=-\frac{C_{0}}{\rho c_{p}}
, so that:

\rho c_{p}\,\partial_{t}\underline{\theta}-\nabla\cdot(k(\phi)\nabla\underline{\theta})=-C_{0}\leq H_{\mathrm{ext}}+\alpha L_{c}\,\partial_{t}c+\alpha L_{\phi}\,\partial_{t}\phi\quad\text{a.e. in }Q_{\mathcal{T}},

by hypothesis
(H4)
. Moreover,
\underline{\theta}(0)=\theta_{*}\leq\theta_{0}(x)

a.e. in
\Omega
, and
\nabla\underline{\theta}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

Set
w=\theta-\underline{\theta}
. By linearity,
w
satisfies

\rho c_{p}\,\partial_{t}w-\nabla\cdot(k(\phi)\nabla w)=S(x,t)+C_{0}\geq 0\quad\text{a.e. in }Q_{\mathcal{T}},

with
\nabla w\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
and
w(\cdot,0)=\theta_{0}-\theta_{*}\geq 0
.

We multiply the equation on
w
by
-\left[w\right]^{-}\leq 0
and integrate over
\Omega
. Using

\partial_{t}w\cdot\left[w\right]^{-}=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}([\left[w\right]^{-}]^{2})
and integration by parts,

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot(k(\phi)\nabla w)\cdot(-\left[w\right]^{-})\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}k(\phi)|\nabla\left[w\right]^{-}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 0,

we obtain:

\frac{\rho c_{p}}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left\|\left[w\right]^{-}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+k_{*}\left\|\nabla\left[w\right]^{-}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 0.

Hence

is non-increasing. Since

w(\cdot,0)\geq 0
implies
\left[w\right]^{-}(\cdot,0)=0
, we conclude:

\left\|\left[w\right]^{-}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=0\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,T_{0}),

whence

, i.e.,
\theta(x,t)\geq\underline{\theta}(t)>0
a.e. in
Q_{\mathcal{T}}
.

In particular, for every
\mathcal{T}<T_{0}

\underline{\theta}_{\mathcal{T}}=\min_{t\in[0,\mathcal{T}]}\underline{\theta}(t)=\theta_{*}-\frac{C_{0}}{\rho c_{p}}\,\mathcal{T}>0.

(3.1)

Since

is decreasing on

\theta(x,t)\geq\underline{\theta}_{\mathcal{T}}\quad\text{a.e. in }Q_{\mathcal{T}}.

\Omega

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}c\cdot(-\left[c\right]^{-})\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left\|\left[c\right]^{-}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
,
and the diffusion term, after integration by parts, gives

d_{c}\left\|\nabla\left[c\right]^{-}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq 0
. For the reaction term, on the set

\{c<0\}
where
\left[c\right]^{-}=-c>0
:

\displaystyle(-\beta K_{d}(\theta)\,c+\gamma K_{r}(\theta)(1-c))\cdot(-\left[c\right]^{-})

\displaystyle=(-\beta K_{d}\cdot(-\left[c\right]^{-})+\gamma K_{r}(1+\left[c\right]^{-}))\cdot(-\left[c\right]^{-})

\displaystyle=-\beta K_{d}(\left[c\right]^{-})^{2}-\gamma K_{r}\left[c\right]^{-}-\gamma K_{r}(\left[c\right]^{-})^{2}\leq 0,

since
K_{d},K_{r}\geq 0
(Corollary
3.3
) and
\left[c\right]^{-}\geq 0
. We obtain:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left\|\left[c\right]^{-}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+d_{c}\left\|\nabla\left[c\right]^{-}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 0.

The monotone decrease of
t\mapsto\left\|\left[c\right]^{-}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
and the initial condition

c_{0}\geq 0
conclude.

Upper bound (
c\leq 1
).

Set
v=c-1
, which satisfies

\partial_{t}v-d_{c}\Delta v=-(\beta K_{d}+\gamma K_{r})\,v-\beta K_{d}
,
with
v(\cdot,0)=c_{0}-1\leq 0
and homogeneous Neumann conditions. We multiply by
\left[v\right]^{+}\geq 0
.
The reaction term on
\{v>0\}=\{c>1\}
equals

[-(\beta K_{d}+\gamma K_{r})(\left[v\right]^{+})^{2}-\beta K_{d}\left[v\right]^{+}]\leq 0
, hence:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left\|\left[v\right]^{+}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+d_{c}\left\|\nabla\left[v\right]^{+}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 0.

The initial condition
\left[v(\cdot,0)\right]^{+}=0
concludes.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The proof of the upper bound
c\leq 1
uses only
K_{d},K_{r}\geq 0
,
\beta,\gamma\geq 0

and
c_{0}\leq 1
. It does not directly require the positivity of
\theta
, which enters
only indirectly to ensure that
K_{d}(\theta)
and
K_{r}(\theta)
are well-defined. The
lower bound
c\geq 0
, in turn, uses
\gamma K_{r}(\theta)\geq 0
, which is automatic
as soon as
\theta>0
. Both proofs are therefore logically conditioned by
Theorem
3.1
.

3.3
Invariance of
[0,1]
for the phase variable
\phi

We begin with a lemma on the sign of
F^{\prime}
at the boundary of the interval
[0,1]
,
which is indispensable for handling the term
F^{\prime}(\phi)
in the subsequent proofs.

Lemma 3.6

(
Sign of

F^{\prime}

at the boundary of

[0,1]
)
.

With
F^{\prime}(\phi)=\tfrac{1}{2}\phi(1-\phi)(1-2\phi)
, we have:

•

For
\phi<0
:
F^{\prime}(\phi)\cdot\left[\phi\right]^{-}\leq 0
, i.e.,

F^{\prime}(\phi)\cdot(-\left[\phi\right]^{-})\geq 0
.

•

For
\phi>1
:
F^{\prime}(\phi)\cdot\left[\phi-1\right]^{+}\geq 0
.

Proof.

For
\phi<0
, we have
1-\phi>0
and
1-2\phi>0
, so

F^{\prime}(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\phi(1-\phi)(1-2\phi)\leq 0
(since
\phi<0
),
and
\left[\phi\right]^{-}=-\phi>0
, whence
F^{\prime}(\phi)\cdot\left[\phi\right]^{-}\leq 0
.

For
\phi>1
, we have
\phi>0
,
(\phi-1)>0
and
1-2\phi<0
, hence

F^{\prime}(\phi)=-\frac{1}{2}\phi(\phi-1)(-(1-2\phi))=\frac{1}{2}\phi(\phi-1)(2\phi-1)\geq 0
,
and
\left[\phi-1\right]^{+}=\phi-1>0
, whence
F^{\prime}(\phi)\cdot\left[\phi-1\right]^{+}\geq 0
.
∎

Theorem 3.7

(
Confinement of
\phi
in

[0,1]
)
.

Under hypotheses
(H1)
-
(H6)
and assuming that

c\in[0,1]
a.e. in
Q_{\mathcal{T}}

(Theorem
3.4
)
, any solution
\phi

of the system (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) satisfies:

0\leq\phi(x,t)\leq 1\quad\text{a.e. in }Q_{\mathcal{T}}.

Proof.

Lower bound (
\phi\geq 0
).

We multiply the third equation of (
1.1
) by
-\left[\phi\right]^{-}\leq 0
and integrate
over
\Omega
. The time term gives
\frac{\tau_{\phi}}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left\|\left[\phi\right]^{-}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
.
The diffusion term gives
\varepsilon^{2}\left\|\nabla\left[\phi\right]^{-}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq 0
after
integration by parts. The potential term satisfies

\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime}(\phi)\cdot(-\left[\phi\right]^{-})\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 0
by Lemma
3.6
. For the
forcing term, on
\{\phi<0\}
with
\left[\phi\right]^{-}=-\phi>0
and
c\geq 0
:

\lambda(c-\phi)(-\left[\phi\right]^{-})=-\lambda c\left[\phi\right]^{-}-\lambda[\left[\phi\right]^{-}]^{2}\leq 0.

We therefore obtain:

\frac{\tau_{\phi}}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left\|\left[\phi\right]^{-}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\left\|\nabla\left[\phi\right]^{-}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 0.

The monotone decrease of
t\mapsto\left\|\left[\phi\right]^{-}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
and the condition

\phi_{0}\geq 0
conclude.

Upper bound (
\phi\leq 1
).

Set
\psi=\phi-1
, which satisfies

\tau_{\phi}\,\partial_{t}\psi-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\psi+F^{\prime}(\psi+1)=\lambda(c-\psi-1)
,
with
\psi(\cdot,0)=\phi_{0}-1\leq 0
and homogeneous Neumann conditions. We multiply by

\left[\psi\right]^{+}\geq 0
. The potential term satisfies

\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime}(\phi)\left[\psi\right]^{+}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq 0
by Lemma
3.6
. For the
forcing term, on
\{\psi>0\}=\{\phi>1\}
, using
c\leq 1
:

\lambda(c-\phi)\left[\psi\right]^{+}=\lambda(c-1)\left[\psi\right]^{+}-\lambda[\left[\psi\right]^{+}]^{2}\leq 0,

since
c-1\leq 0
and
\left[\psi\right]^{+}\geq 0
. We obtain:

\frac{\tau_{\phi}}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left\|\left[\psi\right]^{+}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\left\|\nabla\left[\psi\right]^{+}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\left[\psi\right]^{+}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 0.

The initial condition
\left[\psi(\cdot,0)\right]^{+}=0
concludes.
∎

4
Local asymptotic stability analysis

The results of the preceding section ensure the invariance of the domain

\mathcal{D}=\{(\theta,c,\phi):\theta>0,\;0\leq c\leq 1,\;0\leq\phi\leq 1\}

on every interval
[0,\mathcal{T}]
with
\mathcal{T}<T_{0}
, as well as the existence of a uniform bound

\theta(x,t)\geq\underline{\theta}_{\mathcal{T}}>0\quad\text{in }Q_{\mathcal{T}}.

These properties allow us to control the nonlinearities of the system, in particular the Arrhenius-type reaction terms.

We are interested in this section in the long-time behaviour of the system in the free regime, that is, in the absence of external forcing (
H_{\mathrm{ext}}=0
). We analyse the stability of a homogeneous stationary state
(\bar{\theta},\bar{c},\bar{\phi})
by means of an energy method.

4.1
Perturbation system

We introduce the perturbations

u=\theta-\bar{\theta},\qquad v=c-\bar{c},\qquad w=\phi-\bar{\phi}.

Substituting into (
1.1
), the perturbation system reads:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \rho c_{p}\partial_{t}u-\nabla\cdot(k(\phi)\nabla u)&=\alpha L_{c}\partial_{t}v+\alpha L_{\phi}\partial_{t}w,\\[4.0pt]
\partial_{t}v-d_{c}\Delta v&=\mathcal{R}(\theta,c)-\mathcal{R}(\bar{\theta},\bar{c}),\\[4.0pt]
\tau_{\phi}\partial_{t}w-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta w+(F^{\prime}(\phi)-F^{\prime}(\bar{\phi}))&=\lambda(v-w),\end{aligned}\right.

(4.1)

with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, where we have set

\mathcal{R}(\theta,c)=-\beta K_{d}(\theta)\,c+\gamma K_{r}(\theta)\,(1-c)
.

4.2
Relative energy functional and coercivity

We associate to the perturbations
(u,v,w)
the relative energy functional:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\rho c_{p}}{2}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\tau_{\phi}}{2}\left\|w(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left\|\nabla w(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

(4.2)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}G(w(t))\,\mathrm{d}x-\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}u\,v\,\mathrm{d}x-\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}u\,w\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
G(z)=F(\bar{\phi}+z)-F(\bar{\phi})-F^{\prime}(\bar{\phi})\,z
is the residual quadratic part
of the potential
F
near
\bar{\phi}
.

Lemma 4.1

(
Local coercivity of the energy
)
.

If
\mathcal{T}<T_{0}
, there exist
\delta_{0}>0
and
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that
if
\left\|w\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\delta_{0}
and
\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{0})
, where
\alpha_{0}
is defined in (
1.6
), then

C_{1}\bigl(\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|w\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\bigr)\leq\mathcal{E}(t)\leq C_{2}\bigl(\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|w\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\bigr).

Proof.

Assume
\mathcal{T}<T_{0}
. Since
F\in C^{2}
and
F^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\phi})>0
, there exist
\delta_{0},m_{F},M_{F}>0
such that

m_{F}z^{2}\leq G(z)\leq M_{F}z^{2}
for
|z|\leq\delta_{0}
. By integration,

m_{F}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}G(w)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq M_{F}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
.
The cross terms are controlled by Young’s inequality:

\left|\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}u\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\frac{\rho c_{p}}{4}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\alpha^{2}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad\left|\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}u\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\frac{\rho c_{p}}{4}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\alpha^{2}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

For
\alpha<\alpha_{0}
, the conditions

\dfrac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}^{2}}{\rho c_{p}}<\dfrac{1}{2}

and

\dfrac{\alpha^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}}{\rho c_{p}}<\dfrac{2m_{F}+\tau_{\phi}}{2}

are satisfied, and the cross terms are absorbed into the diagonal terms.
∎

4.3
Lipschitz continuity of the reaction term

Lemma 4.2

(
Lipschitz control of

\mathcal{R}
)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{T}<T_{0}
. The function

\mathcal{R}(\theta,c)=-\beta K_{d}(\theta)\,c+\gamma K_{r}(\theta)\,(1-c)

is Lipschitz continuous in
(\theta,c)
on
[\underline{\theta}_{\mathcal{T}},\infty)\times[0,1]
: there
exists
L_{R}>0
such that

|\mathcal{R}(\theta,c)-\mathcal{R}(\bar{\theta},\bar{c})|\leq L_{R}\bigl(|u|+|v|\bigr)\quad\text{a.e. in }Q_{T}.

Proof.

Assume
\mathcal{T}<T_{0}
. The functions
K_{d}
and
K_{r}
are of class
C^{1}
on
(0,\infty)
, hence Lipschitz continuous
on every compact subset of
[\underline{\theta}_{\mathcal{T}},\infty)
. Since
c,\bar{c}\in[0,1]
, the conclusion
follows by a direct computation using the triangle inequality on each term.
∎

4.4
Local asymptotic stability

Theorem 4.3

(
Local asymptotic stability
)
.

Under hypotheses
(H7)
-
(H10)
, there exist
\delta>0
and
\kappa>0
such
that if

\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|v_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|w_{0}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\delta,

then the corresponding solution satisfies, for all
t\in[0,T]
:

\mathcal{E}(t)\leq\mathcal{E}(0)\,e^{-\kappa t}.

In particular,

\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|v(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|w(t)\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\,e^{-\kappa t},

and therefore
(\theta,c,\phi)\to(\bar{\theta},\bar{c},\bar{\phi})
as
t\to+\infty
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in seven steps. For simplicity, we adopt the notation
\left\|\cdot\right\|=\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.

Step 1: Thermal estimate.

We test the first equation of (
4.1
) by
u
:

\frac{\rho c_{p}}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|u\right\|^{2}+k_{*}\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}\leq\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}v\,u\,\mathrm{d}x+\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}w\,u\,\mathrm{d}x,

(4.3)

using
k(\phi)\geq k_{*}>0
by
(H1)
.

Step 2: Chemical estimate.

We test the second equation of (
4.1
) by
v
:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|v\right\|^{2}+d_{c}\left\|\nabla v\right\|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{R}(\theta,c)-\mathcal{R}(\bar{\theta},\bar{c}))\,v\,\mathrm{d}x.

By Lemma
4.2
and Young’s inequality (
\eta>0
to be chosen):

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|v\right\|^{2}+d_{c}\left\|\nabla v\right\|^{2}\leq\eta\left\|u\right\|^{2}+C_{\eta}\left\|v\right\|^{2}.

(4.4)

Step 3: Phase estimate.

We test the third equation of (
4.1
) by
w
:

\frac{\tau_{\phi}}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|w\right\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\left\|\nabla w\right\|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}(F^{\prime}(\phi)-F^{\prime}(\bar{\phi}))\,w\,\mathrm{d}x=\lambda\int_{\Omega}v\,w\,\mathrm{d}x-\lambda\left\|w\right\|^{2}.

Under hypothesis
F^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\phi})>0
, there exists
m_{F}>0
such that for
w
sufficiently small,

\int_{\Omega}(F^{\prime}(\phi)-F^{\prime}(\bar{\phi}))\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\geq m_{F}\left\|w\right\|^{2}
. By Young’s inequality:

\frac{\tau_{\phi}}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|w\right\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\left\|\nabla w\right\|^{2}+\left(m_{F}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\left\|w\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|v\right\|^{2}.

(4.5)

Step 4: Time derivative of the cross terms and cancellation.

We compute the time derivative of the two cross terms of

\mathcal{E}(t)
defined in (
4.2
).

(i)

By Leibniz’s rule, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(-\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}u\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\right)=-\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}u)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x-\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}u\,(\partial_{t}v)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(4.6)

The second term (s.t.) of (
4.6
) exactly cancels the contribution

+\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}v)\,u\,\mathrm{d}x
appearing on the right-hand side
of (
4.3
):

\underbrace{+\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}v)\,u\,\mathrm{d}x}_{\text{Step 1}}+\underbrace{\left(-\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}u\,(\partial_{t}v)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)}_{\text{s.t. of (4.6)}}=0.

It remains to handle
-\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}u)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x
.
We extract
\partial_{t}u
from the first equation of (
4.1
):

\rho c_{p}\,\partial_{t}u=\nabla\cdot\bigl(k(\phi)\nabla u\bigr)+\alpha L_{c}\,\partial_{t}v+\alpha L_{\phi}\,\partial_{t}w,

whence, after dividing by
\rho c_{p}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}u)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x&=-\frac{\alpha L_{c}}{\rho c_{p}}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\bigl(k(\phi)\nabla u\bigr)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad-\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}^{2}}{\rho c_{p}}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}v)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}L_{\phi}}{\rho c_{p}}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}w)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(4.7)

For the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.7
), integration by parts under homogeneous
Neumann conditions gives

-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\bigl(k(\phi)\nabla u\bigr)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}k(\phi)\,\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x,

and Young’s inequality with
\varepsilon_{1}>0
, using

k(\phi)\leq k^{*}
by
(H1)
, yields

\frac{\alpha L_{c}}{\rho c_{p}}\left|\int_{\Omega}k(\phi)\,\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\frac{\alpha L_{c}\,k^{*}}{\rho c_{p}}\left\|\nabla u\right\|\,\left\|\nabla v\right\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2}\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}^{2}\,(k^{*})^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{1}\,\rho^{2}c_{p}^{2}}\left\|\nabla v\right\|^{2}.

For the second term on the right-hand side of (
4.7
), we have

-\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}^{2}}{\rho c_{p}}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}v)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}^{2}}{2\rho c_{p}}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|v\right\|^{2},

which is absorbed into the term
\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{d}{dt}\left\|v\right\|^{2}
from (
4.4
) since
\alpha<\alpha_{0}
implies

\dfrac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}^{2}}{\rho c_{p}}<\dfrac{1}{2}<1
.
For the third term on the right-hand side of (
4.7
), Young’s inequality gives

\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}L_{\phi}}{\rho c_{p}}\left|\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}w)\,v\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}L_{\phi}}{2\rho c_{p}}\bigl(\left\|\partial_{t}w\right\|^{2}+\left\|v\right\|^{2}\bigr),

terms which will be absorbed into the dissipative contributions of
(
4.5
) and into
\mu_{4}\left\|w\right\|^{2}
from Step 5 for small
\alpha
.

(ii)

By a strictly analogous computation, we find

\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(-\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}u\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\right)=-\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}u)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x-\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}u\,(\partial_{t}w)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(4.8)

The second term on the right-hand side of (
4.8
) exactly cancels the contribution

+\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}w)\,u\,\mathrm{d}x
from (
4.3
) in Step 1:

\underbrace{+\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}w)\,u\,\mathrm{d}x}_{\text{Step 1}}+\underbrace{\left(-\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}u\,(\partial_{t}w)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)}_{\text{s.t. of (4.8)}}=0.

For the remaining term
-\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}u)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x
,
we again substitute
\rho c_{p}\,\partial_{t}u
from (
4.1
) to obtain

\displaystyle-\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}u)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha L_{\phi}}{\rho c_{p}}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\bigl(k(\phi)\nabla u\bigr)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}L_{\phi}}{\rho c_{p}}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}v)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}}{\rho c_{p}}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}w)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x.

After integration by parts and Young’s inequality with

\varepsilon_{2}>0
, we obtain

\frac{\alpha L_{\phi}}{\rho c_{p}}\left|\int_{\Omega}k(\phi)\,\nabla u\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\,(k^{*})^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{2}\,\rho^{2}c_{p}^{2}}\left\|\nabla w\right\|^{2}.

The term involving
\partial_{t}w
gives

-\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}}{\rho c_{p}}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}w)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}}{2\rho c_{p}}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|w\right\|^{2},

which is absorbed into
\dfrac{\tau_{\phi}}{2}\dfrac{d}{dt}\left\|w\right\|^{2}
from
(
4.5
) since
\alpha<\alpha_{0}
implies

\dfrac{\alpha^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}}{\rho c_{p}}<\tau_{\phi}
.
The remaining cross term is handled by Young’s inequality:

\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}L_{\phi}}{\rho c_{p}}\left|\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}v)\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}L_{\phi}}{2\rho c_{p}}\bigl(\left\|\partial_{t}v\right\|^{2}+\left\|w\right\|^{2}\bigr).

(iii)

Gathering the contributions from Steps (i) and (ii), we obtain,
for all
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
and
\alpha
sufficiently small, that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(-\alpha L_{c}\int_{\Omega}uv\,\mathrm{d}x-\alpha L_{\phi}\int_{\Omega}uw\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

\displaystyle\leq(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}+C_{\varepsilon_{1}}\alpha^{2}\left\|\nabla v\right\|^{2}+C_{\varepsilon_{2}}\alpha^{2}\left\|\nabla w\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+C\alpha^{2}\bigl(\left\|v\right\|^{2}+\left\|w\right\|^{2}\bigr),

where the constants
C_{\varepsilon_{i}}>0
depend only on

L_{c},L_{\phi},k^{*},\rho,c_{p}
.
We choose
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=\dfrac{k_{*}}{4}
, so that

\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}=\dfrac{k_{*}}{2}
, in order to absorb these terms
into
k_{*}\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}
from Step 1. For
\alpha<\alpha_{0}
, the conditions

\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{c}^{2}\,(k^{*})^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{1}\,\rho^{2}c_{p}^{2}}\leq\frac{d_{c}}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\,(k^{*})^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{2}\,\rho^{2}c_{p}^{2}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}

are satisfied, and the contributions of order
\alpha^{2}
are absorbed into

d_{c}\left\|\nabla v\right\|^{2}
and
\varepsilon^{2}\left\|\nabla w\right\|^{2}
from Steps 2 and 3. This prepares the global energy inequality of Step 5.

Step 5: Global energy inequality.

Summing the estimates from Steps 1 to 4, with
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=\dfrac{k_{*}}{4}
,

\eta\leq\dfrac{\mu_{1}C_{P}}{2}
and
\alpha<\alpha_{0}
, we obtain:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t)+\mu_{1}\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}+\mu_{2}\left\|\nabla v\right\|^{2}+\mu_{3}\left\|\nabla w\right\|^{2}+\mu_{4}\left\|w\right\|^{2}\leq C\bigl(\left\|u\right\|^{2}+\left\|v\right\|^{2}\bigr),

with constants
\mu_{i}>0
.

Step 6: Closure by Poincaré’s inequality.

Since
\int_{\Omega}u\,\mathrm{d}x=0
for all time (conservation of the thermal mean under homogeneous Neumann conditions and
H_{\mathrm{ext}}=0
), Poincaré’s inequality
then gives
\left\|u\right\|^{2}\leq C_{P}\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}
. For

Conclusion

In this work, we have studied a strongly coupled thermo-reaction-phase system modelling the thermal degradation of heat-sensitive materials. Despite the presence of singular Arrhenius-type nonlinearities and couplings involving time derivatives, we have established a maximum principle ensuring the positivity of the temperature as well as the invariance of the physically admissible domain. We have then proved the local asymptotic stability of a homogeneous stationary state in the free regime, by means of a suitably chosen relative energy functional, leading to exponential decay of perturbations. Finally, the proposed system provides a mathematical framework for describing thermally induced degradation processes. More generally, it belongs to a broader class of coupled diffusion–reaction–phase systems, where the phase variable may represent different macroscopic quantities depending on the context. In this work, it is interpreted as a measure of visibility, allowing one to relate this phenomenon to physical parameters and environmental conditions. The present analysis establishes the theoretical foundations for subsequent quantitative studies, such as sensitivity analysis and parameter optimization, which will be addressed in future work.
