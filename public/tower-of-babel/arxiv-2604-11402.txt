Abstract
Scene change detection (SCD) is crucial for urban monitoring and navigation but remains challenging in real-world environments due to lighting variations, seasonal shifts, viewpoint differences, and complex urban layouts. Existing methods rely primarily on low-level visual features, limiting their ability to accurately identify changed objects amid the visual complexity of urban scenes. In this paper, we propose LangSCD, a vision–language framework for scene change detection that overcomes this single-modal limitation by incorporating semantic reasoning through language. Our approach introduces a modular language component that leverages vision-language models (VLMs) to generate textual descriptions of scene changes, which are fused with visual features through a cross-modal feature enhancer. We further introduce a geometric–semantic matching module that refines the predicted masks by enforcing semantic consistency and spatial completeness. Existing real-world scene change detection benchmarks provide only binary change annotations, which are insufficient for downstream applications requiring fine-grained understanding of scene dynamics. To address this limitation, we introduce NYC-CD, a large-scale dataset of 8,122 real-world image pairs collected in New York City with multiclass change annotations generated through a semi-automatic pipeline. Extensive experiments across multiple street-view benchmarks demonstrate that our language and matching modules consistently improve existing change-detection architectures, achieving state-of-the-art performance and highlighting the value of integrating linguistic reasoning with visual representations for robust scene change detection.

Scene Change Detection (SCD) is a fundamental research challenge with significant industrial applications. It addresses a core question: given two images of the same location at different times, how can we determine what has changed while distinguishing structural modifications from transient illumination variations (see Fig.
1
).
High-quality SCD provides the actionable signals necessary for database maintenance, map updating, and Visual Place Recognition (VPR).
By identifying these changes, new objects can be incorporated, removed structures can be pruned, and appearance changes can be reflected.
Crucially, this change signal should be
object–aware
(beyond coarse pixel differencing) so that updates target semantically meaningful entities rather than transient noise.

Two primary downstream applications depend on high-accuracy SCD:
(1)
Visual Place Recognition (VPR)
: VPR seeks to identify a previously visited location from visual input. However, performance often degrades as the temporal gap between query and database images widens, as shown in Fig.
2
2(a)
.
Seasonal shifts, urban construction, and vegetation growth gradually make static databases obsolete, causing retrieval recall to collapse. Fig.
2
2(b)
illustrates a real-world scene observed over several months, where construction has significantly altered the environment.
(2)
Long-term Dataset Maintenance
: A naïve maintenance strategy simply appends newly captured images to a database, leading to unbounded growth and slower retrieval. In contrast, SCD enables selective updates by identifying persistent structural modifications rather than transient visual distractors. This results in a more compact database, faster retrieval, and improved long-term robustness.
Beyond these, SCD enables applications such as anomaly detection
Huang et al. 2022
, mobile robotics
Nehmzow 2012
and autonomous vehicles
Janai et al. 2020
.

Despite significant progress, existing SCD solutions remain constrained by a single-modal bottleneck: they rely on low-level visual consistency while lacking high-level semantic priors to distinguish task-relevant changes from environmental noise. Simulation-based methods
Park et al. 2021
often fail to generalize to real environments, while fixed-viewpoint approaches
Wang et al. 2023b
cannot handle the unconstrained camera motion from mobile platforms. Recent pipelines leveraging vision foundation models
Kim and Kim 2025
produce fragmented predictions (see Fig.
1
), as they struggle to reason about what has changed in a semantically meaningful way.

This limitation is particularly acute in urban environments, which we highlight to distinguish from more structured indoor or satellite settings. Urban SCD presents a unique confluence of three challenges:
(1) Diverse Change Space: structural alterations—such as construction progress, storefront updates, and billboard changes—co-occur with dynamic traffic and foliage cycles.
(2) Unconstrained Acquisition: drastic viewpoint variations introduce depth-dependent parallax and occlusions, severely complicating spatial correspondence.
(3) High Appearance Volatility: extreme illumination shifts, shadows, and mirror reflections create visual “pseudo-changes” that mimic structural updates.

These challenges reveal a fundamental gap: existing SCD methods lack explicit, object-level semantic priors that help distinguish meaningful structural changes from nuisance visual variations. Purely visual pipelines struggle to reason about
what
changed in complex urban scenes, while directly applying off-the-shelf Vision-Language Models (VLMs) is insufficient. Although VLMs possess strong semantic reasoning capabilities, they often fail to respect the geometric constraints of street-view imagery; under large viewpoint shifts they frequently produce hallucinated regions or coarse localization rather than precise change masks. To address these limitations, we propose
LangSCD, a vision-language framework that tightly couples language-derived semantics with robust visual features. Our key insight is that language can provide structured semantic priors describing
what
changed, while geometric reasoning ensures spatial consistency across views. Specifically, we introduce a Cross-modal Feature Enhancer that injects language-derived semantic cues into visual representations to suppress appearance-driven pseudo-changes, and a Geometric–Semantic Matching Module that refines predictions by enforcing spatial completeness and cross-view consistency. Together, these components enable reliable object-level change detection in unconstrained urban environments.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.

•

We present NYC-CD, a new real-world street-view SCD benchmark. This is the first multi-class SCD benchmark featuring object, appearance, and viewpoint changes in diverse urban environments. To build this benchmark, we provide a scalable, semi-automatic data annotation pipeline that yields high-quality, object-level change masks.

•

We propose LangSCD, a modular framework for SCD that injects language priors about changes and fuses them with visual features to produce precise object-aware change masks robust to appearance and viewpoint shifts. LangSCD enables robust urban SCD under viewpoint variation, detecting object, appearance, and viewpoint-induced changes jointly.

•

Strong experimental evidence demonstrates that our plug-and-play modules consistently improve performance across multiple SCD backbones. The gains remain robust under different choices of VLMs including open-source and proprietary models. LangSCD outperforms existing uni-modal or naive VLM-based approaches on both binary and multi-class SCD.

3.1

Overall Architecture

LangSCD integrates language guidance into scene change detection, addressing the fundamental limitations of uni-modal approaches. As illustrated in Figure
3
, a standard SCD backbone consists of a shared-weight image encoder, a feature merging module, and a segmentation head; existing methods primarily differ in whether the encoder is CNN-based, DINOv2-based, or SAM-based
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Lin et al. 2025
;
Kim and Kim 2025
. We augment this pipeline with two plug-and-play modules. Specifically, the cross-modal feature enhancer takes the encoded feature of Image
T_{1}
and enhances it with text features encoding descriptions of new objects in
T_{1}
. The enhanced
T_{1}
feature is then compared with the Image
T_{0}
feature using the original backbone’s merging module and segmentation head, producing an initial pixel-level change mask. Building upon this output, the geometric and semantic matching modules are applied to refine fragmented initial mask into object-consistent masks by jointly leveraging SAM2’s class-agnostic tracking and Grounded SAM’s semantically informed segmentation. This design yields a new end-to-end architecture that integrates
semantic reasoning and geometric consistency
in a simple yet effective manner, enabling existing SCD models to address the previously under-explored challenge of street-view semantic change detection.

3.2

Language Module and Cross-modal Enhancer

The language module provides semantic guidance to the SCD pipeline through vision–language reasoning. Given an image pair, a Visual-Language Model (VLM) such as GPT-4o, generates a natural language description of objects that appear in
T_{1}
but not in
T_{0}
, and objects with different appearances. This description serves as a high-level hypothesis of potential scene changes. The generated text is then encoded by a BERT
Devlin et al. 2019
text backbone, from which we extract the full sequence of token embeddings from the final hidden layer to obtain text features.

To integrate language features with visual representations from
F_{1}
, we employ a cross-modal feature enhancer adapted from GroundingDINO
Liu et al. 2024
. As illustrated in Fig.
3
block 2, Each enhancement layer performs deformable self-attention over image tokens, enabling adaptive focus on relevant regions, while text tokens undergo self-attention to preserve linguistic structure. Cross-modal interaction is achieved through bidirectional per-token cross-attention:

•

Text-to-image attention
allows each spatial image token to attend over all BERT tokens, grounding textual concepts in specific visual regions.

•

Image-to-text attention
enables each text token to refine its embedding using the corresponding visual context, reinforcing semantic alignment.

This cross-modal interaction operates at the token level—each image token attends to all text tokens and vice versa—producing enhanced visual features
F_{1}^{\prime}
that combine spatial localization with semantic cues from the language module.

3.3

Geometric and Semantic Matching Module

The geometric and semantic matching module refines the initial segmentation masks to elevate change detection from pixel-level responses to object-level reasoning. It combines complementary matching strategies to address two key challenges: incomplete object segmentation and noise from irrelevant changes.

The geometric matching component leverages SAM2’s tracking capabilities to ensure spatial completeness of detected changes
Ravi et al. 2024
. By generating class-agnostic object proposals and tracking them across the image pair, SAM2 identifies object masks with inconsistency. We evaluate the overlap ratio
\alpha
between each inconsistent mask and the initial prediction, retaining masks where
\alpha>\alpha_{t}
. Rather than selecting
\alpha_{t}
heuristically, we determine its value through a threshold sweep summarized in the supplementary material. This analysis reveals a precision–recall trade-off, and we adopt
\alpha_{t}=0.20
as the final operating point. Geometric matching completes fragmented object segments that are partially detected in the initial mask, ensuring full-object consistency across views.

The semantic matching component employs the open vocabulary segmentation model Grounded SAM
Ren et al. 2024
to filter out noise and validate semantic relevance. Guided by VLM-generated descriptions,
Ren et al. 2024
produces segmentation masks aligned with the candidate changed objects. We compute overlap ratios between these semantic masks and the initial predictions and retain those satisfying
\alpha>\alpha_{g}
. As with geometric matching, the value of
\alpha_{g}
is chosen through a threshold sweep, and we select
\alpha_{g}=0.10
. Both
\alpha_{t}
and
\alpha_{g}
are selected on the NYC-CD validation set using the RSCD+LangSCD configuration, and the same fixed thresholds are then applied uniformly across
all backbones and all datasets
,
without any test-set tuning
. This semantic filtering step effectively removes false positives caused by shadows, reflections, or other non-structural variations.

Geometric and semantic matching operate in synergy: geometric matching ensures spatial completeness, while semantic matching enforces semantic relevance (refer to the supplementary material for qualitative examples). This dual-validation process transforms noisy, incomplete pixel-level predictions into coherent object-level change masks, improving precision and interpretability. The output provides semantically grounded, object-aware change detection masks.

4

Annotation Method

Our annotation method aims to identify and categorize scene changes between image pairs to create high-quality training data. Given a pair of images
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
captured at different times from different viewpoints, our method annotates three distinct types of change on
T_{1}
with respect to
T_{0}
: (1) new/missing objects, representing objects that appear on
T_{1}
but not on
T_{0}
; (2) vegetation appearance changes, capturing seasonal alterations in plants; and (3) objects not in view due to viewpoint change, distinguishing geometric occlusions from actual scene changes. This multiclass annotation approach enables more nuanced understanding of urban scene dynamics compared to binary change detection.

Figure
4
presents the pipeline overview. We dive into each module below.

Change Captioning. The annotation process begins by using GPT-4o to generate descriptions of changes between pairs of images. We provide the model with
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
along with text prompts for vegetation appearance and object changes (see supplementary material). In both cases, GPT-4o is explicitly instructed to exclude weather variations, lighting differences, and pedestrians (which are already anonymized in our dataset) to focus on semantically meaningful changes. The model is tasked with identifying objects that appear in
T_{1}
but not in
T_{0}
as well as vegetation changes. For vegetation appearance changes, we request descriptive attributes such as “green trees” or “bare bushes” to capture seasonal variations. This process yields two lists: changed objects with their descriptions and changed vegetation with appearance-aware attributes.

Open Vocabulary Segmentation. The change description from GPT-4o are processed through Grounded SAM 2
Ren et al. 2024
to produce semantically changed masks. It segments all instances of changed objects in
T_{1}
, regardless of whether they represent actual changes. For instance, if GPT identifies “chairs” as a change due to additional chairs appearing in
T_{1}
, Grounded SAM 2 segments both preexisting and new chairs. Similarly, vegetation appearance changes are segmented using descriptive phrases like “bare trees” or “green foliage,” where the descriptive nature inherently indicates that these segments represent changes.

SAM2 Tracking. To identify inconsistent objects, we use SAM2
Ravi et al. 2024
tracking capabilities across the image pair. The process begins with automatic segment generation in
T_{1}
to produce object segments. These segments are then used as prompts for
T_{0}
, allowing us to identify segments present in
T_{1}
but absent in
T_{0}
—potential indicators of new objects. However, this raw output often includes irrelevant changes such as shadows or reflections. These geometrically inconsistent masks require refinement through matching with the semantic masks.

Object Matching. We refine the change detection by computing the overlap between geometrically inconsistent SAM2 masks and semantically changed G-SAM masks. We retain SAM2 mask if it overlaps with any Grounded SAM2 mask. This matching process leverages the complementary strengths of both approaches: SAM2 tracking identifies changing regions but may include visual distractors, while Grounded SAM 2 identifies semantically relevant objects that are potentially changing regions. The intersection yields high-confidence masks that represent the actual changes in relevant objects.

Common View Estimation and Matching. To distinguish between actual scene changes and viewpoint-induced variations, we employ MAST3R
Leroy et al. 2024
for robust dense matching between image pairs. MAST3R excels in two-view regimes, predicting dense correspondences and estimating the common-view region that is the area visible in both images. We calculate the overlap between each filtered object mask and the common-view mask. Objects overlapping with the common view are classified as genuine new/missing object changes, while those outside are categorized as objects not in view. The same classification process applies to the appearance masks in Grounded SAM 2, distinguishing actual appearance changes from viewpoint-induced occlusions. This step produces our initial pseudo-masks with multi-class labels.

Manual Inspection. The final stage involves careful human verification of the initial pseudo-masks to ensure annotation quality. We review each image pairs, discarding those with false negatives (unlabeled changes in the initial masks) to maintain the completeness of the data set. False positive object masks are manually removed to ensure precision. This semi-automatic approach efficiently combines the scalability of automated methods with the accuracy of human judgment, producing high-quality multi-class change detection annotations for complex urban scenes. NYC-CD Dataset

The NYC-CD dataset comprises 8,122 carefully curated image pairs extracted from the NYU-VPR dataset
Sheng et al. 2021
, providing a comprehensive benchmark for urban scene change detection. The source NYU-VPR dataset contains street-level imagery recorded throughout Manhattan, New York from May 2016 to March 2017. All images include GPS tags for spatial reference, while pedestrians and vehicles are replaced with white pixels using MSeg
Lambert et al. 2020
to preserve privacy. This rich temporal and spatial coverage of one of the world’s most dynamic urban environments provides an ideal foundation for studying complex scene changes.

To construct meaningful image pairs for change detection, we employ VPR instead of simple GPS matching. VPR retrieves the most visually similar images for a query from a database of images with known camera poses, ensuring sufficient visual overlap between the paired images. We specifically use images from different quarters as database and queries (Q1 paired with Q3, Q2 paired with Q4), guaranteeing a minimum three-month temporal gap between image pairs to capture meaningful scene changes. Using MixVPR
Ali-Bey et al. 2023
, a state-of-the-art VPR method, we pair each database image with its top-1 retrieval result to form potential change pairs. This VPR-based approach is crucial because GPS coordinates alone would often yield image pairs with minimal or no viewpoint overlap, making change detection infeasible. By prioritizing visual similarity while maintaining temporal separation, we ensure that each potential pair contains sufficient common content for meaningful change analysis while capturing the natural viewpoint variations that occur in real-world scenarios.

The NYC-CD dataset introduces several unique characteristics that distinguish it from existing benchmarks. Most notably, it explicitly incorporates and annotates viewpoint-induced changes, addressing a critical gap in current datasets that either assume static cameras or attempt to minimize viewpoint differences. In practical applications like autonomous navigation and augmented reality, viewpoint variations are inevitable and can create apparent changes that are purely geometric rather than semantic. By categorizing these as a distinct change type, our dataset enables algorithms to differentiate genuine scene changes from perspective-induced variations. Furthermore, the dataset captures the full complexity of dense urban environments with large temporal spans. The urban environment is rich with diverse changes, including seasonal foliage variations, ongoing construction, evolving storefront appearances, and dynamic objects in motion. Unlike controlled simulated environments that cannot replicate real-world unpredictability, NYC-CD provides authentic urban scene dynamics, making it an essential resource for developing robust change detection systems capable of handling the complexities of real-world deployment.

6.1

Setup

We plug our proposed modules into three representative baselines that differ primarily in their image encoder design for SCD.
C-3PO
Wang et al. 2023b
represents traditional CNN-based approaches, using convolutional neural networks as the visual backbone for pixel-level change detection.
RSCD
Lin et al. 2025
exemplifies transformer-based methods built on DINOv2
Oquab et al. 2023
, leveraging its pretrained visual representations with full-image cross-attention.
GeSCF
Kim and Kim 2025
relies on SAM as the underlying visual encoder, enabling zero-shot change detection without task-specific training.

Multi-class supervision.

While existing baselines are originally designed for binary change detection, for experiments on NYC-CD we extend RSCD to support
multi-class change prediction
: we modify the RSCD segmentation head to output multiple classes.
The modified RSCD is trained using standard pixel-wise cross-entropy loss with the multi-class annotations provided by NYC-CD.

Evaluation protocol.

Our evaluation encompasses four diverse datasets that challenge change detection methods across scenarios and viewpoints.
For street-view evaluation, we use NYC-CD, VL-CMU-CD
Alcantarilla et al. 2018
, PSCD
Sakurada et al. 2020
, and ChangeVPR
Kim and Kim 2025
, which span urban development, seasonal variation, and natural disasters.

NYC-CD contains 8,122 image pairs, randomly split into 5,195 training pairs, 1,299 validation pairs, and 1,630 test pairs.
For binary evaluation on NYC-CD, all methods are evaluated using binary masks formed as the union of new/missing objects, appearance changes, and viewpoint-induced changes, ensuring fair comparison with binary baselines.
For multi-class evaluation, RSCD and RSCD + LangSCD are evaluated, as they explicitly predict multi-class outputs.

As noted in prior work
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Kim and Kim 2025
, the VL-CMU-CD dataset contains samples with clearly inaccurate ground-truth masks. We perform a manual quality check and remove image pairs with evidently incorrect annotations (e.g., ground-truth masks that completely miss a changed object). Representative examples of noisy labels and the result on the original VL-CMU-CD dataset are shown in the Supplementary Material. The same filtered dataset is used to retrain and evaluate all baselines to ensure fair comparison.

Qualitative comparison. In Fig.

Latency. Since captions are generated online at inference, the runtime is dominated by the language component. Our goal is not real-time perception but enabling a new capability for street-view scene change detection under large viewpoint differences, a challenging setting where existing visual-only methods often fail. In practice, such change detection is primarily used for offline tasks such as updating VPR databases or maintaining long-term maps, where images are processed asynchronously rather than in real time. In these scenarios, reliable change identification is more critical than per-frame speed. Improving inference efficiency, for example, through lightweight language models, caching, or selective invocation of the language module, remains an important direction for future work.

Conclusion

In this work, we present LangSCD, a framework that enables robust urban scene change detection in unconstrained street-view environments. By injecting semantic priors through a cross-modal feature enhancer and enforcing object-complete consistency with geometric-semantic matching, our approach resolves distractors and viewpoint-induced ambiguities that challenge existing visual-only methods. We further introduce a real-world street-view benchmark NYC-CD with a scalable semi-automatic annotation pipeline to support object-level change detection. Extensive experiments across multiple backbones and datasets demonstrate consistent improvements over existing uni-modal and naive VLM-based approaches. Future work will focus on reducing annotation bias, improving robustness to extreme viewpoint changes, and extending the framework toward unified foundation models for large-scale change detection.

Training Protocol

This section provides detailed information on the training configuration, including which components are trainable, dataset usage, and optimization settings.

Trainable and Frozen Components.

LangSCD is designed as a lightweight extension to existing scene change detection (SCD) backbones. During training, the Cross-modal Feature Enhancer, Merge Module, and Convolution & Upsampling segmentation head are trainable. All other components are frozen, including the Multimodal LLM used for change captioning, the Text Feature Backbone (BERT), the Image Encoder backbone (e.g., DINOv2 or CNN encoders depending on the baseline), and the Geometric–Semantic Matching module, which relies on pretrained SAM2 and Grounded SAM models.

Dataset-specific Training.

For fair comparison, models are trained separately on each dataset used for evaluation rather than performing cross-dataset transfer. For example, when evaluating on VL-CMU-CD, PSCD, ChangeVPR, or NYC-CD, the corresponding training split of that dataset is used for training and the validation split is used for model selection. This protocol avoids cross-dataset leakage and ensures consistency with the training procedures adopted in prior work.

Optimization and Hyperparameters.

Training follows the configuration of the underlying SCD backbone with minimal modification. For RSCD-based experiments, the DINOv2 encoder is kept frozen while the remaining trainable modules are optimized. Models are trained for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
. A cosine learning-rate scheduler is used together with a warm-up stage at the beginning of training. The batch size is 4 and training uses mixed precision to improve efficiency. Random horizontal flipping with probability 0.5 is used for data augmentation. Input resolution follows the dataset protocol (e.g.,
504\times 504
for VL-CMU-CD).

For C-3PO experiments, we adopt the same optimization strategy while using a VGG16-based DeepLabV3 architecture with an input resolution of
640\times 640
.

Loss Function.

Binary scene change detection is optimized using pixel-wise cross-entropy loss. To address foreground–background imbalance, fixed class weights of [0.025, 0.975] are applied during training to emphasize changed regions.

Threshold Selection.

The thresholds used in the geometric and semantic matching modules are selected using the NYC-CD validation set. A threshold sweep is performed to determine the operating point, resulting in
\alpha_{t}=0.20
for geometric matching and
\alpha_{g}=0.10
for semantic matching. These thresholds are then fixed across all datasets and backbones without further tuning.

Training Hardware.

All models are trained using four NVIDIA RTX 8000 GPUs.

Evaluation Metric

We employ two standard metrics for the quantitative evaluation of binary change detection performance: Intersection over Union (IoU) and F-1 score. IoU measures the overlap between predicted and ground-truth masks by dividing the intersection (pixels correctly identified as changed) by the union (all pixels marked as changed in either prediction or ground-truth). The F-1 score, calculated as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, provides a balanced measure of detection precision. Higher F-1 score and IoU indicate better alignment with ground truth (Figure
8
).
