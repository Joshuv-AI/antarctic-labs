DeepRefine: Agent-Compiled Knowledge Refinement via Reinforcement Learning
Haoyu Huang, Jiaxin Bai, Shujie Liu, Yang Wei, Hong Ting Tsang, Yisen Gao, Zhongwei Xie, Yufei Li, Yangqiu Song

Abstract
Agent-compiled knowledge bases provide persistent external knowledge for large language model (LLM) agents in open-ended, knowledge-intensive downstream tasks. Yet their quality is systematically limited by incompleteness , incorrectness , and redundancy , manifested as missing evidence or cross-document links, low-confidence or imprecise claims, and ambiguous or coreference resolution issues. Such defects compound under iterative use, degrading retrieval fidelity and downstream task performance. We present DeepRefine , a general LLM-based reasoning model for agent-compiled knowledge refinement that improves the quality of any pre-constructed knowledge bases with user queries to make it more suitable for the downstream tasks. DeepRefine performs multi-turn interactions with the knowledge base and conducts abductive diagnosis over interaction history, localizes likely defects, and executes targeted refinement actions for incremental knowledge base updates. To optimize refinement policies of DeepRefine without gold references, we introduce a Gain-Beyond-Draft (GBD) reward and train the reasoning process end-to-end via reinforcement learning. Extensive experiments demonstrate consistent downstream gains over strong baselines.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities in handling natural language understanding and generation tasks
(
44
;
17
)
. And retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds LLMs in external knowledge bases outside model parameters
(
23
;
9
)
. A dominant recipe indexes external knowledge as vectors for dense retrieval. Graph-based RAG systems additionally organize salient content into graph-structured knowledge bases to support multi-hop retrieval and reasoning
(
7
;
10
;
11
;
15
;
45
)
. In both cases, the external knowledge base is usually built once and static at serving time, which provides LLMs with richer external knowledge and enhances their capabilities in domain-specific or knowledge-intensive tasks.

Yet a static knowledge base is largely stateless with respect to ongoing use. It does not accumulate structured lessons from repeated queries, user-specific context, or iterative agent workflows. Recent agent-compiled knowledge bases (e.g., LLM-Wiki
(
22
)
) address this gap by having LLM-based agents ingest sources, synthesize articles, and maintain cross-references over time. For example, toolkits that compile documents using agentic workflows and file outputs back into a user-owned persistent store. As shown in the left part of Figure
1
, such systems make external knowledge bases evolvable rather than frozen at test time.

Figure 1
:
The quality problems of agent-compiled knowledge bases (left) and the challenges of knowledge base refinement (right).

Despite this progress, agent-compiled knowledge bases often exhibit systematic quality defects, including
incompleteness
,
incorrectness
, and
redundancy

(
37
;
5
)
. In practice, these defects manifest as missing evidence chains or cross-document links, low-confidence or imprecise assertions, and ambiguous or coreference resolution issues. Such issues compound and degrade both retrieval fidelity and end-task performance. Recent studies mainly optimize the construction policy of knowledge base builders
(
40
;
39
)
, however, in many real deployments the knowledge base has already been constructed, so re-training the constructor and re-compiling the whole knowledge base is often expensive and operationally impractical at scale
(
4
;
41
)
, motivating a post-construction agent-compiled knowledge refinement paradigm.

Achieving effective agent-compiled knowledge refinement presents two core challenges:
(1) defect localization
, i.e., identifying problematic regions in a large knowledge base without performing expensive traversal checks; and
(2) policy optimization without golden references
, i.e., learning effective refinement strategies when no reference refinement actions or golden knowledge bases are available.

As shown in the right part of Figure
1
, Challenge (1) arises because compiled knowledge bases are typically large and relationally connected, making exhaustive inspection infeasible for locating defects that stem from incompleteness, incorrectness, or redundancy
(
6
)
. Challenge (2) arises because the LLM-based agents have difficulty refining knowledge bases and learning how to refine knowledge bases. The real-world refinement lacks golden references. We generally do not observe the “correct” sequence of refinement actions that best aligns a knowledge base with downstream tasks. Consequently, learning effective policies requires training objectives that can evaluate refinement quality through downstream task utility signals.

To address these challenges, we propose
DeepRefine
, a general LLM-based reasoning model for agent-compiled knowledge refinement that improves any pre-constructed knowledge base with user queries to make it more suitable for the downstream tasks. DeepRefine follows a three-step reasoning process:
Answerability Judgement Loop
,
Error Abduction
, and
Refinement Actions Generation
. For Challenge (1), instead of traversing the full knowledge base, DeepRefine performs multi-turn query-conditioned interaction with external knowledge to localize potentially defective neighborhoods, substantially reducing refinement complexity while preserving effectiveness. For Challenge (2), DeepRefine abductively infers likely defect causes from interaction histories, then generates refinement actions to update the knowledge base incrementally. Because the utility of refinement actions is only observable through downstream task performance and is non-differentiable, we introduce a
Gain-Beyond-Draft (GBD) reward
and optimize the refinement policy end-to-end via reinforcement learning.

In summary, we make the following main contributions:

•

To the best of our knowledge,
DeepRefine
is the first general LLM-based reasoning model for
agent-compiled knowledge refinement
, capable of refining any pre-constructed knowledge base using only user queries to make it more suitable for the downstream tasks.

•

We design a three-step reasoning process including
Answerability Judgement Loop
,
Error Abduction
, and
Refinement Actions Generation
. We further introduce a
Gain-Beyond-Draft (GBD) reward
with reinforcement learning to enhance the knowledge base refinement capabilities of LLMs.

•

We conduct extensive experiments across five datasets to demonstrate that DeepRefine can steadily improve the performance of state-of-the-art baseline methods.

,

. Classical knowledge graph (KG) completion is one of the knowledge refinement methods, such as KG-BERT

3
Preliminaries

In this section, we formalize the knowledge base and the refinement task with respect to RAG-based downstream tasks. We represent a knowledge base as structured triples that are more general and not limited to classical KG triples. The heads and tails may be entities, events, or document-grounded spans, which we term as knowledge items, and relations may capture taxonomy, time, causality, or cross-document linkage.

We denote the pre-constructed knowledge base by a set of triples
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}=\{(h,r,t)\mid h,t\in\mathcal{E}_{\text{f}},\,r\in\mathcal{R}_{\text{f}}\}
, where
\mathcal{E}_{\text{f}}
is the set of knowledge items and
\mathcal{R}_{\text{f}}
is the set of relations. The knowledge base may be produced by any LLM-based constructors. We are given a set of user queries
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{N}\}
related to the
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}
. The goal of agent-compiled knowledge base refinement is to update
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}
with user queries in
\mathcal{Q}
to make it more suitable for the downstream RAG tasks.

Then we further formulate knowledge base refinement as generating sequences of actions that mitigate
incompleteness
,
incorrectness
, and
redundancy
in
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}
. This process can be represented as:

\underset{S_{\mathcal{A}_{\text{q}}}\in S}{\arg\max}\penalty\ p_{\theta}(S\mid\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}},\mathcal{Q})=\{\mathcal{A}_{\text{q}}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{\text{q}}^{|\mathcal{Q}|}\},

(1)

where
S_{\mathcal{A}_{\text{q}}}
is the set of action sequence
\mathcal{A}_{\text{q}}^{i}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{M_{i}}\}
with respect to the
i
-th query
q\in\mathcal{Q}
,
S
is the possible output set, and
p_{\theta}
is the policy model parameterized by
\theta
. These actions are then applied to improve the quality of the knowledge base
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}
.

4.1
Reasoning Steps Design

Answerable Judgement Loop.
Initially, DeepRefine will judge the answerability of the given query with several interactions with the knowledge base
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}
, as illustrated in the loop of the rollout module in Figure
2
. If the query is answerable in the first interaction, DeepRefine will directly skip that. Otherwise, DeepRefine will refine
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}
in later reasoning steps to make it more easily to be answered. In the first interaction with
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}
, as shown in Eq.
2
, DeepRefine will conduct a simple dense retrieval to obtain the top-
N
related triples to the query with their embeddings, which can be considered as a
0
-hop retrieved subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}_{\text{q}}
.

\mathcal{G}^{(0)}_{\text{q}}=\text{Top-}k(q,\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}},N).

(2)

Then for each of the following interactions, DeepRefine will continue to expand the retrieved subgraph if the answerability is not satisfied in the previous interaction. To be specific, the subgraph expanding procedure is defined as follows:

\mathcal{G}_{\text{cand}}^{(i)}=\{(h,r,t)\in\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}\mid h\text{ or }t\in\mathcal{E}^{(i-1)}_{\text{q}}\},

(3)

\mathcal{G}_{\text{pruned}}^{(i)}=\text{Top-}k(q,\mathcal{G}_{\text{cand}}^{(i)},M),

(4)

\mathcal{G}^{(i)}_{\text{q}}=\mathcal{G}^{(i-1)}_{\text{q}}\cup\mathcal{G}_{\text{pruned}}^{(i)},

(5)

where
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}_{\text{cand}}
is a set of new candidate triples that connect to the knowledge items in the subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{(i-1)}_{\text{q}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{(i-1)}_{\text{q}}
is the knowledge item set of the subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{(i-1)}_{\text{q}}
. To control the size of the retrieved subgraph with respect to the query
q
, we further prune the candidate triples with their embeddings, and select the top-
M
related triples to the query, as shown in Eq.
4
. Then we merge these new triples with the previous subgraph and obtain the
i
-hop retrieved subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{(i)}_{\text{q}}
. The process of interactions will continue until the answerability is satisfied or the maximum number of expansion hops is reached. The judgement output will be enclosed within
<judge>
and
</judge>
tags as the prompt template demonstrated in Figure
6
of Appendix
E
. After it terminates, the query-specific interaction history
\mathcal{H}_{\text{q}}
with a total length of
L
can be finally obtained for the following reasoning steps. The interaction history consists of the retrieved subgraph at each interaction step and the query:

\mathcal{H}_{\text{q}}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{L-1}\{(q,\mathcal{G}^{(i)}_{\text{q}},\mathcal{J}^{(i)}_{\text{q}})\},

(6)

where
\mathcal{J}^{(i)}_{\text{q}}
is the answerable judgement result of the query
q
at the
i
-th interaction step. Due to context length limitations of the LLMs, we set a horizon length
L_{h}
for the interaction history. Thus, the last
L_{h}
interaction experiences
\mathcal{H}_{\text{q}}[-L_{h}:]
will be used for the subsequent reasoning steps.

Error Abduction.
We assume that there are some potential issues in the knowledge triples of a query-specific subgraph if the query is not answerable with the
0
-hop retrieved subgraph. In this reasoning step, given the interaction history
\mathcal{H}_{\text{q}}[-L_{h}:]
, DeepRefine will abductively reason the potential issues
\mathcal{I}_{\text{q}}
in the retrieved subgraphs from three perspectives: incompleteness, errors and redundancy, which are based on the definition of KG refinement
(
37
)
proposed by
5
. The error reasons will be generated within the
<abduction>
and
</abduction>
tags as the prompt template demonstrated in Figure
7
of Appendix
E
. For example, if the query is about a multi-hop relationship between two knowledge items, but the retrieved subgraphs wrongly include or even do not contain some intermediate knowledge items or relations, DeepRefine will reason the incorrect or missing parts that may lead to the unanswerable result. Similarly, as for redundancy, DeepRefine will also check if there is any ambiguous knowledge that would affect the answerability. The analysed results in this step will be served as the reference for the following refinement actions generation.

Refinement Actions Generation.
Based on the analysed potential issues
\mathcal{I}_{\text{q}}
in the subgraphs retrieved from the previous interactions, DeepRefine will edit the full knowledge base
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}
accordingly to improve its quality. To reduce token costs and improve the refinement efficiency, instead of reconstructing the subgraphs and then inserting into the full knowledge base, for each query
q
, DeepRefine will generate a series of refinement actions
\mathcal{A}_{\text{q}}
within the
<refinement>
and
</refinement>
tags to edit the full knowledge base directly:

\mathcal{A}_{\text{q}}=\underset{\mathcal{A}_{\text{q}}\in\mathcal{A}}{\arg\max}\penalty\ p_{\theta}(\mathcal{A}\mid\mathcal{G}_{\text{q}}^{(L)},\mathcal{I}_{\text{q}}),

(7)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the set of possible output from DeepRefine parameterized by
\theta
. The generated refinement actions will be parsed into three pre-defined types of operators:
insert_edge()
,
delete_edge()
, and
replace_node()
. The action scope is designed to refine the knowledge base along the three main targets discussed above. Edge insertion action complements missing relations or knowledge items to address incompleteness. Edge deletion action removes incorrect or redundant relations to mitigate errors and redundancy. And node replacement action resolves ambiguity to further reduce redundancy. Note that these actions are integrations of common atom operators defined in graph databases like NetworkX
(
12
)
. For example, the node replacement action can be implemented through an integration of the original node deletion, original node related edge deletion, new node insertion and the edge insertion of the new node. So the three pre-defined operators are just interfaces, which makes this paradigm can be generalizably applied in any databases. After applying the generated refinement actions, we will obtain the refined knowledge base
\hat{\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}}
.

4.2
Refinement Policy Optimization

Reward Design
. To train DeepRefine’s knowledge refinement policy
\pi_{\theta}
, we consider knowledge refinement as an end-to-end RL problem. Specifically, as shown in the Figure
2
, we introduce Gain Beyond Draft (GBD) as the reward objective, which quantifies the gain in RAG generation accuracy achieved by the refined knowledge base
\hat{\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}}
relative to the draft knowledge base
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}
:

\text{GBD}(q)=\text{ACC}(A_{\text{refined}},A)-\text{ACC}(A_{\text{draft}},A),

(8)

where
A_{\text{refined}}
and
A_{\text{draft}}
are the generated answers with the refined knowledge base
\hat{\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}}
and the draft one
\mathcal{G}_{\text{f}}
, which are both obtained through the simple dense retrieval method we mentioned before.
A
is the golden answer for the query and
\text{ACC}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a task-specific metric following the prior work
(
18
)
(details see Appendix
A.2
).

Policy Optimization
. To optimize the knowledge base refinement policy
\pi_{\theta}
of DeepRefine, we adopt the Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
36
)
algorithm to finetune the policy with the GBD reward. Similar to
39
, we simplify the training procedure by removing the KL divergence term in the loss computation and considering that as the reward penalty to lower the computational overhead and save memory usage without damaging the training
(
26
;
14
)
. Formally, the objective is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\textbf{q}\sim P_{Q},\{\textbf{o}_{i}\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|\textbf{q})]

(9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G}\sum^{G}_{i=1}\sum^{|\textbf{o}_{i}|}_{t=1}\Bigl\{\text{min}\Big[r_{i,t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{i,t},\text{clip}\Bigl(r_{i,t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\Bigr)\Big]\hat{A}_{i,t}\Bigr\},

where
r_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}|\textbf{q},\textbf{o}_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}|\textbf{q},\textbf{o}_{i,<t})}
denotes the probability ratio and
\hat{A}_{i,t}=\frac{R_{i}-\mu_{R}}{\sigma_{R}}
represents the group-relative advantage for the entire refinement reasoning process. During the training process, DeepRefine will generate the knowledge refinement actions together with the reasoning steps we described in Section
4.1
token by token, where
\textbf{o}_{i}=\{o_{i,1},\ldots,o_{i,T}\}
represents the sequence of tokens forming the full reasoning output at the
i
-th group of
G
samples. And the reward signal
R_{i}
for the
i
-th query sample is calculated through the reward function, as illustrated in Eq. (
8
).
\epsilon
is a small clipping hyperparameter that ensures stable updates by preventing the new policy from straying too far from the old policy.

4.3
Inference

Figure 3
:
An overview of the inference process of DeepRefine.

After the RL training, DeepRefine can be directly plugged into any pre-constructed knowledge base. As shown in Figure
3
, during inference time, there could be two streams: one is the online query stream, the other one is the knowledge base evolve stream. The first stream is intuitive, because users will sequentially ask the agent-compiled knowledge base questions once it has been constructed. And the knowledge refinement process conducted by a frozen DeepRefine with respect to an online query will forma the second stream. Note that the refinement process can be asynchronous with the online service, which will not introduce any systematic latency. DeepRefine will process these queries sequentially with multi-step reasoning chains to improve the quality of the agent-compiled knowledge base and make it more suitable for the downstream tasks.

5
Experiments

In this section, we will report the performance of DeepRefine on both RAG and long-term conversation memory benchmarks in Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) settings. We focus on the performance gain of DeepRefine over various baseline methods and the efficiency of the knowledge refinement process.

-

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose DeepRefine, a general LLM-based reasoning model for agent-compiled knowledge refinement. DeepRefine is designed to iteratively refine any pre-constructed knowledge base by leveraging the multiple interactions with the knowledge base, which is trained with a newly designed GBD reward finetuned RL policy. Experimental results under the OOD settings show that DeepRefine can consistently improve the quality of the constructed knowledge base and achieve better downstream performance compared with that of the original knowledge base.

A.1
Metrics for Benchmark Evaluation

Following existing works
[
38
,
19
,
43
]
, we employ the token-level F1 score as the metric for benchmark evaluation.

A.2
Metrics for GBD Reward

Following the prior work
[
18
]
, we also use the Generation Accuracy (GenAcc) as the task-specific metric for the GBD reward, which is a more robust and stable metric than the token-level F1 score and is more suitable for RL training of DeepRefine. The GenAcc is defined as follows:

\text{GenAcc}=\text{span\_check}\lor\text{judge\_check},

(10)

which is a combination of span-matching test
[
28
,
24
]
and a LLM-based correctness check with DeepSeek-V3
[
25
]
as a judge.

Additionally, to effectively shape the refinement behavior of DeepRefine with the GBD reward, we further design a transition-based reward matrix that evaluates the model’s action by comparing the GenAcc of the draft answer (
A_{\text{draft}}
) and the refined answer (
A_{\text{refined}}
). Specifically, we define the reward gain
R(A_{\text{draft}},A_{\text{refined}})
over four possible GenAcc transition states: a successful correction (
0\rightarrow 1
) receives the highest reward of
+1.0
to strongly incentivize the model to leverage retrieved evidence for error correction, which is the same as the original performance gain of the GBD reward; an erroneous alteration of a correct draft (
1\rightarrow 0
) incurs a penalty of
-0.3
to discourage over-editing, which is bigger than the original performance gain
-1.0
in the GBD reward to prevent the policy from receiving excessive penalties when performing exploration; a correct preservation (
1\rightarrow 1
) receives a modest reward of
+0.2
to reinforce confidence in already accurate answers; and a failed correction (
0\rightarrow 0
) is assigned a neutral reward of
0.0
, as penalizing this state would disproportionately destabilize training given its high natural frequency in complex multi-hop reasoning tasks.

Appendix C
Additional Experiments

To further evaluate the effectiveness of DeepRefine on resolving incompleteness, incorrectness or redundancy issues, we provide a rigorous evaluation. We construct a high-quality benchmark characterized by explicit error types and diverse corruption patterns. By editing the knowledge bases with controlled defects that mirror real-world failure modes, it enables a precise assessment of a model’s performance in defect localization and refinement processes across various error types.

We constuct the benchmark based on the HotpotQA (Dev-Distractor)
[
46
]
dataset. It consists of 501 samples, with 167 samples dedicated to each of the three systematic error types defined in our study. Each data point includes the original query, the corrupted knowledge base, and comprehensive metadata (e.g., corruption details and action counts). The construction pipeline utilizes
Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct
(denoted as the corruption model) for generating defects,
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct
as the base knowledge base constructor, and
Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B
for vector-based retrieval.
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
serves as the reader model for RAG-based evaluation.

Error Types and Corruption Strategies.
For each error type, we apply a specific corruption strategy to a high-quality knowledge base:
Incompleteness
: We identify and remove critical triples that are essential for the reasoning path, creating "missing evidence" scenarios.
Incorrectness
: We modify correct knowledge items into incorrect claims based on the original context, simulating low-confidence or imprecise information.
Redundancy
: This is achieved via two methods: (1) Inserting semantically similar but redundant triples to introduce ambiguity. (2) Replacing entities in existing triples with aliases or synonymous names to create coreference resolution challenges.

Construction Pipeline.
The construction pipeline follows a rigorous multi-stage pipeline. Initially, for each sample, we construct an individual KB based on its context. We then perform RAG. Only samples achieving a F1 score higher than
0.95
are retained. This ensures that the initial KB is relatively accurate and contains sufficient information to answer the query. Then, for the filtered samples, the corruption process is specifically targeted at the top-
N
triples retrieved during the previous RAG stage. This ensures that the induced defects are concentrated on the critical knowledge components most relevant to the query, rather than introducing peripheral noise. Within this focused scope, the corruption model generates a series of actions. To ensure the generation of ideal, high-quality defects, we designed specialized prompt templates and carefully curated few-shot samples tailored to each specific error type. These actions are subsequently parsed into three predefined operators: insert_edge(), delete_edge(), and replace_node(). The action space is designed to be symmetric with DeepRefine’s refinement action set to facilitate direct performance mapping. Notably, the replace_node() operator is restricted to acting upon a single, specific edge. This fine-grained precision allows us to create nuanced coreference resolution issues and subtle semantic defects that test the model’s diagnostic depth. To maintain the structural integrity and ensure the refinability of the knowledge base, the total number of corruption actions is strictly limited to 5 per sample. Then we perform a second RAG evaluation on the corrupted KB. Only samples where the RAG F1 score drops below 0.6 are included in the final benchmark. This ensures that the corruption is effective, which suggests that it significantly impairs the model’s ability to answer the query without refinement.

Appendix F
Limitations

In this work, we propose a general LLM-based reasoning model for agent-compiled knowledge base refinement. Although DeepRefine has shown promising performance in the experiments, there are still some potential improvements that can be made to the model. For example, we only construct the training data from HotpotQA with easy filtering criteria, which makes the quality of the training data not high enough. Finding out higher quality and more diverse training data is a promising direction for future work. And how to combine DeepRefine with a robust systematic refinement framework to make the performance better and more stable is also underexplored.
