Parity in Patency? Efficacy of Resorbable Scaffold by Sex in LIFE-BTK
Authors: Apoorva Sharma, Khansa Ahmad, Shea E Hogan
Published: 2026

Chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI) is the most severe manifestation of peripheral artery disease (PAD) and is associated with poor health-related quality of life,1 high resource utilization,2 and high rates of death and major limb loss.3 Lower extremity revascularization (LER) strategies for CLTI include surgical bypass using native saphenous vein or prosthetic conduit; endovascular therapy utilizing angioplasty with plain or specialty balloons, atherectomy, bare metal stents, and/or drug-eluting balloons and stents; or a hybrid approach including both open and endovascular revascularization.

An important factor to consider when deciding LER strategy is the presence of infrapopliteal artery disease, as the tibial arteries are smaller, more calcified, and are more likely to have target vessel failure. Although randomized controlled trial data have demonstrated that patients with CLTI who qualify for surgical or endovascular approach have fewer major adverse limb events and all-cause death when surgical bypass with a single saphenous vein conduit is utilized,3 randomized controlled trial data of patients with CLTI undergoing LER for infrapopliteal artery disease showed better amputation-free survival with an endovascular approach.4

Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) in the infrapopliteal segments has important limitations—among them, dissection, elastic recoil, and restenosis.5 A novel solution for this problem is the use of drug-eluting resorbable scaffolds (DRS), which have the advantage of short-term mechanical support and delivery of antiproliferative drug without the long-term complications associated with permanent metal stents. One- and 2-year data have revealed improved patency, decreased restenosis, and comparable safety of the Esprit BTK DRS (Abbott) compared with PTA for below-the-knee arterial disease.5,6

A crucial aspect of original device evaluation includes the assessment of populations who may experience greater/lesser efficacy or risk. Differences in female and male CLTI populations have largely been established from observational data given the well-known underrepresentation of female patients in PAD clinical trials. Female patients with CLTI present at an older age, are less likely to be current/former smokers, are more likely to have CLTI as the first manifestation of PAD, and are more likely to have rest pain than ischemic wounds.7, 8, 9 Female patients with CLTI are less likely to undergo LER, but those who do are more likely to undergo an endovascular approach8 and to need subsequent, unplanned LER of the index limb.9 Although female patients with CLTI are less likely to undergo major lower limb amputation than their male counterparts,10 they are more likely to undergo above-the-knee (compared with below-the-knee) amputation.11

In this issue of JSCAI, Bajakian et al12 examined baseline sex characteristics, anatomic and procedural findings, and clinical outcomes for PTA vs DRS in the LIFE-BTK trial.5,6 The trial’s primary efficacy end point was a composite of (1) freedom from above-ankle amputation of the target limb, (2) binary restenosis (defined as >50% by angiography or peak systolic velocity ratio ≥2.0 by Doppler ultrasonography) of the target lesion, and (3) clinically driven target lesion revascularization (CD-TLR).

Of the 261 participants with CLTI randomized in the cohort, 173 (32%) were female. Their analysis revealed numerous sex-specific differences. Females were older and had fewer cardiovascular comorbidities, including lower tobacco use, a lower incidence of ischemic wounds, and lower ankle-brachial indices (ABIs) on presentation. The lower ABIs in females have been noted in observational data and likely reflect a higher incidence of falsely elevated (ie, noncompressible) ABIs in males due to calcified tibial artery disease. Anatomic disease characteristics also differed: Females had smaller vessel diameters, shorter lesions, and a higher incidence of disease in the peroneal artery. Moderate calcification was present in roughly one-third of both female and male cohorts, but severe calcification was present only in males. Procedurally, females had better results, including less residual stenosis and increased likelihood of achieving <30% stenosis after intervention.12 Taken together, these findings add to the collective data demonstrating different CLTI anatomy and pathophysiology between sexes.

Despite these different clinical, anatomic, and procedural findings between females and males, the primary efficacy end point was significantly and meaningfully lower with DRS than PTA in both sexes, and no sex-by-treatment interaction was observed. At 1 year, rates were over twofold higher in the PTA arm (females, 23.5% vs 57.1%; P = .008; males, 26.5% vs 56.0%; P = .006). At 2 years, the effect remained significant but less pronounced, especially in males (females, 34.1% vs 68.4%; P = .02; males, 40.7% vs 66.7%; P = .006). The primary safety end point (freedom from major adverse limb events and perioperative death) occurred rarely and was not different between arms. It is worth emphasizing that the primary driver of the composite end point in females at 1 year was binary restenosis (DRS, 46.0% vs PTA, 23.5%; P < .01); CD-TLR and above-ankle amputation were not different between groups. Males had significantly decreased binary restenosis at 1 year, and a trend toward greater CD-TLR (DRS 7.8% vs PTA 18.2%, P = .06). It is further worth noting that the primary efficacy end point was revised to include freedom from binary restenosis following a prespecified, blinded interim analysis. Thus, the primary efficacy results were driven by anatomic findings and not hard clinical events.

The authors acknowledge this limitation: despite higher binary restenosis rates, females in both treatment arms were much less likely to undergo CD-TLR at 1 and 2 years. It is unclear why repeat LER was performed so much more frequently in males than females. The authors hypothesize that it may have been related to concerns about complications in females given smaller tibial artery size or due to the relative lack of wounds in the female cohort. The Supplementary Appendix for the original trial5 indicates that repeat ABIs, Rutherford category, and patient-reported outcomes (including the Walking Impairment Questionnaire and the Peripheral Arterial Questionnaire) are being collected at regular intervals during the trial, and these results will hopefully help clarify the sex-associated discordance between binary restenosis and CD-TLR. Regardless, these findings support efforts to shift CLTI end points in clinical trials to those that are clinically meaningful to patients and clinicians.

Despite the above caveats, Bajakian et al are to be congratulated for their work demonstrating efficacy and safety of DRS to treat tibial artery disease in females and males with CLTI. This information will not only help inform contemporary patient-shared decision making but will hopefully drive device development in the infratibial CLTI space. Additionally, their work should motivate future clinical trials to adopt meaningful primary end points and ensure adequate female representation to detect sex differences in outcomes.
