Associations of dietary patterns with mild cognitive impairment in older Singaporean adults: findings from the diet and healthy aging cohort
Authors: Tao Liu, Na Li, Shiyun Feng, Lei Feng, Shusheng Shi, Kaisy Xinhong Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs the global population ages, the prevalence of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia is increasing substantially. Studies conducted predominantly in Western populations suggest that adherence to healthy dietary patterns may help preserve cognitive health. However, evidence in Asian populations remains limited and inconsistent.ObjectiveTo investigate the cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between adherence to the alternate Mediterranean diet (aMED), Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), and Healthy Diet Indicator (HDI) dietary patterns and odds of MCI among older Singaporean adults.DesignCross-sectional and longitudinal analyses of data from the Diet and Healthy Aging cohort.SettingCommunity-dwelling older adults in Singapore.ParticipantsA total of 620 older adults (mean age 67.7 ± 6.0 years; 71.8% women) were included in the cross-sectional analysis, while 313 cognitively normal participants were included in the longitudinal analysis.MeasurementsDietary adherence scores were derived from a validated food frequency questionnaire. MCI was diagnosed using the Singapore-modified Mini-Mental State Examination (SM-MMSE), Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), and a battery of standardized neurocognitive tests. Logistic regression models adjusted for potential confounders were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsIn cross-sectional analyses, higher adherence to aMED, but not DASH or HDI, was associated with lower odds of MCI (OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.17−0.73, P-trend < 0.005). In longitudinal analysis, none of the dietary patterns were significantly associated with incident MCI.ConclusionHigher adherence to aMED was associated with lower odds of MCI cross-sectionally, although no significant longitudinal associations were observed. Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and repeated dietary assessments are needed to clarify the role of predefined dietary patterns in cognitive health among Asian older adults.

Introduction

The global population is ageing, and advancing age is one of the strongest risk factors for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia. Currently, more than 55 million people worldwide are living with dementia, and this number is projected to increase to 78 million by 2030 and 139 million by 2050 [1]. Dementia is one of leading causes of disability and dependency among older adults and is currently the seventh leading cause of death globally. Individuals with dementia experience progressive impairments in memory, language, problem-solving, and other cognitive abilities that ultimately affect their capacity to perform daily activities. The estimated lifetime cost of care for an individual living with dementia is approximately US$0.34 million [2]. Consequently, dementia imposes a substantial burden not only on affected individuals and their families, but also on healthcare and public health system [1]. Given the limited efficacy of current pharmacological therapies, effective preventive strategies are urgently needed to mitigate the impact of population ageing on cognitive health.

There is growing interest in dietary interventions as non-pharmacological strategies for delaying the onset and progression of age-related cognitive decline. The Mediterranean diet (MED), a dietary pattern traditionally followed in Mediterranean Basin countries since the early 1960s [3], has been extensively studied and shown to be associated with reduced risk of cognitive decline and dementia [[4], [5], [6]]; In addition, MED has demonstrated benefits in the prevention of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [7], stroke and depression [8], which are themselves recognized risk factors for dementia. Components of the MED provide several nutrients, including unsaturated fatty acids, antioxidants, and vitamins, that have been implicated in cognitive function and neuroprotection [9,10].

The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, originally developed in the United States in the 1990s, has also been associated with potential neuroprotective benefits [[11], [12], [13]]. Although DASH was initially designed to reduce hypertension [14], it has also been shown to lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels associated with CVD risk [15]. Long-term adherence to DASH has additionally been associated with better cognitive function among older adults [16].

Unlike MED and DASH, the Healthy Diet Indicator (HDI) was developed for global application to facilitate dietary comparisons across different populations and cultures [17]. The HDI is based on the World Health Organization (WHO) dietary recommendations for the prevention of chronic diseases. Despite the potential benefits of these dietary patterns (DPs) in reducing MCI risk, most previous studies have been conducted in Mediterranean or Western populations, and relatively few studies have investigated the neuroprotective effects of these DPs in Asian populations [[18], [19], [20], [21]].

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate both cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between adherence aMED, DASH, and HDI dietary patterns and risk of MCI in a population-based cohort of older adults in Singapore.

Methods

Study design

We analysed data from the Diet and Healthy Aging cohort (DaHA), a population-based cohort study of Singaporean older adults aged 60 and above [22]. The cohort recruited 1,010 subjects from geographically defined areas in Jurong in Singapore from 2011 to 2016. On an average of approximately 5 years after baseline assessment, participants were followed up between 2017 and 2020 to provide longitudinal data. The study was conducted in-person at the Training and Research Academy at Jurong Point (TaRA@JP). Participants completed a structured questionnaire that collected information on demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, medical history, and were assessed with the geriatric depression scale [23,24], and the geriatric anxiety inventory [25]. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the National University of Singapore [Reference number 10–517]. All participants provided written informed consent.

Dietary assessments

A validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was used to measure habitual dietary information at baseline. The FFQ was developed by the Singapore Health Promotion Board (HPB) and contained 367 food items, including common locally consumed foods that are not seen in other countries [26]. Questions were asked in terms of number of times per day, per week, per month and rarely/never. Portion size pictures/food models and the Compendium of Food Pictures [27] were used to guide participant responses.

aMED

We adapted the alternate MED (aMED) scoring system by Fung et al. [28] to include nuts and fish in the consumption pattern. aMED is a modified version of the original MED to reflect eating patterns in the United States and has been the most widely used system in studies involving Asian populations [29]. It includes nine components: vegetables, fruits, wholegrains, nuts, legumes, fish, alcohol, monounsaturated fatty acid to saturated fatty acid ratio (MUFA: SFA) ratio, and red and processed meats. Dichotomous points (0 or 1) were assigned to each dietary component by using the study population-specific medians as cut offs. Specifically, people whose consumption of vegetables (excluding potatoes), fruits, nuts, legumes, fish, and MUFA: SFA meet or above the median consumption of the population were assigned a value of 1, otherwise, a value of 0 is assigned. By contrast, people whose consumption of red and processed meats was equal to or above the median consumption of the study population were assigned a value of 0, and the others assigned a value of 1. A moderate intake of alcohol (10–25 g/day in males and 5–15 g/day in females) was assigned a value of 1. However, alcohol intake in this cohort was generally low, resulting in limited variability for this component. The total score for aMED was calculated by summing the dichotomous points for each item, so the possible score range for aMED was 0−9. Participants were assigned to each of the three categories of aMED, low adherence (0–3), moderate adherence (4–5), and high adherence (6–9).

DASH

DASH scores were generated using the Fung method [30], which focuses on 8 components (high intakes of vegetables, fruits, wholegrains, and low-fat dairy products and low intakes of sodium, sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) (include fruit juices), and red and processed meats. Sodium intake was not available from the nutrient output data, so it was emitted in the scoring process, and the number of scoring components was adjusted to 7. Participants were classified into quintiles according to their intake ranking. For food components with a high recommended intake, the score was rated on a scale of 1 to 5 using their quintile number. For example, participants in Quintile 1 (lowest intake) were given a score of 1, and those in Quintile 5 (highest intake) were given a score of 5. Conversely, food components with a low recommended intake were scored on a reverse scale with lower intake receiving a higher score. By summing up the component scores a total score for DASH was obtained and ranged from 5−35. Participants were categories into either high adherence (³ 75%) or low adherence (<75%) [31].

HDI

We followed the HDI scoring system based on the 2003 WHO dietary guidelines for the prevention of chronic diseases [17]. The HDI comprised seven components classified into three categories: moderation, moderation range, and adequacy. The moderation components included saturated fatty acids (SFA; % of total energy intake), mono- and disaccharides (% of total energy intake), and cholesterol (mg/day). Consistent with the methodology of Jankovic et al., mono- and disaccharides were used as a proxy for free sugars because free sugar intake was not available from the dietary database. The moderation range components included protein (% of total energy intake) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA; % of total energy intake). The adequacy components included dietary fibre (g/day) and fruit and vegetable intake (g/day).

Each component was scored on a continuous scale ranging from 0 to 10 points. For moderation components, participants received 10 points when intake met the WHO recommendation (SFA < 10% energy, mono- and disaccharides <10% energy, cholesterol <300 mg/day). Scores decreased proportionally as intake exceeded the recommendation and reached 0 points at the upper cutoff values (>15% energy for SFA, >30% energy for mono- and disaccharides, and >400 mg/day for cholesterol). For moderation range components, participants received 10 points when intake fell within the recommended range (protein 10–15% energy and PUFA 6–10% energy). Scores decreased proportionally outside these ranges and reached 0 points at the predefined lower or upper cutoffs (protein >20% energy; PUFA >10% energy). For adequacy components, participants received 0 points at zero intake and scores increased proportionally until the recommended intake was achieved (>25 g/day for dietary fibre and >400 g/day for fruits and vegetables), at which point 10 points were awarded. The total HDI score ranged from 0 to 70, with higher scores indicating greater adherence to WHO dietary recommendations. Participants were categorized into tertiles representing low, moderate, and high adherence. The nutrient units, cut-off values, and scoring criteria used for HDI are presented in Table 1. Detailed HDI scoring equations are provided in Supplementary Text S1.

Diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment

Several cognitive assessment tools were used to establish study diagnoses of MCI and dementia. A Singapore - modified Mini-Mental State Examination (SM-MMSE, range 0–30) was used to access cognitive function [32]. Participants who scored below the pre-specified SM-MMSE cut-off values (≤27 for subjects without formal education; ≤ 28 for primary school education level, and ≤29 for secondary school education and above) underwent further evaluation using the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale [33] and a battery of standard neurocognitive tests. The procedures for each neurocognitive test are described in Supplementary Text S2.

Completed clinical and psychometric data were reviewed by a panel consisting of two senior consultant psychiatrists, the principal investigator, and all cognitive assessors. Objective MCI was determined using local age- and education-adjusted normative data from the neurocognitive tests together with Petersen’s criteria for MCI. Dementia was diagnosed according to DSM-IV criteria.

For the longitudinal analysis, incident MCI was defined as participants free of MCI and dementia at baseline who fulfilled Petersen’s criteria for MCI at follow-up assessment. Dementia cases identified at follow-up were excluded from the longitudinal analysis. Cognitive status at follow-up was assessed using the same neuropsychological assessments and diagnostic procedures applied at baseline. Additional details regarding cognitive assessments have been descried in the cohort profile paper [22].

Measurement of covariates

Other covariates were collected during baseline interview to account for potential confounders. These include demographic characteristics (baseline age, sex, race), years of schooling (continuous), marital status (single, married, divorced, widowed), living status (alone, with others), participations in physical, social and cognitive activity (yes, no), smoking status (yes, no), weight, height, waist and hip circumferences, medical history (physician-diagnosed diabetes, hypertension, Hypercholesterolemia), and geriatric depression score (continuous), and geriatric anxiety score (continuous). Body-Mass-Index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kilograms) divided by height (meters) squared. Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was calculated as waist circumference (centimetres) divided by hip circumference (centimetres).

Statistical analysis

Only participants who completed all questionnaires, including the FFQ, were included in the analyses. Participants with implausible daily energy intakes (<700 kcal/day or >3700 kcal/day for men; <600 kcal/day or >3000 kcal/day for women) and participants with study diagnosed dementia excluded to minimize potential recall bias.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to compare baseline characteristics between participants retained in the longitudinal analysis and those lost to follow-up. Additional sensitivity analyse were performed to examine differences between included and excluded participants in the analytical sample.

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize baseline participant characteristics according to adherence categories of the three DPs. Correlations between DP scores were assessed by using Pearson’s correlation coefficient.

Logistic regression models were used to examine the associations between DP scores and odds of MCI in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. For the longitudinal analysis, incident MCI was defined as participants free of MCI and dementia at baseline who fulfilled Petersen’s criteria for MCI at follow-up assessment. Logistic regression was selected because follow-up duration was relatively similar across participants (mean follow-up: 4.9 ± 0.9 years).

Odd ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated across adherence categories of each DPs, with the lowest adherence category used as the reference group. Multivariable models were adjusted for the above-mentioned covariates. After exclusion of participants with missing dietary information, implausible energy intake, or dementia, all remaining participants had complete data for the covariates included in the analyses. Therefore, no imputation procedures were required and all eligible participants were retained in the regression models. Tests for linear trend were performed by assigning the median value of each adherence category and modelling these values as continuous variables.

Participants diagnosed with dementia at follow-up were excluded from the longitudinal analysis as the primary outcome was incident MCI. Owing to the small number of dementia cases identified during follow-up, dementia could not be examined as a separate outcome.

In secondary analyses, multiplicative interaction terms were included in the fully adjusted models to examine potential effect modification of the associations between DP scores and MCI according to age (<65 or ³ 65) sex, physical activity (yes or no), smoking status (yes or no), and history of diabetes (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), and high blood cholesterol (yes or no). Statistical interactions were evaluated by including interaction terms in the regression models.

All statistical analyses were conducted with Stata (version 15.1, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Sample characteristics

During a mean follow-up of 4.9 ± 0.9 years between 2017 and 2020, 383 participants from the original cohort of 1,010 eligible participants refused follow-up participation, resulting in a dropout rate of 37.9%. Reasons for dropout are outlined in Supplementary Table S1. The main reasons for dropout were loss of interest despite being healthy (38.1%), being uncontactable (28.2%), and work commitments with refusal to participate (11%). Home visits were initially conducted to improve follow-up participation; however, these procedures were halted during phases 1 and 2 of the COVID-19 pandemics, and many participants were also unwilling to undergo home visits during this period.

Sensitivity analyses comparing participants who completed follow-up with those lost to follow-up were conducted based on the original cohort of 1,010 eligible participants. Participants retained in the study were younger (p < 0.001), more likely to be female (p = 0.006), had more years of schooling (p = 0.006), participated in physical exercise (p = 0.005), were less likely to smoke (p < 0.001), and had lower prevalence of hypertension (p = 0.005) and diabetes (p = 0.041). They also had lower GDS scores (p < 0.001). In addition, a significantly greater proportion of participants lost to follow-up had baseline MCI (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S2). These findings suggest possible selection bias toward healthier participants in the longitudinal analysis and may have reduced statistical power.

Not all participants provided dietary information. After excluding participants without dietary information (n = 272), those with implausible energy intake (n = 117), and participants diagnosed with dementia at baseline (n = 1), 620 participants remained for the baseline cross-sectional analysis, of whom 102 (16.5%) had MCI. Sensitivity analysis showed that excluded participants did not differ significantly from included participants, except for adjusted SM-MMSE score (p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table S3).

For the longitudinal analysis, participants with baseline MCI (n = 45), dementia (n = 1), and loss to follow-up (n = 262) were excluded, leaving 313 cognitively normal participants for analysis. Incident MCI was defined as participants free of MCI and dementia at baseline who fulfilled Petersen’s criteria for MCI at follow-up assessment. Dementia cases identified at follow-up were excluded from the longitudinal analysis. During follow-up, 57 participants developed incident MCI. Cognitive status at follow-up was assessed using the same neuropsychological assessments and diagnostic procedures applied at baseline. Interaction analyses with demographic and clinical factors

Interaction analyses showed that sex, physical activity, smoking status, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and diabetes did not significantly modify the associations between DP scores and MCI (all P-interaction >0.05) (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

Past nutrition research, whether focused on single nutrient/food or multiple components, has contributed to the understanding of many nutrition factors in cognitive health. However, given the complex and multifactorial nature of age-related cognitive decline and dementia, a multi-component preventive approach is likely to be more appropriate than focusing on single nutrients or foods alone.

In the current study, we assessed, both cross-sectionally and longitudinally, the relationships between healthy diets, as evaluated by aMED, DASH, HDI, and the odds of MCI in a population-based cohort of Singaporean older adults. We identified a cross-sectional association between aMED and MCI, while no associations were found for the DASH diet and HDI. The longitudinal analysis indicated no associations between the predetermined DPs and odds of MCI.

Mediterranean diet has been the most investigated DP with evidence supporting protection against many age-related diseases, including cognitive decline [[4], [5], [6],19,34,35]. A recent meta-analysis of 17 prospective cohort studies showed that high adherence to aMED was associated with a 25% lower risk of MCI and 29% lower risk of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [34]. Earlier meta-analysis showed similar conclusions [[36], [37], [38]]. In a meta-analysis of two randomized controlled trials, aMED was associated with better episodic memory and working memories compared to the control group [34]. The potential cognitive benefits of aMED may be attributable to its high content of foods and nutrients previously associated with neuroprotection, including fish, unsaturated fatty acids, vitamin B12, folate, carotenoids, and flavonoids [9,39].

Although our cross-sectional analysis revealed a significant association between aMED and MCI, this association was not observed in the longitudinal analysis. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, the longitudinal analysis involved a substantially smaller sample size due to attrition, which may have reduced statistical power to detect associations. Sensitivity analyses also suggested possible selection bias, as participants retained in the longitudinal cohorts were generally younger and healthier compared with those lost to follow-up. Second, dietary habits in Asian populations differ considerably from those in Western populations, including higher consumption of rice-and soy-based foods and lower consumption of dairy products, red meat, nuts, and alcohol [40]. These differences may limit the applicability and discriminatory ability of predefined Western-derived DPs such as aMED in this population.

There are high degrees of similarities between the DASH diet score and aMED, explaining the high correlation score between the two DPs. Both DPs emphasize the consumption of fruits, vegetables, wholegrains, nuts, legumes, while limiting the consumption of red meats. However, the DASH diet does not emphasise the consumption of MUFA, fish and moderate alcohol. Instead, it focuses on limiting sodium and sugar consumption to reduce hypertension and diabetes. Limited studies have examined the DASH diet in relation to cognitive function [41]. Evidence regarding the association between the DASH diet and cognitive outcomes remains inconsistent [42]. While some prospective studies have reported slower cognitive decline with higher DASH adherence [43], several others observed no significant associations with cognitive decline or dementia outcomes [[44], [45], [46]].

The DASH diet may influence cognitive function indirectly through improvements in vascular and metabolic health, particularly blood pressure regulation. In a randomized controlled trial, overweight/obese individuals with prehypertension and hypertension showed improved neurocognitive function after 4 months of aerobic exercise combined with the DASH diet and caloric restriction. However, no such effect was observed for the DASH diet alone [47]. Our findings also suggest no association between the DASH diet and cognitive outcomes, as indicated by MCI risk and cognitive decline. However, sodium intake was unavailable in the study and could not be incorporated into the DASH scoring system. As sodium restriction is a key component of the DASH diet, omission of this component may have reduced the discriminatory ability of the modified DASH score.

The HDI diet has been one of the least explored DPs. It was developed based on WHO’s 2003 dietary recommendations to prevent chronic diseases worldwide [48]. Unlike aMED and the DASH diet, HDI is a globally applicable diet quality index that is based on absolute intake values rather than relative dietary intakes within the study population, allowing comparisons across different geographical regions. It has been shown to be associated with all-cause mortality [17,49,50]. In addition to fruits and vegetables, it also includes recommendations on the intakes of dietary fatty acids (i.e., less saturated fat, and more polyunsaturated fat), protein, dietary fiber, cholesterol and sugar intake.

Since the establishment of WHO’s 2003 dietary recommendations, there has been only one prospective study that examined the association between HDI and cognitive health outcomes. It was found that HDI was not associated with any of the outcomes, including AD, all-type dementia, and all-type cognitive impairment, in a cohort of 1,138 elderly Swedish men over 12 years [51]. Additional longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm these findings and further minimize the possibility of reverse causality.

Several risk factors have been found to increase the risk of dementia and AD. Vascular risk factors, primarily diabetes and hypertension, have been shown to be associated with a higher risk of AD in several large population-based clinicopathologic or clinicoradiologic studies [[52], [53], [54]]. Tobacco smoking and high blood cholesterol have also been associated with increased AD risk [55,56]. As vascular and metabolic risk factors frequently conexist and may collectively contribute to neurodegenration [55], we further explored whether these factors modified the relationship between DPs and MCI risk. However, no significant effect modication by these vascular and metabolic risk factors was observed.

The study has several limitations. First, 37.9% of participants were lost to follow-up, which reduced the sample size available for longitudinal analyses and may have introduced selection bias. Furthermore, dementia was not examined as a separate longitudinal outcome because only two dementia cases were identified among participants included in the analytical sample, while some participants were lost to follow-up due to dementia. Consequently, the study was not adequately powered to evaluate progression to dementia. Second, dietary intake was assessed only at baseline, and potential changes in dietary habits over time were not captured. Although substantial dietary changes may be less likely in older adults, some degree of misclassification remains possible [57]. Third, because Mediterranean dietary patterns differ from dietary practices commonly observed in Asian populations, olive oil, a principal fat source in the traditional MED, was substituted with vegetable oils to better reflect local dietary habits and MUFA intake. Some evidence has shown that olive oil plays a key role in MED and may protect against AD, especially in Apolipoprotein-ε4 (APOE-ε4) carriers [58]. In addition, alcohol consumption in this cohort was generally low, which may have reduced the discriminatory contribution of the alcohol component within the aMED score. Finally, as the study population consisted predominantly of Chinese older adults, the findings may not be directly generalisable to other ethnic groups or populations with different dietary practices, cultural backgrounds, and genetic susceptabilities.

Conclusion

In conclusion, adherence to predefined healthy dietary patterns, including aMED, DASH, and HDI, was not prospectively associated with MCI among community-dwelling older Singaporeans in this cohort. Although cross-sectional associations were observed for aMED, these findings were not maintained longitudinally. No significant effect modification by vascular and metabolic risk factors was identified. Despite these findings, healthy dietary patterns remain important for overall health promotion, particularly for cardiovascular and metabolic health. Given the cultural and dietary differences between Asian and Western populations, further longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes, repeated dietary assessments, and more ethnically diverse Asian populations are warranted to determine whether existing predefined dietary patterns are suitable for evaluating cognitive health outcomes in Asian settings, or whether locally derived dietary patterns may be more appropriate.
