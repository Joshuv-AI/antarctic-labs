Quantitative trait loci analysis identifying novel genomic regions for off-target dicamba tolerance in soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.]
Authors: Dongho Lee, Emanuel Ferrari do Nascimento, Andrea Acuna-Galindo, Derrick Harrison, Liliana Florez-Palacios, Rafael Goncalves Marmo, Chengjun Wu, Daniel Rogers, Francia Ravelombola, Feng Lin, James G Shannon, Henry T Nguyen, Caio C Vieira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOff-target dicamba exposure continues to cause substantial injury and yield loss in non-dicamba-tolerant (non-DT) soybean production areas, creating an urgent need for new cultivars with inherent tolerance to unintended dicamba exposure. To support the development of the soybean breeding pipeline, this study aimed to identify genomic regions associated with natural variation for off-target dicamba tolerance. A bi-parental recombinant inbred line population of 188 F4-derived lines was evaluated across four environments, and quantitative trait loci (QTL) analysis identified six loci on chromosomes 6, 9, 10, 13, 19, and 20, collectively explaining 44.7% of the phenotypic variation. Two loci, qDIC_09.1 and qDIC_13.1, were classified as major QTLs based on their higher phenotypic variation explained (9.9% and 13.5%, respectively). Within the physical intervals of these major QTLs, 43 putative candidate genes were selected based on the functional annotations of differentially expressed genes previously characterized under dicamba exposure in Arabidopsis. These included genes encoding glutathione S-transferase, cytochrome P450, and uridine 5’-diphosphate-glycosyltransferase proteins, which participate in key phases of herbicide detoxification. Of 43 putative candidate genes, gene expression analysis revealed 10 putative candidate genes that are highly expressed in the aboveground tissues in soybean. Nucleotide divergence analyses further revealed a large selective sweep spanning qDIC_13.1, suggesting domestication-driven erosion of potentially beneficial alleles associated with detoxification capacity. The genomic regions and putative candidate genes identified here provide valuable resources for marker development, allele mining, and breeding strategies to improve tolerance to off-target dicamba exposure in non-DT soybean cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09241-y.Keywords: Soybean, Dicamba, Quantitative trait loci, Detoxification, Gene expression atlas, Nucleotide divergence

Introduction

Dicamba (3,6-dichloro-2-methoxybenzoic acid) is a synthetic auxin herbicide (Group 4) that has been widely used for more than 50 years to control broadleaf weeds postemergence in pastures, turf, and cereal crops [1]. Between 2015 and 2020, dicamba use in the United States increased nearly 97-fold, expanding from 0.078 to 7.6 million kg of active ingredient, primarily driven by the development and rapid adoption of genetically engineered dicamba-tolerant (DT) crops [2]. By 2021, genetically engineered DT soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.] cultivars accounted for approximately 55% of U.S. soybean acreage [3]. However, because soybeans are inherently sensitive to dicamba, the widespread adoption of genetically engineered DT cultivars, coupled with the herbicide’s high volatility, has resulted in extensive off-target injury and yield loss in non-DT crops [4–8]. In 2021 alone, off-target dicamba exposure resulted in 3,461 incidents affecting over one million acres of non-DT soybean and other crops nationwide [9].

Typical dicamba injury symptoms in non-DT soybean include crinkling and cupping of young leaves, epinasty, stunted growth, chlorosis, apical meristem death, pod malformation, and ultimately, reduced yield [10–14]. The extent of the symptoms and associated yield loss depends on growth stage, dosage, frequency, duration of exposure, and genetic background [13–17]. Egan et al. [15] reported soybean yield losses of 48% or greater at the dicamba concentration of 56.1 g ha− 1 across all soybean growth stages. Given that the label rate is 560 g ha− 1, the result demonstrates that even one-tenth of the labeled application rate can cause severe yield damage in soybean. Recently, Vieira et al. [17] also demonstrated a yield penalty of 8.8% for every increment on a 1–4 scale of off-target dicamba damage, with losses reaching as high as 40% in non-DT soybean breeding lines. Injury is further exacerbated by environmental conditions that promote dicamba movement, including high temperatures and elevated relative humidity [18], as well as lower soil pH [19]. Additionally, non-DT soybeans are especially vulnerable to yield loss when exposure occurs during early reproductive growth stages compared with vegetative growth stages [16, 20].

Genetic characterization of tolerance to off-target dicamba exposure has been investigated in a limited number of genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Vieira et al. [21] evaluated 382 soybean accessions and identified genomic regions associated with tolerance on chromosomes (Chrs.) 10, 11, 14, 15, 18, and 19, with signals on Chrs. 10 and 19 consistently detected across GWAS models. Candidate genes near these genomic regions included uridine 5’-diphosphate (UDP)-dependent glycosyltransferases involved in Phase II plant herbicide detoxification (conjugation) and multidrug resistance proteins associated with Phase III detoxification (transport and sequestration) [22]. A subsequent GWAS of 551 conventional breeding lines reported loci on Chrs. 6, 8, 9, 10, and 19, again co-localizing regions on Chrs. 10 and 19 with the earlier study [23]. Candidate genes from this study included glycosyltransferases, oxidoreductases, stress-response, and ATP-binding cassette transporter proteins, many of which belong to families linked to Phase II detoxification pathways. These studies pointed to reproducible genomic regions and suggested that off-target dicamba tolerance may involve coordinated detoxification-related gene networks, yet broader genetic resolution and validation through quantitative trait loci (QTL) analysis remain needed.

The objectives of this study were to: (i) identify novel QTLs associated with off-target dicamba tolerance in soybean using a bi-parental recombinant inbred line (RIL) population developed from a tolerant germplasm (PI 603497) identified in Vieira et al. [21], and (ii) confirm previously reported genomic regions and putative candidate genes associated with off-target dicamba tolerance. Putative candidate genes were extrapolated based on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified under dicamba exposure in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) [24]. Furthermore, nucleotide divergence analyses, including fixation index (Fst) [25], nucleotide diversity (π) [26], and Tajima’s D [27], offered valuable insights into how the domestication process has led to the erosion of beneficial alleles potentially associated with herbicide detoxification capacity. Identifying and utilizing novel genetic resources for off-target dicamba tolerance may enable the development of both genetically engineered non-DT and conventional soybeans with off-target dicamba tolerance, thereby sustaining production in regions with recurrent off-target dicamba exposure.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and field experiments

A bi-parental RIL population consisting of 188 F4-derived lines (DIC_M) was developed from a cross between the parents PI 603497 and S16-9666, which showed contrasting responses to off-target dicamba exposure (Fig. 1). PI 603497 is a tolerant accession (maturity group III, China) selected from a previous GWAS study [21], while S16-9666 is a susceptible conventional breeding line (maturity group IV, University of Missouri). The initial hybridization was performed in Summer 2020, and the population was advanced from F1 to F4 generations in an off-season nursery in Costa Rica using a modified single-pod descent method [28]. At the F4 generation, single plants were selected based on synchronized maturity group IV and growth habit. The 188 F4-derived RILs, along with both parents, a genetically engineered DT soybean (AG43XF2, XtendFlex®, Bayer Crop Science), a genetically engineered non-DT soybean (P42A84E, Enlist-E3®, Corteva Agriscience), and a conventional off-target dicamba tolerant soybean (S16-12774, University of Missouri), were evaluated for off-target dicamba tolerance at the Arkansas Agricultural Experiment Station, Lon Mann Cotton Research Station, in Marianna, Arkansas (34°43’54.57’’ N, 90°46’0.31’’ W), and at the Fisher Delta Research, Extension and Education Center, in Portageville, Missouri (36°23’44.2” N; 89°36’52.3” W), in 2023 (F4:6) and 2024 (F4:7). The detailed weather information, including temperatures, dew points, and wind, across testing environments on a monthly basis obtained from nearby weather stations (https://www.wunderground.com/) is available in Suppl. Table 1. Trials were conducted using a randomized complete block design with two replications, and each plot consisted of a single 3.0-m row. Environments were exposed to prolonged off-target dicamba, although the source, frequency, timing, and dosage of exposure were not controlled, as described in Vieira et al. [17, 21, 23].

Evaluation of off-target dicamba tolerance and statistical analysis

Soybean genotypes were visually rated for off-target dicamba injury on a 1–4 scale, with 0.5 increments, between the R1 and R3 growth stages [29]. Following the criteria described by Vieira et al. [17], a score of 1 represented symptomology comparable to the DT soybean (AG43XF2), with none to minimal visible injury. A score of 2 indicated moderate tolerance, characterized by slight cupping of young leaves without noticeable reduction in canopy coverage or vegetative growth. A score of 3 reflected intensified leaf cupping with moderate reductions in canopy coverage and vegetative growth, whereas a score of 4 denoted severe injury with pronounced cupping and drastic canopy and growth suppression comparable to the susceptible parent (S16-9666) and non-DT commercial soybean (P42A84E). Consistency of scoring across environments and reliability within environments were evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients and Cronbach’s alpha (α), respectively, as reported by Vieira et al. [17].

The adjusted means for dicamba injury were used as the response variable for the QTL analysis. These were obtained using the function ‘emmeans’ of the R package ‘emmeans’ [30] based on a mixed linear model fitted using the function ‘lmer’ of the R package ‘lmerTest’ [31, 32]. The model included genotype as a fixed effect, and environment (defined as the combination of location and year), genotype × environment interaction, and replicate nested within environment as random effects (Eq. 1).

Cronbach’s alpha (α) score [33] was calculated using ‘alpha’ function of the R package ‘psych’ [34] to evaluate the internal consistency of the off-target dicamba injury scores across environments. A broad-sense heritability (entry-mean basis) of DIC_M population was calculated using ‘H2cal’ function of the R package ‘inti’ [35].

Genotypic data collection

The genomic DNA of S16-9666 was genotyped using the Illumina Infinium BARCSoySNP6K chip (~ 6,000 SNPs) at the USDA-ARS Soybean Genomics & Improvement Laboratory, Beltsville, MD [36]. The SoySNP50K marker data (~ 50,000 SNPs) of PI 603497 was obtained from the publicly available dataset in Soybase (https://www.soybase.org/) [37]. Genomic DNA from all 188 F4:7 RILs was genotyped with a custom subset of the SoySNP50K, comprising 4,999 SNP markers (SNP5K, Corteva Agriscience). As BARCSoySNP6K and SNP5K are subsets of SoySNP50K, respectively, the overlapping SNP markers mapped on the same physical positions on the Wm82.a2.v1 reference genome assembly [38] across the three genotyping platforms were selected for the QTL analysis. As a result, a total of 3,222 SNP markers were obtained and filtered for monomorphism, minor allele frequency < 0.05, and heterozygosity and missingness rates > 0.10. Consequently, a final set of 919 SNP markers was retained to construct the genetic linkage map for QTL analysis.

Genetic linkage map construction and QTL analysis

The genetic linkage map was constructed using the software JoinMap version 5.0 [39]. The linkage groups were clustered based on the logarithm of the odds (LOD) scores of 2 to 15. A regression mapping algorithm with Kosambi’s mapping function was used to convert recombination frequencies into genetic distances (centiMorgans, cM). Then, the QTL analysis was performed using the software MapQTL version 6.0 [40] by following procedures: interval mapping, automatic cofactor selection, and the multi-QTL method. The genome-wide LOD threshold of 3.3 was determined based on a 1000-permutation test at a p-value of 0.05. The significant QTL region was defined by flanking SNP markers encompassing the significant marker(s) with a LOD score greater than the threshold. Hereby, the QTLs identified in this study were named according to the following nomenclature rule: qDIC_chromosome.QTL number (e.g., qDIC_06.1, qDIC_09.1, etc.).

Putative candidate gene selection

Putative candidate genes within the major QTL regions were identified based on the DEGs studied for dicamba tolerance in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) (hereafter referred to as “Ara_DEGs”) [24]. The Ara_DEGs were identified by microarray analysis under dicamba treatment (Suppl. Table 2). The gene ID and functional annotations of Ara_DEGs were based on the Araport11 reference genome assembly [41]. Any genes within the major QTLs showing the same functional annotation as Ara_DEGs were considered putative candidate genes associated with off-target dicamba tolerance. Furthermore, amino acid sequences between putative candidate genes and Ara_DEGs were compared using the software NCBI-BLASTP [42] to assess sequence similarity. Gene regulation (up- and down-regulations) of putative candidate genes was extrapolated from Ara_DEGs with the same functional annotations.

Gene expression atlas of putative candidate genes

Gene expression patterns of putative candidate genes within the major QTLs were evaluated across six aboveground tissues, including flower, hypocotyl, leaf, petiole, seedling, and shoot, using the Soybean Expression Atlas v2 [43]. A total of 3,019 individual samples (BioSamples from NCBI) were used to obtain the raw mRNA dataset. Only the putative candidate gene IDs available in the Wm82.a4.v1 reference genome assembly were used in this analysis. The gene expression patterns were visualized in a heatmap based on the log2-transformed Transcripts Per Million (TPM) values using ‘pheatmap’ function of the R package ‘pheatmap’ [44].

Analysis of genetic divergence during domestication

Genetic divergence analyses for fixation index (Fst), nucleotide diversity (π), and Tajima’s D were conducted using 32 million annotated SNPs derived from 1,308 G. max and 203 G. soja lines [45] to investigate the selective sweep regions within the major QTLs. These analyses assessed different aspects of genetic divergence: (i) The fixation index measures the degree of genetic differentiation between populations (G. max vs. G. soja), ranging from 0 (no genetic differentiation) to 1 (complete differentiation); (ii) nucleotide diversity reflects the level of genetic variation within a population, with lower π values indicating reduced genetic variation; and (iii) Tajima’s D suggests the presence or absence of natural selection and demographic events, where positive values (D > 0) indicate balancing selection and negative values (D < 0) indicate positive selection (selective sweeps). The analyses were performed using the software VCFtools version 0.1.14 [46].

Results

Dicamba scores of DIC_M across testing environments

The frequency of dicamba injury scores in DIC_M population exhibited a normal distribution across four environments (Fig. 2a). The tolerant parent, PI 603497, consistently exhibited high tolerance to off-target dicamba (score of 1.5), whereas S16-9666, the sensitive parent, showed a score of 3.8. Pearson’s correlation coefficients across all replications of environment indicated overall dicamba injury scores of DIC_M were consistent within and across environments, except for MAR_2023_2 (r = 0.42–0.68) (Fig. 2b). The lowest average injury score was observed in MAR_2023 (2.5), while the highest average score was observed in PORT_2024 (2.9) (Table 1). Cronbach’s alpha and broad-sense heritability were 0.76 and 0.66, respectively. Variance component estimates showed that the genotype × environment interaction accounted for the most significant proportion of non-residual variation, whereas the effects of environment and replicate within environment were minimal, indicating moderate genotype × environment and high within-trial uniformity despite uncontrolled off-target dicamba exposure and the subjective nature of visual scoring (Suppl. Genetic linkage map and QTL mapping for off-target dicamba tolerance

The genetic linkage map comprised 919 SNPs distributed across 20 chromosomes (Suppl. Table 4). The QTL mapping analysis identified six QTLs associated with off-target dicamba tolerance on Chrs. 6 (qDIC_06.1), 9 (qDIC_09.1), 10 (qDIC_10.1), 13 (qDIC_13.1), 19 (qDIC_19.1), and 20 (qDIC_20.1) (Fig. 3; Table 2). The LOD values ranged from 3.4 (qDIC_06.1) to 9.6 (qDIC_13.1), and phenotypic variations explained (PVE) by each QTL ranged from 3.8% (qDIC_10.1) to 13.5% (qDIC_13.1). The total variation explained by all identified QTLs was determined by the adjusted R-square of the multiple regression model, which was 44.7%, whereas the ones combining all possible combinations of five, four, three, and two QTLs ranged 33.4–41.6%, 25.4–35.9%, 14.6–29.6%, and 8.6–22.4%, respectively (Suppl. Table 5). Two of these, qDIC_09.1 and qDIC_13.1, were considered major QTLs based on LOD values and PVE, of which the physical positions were 38,864,545–39,823,876 bp on Chr. 9 and 37,963,961 – 39,620,391 bp on Chr. 13, respectively (Table 2).

Allelic effects of the most significant SNP markers for QTLs

With the most significant SNP markers for each QTL, phenotypic variations within the population were evaluated based on the number of favorable alleles and the combinations of the markers (Fig. 4). The result demonstrated a negative correlation (y = -0.2x + 3.3), indicating that the RILs with more favorable alleles showed greater tolerance to off-target dicamba, ranging from 3.5 (none) to 2.1 (all), on average (Fig. 4a). The dicamba scores for the 56 observed allele combinations within the population are shown in Fig. 4b. The allele combinations conferring ‘Tolerant’ class (dicamba score ≤ 2) were qDIC_06.1 + qDIC_09.1 + qDIC_13.1 + qDIC_19.1 + qDIC_20.1 and qDIC_06.1 + qDIC_09.1 + qDIC_10.1 + qDIC_13.1 + qDIC_20.1. The combination containing all favorable alleles showed a dicamba score of 2.1. Notably, the favorable allele of qDIC_13.1 appeared most frequently in the top half of the combinations (21 out of 28), while it was found in only seven out of the 28 combinations in the bottom half (Fig. 4b).

Putative candidate genes in two major QTLs

A total of 220 genes were identified within the physical interval of two major QTLs on Chrs. 9 and 13 (Suppl. Table 6). Out of these, 43 putative candidate genes (15 and 28 genes on Chrs. 9 and 13, respectively) were selected based on the functional annotations of the Ara_DEGs (Suppl. Table 2; Table 3). The list of Ara_DEGs includes several well-known genes involved in plant defense and detoxification processes, such as glutathione S-transferase, cytochrome P450, and UDP-Glycosyltransferase superfamily protein. Other candidate genes regulating hormonal signal responses of auxin, ethylene, gibberellin, and ABA, such as pinoid-binding protein, bHLH DNA-binding superfamily protein, auxin-responsive GH3 family protein, WRKY DNA-binding protein, gibberellin 2-oxidase, and myb-like HTH transcriptional regulator family protein, were also identified within the major QTLs. A total of 23 genes were successfully blasted against the Ara_DEGs with the protein sequence similarity of 31–79% (Table 3). The gene encoding an auxin-responsive GH3 family protein, Glyma.13G284600, showed the highest similarity (79%) to AT5G54510, which was expressed almost twice as much in the Arabidopsis (Suppl. Table 2).

Tissue-specific gene expression of putative candidate genes in soybean

Out of 43 putative candidate genes, a total of 38 putative candidate genes were available in the Wm82.a4.v1 reference genome and examined for gene expression levels across six aboveground tissues (flower, hypocotyl, leaf, petiole, seedling, and shoot) in soybean (Fig. 5). Among them, 10 genes, including Glyma.09G167900, Glyma.09G168300, Glyma.13G283600, Glyma.13G283900, Glyma.13G285300, Glyma.13G286300, Glyma.13G286700, Glyma.13G289600, Glyma.13G291700, and Glyma.13G293700, were highly expressed compared to other genes. Selective sweep region within qDIC_13.1

Nucleotide divergence analyses using 1,308 G. max and 203 G. soja germplasms were conducted for two major QTLs (qDIC_09.1 and qDIC_13.1) and identified one large selective sweep region (464 Kbp from 38,679,000 bp to 39,143,000 bp) within qDIC_13.1 (Fig. 6). This selective sweep region exhibited a high degree of genetic divergence driven by domestication, which was reflected by the mean fixation index (Fst) of 0.7. Based on the nucleotide diversity analysis, G. max (π = 0.04) possesses only a tenth of the genetic variation found in G. soja (π = 0.4). Also, the average Tajima’s D values in the selective sweep region were − 0.8 and 2.4 for G. max and G. soja, respectively. This region contains 13 putative candidate genes associated with off-target dicamba tolerance, including a cluster of three UDP-Glycosyltransferase superfamily proteins (Glyma.13G289000, Glyma.13G289100, and Glyma.13G289200) (Table 3).

Discussion

Since genetically engineered DT soybean was first commercialized, the use of dicamba herbicide and subsequent off-target movement have continuously increased [8, 47, 48]. Although soybeans are inherently sensitive to off-target dicamba exposure, several breeding lines and diverse accessions carrying natural variations associated with off-target dicamba tolerance have been reported [21, 23]. These genetic resources, including the donor line PI 603497, are valuable as parental lines not only for developing genotypes with superior off-target dicamba tolerance but also for dissecting the genomic regions underlying off-target dicamba tolerance through QTL mapping studies. In this study, we also suggested 10 RILs for potential germplasm releases to improve off-target dicamba tolerance in soybean (Table 4). A limitation of the current study was the absence of information on the frequency, timing, and dosage levels of off-target dicamba. However, spatial variations in dicamba exposure within each testing location were considered homogeneous based on (1) the consistent visual injury scores of susceptible parent (S16-9666) and non-DT commercial check (P42A84E) across testing environments with a complete randomized block design and replications (Table 1); and (2) the previous homogeneity test of off-target dicamba exposure in the same testing location in Portageville, MO [17]. Cronbach’s alpha score of 0.76 further indicated the consistency of the visual injury scores across testing environments, with scores above 0.70 considered acceptable [33]. Pearson’s correlation coefficient, ranging from 0.42 to 0.68, further demonstrated the consistency and reliability of visual injury scores across testing environments in this study (Fig. 2b). Broad-sense heritability was estimated at 0.66, indicating moderate to high genetic control of off-target dicamba tolerance within the DIC_M. Combined with Cronbach’s alpha (0.76) and Pearson’s correlation coefficient (0.42–0.68), these results suggest that the visual scoring system was sufficiently robust for QTL mapping analysis. Another limitation of the current study is the absence of records on the days to flowering and maturity of RILs. The differences in flowering time among the RILs could introduce bias by altering the developmental stage at which plants were exposed to or scored for dicamba injury. However, the effect of different flowering times on phenotypic assessment within the DIC_M was minimized by selecting only RILs showing a synchronized flowering time during the population development process. Furthermore, none of the QTL regions identified in this study overlap with well-known major flowering genes, including E1 [49], E2 [50], E3 [51], E4 [52], E9 [53], E10 [54], and J [55].

The overall off-target dicamba tolerance is closely associated with the plant detoxification, which is typically divided into three consecutive phases (Phase I, II, and III). Phase I is responsible for catalyzing chemical reactions (oxidation or hydrolysis) that introduce functional groups (e.g., -OH, -NH2, and -COOH) onto the herbicide molecule, thereby modifying its chemical structure for the subsequent detoxification [56, 57]. One of the most crucial enzymes in Phase I is cytochrome P450 [58]. Phase II is responsible for conjugating the modified herbicide molecules to water-soluble endogenous molecules, such as glutathione and glucose, to neutralize the herbicide’s phytotoxic activity [22]. The crucial enzymes in Phase II for the conjugation with glutathione and glucose are glutathione S-transferases and UDP-dependent glycosyltransferases, respectively [22]. The final phase, Phase III, involves transporting the non-phytotoxic herbicide conjugates into the vacuole, regulated by multidrug resistance proteins or ATP-binding cassette transporters, which are the most critical enzymes [22, 56].

At the time of publication, it is the belief of the authors that this is the first study to identify QTL regions associated with off-target dicamba tolerance in soybean using a bi-parental mapping population. Herein, a total of six QTLs on Chrs. 6, 9, 10, 13, 19, and 20 were identified (Fig. 3; Table 2). Our results are well-aligned with significant SNP markers from previous GWAS results for off-target dicamba and other herbicide tolerance. A QTL on Chr. 6 (qDIC_06.1) spanned a relatively large physical interval (29,864,353–47,518,032 bp) primarily due to low marker density and a lack of recombination events in this region. Despite the broad interval, qDIC_06.1 was mapped close to a significant SNP (ss715594836, LOD score of 7.5) at 48,080,474 bp identified using 551 advanced breeding lines developed by the University of Missouri [23]. Although the herbicide’s mode of action is different, another GWAS for bentazone tolerance in soybean identified two significant SNPs on Chr. 6 (45,537,285 and 45,542,880 bp, respectively) using 418 soybean accessions from the South Korean core collection [59]. A QTL on Chr. 10 (qDIC_10.1), located at 4,991,089–5,588,824 bp, is mapped approximately 3.7 Mbp upstream of a significant SNP ss715605561 (1,227,933 bp), which was identified in two separate studies using 382 soybean accessions and 551 advanced breeding lines, respectively [21, 23]. A major QTL on Chr. 13 (qDIC_13.1), located at 37,963,961–39,620,391 bp, is mapped approximately 3.3 Mbp downstream of ss715616396 (42,903,598 bp). The SNP ss715616396 was one of the significant predictors of the highest classification accuracy for off-target dicamba tolerance when implementing Random Forest and Support Vector Machine models [23]. Vieira et al. [23] also reported another significant SNP ss715633252 at 1,701,069 bp on Chr. 19, which is mapped close to qDIC_19.1 (975,886–1,610,209 bp). A major QTL on Chr. 9 (qDIC_09.1; 38,864,545–39,823,876 bp) and a minor QTL on Chr. 20 (qDIC_20.1; 45,959,668–46,691,512 bp) were novel genomic regions associated with off-target dicamba tolerance in soybean. Despite the relatively large genomic distances between the QTLs and the previously reported SNPs (e.g., qDIC_10.1 and qDIC_13.1), these associations can still be considered meaningful given the marker densities and the population structures used in each study.

The distribution of dicamba injury scores among allele classes showed a general additive pattern in which increased numbers of favorable alleles were associated with greater tolerance. However, the apparent plateau between genotypes carrying five versus six favorable alleles does not necessarily indicate that the additional loci have no effect. Genotypes that lacked only the favorable allele at qDIC_10.1 or qDIC_19.1 still exhibited mean scores below 2.0 (Fig. 4b), but these allele classes were represented by only a single genotype each. Thus, the phenotypic pattern likely reflects insufficient sampling of specific allele combinations rather than a true absence of effect. This limited representation prevents reliable inference regarding the contribution of qDIC_10.1 and qDIC_19.1, and additional lines carrying these combinations would be required to determine whether these loci are necessary for enhanced tolerance. Potential QTL × environment interactions may further contribute to the observed response patterns [60].

In this study, the putative candidate genes within the major QTLs (qDIC_09.1 and qDIC_13.1) were studied by using the Ara_DEGs identified in Arabidopsis [24]. Although the low marker density resulted in a relatively large physical interval for the qDIC_13.1 (37,963,961–39,620,391 bp; approximately 1.7 Mbp), encompassing approximately 150 genes, 28 putative candidate genes were effectively selected by comparing functional annotations and protein sequences of Ara_DEGs with genes located in the qDIC_13.1 (Table 3). Gleason et al. [24] used a 7 mM concentration of dicamba for treatment at the early vegetative stage (4-day-old seedlings) to mimic agricultural practices. Although the precise frequency, timing, and dosage of off-target dicamba exposure were unknown, we hypothesized that the underlying molecular and biological response mechanisms in soybean would be similar to those identified in Arabidopsis. As both soybean and Arabidopsis are dicotyledonous plants with conserved auxin signaling, they share the core mode of action for synthetic auxin herbicides [12, 61, 62]. This conserved mode of action results in similar physiological symptoms in both soybean and Arabidopsis, including epinasty, leaf cupping, and stem distortion, which have been well documented in both species in response to the synthetic auxin herbicide exposure [12, 63]. Furthermore, similar downstream responses, including overproduction of ethylene and accumulation of ABA, are observed in both species, contributing to senescence and growth inhibition [63–65]. Therefore, the use of differentially expressed genes identified in Arabidopsis provides a biologically justified foundation for candidate gene identification in soybean. Five candidate genes, Glyma.09G167300, Glyma.13G285300, Glyma.13G289000, Glyma.13G289100, and Glyma.13G289200, encode well-known proteins closely involved in two main phases of the plant detoxification process (Table 3). A candidate gene, Glyma.09G167300, encodes glutathione S-transferase, which neutralizes reactive toxic substances by adding a molecule of glutathione (Phase II detoxification) [22, 66]. The cytochrome P450 encoded by Glyma.13G285300 is crucial for Phase I detoxification, which is a process that modifies toxins via oxidation, preparing them for subsequent metabolism in Phase II [58]. The UDP-Glycosyltransferase superfamily protein encoded by Glyma.13G289000, Glyma.13G289100, and Glyma.13G289200 is a key enzyme of glycosylation in Phase II detoxification, which neutralizes toxic substances by attaching a sugar molecule (glucose) [22]. However, some candidate genes showed significant divergence (approximately 30% of protein sequence similarity) or no detectable homology in protein sequences between soybean and Arabidopsis (Table 3). This disparity possibly reflects the evolutionary distance between these species [67]. Furthermore, essential core domains that confer the same biological functions in plant detoxification across two species may be retained during multiple duplication and mutation events, even though substantial sequence drift has occurred [68, 69]. Also, these proteins may adopt similar three-dimensional folds to maintain functional equivalence, even though their primary sequences differ [70]. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct further structural and biological validation to confirm the roles of these highly divergent candidate genes with the same functional annotations between soybean and Arabidopsis.

Besides those well-known genes, other candidate genes are responsible for hormonal signaling responses to auxin, ethylene, and ABA, as dicamba is an auxinic herbicide that increases ethylene production and ABA biosynthesis [71, 72]. A candidate gene, Glyma.13G283600, encodes a pinoid-binding protein, which is a crucial regulator of auxin transport and its directional flow by adding a phosphate group to pin-formed (PIN) proteins [73, 74]. The phosphorylated PIN proteins relocate to the apical (top) position in the cell and disrupt overall auxin transport. Overexpression of pinoid-binding protein may confer off-target dicamba tolerance by counteracting the abnormal auxin transport, which is dysregulated by synthetic auxin overload, thereby mitigating subsequent developmental defects. Another candidate gene related to regulating auxin concentration is an auxin-responsive GH3 family protein, which is encoded by Glyma.13G284600. The auxin-responsive GH3 proteins are induced to mitigate high levels of auxin in plants by conjugating auxins for degradation. Two auxin-regulated gibberellin 2-oxidase genes (Glyma.13G287600 and Glyma.13G28800) were also identified [75]. In qDIC_13.1, four genes (Glyma.13G284400, Glyma.13G286700, Glyma.13G291400, and Glyma.13G296100) encoding bHLH DNA-binding superfamily proteins were identified. The bHLH DNA-binding superfamily proteins are well-characterized families of transcription factors that are responsible for regulating genes in the ethylene and ABA signaling pathways and modulating abiotic stress tolerance [76–78]. Across multiple dicot families (Solanaceae, Rubiaceae, and Fabaceae), auxinic herbicides significantly increased ABA levels, particularly in the shoots, whereas crop species with natural tolerance to these herbicides showed no such increases in ABA levels [64]. WRKY DNA-binding protein and myb-like HTH transcriptional regulator family protein are also transcriptional regulators inducible by increased ethylene in plants under abiotic and biotic stresses [79–81]. Notably, we also found detoxification-related candidate genes within the other QTLs, highlighting a large cluster of 37 and nine SAUR-like auxin-responsive proteins on qDIC_06.1 and qDIC_10.1, respectively (Suppl. Table 7). SAUR-like auxin-responsive proteins are rapidly induced regulatory proteins that mediate rapid auxin-driven growth responses, primarily cell expansion through modulation of cellular signaling pathways [82]. Further gene expression atlas analysis showed that 10 putative candidate genes highly expressed in the aboveground tissues could be potentially relevant to the response to off-target dicamba exposure in soybean. Also, genes without any functional annotations exhibiting higher gene expression in the aboveground tissues within the major QTLs would be worth investigating further as potential soybean-specific novel genes for herbicide tolerance.

Intense selection for domestication-related traits, such as seed dormancy, pod shattering, and agronomic performance, including lodging, grain yield, and stem growth habit, has created extensive selective sweeps in modern soybean cultivars [83, 84]. The drastic reduction of genetic diversity (50% of genetic diversity and 81% of rare alleles from G. soja), often compounded by linkage drag, has led to the loss of potentially valuable alleles [83, 85, 86]. The presence of a selective sweep region (~ 464 Kbp from 38,679,000 bp to 39,143,000 bp) within the qDIC_13.1 provides compelling evidence that domestication-driven selection reduced genetic diversity within this genomic interval, potentially leading to the loss of nearby detoxification-related alleles. Interestingly, this selective sweep region overlaps with several other agronomically important QTLs, including those for plant height [87], root nodule number and weight [88], and main stem length [89]. Furthermore, other QTLs, including stem strength [90], grain yield trait [91, 92], and plant height [91], were also mapped near qDIC_13.1.

Further bioinformatic analyses, including RNA-seq and whole-genome sequencing of selected RILs and parental lines, are warranted to validate the candidate genes identified within the major QTLs in this study. These analyses will confirm the gene expression pattern between tolerant and susceptible RILs and identify SNPs and small insertions and deletions (INDELs) that potentially induce causal variants. Subsequently, the biological functions of candidate genes should be confirmed through downstream approaches such as gene editing and overexpression assays. Although six QTLs were identified using a single mapping population in this study, validation of these loci in independent populations would strengthen their robustness and general applicability, such as molecular marker development. Therefore, future studies will be required to validate the effects and stability of these QTLs across multiple genetic backgrounds and environments. Controlled-dosage experiments using the RIL population across multiple growth stages are essential to more precisely define the physiological thresholds that differentiate tolerant and susceptible genotypes.

Conclusion

The QTL analysis using a RIL population identified six QTLs across Chrs. 6, 9, 10, 13, 19, and 20 associated with off-target dicamba tolerance in soybean. The 43 potential candidate genes selected from two major QTLs, qDIC_09.1 and qDIC_13.1, include several genes encoding well-characterized proteins involved in the detoxification process, such as glutathione S-transferase, cytochrome P450, and UDP-Glycosyltransferase superfamily protein. A large selective sweep on qDIC_13.1 indicates a loss of genetic diversity and pinpoints a region that harbors potentially rare alleles involved in herbicide detoxification. The novel genetic resources for off-target dicamba tolerance identified in this study offer significant benefits to both soybean breeders and growers to minimize off-target dicamba damage in genetically engineered non-DT and conventional soybean systems.
