STARA technology application and innovative behavior among Chinese employees: the roles of idea exploration support, STARA awareness, and leaders’ STARA symbolization
Authors: Yanjuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the increasing application of smart technologies, artificial intelligence, robotics and algorithms (STARA) in workplace settings, creative work has become essential for human employees. Although STARA technology’s application has the potential to enhance employees’ innovative behavior, evidence also suggests that it may inhibit it. To reconcile these conflicting perspectives, this study—based on questionnaire surveys and empirical data analysis—introduces Idea exploration support, STARA awareness, and leaders’ STARA symbolization to explore the mechanism through which STARA influences innovative behavior. The findings reveal a positive association between STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior, mediated by both idea exploration support and STARA awareness, and moderated by leaders’ STARA symbolization. This study proposes a novel dual-path cognitive model that integrates both support-based and threat-based psychological mechanisms, advancing current literature with a holistic perspective on how STARA influences employees’ innovative behavior, while introducing leaders’ STARA symbolization as a key boundary condition. These findings not only enrich theories in human resource management but also offer practical insights to improve the efficiency of human resource utilization in organizations.Keywords: STARA technology application, Idea exploration support, STARA awareness, Leaders’ STARA symbolization, Employees’ innovative behavior

Introduction

Over the past few decades, technologies such as smart technologies, artificial intelligence, robotics and algorithms (STARA) have witnessed rapid development. Capitalizing on STARA technology’s remarkable advantages in optimizing decision - making and enhancing efficiency [1–3], these technologies have been widely adopted across the globe in a wide range of industries and have become well - established in multiple application scenarios [4–7]. For example, numerous scholars have conducted empirical analyses on the STARA technology application in service industry [8], hospitality industry [9], and manufacturing industry [10], as well as its impact on employee behavior. The results show that the industrial STARA technology application can not only optimize operations, reduce costs, and enhance innovation capabilities [3, 11], but may also undermine employees’ psychological safety, organizational commitment, job engagement, career satisfaction, and competitiveness [12–14], and intensify work overload, burnout, turnover intentions, depression, and cynicism [8, 9, 15].

Innovative behavior refers to the actions of employees when they generate novel ideas and implement them in their daily work routines [16–18]. As STARA has evolved from auxiliary tools to work companions within organizations [3, 19], it has profoundly transformed current occupational patterns and organizational ecosystems [20]. STARA plays a crucial role in streamlining work processes and promoting technological innovation [21]. Meanwhile, it may also subtly reshape the employee-organization relationship dynamics [22–24], thus influencing innovative behaviors [25–27]. However, in the existing research surrounding the impact of STARA technology application on employees’ innovative behavior, scholars have reached opposing conclusions. On one hand, STARA promotes employees’ innovative behavior by handling complex tasks and adapting to environmental changes [28], as well as achieving user preference matching through situational learning [29]. Moreover, the STARA technology application reconstructs the employees’ responsibility system, shifting it towards system maintenance and creativity-oriented tasks [30]. This transformation creates the fundamental conditions for human-machine collaboration. When employees are motivated to innovate, organizations can fully leverage the advantages of human-machine collaboration [31]. Eventually, through the collaborative mechanism of resource integration and complementary capabilities, employees’ innovative behavior is significantly stimulated [27, 32, 33]. On the other hand, the innovation-stimulating potential of STARA is significantly limited. Solving creative problems still essentially depends on human wisdom [34]. The application of this technology may damage mental health [35], ultimately leading to avoidance behaviors that actively suppress employees’ innovation [8].

This phenomenon raises two fundamental questions: first, does implementing STARA technology have a positive or negative impact on employees’ innovative behavior? Second, what underlying mechanism explains this relationship? To reconcile these conflicting perspectives and address the research gap, this study develops a novel dual-path cognitive model. This model captures two contrasting yet coexisting cognitive pathways: one reflecting opportunity-oriented cognition that fosters innovation, and the other reflecting threat-oriented cognition that constrains it. Moreover, existing research largely overlooks the critical role of leadership in shaping employees’ interpretations and responses to technological stimuli. This study further identifies a key contextual boundary condition related to leadership symbolism.

Theoretical foundation and research hypotheses

Theoretical foundation

This research is firmly rooted in the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which postulates that individual cognition, behavior, and the environment interact within a triadic system [36]. Within the architecture of the SCT model, each variable assumes a distinct and significant role. The STARA technology application serves as a technological impetus within the environmental system. It revolutionizes work processes through the utilization of automated tools and intelligent systems, while also communicating the organization’s aspirations for innovation. These two underlying mechanisms imply a certain degree of correlation between the STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior.

Idea exploration support and STARA awareness are variables related to individual perception. Idea exploration support, which represents the resources and cultural support that enable employees to explore novel ideas in the workplace [37], is provided by STARA as it integrates knowledge from multiple domains and broadens employees’ cognitive horizons [38, 39]. This support is of utmost importance for organizational innovation. STARA awareness, which refers to employees’ perception of the potential substitution of their jobs by STARA technology [8, 40], addresses the concerns arising from the potential threats posed by STARA, despite its advantages in reducing workload and enhancing efficiency [8, 10, 13, 41]. Therefore, idea exploration support and STARA awareness may potentially serve as mediators in the relationship between the STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior.

Leaders’ STARA symbolization is an intensifying variable in the symbolic environment that modifies the cognitive prominence of environmental stimuli. As STARA technology progresses, leaders are inclined to demonstrate their attention through actions or symbolic manifestations, a phenomenon termed “leaders’ STARA symbolization”, similar to “leaders’ AI symbolization” [42]. This provides employees with the information necessary to interpret leadership intentions. Given that STARA technology drives organizational transformation and presents opportunities for advancement [43], leaders’ active support may prompt employees to recognize their foresight and innovative spirit [44, 45].

The STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior

According to SCT, environmental factors shape human cognition and behavior through triadic reciprocal determinism, in which personal factors, environmental influences, and behavior continually interact [36]. The application of STARA technology serves as a critical environmental input that influences employees’ cognitive processes and behavioral outcomes by enhancing self-efficacy and positive outcome expectations, two core motivational mechanisms in SCT. Firstly, STARA technology can autonomously handle repetitive, complex, and high-cognitive-demand tasks [46], helping employees access idle resources and enhancing their flexibility. By fully exercising their discretion, employees can independently arrange their work processes, thereby enhancing their perceived autonomy [46, 47], which in turn increases the likelihood of generating innovative ideas and engaging in innovative practices. Secondly, STARA technology, through streamlining work processes and optimizing resource allocation, provides employees with more time and energy to devote to innovative activities [30, 48]. For example, automation tools efficiently manage repetitive tasks, enabling employees to focus on more creative and strategic work [30, 49]. This efficiency not only significantly lightens the workload of employees [47] but also creates more opportunities for innovation. Lastly, STARA technology, through data analysis and intelligent decision support, provides employees with more comprehensive information and insights, thereby stimulating innovative thinking. For example, STARA technology can continuously capture, mine, and understand large volumes of data, helping employees discover new solutions or optimize existing processes [11]. This data-driven approach to innovation can significantly enhance employees’ creativity and problem-solving abilities. Consistent with the logic of SCT, such environmental inputs shape personal cognition and further drive innovative behavior. Therefore, this study proposes:

H1: There is a positive correlation between the STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior.

The mediating role of idea exploration support

Within the SCT framework, cognitive and personal factors serve as critical mediators, translating environmental stimuli into behavioral responses through evaluative and self-regulatory processes [36]. The theory emphasizes that environmental inputs undergo cognitive processing before manifesting as behavior, with evaluative judgments and regulatory mechanisms serving as pivotal explanatory frameworks. Specifically, support for idea exploration is a cognitive-perceptual variable that reflects employees’ evaluation of resource availability and organizational support for innovation activities. It serves as a crucial bridge that conveys the impact of the environment on behavioral outcomes. In line with SCT, this cognitive-perceptual factor links external environmental inputs to internal motivation and subsequent behavior. In the context of STARA technology usage, employees gain support in areas such as creative decision-making assistance, feedback, and improvement, thereby increasing their stock of creative resources and subsequently perceiving idea exploration support. On one hand, STARA, through intelligent data analysis, can accurately identify potential correlations and trends within data [50], enabling employees to gain deeper insights into market demands and make more informed creative decisions. On the other hand, STARA can use historical data to run algorithmic simulations [4], helping employees evaluate the opportunities and challenges their innovative ideas might face in real-world scenarios, thereby continuously refining and optimizing these ideas. It is evident that employees can explore valuable ideas through the assistance and feedback provided by STARA, thereby perceiving strong Idea exploration support.

Some research indicates that the value of resources lies in their ability to help individuals achieve work-related goals [51]. For the creative resources acquired, employees need to invest them into actual innovative actions to fully realize their value. In reality, one of the key reasons employees persist with innovative ideas is their belief in their value and potential, convinced that implementing them will lead to success and organizational recognition [52]. This suggests that when employees perceive they have abundant creative resources, they believe they can achieve performance advantages through innovation [53]. Therefore, it can be inferred that the perception of Idea exploration support formed through the STARA technology application reinforces employees’ innovative direction and motivates innovative practices. This motivational process is fully consistent with the self-regulation mechanism proposed by SCT. Therefore, this study proposes:

H2: Idea exploration support mediates the relationship between the STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior.

The mediating role of STARA awareness

SCT emphasizes that cognitive appraisal processes fundamentally determine how individuals interpret and respond to environmental stimuli, with threat perceptions triggering distinct cognitive and behavioral patterns [36]. The theory suggests that when individuals perceive environmental factors as threatening, they experience evaluative shifts and regulatory disruptions, which subsequently lead to avoidance behaviors rather than proactive engagement. This cognitive mechanism clarifies why the same environmental stimulus can generate different behavioral outcomes based on individuals’ appraisal processes. It emphasizes the significance of cognitive factors in the relationship between the environment and behavior. According to SCT, such negative cognitive appraisal directly reduces self-efficacy and inhibits proactive engagement. Some research suggests that the stronger an individual’s perception of a stressor as harmful or threatening, the higher the likelihood that they will resort to emotion-focused coping strategies [54]. These strategies involve negative psychological states and avoidance behaviors, such as job withdrawal and processing negative emotions, rather than addressing the problem directly [55]. With the rapid development of STARA, employees must ensure the system’s proper and safe functioning [56]. The advanced nature of STARA is gradually shifting the dynamic from “machine assisting humans” to “humans assisting machines” [57], potentially marginalizing employees’ roles in the value chain [58]. This shift diminishes employees’ value and importance, leading to job insecurity. In contrast, lower levels of STARA application do not threaten employees and can enhance self-identity, reducing internal group bias and tension [59, 60]. Therefore, when employees perceive STARA technology as a threat, their self-esteem is threatened, they experience anxious emotions [61, 62], and they develop psychological discomfort and job insecurity [35, 63, 64], thereby triggering STARA awareness.

STARA awareness mirrors job insecurity due to smart technology, reducing commitment and satisfaction, and fostering burnout and turnover intentions [8, 9, 13], resulting in avoidance and escape from positive behaviors like innovation [10]. Firstly, employees may reduce the quantity, scope, or type of innovative work through neglect or avoidance of certain work elements [65], thereby weakening the emergence of innovative intentions. Secondly, employees may reduce the quantity and quality of human-machine interactions, thereby diminishing the role and capabilities of artificial intelligence in the work process and limiting the exploration and learning of their innovative skills and abilities [66], which in turn suppresses innovative behavior. Finally, avoidance psychology and escape behaviors cause employees to view STARA through biased and narrow lenses, preventing them from developing broad-minded thinking to stimulate innovation-oriented cognitive processing [25], thus hindering the generation of innovative behaviors. This process aligns with SCT’s view that threat-related cognitive appraisal leads to defensive and avoidant behaviors. Therefore, this study proposes:

H3: STARA awareness mediates the relationship between the STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior.

Moderating role of leaders’ STARA symbolization

Consistent with SCT’s emphasis on the symbolic environment within the triadic reciprocal causation model, leaders’ STARA symbolization serves as a symbolic environmental factor that influences how employees cognitively appraise and behaviorally respond to STARA technology [33]. According to SCT, symbolic cues from leadership modify employees’ self-efficacy beliefs and outcome expectations, thereby moderating the impact of environmental factors on behavioral outcomes. This reflects the core view of SCT that the symbolic environment shapes individual cognitive interpretation. In real-world work settings, employees look for cues to comprehend and form perceptions of their work environment [55]. Among these cues, leader behavior plays a pivotal and crucial role [42]. In this context, leaders’ STARA symbolization plays a key moderating role in the relationship between STARA technology application and employee innovative behavior. On one hand, employees cognitively process emotional information conveyed by leaders to appraise leaders’ traits, inclinations, and capabilities [67]. Leaders’ STARA symbolization communicates positive emotions towards STARA technology [42]. Upon observing leaders’ symbolic behaviors regarding STARA, employees perceive leaders’ confidence in the technology’s future development [68]. This positive outlook prompts employees to assess leaders’ readiness for change, integrating this positive sentiment as a significant input in their perceptions. Thus, leaders’ STARA symbolization not only signals support for the technology but also conveys an optimistic attitude towards change, significantly enhancing employees’ perception of leaders’ readiness for change [69]. On the other hand, employees infer leaders’ cognitive beliefs about change through leaders’ STARA symbolization [70]. A key aspect in assessing leaders’ readiness for change is their recognition of the necessity for change [71]. Leaders’ STARA symbolization indicates attention to STARA technology, reflecting foresight and sensitivity to change, leading employees to infer that leaders value the necessity of change. Additionally, leaders’ skills, knowledge, and abilities are crucial factors in employees’ assessment of their cognitive beliefs about change [72]. Leaders’ STARA symbolization conveys a preference for STARA technology [42], based on rational judgments about its potential benefits, suggesting that STARA application can bring positive changes [73, 74]. By symbolically supporting STARA’s organizational application, leaders not only enhance operational efficiency but also communicate their ability to identify and adapt to change. As such, in high levels of leaders’ STARA symbolization, employees are more likely to perceive STARA technology use as an opportunity for personal growth and development, leading to positive evaluations [42], and reduced concerns about STARA, focusing instead on its positive aspects [42]. This encourages proactive learning of STARA-related knowledge and skill enhancement, better equipping employees to handle stress and reducing the perception of STARA use as a threat, thereby stimulating innovative behavior [42]. Conversely, in low levels of leaders’ STARA symbolization, employees focus more on the risks of job replacement and potential losses from STARA technology use, adopting more conservative approaches to environmental changes. Following SCT’s reasoning, such symbolic cues determine whether employees interpret technology as opportunity or threat. Therefore, this study proposes:

H4: Leaders’ STARA symbolization moderates the relationship between STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior.

Methods

Data collection

This study adopted a positivist research paradigm and employed a cross-sectional survey design to test the proposed hypotheses through empirical data. To ensure measurement validity, the questionnaire was developed using established scales from prior studies. It consisted of two main sections. The first section introduced the study’s purpose and included the informed consent form. Participants were asked to read the information carefully and provide consent before proceeding to the survey items. The second section included demographic questions and items measuring the main variables, namely STARA technology application, idea exploration support, STARA awareness, leaders’ STARA symbolization, and employees’ innovative behavior. To help respondents better understand the technological context, the questionnaire provided several examples of STARA-related applications used by well-known Chinese enterprises. These included JD.com’s “Yanxi” intelligent customer-service system, which automates responses to customer inquiries; Haier’s COSMOPlat industrial Internet platform, which uses IoT and big-data analytics to support production processes; Ping An’s intelligent risk-control system, which monitors financial transactions through predictive algorithms; Xiaomi’s smart factory, which applies robotic arms and Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs) in assembly and logistics; and Alibaba Cloud’s “Tongyi Qianwen,” an AI assistant that can support corporate knowledge management and decision-making. These examples were intended to improve respondents’ understanding of the STARA technologies examined in this study.

The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Business Administration at Henan University of Animal Husbandry and Economy (Approval No. EA2024069). In accordance with China’s age-of-majority standard, only adults aged 18 or older were included in this study; no minors were involved.

From May 7th to June 30th, 2024, data were gathered via a questionnaire-based survey. The target population was full-time employees of Chinese organizations who had exposure to STARA technology in their work environments. The population frame consisted of employee lists from HR departments across various industries, including technology, manufacturing, finance, and services, in multiple regions of China. To ensure representativeness, the sample included employees from a wide range of Chinese industries who had exposure to STARA technology through both online and offline channels. Based on a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), statistical power of 0.95, significance level of 0.05, and 5 predictors, the required minimum sample size was estimated using GPower 3.1. Considering possible non-response and invalid responses, 540 questionnaires were distributed. The sampling technique employed was stratified random sampling by industry sector to ensure diversity. Researchers collaborated with corporate HR departments to get employee lists and performed random sampling. Data collection was conducted through both online (70%) and physical (30%) distribution methods. Online surveys were distributed via professional survey platforms with personalized links sent through corporate communication channels, while physical questionnaires were administered on-site at collaborating companies with HR coordination during work hours. Researchers approached participants through formal collaboration with corporate HR departments, who sent survey invitations to randomly selected employees via official email and enterprise WeChat groups, and facilitated on-site distribution during arranged sessions. Before the formal survey, 70 questionnaires were pre-tested. Pre-test analysis showed that all variables’ AVEs were at least 0.50 and all item factor loadings exceeded 0.60, indicating acceptable convergent validity of the measurement items. The Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability values also exceeded the recommended thresholds, indicating satisfactory internal consistency.

A total of 540 questionnaires were distributed. Subsequently, 481 questionnaires were retrieved, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the survey. This achievement led to a commendable response rate of 89.1%. This rate is lower than the response rates reported in some relevant studies [75, 76] and higher than those reported in other studies [77, 78]. This indicates that the response rate of this study falls within the normal range. This can be attributed to two main factors. Firstly, the standardized process facilitated by the HR department has fostered a high level of trust among employees. Secondly, pre-testing optimization has enhanced the clarity of the questionnaire items.

Subsequently, sample screening was conducted in three strict steps. First, incomplete responses were eliminated. These were questionnaires with more than 10% of the core items unanswered; 7 were removed in this step. Second, questionnaires with logical errors were deemed invalid and eliminated. These included questionnaires with consistent or contradictory answers across logically related items, such as consistently selecting the same extreme value for all items and contradictory responses between STARA usage frequency and actual application experience. A total of 6 questionnaires were removed in this step. Finally, respondents with no actual exposure, contact, or use of STARA technology in their daily work were excluded from the valid sample to ensure that all participants had genuine STARA-related experience. In this step, 4 questionnaires were excluded. After the three-step screening, 464 valid questionnaires remained, which were used for all subsequent analyses. Following Kline’s [79] recommendation and Hair et al.’s [80] guideline on the ratio of cases to parameters (at least 10:1), the effective sample size of 464 is sufficient for the model, which estimates approximately 40 free parameters. Measure

In this study, the selection of measurement scales for variables is based on the following principles: Firstly, the content of the scales and the variables they measure are highly congruent with the theme of this research, directly fulfilling the research requirements. Secondly, these scales have withstood extensive research verification and boast high internal consistency reliability, ensuring the stability and dependability of the measurement results. Thirdly, the scales are widely used in relevant fields and well-known, which enhances the comparability and generalizability of the research findings. Given these three principles, this study measures the variables as follows:

The measurement of STARA technology application was adapted from the scale developed by Sheng et al. [81], consisting of 4 items. An example item is: “I heavily rely on information resources provided by STARA technology to complete my design work.”

To measure Idea exploration support, we used a 3-item scale adapted from Petzsche et al. [39]. A representative item is: “STARA would enable me to extensively explore new knowledge and ideas.”

To measure STARA awareness, a 4-item scale was used, adapted from Brougham and Haar [8]. An example item is: “I think my job could be replaced by STARA.”

Leaders’ STARA symbolization was assessed using a 6-item scale developed by He et al. [42]. For instance, one item is: “My supervisor initiates discussions related to STARA with me.”

Innovation behavior was measured using a 6-item scale adapted from Bani et al. [82]. A sample item is: “At work, I come up with innovative and creative notions.”

All items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

Tools

Considering the extensive data-analysis advantages of Mplus 8.0, it’s an ideal tool. Firstly, it effortlessly handles complex models such as latent variable and multilevel models common in advanced research. It also supports diverse data types, including missing values, and adapts well to real-world scenarios. Moreover, its user-friendly syntax simplifies the analysis setup, and the robust visualizations help researchers easily interpret and present results. Significantly, its comprehensive estimation methods ensure accurate, reliable analyses for valid conclusions. Thus, it’s logical that this study used Mplus 8.0 for data analysis.

Sample common method bias test

This research used the latent method factor approach to assess common method bias. Given the limitations of a single detection method, Harman’s single-factor test was supplemented with additional methods to provide a more comprehensive and robust assessment. The latent method factor analysis indicated that adding a common method factor slightly improved model fit: RMSEA and SRMR decreased by 0.01 and 0.02, respectively, while CFI and NFI increased by 0.02 and 0.03. Meanwhile, Harman’s single-factor test showed that the first factor explained only 36.51% of the variance, well below the critical threshold. Collectively, although method variance existed to some extent, it did not dominate the overall variance. Therefore, common method bias was not a serious confounding issue in this study and common method bias was unlikely to be a serious concern in this study [83].

Test of mediating effect

Regarding the two mediating paths, the indirect effect of STARA technology application on employees’ innovative behavior through Idea exploration support, namely Ind1: STA → IES → IB, is positive and significant (Estimate = 0.09, S.E. = 0.03, Est./SE = 3.10, p = .002, 95% CI = [0.04, 0.15]). This indicates that the STARA technology application enhances employees’ innovative behavior by increasing support for idea exploration. Thus, Hypothesis H2 is supported.

In contrast, the indirect effect through STARA awareness, namely Ind2: STA → SA → IB, is negative and significant (Estimate = − 0.06, S.E. = 0.02, Est./SE = -2.75, p = .006, 95% CI = [-0.10, − 0.02]). This suggests that the STARA technology application may also increase employees’ STARA awareness, which, in turn, weakens employees’ innovative behavior. Therefore, Hypothesis H3 is supported.

After 5,000 bootstrap iterations, the 95% confidence intervals of both indirect effects do not include zero, indicating that both mediating effects are statistically significant. Since the direct effect remains significant after including the mediators, the results suggest partial mediation. Overall, these findings provide support for Hypotheses H1, H2, and H3.

Specifically, the positive effect of STARA technology application on employees’ innovative behavior is stronger when leaders’ STARA symbolization is higher. Therefore, Hypothesis H4 is supported.

Discussion

This study identifies a positive association between STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior. In line with SCT, STARA functions as an environmental enabler, enhancing employees’ confidence and expectations for creative action. Compared with prior research that emphasizes either the bright or dark side of STARA, the present findings confirm the net innovation-promoting effect of intelligent technology in the Chinese workplace, supporting the view that digital tools empower rather than undermine employee proactivity [25, 26, 30].

A particularly meaningful insight is that STARA simultaneously activates two contrasting psychological pathways: idea exploration support and STARA awareness. While the former strengthens resource-related cognition and fuels innovation, the latter generates threat perceptions that hinder engagement in innovation. This pattern reveals that employees do not hold a unidimensional perception of STARA; instead, they experience opportunity and threat simultaneously. Such a dual response helps explain why existing findings have been highly inconsistent, as different studies may capture either the positive or negative cognitive component rather than the full picture [8, 25].

Furthermore, this study highlights the key signaling function of leaders in technology adoption. Employees’ interpretation of STARA is strongly shaped by leaders’ symbolic attitudes and visible support. This extends prior leadership research by showing that leadership effects go beyond general styles and include domain-specific signals toward new technologies [42]. In digital transformation, leaders’ symbolic engagement can fundamentally alter how employees perceive and interact with intelligent systems, thereby determining whether technology ultimately enhances or inhibits innovation.

Theoretical contribution

First, this study reconciles the conflicting effects of STARA on innovative behavior by establishing an integrated dual-path framework, a substantial advance over one-sided or fragmented investigations. Prior STARA or AI-related studies have treated innovation-enhancing and innovation-inhibiting effects as mutually exclusive or separate mechanisms [8, 11, 25]. In contrast, this research demonstrates that STARA simultaneously triggers two opposing cognitive processes: one that provides resources and opportunities to stimulate innovation, and another that evokes threat and substitution concerns to constrain innovation. This dual-path structure offers a holistic explanation for the mixed evidence in the existing literature and clarifies the double-edged nature of STARA in shaping employee innovative behavior.

Second, this study extends and operationalizes SCT within the STARA context, responding to recent calls for stronger theoretical integration in human–technology interaction research [30, 36, 49]. Whereas prior SCT-based studies in the AI field tend to focus on single cognitive mediators (He & Yan, 2024; Luo et al., 2022) [19, 20], this research validates a dual cognitive system that reflects SCT’s triadic reciprocal determinism. By empirically verifying the coexistence of positive and negative cognitive pathways, this study provides a replicable theoretical lens for understanding how technological environments reshape individual cognition and innovative behavior, thereby enriching the theoretical foundation of human–AI collaboration research.

Third, this study introduces leadership symbolic support as a critical boundary condition, addressing an important gap in prior STARA and AI innovation research. Existing studies focus heavily on the direct technology–employee relationship and largely ignore how leaders shape employees’ cognitive appraisals of new technologies [84, 85]. Drawing on the logic of leader symbolism [42], this study finds that leadership signals toward STARA significantly moderate the direct relationship between STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior. This finding not only highlights the essential role of leadership in technology adoption but also offers a new symbolic perspective on employee responses to intelligent technologies, complementing previous leadership research in the digital era [86, 87].

Practical implications

First, organizations should actively and appropriately implement STARA technologies. As STARA automates repetitive tasks, optimizes operational processes, and provides data-driven decision support, it effectively frees employees from heavy routine work, freeing up more time and cognitive resources and creating favorable conditions for innovative thinking and behavior. Managers should promote the integration of STARA into daily work to enhance efficiency while creating space for employee innovation.

Second, managers need to balance the dual psychological mechanisms by strengthening support for idea exploration and reducing employees’ STARA-related threat perceptions. Organizations should foster a supportive culture and provide adequate resources to encourage the exploration of creative ideas. Meanwhile, targeted training and communication should be used to help employees view STARA as a collaborative partner rather than a replacement threat, thereby alleviating job insecurity and increasing willingness to innovate.

Third, leaders should strengthen STARA symbolism by sending clear, positive signals. Through active participation, public endorsement, and consistent demonstration of support for the STARA application, leaders can shape employees’ positive interpretations of technology. Strong leader STARA symbolization strengthens employees’ recognition of technological opportunities, weakens threat focus, and strengthens the positive impact of STARA on innovative behavior. Together, these steps help organizations maximize the innovation benefits of STARA while managing associated psychological risks.

Limitations and future directions

Limitations

On the one hand, cross-sectional data constrain causal inference between variables. Although theoretically grounded, the temporal dynamics among STARA application, mediators (e.g., idea exploration support and STARA awareness), and innovative behavior remain unclear, as such data cannot capture sequential or reciprocal effects over time. Future research should adopt longitudinal or experimental designs to clarify causal mechanisms and temporal evolution. For example, multi-wave longitudinal tracking (e.g., a three-year study with biannual data collection) could reveal long-term effects, while experimental manipulations (e.g., providing early access to STARA technology for one group versus a control group) could isolate causal effects on mediators and innovation behavior.

On the other hand, the sample’s exclusive selection from China severely restricts the generalizability of the research findings. Collaborative cross-cultural studies could ensure methodological consistency. Furthermore, power distance should be explicitly incorporated as a key moderator to examine how high- versus low-power contexts affect employees’ psychological (e.g., perceived threat or empowerment) and behavioral responses to STARA. Beyond culture, factors like national innovation policies, industry regulations, and local tech ecosystems should be explored as potential moderators. Identifying these will help organizations tailor STARA implementation strategies to optimize innovation outcomes.

Future directions

Future research should adopt longitudinal or experimental designs to better capture the causal mechanisms and explore how these relationships evolve over time. In a longitudinal study, tracking employees’ perceptions and behaviors at multiple well-spaced time points could provide deeper insights into the long-term effects of STARA technology on innovation. For example, a three-year longitudinal study could be conducted, with data collection every six months. This would allow researchers to observe how changes in STARA technology application, Idea exploration support, and STARA awareness interact over time to influence employees’ innovative behavior. In experimental designs, researchers could manipulate the application of STARA technology across different employee groups and measure the resulting changes in mediating variables and innovation behavior. For instance, one group could receive early access to the enhanced version of STARA technology, while another group serves as a control group with no access. By comparing the two groups over a specific period, causal relationships can be more accurately determined.

Regarding sample diversity and cultural differences, future studies should replicate this research across diverse cultural and organizational contexts to validate the findings and enhance their applicability. A comprehensive cross-cultural research project could involve collaborating with research teams in different countries to conduct the same study simultaneously. This would ensure that the research methods and data collection procedures are consistent across different cultures. Furthermore, future studies should explicitly incorporate power distance as a key cultural or organizational moderator. Investigating how high- and low-power-distance climates influence employees’ psychological reactions (such as perceived threat or empowerment) and behavioral responses to STARA technology would yield a more nuanced understanding of its impact across different cultural settings. Beyond cultural values, elements like national innovation policies, industry regulations, and local technological ecosystems merit exploration as potential moderators. By identifying these moderating factors, organizations are empowered to devise more precisely targeted strategies for implementing STARA technology across diverse cultural and organizational landscapes. This approach aims to optimize the technology’s influence on employees’ innovative behavior.

Conclusions

This study introduces variables such as idea exploration support, STARA awareness, and leaders’ STARA symbolization to examine the mechanism by which STARA technology application impacts employee innovative behavior. Research findings include: (1) There is a positive correlation between the STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior; (2) Idea exploration support and STARA awareness mediate the relationship between the STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior; (3) Leaders’ STARA symbolization moderates the relationship between STARA technology application and employees’ innovative behavior. These findings not only enrich HRM theory but also offer practical insights for organizations to improve the efficiency of human resource utilization.
