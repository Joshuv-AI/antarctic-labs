EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

In partnership with Shaping Our Appalachian Region (SOAR) through the U.S. Department of
Energy’s (DOE) Communities – Local Energy Action Program (C-LEAP), the Gateway for
Accelerated Innovation in Nuclear (GAIN) developed a strategic framework to explore near-term
opportunities for Eastern Kentucky to participate in the nuclear energy industry(1).

The Eastern Kentucky Nuclear Energy Industry Engagement Study identified opportunities
within the nuclear energy industry that have the potential to generate jobs and promote economic
growth in Eastern Kentucky, targeting economic development pathways that can be feasibly
pursued and implemented by 2035. GAIN’s scope focused on Eastern Kentucky’s integration
with the existing nuclear fleet in the United States (U.S.). Opportunities associated with the
existing nuclear fleet are expected to continue for decades with owners/operators pursing license
renewals, subsequent license renewals, and power uprates. Additionally, near-term engagement
in the U.S. commercial nuclear energy ecosystem could enable future participation in the
advanced nuclear energy ecosystem.

GAIN conducted a two-tiered study that evaluated 1) the current needs of the nuclear energy
ecosystem and 2) the current capabilities of the Eastern Kentucky region. GAIN identified
opportunities where industry needs aligned with Eastern Kentucky’s capabilities. Workforce
development and supply chain participation were selected as focus areas warranting detailed
investigation and targeted outreach.

Workforce development emerged as a key opportunity, particularly given the nuclear energy
industry’s need for skilled labor. The nuclear energy industry faces a growing workforce
challenge, driven by license extensions and a larger percentage of the workforce approaching
retirement age (Reference 1). A significant portion of nuclear energy jobs support nuclear power
plant outages (i.e., when plants temporarily shut down to refuel and perform necessary repairs).
Outage support positions require skilled workers to temporarily relocate for short durations—
ranging from a few weeks to several months—after which they either return home or travel to
support another outage at a different plant. These roles often do not demand extensive prior
nuclear energy experience, typically requiring only 1–3 years of relevant work experience.

Eastern Kentucky is well-positioned to support nuclear outages due to its proximity to multiple
operating nuclear reactors, residents’ familiarity with power generation, and the alignment
between the skillsets required for many outage-related roles and those already present among the
region’s trade workers.

GAIN engaged with nuclear energy employers to discuss their current and anticipated workforce
needs and facilitated connections between these employers and Eastern Kentucky stakeholders,

Nuclear energy supply chain participation was identified as another opportunity for Eastern
Kentucky to participate in the nuclear energy ecosystem. With an aging nuclear fleet, ongoing
license extensions, and power uprates, demand is increasing for parts and components to support
continued operation. This supports growth opportunities for qualified manufacturers to meet the
growing demand.

Eastern Kentucky has a strong concentration in automotive, electric vehicle, and materials
manufacturing, supported by a skilled workforce and well-established supply chains. This
positions the region as a competitive hub for manufacturers with diverse capabilities. Existing
manufacturers in Eastern Kentucky may already possess, or have the potential to develop, the
capabilities needed to produce parts, components, or equipment for the nuclear energy industry.

GAIN engaged with local suppliers to assess their interest in and ability to participate in the
nuclear energy supply chain. While there is clear interest, challenges such as quality assurance
(QA) requirements and regulatory complexity were identified as potential barriers. Targeted
support from state and regional economic development stakeholders will be critical to enabling
local suppliers’ successful participation in the nuclear energy supply chain. With appropriate
support, Eastern Kentucky suppliers and manufacturers could enter the nuclear energy
ecosystem, creating additional family-sustaining jobs within a 10-year timeframe. Participation
in the nuclear energy industry offers opportunities to diversify revenue streams, increase overall
business revenue, and establish long-term employment opportunities.

Eastern Kentucky is well-positioned to establish a near-term role in the nuclear energy industry.
By prioritizing workforce development and supply chain engagement aligned with the existing
nuclear fleet, the region can also position itself to capture future opportunities beyond the next
decade. Through coordinated investments and collaboration, Eastern Kentucky can leverage
existing strengths to support national energy infrastructure goals while creating sustainable, high-
quality jobs. Recommended next steps for Eastern Kentucky and SOAR are summarized below.

• Workforce Development: Partner with educational institutions, industry, and regional
 stakeholders to develop targeted training, apprenticeships, and outreach programs that
 prepare Eastern Kentucky’s workforce for nuclear energy careers, with a focus on sustainable
 employment and meeting industry needs.
• Supply Chain Advancement: Facilitate strategic partnerships, pilot projects, and
 collaborative frameworks—supported by federal, state, and industry resources—to help
 Eastern Kentucky suppliers gain experience, reduce barriers, and integrate into the nuclear
 energy supply chain.
• Industry Engagement: Maintain and strengthen industry partnerships by facilitating
 ongoing engagement through conferences, workshops, and regional forums to connect
 Eastern Kentucky stakeholders with the nuclear energy sector.

INTRODUCTION AND PURPOSE

The Gateway for Accelerated Innovation in Nuclear (GAIN) partnered with Shaping Our
Appalachian Region (SOAR) through the United States (U.S.) Department of Energy’s (DOE)
Communities – Local Energy Action Program (C-LEAP) to explore near-term opportunities for
Eastern Kentucky to participate in the nuclear energy industry(2).

This report documents the methodology and findings of the Eastern Kentucky Nuclear Energy
Industry Engagement Study. The approach employed by GAIN is intended to be applicable and
repeatable for other communities considering participation in the nuclear energy industry.
However, the findings and recommended actions presented herein are specific to Eastern
Kentucky and are intended to support SOAR’s efforts to effectively advocate for the region,
stimulate employment, and promote economic growth through engagement in the nuclear energy
industry by 2035.

BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION
Coal retirements have had significant impacts on the regional economy of Eastern Kentucky.
Between 1990 and 2016, coal production in the region dropped by 85%, resulting in over
$1 billion in lost income for former coal industry workers (Reference 2). This decline triggered
approximately $500 million in downstream economic losses across key industries such as retail,
food service, transportation, and specialty trades (Reference 2).

The reduction in coal-related employment in Eastern Kentucky contributed to broader, multi-
industry job losses, with employment declining by several thousand jobs annually since 1990
(Reference 2). In 2022, labor force participation in some Eastern Kentucky counties stood at
33%, significantly below the Kentucky state average (58%) and the national average (62%)
(Reference 2). Approximately 420,000 individuals in the Eastern Kentucky region were outside
the labor force in 2022, including an estimated 167,000 working-age adults (ages 18–65)
(Reference 2).

In response to these challenges, SOAR—a nonprofit organization in Eastern Kentucky that
focuses on facilitating regional economic development—applied for and received funding and
technical assistance through the DOE C-LEAP to explore opportunities for Eastern Kentucky to
participate in the nuclear energy industry. Specifically, SOAR was interested in identifying
opportunities that could (a) create high-value jobs, (b) develop supporting infrastructure, (c)
attract long-term private and public investment, and (d) stimulate sustained economic growth
(Reference 3).

COMMUNITIES- LOCAL ENERGY ACTION PROGRAM (C-LEAP)
DOE’s C-LEAP provides technical assistance to low-income, energy-burdened communities that
are facing economic impacts from fossil fuel-related closures, such as coal plant retirements and
mine closures. The program supports competitively selected communities in developing
community-driven strategies for energy–related economic development (Reference 4).

Communities participating in the program are encouraged to explore a range of energy pathways,
individually or in combination, to maximize local benefits. These pathways include
(Reference 4):

• Deployment of advanced nuclear technologies and support for existing reactors
• Carbon capture and storage (CCS)
• Energy planning and infrastructure development
• Community resilience microgrids
• Assessment of critical minerals from energy waste and by-products
• Energy-efficient building upgrades and beneficial electrification
• New or expanded manufacturing
Eastern Kentucky is one of the C-LEAP communities evaluating opportunities in advanced
nuclear technology and support for existing reactors.

SHAPING OUR APPALACHIAN REGION (SOAR)
SOAR is a non-profit organization serving 54 counties in Eastern Kentucky, focused on
facilitating economic development and improving quality of life throughout the Appalachian
region (Reference 5). SOAR’s core initiatives include entrepreneurship, workforce development,
infrastructure, broadband expansion, healthcare access, and community revitalization. Through
these initiatives, SOAR aims to contribute to economic diversification and regional growth.

 11

As part of the C-LEAP requirements, each participating community must include at least one
community-based organization responsible for supporting project oversight and execution
(Reference 4). SOAR served as the designated community-based organization, leading
coordination and stakeholder engagement efforts in Eastern Kentucky for this C-LEAP project.

GATEWAY FOR ACCLERATED INNOVATION IN NUCLEAR (GAIN)
GAIN is an initiative established by the DOE and operates out of Idaho National Laboratory
(INL). GAIN functions as a public-private partnership designed to provide access to technical
expertise, regulatory guidance, and financial resources to advance the commercialization of
nuclear technologies (Reference 6). As nuclear technologies progressed toward deployment,
GAIN’s role expanded to include support for stakeholders—such as states, utilities, and
communities—that are newly exploring nuclear energy.

Under the C-LEAP initiative, GAIN partnered with SOAR to provide technical assistance and
evaluate nuclear energy opportunities in Eastern Kentucky. MPR Associates, Inc. and Envoy
Public Labs supported the effort, as part of the greater GAIN team.

NUCLEAR ENERGY INDUSTRY OVERVIEW
This section provides an overview of the U.S. nuclear energy industry, covering its status, key
trends, challenges, and future opportunities. Essential infrastructure needed to sustain the
ongoing operation and expansion of nuclear power plants is also discussed.

Nuclear Energy Industry Outlook

As global electricity demand continues to rise, nuclear energy is expected to continue to play a
critical role in meeting U.S. energy needs. In the U.S., nuclear power currently provides
approximately 20% of the nation’s electricity via 94 operating reactors (Reference 7). Figure 1
shows a map of all currently operating commercial nuclear power plants in the U.S. Most of the
operating nuclear reactors were constructed between the 1970s and 1990s and were originally
licensed for approximately 40 years of operation (Reference 8). Recognizing the essential role of
nuclear energy in maintaining grid reliability, many plant owners and operators have pursued or
are in the process of pursing license extensions (i.e., extending the licensed duration to 60 years
via license renewals or 80 years via subsequent license renewals) and/or power uprates (i.e.,
increasing capacity of existing nuclear power plants). Utilities (as well as other developers) are
also considering the deployment of new advanced nuclear reactors as part of their long-term
strategy.

 12

 Figure 1. Map of U.S. Operating Commercial Nuclear Power Plants (Reference 9)

A robust and diverse nuclear energy ecosystem is essential to sustain the existing fleet and
support the deployment of new reactors (see Figure 2). However, only two plants have been
constructed in the last ten years, which introduced challenges to the ecosystem. Key segments of
the nuclear energy supply chain were adversely impacted by a reduction in nuclear-grade
suppliers and a national loss in specialized technical skills. These challenges have complicated
the procurement of critical parts and components necessary to maintain the existing nuclear
energy fleet and construct advanced nuclear reactors (Reference 10). Additionally,
approximately 60% of the nuclear energy workforce falls within the 30 to 54 age range,
exceeding proportions in the broader energy sector and national averages. Consequently, a
substantial portion of this workforce is anticipated to retire within the next decade (Reference 1).

While the challenges in the nuclear energy supply chain and workforce are significant, they also
present opportunities for stakeholders to engage in the nuclear energy industry. Specifically,
there is potential to promote supply chain diversification and modernization, as well as to
develop a new generation of highly skilled nuclear energy professionals. Capitalizing on these
opportunities will be important for sustaining the existing fleet and ensuring the successful
deployment of advanced reactor technologies.

Nuclear Energy Ecosystem

The nuclear energy ecosystem refers to the full network of people, infrastructure, processes, and
technologies that work together to support the lifecycle of nuclear power plants—from
construction and operation to decommissioning and spent fuel management.

 13

A diverse and resilient ecosystem enables the safe, reliable, and efficient use of nuclear energy
for electricity generation and for emerging non-electric nuclear applications. For example, a
diverse supply chain and workforce would enhance the nuclear industry’s efficiency and
reliability by reducing risks associated with supplier dependencies and skill shortages. Multiple
suppliers help ensure timely delivery and continuous operation, while a varied workforce fills
skill gaps, promotes collaboration, and supports adaptation to new technologies and regulations.

Understanding the facets of the nuclear energy ecosystem is important for identifying and
evaluating targeted opportunities for communities to participate in the nuclear energy industry.
Similarly, understanding the specific needs of different facets of the ecosystem helps identify
community strengths that can meet those needs.

Figure 2 provides a high-level overview of the nuclear energy ecosystem by outlining the
primary "inputs" and "outputs" associated with both the existing fleet and advanced reactor
technologies. While the specifics of the ecosystem may differ between reactor technologies, the
fundamental inputs required to operate and maintain nuclear power facilities remain consistent.
These include:

• A skilled workforce to design, operate, and maintain the nuclear reactor and support systems
 and processes
• A reliable supply chain to deliver essential materials, components, and services
• Secure and efficient fuel cycle infrastructure for conversion, enrichment, and fuel fabrication
The primary output of a nuclear power plant is electricity. However, depending on the reactor
technology, nuclear reactors also offer the potential to:
• Provide process heat for industrial applications
• Enable hydrogen production through high-temperature steam electrolysis
• Produce medical isotopes for diagnosing and therapeutic use

The final stage of the ecosystem involves the safe and secure management of spent nuclear fuel,
including interim storage, transportation, and eventual long-term disposal.

APPROACH

GAIN developed and implemented a structured methodology to identify and evaluate
opportunities for Eastern Kentucky to participate in the nuclear energy industry. The approach
used for the Eastern Kentucky Nuclear Industry Engagement Study is outlined below. While this
approach was designed specifically for Eastern Kentucky, it could be adapted by other regions to
assess similar opportunities.

• Opportunity Statement Development – The first step of the process was to establish the
 region’s desired objectives.
 This step documented a clear and actionable opportunity statement that outlined the region’s
 interests and defined specific objectives—including desired timeline—that could be used to
 evaluate potential roles within the nuclear energy industry. This statement served as the
 foundational framework for the evaluation.

• Opportunity Assessment – The second step of the process was to conduct an opportunity
 assessment consisting of both a needs and a capabilities assessment.
 This step identified areas within the nuclear energy ecosystem that aligned with the
 opportunity statement, analyzed existing industry gaps and challenges, and evaluated the
 region’s ability to address them. Findings were summarized to highlight opportunities and
 identify potential stakeholders who may be strategically positioned to engage. The sub-steps
 are listed and described below:

 15

 • Alignment of Ecosystem with Opportunity Statement: Identified areas of the
 nuclear energy ecosystem that present viable opportunities aligned with the objectives
 and timeline outlined in the opportunity statement.
 • Needs Assessment: Analyzed the selected industry segments to identify existing
 gaps, challenges, and potential areas for growth.
 • Capabilities Assessment: Evaluated the region’s current capabilities to address the
 identified gaps and determined readiness to support the opportunities.
 • Summary of Opportunities: Consolidated findings from the needs and capabilities
 assessments into a comprehensive summary table, highlighting opportunity areas and
 their alignment with regional assets.
• Detailed Investigation of Selected Opportunities – The third step involved an in-depth
 evaluation of select high-potential opportunities, with a focus on engaging relevant regional
 and industry stakeholders. These opportunities were identified through analysis of the
 Summary of Opportunities table and selection of those that best aligned with the opportunity
 statement and regional capabilities.
 The in-depth evaluation included targeted outreach to key industry organizations and
 regional companies to gauge interest, share available resources, and assess strategic fit.

• Summary of Recommended Actions and Resources for Current and Future
 Engagement – The final step of the process was to summarize the results of the study and
 identify next steps.
 Upon completion of the detailed investigation, outstanding actions required to pursue the
 identified opportunities were documented, along with the development of key resources to
 support continued engagement with the nuclear energy industry.

OPPORTUNITY ASSESSMENT

The opportunity assessment highlighted critical segments of the nuclear energy ecosystem that
align with the region’s goals and timeline, examines current industry gaps and challenges, and
evaluates the region’s capacity to address these needs. The results are consolidated to highlight
high-potential opportunities and stakeholders well-positioned to participate in capturing them.

ALIGNMENT OF ECOSYSTEM WITH OPPORTUNITY STATEMENT
The ecosystems supporting the existing nuclear fleet and the projected advanced nuclear fleet
share fundamental similarities, providing comparable opportunities for engagement in workforce
development, supply chain participation, and the nuclear fuel cycle. Ongoing operation of the
existing nuclear fleet creates near-term opportunities for stakeholder involvement. While the
anticipated needs of advanced reactors are expected to align with those of the current operating
fleet, predicting when these needs will materialize is challenging. Planning, construction, and
commissioning of new nuclear power plants can take many years and vary depending on several
factors, including reactor technology and location (Reference 11).

Given (a) SOAR’s 10-year opportunity window and (b) uncertainty surrounding the amount of
time it will take to deploy advanced reactors and establish a supporting ecosystem, GAIN
focused on opportunities related to the existing nuclear fleet. This strategic focus enables Eastern
Kentucky to pursue near-term integration into the nuclear energy industry by leveraging the
operational status of the existing plants. Near-term engagement with existing nuclear power
plants will also position Eastern Kentucky to support advanced reactors in the future.

GAIN identified workforce development and supply chain involvement (outlined in green in
Figure 3) as the primary focus areas. These areas align directly with SOAR’s opportunity
statement and offer near-term (within ten years) pathways for Eastern Kentucky to participate in
the nuclear energy industry. Separate from this evaluation, SOAR is investigating spent fuel
management opportunities (outlined in yellow in Figure 3) via Energy Communities Alliance’s
(ECA) Community Grant. SOAR, with support from ECA, is investigating the possibility of
siting an interim storage facility in Eastern Kentucky (Reference 12).

Other opportunity areas related to the commercial nuclear energy ecosystem—such as the
nuclear fuel cycle and reactor deployment—were deemed less likely to generate near-term job
growth in Eastern Kentucky. Nuclear fuel cycle activities, including mining, conversion,
enrichment, and fabrication, as well as the development and deployment of nuclear reactors,

require significant capital investment in specialized infrastructure equipped with advanced
safeguards and process controls. These areas are also subject to regulatory oversight due to
concerns related to proliferation, radiation protection, and environmental impact. As a result,
securing the necessary licenses, permits, and approvals can take many years, making these
opportunities less feasible in the near term.

NEEDS ASSESSMENT
GAIN conducted a Needs Assessment to identify current gaps, challenges, and areas of potential
growth within the commercial nuclear energy ecosystem, with a specific focus on workforce and
supply chain needs aligned with SOAR’s opportunity statement. The opportunities identified
through this assessment are intended to provide near-term entry points for Eastern Kentucky,
while also enabling long-term participation as the advanced nuclear industry matures and future
deployment opportunities emerge. The assessment was informed by publicly available data and
supplemented with insights obtained through engagement with industry subject matter experts
(SMEs).

The following sub-sections summarize key findings from the Needs Assessment, with workforce
and supply chain insights presented separately.

 18

Nuclear Energy Workforce
GAIN analyzed the nuclear energy workforce landscape of the existing nuclear fleet (e.g.,
workforce size, demographic trends, and job market demands within the industry) to identify
potential opportunities for Eastern Kentucky. GAIN also reviewed the educational and training
requirements for key roles and explored regional employment opportunities—such as outage
support positions—that can provide regional economic benefits while meeting industry
operational needs. Workforce-related insights from the Needs Assessment (documented below)
were compared to Eastern Kentucky’s workforce capabilities (described in the Capabilities
Assessment section) to identify high-potential opportunities for regional participation in the
nuclear energy workforce.

Workforce Status
In 2023, the nuclear energy industry employed approximately 68,000 workers across the fuel
supply and electric power generation segments, with 86% of those positions concentrated in
electric power generation (Reference 1). While the nuclear energy industry saw an increase of
over 1,800 jobs from the previous year—demonstrating growth and continued demand—
workforce demographics signal a growing challenge (Reference 1). The nuclear energy industry
is experiencing a shortage of younger workers, employing 23% fewer workers under the age of
30 compared to the overall energy workforce (Reference 1). This indicates that a significant
portion of the current workforce is in the mid- to late stages of their careers, which may result in
a significant number of retirements within the next decade. Near-term retirements without proper
knowledge transfer to the next generation raises concerns, particularly given the previous and
future extensions of operating licenses for existing nuclear power plants.

Addressing workforce gaps is key to sustaining the existing nuclear fleet and enabling advance
reactor deployments. To meet the growing demand and maintain the long-term resiliency of the
nuclear energy workforce, it is essential to recruit and train early- to mid-career professionals.
Several in-demand roles across utilities and professional services do not require a four-year
Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) degree—creating accessible
pathways into the industry for individuals with technical certifications or two-year credentials
(Reference 1).

Job Types and Educational Requirements
As part of the workforce-focused Needs Assessment, GAIN evaluated current nuclear job
openings using NukeWorker.com (Reference 13), a specialized job listing platform dedicated to
the nuclear industry. This analysis aimed to identify the types of roles currently in demand and
the typical experience levels required for each position.

GAIN reviewed a broad range of listings across industries such as power generation,
decommissioning, outage support, and regulatory compliance. The review revealed a high
demand for skilled trades positions. As illustrated in Figure 4, there is a need for electricians,

 19

valve technicians, radiation protection technicians (RPT), and non-destructive examination
(NDE) technicians, and other skilled trades throughout the nuclear industry. Many of these roles
require moderate experience—typically zero to five years—making them accessible to early-
career professionals or individuals transitioning from other technical industries. This presents an
important opportunity for individuals seeking to enter the nuclear energy industry, as well as for
trade schools and community colleges that offer focused training programs to prepare these
individuals for industry entry.

Outage Support Opportunities
Currently there are no operating nuclear power plants in Kentucky. As a result, in-demand
nuclear outage support positions are located outside Kentucky, primarily in neighboring states
with operating nuclear power plants (e.g., Tennessee, Ohio, Virginia, and Illinois). Given the
focus of this study on Eastern Kentucky, identifying nuclear energy-related employment
opportunities that enable residents to continue living in the region is a key priority. Outage
support roles within the existing nuclear fleet offer such opportunities.

Outage support typically occurs during spring and fall, when energy demand is lower, as nuclear
power plants temporarily shut down for refueling and maintenance. U.S. nuclear power plants
generally undergo refueling outages every 18 to 24 months, with an average duration of
approximately 35 days (Reference 14). These scheduled outages improve plant capacity factor
by enabling maintenance and inspections that are difficult to perform while the plant is
operating, thereby reducing the need for unplanned shutdowns.

 20

The outage support workforce model enables residents of Eastern Kentucky to participate in the
nuclear energy industry by traveling to nuclear plants during scheduled outage periods while
maintaining permanent residence in the region. This opportunity aligns with SOAR’s objectives
by creating family-sustaining employment opportunities without requiring relocation, thereby
contributing to regional population retention.

Closing these workforce gaps will necessitate focused regional workforce development
initiatives, such as specialized training programs, apprenticeships, and recruitment strategies
aimed at attracting and preparing the next generation of skilled professionals for the nuclear
energy industry. Strategic investment in these areas will enhance the region’s capacity to meet
both current and future workforce demands.

Nuclear Energy Supply Chain
GAIN evaluated the existing nuclear fleet supply chain to identify gaps, challenges, and areas of
potential growth, focusing on the parts, components, and materials essential for operations.
Supply chain insights from the Needs Assessment (documented in this section) were compared
to Eastern Kentucky’s manufacturing capabilities (described below in the Capabilities
Assessment section) to identify high-potential opportunities for regional participation in the
nuclear energy supply chain.

A major challenge facing the existing nuclear fleet is the increasing obsolescence and age-related
degradation of critical parts, components, and equipment (Reference 15). When replacements are
needed, plant personnel typically seek like-for-like replacements—components that are identical
or functionally equivalent to those supplied by the original equipment manufacturer (OEM).
However, sourcing such replacements is becoming more difficult due to a low inventory of spare
parts and challenges replenishing stock, as many OEMs have discontinued production
(Reference 16). This trend among OEMs is largely the result of the multi-decade pause in new
plant construction, which significantly reduced the demand for nuclear-grade components.

These supply chain issues are further compounded by the ongoing efforts to extend the
operational lifespan of existing U.S. nuclear power plants beyond their initial 40-year licenses
through initial and subsequent license renewals (Reference 17). Addressing these challenges will
require an expanded base of qualified suppliers capable of producing or refurbishing critical
components.

To inform this effort, GAIN collaborated with industry SMEs to identify the most critical parts,
components, and materials needed by the existing nuclear fleet. The resulting list (Table 2) will
support a comparative analysis of industry needs and Eastern Kentucky’s current manufacturing
capabilities.

SUMMARY OF RECOMMENDED ACTIONS AND RESOURCES FOR
CURRENT AND FUTURE ENGAGEMENT

The Eastern Kentucky Nuclear Energy Industry Engagement Study identified near-term U.S.
commercial nuclear energy industry workforce and supply chain needs that align with Eastern
Kentucky’s capabilities. Eastern Kentucky is strategically located near multiple states with
operating nuclear reactors and possesses familiarity with power generation, including coal plants.

As the existing nuclear fleet seeks to extend its operating lifespan, there is a rising demand for
skilled labor. This demand presents significant opportunities for Eastern Kentucky’s labor force,
which includes experienced workers seeking stable, family-sustaining employment.
Opportunities such as outage support roles—typically occurring in the spring and fall—allow
residents to participate in the nuclear industry without relocating, contributing to regional
economic stability and population retention.

Simultaneously, the need for reliable replacement components is increasing as the nuclear fleet
ages and original suppliers exit (or have exited) the market. Eastern Kentucky’s manufacturing
capabilities, particularly in automotive and materials industries, position the region to help
address these supply chain gaps. Although nuclear industry supply chain participation requires

 35

meeting stringent regulatory and quality standards, there are viable entry points for regional
manufacturers.

In this section, GAIN presents recommended actions for Eastern Kentucky stakeholders
(including SOAR) to pursue, along with key resources to support continued and future
engagement with the nuclear energy industry.

RECOMMENDED ACTIONS FOR CURRENT AND FUTURE ENGAGMENT
The following recommended actions are proposed for SOAR and Eastern Kentucky stakeholders
—including manufacturers, workforce participants, and state and local government—to ensure
Eastern Kentucky’s participation in the nuclear energy industry has a sustainable impact.
Recommendations are organized around four primary areas: workforce development, supply
chain advancement, industry engagement, and public awareness. Potential action owners are
identified before each recommended action.

Together, the following recommendations are intended to guide SOAR and regional stakeholders
in strategically leveraging identified opportunities and fostering long-term engagement with the
nuclear energy industry.

Workforce Development
Eastern Kentucky possesses a skilled labor pool of experienced workers seeking stable, well-
compensated employment. Collaborations with educational institutions, labor unions, and
nuclear industry partners can establish or enhance targeted training programs and apprenticeship
opportunities. These initiatives will enable the region to efficiently equip its workforce with the
qualifications required for nuclear energy industry roles, specifically those associated with
outage support.

• SOAR/Kentucky - Partner with technical colleges, community colleges, unions, and industry
 organizations to develop new or enhance existing training programs focused on nuclear-
 relevant skilled trades (e.g., radiation protection, valve maintenance, pipefitting, welding).
• SOAR/Kentucky - Support pilot apprenticeship programs tailored to outage and valve
 technician roles, using partners such as Allied Power and Crane Nuclear.
• SOAR - Coordinate workforce development sessions at upcoming regional events (e.g., the
 2025 SOAR Summit) to increase awareness of career opportunities in the nuclear energy
 industry. These sessions could include informational briefings on recent developments and
 regional opportunities, panel discussions with industry representatives, and interactive
 workshops that facilitate direct engagement between job seekers and recruiters.

 36

• SOAR - Continue collaboration with previously engaged industry organizations (Framatome,

 • Day & Zimmerman: Explore implementation of the NWTP to better prepare local
 workers for nuclear energy careers and enhance their competitiveness in the industry.
 • Waymaker: Collaborate with Waymaker to assess the infrastructure and curriculum
 needed to deliver targeted training for outage support roles, ensuring Eastern
 Kentucky workers are adequately prepared.
 • Allied Power: Engage in discussions to identify the training and certification
 requirements needed for candidates to qualify for RPT roles.
 • Crane: Connect with Crane to discuss potential partnerships related to valve training

Eastern Kentucky's existing manufacturing base—particularly in the automotive and materials

suppliers interested in the nuclear energy industry consider the following actions:

 37
