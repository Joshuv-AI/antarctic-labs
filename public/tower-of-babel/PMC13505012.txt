Transformer-based models in dentistry: a systematic review
Authors: Xuhan Zhu, Mingwei Zhou, Masahiro Shimogishi, Eriko Marukawa, Xin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransformer-based architectures have rapidly gained prominence in medical imaging due to their ability to model long-range dependencies and global contextual information more effectively than convolutional neural networks. In dentistry, their applications have expanded across diagnostic, predictive, and generative tasks, yet no comprehensive synthesis has systematically evaluated their performance and clinical relevance. This systematic review provides an up-to-date assessment of Transformer-based models in dental imaging and diagnostics.MethodsA structured search of Medline, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane databases was performed for studies published from 2020 to August 2025, following the PRISMA-DTA guidelines for diagnostic test accuracy systematic reviews and a PROSPERO-registered protocol (CRD420251142603). Eligible studies applied Transformer-based architectures to dental clinical tasks and reported quantitative performance outcomes. Two independent reviewers conducted article screening, data extraction, and bias assessment, resolving discrepancies through consensus.ResultsA total of 112 studies met the inclusion criteria. We found 91 hybrid convolutional neural network (CNN)–Transformer architectures and 23 pure Transformer models for dental image segmentation, classification, anomaly detection, and multimodal fusion tasks. In subgroup analyses, hybrid CNN–Transformer models consistently outperformed pure Transformer architectures. A significant 5–8% performance gap was observed between internal and external validation. These hybrid models demonstrated higher accuracy in tooth and anatomical segmentation, caries and lesion detection, orthopedic and orthodontic analysis, implant localization, and craniomaxillofacial assessment. Generative and predictive applications, such as 3D crown and bone reconstruction, dental age and sex estimation, artifact reduction, and implant position prediction, have achieved promising technical performance in controlled experimental settings, supporting further clinical translational research. Key advantages included enhanced global feature representation, improved robustness in heterogeneous imaging conditions, and the ability to incorporate multimodal inputs. However, major limitations remain: the scarcity of large annotated datasets, heterogeneous evaluation protocols, and limited prospective or real-world validation.ConclusionsTransformer-based models represent a significant methodological advance for dental AI, with superior technical performance in controlled experimental studies of dental imaging and diagnostic tasks. However, their clinical translation remains at an early stage. Broader clinical adoption requires not only standardized datasets, harmonized benchmarks and explainability frameworks, but also rigorous prospective multi-center clinical validation, regulatory and ethical evaluation, and real-world workflow integration assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03627-6.

Background

Oral and dental diseases remain a major global health burden, affecting an estimated 3.5 billion people worldwide, with disproportionate impact in low- and middle-income countries due to limited access to timely, affordable care [1]. Conditions such as dental caries [2], periodontal diseases [3], oral cancers [4], and congenital anomalies [5] like cleft lip and palate contribute to complications ranging from discomfort to life-threatening outcomes when left undiagnosed or untreated. Although technological advances have improved early detection and intervention, the increasing incidence of oral diseases highlights the need for more effective diagnostic solutions [6].

Dental image analysis plays a critical role in the diagnosis and management of oral diseases [7]. Technologies such as panoramic and periapical radiography [8, 9], cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) [10], magnetic resonance imaging [11], and ultrasonography [12] have enhanced diagnostic capabilities. However, these modalities also present challenges, including noise, anatomical overlap, and variability in image quality, which hinder consistent interpretation [13]. Moreover, manual image analysis by clinicians is time-intensive and inherently subjective, leading to inter-observer variability and a risk of diagnostic errors. These limitations underscore the need for more standardized, efficient, and scalable diagnostic tools [14].

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, has emerged as a promising solution for automating and augmenting diagnostic workflows in medical and dental imaging [15]. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have led this progress since 2012 [16], demonstrating substantial success in tasks such as tooth classification [17], segmentation [18], lesion detection [19], endodontic and periodontal diagnosis [20], dental implant classification [21], and even forensic dental applications [22]. Models such as U-Net [23], introduced in 2015, have become foundational in medical image segmentation. However, CNNs are limited by their inductive bias towards local feature extraction, which restricts their ability to model long-range dependencies and global context, an essential requirement for complex image interpretation tasks [24].

Transformer-based models, whose core workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1, have emerged as a powerful alternative to CNNs. First developed for natural language processing (NLP), their self-attention mechanism computes pairwise relationships between all input elements simultaneously, enabling efficient modeling of long-range dependencies and global contextual information [25]. In 2020, Dosovitskiy et al. adapted this architecture to computer vision tasks, introducing the Vision Transformer (ViT) [26], which segments images into fixed-size patches, linearly embeds them, and processes them as sequences, analogous to words in a sentence. This design enables Transformers to model spatial and contextual relationships across the entire image without the locality constraints of convolutional filters.Fig. 1The typical workflow of the Transformer architecture proceeds through several sequential stages. Initially, input tokens are converted into dense vector representations via an embedding process and are subsequently augmented with positional encodings to preserve their sequential information, forming the initial input matrix. This matrix is then processed through the encoder stack, where it undergoes multi-head self-attention and feed-forward neural network operations. The resulting encoded representation, which encapsulates the contextual information of all input values, is passed to the decoder layers. Within the decoder, the data undergoes masked multi-head attention and encoder-decoder attention mechanisms before being processed by additional feed-forward networks. The final output from the decoder is transformed through a linear layer and a Softmax function to generate a probability distribution over the target vocabulary. The flexibility of Transformers extends beyond single-modality image analysis. Their architecture can integrate diverse data types, such as medical images, electronic health records (EHRs), histopathological slides, genomic profiles, and physiological sensor streams, by jointly learning complex dependencies across modalities. This multimodal capability is particularly relevant for dentistry, where patient care often requires correlating radiographic findings with clinical records, histopathology, and procedural data. By leveraging self-attention to align and fuse information from multiple sources, Transformer-based models offer a pathway toward holistic, patient-specific diagnostic and prognostic systems.

Furthermore, recent innovations have refined Transformer-based models for clinical image analysis tasks. Hybrid architectures that combine CNNs with Transformers exploit CNNs’ strength in extracting fine-grained local features while using Transformers to capture global structure and contextual dependencies. Architectures such as Swin Transformer [27], SegFormer [28], and TransUNet [29] incorporate hierarchical feature representation, window-based self-attention, and skip connections, making them more computationally efficient and better suited for high-resolution medical and dental images. In dentistry, these models have shown promise in tasks ranging from tooth segmentation in CBCT volumes to automated detection of caries, lesions, and implant positions [30], with several small-sample retrospective studies reporting performance comparable to that of dental clinicians in specific offline diagnostic tasks, such as classification [31], and anomaly detection [32].

Built on the core self-attention mechanism, Transformer architectures are inherently adaptable to both visual and textual data, with rapidly expanding applications across imaging and clinical NLP tasks in dentistry. This systematic review provides the first comprehensive synthesis of all Transformer-based models in dentistry, spanning ViT)/hybrid CNN-Transformer models for imaging, and Transformer-based large language models (LLMs) for clinical applications. We aim to map current evidence, characterize task-specific performance, and address barriers to clinical translation across these use cases.

Recently, several review articles have explored the application of Transformer-based models in various domains of oral and dental health, including focused studies on stomatological imaging [33]. Moreover, some broader reviews of deep learning in dentistry have included discussions on Transformer architectures [6, 34–36]; however, no systematic review to date has summarized Transformer applications across modalities in dentistry. To fill this gap, we analyzed 112 eligible peer-reviewed articles, with findings structured into four core domains: image segmentation, detection/classification, generative/predictive imaging applications, and Transformer-based LLM clinical applications.

This article follows a structured protocol: Sect. 2 elaborates on the methodology including the focused research question, eligibility criteria, database search strategy, data extraction and analysis, and stratified risk of bias assessment. Section 3 presents the research results covering literature search and selection, dental application classifications of Transformer models, quantitative performance synthesis, and systematic analysis of dataset and validation characteristics. Section 4 discusses the study implications, key limitations and targeted future research recommendations. Section 5 draws the conclusions, summarizing the application value of Transformer-based models in dental imaging and diagnostics and the core requirements for their clinical translation and real-world adoption.

Methods

Protocol development and focused question

This systematic review was conducted in strict adherence to the PRISMA-DTA guidelines for diagnostic test accuracy systematic reviews [37]. The review protocol was registered and allocated with the CRD420251142603 in the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews hosted by the Centre of Reviews and Dissemination (University of York, National Institute for Health Research, United Kingdom). The focus question was “What is the trends, applications, strengths, and challenges associated with the use of Transformer-based models in dental applications?”

Information sources and search strategy

We performed a systematic literature search across five databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). The search yielded a total of 4862 articles across various sources: Scopus (1566), PubMed (1244), Web of Science (978), Embase (977), Cochrane (97). All searches were restricted to English-language publications only. The inclusion criteria, including (MeSH) terms, are specified in Table S2. A second electronic search was conducted in August 2025 to ensure up-to-date conclusions.

Screening methods

For managing the citations, Mendeley Version 2.135.0 was used. Nevertheless, the total number of citations was transferred to Rayyan’s online service (https://www.rayyan.ai) to ease the first selection process based on the article’s title and abstract. The screening process was performed by two independent reviewers (M.Z. and X.Z.). Full- text studies were considered if they followed the inclusion criteria or if they did not provide clear information regarding their methodology and purpose in the abstract. If disagreement was found at any stage of the selection process, it was solved through consensus with a third reviewer (X.Z.). The inter-reviewer reliability was registered by calculating the kappa correlation coefficient for both screenings.

Data extraction and analysis

Two independent reviewers (M.Z. and Xuhan Z.) performed duplicate, blinded data extraction for all included studies using a pre-standardized data extraction form. The following predefined data items were extracted from each eligible study: authors (year), the aim of the study, Transformer-based models used, the core features of models, dataset size and characteristics, primary performance metrics and corresponding values, and key outcomes or conclusions of the study.

A third senior reviewer with clinical expertise in dentistry (Xin Z.) conducted a full review of the extracted data. All discrepancies and disagreements between the two independent reviewers during the data extraction process were resolved through consensus discussion with the third reviewer.

For studies with incomplete or missing key data, we contacted the corresponding authors to request supplementary information and clarification. If the required information could not be obtained promptly, the study was excluded from the final data synthesis. For studies with overlapping patient cohorts or duplicate published results, only the most recent and comprehensive publication was included. For studies that evaluated multiple Transformer-based models in the same experiment, all relevant performance results were extracted and reported to ensure an objective and comprehensive synthesis.

Risk of bias assessment

We adopted a stratified, study-specific risk of bias (RoB) assessment strategy, tailored to the design and objectives of different types of included studies, using internationally validated, domain-specific tools to ensure the appropriateness and rigor of the evaluation. All RoB assessments were independently performed by two reviewers (M.Z. and Xuhan Z.). Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion with a third senior reviewer (Xin Z.), consistent with the study screening and data extraction workflow. Our stratified risk of bias assessment strategy is fully aligned with PRISMA-DTArecommendations for diagnostic test accuracy studies.

For diagnostic accuracy studies focusing on dental anatomy segmentation, caries/lesion detection and classification, and pathological diagnosis (the majority of included studies), we used the QUADAS-AI tool [38], the AI-specific extension of the Cochrane-endorsed QUADAS-2 framework for diagnostic accuracy studies. This tool assessed RoB across four core domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and patient flow and timing. For each domain, we added AI-specific evaluation items, with a focus on data leakage risks, a prevalent and critical flaw in AI diagnostic research, including the rationality and timing of train-test data splitting, the presence of data leakage between training, validation, and test sets, control of overfitting, quality of reference standard annotation, and design of external validation.

For clinical prediction model studies developing or validating clinical prediction models (e.g., dental age/sex estimation, implant position prediction, treatment outcome prediction), we applied the PROBAST (Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool) [39], the gold-standard framework for assessing bias in prediction model research. The assessment covered four key domains: participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis, with each domain rated for risk of bias and concerns regarding applicability.

For LLM and generative model studies exploring LLM clinical applications and generative AI for dental clinical scenarios, we used a structured quality assessment checklist aligned with the CLAIM (Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging) and TRIPOD-AI reporting standards [40, 41]. The checklist focused on model transparency, reproducibility of the analysis, clinical applicability, and completeness of reporting, with each item rated for risk of bias.

The detailed results of the RoB assessment for each included study are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S3–S5).

Transformers have advanced medical image segmentation by capturing global context and long-range dependencies. In dentistry, they have been used across oral endoscope images, CBCT, periapical and panoramic radiographs, and 3D dental models, with CBCT and panoramic X-rays most frequently applied.

Hybrid CNN–Transformer models dominate recent work. For CBCT, Chen et al. proposed CTA-UNet, combining convolutional encoders with Transformer layers, achieving a Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 86.52% on complex 2D slices [42]. Yin et al. introduced CoT-UNet++, integrating CoTNet and ViT, outperforming U-Net, UNet++, and TransUNet on a custom CBCT dataset [52]. Wang et al. developed Trans-VNet, which incorporated cross-attention into V-Net to address blurred boundaries and high inter-class similarity between teeth [44]. Together, these studies show CNN–Transformer hybrids substantially improve CBCT tooth segmentation.

For panoramic X-rays, Sheng et al. used Swin-Unet with superior results over CNNs [43]. Kanwal et al. created M-TransNet by adapting SegFormer and Mask2Former, emphasizing Transformers’ strength in encoding global features [45]. Other teams, including Li, Ghafoor, Manzari, and Ma, also reported improved outcomes with hybrid designs [46, 48, 49, 53]. Hao et al. introduced SemiTNet, a semi-supervised framework reducing annotation requirements [47], while Li et al. pretrained SAM2 to build S2AgScUNet, a lightweight model effective on small datasets [51].

Beyond CBCT and panoramic images, Cao et al. applied FlowgateUNet to micro-CT and multi-organ CT data, showing strong generalizability [50]. Wang et al. advanced multimodal segmentation by integrating intraoral photographs, CBCT, periapical, and panoramic images, outperforming baselines across four benchmarks [54].

In 3D segmentation, Transformers excel due to their ability to model complex volumetric data. Wu et al. proposed a Region Partition Transformer for 3D point cloud tooth segmentation, achieving state-of-the-art results [55]. Ahn et al.’s WCTN, combining sparse convolution with Transformers, performed strongly on intraoral scans [56]. Zhuang et al. built a hybrid Transformer framework for fully automated 3D tooth segmentation and labelling [57], while Alsheghri et al. developed ArchSeg, based on Point Transformer V2, robust even on partial 3D arches [59]. Krenmayr et al. compared four masked self-supervised Transformer frameworks, finding Point-MAE most effective under limited labeled data [61].

Transformers have also improved segmentation of other dental structures. Chen et al. introduced CBTrans, a CNN–Swin hybrid, for mandibular segmentation on panoramic X-rays [62]. For CBCT, Lv et al. developed a model for mandibular canal segmentation with feature fusion and clDice loss [63]. Liu et al. combined Swin-UNETR for tooth instance segmentation with 3D-UNet for bone and nerve canals [64]. Lumetti et al. created MATAP, a memory-augmented Transformer incorporating spatial coordinates for mandibular canal segmentation [65]. Gillot et al. released an open-source UNETR-based tool for skull segmentation [66], and Tian et al. proposed a two-stage 3D UNesT system for cortical and cancellous bone segmentation [67].

Beyond dental anatomy, Transformers also support related regions: Jin et al. used Swin Transformer–U-Net for nasal and pharyngeal airway segmentation from CBCT [58]. Li et al. proposed an attention-guided Transformer for joint jaw lesion segmentation and classification, achieving strong results across both tasks [60].

Classification often follows segmentation, with semantic segmentation categorizing teeth anatomically and instance segmentation distinguishing individual teeth for anomaly detection. Gao et al. proposed a Grouped Bottleneck Transformer integrating 3D convolutions, self-attention, and bottlenecks, improving CBCT-based classification with reduced computational cost [31]. For prosthodontics, Sifat et al. combined ViTs with 3D CNNs and Explainable AI, achieving 90–100% accuracy on 3D dental scans [68]. Wang et al. introduced GeoSwin, a geometric multiscale Transformer that enhanced orthodontic point cloud registration [69]. Zhao et al. integrated a RoI Transformer with ResNet-50, improving precision by aligning detection with natural tooth orientation [70]. Fernandes et al. showed ViT and gMLP achieved performance comparable to or superior to CNNs in radiographic classification [71]. Liu et al. developed a segmentation-free two-stage method using geodesic heatmap encoding and PTv3 backbone for landmark detection, outperforming prior approaches [72]. Çelik et al. applied ESPCN-based super-resolution to radiographs, improving image clarity and classification accuracy [73].

Once teeth are segmented and classified, Transformer-based systems extend to pathology detection. Caries was most frequently studied (n = 17), alongside oral lesions (n = 9), cancers (n = 5), impacted teeth (n = 5), implants (n = 5), plaque (n = 3), fluorosis (n = 2), and other conditions (n = 15), underscoring their versatility.

For dental caries, Transformers have been applied across endoscopic images, intraoral photos, bitewing, periapical, and panoramic radiographs. Felsch et al. used SegFormer for lesion and MIH detection in photos with high precision [30]. Zhou et al. developed a Swin Transformer leveraging tooth type–specific features, achieving near expert-level pediatric caries diagnosis [32]. Jiang et al. proposed RDFNet, a Transformer-augmented YOLOv5s with FReLU activation for efficient digital caries detection [74]. Priya et al. combined ATDUnet++ with ViT-MRDGRU, achieving 96.59% accuracy [75]. Ying et al. designed a Transformer-enhanced U-Net for automated X-ray caries segmentation [77], while Neeraja et al. introduced DentC_BSFLNet (Swin-UNETI + Involution), improving panoramic caries detection accuracy [78]. Chaves et al. employed Mask R-CNN with Swin backbone for bitewing caries [79]. Gao et al. developed GFDet, a PVT-based model with feature fusion and adaptive label assignment for endoscopic caries detection [80]. Huang et al. proposed AEDD-Net, a CNN–Transformer hybrid with dual decoders and edge-aware loss, achieving state-of-the-art X-ray segmentation [81]. In a benchmark comparison, Ying et al. found YOLOv5 outperformed DETR, UNet, and TransUNet on periapical caries, followed by TransUNet [76].

Several studies tackled multiple conditions. Alsakar et al. built a dual-stream MobileNetV2 + Swin Transformer ensemble for panoramic multi-label classification [82]. Nour et al. integrated YOLOv8 and RT-DETR for high-accuracy detection of caries, implants, fillings, missing teeth, lesions, and root treatments [83]. Xu et al. proposed UCL-Net, a lightweight CNN with cross-scale attention and uncertainty-aware learning, excelling in caries, deep caries, lesions, and impactions [84]. Li et al. developed LABANet, a Swin-based segmentation network with synthetic data balancing, accurately detecting wisdom teeth, caries, and residual roots [85]. Alabd-Aljabar et al. achieved 96% accuracy with ViT for multi-condition diagnosis, surpassing CNN–SVM approaches [86]. Asif et al. introduced OralTransNet, a lightweight MobileNet–Transformer hybrid, exceeding 99% diagnostic accuracy with low computational cost [87]. Peng et al. proposed Oralformer, a parallel Transformer segmentation model with uncertainty-driven loss, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy on the ODS dataset [88]. Liu et al. infused agent attention into a Conformer, significantly boosting disease classification accuracy [89].

Transformer models extended to periapical and jaw lesions across OCT, panoramic, histopathology, and CBCT. Chen et al. showed that pretrained Swin-UNETR outperformed U-Net in low-data CBCT lesion segmentation [90]. Ahmed et al. introduced TED, a ViT-based framework improving OCT and CT lesion detection under noisy conditions [91]. Panyarak et al. demonstrated CrossViT models outperformed ResNet/ViT in classifying four jaw lesion types using ECAP augmentation [92]. Hadilou et al. achieved up to 97% accuracy for oral epithelial dysplasia grading with ViTs, surpassing CNNs [94]. Van Nistelrooij et al. found Transformers superior in classifying osteolytic lesions [93]. Thomas et al. proposed DentViT-RCNN, combining Mask R-CNN, ViT, and GAN augmentation for high-accuracy cyst diagnosis [95].

For oral cancer, Flügge et al. applied Swin Transformer to clinical photos, achieving 98.6% accuracy and AUC = 0.99 for oral squamous cell carcinoma [96]. They later used ViT-based Mask R-CNN for carcinoma, oral lichen planus, and leukoplakia with strong results [97]. Song et al. found Swin Transformer outperformed ViT and CNNs on mobile oral cancer images [99]. Maia et al. confirmed comparable CNN and Transformer performance in histopathology using a curated dataset [98].

Discussion

Main findings and interpretation

This study evaluated the last five years of literature research on the applications of dental fields using Transformer-based AI models, using a systematic review. According to the results of this evaluation, some findings that need to be discussed have emerged.

Even if it is not as many as the applications in the field of medicine, the results of the searches made in five databases show the records of 4862 for the last five years, which shows that Transformer is experiencing a golden age in the applications in dentistry. Over the years, Transformer has grown in popularity as a research topic in dental applications. We also found a significant part of the research identified is not from dentistry but from technical sciences and has been published differently.

In dental image analysis, segmentation and object detection often serve as essential preprocessing steps prior to classification or diagnostic analysis. Transformer-based segmentation models are primarily applied to CBCT, periapical and panoramic radiographs, and 3D dental models, owing to the substantial data and annotation requirements inherent to these tasks. The dominance of hybrid CNN–Transformer architectures stems from their complementary strengths: CNNs excel at extracting fine-grained local spatial features, while Transformers address CNNs’ core limitation by modeling long-range global context. This synergy, which preserves anatomical detail while enabling holistic image interpretation, is supported by our head-to-head comparison data as the key driver of performance gains over conventional pure CNN models in most dental tasks.

Sample size emerged as the strongest predictor of model performance and generalizability across all included studies. The median sample size was 1200 cases (IQR: 425–6400), with 21.4% of studies using small datasets (<200 cases) for rare conditions. We observed consistent performance gaps: segmentation models with >1000 cases achieved 7.3% higher median DSC than small datasets; classification models with >5000 cases showed 6.5% higher accuracy; and 3D generative models with >500 scans had 0.118 mm better median Chamfer Distance. Critically, small datasets had a 12.3% median performance drop in external validation, compared to only 4.7% for large datasets, due to overfitting to dataset-specific noise. The scarcity of large annotated dental datasets stems from high expert annotation costs, privacy restrictions, and limited rare disease cases. Future research should prioritize multi-center data sharing, self-supervised learning, and transfer learning to mitigate these limitations.

In the domain of 3D image analysis, the literature remains relatively sparse regarding robust techniques for the representation and processing of 3D data, raising concerns about the optimization of performance in this area. CNNs have long dominated computer vision benchmarks due to their capacity for local spatial feature extraction, translational invariance, and hierarchical data representation, making them well-suited for large-scale image datasets. However, this local focus also limits the ability of CNNs to model long-range dependencies within the data. In contrast, ViTs, a more recent self-supervised architecture, address this shortcoming through self-attention mechanisms that attend to all image patches simultaneously. This approach enables the model to capture global relationships and fine-grained details, which are particularly valuable for complex image interpretation tasks. Studies combining CNNs and Transformers in a unified architecture reflect an ongoing effort to harness their complementary strengths across classification and other clinical applications.

Regarding classification tasks and other generative clinical applications, dental caries emerged as the most frequently studied anomaly in the literature (n = 17). Nevertheless, the majority of other dental conditions were addressed in only one or two studies, underscoring the need for broader exploration in underrepresented areas. While the high performance reported in many of the reviewed studies demonstrates the potential reliability of Transformer-based models, several studies lacked the use of clinically appropriate performance metrics and did not include validation through additional clinical tests. Although most research emphasized the role of AI-supported expert systems as decision-support tools for clinicians, many failed to implement evaluation protocols aligned with this intended purpose.

Additionally, while commercial tools such as ChatGPT have demonstrated success in natural language tasks, their clinical applicability remains unexplored. Integrating LLMs’ capabilities with Transformer-based imaging models may offer a promising direction for future clinical use cases. Two primary deployment paradigms are manily focused in recent years’ studies: automated dental diagnosis and cross-modal/multimodal dental analysis. When augmented with a cross-modal encoder, a single LLM can integrate and reason over multi-source data (e.g., textual notes, radiographs, and clinical images) to facilitate complex clinical decision-making. Studies included illustrated the potential of fully integrated, multi-modal LLM systems for clinical deployment. A significant limitation of this, however, lies in the current lack of clinical specificity. A critical pathway to overcoming this limitation is the fine-tuning of base models with domain-specific knowledge derived from dental textbooks, curated research literature, and de-identified patient records. This process enhances diagnostic accuracy by embedding domain-specific patterns, terminology, and context, thereby enabling the generation of clinically relevant and insightful outputs.

The integration of Transformer-based imaging models and LLM frameworks provides a potential technical path for multi-modal dental decision support systems. However, current research remains in the preclinical exploratory stage, with major barriers to clinical application, including the lack of prospective validation, insufficient data privacy guarantees, inherent model biases, and the absence of standardized clinical evaluation protocols. Future research must therefore focus on these areas to translate the theoretical promise of LLMs into safe, effective, and equitable clinical practice.

Limitations and future recommendations

This systematic review has several limitations. The inclusion and exclusion criteria, while clearly defined, restricted the study selection to peer-reviewed journal articles, excluding conference proceedings, preprints, and book chapters. Additionally, we only included English-language publications, which may introduce language bias and potentially exclude relevant studies published in other languages. Some potentially relevant studies were not included due to limited access or incomplete data incompatible with our summary tables. The data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers, with all discrepancies resolved through consensus review by a third senior reviewer with clinical expertise in dentistry, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the extracted data. As this was a traditional systematic review, no meta-analysis was conducted. Consequently, the findings are presented in a narrative format, organized according to a framework designed to guide future research. Despite these limitations, this review offers a structured overview of current applications of Transformer-based models in dentistry and identifies key directions for advancing their clinical utility.

Conclusions

This systematic review highlights the emerging role of Transformer-based models in the field of dental imaging and diagnostics, reflecting a broader shift toward advanced deep learning techniques in oral healthcare. Transformers have demonstrated considerable potential in tasks involving dental structures such as teeth and bones, outperforming traditional convolutional architectures in capturing global contextual information. These technologies have the potential not only to alleviate the clinical workload but also to democratize access to high-quality diagnostic services by reducing dependence on specialist expertise and associated costs. While deep learning has already shown promising results in the diagnosis of dental anomalies and diseases, the integration of Transformer models in this domain is still in its early stages.

To realize these benefits, future research should prioritize the development of clinically validated Transformer-based systems. This includes establishing standardized datasets, benchmarking protocols, robust performance metrics, and ensuring model interpretability and generalizability across diverse populations and imaging modalities. As the field continues to mature, the continued expansion and refinement of Transformer applications hold great promise for advancing diagnostic precision, treatment planning, and ultimately, patient outcomes in oral and dental healthcare.
