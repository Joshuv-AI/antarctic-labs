The Riemann integral on Dedekind complete f f -algebras
Eder Kikianty, Luan Naude, Mark Roelands, Christopher Schwanke

Abstract
In this paper we develop a theory of integration for locally band preserving functions, introduced by Ercan and Wickstead, on Dedekind complete f f -algebras. Specifically, we construct Darboux and Riemann integrals and show that they are equal. We then connect the theory of integrable functions to the theory of order differentiable functions, introduced by the third and fourth authors, by proving a Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Furthermore, we show that a Mean Value Theorem for Integrals holds and that we can integrate by parts and substitutions.



Keywords:
Riemann integral, vector lattice,
\Phi
-algebra, differentiation, locally band preserving, Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Subject Classification:
Primary: 46A40; Secondary: 46G12

1
Introduction

Recently, an order-theoretic approach to complex analysis was developed in
[
6
,
7
]
for complex
\Phi
-algebras (unital
f
-algebras). The first paper focused on series and power series in universally complete complex
\Phi
-algebras, and the second paper introduced two distinct, but both natural, notions of differentiability using order convergence in Dedekind complete
\Phi
-algebras:
order differentiability
and
super order differentiability
. This theory allows one to do analysis on certain spaces which do not have a natural norm, such as
C^{\infty}(K)
, and it illustrates how order-theoretic notions play a crucial role in the development of the theory of complex analysis.

A natural continuation of the work done in
[
6
,
7
]
is to develop a theory of differential and integral calculus in real Dedekind complete
\Phi
-algebras. The work done on order derivatives in
[
7
]
naturally carries over to the real case, and this paper mainly focuses on the construction and the properties of the Riemann integral.

While developing the theory of Riemann integration,
locally band preserving functions
, first introduced in
[
3
]
, became central to the theory. We further developed their properties in
[
4
]
. Generalisations of classical results were proved for locally band preserving functions in real Dedekind complete
\Phi
-algebras, such as the Intermediate Value Theorem, the Extreme Value Theorem, and the Mean Value Theorem—the last of which uses the notion of order differentiability introduced in
[
7
]
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Some preliminary results are given in Section 2. Primarily, these are results from
[
4
]
that are needed in the remainder of the paper.

The Riemann integral for locally band preserving and order bounded functions
f\colon[a,b]\to E
is constructed in Section 3, where
E
is a Dedekind complete
f
-algebra and
[a,b]
is an order closed interval in
E
. As in the classical case, we show that the Darboux and Riemann constructions yield the same integral.

In Section 4, we prove analogues of the basic classical properties of the integral. We also show that a locally band preserving and uniformly order continuous function is necessarily integrable. It is known that an order continuous function need not be uniformly order continuous in this setting, so it is an open question whether order continuity is sufficient to guarantee integrability.

Section 5 is devoted to defining
\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx
when
a
and
b
are incomparable. To do this, we use the bands
B_{a<b}
,
B_{b<a}
, and
B_{a=b}
, which were introduced in
[
4
]
, to mimic the law of trichotomy in
\mathbb{R}
where we must either have
a<b
,
b<a
, or
a=b
.

Finally, we establish a connection between the integral and the differentiation theory from
[
7
]
by proving a Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in Section 6. We also prove a Mean Value Theorem for Integrals, and use the fundamental theorem to generalise classical integration techniques, namely substitution and integration by parts.

2.1
Band decompositions of
E

In
[
4
, Notation 2.4]
, the notation
B_{x\leq y}
,
B_{x=y}
, and
B_{x<y}
for
x,y\in E
was introduced. By
[
4
, Proposition 2.7]
, these can be defined as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
E
be a vector lattice with the projection property, and consider
x,y\in E
. We write

1.

B_{x\leq y}
for the largest band
B
for which
\mathbb{P}(x)\leq\mathbb{P}(y)
,

2.

B_{x=y}
for the largest band
B
for which
\mathbb{P}(x)=\mathbb{P}(y)
, and

3.

B_{x<y}
for the largest band
B

\mathbb{P}(y-x)
is a weak order unit in

It is shown in
[
4
, Proposition 2.6]
that
E=B_{x<y}\oplus B_{y\leq x}=B_{x<y}\oplus B_{y<x}\oplus B_{x=y}
.

In Section 3, we will work with partitions of an order interval
[a,b]
. Classically, the union of two partitions is again a partition, but this is not the case in
E
since the union may fail to be totally ordered. For this reason, the following lemma and the total orderisation of a finite set will be useful.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
E
be a vector lattice with the projection property, and let
A=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subseteq E
. Then there exists a decomposition
E=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{i}(A)
is totally ordered for
1\leq i\leq m
.

Proof.

The collection of bands
B
of the form

B\coloneqq\bigcap_{1\leq i,j\leq n}B_{i,j}

where
B_{i,j}\in\{B_{x_{i}<x_{j}},B_{x_{i}\geq x_{j}}\}
is such a decomposition of
E
. By definition these bands are disjoint, and that they sum to
E
follows from the fact that
B_{x_{i}<x_{j}}\oplus B_{x_{i}\geq x_{j}}=E
and the distributivity of sums and intersections of bands. Consider the band projection
\mathbb{P}
corresponding to such a
B
. Then, for
1\leq k,l\leq n
, we have that
\mathbb{P}\leq\mathbb{P}_{k,l}
and either
\mathbb{P}_{k,l}(x_{k})\leq\mathbb{P}_{k,l}(x_{l})
or
\mathbb{P}_{k,l}(x_{l})\leq\mathbb{P}_{k,l}(x_{k})
. Hence,
\mathbb{P}(A)
is totally ordered.
∎

The total orderisation
\operatorname{to}(A)
of a nonempty finite subset
A
of a distributive lattice was defined in
[
8
, Definition 2.4]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
L
be a distributive lattice, with
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in L
. For
1\leq k\leq n
, define

\mathcal{M}_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=\bigvee_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n+1-k}\leq n}\left(\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n+1-k}x_{i_{j}}\right).

The set
\{\mathcal{M}_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\}
is called the
total orderisation
of
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
and denoted
\operatorname{to}(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\})
.

For a totally ordered set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
,
\mathcal{M}_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
would simply be the
k
-th smallest element of the set. Therefore, in light of
Lemma

2.3
, an alternative construction of the total orderisation exists for vector lattices with the projection property—where we select the
k
-th smallest element in each of the bands
B_{i}
and add them together to get
\mathcal{M}_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. From this construction, the following lemma follows easily.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
E
be a vector lattice with the projection property, and let
A=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subseteq E
. Then
\operatorname{to}(A)
is the unique totally ordered subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq E
such that there exists a decomposition
E=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{i}(A)=\mathbb{P}_{i}(A^{\prime})
for
1\leq i\leq m
.

.

Let

2.3
Order continuity and uniform order continuity

In
[
4
, Proposition 2.13]
, order continuity was characterised in a way that resembles the classical
\varepsilon-\delta
definition of continuity. A similar argument can be used for uniformly order continuous functions. We give these characterisations below in
Proposition

2.9
.

Definition 2.7
.

Consider
f\colon\operatorname{dom}(f)\to E
. We say
f
is
order continuous at
c\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
if whenever
x_{\alpha}\to c
in
\operatorname{dom}(f)
, we have
f(x_{\alpha})\to f(c)
. The function
f
is called

if it is order continuous at every point in its domain. We say
f
is
uniformly order continuous
if whenever
x_{\alpha}-y_{\alpha}\to 0
in
\operatorname{dom}(f)
, we have
f(x_{\alpha})-f(y_{\alpha})\to 0
.

Notation 2.8
.

The notation
\Delta\downarrow 0
means that
\Delta
is a downwards directed set in
E
with infimum
0
, whereas
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
means that
\mathdutchcal{E}
is a subset of
E
with infimum
0
that needs not be directed.

In the following characterisations of order continuity and uniform order continuity, and later in
Definitions

2.15
and
2.16
, we use
\Delta\downarrow 0
and
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
. It is possible to replace
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
with
\mathdutchcal{E}\downarrow 0
, but
\Delta
must be downwards directed. Therefore, the different arrows indicate when directedness is required

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
E
be a real vector lattice. A function
f\colon\operatorname{dom}(f)\to E
is

1.

order continuous at
c\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
if and only if for every
\Delta\downarrow 0
there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
with the property that for all
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists a
\delta\in\Delta
satisfying

x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\text{ and }\lvert x-c\rvert\leq\delta\implies\lvert f(x)-f(c)\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

2.

uniformly order continuous if and only if for every
\Delta\downarrow 0
there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
with the property that for all
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists a
\delta\in\Delta
satisfying

x,y\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\text{ and }\lvert x-y\rvert\leq\delta\implies\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

2.4
Locally band preserving functions

The notion of a locally band preserving function was first introduced in
[
3
]
, and was investigated further in
[
4
]
. In Section 3, we will construct a Riemann integral for order bounded functions
f\colon[a,b]\to E
, and we will investigate the integrability of
f
primarily when
f
is locally band preserving. What follows is some of the properties of locally band preserving functions that will be needed throughout the remainder of the paper. We use a definition that is equivalent to the one used in
[
3
]
by
[
4
, Proposition 4.4.]
for vector lattices with the projection property.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
E
be a vector lattice with the projection property. A function
f\colon\operatorname{dom}(f)\to E
is
locally band preserving
if for every band projection
\mathbb{P}
and for all
x,y\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(x)=\mathbb{P}(y)
implies
\mathbb{P}(f(x))=\mathbb{P}(f(y))
.

Example 2.11
.

x

1.

A linear function
f\colon E\to E
is locally band preserving if and only if it is band preserving.

2.

Polynomials and power series, as in
[
6
]
, defined on
E
are locally band preserving.

3.

In
\ell^{\infty}
, a function is locally band preserving if and only if it is defined coordinatewise.

A locally band preserving function is precisely one whose behaviour on a band is completely independent of anything outside the band. Given a locally band preserving function
f
and a band
B
, one can define
f_{B}\colon\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{dom}(f))\to B
by
f_{B}(x)=\mathbb{P}(f(y))
where
y\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
with
\mathbb{P}(y)=x
. Then
f(x)=f_{B}(\mathbb{P}(x))+f_{B^{d}}(\mathbb{P}^{d}(x))
. This is the key idea that informs the following properties of locally band preserving functions. These properties are from
[
4
, Proposition 4.12.]
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
E
be a vector lattice with the projection property. Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be locally band preserving with
[c,d]\subseteq[a,b]
. The following statements hold.

1.

If
f
is order bounded above on
[c,d]
by
M
, then
\mathbb{P}(f(x))\leq\mathbb{P}(M)
holds for all
x\in[a,b]
and all band projections
\mathbb{P}
such that
\mathbb{P}(c)\leq\mathbb{P}(x)\leq\mathbb{P}(d)
.

2.

If
f
is order bounded below on
[c,d]
by
m
, then
\mathbb{P}(f(x))\geq\mathbb{P}(m)
holds for all
x\in[a,b]
and all band projections
\mathbb{P}
such that
\mathbb{P}(c)\leq\mathbb{P}(x)\leq\mathbb{P}(d)
.

3.

Let
x\in[a,b]
and
\varepsilon,\delta\in E^{+}
be such that for all
y\in[a,b]
,
\lvert x-y\rvert\leq\delta
implies
\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert\leq\varepsilon
, then for any band projection
\mathbb{P}
,
\mathbb{P}(\lvert x-y\rvert)\leq\mathbb{P}(\delta)
implies
\mathbb{P}(\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert)\leq\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon)
.

Finally, we mention that the Intermediate Value Theorem and the Extreme Value Theorem were generalised for locally band preserving functions in
[
4
, Theorems 5.3 and 5.5]
.

Theorem 2.13

(Intermediate Value Theorem)
.

Let
E
be a Dedekind complete vector lattice. If
f\colon[a,b]\to E
is order continuous and locally band preserving, and
y\in[f(a)\wedge f(b),f(a)\vee f(b)]
, then there exists a
c\in[a,b]
such that
f(c)=y
.

Theorem 2.14

(Extreme Value Theorem)
.

Let
E
be a Dedekind complete vector lattice. If
f\colon[a,b]\to E
is order continuous and locally band preserving, then there exist
c,d\in[a,b]
such that for all
x\in[a,b]
, we have
f(c)\leq f(x)\leq f(d)
.

2.5
Order differentiability and super order differentiability

There are two natural ways of defining derivatives in the vector lattice setting that turn out to be related, but not equivalent. These derivatives were first studied in
[
7
]
, and then further investigated in
[
4
]
. Between those two papers, there is a minor difference in whether
r\gg 0
means
r
is a positive invertible element or a weak order unit—we use the latter, and note that the results in
[
7
]
are still true. To obtain uniqueness of order derivatives with this change, it was necessary to assume
E
satisfies the projection property in
[
4
, Proposition 3.7]
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
E
be a
\Phi
-algebra with the projection property. A function
f\colon\operatorname{dom}(f)\to E
is called
order differentiable at
c\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
with derivative
f^{\prime}(c)
if there exists an
r\gg 0
such that
(c-r,c+r)\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(f)
, and for every
\Delta\downarrow 0
there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
with the property that for all
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists a
\delta\in\Delta
satisfying

x\in(c-r,c+r)\text{ and }\lvert x-c\rvert\leq\delta\implies\lvert f(x)-f(c)-(x-c)f^{\prime}(c)\rvert\leq\lvert x-c\rvert\varepsilon.

Definition 2.16
.

Let
E
be a
\Phi
-algebra with the projection property. A function
f\colon\operatorname{dom}(f)\to E
is called
super order differentiable at
c\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
with derivative
f^{\prime}(c)
if there exists an
r\gg 0
such that
(c-r,c+r)\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(f)
, and for every
\Delta\downarrow 0
there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
with the property that for all
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists a
\delta\in\Delta
satisfying

x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\text{ and }\lvert x-c\rvert\leq\delta\implies\lvert f(x)-f(c)-(x-c)f^{\prime}(c)\rvert\leq\lvert x-c\rvert\varepsilon.

Remark 2.17
.

The above definitions differ only in whether the implication is required to hold only for
x\in(c-r,c+r)
or for all
x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)
. These are not equivalent as it is possible for a net
(x_{\alpha})
to converge to
c
without ever entering
(c-r,c+r)
. It is immediate from the definitions that
f
is order differentiable at
c
if and only if there exists an
r\gg 0
such that
(c-r,c+r)\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(f)
and the restriction
f|_{(c-r,c+r)}
is super order differentiable at
c
.

Order derivatives are always unique, see
[
7
, Remark 3.3]
and
[
4
, Proposition 3.7]
. We also have available the sum and product rules for order differentiability from
[
7
, Theorems 3.15 and 4.3]
, where the reader may also find chain and quotient rules.

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
E
be a
\Phi
-algebra with the projection property. Suppose
f\colon\operatorname{dom}(f)\to E
and
g\colon\operatorname{dom}(g)\to E
are (super) order differentiable at
c\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\cap\operatorname{dom}(g)
. Then

1.

f+g
is (super) order differentiable at
c
with

(f+g)^{\prime}(c)=f^{\prime}(c)+g^{\prime}(c),

and

2.

fg
is (super) order differentiable at
c
with

(fg)^{\prime}(c)=f^{\prime}(c)g(c)+f(c)g^{\prime}(c).

A function being super order differentiable on an interval automatically makes it locally band preserving, see
[
4
, Theorem 4.9 and Corollary 4.10]
. Additionally, it was shown in
[
4
, Proposition 4.11]
that if
f\colon(a,b)\to E
is order differentiable and locally band preserving, then
f^{\prime}
is necessarily locally band preserving too.

We now state a version of the Mean Value Theorem, which is
[
4
, Theorem 5.8]
along with the natural corollary of when it can be used to say that a zero derivative implies a function is constant. The assumption that the function is locally band preserving cannot be dropped (cf.
[
4
, Example 5.11]
).

Theorem 2.19

(Mean Value Theorem)
.

Let
E
be a Dedekind complete
\Phi
-algebra, and suppose
a\ll b
. Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be order continuous on
[a,b]
, order differentiable on
(a,b)
, and locally band preserving. For any
x,y\in[a,b]
, there exists
c\in[x\wedge y,x\vee y]
such that

(y-x)f^{\prime}(c)=f(y)-f(x).

Corollary 2.20
.

Let
E
be a Dedekind complete
\Phi
-algebra. Let
f\colon(a,b)\to E
be locally band preserving and order differentiable with
f^{\prime}=0
. Then
f
is constant.

3
Construction of the Riemann integral

Notation 3.1
.

Throughout this section and the remainder of the paper,
E
always denotes a Dedekind complete
f
-algebra.

We define a Riemann integral for order bounded functions
f\colon[a,b]\to E
, where
[a,b]
is an order interval in
E
. At first, we use the Darboux sum construction of the integral, but it is shown in
Proposition

3.13
that the Riemann sum construction is equivalent.

Definition 3.2
.

A
partition

P
of
[a,b]
is a finite, totally ordered subset of
[a,b]
containing
a
and
b
, i.e.,

P=\{a=x_{0}\leq x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n}=b\}.

For any subinterval
I
of
[a,b]
, we define

m_{I}:=\inf_{x\in I}f(x)\quad\text{and}\quad M_{I}:=\sup_{x\in I}f(x).

If we need to specify the function under consideration, we will write
m_{I}(f)
and
M_{I}(f)
, and when considering a partition
P
as above, we write
m_{i}:=m_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}
and
M_{i}:=M_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be a bounded function. Then the
lower sum

L(f,P)
and the
upper sum

U(f,P)
of
f
with respect to
P
are given by

L(f,P):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}(x_{i}-x_{i-1})\quad\text{and}\quad U(f,P):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}(x_{i}-x_{i-1}).

The
lower integral

L(f)
and the
upper integral

U(f)
of
f
are defined as

\displaystyle L(f)

\displaystyle:=\sup\{L(f,P)\colon P\text{ is a partition of }[a,b]\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle U(f)

\displaystyle:=\inf\{U(f,P)\colon P\text{ is a partition of }[a,b]\}.

If we wish to specify the interval, we write
L(f,[a,b])
and
U(f,[a,b])
.

We say that
f
is
(Darboux) integrable
if
U(f)=L(f)
. In this case, we define the
(Darboux) integral
of
f
over
[a,b]
to be

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx:=U(f)=L(f).

We interest ourselves only in the integration of locally band preserving functions
f
. The decision to do so is motivated by the fact that a function can have a zero order derivative and yet not be constant—an example is provided in
[
4
, Example 5.11]
—unless the function is locally band preserving. Thus, in order to have a Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, assuming
f
is locally band preserving is necessary. The following example exhibits some other problems that occur without this assumption.

Example 3.3
.

Let
f\colon[(0,0),(1,1)]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be given by
f(x,y):=(y,x)
.
Let
P:=\{(0,0),(1,0),(1,1)\}
and
Q:=\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}
. Then,
P\cup Q=\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}
is not a partition of
[a,b]
since
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
are incomparable. Since
L(f,P)=(0,1)
and
U(f,Q)=(1,0)
, we see that
L(f,P)\not\leq U(f,Q)
. This also means
f
is not integrable.

Note that in this example,
f
is a uniformly continuous function and yet not integrable. Because
E
is not totally ordered,
P\cup Q
need not be a partition of
[a,b]
, and then one cannot utilise the classical argument:

L(f,P)\leq L(f,P\cup Q)\leq U(f,P\cup Q)\leq U(f,Q).

This problem can be addressed by taking the total orderisation of
P\cup Q
as a “common refinement” of
P
and
Q
. However, this approach is only sensible when
f
is locally band preserving, because the following two lemmas are needed to find a relationship between
L(f,P)
and
L(f,Q)
, and
L(f,\operatorname{to}(P\cup Q))
—and between the respective upper sums.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f\colon[a\wedge c,b\vee d]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving with
a\leq b
and
c\leq d
, and let
\mathbb{P}
be a band projection.

1.

If
[\mathbb{P}(a),\mathbb{P}(b)]\subseteq[\mathbb{P}(c),\mathbb{P}(d)]
, then

\mathbb{P}(m_{[c,d]})\leq\mathbb{P}(m_{[a,b]})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(M_{[a,b]})\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{[c,d]}).

2.

If
\mathbb{P}(a)=\mathbb{P}(c)
and
\mathbb{P}(b)=\mathbb{P}(d)
, then

\mathbb{P}(m_{[a,b]})=\mathbb{P}(m_{[c,d]})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(M_{[a,b]})=\mathbb{P}(M_{[c,d]}).

Proof.

To prove (i), suppose that
[\mathbb{P}(a),\mathbb{P}(b)]\subseteq[\mathbb{P}(c),\mathbb{P}(d)]
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(m_{[a,b]})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\inf\{f(x):a\leq x\leq b\}\right)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\mathbb{P}(f(x)):a\leq x\leq b\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\mathbb{P}(f(\mathbb{P}(x)+\mathbb{P}^{d}(c))):a\leq x\leq b\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\mathbb{P}(f(y+\mathbb{P}^{d}(c))):\mathbb{P}(a)\leq y\leq\mathbb{P}(b)\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf\{\mathbb{P}(f(y+\mathbb{P}^{d}(c))):\mathbb{P}(c)\leq y\leq\mathbb{P}(d)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(m_{[c,d]}),

where the term
\mathbb{P}^{d}(c)
is added in the third step in order to guarantee that
\mathbb{P}(x)+\mathbb{P}^{d}(c)\in[a\wedge c,b\vee d]
. The final equality follows by reversing the previous steps with
a
and
b
interchanged with
c
and
d
, respectively. The proof for the suprema is similar, and (ii) follows immediately by applying (i) twice.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving. If
P
and
Q
are partitions of
[a,b]
with
\mathbb{P}(P)\subseteq\mathbb{P}(Q)
for some band projection
\mathbb{P}
, then

\mathbb{P}(L(f,P))\leq\mathbb{P}(L(f,Q))\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(U(f,Q))\leq\mathbb{P}(U(f,P)).

Proof.

Suppose
P=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
. We prove the lower sum inequality as the proof of the upper sum inequality is similar. First suppose
P\subseteq Q
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
Q
has one element more than
P
. Say
Q=P\cup\{y\}
with
x_{k-1}\leq y\leq x_{k}
for some
y\in E
and
1\leq k\leq n
. Then,

\displaystyle m_{[x_{k-1},x_{k}]}(x_{k}-x_{k-1})

\displaystyle=m_{[x_{k-1},x_{k}]}(y-x_{k-1})+m_{[x_{k-1},x_{k}]}(x_{k}-y)

\displaystyle\leq m_{[x_{k-1},y]}(y-x_{k-1})+m_{[y,x_{k}]}(x_{k}-y),

so that
L(f,P)\leq L(f,Q)
.

Now suppose that
\mathbb{P}(P)\subseteq\mathbb{P}(Q)
for some band projection
\mathbb{P}
. There exists a subpartition
R\coloneqq\{y_{0},\dots,y_{n}\}
of
Q
such that
\mathbb{P}(x_{i})=\mathbb{P}(y_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
. It follows from the first part of this proof that
L(f,R)\leq L(f,Q)
. Therefore, by using
Lemma

3.4
(ii),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L(f,P))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(x_{i}-x_{i-1})\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{[y_{i-1},y_{i}]}(y_{i}-y_{i-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(L(f,R))\leq\mathbb{P}(L(f,Q)).\qed

We now dedicate ourselves to turning the collection of all partitions of
[a,b]
into a directed set such that
P,Q\preceq\operatorname{to}(P\cup Q)
, which will allow us to see the integral as an order limit of Darboux sums.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the collection of all partitions of
[a,b]
. We define a preorder on
\mathcal{P}
by
P\preceq Q
if and only if there exists a band decomposition
E=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{i}(P)\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{i}(Q)
for
1\leq i\leq m
.

Remark 3.7
.

Classically, partitions are often directed using the notion of mesh. This results in the same integral, but we prefer using
\preceq
as it illustrates the difference between the classical case (where
\preceq
reduces to
\subseteq
) and ours.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving. If
P
and
Q
are partitions of
[a,b]
, then
P,Q\preceq\operatorname{to}(P\cup Q)
.

Proof.

This follows immediately by using
Lemma

2.5
.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving, and let
P
and
Q
be partitions of
[a,b]
. If
P\preceq Q
, then

L(f,P)\leq L(f,Q)\quad\text{and}\quad U(f,Q)\leq U(f,P).

Proof.

By definition of
P\preceq Q
, there exists a band decomposition
E=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{i}(P)\subseteq\mathbb{P}_{i}(Q)
for
1\leq i\leq m
. Using
Lemma

3.5
,

L(f,P)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}_{i}(L(f,P))\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}_{i}(L(f,Q))=L(f,Q).

The other inequality can be shown in the same way.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

(\mathcal{P},\preceq)
is an upwards directed set. Moreover, if
f\colon[a,b]\to E
is order bounded and locally band preserving, then we have
L(f,P)\uparrow L(f)
and
U(f,P)\downarrow U(f)
.

Proof.

Lemma

3.8
shows that
(\mathcal{P},\preceq)
is an upwards directed set, and the rest of the claim is
Lemma

3.9
.
∎

As a consequence of
Proposition

3.10
,
f
is integrable if and only if

U(f,P)-L(f,P)\downarrow 0.

This immediately yields the following integrability condition, mimicking the classical integrability condition.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving. Then
f
is integrable if and only if there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists a partition
P
of
[a,b]
such that
U(f,P)-L(f,P)\leq\varepsilon
.

To end this section, we demonstrate that the integral could have equivalently been defined as a limit of Riemann sums.

Definition 3.12
.

A
tagged partition

(P,\{c_{i}\})
of
[a,b]
consists of a partition
P=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
and a set of points
\{c_{i}:1\leq i\leq n\}
such that
c_{i}\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
for
1\leq i\leq n
. For
f\colon[a,b]\to E
, define the
Riemann sum
of
f
with respect to the tagged partition
(P,\{c_{i}\})
to be

R(f,P,\{c_{i}\}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(c_{i})(x_{i}-x_{i-1}).

Let
\mathcal{T}
be the collection of all tagged partitions of
[a,b]
. We write

(P,\{c_{i}\})\preceq(Q,\{d_{i}\})\quad\mbox{if and only if}\quad P\preceq Q,

and this also defines a preorder on
\mathcal{T}
that directs
\mathcal{T}
upwards. We say
f
is
(Riemann) integrable
with integral
I\in E
if
R(f,P,\{c_{i}\})\to I
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving. Then
f
is Darboux integrable if and only if
f
is Riemann integrable. In this case, the integrals coincide.

Proof.

Suppose
U(f,P)\to I
and
L(f,P)\to I
. Then there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists a partition
P_{\varepsilon}
such that

P\succeq P_{\varepsilon}\implies U(f,P)-I\leq\varepsilon\text{ and }I-L(f,P)\leq\varepsilon.

Let
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
. Choose any
d_{i}
that makes
(P_{\varepsilon},\{d_{i}\})
a tagged partition of
[a,b]
. Then, for any
(P,\{c_{i}\})\succeq(P_{\varepsilon},\{d_{i}\})
, we have that
P\succeq P_{\varepsilon}
and so

R(f,P,\{c_{i}\})-I\leq U(f,P)-I\leq\varepsilon\text{ and }I-R(f,P,\{c_{i}\})\leq I-L(f,P)\leq\varepsilon.

Therefore,
\left\lvert R(f,P,\{c_{i}\})-I\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon
and
R(f,P,\{c_{i}\})\to I
.

Now suppose that
R(f,P,\{c_{i}\})\to I
. Then there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
, there exists a
(P_{\varepsilon},\{c_{i}\})
such that

(P,\{d_{i}\})\succeq(P_{\varepsilon},\{c_{i}\})\implies\lvert R(f,P,\{d_{i}\})-I\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

Let
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
with
P_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
. For
1\leq i\leq n
, let
d_{i}\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
then

I-\varepsilon\leq R(f,P_{\varepsilon},\{d_{i}\})\leq I+\varepsilon.

Taking the supremum and infimum over all choices of
\{d_{i}\}
respectively yields

I-\varepsilon\leq U(f,P_{\varepsilon})\leq I+\varepsilon\text{ and }I-\varepsilon\leq L(f,P_{\varepsilon})\leq I+\varepsilon.

Therefore,
U(f,P)\downarrow I
and
L(f,P)\uparrow I
.
∎

4
Properties of the Riemann integral

In what follows, we obtain generalisations of the basic classical properties of the Riemann integral.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
f\colon[a\wedge c,b\vee d]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving with
a\leq b
,
c\leq d
,
\mathbb{P}(a)=\mathbb{P}(c)
, and
\mathbb{P}(b)=\mathbb{P}(d)
for some band projection
\mathbb{P}
. If
f
is integrable both on
[a,b]
and on
[c,d]
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{c}^{d}f(x)dx\right).

Proof.

For any partition
P
of
[a,b]
, there exists a partition
Q
of
[c,d]
such that
\mathbb{P}(P)=\mathbb{P}(Q)
, since
\mathbb{P}(a)=\mathbb{P}(c)
and
\mathbb{P}(b)=\mathbb{P}(d)
. By
Lemma

3.4
(ii),

\mathbb{P}(L(f,P))=\mathbb{P}(L(f,Q))\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{c}^{d}f(x)dx\right).

Since
\mathbb{P}
is order continuous, taking the limit over all partitions
P
of
[a,b]
yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{c}^{d}f(x)dx\right).

The same argument can be used to show the reverse inequality.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving with
c\in[a,b]
. Then
f
is integrable on
[a,b]
if and only if
f
is integrable on
[a,c]
and
[c,b]
. In this case,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx=\int_{a}^{c}f(x)dx+\int_{c}^{b}f(x)dx.

Proof.

Suppose that
f
is integrable on
[a,b]
. Then there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists a partition
P
of
[a,b]
such that
U(f,P)-L(f,P)\leq\varepsilon
. Fix
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
and let
P\coloneqq\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
be the corresponding partition. Now,
Q\coloneqq P\wedge c=\{y_{0}\coloneqq x_{0}\wedge c,\dots,y_{n}\coloneqq x_{n}\wedge c\}
is a partition of
[a,c]
. Fix
1\leq i\leq n
. Then

\mathbb{P}_{c\leq x_{i-1}}(y_{i}-y_{i-1})=\mathbb{P}_{c\leq x_{i-1}}(c-c)=0,

and

\mathbb{P}_{c>x_{i-1}}(x_{i-1})=\mathbb{P}_{c>x_{i-1}}(y_{i-1})\leq\mathbb{P}_{c>x_{i-1}}(y_{i})\leq\mathbb{P}_{c>x_{i-1}}(x_{i}).

By
Lemma

3.4
(i),

\displaystyle(M_{[y_{i-1},y_{i}]}-m_{[y_{i-1},y_{i}]})(y_{i}-

\displaystyle y_{i-1})=\mathbb{P}_{c>x_{i-1}}\left((M_{[y_{i-1},y_{i}]}-m_{[y_{i-1},y_{i}]})(y_{i}-y_{i-1})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{c>x_{i-1}}\left((M_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}-m_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]})(x_{i}-x_{i-1})\right)

\displaystyle\leq(M_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}-m_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]})(x_{i}-x_{i-1}).

Therefore,
U(f,Q)-L(f,Q)\leq U(f,P)-L(f,P)\leq\varepsilon
. This proves that
f
is integrable on
[a,c]
. Similarly,
f
is integrable on
[c,b]
.

Conversely, suppose
f
is integrable on
[a,c]
and on
[c,b]
. Then, by
Proposition

3.11
, there exist
\mathdutchcal{E}_{a},\mathdutchcal{E}_{b}\searrow 0
such that for any
\varepsilon_{a}\in\mathdutchcal{E}_{a}
and
\varepsilon_{b}\in\mathdutchcal{E}_{b}
there exist partitions
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
of
[a,c]
and
[c,b]
, respectively, such that

U(f,P_{1})-L(f,P_{1})\leq\varepsilon_{a}\quad\text{and}\quad U(f,P_{2})-L(f,P_{2})\leq\varepsilon_{b}.

Then,
P\coloneqq P_{1}\cup P_{2}
is a partition of
[a,b]
such that

U(f,P)-L(f,P)=[U(f,P_{1})-L(f,P_{1})]+[U(f,P_{2})-L(f,P_{2})]\leq\varepsilon_{a}+\varepsilon_{b}.

Since
\mathdutchcal{E}_{a}+\mathdutchcal{E}_{b}\searrow 0
,
f
is integrable on
[a,b]
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq U(f,P)\leq L(f,P)+\varepsilon_{a}+\varepsilon_{b}

\displaystyle=L(f,P_{1})+L(f,P_{2})+\varepsilon_{a}+\varepsilon_{b}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{a}^{c}f(x)dx+\int_{c}^{b}f(x)dx+\varepsilon_{a}+\varepsilon_{b},

showing that
\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\leq\int_{a}^{c}f(x)dx+\int_{c}^{b}f(x)dx
. The reverse inequality can be shown in a similar fashion.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose
f,g\colon[a,b]\to E
are order bounded and locally band preserving. If both are Riemann integrable on
[a,b]
and
c\in E
, then the following holds.

1.

f+g
is Riemann integrable on
[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}(f+g)(x)dx=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx+\int_{a}^{b}g(x)dx.

2.

cf
is Riemann integrable on
[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}(cf)(x)dx=c\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx.

3.

If
f\leq g
, then
\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\leq\int_{a}^{b}g(x)dx
.

4.

\lvert f\rvert\colon x\mapsto\lvert f(x)\rvert
is integrable and
\left\lvert\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\right\rvert\leq\int_{a}^{b}\lvert f(x)\rvert dx
.

Proof.

To prove (i), note that for any subinterval
I
of
[a,b]
,
M_{I}(f+g)\leq M_{I}(f)+M_{I}(g)
and
m_{I}(f)+m_{I}(g)\leq m_{I}(f+g)
. Therefore, for any partition
P
of
[a,b]
, we have that

L(f,P)+L(g,P)\leq L(f+g,P)\leq U(f+g,P)\leq U(f,P)+L(f,P).

Taking the limit over
P
throughout yields

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx+\int_{a}^{b}g(x)dx\leq L(f+g)\leq U(f+g)\leq\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx+\int_{a}^{b}g(x)dx.

Thus,
L(f+g)=U(f+g)
and

\int_{a}^{b}(f+g)(x)dx=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx+\int_{a}^{b}g(x)dx.

The proof of (ii) is similar except we use that for any subinterval
I
of
[a,b]
we have that
m_{I}(cf)=c^{+}m_{I}(f)-c^{-}M_{I}(f)
and
M_{I}(cf)=c^{+}M_{I}(f)-c^{-}m_{I}(f)
. Thus, for any partition
P
of
[a,b]
, we have that

c^{+}L(f,P)-c^{-}U(f,P)=L(cf,P)\leq U(cf,P)=c^{+}U(f,P)-c^{-}L(f,P).

Taking the limit over
P
throughout yields

c\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx=L(cf)\leq U(cf)=c\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx.

Thus,
L(cf)=U(cf)
and

\int_{a}^{b}cf(x)dx=c\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx.

Taking the limit over
P
in
U(f,P)\leq U(g,P)
immediately yields (iii), and the second half of (iv) follows from (iii) after we prove the first half. By
Proposition

3.11
, there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists a partition
P
of
[a,b]
satisfying
U(f,P)-L(f,P)\leq\varepsilon
. For any subinterval
I
of
[a,b]
, we have that for all
x,y\in I
,

\lvert\lvert f(x)\rvert-\lvert f(y)\rvert\rvert\leq\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert,

from which it follows that
M_{I}(\lvert f\rvert)-m_{I}(\lvert f\rvert)\leq M_{I}(f)-m_{I}(f)
. Therefore,

U(\lvert f\rvert,P)-L(\lvert f\rvert,P)\leq U(f,P)-L(f,P)\leq\varepsilon.\qed

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
f,g\colon[a,b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving. If
f
and
g
are both integrable, then
fg
is integrable.

Proof.

Let
K\in E
be such that
\lvert f(x)\rvert,\lvert g(x)\rvert\leq K
for all
x\in[a,b]
.

(i) We first assume
f=g\geq 0
. Since
f
is integrable, there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists a partition
P
of
[a,b]
such that
U(f,P)-L(f,P)\leq\varepsilon
.

Let
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
and
P:=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
as above. Then, for any subinterval
I
of
[a,b]
, we have that
m_{I}(f^{2})=m_{I}(f)^{2}
and
M_{I}(f^{2})=M_{I}(f)^{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle U(

\displaystyle f^{2},P)-L(f^{2},P)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f^{2})-m_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f^{2}))(x_{i}-x_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f)^{2}-m_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f)^{2})(x_{i}-x_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f)+m_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f))(M_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f)-m_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f))(x_{i}-x_{i-1})

\displaystyle\leq 2K\sum_{i=1}(M_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f)-m_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f))(x_{i}-x_{i-1})

\displaystyle=2K(U(f,P)-L(f,P))

\displaystyle\leq 2K\varepsilon.

Since
2K\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
, this shows
f^{2}
is integrable.

(ii) Now assume
f=g
. Since
f
is integrable,
\lvert f\rvert
is integrable by
Proposition

4.3
, and therefore
fg=f^{2}=\lvert f\rvert^{2}
is integrable by part (i).

(iii) Without any additional assumptions on
f
and
g
, showing that
fg
is integrable follows from

fg=\tfrac{1}{4}((f+g)^{2}-(f-g)^{2}).\qed

Next we will define what it means for a net of functions to converge uniformly, and show that a uniform limit of integrable functions is again integrable. Note that to sensibly define what it means to converge uniformly, we must have the same regulating set
\mathdutchcal{E}
when considering convergence at each point, which need not be true when one has pointwise convergence.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
f_{\alpha},f\colon\operatorname{dom}(f)\to E
. We say that
f_{\alpha}\to f

uniformly
if there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
, there exists an
\alpha_{0}
satisfying

x\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\text{ and }\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}\implies\lvert f_{\alpha}(x)-f(x)\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
f_{\alpha},f\colon[a,b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving functions with
f_{\alpha}\to f
uniformly. If
f_{\alpha}
is integrable for each
\alpha
, then
f
is integrable and

\int_{a}^{b}f_{\alpha}(x)dx\to\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx.

Proof.

Since
f_{\alpha}\to f
uniformly, there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists an
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
satisfying

y\in[a,b]\text{ and }\alpha\geq\alpha_{\varepsilon}\implies\lvert f_{\alpha}(y)-f(y)\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

Let
\alpha\geq\alpha_{\varepsilon}
. Then, for any subinterval
I\subseteq[a,b]
,

m_{I}(f_{\alpha})-\varepsilon\leq m_{I}(f)\leq M_{I}(f)\leq M_{I}(f_{\alpha})+\varepsilon,

and hence, for any partition
P=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
of
[a,b]
, we have that

\displaystyle U(f,P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}(f)(x_{i}-x_{i-1})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{I}(f_{\alpha})+\varepsilon)(x_{i}-x_{i-1})

\displaystyle=U(f_{\alpha},P)+\varepsilon(b-a),

and, similarly,
L(f,P)\geq L(f_{\alpha},P)-\varepsilon(b-a)
. Taking the limit over all partitions
P
yields

\int_{a}^{b}f_{\alpha}(x)dx-\varepsilon(b-a)\leq L(f)\leq U(f)\leq\int_{a}^{b}f_{\alpha}(x)+\varepsilon(b-a).

Finally, taking the limit over all
\alpha
shows that

L(f)=U(f)=\lim_{\alpha}\int_{a}^{b}f_{\alpha}(x)dx.\qed

The following two propositions provide examples of integrable functions.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be order bounded, monotone, and locally band preserving. Then
f
is integrable.

Proof.

Assume, without loss of generality, that
f
is increasing. Define

\mathdutchcal{E}\coloneqq\{\tfrac{1}{n}(b-a)(f(b)-f(a)):n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Let
\varepsilon:=\frac{1}{n}(b-a)(f(b)-f(a))
, and define
P=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
, where
x_{i}\coloneqq a+\frac{i}{n}(b-a)
. Then,

\displaystyle U(f,P)-L(f,P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]}-m_{[x_{i-1},x_{i}]})(x_{i}-x_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x_{i})-f(x_{i-1}))({\textstyle\frac{1}{n}}(b-a))

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{n}}(b-a)(f(b)-f(a))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon.

Since
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
,
f
is integrable.
∎

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be uniformly order continuous and locally band preserving. Then
f
is integrable.

Proof.

Since
f
is uniformly order continuous, there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists an
N_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

x,y\in[a,b]\text{ and }\lvert x-y\rvert\leq\frac{1}{N_{\varepsilon}}(b-a)\implies\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

Let
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
, and define
P\coloneqq\{x_{0},\dots,x_{N_{\varepsilon}}\}
, where
x_{k}\coloneqq a+\frac{k}{N_{\varepsilon}}(b-a)
for
0\leq k\leq N_{\varepsilon}
. Then,

\displaystyle U(f,P)-L(f,P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}\left(M_{[x_{k-1},x_{k}]}-m_{[x_{k-1},x_{k}]}\right)(x_{k}-x_{k-1})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\varepsilon}}\varepsilon(x_{k}-x_{k-1})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(b-a).

Since
(b-a)\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
,
f
is integrable.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Note that it is an open question whether the assumption of uniform order continuity in
Proposition

4.8
can be weakened to order continuity. In
[
4
, Example 4.1]
, an example is given of an order continuous function
f\colon[a,b]\to E
that is unbounded (and hence not uniformly order continuous), but we have no such examples for locally band preserving functions. Thus, there are three possibilities for an order continuous and locally band preserving function
f\colon[a,b]\to E
: (i)
f
must be uniformly order continuous (and hence integrable), (ii)
f
must be integrable but need not be uniformly order continuous, or (iii)
f
need not be integrable.

5
Integrals with incomparable endpoints

We now turn our attention to defining integrals where the endpoints
a
and
b
are not necessarily comparable. Classically, we have that if
a\not\leq b
, then
b<a
and we define
\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx:=-\int_{b}^{a}f(x)
. In our setting, we instead have the decomposition

E=B_{a<b}\oplus B_{b<a}\oplus B_{a=b}.

Intuitively, we can integrate normally where
a<b
, we must integrate from
b
to
a
and add a minus sign where
b<a
, and the integral must be zero where
a=b
. This informs the following definition.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
f\colon[a\wedge b,a\vee b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving, where
a,b\in E
. We say that
f
is
Riemann integrable from
a
to
b
if
f
is integrable on
[a\wedge b,a\vee b]
. In this case, we define

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{a<b}\left(\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}f(x)dx\right)-\mathbb{P}_{b<a}\left(\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}f(x)dx\right).

The next proposition is an immediate consequence of the defintion.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
f\colon[a\wedge b,a\vee b]\to E
be order bounded, locally band preserving, and integrable from
a
to
b
. Then

\left\lvert\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\right\rvert=\left\lvert\int_{a\wedge b}^{a\vee b}f(x)dx\right\rvert.

Next we provide generalisations of the properties from
Section

4
, where we now allow the endpoints of the integral to be incomparable. The proofs have been omitted as the generalisations follow immediately.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
f\colon[a\wedge c,b\vee d]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving with
\mathbb{P}(a)=\mathbb{P}(c)
and
\mathbb{P}(b)=\mathbb{P}(d)
. If
f
is integrable both from
a
to
b
and from
c
to
d
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{c}^{d}f(x)dx\right).

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
f\colon[a\wedge b,a\vee b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving with
c\in[a\wedge b,a\vee b]
. Then
f
is integrable from
a
to
b
if and only if
f
is integrable from
a
to
c
and from
c
to
b
. In this case,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx=\int_{a}^{c}f(x)dx+\int_{c}^{b}f(x)dx.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
f,g\colon[a\wedge b,a\vee b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving. If both
f
and
g
are integrable from
a
to
b
and
c\in E
, then the following holds.

1.

f+g
is integrable from
a
to
b
and

\int_{a}^{b}(f+g)(x)dx=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx+\int_{a}^{b}g(x)dx.

2.

cf
is integrable on from
a
to
b
and

\int_{a}^{b}(cf)(x)dx=c\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx.

3.

\lvert f\rvert
is integrable from
a
to
b
.

4.

fg
is integrable from
a
to
b
.

6
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

In this section, we first provide a Mean Value Theorem for Integrals in
Theorem

6.1
, which will be used to prove the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus—of which parts 1 and 2 are
Theorems

6.2
and
6.3
, respectively. As this section connects our theory of integration to the theory of differentiation from
[
7
]
, we now assume
E
to be a Dedekind complete
\Phi
-algebra.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be locally band preserving and order continuous. If
f
is integrable, then, for any
x,y\in[a,b]
, there exists
c\in[x\wedge y,x\vee y]
such that

(y-x)f(c)=\int_{x}^{y}f(z)dz.

Proof.

Assume
x\leq y
. By
Theorem

2.14
, there exist
d_{1},d_{2}\in[x,y]
such that
f(d_{1})\leq f(z)\leq f(d_{2})
for all
z\in[x,y]
. Since

(y-x)f(d_{1})\leq\int_{x}^{y}f(z)dz\leq(y-x)f(d_{2}),

applying
Theorem

2.13
to the function
w\mapsto(y-x)f(w)
shows that there exists
c\in[x,y]
such that
(y-x)f(c)=\int_{x}^{y}f(z)dz
.

Let
x,y\in[a,b]
without restriction. Then there exists
c\in[x\wedge y,x\vee y]
such that

(x\vee y-x\wedge y)f(c)=\int_{x\wedge y}^{x\vee y}f(z)dz.

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{y}f(z)dz

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{x<y}\left(\int_{x\wedge y}^{x\vee y}f(z)dz\right)-\mathbb{P}_{y<x}\left(\int_{x\wedge y}^{x\vee y}f(z)dz\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{x<y}((x\vee y-x\wedge y)f(c))-\mathbb{P}_{y<x}((x\vee y-x\wedge y)f(c))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{x<y}((y-x)f(c))-\mathbb{P}_{y<x}((x-y)f(c))

\displaystyle=(y-x)f(c).\qed

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
a\ll b
. Suppose
f\colon[a,b]\to E
is order continuous, locally band preserving, and integrable. Then
F(x)\coloneqq\int_{a}^{x}f(z)dz
is uniformly continuous on
[a,b]
and super order differentiable on
(a,b)
with
F^{\prime}=f
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in(a,b)
. Let
\Delta\downarrow 0
. Since
f
is order continuous at
x
, there exists an
\mathdutchcal{E}\searrow 0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in\mathdutchcal{E}
there exists a
\delta\in\Delta
satisfying

y\in[a,b]\text{ and }\lvert y-x\rvert\leq\delta\implies\lvert f(y)-f(x)\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

Let
y\in[a,b]
be such that
\lvert y-x\rvert\leq\delta
. By
Theorem

6.1
, there exists a
c\in[x\wedge y,x\vee y]
such that

F(y)-F(x)=\int_{x}^{y}f(z)dz=(y-x)f(c).

Since
\lvert c-x\rvert\leq\delta
, it follows that

\displaystyle\lvert F(y)-F(x)-(y-x)f(x)\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert y-x\rvert\lvert f(c)-f(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert y-x\rvert\varepsilon.

This shows that
F
is super order differentiable on
(a,b)
with
F^{\prime}=f
.

To see that
F
is uniformly order continuous on
[a,b]
, note that
f
is order bounded by
Theorem

2.14
, say
\lvert f(x)\rvert\leq K
for all
x\in[a,b]
. Define
\mathdutchcal{E}\coloneqq K\Delta
. Let
\varepsilon=K\delta\in\mathdutchcal{E}
, then if
x,y\in[a,b]
with
\lvert x-y\rvert\leq\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert F(x)-F(y)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{y}^{x}f(z)dz\right\rvert=\left\lvert\int_{x\wedge y}^{x\vee y}f(z)dz\right\rvert\leq K\left\lvert x\vee y-x\wedge y\right\rvert

\displaystyle=K\lvert x-y\rvert\leq K\delta=\varepsilon.

This proves
F
is uniformly continuous on
[a,b]
.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

Suppose
a\ll b
. Let
f\colon[a,b]\to E
be order bounded and locally band preserving, and let
F\colon[a,b]\to E
be order continuous on
[a,b]
, order differentiable on
(a,b)
with
F^{\prime}=f
, and locally band preserving. If
f
is integrable, then, for any
x,y\in[a,b]
,

\int_{x}^{y}f(z)dz=F(y)-F(x).

Proof.

Assume
x\leq y
. Consider any partition
P\coloneqq\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
of
[x,y]
. For
1\leq i\leq n
, by
Theorem

2.19
, there exists a
y_{i}\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
such that

F(x_{i})-F(x_{i-1})=f(y_{i})(x_{i}-x_{i-1}),

and hence

m_{i}(x_{i}-x_{i-1})\leq F(x_{i})-F(x_{i-1})\leq M_{i}(x_{i}-x_{i-1}).

Summing over
i
then yields
L(f,P)\leq F(y)-F(x)\leq U(f,P)
. Taking the limit over
P
gives us that
\int_{x}^{y}f(z)dz=F(y)-F(x)
.

Let
x,y\in[a,b]
without restriction. Then, using that
F
is locally band preserving,

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{y}f(z)dz

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{x<y}\left(\int_{x\wedge y}^{x\vee y}f(z)dz\right)-\mathbb{P}_{y<x}\left(\int_{x\wedge y}^{x\vee y}f(z)dz\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{x<y}(F(x\vee y)-F(x\wedge y))-\mathbb{P}_{y<x}(F(x\vee y)-F(x\wedge y))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{x<y}(F(y)-F(x))-\mathbb{P}_{y<x}(F(x)-F(y))

\displaystyle=F(y)-F(x).\qed

Remark 6.4
.

[
4
, Example 5.11]
shows that the assumption that
F
is locally band preserving in
Theorem

6.3
is necessary. Although the antiderivative
F
defined in
Theorem

6.2
will always be locally band preserving due to it being super order differentiable, other antiderivatives need not be and for these the result will fail.

Now that we have a Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we can generalise classic integration techniques such as
u
-substitution and integration by parts. These are given in the following two propositions.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
a\ll b
and
c\ll d
. Let
g\colon[a,b]\to E
and
f\colon[c,d]\to E
be locally band preserving, order continuous, and integrable. Suppose
G\colon[a,b]\to[c,d]
is locally band preserving, order continuous on
[a,b]
, and order differentiable on
(a,b)
with
G^{\prime}=g
. If
(f\circ G)g
is Riemann integrable on
[a,b]
, then

\int_{a}^{b}f(G(x))g(x)dx=\int_{G(a)}^{G(b)}f(u)du.

Proof.

Let
F(x):=\int_{c}^{x}f(u)du
. By
Theorem

6.2
,
F
is uniformly continuous on
[c,d]
and super order differentiable on
(c,d)
with
F^{\prime}=f
. Note that
G
differs from a super order differentiable function by a constant using
Theorems

6.2
and
6.3
, and hence
G
is also super order differentiable on
(a,b)
. By the chain rule
[
7
, Theorem 4.3(iii)]
,
F\circ G
is super order differentiable on
(a,b)
with
(F\circ G)^{\prime}=(f\circ G)g
. By assumption,
(f\circ G)g
is integrable and using
Theorem

6.3
now yields

\int_{a}^{b}f(G(x))g(x)dx=F(G(b))-F(G(a))=\int_{G(a)}^{G(b)}f(u)du.

∎

Remark 6.6
.

In
Proposition

6.5
, it is clear that
(f\circ G)g
is order continuous, but in light of
Remark

4.9
it is not known whether this guarantees
(f\circ G)g
is integrable and we must therefore assume it.

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
a\ll b
and
c\ll d
. Let
g\colon[a,b]\to E
and
f\colon[c,d]\to E
be locally band preserving and uniformly order continuous functions. Suppose
G\colon[a,b]\to[c,d]
is uniformly continuous and super order differentiable on
(a,b)
with
G^{\prime}=g
. Then
(f\circ G)g
is Riemann integrable on
[a,b]
and

\int_{a}^{b}f(G(x))g(x)dx=\int_{G(a)}^{G(b)}f(u)du.

Proof.

Since
f
,
G
and
g
are all uniformly continuous,
(f\circ G)g
is uniformly continuous on
[a,b]
. By
Theorem

6.2
,
f
has an antiderivative
F\colon[c,d]\to E
which is uniformly continuous on
[c,d]
and super order differentiable on
(c,d)
with
F^{\prime}=f
. Then,
F\circ G
is uniformly continuous on
[a,b]
and super order differentiable on
(a,b)
with
(F\circ G)^{\prime}=(f\circ G)g
by the chain rule
[
7
, Theorem 4.3(iii)]
. By
Theorem

6.3
,

\int_{a}^{b}f(G(x))g(x)\,dx=F(G(b))-F(G(a))=\int_{G(a)}^{G(b)}f(u)du.\qed

Proposition 6.8
.

Suppose
a\ll b
. Let
f,g,f^{\prime},g^{\prime}\colon[a,b]\to E
be locally band preserving, order continuous on
[a,b]
, and integrable. If
f^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime}
are the order derivatives of
f
and
g
on
(a,b)
, respectively. Then

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)g^{\prime}(x)dx=f(b)g(b)-f(a)g(a)-\int_{a}^{b}f^{\prime}(x)g(x)dx.

Proof.

We know that
fg
is order differentiable on
(a,b)
with
(fg)^{\prime}=fg^{\prime}+f^{\prime}g
by the product rule
[
7
, Theorem 4.3(ii)]
, and
(fg)^{\prime}
is integrable since it is a product and sum of integrable functions. By
Theorem

6.3
,

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)g^{\prime}(x)+f^{\prime}(x)g(x)dx=f(b)g(b)-f(a)g(a).\qed
