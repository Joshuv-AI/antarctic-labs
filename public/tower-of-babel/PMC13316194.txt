Dirofilaria immitis heartworm infections in Hungarian zoo animals
Authors: Alexandra Biácsi, János Gál, Sándor Hornok, Endre Sós, Péter Kertész, László Bakonyi, Edit Venczel, Gábor Majoros, Nóra Takács, Sándor Szekeres, Adrienn Gréta Tóth, Domenico Otranto, Gergő Keve
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDirofilaria immitis is a worldwide spread, mosquito-borne nematode of zoonotic concern, with domestic and wild canids serving as the main reservoirs of the infection in Europe. In this study we describe eleven cases of cardiovascular dirofilariosis in Hungarian zoos, including three Eurasian wolves (Canis lupus lupus), two Arctic wolves (Canis lupus arctos), an African lion (Panthera leo), an African leopard (Panthera pardus pardus), a Red panda (Ailurus fulgens), an Arctic fox (Vulpes lagopus), a Harbour seal (Phoca vitulina), and an Asian small-clawed otter (Aonyx cinereus). Heartworm disease presumably contributed to the deaths of three Eurasian wolves, one Red panda, one Arctic fox and one Harbour seal, as indicated by necropsy results, whereas the remaining infections were regarded as incidental findings. These cases represent the first documented occurrence of D. immitis in captive felids in Hungary and the first report of D. immitis in Arctic wolves. The present case series provides data about host range of dirofilariosis and discusses their potential role as reservoirs of the infection in autochthonous wild animals in Europe underlining the need for implemented surveillance and preventive strategies in zoo animal populations.Keywords: Dirofilaria immitis, Zoo animals, Exotic animals, heartworm, Veterinary case report

Introduction

Dirofilaria immitis is a mosquito-borne nematode, widely distributed in Europe, where it is associated with at least 17 different mosquito species acting as vectors (Hattendorf and Lühken, 2025). Also known as heartworm for their localization primarily in the pulmonary artery, this parasite infects domestic and wild canids, and other animal species, including humans (Hattendorf and Lühken, 2025). Other than D. immitis, Dirofilaria repens is also prevalent in Europe, with adults being embedded in subcutaneous nodules (Hattendorf and Lühken, 2025). Both Dirofilaria spp., as well as others within the Nochtiella subgenus, are mostly associated with wildlife and are of zoonotic concern (Perles et al., 2024). The only other nematode that, lives as an adult in the heart and pulmonary vessels of European terrestrial carnivores is Angiostrongylus vasorum, whereas larvae of Spirocerca lupi can reach the cardiovascular system (Otranto and Wall, 2024). Adults of D. repens can occasionally be found in the heart of canids as a result of aberrant migration (Ferraro et al., 2026). On the other hand, marine mammals such as pinnipeds may harbour filaroid nematode species in their cardiovascular system, for example Acanthocheilonema spirocauda and Otostrongylus circumlitus (Kelly et al., 2005; Lehnert et al., 2016)

Both Dirofilaria immitis and D. repens have been recorded in Hungary (Farkas et al., 2020). Following the first autochthonous case of D. immitis diagnosed in a Domestic dog (Canis familiaris) in 2007 (Jacsó et al., 2009), the parasite has been detected with prevalence ranging from 11.3% (Farkas et al., 2020) up to 17% in specific regions (Jerzsele et al., 2025). Such a relatively high prevalence in dogs paralleled the detection of cases in wild animals, like the Red fox (Vulpes vulpes) (prevalence range: 1.4 - 4%), Golden jackal (Canis aureus) (1.3 - 7.4%), European badger (Meles meles) (0 - 8%), and Raccoon (Procyon lotor) (5.3%) (Nagy et al., 2025; Szentiványi et al., 2026; Tolnai et al., 2014). Furthermore the parasite has been found in a pet ferret (Mustela putorius furo), causing central nervous system signs due to aberrant larva migration in the cranial cavity (Molnár et al., 2010).

Despite reports of D. immmitis in domestic and wild carnivores in Europe (Hattendorf and Lühken, 2025), infection in exotic and zoo animals is scant (Perles et al., 2024). A study on captive pinnipeds in Portugal reported a 43.8% prevalence of D. immitis by real-time PCR, as well as the presence of microfilariae in a single South African fur seal (Arctocephalus pusillus pusillus). These results demonstarted not only the susceptibility of these animals, but also their potential role as reservoirs (Alho et al., 2017). In addition, a patent hearthworm infection was recorded in a Leopard (Panthera pardus pardus) in Italy (Mazzariol et al., 2010), and an occult infection during a necropsy of African lion (Panthera leo) in Spain (Ruiz de Ybáñez et al., 2006). Conversely, in the USA, studies on D. immitis in zoo animals are more prevalent, with an extensive research conducted on carnivores kept in a Texan zoo, with two Asian small-clawed otters (Aonyx cinereus) scoring positive at the D. immitis antigen test, out of 211 animals screened, representing to 39 species (Upton et al., 2023). Again, in a large-scale vector surveillance study conducted in a zoo in Utah, two out of 5458 collected mosquitoes tested molecularly positive for D. immitis (Hammond et al., 2026). In addition, D. immitis has been reported in exotic animals in other USA States by positive antibody test results of several Caracal (Caracal caracal), Tiger (Panthera tigris), Serval (Leptailurus serval), Ocelot (Leopardus pardalis), Leopard (Panthera pardus) and Jaguar (Panthera onca) in North Carolina (Atkins et al., 2005). A Black-footed cat (Felis nigripes) was found to be positive during necropsy in Florida (Deem et al., 1998) and a Bengal tiger (P. tigris) in Tennessee (Kennedy and Patton, 1981). Finally, a Pale-headed saki (Pithecia pithecia) that had been housed in Florida prior to the infection tested positive for antigens at a Texas zoo (Gamble et al., 1998). These records overlap with the reported incidence data of dirofilariosis in dogs from the United States, according to which the southeastern states are the most heavily affected (American Heartworm Society (AHS), 2025). Surveillance of captive exotic animals is important, as their susceptibility to heartworm disease is often poorly understood, and preventive measures may be difficult to implement due to management constraints.

The aim of this study was to retrospectively analyze cases of D. immitis infection detected during post-mortem examinations of captive carnivores in Hungarian zoos as well as cases described form archived parasite material.

Methods

Heartworm cases herein presented derived from necropsies performed between 2017 and 2026 on animals from five zoos located in central, eastern, and southern Hungary (Fig. 1). These cases (Table 1, Table 2) were also documented and photographed and, whenever possible, worm specimens were preserved for further analysis, see below. Most of these archived data were collected from veterinarians working at the zoos, with photographs and morphological and molecular data available for most of them (Table 1). Indeed, the preserved parasites were identified as D. immitis based on morphological and/or molecular analyses for cases 1-8. Conversely, when only photographs of the anatomical site and necropsy records, but not parasites were available, nematodes were referred as to D. cf. immitis (cases 9–10) or Dirofilaria sp. if only the necropsy record was available (case 11) (Table 1).

Morphological identification of D. immitis

Nematodes recovered from animals and preserved in 70% ethanol were identified at species level based on morphological characters (Furtado et al., 2010; Manfredi et al., 2007 ;Skrjabin et al., 1949), following clarification in lactic acid solution (∼50% lactic acid and 50% H2O), for approximately 5-30 min and examination of the anterior and posterior portions (∼1 cm). Shortly, morphological identification was carried out considering the following morphological characters: whole body size (150-310 mm in females, 119-200 mm in males), shape of large and small spicules, number and localization of pre- and post-cloacal papillae, cuticular ridges in males (Fig. 2), as well as the head shape and the esophagus and the position and the shape of the vulva in females (Fig. 3) (Furtado et al., 2010).

Molecular identification of D. immitis

One male specimen from each case 1–5 and case 8 and a female from case 7 were further analyzed molecularly, following DNA extraction from the midsection (∼1–2 cm) of each specimen, using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions, including a 1-h digestion step in tissue lysis buffer with Proteinase K at 56 °C.

A partial-18S rRNA target gene (440 bp in size) was performed as previously described (Liu et al., 2005), using primers (DiF 5’ – GCATCTTAGAACTTGGTCCATCC – 3′ and DiR 5’ – CAAAGGCGTATTTACCGCCAC – 3’) in 25 μl total volume reaction, containing 5 μl template DNA, 2.5 μl 10x CoralLoad Buffer (incl. 15 mM MgCl2), 0.5 μl dNTPs (10 mM), 0.5U HotStar Taq Plus DNA Polymerase (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), 0.5 μl of each primer (50 μM) and 15.9 μl PCR water. The thermal profile was 95 °C for 10 min (single step), 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 49 °C for 45 s, 72 °C for 1 min and final elongation step at 72 °C for 10 min (cases 1–5 and case 8).

The amplification of the 18S rRNA (860 bp) of a nematode from case 7 was conducted following Kobylinski et al. (2012). The primers used for this reaction were: MermF 5’ – CAA GGA CGA AAG TTA GAG GTT C – 3’ (forward) and MermR 5’ – GGA AAC CTT GTT ACG ACT TTT A – 3’ (reverse) in 25 μl reaction mixtures containing 5 μl template DNA, 12.5 μl HotStarTaq Plus Mastermix (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), 2.5 μl CoralLoad Dye, 0.5 μl of each primer (1.0 μM final concentration), and 4 μl ddH2O. The thermal profile consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min.

PCR analysis of the 12S rDNA gene was run according to the protocol of Gioia et al. (2010) for cases 1–5 and case 8. The general primer pair 12SF (5′- GTTCCAGAATAATCGGCTA - 3′) and 12SRdeg (5′- ATTGACGGATG(AG)TTTGTACC -3′) previously designed by Casiraghi et al. (2004) followed by, a specific amplification for D. immitis (204 bp) and D. repens (327 bp) as described in Gioia et al. (2010). PCR analysis of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 gene (cox1) (689 bp) was also performed for cases 2–5 and cases 7–8, following Otranto et al. (2005). Reaction mixture without adding template DNA served as a negative control. PCR products were electrophoresed in 1.5% ethidium-bromide stained agarose gel followed by UV-visualization. Extraction controls and negative controls remained PCR negative in all tests. The PCR products were purified and sequenced by Eurofins Biomi Ltd (Gödöllő, Hungary). The BioEdit program (https://bioedit.software.informer.com/7.2/) was used for quality control and trimming of sequences. The analyses of assembled sequences were performed with BLASTN via GenBank (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The sequence obtained in the current study was deposited in the GenBank database and are available under accession numbers: PZ362961-PZ362966; PZ363998 - PZ364003; PZ366090 - PZ366095; PZ363028 (Table 2).

Results

General information on the animals (e.g., age, location, and time of death) is presented in Table 1, while the corresponding GenBank accession numbers for each case in Table 2.

Between 2017 and 2026, eleven cases of cardiovascular dirofilariosis were diagnosed during necropsies (Table 1). Based on the available morphological and molecular analyses, eight cases were confirmed as D. immitis infections: in three Eurasian wolves (Canis lupus lupus) (cases 1–3), two Arctic wolves (Canis lupus arctos) (cases 4–5), an African leopard (P. pardus pardus) (case 6), an African lion (P. leo) (case 7), and in a Red panda (Ailurus fulgens) (case 8). In addition, Dirofilaria cf. immitis was identified in a Harbour seal (Phoca vitulina) (case 9) and in an Arctic fox (Vulpes lagopus) (case 10), while a single unidentified Dirofilaria specimen was found in an Asian small-clawed otter (Aonyx cinereus) (case 11). None of the animals received medication specifically targeting D. immitis, either for treatment or prevention. Cases 1–3, 8–9 and 11 received no antiparasitic treatment; cases 4–5 received oral praziquantel and fenbendazole twice yearly (February and October); and cases 6–7 and 10 received an oral combination of praziquantel, pyrantel embonate, and fenbendazole three times yearly (February, June and October). Heartworm disease was considered clinically relevant in three Eurasian wolves (Fig. 4), one Red panda, one Harbour seal, and an Arctic fox (Fig. 5), due to the necropsy finding (e.g., cardiomegaly, dilatation of major vessels, pulmonary congestion, and circulatory collapse), likely contributing to the deaths of the animals. In the Eurasian wolf from case 1, grade III haemangiosarcoma was also identified in the lungs, while the other two wolves (cases 2, 3) were euthanized due to lethargy and moribound state, most likely due to heatworm disease. The Harbour seal (case 9) died during anesthesia, presumeably due to acute circulatory collapse. The Arctic fox (case 10) died due to hyperthermia that led to cardiovascular collapse during an extreme heatwave. In the case of the Red panda (case 8), it is also presumed that heartworm disease was the primary cause of death, as this elderly animal (∼15-years-old) exhibited dyspnea and recumbency prior to death, however, the necropsy did not reveal any other relevant findings other than anaemia. In contrast, infections detected in the remaining animals were interpreted as incidental, as the animals showed no relevant symptoms prior to their deaths, and necropsy findings were not consistent with heartworm disease. In those cases, the primary causes of death were unrelated conditions, including trauma (case 7), infectious diseases such as Salmonella sp., feline coronavirus, and feline parvovirus infection (case 6), intestinal volvulus (case 4), general debilitation (case 5), and hypothermia (case 11).

The highest parasite burdens were observed in the Eurasian wolves and in the Red panda (Table 1). In Eurasian wolves (cases 1-3), 22 (16 females and six males), 25 (only five males and six females were preserved) and 6 (two females and four males) adult nematodes were recovered from the heart and major vessels, respectively, while 13 adult worms (eight females and five males) were collected from the right ventricle and pulmonary trunk of the Red panda. Lower parasite burdens were detected in the remaining species, where only one to four nematodes were identified (Table 1, Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

Morphological examination was performed in all confirmed D. immitis cases and molecular analyses in Eurasian wolves, Arctic wolves, the African lion, and the Red panda, supporting the morphological identification (Table 2). Indeed, a homology of 99.48-100% was found with reference sequences of D. immitis in GenBank (NC_005305; AB973231). Microfilariae were detected in the uteri of female worms recovered from all Eurasian wolves and from the Red panda, whereas no microfilariae were observed in female worms obtained from Arctic wolves and the African lion.

Discussion

In this study, cardiovascular dirofilariosis in eleven zoo animals belonging to seven species are reported from across Hungary. Based on the necropsy findings, heartworm disease was observed in three Eurasian wolves, one Red panda, one Arctic fox and one Harbour seal, and it is presumed that this condition contributed to the death of these animals. In contrast, in two Arctic wolves, an African lion, an African leopard, an Arctic fox, and an Asian small-clawed otter, the infections were considered incidental findings.

Five of eleven cases discussed in this study are related to Grey wolves (Canis lupus) (cases 1–5), a species which comprises morphologically and genetically distinct subspecies (Montana et al., 2017; Wilson and Reeder, 2005). However, reports describing heartworm infection do not often specify the subspecies of the examined wolves, and only the species name, Grey wolf (C. lupus sensu lato) is mentioned. According to a case report from Serbia, D. immitis specimens (n = 42) were removed from the heart of a free-ranging wolf (Gavrilović et al., 2015). Infected Grey wolves had been found in at least three European countries so far (Italy, Serbia and Spain) (Hattendorf and Lühken, 2025), with prevalence of 3.4% (17/488) at the necropsy of animals In Italy (Dini et al., 2026). On ELISA and Knott's tests 13 out of 24 captive wolves examined scored positive in Minnesota (USA) (Kreeger et al., 1990). Based on reports that are informative on the subspecies, D. immitis was found in the Eurasian wolf (C. lupus lupus), the Iberian wolf (C. lupus signatus) and in the Italian wolf (C. lupus italicus) all these animals being microfilaraemic (Dini et al., 2024; Estévez-Sánchez et al., 2022; Gomes-de-Sá et al., 2022; Szentiványi and Garamszegi, 2024). This study presents the first reports of dirofilariosis in Arctic wolves.

Within the family Felidae both an African leopard (case 6) and an African lion (case 7) scored positive. An African leopard was already found positive to D. immitis in India, in association with cardiopulmonary pathology and severe clinical signs likely due to 18 adult worms present (Samanta et al., 2007). Other cases of positive leopards were found in endemic areas of Italy (Mazzariol et al., 2010) and North Carolina (USA) (Atkins et al., 2005) though the patogenicity is not fully assessed in this animal species.

According to a case report published in 2006, a 20-year-old male lion was found to be infected by two D. immitis specimens in Spain, though the fatal outcome was likely caused by an adenocarcinoma in the lungs (Ruiz de Ybáñez et al., 2006). Antigen testing of lions (P. leo krugeri) in Texas, USA (n = 15), and Italy (n = 3) were negative (Grillini et al., 2022; Upton et al., 2023).

Dirofilaria immitis infection was also diagnosed in a single Red panda (case 8), as already reported in the United States, in an individual that succumbed to septic shock associated with dental abscesses (Harwell and Craig, 1981). Again, in Japan, D. immitis specimens were collected from three dead Red pandas (Narushima et al., 1984) as well as in China (Sichuan Province) where 17 animals died in a single facility infected by heartworm within a five-year period (Lan et al., 2012). In the latter case, the affected animals exhibited dyspnoea, occasional coughing, and icterus. The same authors had previously screened 48 red pandas using the Knott's technique and antigen assays finding 19 infected individuals, 16 of which showed clinical signs (Lan et al., 2012).

Heartworm disease (caused by D. cf. immitis) was diagnosed in a Harbour seal (case 9) as previously detected in Portugal, in three out of five Harbour seals (syn. Common seal), as well as other pinnipeds, such as California sea lions (Zalophus californianus) and South african fur seals (A. pusillus pusillus) (Alho et al., 2017). Prior to this report, 90 adult D. immitis were removed from the right ventricle, pulmonary artery, portal vein and from the pericardial sac and peritoneal cavity of a Harbour seal (Medway and Wieland, 1975). In addition, two D. immitis were found in the left and right ventricles of a 10-year-old male Harbour seal in Korea (Kang et al., 2002). Dirofilaria immitis infection was also identified in Galápagos sea lions (Zalophus wollebaeki) (Gregory et al., 2023) with a 2% prevalence, based on Knott's test and molecular detection (Culda et al., 2025).

Herein, we report the first case of dirofilariosis in an Arctic fox (caused by D. cf. immitis) (case 10). Despite the lack of knowledge regarding the heartworm infection of this species, several other members of the genus have been so far associated with the disease, including Rüppell's fox (Vulpes rueppeli), and most importantly, the Red fox (Vulpes vulpes), which species is a widely known host of D. immitis (Ionică et al., 2017; Nagy et al., 2025; Szentiványi et al., 2026; Szentiványi and Garamszegi, 2024; Tolnai et al., 2014).

The cardiovascular dirofilariosis by a Dirofilaria sp. of a single Asian-small clawed otter was also recorded (case 11). According to another report, two Asian small-clawed otter tested D. immitis antigen-positive in a zoo in Texas, USA (Upton et al., 2023), and a single male D. immitis was found during necropsy in the same species in Louisiana, USA (Upton et al., 2022). Dirofilaria spectans also occurs in the circulatory system of Lutrinae mustelids in Brazil (De Freitas and Lent, 1949; Upton et al., 2022), being of zoonotic potential (De Freitas and Mayall, 1953).

The clinical severity of D. immitis across different host species should be interpreted with caution as it is based on relatively low animals and variable number of nematodes which could affect the severity of the clinical presentation. For example, the Red panda might be particularly sensitive to heartworm infection, as also suggested in a previous finding (Lan et al., 2012). This species naturally inhabits high-altitude environments (2200–4800 m), where mosquito exposure is limited (Panthi et al., 2019), which may contribute to increased vulnerability in endemic areas. Indeed, it is suspected that some animals which have rarely encountered mosquito-borne pathogens during their evolution can show higher susceptibility to some of these. In this regards, the high sensitivity of Reindeers (Rangifer tarandus) to the mosquito-borne Setaria tundra is demonstrated by severe clinical presentation with peritonitis, perihepatitis and poor body condition (Haider et al., 2018). Conversely, the same parasite is usually asymptomatic in Roe deer (Capreolus capreolus) originating from endemic, warmer regions (Čurlík et al., 2023). These findings emphasise the importance of effective surveillance and preventive strategies in zoos, including other animals and domestic dogs in their vicinity. However, the implementation of continuous prophylactic treatment may be challenging in certain species due to management constraints. While prevention and treatment protocols of some animals, such as wolves and red pandas are available (Kreeger et al., 1990; Lan et al., 2012), their consistent application may not be feasible in all settings. Therefore, the development of safe, long-acting preventive approaches is of particular importance in zoo environments.

As effective, safe, and continuous prophylactic treatment of zoo animals is yet unresolved, and relatively large numbers of susceptible animals may be housed in close proximity, zoos could serve as potential hotspots for transmission in urban areas. By examining the uteri of the available female worms, microfilariae were detected in D. immitis specimens retrieved from Eurasian wolves and a Red panda, but none in the uteri of nematodes recovered from the African lion (where only female worms were present) or from the Arctic wolves (Fig. 3).

The occult infection of the African lion (case 7) from this study is in line with the previous report from Spain, where, despite the simultaneous presence of a male and two females, no microfilariae were present in the blood of the animal (Ruiz de Ybáñez et al., 2006). It is also worh mentioning that antigen testing of lions in endemic areas (Italy, and Texas, USA) yielded negative results (Grillini et al., 2022; Upton et al., 2023). Based on these findings, and on the fact that microfilaraemia in felids is generally rare (Carbonara et al., 2025; Otranto et al., 2025), it is unlikely that lions might serve as effective reservoirs. Although additional data is necessary to assess the reservoir potential of large felids, the currently available evidence suggests that they are unlikely to play a significant role in this regard. Additionally, for diagnostic purposes, blood-based methods alone may have limited sensitivity in these animals. Advanced imaging techniques, such as thoracic radiography and echocardiography, could improve diagnostic accuracy (Carbonara et al., 2025; Litster and Atwell, 2008)

While patent infections have been reported in the European otter (Lutra lutra) (Penezić et al., 2018), only non-patent infections have been documented in the Asian small-clawed otter so far (Righton et al., 2025), which is consistent with our results (case 11), as only a single Dirofilaria sp. was found in the cadaver. Microfilariae were detected in the uteri of female nematodes collected from the Red panda in the current study. Together with findings from China, where 9 of 16 antigen-positive Red pandas also exhibited microfilaraemia, data suggest that this animal species may serve as competent reservoir of D. immitis (Lan et al., 2012), though non-patent infections were recorded in this animals species in previous studies (Narushima et al., 1984).

Microfilariae were detected in the female nematodes collected from all examined Eurasian wolves. Although this may appear persuasive, further studies are required to properly evaluate their reservoir status. The absence of microfilariae in specimens from Arctic wolves should also be interpreted with caution, as only three female worms were available for examination. A large-scale study conducted in Romania has suggested that free-living wild canids, such as the Red fox, may not be an efficient reservoir hosts due to the low prevalence of parasitaemia (Ionică et al., 2017). For example, a study from Italy reported a microfilaraemia prevalence of only 0.41% (2/488) in wild Grey wolves, despite a considerably higher infection rate of 3.48% (17/488) (Dini et al., 2026). However, it remains unknown how various conditions associated with captivity, such as stress and higher population density, might influence this trend. In the USA, 50% (12/24) of the examined captive Grey wolves were microflaraemic (Kreeger et al., 1990).

Overall, dirofilariosis in zoo animals are considered occasional findings, mostly linked to necropsy retrieval. However, in endemic areas for the infection in dogs, survaillance diagnostic procedures should also be implemented in live animals. In this regards, Knott's test results alone, should be interpreted with caution as blood-circulating microfilariae of other species (e.g., D. repens and Acantochelionema reconditum) might be detected in canids and felids, and morphologically misdiagnosed with D. immitis (American Heartworm Society (AHS), 2024; Magnis et al., 2013). Analogously, in pinnipeds, other filaroid nematode species (e.g., A. spirocauda, Acanthocheilonema odendhali and O. circumlitus) may occurr (Kelly et al., 2005; Krucik et al., 2016; Wilhelm et al., 2026). Additionally, D. immitis antigen tests are specific in dogs (American Heartworm Society (AHS), 2024), but evaluating the specificity of new-generation antigen tests in zoo animals would be important for diagnostic purposes. Based on the above, to better assess the epidemiological significance of zoo animals as potential reservoirs, systematic investigations are required using combined diagnostic approaches, including Knott's test and qPCR. Implementing of these methods would provide information not only on prevalence, but also on the level of microfilaraemia and allow for species-specific identification (Aththanayaka et al., 2024).

Conclusion

Although this study is not systematic, it provides new records of D. immitis infections in eight species of animals kept in Hungarian zoos. Over a ten-year period, eleven cases were identified, including clinically relevant heartworm disease in Eurasian wolves, a Red panda, an Arctic fox and a Harbour seal, while infections in other species were considered incidental findings. The infections of an African lion and an African leopard, represent the first documented cases of feline heartworm disease in Hungary. Additionally, two Arctic wolves were found to be infected with D. immitis, constituting the first reports confirming the host status of this subspecies of C. lupus. Finally, Dirofilaria cf. immitis infection was documented in an Arctic fox for the first time. Overall, these results expand current knowledge on the host range of D. immitis in captive wildlife and underline the need for implemented surveillance and preventive strategies in zoo animal populations.
