Stereotactic arrhythmia radioablation for ventricular tachycardia: Mechanisms, workflow, outcomes, and future directions
Authors: Sai Kumar Samala, Khashayar Hematpour, Ramesh Hariharan, Danai Kitkungvan, Sriram Nathan, Biswajit Kar, Jagat Narula, Prapannajeet Biswal, Khashayar Fattah, Xander HT Wehrens, Sotirios Nedios, Felix Hohendanner, Joanne Alfieri, Martin L Bernier, Kenneth W Merrell, Konstantinos C Siontis, Alejandro Vidal Margenat, Sunil Krishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStereotactic arrhythmia radioablation (STAR) is a novel and emerging treatment approach for managing treatment-refractory ventricular arrhythmias (VAs), particularly in patients deemed ineligible for or failing antiarrhythmic medications and catheter ablation. Preclinical studies suggest that STAR generates reactive oxygen species and causes rapid ultrastructural changes in cardiomyocytes, with ion channel modulation and gap junction remodeling that result in electrophysiological reprogramming. Several clinical studies have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of STAR, showing a significant reduction in arrhythmia burden with a low risk of severe treatment-related complications. Despite these promising results, the recurrence rate reported is still high, especially in patients with advanced heart failure. The convergence of advancements and refinements in procedural workflows, such as noninvasive electrocardiographic imaging, respiratory and cardiac motion management strategies, and the seamless integration of multiple radiographic imaging modalities for radiation treatment planning, promises to enhance treatment delivery accuracy and precision. However, questions remain regarding the optimal dose regimens, patient selection criteria, and potential long-term toxicities. Ongoing clinical trials and preclinical studies will provide further insights that can help improve the current practice. STAR is currently considered investigational pending randomized controlled trials comparing it with standard therapies. This review critically appraises STAR’s readiness for broader clinical adoption, examining molecular mechanisms, treatment planning and delivery strategies, clinical outcomes and their heterogeneity, safety concerns, emerging beam technologies, and practical considerations for multidisciplinary teams establishing new STAR programs.Keywords: Cardiac stereotactic body radiation therapy, Ventricular tachycardia, Arrhythmia, Radioablation, EAM integration

Introduction

Cardiac arrhythmias encompass a spectrum of clinical states with irregular fast or slow heartbeats ranging from mild to severe, life-threatening conditions. They arise from several mechanisms: reentry,1 triggered activity, and automaticity,2 with the reentry mechanism being the most common kind, typically seen in patients with structural heart disease such as postischemic scar formation.2 VAs span a spectrum from benign premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) to life-threatening arrhythmias such as ventricular tachycardia (VT) and ventricular fibrillation (VF). In the United States, a study on trends in mortality owing to VT/VF from 1999 to 2022 revealed a total of more than 320,000 deaths, with 10% of the deaths occurring in 2021–2022.3

Management of VTs has evolved, with current strategies involving pharmacotherapy2,4 with antiarrhythmic agents, implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs), and invasive procedures such as catheter ablation (CA).2 Although antiarrhythmic drugs are commonly used as an adjunctive therapy to reduce ICD shocks, their effectiveness is variable, and long-term treatment is often hobbled by intractable side effects.5,6 ICDs do not prevent arrhythmias; however, they have proven benefits in the primary and secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death by terminating life-threatening arrhythmias.2,7

CA is a commonly used minimally invasive procedure for the management of VT.2,8 It is particularly effective in scar-related ischemic VA, with overall success rates of 56%–77%, although the long-term outlook is more measured owing to recurrence of VT episodes and the need for ICD shocks.2,8, 9, 10 CA has limitations; for instance, arrhythmogenic foci deep within the myocardium pose a special clinical challenge. The outcomes associated with cardiac ablation for nonischemic cardiomyopathy (NICM) are likely worse because of the complex substrates.11 In a prospective study comparing the outcomes of CA in nonischemic dilated cardiomyopathy (NIDCM) vs ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM), the long-term outcomes at 1-year follow-up showed that the VT-free survival rate in NIDCM was 40.5% compared with 57% in ICM.12 CA is also associated with an overall procedural complication rate of 7.1% and a mortality rate of 3.7% after VT ablation.13 Novel approaches such as pulsed-field ablation (PFA) are being developed to improve ablation to achieve deeper and durable lesions, aiming to reduce VT recurrence and improve efficacy.14

Stereotactic arrhythmia radioablation (STAR) provides a promising and novel, noninvasive approach for managing patients with treatment-refractory VT who have failed antiarrhythmic medications or CA or those considered high risk for CA of VT.15, 16, 17 Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) is a precise radiation therapy modality in which high doses are delivered to an extracranial target, typically in 1–5 fractions.18 Sharma et al19 established the feasibility of noninvasive cardiac SBRT in 16 swine, evaluating doses ranging from 25 to 80 Gy, where 25 Gy was needed to produce an electrophysiological (EP) effect, and a single fraction of 40 Gy resulted in a bidirectional conduction block at the cavotricuspid isthmus. Targeted heart regions exhibited marked voltage reduction within 3 months, without off-target injury.

The first-in-human treatment involved a patient with refractory VT treated with 25 Gy STAR to the VT substrate, which showed a 7-month reduction in VT with no definite acute or late toxicity.20 Subsequently, early clinical experiences, including case series and a prospective phase I/II trial, have shown evidence of the safety and feasibility of STAR, highlighting its promise as an emerging therapy for refractory VT.15,17,21 A recent meta-analysis of prospective trials on STAR showed that the 1-year freedom from recurrence was 0.30 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.16–0.49) in 61 patients.17 However, STAR for refractory VT is still investigational, lacks randomized control trial data, has limited long-term outcome data, and concerns remain regarding recurrences and efficacy in patients with advanced heart failure.22, 23, 24 A recent European Heart Rhythm Association survey assessing the current role, barriers to application, and future role of STAR revealed an increase in acceptance among cardiology and subspecialized EP communities. The survey also highlights the need for randomized trials to assess efficacy and define a suitable patient population.25 This review critically appraises STAR’s clinical readiness, examining mechanisms, treatment planning, outcomes, safety, emerging technologies, and practical implementation considerations for multidisciplinary teams establishing new STAR programs.

Mechanisms of action of STAR in arrhythmias

The antiarrhythmic effects of STAR are multifaceted (see Figure 1); although the initial hypothesis focused on radiation-induced fibrosis, in their review on mechanistic insights of STAR, Balaji et al26 suggest that STAR results in early effects from days to weeks through electrical reprogramming and late effects emerging from months to years from long-term structural remodeling, resulting in sustained protection against arrhythmias. Notably, mechanistic understanding is predominantly derived from animal models, which may not accurately represent human pathophysiology.

Early effects: EP reprogramming

The early clinical benefits of STAR are best explained by EP reprogramming of the myocardium as opposed to fibrosis, which usually occurs late in the course of treatment. The early biological cascade of events after high-dose radiation of 25 Gy on murine ventricles is evidenced by a rise in γ-H2AX foci, indicating DNA damage, which largely resolves via the DNA repair mechanism by 24 hours.27 These effects also result in alterations at the molecular, protein, and functional levels.28, 29, 30 A study in rat models evaluating proteomic changes in cardiac tissues after 25 Gy revealed considerable proteomic changes in key cardiac proteins within 1 week, affecting proteins involved in cellular stress response, endomembranal organization, and cardiac conduction, supporting a mechanistic basis for early antiarrhythmic effects.29

Zhang et al27 evaluated the impact of single-fraction 25 Gy radiation on the myocardium of murine hearts. They observed increased conduction velocities and upregulation of cardiac conduction proteins, including the voltage-gated sodium channel (Nav1.5) and connexin 43 (Cx43). Transcriptomic analysis performed at 2 and 6 weeks identified the Notch signaling pathway as a key contributor to this effect, with differential expression observed for Jag1, Jag2, and Notch2, suggesting a molecular mechanism for the persistent upregulation of Nav1.5. These molecular changes resulted in reprogramming the electrical substrate with persistently increased conduction velocities, effectively reducing susceptibility to reentrant arrhythmias. When comparing the effects of different radiation doses, the most significant cellular impact was observed at a dose of 25 Gy. However, lower doses of 20 and 15 Gy also demonstrated measurable biological effects.27

Radiation-induced changes in the expression and localization of gap junctions play a vital role in antiarrhythmic effects.27,31,32 Amino et al31 demonstrated that targeted heavy ion radiation (15 Gy) in rabbit myocardial infarction models led to the upregulation of Cx43, thereby improving electrical conduction and reducing arrhythmia susceptibility. In a follow-up study, Amino et al32 observed sustained Cx43 expression in rabbit hearts for up to 1 year after radiation, suggesting a long-term conduction remodeling effect.

These changes to the electrical activity machinery at the cellular and subcellular levels in cardiomyocytes result in alterations in conduction velocity and the propagation of electrical action potentials. For instance, Kim et al30 measured the electrical activities of human-induced pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes that received high-dose radiation. In cells treated with doses greater than 20 Gy, conduction velocity transiently decreased between 6 and 12 hours but returned to normal by 24 hours. After this initial 24-hour period, cells that received 20 Gy or 25 Gy showed a steady increase in conduction velocity over time. Similarly, Cha et al33 examined EP changes after high-dose radiation on rat hearts at varying time points after radiation. The predominant findings were conductive delay and prominent ST-segment changes. Dovetailing these findings, Zhang et al27 also reported that, 6 weeks after 25 Gy, mice show QRS narrowing and increased conduction velocity.

In addition to sodium channels and Cx43 levels, potassium and calcium channels are also affected after STAR. Irradiation of neonatal rat ventricular myocytes with 20 Gy in a single-fraction resulted in altered messenger RNA and ion channel levels at 24 and 96 hours after radiation. The most significant changes were seen at a later point. Several calcium and potassium ion channels are upregulated, including Cacna1c (Cav1.2), Kcnd3 (Kv4.3), Kcnh2 (Kv11.1), Kcnq1 (Kv7.1), Kcnk2 (K2P2.1), Kcnj2 (Kir2.1). There was also an increase in Ryr2 ryanodine receptor 2 and Slc8a1 Na+/Ca2+ exchanger expression at 96 hours, affecting calcium handling.34

To summarize the early effects, STAR results in EP reprogramming through the modulation of ion channels and gene expression, with an increase in gap junction proteins, such as Cx43, resulting in EP changes, including alterations in conduction velocity. However, there is still a lot to be understood about the early mechanisms of STAR.

Late effects: Structural remodeling

The late effects of STAR are seen months to years after treatment and are likely caused by the structural remodeling of the cardiac tissue as opposed to EP reprogramming. Depending on the dose, ionizing radiation can lead to chronic biological effects in cardiac tissues, which are caused by several factors, including oxidative damage, chronic inflammation, metabolic reprogramming, and cellular senescence. Late effects typically involve the activation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, followed by the deposition of collagen in the extracellular matrix. These processes are triggered by several factors, such as transforming growth factors, interleukins, and connective tissue growth factors.35 Cha et al33 reported intercalated disc widening, extracellular edema, and diffuse mitochondrial damage as the predominant histologic findings in hearts of rat models within 1 month after high-dose radiation. A preclinical study using high-energy carbon ions with doses of 25, 40, and 55 Gy delivered to cardiac tissue in pigs observed a complete blockage of cardiac impulse propagation 13–17 weeks after high doses (40–55 Gy) were administered. There were minor fibrotic changes after a dose of 25 Gy, whereas a strong fibrotic response was seen in 40–55 Gy.36 Preclinical studies in swine models have shown myocardial changes after proton radiation, including changes in conduction-related and fibrosis-related gene expression.37

Human histologic correlation is limited to small explant series. In hearts retrieved 12–250 days after 25 Gy STAR, Kiani et al38 described subendocardial necrosis with a rim of fibrosis, myointimal thickening suggestive of microvascular remodeling, and disrupted myofibrils on electron microscopy—distinct from the acute coagulative necrosis of radiofrequency (RF) ablation; in parallel tissue from 4 STAR patients, Zhang et al27 found only minor fibrotic differences between irradiated and nonirradiated regions and less fibrosis than after CA. Together, these data suggest a dose-dependent biology whose late histologic signature is still incompletely defined.

STAR: Treatment workflow and practical considerations

Cardiorespiratory motion management

Cardiorespiratory motion management is a crucial part of accurate planning and delivery of STAR. Proper positioning and motion management are required for reproducible setup for treatment and to address the complex challenges of cardiorespiratory motion. Patient simulation is typically performed in a supine position, with the arms extended over the patient’s head and a custom immobilization cradle placed under the arms. To minimize distortions from varying levels of gastric filling, it is customary to simulate patients on an empty stomach for at least 3–4 hours. Motion mitigation strategies can also be used during simulation and delivery to minimize the planning target volume (PTV) and increase the accuracy of ablative radiation therapy. 4-dimensional (4D) computed tomography (CT) and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can assess the motion and generate an internal target volume (ITV). Techniques such as breath hold, abdominal compression, and respiratory gating can be used to mitigate motion uncertainties.

The relative contributions of cardiac and respiratory motion to target volume margins are patient and target specific. Using 4D cardiac CT in 31 patients, Wu et al39 measured intracardiac landmark motion across the cardiac cycle and found displacements ranging from 1.0 to 15.2 mm depending on location and direction, with left ventricular (LV) lead-tip motion averaging 6.0 ± 2.2 mm in the anterior-posterior direction, comparable with or exceeding typical respiratory excursion in patients with low LV ejection fraction and abdominal compression. The Standardized Treatment and Outcome Platform for Stereotactic Therapy of Re-entrant Tachycardia by a Multidisciplinary Consortium (STOPSTORM) digital-phantom study by Stevens et al40 provided complementary evidence: cardiac contraction–relaxation alone expanded the segmental motion envelope by 49% (range 24%–79%), and the addition of population-average respiratory motion brought the total expansion to 126%, with the diastolic-plus-respiratory envelope the surrogate ordinarily reconstructed from a respiratory-only 4D-CT covering only 68%–75% of the true cardiorespiratory envelope. Free-wall and lateral targets exhibit the greatest cardiorespiratory displacement, whereas septal targets are relatively stable. Another retrospective study of motion and dosimetry analysis in a cohort of patients who underwent STAR observed that cardiac clinical target volume motion was, on average, below 3 mm, was location dependent, and may affect the eventual dosimetry.41 In intensity-modulated proton planning, selection of beam angles significantly affected target coverage and respiratory motion contributed to dose uncertainty greater than that of the heartbeat based on the 4D dynamic dose evaluation.42

Beyond ITV expansion, complementary strategies are emerging to mitigate this motion. Magnetic resonance–guided linear accelerator (MR linac)–based dual gating combines real-time cine-MRI tracking with multileaf-collimator tracking for respiratory motion and cardiac-cycle beam gating, greatly reducing motion-induced dosimetric uncertainty.43 Using a 3-dimensional (3D) computational electrocardiographic (ECG) algorithm, Ho et al44 performed ECG mapping with protocol-guided respiratory gating, resulting in significantly smaller PTVs (71 ± 7 mL) than non–respiratory-gated PTVs (153 ± 35 mL; P < .01). Reis et al45 used real-time ECG acquisition and demonstrated the feasibility of synchronized gating for treatment delivery in STAR. An experimental demonstration by Akdag et al46 showed that real-time ECG-based gating on an MR linac is feasible, reduces residual motion and dose deposited outside the PTV, and improves treatment delivery. Transthoracic ultrasound–guided cardiorespiratory dual gating, recently demonstrated for proton STAR, uses a hands-free echocardiography probe with marked chest positions to feed dual cardiorespiratory gating to the treatment-planning system without compromising beam-angle freedom.47 These techniques highlight the feasibility of real-time cardiac physiology–based gating. These approaches are not yet standardized and are not widely available; the choice between them also depends on target location, beam modality, available platform (linac, MR linac, or proton gantry), and the patient’s ability to tolerate breath hold or compression.

Integration with EP mapping and imaging studies (MRI and positron emission tomography)

Electroanatomic mapping

Identifying electrical activity in the heart with electroanatomic mapping (EAM) helps define the nature and substrate location of arrhythmias, which is essential for accurately delivering ablative radiation. Advancements in integrating 3D mapping with radiographic imaging have enabled accurate target delineation. Advanced high-density EAM systems are beneficial in complex arrhythmias, given that they enable the rapid acquisition of large numbers of data points, producing highly detailed EP maps. This allows the identification of even small areas of arrhythmogenic tissue that might be overlooked in traditional EP mapping. Examples of these advanced EAM systems evaluated in recent clinical studies include CARTO (Biosense Webster, Johnson & Johnson), EnSite (Abbott), and RHYTHMIA (Boston Scientific).48,49 Recent advancements, such as noninvasive panoramic mapping, combine surface electrodes with CT imaging to generate 3D EP maps, avoiding invasive catheter-based mapping.50

Noninvasive mapping

ECG imaging (ECGI) offers unique value for STAR. By reconstructing epicardial electrical activity from body-surface potentials using patient-specific cardiac-torso geometry derived from CT, ECGI can identify VT exit sites and characterize the substrate noninvasively.51 Although ECGI is noninvasive and convenient, it has been used rarely, as reported by current European STAR practice patterns from the STOPSTORM consortium.52 Wider clinical adoption of ECGI into STAR’s workflow in the future would complement STAR’s noninvasive nature and benefit patients who are not candidates for invasive mapping.

Like CA, multimodal imaging is essential for characterizing the arrhythmia substrate, and its integration with EAM is crucial for precise targeting of the arrhythmia. Cardiac CT, MRI, single-photon emission CT, and positron emission tomography can be integrated with EAM and planning CT for target delineation. By highlighting hypometabolic and hypoperfused areas in the myocardium, single-photon emission CT/positron emission tomography helps locate the scar.53 Differences in gadolinium enhancement in cardiac MRI help differentiate healthy myocardium from scar tissue in the case of NICM. The substrate may vary based on etiology; multimodal imaging helps characterize the substrate.54

EAM integration: Challenges and pitfalls

A persistent challenge in integrating EAM information into radiotherapy treatment-planning software is the incompatibility of the imaging datasets, given that simulation CT scans are typically in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. In contrast, most EAM datasets are in .xml or similar non-DICOM formats. This necessitates the creation of custom (often using open-source software offline) workflows for seamless integration of datasets. Brett et al55 described a process to convert catheter-based EAM into a DICOM-compatible format, thereby facilitating EAM integration with planning CT. Another challenge is identifying substrates in NICMs compared with ICMs, where the scar is more easily identifiable. In these cases, the substrate is distributed throughout the midmyocardium or subepicardium, making localization by noninvasive techniques difficult.56

Abdel-Kafi et al57,58 assessed the accuracy and pitfalls of EAM integration with CT by evaluating interobserver variability in localizing gross target volume (GTV). Their analysis showed that manual optimization can achieve high surface registration accuracy, especially when at least 3 chambers are mapped. Nonetheless, substantial differences in GTV size and the nonoverlapping GTV percentage were observed in the lateral wall areas, with the midseptal area displaying the highest 2-dimensional surface shift. Another challenge with target volume delineation through EAM is access to a previous EAM. The VT mapped during CA often differs from that present at the time of STAR. Thus, targeting the precise point of origin of the VT based on a previous EAM map leads to spatial inaccuracies. Most often, the recurrent VT originates from adjacent areas within the same scar; therefore, a scar-based targeting approach is probably more appropriate and may also address potential future VT that could arise from within the scar. These challenges with integrating structural and functional information from previous scans and EP mapping studies must be addressed to improve treatment and reduce heterogeneity in results between centers.

Target volume and critical structure delineation

Target volume delineation involves identifying an arrhythmogenic substrate from EAM and other available imaging modalities and integrating these structure sets with planning CT images. Brownstein et al59 developed an atlas for STAR that uses the American Heart Association 17-segment model, providing detailed, step-by-step instructions for reorienting the heart from conventional orthogonal views to cardiac-specific orientations. This enables better communication among the cardiologist, electrophysiologist, and radiation oncologist regarding the target. The target identified by EAM is then transferred and integrated into the planning CT. However, manual transfer methods are limited and prone to errors; therefore, automated and user-independent systems are required to minimize these errors.60

Consensus is still lacking on the optimal target volume for STAR, particularly regarding whether to include only the arrhythmia exit site or the entire arrhythmogenic substrate.61 In the case of CA, a study comparing the ablation of clinical VT with mappable VT with the ablation of the substrate showed better outcomes in the latter.62 The size of the PTV may influence both treatment outcomes and toxicity. Qian et al63 noted a modest correlation between larger PTV size and less favorable VT reduction. Another study evaluating the recurrence pattern observed that recurrences occur outside the PTV in all patients who underwent CA after recurrence. This suggests that a more expansive clinical target volume encompassing the arrhythmogenic substrate could improve the outcomes.61 A semiautomated workflow for target volume delineation involving multimodal fusion for greatly improved and efficient target delineation was described by Rigal et al.64 Similarly, a multicenter cross-validation study of 2 semiautomated reproducible target transfer methods, that is, 2-dimensional–3D and 3D–3D registration, showed nearly identical 3D structures. These automated systems may help standardize workflow and reduce errors in the future.65

A benchmark study on critical structure contouring from the STOPSTORM.eu consortium centers, where 31 organs at risk (OARs), including thoracic and abdominal structures, in 3 STAR cases showed larger disagreement in cardiac substructures. The consortium established consensus guidelines to standardize the delineation of OARs.66 Another STOPSTORM study on autocontouring models for cardiac substructures shows that models trained on data from patients with VT can create contours with accuracy matching that of manual observers. Although the contour variation of valves and coronary arteries remains large, autocontouring drastically reduces the time taken for contouring.67

STAR planning and treatment delivery

As supported by several preclinical and clinical reports, a single-fraction 25 Gy dose to the PTV is the preferred dose regimen for STAR. A key consideration in treatment planning is to limit radiation dose to adjacent critical normal structures. Therefore, careful dose planning is essential to balance therapeutic efficacy with minimizing potential toxicity to surrounding structures, guided by available single-fraction dose constraints.68 However, there are limited data on the correlation between doses and cardiac substructures, as well as toxicities. Several retrospective studies evaluating thoracic SBRT in patients with lung cancer have suggested a possible association between higher doses to the superior vena cava, the left atrium,69 and pulmonary artery68 and an increased risk of noncancer death.

A volumetric modulated arc therapy with filter-free delivery of 6 MV photons is used most commonly for treatment delivery. Higher photon energy beams are typically avoided to minimize neutron production, especially in the presence of ICDs that may malfunction owing to neutron activation of the boron within their electronic circuitry.70 OAR constraints are usually guided by those established for single-fraction ablative radiotherapy, such as the comprehensive American Association of Physicists in Medicine TG-101 guidelines covering SBRT across various anatomic sites, as well as the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group 0915 protocol used for non–small cell lung cancer.71,72

In a multicenter STOPSTORM treatment-planning benchmarking study involving 67 treatment plans from 20 centers, dose prescription was mainly based on PTV D95% (49%) or D96%–100% (19%). There was a strong agreement in consensus statements for several aspects of planning, including trade-offs between target and extracardiac critical structures. However, no agreement was reached on the dose inhomogeneity in the target and dose limits for cardiac structures.73

Dose optimization: Is 25 Gy the right dose?

The 25 Gy single-fraction prescription has become the standard for STAR, originating from Sharma et al’s19 swine studies demonstrating EP effects at this dose level and subsequently adopted in the first-in-human treatment by Loo et al20 and later by Cuculich et al,15 whereas preclinical studies suggest measurable biological effects at lower doses of 15 and 20 Gy27 and likely dose-response relationship causing conduction block and fibrosis at higher doses.19,36 However, clinical data regarding optimal dose are limited with ongoing trials directly addressing this question. DEFT-STAR (NCT05594368) escalates the ITV dose from 27 Gy to an ITV-equivalent dose of up to 35.1 Gy while maintaining PTV coverage at 25 Gy.74 Conversely, StAR-VT evaluates de-escalation to 20 Gy, with preliminary results in 5 patients suggesting maintained efficacy.75 These trials will determine whether personalized dose prescriptions based on substrate characteristics and target location are warranted.

Practical considerations for establishing a STAR program

Implementing a STAR program requires coordinated infrastructure and a defined multidisciplinary team. STAR is a technically demanding procedure in which multiple imaging modalities and EAMs must be integrated for accurate target delineation, followed by comprehensive planning and plan evaluation before delivery. Patient selection

A multidisciplinary panel of radiation oncologists, cardiologists, and electrophysiologists strongly agreed that potentially eligible candidates for STAR include patients with structural heart disease and recurrent monomorphic VT or electrical storm.76 Patient selection and contraindications are comprehensively addressed in the 2025 European Heart Rhythm Association/Heart Rhythm Society Clinical Consensus Statement.77 Because efficacy and long-term safety data remain limited to small, largely retrospective series, the consensus advises that STAR be delivered within an approved investigational trial, or as an alternative strategy under institutional approval, in patients with monomorphic VT or electrical storm refractory to or unsuitable for dose-escalated antiarrhythmic drugs and CA performed at an expert center.76,77

Workflow, data transfer, and quality assurance

Centers should establish a standard operating procedure for EAM data transfer, given that format incompatibilities between EP and radiation oncology systems remain a persistent challenge. Semiautomated target transfer methods, as validated in the RAVENTA multicenter studies, can reduce transfer errors and improve reproducibility.65 Equally important, programs should complete end-to-end dosimetric commissioning and phantom-based credentialing before treating patients: multicenter benchmarking by the STOPSTORM consortium revealed meaningful intercenter variability in treatment planning and motivated consensus planning recommendations,73 and a STOPSTORM survey documented wide heterogeneity in workflow and dosimetry across European centers.52

ICD/cardiac implantable electronic device safe-dose thresholds

Another concern with high-dose radiation is its effect on the functioning of the ICD and potential issues that might arise. Because most STAR candidates have ICDs, radiation-related device malfunction is an important safety consideration. STAR-specific cardiac implantable electronic device dose constraints are not well established, so practice is generally adapted from the cancer radiotherapy literature, particularly the American Association of Physicists in Medicine TG-203 framework,78 along with the 2017 Heart Rhythm Society Expert Consensus.79 TG-203 stratifies cardiac implantable electronic device risk by cumulative incident dose to the generator and by pacing dependence: a generator dose of ≤2 Gy is generally considered low risk for non–pacing-dependent patients, 2–5 Gy intermediate, and >5 Gy high risk; neutron-producing beams (>10 MV photons or protons) are always high risk regardless of dose.78 ICDs are particularly susceptible to neutron-induced single-event upsets.70 In practice, single-fraction STAR delivered with 6 MV flattening-filter-free photons keeps the ICD-generator dose well below these limits. A retrospective multicenter study evaluated the impact of STAR on ICD function in 43 patients with therapy-resistant VT. The median dose of the ICD-generator reported was 0.1 Gy. STAR did not result in clinically significant changes in ICD lead parameters or radiation-related adverse events, except for 1 device reset case.80 Standard precautions include pre- and posttreatment device interrogation, temporary suspension of ICD tachytherapies during beam delivery, continuous monitoring, and immediate availability of external defibrillation and device reprogramming.

Posttreatment surveillance

A structured follow-up protocol should include regular ICD interrogation and echocardiogram monitoring for pericardial effusion, valvular changes, and LV function; symptom monitoring for gastrointestinal complaints given fistula risk; and annual cardiac imaging for late effects. Programs should also capture patient-reported outcomes and quality-of-life measures alongside arrhythmia metrics. Given the latency of radiation-related cardiac injury, follow-up extending beyond 5 years is prudent.

Cost considerations

Although a head-to-head comparison with CA remains challenging, STAR offers significant cost-saving potential. A time-driven activity-based costing analysis revealed that STAR’s direct and total procedural costs were 49% and 54% lower than those of CA.81 This might facilitate institutional adoption.

Clinical outcomes

Although no randomized clinical trial comparing STAR with other treatment options has been completed, several studies reporting the clinical outcomes of STAR have been published over the past few years, with several reports demonstrating its efficacy in reducing VT burden and improving quality of life (see Table 1). However, current evidence consists predominantly of single-arm observational studies in heterogeneous populations without randomized comparison.

Reduction in VT episodes and ICD shocks

Several prospective studies and case series have assessed VT burden after a blanking period, a predefined timeframe after treatment during which arrhythmic events do not indicate treatment failure. This approach helps differentiate actual treatment failure from a late therapeutic response, allowing for a more accurate evaluation of efficacy. In their pioneering work on STAR, Cuculich et al’s15 5-patient series first established STAR’s feasibility in refractory VT, demonstrating a 99.9% reduction in VT episodes over 46 patient-months after a 6-week blanking period. The initial experience from the UK multicenter analysis of 7 patients with recurrent VT showed acute suppression of VT episodes. In 5 patients with at least 6 months of follow-up, the study reported an 85% reduction in VT burden.16 Similarly, a phase I/II trial involving 19 patients (17 diagnosed as having VT and 2 having PVC) noted a marked decrease in the median number of VT episodes from 119 (range 4–292) in the 6-month pretreatment period to 3 (range 0–31; P < .001) in the 6-month posttreatment period. VT episodes or PVC burden was reduced in 17 of 18 evaluable patients (94%). The antiarrhythmic effect was observed within 6 weeks in most patients, characterized by a decrease in ICD shocks and reduced use of antiarrhythmic medication.21 These results suggest that an early EP reprogramming immediately reduces VT burden and ICD shocks after the blanking period.

Long-term efficacy and recurrence

Studies have also demonstrated the long-term efficacy of STAR for the sustained suppression of VT episodes. In a cohort of high-risk patients where 14 underwent treatment, an analysis of the 12 patients showed a 98% reduction in VT episodes from 6 weeks to 6 months, which increased to a 99% reduction between 6 and 12 months. In addition, there was a trend toward reduced amiodarone dosage over 1 year, suggesting a potential ability to decrease dependence on antiarrhythmic medications.84 The Dutch prospective, single-arm, pre-post intervention study, STARNL-1, also reported sustained long-term efficacy at the end of follow-up, with at least a 50% reduction in VT episodes in 67% of patients.90 Aras et al93 reported a decrease in VT episodes and ICD shocks in 8 patients, with the most favorable outcomes observed from 2 weeks to 3 months after radiation; however, all patients experienced recurrences on follow-up. In the study by Chang et al,89 which had a median follow-up of 12.3 months, 3 of the 6 patients achieved complete remission of VT, 2 achieved partial remissions, and 1 experienced a VT recurrence at 10 months that was successfully suppressed with medical treatment.

The results of the RAVENTA trial have shown the short-term safety of STAR treatment with mild side effects in the 20 patients who received 25 Gy in a single fraction, with 14 of 15 patients VT-free 12 months after treatment, accounting for a total local efficacy (freedom from index VT) of 73.7% (14 of 19). These results emphasize the efficacy of STAR; however, longer follow-up is needed.101

Although the initial studies evaluating efficacy and safety showed promising results,15 recent reports have shown a moderate reduction in VT burden and a reduction in device shocks.24,95 This difference could be caused by differences in approaches targeting arrhythmogenic substrates, PTV volume, and other patient-specific factors. In a group of 10 patients with advanced heart failure, STAR demonstrated moderate efficacy compared with other reports, with a 69% reduction in VT burden and a 68% reduction in ICD shocks. However, censoring a nonresponder showed a significant improvement in VT burden and a potential decrease in ICD shocks.24

A meta-analysis of pooled data from 10 prospective trials on STAR by Miszczyk et al,17 involving 82 patients from 2016 to 2022, showed a significant reduction in VT burden, although 1-year recurrence rates were high. In evaluable patients, the probability of achieving a ≥95% reduction in VT burden was 61% (95% CI, 45–74). 1-year freedom from recurrence was 0.30 (95% CI, 0.16–0.49). Early treatment-related moderate to severe toxicity (grade ≥3) within 90 days was reported in 10% of patients. Despite VT reduction, 1-year overall survival was 73% (95% CI, 61–83), underscoring that outcomes are constrained by advanced heart failure/comorbidity and that attributing mortality to STAR vs disease progression remains challenging.17 Notably, high late recurrences in most patients represent the current limitation of STAR. Possible explanations include inadequate target coverage, transient EP effects without structural modification, or progressive disease of the substrate.

Recurrence location pattern

The pattern of VT recurrence after STAR carries direct implications for whether late failures reflect a dosimetric/margin problem or progression of the underlying cardiomyopathy. In a Swiss STAR cohort reported by Herrera Siklody et al,61 among 20 patients with a median 25-month follow-up, 12 developed sustained VT recurrence, prompting redo invasive mapping, but no recurrence occurred within the PTV. 9 of the recurrences were in proximity to the treated substrate, 3 were remote from the PTV, and 2 involved a diffuse new substrate spanning ≥3 LV segments. Voltage mapping after STAR demonstrated a significant decrease in both bipolar and unipolar signal amplitudes within the irradiated region.61 Taken together, these data suggest that STAR can achieve effective local substrate modification, whereas late VT recurrence often arises from adjacent or remote arrhythmogenic substrate.

STAR efficacy concerns and challenges

This apparent variability in the effectiveness of STAR likely reflects significant variability in patient populations, substrate characteristics, and treatment-planning strategies. In populations with advanced heart failure and complex substrates, improvements in VT burden are generally modest. The differences in clinical outcomes are further confounded by differences in target definition and treatment volumes between institutions. For example, overly broad target definitions aimed at extensive scar homogenization can result in massive treatment volumes and suboptimal VT control, whereas overly conservative treatment volumes may not adequately cover the entire arrhythmia substrate. Despite this variability, the most consistent finding across trials is the marked decrease in VT burden and ICD therapies for VT. However, there is a significant decrease in long-term freedom from recurrence at 1 year, likely secondary to structural changes in the heart, inadequate initial substrate coverage, or the emergence of new arrhythmia substrates.

In the LV assist device (LVAD) setting, STAR has been delivered without acute device-related complications and without compromising subsequent surgical dissection at transplantation, supporting its use as a palliative, last-resort antiarrhythmic option to control refractory VAs while bridging to transplantation or in patients with destination-therapy LVADs with multimodal imaging mitigation of LVAD artifacts.102 Another retrospective study of 14 patients with advanced heart failure, including transplant candidates, reported an immediate reduction of VT burden after STAR and treatment-related acute adverse events. However, long-term follow-up showed late recurrences requiring ablation in patients who survived and did not receive heart transplants. The reported net reduction in VT was 59%, and the reduction in shock frequency was 60%.95 These findings highlight the need for further research to understand patient selection and optimize the workflow of STAR, especially in high-risk groups like those with advanced heart failure. The use of STAR in advanced heart failure and critically ill patients also poses ethical challenges, given the limited long-term outcomes data in this patient population.

Acute and late toxicities

Radiation-induced cardiac toxicities have been reported after radiation therapy for lung, esophageal, and breast cancers, where the heart is considered an OAR that needs to be spared. Long-term follow-up from mediastinal radiotherapy for Hodgkin lymphoma and breast cancer has documented toxicities such as coronary events, valvular defects, and restrictive cardiomyopathies typically manifesting years after fractionated radiation delivered over several weeks at 1.8–2 Gy per fraction. For instance, in a study by Darby et al,103 patients with breast cancer who received radiation therapy showed a proportional relationship between mean cardiac radiation dose and the risk of ischemic heart disease, with major coronary events increasing by 7.4% per Gy of mean heart dose. In STAR, the arrhythmogenic substrate in the heart is the target, with a single ablative fraction delivered to a small, focused target volume rather than incidental whole-heart irradiation. However, limited data exist on cardiac toxicities. Moreover, adjudication of radiation-related toxicity from underlying disease progression is challenging, particularly in critically ill patients. Nevertheless, the absence of long-term follow-up in most STAR cohorts means that late coronary, valvular, and conduction toxicities cannot yet be excluded, underscoring the need for structured cardiac surveillance extending well beyond 5 years.

STAR is generally safe, with a low rate of reported grade 3/4 serious adverse events (SAEs) directly attributed to STAR. Although most studies have shorter-term follow-up data, a longer follow-up is still needed. In a single-institutional, retrospective study on 43 patients with recurrent VT who had failed medical management with antiarrhythmic drugs and failed CA at least once or were medically unfit for CA, 22 received STAR and 21 received repeat CA. With a median follow-up of 3 years, their results show that more CA patients (n = 8 [38%]) developed 1-year treatment-related SAEs than STAR (n = 2 [9%]), whereas the VT control was comparable.82 Ho et al98 observed no severe treatment-related acute adverse events, with 1 case of grade 1 pericardial effusion among 7 patients.

Some reports note acute toxicities such as mild nausea100 and fatigue.89 Reported severe adverse events (grade ≥3) remain low but include heart failure exacerbation, pericarditis, and pneumonitis, emphasizing the importance of precise dosing.17,63,88,95 Robinson et al21 reported treatment-related SAEs, such as heart failure, pericarditis, and pericardial effusion. Another study evaluating the safety outcomes in a cohort comprising 36 patients reported progression of mitral valve regurgitation in 8 patients (3 of them requiring intervention), 2 cases of esophagitis, and 1 death owing to esophagopericardial fistula.87 However, there is considerable heterogeneity in adverse event reporting, with variations in how studies classify and document toxicity. Standardization of adverse event reporting and follow-up protocols would be necessary for an improved and better assessment of toxicities and to differentiate treatment-related vs non–treatment-related toxicities. These acute and late toxicities also highlight the ethical considerations while offering STAR as a treatment option, given that long-term efficacy and safety data are limited. Currently, STAR can be provided to selective patients in a research protocol setting, and efficacy and toxicity concerns are clearly explained with informed consent.

Novel technologies and approaches

Although STAR with photon radiotherapy has been well studied, evidence for particle therapy remains sparse. Proton therapy offers several physical advantages with its unique dose distribution properties (no exit dose beyond the target, sharp lateral penumbra of the incident proton beam) that enable lower OAR doses and likely reduce toxicities. Several preclinical, planning, and early clinical reports have addressed the feasibility of such treatment.104, 105, 106, 107 Building on these, the first prospective early-feasibility study of catheter-free proton cardiac radioablation for refractory VT reported feasibility, a substantial reduction in VT events, and no definite evidence of treatment-related serious toxicity.108 However, STAR treatment using proton therapy presents with distinct challenges, mainly owing to cardiorespiratory motion, which introduces dose uncertainties and complicates radiation delivery. A dosimetry study by Wei et al,109 evaluating the effect of cardiorespiratory motion in intensity-modulated proton therapy, reported dose uncertainties owing to complex cardiorespiratory motion, with respiratory motion contributing more significantly to the interplay effect. Owing to the high sensitivity of protons to motion-related dose variations, respiratory gating is essential for accurate treatment delivery in proton therapy. A retrospective study comparing proton therapy plans with photon therapy in 34 patients with VT who received photon STAR demonstrated a significant reduction in OAR doses such as heart Dmean (3.6 ± 1.5 Gy[relative biological effectiveness] vs 5.5 ± 2.0 Gy; P < .001) and lungs Dmean (0.6 [0.0–1.9] Gy[relative biological effectiveness] vs 1.2 [0.2–2.6] Gy; P < .001). Both plans achieved comparable target coverage.110 To date, only 1 single-arm clinical trial has evaluated proton therapy for this indication, reporting the feasibility of delivering therapeutic doses to the target with minimal doses to the OARs.111 Further clinical trials are needed to determine whether particle therapy offers advantages over photon therapy in this setting.

Although data remain limited, MRI-guided STAR is gaining attention. The first human case of MR-guided STAR with real-time tracking and beam gating for treatment-refractory sustained VT reported acceptable safety.112 Given that MRI-guided STAR provides real-time images, addresses cardiorespiratory motion challenges, and offers superior soft-tissue delineation, this is an especially promising treatment strategy that can be deployed at centers with this capability.

The competitive landscape for refractory VT management is evolving. Each of the 3 modalities available for scar-related VT has a distinct profile and a distinct stage of evidence (Table 2). RF CA remains the default modality worldwide. It has the most mature evidence base, with decades of clinical use, multiple randomized trials, and a well-characterized complication profile of approximately 7% procedural events.8,10,13,118 Its lesion depth in scarred ventricle is comparatively shallow, approximately 3.8 ± 1.7 mm in swine infarct, and 1-year VT-free survival is highly variable, approximately 50%–70% in mixed cohorts and lower in advanced or refractory nonischemic disease (Prospective Heart Centre of Leipzig VT 40.5% NIDCM; 57% ICM).8,12,114

PFA uses nonthermal irreversible electroporation, and that mechanism is its principal differentiator, relatively myocardium selective with collateral sparing demonstrated most strongly in atrial work,116 while achieving greater scar lesion depth than RF in the swine model (approximately 6.1 ± 1.7 mm).114 Preclinical and early clinical studies suggest that PFA could improve the efficacy and safety of cardiac CA by overcoming the main limitations of current standard-of-care energy sources.119 The first-in-human Ventricular Catheter Ablation Study of a high-voltage focal PFA catheter (26 patients, 6-month endpoint) reported 92% acute success and 81.8% (95% CI 67.1–99.8) freedom from VT/VF or ICD shock.113 However, evidence for PFA remains preliminary, and larger, longer follow-up studies are still needed. Substrate depth, hemodynamic tolerance, previous ablation outcomes, evidence base, and center capability should drive modality selection for any given patient. Defining which patients benefit most from STAR vs emerging catheter technologies will be a critical priority for future studies.

Future directions

STAR remains investigational. Its ability to target deep intramural, septal, or epicardial substrate beyond catheter range is a genuine niche advantage for selected patients, and its noninvasive delivery is uniquely suited to those unable to tolerate an invasive procedure or in whom catheter access has failed.17 However, across pooled prospective trials, approximately 80% of treated patients achieve ≥75% VT-burden reduction yet only approximately 30% achieve durable freedom from recurrence at 1 year, the antiarrhythmic effect emerges over days to weeks rather than immediately, accurate targeting still depends on a previous EAM dataset, and the late-toxicity profile (pneumonitis, valvular progression, esophagopericardial fistula) remains incompletely characterized.17 Among the ongoing trials listed in Table 3, RADIATE-VT (NCT05765175) is the most consequential: this multicenter, randomized controlled trial compares STAR directly with repeat CA in patients with high-risk, refractory VT. RADIATE-VT will generate the first randomized evidence comparing STAR to an active comparator, the evidence tier required for clinical adoption beyond the investigational setting.

The widespread adoption of STAR for VAs necessitates addressing multiple challenges. Radiation dose, volume, fractionation, and OAR constraints need to be validated and standardized. There is a need for prospective, randomized trials with uniform cohorts and meticulous assessment of outcomes and toxicity over extended periods; some clinical trials are ongoing (see Table 3). Whether the standard 25 Gy single fraction is optimal remains open; DEFT-STAR (NCT05594368) is testing ITV-equivalent doses up to 35.1 Gy with PTV held at 25 Gy,74 whereas StAR-VT is testing de-escalation to 20 Gy with preliminary efficacy in 5 patients.75 Together, these trials will inform personalized dose prescription.

Adopting emerging technologies, such as artificial intelligence and deep learning algorithms, may facilitate real-time motion tracking, predictive models using multimodal imaging, and EAM integration for prognostication and improved outcomes. Although early preclinical and clinical studies suggest a molecular mechanistic framework to explain the clinical results, much remains unexplained about the true drivers of pathophysiological processes. With a better understanding of the drivers of clinical outcomes and the mechanistic underpinnings of response, we should be able to select the optimal patient for treatment based on biomarkers of response/toxicity, thereby facilitating personalized therapy.

Conclusions

STAR is a promising option for arrhythmia management in patients who are refractory to or ineligible for catheter-based ablations, especially those with deeper arrhythmogenic substrates close to the epicardium, offering the unique advantage of being a noninvasive and safe technique. However, STAR remains investigational, and there is a need for prospective, randomized clinical trials, standardization of protocols, and identification of predictors of outcome. Advancing such a paradigm across institutions, platforms, and workflow routines will require the concerted efforts of multidisciplinary teams of cardiologists, electrophysiologists, radiologists, and radiation oncologists working together to overcome obstacles, rationally triage patients for consideration of STAR, and communicate indications, goals, risks, and alternative treatments while guiding patients in decision making.
