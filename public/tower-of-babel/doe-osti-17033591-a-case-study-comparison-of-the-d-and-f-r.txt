Laboratory Space Weather Summer School. This work was performed under joint mentorship of Dr. Erin

 1. Abstract
 Previous studies have shown that thunderstorms significantly perturb the bottomside ionosphere via
acoustic and gravity waves. In order to further quantify this variability, we use two co-located data collec-
tion techniques. The first uses a newly developed D-region remote sensing technique with data from the
global lightning detection network known as Earth Networks Total Lightning Network (ENTLN). This
measures VLF/LF (very low/low frequency: 30-300 kHz) time waveforms from lightning discharges. We
use the waveform characteristics as a means to probe the D-region (60-90 km) electron density perturba-
tions. We estimate the virtual height of an ionospheric layer from the time difference between the direct
ground wave and the first hop ionospheric reflection wave of negative cloud-to-ground (-CG) lightning
strikes. We then compare these results to data collected simultaneously from a bistatic HF radar technique
using Coastal Ocean Dynamics Applications Radars (CODARs). The frequency-modulated continuous
wave waveforms of these radars can be used to extract group delay, which provides an estimate of the
virtual height of an ionospheric layer in the F-region (90-150 km). The cadence of these observations
is approximately one minute for this investigation. For this study we use three CODAR transmitters lo-
cated along the east coast of the US at a transmission frequency of 5.262 MHz. A single receiver for all
three transmitters is located near Clemson University. F-region variability from the CODAR transmitters
will be compared to D-region data derived from waveforms detected at eleven ENTLN stations across
the Southeastern US. Here we present results from a thunderstorm that produced approximately 300,600
lightning strikes and caused variability in the daytime D-region height of approximately 10 kilometers.
This storm was approximately 800 kilometers in length and stretched across Alabama, Georgia, and
South Carolina on June 15, 2023.

 2. Lightning Basics
 There are two main types of lightning; intra-cloud lightning and cloud-to-ground (CG) lightning. Intra-
cloud refers to a discharge between oppositely charged parts of the cloud, while CG lightning refers to
a discharge between opposite charges in the cloud and on the ground. There are four types of CG:
downward negative, upward negative, downward positive, and upward positive. For this study, only neg-
ative downward CG (-CG) was considered, as it is the most common CG lightning type, accounting for
90% of all CG lightning events. Negative downward CG is defined as negative charges moving from
the cloud to the ground. This occurs when a channel of negative charges from the cloud moves toward
the surface, which in turn causes a positive channel to form on the ground. The two channels connect,
allowing a conductive pathway through which a large vertical current flows. This current produces a ra-
diated electromagnetic (EM) signal across a broadband of frequencies, as well as the optical and acoustic
signal typically associated with lightning (Rakov and Uman, 2003). It is the EM signal in the VLF/LF
frequencies (3-300 kHz) that we analyze below.

 3. ENTLN
 Earth Networks Total Lightning Network (ENTLN) is a global lightning detection network consisting
of thousands of detectors. These sensors detect, locate, classify the type (e.g., CG or in-cloud) and
finally characterize the strike, i.e., the peak current of the strike is recorded. Note that in most cases the
same lightning strike is documented at multiple different sensors, allowing for a precise location to be
determined.

 4. Methodology
 As this section is in the appendix of this thesis the discussion of how this technique works will be kept
to a minimum.
 Previous studies have shown that lightning can be used as a VLF/LF transmitter (Lay and Shao,
2011a,b; Lay et al., 2025), to probe the D region ionosphere. The novelty of this technique is the potential
for increased temporal and spatial resolution of D region measurements. The VLF/LF signal produced by
the lightning can propagate to stations anywhere from 300 - 1000 km away (Lay and Shao, 2011a). The
recorded waveforms from the ENTLN are then averaged together based on station and event location to
create a usable waveform. An example of this is shown in Figure 1. The negative peak, labeled as ground
wave, is the received signal from the ground wave. The positive peak, labeled ionospheric reflection, is
the received signal from the sky wave. A schematic of this is shown in Figure 2. Using the time differ-
ence between the timestamps of the ground wave and the sky wave, the virtual height of the ionospheric
layer can be estimated. This assumes simple spherical geometry, propagation at the speed of light, and
mirror-like reflection at the midpoint.

 5. Results
 Though much of this work is still in progress, a few exciting figures are worth noting. This storm
was incredibly strong and produced roughly 300,000 lightning strikes during its entire duration. Unfor-
tunately, there was significantly better lightning data during the day and better CODAR data at night.
However, a few comparisons were able to be made. The first is shown in Figure 4. The panels labeled
left, center, and bottom correspond to the lightning data probing the region of the three boxes shown in
Figure 3. The bottom panel represents the data collected from one of the CODAR stations. The black and
red dots correspond to X and O propagation modes respectively. This initial comparison shows the small
oscillations in the D region caused by the passing thunderstorm, however it also highlights the inadequate
amounts of overlapping data of the D and F region. To address this concern, the left and center boxes
shown in Figure 3 were combined to increase usable data. This combined data set is shown in Figure 5.
By combining the two regions, a nighttime profile could be resolved. It is in this figure that a bump in
height around 6 UT becomes visible. As a final comparison, Figure 6 now shows the nighttime profile
for both the CODAR-derived and lightning-derived heights. The top panel depicts the oscillations in the
F-region heights, the middle panel demonstrates the oscillations in the D-region heights, and the bottom
panel shows a time series of counts of lightning strikes. Though some of the oscillations are similar in
both the top and middle panels, such as those around 3:30 UT and 6 UT, a further modeling study needs
to be performed to validate the similarities. It should also be noted that the variations in the D region
being significantly smaller than the variations in the F region is to be expected.
 6. Conclusions and Future Work
 The purpose of this study was to further quantify the effects severe thunderstorms have on the bot-
tomside ionosphere by comparing two different data collection techniques. After initial investigation, it
was noted that there does appear to be a slight correlation between the variability on the D and F regions
during the given thunderstorm. However, this must be investigated further due to the gaps in both data
sets. As discussed, the lightning data set is better during the day while the CODAR data set is best at
night. Future work on this study could compare the effects of this storm to others similar in magnitude
as well as increasing the data of the F region. With that being said, after this initial study was performed,
additional CODARs and ionosondes were considered which found quite a bit of oscillations in both foF2
and hmF2 parameters, though those results are very preliminary and will not be shown here.
