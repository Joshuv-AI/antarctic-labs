Identification and validation of programmed cell death-related genes in osteosarcoma: inhibiting CIB1 as a promising therapeutic strategy
Authors: Bin Hu, Li Du, Changchun Lu, Dingding Huang, Mingmang Pan, Feng Xue, Yuchun Shen, Liang Ding, Nuo Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study explores programmed cell death (PCD)-related genes in osteosarcoma through bioinformatics and experimental validation.MethodsAnalysis of datasets from the GEO and TCGA databases identified 5,327 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), among which 294 overlapped with PCD-related genes.ResultsLASSO regression analysis identified six hub genes, and five of these (CIB1, CREB3L1, IL6R, TGFβ2, and TNFRSF10C) were further validated in osteosarcoma tissues. Notably, CIB1 exhibited significantly elevated expression and was selected for in-depth analysis. CIB1 downregulation inhibited osteosarcoma cell proliferation and invasion by inducing apoptosis and causing G2/M phase cell cycle arrest, and was associated with increased p53 expression, decreased phosphorylation of Akt and STAT3, and altered BCL2/BAX ratios, suggesting involvement of these pathways in the observed effects.ConclusionsIn vitro experiments confirmed the tumor-suppressive role of CIB1 inhibition. These findings highlight CIB1 as a promising therapeutic target for osteosarcoma treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16336-0.

Background

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumor, predominantly affecting two distinct patient populations: adolescents undergoing rapid growth and adults aged 50 years and older [1–3]. The disease most frequently involves weight-bearing long bones, such as the distal femur and proximal tibia. Patients with localized appendicular tumors have a five-year relative survival rate of approximately 70%, whereas those with metastatic disease face a survival rate below 30%. Although advances in surgical techniques and radiotherapy have improved the management of localized tumors, the control of metastatic spread remains a significant challenge. Consequently, overall survival rates have remained stagnant over the past two decades, with fewer than half of patients surviving beyond 10 years [4]. A major barrier to improving treatment outcomes is the limited understanding of the tumor microenvironment, which hinders the development of effective strategies to prevent osteosarcoma progression and metastasis. Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic targets is essential for enhancing clinical management and patient outcomes [5–6].

Cell death is a fundamental biological process necessary for maintaining organismal homeostasis. Dysregulation of this process can lead to pathological conditions, including cancer. Cell death is broadly classified into accidental cell death (ACD) and PCD. Multiple PCD pathways have been identified, and increasing evidence suggests their involvement in tumor development and progression. Thus, investigating the connection between PCD and osteosarcoma may offer innovative therapeutic avenues. In particular, identifying key biomarkers involved in PCD could facilitate the development of targeted immunotherapies [7].

However, few studies have focused on identifying PCD-related genetic markers specific to osteosarcoma. This study aims to use bioinformatics approaches to identify PCD-associated genes and subsequently select key targets for experimental validation in vivo and in vitro. The goal is to propose novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for the effective treatment of osteosarcoma.

Methods

Data collection and extraction

Gene expression data for both normal and osteosarcoma samples were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The GSE99671 dataset included 18 control samples (non-tumoral paired samples) and 18 osteosarcoma samples, while GSE21257 contained 53 osteosarcoma samples with accompanying clinical prognosis and overall survival (OS) information. Additionally, gene expression data and clinical outcomes for osteosarcoma were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), which comprised 56 samples. In total, this study included data from 145 patients. Batch effects were corrected using the “sva” R package and the ComBat method. The “limma” and “ggplot2” R packages were applied to analyze and visualize gene expression data. DEGs were identified using a t-test, with selection criteria of P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 0.5. Volcano plots were generated using the “ggplot2” and “ggrepel” R packages, and heatmaps were created using the “pheatmap” and “dplyr” R packages.

Identification of PCD-related genes

A previous study [8] identified 1,254 genes associated with PCD. In this study, DEGs were cross-referenced with these PCD-related genes. To identify genes associated with OS in osteosarcoma patients, the “survival” R package was used for univariate Cox regression analysis.

Identification of genes in different cluster subgroups

The “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package was used to perform consensus clustering on osteosarcoma patients based on the expression profiles of 37 prognostic biomarkers. The optimal number of clusters (k) was selected by evaluating cluster stability and performance across a range of k values (2–9), using cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves and delta area plots to guide selection. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using the “survival” R package to evaluate differences in survival between identified clusters. Dimensionality reduction techniques, including UMAP and t-SNE, were applied using the “Umap” and “TSNE” R packages to visualize the distribution of the two subtypes. Differential expression analysis between the two prognostic subtypes was conducted using the “limma” R package.

Development and validation of the prognostic model

To ensure inclusivity and avoid the omission of potentially relevant genes, a relaxed p-value threshold of 0.2 was applied during the initial screening for survival-associated genes. Prognostic significance in the TCGA cohort was further assessed using Cox regression analysis. To identify potential PCD -related genes associated with osteosarcoma prognosis, the LASSO-Cox regression model was employed using the “glmnet” R package. Based on the resulting model, risk scores were calculated using TCGA mRNA expression data. Patients were then stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score, and survival outcomes between the groups were compared. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the PCD signature was performed using the “prcomp” function from the “stats” R package. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the “timeROC,” “survminer,” and “survival” R packages to evaluate model performance. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (60%) and a testing set (40%). LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was used to select the most predictive genes. The final risk score was calculated as: Risk score = Σ (expression_i × coefficient_i). The coefficients for the six genes are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Prognostic evaluation of the risk score

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were used to identify independent risk factors associated with osteosarcoma. Based on their risk scores, patients in the TCGA dataset were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups. DEGs between these two groups were then subjected to functional enrichment analysis. DEGs were identified using a log2-fold change threshold and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, utilizing the “clusterProfiler” package in R. Functional pathway analysis was conducted using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. To further evaluate immune-related characteristics, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the “gsva” R package. This analysis assessed the infiltration levels of immune cells and the activity of immune-related signaling pathways across the two risk groups.

Tumor microenvironment assessment, immune infiltration analysis, and drug sensitivity prediction

To evaluate the tumor microenvironment, ESTIMATE scores—including immune scores, stromal scores, and tumor purity—were calculated using the R package “estimate.” The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to estimate the absolute abundance of 22 immune cell types. Comparisons of immune cell infiltration between clusters were made, and the results were visualized using the “ggpubr” R package. To investigate differences in immune checkpoint expression among clusters, differential expression analysis was conducted using the “limma” package. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was also performed between the clusters using the “survival” R package. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between risk scores and immune checkpoint expression levels. Drug sensitivity predictions were performed using the “pRRophetic” R package, which estimated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for chemotherapeutic agents in both high-risk and low-risk groups.

Human tissues

Osteosarcoma and adjacent normal tissue samples were obtained from the authors’ affiliated hospital. All participating patients had received at least one line of systemic therapy—adjuvant, neoadjuvant, or metastatic—and demonstrated adequate organ function. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the hospital (Approval No. 2022-YS-117), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study cohort consisted of 13 patients diagnosed with osteosarcoma, including 7 males and 6 females (Table 1). The mean age was 13.5 years, with a range from 8 to 17 years. All patients were classified as Enneking stage II. Each case was categorized as high-grade, conventional osteosarcoma. Among them, 8 patients had the osteoblastic subtype, 3 had the fibroblastic subtype, and 2 had the chondroblastic subtype. Tumor localization was predominantly in the tibia or femur (12 cases), while the remaining cases were distributed as follows: distal radius (1 case), proximal femur (1 case), humerus (1 case), proximal fibula (1 case), and rib (1 case). Adjacent normal tissues were histologically confirmed to be tumor-free and collected at least 2 cm from the tumor margin.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue preparation of osteosarcoma and adjacent non-tumorous samples, along with immunostaining, was performed according to the methods described in a previous study [9]. Immunohistochemistry was conducted using a primary antibody against CIB1 (1:100; Proteintech, China, 11823-1-AP) and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Bioshop, Canada, BLA003A). For quantification, three representative areas were randomly selected from each high-magnification image (200×). Integrated optical density (IOD) was calculated using Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, USA).

Cell lines and cell culture

Human osteosarcoma cell lines (U2-OS, MG-63, and 143B) and the normal human osteoblastic cell line hFOB1.19 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Lentiviral transfection

Cells were transduced with lentiviral particles carrying either negative control shRNA (NC-shRNA) or CIB1-targeting shRNA (GeneChem Corporation, Shanghai, China). The control shRNA sequence was 5′-CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCG-3′. The sequences for the CIB1-targeting shRNAs were as follows: CIB1-shRNA1(5′-CAGCCTTAGCTTTGAGGACTT-3′), CIB1-shRNA2 (5′-GCCTTCCGCATCTTTGACTTT-3′), and CIB1-shRNA3 (5′-GTCTGAGATGAAGCAGCTCAT-3′). These sequences were cloned into the pLKO.1-EGFP-Puro vector. Lentiviral transfection was conducted according to a standard protocol [10]. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well. The following day, after confirming cell adherence, a transfection reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and lentiviral particles at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20 were added to the culture medium. After overnight incubation, the medium was replaced 24 h later. Seventy-two hours post-infection, transfection efficiency was assessed by fluorescence microscopy. Cells were then subjected to puromycin (2 µg/mL; Solarbio, Beijing, China) selection to establish stable transfectants. Knockdown efficiency of CIB1 was evaluated via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting.

Cell viability assays

Cell viability following shRNA transfection was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, C0037). Osteosarcoma cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/mL and allowed to adhere. After 24 h of treatment with either shNC or CIB1-shRNA, 10% CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Synergy HT multimode microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, Vermont, USA) to determine cell viability.

Colony formation assay

A total of 500 cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates. After transfection, cells were cultured for 14 days, with media refreshed every two days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet at room temperature for 20 min. The stained cells were rinsed with distilled water and imaged using a stereomicroscope (Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The number of colonies was then counted.

Wound-healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a concentration of 3 × 10⁴ cells per well and cultured until reaching over 90% confluency. A sterile 200 µL pipette tip was used to create linear scratches across the cell monolayer. The wound areas were observed and imaged under a microscope at 40× magnification at 0 h and again at 24 h post-scratch to evaluate cell migration.

Transwell assay

Transwell inserts were pre-coated with 30 µL of diluted Matrigel (Matrigel: DMEM = 1:5; Corning, NY, USA) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ until solidified. For all groups, cells were suspended in serum-free DMEM at a concentration of 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL and seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 500 µL of DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum to act as a chemoattractant. After 18 h of incubation, non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed using a cotton swab. The remaining cells were fixed with methanol for 30 min, stained with crystal violet for 10 min, and visualized under a microscope to assess invasive capability.

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry using PI/RNase staining buffer (C1052, Beyotime, China). Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 10⁴ cells/mL and allowed to adhere. Afterward, cells were washed with cold PBS, digested with trypsin, and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min. The cell pellet was washed again with cold PBS, then fixed in 1 mL of pre-chilled 70% ethanol and stored at 4 °C for 24 h. Following another PBS wash, 500 µL of PI staining solution was added to each sample, and the cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Cell cycle profiles were analyzed using a BD FACSARIA III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

Cell apoptosis analysis

Cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in Annexin V Binding Buffer. The apoptotic rate was assessed using a BD FACSARIA III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) after staining with fluorescein isothiocyanate and propidium iodide for 15 min in the dark.

Western blotting

Western blot analysis was performed according to previously published protocols [11]. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-p53 (1:1000, 21891-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-AKT (1:10000, ab179463, Abcam, UK), anti-phospho-AKT (1:1000, 2965 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-STAT3 (1:1000, A1192, Abclonal, USA), anti-phospho-STAT3 (1:1000, Ap0474, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), anti-Bcl2 (1:1000, ab59348, Abcam, UK), and anti-Bax (1:5000, ab32503, Abcam, UK), anti-CIB1 (1:1000, 11823-1-AP, Proteintech, USA). β-actin was used as the loading control (1:5000, 81115-1-RR, Proteintech, China). After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 25 °C for 2 h, the bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (P0018, Beyotime, China) following membrane washing.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Biosharp, Hefei, Anhui, China). Reverse transcription into cDNA was performed using the HiScriptⅡQ RT SuperMix kit (KCD-M1003, Cronda, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was conducted using the 2×Q3 SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Universal) (KCD-M1004, Cronda, Beijing, China). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCq method, with β-actin serving as the internal control. All gene-specific primers were synthesized by Hasenbio (China), as detailed in Table 2.

Animal study

Twelve male BALB/c nude mice, aged 4 weeks, were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Yangzhou University, China. The mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. The mice were randomly divided into two groups: sh-NC (n = 6) and CIB1-shRNA (n = 6). Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 1 × 10⁶ osteosarcoma cells suspended in 100 µL of PBS into the right axillary region. The injections were administered with either a lentiviral vector (sh-NC) or CIB1-shRNA. Tumor size was measured every three days over a 14-day period. On the 14th day, the mice were euthanized using CO₂, and tumors were excised and weighed. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Ethics Committee of the authors’ hospital (South Campus).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 19.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For in vitro experiments, at least three independent biological replicates were performed. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was tested using Levene’s test. For transcriptomic analyses, multiple-testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR).Gene expression data were processed using the “limma” and “ggplot2” R packages, while batch effects were adjusted using the ComBat method. DEGs were identified using a t-test, with the criteria of P < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 0.5. Genes were further categorized using consensus clustering methods, with the optimal k-value determined based on the consistency of the CDF and delta area plots. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed with the “survival” R package to evaluate survival differences among clusters. The characteristics of the identified subtypes were visualized through dimensionality reduction techniques, including UMAP and t-SNE, using the corresponding R packages.

Results

Identification of DEGs and prognostic genes through consensus clustering and PCD-related gene screening

In this study, a total of 5,327 differentially expressed mRNAs were identified. Among them, the top 40—comprising 20 upregulated and 20 downregulated genes—were visualized using a heatmap (Fig. 1a). These DEGs were then compared with genes from the PCD-related gene module, resulting in the identification of 294 overlapping genes (Fig. 1b). Subsequently, univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted on these 294 genes, and 37 genes were found to be significantly associated with prognosis (Fig. 2c). A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for these 37 prognostic genes using the STRING database (Fig. 2d).

Consensus clustering based on gene expression similarity was applied to classify the samples. Analysis of the consensus matrix revealed that K = 2 yielded the most stable clustering, as indicated by the relatively flat middle portion of the CDF curve. Clustering at K = 2 also minimized inter-group interference, leading to the identification of two distinct subgroups: Cluster A and Cluster B. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated a statistically significant difference in OS between the two subgroups (P = 0.008), as shown in Fig. 1i. PCA of transcriptional profiles also confirmed clear separation between Cluster A and Cluster B (Fig. 1j, k, and L). Furthermore, a box plot illustrated the differential expression of 20 prognosis-related PCD genes between the subgroups (Fig. 1m).

Immune status analysis in molecular subtypes and functional assessment

To investigate the relationship between immune response and tumor characteristics, the ssGSEA algorithm was employed to assess differences in immune cell function between the molecular subtypes. The analysis revealed significant differences in antigen-presenting cell (APC) co-stimulation and Type I interferon (IFN) response between Cluster A and Cluster B (Fig. 1n). Among the 23 immune cell types analyzed, significant disparities were observed between the two clusters, including activated B cells, CD56^dim natural killer cells, activated dendritic cells, eosinophils, γδ T cells, macrophages, natural killer T cells, mast cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), T follicular helper cells, Th17 cells, and Th2 cells (Fig. 1o). Subsequently, functional enrichment analysis was conducted between the clusters. KEGG analysis highlighted multiple cancer- and immunity-related pathways, such as the calcium signaling pathway, histidine metabolism, and adipocytokine signaling pathway (Fig. 2a). Additionally, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) revealed significant differences in metabolic pathways between the two subgroups, including histidine metabolism, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, N-glycan biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 2b).

Construction and validation of risk scores in subgroups

To evaluate potential clinical outcomes, a risk score model was constructed. Six genes were identified through LASSO regression analysis (Fig. 3a, b). Based on their risk scores, patients were stratified into low- and high-risk groups. The distribution of risk scores, patient survival status, and expression levels of prognostic genes between the groups are illustrated in Figs. 3c–e. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that patients in the high-risk group had significantly poorer survival outcomes compared to those in the low-risk group (p < 0.001).

To assess the predictive performance of the risk model, time-dependent ROC curve analysis was conducted over a 5-year period. The model exhibited strong predictive ability, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.940, 0.981, and 0.930 (Figs. 3f–i). Heatmaps displaying the expression levels of the six hub genes are presented in Fig. 3h. Survival analysis further demonstrated that four of the six genes—BLOC1S2, CIB1, CREB3L1, and IL6R—were significantly associated with prognosis in osteosarcoma. Notably, high expression of CREB3L1 correlated with poor prognosis, whereas high expression levels of BLOC1S2, CIB1, and IL6R indicated favorable outcomes (Supplementary materials Fig. 1).

GSVA and GSEA for immune-related functional analysis

Spearman rank correlation analysis was conducted using the R packages “ggplot2” and “reshape2”. The analysis revealed that Cluster A exhibited higher risk scores than Cluster B (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, a significant correlation was observed between risk score and Treg cell infiltration (p = 0.0098), as shown in Fig. 4b. Additionally, GSEA and GSVA were performed to confirm the relationship between the risk score and immune cells involved in osteosarcoma development. Figure 4c illustrates the correlation analysis, indicating that CD4 memory resting T cells (P < 0.01) and activated NK cells (P < 0.05) were positively correlated with TNFRSF10C. Moreover, regulatory T cells (Tregs) (P < 0.05), CD8 T cells (P < 0.001), plasma cells (P < 0.05), and M0 macrophages (P < 0.01) showed positive correlations with CIB1. CD4 memory activated T cells (P < 0.05) and memory B cells (P < 0.05) were positively correlated with TGFB2, while Tregs (P < 0.05) were positively associated with BLOC1S2. The distribution of 22 immune cell types infiltrating tumors in low- and high-risk osteosarcoma samples was visually represented using bar plots (Fig. 4d) and violin plots (Fig. 4e). Wilcoxon rank sum tests were conducted to determine statistical significance. Figure 4f displays a heatmap showing the correlations among 22 types of tumor-infiltrating immune cells (TICs), with each small box indicating the p-value. The correlation coefficients between cell types are represented by the shading of each colored box, based on Pearson correlation values.

Different risk scores are associated with distinct TMEs

Our study revealed a strong association between the immune checkpoints POLE2 and FEN1 and the risk score (Supplementary materials 2 m). The ESTIMATE algorithm was applied to assess the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in both low- and high-risk groups.

Identifying potential therapeutic drugs

To identify potential drugs for osteosarcoma treatment, we used the pRRophetic tool to predict clinical responses in patients with high or low risk scores. Among the drugs analyzed—including ABT737, Axitinib, AZD8055, AZD8186, BI-2536, BMS-754,807, Cisplatin, GDC0810, I-BRD9, JQ1, Lapatinib, Linsitinib, MIRA-1, Navitoclax, Olaparib, PD0325901, PF-4,708,671, Ribociclib, SB216763, SB505124, SCH772984, Selumetinib, Sinularin, Telomerase Inhibitor IX, Trametinib, Ulixertinib, UMI-77, Uprosertib, and WEHI-539—it was observed that these agents were more effective in treating osteosarcoma in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group ( Supplementary materials Fig. 3).

Patient tissue and cell line expression of CIB1

The mRNA expression levels of BLOC1S2, CIB1, CREB3L1, IL6R, TGFβ2, and TNFRSF10C in osteosarcoma and adjacent normal tissues were further investigated by qRT-PCR. Results shown in Fig. 5a demonstrate significant upregulation of CIB1, CREB3L1, IL6R, TGFβ2, and TNFRSF10C in osteosarcoma tissues, while no significant difference was observed for BLOC1S2. Our findings suggest that CIB1 may play a critical role in osteosarcoma development. CIB1 was selected for further in-depth analysis based on the following criteria: (1) it showed the most significant differential expression among the five validated genes (p < 0.001); (2) its role in osteosarcoma has not been previously reported; (3) it demonstrated significant correlation with multiple immune cell types; and (4) its prognostic impact was significant (p = 0.048). To further validate CIB1 expression, Western blotting (Fig. 5b) and immunohistochemistry (Fig. 5c) were performed on both osteosarcoma and adjacent tissues, confirming significantly elevated CIB1 protein levels in osteosarcoma. Additionally, qRT-PCR analysis of four osteosarcoma cell lines (U2-OS, MG-63, 143B, and hFOB1.19) revealed that CIB1 expression was highest in U2-OS cells (Fig. 5d). Consequently, the U2-OS cell line was selected for subsequent experiments.

Suppression of CIB1 resulted in decreased proliferation and invasion of U2-OS cells through the promotion of apoptosis

To investigate the role of CIB1 in osteosarcoma, we employed CIB1-shRNA1, CIB1-shRNA2, and CIB1-shRNA3 to knock down CIB1 expression in U2-OS cells. The results shown in Fig. 6a demonstrate that all three shRNAs effectively suppressed CIB1 expression, with CIB1-shRNA3 exerting the strongest effect. Consequently, CIB1-shRNA3 was selected for subsequent experiments. The CCK-8 assay revealed a significant reduction in cell proliferation upon CIB1 downregulation compared to the negative control group (Fig. 6b). Results from the transwell and wound-healing assays indicated a marked decrease in U2-OS cell invasion and migration after CIB1-shRNA transfection, as shown in Fig. 6c and e. The colony formation assay further confirmed a significant decline in cell viability in the CIB1-shRNA group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6d). Moreover, apoptosis analysis revealed a substantial increase in the apoptotic rate in cells with CIB1 knockdown (Fig. 6f). These findings suggest that suppressing CIB1 inhibits the proliferation and invasiveness of U2-OS cells by promoting apoptosis.

Downregulation of CIB1 Induced G2/M Phase Arrest in Osteosarcoma Cells The effect of CIB1 on the cell cycle was evaluated by flow cytometry. The CIB1-shRNA group displayed a significant increase in the proportion of U2-OS cells in the G2/M phase accompanied by a decrease in the S phase population compared to controls (Fig. 6g). These results indicate that CIB1 knockdown induces G2/M phase arrest in osteosarcoma cells.

Downregulation of CIB1 resulted in suppression of tumor growth by inducing cellular apoptosis

Western blot analysis showed that CIB1 inhibition led to a significant decrease in the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 and an increase in the pro-apoptotic protein BAX, thereby promoting apoptosis in osteosarcoma cells. The involvement of apoptosis is further supported by evidence linking the Akt and STAT3 signaling pathways to apoptotic regulation. Additionally, activation of p53 is known to induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [12]. Thus, exploring these signaling interactions in human osteosarcoma cells is of great interest. Following CIB1 knockdown in U2-OS cells, p53 expression was elevated (Fig. 7a and b), while the phosphorylation levels of Akt and STAT3 were reduced. These changes, together with decreased BCL2 and increased BAX, indicate that CIB1 inhibition promotes apoptosis potentially through modulation of the p53, Akt, and STAT3 pathways.

An U2-OS Xenograft Model (Fig. 7g) was established to further investigate the role of CIB1 in osteosarcoma in vivo. Tumor growth was markedly suppressed in the CIB1-shRNA group compared to controls (Fig. 7h and i). Additionally, tumor weights were significantly lower in the CIB1-shRNA group than in the control group (Fig. 7j). Collectively, these findings strongly support that downregulation of CIB1 effectively inhibits the growth of U2-OS xenograft tumors in vivo.

Discussion

Osteosarcoma is a highly aggressive bone tumor with a challenging prognosis. Approximately 20% of patients with metastatic osteosarcoma survive beyond 5 years, compared to 65% survival in those with localized disease. PCD, a specific form of intracellular cell death, holds promise as a therapeutic strategy for tumors like osteosarcoma, which often exhibit chromosomal abnormalities. Therefore, this study aimed to identify PCD-associated genes to predict osteosarcoma prognosis and explore potential therapeutic targets.

During our investigation, we identified 37 DEGs with prognostic significance. Using consensus clustering, these genes were grouped into two subtypes based on expression patterns. PCA further revealed distinct clustering characteristics for each subgroup. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that cluster B was associated with a better prognosis. Through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses combined with LASSO regression, six key genes were selected. Patients were then stratified into high- and low-risk groups according to the median risk score. Differences in the tumor immune microenvironment between these groups were assessed by analyzing immune checkpoints, TME scores, and immune activity via ssGSEA. The results showed a higher infiltration of immune cells in the high-risk group. Furthermore, drug sensitivity analyses were performed for both risk groups, providing novel insights and potential therapeutic options for osteosarcoma.

CIB1, also known as calmyrin or KIP, is a 22 kDa protein that interacts with the cytoplasmic domain of platelet integrins βIIb/β3. It regulates the activation of integrin αIIbβ3 and calcium-dependent IP3 receptor-mediated signaling. CIB1 has garnered attention for its involvement in cancer progression, participating in key cellular processes including survival, proliferation, apoptosis, and migration, thereby promoting tumor growth and angiogenesis. By interacting with kinase partners such as Plk3, PKD2, PKD, hTERT, TRF2, and DNA-PKcs, CIB1 plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis. Additionally, CIB1 enhances cell survival and proliferation via modulation of the PI3K/AKT and MEK/ERK signaling pathways, as reported by Leisner et al. Reduced CIB1 levels disrupt AKT and ERK signaling, leading to increased nuclear localization of GAPDH, caspase-independent cell death, and impaired cellular growth. The role of CIB1 in tumor cell motility and metastasis is further supported by its interaction with partners such as αIIbβ3, FVIII, WASP, Rac3, PAK1, and FAK. CIB1 has been implicated in various cancers, including triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), hepatocellular carcinoma, and Kaposi’s sarcoma. However, its role in osteosarcoma remains poorly understood. In this study, qRT-PCR analysis of BLOC1S2, CIB1, CREB3L1, IL6R, TGFB2, and TNFRSF10C mRNA levels in osteosarcoma and adjacent normal tissues revealed a significant upregulation of CIB1 in osteosarcoma. These findings were corroborated by Western blotting and immunohistochemistry, which confirmed elevated CIB1 protein expression in osteosarcoma tissues. Our results suggest that CIB1 may be critically involved in osteosarcoma development. Moreover, CIB1 knockdown inhibited osteosarcoma cell proliferation and invasion by inducing apoptosis and causing cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase. Our data suggest that CIB1 inhibition is associated with increased p53 and decreased p-Akt and p-STAT3, but further studies are needed to establish direct mechanistic links.

Interestingly, high CIB1 expression is associated with a favorable prognosis in our survival analysis, and inhibition of CIB1 shows potential as a therapeutic approach for osteosarcoma in both cell and animal models. Numerous studies have identified CIB1 as a negative prognostic marker in various cancers [13–15], a finding supported by additional research [16]. However, some investigations report that CIB1 expression is not predictive of prognosis in triple-negative breast cancer [17]. Black et al. proposed several hypotheses, including the idea that CIB1, while not inherently oncogenic, is essential for maintaining the malignant phenotype of cancer cells. Similar observations have been made in other studies; for example, HHLA2 has been identified as a positive prognostic factor in renal cell carcinoma [18], whereas PD-L1’s prognostic significance varies across tumor types [19–24]. These discrepancies likely reflect the influence of the distribution and expression patterns of specific molecules and their receptors on tumor immune responses and the tumor microenvironment. Possible explanations include the differential subcellular localization and tumor stage–dependent roles of certain genes, such as CDK5 [25] and P63 [26], which may function either as oncogenes or tumor suppressors. Additionally, the detection of high CIB1 expression in tumor tissues by RNA sequencing does not necessarily indicate tumor cell origin; it may derive from infiltrating immune cells, which could correlate with a better prognosis. Furthermore, differences in datasets or patient cohorts may contribute to varying research outcomes.

A notable discrepancy exists between the prognostic association of CIB1 (high expression correlating with better survival) and the tumor-suppressive effect of CIB1 knockdown in vitro and in vivo. This paradox may be explained by the fact that CIB1 expression in bulk tumor RNA-seq data may derive not only from tumor cells but also from infiltrating immune cells. Our immune infiltration analysis revealed positive correlations between CIB1 and regulatory T cells (Tregs), CD8 + T cells, and plasma cells (Fig. 4c), which could contribute to a favorable immune microenvironment and better prognosis. Thus, high CIB1 expression in patient samples may reflect immune activation rather than tumor cell-intrinsic oncogenic activity. Importantly, the functional experiments using CIB1-specific knockdown directly demonstrate that CIB1 is required for maintaining the malignant phenotype of osteosarcoma cells, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target despite the prognostic observation.

Nonetheless, our study has limitations. The relatively small sample size and lack of detailed patient information limited the range of genes available for analysis. Although data integration was performed to increase sample size, potential systematic biases arising from differing methodologies and experimental conditions were not fully addressed. Moreover, due to resource and time constraints, only one gene (CIB1) among the five identified hub genes was studied extensively. Further research is needed to elucidate the functions of these genes in osteosarcoma.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identified five hub genes (CIB1, CREB3L1, IL6R, TGFβ2, and TNFRSF10C) strongly associated with programmed cell death in osteosarcoma patients. Experimental evidence indicates that CIB1 inhibition is associated with modulation of the p53, Akt, and STAT3 signaling pathways, leading to apoptosis, reduced proliferation and invasion, cell cycle arrest, and suppression of tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. In experimental models, CIB1 inhibition suppressed osteosarcoma cell proliferation, invasion, and tumor growth. Although high CIB1 expression in patient samples correlated with better prognosis-likely reflecting immune cell infiltration-our functional data support that targeting CIB1 may still have therapeutic value. Further studies are warranted to clarify the context-dependent roles of CIB1 in osteosarcoma.
