Fluorescence ureteral navigation in laparoscopic Hartmann’s procedure after aortic repair: a case report
Authors: Hyuga Kawakubo, Shunjin Ryu, Shunsuke Nakashima, Yuta Imaizumi, Teppei Kamada, Junji Takahashi, Atsuko Okamoto, Yasunobu Kobayashi, Yasuhiro Takano, Yasuhiro Takeda, Ryusuke Ito, Eto Ken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Ureteral injury is a critical complication of colorectal surgery, particularly in reoperative fields where severe adhesions obscure the dissection layer. A near-infrared fluorescent ureteral catheter (NIRFUC) provides real-time ureteral visualization, thereby enhancing intraoperative safety.Presentation of case:An 81-year-old male on dialysis for diabetic nephropathy was referred for evaluation of a positive fecal occult blood test. Lower gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed a 0-IIc lesion in the sigmoid colon, pathologically confirmed as moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma. The patient had previously undergone open Y-graft replacement for an abdominal aortic aneurysm. Laparoscopic Hartmann’s procedure was selected due to a high risk of anastomotic failure. Because difficulty in ureteral identification was anticipated, bilateral NIRFUCs were placed cystoscopically under fluoroscopy. The operation was completed uneventfully in 4 hours and 16 minutes. Postoperative ischemic colitis (Clavien–Dindo grade II) resolved without stoma complications. The final pathological stage was pT1bN0M0, Stage I.Clinical discussion:The NIRFUC enabled safe ureteral identification in a complex reoperative field with severe fibrosis. Continuous visualization appeared to facilitate adherence to the embryological plane and safe dissection without ureteral injury, despite a nonstandard lateral approach. This technique appeared to be particularly useful when anatomical landmarks were obscured and may help support intraoperative decision-making in selected complex cases.Conclusion:This case demonstrates the utility of the NIRFUC in laparoscopic colorectal redo surgery with severe adhesions. The NIRFUC may improve intraoperative safety by supporting anatomic orientation and facilitating safe dissection without ureteral complications in complex cases.

Introduction

In laparoscopic surgery for colorectal cancer, it is vital to standardize the surgical procedures and adhere to the embryological dissection plane, which not only improves oncological outcomes but also prevents damage to other organs. Ureteral injury remains a serious complication in colorectal surgery, particularly in laparoscopic and reoperative settings where anatomical identification can be challenging[1–3] However, in redo surgery involving dissection in the same area as the previous operation, the embryological plane is often obscured by adhesions and fibrosis resulting from the initial surgery. In addition, extension of the dissection plane to secure an adequate tumor margin may increase the risk of injury to the ureter or autonomic nerves. Near-infrared fluorescent ureteral catheter (NIRFUC; Cardinal Health, OH, USA) has recently been introduced as a technique that enables real-time visualization of the ureter during surgery; however, its role in complex reoperative settings remains unclear[4,5].

Here, we report a case in which laparoscopic Hartmann’s procedure was safely performed using NIRFUC without ureteral injury in a patient with sigmoid colon cancer after open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair. Given the limited evidence in such challenging cases, particularly in patients with altered vascular anatomy after aortic repair and impaired renal function, individual case reports remain valuable for providing practical insights into surgical planning and ureteral navigation strategies. This case report is presented in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[6].

Case presentation

The patient was an 81-year-old male who was referred to our hospital due to a positive fecal occult blood test. He was undergoing maintenance dialysis for diabetic nephropathy but retained residual urine output, indicating partial preservation of renal function. He had previously undergone open surgery for abdominal aortic aneurysm repair at another hospital, during which Y-graft replacement was performed below the renal arteries. The inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) could not be identified (Fig. 1). He also had a history of percutaneous coronary intervention for myocardial infarction. Blood tests revealed a carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level of 5.44 ng/mL and a carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) level of 13.9 U/mL. Lower gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed a 0-IIc lesion in the sigmoid colon (anal verge, 40 cm). The wall exhibited poor distensibility, and the depth of invasion showed massive submucosal invasion, indicating that endoscopic resection was not feasible. The pathological diagnosis was moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed no obvious lymph node or distant metastases. In anticipation of severe adhesions around the ureter, a NIRFUC was inserted cystoscopically into the ureter under fluoroscopic guidance by a urologist after the induction of general anesthesia. A camera port was inserted at the umbilicus using the open technique. The abdominal cavity was inspected, and no adhesions to the abdominal wall, liver metastases, or peritoneal dissemination were observed. A 12-mm trocar was placed 2 cm medial to the right anterior superior iliac spine, and a 5-mm trocar was inserted approximately 10 cm cranial to it. The same configuration was applied on the left side, and the procedure was performed using a total of five ports. During the previous surgery, the ureter and sigmoid colon had been extensively dissected and mobilized from the caudal end of the renal artery to the common iliac artery, and an artificial blood vessel was placed in the abdominal aorta. As a result, dense adhesions and fibrosis were present in the layer anterior to the aorta. A medial approach toward the IMA was attempted; however, due to severe adhesions, adequate exposure of the sigmoid mesocolon and ureter was not possible. In contrast, adhesions were relatively mild from the left side of the rectum to the lateral peritoneal attachments, and the dissection plane was considered identifiable. Real-time identification of the ureter using the NIRFUC guided the decision to adopt a lateral-first approach to minimize the risk of ureteral injury. During lateral mobilization of the sigmoid colon, the tangential relationship between the energy device and the retroperitoneal tissue increased the risk of ureteral injury; however, real-time visualization of the ureter allowed the selection of a safe dissection line. The white line of Toldt along the descending colon was incised, and dissection was performed between the mesocolon and the renal fascia, followed by dissection along the left side of the rectum. Using the ureter as a landmark with the aid of the NIRFUC, the dissection plane could be adjusted as needed, allowing safe mobilization without injury to the ureter or mesocolon (Figs 2 and 3). In addition, near the ureter, continuous recognition of its position and distance enabled the safe use of energy devices, including an ultrasonic dissector, under real-time fluorescence guidance. This continuous visualization allowed the surgical team to confirm the ureteral course without direct ureterolysis or ureteral taping. Based on the preoperative diagnosis of cT1bN0M0 stage I disease according to the TNM Classification of Malignant Tumors, 8th edition, UICC, lymphadenectomy equivalent to D2, as defined by the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum (JSCCR), was considered oncologically appropriate. Preoperative contrast-enhanced CT did not clearly identify the IMA, and the patient’s history of open Y-graft replacement for abdominal aortic aneurysm suggested that the IMA had likely been ligated, occluded, or incorporated into the fibrotic tissue around the vascular graft during the previous operation. Intraoperatively, severe adhesions and altered anatomy anterior to the aorta further precluded identification of the original IMA root as an intact anatomical landmark. Therefore, high ligation at the IMA root was not pursued. Instead, vascular control was achieved at a more distal level, with ligation of the superior rectal and sigmoid arteries and division of the inferior mesenteric vein (IMV), while maintaining oncological adequacy through lymph node dissection at the pericolic and intermediate levels. In addition, a laparoscopic Hartmann procedure was performed because of the high risk of anastomotic failure associated with the patient’s surgical history and comorbid diseases. The operation lasted 4 hours and 16 minutes and was completed with minimal bleeding. The pathological diagnosis was tubular adenocarcinoma, pT1b, N0, M0, and stage I (TNM Classification of Malignant Tumors, 8th edition, UICC). Postoperative ischemic colitis (Clavien–Dindo grade II) was observed, with no stoma ischemia, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 17.

Discussion

Ureteral injury is among the most serious urological complications in colorectal surgery, with a reported incidence of 0.3–1.5% in colectomies[1]. Previous studies have consistently shown that the risk of ureteral injury is particularly high in laparoscopic sigmoid and rectal resection, as well as in reoperative fields characterized by severe adhesions or fibrosis[2]. Such injuries are associated with increased morbidity, prolonged hospital stays, increased medical costs, long-term impairment of renal function, and reduced quality of life, and they are frequent causes of medicolegal disputes[6]. Large cohort studies have identified rectal cancer, metastatic disease, malnutrition, surgery at educational institutions, and adhesions as significant risk factors for ureteral injury[1].

In recent years, the use of a NIRFUC has been introduced as a novel method for facilitating ureteral identification. Reported visualization rates of the ureter using fluorescence-guided techniques, including catheter-based approaches such as NIRFUC, are generally high, often exceeding 90% in systematic reviews[4]. However, despite improved visualization, robust evidence demonstrating a reduction in ureteral injury rates remains limited[7,8]. Much of the current literature consists of small, non-randomized studies, with considerable heterogeneity across studies[4,7]. Several techniques have been proposed to facilitate intraoperative ureteral identification. Conventional ureteral stenting provides tactile feedback and may assist in ureteral recognition; however, its utility is limited in laparoscopic surgery due to reduced tactile sensation[9]. Indocyanine green has also been applied via direct intraureteral instillation; however, this approach remains off-label and is not routinely performed in standard clinical practice[10,11]. More recently, emerging technologies such as intravenous renally excreted fluorophores [e.g., pudexacianinium (ASP5354)] and artificial intelligence (AI)-based anatomical recognition have been explored[12,13]. However, compared with catheter-based fluorescence navigation, the stability of ureteral visualization using renally excreted fluorophores may be affected by renal excretory function and urinary flow, because fluorescence signals are generated after excretion into the urine and passage through the ureter. This potential limitation may be particularly relevant in patients with impaired renal function or those undergoing dialysis. In contrast, once placed, the NIRFUC enables continuous real-time visualization of the ureter independent of urinary flow or renal excretion[14]. This characteristic was clinically relevant in the present case because the patient was undergoing maintenance dialysis despite preserved residual urine output. Therefore, rather than being interchangeable, these modalities should be considered complementary, with selection tailored to the clinical context, particularly in patients with renal dysfunction. In the present case, severe adhesions and fibrosis around the sigmoid mesocolon and ureter following prior aortic surgery precluded sufficient exposure of the ureter and gonadal vessels using the standard medial approach. Consequently, a lateral approach, which is generally associated with an increased risk of ureteral injury due to limited visualization of retroperitoneal structures, was required[15]. Under these atypical conditions, the use of NIRFUC combined with the overlay imaging mode of the Stryker 1788 4 K camera system enabled continuous real-time identification of the ureter without interrupting the surgical workflow. This system allows simultaneous visualization of fluorescence and standard operative images, which may facilitate safe dissection even during a nonstandard approach and frequent use of energy devices near the ureter. Unlike previous reports demonstrating the feasibility of NIRFUC for ureteral visualization[3], the broader applicability of fluorescence-guided navigation[16], and its potential use in high-risk settings where anatomical identification is challenging[17], its practical utility in laparoscopic colorectal surgery after major vascular surgery for abdominal aortic aneurysm has not been specifically reported. To our knowledge, this is the first detailed case report describing how NIRFUC can support intraoperative strategy during laparoscopic colorectal surgery in a reoperative field following open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair. In this context, the NIRFUC may serve not only for ureteral identification but also as an intraoperative landmark to support maintenance of the appropriate dissection plane. From a surgical decision-making perspective, careful selection of the operative strategy is also essential in such high-risk cases. In the present case, a Hartmann’s procedure was selected instead of primary anastomosis because of multiple factors that may have increased the risk of anastomotic failure, including diabetes mellitus and dialysis dependence[18,19]. Given the importance of adequate perfusion for anastomotic healing, a non-restorative procedure was considered the safer option in this setting. Postoperative ischemic colitis developed but was managed conservatively with bowel rest and intravenous fluid therapy, resulting in gradual improvement without the need for surgical intervention. Although additional management for this complication was required, it did not significantly impact the postoperative course, and the patient was ultimately discharged in stable condition. In the present case, several patient-related factors, including advanced age, diabetes mellitus, maintenance dialysis, and a history of aortic graft replacement, may have predisposed the patient to impaired systemic and mesenteric perfusion, which may have contributed to this complication. The absence of stoma ischemia suggests that the ischemia was localized and that blood flow to the sigmoid colon used for stoma construction was relatively preserved. This may be explained by maintained perfusion via the left colic artery, whereas the splenic flexure, a watershed area between the superior and inferior mesenteric arterial territories, is more susceptible to ischemia under compromised perfusion conditions. These findings support the preoperative concern regarding impaired intestinal perfusion and suggest that the decision to avoid primary anastomosis was appropriate. Given the patient’s history of prior aortic graft replacement and the inability to identify the IMA root, it is possible that ligation of the superior rectal artery may have further compromised mesenteric perfusion in the setting of pre-existing collateral insufficiency, thereby contributing to the development of postoperative ischemic colitis. From an oncological perspective, complete mesocolic excision (CME) and total mesorectal excision (TME) are widely accepted principles in colorectal cancer surgery and are based on precise dissection along embryological planes[20,21]. In redo surgery, however, adhesion and fibrosis of the dissection layer not only increase the risk of ureteral injury but also make the maintenance of the appropriate mesenteric plane challenging, potentially compromising oncological quality[20]. Previous reports have suggested that visualization of the ureter using the NIRFUC may prevent organ injury, with the NIRFUC serving as a useful landmark for identifying the appropriate dissection line on the mesenteric side[22]. In this case, although D3 lymph node dissection at the root of the IMA was not performed, the use of NIRFUC may have facilitated safe mobilization and resection of the sigmoid colon and rectum in accordance with the principles of CME and TME.

In conclusion, this case suggests that NIRFUC can be a valuable adjunct for facilitating ureteral identification and supporting safe oncological dissection during laparoscopic redo colorectal surgery, particularly when standard surgical approaches are not feasible owing to severe adhesions. Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single case report, the findings are inherently limited in their generalizability, and caution is required when extrapolating these results to broader clinical practice. In addition, the reproducibility of this technique may vary across institutions and may depend on the availability of specialized equipment as well as the surgeon’s experience with fluorescence-guided surgery[4]. Furthermore, ureteral catheter placement may be technically challenging or infeasible in patients with severe urethral or ureteral strictures[17]. NIRFUC also has potential limitations, including suboptimal visualization under certain conditions, fluorescence attenuation, and image artifacts, which may affect accurate interpretation. Although these issues were not observed in the present case, they should be considered when interpreting the results[23]. Future studies, including multicenter prospective studies or randomized controlled trials, are warranted to further evaluate the efficacy, safety, and optimal indications of NIRFUC in a broader patient population.

Conclusion

This case suggests that catheter-based fluorescence ureteral navigation may serve as a valuable adjunct in complex redo colorectal surgery. In particular, NIRFUC may be beneficial when standard surgical approaches are challenging and when renal excretion–dependent fluorescence techniques are limited. Further studies are warranted to define the optimal indications and clinical benefits of this technique.
