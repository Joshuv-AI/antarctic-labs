Pairing of high-mannose glycans in human IgE-Fc: implications for secretion and stability
Authors: Sakurako Nomura, Tatsuro Iwabuchi, Haruka Sawataishi, Kizashi Kamo, Noriyoshi Manabe, Ryohei Uematsu, Izumi Sakamoto, Kenji Hirose, Eunsang Kwon, Yoshiki Yamaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmunoglobulin E (IgE) is the least abundant antibody class in serum, but plays a central role in type I allergic responses. The Fc region of human IgE (IgE-Fc) contains four potential N-glycosylation sites: Asn265 and Asn371 are modified with complex-type glycans; Asn394 predominantly carries a high-mannose glycan; and Asn383 remains unmodified. Despite the recognized importance of glycosylation in antibody function, the structure–function relationships of the individual IgE-Fc glycans remain poorly understood. This study aimed to elucidate the structural and functional significance of N-glycans on IgE-Fc, particularly the high-mannose glycan attached to Asn394.MethodsThe expression and secretion of recombinant human IgE-Fc constructs including wild-type IgE-Fc, a high-mannose-deficient mutant (N394Q), and a triple mutant lacking complex-type glycans (N265Q/N371Q/N383Q) was assessed in mammalian Expi293F cells. In addition, the thermal stability of wild-type IgE-Fc treated with Endo H or PNGase F was evaluated by a thermal shift assay. Glycan compositions and pairing patterns in IgE-Fc were characterized by intact mass spectrometry (MS) and liquid chromatography–MS analysis of released glycans.ResultsWild-type IgE-Fc and the triple mutant were stably expressed, whereas secretion of the high-mannose–deficient mutant (N394Q) was markedly impaired, demonstrating that the Asn394 glycan is essential for proper folding and efficient secretion. Endo H treatment significantly decreased the melting temperature of wild-type IgE-Fc, indicating that high-mannose glycans contribute to structural stability. Integration of MS data on the intact protein and LC–MS data on the enzyme-released N-glycans revealed that high-mannose glycans at Asn394 associate in a non-selective manner between heavy chains.ConclusionsThese findings establish that the high-mannose glycan at Asn394 is required for both the secretion and structural stability of IgE-Fc. Furthermore, the observation of non-selective glycan pairing between heavy chains provides insight into the temporal coordination of disulfide bond formation and N-glycan processing during IgE biosynthesis, thereby advancing our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying IgE structure and function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12014-026-09616-1.

Background

Immunoglobulins in humans are classified into five major classes, IgA, IgD, IgE, IgG, and IgM. Of these, IgG is the most abundant in serum and has been the most extensively studied, particularly in the context of therapeutic antibody development. In contrast, IgE, the last immunoglobulin class to be identified [1, 2], is the least abundant in circulation. IgE plays a central role in type I hypersensitivity reactions by mediating the release of inflammatory mediators from mast cells and basophils [3]. It also contributes to immune defense against parasites and protection from venoms [4].

Structurally, IgE is a monomer composed of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains, similar to IgG. However, its domain organization differs from that of IgG: instead of a hinge region, IgE possesses an additional Cε2 domain. Consequently, the Cγ2 and Cγ3 domains of IgG-Fc correspond structurally to the Cε3 and Cε4 domains of IgE-Fc. The overall architecture of IgE-Fc resembles that of the Fc unit of IgM and displays a domain organization similar to avian IgY.

In IgG, a pair of complex-type N-glycans attached to Asn297 in the Cγ2 domain are essential for maintaining structural integrity and mediating effector functions such as Fc receptor binding [5–8]. IgE-Fc also carries an N-glycan at the corresponding position, Asn394 in the Cε3 domain, and the absence of this glycan is known to abolish binding of the Fc receptor (FcεRI) and downstream degranulation [9–11]. However, the glycan at Asn394 is predominantly of the high-mannose type, in contrast to the complex-type glycan commonly found in IgG-Fc [12, 13].

In addition to Asn394, human IgE-Fc contains three other potential N-glycosylation sites, Asn265, Asn371, and Asn383. Asn265 and Asn371 are modified with complex-type glycans, whereas Asn383 lacks glycan modification [12, 14, 15]. Mutations at these three sites have been shown to have little effect on FcεRI binding [9–11, 16]. These findings suggest that the N-glycans on IgE-Fc are site-specifically modified, and that the high-mannose glycan at Asn394 plays a crucial role in structural stability and biological function. Nevertheless, how this glycan contributes to the folding, secretion, and stability of IgE-Fc at the molecular level remains poorly understood [17, 18].

In this study, we have explored the role of the high-mannose glycan attached to Asn394 in the Cε3 domain of IgE-Fc. To specifically evaluate its functional contribution, we designed a triple mutant (N265Q/N371Q/N383Q) in which the other three N-glycosylation sites were abolished while the Asn394 site, structurally equivalent to Asn297 in IgG-Fc, was selectively retained. Recombinant wild-type (WT) and glycosylation-deficient mutants of IgE-Fc were expressed in Expi293F cells and characterized by intact mass spectrometry (MS), liquid chromatography–MS (LC–MS) analysis of released N-glycans, and thermal stability assays. Through these analyses, we aimed to elucidate the detailed compositions of N-glycans attached to IgE-Fc and to clarify their structural and functional significance.

Methods

Plasmid design for mammalian expression

The amino acid sequence of human IgE-Fc was obtained from the UniProt database (accession no. P01854-1). DNA fragments encoding WT and mutant IgE-Fc were codon-optimized for human expression and synthesized by Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Each gene fragment, corresponding to residues 224–548 of human IgE-Fc, was fused to an N-terminal interleukin-2 secretion signal peptide (residues 1–20) and a C-terminal octa-histidine tag (His×8), and cloned into the mammalian expression vector pcDNA3.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Based on a previous report [16], the cysteine residue at position 224 was substituted with alanine (C224A) to prevent unwanted intermolecular disulfide bond formation between Fc units during expression.

Cell culture

Mammalian Expi293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for recombinant protein expression. Cells were cultured in 125-mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing pre-warmed HE400AZ medium (Gmep Co., Ltd.) in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with orbital agitation at 125 rpm in a humidified incubator with 8% CO₂. Cell density and viability were monitored daily via a Countess 3 Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A 10-µL aliquot of cell suspension was mixed with an equal volume of 0.4% trypan blue solution, and 10 µL of the mixture was assessed for viability.

DNA transfection and protein expression

For transient transfection, 30 µg of each expression plasmid was mixed with 1.5 mL of 293 Complexing Solution (Gmep Co., Ltd.) (solution A). Separately, 84 µL of Gxpress 293 TF Reagent (Gmep Co., Ltd.) was incubated with 1.5 mL of 293 Complexing Solution for 5 min at room temperature (solution B). Solutions A and B were then gently mixed and incubated for 5 min to allow complex formation. The resulting transfection solution was added dropwise to Expi293F cultures (typically 30 mL per 125-mL flask) while gently swirling (designated day 0).

The cells were incubated at 37 °C with agitation at 125 rpm in a humidified atmosphere of 8% CO₂. After 18–24 h (day 1), 1.5 mL of Gxpress 293 Enhancer (Gmep Co., Ltd.) was added dropwise with gentle mixing, and cell culture was maintained under the same conditions for up to 7 days. To monitor protein expression, 100-µL aliquots were collected daily (days 1–7): 10 µL was used for assessment of cell viability and density as described above; the remainder was centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant and cell pellet were stored separately at − 20 °C until protein analysis. After 7 days of culture, the cell suspension was centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was dialyzed three times against phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 1.0 mM Na₂HPO₄, 1.8 mM KH₂PO₄, pH 7.4) at 4 °C and used for subsequent analyses of His-tagged IgE-Fc proteins.

For intracellular protein analysis, cell pellets were resuspended in 100 µL of M-PER lysis reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), mixed by vortexing, and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the soluble fractions were collected and analyzed.

Purification by Ni2+ affinity chromatography

Proteins were purified using a column containing 1 mL Ni-Sepharose 6 Fast Flow resin (Cytiva) equilibrated with 5 mL of PBS. Dialyzed samples were passed through a 0.45-µm syringe filter (Millipore) using a 30-mL syringe (Terumo) prior to loading on the column. The FT fraction was collected, and the column was washed and eluted stepwise with PBS containing the following concentrations of imidazole: wash (50 mM), elution 1 (100 mM), elution 2 (200 mM), elution 3 (300 mM), elution 4 (400 mM), and elution 5 (500 mM). Fractions corresponding to each step were collected.

SDS–PAGE analysis of protein fractions

To assess protein expression and purity, 6 µL of each fraction was mixed with 2 µL of sample buffer [167 mM Tris–HCl (pH 6.8), 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 5.3% (w/v) SDS, 6.7% (w/v) sucrose, and 0.007% (w/v) bromophenol blue], heated at 98 °C for 5 min and then briefly centrifuged. Samples (8 µL each) and molecular weight marker (5 µL; Precision Plus Protein™ All Blue Standards, Bio-Rad) were subjected to SDS–PAGE using a 5–20% gradient polyacrylamide gel (ATTO) and a Compact PAGE-Ace system (ATTO) at a constant current of 21 mA for 30 min. The running buffer comprised 25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, and 0.1% (w/v) SDS.

For visualization, gels were washed three times with Milli-Q water (5 min each), stained with CBB Stain One Super (Nacalai Tesque) for 30 min, and then destained with Milli-Q water until a clear background was obtained.

Western blot analysis

After SDS–PAGE, proteins were transferred from the polyacrylamide gel to an Immobilon® PVDF membrane (Millipore) by a standard method. The membrane was washed three times (5 min each) with PBS-T (140 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na₂HPO₄, 1.8 mM KH₂PO₄, 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20, pH 7.4) and blocked in 50 mL PBS-T containing 2.5% (w/v) skim milk for 30 min with gentle shaking. The membrane was again washed three times with PBS-T, and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-6×His tag antibody (FUJIFILM Wako; 1:1000, 10 µL in 10 mL PBS-T) for more than 3 h at 4 °C with gentle shaking. Lastly, the membrane was washed three times with PBS-T and protein bands were visualized using Immobilon® Forte Western HRP substrate (Millipore; 1 mL) and a ChemiDoc TouchMP (BIO RAD).

Protein concentration determination

Protein concentrations were estimated by measuring absorbance at 280 nm using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The molar extinction coefficient of IgE-Fc (WT and Triple mutant) was assumed to be 47,940 M⁻¹·cm⁻¹.

Intact mass analysis

The mass of intact IgE-Fc proteins was measured by using an INTACT™ Mass system (Waters) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC® Protein BEH C4 column (Waters) and Xevo G2-XS QTof (Waters). Samples were injected onto the column, and eluted with a linear gradient using mobile phase A (water containing 0.1% formic acid; Kanto Chemical) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid; Kanto Chemical). The column temperature was maintained at 60 °C, and separation was performed at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min using a 2-min linear gradient from 5% to 95% phase B. Analyses were performed according to the manufacturer’s recommended operating conditions. To determine the mass of IgE-Fc proteins under reducing conditions, IgE-Fc samples were pretreated with 3 M guanidine hydrochloride and 0.5 mM DTT at 60 °C for 30 min, and then analyzed immediately. The resulting spectra were deconvoluted using MassLynx (Waters) to assign the observed molecular species.

LC–MS analysis of released N-glycans

N-glycans were enzymatically released from IgE-Fc proteins using PNGase F (New England Biolabs) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The reducing ends of the released glycans were labeled with RapiFluor-MS™ (Waters) to enable fluorescence detection. The labeled glycans were analyzed by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) using a Xevo G2-XS QTof mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Fluorescence detection was performed using a Waters ACQUITY FLR detector (excitation: 265 nm; emission: 425 nm). MS detection was performed in positive ion mode over the mass range 150–2500 Da. MS and MS/MS data were acquired using MSE mode, with the collision energy ramped from 10 to 50 eV in the high-energy function. Glycan composition was assigned on the basis of chromatographic retention time and accurate mass.

Glycosidase digestion

For Endo H digestion, 5 µL of enzyme (1,000,000 units/mL, New England Biolabs) was added to 100 µL of 800 µg/mL WT IgE-Fc and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h. The sample was applied to 100 µL of Ni-Sepharose beads (Cytiva) in a Centri-Cut column (Kurabo Co.) and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 s to remove the flow through. The beads were washed sequentially with 400 µL of Milli-Q water and PBS at 4 °C with gentle inversion and centrifugation. The digested IgE-Fc protein was eluted sequentially by incubation with increasing imidazole concentrations.

For PNGase F digestion, 6 µL of Remove-iT® PNGase F (225,000 units/mL, New England Biolabs) with a chitin-binding domain was incubated with 100 µL of 800 µg/mL IgE-Fc at 37 °C for 72 h. Separately, chitin magnetic beads (50 µL, New England Biolabs) were captured with a magnetic rack and washed twice with 500 µL of PBS. The beads were then incubated with the deglycosylated IgE-Fc samples at 4 °C for 10 min with gentle inversion to remove the enzyme. The supernatant containing IgE-Fc was collected; the beads were then washed three times with 100 µL of PBS with recovery of any protein in the supernatant.

Digestion efficiency was assessed by SDS–PAGE followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) staining and destaining with Milli-Q water. Successful glycan cleavage was inferred from changes in band mobility and intensity.

Thermal shift assay using a hydrophobic fluorescent probe

Protein samples were prepared at a concentration of 800 µg/mL in PBS (pH 7.4). Next, 10 µL of protein solution was mixed with 2.5 µL of SYPRO™ Orange Protein Gel Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 25-fold dilution in Milli-Q water, in a 24-well plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Thermal unfolding was monitored using a PIKOReal 24 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fluorescence intensity was recorded continuously as the temperature was increased from 20 °C to 95 °C at a rate of 0.1 °C per second. The denaturation temperature (Tm), which corresponds to protein unfolding and exposure of hydrophobic regions, was determined from the midpoint temperature at which the fluorescence intensity reached 50% between its minimum and maximum values.

Results and discussion

Expression and purification of WT and N-glycan-deficient mutants of IgE-Fc

To isolate the role of the Asn394 glycan, we constructed a triple mutant lacking the other N-glycosylation sites (N265Q/N371Q/N383Q) (Fig. 1A). DNA sequences encoding the three designed IgE-Fc constructs, WT, Single mutant (N394Q), and the Triple mutant (N265Q/N371Q/N383Q), were transfected into Expi293F cells for recombinant expression. After Ni-NTA affinity purification of the cell culture, WT IgE-Fc and the Triple mutant were detected on SDS–PAGE, indicating efficient secretion into the culture medium, whereas the Single mutant was not detected (Fig. 1B and D). For both WT and the Triple mutant, protein bands were not observed after the first post-wash (wash 1; data not shown), and complete elution was achieved in fractions 2–5 for WT and 3–4 for the Triple mutant.

To investigate the lack of detection of the Single mutant (N394Q), intracellular fractions from the cell culture were analyzed by Western blot using an anti-histidine tag antibody. A clear IgE-Fc band was detected in the cell lysate, indicating that the Single mutant accumulates intracellularly, consistent with impaired secretion (Supplementary Fig. S1). These results suggest that the N-glycan attached to Asn394 is critical for IgE-Fc secretion, while the other N-glycans are largely dispensable for this process. This finding is consistent with a previous report showing that a double mutant of human IgE-Fc (N265Q/N371Q) is efficiently secreted from mammalian cells [16].

LC–MS analysis of released N-glycans

To determine the composition and relative abundance of the N-linked glycans attached to WT IgE-Fc and the Triple mutant, N-glycans released by enzyme digestion were labeled with a fluorescent tag and analyzed by LC–MS. The resulting chromatograms and the predicted glycan structures for each peak are shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3.

For WT IgE-Fc, peak area analysis revealed that complex-type glycans accounted for 17.9% of all glycans, high-mannose-type glycans for 51.3%, hybrid-type glycans for 0.4%, and unidentified peaks for 20.7%. The proportion of high-mannose glycans was higher than the previously reported estimate (~ 33%). This suggests either that high-mannose glycans may occupy one or both sites normally modified with complex-type glycans, or that the complex-type glycan sites are incompletely occupied, consistent with previous studies showing that Asn371 has sub-100% N-glycan occupancy [14, 15].

As anticipated for the Triple mutant lacking complex-type glycosylation sites, 84.3% of glycans were high-mannose-type and 7.6% were hybrid-type, while no complex-type glycans were detected. Interestingly, minor amounts of two core-fucosylated hybrid-type glycans, Man4GlcNAc3Fuc1 (F(6)M4A1) and Man5GlcNAc3Fuc1 (F(6)]M5A1), were detected (Supplementary Fig. S3). This finding is consistent with a previous MS study on myeloma-derived IgE [19], which identified a Hex5HexNAc4dHex glycan, corresponding to a core-fucosylated hybrid-type structure, attached to Asn394.

Because core fucosylation is catalyzed exclusively by fucosyltransferase 8 in the Golgi, its occurrence on high-mannose or hybrid-type glycans is unusual. Such structures have been reported only under specific conditions, such as in GnT-I-deficient cells or in the presence of glycan-processing inhibitors [20–24]. Therefore, our findings suggest that a small fraction of IgE-Fc may undergo atypical core fucosylation even under normal mammalian expression.

It is possible that the glycosylation profiles obtained from Expi293F cells may not fully reflect those of endogenous IgE produced by B cells, particularly at complex-type glycosylation sites, where cell-type-specific differences in galactosylation and sialylation are known to occur [25, 26]. However, previous site-specific analyses of serum-derived human IgE have consistently shown that the Asn394 site is predominantly occupied by high-mannose-type glycans (e.g., Man2–Man9) [12], supporting the notion that this feature is conserved across expression systems. Therefore, while the overall glycan heterogeneity may be greater in vivo, it seems likely that the high-mannose character of the Asn394 glycan and the non-selective pairing behavior observed in this study reflects intrinsic properties of IgE-Fc biosynthesis.

Glycosidase treatment and thermal stability analysis of WT IgE-Fc

LC–MS analysis of N-glycans confirmed the presence of a high-mannose-type glycan at Asn394 of WT IgE-Fc. To assess the contribution of this glycan to the structural stability of IgE-Fc, we measured the thermal denaturation temperature (Tm) of untreated, Endo H-treated, and PNGase F-treated IgE-Fc by using the SYPRO Orange fluorescent probe assay.

Endo H selectively cleaves high-mannose-type glycans, whereas PNGase F hydrolyzes essentially all N-linked glycans. SDS–PAGE showed distinct band shifts of IgE-Fc after treatment with either enzyme (Fig. 3A), confirming efficient deglycosylation. Thermal denaturation analysis revealed that the Tm of untreated WT IgE-Fc was 61.2 °C. Endo H treatment reduced the Tm by 7.5 °C to 53.7 °C, while PNGase F treatment decreased the Tm by about 7.2 °C to 54.0 °C (Fig. 3B and C).

These results indicate that the high-mannose-type glycan at Asn394 is a major contributor to IgE-Fc stability. Specific removal of this glycan by Endo H markedly decreased thermal stability, and additional removal of other N-glycans by PNGase F did not further reduce Tm significantly. This suggests that the high-mannose glycan at Asn394 plays a unique stabilizing role, probably reflecting specific structural features of the Fc region and its glycan environment that are distinct from other immunoglobulin isotypes. Further structural and dynamic studies will be required to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this glycan-dependent stabilization.

Intact mass analysis of reduced IgE-Fc

To characterize the glycoform heterogeneity of IgE-Fc, we determined the mass of intact WT and Triple mutant proteins under reducing and denaturing conditions (Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5). The IgE-Fc samples were treated with guanidine hydrochloride and DTT to ensure denaturation and complete disulfide bond reduction prior to LC–MS measurement.

For the Triple mutant of IgE-Fc, a molecular species was detected at 38,239 Da (calculated 38,240 Da), corresponding to the IgE-Fc protein carrying a high-mannose–type glycan with five mannose residues (Man5GlcNAc2) (Fig. 4). Three higher-mass peaks separated by ~ 162 Da, consistent with stepwise addition of hexose units, were also observed. This mass distribution closely matched the glycan composition observed in LC–MS analysis, confirming that the Triple mutant predominantly carries a homogeneous Man5GlcNAc2 modification and validating the effectiveness of the glycosylation site mutations.

In contrast, the raw ESI-MS spectrum of WT IgE-Fc exhibited broad, unresolved peaks (Supplementary Figure S5), indicative of extensive and complex glycan heterogeneity. This observation indicates that the WT protein exists as a mixture of multiple glycoforms, differing in their glycan structures and processing states. In line with these data, the intact IgE molecule has been reported to exhibit a broad distribution of peaks across a wide m/z range in intact mass analysis, reflecting extensive glycan heterogeneity [27].

Intact mass analysis of non-Reduced IgE-Fc

To investigate the pairing patterns of high-mannose glycans on IgE-Fc, intact mass analysis was performed on the non-reduced Triple mutant, which has preserved inter-heavy chain disulfide bridges (Supplementary Fig. S6 and Fig. 5A). The deconvoluted spectrum exhibited a peak at 76,465 Da (calculated 76,462 Da), corresponding to an IgE-Fc molecule carrying two Man5GlcNAc2 glycans (M5-M5), plus a series of peaks at higher mass spaced by 162 Da, consistent with incremental hexose additions. These data indicate the presence of multiple glycoform combinations, with M6–M6 (M5–M7) and M6–M7 (M5–M8) pairs being the most abundant, in the non-reduced Triple mutant of IgE-Fc.

Using these glycan composition data, we simulated the theoretical distribution of IgE-Fc mass under the assumption that high-mannose glycans at the Asn394 sites are paired in a non-selective statistical manner (Fig. 5B). The simulated profile closely matched the experimental spectrum of the non-reduced Triple mutant, supporting a model in which available high-mannose-type glycoforms are incorporated without strong pairing preference during interchain disulfide bond formation (Fig. 5C).

This interpretation is consistent with studies on the biosynthesis of immunoglobulins such as IgM, where interchain disulfide bond formation occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum while N-glycans remain in a high-mannose form, and subsequent processing into complex-type glycans takes place in the Golgi apparatus. Notably, the conserved glycosylation site corresponding to Asn394 in IgE (Asn402 in IgM) also retains high-mannose glycans due to limited accessibility within the assembled Fc structure rather than selective processing preferences [28–30]. In contrast, non-random and asymmetric pairing of complex-type glycans between heavy chains has been reported for IgG [31, 32], reflecting a more regulated process of glycan maturation and remodeling after inter-heavy chain association. These differences suggest that, whereas IgG Fc glycosylation undergoes selective processing at later stages, the high-mannose glycans at Asn394 in IgE-Fc are primarily shaped by structural accessibility constraints.

Collectively, these results support a model in which IgE-Fc carrying high-mannose–type glycans forms non-selective statistical glycan pairs via interchain disulfide bridges, followed by further maturation at N-glycosylation sites other than Asn394 to yield complex-type structures. Importantly, this study provides the first direct MS evidence that the pairing of high-mannose glycans at Asn394 reflects the distribution of available glycoforms rather than selective assembly, laying the groundwork for understanding the temporal coordination of N-glycan trimming, disulfide bond formation, and folding during the biosynthesis of IgE-Fc.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully expressed and purified a mutant of IgE-Fc that retains only the N-glycan at Asn394, which is essential for secretion and structural stability. Analysis of enzyme-released N-glycans demonstrated that the Asn394 glycan predominantly adopts a high-mannose–type structure, with Man5GlcNAc2 (M5) being the most abundant species. Intact MS of both reduced and non-reduced forms revealed non-selective interchain pairing of high-mannose glycans, forming combinations such as M6–M6 and M5–M7. Thermal shift assays showed that removal of these high-mannose glycans significantly decreased the melting temperature of IgE-Fc, underscoring their stabilizing role.

Collectively, our findings clarify the structural composition and functional importance of the Asn394-linked N-glycan, providing key insights into how site-specific glycosylation governs IgE-Fc folding, stability, and efficient secretion. Future investigations will aim to determine how the high-mannose glycan at Asn394 structurally stabilizes IgE-Fc and how its processing is temporally coordinated with interchain disulfide bond formation during biosynthesis. In addition, it will be important to establish how such glycan pairing influences IgE effector functions, including receptor binding and downstream signaling. Such studies will deepen our mechanistic understanding of IgE maturation and may ultimately reveal new strategies to regulate IgE production in disease.
