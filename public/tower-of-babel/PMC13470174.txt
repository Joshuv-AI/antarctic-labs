Regulatory roles and translational potential of the Kruppel-like factor family in liver disease
Authors: Hepu Chen, Shusheng Huang, Zhigang Zhou, Jialin Li, Qing Peng, Jian Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Kruppel-like factor (KLF) family comprises transcription factors that share conserved zinc-finger domains and orchestrate key pathophysiological processes in the liver, including glucose and lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and fibrogenesis. In this review, we systematically dissect the roles of major KLF members—specifically KLF2–KLF10, KLF14, KLF15, and KLF16—in the pathogenesis of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), liver fibrosis/cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). We discuss several key findings. First, KLF16 drives fatty acid β-oxidation by activating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), whereas KLF4 and KLF2 suppress de novo lipogenesis through the inhibition of SREBP-1c. Second, with respect to hepatic stellate cell activation, KLF6 and KLF5 promote fibrogenesis, whereas KLF14 has anti-fibrotic effects through the upregulation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ). Third, in HCC, KLF2, KLF4, and KLF6 act as tumour suppressors, and are frequently epigenetically silenced, whereas KLF5, KLF7, and KLF8 function as oncogene products, by modulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling, glycolysis, and immune evasion. Last, emerging modalities, such as proteolysis-targeting chimeras and cell type-specific nanoparticle delivery, are described. These represent potential new avenues for KLF-targeting therapy. By delineating these context-dependent mechanisms, we provide a theoretical framework for the development of KLF-based precision strategies for the treatment of major liver diseases.

Introduction

Liver disease is one of the primary causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and encompasses a spectrum of conditions, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).1 The liver is the body's central metabolic organ, responsible for regulating key physiological processes, including glucose and lipid homeostasis, protein synthesis, and the detoxification of exogenous substances.2 When subjected to persistent pathological stressors, such as obesity, viral infection, or metabolic stress, liver homeostasis is disrupted, triggering the development of progressive disease, comprising a continuum from simple steatosis to inflammatory injury, fibrosis, and HCC.3 Despite significant advances in the therapeutic strategies for MASLD and HCC during recent years, the existing drugs exhibit substantial heterogeneity in their efficacy, are insufficiently specific in their targets, and are associated with notable side effects. This underscores the urgent need to obtain deeper insight into the molecular switches governing the progression of liver disease, in order to identify more promising targets for interventions.

An intricate network regulates hepatic fate, in which transcription factors serve as the master regulators of gene expression, thereby playing a pivotal role. The Kruppel-like factor (KLF) family comprises a group of highly conserved zinc-finger DNA-binding proteins, and are key players. They specifically recognize the CACCC box or GC-rich regions within target gene promoters, thereby activating or suppressing transcription.4,5 Since the discovery of this family, extensive research has confirmed that KLF family members are extensively involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and stress responses, and have particularly significant roles in the maintenance of metabolic homeostasis and cardiovascular disease.6, 7, 8 In recent years, the role of the KLF family in the liver has become a key area of research.2 Multiple KLF family members have been shown to influence the progression of MASLD by regulating hepatic lipid synthesis and oxidation9,10 and to modulate the reversibility of liver fibrosis by mediating the activation and transition of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) from the quiescent state.11 Moreover, the KLF family has complex dual regulatory properties in the malignant progression of HCC.12, 13, 14 This functional versatility, coupled with cell-type and stage-specific expression, positions KLFs as critical nodes linking hepatic metabolism, inflammation, and neoplasia.15 However, despite the increasing number of studies of individual KLF family members, there is still no systematic framework that integrates the findings made to date.

Here, we systematically review the regulatory roles of KLF2–KLF10, KLF14, and KLF15 in liver disease, focusing on MASLD, fibrosis, and HCC. We dissect their effects on lipid metabolism, inflammation, proliferation, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, providing a theoretical basis for KLF-based precision therapies.

Biological characteristics and expression levels of KLF members in the liver

Biological characteristics and classification of KLF members

KLFs share a conserved C-terminal region containing three tandem C2H2 zinc-fingers that mediate sequence-specific DNA binding to GC-rich or CACCC elements.16 The C-terminal zinc-finger domains of all the KLF family members are highly homologous and responsible for DNA binding, whereas the N-terminal regulatory domains are highly variable and determine whether they are transcriptional activators or repressors.17

Based on structural and functional differences, the KLF family can be divided into various subfamilies. For example, KLF1, KLF2, KLF4, and KLF5 typically act as transcriptional activators, functioning by recruiting coactivators such as the histone acetyltransferase EP300,18 whereas KLF3 and KLF8 mediate transcriptional repression by binding to the corepressor C-terminal binding protein (CtBP) via specific motifs in their N-termini. Although some members have structural similarities, they have diverse functions. For instance, both KLF2 and KLF4 possess acidic activation domains, but the former is crucial for vascular homeostasis,19 whereas the latter is primarily involved in cell cycle arrest.20 Furthermore, KLF5 often antagonizes the effects of KLF4, promoting smooth muscle cell proliferation.21 KLF3 fine-tunes erythrocyte differentiation through competitive interactions with DNA binding sites.22

Other members also possess specific functions, owing to their unique domains: KLF6 contains a transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) response element in its N-terminus, which participates in fibrosis,4,23 and variations in the zinc-finger region of KLF9 enable it to regulate the expression of circadian rhythm genes.24 KLF10 and 11 mediate the downstream suppression of TGF-β signaling through interaction with TGF-beta-inducibleearlygene (TIEG) boxes.25 Polymorphisms in KLF14 are associated with lipid metabolism disorders,26,27 and KLF15 regulates gluconeogenesis through a leucine zipper structure.28 This diversity in structure and function allows the KLF family to play precise and complex regulatory roles in cell proliferation, differentiation, and homeostasis.29

Expression patterns and roles of KLF members in various hepatic cell types.

In hepatocytes, KLF15 maintains metabolic homeostasis by regulating gluconeogenesis and fatty acid oxidation genes. KLF6 functions as a tumour suppressor through cell cycle and apoptosis regulation, and its aberrant expression correlates with HCC progression and prognosis.12,40 KLF4 has contrasting effects: it reduces lipid accumulation by inhibiting sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) and regulates epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), but under certain conditions, it may promote inflammation.51 KLF5 participates in lipid metabolism and forms a feedback loop with lactate dehydrogenase A (LDH-A) that promotes glycolysis and exacerbates fibrosis.52 KLF7 is highly expressed in HCC, promoting tumour progression and metastasis through toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and protein tyrosine kinase 2 (PTK2).42 KLF3, KLF9, and KLF10 contribute to hepatic lipid metabolism and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) development.53,54

HSC activation is accompanied by marked changes in KLF expression. Quiescent HSCs primarily express KLF2 and KLF4, whereas KLF6 rises sharply upon activation, driving transcription of extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as collagen.23,34 KLF5 accelerates HSC proliferation and migration through pathways including the LDH-A feedback loop, exacerbating fibrosis.52 In contrast, KLF14 inhibits HSC activation via peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ)-regulated pathways, ameliorating fibrosis.55

In Kupffer cells, KLF expression correlates with immune polarisation. Pro-inflammatory M1 polarisation is associated with high KLF4 and KLF5 levels, which activate nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and the NACHT, LRR and PYD domains-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome 56, 57, 58. Conversely, KLF9 and KLF10 support anti-inflammatory M2 polarisation, restraining excessive inflammation.

In other liver cell types, single-cell sequencing reveals that KLF2 is specifically enriched in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), where it maintains vascular homeostasis and metabolism.59 KLF2 also slows hepatocarcinogenesis by negatively regulating Hedgehog signaling in hepatocytes.13 This cell-type-restricted expression allows fine-tuning of hepatic processes, and its disruption directly contributes to disease. Regulation of hepatic function by KLF family members

KLF family members integrate nutritional, hormonal, oxidative stress, and circadian signals into a complex transcriptional regulatory network that governs diverse hepatic functions, wherein KLF15 directly activates the promoters of rate-limiting gluconeogenic enzymes to maintain fasting blood glucose,28 KLF14 enhances peripheral and hepatic insulin sensitivity by increasing PPARγ transcriptional activity,4,60 KLF16 upregulates PPARα to promote fatty acid β-oxidation, and its deficiency leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and triglyceride accumulation,9 while KLF4 counterbalances these effects by suppressing SREBP-1c transcription and reducing lipid synthesis, collectively establishing the central role of the KLF/PPAR/SREBP axis in hepatic energy metabolism.61 In the realm of detoxification and regeneration, KLF6 and KLF9 modulate cytochrome P450 isoform expression,62,63 KLF4 inhibits cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase to limit excessive bile acid production and protect against cholestatic injury,64 and during liver regeneration, KLF4 drives quiescent hepatocyte re-entry into the cell cycle through direct repression of p21, whereas KLF6 restricts excessive proliferation via the TGF-β/Smad pathway to prevent fibrogenesis,65 while KLF2 maintains the fenestral integrity of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells to provide a supportive microenvironment for hepatocyte proliferation66; moreover, in mesenchymal stem cells, KLF2 and KLF4 preserve stemness by sustaining pluripotency-associated protein expression, whereas KLF15 drives chondrogenic differentiation, and these observations suggest that ex vivo overexpression of KLF2 or KLF4, or transient suppression of KLF15, can enhance MSC engraftment or promote hepatocyte-like differentiation, and when combined with hydrogel-based delivery systems, this approach holds promise as a novel strategy to augment liver regeneration following major hepatectomy.67, 68, 69 The expression and activity of KLF family members are further stringently regulated by circadian rhythms, developmental cues, and epigenetic modifications, as exemplified by the transcriptional regulation of KLF10 and KLF11 by the core circadian clock gene BMAL1,70 and by KLF6-mediated histone acetylation that modulates chromatin accessibility of downstream targets23; it is through this multilayered, dynamic, and finely balanced regulatory network that the KLF family sensitively responds to endogenous and exogenous cues, thereby collectively sustaining liver homeostasis and health.

Regulatory roles of KLF members in metabolic-associated fatty liver disease

KLFs contribute substantially to the initiation and progression of MASLD by modulating lipid synthesis, fatty acid oxidation, bile acid metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses.71

Regulation of lipid metabolism by KLFs

KLF family members regulate hepatic lipid metabolism through a fine-tuned network encompassing fatty acid synthesis, oxidation, and export. KLF16 directly binds and activates the PPARα promoter, promoting fatty acid β-oxidation and limiting lipid accumulation.9,11 KLF16 is downregulated in MASLD patients and high-fat diet-fed mice; its liver-specific knockout impairs fatty acid oxidation, causing mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and exacerbated steatosis and insulin resistance, whereas overexpression in db/db or high-fat diet rodents reverses lipid accumulation through PPARα activation.9

KLF15 exhibits complex, context-dependent effects. In ob/ob mice, KLF15 overexpression reduces hypertriglyceridae by inhibiting sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c), whereas in high-fat diet rodents, KLF15 deficiency promotes fatty acid oxidation via the AMPK/PGC-1α/CPT1A axis, alleviating steatosis. These opposing outcomes suggest that KLF15 function depends on the specific metabolic state.2,72

Lipid synthesis is restrained by several KLFs. KLF2 inhibits SREBP-1c transcription, reducing downstream FAS and stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) expression and de novo lipogenesis. KLF4 overexpression in palmitic acid-induced hepatocyte steatosis reduces triglycerides and total cholesterol while increasing high-density lipoprotein cholesterol.73 KLF6 affects lipid export by epigenetic regulation of the Ras-associated protein RAB31, influencing lipid droplet formation and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) secretion.74

Genetic susceptibility also plays a role: KLF14 polymorphisms are significantly associated with MASLD risk. KLF14 modulates insulin sensitivity and the balance between lipid synthesis and breakdown through PPARγ, representing a key link between genetic background and metabolic phenotype.46

Regulation of bile acid metabolism by KLFs

Members of the KLF family also regulate bile acid metabolism, which is functionally connected with lipid metabolism. For example, ι-carrageenan tetrasaccharides can promote the conversion of cholesterol to bile acids by upregulating CYP7A1 expression through inhibition of Farnesoid X Receptor/Small Heterodimer Partner (FXR/SHP) signaling. Additionally, transcription factors such as KLF10 may regulate gene networks associated with bile acid metabolism. However, whether

ι-Carrageenan tetrasaccharide (ιCTs) have their effects via KLF10 requires confirmation. Similarly, certain dietary components, such as rice protein peptides, ameliorate lipid disorders by modulating the balance between PPARα and γ and altering the gut microbial composition to favor short-chain fatty acid production. These systemic changes may indirectly affect the expression of KLF family members in the liver, thereby forming part of the gut–liver axis.75

Effects of KLFs on oxidative stress

Oxidative stress represents a key link between lipid accumulation and inflammatory injury, and the KLF family is an important regulator of this process. The KLF16/PPARα pathway not only increases fatty acid oxidation but also improves mitochondrial function and curbs ROS production.9 Additionally, KLF4 expression is low in the lungs of patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). The overexpression of KLF4 significantly alleviates bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice by inhibiting the activity of hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) and endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) through a mechanism involving the transcriptional suppression of HIF-1α expression by KLF4 (the JASPAR database predicts five binding sites for KLF4 within the HIF-1α promoter region), thereby reducing the expression of p-IRE1α and ATF6, which are involved in ERS. This reduces pulmonary fibrosis through effects on the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, macrophage polarisation, and inflammatory responses. Similarly, KLF4 has protective effects against liver disease, suggesting that the KLF4/HIF-1α/ERS axis may be a common regulatory mechanism in both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. This provides a theoretical basis for the development of therapies targeting KLF4.76 The AMPK/KLF10 axis transcriptionally represses SREBP-1c, providing a potential therapeutic target for MASLD.61,64

Regulation of inflammation and fibrosis by KLFs

KLF family members play crucial roles in liver inflammation and fibrosis in both parenchymal and non-parenchymal cells by regulating macrophage and Kupffer cell polarisation. KLF4 inhibits nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling and drives macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, reducing the release of tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6).77 Conversely, KLF5 amplifies inflammation by activating the NACHT, LRR and PYD domains-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and promoting interleukin-1β (IL-1β) maturation.58 KLF9, a direct target of CLOCK/BMAL1, exhibits circadian expression in the liver and interacts with Rev-erbα, potentially participating in circadian regulation of hepatic drug-metabolising enzymes; however, its precise roles in metabolic disorders require further clarification given its dual antioxidant and pro-oxidative properties.70,78

Liver sinusoidal endothelial cell (LSEC) dysfunction amplifies inflammation in MASLD. KLF4 maintains endothelial barrier function by transcriptionally regulating VE-cadherin expression; its loss increases vascular permeability and systemic inflammation.79 In the intestinal epithelium, KLF4 improves NF-κB/miR-34a-mediated permeability, mitigating endotoxemia and atherosclerosis.80 KLF4 may regulate LSEC function through analogous mechanisms, influencing MASLD progression, inflammatory infiltration, and fibrosis.

In summary, the KLF family affects lipid metabolism, inflammation, and fibrosis, offering potential therapeutic targets for MASLD/NASH. Compared with agents targeting single pathways such as PPARγ agonists, KLF modulation may exert broader effects.81 For instance, activating the KLF16/PPARα axis promotes lipid clearance, while upregulating KLF4 or inhibiting KLF5 confers anti-inflammatory effects.9 However, challenges including tissue-specific delivery and long-term safety remain.82 Emerging nanoparticle technologies, for example cationic lipid nanoparticles and GalNAc-conjugated carriers, enable hepatocyte-specific siRNA delivery via asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR)-mediated endocytosis.83 These systems have shown superior targeting efficiency in acute liver injury and fibrosis models and offer potential delivery solutions for KLF-targeted therapies, though further research is needed to achieve selective targeting of specific KLF members in hepatocytes.

Regulatory roles of the KLF family in liver fibrosis and cirrhosis and the mechanisms involved

Liver fibrosis results from an imbalance in ECM production and degradation, with HSC activation as the central event. KLFs regulate HSC phenotype at multiple levels.

Heterogeneous expression of KLFs in the various HSC subpopulations

Single-cell RNA-seq has identified at least three HSC subpopulations in normal and fibrotic livers: quiescent (qHSC), inflammatory-like (aHSC-Inf), and myofibroblastic (aHSC-Myo). KLF2 and KLF4 are enriched in qHSCs and decline upon activation. KLF6 is induced in both activated subsets but is particularly abundant in aHSC-Myo, where it drives collagen transcription. KLF5 peaks in proliferative aHSC-Inf cells, consistent with its pro-inflammatory and pro-proliferative roles. KLF14 is predominantly expressed in a small subset of qHSCs that retain high PPARγ, suggesting that these cells represent a regeneration-competent reservoir whose loss favours fibrosis.59,84

Regulation of classical and key signaling pathways by KLFs

The TGF-β/Smad pathway is the most critical fibrogenic pathway, and it extensively interacts with KLF family members. KLF6 has been shown to directly bind to and enhance the transcriptional activity of TGF-β1, thereby amplifying Smad 2/3 phosphorylation.65 Conversely, KLF14 inhibits TGF-β/Smad signaling by upregulating PPARγ expression, which negatively affects HSC activation.46 The role of KLF4 in this pathway is cell type-specific: it inhibits fibrogenic inflammation in macrophages, whereas it is downregulated in hepatocytes secondary to promoter hypermethylation, which promotes the expression of molecules involved in EMT, such as alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), thereby indirectly accelerating fibrosis.85

Platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) is the most potent mitogen driving HSC proliferation and migration,86 but KLF4 can antagonize the fibrogenic effects of PDGF by inhibiting downstream signal transduction.87

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway plays an important role in maintaining HSC activation. KLF5 can promote the nuclear translocation of β-catenin by stabilizing the β-catenin protein, which causes the transcription of proliferative and fibrogenic target genes.88 However, KLF15 competitively binds to the transcription factor TCF4, inhibiting the transcription of Wnt target genes.89

Epigenetic regulation by KLFs

KLF6 recruits histone acetyltransferases to promote H3K9 acetylation at target genes.90 Beyond this, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA methylation critically regulates hepatic stellate cell (HSC) fate. The m6A reader protein insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3) directly binds to m6A-modified sites within jagged 1 (Jag1) mRNA, preventing its degradation and sustaining Notch signaling. This leads to upregulation of hairy and enhancer of split 1 (HES1), a transcriptional repressor that occupies the glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) promoter and suppresses its transcription. Reduced GPX4 expression relieves the brake on ferroptosis; however, when this axis is constitutively active, for instance with KLF14 deficiency or IGF2BP3 overexpression, GPX4 remains elevated, HSC ferroptosis is blocked, and the fibrogenic phenotype persists. Conversely, HSC-specific deletion of Igfbp3 destabilises Jag1 mRNA, attenuates Notch/HES1 signaling, derepresses GPX4, and sensitises HSCs to ferroptotic cell death, thereby ameliorating fibrosis.91 Collectively, these findings position KLF14 as an upstream modulator of the IGF2BP3/Notch/JAG1/GPX4/ferroptosis axis and uncover a post-transcriptional layer of HSC regulation that may be therapeutically exploited by combined targeting of KLF14 and m6A-dependent RNA metabolism. Additionally, ASH1L-mediated histone H3K4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) increases C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) and colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1), promoting M2 macrophage polarisation.92

Regulation of oxidative stress and inflammation by KLFs

KLF family members also regulate the redox state and inflammatory responses within HSCs.93 KLF2 reduces ROS by inhibiting NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) expression, while KLF9 enhances antioxidant defences via nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2).1,94 Regarding inflammation, KLF9 influences the inflammatory state of HSCs by modulating the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.95

Regulation of metabolism and ECM remodeling by KLFs

Liver fibrosis results from an imbalance between the synthesis and degradation of the ECM. KLF family members play key roles in this balance by directly or indirectly regulating the synthesis and cross-linking of ECM components such as collagen, as well as degradative enzymes.

Regulation of ECM degradation and cross-linking by KLFs

The KLF family regulates the balance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase (TIMPs) in a specific spatiotemporal fashion. In activated HSCs, KLF6 upregulates TIMP-1 expression and inhibits MMP-13 activity, thereby promoting fibrotic septum formation.96,97 Conversely, hepatocyte-derived KLF10 enhances the activity of membrane-type MMP-14 (MT1-MMP) through a PPARγ-dependent pathway, thereby accelerating the clearance of abnormal ECM.98 Furthermore, KLF4 directly binds to the enhancer region of the gene encoding lysyl oxidase-like 2 (LOXL2), promoting the cross-linking of collagen fibers, which makes them more resistant to degradation, a crucial step in the formation of cirrhotic nodules.

Regulation of physical properties and the circadian rhythm by KLFs

KLF15 deficiency is associated with the disorganization of elastic fibers, which affects vascular wall remodeling.99 In addition, KLF9 forms a complex with the core circadian clock protein BMAL1, causing the periodic inhibition of transcription of the gene encoding connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), which implies that ECM deposition may be subject to circadian regulation.70,100,101

In conclusion, KLF family members constitute a precise network that regulates HSC activation and ECM metabolism by integrating the effects of key signaling pathways, such as the TGF-β/Smad, PDGF, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, with epigenetic mechanisms including histone modification and RNA methylation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses. Individual KLF family members may have antagonistic effects within the same pathway, and their effects are cell type- and disease stage-specific, highlighting the complexity and importance of this family in liver fibrosis. Future research should make use of technologies such as single-cell sequencing to further analyze the differences in KLF expression profiles across HSC subpopulations and disease stages. Precise intervention strategies based on the tissue specificity and functional plasticity of KLFs, such as the targeted delivery of nucleic acid-based drugs or small molecules that regulate specific KLFs, may provide promising means of reversing liver fibrosis.

Regulatory roles of KLF family members in HCC

KLF family members exert complex, often opposing roles in HCC development, progression, and therapy resistance, influencing cell cycle, apoptosis, EMT, metabolic reprogramming, and the tumour microenvironment.

Targeting KLFs that affect cell death and proliferation

The KLF family is a key hub regulating the fate (proliferation and apoptosis) of HCC cells, and the mechanisms are member-specific and context-dependent. KLF2 is epigenetically silenced by long non-coding RNAs (ANRIL, TUG1), relieving cell cycle inhibition and promoting apoptosis resistance.102,103 KLF4 cooperates with p53 to induce p21-mediated arrest.103 KLF6 downregulation correlates with poor prognosis; it inhibits cyclin D1, but its oncogenic splice variant antagonises this effect.104,105 KLF10, a TGF-β effector, inhibits KLF5 and enhances KLF6-dependent apoptosis.106 KLF5 activates Wnt/β-catenin, upregulates cyclin D1 and c-Myc, and represses Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax).107, 108, 109 KLF8 promotes invasion via EMT and MMP activation.110 KLF13 upregulates HMGCS1 in cholesterol synthesis, supporting rapid proliferation.111

Regulation of the tumour immune and inflammatory microenvironment by KLFs

The KLF family profoundly influences the immune landscape and inflammatory state of HCCs by regulating both tumour and immune cells within the microenvironment, thereby affecting tumour progression and metastasis.

Regulation of tumour-associated inflammation by KLFs

Oncogenic KLFs shape a pro-tumour inflammatory milieu. KLF5 activates the IL-6/STAT3 pathway, promoting tumour cell survival and recruiting immune cells.108,112 KLF11 is highly expressed in tumour-associated macrophages, which further increases the release of proinflammatory molecules.113 KLF7 forms a positive feedback loop with high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), amplifying PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling through TLR4 and RAGE, thereby fostering metastasis.42

Regulation of immune cell function by KLFs

KLF4 plays a key role in macrophage regulation. A previous study showed that KLF4 expression in macrophages induces M2 polarisation.36 However, in the complex HCC microenvironment, various cytokines and signaling interactions may interfere with this process, potentially weakening the anti-inflammatory and anti-tumour effects of M2 macrophages and affecting disease progression and therapeutic outcomes.

Mechanistically, KLF4, together with signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), forms a transcriptional cooperativity network that orchestrates M2 polarisation, a program frequently hijacked by HCC to establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment.114 Upon exposure to tumour-derived factors, interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-13 (IL-13) activate JAK-mediated phosphorylation of STAT6 at Tyr641, promoting its nuclear translocation and binding to response elements in M2-associated gene promoters. Concurrently, ligand-activated PPARγ forms a physical and functional complex with STAT6. KLF4 acts as a critical transcriptional integrator: its zinc-finger domains facilitate cooperative DNA binding at composite regulatory elements, and its N-terminal activation domain recruits the coactivator p300 to enhance histone acetylation and transcriptional elongation at M2 target loci, including Arg 1, Mrc1 (CD206), Retnla, and Chil 3. Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays confirm that STAT6, PPARγ, and KLF4 co-occupy regulatory regions of these genes, with KLF4 stabilizing STAT6 DNA binding and enhancing PPARγ-mediated transactivation.

This cooperativity establishes a feed-forward loop in which KLF4 not only reinforces M2-related gene expression but also sustains PPARγ expression itself, creating a self-amplifying circuit that locks macrophages into an anti-inflammatory, protumorigenic phenotype. Disruption of this axis, for example through genetic ablation of KLF4, pharmacological inhibition of STAT6 phosphorylation, or PPARγ antagonism, destabilises the complex and shifts cells toward M1-like polarisation.115,116 In HCC, tumour-derived factors maintain this program in tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), enabling secretion of C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and other immunosuppressive mediators that support tumour growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis. Importantly, perturbation of cellular redox homeostasis via SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis disrupts this transcriptional axis, reducing p-STAT6, PPARγ, and KLF4 levels and reprogramming TAMs toward an antitumour phenotype.117, 118, 119 These findings establish the STAT6/PPARγ/KLF4 axis as a master transcriptional hub that integrates cytokine signals, lipid metabolism, and redox state to dictate macrophage identity, positioning it as a potential therapeutic node for HCC and other malignancies driven by M2-polarised TAMs.

Beyond macrophages, the effects of KLF family members on adaptive immunity, particularly T-cell function, are of considerable interest. However, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood, and comprehensive investigations in this area are warranted.

Epigenetic and metabolic regulatory networks involving KLFs

KLF family members are not only subject to epigenetic regulation, but they also coordinate epigenetic modifications and are involved in the metabolic reprogramming of tumors.

Cross-talk in the epigenetic regulatory network of KLFs

The expression of KLF family members is precisely regulated by non-coding RNAs such as lncRNAs and miRNAs. For example, the lncRNA DANCR stabilizes KLF5 mRNA by competitively binding miR-216a-5p, forming an RNA network that promotes tumour invasion.120 KLF proteins also participate in chromatin remodeling. For instance, KLF6 regulates the transcription of target genes through modifications to histone acetylation.121

KLFs drive metabolic reprogramming

Beyond KLF13-mediated regulation of cholesterol synthesis, other KLF members broadly influence metabolism. For instance, KLF5 and KLF8 activation is associated with upregulated glycolysis to meet the energy and biosynthetic demands of rapidly proliferating tumour cells.

KLF5 functions as a master transcriptional activator of glycolytic reprogramming in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) during fibrogenesis, directly engaging the promoters of glycolytic enzyme genes, including pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDH-A).52 KLF5 binds to specific GC-rich elements within these promoters, recruiting coactivators and enhancing RNA polymerase II occupancy, which drives glycolytic flux, glucose consumption, lactate production, and extracellular acidification in LSECs. Beyond its canonical role in glycolysis, LDH-A translocates to the nucleus, where it physically interacts with KLF5 and stabilizes its binding to target gene promoters, thereby potentiating its transcriptional activity and establishing a positive feed-forward loop. Specifically, KLF5 upregulates LDH-A transcription, and nuclear LDH-A in turn enhances KLF5-mediated transcription of genes encoding hexokinase 2 (HK2), phosphofructokinase 1 (PFK1), and PKM2. This self-amplifying circuit sustains glycolysis, which is essential for LSEC-mediated pathological angiogenesis, characterised by enhanced migration, tube formation, and expression of angiogenic proteins such as angiopoietin-2 (Ang 2) and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFr2). Disruption of this KLF5/LDH-A loop, for instance through pharmacological KLF5 inhibition or genetic LDH-A manipulation, abrogates glycolysis-dependent LSEC angiogenesis and attenuates liver fibrosis. These findings establish KLF5 as a central node integrating transcriptional control of metabolism with pathological vascular remodeling, and reveal a paradigm wherein a metabolic enzyme directly participates in transcriptional regulation to reinforce pathogenic signaling.

KLF14 affects lipid metabolism via peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), which may regulate energy storage and signal transduction in tumour cells.122 This metabolic remodeling underlies HCC cell adaptation to the microenvironment, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. Mechanistically, KLF14 functions as a transcriptional activator of PPARγ, orchestrating lipogenic programs and maintaining hepatic stellate cell (HSC) quiescence, thereby exerting potent anti-fibrotic effects.55,46 KLF14 directly binds to GC-rich motifs within the PPARγ promoter, recruiting coactivators and increasing RNA polymerase II occupancy, which drives PPARγ expression and promotes cytoplasmic lipid droplet accumulation, a hallmark of quiescent HSCs. Functionally, KLF14-mediated PPARγ transactivation reduces HSC activation markers including alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1), inhibits proliferation and migration, induces G2/M phase arrest, and promotes apoptosis. Pharmacological inhibition of PPARγ with GW9662 or genetic silencing of PPARγ abrogates these effects, confirming PPARγ as the essential downstream effector. Notably, KLF14 expression is epigenetically silenced during HSC activation via enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2)-mediated histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) at its promoter, establishing an EZH2/KLF14/PPARγ regulatory axis wherein EZH2 upregulation represses KLF14, leading to PPARγ downregulation and consequent HSC activation. In vivo, adenoviral KLF14 overexpression attenuates thioacetamide-induced liver fibrosis in a PPARγ-dependent manner, and treatment with the EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat (EPZ-6438) reactivates KLF14, restores PPARγ levels, and ameliorates fibrogenesis. In patient biopsies, KLF14 expression positively correlates with PPARγ and inversely with EZH2 and α-SMA, underscoring clinical relevance. These findings establish KLF14 as a master transcriptional rheostat coupling lipogenic regulation with HSC fate determination, and position the EZH2/KLF14/PPARγ axis as a promising therapeutic target for liver fibrosis.46,122

Potential of KLFs as therapeutic targets and drug development

Given the central position of the KLF family in HCC, targeting KLFs has become a highly promising therapeutic strategy, involving gene therapy, small molecule inhibitors, or combination therapies acting at multiple levels.

Restoring the tumour suppressive function of KLFs

Strategies include the use of histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) or DNA demethylating agents to reverse epigenetic silencing, nanocarrier-mediated delivery of wild-type KLF mRNA or plasmids, and CRISPR-Cas9 editing to eliminate oncogenic splice variants.40,105,123

Inhibition of the pro-oncogenic activity of KLFs

For pro-oncogenic members such as KLF5 and KLF8, the principal therapeutic strategies under investigation are specific small molecule inhibitors and gene silencing. For example, a nanoparticle delivery system for encapsulated KLF5 siRNA has been shown to reduce the expression of target genes, including Survivin, and to enhance sensitivity to chemotherapy in preclinical models.105,124 Interfering with KLF8 function can inhibit EMT and MMP activity, thereby blocking invasion and metastasis.125

Emerging pharmacological modalities targeting KLFs

Beyond gene silencing and nucleic acid-based approaches, small-molecule modulators and protein degraders for KLF family members are now entering preclinical and early clinical development. For the oncogenic KLF5, the first-in-class small molecule ML264 potently and selectively inhibits KLF5 expression and is effective against colorectal cancer in vitro and in vivo, and its analogue SR18662 has an IC50 of 4.4 nM.126 Although not yet in clinical trials for liver disease, these compounds provide a chemical template for the development of KLF5-targeting therapies for liver fibrosis and HCC.32 Moreover, the BRD4-specific PROTAC (compound 6b) inhibits basal-like breast cancer by reducing the expression of KLF5, offering a strategy to target KLF5 indirectly via its upstream epigenetic regulator.127 For the tumour suppressor KLF4, the small-molecule inducer APTO-253 (LOR-253) has completed phase I clinical trials for hematologic malignancies and solid tumors, during which it was well tolerated and showed evidence of KLF4 induction.128 Similarly, the clinical-stage HDAC inhibitor mocetinostat activates KLF4 expression and protects against tissue destruction. For KLF2, statins, and particularly simvastatin, are clinically approved activators. Multiple randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that simvastatin reduces the portal pressure and improves the outcomes of patients with cirrhosis through a KLF2-dependent mechanism.66 These examples collectively demonstrate that KLF family members are becoming tractable therapeutic targets, moving the field beyond hypothetical strategies.

Combination therapy and targeted delivery of KLFs

Owing to functional redundancy and compensatory effects within the KLF network, interventions targeting a single member may have limited efficacy. Therefore, strategies targeting multiple KLF members simultaneously (e.g., a KLF5 inhibitor in combination with a KLF6 activator) or use in combination with existing therapies (e.g., immune checkpoint inhibitors and tyrosine kinase inhibitors) holds greater potential.129 Achieving tissue targeting and reducing off-target toxicity are key challenges for the translation of such approaches. The use of ligands specific to HCC or the tumour microenvironment to modify nanocarriers is an important direction in research and development.129

In summary, the KLF family constitutes a multilayered, dynamic regulatory network in HCC. In the future, single-cell multiomics technologies should be used to precisely analyze the expression profiles and functional specificity of KLFs in various HCC subtypes and cellular components of the tumour microenvironment. In addition, highly selective, targetable KLF modulators should be developed and integrated into individualized combination therapy regimens, in order to advance the field from basic discovery to clinical application, thereby potentially providing new precision treatment paradigms for HCC.

Potential strategies to overcome functional redundancy within the KLF network

Functional redundancy among KLF family members is well documented. For instance, KLF1 and KLF3 share an identical CACCC box binding motif and compete for binding sites in erythroid cells22; KLF2 and KLF4 bind overlapping enhancer networks in endothelial cells, and their combined deletion causes vascular failure while single knockouts are tolerated.130 This redundancy could dilute the efficacy of targeting a single KLF, but several complementary strategies can address this challenge.

Multitargeting approaches, including pooled siRNA or shRNA libraries, CRISPR interference, and dual-specificity proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), could simultaneously degrade multiple pathogenic KLFs such as KLF5 and KLF6 in HSCs. An alternative is to target common upstream regulators, as many KLFs converge on shared pathways including transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)/Smad, Wnt/β-catenin, and MEK/ERK. Pharmacological inhibition of these pathways, for example with TGF-β receptor inhibitors already in clinical trials, could modulate entire KLF subnetworks and bypass isoform-specific compensation. A third strategy exploits the divergent N-terminal domains of KLFs, which mediate unique protein-protein interactions, such as those of KLF3, KLF8, and KLF12 with C-terminal binding protein (CtBP), or KLF5 with LDH-A.52 These interfaces offer opportunities for selective small-molecule or peptide interference that does not affect other family members. Context-dependent and cell type-specific targeting provides another layer of precision. Guided by single-cell transcriptomic data, one could focus on the dominant KLF at a given disease stage, for example, KLF6 in activated HSCs versus KLF2 in quiescent LSECs.23,131 When combined with cell type-specific delivery systems, such as asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR)-conjugated nanoparticles for hepatocytes or vitamin A-coupled liposomes for HSCs, this approach minimises compensatory responses by other KLFs. Finally, epigenetic modulators, including histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors and DNA demethylating agents, could rebalance the KLF network by reactivating tumour-suppressive members and repressing oncogenic ones.

Taken together, these complementary strategies include multitargeting, upstream inhibition, isoform-specific interference, context-dependent delivery, and epigenetic reprogramming. They provide a means to overcome KLF redundancy and maximise the clinical utility of KLF-targeting therapies.

Future prospects

KLFs play multilayered roles in liver physiology and pathology, but limitations remain: incomplete functional studies, unclear tissue-specific mechanisms, and lack of clinical translation.132

Several KLF members have dual, context-dependent functions. KLF4 inhibits sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) in hepatocytes but promotes M2 polarisation in Kupffer cells, and is epigenetically silenced in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).36,37,103,133 KLF6 acts as a tumour suppressor in quiescent hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) but drives collagen transcription upon activation;23 a pro-oncogenic splice variant can antagonize its function. KLF10 suppresses lipogenesis via AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) in hepatocytes but promotes extracellular matrix (ECM) clearance through membrane-type matrix metalloproteinase 14 (MMP14) in fibrosis.54,61 Thus, targeting strategies must be tailored to cell types and disease stages, for example, using asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR)-conjugated nanoparticles for hepatocytes, vitamin A-coupled liposomes for HSCs, and antibody-based carriers for Kupffer cells. Deeper understanding of post-translational modifications, cofactor availability, and chromatin state is needed to control beneficial versus harmful KLF effects.

Despite the promise of KLF targeting, several critical challenges remain. Target specificity is a major concern; small molecules or proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) designed to inhibit KLF5 might also affect KLF4 or KLF2,130 and strategies aimed at activating KLF4, for example with APTO-253, could upregulate other KLFs with opposing roles.128 Achieving isoform-selective modulation will likely require targeting the divergent N-terminal domains that mediate unique protein-protein interactions, such as the C-terminal binding protein (CtBP)-binding motif in KLF3, KLF8, and KLF12, or the lactate dehydrogenase A (LDH-A)-interacting region in KLF5. Delivery strategies must also be tailored: ASGPR-conjugated nanoparticles and GalNAc-siRNA conjugates have shown efficacy for hepatocyte-specific delivery in preclinical models;83 vitamin A-coupled liposomes have been successfully used for HSC-targeted antifibrotic agents;134 and antibody-based carriers, for instance targeting CD163, may selectively modulate KLF4 or KLF10 in Kupffer cells.135 On-target off-tumour effects must be carefully evaluated. Activating KLF4 in hepatocytes to reduce steatosis might simultaneously promote M2 polarisation of tumour-associated macrophages in undiagnosed early HCC, potentially fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Similarly, systemic KLF5 inhibition could impair vascular integrity or intestinal homeostasis. To mitigate these risks, local or transient modulation, for example using short-acting siRNAs, degraders with limited half-lives, or inducible gene-editing systems, should be combined with rigorous preclinical safety studies in relevant animal models.

Future research should address three key questions. First, single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics should be used to analyze KLF expression profiles across liver cell types, particularly the spatiotemporal regulation of KLF5 in Kupffer cell polarisation and KLF15 in LSEC.59 Second, synergistic or antagonistic interactions among KLF members, such as bidirectional regulation of KLF4 and KLF8 during epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and cross-talk between KLF10 and the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)/Smad pathway, require comprehensive exploration. Third, the links between KLF biology and clinical liver regeneration need investigation. Prospective studies in patients undergoing portal vein embolization or two-stage hepatectomy should determine whether baseline KLF2, KLF4, or KLF6 expression predicts kinetic growth rate independently of age, steatosis, or portal pressure.136 Functional imaging, for example, mebrofenin uptake, could assess whether KLFs influence not only volume expansion but also functional quality of regenerating hepatocytes.137 Conditional KLF manipulation in animal models combined with longitudinal imaging could test causality, and a validated KLF signature might guide patient selection for more aggressive interventions such as liver venous deprivation and perioperative pharmacological targeting.138

Alongside cell-based strategies, direct pharmacological modulation of KLF activity has shown preclinical promise. A simvastatin nanoparticle formulation alleviates fibrosis via the KLF2/nitric oxide (NO) pathway,138 and colony-stimulating factor 1-Fc (CSF1-Fc) therapy ameliorates fibrosis through macrophage reprogramming.139 Advances in PROTACs are accelerating clinical translation; multiple protein degraders are in phase I/II trials for malignancies, and previously considered “undruggable” transcription factors such as Ikaros and Aiolos are degraded by the PROTAC KT-413 in a phase I trial for B-cell lymphoma.140 Although KLF-specific PROTACs are not yet in clinical development, the success of bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4)-targeting PROTACs in reducing KLF5 expression provides proof-of-concept. Given the central roles of KLF5 in fibrosis and HCC, and KLF2/KLF4 in vascular protection and tumour suppression, developing selective KLF-targeting PROTACs or small molecules should be a high priority. Repurposed drugs (statins), clinical-stage KLF4 inducers (APTO-253), and preclinical KLF5 inhibitors (ML264) provide a multipronged pipeline warranting further investigation.

From a translational perspective, the KLF family holds significant potential. KLF6 restoration could benefit HCC patients,141 and KLF5-targeting siRNA delivery may attenuate fibrosis.38 Nuclear receptor-KLF interaction networks provide a rationale for combination therapy.142 However, functional pleiotropy may cause off-target effects, necessitating the development of liver-targeting delivery technologies134 and validation in organoid models. With advances in gene editing and epigenetic regulation, KLF-targeted precision interventions are expected to become a new paradigm for liver disease treatment.
