Multifunctional electrospun PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH hybrid nanofiber membrane with superior Hg(II) adsorption efficiency and dual anticancer–antioxidant bioactivity
Authors: Yasmeen A S Hameed, Jawza Sh Alnawmasi, Ali Y Alzahrani, Sahar Sallam, Amnah S Al Zbedy, Ibrahim S S Alatawi, Hatem A Al-Aoh, Nashwa M El-Metwaly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe analysis at hand discusses the creation and multifunctional testing of an electrospun nanofiber membrane completed from polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), chitosan (CS), and Al/Cu-layered double hydroxide (Al/Cu-LDH), which is meant for the simultaneous adsorption of Hg(II) ions and the testing of its biological properties. Thorough physicochemical characterization techniques such as FT-IR, XRD, BET surface area analysis, XPS, and SEM-EDS confirmed the effective incorporation of Al/Cu-LDH and CS into the PVDF matrix to arrangement a highly porous membrane with enriched hydroxyl and amino practical groups that are important in binding interactions with metal ions. Batch adsorption studies revealed an appreciable influence of adsorbent quantity, solution pH, and interaction time, with a maximum Langmuir monolayer adsorption capacity of 422.68 mg.g⁻¹ at pH 6 after 100 min and a quantity of 0.02 g. Adsorption kinetics were best fitted to the pseudo-second-order model, whereas thermodynamic study reflected that the adsorption method was spontaneous and endothermic. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) using a Box–Behnken design (BBD) was applied for the statistical optimization of adsorption parameters with a desirability value of 0.99 and a respectable fit among experimental and projected results. The uptake of Hg(II) ions occurs through surface complexation and ion exchange pathways involving –OH and –NH₂ functional groups, as further validated by XPS investigations. Besides its adsorption abilities, the membrane showed significant biological efficiency, presenting dose-dependent cytotoxicity against MCF-7 (breast cancer) besides HePG-2 (liver cancer) cell lines, along with remarkable antioxidant activity in DPPH radical scavenging tests. These results highlight its potential for dual applications in environmental cleanup and biomedical fields. Altogether, the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane comes out as a high-capacity, regenerable, and biologically active material with future prospects for advanced water purification technologies plus multifunctional therapeutic applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54200-0.Keywords: Chitosan, Al/Cu-LDH, Anticancer activity, Electrospun PVDF nanofibers, Response surface methodologySubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Mercury (Hg) is recognized as one of the most hazardous heavy metals present in the environment, attributable to its formidable toxicity, persistence in ecosystems, and propensity for bioaccumulation and biomagnification within both aquatic and terrestrial food webs1. Even at minimal concentrations, mercury represents a significant risk to human health, being associated with a range of severe conditions including neurological, renal, and developmental disorders. The primary anthropogenic sources responsible for mercury contamination in aquatic systems include industrial discharges from processes such as chlor-alkali production, coal burning, metal plating, mining operations, and the manufacturing of batteries and fluorescent lamps. Due to its high mobility, mercury can manifest in various oxidation states, namely elemental (Hg0), mercurous (Hg+), and mercuric (Hg2+), with the latter being the most stable and toxic form in aqueous environments2. Because of this, the efficient removal and recovery of mercury ions (Hg(II)) from industrial wastewater has become a major environmental issue, underscoring the pressing need for the creation of cutting-edge, effective, and sustainable remediation solutions. Chemical precipitation, membrane filtration, ion exchange, electrochemical treatment, and adsorption are the conventional methods used to remove mercury and other heavy metals from wastewater. Among these techniques, adsorption has been found to be particularly successful, flexible, and economical due to its ease of use, potential for reuse, and ability to extract trace amounts of pollutants without causing secondary contamination3. However, the efficacy of traditional adsorbents, including activated carbon, clays, zeolites, and metal oxides, is frequently restricted by factors such as limited surface area, inadequate selectivity, and constrained regeneration capabilities. In order to overcome these constraints, investigators are increasingly concentrating on the development of advanced hybrid nanomaterials that exhibit elevated adsorption capacities, robust structural integrity, and modifiable surface chemistry, thus facilitating the selective capture of mercury ions through various physicochemical interactions4.

Layered double hydroxides (LDHs), commonly referred to as hydrotalcite-like resources, have garnered significant scholarly interest as prospective adsorbents for heavy metal ions. This interest stems from their distinctive two-dimensional lamellar architecture, elevated anion exchange capacity, and plentiful hydroxyl groups that purpose as active places for the coordination of metal ions5. The inherent structural versatility of LDHs facilitates meticulous modulation of their physicochemical characteristics, which encompass surface charge, interlayer spacing, and chemical reactivity. Notably, among the various LDH configurations, bimetallic Al/Cu-LDHs exhibit commendable adsorption properties attributed to the synergistic interaction between Al3+ and Cu2+ ions, which bolsters surface basicity, redox activity, and the affinity for soft metal cations like Hg(II). Additionally, the incorporation of Cu2+ yields supplementary sites that can establish stable surface complexes with mercury species via coordination and ion-exchange processes6. Notwithstanding their exceptional adsorption capabilities, layered double hydroxide (LDH) powders frequently encounter challenges such as particle aggregation, inadequate mechanical strength, and difficulties associated with separation and reuse post-adsorption7,8. To mitigate these concerns, LDHs are commonly immobilized or combined with polymeric substrates to enhance their structural integrity and facilitate their effective utilization in aqueous environments. For creating nanofibrous membranes with high porosity, large surface area, and interconnected pore networks which offer a multitude of active sites for adsorption and filtration applications electrospinning has become a powerful and versatile method. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) is frequently employed as a matrix polymer owing to its remarkable mechanical properties, chemical stability, and resilience against degradation in severe conditions9,10. However, PVDF, when used in isolation, lacks adequate hydrophilicity and active sites requisite for effective binding of metal ions, thus necessitating surface modification or the incorporation of functional additives. Chitosan (CS), a biopolymer derived from chitin, presents a sustainable and eco-friendly approach to modification owing to its abundant amino (–NH2) and hydroxyl (–OH) functional groups, which can interact with metal ions via chelation and hydrogen bonding mechanisms. By integrating Al/Cu-LDHs and chitosan within an electrospun PVDF matrix, it is possible to develop a hybrid nanofibrous membrane that features multifunctional adsorption sites and enhanced surface accessibility, thereby uniting the mechanical fortitude of PVDF with the hydrophilicity and metal-binding traits of chitosan and LDH nanoparticles. This tri-phase nanocomposite architecture not only improves the dispersion and stability of the LDH within the polymeric matrix, but also generates numerous interfacial active sites adept at capturing mercury ions through complexation, ion exchange, and electrostatic forces. Furthermore, the nanofiber membrane’s interlinked porous structure promotes quick mass transfer and effective interaction between the Hg(II) ions and the functional adsorption sites, leading to increased removal efficacy and quick adsorption dynamics11.

Numerous investigations have substantiated the efficacy of LDH-based polymeric composites in the removal of hazardous metal ions counting Cd(II), Hg(II), Pb(II), and Cr(VI)12. However, many of these systems encounter challenges pertaining to limited selectivity, structural degradation, or inadequate reusability. The innovation of the Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane aims to mitigate these issues by harnessing the synergistic interactions between the inorganic and organic constituents. The Al/Cu-LDH component facilitates the presence of active hydroxyl groups and redox-active copper sites that are instrumental in the complexation of mercury13. Concurrently, chitosan introduces amino and hydroxyl functional groups that augment the chemical affinity and adsorption capabilities. The PVDF backbone is critical for providing mechanical strength, durability, and resistance to chemical degradation, enabling the membrane to sustain its performance across continuous-flow or multi-cycle adsorption settings14. Moreover, the process of electrospinning employed in the fabrication of these nanofibrous composites ensures the nanoscale dispersion of the active LDH particles, thereby significantly increasing the number of accessible reactive sites for effective mercury capture. The Hg(II) adsorption mechanism on the Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH membrane is characterized by a synergistic integration of surface complexation, ion exchange, and electrostatic connections. The incorporation of nitrogen and oxygen donor atoms within the chitosan and LDH layers promotes the establishment of stable Hg–N and Hg–O coordination bonds, while the Cu(II) sites embedded in the LDH lattice enhance surface complexation through partial electron transfer. The collective interplay of these mechanisms produces high binding affinity, rapid adsorption kinetics, and strong chemisorption of mercury ions onto the composite’s surface. Furthermore, the hierarchical architecture of the electrospun membrane improves the accessibility to adsorption sites and inhibits particle agglomeration, thereby preserving a high level of efficiency throughout the entirety of the adsorption method15.

The novelty of this study investigated the synthesis, structural characterization, and adsorption performance of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane towards Hg(II), which functions as an innovative multifunctional adsorbent for mercury extraction from aqueous systems. The integration of Al/Cu-LDH and chitosan into the PVDF matrix via electrospinning produces a resilient hybrid structure that demonstrates improved chemical reactivity, mechanical strength, and environmental durability. Comprehensive characterization utilizing methodologies like FT-IR, XRD, BET, SEM-EDX, and XPS confirms the effective synthesis of the composite and clarifies the adsorption mechanisms associated with Hg(II) ions. This research search for to donate to the expansion of advanced nanostructured materials specifically designed for water purification and environmental cleanup, thereby offering a feasible and sustainable strategy for mitigating mercury pollution in industrial discharges.

Preparation of adsorbent

Preparation of Al/Cu-LDH

The production of layered double hydroxide (LDH) materials via the coprecipitation technique adhered to the methodology depicted in Fig. 1. Firstly, solution A was constituted by liquifying 4.59 mmol (0.612 g) of aluminum chloride (AlCl3) alongside 10.34 mmol (1.39 g) of copper chloride (CuCl2) in 60 mL of distilled water. At the same time, 5 mmol of sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) and 27.57 mmol (1.102 g) of sodium hydroxide (NaOH), were dissolved with the help of an extra 60 mL of distilled water, creating solution B. Upon achieving a pH within the range of 9 to 10 for solution A, incremental additions of solution B were performed. This mixture was then maintained under a nitrogen atmosphere and allowed to homogenize at ambient temperature for a duration of 24 h. The resulting solids were subsequently processed into Al/Cu-LDH precipitate by subjecting them to centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10-min intervals, utilizing ethanol and distilled water to eliminate excess ions. Lastly, the solids were dried for six hours at 80 °C in a lab oven16.

Diagrammatic representation of PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane synthesis for Hg(II) adsorption.

Preparation of PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane

Nanofiber membrane assemblies were synthesized through the electrospinning technique, using a polymer blend consisting of Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) and chitosan (CS) in an 80:20 weight ratio. The solution of polymer was formulated at a concentration of 15% weight/volume by dissolving the constituents in a solvent comprising 90% acetic acid, augmented with 5% weight/weight of Al/Cu-LDH)17. A glass syringe with a blunt-tip 20-gauge needle held this final solution. A syringe pump device was used to precisely manage the polymer solution’s flowing rate at 1 mL/h. An electrical voltage between 27 and 28.5 kV was utilized during the electrospinning process, and the needle was placed 15 cm away from the grounded collection plate (Fig. 1).

Removal and batch studies of Hg(II) via PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane

A comprehensive investigation was undertaken to calculate the adsorption efficacy of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane. This study prioritized the examination of numerous factors, including pH levels, the quantity of the adsorbent employed, the original concentrations of Hg(II), the duration necessary to attain equilibrium, and the variations in temperature18. An analytical batch adsorption investigation was directed to evaluate the effectiveness of Hg(II) ion removal. A stock solution was diluted to generate a 25 mL aliquot for the study, which examined adsorption isotherms at different concentrations spanning from 20 to 590 mg/L. During the course of the experiments, the aliquot volume was consistently held at 25 mL, the pH was regulated at 6, an adsorbent mass of 0.02 g was employed, and the duration of contact was standardized at 100 min under a controlled temperature of 25 °C. In addition, to examine the impact of contact time on adsorption efficiency, supplementary experiments were performed utilizing a constant concentration of 400 mg/L, during which the interaction duration was systematically varied between 5 and 100 min, while other temperature and solution parameters were kept constant. At a concentration of 400 mg/L and a set contact period of 100 min, the impact of temperature was also thoroughly evaluated. A constant volume of 25 mL, a pH of 6, and an adsorbent mass of 0.02 g were maintained while the temperature variation was methodically adjusted from 20 to 45 °C. Both sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid solutions, each at 0.1 N, were used for pH modifications in order to maintain constant pH values throughout the methods of experimentation19. The collected data were then analyzed to look at adsorption isotherms and kinetic behaviors, as well as to assess how temperature affected the thermodynamic characteristics related to the adsorption procedures. Before and after the adsorption phase, the concentrations of Hg(II) ions in the solutions were measured applying a Perkin-Elmer flame atomic absorption spectrometer. Results and discussion

Characterization of PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH and Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membranes, as exposed in Fig. 2a, validate the effective combination and structural development of the LDH embedded within the polymeric matrix, both preceding to and subsequent the adsorption of mercury. The unaltered PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH demonstrates characteristic LDH diffraction peaks at approximately 2θ = 11.5°, 23°, 34°, and 60°, which resemble to the (003), (006), (012), and (110) crystallographic planes associated with hydrotalcite-like structures. Additionally, a broad halo is observed around 2θ = 18–20°, indicative of the semi-crystalline β-phase of PVDF. The distinct peaks attributed to the LDH reveal a well-ordered lamellar architecture, confirming the effective integration of Al/Cu-LDH nanoparticles into the PVDF/CS nanofibrous matrix without any compromise to structural integrity. Following mercury adsorption, the Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH pattern exhibits a significant decrease in diffraction intensity and a broadening of the LDH peaks, particularly observable in the (003) and (006) reflections, alongside a slight shift of the basal (003) peak to a lower 2θ value. These alterations are indicative of partial distortion and increased disorder of the LDH layers, which likely arise from the intercalation or surface complexation of Hg(II) ions through the hydroxyl group functionalities of the LDH and the amino groups present in chitosan, resulting in increased interlayer spacing and diminished crystallinity. Notwithstanding this structural change, the continuity of the amorphous halo of PVDF substantiates the stability of the polymeric framework following the incorporation of Hg(II) ions. In summary, the transformations observed through XRD corroborate that mercury ions were effectively immobilized within the composite via ion exchange and coordination mechanisms, culminating in a partially amorphized yet structurally stable Hg-loaded PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane26.

Isotherm of N2 adsorption/desorption

The isotherm of nitrogen adsorption–desorption and the pore structure analysis of PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH and Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membranes are presented in Fig. 2b–d, elucidating the textural characteristics and the effects of Hg(II) adsorption on the surface traits of the composite materials. According to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), both samples exhibit a classic type IV isotherm, as shown in Fig. 2b, indicating that they are mesoporous materials27. The isotherms exhibit a distinct H3-type hysteresis loop, revealing of slit-shaped pores designed due to the aggregation of plate-like LDH nanoparticles in conjunction with the interstitial connections within the PVDF/CS nanofibrous matrix. The PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH composite reveals a relatively elevated nitrogen uptake at increased relative pressures (P/P₀ > 0.8), attesting to its well-developed mesoporous architecture and considerable surface area. In the case of mercury adsorption, the Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH membrane shows a significant decrease in nitrogen adsorption capacity, which implies partial blockage of pores or surface coverage resulting from the Hg(II) adsorption. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was used to calculate the specific surface area, diminishes from 93.21 m²/g for the pristine PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane to 84.36 m²/g following the Hg(II) adsorption, suggesting the occupation of active sites by mercury ions and a corresponding reduction in the accessible surface area. The analysis of pore radius distribution, illustrated in Fig. 2c, substantiates this observation: the average pore radius contracts from 2.44 nm in PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH to 1.88 nm in Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH, thereby indicating a reduction of pore channels due to the deposition and intercalation of mercury species within the galleries of the LDH and the polymer matrix. Moreover, the pore size distribution illustrated in Fig. 2d indicates a decline in total pore volume from 0.168 cm³/g to 0.136 cm³/g subsequent to Hg(II) loading, aligning with indications of partial filling or obstruction of mesopores during the adsorption process. Collectively, these textural alterations evidence that Hg(II) ions engage significantly with the composite surface via chemisorption and ion exchange mechanisms, leading to diminishing characteristics of surface area, pore size, and overall pore volume while preserving the mesoporous structure. Hence, the results derived from the N₂ adsorption–desorption experiments reinforce the structural integrity and enhanced adsorption efficacy of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber for the elimination of mercury, affirming its potential utility in the effective capture of heavy metals27.

FT-IR

The Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra and two-dimensional correlation infrared analyses of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH and Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membranes, as depicted in Figs. 2e–g, offer comprehensive insights into the practical groups that performance a role in mercury adsorption and the associated molecular interactions. In Fig. 2e, the FT-IR spectrum of the unmodified PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane reveals distinct absorption bands that are characteristic of its composite materials. The broad band observed at approximately 3420 cm⁻¹ can be credited to the stretching vibrations associated with –OH groups originating from interlayer water molecules and hydroxyl groups present in the LDH framework. The peaks that appear in the range of 2920–2850 cm⁻¹ are linked to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of –CH₂ groups from both PVDF and chitosan27. The band situated at 1630 cm⁻¹ is related to the bending vibrations of adsorbed H2O molecules as well as the N–H bending vibrations of the amine groups found in chitosan. Absorption peaks that are noted within the range of 1400–1470 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–H bending and O–H deformation vibrations, whereas the pronounced bands located between 1010 and 1100 cm⁻¹ are credited to C–O–C and C–F stretching vibrations, thereby confirming the existence of PVDF and chitosan within the nanofiber matrix. The distinctive peaks associated with the LDH layers are observed around 620 cm⁻¹ and are linked to the stretching vibrations of M–O and M–O–M (specifically Al–O and Cu–O), thus affirming the structural integrity of the Al/Cu-LDH phase embedded in the polymeric composite. Post-mercury adsorption, the FT-IR spectrum of the Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH reveals several significant alterations28. The –OH stretching band broadens and shows a slight shift toward lower wavenumbers, which indicates a robust interaction and hydrogen bonding among Hg(II) ions and the hydroxyl groups located on the surface of the LDH. Concurrently, the N–H bending vibration of chitosan diminishes in intensity and shifts, which signifies coordination among the Hg(II) ions and the nitrogen atoms from the amino groups. Further supporting the creation of Hg–O bonds is a decrease in the intensity of the M–O vibration bands around 620 cm⁻¹, which suggests that complexation and ion exchange mechanisms inside the LDH structure and at the polymer’s active regions immobilize mercury ions29. A more thorough knowledge of these interactions is provided by the two-dimensional association infrared spectra. The asynchronous 2D correlation spectrum illustrated in Fig. 2f displays cross-peaks predominantly situated in regions associated with –OH (3600–3200 cm⁻¹), C–H (2900–2850 cm⁻¹), and C–O/C–F (1400–1000 cm⁻¹) vibrations. This observation suggests that there are sequential and site-specific alterations in these functional groups because of Hg(II) adsorption. The synchronous spectrum illustrated in Fig. 2g reveals pronounced auto peaks within comparable regions, thereby corroborating the simultaneous perturbations of hydroxyl, amino, and carbonyl functional groups. Collectively, these two-dimensional correlation findings substantiate that the adsorption of Hg(II) is characterized by multidentate coordination interactions involving –OH, –NH₂, and oxygen-containing groups, in addition to potential electrostatic interactions with the LDH structure. In summary, the integrated analyses of FT-IR spectroscopy and two-dimensional correlation techniques affirm that the Hg(II) adsorption onto PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH through a synergistic mechanism encompasses surface complexation, and ion exchange, without any indicators of structural degradation within the composite framework26.

EDX analysis

The energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) spectrum obtained for the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane, as demonstrated in Fig. 2h, provides compelling evidence for the actual integration and uniform distribution of all anticipated elements in the compound matrix. The identified elements—carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), fluorine (F), aluminum (Al), and copper (Cu)—align closely with the chemical components of the composite: chitosan contributing C, N, and O; PVDF contributing C and F; and the Al/Cu-LDH phase contributing Al, Cu, and O. Quantitative elemental analysis indicates that fluorine constitutes the majority element, representing 72.01%, which underscores the substantial presence of the PVDF polymer in the composite. The concentrations of carbon (10.85%), oxygen (9.45%), and nitrogen (4.08%) predominantly derive from the chitosan and PVDF constituents, with nitrogen indicating the existence of amino functional groups in chitosan that facilitate active binding places for metal ions. The quantities of metallic elements aluminum (1.92%) and copper (1.7%) corroborate the incorporation of Al/Cu-LDH nanoparticles within the polymeric framework30. The simultaneous observation of metallic peaks alongside organic elements signifies a well-integrated hybrid structure, suggesting the uniform dispersion of LDH nanoplates throughout the PVDF/CS matrix, with no indications of aggregation. The pronounced fluorine signal and the proportionate presence of aluminum and copper peaks further affirm that the nanofiber membrane retains its intended composition and structural integrity post-synthesis. In summary, the EDX analysis substantiates the formation of a chemically homogeneous and compositionally cohesive PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane, with the observed elemental distribution validating the effective integration of LDH into the polymeric host, as well as the existence of active locations amenable for future heavy metal adsorption31.

The EDX analysis of the Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane, depicted in Fig. 2i, provides unequivocal indication of the actual adsorption and immobilization of mercury ions on the composite surface. The EDX spectrum identifies characteristic peaks corresponding to Hg, alongside the fundamental elemental constituents of the composite, namely C, N, O, F, Al, and Cu. This observation indicates that the structural veracity of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH membrane maintains post-adsorption of Hg(II) ions. Quantitative elemental analysis reveals that mercury accounts for 23.49% of the total detected elements, underscoring a significant loading efficiency and a strong empathy of the composite for mercury ions. The substantial presence of Hg, along with discernible peaks at approximately 2.2, 10.0, and 11.5 keV, validates the chemisorption of Hg(II) species onto both the LDH and polymeric active sites. In comparison to the unmodified PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH membrane, the relative proportions of C (decreased to 5.42%), N (3.07%), O (8.75%), and F (55.96%) exhibit slight reductions, indicative of partial surface coverage by adsorbed mercury ions, which diminishes the signal intensities of the lighter elements. The levels of Al (1.71%) and Cu (1.59%) remain nearly constant, suggesting that the LDH framework retains stability and is not leached during the adsorption method. The emergence of pronounced Hg peaks and the concurrent decrease in the ratios of the other surface elements provide compelling evidence that mercury ions are effectively sequestered through mechanisms like ion exchange, surface complexation, and electrostatic interactions with hydroxyl, amino, and oxygen-containing practical groups present on the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH architecture. In summary, the EDX results robustly validate the effective synthesis of the Hg@PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane and affirm its efficacy in mercury adsorption. The high mercury percentage combined with the conservation of the LDH framework integrity highlight the composite’s exceptional chemical stability and strong binding capacity, placing it as a promising applicant for requests in heavy metal removal32.

SEM analysis

The SEM images of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane show a well-structured three-dimensional network of smooth, continuous, and bead-free nanofibers with uniform morphology, indicating effective synthesis through electrospinning. Fiber diameters range from 68.38 to 116.9 nm, with a mean of approximately 81.06 nm, reflecting controlled electrospinning parameters. The nanofibers are randomly oriented and highly interconnected, creating a porous structure that enhances surface accessibility and promotes effective adsorbate diffusion33. At higher magnifications, the fibers’ surfaces appear slightly textured due to the uniform dispersion of PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH. The absence of nanoparticle agglomeration and consistent nanoparticle distribution suggest robust interfacial interactions, likely from hydrogen bonding and electrostatic forces between LDH hydroxyl groups and the chitosan and PVDF amino and fluorinated groups. This results in a highly porous membrane with a large specific surface area, providing numerous active adsorption sites necessary for heavy metal removal. SEM analysis confirms that the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane has a homogeneous, flexible morphology, making it suitable for efficient adsorption of Hg(II) and other environmental remediation applications (Fig. 3a).

The elemental mapping of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane using Scanning Electron Microscopy Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) shows a uniform distribution of elements C, N, O, F, Al, and Cu within the fibrous structure, confirming the successful fabrication of a homogeneous hybrid membrane. The central SEM image depicts a well-interconnected nanofibrous network, while the mapping indicates that carbon and nitrogen from chitosan are evenly dispersed34. The consistent distribution of oxygen atoms associated with functional groups in both chitosan and LDH suggests active moieties that enhance metal ion binding. The prominent fluorine signal makes up about 75% of the elemental composition, highlighting the PVDF phase’s predominance and the support of its fluoropolymer backbone’s structural integrity35. Zero point of charge

The zero point of charge (pHzpc) for the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane, as depicted in Fig. 5a, was established from the intersection of the ΔpH versus pH curve, where ΔpH is equal to zero, resulting in a pHzpc value of 5.3. This outcome proposes that at a pH of 5.3, the membrane’s surface exhibits an overall neutral charge, with an equimolar distribution of positive and negative sites. When the solution pH falls below 5.3, the useful groups present on the membrane surface, namely hydroxyl (–OH) and amino (–NH₂) groups, undergo protonation to form –OH₂⁺ and –NH₃⁺, respectively. This modification results in surface positively charged that electrostatically repels cationic mercury (Hg²⁺) species, thereby diminishing the efficiency of adsorption. Conversely, for pH values exceeding 5.3, the deprotonation of surface functional groups takes place, chief to the formation of negatively charged places (–O⁻ and –NH⁻) that facilitate strong electrostatic attraction and coordination with Hg²⁺ ions. The negative surface potential significantly augments mercury uptake over a synergistic mechanism that incorporates both electrostatic connections and chemical complexation, mediated by the formation of Hg–O and Hg–N bonds. As a result, the adsorption efficiency for Hg(II) markedly increases within the pH range above the pHzpc, particularly in the vicinity of pH 6–8, where the membrane surface adopts a negative charge, and mercury predominantly exists as free Hg²⁺ or hydrolyzed Hg(OH)⁺ species. Hence, conducting the adsorption process at pH levels exceeding 5.3 creates an advantageous environment for the enhanced removal of mercury, ensuring robust binding affinity, elevated adsorption capacity, and chemical stability of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane.

Effect of pH

The influence of pH on the Hg(II) adsorption onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH is exemplified in Fig. 5b. The adsorption capacity (qₑ) exhibited a strong connection with the solution pH, demonstrating a progressive increase from 158.6 mg/g at a pH of 2 to a peak value of 343.2 mg/g at a pH of 6, surveyed by a minor decline at elevated pH levels44. This pattern is closely related to the pHzpc = 5.3 of the composite, suggesting that the surface charge of the adsorbent is a pivotal factor in mercury ion uptake. Below the pHzpc of 5.3, the membrane’s surface retains a positive charge due to the protonation of –OH and –NH₂ functional groups into –OH₂⁺ and –NH₃⁺, resulting in significant electrostatic repulsion among the adsorbent surface and Hg²⁺ ions, which consequently diminishes adsorption efficiency. As the pH ascends beyond the pHzpc, the functional groups on the surface undergo deprotonation to generate negatively charged –O⁻ and –NH⁻ sites, thereby enhancing electrostatic attraction and coordination with the positively charged Hg²⁺ ions. The optimal adsorption recognized at pH 6, marginally above the pHzpc, can be accredited to improved electrostatic attraction and the formation of stable surface complexes between mercury ions and the active oxygen- and nitrogen-containing groups present in chitosan and Al/Cu-LDH. At this pH, Hg²⁺ species, counting Hg²⁺ and Hg(OH)⁺, are predominantly present, facilitating chemisorption through interactions of Hg–O and Hg–N without the precipitation of Hg(OH)₂. Conversely, at higher pH levels (> 7), the slight reduction in adsorption capacity is likely due to the generation of insoluble Hg(OH)₂ or competition among hydrolyzed mercury species, thereby restricting the availability of free Hg²⁺ ions for adsorption. Accordingly, pH 6 is recognized as the optimal condition for mercury removal, providing a favorable equilibrium among surface charge, ion speciation, and the obtainability of active sites, thus maximizing the adsorption efficiency of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane.

Effect of adsorbent quantity

The influence of adsorbent quantity on the adsorption of Hg(II) ions onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane is illustrated in Fig. 5c. The findings substantiate that both the adsorption capacity (qₑ) and the removal efficiency are significantly affected by the quantity of adsorbent utilized. An increase in adsorbent dosage from 0.02 to 0.5 g results in a dramatic decline in adsorption capacity from 287.5 to 17.1 mg/g, while the removal efficiency escalates from 65.7 to 97.7%. The interplay between the concentration of Hg(II) ions in the solution and the presence of sites of activity helps clarify this opposite relationship. At lower dosages, the restricted number of active sites becomes rapidly saturated due to the elevated concentration of Hg(II), which yields a higher adsorption capacity per unit mass. Conversely, as the dosage increases, the aggregate number of available binding sites also escalates, thereby providing an increased surface area and active functional groups for mercury uptake, ultimately enhancing overall removal efficiency. However, the adsorption capacity per gram diminishes, as the Hg(II) ions are dispersed across a more extensive surface area, resulting in many adsorption sites remaining vacant. Furthermore, increased particle aggregation and the overlapping of active sites resulting from higher dosages may diminish effective surface area and impede mass transfer. Beyond the threshold of 0.4 g, the removal efficiency approaches a nearly stable value (~ 97%), indicating the attainment of equilibrium conditions wherein the majority of Hg(II) ions have been effectively captured, and additional quantities of adsorbent contribute negligibly to further removal improvements. As a result, an ideal adsorbent dosage of roughly 0.25–0.30 g is found, balancing high removal efficiency with efficient adsorbent consumption. These results affirm that the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH displays significant surface activity, abundant functional groups, and a pronounced affinity for Hg(II) ions, facilitating efficient mercury removal even at lower dosages45.

Effect of Hg(II) concentration

The impact of the initial concentration of Hg(II) on the adsorption efficacy of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane is depicted in Fig. 5d and detailed in the accompanying data set. Findings show that adsorption capacity (qe) and removal efficiency are inversely related as Hg(II) concentration varies from 25 to 600 mg/L. As the concentration increases, adsorption capacity rises from 23.8 to 422 mg/g, while removal efficiency declines from 96.8 to 56.8%. This is due to the balance between available binding sites and Hg(II) ions during adsorption. At lower concentrations, more active sites are available, promoting interactions and high removal efficiency. In contrast, at higher concentrations, the increased number of Hg(II) ions leads to a stronger concentration gradient, improving mass transfer and allowing more ions to be adsorbed per unit mass, increasing qe. However, when active sites become saturated, more Hg(II) results in competition for those limited sites, lowering removal efficiency. A noticeable decline beyond 300 mg/L indicates the saturation of surface-active sites like –OH and –NH₂, leading to chemisorption via coordination and electrostatic interactions. The rising qe suggests that the adsorption method is characterized by monolayer chemisorption, consistent with the Langmuir isotherm model. An original concentration range of 200 to 300 mg/L is optimal, balancing high adsorption capacity with effective removal efficiency, highlighting the affinity and reactivity of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH membrane toward Hg(II) ions46.

Effect of contact time

The effect of contact duration on the Hg(II) adsorption by the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane shows a rapid adsorption increase. Adsorption capacity (qt) rises quickly in the first few min, reaching equilibrium around 60 min, indicating an effective adsorption mechanism. In the first 30 min, qt escalates from 56.0 mg/g at 5 min to 287.5 mg/g at 30 min, likely due to numerous active places on the membrane’s surface, with hydroxyl (–OH) and amino (–NH₂) groups, available for interaction with Hg(II) ions. As time developments, the adsorption rate slows due to active site occupation and increased electrostatic repulsion among adsorbed and free Hg(II) ions. After 60 min, equilibrium is reached near 424 mg/g, with no significant increase in qt despite extended contact time (Fig. 5e). Kinetic analysis shows that the Hg(II) adsorption onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH membrane involves a biphasic process. It starts with ions adsorbing on the membrane’s surface, followed by a slower phase of intraparticle diffusion or chemisorption as ions penetrate the membrane. The observed equilibrium time of 60 min reflects the nanofiber composite’s strong affinity and swift mass transfer. This efficiency is due to the membrane’s high porosity and ample active sites from layered double hydroxides and chitosan in the PVDF matrix. The quick establishment of equilibrium highlights the system’s suitability for wastewater treatment, especially for fast removal of heavy metal contaminants47.

Effect of temperature

The impact of temperature on the Hg(II) adsorption efficiency by the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane shows a significant increase in removal efficacy as temperature increases, from 64.6% at 20 °C to 98.6% at 45 °C, indicating an endothermic adsorption process (Fig. 5f). At lower temperatures, the efficiency is limited due to reduced molecular movement and limited Hg(II) ion diffusion towards active sites on the nanofiber membrane. Increased temperature enhances the kinetic energy and mobility of Hg(II) ions, accelerating their diffusion and improving interaction with functional groups in PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH. Higher temperatures may also expand the polymeric matrix, revealing additional binding sites for metal ions. The continued increase in removal efficiency up to 45 °C suggests chemisorption, with elevated temperatures promoting the formation of stable coordination bonds between Hg(II) ions and donor atoms like oxygen and nitrogen. The endothermic nature implies that energy input helps overcome activation barriers for ion exchange and surface complexation. Notably, the lack of efficiency decline at high temperatures indicates the membrane’s thermal stability and structural integrity. In conclusion, temperature significantly enhances Hg(II) ion adsorption onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane by expediting diffusion, promoting chemisorption, and activating more surface sites, achieving 98.6% removal at 45 °C, indicative of the composite’s suitability for mercury remediation under elevated temperature conditions48.

Adsorption isotherm

Employing numerous adsorption isotherm models Langmuir49, Freundlich50, Dubinin–Radushkevich (D–R)51, Temkin52, and Jossens to examine the adsorption of Hg(II) on the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH yields significant scientific insights. The Langmuir isotherm assesses monolayer adsorption on uniform surfaces with identical active sites, enabling calculations of maximum adsorption capacity and confirming the membrane’s effectiveness for metal ion uptake49. In contrast, the Freundlich model describes heterogeneous surfaces, provided that insights into adsorption intensity and surface heterogeneity, reflecting the varied affinities from LDH layers, chitosan functional groups, and PVDF’s nanofibrous structure. The D–R isotherm differentiates among chemical and physical adsorption by estimating mean free energy, clarifying if Hg(II) uptake involves ion exchange, complexation, or physisorption (Table S4). The Temkin model inspects interactions between adsorbents and adsorbates, detailing how adsorption energy may decline linearly with increased surface coverage, relevant for interactions with functional groups like –OH, –COOH, and –NH₂. Lastly, the Jossens isotherm, based on statistical thermodynamics, evaluates the energetic distribution of adsorption sites, particularly beneficial for non-uniform composite materials like LDH–biopolymer membranes. Utilizing these models collectively enhances understanding of adsorption capacity, surface heterogeneity, energy, mechanisms, and interaction strength, elucidating how the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH membrane interacts with Hg(II) for its effective application in wastewater treatment and remediation of the environment (Table S5).

The operation of the Langmuir adsorption isotherm model offers substantial benefits in the assessment of the adsorption characteristics of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane concerning Hg(II), as it facilitates an explicit mechanistic understanding of the adsorption procedure and allows for the extraction of essential quantitative limits. The optimized Langmuir model indicates a maximum monolayer adsorption capacity (qm) of 422.68 mg.g⁻¹, illustrating the remarkably high affinity of the membrane for Hg(II) ions and enabling direct comparisons with previously documented adsorbents49. The Langmuir constant ((KL = 0.0199) L.mg⁻¹) further exemplifies the interaction strength among Hg(II) and the active places present on the surface of the composite, affirming strong binding over mechanisms like ion exchange and complexation. In addition, the computed dimension-less separation factor (RL = 0.18), which falls within the interval (0 < RL < 1), signifies that the adsorption is notably promising under the given experimental settings (Table S6). As a summary, the Langmuir model not only corroborates the evidence of monolayer adsorption on a comparatively uniform surface but also offers a dependable framework for evaluating adsorption favorability, affinity, and capacity, thereby establishing itself as an invaluable instrument for validating the efficacy and practical applicability of the developed membrane in the treatment of mercury-contaminated water (Fig. 6a). The adsorption of Hg(II) onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane can be better understood using the Freundlich adsorption isotherm model. The Freundlich model recognizes the possibility of multilayer adsorption and allows adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces, whereas the Langmuir model assumes homogeneity50. This characteristic aligns more closely with the intricate architecture of the composite membrane, which consists of layered LDH, chitosan functional groups, and a porous PVDF matrix. The Freundlich constant (KF = 44.99) (mg/g) (L/mg)1/n serves as an indicator of the adsorption capacity and reveals a strong empathy for Hg(II), although the adsorption intensity factor (n = 2.42) within the range (1 < n < 10) substantiates the favorable and efficient nature of the adsorption method. Higher values of n correlate with increased surface heterogeneity and enhanced connections among the adsorbate and adsorbent. Unlike models predicated on monolayer adsorption, the Freundlich model accounts for the uneven energetic distribution of active sites, rendering it particularly appropriate for hybrid adsorbents characterized by diverse functional groups and binding sites. Consequently, the Freundlich model contributes essential insights into surface heterogeneity, the favorability of adsorption, and multilayer uptake dynamics, thereby complementing the Langmuir model and providing a more nuanced sympathetic of adsorption phenomena in complex composite materials employed for the removal of mercury.

The Dubinin–Radushkevich (D–R) adsorption isotherm model gifts a significant advantage in the analysis of the uptake of Hg(II) onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane. Unlike the Langmuir besides Freundlich models, the D–R model provides direct insights into the nature of the adsorption mechanism, differentiating among physical and chemical adsorption by approximating the mean adsorption energy (Ea)51. The D–R parameters yield a calculated adsorption capacity (mg.g⁻¹), reaffirming the substantial potential for Hg(II) adsorption by the membrane; concurrently, the constant (mol²/kJ²) facilitates the assessment of the adsorption energy. Notably, the derived mean free energy value (kJ.mol⁻¹) exceeds 8 kJ.mol⁻¹, suggesting that the adsorption process primarily proceeds via chemisorption rather than physical adsorption. This chemisorption process involves robust interactions, including ion exchange, complexation, and coordination with the functional groups of layered double hydroxide (LDH) and chitosan. Hence, the D–R model is particularly robust as it not only estimates the adsorption capacity but also substantiates the underlying adsorption mechanism, thereby demonstrating that the uptake of Hg(II) is driven by chemical bonding rather than the relatively weak van der Waals forces. This finding further corroborates the strong and irreversible binding characteristics observed in the composite membrane (Fig. 6a).

The Temkin adsorption isotherm model demonstrates particular efficacy in assessing the adsorption characteristics of Hg(II) onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane52. This model diverges from the Langmuir besides Freundlich models by factoring in the effect of connections among the adsorbate and the adsorbent, presupposing that the heat associated with adsorption diminishes progressively with a rise in surface coverage. Such a framework renders the model exceptionally applicable to adsorbents possessing chemically active practical groups, like –OH, –NH₂, and –COOH, where the energies of interaction are variable. Specifically, the Temkin binding constant (KT = 0.45) L.mol⁻¹ serves as an indicator of the empathy of Hg(II) for the membrane surface. Concurrently, the Temkin heat parameter (bT = 27.928) J.mol⁻¹ suggests that the adsorption dynamics entail moderate binding energy, indicative of a chemisorption process that is primarily governed by ion exchange and complexation rather than by weak physical forces. A notable advantage of using the Temkin model lies in its capacity to elucidate the energetics of adsorption, allowing researchers to determine whether the interactions between adsorbate and adsorbent are endothermic, uniform, or progressively weakened as adsorption sites become filled. Consequently, this model serves to augment the insights provided by the Langmuir, Freundlich, besides Dubinin-Radushkevich (D–R) isotherms by substantiating that the adsorption of Hg(II) is energetically promising and significantly modulated by the connections of the adsorbate, rather than occurring on a surface characterized by energetic uniformity.

The Jossens adsorption isotherm model, often referred to as the Elovich or statistical isotherm model, suggestions important advantages in the analysis of Hg(II) adsorption onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane52. This model diverges from traditional models like Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, otherwise D–R by elucidating the energetic heterogeneity of adsorption sites as well as the distribution of binding energies across complex composite adsorbents. The Jossens constants derived from the model, specifically K = 10.95 and J = 0.29, suggest that the membrane surface encompasses sites with varying affinities rather than exhibiting uniform characteristics, a finding that aligns with the nature of a multi-component hybrid adsorbent comprising LDH layers, chitosan functional groups, and a porous PVDF network. The observed value of J (< 1) further corroborates the notion that the adsorption mechanism is neither ideal nor homogeneous; instead, it is dictated by a heterogeneous energy distribution. This implies that sites with higher energy are occupied first, followed by those with progressively lower energy as the adsorption process progresses. Consequently, the Jossens model proves particularly relevant for systems characterized by chemisorption involving multiple binding mechanisms, such as ion exchange, complexation, and electrostatic attraction, as well as for materials that have been engineered with multifunctional surface chemistry. In conclusion, the utilization of the Jossens isotherm presents a significant benefit by facilitating the quantification of variations in adsorption energy across a surface. This capability permits a more profound analysis of site heterogeneity, competitive adsorption phenomena, and the actual behavior of surfaces, which simpler models fail to adequately address. Consequently, it serves to enhance the understanding provided by Langmuir, Freundlich, besides D–R models, confirming that the binding of Hg(II) transpires on a statistically heterogeneous surface, in alignment with the characteristics of the mixed LDH–biopolymer nanofiber architecture.

Adsorption kinetics

The application of various adsorption kinetic models, like Pseudo-First-Order53, Pseudo-Second-Order54, Intraparticle Diffusion55, as well as Elovich models56, facilitates a thorough and dependable assessment of the adsorption dynamics of Hg(II) onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane. Each model elucidates distinct facets of both the rate and mechanisms involved in the uptake process. Specifically, the Pseudo-First-Order model aids in determining if the adsorption kinetics are predominantly influenced by physisorption alongside interactions occurring at the external surface. In contrast, the Pseudo-Second-Order model is more adept at addressing scenarios where chemisorption and electron-sharing reactions predominate, thus providing precise predictions of the equilibrium adsorption capacity and substantiating the predominance of chemical bonding in the elimination procedure. The model of Intraparticle Diffusion elucidates whether the adsorption kinetics are restricted by mechanisms such as film or pore diffusion, or by a combination of sequential processes. This aspect is particularly crucial in the context of porous nanofibrous materials, where boundary layer diffusion typically precedes the internal diffusion into the LDH and chitosan networks. Furthermore, the Elovich model demonstrates to be essential in characterizing adsorption phenomena on heterogeneous surfaces that exhibit non-uniform energy distributions, which is indicative of the composite membrane’s intricate structure and the varying activation energies associated with its functional groups (–OH, –NH₂, –COOH). By integrating the insights gained from all four models, researchers can effectively differentiate between the aids of chemical and physical adsorption, ascertain the underlying rate-controlling mechanisms, and determine whether the adsorption procedure is ruled by single-step or multi-stage diffusion. This comprehensive approach provides robust mechanistic insights supporting the efficacy and practical utilization of the membrane for Hg(II) remediation efforts (Tables S4 and S5).

The Pseudo-First-Order kinetic model is applied to analyze Hg(II) adsorption onto PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membranes. This model helps assess whether adsorption rates are primarily controlled by physisorption and external surface interactions instead of chemical bonds. The rate constant (K1 = 0.0326 × 10− 2 min-1) indicates how quickly Hg(II) ions associate with the membrane’s surface sites. Further confirmation arises from using the Pseudo-Second-Order model and analyzing intraparticle diffusion. Ultimately, the Pseudo-First-Order model primarily helps identify the extent of physisorption, laying a foundational kinetic reference to ascertain whether adsorption is driven by weak surface interactions or stronger bindings53.

The Pseudo-Second-Order kinetic model proposals compelling insights suggesting that the adsorption of Hg(II) onto PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane is primarily influenced by chemisorption processes54. Additionally, the determined rate constant (K2 = 4.58 × 10⁻⁴ g.mg⁻¹min⁻¹) designates both the efficacy and rapidity of the chemisorption process, illustrating the membrane’s ability to swiftly establish robust interactions with Hg(II) ions over mechanisms like complexation and ion exchange. In comparison to the Pseudo-First-Order model, the enhanced fit of the Pseudo-Second-Order model substantiates the premise that chemical bonding is the principal adsorption mechanism, rather than surface-limited physical adsorption, thereby reinforcing the notable adsorption capacity and stability of Hg(II) immobilization on the surface of the composite membrane (Fig. 6b). The kinetic model of Elovich effectively characterizes the Hg(II) adsorption on PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane, designed for heterogeneous surfaces with a wide variety of activation energies. The Elovich model’s advantage over the Pseudo-First-Order besides Pseudo-Second-Order models lies in its recognition of chemically controlled, energetically non-uniform adsorption mechanisms. Diffusion mechanism

The intraparticle diffusion analysis for Hg(II) adsorption onto PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH, as depicted in Fig. 6c, reveals three distinct linear segments, thereby substantiating the premise that the adsorption procedure is characterized by a multistep diffusion-controlled mechanism rather than a singular kinetic pathway. In the initial phase (Stage I), the sharply inclined slope indicates rapid adsorption occurring at the external surface or within the boundary layer, where Hg(II) ions are swiftly transported from the bulk phase to the membrane surface facilitated by strong electrostatic interactions and the high availability of active sites55. The subsequent linear segment (Stage II) is indicative of the gradual adsorption process, which is predominantly influenced by intraparticle diffusion as Hg²⁺ ions infiltrate further into the internal pores of the LDH structure, chitosan matrix, and PVDF nanofiber assembly. During this interval, the rate of diffusion diminishes as adsorption sites become increasingly occupied and the concentration gradient weakens. The final segment (Stage III) represents the equilibrium stage, characterized by a significant reduction in the adsorption rate due to the majority of active sites being saturated or hindered spatially, culminating in a plateau approach toward maximum uptake. The observation that none of the linear segments intersect the origin underscores that intraparticle diffusion alone does not dictate the rate-limiting step; rather, it suggests that both film diffusion and pore diffusion collaboratively influence the kinetics of adsorption. This multi-stage response corroborates the notion that the uptake of Hg(II) onto the composite membrane proceeds through a rapid initial surface interaction, followed by a slower process of internal diffusion, ultimately achieving stabilization at equilibrium, which aligns with a heterogeneous and multilayer adsorption model (Fig. 6c).

Adsorption thermodynamics

The thermodynamic properties governing the adsorption of Hg(II) onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane were meticulously examined utilizing the Van’t Hoff and Arrhenius models, as depicted in Fig. 7a–b and summarized in Table S8. The Van’t Hoff plot demonstrating the association among ln Kc and 1/T revealed a linear correlation characterized by a positive slope, substantiating the conclusion that the adsorption process is endothermic in nature. An increase in temperature from 293 to 318 K corresponded with an development in the removal efficiency of Hg(II) ions, indicating that augmented thermal energy enhances the movement of ions and promotes their diffusion to the active places available for adsorption. The derived thermodynamic parameters ΔHo = 102.15 kJ/mol, ΔSo = 352.29 J/mol.K, and ΔGo values ranging from − 1.26 kJ/mol at 293 K to − 10.09 kJ/mol at 318 K further suggest that the adsorption phenomenon is both spontaneous and influenced by temperature57. The positive enthalpy change (ΔHo) signifies an absorption of energy during the adsorption process, corroborating its endothermic classification. Meanwhile, the considerable positive entropy change (ΔSo) designates a rise in disorder at the solid–liquid boundary, occurring as Hg(II) ions displace water molecules and engage with the practical groups existing on the membrane’s surface58. Furthermore, the increasingly negative ΔGo values with rising temperature suggest that the adsorption becomes progressively more thermodynamically favorable at elevated conditions. The Arrhenius plot illustrating ln k₂ vs. 1/T further reinforced these findings, yielding an activation energy (Ea) of 38.42 kJ/mol, a value consistent with typical chemisorption processes, which range from 20 to 80 kJ/mol. This parameter supports the idea that chemical connections, consisting of the sharing or exchange of electrons between the Hg(II) ions and the oxygen and nitrogen donor atoms of the hydroxyl (–OH) and amino (–NH₂) groups intrinsic to chitosan and Al/Cu-LDH, are primarily responsible for the adsorption mechanism. In summation, these results elucidate that the adsorption of Hg(II) onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH constitutes a spontaneous, endothermic, and chemisorptive phenomenon, accentuated by elevated temperatures which facilitate robust surface complexation, enhanced molecular dynamics, and improved adsorption efficacy59.

Mechanism of interaction

The mechanism by which Hg(II) ions are adsorbed onto the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane is characterized by a multifaceted interplay of physical and chemical interactions that jointly facilitate effective removal. In the initial phase, Hg(II) ions are captured primarily via physisorption, which is influenced by van der Waals forces along with the extensive surface area provided by the porous PVDF nanofiber structure, thereby enhancing subsequent chemical interactions60. The attendance of hydroxyl groups (–OH) on the Al/Cu-LDH surface promotes precipitation processes that lead to the formation of insoluble mercury hydroxide or oxide compounds (Hg(OH)₂), thereby immobilizing the Hg(II) ions on the membrane. Simultaneously, an ion exchange process occurs wherein Hg(II) ions replace interlayer ions such as carbonate (CO₃²⁻) or hydroxide (OH⁻) inside the LDH framework, resulting in the establishment of stable Hg–O–M (where M denotes either aluminum or copper) bonds. Furthermore, when the pH of the solution exceeds the point of zero charge (pHzpc ≈ 5.3), the membrane surface acquires a negative charge, which fosters electrostatic attraction with the positively charged Hg(II) ions. Additionally, complexation reactions transpire between Hg(II) and the electron-donating functional groups inherent in chitosan (–NH₂, –OH) and LDH (–COOH, –OH), yielding robust coordination bonds. Collectively, these mechanisms comprising physisorption, precipitation, ion exchange, electrostatic interactions, and complexation enhance both the efficiency and stability of Hg(II) uptake. The chitosan and LDH components contribute a significant number of active sites, while the PVDF matrix provides necessary permeability and structural integrity, thereby enabling sustainable removal of mercury (Fig. S1).

Effect of salinity

Anions have the capacity to effect the elimination of Hg(II) ions by the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane adsorbent through mechanisms of competitive interaction. To evaluate this effect, an assortment of anions (CH3COO−, SO42−, CO32−, and Cl−) was presented individually into a solution comprising Hg(II). Each anionic salt was added at a concentration of 0.1 M to 25 mL of a 200 mg/L Hg(II) solution, utilizing 0.02 g of the adsorbent, while maintaining a pH of 6 during a contact time of 100 min. The impact of these coexisting anions on the removal of Hg(II) is described in Fig. S2. The resultant elimination efficiencies for Hg(II) in the attendance of CH3COO−, CO32−, and Cl− were observed to be 94.6, 87.8, and 76.8%, respectively, signaling a moderate decline in effectiveness44. This outcome suggests that these specific anions exhibit a limited interaction with the active places of the adsorbent. Conversely, the presence of SO42− considerably diminished the adsorption performance of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane for Hg(II), yielding an efficiency of 68.4%. This finding indicates that SO42− possesses a more substantial competitive affinity for the adsorption sites on the nanofiber membrane relative to CH3COO−, CO32−, and Cl−. This phenomenon may be attributed to the capacity of SO42− to establish crosslink interactions with the positively charged sites of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane, thereby facilitating a robust electrostatic attraction among SO42− and the protonated amino groups present in the membrane. Nevertheless, despite the marked reduction in adsorption ability in the presence of SO42−, the sorbent retained a comparatively high adsorption capability of 68.4% for Hg(II).

Reusability

In this investigation, 0.02 g of a composite nanofiber membrane made of PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH was used to treat a 50 mL aqueous solution containing 100 mg/L of Hg(II) ions. The experiment was carried out for 100 min at a regulated pH of 6 in order to maximize the Hg(II) ions’ adsorption effectiveness. The Hg(II)-loaded nanofiber membrane was submerged in a different 50 mL solution (0.05 mol/L) of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) after the adsorption procedure. To promote efficient interaction between the adsorbent and the EDTA, this mixture was continuously stirred for four hours at a constant temperature of 298.15 K. The resulting composite mixture was then cleaned with distilled water after the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH was separated from it using centrifugation. The membrane was made appropriate for reuse in later adsorption cycles after being vacuum-dried at 333 K. There were five replications of the adsorption and reactivation processes. The depletion of active sites and changes in the material’s particular geometric layout, which worsen with each cycle, are responsible for this decline in uptake. These findings show that the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH exhibits a significant capacity for efficient recycling. X-ray diffraction (XRD) investigations were performed both before and after the regeneration procedure to evaluate the stability of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH. The analysis’s findings demonstrate that the adsorbent’s structural integrity was preserved even after five renewal cycles64–66. For up to five cycles, the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH can operate at its best. This result suggests that the adsorbent’s stability is preserved for the course of the experimental evaluation67.

Notably, simultaneous fluctuations in pH and contact time yield more significant increases in adsorption than modulations of either variable in isolation. In summary, the cube plot reinforces the assertion that optimal adsorption is realized under conditions of high pH, extended contact time, and minimal sorbent mass. This supports the quadratic optimization model and confirms that the efficiency of adsorption is contingent upon the collective, rather than isolated, effects of the investigated variables.

Checking of model adequacy

The 3D interaction, contour, and desirability plots collectively elucidate the interactive belongings of pH (A) and adsorbent dosage (B) on the adsorption capacity (qe) of Hg(II) as facilitated by the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane. These illustrations further support the optimization potential afforded by the response surface model. The 3D surface plot exhibits a curvilinear, ascending response trend, wherein qe progressively increases with rising pH levels and decreasing adsorbent doses. This observation substantiates the significant synergistic interaction between deprotonated functional groups at elevated pH values and an increased availability of adsorption sites per gram at lower mass loadings. The associated contour plot reveals well-defined elliptical iso-response lines, thereby indicating a vital interaction between pH and dosage rather than independent effects75. The region of maximum adsorption (350–424 mg/g) is notably concentrated within the pH range of 6 to 8 and doses falling below 0.1 g, aligning with the optimized solutions predicted by the model. Conversely, areas considered by low pH and high dosage yield minimal adsorption, credited to the existence of protonated surfaces and saturation of available sites. The desirability plot further distills this behavior into a dimensionless scale of optimization, where a desirability value approaching 1.0 is attainable only within a restricted area defined by elevated pH coupled with low dosage (D ≈ 0.994). This indicates that this specific region not only maximizes the adsorption response but also adheres to the established optimization criteria. The observed decline in desirability toward higher-dose and lower-pH areas corroborates the statistical prioritization of variable importance, reinforcing the conclusion that pH serves as a positive determinant, while dosage functions as a negative determinant in influencing qe. Collectively, these graphical representations confirm the strength of the quadratic model, substantiate the existence of strong interactive effects, and delineate the most advantageous operational parameters for optimizing the approval of Hg(II) (Fig. 8a).

The predicted adsorption response curves illustrate that qe increases at higher pH and longer contact duration but decreases with higher dosage, highlighting the negative impact of excessive sorbent use. The narrowed confidence bands in cyan confirm model precision and low prediction uncertainty. The optimal solution from the desirability algorithm is pH ≈ 6.2, time ≈ 91 min, and dosage ≈ 0.02 g, achieving a projected capacity of 424 mg/g and a desirability index of 0.994. This result indicates that the parameters effectively achieve the objectives, validating the model’s ability to identify conditions for optimal Hg(II) removal while minimizing sorbent usage and processing time76.

Biological activity

Anticancer activity

Cell line MCF-7

Figures S6(a, b) collectively illustrate the cytotoxic and inhibitory effects of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane and the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane on MCF-7 human breast carcinoma cells, as evaluated via the MTT assay subsequent a 24-hour exposure period. In Fig. S6(a), a concentration-dependent decrease in cell viability is observed for both materials. Notably, the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane (represented by blue closed circles) demonstrates a more substantial and marked reduction in viability compared to the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane78. At lower concentrations (≤ 50 µg/mL), both membranes exhibit moderate cytotoxic effects; however, the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane significantly decreases cell viability beyond this threshold, approaching nearly complete cell death at 500 µg/mL. The calculated IC₅₀ value for the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane is 72.36 ± 2.86 µg/mL, suggesting a high efficacy, whereas the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane presents a higher IC₅₀ of 122.36 ± 1.88 µg/mL, indicating a lower cytotoxic capability. Similarly, Fig. S6(b) depicts the percentage inhibition of MCF-7 cells, which rises proportionally with the concentration of both materials. The PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane achieves higher inhibition percentages across all concentrations tested, exceeding 88% at approximately 300 µg/mL and nearing complete inhibition at 500 µg/mL. In contrast, the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane necessitates higher concentrations to reach comparable levels of inhibition, which corresponds with its elevated IC₅₀. These results imply that the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH has better anticancer qualities, which are probably due to its increased surface reactivity, inherent photocatalytic capabilities, and capacity to cause oxidative stress and apoptosis in breast cancer cells79,80.

Cell line HePG-2

The PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane exhibits a markedly more rapid and pronounced decrease in cell viability in comparison to the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane. At lower concentrations (≤ 50 µg/mL), both membranes demonstrate moderate cytotoxicity; however, the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane reveals a significant decline in viability at elevated concentrations, culminating in nearly complete cellular death at 500 µg/mL (Fig. S6c). The calculated IC₅₀ for the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane is 61.27 ± 1.49 µg/mL, which indicates a higher cytotoxic potential, whereas the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane yields an IC₅₀ of 102.62 ± 2.84 µg/mL, suggesting a lower efficacy. Furthermore, as depicted in Fig. S6d, the inhibition percentage of HePG-2 cells exhibits a notable increase in relation to concentration for both materials80. The PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane consistently achieves superior inhibition rates across all tested concentrations, surpassing 87% at approximately 300 µg/mL and approaching complete inhibition at 500 µg/mL. In contrast, the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane necessitates higher doses to reach comparable inhibition levels. These results elucidate that the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane demonstrates significantly enhanced anticancer activity against hepatocellular carcinoma cells when compared to the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane, which may be ascribed to its increased surface reactivity, enhanced interactions with cells, and its potential to facilitate oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis.

Antioxidant activity

Figure S7 presents the DPPH radical scavenging activity of three different materials: the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane, the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane, and ascorbic acid, which serves as the standard antioxidant for comparison81. The investigation encompasses a wide concentration range from 0 to 1000 µg/mL. Both varieties of nanofiber membranes displayed an increase in scavenging activity contingent upon the concentration. Importantly, the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane consistently surpassed the scavenging performance of the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane across all concentrations tested. At lower concentrations (specifically those below 50 µg/mL), the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane demonstrated a marked antioxidant effect, which intensified significantly up to approximately 200 µg/mL, before reaching a plateau of around 84.2% scavenging efficiency at the highest concentration assessed. In contrast, the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane exhibited a more gradual increase in scavenging activity, culminating in approximately 76.03% scavenging efficacy at the maximum concentration of 1000 µg/mL82. The recorded IC₅₀ values substantiate the enhanced antioxidant properties of the PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH nanofiber membrane, which was quantified at 147.96 ± 5.42 µg/mL, in contrast to the PVDF/CS nanofiber membrane, which exhibited an IC₅₀ value of 228.3 ± 9.42 µg/mL. Ascorbic acid, indicated by blue diamonds, served as the positive control and displayed the highest radical scavenging potential, achieving over 90% inhibition at an approximate concentration of 200 µg/mL. The antioxidant enhancement in PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH is not due to a single factor but rather a multi-mechanism synergy combining the hydrogen-donating capacity by -OH and -NH2 from chitosan, the redox-active copper centers in LDH where electrons are transferred from the membrane surface to the radical, neutralizing it without requiring hydrogen donation, the high surface area of the nanofibrous architecture, and the π-conjugated electronic structure — all working together to achieve superior DPPH radical scavenging compared to PVDF/CS alone81.

Conclusion

This study successfully created and assessed a multifunctional PVDF/CS-Al/Cu-LDH electrospun nanofiber membrane, which exhibited exceptional efficacy in Hg(II) ions removal from aqueous environments while concurrently demonstrating notable biological activity. Structural, morphological, and surface characterizations corroborated the effective combination of Al/Cu-LDH and CS into the PVDF nanofibrous framework, leading to the creation of a highly porous and chemically active adsorbent enriched with hydroxyl and amino groups, capable of robust metal coordination. Adherence to the pseudo-second-order kinetic model, monolayer Langmuir isotherm fitting, and thermodynamic parameters revealing endothermic and spontaneous characteristics all demonstrated that chemisorption dominated the adsorption dynamics, with the membrane achieving a peak maximum adsorption capacity of 422.68 mg.g⁻¹. Optimization through the Box–Behnken experimental design established that influences such as pH, adsorbent quantity, and interaction duration significantly affect uptake efficiency; the model showcased remarkable predictive accuracy, attaining a desirability index of 0.99 under optimal conditions. XPS analysis confirmed that the mechanism of Hg(II) removal was chiefly facilitated by surface complexation and ion-exchange interactions involving the LDH layers and chitosan functionalities. Beyond its application in environmental remediation, the membrane also exhibited promising anticancer activity in contrast to MCF-7 and HePG-2 cancer cell lines and showcased pronounced antioxidant activity as demonstrated through DPPH radical scavenging assays. These multifaceted characteristics underline the material’s versatility, indicating its potential utility in advanced wastewater treatment methodologies as well as in biomedical and antimicrobial applications where concurrent removal of contaminants and bioactivity is advantageous. Future research directions may encompass real wastewater testing, investigation of membrane reusability in continuous-flow systems, and mechanistic studies elucidating the relationship between surface chemistry and biological interaction pathways.
