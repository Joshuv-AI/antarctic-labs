Association between road safety awareness and motorcycle riding behaviors with road traffic crashes among Thai university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sineenart Chautrakarn, Ekachai Jaiprom, Kitbordin Thongduang, Parichat Ong-Artborirak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYoung people, particularly university students, are at significant risk of motorcycle accidents. This study aimed to investigate road safety awareness and motorcycle riding behavior, and their association with road traffic crashes among Thai university students.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 854 university students who rode motorcycles at least three times per week completed an online self-administered survey. The questionnaire included self-reported road traffic crashes during the past year, basic knowledge of traffic regulations, road safety awareness, and motorcycle riding behavior.ResultsThe prevalence of road traffic crashes in the past year was 28.7%. The mean scores for road safety awareness and motorcycle riding behavior were 80.44 ± 9.93 and 88.72 ± 8.25 (out of 100), respectively. Multiple logistic regression analysis showed that road traffic crashes were inversely associated with road safety awareness and motorcycle riding behavior scores (per 10-point increase) after adjusting for confounders (AOR = 0.823, 95% CI: 0.705–0.961 and AOR = 0.811, 95% CI: 0.675–0.975, respectively). When the presence of unsafe riding behaviors was categorized by domains, road traffic crashes were significantly associated with traffic law violations (AOR = 1.530, 95% CI: 1.012–2.313) and driving under the influence of alcohol or drugs (AOR = 1.695, 95% CI: 1.244–2.311).ConclusionsThe findings highlight the need for integrated road safety interventions, including education, behavioral change strategies, and traffic law enforcement, to enhance safe riding practices among university students and reduce traffic-related injuries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28200-8.

Introduction

Deaths and injuries from road traffic accidents (RTAs) remain a major global public health and development challenge. In 2019, RTAs were the leading cause of death among children and young people aged 5–29 years and the 12th leading cause of death overall [1]. Approximately 1.19 million people die each year due to road crashes, with motorcyclists and riders of other powered two- and three-wheelers accounting for 30% of fatalities [1]. An additional 20–50 million people sustain non-fatal injuries, many resulting in long-term disabilities [2]. Nearly two-thirds of road traffic deaths occur among people of working age (18–59 years), creating substantial health, social, and economic burdens [1]. Moreover, 92% of road traffic deaths occur in low-, lower-middle-, and upper-middle-income countries [1–3].

Goal 3 of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) focuses on good health and well-being, specifically addressing one of the world’s most serious yet entirely preventable public health concerns. SDG 3.6 aims to reduce the number of fatalities and injuries caused by road traffic accidents by half by 2030 [4]. As a result, all countries must implement measures to prevent fatalities and injuries caused by traffic accidents.

Road safety remains a critical concern in Thailand despite recent improvements. The country’s road fatality rate continues to exceed the Southeast Asian average. In 2021, road crashes caused an estimated 18,000 deaths, accounting for 3.5% of all fatalities and ranking as the ninth leading cause of death nationwide [5]. In 2022, Thailand had 596 vehicles per 1,000 population, of which 52% were motorcycles [5]. Thailand has more than 22 million registered motorcycles and a population-to-motorcycle ratio of 3:1, one of the highest in ASEAN after Malaysia and Vietnam [6]. The large number of motorcycles contributes to the high road traffic death rate [5, 7].

The burden of RTAs is further exacerbated because most road users are of working age (18–59 years) [2]. Young drivers are particularly vulnerable worldwide, with a significantly higher risk of traffic accidents than other age groups [8]. This increased risk is associated with both inexperience and age-related factors. Young novice riders often lack the skills to recognize, assess, and respond to hazards, while neurocognitive evidence suggests that risky driving behavior is linked to an imbalance between the social-affective brain and the cognitive control system during the transition from adolescence to adulthood [7, 9].

Motorcycle crashes among young riders are also associated with poor road safety awareness and risky behaviors, including speeding, violating traffic laws, racing with peers, riding under the influence of alcohol, failing to wear protective gear, and using mobile phones while riding [7, 10, 11].

In the context of large universities in Thailand, motorcycles are one of the primary modes of transportation used by students for commuting both within and around university campuses due to their convenience, affordability, and mobility. As a result, a substantial proportion of university students are regularly exposed to traffic-related risks associated with motorcycle use. Road traffic injuries remain a significant public health concern among young adults, particularly in countries where motorcycle use is widespread. Despite growing attention to road safety issues, research examining the prevalence of road traffic crashes, risk factors, and associated factors among young adults and university students remains relatively limited in Thailand [12, 13]. This gap is especially evident in large university settings where motorcycle usage is highly prevalent. Therefore, studies addressing these issues are needed to generate evidence that can inform policies and targeted interventions aimed at improving road safety and reducing the burden of traffic-related injuries among university students.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional survey was conducted from April to June 2024 among undergraduate students at a single university in Chiang Mai, Thailand. Chiang Mai is Thailand’s second-largest city and a key regional educational, economic, and cultural hub. The province is home to a number of higher education institutions, including major universities that attract students from across the country.

The university selected for this study is one of the largest in northern Thailand, with an extensive campus comprising faculties across a wide range of academic disciplines, including science and technology, health sciences, humanities, and social sciences. Furthermore, on-campus residential facilities are available to students, resulting in a sizable student population residing on and around the campus. Motorcycles are one of the primary modes of transportation used by students for commuting both within and around university campuses. The large campus area, the availability of student housing, and the convenience and affordability of motorcycles contribute to their widespread use among university students.

Participants

The study population consisted of undergraduate students enrolled at the selected university in Chiang Mai. The sample size was calculated to estimate a population proportion assuming maximum variability (50%), a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level. After accounting for an anticipated 10% rate of incomplete or missing responses, the minimum required sample size was 424 participants. To improve the precision of the estimates, all eligible students who responded during the study period were included. A total of 856 questionnaires were collected using convenience and snowball sampling methods; however, two questionnaires were excluded due to incomplete data, resulting in a final analytic sample of 854 participants.

Procedure

An anonymous, self-administered questionnaire was distributed via a link and QR code to university students. The participants were initially screened for inclusion criteria, which included being aged 18 years or older, currently enrolled in undergraduate programs, regularly riding motorcycles at least three times per week, and willingness to participate in the study. Interested students could access the study information sheet and informed consent form online. Participants provided informed consent by clicking on the first page of the online questionnaire, after which the questionnaire appeared on the screen.

Measurement and interpretation

Data were collected using a five-section self-administered online questionnaire. The first section requested participant characteristics, including gender (male, female or LGBTQ+), age, year of study, faculty, years of riding experience, and motorcycle license status.

The second section assessed self-reported road traffic crashes during the past year. Road traffic crashes were defined as any crash experienced while riding a motorcycle, including both crashes that resulted in injury and those that did not. Participants who reported a crash were further asked to indicate the perceived cause of the incident, which was classified as being due to their own actions (rider-related factors), the actions of other road users, or environmental factors.

The third section of the questionnaire inquired about basic knowledge of traffic regulations. Each question could be answered with a yes, no, or do not know/not sure. Each correct answer received one point, resulting in a total possible score of 5. This questionnaire was developed for this study (Supplementary file).

The fourth section assessed road safety awareness using a 20-item questionnaire developed for this study based on relevant literature and the local road traffic context (see Supplementary File). Example items included compliance with traffic signals, avoiding riding against traffic flow, and ensuring passengers wear helmets. Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 20 to 100; higher scores indicated greater road safety awareness. The questionnaire was reviewed by three experts in public health and road traffic safety to establish content validity, and revisions were made based on their recommendations. Reliability testing demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.72.

The final section inquired about motorcycle riding behaviors, which were divided into five domains: traffic law violations, reckless driving, inconsistent use of personal protective equipment (PPE), mobile phone use while driving, and driving under the influence of alcohol or drugs. It included 20 questions, and respondents were asked to rate the frequency of each behavior on a scale of 1 to 5 (1 = always, 5 = never), with a total score ranging from 20 to 100; higher scores indicated safer behaviors. Responses were also categorized separately for each domain. Participants who reported any unsafe behavior (scores 1–4 on at least one item) were classified as “Yes,” whereas those reporting “never” (score = 5) for all items within a domain were classified as “No.” The questionnaire was reviewed by three experts in public health and road traffic safety to establish content validity, and revisions were made based on their recommendations. Reliability testing demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.81.

Construct validation

To provide additional evidence for the construct validity of the motorcycle riding behavior questionnaire, which was developed based on five theoretical domains, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed. Principal component extraction with Promax rotation and Kaiser normalization was applied because the domains were conceptually related. The suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity.

The EFA results supported the underlying structure of the questionnaire. The KMO value was 0.877, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating that the data were appropriate for factor analysis. Using Promax rotation, five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, consistent with the proposed domain structure. These factors accounted for 59.9% of the total variance. Communalities ranged from 0.39 to 0.88, and factor loadings ranged from 0.33 to 0.95, with most items loading between 0.46 and 0.95. Overall, these findings provide support for the construct validity of the questionnaire (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS version 29 software, employing both descriptive and inferential statistics. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize the data. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was used to examine the association between road safety awareness scores and riding behavior scores. To evaluate associations between participant characteristics and road safety awareness, riding behaviors, and traffic accidents, the independent t-test or one-way ANOVA was used for continuous outcomes, and the chi-square test was used for categorical outcomes. In addition, all participant characteristics, except year of study, which was excluded due to collinearity with age, were entered into separate multiple linear regression models to examine their associations with road safety awareness and riding behaviors. Binary logistic regression was used to estimate unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) to examine the associations of road safety awareness and riding behaviors (including individual domains) with traffic accidents. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

This study included 854 undergraduates. The majority of participants (58.3%) were female, followed by males (38.3%) and LGBTQ+ participants (3.4%). A total of 525 participants (61.5%) were older than 20 years, with a mean age of 20.31 ± 1.58 years. Regarding academic characteristics, 429 participants (50.2%) were enrolled in humanities and social sciences programs, and 479 (56.1%) were in their first or second year of study. Regarding riding experience, 53.3% of participants had ridden motorcycles for five years or more, and 78.0% held a valid motorcycle license (Table 2).

The prevalence of road traffic crashes

This study found the overall prevalence of traffic crashes in the past year was 28.7% (95% CI: 25.6–31.7) (Table 2), with the most common cause being due to their own actions (rider-related factors) (86.5%). Road traffic crash rates were similar across age groups, years of study, academic disciplines, riding experience, motorcycle license status, and basic knowledge of traffic regulations, but differed significantly by gender (p = 0.034) (Table 2).

Basic knowledge of traffic regulations

The study found that the mean score for knowledge of traffic regulations was 4.13 ± 1.13. The findings revealed that almost all participants correctly answered most questions related to traffic regulations (Fig. 1 Basic knowledge of traffic regulations). However, less than 80% of participants correctly answered the question: “Motorcycle drivers are prohibited from making a U-turn in areas with an intersection or junction” (72.8%).

Road safety awareness and riding behaviors

The mean road safety awareness score was 80.44 ± 9.93 out of 100, ranging from 46 to 100. The mean riding behavior score was 88.72 ± 8.25 out of 100, also ranging from 46 to 100. A statistically significant positive correlation was observed between road safety awareness and riding behavior scores (r = 0.629, p < 0.001).

Factors associated with road safety awareness and riding behaviors

Based on the independent t-test and one-way ANOVA analyses, road safety awareness scores differed significantly by gender (p = 0.003), academic discipline (p = 0.023), riding experience (p = 0.011), motorcycle license status (p = 0.010), and knowledge of traffic regulations (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Similarly, riding behavior scores differed significantly by gender (p < 0.001), academic discipline (p = 0.012), riding experience (p = 0.017), and knowledge of traffic regulations (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Consistent with these findings, multiple linear regression analyses showed that gender, academic discipline, riding experience, motorcycle license status, and knowledge of traffic regulations were significantly associated with road safety awareness scores (p < 0.05), whereas age group was not. In addition, gender, academic discipline, riding experience, and knowledge of traffic regulations were significantly associated with riding behavior scores (p < 0.05), whereas age group and motorcycle license status were not (Table 3).

Associations of road safety awareness and riding behaviors with traffic crashes

After adjusting for gender, age, academic discipline, riding experience, motorcycle license status, and basic knowledge of traffic regulations, multiple logistic regression analysis showed that the odds of traffic crashes decreased by 17.7% for every 10-point increase in road safety awareness scores (AOR = 0.823, 95% CI: 0.705–0.961) and by 18.9% for every 10-point increase in riding behavior scores (AOR = 0.811, 95% CI: 0.675–0.975). In addition, traffic crashes were significantly associated with traffic law violations (AOR = 1.530, 95% CI: 1.012–2.313) and driving under the influence of alcohol or drugs (AOR = 1.695, 95% CI: 1.244–2.311). These associations were consistent with the unadjusted analyses (Table 5).

Discussion

The prevalence of road traffic crashes ranges from 5.2% to 55.1%, depending on the country, target population, and vehicle type [7, 14–22]. In the current study, the prevalence of road traffic crashes among university students was 28.7%. When comparing crash prevalence, this study found that male students were more likely to experience higher crash rates. The observed gender differences in road crashes may be explained in part by men’s greater involvement in risky and offending behaviors, higher sensation seeking, and less frequent use of safety devices that could protect them (e.g., seat belts and helmets) [23].

This study found that most participants had a good understanding of traffic regulations. However, despite more than half of the participants having more than five years of riding experience, some misconceptions about riding practices remained. Nearly 30% of motorcycle riders incorrectly believed that making a U-turn at an intersection or junction was permitted. Such misunderstandings may lead to unsafe riding behaviors and increase the risk of traffic crashes. These findings highlight the need for continued interventions to improve knowledge of traffic regulations.

This study demonstrated a moderate-to-strong association between road safety awareness and riding behavior, indicating that the two constructs are closely connected but not identical. Riding behavior involves actual riding practices, whereas road safety awareness reflects knowledge and perceptions of traffic safety. According to the Theory of Planned Behavior, increased awareness may promote safer practices. Previous research has found strong associations among traffic safety awareness, road safety attitudes, and driving behavior, with traffic safety awareness serving as a mediator between attitudes and behavior. This shows that awareness is essential for turning positive attitudes into safer riding practices [24].

Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that gender, academic discipline, riding experience, motorcycle license status, and knowledge of traffic regulations were all significantly associated with road safety awareness scores. Gender, academic discipline, riding experience, and knowledge of traffic regulations were all significantly associated with riding behavior scores.

Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that male and LGBTQ+ students had lower road safety awareness and riding behavior scores than female students. These findings are consistent with a study conducted among undergraduate students in a South Indian state, which found that females were slightly more aware of road safety practices than males [25], as well as another study conducted among undergraduate college students in Delhi, which found that male students were more likely to engage in road risk behaviors than females [3]. This could be explained by differences in male and female risk perceptions, with males perceiving risky behaviors as less serious and showing less concern about the consequences [26].

In addition, students in the science and technology academic discipline had higher road safety awareness scores than those in the humanities and social sciences academic discipline, while students in the science and technology and health sciences academic discipline demonstrated better riding behavior. However, there is limited evidence regarding the association between academic discipline, road safety awareness, and riding behavior. Furthermore, students with five or more years of riding experience had lower road safety awareness and riding behavior scores than those with less riding experience. One possible explanation is that experienced riders may become overly comfortable and confident in their riding abilities and traffic situations. Such confidence may lead to complacency, underestimation of risk, and reduced attention to safety precautions, which could negatively influence both safety awareness and riding behavior [27]. Furthermore, the current study found that students with higher traffic regulation knowledge scores demonstrated greater road safety awareness and safer riding behaviors. Individuals with better knowledge of traffic laws may be more aware of safety issues because they understand both the rules and their purpose [28]. Such knowledge can enhance awareness of potential road hazards and the importance of safe riding practices. However, these findings differ from studies conducted among young people in Spain, the United Kingdom, and Telangana State, which reported that knowledge of traffic rules or road safety was not a significant predictor of actual driving behavior [29–31]. This discrepancy suggests that although individuals may possess adequate knowledge of traffic regulations, such knowledge does not always translate into safe behaviors in practice.

The study also found that students who possessed a valid motorcycle license had higher road safety awareness than those without a license. One possible explanation is that obtaining a motorcycle license reflects greater recognition of the importance of complying with road safety regulations. In addition, the licensing process may increase exposure to traffic rules and safe riding practices, thereby contributing to higher levels of road safety awareness.

Regarding unsafe riding behavior domains among university students in the current study, more than 80% of students reported engaging in traffic violations, while more than two-thirds engaged in reckless driving and used PPE inconsistently. All of these dangerous behaviors contribute to crashes and near-crashes among motorcycle riders [32]. Furthermore, inconsistent PPE use, such as failing to wear helmets or adequate protective equipment, increases the risk and severity of injuries in a crash [6, 12, 14, 33]. Although only about half of the students used their phones while driving and drove under the influence of alcohol or drugs, multiple studies found that these risky practices contributed to motorcycle crashes among university students [34–38]. Interventions aimed at raising safety awareness and enhancing safe riding practices should be strengthened and strictly enforced.

When comparing the prevalence of risky riding behaviors, LGBTQ + and male students were more likely than female students to ride under the influence of alcohol or drugs. This may reflect higher rates of alcohol consumption and risk-taking behaviors among males. Students aged 20 years and older were also more likely to engage in risky riding behaviors, including traffic law violations, reckless riding, and riding under the influence of alcohol or drugs. One possible explanation is that greater riding experience may increase confidence, leading some riders to underestimate risks and pay less attention to safety concerns [27]. Furthermore, students in humanities and social sciences disciplines were more likely to violate traffic laws and use mobile phones while riding than those in other disciplines. However, the reasons for these associations remain unclear and should be interpreted with caution.

In the multiple logistic regression analysis, both road safety awareness and riding behavior were significantly associated with traffic crashes. Each 10-point increase in road safety awareness scores and riding behavior scores was associated with a 17.7% and 18.9% reduction in the odds of traffic crashes, respectively. These findings highlight the potential value of interventions that enhance road safety awareness and encourage safer riding practices among university students.

In addition, the findings highlighted the association between risky riding behaviors and traffic crashes, particularly traffic law violations and riding under the influence of alcohol or drugs. Students who violated traffic laws were more likely to experience traffic crashes than those who did not (AOR = 1.530, 95% CI: 1.012–2.313). Traffic laws are designed to promote safe and orderly road use, and violations increase the risk of crashes and near-crashes among motorcyclists [32]. Similarly, students who rode under the influence of alcohol or drugs had higher odds of traffic crashes than those who did not (AOR = 1.695, 95% CI: 1.244–2.311). Alcohol and drugs impair the physical and cognitive functions required for safe riding, thereby increasing both crash risk and injury severity [39, 40].

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design and focus on a single city during a single season limit the generalizability of the findings. The use of an online questionnaire prevented assessment of actual response rates, while the non-probability sampling approach may have introduced selection bias. Riding exposure was not adequately measured and may have confounded the observed associations. In addition, crash histories were self-reported and not verified through official records, making them susceptible to recall bias and reporting inaccuracies. Finally, the five-item knowledge test may not have been sufficiently sensitive to accurately distinguish participants’ actual levels of knowledge.

Conclusion

The findings indicate that road safety initiatives for university students should go beyond knowledge dissemination. Universities and key stakeholders should develop multifaceted programs that include road safety education, behavior change campaigns, defensive riding training, and increased enforcement of traffic regulations, particularly those addressing impaired driving and other traffic violations. Such integrated interventions may promote safe riding practices while also contributing to national and international initiatives to reduce road traffic injuries and fatalities by 2030.
