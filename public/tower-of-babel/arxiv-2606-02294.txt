Regularized Large Neighborhood Search
Germain Vivier-Ardisson, Laurent Demonet, Axel Parmentier, Mathieu Blondel

Abstract
Operations research practitioners typically tackle NP-hard combinatorial problems using large neighborhood search (LNS), a scalable heuristic that iteratively refines a current solution by locally re-optimizing subsets of its variables. In contrast, most existing approaches for integrating combinatorial optimization layers into neural networks still assume access to an exact global solution, which is computationally intractable. We bridge this gap by introducing regularized LNS (RLNS). By regularizing or perturbing local subproblems, we turn the LNS heuristic into an efficient MCMC sampler over the combinatorial set of feasible solutions, with associated Fenchel-Young losses. Under entropic regularization, we prove that RLNS performs exact block Gibbs sampling. Furthermore, adjusting the number of RLNS iterations allows us to interpolate between pseudolikelihood and exact maximum likelihood estimation, for end-to-end learning without global solvers. We demonstrate our approach on k k -subset selection, generalized assignment, and stochastic vehicle scheduling problems.

Integrating combinatorial optimization solvers into neural networks has emerged as a powerful paradigm for structured prediction

Smoothed maximum operators.

Let
\Omega:\triangle^{D}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.) strictly convex regularization function. The smoothed maximum operator
f_{\Omega}:{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\to{\mathbb{R}}

(
35
)
and the smoothed maximizer
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}:{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\to\Delta^{D}
are defined as

\displaystyle f_{\Omega}({\bm{s}})\triangleq\max_{{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{D}}\,\langle{\bm{s}},{\bm{q}}\rangle-\Omega({\bm{q}}),\quad\text{and}\quad{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}({\bm{s}})\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{D}}\,\langle{\bm{s}},{\bm{q}}\rangle-\Omega({\bm{q}}).

The strict convexity of
\Omega
guarantees that
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}({\bm{s}})
is single-valued. Danskin’s theorem
(
15
)
gives
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}=\nabla f_{\Omega}
, and we have
f_{\Omega}=\Omega^{*}
, where
\cdot^{*}
denotes the Fenchel conjugate. Typical choices for
\Omega
include Shannon’s negative entropy, which yields the
log-sum-exp
operator for
f_{\Omega}
and the
softmax
for
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}
, or
\Omega=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
, which gives the
sparsemax
operator
(
32
)
for
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}
.

Distribution-space Fenchel-Young losses.

Following
11
, we define the
score vector

\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}}\triangleq(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle)_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}
, and cast Problem
1
as an optimization problem on
\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
, the set of distributions over feasible solutions, yielding
\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle=\max_{{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}}\langle\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}},{\bm{q}}\rangle
. This point of view allows us to introduce distribution-space regularization into the problem.

Definition 2.1

(Distribution-space Fenchel-Young loss)
.

Let
\Omega:\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a l.s.c. strictly convex regularization function. The
distribution-space Fenchel-Young loss

L_{\Omega}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
generated by
\Omega
is defined by:

\displaystyle L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}\,;{\bm{p}})\,\triangleq\,\Omega^{*}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})+\Omega({\bm{p}})-\langle\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}},{\bm{p}}\rangle\,=\,f_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})+\Omega({\bm{p}})-\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}}[Y]\rangle.

This loss is non-negative, and such that
L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}\,;{\bm{p}})=0\iff{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})={\bm{p}}
. It is continuously differentiable and convex in
{\bm{\theta}}
, with
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}\,;{\bm{p}})={\mathbb{E}}_{{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})}[Y]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}}[Y]
. If
\Omega
is strongly convex, then
{\bm{\theta}}\mapsto\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}\,;{\bm{p}})
is Lipschitz-continuous.

Differentiating through combinatorial optimization.

Most approaches to differentiate Problem
1
with respect to
{\bm{\theta}}
are based on introducing regularization, based on the type of oracle available. If the original problem admits a dynamic programming solution, one can solve an entropy-regularized version by algorithmic smoothing
(
27
;
35
)
. For linear programs, one can use an interior-point solver to to compute a log-barrier-regularized solution
(
29
)
, or leverage projection oracles to handle quadratic regularization
(
34
)
. Global MAP oracles can also be used to solve the regularized problem, either via a Frank-Wolfe-like routine
(
37
;
25
)
or via Monte-Carlo estimation by adding stochastic perturbations to the solver’s input
(
7
;
14
)
.

Regarding differentiation, several strategies are possible. When the approach only needs to differentiate through a regularized
\max
, as is the case of Fenchel-Young losses
(
11
)
, we can use Danskin’s theorem
(
15
)
. When it requires differentiating a regularized
\argmax
, one can use autodiff on the unrolled solver iterations, or implicit differentiation of the KKT conditions
(
2
;
16
;
10
;
1
)
.
Differently,
48
compute surrogate gradients via continuous interpolation of the solver.

Local oracles in structured prediction.

Exact MAP inference in graphical models is inherently a combinatorial optimization problem. In both domains, the intractability of global inference motivates the use of local oracles. For example, the seminal iterated conditional modes algorithm
(
9
)
deterministically maximizes local conditional probabilities, while randomized variants yield provable global approximation guarantees
(
23
)
; together, they act as the graphical models analogue to (unregularized) LNS. During learning, intractable global marginal inference is typically bypassed using local oracles: pseudolikelihood (or composite likelihood) methods
(
8
;
28
;
45
)
optimize local conditionals of observed data, while local perturb-and-MAP
(
6
)
forms their noise-regularized counterpart. Approaches based on the local polytope
(
37
)
relax constraints, while our approach keeps the same constraints but optimizes over a subset of the variables at each iteration.

While
7
successfully used global perturb-and-MAP
(
38
)
for combinatorial layers, using local oracles as a layer remains a critical gap in the field which our work aims to bridge. Contrastive divergences
(
18
)
interpolate between pseudolikelihood (CD-
1
) to maximum likelihood estimation (CD-
\infty
)
(
21
;
5
)
. We will establish a similar result for the loss function associated with RLNS iterations.

Regularized LNS

3.1
Proposed framework

We now introduce our main contribution: using smoothed maximum operators, we define a stochastic, regularized version of LNS (RLNS), whose iterates follow a Markov chain with state space
{\mathcal{Y}}
. To do so, we first define a family of local regularization functions from a global one, via inclusion maps.

Definition 3.1

(Local regularizers)
.

For any subset of solutions
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}
, we define the inclusion map
\iota_{{\mathcal{Z}}}:\triangle^{\mathcal{Z}}\lhook\joinrel\to\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
from local distributions on
{\mathcal{Z}}
to global distributions on
{\mathcal{Y}}
via padding:

\displaystyle\iota_{{\mathcal{Z}}}({\bm{q}})\triangleq\left(\begin{cases}q_{\bm{y}}&\text{if ${\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}$}\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}\right)_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}.

Then, for any lower semicontinuous, strictly convex global regularizer
\Omega:\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we define its local counterpart
\Omega^{{\mathcal{Z}}}:\triangle^{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by composing it with
\iota_{\mathcal{Z}}
, as
\Omega^{\mathcal{Z}}\triangleq\Omega\circ\iota_{\mathcal{Z}}
.

While standard approaches given in

aim to solve a regularized version of the global optimization problem (
1
), we propose to regularize the LNS subproblems from
Algorithm

\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
, to define local distributions
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})\in\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
. RLNS is then obtained by iteratively sampling from these locally regularized distributions, as shown in

Ergodicity.

To guarantee that the distribution of the iterates
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}
of
Algorithm

2
asymptotically converges to a
unique
stationary distribution independent of the initialization
{\bm{y}}^{(0)}
(i.e., that the Markov chain is
ergodic
), the two following properties are sufficient:

1.

The algorithm employs an
irreducible variable selection scheme
, i.e., the distribution over frozen variables
S^{(k)}
induces a connected transition graph over the feasible set
{\mathcal{Y}}
.

2.

The local update distributions are
dense
, i.e.,
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})({\bm{y}})>0\;\forall{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
.

The first condition ensures that LNS can reach any feasible solution from any other one. This requirement is not very stringent in practice, and is typically met by practical implementations which either consider reduced scope or exploit decomposition into subproblems with specific structure
(
3
)
.
The second condition ensures that any path in the transition graph has a strictly positive transition probability, thereby establishing the irreducibility of the chain. Because the current state is always in its own neighborhood (
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
), dense local updates guarantee positive self-loop probabilities at every state, which in turn ensures aperiodicity.

Definition 3.2

(Stationary distribution of RLNS)
.

When the Markov chain
({\bm{y}}^{(k)})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
defined by
Algorithm

2
is ergodic, we denote by
\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}\in\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
its unique stationary distribution.

Rejection-free sampling.

In contrast to the approach of
46
,
which defines a Metropolis-Hastings-based sampler using local search heuristics,

Algorithm

does not involve any sample rejection mechanism.
As we empirically show in
Fig.

1
, this
enables updating more variables at a time without stagnating,
especially in low-temperature regimes.

3.2
Entropic LNS and Gibbs sampling

We now focus on the case where
\Omega
is Shannon’s negative entropy scaled by

\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle\Omega({\bm{q}})\triangleq-\gamma H^{s}({\bm{q}})=\gamma\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}{\bm{q}}({\bm{y}})\log{\bm{q}}({\bm{y}}).

This choice yields the Gibbs distribution

{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}({\bm{s}})({\bm{y}})\propto\exp(s_{\bm{y}}/\gamma)
. Computing its expectation (i.e.,
performing global marginal inference) is only tractable in certain settings,
e.g., if the global problem admits an exact dynamic programming solution

(
35
)
. The local RLNS update distribution

{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}({\bm{s}}_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})
, however, is a
local
Gibbs distribution on
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
, and
can be sampled from by regularized dynamic programming if the
local

problem can be solved by dynamic programming. This is the case, e.g., of our applications in
Sections

5.1
and
5.2
.

Proposition 3.3

(Entropic LNS as a block Gibbs sampler)
.

Assume
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
with
\gamma>0
. For any current solution
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
and frozen subset
S^{(k)}\subseteq[d]
, the locally regularized distribution matches the global regularized distribution conditioned on the fixed variables:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right)({\bm{y}})={\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\left({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\right)\quad\forall{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}.

Consequently,
Algorithm

2
implements an exact
block Gibbs sampler
with
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
as a target distribution, and if the variable selection scheme is irreducible, we have:

\displaystyle\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}({\bm{y}})={\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{y}})\propto\exp\left(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle/\gamma\right)\quad\forall{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}.

The proof is given in
Section

B.1
. Thus, in the Shannon-regularized case, the stationary distribution of the locally-regularized iterates of
Algorithm

2
matches the globally-regularized distribution. The next proposition shows that
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
is in fact the only separable regularizer with this property. The proof, given in
Section

B.2
, relies on a functional equation-based characterization of Shannon’s entropy from information theory
(
20
;
17
;
35
)
.

Proposition 3.4

(Characterization of stationary exactness)
.

Let
\Omega:\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a l.s.c. separable strictly convex regularization function, normalized such that
\Omega(\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}})=0
for any
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
. The RLNS stationary distribution matches the global target for all finite feasible sets
{\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, all maximization directions
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and all irreducible variable selection schemes, meaning

\displaystyle\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}={\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}}),\quad\textbf{if and only if}\quad\exists\gamma>0:\;\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}.

Rao-Blackwellization.

When using regularized dynamic programming to sample the next RLNS iterate
{\bm{y}}^{(k+1)}
from the local Gibbs distribution
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})
, computing its expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{y}}^{(k+1)}|{\bm{y}}^{(k)},S^{(k)}]
is tractable with the same complexity. Thus, we can perform Rao-Blackwellized MCMC
(
33
)
by simply averaging local expectations instead of simple samples along the Markov chain in order to reduce variance.

Perturbed LNS and tractable updates

In some applications, a solver for
the unregularized local problem in
Eq.

2
(local MAP oracle) is

Definition 3.5

(Perturbation-based regularization)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be a temperature parameter, and
{\mathcal{D}}
be a distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
admitting a positive density. Following

, we define the (global) distribution-space
perturbation-based regularization
function
\Omega_{\varepsilon}:\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}({\bm{q}})\triangleq\sup_{{\bm{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}}\left\{\langle{\bm{s}},{\bm{q}}\rangle-{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\{s_{\bm{y}}+\varepsilon\langle Z,{\bm{y}}\rangle\}\right]\right\}.

In
Section

B.3
, we show that
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is strictly convex on
\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
. The corresponding
global
regularized distribution is the
global
perturb-and-MAP distribution
(
38
)
given by
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{y}})={\mathbb{P}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\argmax_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\langle{\bm{\theta}}+\varepsilon Z,{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle={\bm{y}}\right)
, and its expectation is the expected perturbed maximizer
{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})}[Y]={\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\langle{\bm{\theta}}+\varepsilon Z,{\bm{y}}\rangle\right]
. We now show that the

perturb-and-MAP.

4
Learning with RLNS

In this section, we show how RLNS can be integrated as a combinatorial layer in a neural network.

4.1
RLNS-
\infty
and global Fenchel-Young loss

In this section, we consider the asymptotic regime where the number of RLNS iterations grows large (
K\to\infty
). Let
\Omega:\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a valid distribution-space regularizer, and
L_{\Omega}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be the corresponding global Fenchel-Young loss, as defined in
Definition

2.1
. For any target distribution
{\bm{p}}\in\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
, the gradient of this loss is given by
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}\,;{\bm{p}})={\mathbb{E}}_{{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})}[Y]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}}[Y]
.

Because the expectation of the global regularized distribution
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
is intractable, we can use the RLNS iterates
({\bm{y}}^{(k)})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
Algorithm

2
to construct an MCMC estimator of this gradient:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}}\,;{\bm{p}})\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\bm{y}}^{(k)}-{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}}[Y].

The behavior and bias of this stochastic gradient estimator depend heavily on the chosen regularization and the number of iterations
K
.

Shannon-regularized case.

When
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
,
Proposition

3.3
guarantees that the stationary distribution of the RLNS Markov chain exactly matches the target distribution, i.e.,
\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}={\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
. In this setting, the only source of bias in the gradient estimator stems from the finite mixing time of the Markov chain. While biased for any fixed
K
, the estimator is
consistent
in the
K\to\infty
limit.

General case.

For general regularizers
\Omega
, the estimator accumulates two distinct sources of bias: the finite mixing time of the chain, and the intrinsic distance between the RLNS stationary distribution
\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
and the true global target
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
. In
Appendix

A
, we derive theoretical bounds on this gap in the general setting (
Propositions

A.4
and
A.5
), as well as specific bounds tailored to the perturbation-based setting of
Section

3.3
based on the constraint graph (
Propositions

A.6
and
A.10
).

These bounds show that the approximation error scales with the number of shared constraints between frozen and free variables, providing a theoretical justification for variable selection schemes that minimize such cross-dependencies. We empirically validate them in
Sections

A.4
and
3
.

The practical choice of
K
.

In practice, treating
K
as a hyperparameter allows us to interpolate between two learning paradigms. Setting
K=1
minimizes computational overhead and provides unbiased gradients for a local Fenchel-Young loss (
Section

4.2
), yielding a principled Fenchel-Young counterpart to pseudolikelihood. Conversely, larger values of
K
better approximate the global Fenchel-Young loss gradients but incur higher computational costs.

4.2
RLNS-
1
and local Fenchel-Young loss

We study study the special case of
K=1
RLNS iteration. For any target feasible solution
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
and subset of fixed coordinates
S\subseteq[d]
, we denote the corresponding local neighborhood by
{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}\triangleq\{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}\mid{\bm{y}}^{\prime}_{[S]}={\bm{y}}_{[S]}\}
. Let
\nu\in\Delta^{2^{[d]}}
denote a distribution on coordinate subsets.

Definition 4.1

(Local Fenchel-Young loss)
.

Define the expected smoothed local maximum value function
F_{\bm{y}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{Y}}|}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}

\displaystyle F_{\bm{y}}({\bm{s}})\triangleq{\mathbb{E}}_{S\sim\nu}\left[f_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}}}\left({\bm{s}}_{\left[{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}\right]}\right)\right].

Letting its convex conjugate be
\Omega_{\bm{y}}\triangleq(F_{\bm{y}})^{*}
, the corresponding target-dependent,
local Fenchel-Young loss

L_{\Omega_{\bm{y}}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\mathrm{dom}(\Omega_{\bm{y}})\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is given by:

\displaystyle L_{\Omega_{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\theta}}\,;{\bm{p}})\triangleq F_{\bm{y}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})+\Omega_{\bm{y}}({\bm{p}})-\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}}[Y]\rangle.

The following proposition, which we prove in
Section

B.7
, shows we can obtain unbiased stochastic gradients for this loss directly from a single step of RLNS.

Proposition 4.2

(Unbiased gradients via RLNS-
1
)
.

For any
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, we have
\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}}\in\mathrm{dom}(\Omega_{\bm{y}})
. Moreover, the gradient of the local Fenchel-Young with target
{\bm{y}}
is given by:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\Omega_{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\theta}}\,;\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}})={\mathbb{E}}_{S\sim\nu}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{\left[{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}\right]}\right)}[Y]\right]-{\bm{y}}.

Consequently, executing
Algorithm

2
with ground-truth initialization
{\bm{y}}^{(0)}={\bm{y}}
and a single iteration
K=1
yields an unbiased estimator of
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\Omega_{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\theta}}\,;\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}})
.

Connection with pseudolikelihood.

In the Shannon-regularized setting
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
, this local Fenchel-Young loss recovers the pseudolikelihood (a.k.a. composite likelihood) objective
(
8
)
, which maximizes the likelihood of subsets of observed variables conditioned on the rest.

Proposition 4.3

(Equivalence to pseudolikelihood)
.

When
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
, the local Fenchel-Young loss with target
{\bm{y}}
equals the expected negative log-likelihood of
{\bm{y}}
conditioned on
{\bm{y}}_{[S]}
:

L_{\Omega_{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\theta}}\,;\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}})=-\gamma\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{S\sim\nu}\left[\log\left({\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{y}}_{[S]})\right)\right].

The derivation is given in
Section

B.8
. Thus, since the global Fenchel-Young loss is given by
L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}},\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}})=-\gamma\log{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{y}})
in this setting, the number of entropic RLNS iterations enables interpolating between pseudolikelihood and exact maximum likelihood estimation.

5.2
Generalized assignment problem

We now evaluate RLNS as a combinatorial layer on the generalized assignment problem (GAP)
(
31
)
. In the GAP, we are given
n
items and
m
bins with capacity
C_{j}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Each item-bin assignment has value
\theta_{ij}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and a weight
w_{ij}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The combinatorial problem writes:

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}}\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{ij}y_{ij}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{ij}\leq 1\quad\forall i\in[n],\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{ij}y_{ij}\leq C_{j}\quad\forall j\in[m].

The first constraint ensures each item is assigned to at most one bin, while the second one ensures that the capacity of each bin is not exceeded. Given a dataset of
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
pairs where
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m\times p}
contains relational features for each item-bin pair, the goal is to learn a neural network
f_{W}:{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that predicts assignment scores
\theta_{ij}=f_{W}({\bm{x}}_{ij})
, such that
\hat{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\theta}})\approx{\bm{y}}
.

Local oracles.

We choose to freeze the assignments to all bins except a randomly selected one at each RLNS iteration. The LNS subproblem is then a
0/1
1D Knapsack problem, which admits an exact dynamic programming solution
(
24
)
, and we can sample from the local regularized distributions via perturbed or regularized dynamic programming
(
47
)
. Detailed explanations and full experimental details are given in
Appendix

D
.

Results.

Higher numbers of RLNS iterations
K
better approximate global perturbed Fenchel-Young (PFY,
7
) learning (
Fig.

2(a)
). Augmenting
K
also reduces gradient variance, which is further reduced by Rao-Blackwellization (
Fig.

2(b)
, top). Local MAP oracles are orders or magnitude faster than global ones, especially when increasing scale (
Fig.

2(b)
, bottom) (we set
m=n/10
).

5.3
Stochastic vehicle scheduling

We now evaluate RLNS as a combinatorial layer on a stochastic vehicle scheduling benchmark
(
39
;
14
;
19
)
. The objective is to route a fleet of vehicles in order to serve a set of spatio-temporal tasks, minimizing the sum of the deterministic vehicle cost and the expected delay cost across a set of stochastic scenarios. The problem is framed as finding paths on a directed acyclic graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}})
, where a feasible routing
{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{|{\mathcal{E}}|}
satisfies flow conservation and unit demand constraints. To imitate the ground-truth expert (the optimal solution of a two-stage stochastic MILP with full scenario knowledge), a feed-forward neural network predicts edge costs
\theta_{e}
from edge-level features, and the predicted routing is obtained via a flow LP. Complete experimental details are provided in
Appendix

E
.

Approach and baselines.

We use RLNS-
1
with perturbation-based regularization. At each step, we freeze the edges corresponding to
66\%
of randomly selected vehicle routes, and exactly re-optimize the remaining free variables using a significantly smaller flow LP. We compare RLNS against global perturbed Fenchel-Young learning (PFY,
7
), blackbox differentiation (DBB,
48
), an unstructured binary cross-entropy loss (BCE, see, e.g.,
22
), and a non-learned greedy policy that minimizes only the deterministic vehicle cost.

Results.

All learned methods quickly reach the optimal vehicle cost by using the same number of vehicles as the expert. However, as shown in
Fig.

2(c)
, in terms of delay cost, RLNS-
1
outperforms DBB and BCE baselines and nearly matches the global PFY loss while only requiring the solver to process a subproblem of approximately one-third the original size.

Conclusion

Bridging communities and future work.

While we exhibit links between LNS and Gibbs sampling and prior work bridged local search with Metropolis-Hastings
(
46
)
, we believe more combinatorial optimization metaheuristics can be revisited from a sampling perspective. For instance, LNS can actually use local search heuristics as approximate sub-problem solvers in practice
(
40
)
: one could therefore combine both perspectives and bridge such "local search within LNS" metaheuristics with "Metropolis-within-Gibbs" MCMC samplers
(
41
;
4
)
.

Limitations.

While Shannon regularization theoretically guarantees exact block Gibbs sampling, efficient local marginal oracles can remain intractable even within restricted neighborhoods for many combinatorial problems. Our perturbation-based formulation circumvents this by relying solely on standard local MAP oracles, ensuring RLNS remains tractable whenever standard LNS is. However, this scalability comes at the cost of introducing a structural bias into the stationary distribution.

Appendix A
Geometrical insights and approximation bounds for general regularizers

When
\Omega\neq-\gamma H^{s}
, the locally regularized distributions used by RLNS may differ from the conditional distributions of the global smoothed maximizer
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
, thereby precluding the block Gibbs sampling perspective established in
Proposition

3.3
. In this section, we give geometrical insights into this more general case, and establish bounds on the distance between the algorithm’s stationary distribution
\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
and the target
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
.

A.1
Geometrical insights

We first show that the update distributions of RLNS and the ideal, exact Gibbs sampler with
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\in\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
as target are in fact two different projections of the latter onto the local simplex
\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
.

Proposition A.1

(RLNS and Gibbs updates as Bregman projections)
.

Let
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}
be the neighborhood used by
Algorithm

2
at step
k
, and
B_{\Omega}:\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\times\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
denote the Bregman divergence generated by
\Omega
.
The local RLNS update distribution corresponds to the projection of the global smoothed maximizer
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
onto the local simplex
\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
with respect to
B_{\Omega}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right)=\argmin_{{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}B_{\Omega}\left(\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}})\|{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\right).

Similarly, the update distribution of the ideal block Gibbs sampler with target
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
is its projection onto the local simplex with respect to the Kullback-Leibler divergence:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\left(\cdot\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\right)=\argmin_{{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}})\|{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\right).

Proposition

A.1
, proved in
Section

B.6
, shows that RLNS and the ideal Gibbs sampler update distributions both correspond to Bregman projections of the same target distribution
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
onto the local simplex
\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
, simply with distinct geometries. While RLNS uses the same regularization
\Omega
as the one used by the global target distribution, Gibbs sampling uses Shannon’s negative entropy.

This gives a geometrical point of view on the claim of
Proposition

3.3
. Indeed, since we have
D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\frac{1}{\gamma}B_{-\gamma H^{s}}
where
B_{-\gamma H^{s}}
is the Bregman divergence generated by Shannon’s scaled negative entropy, the corresponding projection problems are equivalent when
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
in the first place: the
B_{\Omega}
-projections match
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
-projections, and RLNS matches an exact Gibbs sampler for
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
.

We now give a block-coordinate ascent perspective on the expected RLNS updates.

Proposition A.2

(Expected RLNS as block-coordinate ascent)
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
, and let
{\mathcal{M}}\triangleq{\mathrm{conv}}({\mathcal{Y}})
. Let
\Omega:\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a strictly convex distribution-space regularizer. Its mean-space counterpart
\Omega_{\mathcal{M}}:{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, defined as:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathcal{M}}({\bm{\mu}})\triangleq\min_{{\bm{p}}\in\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\text{ s.t. }{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{p}}[Y]={\bm{\mu}}}\Omega({\bm{p}}),

is strictly convex on
{\mathcal{M}}
. Furthermore, for any current solution
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
and subset of frozen coordinates
S^{(k)}\subseteq[d]
, the expected RLNS local update matches the exact BCA update of the regularized mean-space objective over the free variables:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right)}[Y]=\argmax_{{\bm{\mu}}\in{\mathcal{M}}\text{ s.t. }{\bm{\mu}}_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}}\left\{\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\mu}}\rangle-\Omega_{\mathcal{M}}({\bm{\mu}})\right\}.

The proof is given in
Section

B.5
.

A.2
Local consistency gap

We first define the
local consistency gap
, which measures the maximum discrepancy between an LNS update and the ideal Gibbs-like update that would preserve the global regularized distribution.

Definition A.3

(Local consistency gap)
.

Let
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
,
S^{(k)}\subseteq[d]
, and let
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
denote the corresponding subset of feasible solutions, as defined in
Algorithms

1
and
2
.

We define the
local consistency gap

\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}
as the maximum TV distance between the global regularized distribution
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
conditioned on the subset of structures matching
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}
on coordinates
S^{(k)}
and the local RLNS update distribution (seen as two elements of
\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
):

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}\triangleq\max_{{\bm{y}}^{(k)},S^{(k)}}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})(\cdot\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]})\,,{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})\right).

As shown in
Section

3.2
, if
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
for some
\gamma>0
, the local updates match the conditionals exactly, implying
\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}=0
. For general regularizers,
\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}>0
. We now bound the total bias of the algorithm.

Proposition A.4

(Bias of RLNS)
.

Let
P
be the transition kernel of the LNS Markov chain defined in
Algorithm

2
. Let
P^{*}
be the transition kernel of the ideal Gibbs sampler targeting
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
, i.e., which uses updates of the form:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{k+1}\sim{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\left(\cdot\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\right).

Let
\tau_{m}(P^{*})
be the Dobrushin contraction coefficient of
(P^{*})^{m}
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\tau_{m}(P^{*})\triangleq\max_{{\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left((P^{*})^{m}({\bm{y}}\,,\cdot)\,,(P^{*})^{m}({\bm{y}}^{\prime},\cdot)\right).

Let
m\geq 1
such that
\tau_{m}(P^{*})<1
. Then, the distance between the LNS stationary distribution
\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
and the target is bounded by:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}\,,{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\right)\leq\frac{m}{1-\tau_{m}(P^{*})}\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}.

Proposition

A.4
, which is proved in
Section

B.9
, enables us to now focus on bounding the local consistency gap
\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}
as it translates to a global bound on the distance between
\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}
and
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
.

We first bound the local consistency gap under the assumption that the deviation of the regularization from the scaled Shannon’s negative entropy
\Omega-(-\gamma H^{s})
has a Lipschitz gradient with respect to the
\ell_{1}
norm.

Proposition A.5

(Consistency gap bound via relative smoothness)
.

Let
\gamma>0
be a reference temperature, and
h_{\gamma}:\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the deviation of the regularization
\Omega
from the scaled Shannon’s negative entropy:

\displaystyle h_{\gamma}({\bm{p}})\triangleq\Omega({\bm{p}})-(-\gamma H^{s})({\bm{p}})=\Omega({\bm{p}})+\gamma\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}p_{\bm{y}}\log p_{\bm{y}}.

If
h_{\gamma}
is
L_{\Omega,\gamma}
-smooth with respect to the
\ell_{1}
norm, we have:

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}\leq\frac{L_{\Omega,\gamma}}{\gamma}.

The proof is given in
Section

B.10
, and leverages the characterization of the RLNS and Gibbs updates as Bregman projections given in
Proposition

A.1
.

We now bound the consistency gap
\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}
in the special case of perturbation-based regularizers.

Proposition A.6

(Consistency gap bound in the perturbation-based setting)
.

Let
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
be a perturbation-based regularization function, as defined in
Definition

3.5
. Let
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
denote the corresponding global regularized distribution.

The local consistency gap
\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega_{\varepsilon}}
is bounded by the maximum probability mass of the global regularized distribution
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
falling outside a local neighborhood:

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\leq\max_{{\bm{y}}^{(k)},S^{(k)}}{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\mathcal{Y}}\setminus{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}),

where the maximum is taken over all feasible solutions
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
and subsets of coordinates
S^{(k)}\subseteq[d]
, which define the neighborhood
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
.

The proof is given in
Section

B.11
.

A.3
Constraint graph and cut-bounded bias

In this section, we analyze the bias of perturbed LNS (i.e., RLNS with perturbation-based regularization as defined in
Section

3.3
) through the lens of its variable selection scheme. We begin with an idealized scenario: when the feasible set perfectly factorizes into independent blocks of variables, perturbed LNS achieves zero bias.

Proposition A.7

(Exactness of Perturbed LNS on product spaces)
.

Let the coordinates
[d]
be partitioned into
M
disjoint blocks
[d]=\bigsqcup_{m=1}^{M}B_{m}
, and assume the feasible set factorizes accordingly:
{\mathcal{Y}}=\prod_{m=1}^{M}{\mathcal{Y}}_{m}
, where
{\mathcal{Y}}_{m}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{|B_{m}|}
.

Assume that the noise distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
factorizes over this partition, meaning that if
Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}
, the block subvectors
Z_{[B_{m}]}
are mutually independent.

If, at each step
k
, the frozen subset of coordinates
S^{(k)}
is a union of blocks (i.e.,
S^{(k)}=\bigsqcup_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}^{(k)}}B_{m}
for some index set
{\mathcal{M}}^{(k)}\subset[M]
), then the local consistency gap is zero (
\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega_{\varepsilon}}=0
), and
Algorithm

3
implements a block Gibbs sampler for the global regularized distribution.

Consequently, its stationary distribution is exactly:

\displaystyle\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega_{\varepsilon}}={\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}}).

While perfect factorization guarantees zero bias, most combinatorial optimization problems feature interacting variables bound by shared constraints. To systematically analyze these interactions, we formalize the problem structure using a constraint graph. Assume the feasible set is defined by a set of linear constraints
C\triangleq\{1,\dots,m\}
as:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Y}}\triangleq\{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}\mid{\bm{A}}{\bm{y}}\leq{\bm{b}}\},

where
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times d}
and
{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

Definition A.8

(Constraint hypergraph)
.

The
constraint hypergraph
associated with the feasible set
{\mathcal{Y}}
is a hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}_{C}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}}_{C})
, where the vertices correspond to the decision variables
{\mathcal{V}}=[d]
, and and each hyperedge
e_{c}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{C}
corresponds to a constraint
c\in C
, containing exactly the variables involved in that constraint:

{\mathcal{E}}_{C}\triangleq\{e_{c}\mid c\in C\}\quad\text{where}\quad e_{c}\triangleq\{i\in[d]\mid A_{c,i}\neq 0\}.

At any iteration
k
, the choice of frozen variables
S^{(k)}\subseteq[d]
and free variables
F^{(k)}\triangleq[d]\setminus S^{(k)}
essentially partitions the vertices of this constraint hypergraph. This partition naturally divides the constraint indices
C
(and thus the hyperedges) into three disjoint sets:

•

C_{F}(S^{(k)})
: constraints exclusively involving free variables
F^{(k)}
;

•

C_{S}(S^{(k)})
: constraints exclusively involving frozen variables
S^{(k)}
;

•

C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})
: the
cut constraints
, which involve at least one variable in
F^{(k)}
and one in
S^{(k)}
(corresponding to the hyperedges in the cut-set of
{\mathcal{H}}_{C}
).

We can therefore decompose the constraints and write the cut constraints evaluated on a vector
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as
{\bm{A}}_{[C_{\partial}(S^{(k)}),F^{(k)}]}{\bm{y}}_{[F^{(k)}]}+{\bm{A}}_{[C_{\partial}(S^{(k)}),S^{(k)}]}{\bm{y}}_{[S^{(k)}]}\leq{\bm{b}}_{[C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})]}
.

To isolate the bias introduced specifically by these cut constraints, we define a relaxed space where they are dropped. This effectively forces the subproblem back into the idealized product-space setting described in
Proposition

A.7
.

Definition A.9

(Relaxed product space)
.

For a given partition defined by
S^{(k)}
, we define the
relaxed feasible set

{\mathcal{Y}}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)})\triangleq{\mathcal{Y}}_{F}(S^{(k)})\times{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}(S^{(k)})
, where:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Y}}_{F}(S^{(k)})

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{{\bm{y}}_{F}\in\{0,1\}^{|F^{(k)}|}\mid{\bm{A}}_{[C_{F}(S^{(k)}),F^{(k)}]}{\bm{y}}_{F}\leq{\bm{b}}_{[C_{F}(S^{(k)})]}\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}(S^{(k)})

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{{\bm{y}}_{S}\in\{0,1\}^{|S^{(k)}|}\mid{\bm{A}}_{[C_{S}(S^{(k)}),S^{(k)}]}{\bm{y}}_{S}\leq{\bm{b}}_{[C_{S}(S^{(k)})]}\right\}.

Notice that
{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)})
, and equality holds if and only if
C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})=\emptyset
. Thus, the relaxed space strictly encompasses the true feasible set, allowing us to bound the local consistency gap by measuring how often the unconstrained, relaxed updates violate the dropped cut constraints.

Proposition A.10

(Cut-bounded consistency gap)
.

For any feasible solution
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
and subset of frozen coordinates
S^{(k)}
, let
V_{c}(S^{(k)},{\bm{y}}^{(k)})\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}_{F}(S^{(k)})
denote the set of assignments for the free variables that violate the cut constraint
c\in C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})
when combined with
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}
:

\displaystyle V_{c}(S^{(k)},{\bm{y}}^{(k)})\triangleq\left\{{\bm{y}}_{F}\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{F}(S^{(k)})\mid{\bm{A}}_{[\{c\},F^{(k)}]}{\bm{y}}_{F}+{\bm{A}}_{[\{c\},S^{(k)}]}{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}>b_{c}\right\}.

Moreover, define the relaxed perturbed maximizer as:

\displaystyle Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)})\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)})}\langle{\bm{\theta}}+\varepsilon Z,{\bm{y}}\rangle.

The local consistency gap of the perturbed LNS is bounded by:

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\leq 2\max_{{\bm{y}}^{(k)},S^{(k)}}{\mathbb{P}}\left((Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)}))_{[F^{(k)}]}\in\bigcup_{c\in C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})}V_{c}(S^{(k)},{\bm{y}}^{(k)})\right).

Furthermore, applying a union bound and bounding by the worst-case cut constraint yields:

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\leq 2\max_{{\bm{y}}^{(k)},S^{(k)}}|C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})|\max_{c\in C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})}{\mathbb{P}}\left((Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)}))_{[F^{(k)}]}\in V_{c}(S^{(k)},{\bm{y}}^{(k)})\right).

Motivation for min-cut variable selection.

Proposition

A.10
, proved in
Section

B.13
, provides a justification for constraint-graph heuristics in LNS. The upper bound on the bias of the local update scales with
|C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})|
, the number of cut constraints separating the frozen variables
S^{(k)}
from the free variables
F^{(k)}
. This motivates the design of variable selection schemes that minimize
|C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})|
in RLNS. In
Section

A.4
, we empirically validate this theoretical on the maximum weight independent set problem.

A.4
Min-cut RLNS on the maximum weight independent set

To empirically validate the cut-size-based bias bound from
Section

A.3
, we evaluate RLNS with pertubation-based regularization on the maximum weight independent set (MWIS) polytope. Given an undirected graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{N}},{\mathcal{E}})
, the MWIS problem writes:

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{|{\mathcal{N}}|}}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in{\mathcal{N}}}\theta_{n}y_{n}

s.t.

\displaystyle y_{u}+y_{v}\leq 1\quad\forall(u,v)\in{\mathcal{E}}.

Constraint graph.

For the MWIS, the constraint hypergraph
{\mathcal{H}}_{C}
exactly reduces to the standard problem graph
{\mathcal{G}}
: since each constraint involves exactly two nodes, every hyperedge has size 2 and corresponds directly to an edge in
{\mathcal{G}}
. To test the impact of the cut constraint size isolated in our theoretical bound, we design variable selection schemes with varying average cut sizes.

We report results averaged across 10 random seeds, where each seed controls the generation of a distinct random graph instance. Specifically, we generate soft random geometric graphs by randomly placing
|{\mathcal{N}}|=100
nodes in a 2D space and assigning edges with a probability that decays exponentially with the Euclidean distance
d(u,v)
between nodes.

Variable selection scheme.

For each graph instance, we perform an offline phase where we generate a pool of
100
node partitions, which all divide
{\mathcal{N}}
into 5 blocks of
|{\mathcal{N}}|/5=20
nodes each. These partitions are generated via
10^{4}
steps of Metropolis-Hastings MCMC (where a proposal partition is obtained by exchanging the block labels of two random nodes) to be sampled approximately proportionally to
\exp(-\beta\cdot c)
, where the cut size
c
is the total number of edges
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{E}}
such that
u
and
v
belong to different blocks in the partition. By adjusting the inverse temperature
\beta
, we can smoothly interpolate the variable selection scheme from favoring max-cuts (
\beta<0
) to min-cuts (
\beta>0
): see
Fig.

3
(bottom) for a visualization. During the online RLNS procedure, at each step, we uniformly sample a partition from the offline pool and pick exactly one of its blocks uniformly at random to serve as the free variables, keeping the assignments of all other nodes frozen.

Results.

As illustrated in
Fig.

3
(top), the results validate our theoretical insights from
Section

A.3
: variable selection schemes with minimal cut size
|C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})|
in the constraint graph yield a better convergence to the exact global regularized distribution
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
.

B.1
Proof of
Proposition

3.3
(
Proposition 3.3

(Entropic LNS as a block Gibbs sampler)
.
)

Proof.

When
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
, we have, for any
D\in{\mathbb{N}},\,{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}({\bm{x}})=\left(\frac{\exp(x_{i}/\gamma)}{\sum_{j=1}^{D}\exp(x_{j}/\gamma)}\right)_{i=1}^{D}.

Thus, on the one hand, the local regularized distribution writes, for all
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
and
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right)({\bm{y}})=\frac{\exp\left(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle/\gamma\right)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle/\gamma)}.

On the other hand, the conditional probability of the global regularized distribution is given by:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\left({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{y}})}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}\\
\text{s.t.}{\bm{y}}^{\prime}_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\end{subarray}}{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{y}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{y}})}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{y}}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle/\gamma)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\rangle/\gamma)}\times\left(\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\frac{\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle/\gamma)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\rangle/\gamma)}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle/\gamma)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\rangle/\gamma)}\times\frac{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime\prime}\rangle/\gamma)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle/\gamma)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle/\gamma)}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\exp(\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\rangle/\gamma)},

which therefore gives:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right)({\bm{y}})={\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\left({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\right).

Thus,
Algorithm

2
implements a block Gibbs sampler for the target distribution
\pi\triangleq{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
. To check that detailed balance is satisfied, let
\nu(S^{(k)})
denote the probability of selecting the frozen subset
S^{(k)}
. The transition probability from
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}
to
{\bm{y}}
is:

\displaystyle P({\bm{y}}^{(k)}\to{\bm{y}})=\sum_{S^{(k)}\subseteq[d]}\nu(S^{(k)})\mathds{1}_{\{{\bm{y}}_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\}}\pi\left({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\right).

Multiplying by the stationary distribution candidate
\pi({\bm{y}}^{(k)})
, we get:

\displaystyle\pi({\bm{y}}^{(k)})P({\bm{y}}^{(k)}\to{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S^{(k)}\subseteq[d]}\nu(S^{(k)})\mathds{1}_{\{{\bm{y}}_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\}}\pi({\bm{y}}^{(k)})\frac{\pi({\bm{y}})}{\sum_{{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\pi({\bm{z}})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S^{(k)}\subseteq[d]}\nu(S^{(k)})\mathds{1}_{\{{\bm{y}}_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\}}\frac{\pi({\bm{y}}^{(k)})\pi({\bm{y}})}{\sum_{{\bm{z}}:\,{\bm{z}}_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}}\pi({\bm{z}})}.

Because the indicator function enforces
{\bm{y}}_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}
, the denominator is strictly identical whether we condition on
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}
or
{\bm{y}}_{[S^{(k)}]}
. The expression inside the sum is therefore perfectly symmetric with respect to
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}
and
{\bm{y}}
, which immediately yields:

\displaystyle\pi({\bm{y}}^{(k)})P({\bm{y}}^{(k)}\to{\bm{y}})=\pi({\bm{y}})P({\bm{y}}\to{\bm{y}}^{(k)}),

so that
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
is a stationary distribution of the Markov chain. Finally, an irreducible variable selection scheme ensures the chain is ergodic (since the strictly positive entropic update distributions guarantee dense transitions and aperiodicity via self-loops). This gives that the stationary distribution is uniquely equal to
\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}={\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
, and that the chain converges to it.
∎

B.2
Proof of
Proposition

3.4
(
Proposition 3.4

(Characterization of stationary exactness)
.
)

Proof.

The sense (
\impliedby
) is established by
Proposition

3.3
, so we focus on the (
\implies
) implication.

Assume that
\Omega:\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is separable, meaning
\Omega({\bm{p}})=\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\omega(p_{\bm{y}})
for some strictly convex function
\omega
. Since
\Omega(\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}})=0\;\;\forall{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, we assume standard simplex normalization
\omega(0)=\omega(1)=0
without loss of generality. We assume that
\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}={\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
for all score vectors
\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}}
, and we will show this forces
\Omega=-\gamma H^{s}
for some
\gamma>0
.

Problem definition.

Consider a state space of dimension
d=3
with feasible set
{\mathcal{Y}}=\{{\bm{e}}_{1},{\bm{e}}_{2},{\bm{e}}_{3}\}
. Let
{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be any maximization direction. Note that in this setting, we have
\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}}={\bm{\theta}}
. We instantiate the following specific variable selection strategy for
Algorithm

2
: at each step
k
, given a current solution
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}={\bm{e}}_{i}
, the algorithm samples a frozen coordinate subset
S^{(k)}\in\{\{1\},\{3\}\}
uniformly at random. We strictly prevent freezing coordinate
2
.

This choice restricts the possible transitions. For instance, if
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}={\bm{e}}_{1}
(meaning
y^{(k)}_{1}=1
), freezing coordinate
3
(where
y^{(k)}_{3}=0
) creates the local neighborhood
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}=\{{\bm{e}}_{1},{\bm{e}}_{2}\}
. However, it is impossible to transition directly between
{\bm{e}}_{1}
and
{\bm{e}}_{3}
in a single step, as that would require freezing coordinate
2
, which our algorithm never does. The state transition graph on
{\mathcal{Y}}
is therefore a simple line graph:
{\bm{e}}_{1}\leftrightarrow{\bm{e}}_{2}\leftrightarrow{\bm{e}}_{3}
.

Detailed balance.

Because the transition graph is a tree (it contains no cycles), any stationary distribution on this graph must satisfy detailed balance. Since we assume the LNS stationary distribution matches the global target (
\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega}={\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
), the target distribution
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
itself must satisfy detailed balance along the edges of the tree. Let
\bm{\pi}\triangleq{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
, i.e.,
\pi_{i}\triangleq{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{e}}_{i})
. The balance equation between
{\bm{e}}_{1}
and
{\bm{e}}_{2}
is:

\displaystyle\pi_{1}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}({\bm{e}}_{1}\to{\bm{e}}_{2})=\pi_{2}\cdot{\mathbb{P}}({\bm{e}}_{2}\to{\bm{e}}_{1}).

The transition from
{\bm{e}}_{1}
to
{\bm{e}}_{2}
(resp. from
{\bm{e}}_{2}
to
{\bm{e}}_{1}
) occurs by choosing to freeze coordinate
3
(which occurs with probability
1/2
) and then sampling
{\bm{e}}_{2}
(resp.
{\bm{e}}_{1}
) from the local distribution
q^{\{1,2\}}\triangleq{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})
(where
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}=\{{\bm{e}}_{1},{\bm{e}}_{2}\}
). To simplify notation, let
q_{1}\triangleq q^{\{1,2\}}({\bm{e}}_{1})
and
q_{2}\triangleq q^{\{1,2\}}({\bm{e}}_{2})
. We have:

\displaystyle\pi_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}q_{2}\right)=\pi_{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}q_{1}\right).

Since local probabilities sum to
1
, substituting
q_{2}=1-q_{1}
immediately yields:

\displaystyle q_{1}=\frac{\pi_{1}}{\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{2}=\frac{\pi_{2}}{\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}}.

(3)

Optimality conditions.

Because
\Omega({\bm{p}})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\omega(p_{i})
is strictly convex and differentiable on the interior of the simplex, the global distribution
\bm{\pi}={\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
is by definition the unique solution to the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{p}}>0}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\big(\omega(p_{i})-\theta_{i}p_{i}\big)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}p_{i}=1.

We introduce a Lagrange multiplier
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}
for the simplex equality constraint. The Lagrangian is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bm{p}},\nu)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\big(\omega(p_{i})-\theta_{i}p_{i}\big)-\nu\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}p_{i}-1\right).

The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) stationarity conditions require the partial derivatives of the Lagrangian with respect to each
p_{i}
to be zero at optimality:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\pi_{i}}(\bm{\pi},\nu^{\star})=\omega^{\prime}(\pi_{i})-\theta_{i}-\nu^{\star}=0\implies\omega^{\prime}(\pi_{i})=\theta_{i}+\nu^{\star}.

By subtracting the optimality condition of
i=2
from
i=1
, the multiplier
\nu
cancels out, yielding:

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}(\pi_{1})-\omega^{\prime}(\pi_{2})=\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}.

(4)

Similarly, the local distribution
q^{\{1,2\}}
solves by definition the local constrained problem:

\displaystyle\min_{q_{1},q_{2}>0}\quad

\displaystyle\big(\omega(q_{1})-\theta_{1}q_{1}\big)+\big(\omega(q_{2})-\theta_{2}q_{2}\big)

s.t.

\displaystyle q_{1}+q_{2}=1.

Note that the
\omega(0)
term due to padding the local distribution
{\bm{q}}
into a global one (see the definition of the local regularizer in
Definition

3.1
) vanishes since we assumed
\omega(0)=\omega(1)=0
.
Introducing a local Lagrange multiplier
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the local Lagrangian is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{local}}({\bm{q}},\mu)=\big(\omega(q_{1})-\theta_{1}q_{1}\big)+\big(\omega(q_{2})-\theta_{2}q_{2}\big)-\mu(q_{1}+q_{2}-1).

The KKT stationarity conditions for the local problem similarly yield
\omega^{\prime}(q_{i})=\theta_{i}+\mu^{*}
. Subtracting the condition for
q_{2}
from
q_{1}
gives:

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}(q_{1})-\omega^{\prime}(q_{2})=\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}.

(5)

Combining
Eq.

4
and
Eq.

5
, we get:

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}(\pi_{1})-\omega^{\prime}(\pi_{2})=\omega^{\prime}(q_{1})-\omega^{\prime}(q_{2}).

Finally, substituting the expressions of
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
derived in
Eq.

3
, we get:

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}(\pi_{1})-\omega^{\prime}(\pi_{2})=\omega^{\prime}\left(\frac{\pi_{1}}{\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}}\right)-\omega^{\prime}\left(\frac{\pi_{2}}{\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}}\right).

(6)

Functional equation.

Let
y\triangleq\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}
and
x\triangleq\frac{\pi_{1}}{\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}}
, such that
\pi_{1}=xy
and
\pi_{2}=(1-x)y
. Since the global scores
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}
are unconstrained in
{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}
is surjective onto the relative interior
\mathrm{ri}(\triangle^{3})
, we have that
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
independently span
(0,1)
, so that
x
and
y
also independently span
(0,1)
.

Substituting
\pi_{1}=xy
and
\pi_{2}=(1-x)y
into
Eq.

6
yields:

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}(xy)-\omega^{\prime}((1-x)y)=\omega^{\prime}(x)-\omega^{\prime}(1-x).

We integrate this equation with respect to
x
(holding
y
constant):

\displaystyle\int\left[\omega^{\prime}(xy)-\omega^{\prime}((1-x)y)\right]dx

\displaystyle=\int\left[\omega^{\prime}(x)-\omega^{\prime}(1-x)\right]dx

\displaystyle\implies\frac{1}{y}\omega(xy)+\frac{1}{y}\omega((1-x)y)

\displaystyle=\omega(x)+\omega(1-x)+C(y).

Multiplying by
y
, we get:

\displaystyle\omega(xy)+\omega((1-x)y)=y(\omega(x)+\omega(1-x))+yC(y).

(7)

To determine the integration term
yC(y)
, we evaluate the limit as
x\to 1
. Because we normalized
\omega(0)=\omega(1)=0
, taking
x\to 1
yields:

\displaystyle\omega(y)+\omega(0)=y(\omega(1)+\omega(0))+yC(y)\implies\omega(y)=yC(y).

Substituting
yC(y)=\omega(y)
back into
Eq.

7
yields the functional equation:

\displaystyle\omega(xy)+\omega((1-x)y)-\omega(y)=y(\omega(x)+\omega(1-x))\quad\forall\;x,y\in(0,1).

As first established in
20
and extended in
17
, all measurable solutions to this functional equation take the form
\omega(p)=\gamma p\log p+cp
for some constants
\gamma,c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since we assumed
\omega(1)=0
, we have
c=0
. Moreover, since we assumed
\omega
to be strictly convex, we get
\gamma>0
, which concludes the proof.
∎

B.3
Strict convexity of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}

In this section, we establish that the perturbation-based regularizer
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
defined in
Definition

3.5
is strictly convex over the entire closed simplex
\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
.

Proposition B.1

(Strict convexity of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
)
.

Let
\Omega_{\varepsilon}:\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the perturbation-based regularization function defined in
Definition

3.5
. If the noise distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
admits a strictly positive density on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, then
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is strictly convex on the entire closed simplex
\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
.

Proof.

Let
{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
be two distinct probability distributions, and let
\lambda\in(0,1)
. We define their convex combination
{\bm{r}}\triangleq\lambda{\bm{p}}+(1-\lambda){\bm{q}}
. The support of
{\bm{r}}
is the union of the supports of
{\bm{p}}
and
{\bm{q}}
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{Z}}\triangleq\mathrm{supp}({\bm{r}})=\mathrm{supp}({\bm{p}})\cup\mathrm{supp}({\bm{q}})\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}
.

Consider the local simplex
\triangle^{\mathcal{Z}}
and the inclusion map
\iota_{\mathcal{Z}}:\triangle^{\mathcal{Z}}\lhook\joinrel\to\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
as defined in
Definition

3.1
. By construction,
{\bm{p}}
and
{\bm{q}}
belong to the face
\iota_{\mathcal{Z}}(\triangle^{\mathcal{Z}})
, and
{\bm{r}}
lies strictly in the relative interior of this face, meaning
{\bm{r}}\in\mathrm{ri}(\iota_{\mathcal{Z}}(\triangle^{\mathcal{Z}}))
.

To prove strict convexity, we must show that:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}({\bm{r}})<\lambda\Omega_{\varepsilon}({\bm{p}})+(1-\lambda)\Omega_{\varepsilon}({\bm{q}}).

Assume for contradiction that equality holds. Since
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is a supremum of affine functions, it is convex and lower semi-continuous. The equality at an interior point of the segment
[{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}]
implies that
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
must be strictly affine on the entire segment
[{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}]
. Because
{\bm{r}}\in\mathrm{ri}(\iota_{\mathcal{Z}}(\triangle^{\mathcal{Z}}))
, there exists a small line segment centered at
{\bm{r}}
, contained entirely within
[{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}]
, that lies completely within the relative interior
\mathrm{ri}(\iota_{\mathcal{Z}}(\triangle^{\mathcal{Z}}))
. If
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is affine on
[{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}]
, it must be affine on this sub-segment.

We now analyze the restriction of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
to this face. As shown in
B.4
Proof of
(
)
, the local regularizer
\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{\mathcal{Z}}\triangleq\Omega_{\varepsilon}\circ\iota_{\mathcal{Z}}
is exactly the Fenchel conjugate of the local perturbed maximum function
F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Z}}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{Z}}|}\to{\mathbb{R}}
:

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Z}}}({\bm{u}})\triangleq{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\left\{u_{\bm{y}}+\varepsilon\langle{\bm{y}},Z\rangle\right\}\right].

By
13
, we have that:

1.

The domain of the conjugate of
F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Z}}}
is
\triangle^{\mathcal{Z}}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{dom}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{\mathcal{Z}})=\triangle^{\mathcal{Z}}
.

2.

F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Z}}}
is l.s.c., proper, convex and differentiable on
{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{Z}}|}
, which makes it essentially smooth.

42
then gives that its Fenchel conjugate

is essentially strictly convex. Therefore, it is strictly convex on the relative interior of its domain,

This establishes that

B.4
Proof of
Proposition

3.6
(
Proposition 3.6

(Tractability of perturbed LNS)
.
)

Proof.

Let
F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
denote the following local perturbed maximum function, defined on scores restricted to the neighborhood
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
:

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}|}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\left\{u_{\bm{y}}+\varepsilon\langle{\bm{y}},Z\rangle\right\}\right].

We first establish that the local regularization function
\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\triangleq\Omega_{\varepsilon}\circ\iota^{(k)}
is the Fenchel conjugate of
F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
.
Starting from the definition of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, we have for any local distribution
{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}({\bm{q}})

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\varepsilon}(\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}}))

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\bm{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}}\left\{\langle{\bm{s}},\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}})\rangle-{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\max_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{s_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}+\varepsilon\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},Z\rangle\right\}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\bm{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}}\left\{\left(\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}s_{\bm{y}}q_{\bm{y}}\right)-{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\max_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{s_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}+\varepsilon\langle{\bm{y}}^{\prime},Z\rangle\right\}\right]\right\}.

We decompose the global score vector
{\bm{s}}
into components inside
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
(denoted
{\bm{s}}_{\mathrm{in}}
) and outside
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
(denoted
{\bm{s}}_{{\mathrm{out}}}
). To maximize the objective inside the supremum, we observe that
{\bm{s}}_{{\mathrm{out}}}
only appears in the negative expectation term. Sending the coordinates of
{\bm{s}}_{{\mathrm{out}}}
to
-\infty
minimizes the expectation term (by eliminating
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\notin{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
from the maximum) without affecting the linear term
\langle{\bm{s}}_{{\mathrm{in}}},{\bm{q}}\rangle
.

Formally, we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}\to-\infty\\
\forall{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}\setminus{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{s_{{\bm{y}}}+\varepsilon\langle{\bm{y}},Z\rangle\right\}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\left\{({\bm{s}}_{\mathrm{in}})_{{\bm{y}}}+\varepsilon\langle{\bm{y}},Z\rangle\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{s_{{\bm{y}}}+\varepsilon\langle{\bm{y}},Z\rangle\right\}\right]\;\forall{\bm{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}.

Substituting this back into the conjugate expression:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}({\bm{q}})

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\bm{s}}_{{\mathrm{in}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}|}}\left\{\langle{\bm{s}}_{{\mathrm{in}}},{\bm{q}}\rangle-{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\left\{({\bm{s}}_{{\mathrm{in}}})_{{\bm{y}}}+\varepsilon\langle{\bm{y}},Z\rangle\right\}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}|}}\left\{\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{q}}\rangle-F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}({\bm{u}})\right\}

\displaystyle=F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}^{*}({\bm{q}}).

Thus, the local regularizer is exactly the conjugate of the local perturbed maximum function.

Finally, we compute the local regularized distribution. By definition:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}\left\{\langle(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]},{\bm{q}}\rangle-\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}({\bm{q}})\right\}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}\left\{\langle(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]},{\bm{q}}\rangle-F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}^{*}({\bm{q}})\right\},

so that Danskin’s theorem gives:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right)=\nabla F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}^{**}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right).

Since
F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
is closed proper convex, it is equal to its biconjugate by the Fenchel-Moreau theorem, and we have in fact:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right)=\nabla F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right).

We then invert the differentiation and expectation signs (this application of the
path-wise gradient estimator
, or the
reparameterization trick
, is possible since the the expectation is taken with respect to
Z
which does not depend on
\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}}

(
12
)
), and further apply Danskin’s theorem a second time:

\displaystyle\nabla F_{\varepsilon,{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}}{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\left\{\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}\right)_{\bm{y}}+\varepsilon\langle{\bm{y}},Z\rangle\right\}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\nabla_{(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]}}\max_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\left\{((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})_{\bm{y}}+\varepsilon\langle{\bm{y}},Z\rangle\right\}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\delta^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}_{\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\langle{\bm{\theta}}+\varepsilon Z,{\bm{y}}\rangle}\right].

∎

Proof of
Proposition

(

(Expected RLNS as block-coordinate ascent)

Proof.

We first establish the strict convexity of the mean-space regularizer
\Omega_{\mathcal{M}}
.
Let
{\bm{\mu}}_{1},{\bm{\mu}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{M}}
such that
{\bm{\mu}}_{1}\neq{\bm{\mu}}_{2}

\lambda\in(0,1)

\triangle^{\mathcal{Y}}
is compact and

is uniquely attained for any
{\bm{\mu}}\in{\mathcal{M}}

{\bm{p}}_{1}
and
{\bm{p}}_{2}
be the unique minimizers corresponding to
{\bm{\mu}}_{1}
and
{\bm{\mu}}_{2}

{\bm{\mu}}_{1}\neq{\bm{\mu}}_{2}

{\bm{p}}_{1}\neq{\bm{p}}_{2}

For any coordinate

•

{\bm{y}}

By definition, the global smoothed maximizer

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{p}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}}),\nu^{\star})

By definition of the local regularized distribution,

is the solution to:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})

Equivalence to projection.

We now analyze the projection problem. We minimize the Bregman divergence between the global solution
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
and a distribution
{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
restricted to the local simplex:

\displaystyle B_{\Omega}\left(\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}})\|{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\right)\triangleq\;

\displaystyle\Omega(\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}}))-\Omega({\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}}))

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla\Omega({\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})),\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}})-{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\rangle.

Substituting the global optimality condition from

B.6
into the linear term:

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\Omega({\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})),\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}}-\nu^{\star}\bm{1},\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}},\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}})\rangle-\nu^{\star}\quad(\text{since }{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}).

\displaystyle B_{\Omega}\left(\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}})\|{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\right)=\;

We now turn to the proof that the Gibbs update distributions match the KL-projection of the target onto the local simplex.

Proof.

Conditional distribution.

The conditional distribution is defined by renormalizing the global mass restricted to
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})\left({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\right)=\frac{{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{y}})}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}{\bm{p}}_{\Omega}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})({\bm{y}}^{\prime})}\quad\forall{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}.

(10)

KL projection.

Let
{\bm{q}}\in\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
. We have:

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\iota^{(k)}({\bm{q}})\|\bm{\pi})=\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}q_{\bm{y}}\log\left(\frac{q_{\bm{y}}}{\pi_{\bm{y}}}\right).

We define the Lagrangian with a multiplier
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
for the constraint

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{q}},\lambda)=\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}q_{\bm{y}}\log(q_{\bm{y}})-\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}q_{\bm{y}}\log(\pi_{\bm{y}})+\lambda\left(\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}q_{\bm{y}}-1\right).

\displaystyle q_{\bm{y}}^{\star}=\pi_{\bm{y}}\exp(-(1+\lambda^{\star})).

\displaystyle q_{\bm{y}}^{\star}=\frac{\pi_{\bm{y}}}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\pi_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}}}.

This matches the definition of the conditional distribution in

For

to be finite, we require

and

. This means
{\bm{p}}
must be a zero-padded distribution from the local simplex, so

.

is a sum of scaled functions:

Let

. A known property of the Fenchel conjugate then gives that for any

Consequently, the effective domain of the scaled conjugate is scaled by the same factor:

Thus,

.

The Fenchel conjugate of a sum of functions is the inf-convolution (denoted by
\square

(
42
)

\Omega_{\bm{y}}=(F_{\bm{y}})^{*}=\square_{S\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu)}g_{S}^{*}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}(\Omega_{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu)}\mathrm{dom}(g_{S}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu)}\nu(S)\mathrm{dom}(f_{S}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu)}\nu(S)\iota^{{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}}(\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}}).

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu)}\nu(S)\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}}=\left(\sum_{S\in\mathrm{supp}(\nu)}\nu(S)\right)\cdot\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}}=\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}}.

This proves that

, ensuring the target-dependent Fenchel-Young loss is well-defined and finite at the ground-truth distribution.

Now, assuming that

{\bm{s}}
, we can exchange the expectation and the gradient signs. The gradient of

is then given by Danskin’s theorem and the chain rule:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{s}}F_{\bm{y}}({\bm{s}})={\mathbb{E}}_{S\sim\nu}\left[\iota^{{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}}\left({\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}}}\left({\bm{s}}_{\left[{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}\right]}\right)\right)\right].

The gradient of the target-dependent loss

with respect to

at

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{\Omega_{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\theta}}\,;\delta^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\bm{y}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}F_{\bm{y}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})-{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{S\sim\nu}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{Y\sim{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}}}\left((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{\left[{\mathcal{Y}}_{S}\right]}\right)}[Y]\right]-{\bm{y}}.

This exactly matches the expected update of RLNS:

Thus, the sampling procedure initialized at

and run for

\displaystyle\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\{\langle{\bm{\theta}}+\varepsilon Z,{\bm{y}}\rangle\},

\displaystyle\hat{Y}

\displaystyle\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\{\langle{\bm{\theta}}+\varepsilon Z,{\bm{y}}\rangle\}.

By definition of the perturbation-based regularizer
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
(
Definition

3.5
) and
Proposition

3.6
, the distributions of these variables correspond exactly to the global and local regularized distributions:

\displaystyle Y^{*}\sim\pi^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{Y}\sim\hat{\bm{q}}.

Coupling event.

Let
E
be the event that the global perturbed maximizer falls within the local neighborhood:

\displaystyle E\triangleq\{Y^{*}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}\}.

Since
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}
, if the maximum over the larger set
{\mathcal{Y}}
belongs to the subset
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
, it must strictly coincide with the maximum over the subset
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
(assuming unique maximizers almost surely, which holds if
{\mathcal{D}}
has a density). Therefore:

\displaystyle\forall\omega\in E,\quad Y^{*}(\omega)=\hat{Y}(\omega).

(18)

We denote the probability of this event by
\alpha\triangleq{\mathbb{P}}(E)=\pi^{*}({\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)})
.

Decomposition of the local distribution.

The ideal conditional distribution
{\bm{q}}^{*}
corresponds to the distribution of
Y^{*}
conditioned on
E
:

\displaystyle\forall{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)},\quad q^{*}_{\bm{y}}={\mathbb{P}}(Y^{*}={\bm{y}}\mid E).

We decompose the probability mass function of
\hat{Y}
(which is
\hat{q}
) by conditioning on
E
:

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}(\hat{Y}={\bm{y}})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}(\hat{Y}={\bm{y}}\mid E){\mathbb{P}}(E)+{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{Y}={\bm{y}}\mid\overline{E}){\mathbb{P}}(\overline{E}).

Using the coupling implication from
Eq.

18
, we have
\hat{Y}=Y^{*}
on
E
. Thus:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{Y}={\bm{y}}\mid E)={\mathbb{P}}(Y^{*}={\bm{y}}\mid E)=q^{*}_{\bm{y}}.

Substituting this back, and letting
{\bm{r}}\in\triangle^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}
be defined by
r_{\bm{y}}\triangleq{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{Y}={\bm{y}}\mid\overline{E})
, we obtain the mixture:

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{\bm{y}}=\alpha q^{*}_{\bm{y}}+(1-\alpha)r_{\bm{y}}.

Total variation bound.

We now compute the total variation distance between
\hat{\bm{q}}
and
{\bm{q}}^{*}
:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat{\bm{q}},{\bm{q}}^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}|\hat{q}_{\bm{y}}-q^{*}_{\bm{y}}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}|\alpha q^{*}_{\bm{y}}+(1-\alpha)r_{\bm{y}}-q^{*}_{\bm{y}}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}|(1-\alpha)(r_{\bm{y}}-q^{*}_{\bm{y}})|

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}|r_{\bm{y}}-q^{*}_{\bm{y}}|\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}({\bm{r}},{\bm{q}}^{*}).

Since the total variation distance is bounded by
1
, we then have:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat{\bm{q}},{\bm{q}}^{*})\leq 1-\alpha=1-\pi^{*}({\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}).

Conclusion.

The local consistency gap
\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}
is defined as the maximum of this total variation distance over all choices of
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}
and
S^{(k)}
(
Definition

A.3
). Taking the maximum yields:

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega}\leq 1-\min_{{\bm{y}}^{(k)},S^{(k)}}\pi^{*}({\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}).

∎

B.12
Proof of
Proposition

A.7
(
Proposition A.7

(Exactness of Perturbed LNS on product spaces)
.
)

Proof.

Factorization of the global distribution.

Recall that the global regularized distribution is defined by the law of the perturbed maximizer
Y^{*}\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\langle{\bm{\theta}}+\varepsilon Z,{\bm{y}}\rangle
with
Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}
.
Because the feasible set is a product space
{\mathcal{Y}}=\prod_{m=1}^{M}{\mathcal{Y}}_{m}
, the dot product additively decomposes over the blocks
B_{m}
. The global maximization therefore separates into
M
independent local maximizations:

\displaystyle Y^{*}=\left(\argmax_{{\bm{y}}_{m}\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{m}}\langle{\bm{\theta}}_{[B_{m}]}+\varepsilon Z_{[B_{m}]},{\bm{y}}_{m}\rangle\right)_{m=1}^{M}.

Since the noise blocks
(Z_{[B_{m}]})_{m=1}^{M}
are mutually independent, the components
(Y^{*}_{[B_{m}]})_{m=1}^{M}
are independent random variables. Thus, the global regularized distribution
\pi^{*}\triangleq{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})
factorizes over the blocks:

\displaystyle\pi^{*}({\bm{y}})=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\pi^{*}_{m}({\bm{y}}_{[B_{m}]}),\quad\text{where}\quad\pi^{*}_{m}({\bm{y}}_{m})\triangleq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\argmax_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}_{m}\in{\mathcal{Y}}_{m}}\langle{\bm{\theta}}_{[B_{m}]}+\varepsilon Z_{[B_{m}]},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}_{m}\rangle={\bm{y}}_{m}\right).

Conditional distribution.

Let
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
be a current solution, and
S^{(k)}
be a union of blocks defining the frozen variables. Let
F^{(k)}\triangleq[d]\setminus S^{(k)}
be the complement (the free variables), which is also a union of blocks. Due to the independence of the blocks, conditioning the global distribution
\pi^{*}
on the frozen variables
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}
simply restricts the product to the free blocks:

\displaystyle\pi^{*}\left({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\right)=\prod_{B_{m}\subseteq F^{(k)}}\pi^{*}_{m}({\bm{y}}_{[B_{m}]})\quad\text{for any }{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)},

(19)

where
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}=\{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}\mid{\bm{y}}_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\}
.

Local RLNS update.

By
Proposition

3.6
, the local perturbed RLNS update samples from
{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})
by computing
\hat{Y}\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}\langle{\bm{\theta}}+\varepsilon Z,{\bm{y}}\rangle
.
Because
{\bm{y}}_{[S^{(k)}]}
is fixed to
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}
for all
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
, the maximization over
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
reduces to maximizing over the free blocks:

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{[F^{(k)}]}=\argmax_{{\bm{y}}_{\text{free}}\in\prod_{B_{m}\subseteq F^{(k)}}{\mathcal{Y}}_{m}}\sum_{B_{m}\subseteq F^{(k)}}\langle{\bm{\theta}}_{[B_{m}]}+\varepsilon Z_{[B_{m}]},{\bm{y}}_{[B_{m}]}\rangle.

This subproblem is structurally identical to the one defining the free components of the global maximizer
Y^{*}
. Since
Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}
, the distribution of
\hat{Y}_{[F^{(k)}]}
is exactly
\prod_{B_{m}\subseteq F^{(k)}}\pi^{*}_{m}
.

Thus, the local regularized distribution exactly matches the conditional global distribution from
Eq.

19
:

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})({\bm{y}})=\pi^{*}\left({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\right).

By
Definition

A.3
, this exact match implies that the local consistency gap is
\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega_{\varepsilon}}=0
.
Since the local updates are equivalent to conditional sampling from
\pi^{*}
,
Algorithm

3
precisely operates as a block Gibbs sampler targeting
\pi^{*}
, and its stationary distribution is exactly:

\displaystyle\pi^{\mathrm{LNS}}_{\bm{\theta},\Omega_{\varepsilon}}={\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}}).

∎

B.13
Proof of
Proposition

A.10
(
Proposition A.10

(Cut-bounded consistency gap)
.
)

Proof.

Consider a fixed current solution
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
and a subset of frozen coordinates
S^{(k)}\subseteq[d]
. Let
F^{(k)}=[d]\setminus S^{(k)}
be the free coordinates.
Let
\hat{\bm{q}}\triangleq{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})
denote the local RLNS update distribution, and
{\bm{q}}^{*}\triangleq{\bm{p}}_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})(\cdot\mid{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]})
denote the ideal global conditional distribution.

1. Relaxed local update.

Define the relaxed local neighborhood
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}_{\text{relax}}\triangleq{\mathcal{Y}}_{F}(S^{(k)})\times\{{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\}
, as well as the relaxed maximizer over this set:

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{\text{relax}}\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}_{\text{relax}}}\langle{\bm{\theta}}+\varepsilon Z,{\bm{y}}\rangle.

Since
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}_{\text{relax}}
places no constraints between the free variables and the frozen variables
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}
, the optimization over
F^{(k)}
is identical to the one defining the free block of the global relaxed maximizer
Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)})
. Therefore, the distribution of
(\hat{Y}_{\text{relax}})_{[F^{(k)}]}
is exactly the distribution of
(Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)}))_{[F^{(k)}]}
.
Let
{\bm{q}}^{*}_{\text{relax}}
denote the law of
\hat{Y}_{\text{relax}}
. By
Proposition

A.7
,
{\bm{q}}^{*}_{\text{relax}}
is also exactly the conditional distribution of the relaxed global maximizer:

\displaystyle{\bm{q}}^{*}_{\text{relax}}(\cdot)={\mathbb{P}}(Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}=\cdot\mid(Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)}))_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}).

2. Bounding the distance to the local RLNS update.

The true local RLNS update
\hat{Y}
optimizes over
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}=\{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}\mid{\bm{y}}_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}\}
. Notice that
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}_{\text{relax}}
.

Let
E_{\partial}
be the event that
\hat{Y}_{\text{relax}}\notin{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
. This event occurs if and only if the relaxed local update violates at least one cut constraint, meaning
(\hat{Y}_{\text{relax}})_{[F^{(k)}]}\in\bigcup_{c\in C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})}V_{c}(S^{(k)},{\bm{y}}^{(k)})
for some
c\in C_{\partial}
.
If the complement event
\overline{E_{\partial}}
occurs, then
\hat{Y}_{\text{relax}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}
. Because
{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}_{\text{relax}}
and the maximizer over the larger set belongs to the smaller set, it must be that
\hat{Y}=\hat{Y}_{\text{relax}}
.
By the standard coupling inequality, the total variation distance between their laws is bounded by the probability of them differing:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat{\bm{q}},{\bm{q}}^{*}_{\text{relax}})\leq{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{Y}\neq\hat{Y}_{\text{relax}})\leq{\mathbb{P}}(E_{\partial}).

(20)

3. Bounding the distance to the ideal conditional.

Let
A
be the event that the global relaxed maximizer is globally feasible:
A\triangleq\{Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}\}
. On the event
A
, we have
Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)})=Y^{*}
, where
Y^{*}
is the true global maximizer drawn from
\pi^{*}
.
The ideal conditional
{\bm{q}}^{*}
is the law of
Y^{*}
conditioned on
Y^{*}_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}
. Using the fact that
Y^{*}=Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}
on
A
, we can bound the total variation distance between the conditional distributions
{\bm{q}}^{*}
and
{\bm{q}}^{*}_{\text{relax}}
by the probability of
\overline{A}
conditioned on the frozen variables:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}({\bm{q}}^{*},{\bm{q}}^{*}_{\text{relax}})\leq{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{A}\mid(Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)}))_{[S^{(k)}]}={\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}).

(21)

Because
(Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)}))_{[F^{(k)}]}
and
(Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)}))_{[S^{(k)}]}
are independent, conditioning on the frozen variables simply fixes their value to
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}_{[S^{(k)}]}
. The event
\overline{A}
conditioned on this fixed assignment is exactly the event that
(Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)}))_{[F^{(k)}]}
violates at least one cut constraint in
C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})
. This is identical to the probability of the local boundary event
E_{\partial}
defined earlier. Thus:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}({\bm{q}}^{*},{\bm{q}}^{*}_{\text{relax}})\leq{\mathbb{P}}(E_{\partial}).

4. Triangle inequality and union bound.

Applying the triangle inequality to the results of Step 2 and Step 3 yields:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat{\bm{q}},{\bm{q}}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\hat{\bm{q}},{\bm{q}}^{*}_{\text{relax}})+d_{\mathrm{TV}}({\bm{q}}^{*}_{\text{relax}},{\bm{q}}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathbb{P}}(E_{\partial}).

Taking the maximum over all feasible
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
and subsets
S^{(k)}
yields the first bound on the local consistency gap
\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega_{\varepsilon}}
:

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}},\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\leq 2\max_{{\bm{y}}^{(k)},S^{(k)}}{\mathbb{P}}\left((Y^{*}_{\text{relax}}(S^{(k)}))_{[F^{(k)}]}\in\bigcup_{c\in C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})}V_{c}(S^{(k)},{\bm{y}}^{(k)})\right).

Finally, applying a union bound over the individual cut constraints
c\in C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})
and extracting the maximum probability gives:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(E_{\partial})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{c\in C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})}{\mathbb{P}}\left((\hat{Y}_{\text{relax}})_{[F^{(k)}]}\in V_{c}(S^{(k)},{\bm{y}}^{(k)})\right)

\displaystyle\leq|C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})|\max_{c\in C_{\partial}(S^{(k)})}{\mathbb{P}}\left((\hat{Y}_{\text{relax}})_{[F^{(k)}]}\in V_{c}(S^{(k)},{\bm{y}}^{(k)})\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

\kappa-l

Appendix D
Experimental details for the generalized assignment problem

This section presents the experimental details for the numerical results presented in
Section

5.2
.

Instance generation.

We consider GAP instances with
n=50
items and
m=5
bins. For each instance, item-bin weights
w_{ij}
are sampled uniformly from the discrete set
\{1,\dots,10\}
. To create tight constraints that force non-trivial combinatorial trade-offs, the capacity
C_{j}
for all bins in a given instance is set identically to
C=\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{ij}/m^{2}\rfloor

Dataset generation and expert policy.

We generate a dataset of
1000
independent GAP instances. For each instance, we generate latent profile vectors to construct relational features:

•

Item features:

{\bm{v}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{10})
for
i\in[n]

•

Bin features:

{\bm{u}}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{10})
for
j\in[m]

{\bm{x}}_{ij}=[{\bm{v}}_{i},{\bm{u}}_{j}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{20}
. To generate the ground-truth assignment labels

24

{\bm{p}}_{\Omega^{{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}}}((\bm{s}_{\bm{{\bm{\theta}}}})_{[{\mathcal{Y}}^{(k)}]})

47
.

Training details.

For
Fig.

2(a)

10,000
steps using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-3}
and a batch size of
32

64
GB of RAM and
16

Gradient variance analysis.

2(b)

64
logit vectors
{\bm{\theta}}
from a standard Normal distribution. For each score vector, we draw
50

64
queried score vectors. All error bars indicate the

1.96

Oracle execution time benchmarking.

To ensure statistically significant time comparisons between the local dynamic programming oracle and the global MILP solver in
Fig.

2(b)

100
independent trials for each problem scale (
(n,m)=(100,10)
,
(n,m)=(200,20)

(n,m)=(400,40)

{\bm{\theta}}

100

Appendix E
Experimental details for stochastic vehicle scheduling

This section details the complete experimental setup, dataset generation procedure, model architecture, and hyperparameters for the stochastic vehicle scheduling problem (SVSP) experiments presented in
Section

5.3
. While the problem formulation and dataset generation process are standard and identical to, e.g.,
39
;
14
;
19
, we detail them here for completeness.

Problem formulation

The problem is modeled on a directed acyclic graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}})
with
|{\mathcal{V}}|=n+2
nodes (a source depot,
n
job nodes, and a sink depot) and
|{\mathcal{E}}|
edges. An edge
(i,j)\in{\mathcal{E}}
exists from source to every job, from every job to sink, and from job
i
to job
j
if the deterministic end time of task
i
plus travel time allows reaching task
j
before its start. The number of edges varies across instances, as it depends on the spatial and temporal configuration of the tasks.

A feasible routing
{\bm{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{|{\mathcal{E}}|}
satisfies flow conservation and unit demand constraints:

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\delta^{-}(v)}y_{e}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{e\in\delta^{+}(v)}y_{e}=1\quad\forall v\in{\mathcal{V}},

where
\delta^{-}(v)
and
\delta^{+}(v)
denote the sets of incoming and outgoing edges of
v
. Each vehicle corresponds to a path from source to sink, so the number of vehicles is
\sum_{e\in\delta^{+}(0)}y_{e}
.

Given a routing
{\bm{y}}
and a scenario
\omega\in[S]
, delays propagate along each vehicle path via the recursion
R_{v}^{(\omega)}=\varepsilon_{v}^{(\omega)}+\max(R_{u}^{(\omega)}-\sigma_{uv}^{(\omega)},\,0)
, where
\varepsilon_{v}^{(\omega)}
is the intrinsic delay at node
v
,
\sigma_{uv}^{(\omega)}
is the slack (time margin) on edge
(u,v)
, and
(u,v)
is the predecessor edge of
v
in the routing. The total cost of routing
{\bm{y}}
is the sum of the vehicle cost and the average total delay cost over the scenarios:

\displaystyle c({\bm{y}})=c_{\mathrm{vehicle}}\cdot\sum_{e\in\delta^{+}(0)}y_{e}+c_{\mathrm{delay}}\cdot\frac{1}{S}\sum_{\omega=1}^{S}\sum_{v=1}^{n}R_{v}^{(\omega)}.

E.1
Dataset generation and expert policy

We generate a dataset of
1000
independent SVSP instances. For each instance, we sample a random city layout simulating spatial areas and transit delays.

•

Stochasticity:
Task locations, start times, and travel times are sampled randomly. Intrinsic task delays (
\varepsilon_{v}^{(\omega)}
) and regional inter-area travel delays follow LogNormal distributions, generating the
10
distinct delay scenarios per instance.

•

Expert policy:
The ground-truth solution
{\bm{y}}
for each instance is computed by solving a two-stage stochastic MILP that minimizes the expected total cost
c({\bm{y}})
with full knowledge of all
S
scenarios. To handle the bilinear delay-propagation term
y_{uv}R_{u}^{(\omega)}
without requiring a big-M parameter, we use exact McCormick envelope linearizations. The MILP is solved using SciPy with a time limit of
120
seconds per instance.

•

Size:
Each instance features
n=50
job tasks and
S=10
stochastic scenarios, which is the largest problem size handled by the expert solver. In terms of industrial applications, this scale corresponds to standard long-haul fleet aircraft routing problems.

E.2
Features and model architecture

For each edge
e\in{\mathcal{E}}
in a given graph, we construct a
20
-dimensional feature vector
{\bm{x}}_{e}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{20}
. The features capture the deterministic and stochastic characteristics of the edge:

•

Feature 1:
The deterministic travel time for the edge.

•

Feature 2:
The vehicle cost (equal to
c_{\mathrm{vehicle}}
if the edge originates from the source depot, and
0
otherwise).

•

Features 3–11:
The
9
deciles of the slack (time margin) distribution
\sigma_{uv}^{(\omega)}
computed across the
S=10
scenarios.

•

Features 12–20:
The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the slack distribution evaluated at
9
predefined time thresholds:
\{-100,-50,-20,-10,0,10,50,200,500\}
.

The predictive model uses an standard feed-forward architecture. It applies a shared two-layer perceptron with a hidden dimension of
64
independently to each edge’s feature vector
{\bm{x}}_{e}
to output the predicted scalar cost
\theta_{e}
.

Given predicted edge costs
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{E}}|}
, the combinatorial optimization layer is then a flow linear program (LP)
\hat{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\theta}})\triangleq\argmax_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\langle{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{y}}\rangle
.

E.3
Training details and hyperparameters

All models are trained for
10,000
steps using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
10^{-4}
and a batch size of
32
. The dataset is split into
70\%
training,
15\%
validation, and
15\%
testing. All training configurations are repeated across
10
different random seeds controlling dataset and model initialization randomness for statistical significance. Each training is performed on a CPU with
64
GB of RAM and
16
cores.

RLNS-specific parameters.

Our proposed method is parameterized as follows:

•

Iterations:
We run
K=1
RLNS iteration per training step to compute the gradient estimator.

•

Regularization:
We use perturbation-based regularization with
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
standard Gaussian noise and a temperature scaling parameter
\varepsilon=1.0
.

•

Initialization:
RLNS is initialized directly at the ground-truth expert solution.

•

Variable selection scheme:
We use a route-based mask generation heuristic. At each step, we identify the distinct vehicle routes present in the current routing assignment. We select
66\%
of these routes uniformly at random and freeze all variables corresponding to the tasks visited by those routes.

Baseline parameters.

The baselines share the identical underlying model architecture, optimization hyperparameters, and evaluation metrics:

•

Global perturbed Fenchel-Young loss (PFY)
(
7
)
:
Uses the same perturbation-based regularization setup (Gaussian noise,
\varepsilon=1.0
,) but calls the exact global MAP oracle over the full graph
{\mathcal{G}}
at every training step.

•

Blackbox differentiation (DBB)
(
48
)
:
Continuous interpolation of the solver. We performed a hyperparameter sweep over the interpolation strength
\lambda\in\{10^{-1},5\times 10^{-1},1,5,10^{1},10^{2},5\times 10^{2},10^{3},5\times 10^{3},10^{4}\}
and report the best performing configuration, which was
\lambda=10^{2}
.

•

Binary cross-entropy (BCE):
An unstructured binary cross-entropy loss applied directly to the edge-level predictions
{\bm{\theta}}
against the expert binary labels
{\bm{y}}
. See, e.g.,
22
for a similar approach on a routing combinatorial optimization problem.

•

Greedy policy:
A non-learned baseline that solves a standard minimum-cost flow problem on the DAG, placing negative costs on the source edges to minimize the total number of vehicles, entirely ignoring the stochastic delays.
