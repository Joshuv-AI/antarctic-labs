AutoAdapt: An Automated Domain Adaptation Framework for Large Language Models
Sidharth Sinha, Anson Bastos, Xuchao Zhang, Akshay Nambi, Chetan Bansal, Saravan Rajmohan

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) excel in open domains but struggle in specialized settings with limited data and evolving knowledge. Existing domain adaptation practices rely heavily on manual trial-and-error processes, incur significant hyperparameter complexity, and are highly sensitive to data and user preferences, all under the high cost of LLM training. Moreover, the interactions and transferability of hyperparameter choices across models/domains remain poorly understood, making adaptation gains uncertain even with substantial effort. To solve these challenges, we present AutoAdapt , a novel end-to-end automated framework for efficient and reliable LLM domain adaptation. AutoAdapt leverages curated knowledge bases from literature and open-source resources to reduce expert intervention. To narrow the search space, we design a novel multi-agent debating system in which proposal and critic agents iteratively interact to align user intent and incorporate data signals and best practices into the planning process. To optimize hyperparameters under tight budgets, we propose AutoRefine , a novel LLM-based surrogate that replaces costly black-box search. Across 10 tasks, AutoAdapt achieves a 25% average relative accuracy improvement over state-of-the-art Automated Machine Learning baselines with minimal overhead.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have rapidly advanced the state of the art in natural language processing, enabling impressive performance across a wide spectrum of tasks and domains. However, despite their broad pre-training, LLMs often fall short when applied to specialized domains where data is scarce, proprietary, or subject to privacy constraints. Further, in scenarios where data evolves with time, pretrained LLMs would not have the updated knowledge.
In such settings, domain adaptation of LLMs is imperative by resorting to techniques like in-context prompting
(
Dong et al. 2024
)
using retrieval from current data (RAG)
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
or finetuning the models (SFT)
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
on new data for optimal performance.
The current process of adapting LLMs to a specific task is largely manual and remains limited to a set of best practices, assuming a one-template-fits-all approach across tasks and models. Further, effective fine tuning relies on expert knowledge because small choices in data, objectives, and training settings can strongly affect the performance.

Domain adaptation for LLMs involves a series of intricate steps: curating domain-specific datasets, selecting appropriate model architectures, configuring prompting or fine-tuning strategies, and iteratively optimizing hyperparameters for optimal performance. Each decision point, ranging from model selection, context engineering, training technique (e.g., supervised fine-tuning (SFT), parameter-efficient adaptation (PeFT) e.g. LoRA/Prefix Tuning, preference alignment e.g. DPO) to the choice of learning rates, batch sizes, and regularization techniques, can significantly impact the final model’s quality, stability and cost. The challenge of exploring the vast search space is compounded in LLMs due to the high time and cost of finetuning these large models. The combinatorial explosion of possible configurations, coupled with the high time and resource utility, means that even experienced teams must engage in extensive trial-and-error, often spending weeks or months to achieve robust, high-quality results.

Recent research
(
Guo et al. 2024
;
Trirat et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
has explored automating general Machine Learning (ML) pipelines using agent-based frameworks offering partial solutions for model selection and hyperparameter tuning, albeit in the general ML domain. However, the following
challenges exist
:
1) Existing methods either rely on the pretraining knowledge or in-context learning ability of LLMs, which we show is insufficient for the task of LLM adaptation. Further, retrieval based methods
(
Guo et al. 2024
;
Trirat et al. 2025
)
lack a structured knowledge base of LLM use cases, limiting their usage to only classical ML methods.
2) These approaches typically rely on exhaustive or heuristic search strategies, which are computationally expensive and ill-suited to the scale and complexity of LLM adaptation.
3) Existing methods rely on the LLM agents to generate code. However, agents may have limited and outdated pretraining context on LLM adaptation.
This results in low success rate for end-to-end runs as we show in our experiments.
4)
LLM adaptation pipelines often require multiple stages such as pretraining, followed by finetuning, alignment, in-context prompting using RAG techniques and so on. Existing methods fail in planning such a multi-stage pipeline due to a lack of a structured and well defined hierarchical plan.
5) LLMs incur an exorbitant cost and latency for training and thus standard Hyperparameter Optimization (HPO) techniques employed in existing methods
(
Trirat et al. 2025
)
will hardly scale to LLM adaptation.
6) Most agents do not consider the data characteristics and explicit adherence to user preferences during the planning stage
or merely loop on failing execution which degrades efficiency.

To address these challenges, we introduce
AutoAdapt
, an automated domain adaptation framework that proposes to solve the adaptation problem as a multi-agent system grounded in a knowledge-driven hierarchical workflow.
We further use Gaussian processes
(
Beckers 2021
)
for probabilistic function estimation, modeling uncertainty over the adaptation objective function.

AutoAdapt

RAG

,

The framework is composed of a system of proposal and critic agents to generate, debate, verify and refine executable pipelines. We ground the multi-agent system by integrating best practices from thousands of open-source models and datasets, as well as user-specified requirements and data characteristics. We further perform online retrieval from web sources and literature. Thus,
AutoAdapt
dramatically reduces the need for manual intervention and expert oversight. This produces an adaptation pipeline with narrowed search spaces (e.g.
1e^{-4}\leq\texttt{learning rate}\leq 1e^{-6}
) to optimize.
The framework further incorporates a feedback-driven optimization loop, called
AutoRefine
, to optimize parameters (e.g. exact
SFT
parameters like
\texttt{learning rate}=2e^{-5}
from the narrowed search space). AutoRefine efficiently explores the hyperparameter space using LLM-based oracle that factors performance from prior runs.
However naive usage of LLMs to predict performance (surrogate modeling)
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
in the multi-dimensional LLM hyperparameter space, is prone to hallucination. We propose to solve this by grounding the LLM surrogate with function estimation techniques.

Through extensive empirical evaluation across diverse domains, including mathematical and general reasoning, medical question answering, legal analysis, programming, agent tool calling, e-commerce, and real world cloud incident diagnosis, we demonstrate that
AutoAdapt
consistently outperforms existing AutoML baselines, delivering significant accuracy gains with minimal additional computational overhead (w.r.t. default choices). By
automating the end-to-end LLM adaptation process
,
AutoAdapt
makes robust, domain-specific LLMs accessible to a broader range of users and organizations,
bridging the gap between cutting-edge research and practical real-world applications
.
In summary:

•

We propose a novel method for end-to-end LLM domain adaptation guided by a structured adaptation workflow ensuring a high success rate in executing the pipeline. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to propose an automated domain adaptation framework for LLMs.

•

We curate a knowledge base for LLM domain adaptation from open source implementations, datasets, models, and best practices to ground the recommendations.

•

We address the exponential search space in LLM adaptation, by designing a novel multi-agent framework that traverses the adaptation graph incorporating the user constraints, data signals, and best practices, to recommend adaptation pipelines and hyperparameter ranges.

•

We formulate a first-of-its-kind probabilistic function estimation based technique in conjunction with LLMs to efficiently perform refinement of the parameter choices and ranges for the selected pipeline.

•

We conduct extensive experiments on datasets spanning various benchmark and real world datasets across multiple domains to demonstrate the efficacy of our method in adapting LLMs to the specialized tasks.

Problem Formulation

We formulate domain adaptation of large language models (LLMs) as a
multi–stage optimization problem. Let

D=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}

denote the user–provided dataset for a downstream task, and let

c
represent the user context (e.g., hardware constraints, latency
requirements, or user preferences such as PEFT vs. full fine–tuning).
An adapted model is constructed through a sequence of

T
transformation stages (e.g., continual pretraining, supervised
fine–tuning, preference optimization, inference–time augmentation).
Each stage
t
is parameterized by a configuration

h_{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
.
The final model produced by the pipeline is denoted as
f_{\theta}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{T}).

AutoAdapt

differs from existing AutoML techniques in this aspect of planning each component of the pipeline sequentially following the hierarchy of the adaptation graph.

Our goal is to find a feasible configuration sequence

(h_{1},\ldots,h_{T})
that minimizes expected task loss:

\min_{h_{1},\ldots,h_{T}\in\mathcal{H}(c)}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}\left[L\big(f_{\theta}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{T})(x),y\big)\right],

where the feasible set
\mathcal{H}(c)
encodes context‑dependent
constraints such as GPU memory, training budget, or user‑specified
preferences.

Prior‑Guided Sequential Configuration Space:

Each stage
t
has a set of hyperparameters,
h_{t}=\{h_{t,1},\ldots,h_{t,n_{t}}\},

and rather than selecting all hyperparameters jointly, we construct them
sequentially.
Let
h_{<t}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{t-1})
denote configurations from previous
stages, and
h_{t,<i}=(h_{t,1},\ldots,h_{t,i-1})
denote earlier
choices within stage
t
.
Define a prior‑guided feasible region for stage
t
as

\mathcal{P}_{t}(c,h_{<t})=\left\{h_{t}:h_{t,i}\in\mathcal{P}_{t,i}(c,h_{<t},h_{t,<i}),\;i=1,\ldots,n_{t}\right\},

where each sub‑policy
\mathcal{P}_{t,i}
incorporates
domain knowledge (e.g., best‑practice hyperparameter ranges),
conditional dependencies (e.g., selecting LoRA activates

\text{rank},\alpha,\text{dropout}
), and context constraints.
The multi–stage adaptation problem thus becomes:

\displaystyle\min_{h_{1},\ldots,h_{T}}\;\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}\left[L\big(f_{\theta}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{T})(x),y\big)\right]\quad

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad h_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{t}(c,h_{<t}),\;\forall t

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}(c)
be the feasible configuration space and

\mathcal{P}(c)=\mathcal{P}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{P}_{T}
the
prior‑guided subspace.
Denote the expected task loss as

L_{\mathbb{E}}(h)
,
the optimal loss over the full space is
L_{\mathcal{H}}^{*}=\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}(c)}L_{\mathbb{E}}(h)
and the optimal loss over the prior‑guided space is
L_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}=\min_{h\in\mathcal{P}(c)}L_{\mathbb{E}}(h)
. Considering
L_{\mathbb{E}}(h)
is Lipschitz continuous in hyperparameters, the prior‑guided subspace satisfies the generalized

\varepsilon
-covering bound:

L_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\;\leq\;L_{\mathcal{H}}^{*}\;+\;L\varepsilon,

where
L
is the Lipschitz constant and
\varepsilon
is the distance between the optimal parameters in
\mathcal{H}(c)
and
\mathcal{P}(c)
.

The above result is straightforward to verify after continuous relaxation
(
Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004
)
of the discrete space. It implies that restricting search to the prior‑guided subspace incurs at most a controlled additive suboptimality while yielding dramatically improved tractability.
For the optimization over the constrained search space we propose to use LLMs together with function estimation (using Gaussian process) techniques. More details on Gaussian processes can be found in Appendix
A
and
Beckers 2021
.

Note, the above formulation is intended solely for clarity of exposition and the proposed method does not depend on the stated assumptions.

Method

Framework Overview:

This section details the components of our method. Figure
2
gives an overview of
AutoAdapt
.
The user provides the task description, data and constraints.
AutoAdapt
then runs to find the optimal adaptation pipeline through the following workflow:
(i) The
Adaptation Configuration Graph (ACG)
is executed to decompose the decision making in a structured workflow. (ii) A
multi‑agent debate pipeline
is run at every node of the ACG to propose an executable adaptation plan with narrowed hyperparameter ranges. The multi-agent system is grounded in
best practices
from literature (processed in a KB or queried real time from web search, huggingface, github etc.) (iii) On obtaining the adaptation pipeline from the multi-agent system,
AutoRefine
is run to obtain the exact parameter set. AutoRefine is a feedback‑driven optimization loop that combines LLM surrogate reasoning with probabilistic function estimation to efficiently and effectively select hyperparameters.
(iv) Once the optimization is complete, the pipeline is executed to produce the adapted LLM for the user task.

4.1
Best Practices Knowledge Base

The multi-agentic system needs to be grounded in the real world LLM usage ecosystem. For this we curate the best-pratices corpus from public LLM ecosystems capturing model choices, training pipelines, and hyperparameters at scale. The sources include Hugging Face model/data cards and tags (e.g., sft, dpo, domain tags like medical), PwC leaderboards and notebooks (github, unsloth) and practitioner guides on web.
This corpus helps in the hierarchical decision of strategy selection (e.g.,
RAG
vs
SFT
,
LoRA
vs.
Prefix Tuning
), model choice (e.g.
Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct
vs
Qwen2.5-3B
) and hyperparameter ranges (e.g.,
learning_rate
,
batch_size
etc.).

We extract knowledge from
\sim 100
K model and data cards available from the sources.
We prompt an LLM to extract features such as model family, size, training pipeline and parameters if available. Similarly for the datasets we extract the dataset size, domain, best performing models among other details.
These structured extractions (training technique, dataset size, hyperparameters, evaluation metrics, source links) form the schema of the KB.

Extraction pipeline
The system applies prompt‑driven parsers over model/data cards and notebooks to obtain details like training technique, model/dataset size, hyperparameters, evaluation, model source etc. The entries are indexed in a vector store by task description, domain and dataset descriptors. The KB is then queried given an incoming task (e.g. medical QA), returning top‑k matched datasets/models and their reported training configurations to initialize
the planning.

The ACG formalizes adaptation planning as a directed acyclic graph.
The nodes are of two types: selection nodes for discrete strategy/technique (e.g.
RAG

SFT
) choices and parameter nodes for hyperparameters within the selected technique (e.g.

Construction:
We construct the ACG manually based on best practices. The start selection node selects between
RAG
,
SFT
and

. Along
SFT
, the decision path includes
PEFT
and within
PEFT
the choices include
{LoRA, DoRA, Prefix Tuning}
. The selection nodes for

can be invoked post
SFT
or without
SFT
(using a pretrained model). The choices inside

include
{DPO, RLHF}
. The Parameter nodes, present at the leaf of a traversed path, represent the decision for the continuous/categorical parameter ranges for e.g. parameters for
SFT
include
epochs, learning_rate, LoRA rank/alpha/dropout, batch_size, gradient_accumulation, quantization
etc. Edges encode dependencies e.g. choosing

in

{lora_r, lora_alpha, lora_dropout}
.

Hierarchical Decision over the ACG:
Figure
3
shows a sample ACG workflow. The planner (multi-agent debate system outlined next) starts at high‑level decision nodes (e.g.,
RAG
,
SFT
and
Alignment
) and traverses until leaf nodes (complete executable plan). At each node, the planner retrieves information from the best‑practice KB
and aggregates them with user constraints (compute/time/budget) to decide the pipeline.
This structured approach constrains search making the adaptation efficient and ensures reliable execution. The traversed path need not adhere to a linear chain graph but could also involve multiple paths e.g.
SFT
followed by
Alignment
or
RAG
as decided by the system.

4.3
Multi-Agent Debate Planner

In order to make the decision at the nodes of the ACG, we design a multi-agent pipeline.
The selection nodes of the ACG could also be treated as hyperparameters and evaluated. However this increases the search space which becomes infeasible for the case of LLMs.
Thus we need an efficient planning system akin to expert humans selecting techniques from knowledge or literature.

The multi‑agent system consists of proposal and critic agents that debate in turns to come up with the final decision on the technique, model and parameter ranges.
Below we briefly describe the agents (with details in Appendix
E
)

Proposal Agents:

Proposal agents are responsible for constructing and iteratively refining candidate domain adaptation pipelines.
These agents are (i)
best practice agent
: consolidates empirical knowledge from prior training pipelines, model cards, and published benchmarks, and (ii)
knowledge retrieval agent
: dynamically retrieves evidence from recent literature, technical reports, and online resources. (iii)
research agent
: utilizes the research information retrieved from arxiv, KBs etc. to propose techniques.
These agents initialize the search process in regions of the configuration space that historically yield strong performance while remaining responsive to emerging techniques.

Critic Agents:

Critic agents verify the proposed configurations against user, data and resource constraints.
(i) The
user agent
enforces user‑specified requirements, such as compute budgets, training preferences, methodological restrictions, and domain‑specific constraints.
In parallel, (ii) the
data agent
performs data‑centric critique by analyzing statistics of the target dataset, including scale, token distributions, domain characteristics etc.,
and mapping these signals to concrete constraints on adaptation choices.

After each round of debate, an
aggregator agent
aggregates the proposals and critiques.
The proposal agents further revise the proposals based on the critiques and unsatisfied constraints.
In this manner, each ACG node undergoes
proposal
\xrightarrow{}
critique
\xrightarrow{}
revision
iterations.
The loop terminates when all active constraints are satisfied and the plan is deemed executable (with runnable code). The AutoRefine module is then invoked to decide the exact parameter values.

4.4
AutoRefine

This module decides the optimal hyperparameter set using an LLM surrogate method.
Prior works
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
have shown that transformers (and thus LLMs) have limitations in time series forecasting. This issue is bound to be amplified in the multivariate forecasting by works using LLMs as surrogates
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
to predict results. Thus we propose a principled approach grounded in function estimation theory to guide the surrogate modeling from prior knowledge of historic runs.
We use Gaussian Process (GP) model for function estimation.

Algorithm
1
provides a pseudo-code for AutoRefine.
We start by sampling a coreset
(
Jubran et al. 2019
)
of the data for efficient execution. We then perform few (
\sim 3
) execution runs on the selected pipeline (e.g.
SFT
) by invoking an LLM to sample initial parameters from the narrowed search space (lines 2-5).
Inspired by
(
Requeima et al. 2024
)
, we prompt the LLM to predict the results along each parameter dimension from the evaluated points (lines 9-12). We reason that predicting the trend along each parameter axis will help reduce hallucinations compared to directly estimating the results on the higher dimensional space. These historic and predicted results are used to train a GP model to obtain a function estimation of the remaining points in the search space (line 13). Note the GP model requires hundreds of data points to learn the function and it is not possible to finetune/evaluate LLMs for many iterations. Hence, we obtain the parameter-axis wise trends of the results to augment data. Once the model of the results over the search space is obtained we need an acquisition function that would maximize the result of the acquired hyperparameter set. For acquisition, we find the optimal point of function using LBFGS
(
Liu & Nocedal 1989
)
(after continuous relaxation of the categorical variables), starting from each of the historic evaluated samples (lines 14-18). Post discretization, the LLM is prompted in-context with these optimal points to choose the next parameter set to evaluate on (lines 19-21), balancing exploration and exploitation. Thus the LLM surrogate is guided by the optimal hyperparameter as per the estimated function that is grounded in results from prior runs, reducing hallucinations. We allow a maximum of
\mathcal{B}
(loops starting line 2,7) function evaluations (e.g. finetuning followed by testing on validation set) on the selected parameters.
Following theoretical result analyzes the regret (difference of result on selected parameter from true optimal) bounds of AutoRefine
(cf. Appendix
C
for details).
The regret grows sub-linearly with the iterations and the constants are improved by the LLM augmentation factor.

Lemma 4.1
.

Pick
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then with probability at least
1-\delta
, the cumulative regret of the algorithm in
1
is bounded as
R_{T_{a}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{T}{F_{a}}log^{2}\left(\frac{T}{F_{a}}\right)}\right)
. Further, to guarantee the average regret
r_{T}\leq\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
, we have
T_{a}=\Omega\!\Bigg(\frac{\ln^{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{F_{a}\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigg)
.
Here
T_{a},T
is the number of actual function evaluations and total iterations (actual evals + LLM predictions) of the algorithm respectively,
F_{a}
is the LLM surrogate augmentation factor

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce
AutoAdapt
, an automated framework for domain adaptation of large language models.

AutoAdapt
ensures reliable planning using a structured workflow graph. The proposed multi-agent pipeline grounded in best practices KB narrows the search space. Further, AutoRefine performs efficient optimization of the pipeline hyperparameters using a novel LLM guided algorithm grounded in probabilistic function estimation.
Through extensive experiments across diverse domains, we show that
AutoAdapt
consistently outperforms SoTA AutoML baselines.

AutoAdapt
takes the first step towards end to end automation of LLM adaptation to real world scenarios.

Impact Statement

AutoAdapt
aims to lower the technical and resource barriers to domain adaptation of large language models by automating pipeline design, configuration selection, and refinement. By enabling efficient customization using smaller datasets and constrained budgets,
AutoAdapt
can reduce redundant experimentation, lower computational cost, and democratize access to high‑quality domain‑specific models for researchers and practitioners with limited resources. This can benefit applications in areas such as healthcare, education, legal analysis, and scientific research, where domain expertise exists but large‑scale machine learning infrastructure may not.

At the same time,
AutoAdapt
inherits the risks associated with adapting LLMs to sensitive domains, including the potential amplification of dataset biases or inappropriate behaviors if user‑provided data is flawed or unrepresentative. While
AutoAdapt
does not generate new data on its own in current state, its automation could accelerate deployment of domain‑adapted models without sufficient human review. To mitigate these risks,
AutoAdapt
is explicitly designed to surface configuration decisions, maintain modularity across stages, and enable human oversight through interpretable planning stages. We view
AutoAdapt
as a decision‑support system rather than a replacement for responsible model building evaluation.

Overall,
AutoAdapt
contributes to more efficient and transparent LLM adaptation workflows, with the potential to reduce environmental impact through compute savings while emphasizing the importance of careful downstream evaluation and governance in real‑world deployments.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

Multi-agent frameworks
have emerged as a powerful paradigm for complex decision-making tasks, enabling distributed reasoning through specialized agents that collaborate via a plethora of communication workflows. In the context of automated machine learning and domain adaptation, these frameworks allow decomposition of pipeline design into modular decisions, improving scalability and adaptability compared to monolithic approaches.

A.1
Bayesian Optimisation

Bayesian optimization (BO)
(
Frazier 2018
)
is a sequential model-based approach for optimizing expensive black-box functions. It assumes an unknown objective function
f(\mathbf{x})
and models it using a probabilistic surrogate, typically a Gaussian Process (GP)
(
Beckers 2021
)
. The GP defines a prior over functions and updates it with observed data
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t}
. The posterior predictive distribution at a new point
\mathbf{x}_{*}
is:

\mu(\mathbf{x}_{*})=k(\mathbf{x}_{*},X)^{\top}K^{-1}\mathbf{y},

\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{*})=k(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{x}_{*})-k(\mathbf{x}_{*},X)^{\top}K^{-1}k(X,\mathbf{x}_{*})

where
K
is the kernel matrix with entries
k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
.

The next query point is chosen by maximizing an
acquisition function

\alpha(\mathbf{x})
, which balances exploration and exploitation. Common choices include:
1)
Expected Improvement (EI):

\alpha_{\text{EI}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[\max(0,f(\mathbf{x})-f^{+})]=(\mu(\mathbf{x})-f^{+})\Phi(z)+\sigma(\mathbf{x})\phi(z)

where
z=\frac{\mu(\mathbf{x})-f^{+}}{\sigma(\mathbf{x})}
,
f^{+}
is the best observed value, and
\Phi,\phi
are the CDF and PDF of the standard normal distribution.
2)
Upper Confidence Bound (UCB):

\alpha_{\text{UCB}}(\mathbf{x})=\mu(\mathbf{x})+\kappa\sigma(\mathbf{x})

where
\kappa>0
controls the exploration–exploitation trade-off.

(

(

)
and AutoML-Agent
(
Trirat et al. 2025
)
, generate training plans and code
, offering better alignment with user tasks but often struggling with reliability and the complexity for long, multi-stage adaptation pipelines.

Existing AutoML agents
:
AutoGPT
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
and HuggingGPT
(
Shen et al. 2023
)
connect LLMs with external tools and models to solve generic AI tasks.
Agents such as
(
Guo et al. 2024
;
Hong et al. 2024

Liu et al. 2025
)
focus on specific aspects of the ML training pipeline such as data preparation, analysis or HPO and do not consider the end to end pipeline for LLM adaptation.

MLCopilot

(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
uses a two-stage process: an offline stage to build a knowledge pool from historical data and experience, and an online stage to retrieve demonstrations and generate solutions based on new task descriptions.

(

)

Unlike prior methods that typically support only single-stage fine-tuning, AutoAdapt provides a comprehensive framework for full domain adaptation pipelines, including multi-stage processes such as in-context prompting (eg. AutoAdapt aims to
reliably
and

generate executable, robust, and user-aligned

plans, overcoming the limitations of existing AutoML systems.

Appendix C
Theoretical Bounds on the Autorefine algorithm

This section details the cumulative regret of AutoRefine algorithm.

Setting.

Let
\Theta
be a compact hyperparameter domain equipped with a continuous–categorical relaxation, and let
\widehat{\Theta}\subset\Theta
be the finite discretization used by the acquisition optimizer (e.g., multi-start LBFGS followed by discretization).
Assume the black-box objective
f:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
is a sample from a zero-mean Gaussian process with a squared-exponential (SE/RBF) kernel

k_{\text{SE}}(x,x^{\prime})=\sigma_{f}^{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}}{2\ell^{2}}\right),

and that observations are noisy:

y_{t}=f(\theta_{t})+\varepsilon_{t}
,
where
\varepsilon_{t}
are
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian (independent).
The AutoRefine posterior
\big(\mu_{t},\sigma_{t}\big)
is fit on the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
that combines ground-truth evaluations
\{(\theta_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t}
and LLM-derived trend pseudo-observations
\{(\tilde{\theta}_{j},\tilde{y}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m_{t}}
,
which are treated as
unbiased, high-variance
measurements of
f
(conditional on the GP), i.e.,

\tilde{y}_{j}=f(\tilde{\theta}_{j})+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j},\quad\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}\ \text{is }\tilde{\sigma}\text{-sub-Gaussian with }\tilde{\sigma}\gg\sigma,

and thus incorporated with inflated noise variance so they do not over-confidently shrink
\sigma_{t}(\cdot)
.
At round
t
, AutoRefine algorithm selects

\theta_{t}\in\arg\max_{\theta\in\widehat{\Theta}}\;\mu_{t-1}(\theta)+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\,\sigma_{t-1}(\theta),

with a confidence schedule
\beta_{t}
defined as
(
Iwazaki 2025
)
.

\beta_{t}=2\ln\left(\frac{2t^{2}\pi^{2}}{3\delta}\right)+2d\ln\left(t^{2}dbr\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{4da}{\delta}\right)}\right)

(1)

where
a,b
are constants defined in lemma 1 of
(
Iwazaki 2025
)
,
d
is the dimension of the domain and
r
is the length of the domain. Please refer
(
Iwazaki 2025
)
for details. One subtle technical point to note is that in line 19 of Algorithm
1
, the acquisition is done by an LLM that is prompted in-context using the optimal point from the GP-UCB algorithm. We prompt the LLM to decide between exploiting the optmized GP-UCB hyperparameters and exploring novel ones in the search space. As the algorithm progresses, there would be fewer novel points to explore and the algorithm would rely more on the GP-UCB optimized point. Thus this acquisition is equivalent to the epsilon-greedy policy with decay
(
Sutton & Barto 2018
)
, which asymptotically reduces to the GP-UCB acquisition itself. We need the LLM sampler as the acquisition as otherwise optimizing the function manifold may result in the sampler collapsing to the same optimal point (or one in the vicinity). The LLM sampler acts as a guardrail to prevent such trivial sampling using its domain knowledge.
Let the cumulative regret be
R_{T}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(f(\theta^{\star})-f(\theta_{t})\big)
, where
\theta^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}f(\theta)
.

In this part of the proof, we bound the cumulative regret
R_{T}
over time
T
. Here, the time
T
represents the number of iterations over which the points in the domain (parameter space) are queried to obtain the function value (results). Note we use an LLM as a surrogate model to obtain intermediate results and do not perform evaluations by fitting the network over the sampled parameters and evaluating each iteration. The complexity of an LLM call is negligible compared to actually finetuning an LLM over the sampled parameters and evaluating to obtain the results at that point. As such we only consider the ”actual function evaluations‘ for the complexity. Define
F_{a}
to be the augmentation factor i.e. the fraction of the number of data points queried (by actual function evaluation and LLM surrogate) to the number of actual model evaluation.

We make use of the below result from
(
Srinivas et al. 2010
)
(provides bounds on the function value w.r.t. UCB):

Lemma C.1

(Confidence bounds
(
Srinivas et al. 2010
)
)
.

With probability
1-\delta
(
\delta\in(0,1)
), For any
t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, the function value at the queried point
x_{t}
satisfies

\mu(x_{t};X_{t-1},y_{t-1})-\beta_{t}^{1/2}\,\sigma(x_{t};X_{t-1})\leq f(x_{t}).

Furthermore, for any
t\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, any function value
f(x)
on
\mathcal{X}_{t}
satisfies

f(x)\leq\mu(x;X_{t-1},y_{t-1})+\beta_{t}^{1/2}\,\sigma(x;X_{t-1}).

Using the above result we bound the regret for Autorefine as below:

Claim I.

For the SE/RBF kernel, the cumulative regret of the Autorefine algorithm (
1
), with probability at least
1-\delta
(for any
\delta\in(0,1)
) is,

R_{T_{a}}\;=\;\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{T}{F_{a}}\,\ln^{2}\left(\frac{T}{F_{a}}\right)}\right),

Proof.

The proof follows the GP–UCB template
(
Iwazaki 2025
)
with two ingredients:
(i) high-probability confidence envelopes over the posterior; and
(ii) summability of posterior uncertainties at the points chosen by UCB.

(1) Confidence Bound:

By standard sub-Gaussian concentration and union bounds on the finite set
\widehat{\Theta}
with the above
\beta_{t}
, we have w.h.p. (probability
\geq 1-\delta
) for all
t
and all
\theta\in\widehat{\Theta}
(c.f. lemma 1 in
(
Srinivas et al. 2010
)
and
C.1
):

f(\theta)-\mu_{t-1}(\theta)\;\leq\;\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\,\sigma_{t-1}(\theta).

(2)

f(\theta)-\mu_{t-1}(\theta)\;\geq\;-\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\,\sigma_{t-1}(\theta).

(3)

The inclusion of unbiased pseudo-observations with inflated noise variance preserves the validity of (
2
) and (
3
), as they are treated as additional high-variance measurements; such points only affect constants in the posterior covariance and thus in
\sigma_{t-1}(\cdot)
.

(2) Instantaneous regret under UCB:

Let
\Delta_{t}:=f(\theta^{\star})-f(\theta_{t})
, where
f
is the actual evaluation (with possible finetuning using the parameters) of the network using selected parameters and the given data points.

Since the acquisition function (
\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}f(\theta)
) at time
t
selected
\theta_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{t-1}(\theta_{t})+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma_{t-1}(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{t-1}(\theta^{\star})+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma_{t-1}(\theta^{\star})

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{t-1}(\theta^{\star})-\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma_{t-1}(\theta^{\star})

\displaystyle\therefore\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma_{t-1}(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{t-1}(\theta^{\star})-\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma_{t-1}(\theta^{\star})

\displaystyle-\mu_{t-1}(\theta)

\displaystyle\therefore f(\theta^{\star})-\mu_{t-1}(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma_{t-1}(\theta_{t})

, {…From UCB eq. }

\displaystyle f(\theta^{\star})-(\mu_{t-1}(\theta)

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma_{t-1}(\theta_{t}))\leq 2\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma_{t-1}(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle f(\theta^{\star})-f(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma_{t-1}(\theta_{t})

, {…From LCB eq. }

Thus from above analysis we have

\Delta_{t}\;\leq\;2\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\,\sigma_{t-1}(\theta_{t}).

(4)

(3) Summation and improved SE bound.

Since we are only interested in the regret at every iteration where the function is actually evaluated at the query point and the GP updated, we discard the iterations with LLM surrogate queries. Summing (
4
) over
t=1,\dots,\frac{T}{F_{a}}
gives

\displaystyle R_{T_{a}}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;2\sum_{t=1}^{\frac{T}{F_{a}}}\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\,\sigma_{t-1}(\theta_{t})

For the SE/RBF kernel, a refined analysis of the
realized
input sequence under UCB shows that the cumulative uncertainty weighted by
\sqrt{\beta_{t}}
grows as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T\,\ln^{2}T})
(c.f.
(
Iwazaki 2025
)
).
This yields

R_{T_{a}}\;=\;\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{T}{F_{a}}\,\ln^{2}\left(\frac{T}{F_{a}}\right)}\right)

(5)

with high probability.

Effect of pseudo-observations.

If we consider the LLM pseudo observations in the regret, the bound comes out to be the following (same as in
(
Iwazaki 2025
)
)

R_{T}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{T\,\ln^{2}T}\right)

(6)

Because pseudo-observations are incorporated as unbiased high-variance points, they do not invalidate (
3
), (
2
); they only alter constants in the posterior computation and hence in the final constant multiplying
\sqrt{T\,\ln^{2}T}
.
Therefore the rate is unchanged, concluding the proof of Part I bounding the regret from above.
∎

For the part II on the lower bound for the number of iterations, consider the
simple regret
after
T
rounds to be

r_{T}:=f(\theta^{\star})-\max_{1\leq t\leq T}f(\theta_{t}),

where
\theta^{\star}
is the global maximiser of
f
.
From equation
6
we obtain the average simple regret (over
T
iterations) as

r_{T}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\ln^{2}T}{T}}\Bigg).

Claim II.

To guarantee
r_{T}\leq\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
, the number of actual function evaluation iterations
T_{a}
must satisfy

T_{a}=\Omega\!\Bigg(\frac{\ln^{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{F_{a}\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigg).

Proof.

From the regret rate,

r_{T}\leq c\sqrt{\frac{\ln^{2}T}{T}}

for some constant
c>0
. To enforce
r_{T}\leq\varepsilon
, we require

c\sqrt{\frac{\ln^{2}T}{T}}\leq\varepsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sqrt{\frac{\ln^{2}T}{T}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{c}.

Squaring both sides gives

\frac{\ln^{2}T}{T}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{c^{2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T\geq\frac{c^{2}\ln^{2}T}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

This is an implicit inequality because
T
appears inside
\ln^{2}T
. For large
T
,
\ln T
grows slowly compared to
T
, so we approximate
\ln T\approx\ln(1/\varepsilon)
(since
T
will scale like
1/\varepsilon^{2}
in lower bound). Substituting yields

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\Omega\!\Bigg(\frac{\ln^{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle F_{a}T_{a}

\displaystyle=\Omega\!\Bigg(\frac{\ln^{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle T_{a}

\displaystyle=\Omega\!\Bigg(\frac{\ln^{2}(1/\varepsilon)}{F_{a}\varepsilon^{2}}\Bigg)

which establishes the lower bound up to polylogarithmic factors.
∎

Remark C.2

(On LLM guidance and constants)
.

If trend points are slightly misspecified with bounded bias
b(\theta)
but are re-centered (or modeled with hyperparameters that absorb the bias), the confidence envelopes remain valid up to a constant slack, which is absorbed into the choice of
\beta_{t}
; the asymptotic
\sqrt{T\,\ln^{2}T}
rate is preserved while constants improve when LLM guidance focuses the search in informative regions.
If a fixed bias
B>0
were injected without correction, an additional linear term
\mathcal{O}(BT)
would appear from
\Delta_{t}\leq 2\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\sigma_{t-1}(\theta_{t})+2B
; thus, bias calibration or high-variance downweighting is essential to maintain sublinear regret.

Remark C.3

(On iteration complexity)
.

The iteration complexity grows quadratically with
1/\varepsilon
and only polylogarithmically with the domain size (via
\ln^{2}(1/\varepsilon)
). For example, to achieve
\varepsilon=0.05
(for e.g. within 5% of the optimal accuracy) with augmentation factor
F_{a}=100
, we need

\displaystyle T_{\min}

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ln^{2}(20)}{100\times(0.05)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(3)^{2}}{0.25}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{O}\left(10\right)\text{ iterations.}

Note, our algorithm does not change the asymptotic complexity compared to the GP-UCB algorithm but only changes the constant factors in the complexity. However, the gains are practically significant as our algorithm only needs few tens of iterations vs perhaps thousands to converge to the optimal point, which is desirable considering the costly training of LLMs.

Appendix D
Adaptation Configuration Graph

In this section, we describe in detail the design of the ACG utilized to run the multi agent planning pipeline.
Figure
8
illustrates the Adaptation Configuration Graph (ACG) used in our framework to structure and constrain the space of large language model (LLM) domain adaptation workflows. The ACG is a directed acyclic graph that explicitly encodes high‑level adaptation strategies, their dependencies, and the corresponding parameterized training configurations, enabling systematic and reliable planning of multi‑stage adaptation pipelines.

At the highest level, the ACG branches from a root adapt node into alternative adaptation strategies, including retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG), supervised fine‑tuning (SFT), and preference‑based alignment (DPO). Each strategy node captures a semantically distinct mode of adaptation and is connected via requires edges to the concrete configuration blocks needed to instantiate that strategy. This design allows the planner to reason explicitly over which adaptation techniques are applicable under given data, resource, and user constraints, rather than treating the pipeline as an unstructured sequence of hyperparameters.

For RAG, the graph specifies the required retrieval configuration, including the embedding model, retriever parameters (e.g., top‑k), and the base model used for generation. This separation allows retrieval‑specific choices to be optimized independently of downstream training decisions.
For SFT and DPO, the ACG links the corresponding strategy nodes to detailed training configurations that define the base model, optimization hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, gradient accumulation), logging and evaluation intervals, and training duration. By explicitly modeling these configurations as graph nodes, the ACG exposes the hierarchical structure of LLM adaptation decisions.

The graph further captures parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (PEFT) as an intermediate abstraction. Both SFT and DPO may route through a PEFT node, which in turn branches into concrete techniques such as LoRA and prefix tuning. Each PEFT variant is associated with its own parameter block, specifying method‑specific hyperparameters (e.g., LoRA rank and scaling factors, prefix length) as well as shared efficiency settings such as quantization type. This explicit factoring enables the planner to reuse shared structure while still exploring method‑specific configurations.

Overall, the ACG serves two key purposes. First, it narrows the effective search space by enforcing valid structural dependencies between adaptation stages and parameters, thereby preventing ill‑formed or non‑executable pipelines. Second, it provides a transparent and interpretable representation of the adaptation workflow, which can be traversed and refined by downstream optimization components. By grounding pipeline construction in the ACG, our framework achieves reliable execution while retaining flexibility to support diverse adaptation strategies and training regimes.

Appendix G
Best-Practices Knowledge Base

In this section we explain the construction of the best practices KB and provide studies on the best practices retrieved and the consistency and relevance of the recommendations provided using these.

G.1
KB Construction

To ground the adaptation planning process in empirical evidence and widely adopted practices, we construct a Best‑Practices Knowledge Base (KB) that aggregates and structures information from diverse public sources on large language model (LLM) training, adaptation, and evaluation. The primary goal of the KB is to expose reliable priors over model choices, adaptation strategies, and hyperparameter configurations, which can be leveraged by the multi‑agent planner to generate executable and effective adaptation pipelines.

Knowledge Sources

We curate the KB from multiple complementary sources that reflect both community standards and state‑of‑the‑art empirical results. Specifically, we collect information offline from: (i) model cards and dataset cards hosted on Hugging Face, which provide structured descriptions of training regimes, datasets, parameter choices, evaluation metrics, and reported performance; (ii) fine‑tuning code repositories and notebooks from platforms such as GitHub and Unsloth, which expose concrete implementation details and hyperparameter settings used in practice; and (iii) leaderboards and evaluation summaries from Papers with Code, which link datasets to top‑performing models and report comparative metrics. These sources provide complementary perspectives, ranging from high‑level summaries to low‑level configuration details.

Knowledge Extraction and Structuring

Raw information from the above sources is heterogeneous and largely unstructured. To address this, we employ an LLM‑based extraction pipeline to convert documents and code into structured representations. For each model or dataset entry, we extract salient attributes such as dataset domain, size, task type, input–output characteristics, model family, parameter count, training pipeline (e.g., SFT, PEFT, DPO), and reported hyperparameter ranges. In the case of code repositories, we further extract explicit parameter instantiations (e.g., learning rate, batch size, number of epochs, PEFT‑specific settings) from training scripts and notebooks. All extracted items are normalized and stored as structured records. We manually validate a handful of the extracted data, however due to the scale of the data manual verification of the entire KB is left to future works.

Indexing and Retrieval

Each record in the KB is indexed using a combination of semantic descriptors (task type, domain, dataset characteristics) and model attributes (architecture family, training method, parameter scale). At planning time, the user task description and dataset metadata are used to query the KB and retrieve a set of relevant model–dataset–configuration tuples. This retrieval step provides the multi‑agent system with concrete empirical precedents drawn from similar tasks and domains, serving as an evidence‑based starting point for decision making.

Leveraging Best Practices for Adaptation Planning

The KB is directly consumed by the Best‑Practice Agent during the planning stage. Given a candidate adaptation decision (e.g., selecting PEFT over full fine‑tuning), the agent uses retrieved KB entries to justify choices and to propose bounded hyperparameter ranges rather than arbitrary or overly broad search spaces. For example, learning rate ranges, batch size options, or training epoch counts are derived from configurations that have been demonstrated to work on comparable tasks and model scales. These KB‑derived ranges are subsequently refined during the AutoRefine stage based on user data and observed performance. Figure

Scope and Limitations

The KB is not intended to be exhaustive. Instead, it functions as a practical, curated prior over adaptation decisions for the domains and tasks considered in our experiments. G.2
Dispersion Index of Recommended Parameter Ranges

To quantify the diversity of hyperparameter range recommendations produced by our multi-agent framework, we introduce the
Dispersion Index (DI)
. The DI captures the degree of variability across a set of configurations by jointly considering categorical and numeric dimensions. Unlike pairwise similarity measures that focus on individual comparisons, our metric summarizes the overall spread of the recommendation set, providing a single interpretable score.

Formally, let
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{N}\}
denote the set of recommended configurations. Each configuration comprises categorical parameters (e.g., activation functions, optimizer choices) and numeric parameters (e.g., learning rate, batch size). For any pair
(c_{i},c_{j})
, we compute:

\text{Sim}(c_{i},c_{j})=\alpha\cdot J(c_{i},c_{j})+(1-\alpha)\cdot\overline{\text{IoU}}(c_{i},c_{j}),

(7)

where
J(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Jaccard similarity over categorical tokens,
\overline{\text{IoU}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the weighted average Intersection-over-Union across numeric ranges, and
\alpha\in[0,1]
balances the contributions of categorical and numeric components. The
Dispersion Index
is then defined as:

\text{DI}=\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i<j}\big(1-\text{Sim}(c_{i},c_{j})\big).

(8)

Intuitively, DI approaches zero when all configurations are nearly identical and increases as recommendations become more diverse. By incorporating both discrete and continuous dimensions, DI provides a holistic measure of variability that is particularly suited for mixed-type hyperparameter spaces. This metric enables us to assess whether the optimizer explores the search space broadly or converges to a narrow region, which is critical for evaluating exploration–exploitation trade-offs in automated tuning.

We then average these scores across all sources for a given task and run. We experiment on the three datasets: PEM, RCA, MedQA and run the best practice agent 10 times per dataset. (

Appendix H
Experimental Setup

In this section, we highlight the detailed experimental setup and justify its completeness in proving the efficiency of
AutoAdapt
over existing LLM-based AutoML based methods. To make the comparison fair across all baselines, all agents are implemented using the same model as AutoAdapt (i.e OpenAI’s GPT-4.1-mini). In order to prevent data leakage, we remove entries of the exact benchmark dataset being evaluated, if present in KB. More concretely, experiments are conducted within the following two setups:

•

Template Free (TF) Setting
: Within this setup, we evaluate each method’s proficiency at generating an end-to-end pipeline under no explicit constraints, except for a simple prompt describing user requirements with respect to domain, accuracy and inference speed - mimcing the requirements of a user with no domain knowledge of LLMs. This gives the candidates full autonomy over the choice of model, adaptation technique and parameters. The prompt is kept consistent across all baselines. However, despite a 100% success rate demonstrated by
AutoAdapt
, other methods fail to produce final code templates that execute without any errors. To quantify the ability of the baselines to produce executable code, we manually fix errors in the code templates produced by baseline methods, and report the Success Rate of each method as:

\text{Success Rate (SR)}=\frac{1}{1+\text{number of iterations}}
