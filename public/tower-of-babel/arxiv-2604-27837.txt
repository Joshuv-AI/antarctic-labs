Distributionally Robust Insurance under Bregman-Wasserstein Divergence
Wenjun Jiang, Qingqing Zhang, Yiying Zhang

Abstract
This paper investigates two optimal insurance contracting problems under distributional uncertainty from the perspective of a potential policyholder, utilizing a Bregman-Wasserstein (BW) ball to characterize the ambiguity set of loss distributions. Unlike the p p -Wasserstein distance, BW divergence enables asymmetric penalization of deviations from the benchmark distribution. The first problem examines an insurance demand model where the policyholder adopts an α \alpha -maxmin preference with Value-at-Risk (VaR). We derive the optimal indemnity function in closed form and study, both analytically and numerically, how the asymmetry inherent in BW divergence influences the optimal indemnity structure. The second problem employs a robust optimization framework, where the policyholder aims to secure robust insurance indemnity by minimizing the worst-case convex distortion risk measure while adhering to a guaranteed VaR constraint. In this context, we provide explicit characterizations of both the optimal indemnity and the worst-case distribution in closed form through a combined approach using the Lagrangian method and modification arguments. To illustrate the practical implications of our theoretical findings, we include a concrete example based on Tail Value-at-Risk (TVaR). Keywords: Distributional uncertainty; optimal insurance; Bregman-Wasserstein divergence; α \alpha -maxmin model; guaranteed performance; VaR; distortion risk measures. JEL Classification Codes: C61, G22, G32 MSC Codes: 62P05, 91B30

Introduction

Insurance serves as an efficient risk‑hedging tool that protects individuals from suffering catastrophic financial losses. An insurance contract is typically characterized by an indemnity function and a premium, where the former specifies the compensation paid to the insurance buyer as a function of the incurred loss, while the latter is the payment made by the insurance buyer to the seller before the contract takes effect. In most practical settings, the premium is determined according to the chosen indemnity function, which simplifies the optimal insurance design problem to the selection of an appropriate indemnity structure. The seminal works
13
and
2
established the optimality of excess-of-loss indemnity function when insurance buyers aim to minimize the variance of their terminal losses or maximize the expected utility (EU) of their terminal wealth under the expected-value premium principle. Since then, extensive research has been devoted to extending these classical results into more general frameworks. For a comprehensive overview of recent developments on optimal insurance design along various directions, we refer readers to, for example,
1
and
14
.

A fundamental assumption adopted in most of the literature on optimal insurance contracting is that the insurance buyer possesses perfect knowledge of the loss distribution. However, this assumption is overly idealistic in practice, given the limited or even scarce loss data available to the buyer. The past few years have witnessed growing interest in extending the classical optimal insurance framework, which assumes the loss distribution is known a priori, to settings where the buyer faces ambiguity regarding this distribution, yielding the so-called distributionally robust insurance design problems, which we simply refer to as robust insurance problems in this paper

This paper aims to complement the existing studies of robust insurance problems by considering a new uncertainty set—the Bregman-Wasserstein (BW) ball. The BW divergence possesses distinct features compared to the popular Wasserstein distance, including asymmetry and differing penalties for positive versus negative deviations from the benchmark distribution. Such an uncertainty set has been employed by
30
to solve a robust portfolio choice problem. We investigate two problems in this paper. The first problem considers an ambiguity-averse decision maker (DM) whose preference is modeled by a VaR-based
\alpha
-maxmin criterion. We derive explicit expressions for the worst- and best-case VaR of the loss when its distribution belongs to a BW ball, as well as for the optimal indemnity function. We find that for a more ambiguity-averse DM, increased sensitivity to positive deviations from the benchmark quantile function reduces insurance demand. In contrast, greater sensitivity to negative deviations does not consistently influence demand. This result can be reversed when the DM is ambiguity-seeking.

In the second problem, we consider the robust optimal insurance problem when the DM adopts a general convex distortion risk measure and the distributional uncertainty set is characterized by a BW ball under a constraint placed on the worst-case VaR of the DM’s terminal loss, which is referred to as the guaranteed VaR performance model. This model is conceptually aligned with the robust mean-variance framework of
9
, in which the worst-case portfolio variance is minimized subject to a constraint on the worst-case expected return. We derive the optimal indemnity function and the worst-case survival function in closed form through a combined approach using the Lagrangian method and modification arguments, which contrasts markedly in techniques with existing literature on robust insurance problems. Our results indicate that as the guaranteed VaR constraint becomes more stringent, the DM is increasingly inclined to exaggerate tail losses while adhering to the benchmark distribution for small and moderate losses.

2.1
Distortion risk measures

The distortion risk measure stems from Yaari’s dual theory of choice
(
36
)
and evaluates a risk based on its distorted expectation. For a random variable

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})

\rho_{g}^{\mathbb{P}}(Z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(\mathbb{P}(Z>z))dz+\int_{-\infty}^{0}[g(\mathbb{P}(Z>z))-1]dz,

(1)

where

[0,1]
and satisfies
g(0)=0
and
g(1)=1
. The popularity of the distortion risk measure is due to its nice mathematical properties, such as monotonicity, positive homogeneity, translation invariance, and comonotonic additivity. The predominant risk measures in finance and insurance, VaR and TVaR, are both special cases of the distortion risk measure. For a random variable

, its VaR at the confidence level

is defined as its left quantile at
\alpha

\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(Z)=\inf\left\{z:\ \mathbb{P}(Z\leq z)\geq\alpha\right\}
, and its TVaR at the same confidence level is defined as
\mathrm{TVaR}_{\alpha}(Z)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\mathrm{VaR}_{s}(Z)\,ds
. The distortion functions of
\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
and
\mathrm{TVaR}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
are
g_{V,\alpha}(t)=\mathds{1}_{(1-\alpha,1]}(t)
and
g_{T,\alpha}(t)=\frac{t}{1-\alpha}\wedge 1
respectively.

If
g
is concave, the distortion risk measure is convex. The convex distortion risk measure is coherent in the sense of
3
and preserves the increasing convex order between random variables. In the rest of this paper, we will particularly focus on VaR and convex distortion risk measures, which are common choices for modeling the preferences of decision makers. For more examples and applications of distortion risk measures, we refer the interested readers to
20

2.2
Bregman–Wasserstein divergence

The BW divergence is the optimal transport cost when the cost function in the Monge-Kantorovich problem is replaced by the Bregman divergence. Although the literature is now rich with introduction of the BW divergence, we provide a brief review in this section to make the paper self-contained. We recall the Bregman divergence in the following.

Definition 2.1

(Bregman divergence)
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly convex and continuously differentiable function,
called a
Bregman generator
. The Bregman divergence with generator
\varphi

is defined as

B_{\varphi}(x,y):=\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)-\varphi^{\prime}(y)(x-y),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R},

where
\varphi^{\prime}(x):=\tfrac{d}{dx}\varphi(x)
denotes the derivative of
\varphi
.

The Bregman divergence includes some well-known distances and divergences as special cases. For example, if letting
\varphi(x)=x^{2}
, the Bregman divergence becomes the squared distance
B_{\varphi}(x,y)=(x-y)^{2}
(or squared Euclidean distance for higher-dimensional space). When focusing on the discrete distributions, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{x}=(x,\dots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
, if letting
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\ln x_{i}
, the Bregman divergence becomes the Kullback-Leibler divergence. We refer the interested readers to Table 1 of
6
for more special cases of the Bregman divergence. In the statistical field, the most often used statistical functional is the mean. It can be easily verified that the mean is elicitable by the Bregman divergence, and
23
shows that the scoring function being a Bregman divergence is not only sufficient but also necessary for doing so.

We are now ready to give the definition of the BW divergence that is built on the space of CDFs, which is simply the minimum cost resulting from solving the following Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport problem

\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}[F_{1},F_{2}]:=\inf_{X\sim F_{1},Y\sim F_{2}}\ \mathbb{E}[B_{\varphi}(X,Y)].

It is shown by
31
that the infimum is attained by the comonotonic coupling of
X
and
Y
, i.e.,
(X,Y)\overset{d}{=}(F_{1}^{-1}(U),F_{2}^{-1}(U))
with
U
being a standard uniform random variable. Consequently, the BW divergence from
F_{1}
to
F_{2}
can be written in terms of their quantile functions

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}[F_{1},F_{2}]

\displaystyle=\inf_{X\sim F_{1},Y\sim F_{2}}\ \mathbb{E}[B_{\varphi}(X,Y)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[B_{\varphi}(F_{1}^{-1}(U),F_{2}^{-1}(U))]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}B_{\varphi}\big(F_{1}^{-1}(t),F_{2}^{-1}(t)\big)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big(\varphi(F_{1}^{-1}(t))-\varphi(F_{2}^{-1}(t))-\varphi^{\prime}(F_{2}^{-1}(t))\big(F_{1}^{-1}(t)-F_{2}^{-1}(t)\big)\big)\,dt.

Note that
\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}
reduces to the 2-Wasserstein distance when
\varphi(x)=x^{2}
. For additional geometric properties and more detailed interpretations of the BW divergence, we refer the reader to
27
. In addition to the classical quantile representation,
\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}[F_{1},F_{2}]
admits another survival-function-based representation under mild conditions, which is summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

If the support of both
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
is
[0,M]
and
\sup_{x\in[0,M]}|\varphi^{\prime}(x)|<\infty
, where
0<M<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}[F_{1},F_{2}]=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{M}[\varphi^{\prime}(x)-\varphi^{\prime}(0)]S_{1}(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{M}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)xS_{2}(x)\,dx

(2)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{M}\int_{0}^{M}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(y)S_{2}(y)\wedge S_{1}(x)\,dy\,dx.

Typical examples of
\varphi
that fulfill the condition of Lemma
2.1
are, for instance,
\varphi(x)=x^{p}
for
p>1
and
\varphi(x)=(x+a)\ln(x+a)
for
a>0
.

Notably, the BW divergence is non-symmetric and penalizes the positive and negative deviations from the benchmark distribution differently in the sense that
\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}[F_{1},F_{2}]\neq\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}[F_{2},F_{1}]
and
\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}[F_{2}+\epsilon\delta,F_{2}]\neq\mathscr{B}_{\varphi}[F_{2}-\epsilon\delta,F_{2}]
,

\epsilon

\delta
are chosen such that

as elaborated by

2.3
Problem formulation

In this section, we set up the problems that will be studied in this paper. We focus on a one-period economy. Let
X
now be a non-negative bounded loss faced by a potential policyholder (hereafter referred to as decision maker, or DM for short), whose support is a subset of
[0,M]
with
0<M<\infty
. The DM is interested in purchasing insurance
(I,\pi(\cdot))
from an insurer. We denote by
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}

\pi(I(X))=(1+\theta)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[I(X)]

Aligning with the literature, we assume that the indemnity function is chosen from the following class

\mathcal{I}=\left\{I:[0,M]\mapsto[0,M]:\ I(0)=0,\ 0\leq I(y)-I(x)\leq y-x,\ \forall\ x,y\in[0,M]\right\}.

(3)

With the insurance contract, the DM’s terminal loss becomes

Problem 2.1

Let

Let

\mbox{s.t.}&\ \sup_{F_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(F_{0},\epsilon)}\mathrm{VaR}^{F_{2}}_{\alpha}(X-I(X)+\pi(I(X)))\leq A.\end{aligned}\right.

\varphi(x;k)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &x^{2},&\quad&x\in[0,q_{\alpha}),\\
&q_{\alpha}^{2}+2q_{\alpha}(x-q_{\alpha})+k(x-q_{\alpha})^{2},&\quad&x\in[q_{\alpha},M].\end{aligned}\right.

Note that
\varphi(x;k_{2})-\varphi(x;k_{1})
is convex if
k_{2}>k_{1}
, thus
B_{\varphi(\cdot;k_{2})}(D,F_{0}^{-1}(t))>B_{\varphi(\cdot;k_{1})}(D,F_{0}^{-1}(t))
for all
t\in(\alpha,F_{0}(D))
when
D>F_{0}^{-1}(\alpha)
. Apparently, a larger
k
penalizes more the CDFs whose quantile functions deviate positively from the benchmark quantile function (or shows that the DM is more confident about the benchmark distribution). Under a given
\epsilon
(which is 0.5 in this example), it is not a surprise to find that
\overline{V_{\alpha}}
decreases with
k
. Under our setting, it can be easily calculated that
d_{1}^{*}\approx 0.406
,
d_{2}^{*}\approx 0.511
and
\underline{V_{\alpha}}\approx 0.563
, where the
\underline{V_{\alpha}}
remains the same across different values of
k
. Hence, the optimal indemnity function is given by
I^{*}(x)=(x\wedge\overline{V_{\alpha}}-d_{1}^{*})_{+}
, and the DM would lower her demand for insurance when being more sensitive to the positive deviation from the benchmark quantile function. The corresponding optimal indemnity functions are shown in the right panel of Figure
1
.

In contrast, if the DM penalizes more the CDFs whose quantile functions deviate negatively from the benchmark quantile function,
\underline{V_{\alpha}}
increases with
k
while
\overline{V_{\alpha}}
remains unchanged. Under the setting of this example, we can easily verify that the DM would not change her choice of the indemnity function.

\varphi
under different values of
k
; (Right) The corresponding optimal indemnity functions.

We remark that in the above example an ambiguity-averse DM is assumed, where
\kappa
is close to
1
. If the DM is ambiguity-seeking, i.e. a small
\kappa
is assigned to Problem
2.1
, the resulting conclusion could differ substantially. The closed-form indemnity function in (
9
) elegantly encapsulates how these differences in ambiguity preference manifest in the optimal contract.

4
The solution to Problem
2.2

4.1
The optimal indemnity function

With the results developed in Section
3
, we can rewrite the constraint of Problem
2.2
as

\pi(I(X))-I(\overline{V_{\alpha}})\leq A-\overline{V_{\alpha}}.

(10)

Before solving Problem
2.2
, we adopt the following assumption throughout this section.

Assumption 4.1
.

\inf_{I\in\mathcal{I}}\ \pi(I(X))-I(\overline{V_{\alpha}})<A-\overline{V_{\alpha}}
.

If Assumption
4.1
is violated, the solution to Problem
2.2
either reduces trivially to the minimizer of
\inf_{I\in\mathcal{I}}\ \pi(I(X))-I(\overline{V_{\alpha}})
or fails to exist.

With Assumption
4.1
, a solution to Problem
2.2
exists, which is presented in the next theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let Assumption
4.1
hold. There exists a solution to Problem
2.2
.

Note that
I\mapsto\sup_{F\in\mathcal{B}(F_{0},\epsilon)}\rho_{g}^{F}(X-I(X)+\pi(I(X)))
is convex and the constraint (
10
) is linear in
I
. Under Assumption
4.1
, the Slater’s condition holds, which makes solving Problem
2.2
equivalent to solving

\inf_{I\in\mathcal{I}}\left\{\sup_{F\in\mathcal{B}(F_{0},\epsilon)}\rho^{F}_{g}(X-I(X)+\pi(I(X)))+\lambda(\pi(I(X))-I(\overline{V_{\alpha}})-A+\overline{V_{\alpha}})\right\}

(11)

for some
\lambda\geq 0
.

Before proceeding, we recall the following well-known minimax theorem.

Lemma 4.1

(Minimax theorem
(
21
)
)
.

Let
\Xi_{1}
be a non-empty convex compact subset of a Hausdorff topological vector space and
\Xi_{2}
be a convex set. If
\mathcal{H}
is a real-valued function defined on
\Xi_{1}\times\Xi_{2}
such that

(a).

\xi_{1}\mapsto\mathcal{H}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
is convex and lower semi-continuous on
\Xi_{1}
for each
\xi_{2}\in\Xi_{2}
;

(b).

\xi_{2}\mapsto\mathcal{H}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
is concave on
\Xi_{2}
for each
\xi_{1}\in\Xi_{1}
,

then
\inf_{\xi_{1}\in\Xi_{1}}\sup_{\xi_{2}\in\Xi_{2}}\mathcal{H}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\sup_{\xi_{2}\in\Xi_{2}}\inf_{\xi_{1}\in\Xi_{1}}\mathcal{H}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
.

Note that the set
\mathcal{I}
is compact under the
L^{\infty}
metric (see, for example,
37
). Moreover, the convexity of
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{B}(F_{0},\epsilon)
and the conditions (a) and (b) can be easily verified. Therefore, Lemma
4.1
is applicable here, which turns Problem (
11
) into

\sup_{F\in\mathcal{B}(F_{0},\epsilon)}\inf_{I\in\mathcal{I}}\ \left\{\rho_{g}^{F}(X-I(X)+\pi(I(X)))+\lambda\left(\pi(I(X))-I(\overline{V_{\alpha}})-A+\overline{V_{\alpha}}\right)\right\}

(12)

for which the objective function can be simplified as

\displaystyle\rho_{g}^{F}(X-I(X)+\pi(I(X)))+\lambda\left(\pi(I(X))-I(\overline{V_{\alpha}})-A+\overline{V_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\rho_{g}^{F}(X)-\lambda(A-\overline{V_{\alpha}})+(1+\lambda)\pi(I(X))-\rho_{g}^{F}(I(X))-\lambda I(\overline{V_{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\rho_{g}^{F}(X)-\lambda(A-\overline{V_{\alpha}})+\int_{0}^{M}\left\{(1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)-g(S(x))-\lambda\mathds{1}_{[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}]}(x)\right\}dI(x).

For given
F\in\mathcal{B}(F_{0},\epsilon)
and
\lambda\geq 0
, the optimal indemnity function can be derived straightforwardly. For that purpose, we define

\mathcal{S}(S_{0},\epsilon)=\{S:S(x)=1-F(x),\ \forall x\in[0,M]\ \text{with}\ F\in\mathcal{B}(F_{0},\epsilon)\},

where
S_{0}(x)=1-F_{0}(x)
, and

H(x;S,\lambda)=(1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)-g(S(x))-\lambda\mathds{1}_{[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}]}(x).

We summarize the result of the optimal indemnity function in the following theorem. The proof follows the standard marginal indemnification function (MIF) method employed in
38
and is therefore omitted here.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}(S_{0},\epsilon)
and
\lambda\geq 0
be given. The optimal indemnity function that solves the inner problem of (
12
), that is,

\inf_{I\in\mathcal{I}}\ \left\{\rho_{g}^{F}(X-I(X)+\pi(I(X)))+\lambda\left(\pi(I(X))-I(\overline{V_{\alpha}})-A+\overline{V_{\alpha}}\right)\right\}

(13)

is given by
I^{*}(x;S,\lambda)=\int_{0}^{x}{I^{*}}^{\prime}(t;S,\lambda)\,dt
, where

{I^{*}}^{\prime}(t;S,\lambda)=\mathds{1}_{\{t:H(t;S,\lambda)<0\}}(t)+\tilde{\eta}(t)\mathds{1}_{\{t:H(t;S,\lambda)=0\}}(t),

(14)

where
\tilde{\eta}(t)
is a
[0,1]
-valued Lebesgue measurable function.

We interpret
H(x;S,\lambda)
as the net price of marginal indemnity, and the DM will always purchase the full marginal indemnity when the net price is negative and is indifferent between with and without insurance when this price is zero.

With Theorem
4.2
, the objective function of Problem (
11
) can be simplified as

\displaystyle\rho_{g}^{F}(X)-\lambda(A-\overline{V_{\alpha}})+\int_{0}^{M}\left\{(1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)-g(S(x))-\lambda\mathds{1}_{[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}]}(x)\right\}dI^{*}(x;S,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\rho_{g}^{F}(X)-\lambda(A-\overline{V_{\alpha}})-\int_{0}^{M}\left(g(S(x))+\lambda\mathds{1}_{[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}]}(x)-(1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)\right)_{+}\,dx

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{M}g(S(x))\wedge\left((1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)-\lambda\mathds{1}_{[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}]}(x)\right)\,dx-\lambda(A-\overline{V_{\alpha}}).

This reduces Problem (
11
) to

\displaystyle\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}(S_{0},\epsilon)}\int_{0}^{M}g(S(x))\wedge\left((1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)-\lambda\mathds{1}_{[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}]}(x)\right)\,dx-\lambda(A-\overline{V_{\alpha}}).

(15)

In the next section, we characterize the worst-case survival function that solves problem (
15
).

4.2
The worst-case distribution

It is now sufficient to focus on the problem

\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}(S_{0},\epsilon)}\ \int_{0}^{M}g(S(x))\wedge\left((1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)-\lambda\mathds{1}_{[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}]}(x)\right)\,dx

(16)

for some
\lambda\geq 0
. Note that the objective function of (
16
) is monotone in
S(x)
, which directly yields the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

The worst-case survival function
S^{*}(x)
that solves Problem (
16
), if exists, must satisfy
S^{*}(x)\geq S_{0}(x)
for all
x\in[0,M]
,
whose corresponding quantile function satisfies
F^{*-1}(t)\geq F_{0}^{-1}(t)
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Therefore, for Problem (
16
) we can restrict ourselves to the following smaller uncertainty set:

\tilde{\mathcal{S}}(S_{0},\epsilon):=\big\{S\in\mathcal{S}(S_{0},\epsilon):S(x)\geq S_{0}(x),\ \forall x\in[0,M]\big\}.

With the help of Lemma
2.1
, the BW constraint can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{M}\Big(\big[\varphi^{\prime}(x)-\varphi^{\prime}(0)\big]S(x)-\int_{0}^{M}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(y)\big(S_{0}(y)\wedge S(x)\big)\,dy\Big)\,dx\leq\zeta,

where
\zeta:=\epsilon-\int_{0}^{M}\varphi^{\prime}(y)yS_{0}(y)\,dy
. Since the objective in
(
16
)
is concave in
S(\cdot)
, and the constraint is convex in
S(\cdot)
, with
S_{0}
being a strictly feasible solution, strong duality holds because Slater’s conditions are fulfilled. Similar to Theorem
4.1
, we first verify the existence of a solution to Problem (
16
).

Theorem 4.3
.

Given any
\lambda\geq 0
, there exists a solution to Problem (
16
).

To this end, solving Problem (
16
) boils down to solving

\displaystyle\sup_{S\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}(S_{0},\epsilon)}\ \mathscr{L}(S(x);\beta):=\int_{0}^{M}\bigg\{

\displaystyle g(S(x))\wedge\left((1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)-\lambda\mathds{1}_{[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}]}(x)\right)

(17)

\displaystyle-\beta\Big(\big[\varphi^{\prime}(x)-\varphi^{\prime}(0)\big]S(x)-\int_{0}^{M}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(y)S_{0}(y)\wedge S(x)\,dy\Big)\bigg\}\,dx,

for some
\beta\geq 0
.

While the objective function in (
17
) is concave in
S(\cdot)
, it is discontinuous in
x
. Consequently, the pointwise maximizer may contain an upward jump at the point of discontinuity, violating the definition of a survival function. We thus temporarily deviate from Problem (
17
) and first solve the following auxiliary problem:

\sup_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\ \mathscr{L}(G(x);\beta)

(18)

where

\mathcal{G}:=\{G:G(x)\in[S_{0}(x),1],\ \forall x\in[0,M]\}.

We denote by
G^{*}(x;\beta)
the solution to Problem (
18
). We will show later that the solution to Problem (
17
) can be obtained via a modification approach based on the solution to Problem (
18
).

To solve Problem (
18
), we define

x_{1}:=\sup\bigg\{x\in[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}):S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)\geq\tfrac{1}{1+\theta}\bigg\},\quad x_{2}:=\sup\bigg\{x\in[\overline{V_{\alpha}},M):S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)\geq\tfrac{1}{(1+\lambda)(1+\theta)}\bigg\}

and

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{1}:=\{x\in[x_{1},\overline{V_{\alpha}}):H(x;S_{0},\lambda)\geq 0\},\quad\mathscr{B}_{1}:=[x_{1},\overline{V_{\alpha}})\setminus\mathscr{A}_{1},

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}_{2}:=\{x\in[x_{2},M]:H(x;S_{0},\lambda)\geq 0\},\quad\mathscr{B}_{2}:=[x_{2},M]\setminus\mathscr{A}_{2}.

The following proposition characterizes the worst-case functions in
\mathcal{G}
that solve Problem (
18
).

Proposition 4.1
.

The solution to Problem
(
18
)
is given by

\displaystyle G^{*}(x;\beta)=

\displaystyle\hat{G}(x;\beta)\mathds{1}_{[0,x_{1})}(x)+\big(\hat{G}(x;\beta)\wedge g^{-1}\left((1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)-\lambda\right)\big)\mathds{1}_{\mathscr{A}_{1}}(x)+S_{0}(x)\mathds{1}_{\mathscr{B}_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle+\hat{G}(x;\beta)\mathds{1}_{[\overline{V_{\alpha}},x_{2})}(x)+\big(\hat{G}(x;\beta)\wedge g^{-1}\left((1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)\right)\big)\mathds{1}_{\mathscr{A}_{2}}(x)+S_{0}(x)\mathds{1}_{\mathscr{B}_{2}}(x),

where

\hat{G}(x;\beta)=\inf\{t\in[S_{0}(x),1]:K^{\prime}(t;x)\leq 0\}

and

K(t;x):=g(t)-\beta\!\left(\big[\varphi^{\prime}(x)-\varphi^{\prime}(0)\big]t-\int_{0}^{M}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(y)S_{0}(y)\wedge t\,dy\right).

Furthermore,
\hat{G}(x;0)=g^{-1}(1)\vee S_{0}(x)
and

G^{*}(\cdot;0)\in\arg\inf_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\ \int_{0}^{M}\Big(\big[\varphi^{\prime}(x)-\varphi^{\prime}(0)\big]G(x)-\int_{0}^{M}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(y)\big(S_{0}(y)\wedge G(x)\big)\,dy\Big)\,dx,

where

\mathcal{G}_{0}=\{\tilde{G}\in\mathcal{G}:\ \mathscr{L}(\tilde{G}(x);0)=\sup_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\ \mathscr{L}(G(x);0)\}.

One can verify that
G^{*}(x;\beta)
is decreasing on the intervals
[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}})
and
[\overline{V_{\alpha}},M]
, respectively. However, it may fail to be decreasing on the entire interval
[0,M]
. For instance, if
\overline{V_{\alpha}}-\in\mathscr{A}_{1}
,
\overline{V_{\alpha}}\in\mathscr{A}_{2}
, and
S_{\mathbb{Q}}
is continuous at
\overline{V_{\alpha}}
, then

\displaystyle G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}}-;0)=g^{-1}\big((1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(\overline{V_{\alpha}})-\lambda\big)\leq G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};0)=g^{-1}\big((1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(\overline{V_{\alpha}})\big),

and for
\beta>0
,

\displaystyle G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}}-;\beta)=\hat{G}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};\beta)\wedge g^{-1}\big((1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(\overline{V_{\alpha}})-\lambda\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};\beta)=\hat{G}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};\beta)\wedge g^{-1}\big((1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(\overline{V_{\alpha}})\big).

If such an upward jump exists, i.e.,
G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}}-;\beta)<G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};\beta)
, the worst-case survival function solving Problem (
17
) can be derived via the following constructive modification procedure. Let

\displaystyle S(x;b(\beta))=

\displaystyle\ \left(G^{*}(x;\beta)\vee b(\beta)\right)\mathds{1}_{[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}]}(x)+\left(G^{*}(x;\beta)\wedge b(\beta)\right)\mathds{1}_{[\overline{V_{\alpha}},M]}(x)

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \begin{cases}G^{*}(x;\beta),&x\in[0,a_{1}(\beta))\cup[a_{2}(\beta),M],\\
b(\beta),&x\in[a_{1}(\beta),a_{2}(\beta)),\end{cases}

where

a_{1}(\beta):=\sup\big\{x\in[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}):G^{*}(x;\beta)\geq b(\beta)\big\},\quad\ a_{2}(\beta):=\sup\big\{x\in[\overline{V_{\alpha}},M):G^{*}(x;\beta)\geq b(\beta)\big\}.

Note that
S(x;b(\beta))
is a well-defined survival function on
[0,M]
. To present the main result of this section, we define

\Psi(\beta):=\int_{0}^{M}\left([\varphi^{\prime}(x)-\varphi^{\prime}(0)]S(x;b^{*}(\beta))-\int_{0}^{M}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(y)(S_{0}(y)\wedge S(x;b^{*}(\beta)))\,dy\right)\,dx.

(20)

The following theorem establishes the optimality of (
19
) for Problem (
17
) and presents the solution to Problem (
16
).

Theorem 4.4
.

Let

\mathcal{O}_{b(\beta)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &[G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}}-;\beta),G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};\beta)],&\quad&\text{if}\ G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}}-;\beta)<G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};\beta),\\
&\{G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};\beta)\},&\quad&\text{if}\ G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}}-;\beta)\geq G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};\beta).\end{aligned}\right.

For any survival function
S\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}(S_{0},\epsilon)
, there exists a
b(\beta)\in\mathcal{O}_{b(\beta)}
, such that
S(\cdot;b(\beta))\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}(S_{0},\epsilon)
and

\mathscr{L}(S(x;b(\beta));\beta)\geq\mathscr{L}(S(x);\beta).

Let

b^{*}(\beta)\in\arg\sup_{b(\beta)\in\mathcal{O}_{b(\beta)}}\ \mathscr{L}(S(x;b(\beta));\beta),

then
S(x;b^{*}(\beta))
solves Problem
(
17
)
. Furthermore, if
\Psi(0)\leq\zeta
, then
\beta^{*}=0
,
and
S(x;b^{*}(0))
solves Problem (
16
). Otherwise, there exists a
\beta^{*}>0
such that
\Psi(\beta^{*})=\zeta
and
S(x;b^{*}(\beta^{*}))
solves Problem (
16
).

:
Graphical illustration of Theorem
4.4
when
\beta^{*}=0
: (Left)
S(\overline{V_{\alpha}})\in\big(G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}}-;0),\,G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};0)\big)
; (Middle)
S(\overline{V_{\alpha}})\leq G^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}}-;0)
; (Right)

.

Figure
2
provides a graphical illustration of Theorem
4.4
when
\beta^{*}=0
. We are now prepared to present the interpretations of the results of Theorem
4.4
, with a focus on the case where
\beta^{*}=0
, along with findings regarding the effect of
\lambda
on the worst-case distribution. The results for the case where
\beta^{*}>0
can be interpreted similarly. Since the worst-case survival function is derived from the solution to (
18
), key insights focus on the behavior and characteristics of
G^{*}(x;\beta)
.

(a).

By (
16
), the DM has no incentive to exaggerate the loss if
g(S_{0}(x))=1
, resulting in the worst-case survival function
g^{-1}(1)\vee S_{0}(x)
over
[0,x_{1})\cup[\overline{V_{\alpha}},x_{2})
.

(b).

As explained in Section
4.1
, the DM purchases full marginal insurance when the net premium
H(x;S,\lambda)
is negative. Since
H(x;S,\lambda)\leq H(x;S_{0},\lambda)
for
S\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}(S_{0},\epsilon)
, the DM has no incentive to consider the worst-case losses in
\mathscr{B}_{1}\cup\mathscr{B}_{2}
as those losses are ceded to the insurer.

(c).

The net premium
H(x;S,\lambda)
increases with
\lambda
when
x\in[0,x_{1})\cup[\overline{V_{\alpha}},M]
but decreases with
\lambda
when
x\in[x_{1},\overline{V_{\alpha}})
. Consequently, the incentive to insure the loss is reduced over the former intervals and increased over the latter when
\lambda
increases. This results in smaller
\mathscr{A}_{1}
and
\mathscr{B}_{2}
but larger
\mathscr{B}_{1}
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}
. Thus, the DM tends to exaggerate large losses (beyond the worst-case VaR) while staying with the benchmark distribution for small ones under a tighter constraint.

(d).

The finding of (c) explains the possible upward jump of
G^{*}(x;\beta)
at
\overline{V_{\alpha}}
. Note that the upward jump occurs exclusively when
\lambda>0
, i.e., when the constraint becomes binding. A binding VaR constraint, which restricts the distribution only by the value of its
\alpha
-quantile and not by the allocation of probability mass around it, directly produces the flat segment observed in
S(x;b)
.

Note that Problem (
16
) depends on
\lambda
. We now abuse the notation a bit and write the solution to Problem (
16
) as
S_{\lambda}(x;b_{\lambda}^{*}(\beta_{\lambda}^{*}))
. We end this section by presenting the following Theorem that contains the solution to Problem
2.2
.

Theorem 4.5
.

The optimal insurance indemnity function that solves Problem
2.2
is given by
I^{*}(x;S_{\lambda^{*}}(x;b_{\lambda^{*}}^{*}(\beta^{*}_{\lambda^{*}})),\lambda^{*})
, as established in Theorem
4.2
, and the corresponding worst-case survival function is given by
S_{\lambda^{*}}(x;b_{\lambda^{*}}^{*}(\beta^{*}_{\lambda^{*}}))
, as demonstrated in Theorem
4.4
. The function
\tilde{\eta}
appearing in Theorem
4.2
and the Lagrangian parameter
\lambda^{*}
are chosen to satisfy the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}(\pi(I^{*}(X;S_{\lambda^{*}}(x;b_{\lambda^{*}}^{*}(\beta^{*}_{\lambda^{*}})),\lambda^{*}))-I^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};S_{\lambda^{*}}(x;b_{\lambda^{*}}^{*}(\beta^{*}_{\lambda^{*}})),\lambda^{*})-A+\overline{V_{\alpha}})=0

and

\displaystyle\pi(I^{*}(X;S_{\lambda^{*}}(x;b_{\lambda^{*}}^{*}(\beta^{*}_{\lambda^{*}})),\lambda^{*}))-I^{*}(\overline{V_{\alpha}};S_{\lambda^{*}}(x;b_{\lambda^{*}}^{*}(\beta^{*}_{\lambda^{*}})),\lambda^{*})\leq A-\overline{V_{\alpha}}.

In view of Theorem
4.1
, together with the strong duality for Problem
2.2
, the existence of the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda^{*}
, as well as the associated KKT conditions, follows from Theorem 1 in Section 8.3 of
29
.

4.3
A concrete example under TVaR

This section presents a specific example of Problem (
16
) with
\rho_{g}(\cdot)=\mathrm{TVaR}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
. To ease our discussions, we assume that
S_{0}=S_{\mathbb{Q}}
, i.e., the DM applies the loss distribution under the insurer’s subjective belief as the benchmark distribution, and that
S_{\mathbb{Q}}
has continuous support over
[0,M]
. In practice, the confidence level
\alpha
for
\mathrm{TVaR}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
is set to be large (close to
1
). Hence, we focus on the case where
\frac{1}{1+\theta}>1-\alpha
in this example. To derive the worst-case distribution, we first need to compute
G^{*}(x;\beta)
, which involves calculating
\hat{G}(x;\beta)
and partitioning the domain
[0,M]
into the sub-domains
[0,x_{1})
,
\mathscr{A}_{1}
,
\mathscr{B}_{1}
,
[\overline{V_{\alpha}},x_{2})
,
\mathscr{A}_{2}
and
\mathscr{B}_{2}
.

After simple calculations, the right-hand derivative of the function
K(t;x)
is

K^{\prime}(t;x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\beta[\varphi^{\prime}(x)-\varphi^{\prime}(F_{\mathbb{Q}}^{-1}(1-t))],&\quad&t\geq 1-\alpha,\\
&\frac{1}{1-\alpha}-\beta[\varphi^{\prime}(x)-\varphi^{\prime}(F_{\mathbb{Q}}^{-1}(1-t))],&\quad&t<1-\alpha,\end{aligned}\right.

which yields

\hat{G}(x;\beta)=S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)\mathds{1}_{[0,\tilde{x}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{Q}})}(x)+(1-\alpha)\mathds{1}_{[\tilde{x}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{Q}},\hat{x})}(x)+\hat{S}_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)\mathds{1}_{[\hat{x},M]}(x),

where
\tilde{x}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}^{F_{\mathbb{Q}}}(X)
,
\hat{x}=[\varphi^{\prime}]^{-1}(\varphi^{\prime}(\tilde{x}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{Q}})+\frac{1}{\beta(1-\alpha)})
and
\hat{S}_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)=S_{\mathbb{Q}}([\varphi^{\prime}]^{-1}(\varphi^{\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{\beta(1-\alpha)}))
.

In our setting, the net price with
S_{0}=S_{\mathbb{Q}}
is
H(x;S_{\mathbb{Q}},\lambda)=(1+\lambda)(1+\theta)S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x)-g(S_{\mathbb{Q}}(x))-\lambda\mathds{1}_{[0,\overline{V_{\alpha}}]}(x)
. Figure
3
summarizes the three possible cases, which are elaborated as follows:

•

Case (i):
(1+\lambda)(1+\theta)<\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
.

This case is illustrated in the first sub-figure of Figure
3
. In this case, we have
H(x;S_{\mathbb{Q}},\lambda)<0

Concluding remarks

In this paper, we study two optimal insurance problems from a buyer’s perspective under distributional uncertainty. Unlike the existing literature that focuses on distance-based ambiguity sets of loss distributions, the BW ball is employed in this paper, where the BW divergence features asymmetry on penalizing deviations from the benchmark distribution. In the first problem, we model the DM’s ambiguity aversion using the conventional
\alpha
-maxmin model, with VaR serving as the risk measure that captures the DM’s risk attitude. We derive the optimal indemnity function for this model in its closed form and analyze how the asymmetry of BW divergence shapes the structure of the indemnity function analytically and numerically. In the second problem, we revisit the worst-case-based insurance problem under the convex distortion risk measure, but subject to a constraint imposed on the worst-case VaR of the DM’s terminal loss. We analytically derive the optimal indemnity function and the worst-case loss distribution, and examine their sensitivity to the acceptable worst-case VaR, offering economic interpretations of these relationships. Additional insights are illustrated through a concrete example involving TVaR.

Several promising avenues for future research emerge from this study. First, we note that the methodology for the guaranteed VaR performance model is still applicable when imposing multiple guaranteed VaR constraints, and there will be more parameters to optimize correspondingly. Developing an efficient algorithm for such double-layer optimization would make our model easier to implement in practice. Second, a natural theoretical extension to the guaranteed VaR performance model is to replace VaR with alternative risk measures in the constraint, which introduces significant complexity since the worst-case loss distribution for the constraint may become intertwined with the indemnity choice. Developing a methodology to resolve this issue could enable a broader framework that accommodates multiple risk measures and accounts for preference uncertainty when a finite set of preferences is available. These directions warrant further exploration.

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X<-x,\,Y>y)\,dx\,dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X<-x,\,Y<-y)\,dx\,dy.

Proof:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{-}Y^{-}]

\xi_{3}\in(0,\alpha)

Proof of Theorem

We first show

. Define the distribution function

\hat{F}^{-1}(t)=\begin{cases}\underline{V_{\alpha}},&t\in(F_{0}(\underline{V_{\alpha}}),\alpha],\\
F_{0}^{-1}(t),&t\notin(F_{0}(\underline{V_{\alpha}}),\alpha].\end{cases}

(A.4)

\hat{F}^{-1}

\hat{F}

\underline{V_{\alpha}}

\hat{F}\in\mathcal{B}(F_{0},\epsilon)
.

\underline{V_{\alpha}}\geq\inf_{F\in\mathcal{B}(F_{0},\epsilon)}\mathrm{VaR}^{F}_{\alpha}(X)
