Dual antibacterial and pro-healing effects of thymoquinone in an experimental infected diabetic wound model
Authors: Saffet Ulutas, Mehmet Mutaf, Sibel Cangi, Recep Dokuyucu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImpaired wound healing and persistent infection are major challenges in diabetic wounds. This study aimed to investigate the therapeutic effects of topical thymoquinone formulated in peanut oil on wound healing and bacterial burden in an infected diabetic wound model.MethodsDiabetes was induced in rats using streptozotocin, followed by creation of standardized full-thickness excisional wounds that were subsequently infected with Staphylococcus aureus. Animals were randomly assigned to four groups: untreated negative control, vehicle (peanut oil), thymoquinone, and positive control (topical mupirocin). Treatments were applied topically once daily for 14 days. Wound closure was assessed at predefined time points, bacterial load was quantified as log₁₀ CFU/g tissue, and histopathological evaluation was performed using semi-quantitative scoring of re-epithelialization, inflammatory cell infiltration, granulation tissue formation, and collagen deposition.ResultsMean wound closure rates in the thymoquinone group were 27.6 ± 6.1% on day 3, 55.1 ± 8.4% on day 7, 72.4 ± 7.5% on day 10, and 88.2 ± 5.6% on day 14, which were significantly higher than those observed in the untreated negative control group (12.4 ± 4.6%, 28.7 ± 6.9%, 41.9 ± 8.1%, and 58.6 ± 9.3%, respectively) and the vehicle (peanut oil) group (18.9 ± 5.2%, 36.4 ± 7.3%, 52.7 ± 8.6%, and 69.8 ± 8.9%, respectively; p = 0.004 on day 3, p = 0.002 on day 7, p = 0.001 on day 10, and p = 0.001 on day 14), while remaining comparable to the positive control (mupirocin) group without demonstrating statistical superiority. Mean bacterial counts were 5.94 ± 0.46 log₁₀ CFU/g on day 3 and 4.73 ± 0.51 log₁₀ CFU/g on day 7 in the thymoquinone group, compared with 7.62 ± 0.41 and 6.89 ± 0.44 log₁₀ CFU/g in the negative control group and 7.18 ± 0.38 and 6.32 ± 0.40 log₁₀ CFU/g in the peanut oil group (p = 0.003 on day 3 and p = 0.002 on day 7). Histopathological evaluation revealed significantly improved re-epithelialization (3.8 ± 0.4 vs. 1.6 ± 0.5 in controls, p = 0.002), increased granulation tissue formation (4.0 ± 0.5 vs. 1.8 ± 0.6, p = 0.003), and enhanced collagen deposition (3.9 ± 0.4 vs. 1.5 ± 0.5, p = 0.002), together with reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (1.9 ± 0.4 vs. 4.1 ± 0.4, p = 0.001) in thymoquinone-treated wounds compared with controls.ConclusionTopical thymoquinone formulated in peanut oil improved wound closure, reduced bacterial burden, and enhanced histopathological indicators of tissue repair in this experimental infected diabetic wound model. Further studies are warranted to confirm these findings and explore their translational relevance.Keywords: Thymoquinone, Diabetic wound, Wound healing, Bacterial burden, Staphylococcus aureus, Histopathology

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus profoundly impairs cutaneous wound healing through microvascular dysfunction, neuropathy, immune dysregulation, and a persistently pro-inflammatory tissue environment, leading to chronic non-healing ulcers such as diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs). These wounds frequently fail to progress beyond the inflammatory phase, resulting in defective re-epithelialization, impaired angiogenesis, and abnormal extracellular matrix remodeling. Superimposed infection further exacerbates tissue injury and sustains inflammation, thereby promoting chronicity. In this context, biofilm formation is a critical pathological feature, as it protects microorganisms from host immunity and antimicrobial therapies and is a major driver of treatment failure in diabetic wounds [1, 2]. Staphylococcus aureus is among the most prevalent pathogens in DFUs and a potent biofilm former that perpetuates inflammation and disrupts granulation tissue formation. Accordingly, effective management of infected diabetic wounds increasingly requires adjunctive therapeutic strategies that simultaneously reduce microbial burden, including biofilms, and restore dysregulated healing responses [3–5].

To support translational investigation, infected diabetic wound animal models have been refined to recapitulate key clinical features such as persistent inflammation and bacterial colonization, enabling controlled evaluation of topical interventions. Standardized S. aureus–infected diabetic rodent models are now widely used to assess therapies that integrate antimicrobial efficacy with pro-healing effects, reflecting the dual clinical imperative of infection control and tissue regeneration [6–9].

Thymoquinone (TQ), the principal bioactive component of Nigella sativa, has attracted increasing attention due to its multifaceted biological activities relevant to wound healing [10–13]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that TQ exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, modulates cytokine signaling, promotes fibroblast proliferation, enhances collagen synthesis, and supports angiogenesis [12–17]. In addition to its pro-healing properties, TQ has also shown antimicrobial activity against clinically relevant pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, with potential effects on bacterial viability and virulence mechanisms [18–20]. These combined properties provide a rationale for investigating TQ in infection-complicated diabetic wounds, where impaired tissue repair and persistent microbial burden coexist.

Beyond planktonic bacterial killing, antibiofilm activity is particularly attractive for chronic diabetic wounds because biofilms are a recognized driver of recalcitrance. A well-cited review on plant-derived antibiofilm agents discusses essential oils and related compounds as sources of antibiofilm activity and specifically notes thymoquinone in the context of antibiofilm approaches against S. aureus [21]. Given that biofilms can shield microbes from immune clearance and antibiotics, a topical intervention with both antibiofilm and immunomodulatory properties may influence wound microenvironment dynamics and therefore warrants experimental investigation [2, 21].

Formulation is a critical determinant of whether thymoquinone’s promising pharmacology translates into meaningful effects in vivo. As a lipophilic compound, thymoquinone often requires an appropriate carrier system to support solubilization, stability, and adequate tissue exposure. Recent formulation studies in diabetic wound contexts have explored advanced delivery systems—such as nanoemulgel-based approaches—to enhance thymoquinone’s local bioavailability while leveraging its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and pro-angiogenic potential. A 2024 study evaluating a thymoquinone-loaded nanoemulgel in a streptozotocin-induced diabetic wound setting reported favorable effects consistent with enhanced healing biology [10]. While such delivery platforms are technologically appealing, simpler oil-based carriers remain widely used in preclinical topical research because they are accessible, scalable, and can facilitate local delivery of lipophilic molecules.

Within this framework, peanut oil represents a pragmatic lipophilic vehicle candidate for topical thymoquinone delivery. However, oil-based carriers are not necessarily biologically inert; they can alter wound hydration, barrier function, and local inflammatory tone, potentially affecting healing outcomes [22–24]. Therefore, rigorous experimental designs must distinguish the therapeutic contribution of thymoquinone from that of the vehicle by including an appropriate oil-only control arm, alongside standard-of-care comparators. This is particularly important in an infected diabetic wound model, where microbial endpoints (e.g., colony counts) should be interpreted together with healing kinetics and histopathology to avoid conflating reduced exudate or improved moisture balance with true antimicrobial or tissue-regenerative effects [25, 26].

Experimental studies provide strong evidence supporting the role of thymoquinone in wound repair and infection control. Thymoquinone has been shown to modulate inflammatory cytokines, reduce oxidative stress, promote fibroblast proliferation, and enhance collagen synthesis, thereby supporting key processes in tissue repair [12, 14, 15, 27]. In animal models, it accelerates re-epithelialization and improves granulation tissue formation and collagen deposition [12, 26, 28]. In addition to these pro-healing effects, thymoquinone exhibits antimicrobial activity against pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, including effects on bacterial virulence and biofilm formation [18, 19, 21]. Given its lipophilic nature, topical delivery in an oil-based vehicle may enhance local bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy [16].

Accordingly, the present study investigated the therapeutic effects of thymoquinone formulated in peanut oil in an infected diabetic wound model by evaluating infection control, assessed through bacterial burden, and wound repair, assessed through wound closure dynamics and histopathological healing features. By integrating validated infected diabetic wound modeling practices with a phytochemical candidate supported by contemporary mechanistic and antimicrobial evidence, this study aimed to determine whether thymoquinone provided additive benefits beyond those attributable to the vehicle alone in the challenging setting of infection-complicated diabetic wounds.

Materials and methods

Animals and induction of diabetes mellitus

Male Wistar albino rats (250–300 g, 8–10 weeks old) were obtained from the institutional animal facility and housed under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. The study protocol was approved by the Mustafa Kemal University Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2024-05/7, Date: 10/06/2024), and all experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Experimental diabetes was induced by a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ; 55 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) freshly dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 4.5), following an overnight fast, as previously described [29, 30]. Blood glucose levels were measured from tail vein samples using a glucometer 72 h after STZ administration. Rats with fasting blood glucose levels ≥ 250 mg/dL were considered diabetic and included in the study.

All animals were monitored daily for general health status and signs of distress. Humane endpoints were predefined according to institutional animal care guidelines and included severe weight loss (> 20% of baseline body weight), persistent lethargy, inability to access food or water, severe wound infection with systemic signs, or uncontrolled hyperglycemia associated with marked deterioration in general condition. Animals meeting any of these criteria were humanely euthanized before the scheduled study endpoint. At the end of the experimental period (day 14), animals were euthanized under deep anesthesia using an overdose of ketamine (200 mg/kg) and xylazine (20 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiration, lack of heartbeat, and absence of corneal and pedal reflexes before tissue collection. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the institutional animal ethics committee approval.

Excisional wound creation

After confirmation of diabetes and a stabilization period of 7 days, animals were anesthetized with ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) intraperitoneally. The dorsal surface was shaved and disinfected with 70% ethanol. A full-thickness excisional wound (10 mm in diameter) was created on the dorsum using a sterile biopsy punch, extending through the panniculus carnosus, as described in established diabetic wound models [7, 8] (Fig. 1).

Experimental infected diabetic wound model

Bacterial inoculation and infected wound model

Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and used throughout the study as a standardized reference strain. The bacteria were cultured overnight in tryptic soy broth at 37 °C under aerobic conditions before preparation of the inoculum. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to approximately 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL using spectrophotometric calibration. The use of a well-characterized reference strain ensured reproducibility and allowed reliable interpretation of antimicrobial responses, including the activity of mupirocin in the positive control group. Immediately after wound creation, 50 µL of the bacterial suspension (5 × 10⁶ CFU) was applied directly to the wound bed to establish infection, consistent with validated infected diabetic wound protocols [7–9]. Wounds were left uncovered for 30 min to allow bacterial adherence. Infection establishment was based on standardized inoculum concentration and previously validated infected wound protocols. No additional pre-treatment microbiological confirmation (e.g., swab or tissue sampling) was performed prior to treatment initiation.

Thymoquinone (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in pharmaceutical-grade peanut oil to obtain a final concentration of 1% (w/v). This concentration was selected based on previous in vivo experimental studies reporting therapeutic effects of thymoquinone in wound healing models, as well as its physicochemical compatibility with topical oil-based formulations [12, 16]. However, no dose-ranging analysis was performed, and therefore the selected concentration should be considered exploratory.

Animals were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 8 per group) using a computer-generated simple randomization method:

Treatments were applied topically once daily for 14 consecutive days under sterile conditions.

Wound area measurement

Digital photographs of wounds were obtained on days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14 using a standardized camera setup with a fixed distance and scale reference. Wound areas were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA), and wound closure was expressed as a percentage of the initial wound area, as previously described [7, 8]. Wound area measurements using ImageJ were performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation to minimize measurement bias.

Microbiological analysis

On days 3 and 7, wound tissue samples were collected under sterile conditions for quantitative bacteriology. Tissues were homogenized in sterile phosphate-buffered saline, serially diluted, and plated on tryptic soy agar. Colony-forming units (CFU) were counted after 24 h incubation at 37 °C and expressed as CFU per gram of tissue (CFU/g) [7, 9]. Microbiological sampling was limited to days 3 and 7 to capture the early and mid-phase antibacterial effects of the interventions, which are critical periods for bacterial colonization and infection control in this model. Terminal microbiological assessment at day 14 was not performed in order to preserve tissue integrity for comprehensive histopathological evaluation. Microbiological assessments, including colony counting, were conducted by a laboratory investigator blinded to group allocation.

Histopathological evaluation

On day 14, animals were euthanized, and wound tissues were excised and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. Paraffin-embedded Sect. (5 μm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome. Histological evaluation was performed by an experienced pathologist who was blinded to group allocation and treatment conditions. Histopathological scoring was performed using a semi-quantitative scale ranging from 0 to 4, where 0 indicated absence and 4 indicated the highest degree of the evaluated parameter. The same scoring system was applied consistently across all parameters, including re-epithelialization, inflammatory cell infiltration, granulation tissue formation, and collagen deposition. Specifically, for re-epithelialization, scores were defined as follows: 0 = no epithelialization; 1 = minimal epithelial migration; 2 = partial epithelial coverage; 3 = near-complete epithelialization; 4 = complete epithelial coverage. For inflammatory cell infiltration, scores were defined as: 0 = no inflammatory cells; 1 = minimal infiltration; 2 = mild infiltration; 3 = moderate infiltration; 4 = dense inflammatory infiltration. For granulation tissue formation: 0 = absent; 1 = minimal; 2 = mild; 3 = moderate; 4 = well-developed granulation tissue. For collagen deposition: 0 = absent; 1 = minimal; 2 = mild; 3 = moderate; 4 = dense and well-organized collagen fibers. This scoring system was adapted from previously validated histopathological assessment methods reported in the literature [8, 31, 32].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, whereas non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Repeated wound area measurements were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Time-dependent wound closure in infected diabetic wounds

Quantitative bacterial counts in wound tissue were shown in Table 4. On day 3 after wound creation, a significant reduction in bacterial burden was observed among the experimental groups, with mean bacterial counts of 7.62 ± 0.41 log₁₀ CFU/g in the negative control group, 7.18 ± 0.38 log₁₀ CFU/g in the peanut oil group, 5.94 ± 0.46 log₁₀ CFU/g in the thymoquinone group, and 5.71 ± 0.43 log₁₀ CFU/g in the positive control group. Intergroup comparison revealed a statistically significant difference at this time point (p = 0.003). By day 7, bacterial loads decreased in all groups; however, the reduction was markedly greater in the thymoquinone and positive control groups compared with the negative control and peanut oil groups. This difference remained statistically significant (p = 0.002). No statistically significant difference was observed between the thymoquinone and positive control (mupirocin) groups at either time point (day 3 and day 7; p > 0.05), indicating comparable antibacterial efficacy (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this infected diabetic wound model, TQ formulated in peanut oil produced a consistent dual benefit that it accelerated wound closure over time and reduced bacterial burden at both microbiological sampling points, while also improving key histological healing domains (re-epithelialization, granulation tissue, and collagen deposition) and decreasing inflammatory cell infiltration. Importantly, the presence of a vehicle-only arm demonstrated that peanut oil exerted only modest effects compared with the active compound, supporting an additive benefit beyond vehicle.

Importantly, although the therapeutic effects observed in the thymoquinone group were comparable to those achieved with the positive control (mupirocin) across multiple outcome measures, statistical superiority over the standard treatment was not demonstrated. In particular, wound closure rates and bacterial burden reduction showed closely aligned values between these two groups without significant intergroup differences. These findings indicate that thymoquinone produced antibacterial and wound-healing effects comparable to those observed in the positive control group within this experimental model. However, as this study was not designed as a non-inferiority trial, these results should be interpreted cautiously. The findings indicate that thymoquinone demonstrated both antibacterial and wound-healing effects in this experimental model; however, further studies are required before any therapeutic role can be established.

Chronic diabetic wounds fail to transition efficiently from inflammation to proliferation and remodeling, leading to delayed re-epithelialization, impaired angiogenesis, and dysfunctional matrix remodeling. Wilkinson et al. summarized how persistent inflammatory signaling and altered cellular responses are central drivers of pathological healing, providing a mechanistic framework for why diabetic wounds are particularly vulnerable to chronicity [15]. In parallel, biofilm biology further complicates infected diabetic wounds by shielding bacteria from host immunity and antimicrobials and by sustaining a pro-inflammatory wound microenvironment. Wu et al. emphasized that chronic wound biofilms are a major contributor to delayed healing and treatment failure, and Afonso et al. highlighted biofilms specifically in diabetic foot ulcers as drivers of chronic inflammation and poor responsiveness to therapy [2, 4]. Astrada et al. further noted that biofilms may be present even without overt infection signs, underscoring why therapies that act on both microbial load and host healing are attractive in this setting [1].

Our experimental approach aligns with contemporary efforts to standardize infected diabetic wound models to enable robust testing of topical therapeutics. Mendes et al. reported an optimized diabetic model of MRSA-infected wounds that recapitulates key clinical features such as persistent inflammation and impaired healing, emphasizing the importance of reproducible inoculation and outcome measures [7]. Huon et al. similarly described a murine model of S. aureus infected chronic diabetic wounds designed to evaluate alternative therapeutics [8]. Within this modeling context, our data show that TQ improved healing kinetics and tissue repair quality while lowering bacterial counts, supporting the idea that a successful intervention in infected diabetic wounds should be evaluated through both healing and infection endpoints rather than either alone.

Kmail et al. synthesized mechanistic evidence that TQ can influence multiple stages of wound repair, including modulation of inflammatory cytokines, antioxidant defenses, angiogenesis-related signaling, fibroblast activity, and collagen deposition [12]. Our histopathological profile—higher re-epithelialization and collagen deposition with lower inflammatory infiltration in the TQ group—maps well onto those proposed mechanisms and indicates that TQ likely supports the transition from an inflammatory to a reparative wound environment.

Formulation and delivery are also central to TQ performance in vivo because TQ is lipophilic and local exposure can be formulation-limited. Anuradha et al. reported favorable diabetic wound healing effects using a TQ-loaded nanoemulgel system, consistent with the view that local delivery strategies can enhance TQ’s biological impact [26]. While our formulation was intentionally simpler (oil-based), our results similarly support that topical TQ can meaningfully shift healing outcomes in diabetic wounds, including under infectious pressure.

A key strength of the present study is the demonstration of significant bacterial load reduction in the TQ group at both day 3 and day 7. This finding is biologically plausible given the expanding antimicrobial literature for Nigella sativa bioactives. Abbas et al. reviewed antimicrobial properties of Nigella sativa constituents and highlighted broad therapeutic potential for infection-relevant contexts [19]. More specifically, Ahmad et al. showed that TQ can impair S. aureus NorA efflux pump activity and modulate efflux-associated tolerance pathways, a mechanism that may contribute to reduced bacterial persistence and improved infection control [18].

Because biofilms contribute strongly to persistence in chronic wounds, antibiofilm context is also relevant. Bazargani et al. discussed antibiofilm activity of essential oils and plant extracts against S. aureus biofilms, supporting the general rationale for testing plant-derived compounds in biofilm-prone infections [21]. Although our study did not directly quantify biofilm biomass or biofilm-associated markers, the observed reduction in CFU alongside improved histology is consistent with an intervention that lowers bacterial burden sufficiently to reduce inflammation and allow productive tissue repair.

Oil-based vehicles are often used to dissolve lipophilic compounds and to improve local contact with the wound bed. However, oils are not always biologically inert because they can modify wound hydration, barrier properties, and local inflammatory tone. The presence of a peanut-oil–only group therefore strengthens interpretability by showing that the magnitude of improvement in the TQ group exceeded the modest vehicle-associated effects observed in wound closure, bacterial burden, and histopathology. This design choice is particularly important in infected diabetic wounds where reduced exudate or improved moisture balance could otherwise be mistaken for antimicrobial efficacy.

In clinical DFU care, the “dual imperative” is controlling infection while enabling healing. Ramirez-Acuña et al. reviewed emerging DFU therapies and emphasized the breadth of antimicrobial and regenerative approaches being explored, reflecting the need for adjunctive strategies beyond standard antibiotics in complex wounds [6]. Against that backdrop, our findings demonstrate that topical TQ was associated with antibacterial and pro-healing effects in this preclinical model. Nevertheless, translation would require additional work to define optimal dosing, safety/irritation thresholds, formulation stability, and effectiveness across different pathogens and polymicrobial/biofilm-dominant infections.

Limitations

The major strengths of this study included an infected diabetic wound design with reproducible outcomes, consistent with established infected-wound model methodology, the inclusion of both a vehicle-only arm and a positive control to contextualize effect size, and the integration of clinically meaningful endpoints across wound closure, quantitative bacteriology, and histopathology.

Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, infection was induced using a single S. aureus strain, whereas diabetic foot ulcers are typically polymicrobial and often biofilm-dominant; future studies should include polymicrobial inocula and direct biofilm quantification. Second, mechanistic biomarkers (e.g., oxidative stress markers, cytokine panels, and angiogenesis markers such as VEGF/CD31) were not assessed, limiting the ability to determine whether the observed healing effects were primarily driven by antimicrobial activity, immunomodulation, or both. Third, the sample size was modest (n = 8 per group) and outcomes were assessed only up to day 14; longer follow-up would allow evaluation of remodeling-stage healing and scar quality. In addition, only a single concentration of thymoquinone (1% w/v) was evaluated without dose–response or pharmacokinetic analysis, and therefore the dosing strategy should be considered exploratory. The absence of microbiological assessment at the study endpoint (day 14) further limits interpretation, as the infection status at the time of histopathological evaluation remains uncertain. Moreover, infection establishment was not microbiologically confirmed prior to treatment initiation, introducing the possibility of variability in baseline infection status among animals. Future studies incorporating dose–response analysis, pharmacokinetic assessment, early infection verification, and endpoint microbiological sampling would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between bacterial clearance and tissue repair. Finally, although only one formulation was tested, comparison with alternative delivery systems may help define optimal therapeutic strategies. The use of a standardized and explicitly defined semi-quantitative scoring system strengthens the reproducibility and interpretability of the histopathological findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that topical thymoquinone formulated in peanut oil was associated with improved healing outcomes in an infected diabetic wound model. Thymoquinone treatment accelerated wound closure, reduced bacterial burden, and enhanced histopathological indicators of tissue repair, including re-epithelialization, granulation tissue formation, and collagen deposition, while attenuating inflammatory cell infiltration. Importantly, these beneficial effects exceeded those observed with the vehicle alone and were comparable to the positive control treatment. However, the absence of endpoint microbiological data should be considered when interpreting the antibacterial efficacy at later stages of healing. Collectively, these findings indicate that thymoquinone was associated with reduced bacterial burden and improved healing-related outcomes in this experimental infected diabetic wound model. Further studies incorporating mechanistic biomarkers, biofilm-specific endpoints, and alternative formulations are warranted to clarify its therapeutic potential and translational relevance.
