Delayed intubation and 60-day mortality in severe COVID-19-associated acute respiratory failure in an emulated target trial using the OUTCOMEREA network
Authors: Mathilde Phillips-Houlbracq, Mathilde Neuville, Karl Bitar, Shidasp Siami, Yves Cohen, Virginie Laurent, Bruno Mourvillier, Jean Reignier, Dany Goldgran-Toledano, Carole Schwebel, Stéphane Ruckly, Etienne de Montmollin, Niccolo Buetti, Charles Cerf, Jean-François Timsit, Claire Dupuis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The benefits of non-invasive respiratory support strategies (NIRS), such as high-flow nasal oxygen therapy, in delaying intubation remain uncertain. We used an emulated target trial approach to evaluate outcomes associated with late intubation in patients with severe acute respiratory failure due to COVID-19. We conducted a retrospective multicentre cohort study using the French prospective OUTCOMEREA database. Adult patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) for severe SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia with an ICU length of stay of at least seven days were included in the study. Descriptive analyses were used to compare patients who were intubated after day 6 with those who remained under NIRS. In the emulated target trial, patients who were eligible between ICU days 7 and 12 were assigned to undergo late intubation or to remain under NIRS. Weighted Cox proportional hazards models were used. The primary outcome was 60-day mortality. Of the 1206 ICU patients with SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia, 288 were still in the ICU on day 7. Of these, 65 (22.6%) subsequently underwent late intubation and had a 60-day mortality rate of 78.5%. In the emulated target trial, which included 234 patients at risk of intubation, 57 underwent late intubation. Late intubation was associated with a higher risk of death (adjusted hazard ratio: 2.89; 95% confidence interval: 1.50–3.92). The estimated absolute difference in 60-day survival was − 0.34 (95% CI − 0.62 to 0.30). The restricted mean survival time was 17.8 days shorter in the late intubation group (95% CI − 30.6 to 10.8). In this multicentre cohort, patients requiring late intubation had a very high mortality rate. In the emulated target trial analysis, late intubation was associated with poorer survival compared to continued NIRS, suggesting a subgroup of patients with progressive respiratory failure rather than a direct effect of intubation timing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-47102-8.

Introduction

In almost 20% of cases of Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pneumonia1, people presented with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure (ARF). Among them, around 39% were admitted to an Intensive Care Unit (ICU), where reported mortality ranged from 26 to 80%2–4.

During the first wave, most of the patients with ARF were intubated immediately upon ICU admission (early intubation), leading to prolonged ventilation duration and a high rate of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and poor prognosis3,5,6. During subsequent waves, however, the prognosis for ICU patients significantly improved3,7, partly thanks to the use of corticosteroids8,9. Furthermore, strategies to delay or avoid intubation, such as non-invasive respiratory support (NIRS) strategies (e.g. High Flow Nasal Oxygen (HFNO), Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP), and Non-Invasive Ventilation (NIV)) were used more frequently10–12. These strategies not only provided oxygen support for numerous patients inside and outside ICUs but also allowed clinicians to delay and even avoid intubation and associated complications in some cases. However, NIRS were most of the time associated with delayed intubation but not with a decrease in mortality rate12–15. Moreover, delayed intubation is also thought to potentially cause harm due to Patient-Self Induced Lung Injury (P-SILI)16. Cohort studies eventually showed that NIRS strategies failed in 30–45% of cases, leading to intubation12,17.

Consequently, the optimal timing and indications for invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) in COVID-19-related ARF remain debated. Several studies have explored the relationship between intubation timing and outcomes in COVID-19 ARF18. Delays between hospital admission and intubation have been associated with increased ICU mortality19–23. In a prospective cohort of 205 critically ill COVID-19 patients requiring IMV, intubation performed more than 48 h after the initiation of respiratory support was associated with increased hospital mortality and worse pulmonary sequelae during follow-up, including a significantly lower diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), a greater number of affected lung lobes, and higher chest CT total severity scores24. Similarly, a French cohort study of 574 patients reported an 87% mortality rate among those intubated after day 725. These findings raise questions about the potential futility of very late intubation in this population.

Against this backdrop, our study aimed to evaluate the prognosis of the patients intubated lately, after NIRS strategy failure in the ICU, using an emulated target trial analysis.

Materials and methods

Data source

We conducted a retrospective target trial emulation using prospectively collected data from the French multicenter OUTCOMEREA database, in which clinical data are recorded prospectively in participating ICUs. This database contains detailed clinical information from 11 ICUs across France that participate in the OUTCOMEREA network. Methods for data collection and validation have been previously described26. The database has been approved by both the French Advisory Committee for Data Processing in Health Research (CCTIRS) and the French National Commission for Information Technologies and Civil Liberties (CNIL; registration no. 8999262). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital (IRB no. 5891), with a waiver of informed consent, as per French law. The investigators did not have access to any directly identifying participant information. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study population

Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) admitted to one of the participating ICUs for severe COVID-19 pneumonia between March 1, 2020, and August 31, 2022, with diagnosis confirmed by a positive RT-PCR test. Inclusion required an ICU length of stay (LOS) of at least 7 days. Patients were excluded if they were transferred from another ICU or if a decision had been made to withhold or withdraw life-sustaining treatment within the first six days after ICU admission.

Data collection

Data were extracted on January 1, 2023. Variables were prospectively collected and included patient demographics, comorbidities (as per the Knaus scale27), and baseline severity indexes: SAPS II (Simplified Acute Physiology score)28 and SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment Score) score29. Treatments administered at admission were recorded, including corticosteroids, interleukin-6 (IL-6) or IL-1 receptor antagonists, and previous antibiotic or antiviral treatments. Several variables were recorded throughout the ICU stay, including clinical and biological parameters, the requirement for NIRS and IMV, and other forms of organ support, such as vasopressors, renal replacement therapy (RRT) and Extracorporeal life support (ECLS). Routinely recorded outcome measures included the occurrence of hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP), VAP and ICU bloodstream infection (ICU-BSI), ICU and hospital LOS, and vital status during the ICU stay, at hospital discharge, and at day 60 after ICU admission. The presence or absence of HAP-VAP and ICU-BSI was documented according to the standard definitions developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention30, and COVID-19-associated invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (CAPA) according to the guidelines31.

Definitions, group assignment, and respiratory support modalities

We defined patients at risk of intubation as ‘severe NIRS’ (s-NIRS) patients. These were patients treated with NIRS for a severe ARF. Eligibility criteria for the emulated trial

Eligible patients were those who were still in the ICU on day 7 and had not been intubated before day 6; or those who were still in the ICU on day 8 and had not been intubated before day 7, and so on from day 8 to day 12.

On each of the days from day 7 to day 12, eligible patients who had not previously been intubated were assigned to one of the following groups:Late oro-tracheal intubation (Late-OTI) group: intubated on that day, orNon-late-OTI group (control group): not intubated on that day.

Late oro-tracheal intubation (Late-OTI) group: intubated on that day, or

Late oro-tracheal intubation (Late-OTI) group: intubated on that day, or

Non-late-OTI group (control group): not intubated on that day.

Non-late-OTI group (control group): not intubated on that day.

Patients could be included in multiple “daily trials” as long as they remained eligible and not intubated. Patients in the non-late-OTI group who were subsequently intubated were censored the day before intubation, in order to maintain adherence to the comparison strategies. The final analysis dataset pooled observations across these six sequential daily sequential trials.

Endpoints and estimands

The primary endpoint was all-cause 60-day mortality. Survival time was calculated from the day on which the eligibility criteria were met until death, with administrative censoring at 60 days. The primary estimand was the marginal survival probability at 60 days under each treatment strategy (late-OTI and non-late-OTI). The secondary estimand was the hazard ratio (HR) for death associated with late-OTI, conditional on baseline characteristics and variables on the time of inclusion.

Analysis using sequential trials approach: data set-up

Data from the OUTCOMEREA database were used to emulate the target trial. The estimands specified could be estimated using observational data under the following assumptions: (1) conditional exchangeability, meaning that after adjustment for measured confounders the timing of intubation is independent of potential outcomes; (2) positivity, requiring that all eligible patients have a non-zero probability of receiving each strategy; (3) consistency, whereby the observed outcomes correspond to the potential outcomes under the assigned strategy; and (4) no interference, assuming that the treatment received by one patient does not affect the outcomes of another34.

Eligibility criteria were checked daily from day 7 to day 12 for each patient. Day 7 denoted the first day. An “emulated trial” dataset was created for each day between day 7 and day 12, as follows. Among patients who met the eligibility criteria on day 7 (s-NIRS), those who were intubated followed treatment strategy (i) and were referred to as the late-OTI group. The remaining patients who were not intubated on day 7 followed treatment strategy (ii) on that day and were referred to as the non-late-OTI group. Some individuals in the non-late-OTI group on day 7 were subsequently intubated, therefore deviating from treatment strategy (ii). They were artificially censored the day before intubation. This process was repeated for each subsequent day from day 8 to day 12 for patients who were still alive, met the eligibility criteria and had not yet been intubated. A given patient could be included in the non-late-OTI group in several trials but could only be included in the late-OTI group once. Days 7–12 were called landmark time points. The final analysis dataset (referred to as the “pooled” dataset) was obtained by pooling the data from the six sequential trials.

Analysis using sequential trials approach: estimation

To estimate the effect of late-OTI use on survival, we needed to account for confounding factors in this association. We also needed to account for the dependent censoring created by our artificial censoring of patients in the control group at the start of a given trial who deviated from the treatment strategy (ii) when intubated. These were respectively handled in the analysis by adjustment for confounders measured at the start of each “trial” and by use of inverse probability of censoring (IPC) weights.

The following covariates were prespecified based on clinical judgement and prior knowledge, using a directed acyclic graph (DAGITTY v3.1 software)35 (Fig. S1): age, sex, inclusion period (before or after August 30, 2020), body mass index (< or ≥ 30 kg/m2), time from first symptoms to ICU admission (< or ≥ 10 days), diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular chronic disease, respiratory chronic disease, renal chronic disease, immunodeficiency, pneumonia on admission, vasopressors, RRT, steroids, remdesivir, tocilizumab, SOFA score on admission > 5, SOFA score the day before inclusion > 5, respiratory rate the day before inclusion > 30. Further details regarding the specification of confounders and weight models can be found in the online data Supplement. No automated variable selection procedures were used; all the covariates identified through the causal framework were included in the adjustment models.

The analysis used a Cox regression fitted to the pooled dataset, using the IPC weights (see below). The model included late-OTI status at the start of the trial (i.e., late-OTI or non-late-OTI group) as a covariate, plus all covariate measures (as recorded at the start of each trial for time-varying covariates). The coefficient for late-OTI status was assumed to be identical across the six trials. A smooth plot of the Schoenfeld residuals was used to select and appropriate functional form for the log HR over time.

IPC weighting was employed to account for the artificial informative censoring of patients in the control group who initiated late-OTI at a later stage of follow-up (‘protocol deviation’). Cox regression was employed to calculate the denominator of the weights, representing the estimated probability of not being censored up to a specific follow-up time, conditional on patient characteristics at each landmark time point and during ICU follow-up. This calculation used the same set of covariates as in the primary analysis. The weights were stabilised using the estimated probability of not being censored up to a given time, obtained from the Kaplan–Meier estimator, in the numerator. Individual weights were estimated separately in each trial.

Marginal survival curves for the late-OTI and non-late-OTI groups were estimated from the regression coefficients, and the cumulative baseline hazard was estimated from the weighted Cox regression analysis. As the pooled dataset included several copies of the same individual that did not correspond to any well-defined population, survival curves were estimated using an evaluation cohort composed of unique individuals who met the eligibility criteria at some point during the study, with the covariates set to the values they had at the time they first met the eligibility criteria (which may have occurred on different days for different individuals). For each patient in the evaluation cohort, two sets of survival probabilities were estimated: one if they had initiated late-OTI, and one if they had not. We then calculated the empirical average of these predicted survival probabilities. Although our focus was on the time horizon of 60 days, we also repeated this process for each day of follow-up in order to create estimated marginal survival curves (population averages for the evaluation cohort) under the two treatment strategies. As the same individual may appear in more than one trial, model-based variance estimators were not appropriate due to the use of IPC weighting. Non-parametric bootstrapping was used to estimate 95% normal-based confidence intervals for marginal survival probabilities and HRs (with 300 bootstrap replications). We repeated the estimation of weights and the multivariable Cox model in each bootstrap sample.

Sensitivity analyses

We also considered emulated trials in which patients were considered to be at risk of OTI if they required either (1) HFNO and FiO2 ≥ 80% or 2) HFNO and FiO2 ≥ 60%.

We also conducted landmark analyses at days 7, 10 and 14. On day 7, we compared patients who were intubated on days 5–6 due to acute respiratory failure (OTI7) with patients with severe ARF under NIRS on days 5–6 (s-NIRS5). Similar analyses were performed at day 10, comparing intubation on days 7–9 (OTI10 vs. s-NIRS10), and at day 14, comparing intubation on days 10–13 (OTI14 vs. s-NIRS14).

Other statistical analysis considerations

Patient characteristics were expressed as n(%) for categorical variables and as median (interquartile range (IQR)) for continuous variables. Comparisons were made using exact Fisher’s tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon tests for continuous variables. The risk of death according to the timing of intubation since ICU admission and since the time of intubation was assessed using a Cox survival model.

For all tests, a two-sided α of 0.05 was considered significant. Missing baseline variables were handled through multiple imputation of a single dataset. Further details regarding the imputation method and bootstrap procedure can be found in the online Data Supplement. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (Version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R (Version 3.6.3).

Discussion

Among ICU patients with acute respiratory failure (ARF) due to SARS-CoV-2 infection, late intubation (after day 6) was associated with high mortality. Very late intubation (after day 14) was also associated with an increased risk of death. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that late intubation itself causes poor outcomes. Rather, late intubation may reflect a subgroup of patients experiencing progressive clinical deterioration despite non-invasive respiratory support. Although target trial emulation helps reduce immortal-time and selection biases, causal interpretation remains limited by the availability and quality of measured confounders.

These results warrant further discussion.

Comparison with previous studies

The poor outcomes experienced by patients who were intubated late have already been described, but only in few studies. In Camous et al.’s study, ICU mortality was 87% in patients intubated seven days or more after dexamethasone initiation, 49% in those intubated between days two and seven, and 54% in those intubated on the day of dexamethasone initiation (p < 0.001)25. Karagiannidis et al. reported an increase in mortality rates with the time between NIV initiation and failure: 32% in patients requiring OTI on day 1, compared to 72% in patients intubated on day 5 or later. The NIV failure rate in their cohort was 44%, and switching from initial NIV to IMV resulted in the longest periods of invasive ventilation36.

Together, these studies support the hypothesis that delayed escalation to IMV may be associated with poor outcomes in selected patients.

Potential mechanisms

Several mechanisms may explain the poor prognosis observed among patients intubated very late. One proposed explanation is P-SILI, a concept that has emerged in recent years as a potential mechanism of lung damage in spontaneously breathing patients with severe hypoxemia16. Persistent high respiratory effort during NIRS may aggravate lung injury and contribute to worse outcomes when intubation ultimately becomes necessary37–39. Due to the remarkable ‘happy hypoxaemia’40 in severe COVID-19 patients, respiratory distress symptoms may be absent under NIRS even after several days of severe hypoxaemia. This can potentially lead to P-SILI lesions and an adverse outcome following NIRS failure14.

Another explanation for this can be found in the increasing prevalence of infectious complications over time among IMV patients. We reported a high rate of CAPA among patients intubated later. This opportunistic infection is known to worsen patients’ prognoses41,42. The main risk factors of CAPA described in the literature are older patients, use of dexamethasone and prolonged ventilation, and these were also found in our cohort43. Similarly, VAP is also known to be associated with an increased risk of mortality44. In our cohort, the prevalence of VAP ranged from 23 to 44% among patients who were ventilated recently. HSV-1 reactivation was also frequently diagnosed in these patients and is associated with a higher prevalence of VAP and mortality in the literature45. All these nosocomial infections could have led to late intubation and could have played an important role in the very high mortality rates observed. Patients under NIRS were less likely to have invasive samples taken, and therefore their lower rate of nosocomial infections may be partly due to underdiagnosis.

Clinical interpretation

NIRS is being used increasingly to treat COVID-19 ARF, with encouraging results in avoiding intubation and its complications46–48. In ICU settings, clinicians closely monitor patients receiving NIRS and remain attentive to signs of respiratory deterioration, such as increased work of breathing, mental status changes, or clinical distress. The key clinical challenge is determining the optimal timing for intubation—avoiding both premature intubation and excessive delay. In our study, survival among patients intubated after day 10 was particularly poor, suggesting that these patients may already have reached an advanced stage of respiratory deterioration when intubation occurred. However, this observation should be interpreted cautiously, as the timing of intubation reflects clinical judgment and evolving patient severity rather than a randomized intervention.

Delayed intubation may also have implications for long-term pulmonary outcomes. Previous studies have reported that patients requiring IMV for COVID-19 may experience persistent lung abnormalities and impaired diffusing capacity (DLCO) months after ICU discharge, reflecting the severity of the initial lung injury24,39.

Strengths

Our study has several strengths. First, it relied on a large multicenter prospective database with high-quality data collection. Second, the use of target trial emulation combined with sequential landmark analyses helped reduce immortal-time bias associated with the timing of intubation. These methodological approaches strengthen the validity of the observed associations49.

Limitations

Our study also has several limitations. The definition of early versus late intubation remains debated, and the choice of seven days as the threshold was based on previous literature25. We compared late intubation with a group of patients receiving severe NIRS (s-NIRS), defined using an objective PaO2/FiO2 threshold; however, the decision to intubate in clinical practice is never based solely on oxygenation. Clinical signs of respiratory fatigue or neurological deterioration may prompt intubation independently of gas exchange parameters, and these variables were not available in our database.

Several time-varying markers of respiratory distress that could influence intubation decisions—such as work of breathing, ROX index, neurological status, or clinical signs of fatigue—were also unavailable. Consequently, residual confounding by unmeasured severity indicators is highly plausible. The estimated treatment effects from the emulated target trial therefore rely on measured covariates only and should be interpreted cautiously.

In addition, respiratory rate was recorded only once daily in our database, preventing the calculation of the ROX index. The sample size decreased substantially after day 7, limiting statistical power for very late intubation analyses. Long-term outcomes were not available in our dataset. Furthermore, patients were included over multiple pandemic waves, during which treatment strategies evolved, including the use of corticosteroids, remdesivir, tocilizumab, awake prone positioning, and vaccination, as well as the emergence of different viral variants.

We acknowledge that intubation was decided by physicians, and therefore comparison with non-intubated patients remains difficult to achieve retrospectively. Therefore, intubation should be considered a clinical decision reflecting deterioration rather than an independent exposure randomly assigned to patients. Indeed, some studies have shown that the intubation criteria used in clinical practice are very difficult to define, and that the criteria defined in clinical trials are rarely applied in routine clinical practice50–53. Several patients left the ICU under HFNO and could have undergone s-NIRS thereafter, and could therefore have been included in our study. To minimise the bias due to this competing risk, we performed several sensitivity analyses. Decisions to withhold or withdraw treatment may also have biased the results. All patients who had received such a decision before day 7 were excluded.

Conclusion

Our multicentre prospective study highlights the very high mortality rate among patients requiring intubation after receiving non-invasive respiratory support for more than seven days. Late intubation was associated with poorer outcomes, which may indicate a subgroup of patients with more severe or progressive respiratory failure, rather than reflecting a harmful effect of intubation itself. These findings emphasize the difficulty of identifying the optimal timing for invasive ventilation in patients with severe ARF due to SARS-CoV-2 infection, and must be confirmed in prospective studies that record time-varying clinical markers of respiratory deterioration.
