Preterm birth and birth weight extremes are associated with periodontal disease and tooth loss in the ARIC study
Authors: Maria Doughan, Eung-Kwon Pae, Lisa Yanek, Omar Chehab, Bassel Doughan, Kevin Moss, James D Beck, Ronald M Harper, Erin D Michos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate whether preterm birth (PTB) and extreme birth weights are associated with the prevalence of periodontal disease and tooth loss later in life, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 6152 adults (62.3 ± 5.6 years) from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study, who underwent a periodontal examination at Visit 4 (1996–1998). Preterm birth (PTB) (< 37 weeks) and birth weight categories (low birth weight (LBW) < 2.5 kg and high birth weight (HBW) > 4.0 kg) were self‑reported. Periodontal status was assessed via clinical examination and classified using the Periodontal Profile Class (PPC). Multivariable logistic regression models estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for prevalent periodontal disease (PD) and tooth loss, adjusting for sociodemographic, behavioral, and cardiovascular risk factors. LBW and PTB were associated with increased odds of PD (OR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.15–2.16 and OR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.02–2.27, respectively), but not with tooth loss. Conversely, HBW was not associated with PD but was linked to greater odds of tooth loss (OR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.06–2.07). Adverse birth outcomes are associated with periodontal health in later life, supporting life-course approaches to risk assessment and prevention in both oral and systemic health.Keywords: Preterm birth, Birth weight, Periodontal disease, Tooth loss, EpidemiologySubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Preterm birth (PTB), defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation, and low birth weight (LBW), defined as a birth weight (BW) below 2500 g, are among the most common adverse birth outcomes worldwide with lifelong implications. In the United States (U.S.), approximately 10.4% of infants are born preterm, and 9% with LBW annually1,2 with rates increasing in recent years3. At the opposite end of the BW spectrum, high birth weight (HBW, ≥ 4000 g) affects approximately 7.8% of U.S. infants annually4. While the immediate perinatal complications of these birth outcomes are well documented, growing evidence indicates that these adverse early-life exposures are associated with long-term disturbances in skeletal, cardiovascular, metabolic, and immune function5–8.

Individuals born preterm or with LBW often experience impaired bone mineralization, reduced peak bone mass, and an increased risk of osteopenia and osteoporosis later in life5,9. This includes a greater incidence of childhood fractures among those born prematurely10. LBW is also associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and elevated risk of hypertension and ischemic heart disease in adulthood11,12. Conversely, HBW is associated with altered metabolic programming, with increased risk of obesity, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome in childhood and adulthood13. HBW infants may exhibit differences in bone mineralization and density compared to normal BW infants14. HBW can alter enamel formation and mineralization during development15, and recent research has identified HBW as a risk factor for early childhood caries and other oral health issues16.

Together, these findings support the role of BW extremes, both low and high, as well as PTB, as early determinants of chronic inflammatory conditions, which aligns with the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis. This framework proposes that early environmental exposures can program physiological systems, predisposing individuals to chronic diseases later in life17.

Periodontitis, a prevalent chronic inflammatory oral disease, is characterized by the progressive destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone18, affecting 42.2% of adults aged 30 and older and nearly 60% of those over 65 in the U.S.19,20. The disease is caused by a dysregulated host immune response to oral microbial biofilms influenced by behavioral, genetic, and environmental factors. Beyond its local effects, periodontitis has been increasingly recognized for its role in systemic inflammation and its association with broader health outcomes, such as diabetes, metabolic syndrome, osteoporosis, and cardiovascular disease (CVD)21–25.

The chronic inflammatory nature of periodontitis may be associated with metabolic and prenatal programming, whether from growth restriction, excessive fetal growth, or preterm delivery26. Extensive evidence links maternal PD to an increased risk of PTB and LBW, underscoring the influence of maternal oral health on adverse pregnancy outcomes and early development27,28. However, the later-life oral health consequences of having been born prematurely and/or with abnormal BW remain poorly understood. Clarifying these associations may inform risk stratification and prevention. The objective of this study was to examine whether PTB, LBW, and HBW are associated with the prevalence of PD and tooth loss in adulthood, using the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study cohort. To our knowledge, this is a unique study that aims to comprehensively examine both BW extremes and prematurity in relation to adult periodontal outcomes in a large, community-based cohort. We hypothesized that individuals born with LBW or HBW, as well as those born preterm, may have a higher prevalence of PD and tooth loss in adulthood, independent of conventional sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical risk factors.

Material and methods

Study population

The ARIC study is a prospective cohort designed to identify risk factors for coronary heart disease (CHD) and to describe the natural progression of atherosclerosis. Between 1987 and 1989, the study enrolled 15,792 participants, predominantly of Black or White race, aged 45–64 years, from four U.S. communities: Forsyth County, North Carolina; Jackson, Mississippi; the northwestern suburbs of Minneapolis, Minnesota; and Washington County, Maryland. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. The ARIC Study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at each participating institution, including the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Johns Hopkins University, the University of Minnesota, the University of Mississippi Medical Center, and Wake Forest University, and all participants provided written informed consent29. Details of the ARIC study and its dental component have been previously published29–32.

Of the 11,656 participants who attended Visit 4 (1996–1998), 6793 had complete periodontal examination data. Among these, 6409 had available BW and birth term status. We excluded 211 participants due to missing key covariables, and 46 participants from minority ethnic groups with insufficient representation for stratified analysis (i.e., participants other than White or Black, and Black participants from Minneapolis or Washington County). A total of 6152 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Study measures

Birth weight and birth term

Prematurity was determined from self-reported responses to whether the participant was born a month or more before their expected due date, corresponding to a gestational age of less than 37 weeks. For BW, participants were asked to report their weight at birth in pounds and ounces. If unable to recall the exact value, they were given the option to select from three categories: low, medium, or high. Reported exact BWs were categorized into three groups: LBW < 5.5 lbs (< 2.5 kg), normal birth weight (NBW) 5.5–9 lbs (2.5–4.0 kg), and HBW > 9 lbs (> 4 kg). Both exact and categorized responses were combined into a single BW variable for the analysis.

Periodontal assessments

Trained and calibrated examiners performed full-mouth periodontal assessments using standardized protocols; details of examiner training and calibration procedures have been published previously33. PD status was classified according to the Periodontal Profile Class (PPC)-Stages, a validated system developed through latent class analysis of seven key periodontal indices commonly collected in dental clinics30,34. The PPC-Stages categorize periodontal status into seven levels: Stage I, “Health/Incidental disease”; Stage II, “Mild PD”; Stage III, “Moderate PD”; Stage IV, “Severe PD”; Stage V, “Mild Tooth Loss with High Gingival Inflammation”; Stage VI, “Moderate Tooth Loss with Reduced Periodontium”; and Stage VII, “Severe Tooth Loss.” For the purposes of analysis, these stages were consolidated into three broader categories: healthy (Stage I), PD (Stages II–V), and tooth loss (Stages VI–VII).

Demographics and comorbidities

Demographic data, including age, sex, race (Black or White), education level and reproductive factors, were collected at baseline (Visit 1). Physical activity was assessed at Visit 3 (1993–1995) using a validated, modified version of the Baecke questionnaire35,36. At Visit 4 (1996–1998), additional covariates were obtained via standardized questionnaires (e.g., smoking status, income, health insurance, alcohol intake, medication use), clinical examinations, and laboratory analysis of blood samples.

At Visit 4, participants underwent a clinical examination after fasting for at least 8 h. Blood samples were collected to assess lipid profiles. Total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides were directly measured, and LDL cholesterol was calculated using the Friedewald equation 37. Current use of lipid-lowering medications, including statins, was recorded based on self-report and a medication inventory. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg (based on the average of two seated readings taken after a 5-min rest), or current use of antihypertensive medication38. C-reactive protein (CRP) was measured using an immunonephelometric assay39. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a self-reported physician diagnosis, a fasting glucose level ≥ 126 mg/dL (or ≥ 200 mg/dL if non-fasting), or current use of glucose-lowering medication40.

Prevalent CHD and heart failure (HF) at Visit 4 were defined as a combination of baseline diagnoses and adjudicated events that occurred between Visit 1 and Visit 4, ascertained using standardized ARIC surveillance protocols. CHD included self-reported history of myocardial infarction (MI), prior coronary bypass surgery or angioplasty at baseline, or evidence of MI on baseline electrocardiogram (ECG), as well as hospitalized or fatal CHD events identified through surveillance. HF included cases identified at baseline by the Gothenburg criteria or use of HF medications, in addition to adjudicated incident HF events during follow-up. Stroke was defined using standardized ARIC surveillance protocols and included both prevalent stroke at Visit 1 and adjudicated stroke events identified through ongoing surveillance, based on hospital discharge summaries, physician documentation, participant interviews, and standardized abstraction methods41,42.

Use of specific medications, including antidepressants, thiazide diuretics, bisphosphonates, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), and glucocorticoids, was self-reported by participants and verified by study staff. Verification was based on a medication inventory provided by participants at the clinic visit.

Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation, based on serum creatinine, age, sex, and race, and expressed in mL/min/1.73 m2 of body surface area43.

At Visit 1, female participants completed an interviewer-administered questionnaire detailing their reproductive history, including parity (number of live births), menopausal status, and current or prior use of hormone replacement therapy (HRT), as described elsewhere44; Menopausal status and current or prior use of HRT were updated at Visit 4.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables. Visit 4 covariates were used except when noted above. Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Differences in continuous variables across periodontal categories were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) when assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were met, or Kruskal–Wallis tests when these assumptions were not met. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables.

To examine the associations between birth characteristics and adult periodontal outcomes, two separate binary logistic regression models were constructed. In the first model, participants with periodontal disease (PD; PPC Stages II–V) were compared to periodontally healthy participants (PPC Stage I). In the second model, participants with tooth loss (PPC Stages VI–VII) were compared to periodontally healthy participants (PPC Stage I). NBW served as the reference category for all birth weight comparisons, and full-term birth served as the reference for birth term comparisons. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each outcome separately.

Four progressively adjusted models were developed. Variables were selected based on their established roles as shared risk factors on the life-course causal pathway linking early-life exposures to adult oral health45–48. Model 1 adjusted for fundamental demographic confounders (age, sex, and race/center)45. Model 2 added sociodemographic and behavioral factors (education, income, insurance status, smoking, alcohol use, and physical activity) that influence both maternal health/birth outcomes and adult oral hygiene/disease susceptibility46,47. Model 3 included CVD and metabolic risk factors (BMI, blood pressure, diabetes, lipids, eGFR) to account for the systemic inflammatory and metabolic pathways that are programmed by adverse birth outcomes and concurrently exacerbate periodontal disease45. Finally, Model 4 additionally incorporated medications affecting bone metabolism and inflammation, given the potential for early-life growth restriction to impair skeletal homeostasis48. All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Demographics and periodontal disease categories of ARIC participants

At Visit 4, the mean age of the 6152 eligible ARIC participants was 62.3 years (SD ± 5.6), 54.9% were female, and 17.4% identified as Black. Based on the PPC system, 28.1% of participants were classified as periodontally healthy (Stage I), 47.7% had PD (Stages II–V), and 24.2% had tooth loss (Stages VI–VII).

Significant differences in demographic, behavioral, and clinical variables were observed across periodontal status categories (Table 1). Participants with worse periodontal status were generally older, had lower education levels, and reported lower household incomes. These individuals were more likely to rely on Medicare/Medicaid or to lack insurance. Behavioral risk factors were more prevalent in this group, including higher rates of current or former smoking, greater BMI, and a higher prevalence of diabetes. In addition, participants with PD or tooth loss exhibited a more adverse cardiovascular risk profile. They had a higher prevalence of CHD and HF, elevated systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and more frequent use of antihypertensive medications.

Among female participants, surgical menopause was more prevalent among those with worse periodontal health. Grand multiparity (defined as five or more live births) was more common among females with tooth loss, whereas those with 1 to 4 births were more likely to have healthier periodontal status.

Use of medications that impact bone metabolism, such as antidepressants, thiazide diuretics, bisphosphonates, and PPIs, was more frequent among participants with PD and tooth loss. Participants with PD were more likely to have been born with LBW, while those with tooth loss had a higher prevalence of HBW.

Association between birth weight and prevalent periodontal outcomes

Table 2 presents the associations between BW categories and the prevalence of PD and tooth loss across progressively adjusted models. In unadjusted analysis, LBW was not associated with PD (OR = 1.26, 95% CI 0.93–1.67, P = 0.10). However, after adjustment for sociodemographic, behavioral, cardiovascular, and bone-related medication factors, LBW was associated with higher odds of PD (P = 0.01). In the fully adjusted Model 4, LBW was associated with 57% higher odds compared to NBW (OR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.15–2.16). No significant association was observed between HBW and PD across all models.

In contrast, HBW was consistently associated with an increased risk of tooth loss across all models. In fully adjusted analyses, individuals with HBW had 1.49 times the odds of tooth loss compared to those with NBW (OR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.06–2.07, P = 0.02). Association between premature birth and prevalent periodontal outcomes

Table 3 summarizes associations between PTB and the prevalence of PD and tooth loss across progressively adjusted models. PTB was associated with higher odds of PD in all adjusted models (P = 0.02-0.04). In the fully adjusted Model 4, PTB was associated with 52% higher odds of PD compared to full-term birth (OR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.02–2.27, P = 0.04).

Discussion

In this large, multicenter, community-based cohort, a history of PTB or LBW was associated with higher odds of PD in adulthood (OR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.15–2.16 and OR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.02–2.27, P < 0.05, respectively), while HBW was associated with higher odds of tooth loss (OR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.06–2.07, P = 0.02) (Fig. 2). These associations remained significant after comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, suggesting robust associations between birth characteristics and adult oral health outcomes.

The biological plausibility of these findings is supported by developmental and neuroendocrine mechanisms. Early-life growth restriction has been linked to persistent autonomic dysregulation, including elevated sympathetic activity49,50, which may impair skeletal homeostasis through downregulation of osteoblast activity and enhanced osteoclastogenesis via β-adrenergic signaling51–53. These disruptions may contribute to chronic inflammation and impaired bone remodeling, consequently increasing susceptibility to periodontal breakdown. Mechanistic studies in rodent models, simulating respiratory instability, reinforce this hypothesis: neonatal exposure to intermittent hypoxia (IH), a common feature of prematurity, has been shown to reduce osteogenic capacity, increase osteoclastic resorption, suppress growth hormone expression, and inhibit craniofacial bone maturation54–56. These studies support the biological link between early respiratory dysfunction and skeletal vulnerability. In parallel, human studies have shown that autonomic dysfunction is common in adolescents and adults born preterm, with findings such as elevated sympathetic tone and impaired baroreflex sensitivity57. These autonomic alterations have been suggested to contribute to the earlier onset of hypertension and increased cardiovascular risk58. The effects appear to be mediated by sympathetic overactivity, dysregulated zinc homeostasis, and elevated norepinephrine levels54,59–62. Additionally, chronic IH has been shown to remodel autonomic nerve networks, linking early respiratory stress to long-term cardiovascular and skeletal dysregulation63.

Our findings align with the DOHaD framework, including the Barker hypothesis, which proposes that early-life growth restriction predisposes individuals to chronic inflammatory conditions, such as cardiovascular and metabolic diseases17,64,65. A prior ARIC study reported that LBW was associated with elevated systemic inflammation and endothelial activation in midlife (RR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.05–1.29)11. Another investigation found that each 1-lb decrease in BW was associated with a 13.4 nmol/L higher level of plasma cortisol (95% CI 4.5- 22.4, P < 0.01)11,66, suggesting that altered endocrine feedback in individuals with LBW may amplify disturbances in immune-endocrine responses to oral pathogens67,68. Moreover, a recent population-based study in a Japanese cohort reported that BW below 1,500 g was significantly associated with CVD (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR] = 1.76, 95% CI 1.37–2.26), hypertension (aPR = 1.29, 95% CI 1.17–1.42), and diabetes (aPR = 1.53, 95% CI 1.26–1.86)69. A Mendelian randomization study further demonstrated that lower genetically predicted BW was causally associated with coronary artery disease (OR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.06–1.37; P = 0.03)70. Collectively, these systemic alterations, including chronic inflammation, elevated blood pressure, and metabolic dysregulation, are recognized contributors to periodontal pathogenesis71, and may underlie the observed associations between adverse birth outcomes and oral health in adulthood.

In contrast to LBW and PTB, HBW was not associated with PD but was specifically linked to tooth loss (OR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.06–2.07, P = 0.02). Tooth loss is a cumulative endpoint that commonly reflects the long-term burden of untreated caries and periodontal diseases72. This relationship likely reflects early-life metabolic programming, potentially driven by maternal diabetes or overnutrition, which increases the risk of obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes in later life73. A comprehensive meta-analysis of 7,646,267 participants demonstrated J-shaped associations between BW and both type 2 diabetes and CVD. This analysis showed that participants with BW > 4.5 kg had 19% higher odds of diabetes (OR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.04–1.36) and 22% higher odds of CVD (OR = 1.22, 95% CI 1.08–1.37) compared with those from the reference BW group (4.0–4.5 kg)74. These metabolic conditions are established risk factors for tooth loss through mechanisms involving chronic inflammation, altered immune response, and dysregulated glucose metabolism75–78. A population-based study of 8,446 individuals with 13 years of follow-up demonstrated that missing teeth can predict metabolic outcomes, with ≥ 5 missing teeth associated with a 60% to 140% increased hazard for incident CHD events (P < 0.02) and acute MI (P < 0.01), while ≥ 9 missing teeth were associated with incident CVD (P < 0.04), diabetes (P < 0.04), and all-cause mortality (P < 0.02)76. HBW was also significantly associated with increased prevalence of dental caries in childhood (OR = 2.00, 95% CI 1.06–3.77)16. In this study, the association between HBW and tooth loss persisted despite adjustment for sociodemographic and behavioral risk factors, suggesting that biological mechanisms related to early metabolic exposures contribute independently to tooth loss risk. HBW may alter enamel formation and mineralization during development79, while subsequent metabolic dysregulation could impair dentin repair mechanisms and salivary function, collectively increasing susceptibility to dental caries rather than periodontal breakdown15,80. Thus, the relationship between HBW and tooth loss likely reflects pathways involving metabolic dysregulation established in early life that specifically impacts dental structures rather than periodontal tissues.

While both PD and CVD share inflammatory mediators, we found no significant differences in eGFR across periodontal status groups in our cohort. This outcome suggests that kidney dysfunction is unlikely to confound the observed associations, an important consideration given that prior ARIC studies have shown that kidney disease is an independent risk factor for bone fractures81, diabetes-related complications82, and vitamin D–related genetic pathways83.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes determination of temporality or causality, and tooth loss is a cumulative endpoint for which we could not determine timing or primary etiology. Second, birth characteristics were self-reported decades after birth and may be subject to recall bias and misclassification. However, previous validation studies have shown strong accuracy for categorical BW and prematurity status84, and any nondifferential misclassification of these exposures would likely bias our results toward the null, suggesting that the true associations may be stronger than observed. Third, although models were adjusted for key adult sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical factors, residual confounding from early-life exposures (such as childhood socioeconomic status, maternal health, early nutrition, and access to dental care) may persist. Fourth, the absence of longitudinal oral health data limits our ability to assess disease progression over time. Additionally, periodontal status was classified using the PPC system. Although well validated within the ARIC cohort34, this approach differs from the contemporary 2017/2018 World Workshop classification85. Therefore, comparisons of our prevalence estimates and effect sizes with more recent studies using staging and grading frameworks should be interpreted with caution. Finally, selection and survivor bias are important limitations. Our analyses were restricted to the 6152 participants who survived to Visit 4, attended the clinic visit, completed a periodontal examination, and had available birth history data, excluding a substantial portion of the original ARIC cohort. Individuals lost to follow-up, deceased before Visit 4, or not examined dentally may have had worse adverse early-life exposures and greater systemic and oral disease burden, potentially leading to underestimation of the true strength of the association between adverse birth outcomes and adult periodontal disease. Moreover, the characteristics of long-term cohort participants may differ from those of the general population, which may limit generalizability. Nonetheless, our study has notable strengths. It is based on a large, biracial, community-based cohort with well-characterized cardiovascular and sociodemographic data32, and periodontal status34 determined through formal dental examinations rather than self-report, enhancing outcome validity. Future research should incorporate prospective, life-course designs with serial oral health assessments and detailed early-life data. Investigating inflammatory, metabolic, and microbiome-related pathways may further clarify mechanisms linking adverse birth outcomes to adult oral health.

Our findings support the hypothesis that early-life factors captured by BW extremes and prematurity may be linked to adult oral health, potentially through distinct but plausible biological mechanisms. LBW and PTB are associated with adult PD, potentially through pathways involving autonomic dysregulation, systemic inflammation, and altered bone metabolism, while their lack of association with tooth loss likely reflects the complex and multifactorial nature of this condition. Conversely, HBW’s significant association with tooth loss but not PD suggests distinct mechanisms related to early metabolic programming, which may increase susceptibility to dental caries and impair alveolar bone repair independent of periodontal inflammation. These mechanistic explanations remain speculative in this observational analysis and should be evaluated in prospective life-course studies with detailed perinatal data and longitudinal oral health assessments.

From a public health perspective, these findings underscore the importance of integrating oral health into the broader life-course approach to chronic disease prevention. Recognizing that individuals born prematurely or with abnormal birth weights may carry an elevated, lifelong risk for periodontal disease and tooth loss highlights the need for early, targeted interventions. Public health policies should promote comprehensive maternal and prenatal care to optimize birth outcomes, while simultaneously ensuring that individuals with a history of adverse birth outcomes receive early, intensive, and continuous preventive dental care. Integrating birth history into routine medical and dental risk assessments could facilitate personalized preventive strategies, bridging the gap between early-life systemic exposures and long-term oral health disparities.

In this large, community-based cohort, PTB and LBW were associated with higher odds of prevalent PD in adulthood, whereas HBW was associated with higher odds of tooth loss. These findings support further investigation of birth history as a potential marker for later-life oral health risk and underscore the value of life-course approaches to prevention across oral and systemic health.
