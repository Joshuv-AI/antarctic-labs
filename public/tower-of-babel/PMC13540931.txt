Problematic and unprofessional physician behaviors toward medical representatives in Japan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hiroshi Ito, Aiko Harada, Taiju Miyagami, Takashi Watari, Toshio Naito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective communication is essential in healthcare and influences patient outcomes and care quality. While physician-related behaviors toward other healthcare professionals have been widely studied, less is known about interactions with external stakeholders such as medical representatives (MRs). This study aimed to describe problematic or unprofessional physician-related behaviors and related communication barriers experienced by MRs in Japan.MethodsA cross-sectional web-based survey was conducted among MRs registered with Doctor’s Prime Academia, an online platform for healthcare professionals and industry employees. The survey collected demographic and professional characteristics and assessed experiences of physician-related behaviors, including problematic communication, sexual harassment, and prolonged waiting before meetings. Group comparisons were performed using non-parametric and exact tests. Overall, 88.8% reported experiencing some form of physician-related problematic or unprofessional behavior, with refusal to respond to work-related matters being the most common (58.6%). Background

Communication skills are an essential element of professionalism for physicians [1]. Physicians with strong communication skills have been shown to improve patient outcomes and satisfaction [2, 3]. Conversely, insufficient communication can contribute to adverse events [4], and disruptive behaviors, such as sexual harassment [5, 6], represent significant problems in medical settings. These behaviors can lower the quality of care and negatively affect both healthcare professionals and patients [5]. Beyond overtly disruptive behaviors, physician-related interactional factors, such as communication barriers or prolonged waiting before scheduled interactions, may also affect professional communication, although they are not necessarily equivalent to disruptive behavior [7, 8].

Relationships between physicians and other healthcare professionals, such as nurses and medical staff, have been extensively studied, particularly regarding the impact of physicians’ disruptive behavior [5]. However, less is known about physician-related behaviors toward external stakeholders outside the clinical team, such as medical representatives (MRs). MRs provide physicians with pharmaceutical information and act as intermediaries between healthcare providers and pharmaceutical companies, influencing clinical decision-making and the healthcare supply chain [9–12]. Problematic or unprofessional behaviors by physicians toward MRs may hinder effective communication and information exchange, potentially affecting clinical practice and collaboration. In addition, communication difficulties and prolonged waiting may create practical barriers to timely information exchange between physicians and MRs, even when such delays are partly influenced by clinical workload or organizational factors.

To address this gap, we investigated the extent to which MRs in Japan experience various physician-related behaviors, including communication difficulties, sexual harassment, and prolonged waiting before meetings. Given the exploratory nature of this study, we aimed to describe the prevalence and patterns of these experiences among MRs and to explore potential factors associated with them.

Methods

Setting and participants

This cross-sectional web-based survey involved MRs registered with Doctor’s Prime Academia. Doctor’s Prime Academia (operated by Doctor’s Prime Inc.) is an online platform that provides educational content (e.g., e-learning materials) to healthcare professionals and employees of medical companies across Japan. MRs registered with Doctor’s Prime Academia who had participated in a study session were notified about the study and invited to participate. Participation was voluntary, and MRs accessed the survey through the platform. Only MRs who agreed to participate were able to complete the survey between April 5 and July 22, 2024. In accordance with the platform’s specifications, all questions were mandatory, and incomplete responses were not recorded. The total number of eligible MRs registered with Doctor’s Prime Academia and the survey response rate were not available from the platform administrator.

Survey instrument

Background data collected from MRs included sex, age, years of experience as an MR, educational background (high school, university, master’s program, doctoral program), managerial position, specialty field, the type of the main institution they were responsible for (clinic, community hospital, or university hospital), location of the institution (urban or rural), and the number of institutions they were in charge of. Urban areas were defined as the 20 cities designated by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and the 23 wards of Tokyo; all other areas were classified as rural.

The survey consisted of three core Yes/No questions and additional follow-up items for MRs who answered “Yes.” The core questions were as follows: (1) “Have you experienced physician-related problematic or unprofessional behavior?” (2) “Have you experienced sexual harassment from a physician?” and (3) “Have you been kept waiting for more than 1 hour before a meeting with a physician?” In this study, “physician-related problematic or unprofessional behavior” was used as an umbrella term for behaviors or interactional experiences that could interfere with respectful communication, information exchange, or professional collaboration between physicians and MRs.

For each core question, MRs first responded with either “Yes” or “No.” MRs who answered “Yes” were then asked to select the most memorable episode from predefined response options. For question (1), these options described the type of physician-related problematic or unprofessional behavior experienced. For question (2), follow-up items included the type of sexual harassment, whether the MR consulted someone, and the availability or use of a workplace reporting department. For question (3), follow-up items included the physician’s age group, specialty, and type of institution, as well as the maximum waiting time and work-related time allocation.

Communication difficulties, sexual harassment, and prolonged waiting were treated as distinct domains because they differ in severity, mechanisms, and implications for professionalism.

The first question was included to describe the overall prevalence and types of physician-related problematic or unprofessional behaviors experienced by MRs. Questions (2) and (3) were selected for further subgroup analyses because sexual harassment and prolonged waiting were considered particularly relevant to professionalism, workplace interactions, and communication between physicians and MRs.

A waiting time of more than one hour was used because such delays were considered likely to substantially interfere with scheduled professional interactions between physicians and MRs.

The questionnaire items were developed by experts in physician-related behaviors and professionalism (TM and TW) based on prior literature and clinical experience.

The main tables present group comparisons according to experiences of sexual harassment and prolonged waiting, whereas the supplementary tables provide descriptive details from the follow-up items among MRs who reported each experience.

Ethics

This study used anonymized data provided by Dr. Prime Inc. (available at https://drsprime.com/news/6Tb4Fxu7). Because the data were publicly available and contained no identifiable personal information, the study did not require review by an institutional ethics committee under the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects in Japan. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables are presented as n (%). Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Because this was an exploratory study with a limited sample size and a relatively small number of outcome events, multivariable analyses were not performed to avoid overfitting and unstable estimates. All analyses were conducted to describe patterns of physician-related behaviors and to explore potential associations. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR version 1.65 [13]. (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan). A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Background information

A total of 98 MRs participated in the survey, of whom 15 (15.3%) were female. The median age was 40 years (interquartile range [IQR], 33–46), and the median duration of experience as an MR was 15 years (IQR, 9.8–20). Most MRs were university graduates (76.5%), and 25 (25.5%) held managerial positions. The majority of MRs were responsible for university hospitals (68.4%), with others covering community hospitals (20.4%) and clinics (11.2%). Overall, 87 MRs (88.8%) reported experiencing some form of physician-related problematic or unprofessional behavior, with the most commonly reported issue being refusal to respond to work-related matters (58.6%) (Appendix Table 1).

Sexual harassment by physicians

Fourteen MRs (14.3%) reported experiencing sexual harassment from physicians. MRs who reported sexual harassment were younger (median age 33 vs. 40 years, p = 0.008) and more likely to be female (64.3% vs. 7.1%, p < 0.001) compared with those who did not report such experiences (Table 1). The most frequently reported form of sexual harassment was sexual objectification, reported by 13 MRs (92.9%) (Appendix Table 2). Gender harassment was reported by six MRs (42.9%), and sexual coercion by two MRs (14.3%). Regarding responses to these experiences, five of five male MRs reported consulting someone, whereas four of nine female MRs (44.4%) did not report their experiences to anyone. Because only 14 MRs reported sexual harassment, including five male MRs and nine female MRs, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously. Among those who experienced sexual harassment, 10 MRs (71.4%) reported that their workplace had a department for handling such issues.

Prolonged waiting before meetings with physicians

Thirty-eight MRs (38.8%) reported being kept waiting for more than 1 h before meeting with physicians. MRs who reported prolonged waiting were younger (median age 35 vs. 42 years, p = 0.003) and had fewer years of experience (11 vs. 18 years, p = 0.005) compared with those who did not (Table 2). Educational background also differed between groups, with a higher proportion of MRs with a master’s or doctoral degree reporting prolonged waiting (36.8% vs. 13.3%, p = 0.02). Among reported cases of prolonged waiting, physicians in their 40s were most frequently involved (42.1%), followed by those in their 50s (34.2%) (Appendix Table 3). The most common types of institutions were community hospitals (44.7%) and university hospitals (42.1%), and the most common specialty was internal medicine (52.6%). The median waiting time was 2.0 h (IQR, 1.8–3.0 h).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, most MRs in Japan reported experiencing some form of physician-related problematic or unprofessional behavior. In addition, MRs who reported sexual harassment tended to be younger and more likely to be female, whereas those who reported prolonged waiting tended to be younger, less experienced, and more likely to have a higher educational background. These findings describe patterns of challenges in interactions between physicians and MRs, particularly among specific subgroups, although these findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating due to the exploratory design and small subgroup sizes.

The finding that younger and female MRs more frequently reported experiences of sexual harassment is consistent with previous literature indicating that younger women are at increased risk of workplace harassment [14]. Previous studies have shown that sexual harassment is prevalent among healthcare workers, including nurses and other staff, and may originate from both patients and colleagues [15, 16]. Although MRs are not directly involved in clinical care, they frequently interact with physicians in professional settings, which may expose them to similar risks. These findings indicate that interactions between physicians and external stakeholders, such as MRs, occur within the broader context of workplace professionalism and harassment prevention. However, only 14 MRs reported sexual harassment in this study, including five male MRs and nine female MRs; therefore, subgroup findings regarding sex differences and disclosure behaviors should be interpreted with particular caution. In addition, the sex of the physicians involved in sexual harassment episodes was not collected, limiting interpretation of the findings among male MR victims.

MRs who reported prolonged waiting were younger and less experienced, which could be associated with hierarchical dynamics in professional interactions. In Japan, traditional workplace structures have often emphasized seniority, and younger individuals may be more likely to experience lower prioritization in professional settings [17]. In addition, MRs with higher educational backgrounds were more likely to report prolonged waiting. Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, one possible explanation is that individuals with advanced degrees may be assigned to more complex roles or institutions, leading to less predictable schedules or longer waiting times. Alternatively, higher educational attainment may reflect differences in career stage or job responsibilities. These interpretations are speculative and warrant further investigation. Importantly, prolonged waiting should not be interpreted solely as an indicator of physician professionalism. Waiting before meetings may be influenced by clinical workload, emergency situations, scheduling practices, and institutional workflow. In this study, prolonged waiting was treated as a potential barrier to timely physician–MR communication rather than as direct evidence of unprofessional conduct by individual physicians.

This study has several strengths. First, it focuses on MRs, a group that has received relatively little attention in research on physician-related behaviors. By examining experiences among MRs, this study expands the scope of existing literature beyond intra-hospital relationships. This study also complements existing literature on physician–industry relationships, which has primarily examined prescribing behavior, conflicts of interest, and pharmaceutical information exchange, by highlighting the interpersonal and workplace dimensions of these interactions. Second, the findings provide descriptive data on a range of behaviors, including communication difficulties, harassment, and operational barriers, offering a broader perspective on physician–MR interactions. Practical responses may include incorporating respectful interactions with external stakeholders into physician professionalism education, establishing clear institutional expectations for scheduled meetings, and strengthening reporting pathways for harassment involving non-employee professionals.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as an exploratory cross-sectional study with a limited sample size and no multivariable analyses, the observed group differences may be influenced by unmeasured confounding factors, precluding causal inferences. The small number of MRs reporting sexual harassment also limited the stability and interpretability of subgroup comparisons. Second, the study population was limited to MRs registered on a specific online platform, which may affect generalizability and introduce selection bias if those with negative experiences were more likely to participate. In addition, the total number of eligible MRs and the survey response rate were unavailable, preventing assessment of representativeness and non-response bias. Third, the self-reported nature of the data may be subject to recall bias, and the survey’s focus on negative experiences may have influenced responses. Because the survey did not specify a time frame for past experiences, recall may have differed according to age or years of MR experience; older or more experienced MRs may have had longer exposure periods but may also have had more difficulty recalling specific events. Finally, the questionnaire, though reviewed by experts, was developed specifically for this study and has not been formally validated. Despite these limitations, this study provides preliminary insights into physician–MR interactions and highlights areas for further investigation. Future research with larger, more representative samples and validated tools is needed to better understand these relationships and to identify strategies for improving professional interactions.

Conclusion

Most MRs in Japan reported experiencing some form of physician-related problematic or unprofessional behavior. Younger and female MRs more frequently reported experiences of sexual harassment, whereas younger and less experienced MRs, as well as those with higher educational backgrounds, more frequently reported prolonged waiting. Given the exploratory nature of this study, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, they highlight the need to consider interactions between physicians and external stakeholders as part of broader efforts to promote professionalism and improve workplace environments in healthcare.
