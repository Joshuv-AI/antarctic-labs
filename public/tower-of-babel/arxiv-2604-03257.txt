Robust LLM Performance Certification via Constrained Maximum Likelihood Estimation
Minghe Shen, Ananth Balashankar, Adam Fisch, David Madras, Miguel Rodrigues

Abstract
The ability to rigorously estimate the failure rates of large language models (LLMs) is a prerequisite for their safe deployment. Currently, however, practitioners often face a tradeoff between expensive human gold standards and potentially severely-biased automatic annotation schemes such as "LLM-as-a-Judge" labeling. In this paper, we propose a new, practical, and efficient approach to LLM failure rate estimation based on constrained maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE). Our method integrates three distinct signal sources: (i) a small, high-quality human-labeled calibration set, (ii) a large corpus of LLM-judge annotations, and, most importantly, (iii) additional side information via domain-specific constraints derived from known bounds on judge performance statistics. We validate our approach through a comprehensive empirical study, benchmarking it against state-of-the-art baselines like Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI). Across diverse experimental regimes—spanning varying judge accuracies, calibration set sizes, and LLM failure rates—our constrained MLE consistently delivers more accurate and lower-variance estimates than existing methods. By moving beyond the "black-box" use of automated judges to a flexible framework, we provide a principled, interpretable, and scalable pathway towards LLM failure-rate certification.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being deployed in consequential, real-world systems such as content moderation, recommendation pipelines, and decision-support tools
33
;
16
. In these contexts, deployment hinges on more than just empirical performance; it requires assessing model behavior with enough statistical confidence to satisfy safety and reliability standards—a process that often incurs substantial evaluation costs.

In practice, LLM evaluation typically relies on either benchmark datasets or expert human assessments. Benchmarks enable scalable comparisons, but are increasingly affected by contamination, annotation noise, and over-optimization
4
;
29
, while expert human evaluation, though more reliable, is costly and difficult to scale
31
;
17
. As a result, reliability assessment is often framed as a statistical estimation problem, where the goal is to estimate failure rates with quantified uncertainty, typically requiring large calibration datasets
2
;
8
.

Many modern pipelines also adopt the “LLM-as-a-Judge” paradigm to reduce annotation costs, in which an automated model is used to evaluate the outputs of a target LLM at scale
15
;
6
. However, the judge itself is imperfect and stochastic, with unknown, task-dependent error rates
26
;
34
. Despite this, judge outputs are often treated as ground truth—effectively ignoring evaluator uncertainty, and leading to potentially flawed performance assessments
15
;
32
;
6
.

To address these limitations in practice, reliable deployment pipelines often utilize asymmetric supervision: a small subset of outputs receives high-quality human annotations, while a much larger set is labeled only by an automated judge
5
;
21
.

In this work, as demonstrated in Figure

Concretely, rather than treating judge quality as either fully known or entirely unknown, we introduce a constrained maximum likelihood estimation (CMLE) framework that explicitly captures the fact that the judge’s quality – captured via its true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) – can vary within plausible ranges; such knowledge about the judge quality can be indeed derived from other related tasks subject to some uncertainty. Such constraints imply that the proposed estimator can deliver more reliable estimates of an LLM failure rate, reducing variance without introducing bias, while making all assumptions about judge behavior explicit and interpretable.

Our main contributions are threefold:

•

We introduce a CMLE framework for estimating the failure rate of a target LLM in the presence of an imperfect LLM-based judge, using only a small set of high-quality ground-truth annotations together with a substantially larger dataset labeled by the LLM-based noisy judge; our framework explicitly parameterizes the judge’s error behavior through its true and false positive rates, capturing the fact that such parameters can be partially known via a set of constraints.

•

We provide extensive empirical results to demonstrate that the proposed CMLE framework can significantly outperform other competing approaches, including state-of-the-art prediction-powered inference (PPI) methods.

•

We also provide extensive results to illustrate how our CMLE framework behaves in the presence of mismatch, including where the judge true positive and false positive rate constraints are estimated on an auxiliary dataset/task and transferred to the target evaluation dataset/task.

Statistical frameworks for certified LLM evaluation.

We are interested in the estimation of the failure rate of a target LLM in a setting where high-quality human supervision is limited, but automated evaluations are abundant and noisy — see Figure

S_{J}=1

S_{J}=0

(1)

by leveraging the two sources of supervision: (1) the small

The challenge relates to how to combine in a principled manner the labeled data, providing direct information about the target LLM failure rate, and the unlabeled data, which only provides indirect but high-volume information about the LLM failure rate via the noisy judge annotations, in order to estimate the LLM failure rate reliably. We will address this challenge by proposing maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) frameworks that can explicitly leverage two key parameters associated with an LLM-as-a-judge operation:

\mathrm{TPR}\;=\;\Pr(S_{J}=1\mid S_{M}=1),\qquad\mathrm{FPR}\;=\;\Pr(S_{J}=1\mid S_{M}=0),

(2)

corresponding to the judge’s TPR, which captures the rate with which the judge correctly detects true failures, and the judge’s FPR, which captures the rate with which the judge incorrectly flags correct answers as failures, respectively. We will also address this challenge by describing how our framework can easily capture additional information one may acquire about the LLM-as-a-judge operation, such as bounds on the judge TPR and FPR transferred from another task to the target one.

4
Approach: Constrained Maximum Likelihood Estimation

We now propose our approach to estimate a target LLM failure rate by adopting a maximum likelihood estimation framework that explores the two sources of supervision; in doing so, our framework also explicitly exposes the role of the judge operation, thereby allowing us to incorporate additional
a priori
knowledge about the judge TPR and FPR. We describe the various likelihood functions, the maximum likelihood estimation problem, and the corresponding estimation algorithm in the sequel.

4.2
Maximum Likelihood Estimation Problems and Algorithms

We now propose two approaches that depend on the level of
a priori
knowledge one may have about an LLM-as-a-judge operation:

•

Our unconstrained maximum likelihood estimation (UMLE) approach does not assume any
a priori
knowledge about the judge’s true positive rate and false positive rate, i.e., it assumes
\mathrm{TPR}\in[0,1]
and
\mathrm{FPR}\in[0,1]
only.

•

Our constrained maximum likelihood estimation (CMLE) approach in turn assumes that one knows that the judge true and false positive rates lie within some plausible range of values, i.e., it assumes
\mathrm{TPR}\in[\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{L}},\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{U}}]=\mathcal{T}
and
\mathrm{FPR}\in[\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{L}},\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{U}}]=\mathcal{F}
.

We argue that it is plausible to constrain the judge’s TPR and FPR via explicit bounds, since in many deployment scenarios one may have access to partial prior knowledge about judge quality from calibration experiments, model documentation, or historical evaluation data
27
;
23
. While we focus on deterministic bounds in this work, it is also possible to consider bounds that hold only with high probability, which we leave to future work.

UMLE (unknown judge parameters).

In the absence of prior information about the quality of the judge, we estimate

(\theta,\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
by posing the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle(\hat{\theta},\widehat{\mathrm{TPR}},\widehat{\mathrm{FPR}})\in\arg\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in[0,1],\,\mathrm{TPR}\in[0,1],\,\mathrm{FPR}\in[0,1]\end{subarray}}\ell(\theta,\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR}).

(8)

We then solve this optimization problem by adopting a projected gradient ascent optimization algorithm.
Specifically, for a step size
\eta>0
, we iterate

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{[0,1]}\!\left(\theta^{(t)}+\eta\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\theta^{(t)},\mathrm{TPR}^{(t)},\mathrm{FPR}^{(t)})\right),

\displaystyle\mathrm{TPR}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{[0,1]}\!\left(\mathrm{TPR}^{(t)}+\eta\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{TPR}}\ell(\theta^{(t)},\mathrm{TPR}^{(t)},\mathrm{FPR}^{(t)})\right),

\displaystyle\mathrm{FPR}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{[0,1]}\!\left(\mathrm{FPR}^{(t)}+\eta\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{FPR}}\ell(\theta^{(t)},\mathrm{TPR}^{(t)},\mathrm{FPR}^{(t)})\right),

(9)

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}
denotes Euclidean projection onto a set
\mathcal{A}
.
In practice, we initialize
\theta
using the empirical failure rate on
\mathcal{D}_{M}
,
and initialize
(\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
using their empirical estimates computed on

\mathcal{D}_{M}
, which leads to stable and reliable convergence. We report
\hat{\theta}
as the estimate of the failure rate of the target LLM.

CMLE (partially known judge parameters).

In the presence of prior information about the quality of the judge, we estimate

(\theta,\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
by solving the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle(\hat{\theta},\widehat{\mathrm{TPR}},\widehat{\mathrm{FPR}})\in\arg\max_{\theta\in[0,1],\,\mathrm{TPR}\in\mathcal{T},\,\mathrm{FPR}\in\mathcal{F}}\;\;\ell(\theta,\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR}),

(10)

We also solve this constrained optimization problem using projected gradient ascent – the projection operations (a truncation operation) force the judge’s true positive rate and judge false positive rate estimates to lie within the feasible (postulated) ranges. We also report
\hat{\theta}
as the estimate of the failure rate of the target LLM.

Constrained maximum likelihood estimation can be seen as a generalization of unconstrained maximum likelihood estimation. Intuitively, the constraints guide the optimization algorithm to capture the true judge behavior by shrinking the optimization parameter space, thereby leading to more robust LLM failure rate estimates

We will report in the sequel the advantages of constrained maximum likelihood estimation over unconstrained maximum likelihood estimation and other baselines; concretely, we will show that constrained MLE can lead to substantial variance reductions with negligible bias; we will also show that constrained MLE is relatively robust to possible misspecification of the judge TPR and FPR constraints.

6
Conclusion

We propose a likelihood-based framework for estimating LLM failure rates under imperfect LLM-as-a-judge supervision. By explicitly modeling judge behavior via its TPR and FPR, UMLE provides a principled baseline competitive with state-of-the-art PPI methods, while CMLE incorporates partial prior knowledge to achieve substantial variance reduction and improved stability. Experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets show that CMLE consistently delivers the strongest performance across a wide range of regimes, even when one transfers knowledge about the judge quality from related tasks.

Our approach also has immediate practical ramifications: in view of the fact that it is often possible to estimate judge quality parameters – its TPR and FPR – from auxiliary related tasks, one can immediately adopt CMLE frameworks to offer superior LLM certification compared to other state-of-the-art approaches.

Appendix E
Extended Related Work

We review prior work on evaluation methodologies for large language models, the use of LLMs as automated judges, and statistical frameworks for inference and certification under noisy or proxy supervision.

LLMs evaluation paradigms.

Evaluation of large language models has traditionally followed two primary paradigms: benchmark-driven automatic evaluation and human expert assessment. Benchmark-based evaluation relies on standardized public datasets and predefined tasks to measure model performance, enabling scalable and reproducible comparisons across models
20
;
13
;
24
. Despite their widespread adoption, benchmark-driven evaluations are increasingly recognized to suffer from dataset contamination, annotation noise, and sensitivity to over-optimization toward benchmark metrics, which can limit their reliability as indicators of real-world model behavior
4
;
29
.

Human expert evaluation, by contrast, is widely regarded as the most trustworthy approach for assessing model quality, safety, and alignment, particularly in open-ended or high-stakes settings
31
;
17
. However, human assessment is costly, time-consuming, and difficult to scale to the large sample sizes required for statistically reliable guarantees. These limitations have motivated the development of hybrid evaluation strategies that combine limited ground-truth supervision with scalable automated assessments.

LLM-as-a-Judge: scalability and limitations.

A prominent hybrid evaluation paradigm replaces or augments human judgments with automated assessments produced by a separate language model, commonly referred to as
LLM-as-a-Judge
. By shifting evaluation to an LLM-based judge, this approach enables large-scale benchmarking, continuous monitoring, and rapid iteration at a fraction of the cost of human annotation
10
;
30
.

At the same time, a growing body of work has documented systematic limitations of LLM-based judges, including sensitivity to prompt formulation, domain dependence, and various forms of biases
26
;
34
. While numerous heuristics—such as prompt ensembling, self-consistency, and learned judge models—have been proposed to mitigate these issues, they typically treat judge outputs as ground-truth labels
28
;
22
;
14
. As a result, the uncertainty introduced by judge errors is rarely modeled explicitly, and downstream evaluation often relies on implicit and unverified assumptions about judge accuracy.

Statistical frameworks for LLM certification under noisy supervision.

Several lines of work approach LLM evaluation from a statistical perspective, aiming to provide principled guarantees under limited or imperfect supervision. Classical hypothesis testing and finite-sample inference offer tools for certifying whether a population-level error rate satisfies a prescribed threshold, and have been applied to reliability assessment and factuality evaluation in language models
18
. However, hypothesis testing–based procedures can become impractical when only a small amount of ground-truth data is available, limiting their applicability in many realistic evaluation settings.

To address this limitation, several statistically rigorous approaches aim to combine a small set of high-quality labels with a much larger collection of noisy or weak supervision, such as outcomes produced by automatic evaluators or LLM-based judges. For example, prediction-powered inference (PPI) provides a general framework for leveraging auxiliary predictions to estimate target quantities of interest efficiently. PPI has therefore been proposed to estimate LLM failure rates by treating judge outputs as noisy predictors that can be corrected using a limited amount of trusted human annotations
1
.
Subsequent extensions, including PPI++ and Ridge PPI, further enhance variance reduction through learned correction weights and regularization
3
;
11
;
12
. Despite their effectiveness, PPI-based methods typically treat auxiliary predictions as black-box proxies and do not explicitly model structured error mechanisms such as false positive and false negative rates.

We also develop an approach to estimate the LLM failure rate rooted in well-established statistical principles. The key novelty relates to how we incorporate partial prior knowledge that one may be able to derive about a judge in order to offer more robust failure rate estimates.

Experimental setup

F.1
Benchmarks

We evaluate our proposed methods on a set of datasets encompassing synthetic and real-world ones.

Synthetic benchmarks.

Synthetic data allows precise control over both the target model’s failure rate and the judge’s reliability, making it well-suited for controlled and systematic evaluation of different estimation methods. Synthetic data are generated according to a failure rate
\theta
, with
S_{M}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\theta)
. Conditional on
S_{M}
, the judge label
S_{J}
is produced through a noisy Bernoulli channel, with success probability
\mathrm{TPR}_{\text{true}}
when
S_{M}=1
and
\mathrm{FPR}_{\text{true}}
when
S_{M}=0
.
All methods are evaluated under this common experimental setting to isolate the effects of judge quality, constraint tightness, and dataset size on estimation performance.

Classification benchmarks.

Jigsaw Toxic Comment Classification
7
and Hate Speech Offensive
9
allow us to understand how our methods can potentially perform in real-world toxic comment/hate speech classification deployments. In this setting, misclassification events of the target LLM-based classifier define the latent variable
S_{M}
, where
S_{M}=1
indicates that the classifier’s prediction is incorrect. Judge predictions define
S_{J}
, where
S_{J}=1
indicates that the judge disagrees with the classifier’s output. We focus on regimes with limited ground-truth annotations and substantially larger judge-labeled datasets, reflecting realistic evaluation constraints. In our experiments, we use Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct and LLaMA-3.2-1B-Instruct as target LLM-based classifiers, with LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct serving as the judge. The prompts used in our experiments are provided in Appendix
I
.

Generative benchmarks.

SafeRLHF
19
is a human-labeled dataset that provides safety annotations for LLM-generated content, with fixed LLMs used as the content generator. In this setting, the safety status of a model response defines the latent variable
S_{M}
, where
S_{M}=1
indicates an unsafe output, while predictions from an LLM-based judge define
S_{J}
, where
S_{J}=1
indicates that the judge deems the content generated by the target LLM to be unsafe. We focus on regimes with limited ground-truth annotations and a substantially larger set of judge-labeled examples, reflecting realistic evaluation constraints. In our experiments, we use subsets of SafeRLHF in which Alpaca-7B serves as the target LLM for content generation, and LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct act as judges. The prompts used in our experiments are provided in Appendix
I
.

F.2
Estimators

We also evaluate our proposed methods against a range of baseline and competing approaches, including the Standard Estimator
25
, a Denoise Estimator, an Oracle Estimator, and PPI++
3
;
11
;
12
.
A brief description of the baseline and MLE-based estimators follows.

F.2.1
Standard Estimator

The
Standard Estimator
uses only the clean labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{M}
of size
n_{M}
.
The LLM failure rate estimate is given by

\hat{\theta}=\frac{1}{n_{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{M}}S_{M_{i}},

(11)

Note that this estimator does not use the judge labels present in the labeled dataset or the judge dataset.

F.2.2
Judge Estimator

The
Judge Estimator
uses only the judge-labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{J}
of size
n_{J}
.
The LLM failure rate estimate is given by

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{Judge}}=\frac{1}{n_{J}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{J}}\tilde{S}_{J_{j}}.

(12)

Note that this estimator does not use any clean labels and directly treats the empirical mean of the judge labels as an estimate of the target LLM’s failure rate
\theta
.

F.2.3
Denoise Estimator

The
Denoise Estimator
uses the noisy unlabeled dataset together with empirical estimates of the judge parameters
\widehat{\mathrm{TPR}}
and
\widehat{\mathrm{FPR}}
derived from the labeled dataset, and operates under the assumption that the judge performs better than random guessing, so that
\widehat{\mathrm{TPR}}>\widehat{\mathrm{FPR}}
.
The LLM failure rate estimate is obtained via denoising as follows:

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{Denoise}}=\frac{\bar{S}_{J}-\widehat{\mathrm{FPR}}}{\widehat{\mathrm{TPR}}-\widehat{\mathrm{FPR}}}.

(13)

where
\bar{S}_{J}
denotes the mean of the noisy judge labels present in the unlabeled dataset.

F.2.4
Oracle Estimator

The
Oracle Estimator
also uses the noisy unlabeled dataset together with the true judge parameters
\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{true}}
and
\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{true}}
derived from an oracle, and operates under the assumption that the judge performs better than random guessing, i.e.,
\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{true}}>\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{true}}
. The LLM failure rate estimate is also obtained by denoising as follows:

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{Oracle}}=\frac{\bar{S}_{J}-\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{true}}}{\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{true}}-\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{true}}}.

(14)

where
\bar{S}_{J}
also denotes the mean of the noisy judge labels present in the unlabeled dataset. Note that this estimator represents an idealized benchmark in which the judge’s parameters are perfectly known.

F.2.5
PPI++ Estimator

The
PPI++
estimator
3
leverages both a clean labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{M}
of size
n_{M}
and a noisy judge-labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{J}
of size
n_{J}
to construct a statistically efficient estimate of the target failure rate. PPI++ improves upon the Standard Estimator by introducing an optimally weighted linear correction that exploits the covariance between the clean labels and the corresponding noisy judge labels, with the weight chosen to minimize the estimator variance. We include PPI++ as a representative baseline of the PPI family due to its strong empirical performance and conceptual simplicity.

Let
\bar{S}_{M}=\frac{1}{n_{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{M}}S_{M_{i}}
denote the empirical mean of the clean labels on
\mathcal{D}_{M}
,

\bar{S}^{\prime}_{M}=\frac{1}{n_{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{M}}S^{\prime}_{M_{i}}
the empirical mean of the judge labels evaluated on
\mathcal{D}_{M}
,
and
\bar{S}^{\prime}_{J}=\frac{1}{n_{J}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{J}}S^{\prime}_{J_{j}}
the empirical mean of the judge labels on
\mathcal{D}_{J}
.
Define the empirical joint moment

\hat{S}_{11}=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\mathbf{1}\{S_{M}=1,S^{\prime}_{M}=1\}\right]=\frac{1}{n_{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{M}}\mathbf{1}\{S_{M_{i}}=1,S^{\prime}_{M_{i}}=1\}.

(15)

PPI++ then computes

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\frac{\bar{S}^{\prime}_{J}(1-\bar{S}^{\prime}_{J})}{n_{J}}+\frac{\bar{S}^{\prime}_{M}(1-\bar{S}^{\prime}_{M})}{n_{M}},

(16)

\displaystyle\hat{B}=\frac{\hat{S}_{11}-\bar{S}_{M}\bar{S}^{\prime}_{M}}{n_{M}},

(17)

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{PPI++}}=\frac{\hat{B}}{\hat{A}},

(18)

and outputs the final estimate

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{PPI++}}=\bar{S}_{M}+\hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{PPI++}}\bigl(\bar{S}^{\prime}_{J}-\bar{S}^{\prime}_{M}\bigr).

(19)

F.2.6
PPI++ (Projected) Estimator

The
PPI++ (Projected)
estimator augments the standard PPI++ estimator by enforcing feasibility with respect to uncertainty in the judge error rates.
Similar to CMLE, we assume that the true judge’s operating characteristics lie in relative neighborhoods of the nominal values,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TPR}

\displaystyle\in\big[(1-\delta)\,\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{true}},\;(1+\delta)\,\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{true}}\big],

(20)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FPR}

\displaystyle\in\big[(1-\delta)\,\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{true}},\;(1+\delta)\,\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{true}}\big].

Let
\bar{S}_{J}
denote the mean of the noisy judge labels present in the unlabeled dataset. From
\bar{S}_{J}=\mathrm{FPR}+\theta(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})
, for any
(\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
with
\mathrm{TPR}>\mathrm{FPR}
,

\theta(\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})=\frac{\bar{S}_{J}-\mathrm{FPR}}{\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR}}.

(21)

The extrema of
\theta(\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
are attained at the corner points. Hence

\underline{\theta}=\max\!\left\{0,\;\min_{(\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{\bar{S}_{J}-\mathrm{FPR}}{\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR}}\right\},\quad\overline{\theta}=\min\!\left\{1,\;\max_{(\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{\bar{S}_{J}-\mathrm{FPR}}{\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR}}\right\},

(22)

where

\mathcal{C}=\Bigl\{(\underline{\mathrm{TPR}},\underline{\mathrm{FPR}}),(\underline{\mathrm{TPR}},\overline{\mathrm{FPR}}),(\overline{\mathrm{TPR}},\underline{\mathrm{FPR}}),(\overline{\mathrm{TPR}},\overline{\mathrm{FPR}})\Bigr\}.

(23)

The target
\theta
must lie in a corresponding feasible interval

\theta\in[\underline{\theta},\;\overline{\theta}],

(24)

where the bounds are induced by the above ranges of TPR and FPR.

Let
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{PPI++}}
denote the unconstrained PPI++ estimator defined in
equation
F.2.5
. The projected PPI++ estimator is then given by the clipping operation

\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{PPI++}}^{\mathrm{Proj}}=\min\!\left\{\max\!\left\{\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{PPI++}},\,\underline{\theta}\right\},\,\overline{\theta}\right\}.

(25)

This projection forces
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{PPI++}}
to be consistent with the LLM failure rate implied by the constraints on the judge TPR and FPR.

F.2.7
Maximum Likelihood Estimators

We also offer additional information about the maximum likelihood estimators used in our experiments, including how we set the constraints associated with the CMLE both in the fully specified and the misspecified settings.

Unconstrained MLE.

The UMLE jointly estimates

(\theta,\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
by maximizing the joint likelihood in equation
7
induced by the labeled dataset and the noisy unlabeled dataset, with no restrictions imposed on the judge parameters. We solve the UMLE optimization problem using the projected gradient ascent algorithm in equation
9
with a fixed learning rate of
10^{-6}
with 200 iterations. The parameters
(\theta,\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
are initialized using the labeled dataset. This estimator serves as a baseline that does not rely on any prior knowledge about the judge.

Constrained MLE (with correctly specified constraints).

The CMLE – a constrained variant of the UMLE – estimates

(\theta,\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
by solving the optimization problem in equation
10
.
The judge-related parameters are restricted to lie within intervals centered at their ground-truth values:

\displaystyle\mathrm{TPR}

\displaystyle\in\big[(1-\delta)\,\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{true}},\;(1+\delta)\,\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{true}}\big],

(26)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FPR}

\displaystyle\in\big[(1-\delta)\,\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{true}},\;(1+\delta)\,\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{true}}\big],

where
\delta\geq 0
controls the width of the constraint region. In the synthetic experiments, the ground-truth values
\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{true}}
and
\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{true}}
are known by construction. In real-world datasets, where the true noise parameters are not directly observable, we define
\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{true}}
and
\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{true}}
as empirical reference values computed from the full datasets. We solve this CMLE optimization problem using the projected gradient ascent algorithm in equation
9
with a fixed learning rate of
10^{-6}
with 200 iterations. The parameters
(\theta,\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
are initialized using the small labeled dataset.

Constrained MLE (with misspecified constraints).

The CMLE also estimates
(\theta,\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
by solving the optimization problem in equation
10
. However, the constraints are centered at anchor values,
\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{anchor}}
and
\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{anchor}}
, that may differ from the true values as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{TPR}

\displaystyle\in\big[(1-\delta)\,\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{anchor}},\;(1+\delta)\,\mathrm{TPR}_{\mathrm{anchor}}\big],

(27)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FPR}

\displaystyle\in\big[(1-\delta)\,\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{anchor}},\;(1+\delta)\,\mathrm{FPR}_{\mathrm{anchor}}\big].

In the synthetic experiments, the anchor values for the judge’s parameters, namely TPR and FPR, can be intentionally set to differ from their ground-truth values used in the data-generating process.
In the real-world datasets, the anchor values for the judge’s parameters are obtained from other related datasets using the same target LLM and judge. We also solve this CMLE optimization problem using the projected gradient ascent algorithm in equation
9
with a fixed learning rate of
10^{-6}
with 200 iterations. The parameters
(\theta,\mathrm{TPR},\mathrm{FPR})
are initialized using the small labeled dataset.

F.3
Performance Metrics

We evaluate all estimators by computing the mean and the variance of the estimates via a Monte Carlo (MC) protocol with
B=2000
independent replications. Concretely, for each specific experimental configuration, we estimate the mean and variance of the estimator as follows:

\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{\theta}]\;=\;\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{\theta}^{(b)}

(28)

\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\!\left(\hat{\theta}\right)\;=\;\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\Big(\hat{\theta}^{(b)}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{\theta}]\Big)^{2},

(29)

We also estimate the bias and mean-squared error of the estimator as follows:

\widehat{\mathrm{Bias}}\;=\;\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{\theta}]-\theta,

(30)

\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}\;=\;\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\Big(\hat{\theta}^{(b)}-\theta\Big)^{2}.

(31)

Note that in experiments with the synthetic datasets, the true parameter value
\theta
is pre-known by construction. In experiments with real-world datasets, where the true parameter is not directly observable, we define
\theta
as an empirical reference value computed from the full available dataset.
