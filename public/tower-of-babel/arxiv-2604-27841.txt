On the free Banach lattice generated by a lattice

. We study structural properties of the free Banach lattice FBL ​ ⟨ L ⟩ \mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle generated by a distributive lattice L L . 1.
Introduction

The construction of free Banach lattices has become a useful tool for
studying the interaction between universal properties, Banach space versus lattice
properties, and order structure. Quite some time after the introduction of free vector lattices in
[
10
]
, the first appearance of free Banach lattices is due to
de Pagter and Wickstead who introduced the free Banach lattice
\mathrm{FBL}(A)

associated with a set
A

[
16
]
:
\mathrm{FBL}(A)
is a Banach lattice together with a bounded map
\delta:A\rightarrow\mathrm{FBL}(A)
such that for every Banach lattice
X
and bounded map
T:A\rightarrow X
, there is a unique Banach lattice homomorphism
\widehat{T}:\mathrm{FBL}(A)\rightarrow X
making the following diagram commutative

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces FBL(A)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widehat{T}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\delta}

and such that
\|\widehat{T}\|=\sup_{a\in A}\|Ta\|
.

Avilés, Rodríguez, and the second author
subsequently constructed the free Banach lattice
\mathrm{FBL}[E]
generated
by a Banach space
E

[
7
]
, which generalizes the above construction, allowing to identify
\mathrm{FBL}(A)

lattice isometrically with
\mathrm{FBL}[\ell_{1}(A)]
. In recent years, these objects have received considerable attention in a number of different research directions, including: projectivity
[
1
]
, chain conditions
[
4
]
, convexity
[
19
]
, octahedrality
[
12
]
, approximation properties
[
22
]
, complex Banach lattices
[
14
]
, duality
[
18
]
, upper
p
-estimates
[
17
]
, quasi-Banach spaces
[
24
]
,
f
-algebras
[
21
]
… We refer to the monograph
[
23
]
for further developements.

It is of course natural to consider how this construction changes when the generating object
already carries an order structure. For a distributive lattice
L
, Avilés and
Rodríguez Abellán introduced the free Banach lattice
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
generated
by
L

[
5
]
. This is characterized by a bounded lattice homomorphism

\delta_{L}:L\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
with the following universal property: for every
Banach lattice
X
and every bounded lattice homomorphism
T:L\to X
, there
is a unique Banach lattice homomorphism
\widehat{T}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to X
satisfying

\widehat{T}\delta_{L}=T
and
\|\widehat{T}\|=\sup_{x\in L}\|Tx\|
. See also
[
15
]
for free Banach lattices generated by ordered Banach spaces.

Several structural aspects of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
have been studied. In particular, Avilés and Rodríguez Abellán
[
5
]
gave a concrete description of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
as a Banach lattice of real-valued functions on the set
L^{*}
of all lattice homomorphisms from
L
to
[-1,1]
. Later, in
[
3
]
, they analyzed the behavior of the canonical homomorphism induced by a lattice embedding
i:L\hookrightarrow M
, proving that the induced operator

\overline{i}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\longrightarrow\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle

is injective if and only if every lattice homomorphism on
L
extends to
M
, and that it is isometric when
L
is locally complemented in
M
. They also obtained several projectivity results and the surprising fact that
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is lattice isomorphic to an AM-space (see also
[
2
,
6
]
).

The purpose of this paper is to continue the investigation of the structural properties of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
by exploiting its functional representation. Our main contributions are as follows. We first characterize exactly when
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
admits a strong unit: this happens if and only if
L
possesses both a maximum and a minimum (Theorem
3.2
). As a consequence, we determine when
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
has the strong Nakano property. We then compute the density character of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
, showing that
\operatorname{dens}(\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle)=\max\{\aleph_{0},|L|\}
(Theorem
4.1
); in particular,
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is separable precisely when
L
is countable. Turning to order intervals, we demonstrate that, unlike the case of
\mathrm{FBL}[E]
, order intervals in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
can have very different density characters. We find a connection between order density of the vector sublattice
FVL\langle L\rangle
in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
in terms of countable order bounded lattices (Theorem
4.9
). We also prove that
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
possesses no non-trivial projection bands whenever
|L|>1
(Theorem
5.4
), and we characterize the existence of a quasi-interior point in terms of a countable separating subset of
L
(Theorem
6.1
).

Furthermore, we study Banach lattice homomorphisms between free Banach lattices over lattices. We show that the canonical extension
\overline{T}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle
of a lattice homomorphism
T:L\to M
is given by composition with the dual map, and we prove that surjectivity of
T
implies surjectivity of
\overline{T}
(Theorem
7.3
). Finally, we show that
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is always lattice isometric to
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L^{\mathrm{op}}\rangle
(Proposition
8.2
). These results provide a comprehensive structural picture of the free Banach lattice generated by a lattice.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Lattices and lattice homomorphisms

We recall the order-theoretic notation and the construction of the free
Banach lattice generated by a lattice. All lattices considered below are
assumed to be non-empty. When the functional representation of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is
used,
L
is assumed to be distributive.

Definition 2.1

(Lattice)
.

A
lattice
is a set
L
equipped with a partial order
\leq
such that
every pair
x,y\in L
admits a supremum

x\vee y=\sup\{x,y\}

and an infimum

x\wedge y=\inf\{x,y\}.

Definition 2.2

(Distributive lattice)
.

A lattice
(L,\vee,\wedge)
is called
distributive
if for all

x,y,z\in L
one has

x\wedge(y\vee z)=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z),

and, equivalently,

x\vee(y\wedge z)=(x\vee y)\wedge(x\vee z).

Definition 2.3

(Lattice homomorphism)
.

Let
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
be lattices. A map
\phi:L_{1}\to L_{2}
is a

lattice homomorphism
if

\phi(x\vee y)=\phi(x)\vee\phi(y),\qquad\phi(x\wedge y)=\phi(x)\wedge\phi(y),\qquad x,y\in L_{1}.

For a subset
A
of a Banach lattice, we denote by
lat(A)
the smallest
vector sublattice containing
A
, that is, the set of all lattice-linear
combinations of elements of
A
.

2.2.
Construction of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle

Let
L
be a distributive lattice. We recall the main constructions of the
free Banach lattice generated by
L
, introduced in
[
5
]
and further
developed in
[
2
,
3
]
.

Definition 2.4

(Free Banach lattice generated by a lattice)
.

The
free Banach lattice generated by
L
is a Banach lattice
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle

together with a bounded lattice homomorphism
\delta_{L}:L\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
satisfying
the following universal property: for every Banach lattice
X
and every
bounded lattice homomorphism
T:L\to X
, there exists a unique Banach lattice
homomorphism
\widehat{T}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to X
such that

\widehat{T}\circ\delta_{L}=T
and

\|\widehat{T}\|=\sup_{x\in L}\|Tx\|
.

The universal property determines
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
uniquely up to canonical lattice
isometric isomorphism; see
[
5
, Section 2]
.

Let
L^{*}
denote the set of all lattice homomorphisms

x^{*}:L\to[-1,1]
.
For each
x\in L
define

\delta_{x}(x^{*})=x^{*}(x),\qquad x^{*}\in L^{*}.

A function
f:L^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
positively homogeneous
if

f(\lambda x^{*})=\lambda f(x^{*})
for every
x^{*}\in L^{*}
and every

\lambda\geq 0
such that
\lambda x^{*}\in L^{*}
, where scalar multiplication is
understood pointwise. For such a function, let

(1)

\|f\|=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}|f(x^{*}_{i})|:m\in\mathbb{N},\ x^{*}_{1},\dots,x^{*}_{m}\in L^{*},\ \sup_{x\in L}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|x^{*}_{i}(x)|\leq 1\right\}.

For
x\in L
, the evaluation map
\delta_{x}:L^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\delta_{x}(x^{*})=x^{*}(x)
is positively homogeneous and satisfies

\|\delta_{x}\|\leq 1
.

Consider the Banach lattice generated by
\{\delta_{x}:x\in L\}
inside
the space of all positively homogeneous functions
f\in\mathbb{R}^{L^{*}}
with

\|f\|<\infty
, endowed with this norm and the pointwise lattice operations.
Together with the assignment
\delta_{L}(x)=\delta_{x}
, this Banach lattice has
the universal property above. Thus
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
can be represented as a Banach
lattice of functions on the dual object
L^{*}
.

We also recall an alternative description using a quotient of a free Banach lattice generated by a set: Forget the lattice structure of

L
temporarily and regard it as a set. Let
\mathrm{FBL}(L)
be the free
Banach lattice generated by this set, with canonical map

u:L\to\mathrm{FBL}(L)
, as in
[
16
]
. Let
I
be the closed ideal of

\mathrm{FBL}(L)
generated by

u(x)\vee u(y)-u(x\vee y),\qquad u(x)\wedge u(y)-u(x\wedge y),\qquad x,y\in L.

These relations force the image of
L
to respect the lattice operations. If

q:\mathrm{FBL}(L)\to\mathrm{FBL}(L)/I
denotes the quotient map, then

\delta_{L}:=q\circ u
is a lattice homomorphism, and

\mathrm{FBL}(L)/I
has the universal property defining
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
.

It should be noted that although
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
can be actually defined even when
L
is not distributive, in that case, the
\delta
map is not injective. In fact, it is shown in
[
5
]
that one can replace
L
with
L^{\prime}=\delta(L)
and identify
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
with
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L^{\prime}\rangle
. Thus, for our purposes and throughout this paper we will assume all lattices are distributive unless stated otherwise.

3.
Strong units in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle

By
[
2
, Lemma 2.5]
, if
L
has a maximum
M_{L}
and a minimum
m_{L}
,
then
|\delta_{m_{L}}|\vee|\delta_{M_{L}}|
is a strong unit for
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
.
Moreover,
[
2
, Theorem 2.7]
identifies
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
, up to an equivalent
norm, with
C(K_{L})
, where

K_{L}=\{x^{*}\in L^{*}:\max\{|x^{*}(m_{L})|,|x^{*}(M_{L})|\}=1\}.

We prove that the existence of both a maximum and a minimum is also necessary
for
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
to have a strong unit. This is analogous to

[
23
, Proposition 9.1]
, where
\mathrm{FBL}[E]
is shown to have a
strong unit precisely when
E
is finite-dimensional.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
L
be a distributive lattice and let
y\nleq x
in
L
. Then there exists

x^{*}\in L^{*}
such that
x^{*}(x)=0
,
x^{*}(y)=1
, and

0\leq x^{*}(z)\leq 1
for every
z\in L
.

Proof.

Since
L
is distributive,
[
5
, Proposition 3.1]
implies that

\delta:L\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is injective. Hence there is
x_{0}^{*}\in L^{*}
such that

x_{0}^{*}(x)<x_{0}^{*}(y)
. For
z\in L
, define

y^{*}(z)=x_{0}^{*}(x)\vee\bigl(x_{0}^{*}(z)\wedge x_{0}^{*}(y)\bigr),

and set

x^{*}(z)=\frac{y^{*}(z)-y^{*}(x)}{y^{*}(y)-y^{*}(x)}.

Then
x^{*}
is a lattice homomorphism satisfying the required conditions.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
L
be a lattice. Then
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
has a strong unit if and only if
L

has a maximum and a minimum.

Proof.

By
[
5
, Proposition 3.2]
, we may assume without loss of generality that

L
is distributive. If
L
has a maximum and a minimum, the result follows
from
[
2
, Lemma 2.5]
.

Conversely, suppose that
f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is a strong unit. Then, for every

x\in L
, we have
|\delta_{x}|\leq f
. Let

g\in lat\{\delta_{x}:x\in L\}
. Then
g
is a lattice-linear combination of

\delta_{x_{1}},\dots,\delta_{x_{n}}
for some
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in L
.

Suppose first that
L
has no maximum. Then there exists

y\in L
such that
y>x_{1}\vee\cdots\vee x_{n}
. Applying
Lemma
3.1
with

x=x_{1}\vee\cdots\vee x_{n}
, we obtain
z^{*}\in L^{*}
such that

z^{*}(y)=1
,
z^{*}(x)=0
, and
0\leq z^{*}(z)\leq 1
for all
z\in L
.
In particular,
z^{*}(x_{i})=0
for every
i
, and therefore
g(z^{*})=0
.
Hence

\|f-g\|\geq|f(z^{*})-g(z^{*})|=|f(z^{*})|\geq|\delta_{y}(z^{*})|=1.

Thus
f
cannot be approximated by elements of
lat\{\delta_{x}:x\in L\}
,
contradicting the definition of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
as the closed Banach lattice
generated by the functions
\delta_{x}
.

If
L
has no minimum, we apply the same argument to the opposite lattice

L^{\mathrm{op}}
. Then
L^{\mathrm{op}}
has no maximum, and
Proposition
8.2
shows that
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is lattice
isometric to
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L^{\mathrm{op}}\rangle
. Thus a strong unit in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
would
yield a strong unit in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L^{\mathrm{op}}\rangle
, contradicting the previous
paragraph. Hence
L
has both a maximum and a minimum.
∎

Recall that a Banach lattice
X
has the
strong Nakano property
if
every upward directed norm-bounded subset of
X
has a supremum. The strong Nakano property was studied in the context of free Banach lattices generated by a Banach space in
[
8
]
. For
AM-spaces this property is equivalent to the existence of a strong unit.
The following corollary is therefore an immediate consequence of
Theorem
3.2
and the fact that
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is lattice
isomorphic to an AM-space
[
3
, Theorem 3.9]
.

Corollary 3.3
.

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
has the strong Nakano property if and only if
L
has a maximum and a minimum.

Proof.

An AM-space has the strong Nakano property if and only if it has a strong
unit
[
8
, Proposition 2.1]
. Since
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is lattice isomorphic to an
AM-space by
[
3
, Theorem 3.9]
, the result follows from
Theorem
3.2
.
∎

4.
Density character in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle

Recall that the density character of a topological space
S
, denoted by

\operatorname{dens}(S)
, is the least cardinality of a dense subset. For a Banach space

E
, it is easy to check that
\operatorname{dens}(\mathrm{FBL}[E])=\operatorname{dens}(E)

[
7
, Section 3]
. In this section we study the corresponding question
for
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
and then discuss the density character of order intervals.

Theorem 4.1
.

For every non-empty lattice
L
, we have

\operatorname{dens}(\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle)=\max\{\aleph_{0},|L|\}.

Proof.

By
[
5
, Section 3]
, we may assume without loss of generality that
L

is distributive. Given
x\in L
, the line

\{t\delta_{x}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is contained in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
, and hence

\operatorname{dens}(\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle)\geq\aleph_{0}
.

Also, if
x\neq y
in
L
, then, after interchanging
x
and
y
if
necessary, we may assume that
x\wedge y<x
. By
Lemma
3.1
, there exists
x^{*}\in L^{*}
such that

x^{*}(x\wedge y)=0
,
x^{*}(x)=1
, and
0\leq x^{*}(z)\leq 1
for every
z\in L
.
Since
x^{*}
is a lattice homomorphism, this gives
x^{*}(y)=0
, and therefore

\|\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}\|\geq|x^{*}(x)-x^{*}(y)|=1>0.

Thus
\{\delta_{x}:x\in L\}
is a
1
-separated subset of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
, and so

\operatorname{dens}(\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle)\geq|L|.

Conversely,
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is the quotient of
\mathrm{FBL}(L)
by the closed ideal
generated by

\{u(x)\vee u(y)-u(x\vee y),\ u(x)\wedge u(y)-u(x\wedge y):x,y\in L\},

where
u:L\to\mathrm{FBL}(L)
is the canonical embedding; see

[
5
, Section 2]
. Hence

\operatorname{dens}(\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle)\leq\operatorname{dens}(\mathrm{FBL}(L))=\max\{\aleph_{0},|L|\}.

The equality
\operatorname{dens}(\mathrm{FBL}(L))=\max\{\aleph_{0},|L|\}
follows from

[
16
, Section 8]
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In particular,
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is separable if and only if
L
is countable.

In the free Banach lattice generated by a Banach space, all order intervals
have the same density character
[
7
, Section 3]
. As we will see, the
situation in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is more involved. For
a\leq b
in
L
, write

[a,b]_{L}=\{x\in L:a\leq x\leq b\}.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
L
be a distributive lattice and let
a,b\in L
with
a<b
. Then

\max\{\aleph_{0},|[a,b]_{L}|\}\leq\operatorname{dens}\big([\delta(a),\delta(b)]_{\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle}\big)\leq\max\{\aleph_{0},|L|\}.

Proof.

Since
\delta(a)+t(\delta(b)-\delta(a))\in[\delta(a),\delta(b)]_{\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle}

for every
t\in[0,1]
, we have

\operatorname{dens}([\delta(a),\delta(b)]_{\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle})\geq\aleph_{0}
. Also, for distinct

x,y\in[a,b]_{L}
, Lemma
3.1
gives

x^{*}\in L^{*}
such that
x^{*}(x)=0
and
x^{*}(y)=1
, or vice versa. Hence

\|\delta(x)-\delta(y)\|\geq|x^{*}(x)-x^{*}(y)|\geq 1.

Since
\delta(x),\delta(y)\in[\delta(a),\delta(b)]_{\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle}
, the set

\{\delta(x):x\in[a,b]_{L}\}
is
1
-separated in the interval. Therefore

\operatorname{dens}\big([\delta(a),\delta(b)]_{\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle}\big)\geq|[a,b]_{L}|.

The upper estimate follows from

\operatorname{dens}\big([\delta(a),\delta(b)]_{\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle}\big)\leq\operatorname{dens}(\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle)=\max\{\aleph_{0},|L|\},

using Theorem
4.1
.
∎

The preceding estimate does not imply that every interval must have large
density when
|L|
is large, as the following example shows.

Example 4.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a set and let
L=\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
, with

\wedge=\cap
and
\vee=\cup
. Fix
\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma
and put

a=\emptyset
and
b=\{\gamma_{0}\}
. We claim that

[\delta(a),\delta(b)]_{\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle}
is separable.

Define

U=\{x^{*}\in L^{*}:x^{*}(b)>x^{*}(a)\},

and consider the space
X_{U}
of real-valued continuous functions on
U
,
equipped with the norm

\|f\|=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}|f(x^{*}_{i})|:m\in\mathbb{N},\ x^{*}_{1},\dots,x^{*}_{m}\in U,\ \sup_{x\in L}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|x^{*}_{i}(x)|\leq 1\right\}.

Let
r:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to X_{U}
be the restriction map to
U
. Then
r
is a lattice
homomorphism and is isometric on
[\delta(a),\delta(b)]
: if

f\in[\delta(a),\delta(b)]
and
x^{*}\notin U
, then

f(x^{*})=x^{*}(a)=x^{*}(b)
.

For
A\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
, if
\gamma_{0}\notin A
, then

r(\delta(A))=r(\delta(a))
, while if
\gamma_{0}\in A
, then

r(\delta(A))=r(\delta(b))
.

Thus
r[\delta(a),\delta(b)]
is contained in the separable sublattice
generated by
r(\delta(a))
and
r(\delta(b))
. Since
r
is isometric on the
interval,
[\delta(a),\delta(b)]
is separable.

Remark 4.5
.

Thus order intervals in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
need not have the same density character.
Indeed, let
\Gamma
be infinite and let
L=\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
as above.
Then

\operatorname{dens}([\delta(\emptyset),\delta(\{\gamma_{0}\})])=\aleph_{0}.

On the other hand, for every infinite
A\subset\Gamma
,

\operatorname{dens}([\delta(\emptyset),\delta(A)])\geq|[\emptyset,A]_{L}|=2^{|A|}>\aleph_{0}.

Remark 4.6
.

In general, there is no upper bound on
\operatorname{dens}([\delta(a),\delta(b)])

depending only on
|[a,b]_{L}|
. To see this, fix an infinite cardinal

\kappa
and choose a set
\Gamma
with
|\Gamma|=\kappa
.
Let

L=\{\hat{0}\}\cup\mathcal{P}(\Gamma),

ordered so that
\hat{0}
is a new minimum below every element of

\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
, while on
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
we use the usual
operations
\wedge=\cap
and
\vee=\cup
. Then
L
is a distributive lattice.

Define

a=\hat{0},\qquad b=\emptyset\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma).

Then
a<b
and

|[a,b]_{L}|=|\{\hat{0},\emptyset\}|=2.

For each
\gamma\in\Gamma
, define
x^{*}_{\gamma}\in L^{*}
by

x^{*}_{\gamma}(\hat{0})=-1,\qquad x^{*}_{\gamma}(A)=\chi_{A}(\gamma)\quad(A\subseteq\Gamma).

For each
\gamma\in\Gamma
, consider

f_{\gamma}=\bigl((\delta(a)+\delta(\{\gamma\}))\vee\delta(a)\bigr)\wedge\delta(b)\in[\delta(a),\delta(b)]\subset\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle.

Note that

\delta(a)(x^{*}_{\gamma})=x^{*}_{\gamma}(\hat{0})=-1,\qquad\delta(b)(x^{*}_{\gamma})=x^{*}_{\gamma}(\emptyset)=0,

and

\delta(\{\gamma\})(x^{*}_{\gamma})=x^{*}_{\gamma}(\{\gamma\})=1,\qquad\delta(\{\gamma^{\prime}\})(x^{*}_{\gamma})=x^{*}_{\gamma}(\{\gamma^{\prime}\})=0\quad(\gamma^{\prime}\neq\gamma).

Therefore,

f_{\gamma}(x^{*}_{\gamma})=\min\{\max\{0,-1\},0\}=0,\qquad f_{\gamma^{\prime}}(x^{*}_{\gamma})=\min\{\max\{-1,-1\},0\}=-1\quad(\gamma^{\prime}\neq\gamma),

so

|(f_{\gamma}-f_{\gamma^{\prime}})(x^{*}_{\gamma})|=1\qquad(\gamma\neq\gamma^{\prime}).

Therefore

\|f_{\gamma}-f_{\gamma^{\prime}}\|\geq\sup_{x^{*}\in L^{*}}|(f_{\gamma}-f_{\gamma^{\prime}})(x^{*})|\geq|(f_{\gamma}-f_{\gamma^{\prime}})(x^{*}_{\gamma})|=1.

Thus
\{f_{\gamma}:\gamma\in\Gamma\}\subseteq[\delta(a),\delta(b)]
is a

1
-separated set of cardinality
\kappa
, and hence

\operatorname{dens}([\delta(a),\delta(b)])\geq\kappa.

Since
\kappa
was arbitrary while
|[a,b]_{L}|=2
, no upper bound on

\operatorname{dens}([\delta(a),\delta(b)])
can depend only on
|[a,b]_{L}|
.

Recall that in the setting of the free Banach lattice generated by a Banach space,
FVL[E]
, the vector lattice generated by
\delta(E)
, is order dense in
FBL[E]
if and only if
E
is finite dimensional (see
[
9
,
23
]
). Our next aim is to study which lattices
L
satisfy that
\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle
, the vector lattice generated by
\delta(L)
, is order dense in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
. We need some preparatory lemmas first.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
L
be a lattice with minimum
0_{L}
and maximum
1_{L}
, and let

K_{L}=\{x^{*}\in L^{*}:\max\{|x^{*}(0_{L})|,|x^{*}(1_{L})|\}=1\}

be endowed with the product topology. If
U\subseteq K_{L}
is open,

x_{0}^{*}\in U
, and
\varepsilon>0
, then there exists

g\in\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle_{+}
such that
g(x_{0}^{*})>0
,

g\leq\varepsilon
on
U
, and
g=0
on
K_{L}\setminus U
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is lattice isomorphic to
C(K_{L})

[
2
, Section 2]
. Under this isomorphism,

\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle
corresponds to a dense sublattice of
C(K_{L})

containing the constant functions. Indeed, the constant function
1

corresponds to
|\delta_{0_{L}}|\vee|\delta_{1_{L}}|
.

Let
U\subseteq K_{L}
be open, let
x_{0}^{*}\in U
, and let
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
K_{L}
is compact Hausdorff, Urysohn’s lemma gives

v\in C(K_{L})
such that

0\leq v\leq 1,\qquad v(x_{0}^{*})=1,\qquad v=0\text{ on }K_{L}\setminus U.

Choose
u\in\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle
such that

\|v-u\|_{\infty}<1/3
.

Set
w=(u-1/3)^{+}
. Then
w\in\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle_{+}
.
If
x^{*}\in K_{L}\setminus U
, then
v(x^{*})=0
and
u(x^{*})<1/3
, so

w(x^{*})=0
. Moreover,
u(x_{0}^{*})>2/3
, and hence
w(x_{0}^{*})>1/3
.
Finally, since
u\leq v+1/3\leq 4/3
, we have
w\leq 1
.

Set
g=\varepsilon w
. Then
g\in\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle_{+}
,

g(x_{0}^{*})>0
,
g=0
on
K_{L}\setminus U
, and
g\leq\varepsilon
on
U
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
L
be a distributive lattice with a maximum and a minimum. Then

\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle
is order dense in

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
.

Proof.

Let
0_{L}
and
1_{L}
denote the minimum and maximum of
L
, respectively.
Recall that
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is lattice isomorphic to

C(K_{L})

[
2
, Section 2]
, where

K_{L}=\{x^{*}\in L^{*}:\max\{|x^{*}(0_{L})|,|x^{*}(1_{L})|\}=1\}

is endowed with the product topology.

Let
0<f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
. Choose
x_{0}^{*}\in K_{L}
such that

f(x_{0}^{*})>0
. By continuity, there are an open neighbourhood

U\subseteq K_{L}
of
x_{0}^{*}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

f(x^{*})>\varepsilon
for all
x^{*}\in U
.

By Lemma
4.7
, there exists

g\in\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle_{+}
such that
g(x_{0}^{*})>0
,

g\leq\varepsilon
on
U
, and
g=0
on
K_{L}\setminus U
.

If
x^{*}\in U
, then
g(x^{*})\leq\varepsilon<f(x^{*})
, while if

x^{*}\in K_{L}\setminus U
, then
g(x^{*})=0\leq f(x^{*})
. Hence

0<g\leq f
. Since
f>0
was arbitrary,

\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle
is order dense in

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
.
∎

Let us say that a lattice
L
is
countably order bounded
if
for every countable subset
A\subseteq L
there are
a,b\in L
such that

a\leq x\leq b\qquad(x\in A).

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
L
be a distributive lattice. If
L
is countably order bounded, then
FVL\langle L\rangle
is order dense in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
. If
L
is totally ordered, the converse also holds.

Proof.

Suppose first that
L
is countably order bounded and let

0<f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle_{+}.

Since
f
depends on a countable set
A\subseteq L
, there are
a,b\in L
such that

a\leq x\leq b\qquad(x\in A).

Let
I=[a,b]_{L}
. This interval is a bounded distributive lattice, with
minimum
a
and maximum
b
, and it contains
A
. The maps

\begin{array}[]{cclcc}i:I\longrightarrow L,&&i(x)=x,&&\text{ and }\\
r:L\longrightarrow I,&&r(x)=a\vee(x\wedge b),&&\end{array}

are lattice homomorphism with
ri(x)=x
for
x\in I
. The universal property allows to extend these to Banach lattice homomorphisms
\overline{i}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle I\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
and
\overline{r}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle I\rangle
(see Section
7
). By
[
3
, Proposition 3.1]
,
\overline{i}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle I\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is an isometric embedding, so we can consider
\mathrm{FBL}\langle I\rangle
as a complemented sublattice of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
(the projection being the composition
\overline{i}\overline{r}
.)

By Lemma
4.8
, there is
g\in FVL\langle I\rangle
such that
0<g\leq\overline{r}(f)
.
Since
f
depends only on the coordinates in
A\subseteq I
, we have that

0<\overline{i}g\leq\overline{i}\overline{r}f=f.

Since
\overline{i}g\in FVL\langle L\rangle
, it follows that
FVL\langle L\rangle
is order dense in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
.

For the converse, we assume that
L
is totally ordered. Suppose first that
L

has a countable subset with no common upper bound. Replacing it by the
sequence of its finite suprema, we have an increasing sequence

(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with no upper bound. Fix positive scalars

\alpha_{n}>0
with
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}<1
, and define

f(x^{*})=\left(|\delta_{u_{0}}|(x^{*})-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}|\delta_{u_{n}}|(x^{*})\right)_{+},\qquad x^{*}\in L^{*}.

The series converges uniformly, so
f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle_{+}
. Moreover,
the constant homomorphism
\mathbf{1}:L\to[-1,1]
,
\mathbf{1}(x)=1
, gives

f(\mathbf{1})=1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}>0,

so
f\neq 0
.

Let
0\leq h\in FVL(L)
and suppose
h\leq f
. The function
h

is in the lattice generated by some finite set
\{\delta_{x_{1}},\dots,\delta_{x_{n}}\}
. Let
F
be the finite sublattice of
L
generated by
\{u_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, and let
v=\bigvee F
.

Since
(u_{n})
has no upper bound, and
L
is totally ordered, choose
n
with
v<u_{n}
. Define the
map

y^{*}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x<u_{n},\\
1,&x\geq u_{n}.\end{cases}

Since
L
is totally ordered,
y^{*}\in L^{*}
. Clearly,
y^{*}
vanishes on
F
and
y^{*}(u_{n})=1
.

If
h\neq 0
, then we can choose
x^{*}\in L^{*}
with
h(x^{*})>0
. For
0<t\leq 1
, put

z_{t}^{*}=tx^{*}+(1-t)y^{*}.

Since
L
is totally ordered,
L^{*}
is closed under convex combinations, so

z_{t}^{*}\in L^{*}
. On the coordinates on which
h
depends, we have that

z_{t}^{*}=tx^{*}
. Hence,

h(z_{t}^{*})=t\,h(x^{*})>0.

On the other hand,

f(z_{t}^{*})\leq\left(t|x^{*}(u_{0})|-\alpha_{n}|tx^{*}(u_{n})+(1-t)|\right)_{+}.

For sufficiently small
t>0
, the expression inside the positive part is
negative, and therefore
f(z_{t}^{*})=0
. This contradicts
0\leq h\leq f
.
Hence no non-zero element of
FVL\langle L\rangle_{+}
is dominated by
f
, so
order density fails.

The case where
L
has a countable subset with no common lower bound is
similar.
∎

We do not know whether the hypothesis of
L
being totally ordered can be removed completely from the previous result.

5.
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
has no non-trivial projection bands

The study of projection bands in free Banach lattices goes back to de Pagter
and Wickstead
[
16
]
for the case of a generating set, and was later
observed to extend to free Banach lattices generated by Banach spaces in
[
23
, Section 9]
. In this section, we prove the corresponding result for free Banach lattices generated by
lattices: when
|L|>1
, the only projection bands in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
are
\{0\}

and
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
. We then derive the corresponding consequences for order
completeness, order continuity of the norm, and atoms.

Lemma 5.1
.

For a distributive lattice
L
, the set
L^{*}
contains a non-constant
element if and only if
|L|>1
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove that if
|L|>1
, then
L^{*}
contains a non-constant
element. Take
x\neq y
in
L
. Replacing
x,y
by
x\wedge y
and

x\vee y
, we may assume that
x<y
. Let

J=\{z\in L:z\leq x\},\qquad G=\{z\in L:z\geq y\}.

Then
J
is an ideal,
G
is a filter, and
J\cap G=\emptyset
. By

[
13
, Theorem 10.21]
, there is a prime ideal
I
such that

J\subset I
and
I\cap G=\emptyset
. Hence we can find
x^{*}\in L^{*}
such
that
x^{*}(z)=0
precisely when
z\in I
. This
x^{*}
is not constant.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

If
|L|>1
, then
L^{*}\setminus\{0\}
is path connected.

Proof.

Let
u\in L^{*}
be non-constant, as in the preceding lemma. Hence, the affine paths

t\mapsto(1-t)u+t\mathbf{1}
and
t\mapsto(1-t)u-t\mathbf{1}
do not pass
through the zero function; otherwise
u
would be constant. Thus every
non-constant element is connected to both
\mathbf{1}
and
-\mathbf{1}
.
A non-zero constant homomorphism is connected to either
\mathbf{1}
or

-\mathbf{1}
through non-zero constant homomorphisms. Hence every element
of
L^{*}\setminus\{0\}
lies in the same path component.
∎

Definition 5.3
.

Let
X
be a non-empty set and let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
. Define

O_{f}=\{x\in X:f(x)\neq 0\}.

If
W
is a non-empty subset of
\mathbb{R}^{X}
, define

O_{W}=\bigcup_{f\in W}O_{f}.

Theorem 5.4
.

If
L
is a distributive lattice with
|L|\geq 2
, then the only projection
bands of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
are
\{0\}
and

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
.

Proof.

By
[
11
, Proposition 7.4]
, we may identify

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
with the space
C_{ph}(L^{*})
of continuous
positively homogeneous functions on
L^{*}
. By
[
16
, Proposition 6.1]
,
it is enough to show that
O_{\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle}
is connected.
For
l\in L
, write

O_{\delta_{l}}=\{\xi\in L^{*}:\xi(l)\neq 0\}.

Since

\bigcup_{l\in L}O_{\delta_{l}}=L^{*}\setminus\{0\}

and every positively homogeneous function vanishes at
0
, we obtain

O_{\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle}=L^{*}\setminus\{0\}.

By the preceding lemma, this set is connected. Therefore the only projection
bands are
\{0\}
and the whole space.
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

If
|L|\geq 2
, then:

(1)

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is not Dedekind
\sigma
-complete.

(2)

The norm on
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is not order continuous.

(3)

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
has no atoms.

Proof.

(1)

This follows from
[
20
, Proposition 1.2.11]
.

(2)

This follows from
[
20
, Theorem 2.4.2]
.

(3)

The closed linear span of an atom generates a projection band. Since
there are no non-trivial projection bands, no atoms can exist.

∎

Corollary 5.6
.

If
l\in L
, then
|\delta_{l}|
is a weak order unit of

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
and

f\perp|\delta_{l}|
. Then

O_{f}\subset\{\xi\in L^{*}:\xi(l)=0\}.

The set on the right has empty interior, so
O_{f}=\emptyset
and hence

f=0
.
∎

6.
Quasi-interior points in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle

For a Banach lattice
E
and
u\in E_{+}
, recall that
u
is a

quasi-interior point
if the principal ideal

E_{u}=\{x\in E:|x|\leq cu\text{ for some }c\geq 0\}

is dense in
E
.

For free Banach lattices generated by Banach spaces,
\mathrm{FBL}[E]
has
a quasi-interior point if and only if
E
is separable

[
23
, Theorem 10.4]
. In the lattice setting, the
corresponding condition is the existence of a countable separating subset.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
L
be a distributive lattice. Then
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
has a
quasi-interior point if and only if
L
contains a countable separating
subset, that is, a countable set
S\subset L
such that for every non-zero

\phi\in L^{*}
there exists
s\in S
with
\phi(s)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
u\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle_{+}
is a
quasi-interior point. Since
lat\{\delta_{x}:x\in L\}
is dense in

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
, there is a sequence
(f_{n})
in

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
such that:

(i)

f_{n}\to u
in norm;

(ii)

each
f_{n}
belongs to the vector sublattice generated by

\{\delta_{x}:x\in F_{n}\}
for some finite set
F_{n}\subset L
.

Set
S=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}F_{n}
. Then
S
is countable.

Let
0\neq\phi\in L^{*}
. Choose
x\in L
with
\phi(x)\neq 0
. Since
u
is
quasi-interior, the ideal
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle_{u}
is dense in

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
. Hence there is a sequence
(g_{k})
in this
ideal such that
g_{k}\to\delta_{x}
in norm. Evaluating at
\phi
, we get

g_{k}(\phi)\to\phi(x)\neq 0
, so
g_{k}(\phi)\neq 0
for some
k
. Since

|g_{k}|\leq c_{k}u
for some
c_{k}\geq 0
, it follows that
u(\phi)>0
.

Since
f_{n}\to u
in norm, and evaluation at
\phi
is continuous, we have

f_{n}(\phi)>0
for all sufficiently large
n
. Because
f_{n}
belongs to the
vector sublattice generated by
\{\delta_{s}:s\in F_{n}\}
, this forces

\phi(s)\neq 0
for some
s\in F_{n}\subset S
. Thus
S
is separating.

Conversely, let
S=\{s_{n}:n\geq 1\}
be a countable separating subset. Define

u=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}|\delta_{s_{n}}|\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle_{+}.

The series converges in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
. If

\phi\in L^{*}\setminus\{0\}
, then there exists
s_{n}\in S
such that

\phi(s_{n})\neq 0
, and consequently

u(\phi)\geq 2^{-n}|\delta_{s_{n}}(\phi)|>0.

We prove that
u
is quasi-interior. Fix

f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
and
\varepsilon>0
.

Since
f
is continuous on
L^{*}
and
f(0)=0
, there is a neighbourhood

V
of
0
in
L^{*}
such that

|f(\phi)|<\varepsilon/2\qquad(\phi\in V).

Put
K=L^{*}\setminus V
. Then
K
is compact and
0\notin K
. Since
u
is
continuous and
u(\phi)>0
for every
\phi\in K
,

\delta:=\min_{\phi\in K}u(\phi)>0.

Choose an integer
m
such that
m\delta>\|f\|_{\infty}
and set

f_{m}=(f\wedge mu)\vee(-mu)\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle_{u}.

For
\phi\in K
, the inequality

mu(\phi)\geq m\delta>\|f\|_{\infty}\geq|f(\phi)|
implies

f_{m}(\phi)=f(\phi)
.

For
\phi\in V
, the elementary inequality

|(a\wedge b)\vee(-b)|\leq|a|
for
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\geq 0
gives

|f_{m}(\phi)|\leq|f(\phi)|
. Hence

|f_{m}(\phi)-f(\phi)|\leq|f_{m}(\phi)|+|f(\phi)|<\varepsilon.

Thus
\|f_{m}-f\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary,

f
belongs to the closure of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle_{u}
. Therefore

\overline{\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle_{u}}=\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
, so
u
is a quasi-interior point.
∎

7.
Banach lattice homomorphisms on
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle

7.1.
Induced Banach lattice homomorphisms

Given a lattice homomorphism
T:L\to M
between distributive lattices, the
universal property yields a unique Banach lattice homomorphism

\overline{T}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle
satisfying

\overline{T}\circ\delta_{L}=\delta_{M}\circ T
. This is analogous to the
operator induced by a bounded linear map between Banach spaces in the theory
of
\mathrm{FBL}[E]
. We describe
\overline{T}
as a composition operator
and discuss surjectivity.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
T:L\to M
be a lattice homomorphism. Then the induced extension

\overline{T}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle

is given, for
f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
, by

\overline{T}(f)=f\circ T^{*},

where
T^{*}:M^{*}\to L^{*}
is defined by
T^{*}(x^{*})=x^{*}\circ T
.

Proof.

Consider the composition operator induced by
T^{*}
:

C_{T^{*}}f(x^{*})=f(T^{*}x^{*})\qquad(f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle,\ x^{*}\in M^{*}).

Then
C_{T^{*}}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to C(M^{*})
is a lattice homomorphism. Moreover, for
every
x\in L
,

C_{T^{*}}(\delta_{x})=\delta_{Tx},

so the uniqueness part of the universal property gives

\overline{T}=C_{T^{*}}.

∎

Remark 7.2
.

If
\overline{T}
is injective, then
T
is injective. The converse does not
hold in general; see
[
3
, Example 4.4]
.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
T:L\to M
be a lattice homomorphism. If
T
is surjective, then the
induced Banach lattice homomorphism

\overline{T}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle

is surjective.

Proof.

Let

Z=\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle/\ker(\overline{T})

and let
Q:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to Z
be the quotient map. There is
a Banach lattice homomorphism
S:Z\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle
such that

\overline{T}=SQ.

Since
T
is surjective, define
R:M\to Z
by

R(Tx)=Q\delta_{L}(x)\qquad(x\in L).

This is well defined: if
T(x)=T(y)
, then

\overline{T}(\delta_{L}(x))=\delta_{M}(Tx)=\delta_{M}(Ty)=\overline{T}(\delta_{L}(y)),

so
\delta_{L}(x)-\delta_{L}(y)\in\ker(\overline{T})
and

Q\delta_{L}(x)=Q\delta_{L}(y)
. Thus
Q\delta_{L}=RT
.

Let
\widehat{R}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle\to Z
be the canonical
extension of
R
. For
y\in M
, choose
x\in L
with
y=Tx
. Then

S\widehat{R}\delta_{M}(y)=SR(y)=SRT(x)=SQ\delta_{L}(x)=\overline{T}\delta_{L}(x)=\delta_{M}(y).

Thus
S\widehat{R}
is the identity on the range of
\delta_{M}
, and hence on
the sublattice generated by it. Since this sublattice is dense in
\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle
,

S\widehat{R}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle}
. Therefore
S
is surjective, and consequently

\overline{T}=SQ
is surjective.
∎

Remark 7.4
.

A modification of
[
3
, Example 4.4]
shows that the converse to
Theorem
7.3
fails in general. Let

L=\{0,a,b,1\}
with
a\wedge b=0
and
a\vee b=1
, and let

M=\{0,a,1\}
be a proper sublattice. The inclusion

\iota:M\to L
is not surjective. We claim, however, that

\overline{\iota}:\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
is onto. First observe that

\iota^{*}:L^{*}\to M^{*}
, given by
\iota^{*}x^{*}=x^{*}\circ\iota
, is injective.
Indeed, for every
x^{*}\in L^{*}
we have

\{x^{*}(0),x^{*}(1)\}=\{x^{*}(a),x^{*}(b)\}
. If

\iota^{*}x_{1}^{*}=\iota^{*}x_{2}^{*}
, then

\{x_{1}^{*}(a),x_{1}^{*}(b)\}=\{x_{1}^{*}(0),x_{1}^{*}(1)\}=\{x_{2}^{*}(0),x_{2}^{*}(1)\}=\{x_{2}^{*}(a),x_{2}^{*}(b)\}.

It follows that
x_{1}^{*}(b)=x_{2}^{*}(b)
, and hence
x_{1}^{*}=x_{2}^{*}
. Thus

\iota^{*}
is injective. Since
\overline{\iota}
is the composition operator
associated with
\iota^{*}
, surjectivity of
\overline{\iota}
follows.

Remark 7.5
.

For a lattice homomorphism
T:M\to L
, Theorem
7.3
shows that
surjectivity of
\overline{T}
is equivalent to surjectivity of

\overline{\iota}
, where
\iota:T(M)\hookrightarrow L
is the inclusion. If

L
has a maximum
M_{L}
and a minimum
m_{L}
, with
m_{L},M_{L}\in T(M)
, then

[
2
, Theorem 2.7]
and standard facts on composition operators between

C(K)
spaces show that surjectivity of
\overline{T}
is equivalent to
injectivity of
\iota^{*}:K_{L}\to K_{M}
.

7.2.
General lattice homomorphisms on
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle

For free Banach lattices over Banach spaces, lattice homomorphisms

\mathrm{FBL}[E]\to\mathrm{FBL}[F]
can be represented through maps

F^{*}\to E^{*}
and a composition formula
[
23
, Section 10]
. We now
record the analogous construction for
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
. Given a lattice homomorphism

T:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle
, we associate a map
\Phi_{T}:M^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{L}
and
study its basic properties.

Let
L
and
M
be distributive lattices, and let

T:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle
be a
lattice homomorphism. For
x^{*}\in M^{*}
, define

\Phi_{T}(x^{*}):L\to\mathbb{R}
by

(2)

\Phi_{T}(x^{*})(x)=(T\delta_{x})(x^{*})\qquad(x\in L).

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
L
and
M
be lattices, and let

T:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle

be a lattice homomorphism. Let
\Phi_{T}:M^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{L}
be defined by
(
2
). Then:

(1)

For every
x^{*}\in M^{*}
, the map
\Phi_{T}(x^{*}):L\to\mathbb{R}
is a
lattice homomorphism.

(2)

For every
x^{*}\in M^{*}
and every
x\in L
,

|(\Phi_{T}(x^{*}))(x)|\leq\|T\|.

In particular, if
\|T\|\leq 1
, then
\Phi_{T}(M^{*})\subset L^{*}
.

(3)

The map
\Phi_{T}
is positively homogeneous: if
x^{*}\in M^{*}
and

\lambda\geq 0
are such that
\lambda x^{*}\in M^{*}
, then

\Phi_{T}(\lambda x^{*})=\lambda\Phi_{T}(x^{*}).

(4)

If
\|T\|\leq 1
, then for every
f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
and every
x^{*}\in M^{*}
,

(Tf)(x^{*})=f(\Phi_{T}(x^{*})).

Proof.

(1) Fix
x^{*}\in M^{*}
. For
x,y\in L
,

(\Phi_{T}(x^{*}))(x\vee y)=(T\delta_{x\vee y})(x^{*})=(T(\delta_{x}\vee\delta_{y}))(x^{*})=\max\{(T\delta_{x})(x^{*}),(T\delta_{y})(x^{*})\}.

Thus
\Phi_{T}(x^{*})
preserves joins. The proof for meets is analogous.

(2) For
x^{*}\in M^{*}
and
x\in L
,

|(\Phi_{T}(x^{*}))(x)|=|(T\delta_{x})(x^{*})|\leq\|T\delta_{x}\|\leq\|T\|.

(3) If
x^{*}\in M^{*}
and
\lambda\geq 0
are such that

\lambda x^{*}\in M^{*}
, then, for
x\in L
,

(\Phi_{T}(\lambda x^{*}))(x)=(T\delta_{x})(\lambda x^{*})=\lambda(T\delta_{x})(x^{*})=\lambda(\Phi_{T}(x^{*}))(x).

(4) Assume that
\|T\|\leq 1
. Then
\Phi_{T}(x^{*})\in L^{*}
by (2). Let

g\in\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle
. We first prove that

(Tg)(x^{*})=g(\Phi_{T}(x^{*}))\qquad(x^{*}\in M^{*}).

For
g=\delta_{x}
, this follows from

(T\delta_{x})(x^{*})=(\Phi_{T}(x^{*}))(x)=\delta_{x}(\Phi_{T}(x^{*})).

Since both sides are preserved under linear combinations and lattice
operations, the identity holds for every
g\in\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle
.

Let
f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
, and choose

(g_{n})\subset\mathrm{FVL}\langle L\rangle
with
g_{n}\to f
in norm. Since

T
is bounded and evaluation at
x^{*}\in M^{*}
is continuous,

(Tf)(x^{*})=\lim_{n\to\infty}(Tg_{n})(x^{*})=\lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n}(\Phi_{T}(x^{*})).

Since
\Phi_{T}(x^{*})\in L^{*}
and evaluation at
\Phi_{T}(x^{*})
is continuous,

\lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n}(\Phi_{T}(x^{*}))=f(\Phi_{T}(x^{*})).

This proves the formula.
∎

Proposition 7.7
.

Let
L
and
M
be lattices, and let

T:\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle\to\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle

be a lattice homomorphism. Then the associated map

\Phi_{T}:M^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{L}
has the following properties.

(1)

The map
\Phi_{T}
is continuous when
M^{*}
and
\mathbb{R}^{L}
are
endowed with the product topologies.

(2)

If
T
has dense range and
\|T\|\leq 1
, then
\Phi_{T}
is injective.

(3)

If
T
is an isometric lattice isomorphism, then
\Phi_{T}
is bijective and

\Phi_{T^{-1}}=(\Phi_{T})^{-1}.

(4)

For every
x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{m}^{*}\in M^{*}
,

\sup_{y\in L}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Phi_{T}(x_{i}^{*})(y)|\leq\|T\|\sup_{x\in M}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|x_{i}^{*}(x)|.

Proof.

(1) For each
x\in L
, the coordinate map

x^{*}\mapsto(\Phi_{T}(x^{*}))(x)=(T\delta_{x})(x^{*})
is continuous on
M^{*}
, since

T\delta_{x}\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle\subset C(M^{*})
.

(2) Assume that
T
has dense range and
\|T\|\leq 1
. Let

x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*}\in M^{*}
with
x_{1}^{*}\neq x_{2}^{*}
. Since

\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle
separates points of
M^{*}
, choose

h\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle
with
h(x_{1}^{*})\neq h(x_{2}^{*})
. By density
of the range of
T
, there is
f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
such that

(Tf)(x_{1}^{*})\neq(Tf)(x_{2}^{*})
. Proposition
7.6
gives

f(\Phi_{T}(x_{1}^{*}))\neq f(\Phi_{T}(x_{2}^{*})),

so
\Phi_{T}(x_{1}^{*})\neq\Phi_{T}(x_{2}^{*})
.

(3) Since
\|T\|=\|T^{-1}\|=1
, Proposition
7.6

shows that
\Phi_{T}(M^{*})\subset L^{*}
and

\Phi_{T^{-1}}(L^{*})\subset M^{*}
.

For
x^{*}\in M^{*}
and
y\in M
, Proposition
7.6

applied to
T^{-1}
gives

(T^{-1}\delta_{y})(\Phi_{T}(x^{*}))=\delta_{y}(\Phi_{T^{-1}}(\Phi_{T}(x^{*}))).

Since
T(T^{-1}\delta_{y})=\delta_{y}
, the left-hand side equals

\delta_{y}(x^{*})=x^{*}(y)
. Hence

\Phi_{T^{-1}}\circ\Phi_{T}=\mathrm{Id}_{M^{*}}
.

The reverse identity is obtained in the same way, so
\Phi_{T}
is bijective
with inverse
\Phi_{T^{-1}}
.

(4) Fix
y\in L
. By the definition of
\Phi_{T}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Phi_{T}(x_{i}^{*})(y)|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|(T\delta_{y})(x_{i}^{*})|.

The definition of the free norm gives

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|(T\delta_{y})(x_{i}^{*})|\leq\|T\delta_{y}\|\sup_{x\in M}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|x_{i}^{*}(x)|.

Since
\|T\delta_{y}\|\leq\|T\|
, it follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\Phi_{T}(x_{i}^{*})(y)|\leq\|T\|\sup_{x\in M}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|x_{i}^{*}(x)|.

Taking the supremum over
y\in L
yields the result.
∎

8.
Lattice isometries on
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle

If two lattices
L
and
M
are lattice isomorphic, then
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
and

\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle
are Banach lattice isometric. The converse does not hold in
general. Recall that the
opposite lattice

L^{\mathrm{op}}
has the
same underlying set as
L
, with the order reversed:
x\leq y
in

L^{\mathrm{op}}
precisely when
y\leq x
in
L
. Thus

x\vee_{L^{\mathrm{op}}}y=x\wedge_{L}y,\qquad x\wedge_{L^{\mathrm{op}}}y=x\vee_{L}y.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
L
be a lattice and let
L^{\mathrm{op}}
be its opposite lattice.
Then

x^{*}\in L^{*}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad-x^{*}\in(L^{\mathrm{op}})^{*}.

Consequently, the map
x^{*}\mapsto-x^{*}
is a bijection from
L^{*}
onto

(L^{\mathrm{op}})^{*}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
x^{*}\in L^{*}
. We show that
-x^{*}:L^{\mathrm{op}}\to[-1,1]

is a lattice homomorphism. If
a\leq_{L^{\mathrm{op}}}b
, then

b\leq_{L}a
, so
x^{*}(b)\leq x^{*}(a)
and

-x^{*}(a)\leq-x^{*}(b).

Thus
-x^{*}
is order preserving on
L^{\mathrm{op}}
.

For joins in
L^{\mathrm{op}}
,

\displaystyle(-x^{*})(a\vee_{L^{\mathrm{op}}}b)

\displaystyle=-x^{*}(a\wedge_{L}b)

\displaystyle=-\min\{x^{*}(a),x^{*}(b)\}

\displaystyle=\max\{-x^{*}(a),-x^{*}(b)\}

\displaystyle=(-x^{*})(a)\vee(-x^{*})(b).

Similarly, for meets in
L^{\mathrm{op}}
,

\displaystyle(-x^{*})(a\wedge_{L^{\mathrm{op}}}b)

\displaystyle=-x^{*}(a\vee_{L}b)

\displaystyle=-\max\{x^{*}(a),x^{*}(b)\}

\displaystyle=\min\{-x^{*}(a),-x^{*}(b)\}

\displaystyle=(-x^{*})(a)\wedge(-x^{*})(b).

Thus
-x^{*}\in(L^{\mathrm{op}})^{*}
. The converse follows by applying the same
argument to
L^{\mathrm{op}}
. The map
x^{*}\mapsto-x^{*}
is its own inverse,
so it is a bijection.
∎

Proposition 8.2
.

For every lattice
L
, the Banach lattices
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
and

\mathrm{FBL}\langle L^{\mathrm{op}}\rangle
are lattice isometric.

Proof.

Let
S:(L^{\mathrm{op}})^{*}\to L^{*}
be given by
S(x^{*})=-x^{*}
, and define

\Phi f=f\circ S\qquad(f\in\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle).

By Lemma
8.1
,
S
is a bijection. It is immediate
that
\Phi
preserves linear and lattice operations. Moreover, the defining
norm formula for free Banach lattices is invariant under the change of
variables
x^{*}\mapsto-x^{*}
, and therefore
\|\Phi f\|=\|f\|
. Since

\Phi^{-1}
is defined in the same way,
\Phi
is a lattice isometry.
∎

Remark 8.3
.

There are lattices that are not isomorphic to their opposite lattices, while
their free Banach lattices are lattice isometric by
Proposition
8.2
. For instance, one may take a five-point
lattice
L=\{0,1,a,b,c\}
with
0<a=b\wedge c<1
and
b\vee c=1
.

Motivated by
[
23
, § 10.3]
, it is natural to ask for conditions on a
lattice
L
under which lattice isometry of
\mathrm{FBL}\langle M\rangle
and
\mathrm{FBL}\langle L\rangle
forces

M
to be lattice isomorphic to either
L
or
L^{\mathrm{op}}
.
