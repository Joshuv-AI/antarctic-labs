Post-procedural computed tomography comparison of predicted and actual neo-left ventricular outflow tract after one-session double valve-in-valve implantation: a case report
Authors: Weili Liu, Ju Zhang, Kai Zhang, Yanchao Li, Dacheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction is a serious complication of transcatheter mitral valve replacement and mitral valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures. Computed tomography (CT)-based virtual valve implantation predicts the neo-LVOT area, but it assumes idealized seating and may not fully reflect procedural interaction during double-valve intervention.Case summaryA 74-year-old woman with degenerated surgical mitral and aortic bioprostheses presented with NYHA class IV heart failure. Full-cycle ECG-gated 4D-CT identified the minimum predicted neo-LVOT at 43% of the R–R interval (300.9 mm2). A one-session transapical double ViV procedure was performed. The aortic ViV was performed first because CT simulation showed that the aortic balloon would expand between the two strut posts of the surgical mitral prosthesis; implanting the mitral valve first could have exposed the newly deployed mitral prosthesis to compression during subsequent aortic balloon expansion. Post-procedural 4D-CT, evaluated using the same workflow and compared at 43%, showed a neo-LVOT area of 280.6 mm2, a 20.3 mm2 reduction, with approximately 1.2 mm asymmetric ventricular protrusion of the mitral prosthesis. Echocardiography showed normal valve-in-valve function and no haemodynamic LVOT obstruction.DiscussionThis case demonstrates that CT-based planning correctly identified procedural feasibility, while post-procedural CT revealed measurable geometric variability. The finding remained well above high-risk thresholds and should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating geometric observation rather than clinically significant obstruction.

Introduction

LVOT obstruction is a feared complication of transcatheter mitral valve replacement and mitral ViV interventions.1,2 Computed tomography (CT)-based virtual valve implantation is the reference imaging method for predicting the neo-LVOT and guiding procedural planning.3,4 Predicted neo-LVOT thresholds of approximately 170–200 mm2 are commonly used to define high-risk anatomy.4–6 Static simulations, however, assume symmetric expansion, coaxial alignment, and fixed landing depth; computational studies show that small modelling differences can influence neo-LVOT estimates.7,8 One-session double ViV procedures add a further issue: sequential aortic-mitral prosthetic interaction, for which CT assessment of the aortic virtual basal ring, mitral sewing ring, and mitral posts has been proposed.9 We report pre- and post-procedural CT comparison after transapical aortic and mitral ViV implantation.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old woman with rheumatic heart disease, prior surgical mitral and aortic bioprosthetic valve replacement, tricuspid repair, coronary artery bypass grafting, and atrial fibrillation presented with 8 months of progressive dyspnoea and 2 months of orthopnoea, pleural effusion, ascites, and leg oedema. She was admitted with NYHA class IV heart failure. Vital signs were stable; ECG showed atrial fibrillation with ST-T abnormalities. Echocardiography showed severe mitral bioprosthetic stenosis (mean gradient 11 mmHg), moderate aortic bioprosthetic stenosis (mean gradient 34 mmHg), severe tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension, and right ventricular dysfunction. Both prostheses showed leaflet thickening and calcification consistent with structural degeneration and predominant stenosis.

Operative records identified a 21-mm Edwards 2800TFX aortic bioprosthesis and a 27-mm Edwards 6900PTFX mitral bioprosthesis. Full-cycle ECG-gated 4D-CT datasets were reconstructed across the cardiac cycle before and after intervention. The neo-LVOT was reviewed across phases, and the smallest area occurred at 43% of the R-R interval; this same minimum-area phase was therefore used for the matched pre/post comparison. At 43%, the predicted neo-LVOT area was 300.9 mm2 and the mitral inflow-septal distance was 9.1 mm. Aortic planning showed a prosthetic ring area of 289.4 mm2, effective diameter of 19.1 mm, frame diameter of 20.4 mm, and left and right coronary heights of 14.2 and 12.0 mm; the mitral frame diameter was 26.5 mm. DICOM datasets were analysed using GE Advantage Workstation (AW) version 4.7. Segmentation included the left ventricular cavity, septum, aortic root, surgical frames, and virtual or implanted valve frames. The virtual mitral prosthesis was modelled as a 27-mm Renatus valve, and the neo-LVOT was measured as the minimal cross-sectional area orthogonal to the LVOT centreline. Measurements were independently repeated by two observers and finalized by consensus. The corresponding full-cycle pre- and post-procedural ECG-gated 4D-CT cine loops are provided as Videos 1 and 2.

Redo surgery was considered high risk (EuroSCORE II 13.45%), and the Heart Team selected a one-session transapical strategy, consistent with guideline-supported individualized decision-making for high-risk prosthetic valve dysfunction.10 Pre-procedural CT showed the projected aortic ViV balloon expanding between two surgical mitral strut posts, a favourable interaction pattern described for aortic balloon-to-bioprosthetic mitral post-contact.9 Therefore, the aortic ViV was deployed first: if the mitral ViV had been implanted first, subsequent aortic balloon expansion could have compressed or deformed the newly deployed mitral transcatheter frame and altered the aortic–mitral spatial relationship. No cerebral embolic protection device was used. A bailout plan for LVOT obstruction included immediate TEE assessment, haemodynamic support, and surgical conversion if required.

Under general anaesthesia with TEE and fluoroscopic guidance, right radial arterial pressure monitoring, a femoral pigtail catheter, temporary pacing, and systemic heparinization were used. Through fifth-intercostal transapical access, a 21-mm Renatus valve was deployed in the aortic position at 6 atm and aligned with the surgical strut posts. After confirmation of stable aortic valve position without LVOT compromise, a 27-mm Renatus valve was deployed in the mitral position at 6 atm. Final angiography and TEE showed stable prostheses without significant valvular or paravalvular regurgitation. Estimated blood loss was approximately 100 ml.

Immediate echocardiography showed normal valve-in-valve function: aortic peak velocity 1.6 m/s and peak gradient 10 mmHg; mitral peak velocity 1.0 m/s and peak gradient 4 mmHg; LVEF 65%. Early transthoracic echocardiography showed an aortic peak/mean gradient of 21/10 mmHg and a mitral peak/mean gradient of 13/4 mmHg, with LVEF 57%. Serial follow-up at approximately 7 weeks and 4 months showed stable valve-in-valve gradients and preserved LVEF. No provoked invasive LVOT gradient was performed and no specific Doppler LVOT gradient was recorded; absence of haemodynamic obstruction was based on procedural stability, TEE, follow-up transthoracic echocardiography, and clinical recovery.

Post-procedural 4D-CT assessed with the same workflow demonstrated a neo-LVOT area of 280.6 mm2 at the matched 43% minimum-area phase, a reduction of 20.3 mm2. Long-axis reconstruction showed approximately 1.2 mm deeper ventricular protrusion of the mitral prosthesis on the LVOT-facing side (Figure 1).

Discussion

This case supports full-cycle 4D-CT planning for complex ViV intervention while illustrating a practical limitation of virtual implantation. The predicted minimum neo-LVOT of 300.9 mm2 correctly identified procedural feasibility, but actual deployment resulted in a measurable reduction to 280.6 mm2 at the same 43% phase. The most plausible mechanism was slight asymmetric mitral prosthesis seating; predominant stenotic degeneration made acute ventricular remodelling less likely.

The 20.3 mm2 reduction should not be overinterpreted. Both values remained well above commonly cited high-risk thresholds of 170–200 mm2, and there was no Doppler, haemodynamic, or clinical evidence of LVOT obstruction. Thus, this is a geometry-based, hypothesis-generating observation rather than proof of clinically meaningful obstruction.

The procedural sequence was central to risk reduction. A recent bench-model classification of left-sided prosthetic valve interaction distinguishes potentially benign interaction of the aortic balloon with surgical mitral posts from compression of a transcatheter mitral frame by a subsequent aortic balloon, which may impair mitral prosthesis function or cause embolization.9 In our case, CT showed favourable balloon alignment between surgical mitral strut posts; therefore, aortic ViV was performed before mitral ViV. This risk is additional to conventional neo-LVOT narrowing mechanisms considered in TMVR planning,11 and safety margins and bailout strategies, including LAMPOON in selected patients, remain important.12

Limitations

Limitations include the single-case design, focus on the minimum-area 43% phase for quantitative comparison despite full-cycle 4D-CT review, absence of computational fluid dynamics, and lack of provoked invasive LVOT gradient assessment.

Conclusion

CT-predicted neo-LVOT correctly identified procedural feasibility in this one-session transapical double ViV case. Post-procedural CT showed a small but measurable geometric reduction caused by asymmetric mitral seating. Explicit procedural sequencing and safety margins should complement CT simulation in complex multivalvular interventions.
