Occupational Morbidity and Fatal Injuries in Thailand, 2007–2024: A Nationwide Time-trend Study Using Workers’ Compensation Records
Authors: Pirawan Wangupadcha, Wor Mi Thi, Thodsaphon Lunnoo, Ratanee Kammoolkon, Birabongse Hardthakwong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOccupational injuries and work-related diseases remain a significant and persistent threat to the safety, health, and well-being of workers around the globe. This nationwide study aimed to examine long-term trends in occupational injuries and work-related diseases in Thailand from 2007 to 2024.MethodsAdministrative data from the Thailand Social Security Office were used to assess trends and to identify factors associated with fatal outcomes among reported occupational injury cases in 2024. Annual injury rates were calculated using decided cases as the numerator and the average number of registered employees as the denominator. Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated.ResultsA total of 2,006,840 occupational injury cases were recorded. While the number of registered employees increased over time, overall injury rates declined from 24.57 to 7.29 per 1,000 workers and severe injury rates from 6.75 to 2.13 per 1,000 workers. However, fatal injury rates declined markedly but less consistently during the study period, from 9.61 to 4.94 per 100,000 workers. Musculoskeletal disorders remained the most frequently reported work-related disease. In 2024, older age (≥55 years; RR = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.56–2.62), construction-site injury (RR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.64–2.42), vehicle-related injury (RR = 28.70, 95% CI: 24.55–33.55), and employment in establishments with ≤1,000 workers (RR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.35–2.27) were associated with higher risk of fatal outcomes.ConclusionWhile overall occupational injury rates declined substantially over time, fatal injury rates showed a less consistent decline, indicating the need for continued efforts to prevent severe occupational injuries and fatalities.

Introduction

Occupational injuries remain a major occupational health and safety problem and labor protection concern worldwide, particularly in rapidly industrializing and middle-income countries such as Thailand [1]. Globally, occupational injuries and diseases were responsible for 1.9 million deaths in 2016 [2]. Workers are frequently exposed to physical, mechanical, chemical, and ergonomic hazards that may result in outcomes ranging from minor injuries to severe disabilities and deaths [[3], [4], [5]]. Despite advancements in occupational safety regulations and preventive interventions, occupational injuries continue to impose a substantial burden with significant health, social, and economic consequences. In Thailand, occupational injuries represent a growing public health challenge amid rapid economic development and workforce expansion [6]. Growth in industrial, construction, and service sectors has increased exposure to occupational hazards and elevated injury risks across diverse labor groups [7]. Understanding long-term trends and epidemiological characteristics is therefore essential for evaluating existing safety policies and identifying vulnerable worker populations. However, comprehensive national-level analyses over extended periods remain limited in the Thai context. The magnitude of occupational injuries in Thailand is reflected in persistently high annual case counts, including both fatal and nonfatal outcomes [8]. These injuries contribute to workforce morbidity through lost workdays, disability, and long-term health consequences. Prevalence and incidence rates are key indicators of workplace safety and occupational health burden, while mortality rates reflect the severity of injuries. Injury-related disabilities also reduce work capacity, increase dependence on social support systems, and diminish quality of life [9]. In addition to health impacts, occupational injuries impose substantial economic costs, including direct medical expenses and indirect costs such as productivity loss, compensation, and long-term social security expenditures [10]. In Thailand, the social security system plays a critical role in compensating injured workers, making accurate epidemiological data essential for policy planning, resource allocation, and system sustainability [Social Security Office (SSO)] [11]. Previous studies have demonstrated the significant burden of occupational injuries in Thailand. For example, Thepaksorn and Pongpanich (2014) reported that in 2010, there were 52,074 nonfatal occupational injury cases, with an incidence rate of 16.9 per 1,000 workers, and substantial economic costs totaling USD 13.87 million, including medical expenses and compensation, with additional losses from noncompensated earnings [6,12]. However, existing research remains fragmented, primarily focusing on specific occupational groups such as agricultural workers, construction workers, and selected sectors [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Consequently, comprehensive national-level analyses covering all sectors are lacking. This gap underscores the need for systematic, nationwide evaluation of occupational injury and disease data to support policy development and occupational health advancement in Thailand. This study is the first to provide a comprehensive national assessment of both occupational injuries and work-related diseases over nearly two decades in Thailand, integrating long-term trend analysis with case-based fatal risk assessment.

Given the significant health and economic impacts, a systematic analysis using national data is essential to inform evidence-based policy decisions. Therefore, this study aimed to examine long-term trends and epidemiological characteristics of occupational injuries and work-related diseases in Thailand from 2007 to 2024 and to identify factors associated with fatal outcomes among reported occupational injury cases in 2024.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study used a nationwide descriptive time-trend design based on secondary administrative data, with an additional cross-sectional analytical component for the 2024 injury-case analysis. The data were extracted from Thailand’s national occupational injury surveillance system, administered by the SSO, Ministry of Labour of Thailand. The study analyzed aggregated annual occupational injury records of all insured workers, including both domestic and migrant workers, enrolled under the Thai Social Security Insurance Scheme who experienced occupational injuries or work-related fatalities during the period 2007–2024. As individual-level data were not available, analyses were conducted at the population level using aggregated annual counts and rates.

Data source and case definition

Data on annual decided occupational injury cases, comprising the number of registered insured employees, fatal cases, permanent disability cases, loss of organ cases, lost-time injuries involving more than three days of work absence, and lost-time injuries involving three days or less than three days, were obtained from national occupational injury database of the Thai SSO. All data are publicly available from the official SSO website (https://www.sso.go.th) [17].

Occupational injuries were operationally defined as work-related events resulting in physical harm occurring during work activities or in the course of employment, consistent with the Workmen’s Compensation Act (1994) classification criteria. Injury outcomes were classified into five severity categories based on clinical and functional consequences. Fatal outcomes were defined as deaths directly attributable to occupational injuries, while permanent disability referred to total or partial permanent loss of working capacity resulting from occupational injury. Loss of organ was defined as permanent physical impairment involving anatomical or functional loss of a bodily organ. Severe nonfatal injuries were defined as those requiring work absence exceeding three consecutive days, whereas nonsevere injuries were defined as those resulting in work absence not exceeding three days [18]. For analytical purposes, injury cases were further aggregated into two operational categories based on injury severity. Severe cases were defined as the aggregate of fatal outcomes, permanent disability, loss of organ, and lost-time injuries involving more than three days of work absence. All cases comprised the total number of decided occupational injury cases across all five severity categories, including both severe cases and nonsevere injuries involving work absence not exceeding three days. The size of the factories was categorized as small factories (≤1,000 employees) and large factories (>1,000 employees). The Workmen’s Compensation Fund is a compulsory scheme administered by the SSO under the Ministry of Labour of Thailand, in which employers are legally required to report occupational injuries, illnesses, disabilities, and deaths among insured workers. Claims are reviewed and approved before inclusion in annual statistics, meaning the database primarily captures events resulting in formal compensation claims and likely under-represents minor unreported injuries. Structural coverage gaps also exist: informal workers, the self-employed people, and agricultural workers—a substantial portion of Thailand’s labor force—are excluded from the scheme. Despite these limitations, the Workmen’s Compensation Fund database remains the most comprehensive routinely collected source of occupational injury data available for Thailand’s formal workforce. However, individual-level variables such as sex, educational attainment, occupation, ethnicity, injury diagnosis, and hours worked are not available in the publicly released aggregated statistics, which precludes subgroup analyses and should be considered a limitation when interpreting the findings.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software, version 18 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to characterize occupational injury cases across the study period. Annual counts, incidence rates, and proportions were calculated for each calendar year. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation with minimum–maximum (min–max) ranges, as appropriate. Occupational injury incidence rates were estimated using decided occupational injury cases as the numerator and the average annual number of registered employees as the denominator, thereby accounting for workforce fluctuations throughout each calendar year. The overall occupational injury rate (all cases) was calculated as the number of decided occupational injury cases divided by the average annual workforce and multiplied by 1,000 (Equation 1). Comparative analyses were conducted across calendar years (2007–2024) to evaluate temporal trends in occupational injury occurrence. Comparisons were also made across injury severity categories, comprising nonsevere injuries, severe injuries, permanent disability, and fatal outcomes. Subgroup analyses were additionally performed by age group to examine variations in injury patterns over the study period. For the 2024 factor analysis, subgroup-specific denominator data for the total insured workforce were not available. Therefore, the analytical comparison was restricted to reported occupational injury cases and examined factors associated with a fatal outcome among injury cases. For each subgroup, the total number of injury cases was defined as the sum of fatal and nonfatal cases. Risk ratios (RRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated by comparing the proportion of fatal cases across categories of age, type of business, job function, cause of injury, and establishment size, using the designated reference group for each factor. The estimated RRs should therefore be interpreted as relative risks of fatal outcome among reported occupational injury cases rather than population-based risks among all insured workers. Statistical significance was determined at a p value <0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study used anonymized secondary data with no personal identifiers. Ethical approval was therefore waived in accordance with national regulations for studies using publicly available or administrative datasets. The data obtained from the SSO were fully anonymized. As this was a secondary data analysis, ethical approval was not required from the university’s human ethics subcommittee.

Results

Occupational injury rates

Data from the Thailand SSO showed that the number of registered employees was lowest in 2009 (8,037,765) and rose to 12,035,882 in 2024. From 2007 to 2024, a total of 2,006,840 decided occupational injury cases were recorded, with an annual mean of 111,491 ± 36,061 cases. Injuries involving leave from work for fewer than 3 days accounted for the largest proportion of cases (69.5%), followed by injuries requiring leave for more than 3 days (28.5%). Fatal injuries remained uncommon, averaging 0.59% of all cases, or 613 deaths per year. Although fatal case numbers fluctuated slightly, their proportion increased from 0.37% in 2007 to 0.78% in 2022, then declined to 0.68% in 2024. Permanent disability was rare, averaging 13.4 cases per year (0.01%), while loss-of-organ cases accounted for 1.43% on average, peaking at 2.71% in 2013. Overall occupational injury cases declined markedly from 198,652 in 2007 to 87,745 in 2024. Severe cases showed a similar decline, from 54,541 to 25,663. Fatal and nonfatal injury

As presented in Table 2 and Fig. 1, occupational injury rates in Thailand declined substantially between 2007 and 2024. The rate for all occupational injury cases decreased from 24.57 per 1,000 workers in 2007 to 7.29 per 1,000 workers in 2024. This downward trend was most pronounced during 2007–2018, after which the rate remained below 10 per 1,000 workers, with a slight increase in 2019 and again in 2023–2024. Severe occupational injury rates showed a similar pattern, falling from 6.75 per 1,000 workers in 2007 to 2.13 per 1,000 workers in 2024. However, fatal occupational injury rates declined considerably but less consistently than nonfatal injuries, decreasing from 9.61 per 100,000 workers in 2007 to 4.94 per 100,000 workers in 2024, with fluctuations observed throughout the study period. Cause of occupational injury

Fig. 2 shows trends in nonfatal occupational injury rates by mechanism per 1,000 workers from 2007 to 2024. Injuries caused by cuts or stabs from materials or objects remained the leading mechanism throughout the study period, despite a marked decline from about 47 per 1,000 workers in 2007 to 21 per 1,000 workers in 2024. Injuries caused by being hit or crashed by materials or objects also decreased substantially, from roughly 33 to 13 per 1,000 workers. Eye injuries due to splashes from materials, objects, or chemicals declined steadily from about 32 to below 10 per 1,000 workers. Injuries caused by collapsing or falling objects also fell overall, from approximately 26 to 13 per 1,000 workers, although they became the second most common mechanism in several later years. In contrast, injuries due to pinching or pulling by materials or objects remained the least common, decreasing from about 13 to 7 per 1,000 workers. Overall, nonfatal injury rates declined across all major mechanisms, with only modest year-to-year fluctuations and slight changes in ranking over time.Fig. 2Trends in nonfatal occupational injury rates by mechanism of injury in Thailand, 2007–2024.

Occupational and work-related diseases

Analysis of occupational disease statistics in Thailand during 2015–2019 showed that musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) were the most frequently reported occupational diseases. A total of 7,543 MSD cases were recorded over the 5-year period, corresponding to an annual mean of 1,508.6 ± 435.3 cases. Dermatitis was the second most commonly reported condition, with 1,200 cases in total and a mean of 240.0 ± 99.1 cases per year. Diseases caused by chemical exposure were less frequently reported, accounting for 138 cases overall, with an annual mean of 27.6 ± 20.1 cases. Diseases caused by physical agents accounted for 124 cases, corresponding to a mean of 24.8 ± 12.6 cases per year. Respiratory diseases were the least commonly reported, with a total of 58 cases and an average of 11.6 ± 2.6 cases annually. Overall, MSDs accounted for the largest proportion of occupational diseases during 2015–2019 (83.23%), followed by dermatitis (13.24%), diseases caused by chemical exposure (1.52%), diseases caused by physical agents (1.37%), and respiratory diseases (0.64%) (Fig. 3a). Statistics for 2020–2024 showed a somewhat different pattern of work-related diseases in Thailand, although MSDs remained the most commonly reported condition. During this period, 2,946 MSD cases were recorded, with an annual mean of 589.2 ± 456.0 cases, accounting for 59.54% of all reported work-related diseases. Dermatitis remained the second most common condition, with 1,589 cases in total and an average of 317.8 ± 331.7 cases per year, representing 32.11% of reported cases. Infectious diseases constituted a notable proportion during this period, accounting for 303 cases and an annual mean of 60.6 ± 27.3 cases. Hearing loss was less frequently reported, with 77 cases overall and an annual mean of 15.4 ± 13.1 cases, while pneumoconiosis was the least commonly reported condition, with 33 cases and an average of 6.6 ± 4.3 cases per year. In proportional terms, infectious diseases accounted for 6.12% of all reported work-related diseases, followed by hearing loss (1.56%) and pneumoconiosis (0.67%) (Fig. 3b). Overall, the findings indicate that MSDs remained the predominant occupational and work-related health problem among Thai workers across both periods. Discussion

This study provides national-level evidence on long-term trends in occupational injuries and work-related diseases in Thailand from 2007 to 2024. The findings indicate a substantial decline in overall and severe occupational injury rates over time, while fatal injury rates also declined over time, although the reduction was less pronounced and less consistent than that observed for nonfatal injuries. This pattern suggests that workplace safety measures in Thailand may have been more effective in reducing common and less severe injuries than in preventing rare but high-consequence events. The observed reduction in overall injury rates is consistent with earlier Thai studies reporting declines in occupational injuries over time, likely reflecting gradual improvements in workplace safety regulation, awareness, and compensation-based surveillance [6,12,19]. Our findings are consistent with global trends reported by previous studies. Lai et al. (2025) documented a sustained decline in occupational injury burden, in both death numbers and rates, globally over the past three decades [20]. Internationally, fatal occupational injury rates vary considerably. Within Southeast Asia, Singapore reported the lowest fatal occupational injury rate at 1.0 per 100,000 workers in 2023, while Malaysia recorded the highest at 14.6 per 100,000 workers [21]. In Europe, the incidence of fatal occupational accidents averaged 1.63 per 100,000 employed persons in 2023, though substantial variation existed across member states; the Netherlands and Germany reported rates below 1.00 per 100,000 workers, whereas Austria, Latvia, Cyprus, Bulgaria, and France exceeded 3.00 per 100,000 workers [22]. In the United States, fatal occupational injury rates have generally declined over time, with the notable exception of the construction industry, where rates have remained persistently elevated. Globally, mortality rates over the past decade (2009–2018) have been broadly estimated at 0.5–5.0 per 100,000 workers overall, 0.5–2.5 per 100,000 in manufacturing, and 1.0–15.0 per 100,000 in construction, with Germany, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom consistently recording among the lowest rates [23]. However, cross-national comparisons should be interpreted with caution as differences in injury surveillance systems, case definitions, and workforce structures may limit direct comparability.

Several factors may explain the declining occupational injury rates observed during the study period. A key contributor is the strengthening of Thailand’s occupational safety and health (OSH) regulatory framework. The Occupational Safety, Health and Environment Act B.E. 2554 (2011) established a comprehensive legal basis for hazard prevention and risk control and has since been supplemented by numerous ministerial regulations addressing sector-specific hazards [24]. More recently, the Ministerial Regulation on Safety Management System Standards B.E. 2565 (2022) mandated that establishments in designated sectors implement certified OSH management systems, recognized under standards such as Thai Industrial Standards Institute (TISI), International Organization for Standardization (ISO), International Labour Organization (ILO), or Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) [25]. Evidence from Thailand also suggests that effective OSH management systems improve worker safety behaviors through stronger policies, communication, and employee participation [26,27]. Together, these regulatory developments may have contributed to safer workplace practices and the observed decline in injury rates, though causal relationships cannot be established from this descriptive study. Beyond structural regulatory changes, external disruptions also appear to have shaped injury trends during the study period. The COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to the decline in occupational injury rates observed during 2020–2021. Public health measures, workplace restrictions, and reduced economic activity likely decreased workers’ exposure to occupational hazards, resulting in fewer reported injuries. Similar pandemic-related declines in workplace injuries have been documented in other countries, suggesting that changes in work patterns and workforce exposure were a contributing factor during this period [28,29].

The number of registered insured workers increased substantially during the study period, from approximately 8 million workers in 2009 to more than 12 million workers in 2024. This increase likely reflects expansion of the formal workforce, broader social security coverage, improved employer compliance with registration requirements, and growth in registered migrant worker employment. Such changes may have increased the population covered by the Workers’ Compensation Fund and contributed, in part, to lower injury incidence rates through expansion of the denominator. Nevertheless, the absolute number of occupational injury cases also declined markedly during the same period. This finding suggests that the observed reduction in injury rates is unlikely to be explained solely by changes in workforce coverage and may also reflect genuine improvements in occupational safety and injury prevention. However, because the present study relied on aggregated administrative statistics, the relative contribution of workforce expansion and improvements in workplace safety could not be quantified. Future studies using individual-level data and industry-specific workforce information are needed to better assess the impact of changes in workforce composition and coverage on occupational injury trends.

However, the relative stability of fatal injury rates indicates that major hazards remain insufficiently controlled. The mechanism-specific trends further support this interpretation. Nonfatal injuries due to cuts or stabs, being struck by materials or objects, eye splashes, and collapsing objects showed a declining trend over the study period, suggesting overall improvements in routine hazard control. In contrast, the factor analysis for 2024 demonstrated that vehicle-related injuries had by far the strongest association with fatal outcomes among reported injury cases, underscoring the critical role of transportation-related hazards in occupational mortality. Similarly, Champahom et al. (2025) reported that rider characteristics, including age, experience, education level, income, work frequency, and rest periods, significantly influence crash injury severity [30], while injuries occurring on construction sites were also more likely to result in fatal outcomes than those occurring in nonconstruction settings. These findings are consistent with previous evidence from Thailand indicating that high-energy events, particularly transport-related incidents, falls, and other construction-related hazards, disproportionately contribute to severe and fatal occupational injuries [6,7,14]. Taken together, these results suggest that future prevention strategies should move beyond general injury control and place greater emphasis on transport safety, fall prevention, vehicle segregation, contractor supervision, and engineering controls in high-risk work environments.

MSDs remained the predominant occupational and work-related disease category in both periods examined, despite a reduction in their proportional contribution from 2015–2019 to 2020–2024. This finding is in line with prior Thai studies showing a substantial burden of ergonomic problems, particularly back, shoulder, and neck symptoms, among workers exposed to repetitive tasks, awkward postures, and manual handling [13,31,32]. The persistence of MSDs in the present study indicates that ergonomic hazards remain widespread across Thai workplaces. Although dermatitis remained the second most common condition in both periods, the increased proportional contribution of infectious diseases in 2020–2024 suggests that occupational health surveillance should address not only traditional physical and ergonomic hazards but also biological exposures and emerging workplace health risks.

Among reported occupational injury cases in 2024, older workers had a higher likelihood of fatal outcome than younger workers. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that older workers experience more severe injury consequences and higher fatal occupational injury rates than younger workers [33,34]. Several mechanisms may explain this pattern, including reduced physiological reserve, slower reaction times, delayed recovery, and greater vulnerability to trauma severity. These findings support the need for age-sensitive occupational safety policies, including job redesign, ergonomic adaptation, targeted safety training, and closer supervision for older workers in hazardous tasks. The study also found that injury cases occurring in establishments with 1,000 workers or fewer were more likely to result in death than those occurring in larger establishments. This result is broadly consistent with earlier literature suggesting that organizational size influences occupational injury patterns through differences in safety management capacity, regulatory oversight, and availability of occupational health resources [35]. Larger establishments may benefit from more formalized safety systems, dedicated supervisors, and better access to training and emergency response, whereas smaller enterprises may face financial and technical constraints that limit implementation of effective preventive measures. Notably, job function was not associated with fatal outcomes in the present study. This may reflect the broad classification used in the dataset, which may have obscured meaningful differences within occupational groups. These findings should also be interpreted in light of the surveillance system on which the study was based. Compensation data are valuable for national trend monitoring, but under-reporting of minor injuries and inconsistencies in reporting practices may still affect the completeness of the database, as highlighted in other occupational surveillance research [36]. In addition, because subgroup-specific denominator data for all insured workers were unavailable, the 2024 factor analysis estimated associations with fatal outcome among reported injury cases, rather than population-based risks among all workers. Even so, the results identify clear priority groups and contexts for prevention, particularly older workers, construction settings, vehicle-related incidents, and smaller establishments.

Overall, the present study indicates that Thailand has made meaningful progress in reducing the overall burden of occupational injuries, especially nonfatal and severe cases. Nonetheless, the more limited improvement in fatal injury rates, together with the persistent burden of MSDs and the concentration of fatal outcomes in specific high-risk contexts, highlights the need for more targeted prevention strategies. Future efforts should focus on strengthening control of high-energy hazards, improving ergonomic risk management, supporting safety capacity in smaller enterprises, and enhancing the completeness and quality of national occupational injury surveillance.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It utilized nationwide administrative data from the Thailand SSO over an 18-year period, enabling a comprehensive assessment of long-term trends in occupational injuries and work-related diseases in Thailand. The large, continuous dataset provides valuable evidence for occupational health surveillance, priority setting, and policy development in the formal workforce. In addition, combining trend analysis with a focused 2024 injury-case analysis enabled identification of characteristics associated with fatal outcomes among reported cases.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the data were collected for administrative and compensation purposes rather than research, potentially leading to under-reporting or misclassification, particularly for minor nonfatal injuries. Second, occupational diseases are likely under-represented in workers’ compensation records relative to occupational injuries. This under-representation is particularly evident for diseases associated with chemical exposures and hearing loss, which may be affected by low disease recognition, diagnostic challenges, and obstacles in the compensation process. Therefore, the observed proportions of occupational diseases should be interpreted cautiously as they may underestimate the true burden of work-related morbidity in Thailand. Third, inclusion was limited to workers covered by the social security system, restricting generalizability to informal, uninsured, or undocumented workers. Fourth, the use of aggregated secondary data limited adjustment for key individual- and workplace-level confounders (e.g., sex, occupation, industry subgroup, employment status, and working hours); thus, RRs should be interpreted as unadjusted associations rather than causal effects. Finally, due to the absence of subgroup denominator data, the 2024 analysis was restricted to reported injury cases and examined factors associated with fatal outcomes rather than population-based risks; its reliance on a single year also limits temporal generalizability.

Despite these limitations, the study provides robust national-level evidence on occupational injury and disease patterns in Thailand and highlights priority areas for targeted prevention and improved surveillance.

Conclusion

Overall and severe occupational injury rates declined substantially over time, indicating progress in workplace safety and prevention. However, fatal injury rates changed only modestly, highlighting persistent challenges in preventing high-consequence events. MSDs remained the most common work-related disease in both periods, although their proportional contribution was smaller in 2020–2024 (59.54%) than in 2015–2019 (83.23%). In 2024, older age, construction-site injury, vehicle-related injury, and employment in establishments with fewer than 1,000 workers were associated with higher risk of fatal outcomes. These findings suggest that, despite overall improvements, targeted strategies are still needed to prevent fatal injuries, strengthen protection in high-risk settings, improve ergonomic risk management, and enhance national occupational injury surveillance.
