T2Pdecoder enables protein-centric analyses from transcriptomic data
Authors: Hui Wang, Jihong Tang, Yimeng Qiao, Quanhua Mu, Yumeng Guo, Jiguang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protein quantification is not as extensive as RNA quantification, especially for isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutant gliomas. Predicting protein abundance from RNA is valuable for leveraging existing data to understand biological processes, though the weak correlation between RNA and protein poses a significant challenge. Most existing methods predict limited protein subsets from transcriptome, constraining their broader proteomic applications. Here, we present T2Pdecoder, an integrative multi-omics deep learning model designed to predict broad protein abundance profiles by learning the shared embedding space of protein and RNA. T2Pdecoder is evaluated on different glioma datasets, achieving modest but consistent improvements over RNA-only baselines in concordance with measured protein abundance, while more accurately recapitulating protein-level pathway enrichment patterns. The applications of T2Pdecoder on glioma bulk RNA data uncover functional subgroups with significant survival differences. Furthermore, T2Pdecoder reduces batch-associated variation in single-cell RNA data and identifies distinctive cell markers. Collectively, these results suggest that T2Pdecoder enables protein-centric analyses from transcriptomic data and may provide complementary biological insights beyond conventional RNA-only analyses in cancer research.

Introduction

Gliomas are the most common primary malignant brain tumors in adults and are associated with poor prognosis despite advances in surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy1,2. Understanding the molecular underpinnings of glioma is crucial for identifying biomarkers and therapeutic targets3. While transcriptomic analyses have revealed key characterizations across glioma subtypes4,5, proteomic profiling remains essential for elucidating actual cellular phenotypes, as proteins are the primary effectors of most biological processes6,7.

Proteins carry out a vast array of cellular functions and provide insights into disease mechanisms through their structural diversity, dynamic abundance, and post-translational modifications8. Mass spectrometry (MS) is the primary technology for quantifying protein abundance9. However, owing to technical challenges and higher experimental costs, MS-based protein quantification is currently not as extensive as sequencing-based RNA measurements10. Consequently, many glioma datasets consist of transcriptomic but not proteomic data11,12. While RNA profiling provides valuable gene regulatory insights across biological systems and demonstrates clinical relevance, extensive studies reveal poor RNA-protein abundance correlations13–17, highlighting critical limitations in using transcriptional data as proteomic proxies, especially in complex diseases such as glioma.

According to the central dogma, proteins are translated from RNA, therefore they inherently share fundamental biological information. This essential connection establishes the theoretical foundation for inferring protein abundance through RNA expression. Substantial research efforts have been dedicated to bridging the RNA-protein abundance gap. The emergence of CITE-seq technology18, which enables simultaneous measurement of single-cell transcriptomes and surface proteomes, provides multimodal datasets critical for developing RNA-to-protein predictive deep learning frameworks. While existing algorithms, including LIGER19, Seurat20, cTP-Net21, CMAE22, totalVI23, BABEL24, scArches25, sciPENN26, scVAEIT27, scMOGNN28, and scTranslator29 demonstrate partial success, current implementations predominantly face two fundamental constraints. First, the proteomic coverage achieved by existing solutions remains limited (typically no more than 300 proteins), constraining system-level biological interpretations that require comprehensive proteomic profiling29. Second, existing models are primarily optimized for single-cell data, lacking reliable applicability to bulk samples, resulting in persistent obstacles for clinical proteogenomic studies. Furthermore, because RNA-protein relationships are often weak and context dependent, large improvements in direct protein abundance prediction may not always be expected. Nevertheless, modest but consistent improvements may still support biologically informative proteome-centric analyses when applied at scale.

In this work, we develop an integrative multi-omics deep learning model called T2Pdecoder. T2Pdecoder learns low-dimensional representations of samples based on pan-transcriptomic and proteomic data, enabling the prediction across a broad spectrum of protein abundance profiles. T2Pdecoder supports proteome-scale prediction across 5738 proteins and can be applied to both bulk and single-cell transcriptomic datasets. Across multiple glioma cohorts, T2Pdecoder shows modest but consistent improvements over RNA-only baselines in concordance with measured protein abundance, while more accurately recapitulating protein-level enrichment patterns. Benchmarking in a CITE-seq glioblastoma (GBM) dataset30 demonstrates comparable performance relative to existing methods for proteins with experimental measurements. Single-cell implementation successfully reduces batch-associated variation and detects protein markers not discernible from scRNA-seq data alone. In 1920 glioma samples, T2Pdecoder-derived protein profiles identify functional subgroups associated with robust survival stratification across molecular subtypes. Together, these findings suggest that T2Pdecoder provides a scalable framework for proteome-centric analyses from transcriptomic data in contexts where large-scale proteomic profiling is not feasible.

Results

Overview of T2Pdecoder

T2Pdecoder is a transfer-learning framework designed to transform gene expression profiles into protein profiles, thereby predicting the abundance of a wide range of proteins in biological samples with existing whole transcriptome expression data. The core functionality of T2Pdecoder involves: (1) training two distinct encoders–one for RNA patterns, another for protein abundance; (2) establishing a shared embedding space for cross-modal representation; and (3) developing a decoder to reconstruct protein abundance profiles from the embedded vectors. The first component is a Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP) model31, originally developed for multimodal learning between text and image domains. In T2Pdecoder, we adapt this method to establish cross-modal alignment between RNA and protein embeddings. The model is pre-trained using pan-cancer RNA-protein paired data from Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC)32, and subsequently fine-tuned on glioma RNA-protein paired cohorts for cancer-type-specific applications.

Secondly, a multi-task Variational Autoencoder (VAE) model33 is employed to generate low-dimensional protein abundance embeddings, leveraging the largest possible set of high-quality proteomics samples without requiring matched RNA data. For each cancer type, training is performed on 90% of protein profiles with the remaining 10% held out for validation. The architecture incorporates CLIP’s protein encoder as a supervisory signal via consistency constraints, enforcing alignment between the VAE-derived representations and the CLIP embedding space during multi-task learning.

In the final integration component, T2Pdecoder implements a generative architecture that combines the pre-trained RNA encoder from the CLIP framework with the optimized protein decoder from the VAE framework, enabling systematic prediction of protein abundance profiles from RNA input. T2Pdecoder can be used in multi-scale analyses, including not only molecular subtyping and pathway enrichment analysis in bulk cohorts but also cellular state mapping and marker identification at a single-cell resolution (Fig. 1a).

Based on IDH1 mutation status and chromosome 1p/19q co-deletion, gliomas are classified into three major subtypes: IDH-wildtype (IDHwt), IDH-mutant 1p/19q-non-codeleted (IDHmut-non-codel), and IDH-mutant 1p/19q-codeleted (IDHmut-codel). Analysis of subtype distributions reveals a marked disparity between available transcriptomic and proteomic datasets (Fig. 1c). Transcriptomic studies show a relatively balanced representation of the three subtypes, with IDHmut-codel accounting for approximately 21% of cases. In contrast, existing proteomic studies are predominantly focused on the IDHwt subtype, which constitutes 88% of available proteomic data. This imbalance and the limited availability of proteomic measurements for IDH-mutant gliomas motivated the application of T2Pdecoder to bridge this disparity, enabling protein-level analyses across subtypes.

Training and evaluating the CLIP model in RNA-protein paired data

To establish high-fidelity RNA-protein associations, we enrolled pan-cancer samples from CPTAC for CLIP model pretraining. Since the samples in CPTAC were cryopulverized and then divided into aliquots for DNA/RNA isolation and proteomics34, this process minimizes the impact of sample heterogeneity compared to other cohorts. As a result, the dataset is particularly well-suited for deciphering the shared embedding space of RNA and protein. After data preprocessing, we collected a total of 1351 pan-cancer samples with 5738 protein abundance and 18,860 RNA expression data (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

Convergence analysis across 100 training epochs revealed optimal validation loss at epoch 56 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Model performance was assessed by computing cosine similarity between RNA-protein embedding vectors from the same samples (the Match group) versus mismatched pairs (the No-match group) (Fig. 2c). The Match group demonstrated substantially higher similarity scores than the No-match group in both training and validation sets. Specifically, Match group demonstrated probability density peaking near one, whereas the No-match group exhibited a distribution with a mean close to zero. After that, we refined the CLIP framework using 210 glioma samples (99 from CPTAC, 111 from CGGA) with matched RNA and protein data through 20-epoch fine-tuning with early stopping at epoch 11 (minimal validation loss; Supplementary Fig. 2b). Multi-cohort validation encompassing glioma, and a held-out “Unseen” cohort6 confirmed preserved discriminative capacity between Match and No-match groups across all datasets (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, we projected RNA and protein embeddings into a unified t-SNE space and quantified pairwise distances between matched samples versus random pairs (Supplementary Fig. 2c–h). Matched sample pairs exhibited substantially closer distance compared to mismatched counterparts. These analyses validated the framework’s capacity to derive coordinated cross-modal representations through joint dimensionality reduction, establishing intrinsic RNA-protein correspondence in the shared latent space.

Training the multi-task VAE model and evaluating T2Pdecoder performance in glioma and breast cancer cohorts

To establish a robust glioma proteomic training set, we systematically consolidated global protein quantification data across nine cohorts, obtaining a merged dataset of 1422 glioma samples (Fig. 2a, b). The model completed 200 training epochs, with epoch 182 selected as optimal (Supplementary Fig. 2i, j).

Spearman correlation coefficient (SCC) analysis revealed robust concordance between reconstructed and experimental proteomic profiles, with median SCC values of 0.597 in the training set and 0.532 in the validation set (Supplementary Fig. 2k), highlighting the effectiveness of latent space constraints in recovering protein distributions.

After training the CLIP and VAE models, we evaluated T2Pdecoder’s performance by predicting protein abundance from RNA data in samples with matched RNA and protein data. The predicted proteins showed consistent concordance with experimental measurements across training, validation, and independent test datasets (Noted as “Unseen”, Fig. 2e). Notably, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)35 of the most accurately predicted proteins using the Hallmarks of Cancer gene sets (MSigDB)36 identified two strongly predicted pathways: cell proliferation-related processes and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (Fig. 2f).

To validate cross-tissue applicability, we expanded the training and evaluation of T2Pdecoder to breast cancer, using integrated cohorts comprising 573 tumor samples from CPTAC, Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (GGH)37, and NC202238, including 249 samples with paired RNA–protein data and 300 samples with protein-only data (Supplementary Fig. 3a).

T2Pdecoder achieved comparable performance to their glioma implementations when pretrained on pan-cancer data and fine-tuned using breast cancer samples (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). RNA-protein pairs from the same samples (the Match group) demonstrated significantly higher similarity scores compared to inter-sample pairs (the No-match group) (Supplementary Fig. 3b). T2Pdecoder’s protein predictions exhibited stronger correlations with experimental proteomic data than direct RNA-protein correlations (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Although performance in breast cancer was modestly attenuated relative to glioma, likely reflecting the smaller number of training samples, the model maintained consistent prediction of biologically relevant pathways, including cell proliferation and OXPHOS (Supplementary Fig. 3f). These findings confirm T2Pdecoder’s adaptability across cancer types while preserving biological pathway relevance.

Comparison of T2Pdecoder and baseline models

We then evaluated T2Pdecoder’s advantage over simply equating RNA expression to protein abundance. T2Pdecoder-predicted proteins showed systematically stronger correlations with experimental proteomic measurements compared to direct RNA-protein correlations across all datasets. The corresponding effect sizes (95% confidence intervals) were 0.196 (0.188–0.204) in the training set, 0.088 (0.075–0.098) in the validation set, and 0.075 (0.067–0.082) in the unseen set, indicating attenuation of predictive gain in independent cohorts while preserving consistent directionality (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Fig. 2l, and Supplementary Data 1). Moreover, upon counting the genes where T2Pdecoder outperformed RNA in predicting protein abundance, we observed that T2Pdecoder consistently provided better predictions for actual proteins compared to RNA across all datasets for most genes (Fig. 2h). Finally, functional analysis of these better-predicted genes revealed enriched association with cell proliferation pathways and OXPHOS processes (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), particularly involving mitochondrial membrane complexes as defined by Gene Ontology (GO)39 cellular component (CC) terms such as Mitochondrial Protein Complex (MPC), Inner Mitochondrial Membrane Protein Complex (IMMPC), and Respirasome (Fig. 2i). These findings suggest that modest improvements in protein abundance inference may still preserve biologically informative pathway-level signals.

To directly address the motivation that RNA is a poor proxy for protein levels, we rigorously evaluated T2Pdecoder’s performance on genes with inherently weak RNA–protein correlation. For each cohort, we defined a gene subset where the measured SCC between RNA expression and protein abundance was low (SCC < 0.2). Comparative analysis restricted to these challenging genes confirmed that accurate prediction is more difficult, leading to a modest overall reduction in performance metrics. However, T2Pdecoder’s predictions for these weakly correlated genes achieved consistently and significantly higher SCC than the original RNA expression did (Supplementary Fig. 2m).

We next compared T2Pdecoder with two baseline models for protein abundance prediction: Lasso regression40 and a random forest-based ensemble model, which is the winner method of the NCI-CPTAC DREAM proteogenomics Challenge41,42. While T2Pdecoder’s integrative architecture leverages both paired and unpaired proteomic data, these baseline models fundamentally require strictly paired RNA-protein datasets for model construction. Accordingly, all available glioma paired RNA-protein data (n = 371) were aggregated and split into training dataset and validation dataset (training: 80%, n = 296; validation: 20%, n = 75) for model fitting and evaluation. Despite the incorporation of L1-regularization, the Lasso model exhibited profound overfitting in high-dimensional biological data. It achieved near-perfect training performance (median SCC = 0.988) but collapsed on the validation set (median SCC = 0.399) and the independent CITE-seq GBM single-cell test data (median SCC = 0.063). The ensemble model similarly overfitted the training data (median SCC = 0.606), yet performed poorly on the validation set (median SCC = 0.431) and the single-cell test data (median SCC = 0.150). In contrast, T2Pdecoder exhibited more stable performance across all evaluations, achieving a median SCC of 0.465 on the validation set and 0.452 on the CITE-seq GBM dataset (Fig. 2j). This improvement is further supported by effect size analysis: effect sizes (95% confidence intervals) for T2Pdecoder versus Lasso and T2Pdecoder versus Ensemble were 0.061 (0.055–0.066) and 0.025 (0.019–0.032) in the validation set, and 0.280 (0.248–0.330) and 0.235 (0.183–0.260) in the CITE-seq GBM dataset.

Comparative performance evaluation of T2Pdecoder and existing single-cell proteomic prediction algorithms

To enable direct comparison with single-cell-specific algorithms, we adapted Hu et al. benchmarking framework43 using CITE-seq GBM data30 (21,589 cells; 14,131 RNA features, 268 surface proteins). Although T2Pdecoder achieves pan-proteomic prediction (5738 proteins) across bulk and single-cell samples, the benchmarking necessitated restrictive evaluation on 79 proteins (intersection between T2Pdecoder’s predictive capacity and experimentally measured markers) compatible with other single-cell models. (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b).

After applying quality control filters consistent with source data specifications, we partitioned the dataset into training (80%) and test (20%) sets via random sampling. All algorithms were trained or fine-tuned on the training set and evaluated on the test set. Performance quantification was based five complementary metrics: (1) Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) comparing predicted versus ground-truth matrices at both cell-cell pairs and protein-protein pairs; (2) correlation matrix distance (CMD) assessing differences between predicted and experimental covariance patterns at both cell-level and protein-level; (3) root mean square error (RMSE) measuring global prediction deviation.

Benchmarking analysis of 13 computational methods across five evaluation metrics showed that no single algorithm consistently outperformed others across all criteria, with different methods exhibiting strengths in different aspects of performance. We therefore use RMSE as the primary metric for quantitative comparison (Fig. 2k), while presenting the remaining four metrics as complementary measures that capture distinct characteristics of prediction quality (Supplementary Fig. 4c–f). Under RMSE-based evaluation, the methods show no clear separation, with most algorithms exhibiting similar error levels and T2Pdecoder ranking among the better-performing approaches. To facilitate interpretation of the multi-metric results, we further visualize the performance profiles of each method using radar charts (Supplementary Fig. 4g, h), which illustrate relative strengths and trade-offs across evaluation dimensions. Together, these results indicate that T2Pdecoder achieves performance comparable to existing approaches under single-cell benchmarking settings, supporting its extensibility to single-cell data without relying on evaluation metrics optimized specifically for single-cell models.

To further assess the robustness of T2Pdecoder in single-cell settings, we additionally evaluated the model on an external glioblastoma CITE-seq dataset (TSKGA44), which profiles over 60,000 cells with paired RNA expression and antibody-derived protein measurements. After intersecting with T2Pdecoder’s predicted protein space, 47 proteins were available for validation (Supplementary Fig. 4i). Using the same fine-tuning and evaluation protocol as in the primary single-cell analysis, T2Pdecoder achieved stable and consistent predictive performance across training and validation splits (Supplementary Fig. 4j). These results support the reproducibility of T2Pdecoder’s performance in single-cell datasets and further demonstrate its extensibility to single-cell proteogenomic data.

Samples were stratified into four molecular subtypes defined by Garofano et al.7,45 (GPM: glycolytic/plurimetabolic; MTC: mitochondrial; NEU: neuronal; PPR: proliferative/progenitor). We observed several shared enrichment patterns across all three results. Pathways such as Inflammatory response, Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT), Interferon-gamma/alpha response were consistently present across all versions. Additionally, some functional pathways were enriched exclusively in both the actual protein data and T2Pdecoder-predicted proteins within specific subtypes, but not in RNA expression data. These pathways include OXPHOS, Cell Cycle-related pathways, and Fatty acid metabolism (highlighted in red). In contrast, some pathways, such as TGF-beta signaling and Protein secretion, were enriched solely in RNA expression, with no corresponding enrichment in either actual protein or T2Pdecoder-predicted protein abundance (highlighted in blue).

To provide a systematic quantitative assessment beyond the visual patterns in the heatmap, we computed pairwise correlations of ssGSEA enrichment scores across all gene sets derived from the three input types (Supplementary Fig. 5c–e). This analysis revealed that enrichment profiles based on T2Pdecoder predictions showed strong overall concordance with those from actual protein measurements, whereas profiles based on RNA expression correlated substantially more weakly with the proteomic data. The direct relationship between T2Pdecoder-predicted and RNA-based enrichment closely mirrored the underlying RNA-protein correlation, indicating that T2Pdecoder’s primary advantage lies in its improved approximation of protein-level biology. Notably, the most pronounced individual discrepancy was observed for the enrichment of the OXPHOS pathway specifically within the NEU subtype, consistent with the qualitative pattern highlighted in Fig. 3a.

Next, we conducted a detailed comparison of T2Pdecoder and RNA expression, selecting OXPHOS as a representative example. We generated boxplot visualizations across the three versions using normalized enrichment scores (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 5f). Within the NEU subtype, the OXPHOS pathway exhibited comparable enrichment scores between experimental and predicted proteomic profiles. Notably, both proteomic datasets demonstrated statistically superior enrichment scores relative to transcriptomic-derived values.

In our analysis of genes associated with the OXPHOS pathway, T2Pdecoder demonstrated higher concordance with experimental proteomic measurements compared to RNA expression for most genes (Fig. 3c). We identified the 20 genes exhibiting maximal divergence between T2Pdecoder and RNA expression, subsequently comparing their correlation patterns across CPTAC cohorts for experimental protein profiles, T2Pdecoder predictions, and transcriptomics (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 5g, and Supplementary Data 3). This multi-modal comparison revealed consistent concordance between T2Pdecoder-predicted and experimental protein quantities (Pearson’s r > 0.65, red annotations), whereas transcriptomics exhibited a negligible correlation with experimental protein quantities (r < −0.1, blue annotations).

In summary, T2Pdecoder-predicted proteomic profiles more closely matched experimentally measured pathway-level protein patterns than transcriptomic data alone. The distinct pathway activation patterns observed between transcriptomic and proteomic measurements within the same samples further suggest that protein-level inference may provide complementary biological information for functional pathway characterization.

T2Pdecoder has demonstrated comparable predictive accuracy when fine-tuned for targeted single-cell protein prediction. We next investigate the biological insights revealed through systematic application of T2Pdecoder to predict all 5738 proteins in single cells. Compared to conventional approaches targeting dozens to hundreds of proteins, pan-proteomic profiling enables discovery of biological signatures at single-cell resolution.

A critical strength of T2Pdecoder lies in its ability to handle sparse single-cell transcriptomic data through its CLIP RNA encoder, which compresses high-dimensional RNA profiles into low-dimensional latent representations while preserving biological signals. Visualization of CLIP-derived RNA embeddings demonstrated robust segregation of major cellular identities, despite the inherent sparsity and technical noise of scRNA-seq data (Supplementary Fig. 6a). This capability underscores T2Pdecoder’s robustness in resolving cellular heterogeneity and its potential to bridge bulk and single-cell protein predictions.

Notably, single-cell RNA analyses revealed pronounced batch effects among patients. However, T2Pdecoder-predicted protein profiles mitigated these technical artifacts. Because malignant cells are expected to exhibit substantial inter-patient biological heterogeneity, we next assessed whether such biological variation was preserved in the predicted protein space by restricting the analysis to tumor cells and visualizing their embeddings using t-SNE (Supplementary Fig. 6b). When annotated by tumor subclusters, malignant cells formed well-separated groups, indicating that major tumor-intrinsic programs and substructure were retained. Importantly, when the same embedding was annotated by patient identity, samples remained largely distinguishable rather than collapsing into a fully mixed distribution, suggesting that inter-patient heterogeneity was not removed. These observations suggest that contrastive learning on paired RNA-protein data enhanced the model’s capacity to prioritize biological over technical variation. Furthermore, the VAE framework incorporated intrinsic denoising capabilities, enabling T2Pdecoder-predicted proteins to more reliably elucidate biological differences across cellular populations.

To further compare T2Pdecoder-predicted protein profiles with scRNA-seq data, we performed genome-wide differential expression analysis across cell states in both modalities (Fig. 4b). Established molecular markers for each cell state (annotated in red) were observed relatively high average expression levels in both protein and RNA profiles. Notably, over 80% of marker genes exhibited predicted protein abundance exceeding overall average abundance within their respective cell states (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Data 4), demonstrating strong functional concordance between T2Pdecoder predictions and transcriptional data in capturing cell identity signatures.

Moreover, we sought to determine whether T2Pdecoder’s predictions captured unique biological features beyond those revealed by scRNA-seq data. Building on bulk-level evidence of enhanced OXPHOS pathway representation in predicted proteins, we analyzed the expression dynamics of the previously identified 20-gene OXPHOS-associated signature across glioma stem-like (Stem-like), proliferating stem-like (Prolif.stem-like), and differentiated (Diff.-like) cellular states (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Data 5). Previous studies have indicated that glioma stem cells have different states and rely on different energy supply mechanisms47,48: quiescent stem cells primarily depend on OXPHOS for ATP production, whereas proliferating stem cells shift toward glycolysis to meet increased energy demands. Upon differentiation, cells revert to relying predominantly on OXPHOS, although at lower levels compared to undifferentiated stem cells49–51.

Analysis of T2Pdecoder-predicted proteomes captured these established metabolic state dynamics. Rank-based evaluation of OXPHOS-associated proteins revealed pronounced abundance differences across cellular states: Stem-like cells showed highest expression levels, Prolif.stem-like cells exhibited minimal values, and Diff.-like cells displayed intermediate profiles (Fig. 4e). In contrast, transcriptomic rankings of identical OXPHOS genes showed no state-dependent stratification. This proteome-specific signature remained statistically robust across different gene set sizes (top 10/50/all genes; Supplementary Fig. 6c–h), confirming T2Pdecoder’s unique capacity to resolve metabolic state transitions at the single-cell level.

To further investigate differences in OXPHOS-related programs across malignant cell states, we re-annotated all malignant cells using the single-cell state framework defined by ref. 52 and repeated the analysis following the same strategy applied to the original cell-state annotations. The correspondence between the two annotation schemes is shown in Supplementary Fig. 6i. Using the same statistical framework, we compared rank percentiles of the top 20 OXPHOS-related genes across the four cell states. At the predicted protein-level, OXPHOS-associated proteins were significantly different among all four states. In contrast, RNA-based analysis revealed significantly reduced OXPHOS expression only in the MES-like state, with no significant differences among the remaining three states. The lower OXPHOS signal in MES-like cells is consistent with their enrichment for hypoxia-associated programs, providing a plausible biological interpretation (Supplementary Fig. 6j, k).

In addition, our analysis revealed distinct divergence patterns between T2Pdecoder-predicted proteomes and RNA expression profiles. In endothelial cells, laminin family genes (LAMB2/LAMA5/LAMC1) showed elevated predicted protein abundance despite negligible RNA expression (Fig. 4f). Conversely, Prolif.stem-like cells exhibited high DDX5 protein predictions while displaying notably low RNA expression levels (Fig. 4g). This observation is consistent with prior studies demonstrating DDX5’s critical role in promoting glioma proliferation53–55, suggesting that T2Pdecoder capture post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying oncogenic processes.

Within glioblastoma functional subtypes, the PPR subtype is characterized by heightened tumor proliferation. Leveraging CPTAC bulk protein datasets, we compared DDX5 expression between PPR and non-PPR samples (Fig. 4h). While transcriptomic analysis revealed no significant difference, both experimental and predicted proteomics profiles showed elevated DDX5 abundance in the PPR subtype. These results support the biological relevance of T2Pdecoder’s DDX5 predictions observed in single-cell analyses, particularly within proliferative stem-like populations.

Collectively, the systematic analysis demonstrates that T2Pdecoder provides single-cell proteomic insights that are complementary to scRNA-seq data, underscoring its potential to resolve functional heterogeneity across cancer cell states.

Clustering glioma samples based on T2Pdecoder

We aimed to leverage T2Pdecoder for large-scale proteomic profiling of gliomas to identify biologically relevant subclusters. Following RNA data normalization, T2Pdecoder-predicted protein abundance matrices were subjected to unbiased consensus clustering56, resulting in four protein-defined subclusters per molecular subtype. To better characterize the subgroups, we performed GSEA using the Hallmarks of cancer gene sets for each group and observed a high degree of similarity among the four subgroups across all three molecular subtypes (Fig. 5b). Subgroup 1 was predominantly enriched in pathways related to OXPHOS, Fatty acid metabolism, and Myogenesis. Subgroup 2 showed enrichment in DNA repair, E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, and MYC targets. Subgroup 3 was enriched in Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), coagulation, complement, and immune response pathways. Subgroup 4 was primarily associated with OXPHOS.

We subsequently performed expanded GSEA leveraging Reactome pathway annotations (Supplementary Fig. 7b), which further confirmed the conserved functional profiles across all four subgroups spanning molecular subtypes. Notably, our functional analysis revealed direct correspondence between these subgroups and the four functional categories previously defined in GBM: Subgroup 1 mapped to MTC, Subgroup 2 to PPR, Subgroup 3 to GPM, and Subgroup 4 to NEU. These findings validated the biological relevance of unsupervised proteomic clustering via T2Pdecoder predictions, which not only re-defined established IDHwt glioma subtypes but also suggested a universal classification independent of IDH mutational status, thereby establishing a robust orthogonal classification framework for glioma subtyping.

We further annotated bulk RNA-seq samples using the transcriptional subtype framework defined by ref. 5, including Classical (CL), Mesenchymal (MES), and Proneural (PN) subtypes. We then systematically mapped the T2Pdecoder-predicted proteomic subgroups onto these RNA-based classes across glioma molecular subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 7c–e). Within IDHwt tumors, we observe a clear correspondence between the T2Pdecoder-derived Subgroup 3 and the MES transcriptional subtype, consistent with the enrichment of EMT and immune-related pathways identified in this subgroup. In contrast, for IDH-mutant tumors, the transcriptional framework shows limited discriminative power, as it was originally developed in IDHwt gliomas, resulting in the majority of IDH-mutant samples being assigned to the PN class. Together, these observations further highlight the value of T2Pdecoder in enabling protein-based classification that captures biological structure not fully resolved by transcriptomic stratification alone.

It is worth noting that we also conducted GSEA for these four subgroups using RNA expression profiles (Supplementary Fig. 7a). While lots of pathway enrichment overlaps were observed between RNA-based and predicted proteomic analyses, transcriptomic data inadequately resolved key subgroup-defining features, particularly OXPHOS and other metabolic pathway signatures. In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) based on T2Pdecoder-predicted protein profiles revealed clear separation of the four subgroups across all three glioma molecular subtypes, with the exception of the IDHmut-codel subtype, in which subgroup 1 and subgroup 3 showed partial overlap. In contrast, PCA based on RNA expression data failed to distinguish the subgroups, showing substantial mixing across all three subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 7g). This limitation aligned with our prior single-sample GSEA findings in the CPTAC cohort (Fig. 3a), reinforcing the necessity of proteome-level resolution for comprehensive functional characterization.

Additionally, we conducted association analysis for clinical and genomic information across the proteomic subgroups (Fig. 5c). The results showed a clear correspondence between our unbiased subgroups derived from predicted proteins and established functional subtypes. Furthermore, we observed several significant associations, including Grade 4, PDGFRA amplification, CDK4 amplification, CDKN2A deletion, and PTPRZ1--MET gene fusions with IDHmut-non-codel subgroup 2, which were consistent with existing knowledge57–59.

To assess the clinical relevance of the protein-based subgroups identified by T2Pdecoder, we conducted survival analyses within each of the three major glioma molecular subtypes (IDHwt, IDHmut-non-codel, and IDHmut-codel), accounting for the critical confounding factor of tumor grade. A consistent survival pattern emerged across all subtypes, with Subgroups 1 and 4 associated with more favorable outcomes and Subgroups 2 and 3 with poorer prognosis (Fig. 5d–f). Critically, this pattern is not solely driven by uneven grade distribution. In the IDHwt cohort, where all tumors are WHO grade 4, grade cannot confound the observed survival differences. In IDHmut cohorts, while Subgroup 2 is enriched for grade 4 tumors, Subgroup 3 exhibits similarly poor survival without such enrichment, suggesting captured biology beyond grade. To formally address this, we performed multivariable Cox regression adjusting for grade in the IDHmut cohorts. Proteomic subgroup membership remained a significant, independent predictor of survival after grade adjustment (Supplementary Fig. 7h–k). Specifically, Subgroups 2 and 3 consistently showed significantly higher hazard ratios compared to Subgroup 1. The recurrence of this prognostic stratification across molecular contexts, maintained after rigorous adjustment for tumor grade, demonstrates that the T2Pdecoder-derived proteomic classification captures fundamental biological drivers of tumor aggressiveness and provides independent prognostic value.

Furthermore, the biological and clinical relevance of these four predicted protein subgroups is strongly supported by their alignment with classifications derived from actual proteomic data. In our parallel study focused on IDHmut-non-codel gliomas, a direct proteome-based classification of the measured proteomes yielded clusters that closely mirrored the subgroups defined by T2Pdecoder predictions (Supplementary Fig. 7f). Notably, the distinct characteristics of Subgroup 3 were further investigated and validated in that work, confirming it as a novel subtype with defined biological features and independent prognostic value59. This convergence between prediction-based and measurement-based frameworks reinforces that T2Pdecoder enables a biologically meaningful stratification of glioma with direct clinical translation potential.

Discussion

T2Pdecoder provides researchers with a framework to explore samples with RNA expression data from a protein-centric perspective, enabling the discovery of biological insights may be less apparent in conventional RNA expression analyses. Our framework offers two main advantages in protein abundance inference. Firstly, T2Pdecoder eliminates the requirement for gene-level correspondence between RNA features and protein identities. Instead of relying on one-to-one RNA-protein gene matching, T2Pdecoder learns a many-to-many mapping from transcriptomic profiles to protein abundance through a shared latent embedding. This design enables the model to leverage the full transcriptomic context to predict protein abundance without imposing gene-symbol alignment constraints, thereby reducing data loss commonly introduced by RNA–protein gene matching procedures. Secondly, T2Pdecoder enables prediction of a wide range of protein abundances. Previous protein prediction methods typically limit predictions to tens or hundreds of proteins, requiring preselection of target proteins and potentially restricting downstream analyses. In contrast, the broader protein coverage provided by T2Pdecoder facilitates diverse proteome-centric analyses across transcriptomic datasets.

The architecture of T2Pdecoder also helps address the limited availability of paired RNA-protein data. By decoupling RNA-protein representation learning from protein abundance prediction, T2Pdecoder allows the predictive component to be trained using protein-only datasets, which are more widely available. In this context, cancer-type-specific adaptation is not strictly required but may further refine the representation space when such data are available. Notably, protein abundance prediction is not performed by a standalone pretrained model but relies on context-specific training, reflecting the variability of RNA-protein relationships across biological systems.

T2Pdecoder demonstrated comparable performance when fine-tuned on CITE-seq data compared to existing algorithms in the overlapping 79 proteins. Its application to single-cell prediction of 5738 proteins revealed reduction of batch-associated variation through dimensional alignment, consistent with observations in autoencoder-based multi-omics integration models despite lacking explicit batch correction training. Importantly, T2Pdecoder identified OXPHOS-related protein abundance shifts across tumor cell states that were less apparent from scRNA-seq data, suggesting that inferred protein profiles may provide complementary biological information in single-cell settings. Given the limited availability of experimentally measured proteins in current CITE-seq datasets, pan-proteomic single-cell predictions are interpreted here as exploratory. Accordingly, quantitative validation is restricted to proteins with available measurements, and predictions across the full 5738-protein panel are considered hypothesis-generating for downstream investigation.

Applying T2Pdecoder to glioma bulk samples, unsupervised clustering of IDHwt cases revealed four functional subtypes showed substantial concordance with subtypes derived from experimental protein data. Notably, the same subtypes persisted in IDHmut-non-codel/IDHmut-codel cohorts, showing conserved clinical correlations, pathway enrichments, and significant survival differences across all molecular subtypes. These subtypes in IDHmut-non-codel have recently been validated and carefully studied, whereas the proposed subtyping in IDHmut-codel patients remains underexplored. These findings suggest that T2Pdecoder-derived proteomic profiles may support biologically and clinically informative stratification analyses in glioma.

While T2Pdecoder has thus far been evaluated only in glioma and breast cancer, the framework may be extensible to additional cancer types and normal tissue contexts as larger paired RNA-protein datasets become available. Although predictive improvements attenuated in validation and unseen cohorts, biologically relevant pathway-level signals remained detectable in several downstream analyses. Model performance is also likely to depend on cohort composition, protein characteristics, and the availability of matched multi-omics data. Future studies incorporating larger and more diverse proteogenomic datasets, additional validation strategies, and expanded multimodal integration approaches may further improve model generalizability and biological interpretability. Potential extensions could include integration with DNA methylation, spatial transcriptomics, and histopathological imaging data to support broader multi-omics analyses.

Methods

Data collection and preprocessing

Pan-cancer datasets

Pan-cancer RNA expression and protein abundance data were obtained through the official CPTAC package (https://pypi.org/project/cptac). The CPTAC resource encompasses 10 cohorts representing distinct cancer types. Each cohort contains tumor samples and may additionally include normal and/or adjacent normal tissue samples. Among these, 1351 samples have both RNA and protein data. Proteins detected in fewer than 50% of samples per cohort were excluded, yielding 5738 consistently quantified proteins. Similarly, non-coding genes were filtered, retaining 18,860 protein-coding genes expressed across all samples (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Before integrating data across cohorts, quantile normalization was applied to each sample across all proteins. Missing values were subsequently imputed with zeros, followed by z-score normalization of each protein across all samples. The same z-score normalization procedure was applied to RNA expression data. After cohort integration, t-SNE visualizations were generated to assess potential batch effects across cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 1b).

Glioma bulk datasets

Glioma proteomic data were aggregated from nine cohorts: CPTAC34, HS60, NC_Imm61, NeuO_162, NeuO_262, ANC_LGG63, CGGA (in-house data), AN_NAT64 and CRM_IDHmut65. Protein quantification matrices were accessible via original publications for most cohorts (excluding NC_Imm, CGGA, and HS), with raw mass spectrometry data available for these three exceptions.

For the NC_Imm cohort, raw mass spectra were processed using MaxQuant v2.1.4.066 with standard parameters, searching against reviewed UniProt human protein entries (20,571 sequences, October 2022 release). Fixed modifications included carbamidomethylation (+57.0214 Da) of cysteine residues, while variable modifications encompassed N-terminal acetylation (+42.0106 Da) and methionine oxidation (+15.9949 Da). Precursor mass tolerance was set to 4.5 ppm with 0.5 Da tolerance for fragment ions. Both protein and peptide identifications were filtered at 1% false discovery rate (FDR) using reverse decoy databases. Label-free quantification (LFQ) was performed using razor/unique peptides with intensity-based normalization. Reverse database hits and contaminant proteins were systematically excluded.

For CGGA and HS cohorts, DIA-NN v1.8.167 analyses employed identical protein libraries with standard configurations: Trypsin/P enzyme specificity allowing one missed cleavage; cysteine carbamidomethylation as fixed modification; variable modifications limited to one per peptide; 5–30 amino acid peptide lengths; precursor charges 1–4; m/z ranges 200–1800 for both MS1 and MS2; MS1 accuracy and scan window parameters maintained at instrument default values (0 ppm). Breast cancer bulk datasets

Preprocessing procedures, including log2 transformation, quantile normalization, and missing value imputation with downshifted normal distribution parameters, were executed following the identical protocol established for glioma proteomic data.

CILP model construction in T2Pdecoder

Architectures

Two identical fully connected neural architectures were constructed in PyTorch77 v2.0.1 to function as RNA and protein encoders. Each encoder contained five sequentially connected layers: an input layer receiving high-dimensional features, three hidden layers, and an output layer generating low-dimensional latent representations. Layer-wise processing incorporated batch normalization followed by ReLU activation across all hidden layers, while the final output layer exclusively employed ReLU transformation without normalization.

Hyperparameters of CLIP

Hyperparameter optimization was conducted on both CPTAC and CGGA datasets through an efficient random search strategy78, which systematically explored high-dimensional parameter spaces. Final configurations included RNA and protein encoder input dimensions of 18,860 and 5738, respectively, with three hidden layers progressively reducing dimensionality (951 nodes, 184 nodes, 162 nodes) before projecting into a 12-dimensional latent space. A consistent dropout rate of 0.1 was applied across hidden layers during training. The batch size was set to 68. Optimization employed the Adam algorithm79 with learning rate = 0.0005 and weight decay = 0.00001. A random 10% subset of the samples was reserved for validation, while the remaining samples were used to train the model.

During model adaptation, hyperparameter configurations derived from initial optimization were rigorously maintained for glioma data applications. For breast cancer fine-tuning, the batch size was specifically adjusted to 16 samples per batch while preserving all other architectural parameters.

Multi-task BN-VAE construction in T2Pdecoder

Architectures

A dual-branched neural architecture comprising symmetrical encoder-decoder modules was constructed in PyTorch v2.0.1. The encoder module incorporated four layers: an input layer mapping high-dimensional features to three hidden layers, culminating in a latent bottleneck. Mirroring this topology, the decoder module reversed the dimensional progression through three expanding hidden layers, terminating in an output reconstruction layer. ReLU activations governed all but the final encoding layer.

For the bottleneck layer, the mean and variance of the latent representation were calculated by the encoder, followed by a batch normalization layer. Reparameterization was then performed based on the scaled mean and variance to generate a random “Code”, which was fed into the decoder.

Hyperparameters of BN-VAE

Hyperparameter configurations for the BN-VAE framework were independently optimized for glioma and breast cancer datasets through randomized search protocols. In glioma implementations, encoder-decoder architectures were structured with symmetrical 5738-dimensional input/output layers interconnected by three hidden layers of sequentially decreasing dimensionality (470 nodes, 462 nodes, 244 nodes), compressing features into a 12-dimensional latent representation. Hidden layer dropout regularization was consistently maintained at 0.1 during training phases. Optimization procedures employed the Adam algorithm with learning rate 0.0005 and weight decay 0.00001, utilizing batches of 184 samples to balance computational efficiency and gradient stability.

Breast cancer configurations preserved shared architectural foundations, including identical input/output dimensions (5738 nodes) and latent space size (12 dimensions), while adapting hidden layer topologies to 748, 487, and 421 nodes. Mini-batch size was reduced to 20 samples to accommodate smaller cohort characteristics, coupled with a lowered learning rate (0.0001) to prevent overfitting. Weight decay parameters remained fixed at 0.00001 across both implementations, with dropout rates maintained at 0.1.

Mapping criterion between protein and RNA

The analysis was restricted to canonical RNA-protein pairs to eliminate confounding effects from isoform-level complexity. Genes were retained to calculate the correlations only when paired protein-RNA expression measurements were available, establishing a strict one-to-one mapping between mRNA transcripts and their corresponding proteins.

Implementation of baseline models

The Lasso regression model was implemented using the sklearn.linear_model module in Python, with default parameters except for the regularization strength (alpha), which was set to 0.01. For the ensemble model, we adopted the top-performing approach from the NCI-CPTAC DREAM Proteogenomics Challenge41, using the preprocessed RNA–protein paired data from T2Pdecoder, and retaining all three models (Generic, Gene-specific, and Trans-tissue models) with parameter settings consistent with the original implementation. Training of the Trans-tissue model was performed using the CPTAC pan-cancer cohort. For each of the 5738 proteins, a random forest model was trained using all available CPTAC paired samples (n = 1351) and the full set of 18,860 RNA expression features. The final prediction was obtained by integrating outputs from the three components (Generic, Gene-specific, and Trans-tissue models) using the original weighting scheme described in the winning method.

Comparison of 13 single-cell protein prediction algorithms

The 11 algorithms (LIGER, Seurat, cTP-Net, CMAE, totalVI, BABEL, scArches, sciPENN, scVAEIT, scMOGNN) benchmarked in the prior comparative study (BM study) were re-implemented using the standardized protocols established in the original investigation. Training and evaluation pipelines were directly replicated from BM study and applied to CITE-seq glioblastoma (GBM) datasets without procedural modifications.

For model-specific implementations, the bulk glioma-pretrained T2Pdecoder was fine-tuned on CITE-seq GBM data with a batch size of 1024 across 500 epochs. During fine-tuning of the T2Pdecoder, only output nodes corresponding to the 79 selected proteins were included in the loss calculation. scTranslator implementation followed the author-specified protocol from the original repository, utilizing the “scTranslator 2 M” foundation model with a batch size of 4 and 100 training epochs.

(1) For each algorithm, the median PCC across all proteins (protein-protein PCC) or all cells (cell-cell PCC) was used to represent the overall PCC value.

Effect size and confidence interval calculation

Effect size was defined as the median of paired differences in SCC between predicted and measured protein abundance for two methods (where each pair corresponds to the same protein). Confidence intervals were estimated using a paired bootstrap procedure. Briefly, proteins were resampled with replacement while preserving pairing, and the median difference in SCC was recalculated for each bootstrap replicate (n = 1000). The 95% confidence interval was obtained from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution.

Four functional subtypes of glioma classification

The signature gene lists of all four subtypes can be found in supplementary tables of the previous article7. The Competitive Gene Set and regulon test was used to check whether the signature of four subtypes was highly ranked in each sample’s expression with the help of mwwGST function (in yaGST, R package https://rdrr.io/github/miccec/yaGST/man/yaGST-package.html). The mwwGST can calculate the normalized enrichment score (NES) of each subtype for all samples with RNA expression data. The subtype with the highest NES of all subtypes would be considered as the final subtype of the given sample.

Glioma subtype annotation based on bulk RNA-seq

Bulk RNA-seq glioma samples were annotated into transcriptional subtypes following the classification framework described by ref. 5. Subtype assignment was performed using the official R implementation provided by the authors, which was downloaded from the supplementary materials of the original publication. Briefly, normalized bulk RNA-seq expression matrices were used as input to the package, and subtype scores were calculated based on the predefined gene signatures corresponding to the three canonical glioma transcriptional programs: Classical (CL), Mesenchymal (MES), and Proneural (PN). This annotation procedure was applied uniformly across all bulk datasets analyzed in this study, including IDH-wildtype, IDH-mutant non-codeleted, and IDH-mutant codeleted gliomas. The resulting transcriptional subtype labels were used for downstream comparisons with T2Pdecoder-predicted proteomic classes.

Consensus clustering

Consensus clustering was performed in three molecular subtypes of glioma samples using the ConsensusClusterPlus83 (R package) based on the top 2000 most variable predicted protein abundance. The clustering algorithm was set to K-means clustering with Euclidean distance; Each clustering was repeated 100 times; Each time 90% samples were selected randomly with all features.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA35) and single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) were conducted using the GSEAPY Python package. The analysis utilized the 50 hallmark gene sets (h.all.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt), the 1026 gene sets derived from GO Cellular Component ontology (c5.go.cc.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt) and the 1736 Canonical Pathways gene sets derived from the Reactome pathway database (c2.cp.reactome.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt) from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB36). Permutations were performed 1000 times within each gene set.

Survival analysis

Survival analysis was conducted with KaplanMeierFitter and CoxPHFitter in python package “lifelines”84 and R package “survival”85. The log-rank test was applied to estimate the different survival outcomes across different subgroups.

Copy number variation prediction

Copy Number Alteration Prediction from gene Expression in human cancers (CNAPE)86 was used to predict the copy number variation of each glioma sample based on RNA expression.

Statistical and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using the SciPy Python package, with implementation of the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for group comparisons, Spearman correlation coefficient, Pearson correlation coefficient for similarity measurement, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical contingency analysis. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses.
