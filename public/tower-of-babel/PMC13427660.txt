Automatic prediction of patients early discontinuation in oncology clinical trials
Authors: L Olgiati, Q Filori, S Toueress, C Brutti-Mairesse, L Zidane, N Ikhlef, L Gueguen, L Verlingue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly discontinuation (ED) in clinical trials (CTs) is frequent and deleterious for the patients, the care team, and the study duration. ED comprises screening failure or discontinuation during the first month of the treatment phase, and is often difficult to predict by clinicians. We aim at predicting ED by automatic analysis of patient’s clinical record using language models (LMs).Materials and methodsWe fine-tuned a French LM on the oncology clinical reports of a French cancer center, and obtained a pretrained LM named OncoBERT. We then selected consultation reports of patients included in oncology CTs for any tumor type that we used to fine-tune OncoBERT and obtained a new model for ED prediction. We carried out a retrospective and prospective evaluation and used eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) methods to interpret the predictions.ResultsOn the retrospective test cohort of 1007 reports, the model achieved a precision of 0.77, recall of 0.95, and could have decreased the ED rate from 25.3% to 21.3%. On the prospective test cohort it reached a precision of 0.75, recall of 0.75, and could have decreased the ED rate from 33.7% to 27%. Using XAI showed that the words used by the model to predict ED reflect deterioration of general condition, a well-known factor of ED.ConclusionWe have developed a LM that is portable, explainable, with near-human performances for ED prediction in oncology CTs. We anticipate that democratization of automatic trial matching tools should be complemented by ED prediction tools to fully optimize access to CTs and patient recruitment.Key words: artificial intelligence, clinical trials, language model, Molecular Tumor Board, oncology

Introduction

Clinical trials (CTs) represent significant therapeutic options in oncology,1 but <8% of adult patients diagnosed with cancer participate in a CT in their medical history.2 Limited patient recruitment is mainly explained by the complex task of reviewing the selection criteria for each patient–trial pair. CT matching tools are performing this task (semi-)automatically.3 Language models (LMs) may allow to fully automatize this task and could thus significantly increase the number of patients referred to early and late phase CTs.4, 5, 6

Once included in a CT, patients go through a screening phase of ∼30 days during which their eligibility is verified. Afterward, the first 28 days of the treatment phase of an early phase CT are used to determine dose-limiting toxicity (DLT). We define early discontinuation (ED) as patient discontinuation from the CT within the screening or DLT periods. Around 40% of patients included in an early phase CT are affected by ED.7 To our knowledge, the ED rate for phase III CTs has not been reported in the literature.

The main reasons causing ED are screening failure, a new medical event related to the tumor evolution (meeting an exclusion criteria, or severe enough to motivate end of study participation), death, or withdrawal of consent.8,9 ED is detrimental for patients, who face a potentially deteriorated end of life by participating in the demanding procedures of the CT without benefiting from the therapy. It also impacts the CT as (i) the patient must be replaced, increasing duration and cost of the study, and (ii) in case of a serious adverse event, it is difficult to determine whether the event is attributable to the investigational drug.10 Additionally, it increases the workload of the care team and limits the inclusion of other patients.

To limit ED, many prognostic scores have been developed7 (Supplementary Material S1, Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). Some rely on clinical markers such as lactate dehydrogenase, albumin, and metastatic sites, while newer tools incorporate broader data and artificial intelligence (AI) techniques to improve prediction accuracy. Each tool has specific strengths and limitations depending on the treatment type and data complexity. Computing these scores is time consuming and has limited impact on the ED rate.8,9 Delorme et al.11 presented a first step toward predicting ED from patients’ clinical reports using machine learning and obtained strong results that didn’t generalize well to data from other hospitals.

There is a need for a tool specialized in predicting ED, automatized and interpretable, to help the decision of clinicians and reduce the workload. We propose an LM that predicts ED for all phases of CTs in oncology by automatic analysis of the patient’s clinical record before inclusion. We evaluate this tool both retrospectively and prospectively on real-world data from a French cancer center. Additionally, we study the model interpretation to highlight factors related to ED. Finally, we discuss the feasibility and ethical considerations of integrating this tool in a clinical workflow.

Materials and methods

Dataset

Cohort selection

For the retrospective dataset we used the clinical reports of patients seen at our institution between 1 January 2000 and 5 April 2023 who did not refuse access to their data. For the pretraining step (OncoBERT), we did not perform any further selection of data. For the task of ED prediction, we selected patients who were included in at least one CT during this period. We collected the pseudonymized patients’ textual reports as well as structured data concerning their inclusion in a CT, such as the trial phase and the dates of consent, first dose and end of trial participation (EOT). We conducted this study in compliance with the Helsinki declaration on human research. The study received local ethical approbation R201-004-579 on 1 July 2025. The study was not interventional, meaning that we did not use the results of the clinical trial matching tools to support the clinical decision.

Each time a patient was included in a CT, we computed the number of days between consent and EOT to determine the ED label. If there was >60 days between patient consent and EOT, the ED label is equal to 1, if not it is equal to 0. When a patient participated in two trials with consent dates within 30 days of each other, we applied the following rule: (i) if both inclusions have the same ED label, we keep the oldest inclusion and (ii) if the inclusions have different ED labels we discard both. If one patient had several inclusions for the same CT, we kept only the oldest one. We discarded reports with <250 characters (too short to be relevant), as well as reports with >2000 words (that would exceed the model context length). Finally, we selected one report per inclusion using a scoring function (Supplementary Material S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728).

For the task of ED prediction, we randomly split the retrospective dataset in a train set (75%), validation set (15%), and test set (10%). The splitting was carried out at the patient level to avoid any data leaking.

We generated two prospective datasets of patients seen in multidisciplinary boards primarily dedicated to orientation to CTs: (i) phase I board and (ii) Molecular Tumor Board (MTB). We gathered data of the patients seen at the phase I tumor board between October and November 2024 who had an early CT proposed. We selected patients with available medical reports, in numeric format (excluding PDF). We labeled each patient ED or trial continuation (TC), depending on their participation fewer or >60 days. We also gathered data on patients seen at the MTB between December 2024 and April 2025. We selected patients for which medical reports were available. These data were not annotated. For both prospective datasets, we selected one report per patient using a scoring function (Supplementary Material S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728).

Pseudonymization

All documents from the retrospective and prospective datasets were de-identified through an in-house model removing patient names, addresses, dates, and identifiers, following CNIL-compliant procedures.12 The documents were further pseudonymized using a Named Entity Recognition (NER) model on proper nouns including doctors names, hospital names, or cities. For the latter, we compared several open models (DistilCamemBERT base,13 GLiNER’s gliner_multi-v2.1,14 Flair’s ner-french,15 and spaCy fr_core_news_sm and fr_core_news large16) and manually revised the tokens identified by the models as proper nouns and classified them as true positives or false positives, allowing us to compute the F1-score and select the best NER model.

Data quality

There exist multiple types of textual reports (consultation, medical imaging, biology, etc.). We evaluated the amount of information in the texts to determine the types of reports that are the most relevant to predict ED. A clinician defined five criteria that are expected to help predict ED, as well as levels of completion for each criterion: (i) cancer type (complete or missing); (ii) disease history (complete, partial, or missing); (iii) general state (complete, partial, or missing); (iv) previous lines of treatment (complete, partial, or missing); and (v) molecular alterations (complete or missing). For a subset of our dataset, we manually annotated the completion of these criteria. This allowed us to determine the types of reports that are most relevant to predict ED.

Pretraining of OncoBERT

OncoBERT is a French foundation LM specialized in oncology, developed from the CamemBERT (RoBERTa-base) architecture.17,18 We applied a Continual Pretraining strategy,19 further training the original CamemBERT weights on domain-specific oncology data to improve adaptation to clinical language. The model was trained using the Masked Language Modeling objective, randomly masking 15% of tokens [80% replaced by (MASK), 10% kept, 10% randomly substituted]. OncoBERT preserves the 110 million parameters of CamemBERT and was trained for 50 epochs on a single NVIDIA A40 Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) with a 5e-5 learning rate.

Supervised fine-tuning for ED prediction

We added a binary classification layer on top of OncoBERT to predict TC or ED (Figure 1). The prediction is also used to generate an explanation using Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME; see Section “Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations”). We used a weighted cross-entropy loss to mitigate the anticipated class imbalance. We used a batch size of 32. We trained the model for 30 epochs with early stopping based on the validation set.

A binary supervised classifier predicts the label trial continuation (TC) or early discontinuation (ED). An explanation of the prediction is generated with LIME.

Hyperparameter search

The hyperparameters of the ED prediction training step included removal of numbers, punctuation, and stopwords. Because the pretrained model we used had a maximum embedding size of 512 tokens, we implemented different ways to truncate the reports. We implemented two methods of pooling the input embeddings of the classification layer. We carried out two steps of hyperparameter optimization, the first one to optimize preprocessing parameters and the second one to optimize training parameters (Table 1).

LIME explanations

The LIME method20 is an eXplainable artificial intelligence (XAI) method that generates model-agnostic local explanations for predictions of machine learning or deep learning models (Supplementary Material S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). We applied this method to our predictions and obtained token importance values that can be visually interpreted (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). The 10 tokens with the strongest weights are displayed. Tokens highlighted in blue pushed the prediction toward ED, and tokens highlighted in orange pushed the prediction toward TC.

Model evaluation

Retrospective evaluation

We chose the best model on the validation area under curve - receiver operating characteristics (AUROC). We also used the validation set to determine the optimal threshold of the classifier. We evaluated the model performance on the test set by computing the accuracy, precision, recall, negative predictive value, and AUROC.

Prospective evaluation

We evaluated the model prospectively on two different datasets.

•Phase I board

Phase I board

We measured human performance on the task of ED prediction. Two clinicians reviewed the reports without seeing the model predictions and assigned and ED/TC score between 0 and 1 (0 denoting the highest confidence that the patient will discontinue early, and 1 denoting the highest confidence that the patient will not).

We also used this dataset to qualitatively assess model performance. We generated explanations for the predictions using LIME (see Section “Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations”). Two clinicians reviewed the explanations to determine the relevance of the tokens used by the model for its prediction. They categorized each token as relevant or irrelevant. We identified true positives as relevant tokens used by the model and false positives as irrelevant tokens used by the model. Moreover, the clinicians annotated relevant tokens that were not used by the model (false negatives). Lastly, for each annotated token, they specified if the relevance or irrelevance related to the token on its own or to its semantic context.

•MTB

MTB

This dataset allowed us to determine predominant ED factors and see if they match the factors found in the literature (see Supplementary Material S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). For each patient, the model predicted ED or TC. We interpreted the predictions of the model using LIME. By pooling the results on all patients analyzed, we were able to determine the tokens that were most frequently associated with ED.

Calibration

We implemented two methods to improve model calibration: temperature scaling and Platt scaling.21 Temperature scaling consists in dividing the prediction logits (i.e. the inputs to the softmax function) by a learned scalar parameter. In our case this parameter was learned on the validation set. Platt scaling consists in modifying the softmax function to integrate a logistic regression which parameters were learned on the validation set.

We evaluated the calibration of the fine-tuned model with standard metrics, such as the Brier score, the Expected Calibration Error (ECE, average of the probability predicted by the model), and the Maximum Calibration Error (MCE, difference between the probability predicted by the model and the truth), and by analyzing the calibration curve. A perfectly calibrated model would have a Brier score of 0, and a model that always predicts 0.5 would have a Brier score of 0.25.

Results

Data description

For the pretraining of OncoBERT, we used 2.7 million de-identified medical reports from our institution (2000-2023), including hospitalization summaries, consultation notes, and pathology reports (see Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). For the training of ED prediction on the retrospective dataset, we used medical reports from 9039 unique patients (937 patients had >1 CT) that were included in 1234 unique CTs between January 2000 and 5 April 2023.

We analyzed the completeness of the reports in order to automatically select the best reports for ED prediction. We manually annotated a subset of 471 reports from 38 patients. A total of 72.8% of reports did not mention the type of cancer, and 91.3% did not mention the presence and/or type of molecular alterations (Figure 2). The types of reports with the most exhaustive descriptions were medical oncology consultation reports (57% with cancer types mentioned and 79% with complete or partial general status) and medical oncology first visit reports (with 100% of complete or partial information on cancer type, disease history, previous lines of treatment, and general state). Nevertheless, only 33% of patients had first visit reports. For each patient we automatically selected first visit reports or if absent, consultation reports.

Description of information available in all the reports of patients before inclusion in a clinical trial. (A) All types of reports (471 reports for 38 patients); (B) consultation reports (100 reports for 36 patients); (C) first visit reports (4 reports for 4 patients). The variables analyzed were the presence of the type of cancer, the disease history, the general patients’ status, the previous lines of treatment, and the molecular alterations.

We obtained 10 071 patient–trial pairs with at least one available report. On this dataset, 24.6% of each patient–trial pair met the criteria of ED. The patients were affected with various types of cancer, mainly breast (15.8%) and gastrointestinal (13.5%). The average age at consent was 56.5 (standard deviation [sd] 15.9). The average number of days between consent and EOT was 245 (sd 432). There were 4131 (45.7%) patients included in phase III, 3272 (36.2%) in phase II, and 1636 (18.1%) in phase I CTs. The average number of words in reports was 412 (sd 247). We carried out several anonymization steps obtaining a F1-score of 0.92 on proper nouns with DistilCamemBERT base13 (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). We randomly split the ED prediction dataset to obtain a training set of 7554 reports (75%), a validation set of 1510 reports (15%), and a test set of 1007 texts (10%).

Performance of the models

The pretraining of OncoBERT obtained a better F1 score than state of the art by 1% to 5% on NER and classification tasks on clinical oncology documents.

For the ED prediction task, the hyperparameter optimization obtained best results by removing stopwords, keeping numbers in the text, truncating the end of reports, freezing most of the layers of the pretrained OncoBERT model, pooling token embeddings to get document embeddings with the classification token and not applying dropout (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). We further identified that the best threshold for the classifier was 0.3 (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728).

The best ED prediction model selected on the validation dataset was trained for 10 epochs during 10 min on a Nvidia A40 GPU. This model achieved, on the retrospective test set, an accuracy of 0.75 (75% of the predictions are correct), a precision of 0.77 (77% of TC predictions are labeled TC), a recall of 0.95 (95% TC labeled reports are predicted TC), a negative predictive value of 0.53 (53% of ED predictions are labeled ED), and an AUROC of 0.69. The confusion matrix on the test set predictions (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728) shows that the model predicts TC 93% of the time: 95% of TC labeled reports are predicted TC, and 84% of ED labeled reports are predicted TC. Using our model on this retrospective dataset, we could have decreased the ED rate from 25.3% to 21.3%.

The phase I board dataset (Table 2) consisted of 476 patients seen between October 2024 and March 2025. Among those, 313 patients (66%) were discarded (because they were not included in a CT following the board, did not have reports preceding the inclusion or did not have narrative reports in digital format usable by the model), and we thus carried out the prospective evaluation on 163 patients (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). The selected 163 patients were representative of the initial population (Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). ED concerned 55 (33.7%) of these patients. The model achieved an accuracy of 0.64, a precision of 0.75, a recall of 0.75, a negative predictive value of 0.33, and an AUROC of 0.67. The confusion matrix on the phase I board predictions is presented in Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728. Sensitivity and specificity values for various thresholds are presented in Supplementary Table S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728. The human performance computed on this dataset was slightly better than the model, with an accuracy of 0.67 and an AUROC of 0.70 (the AUROC is prone to bias as it is challenging for experts to estimate a continuous score precisely).

We have estimated the calibration of the model on the test set, and obtained a Brier score of 0.20 (Supplementary Table S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728), indicating that our model is not very well calibrated; thus, the predicted probabilities are not entirely reliable. The ECE is 0.10, meaning that on average the probability predicted by the model is 0.1 above the actual probability. The MCE is 0.56, meaning that the probability predicted by the model is at most 0.56 above the actual probability. We observed that the calibration curve of the noncalibrated model is relatively well aligned with the theoretical curve of a perfectly calibrated model, especially in the low and high probabilities (Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). The calibration curve is consistently above the theoretical baseline, meaning that the model tends to be overconfident in its predictions.

The temperature scaling and Platt scaling methods have a limited impact on the model calibration: the Brier score is almost unaffected, the ECE increases, and the MCE decreases. We can observe that the curves for temperature and Platt scaling are straighter than the noncalibrated curve (lower MCE) but globally further from the theoretical baseline (higher ECE).

Model interpretation

To interpret the prediction, we used a cohort of 429 patients seen during the MTB between December 2024 and July 2025. Among those, 272 patients (63.5%) didn’t have narrative reports in numeric format usable by the model. Among the remaining 157 patients for which we generated the LIME, 80 were predicted ED (51%).

The words most commonly associated with ED on this cohort were hepatic (16.3%), breast (14.1%), progression (9.8%), pain (9.2%), asthenia (8.2%), hospitalization (6.5%), tiredness (5.4%), anorexia (5.4%), mastectomy (5.4%), carboplatine (3.8%), and dyspnea (3.8%). The words asthenia, tiredness, anorexia, and dyspnea reflected a deterioration of general condition, which is considered a relevant indicator of ED and coherent with the literature (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728). The model did not use the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score or the Karnofsky score, usually present in consultation reports. The importance of the word breast may be explained by patients affected by breast cancer in the training dataset being more frequently ED (38.5% versus 24.6% for the cohort); however, the model performance on breast cancer patients is similar to global performance (Supplementary Table S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728).

On the 163 patients of the phase I board dataset, we randomly selected 45 for the LIME interpretation analysis (Figure 3). We observed that 68% of the tokens classified as important were deemed irrelevant by the annotators for predicting ED. Thus, the model did not rely on the same elements as experts to make its predictions. In addition, only 24% of tokens deemed relevant by the annotators were important for the model. More precisely, among tokens deemed relevant in their semantic context (80% of relevant tokens), 18% were important for the model, whereas among tokens deemed relevant on their own (20%), 45% were important for the model. Thus, the model is better at detecting relevant tokens when they do not depend on their semantic context.

Discussion

We have pretrained and fine-tuned an LM to predict ED from oncology CTs using narrative medical reports of patients seen at our institution between 2000 and 2023. On the retrospective test set, the model achieved an accuracy of 0.75. Our choice of threshold influenced the model to predict TC disproportionately compared with the actual TC proportion (93% of reports predicted TC versus 75% of reports labeled TC). This choice favors a high recall (0.95), which illustrates a small proportion of TC labeled reports being predicted ED. However, the negative predictive value of 0.53 indicates that almost half of reports predicted ED were actually TC, which could seem concerning. This figure is mitigated by the low ratio of reports predicted ED: in reality, only 3.5% of the total number of patients are wrongly predicted ED (false negatives). So the risk of not including a patient in a CT when he could have been suitable is relatively limited. By contrast, 84% of ED were predicted as TC, meaning that using our model there would still be a high number of patients included in trials when they’re not suitable. Using our model on this dataset, we could have reduced the ED rate from 25.3% to 21.3%. This limited improvement can be explained by several factors, mainly the class imbalance, the varying quality of input data, and the difficulty of the task.

We hypothesize that good predictions depend on the quality of the information contained in the reports. We recommend using complete reports containing the disease history, previous treatments, and general state of the patient. This selection is difficult to automatize, as there is no general rule to detect the best report, and would suggest future studies with new approaches. Besides the quality of reports, one limitation of the model is the limit of the context length to 512 tokens, which compels us to truncate the reports. Using a model with a larger context length could potentially yield better performance. We also believe that adding data types to the prediction may improve the task of ED prediction. For example, blood test values are also commonly highlighted as ED factors, but we were not able to acquire sufficient data at the time of the study to use it in the analysis. Also we can hypothesize that using time dependent values (as deterioration of general condition in time, or a blood marker such as liver tests) can improve the prediction of ED. The present study uses a BERT architecture with limited input size that would require further improvements to tackle and evaluate this hypothesis.

As our model produces a significant number of errors, it is essential that it is only used under human supervision. Any prediction should be cautiously interpreted, taking into account the quality of the report that was used. Furthermore, the explanations provided by LIME should inform the clinician on the relevance of the model prediction. For example, the statistical proxies that the model relies on (like the word breast being used to predict ED) should be considered as alerts of a potential prediction error.

The explanations show that the model mostly relies on indications of deterioration of general condition to predict ED. This reflects the literature (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100728), where the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (representing fatigue) appears several times. Finally, the presence of liver metastases was highlighted as an ED factor in two articles,22,23 which supports the frequent association of ED with the word hepatic (that can encompass hepatic metastasis, complication, or abnormal liver enzyme values and/or liver failure). It should be noted that LIME don’t reflect the context-dependent workings of the model and should thus be interpreted with caution.

The integration of this tool in a clinical workflow faces other challenges. Firstly, in order to get an instantaneous prediction the inference needs to run on GPU (∼20 min on central processing unit), which means that the hospital must install and maintain a dedicated setup. Secondly, the study on the model calibration shows that some errors may occur. Lastly, ethical issues emerge, as using the model to reconsider the inclusion of a patient in a clinical trial could be seen as a denial of treatment opportunity, which motivates a usage as a human supervised decision support tool.

Future work on this project will include the evaluation on data from other hospitals to assess generalization ability of the model, as well as the integration of the model, paired with a trial matching tool, to a fully automatized workflow evaluated in the MTB.

Conclusion

We developed an AI tool that could have helped reduce the ED rate from 25.3% to 21.3% on a retrospective dataset. Within the broader challenge of providing more patients with personalized treatment options in Europe, integrating this kind of tool in clinical routine will be essential. Our results confirm that our method has potential, but several challenges remain before full integration.
