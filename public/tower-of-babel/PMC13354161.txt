Facilitators of, barriers to and strategies for health-system guidance implementation: a critical interpretive synthesis
Authors: Qi Wang, Junjie Ren, Xinyu Huang, Kehu Yang, Yaolong Chen, Michael G Wilson, Holger J Schünemann, John N Lavis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to develop a theoretical framework to support the implementation of health-system guidance, contributing to broader efforts to promote evidence-informed policymaking in health systems. A critical interpretive synthesis was conducted to identify relevant literature from 11 databases and gray literature sources. The documents were assessed for relevance and quality, and data were extracted and synthesized using an interpretive analytic method. Based on the synthesized data, the framework was developed and subsequently refined by the authors. A total of 21 documents published from 2004 to 2024 were included, with 15 (71%) empirical studies and six (29%) non-empirical. The resulting theoretical framework for supporting health-system guidance implementation identified 10 facilitators, 10 barriers, nine combined facilitators and barriers, and 11 strategies across six levels: political system, health system, community, organizational, providers and patients/public. This framework offers a structured approach to comprehensively understanding the various factors and levels involved in health-system guidance implementation. With further development into a practical tool, it may help inform implementation efforts across diverse topics and contexts.Subject terms: Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Health systems are defined as “the governance, financial and delivery arrangements for health care and public health services, implementation considerations for reforming or strengthening these arrangements, and broader economic, legal, political and social contexts in which these arrangements are negotiated and operate”1. Over the past decades, substantial improvements in global health have been achieved, including increased life expectancy and reduced neonatal mortality2. Persistent disparities in health outcomes, access, and quality of care underscore the need to further optimize health-system arrangements to achieve the healthcare quadruple aim—enhancing patient experience, improving population health, reducing per capita healthcare costs, and enhancing provider well-being3. Addressing these challenges requires strong stewardship from policymakers, supported by collaboration with health providers, researchers, and other relevant actors, to ensure the delivery of safe, effective, and cost-effective health interventions. Yet, weaknesses within existing arrangements continue to limit their reach and impacts4,5, underlining the need for robust health system strengthening.

Health-system guidance (HSG) serves as a crucial tool in this context, by providing systematically developed statements, generally produced at global levels, to offer options and supporting evidence that assists with decision-making in addressing health system challenges. It facilitates the implementation, monitoring, and evaluation of these options, primarily supporting policy development and reform at national or sub-national levels and within international organizations, thereby contributing to the strengthening of health systems6–8. For example, World Health Organization (WHO) produces HSG for use by its Member States and staff, as it has done on topics such as supporting the optimization of community health worker roles in 20189.

Bosch-Capblanch et al. highlight key conceptual, methodological, and practical challenges in HSG, arising from the complexity of health systems, the diversity of evidence, and the context-sensitive and multifactorial nature of policymaking process6–8,10,11. To address these challenges, WHO commissioned an expert panel to produce a handbook for HSG development10, and other scholars have developed approaches and tools to support quality in development, appraisal and contextualization12,13. The implementation context for HSG is inherently more complex and multi-level, yet most existing frameworks focus primarily on clinical practice guidelines14–20. Policymakers and other actors involved in the implementation of global HSG at national and sub-national levels therefore require a comprehensive and well-organized theoretical framework to support this process.

We aim to develop a theoretical framework that identifies facilitators of, barriers to and strategies for HSG implementation across various levels, guided by two compass questions: (1) What factors – at system, community, organizational and individual levels – facilitate or hinder HSG implementation processes and outcomes? (2) What strategies at these levels can leverage facilitators of and address barriers to HSG implementation processes and outcomes?

Results

Search results and article selection

Our search across nine electronic databases yielded 159,983 records, with 90,597 remaining after duplicates were removed. After screening titles and abstracts, 325 records were selected for full-text review. A random sample of 50 records (approximately 15%) was reviewed by four pairs of independent reviewers, with an average Kappa value of 0.77 (Range: 0.49–1.00), indicating substantial agreement. Following the full-text screening, 314 records were excluded, leaving 11 documents from the database search. An additional ten documents were included from supplementary sources (including gray literature, websites, citation screening, and research team identification), yielding a total of 21 documents for analysis (Fig. 1).

Nine studies focused on low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), two on high-income countries (HICs), one on both LMICs and HICs, and nine did not specify a region. Six levels of the framework

Our analysis resulted in a conceptual framework (Fig. 2) that identifies HSG implementation barriers, facilitators and strategies across six levels: political system, health system, community, organization, providers, and patients/public. For each level, the framework identifies relevant barriers and facilitators (or concerns/realities, capabilities/opportunities/motivations) and suggests actionable strategies. The detailed findings are presented in Tables 2–7, and interpretation and examples of HSG implementation at each level are discussed below.

Facilitators, barriers and strategies at the political-system level

At the political-system level (Table 2), political stability and a predictable governance environment7,13,22,26 are important for successful HSG implementation. Additional facilitators include sustained political commitment13,17,22,28,30,33–35, support and buy-in from key actors7,13,17,22,33,34, and robust evidence infrastructures to support HSG implementation7,13,28,33,34. These infrastructures include established evidence-support systems that institutionalize the use of research evidence and implementation feedback in policy and decision-making processes. In several settings, systematic reviews and other synthesized evidence have been used alongside routine monitoring data to guide adjustments to HSG implementation over time.

However, several political and institutional challenges may hinder implementation. Political instability22,29,31, staff turnover among supportive policymakers7,13,28,31,33, and pressure from competitive interest groups7,13,28 may disrupt policy continuity and weaken support for HSG implementation. For example, leadership transitions or shifting policy priorities following elections may delay or deprioritize ongoing initiatives. Addressing these barriers requires strategic political engagement, including securing policymaker buy-in and advocating for supportive policy changes10,13,22,23,33, and developing HSG-informed policy briefs to support evidence-informed decision-making and facilitate uptake7,17,31,33.

Facilitators, barriers and strategies at the health-system level

At the health-system level (Table 3), strong networks and multisectoral collaboration10,22,26,28,33 facilitate HSG implementation by enabling coordinated planning, clearly defined roles, and shared accountability across sectors, while adequate resources—including sustained financing, workforce capacity, infrastructure, and functional monitoring systems—support implementation feasibility and continuity7,10,13,16,22,23,28,30,31,33,34,36. For example, the establishment of cross-sector partnerships has been used to coordinate implementation activities and strengthen system-wide collaboration, and integrated monitoring systems can support performance tracking and continuous improvement. In contrast, financial constraints and resource limitations7,13,22,24,26–30,32,33, together with the need for substantial adjustments to existing health-system structures and processes7, may disrupt service delivery and hinder implementation. Strategies such as establishing robust HSG implementation networks10,22,31, ensuring sufficient and well-planned resource allocation10,22,31, and engaging diverse health-system actors throughout implementation7,13,17,22,23,26,31,33–36 have been reported to strengthen coordination, enhance implementation capacity, and improve the acceptability and sustainability of HSG implementation.

Concerns, realities and strategies at the community level

When considering the community level, three key concerns and realities may function as either facilitators or barriers to HSG implementation (Table 4): (1) prevailing social norms and values within local communities that influence acceptance of HSG recommendations and related services7,17,22,33; (2) community-level facilities and infrastructure, referring to the availability, accessibility, and functioning of local healthcare facilities, service providers, supply mechanisms, and referral or information systems required to support implementation7,13,23; (3) the nature and extent of community engagement in mobilizing support and resources for HSG implementation13,22,23,28,29,32,33. Depending on context, these factors may support implementation when aligned with local needs, or constrain implementation when insufficient or poorly coordinated. One approach to addressing these contextual dynamics is the use of phased implementation with periodic review to inform subsequent scale-up17,22,26,35,36.

Facilitators, barriers and strategies at the organizational level

At the organizational level (Table 5), physically and intellectually supportive environments7,10,22,24,29 and alignment of HSG recommendations with organizational culture and priorities10,22,27,28,31 facilitate implementation by enabling the integration of recommendations into routine workflows and institutional decision-making. However, limited organizational capacities—such as weak leadership engagement, resistant cultures, and insufficient resources7,24,27,29,32,33—together with limited inter-organizational collaboration across facilities or service levels24,33, can constrain coordinated and sustained implementation. For example, in some settings, existing staffing structures and workflows cannot accommodate additional activities required for new guidance, while weak coordination between service levels may lead to inconsistent adoption across facilities. To address these challenges, strategies such as providing structured on-site supervision and clear operational guidance22,29,33, and linking HSG implementation to ongoing organizational improvement programs with defined indicators and monitoring processes10,22,29,33, can help support and sustain implementation process.

Capabilities, opportunities, motivations and strategies at the provider level

At the provider level (Table 6), implementation of HSG is influenced by providers’ capabilities, opportunities, and motivations as conceptualized in the COM-B model. Capability may be constrained by training challenges10,22,27,30,33, limited HSG knowledge10,22,27,30,33, and persistence of established clinical routines22,29, while opportunity is affected by workload pressures22 and by HSG features perceived as unclear or weakly supported by evidence16,26,34, although support from opinion leaders can reinforce professional norms and confidence in HSG29. Motivation is shaped by providers’ perceptions and experiences of HSG implementation, including perceived benefits, recognition or incentives7,29,31,33,34 as well as their positive or negative feelings towards the relevance of recommendations in practice22,33. Corresponding strategies focus on strengthening capability and motivation through structured training to enhance knowledge, attitudes, and skills10,13,16,22,23,29–31,33,36, and on creating supportive opportunities through audit and feedback mechanisms that enable performance monitoring and quality improvement16,22,23.

Capabilities, opportunities, motivations and strategies at the patient/public level

At the patient and public level (Table 7), behavioral conditions affecting engagement with HSG relate to capability, opportunity, and motivation. Patients’ capability largely depends on their understanding of HSG recommendations and their perceived benefits and risks16,21,29,30,33. Opportunity may be constrained by socioeconomic disadvantages such as financial and time limitations7,33, while support from family members can facilitate adherence to recommended care21,33. Motivation is shaped by the perceived value of recommended interventions21,33 and by incentives that reinforce adherence7,29. To address these factors, providing structured education and supportive activities16,21,33 is identified as a key strategy to improve understanding of HSG and support informed adherence.

Discussion

The theoretical framework for supporting HSG implementation identified 10 facilitators, 10 barriers, nine combined facilitators and barriers, and 11 strategies across six levels, with 28 general (versus 12 specific). This framework offers a structured approach to comprehensively understanding the various factors and levels involved in HSG implementation. By examining the political system, health system, and organizational levels, as well as the community, provider, and patient perspectives, the framework provides a holistic view of the HSG implementation.

Our framework, developed specifically for HSG implementation, differs from existing frameworks and tools for clinical practice guidelines14–20, although some common considerations such as resource availability and engagement of key actors are shared6,7,8,10,12,13,22. Unlike clinical practice guideline implementation, which typically operates within established health system arrangements including governance, financial and service delivery, HSG implementation often involves changes to these arrangements to support policy decisions about health systems. Such changes may range from incremental adjustments to broader system reforms and generally require longer timeframes, negotiation across multiple actors, and substantial system-wide resources. These processes may also be influenced by political considerations, particularly for high-visibility decisions or those affecting powerful groups. In this context, the framework reflects the multi-level and context-sensitive nature of HSG implementation.

In the HSG field, several tools have been developed to support different phases, ranging from development and appraisal to reporting and contextualization6–8,10,12,13. Our HSG implementation framework complements these tools by synthesizing key implementation-related elements across them. While the contextualization workbook13 includes a step for identifying implementation barriers and strategies, our framework provides a more comprehensive categorization of facilitators, barriers, and strategies across six levels, from macro-level political and health system contexts to micro-level individual actors. It also reflects the complexity of HSG implementation, in which micro-level decisions are embedded within broader macro-level contexts and shaped by influences operating across multiple levels rather than a single level.

Furthermore, our framework introduces a perspective on the roles of providers, patients, and the public in HSG implementation. These groups are viewed not merely as passive recipients of health-system changes resulting from HSG implementation but as integral contributors who can share insights and values to strengthen health systems7,10,17. Consistent with other implementation studies37,38, we provide examples of potentially effective strategies by aligning the facilitators and barriers categorized within the COM-B model39 with the most relevant TDF domains40. This approach broadens the range of factors and strategies that can be considered and enriches understanding of HSG implementation from an individual-level perspective.

Our study has several strengths. First, the CIS approach enabled rigorous development of a theoretical framework from a diverse and complex body of literature. Second, independent reviewers, strong inter-rater agreement and team discussions supported reliable article selection and interpretation. Third, our synthesis identified facilitators, barriers, and strategies across multiple levels and provided a structured framework specifically designed to support understanding of HSG implementation. The framework also offers a basis for developing a practical tool to further support implementation efforts. However, not all identified facilitators, barriers, and strategies were specific to the complexity and context sensitivity of HSG, although 79% of included documents were directly relevant. We mitigated this by incorporating input from our interdisciplinary research team. In addition, implementation strategies were often poorly reported, with limited detail on their key components and outcomes. We addressed this by summarizing them using key dimensions (actors, actions, and targets) to improve clarity.

This framework provides a structured synthesis of factors and strategies influencing HSG implementation across multiple levels and may serve as an analytical resource for those involved in planning or supporting implementation. By highlighting context-specific influences and potential strategies, it may help implementation teams and decision-makers consider relevant actions within their own settings. It may also inform guideline developers during HSG development by encouraging explicit consideration of implementation influences when formulating recommendations. While not all strategies can be adopted immediately, the framework may therefore serve as a reference to consider implementation influences and potential strategies across contexts. Building on this work, we plan to develop a practical tool to facilitate application of the framework in real-world settings. Future research could apply and refine the framework across different HSG topics and contexts to examine how implementation influences and strategies vary in practice.

Methods

We used a critical interpretive synthesis (CIS) approach to develop a theoretical framework, which involves an inductive, interpretive, and iterative process to develop concepts and theories from diverse and complex literature, focusing on conceptual insights rather than data aggregation41–43. Since its introduction by Dixon-Woods et al. for healthcare access for vulnerable populations42, CIS has been widely used to generate or refine theories and frameworks12,44,45, including Ako-Arrey et al.’s identification of 30 key concepts related to HSG appraisal12. CIS is particularly appropriate for our study because it synthesizes diverse, complex literature, which suits the heterogeneous nature of HSG research10,12,13. Following our published protocol46, the CIS was conducted and reported using the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [Supplementary Table 3]47. The study was registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42020214072).

Identifying relevant articles

We conducted a phased literature search guided by the two compass questions, using a strategy developed with librarians. Search terms included “health system,” “guidance,” and “implementation” and their synonyms (Supplementary Table 4) were applied across 11 databases (e.g., CNKI, PubMed, Web of Science) and relevant grey literature sources (Supplementary Table 5). Additional papers were identified through reference screening and targeted identification by senior members of the research team to address conceptual gaps45. To ensure coverage of recent and conceptually relevant literature, an updated targeted search was conducted by the first author, screening 100 results each from Google Scholar and Google using combinations of the terms “implementation,” “guideline,” and “health system” up to December 2024.

Selecting eligible articles

All reviewers participated in online training to ensure consensus on inclusion criteria. A total of 24 reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts using Covidence, with each record assessed by two reviewers and disagreements resolved by the lead author (QW). Full-text screening was conducted by QW and three graduate students, with each record independently assessed by two reviewers using Kappa statistics48 to assess agreement and refine eligibility criteria. We included both empirical and non-empirical studies addressing implementation facilitators, barriers, and strategies for HSG recommendations, specifically related to health-system arrangements49 at any level. Studies on clinical practice guidelines or health interventions were excluded. There were no restrictions on time frame, context, study design, or language. An asterisk (*) was used to denote findings applicable across health system, public health, and clinical practice guidelines rather than those specific to HSG.

Assessing relevant articles

Paired reviewers independently assessed relevant articles, resolving disagreements through discussion. While CIS does not typically include formal quality assessments, we applied one relevance criterion and five quality criteria. Relevance was defined as a paper’s ability to contribute concepts or insights addressing the compass questions42. For quality, we excluded fatally flawed empirical papers based on modified NHS National Electronic Library for Health criteria, focusing on research aims, design, data support, and analysis appropriateness42.

Extracting data and information

The lead author (QW) extracted data, with a randomly selected sample checked by other reviewers for consistency. A standardized form (Supplementary Table 6) was used to extract details such as title, author, publication year, study design, country focus, and implementation object, along with key findings. Facilitators, barriers, and strategies were identified when explicitly mentioned in the text. Facilitators are factors that drive motivation or prompt action to implement HSG, such as enablers, motivators, or opportunities, while barriers are factors that obstruct implementation, including challenges, obstacles, or limitations21. Some factors may function as both, depending on the context. Implementation strategies are methods to enhance adoption and sustainability, including approaches, actions or tools50, and are defined as actions directly controlled by HSG implementers when overlapping with facilitators7. These elements were categorized based on four frameworks39,40,49,51 and labeled in theme tables with symbols: facilitators (+), barriers (-), neutral factors (±), and strategies (✓).

At the system level, we applied the 3I + E framework (institutions, interests, ideas, and external factors) to examine political system influences on HSG implementation (Supplementary Table 7)7,11,51, and used the HSE framework to organize governance, financial, and delivery arrangements within health systems (Supplementary Table 8)49. At the community level, defined as local healthcare delivery contexts, the HSE framework49 was used to extract key concerns, contextual realities, and potential strategies relevant to implementation across community-based settings (e.g., retirement homes, and hospices)52. For the organizational level, the HSE framework49 was also applied to identify implementation facilitators, barriers and strategies within health-related organizations including hospitals, clinics and international bodies such as WHO.

At the individual level, identified facilitators and barriers were first mapped to the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) (Supplementary Table 9)39 and Behavior Change Wheel (BCW) (Supplementary Fig. 1)40 and then organized using the COM-B model40. This approach enabled examination of capability-, opportunity-, and motivation-related determinants of behavior among healthcare providers and intended recipients of HSG (patients/public), and informed corresponding implementation strategies. In this study, capability refers to the knowledge and skills required to understand and apply HSG; opportunity refers to external conditions such as workload and social support that enable or constrain implementation; and motivation refers to attitudes, beliefs, and perceived or experienced outcomes that influence willingness to adopt HSG-informed practices.

Synthesizing and integrating findings

We used an interpretive analytic approach with a constant comparative method53 to synthesize findings from the included documents. A 3 × 6 matrix (facilitators, barriers, strategies across six levels: patients/public, providers, organizational, community, health-system and political-system) was used to map and categorize the literature, identify gaps, and guide further sampling54. Iterative discussions within the research team supported refinement of the analytic process. Findings were organized using established frameworks and the analysis followed these steps: (1) identifying common themes, (2) developing theoretical constructs, (3) refining constructs to address gaps, (4) conducting additional sampling until theoretical saturation was reached, and (5) integrating constructs into a theoretical framework for HSG implementation54. The lead author developed the framework, which was then reviewed and revised by the team.
