Rethinking LoRA for Data Heterogeneous Federated Learning: Subspace and State Alignment
Hongyi Peng, Han Yu, Xiaoxiao Li, Qiang Yang

Abstract
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is widely used for federated fine-tuning. Yet under non-IID settings, it can substantially underperform full-parameter fine-tuning. Through with-high-probability robustness analysis, we uncover that this gap can be attributed to two coupled mismatches: (i) update-space mismatch , where clients optimize in a low-rank subspace but aggregation occurs in the full space; and (ii) optimizer-state mismatch , where unsynchronized adaptive states amplify drift across rounds. We propose FedGaLore , which combines client-side GaLore-style gradient-subspace optimization with server-side drift-robust synchronization of projected second-moment states via spectral shared-signal extraction, to address this challenge. Across NLU, vision, and NLG benchmarks, FedGaLore improves robustness and accuracy over state-of-the-art federated LoRA baselines in non-IID settings.

Prior work mitigates this by further restricting the update space (e.g., FFA-LoRA shares a factor across clients), which can improve robustness under severe data heterogeneity, with reduced IID performance
(
52

. We also observe a local–global mismatch with adaptive optimizers (e.g., Adam/AdamW): local training loss decreases while global validation performance improves little or even degrades. These observations point to two coupled mechanisms: (i) misalignment of client updates in the parameter space, and (ii) drift of adaptive optimizer states across clients and rounds.

To formalize this and complement standard expectation-based analysis, we develop a with-high-probability (W.H.P.) robustness framework that controls the probability of rare but catastrophic deviations under heterogeneity. We view federated fine-tuning as a perturbed implementation of a centralized optimizer, and decompose each round into client training (
\mathcal{T}
), server aggregation (
\mathcal{A}
) and state synchronization (
\mathcal{S}
). This lens isolates two failure modes:

(1) Update-space mismatch:
LoRA constrains each client update to a low-rank subspace, while
\mathcal{A}
aggregates in the full parameter space.

(2) Optimizer-state mismatch:
inconsistencies in adaptive states (arising from imperfect
\mathcal{S}
and amplified by
\mathcal{T}
) propagate through local dynamics and amplify client drift.
Together, these effects explain the brittleness of federated LoRA under data heterogeneity.

To address these limitations, we propose
FedGaLore
. It couples
gradient-subspace client optimization
with
drift-robust state synchronization
. On the client side, we replace LoRA’s fixed parameter-subspace updates with GaLore-style gradient-subspace optimization: each client computes gradients on the adapted modules and updates in an adaptively estimated rank-
r
subspace, preserving LoRA-like parameter efficiency while improving aggregation robustness. Clients use a GaLore-based AdamW variant to maintain local adaptivity.
On the server side, we mitigate optimizer-state mismatch with minimal communication by synchronizing only the
projected second-moment state
that defines the preconditioner. Specifically, clients upload low-rank projected second moments, and the server applies an AJIVE filter
(
14
)
to extract the shared component across clients and broadcasts it as the next-round initialization, stabilizing training under non-IID data.
Extensive experimental evaluation has been conducted on the NLU, vision and NLG benchmarks. The results show that
FedGaLore
significantly improves robustness to data heterogeneity and stability over state-of-the-art federated LoRA baselines.

4
A W.H.P. Lens for Federated Learning

Why W.H.P. robustness?

Expectation-based analyses describe
average
behavior but can obscure instability under severe heterogeneity. In federated fine-tuning, client drift and mini-batch noise can interact to produce rare but catastrophic trajectories that leave a stable basin and derail aggregation. With-high-probability (W.H.P.) guarantees address this by controlling the entire optimization path with confidence
1-\delta
over the stochasticity. For instance, if a method converges for
90\%
of realizations but diverges for the remaining
10\%
, an in-expectation guarantee may still look favorable, whereas a W.H.P. statement cannot hold at confidence levels above
0.9
.

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{traj}}
reflects drift and optimizer-state mismatch, while

captures aggregation instability.

4.1
Update-space Mismatch

We analyze aggregation stability, which governs
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{agg}}(K)
: even if all clients remain in a stable region, aggregation might push the global iterate outside it. Given round
k
and assume
\theta^{i,k}_{T}\in Q
for all participating clients, the question is whether the server update
\bar{\theta}^{k+1}=\mathcal{A}(\{\theta^{i,k}_{T}\})
still lies in
Q
. Under convexity this is immediate: if
Q
is convex and
\mathcal{A}
is a convex combination, aggregation cannot leave
Q
.

Lemma 4.1

(Aggregation preserves convex regions)
.

Let
Q
be convex. If
\theta_{i}\in Q
for all
i\in[M]
and
\bar{\theta}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\tilde{p}_{i}\theta_{i}
with
\tilde{p}_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}\tilde{p}_{i}=1
, then
\bar{\theta}\in Q
.

Lemma
4.1
explains why aggregation is rarely a bottleneck in convex analyses, but this closure property typically fails for deep networks where stable regions (e.g., PL basins) are local and non-convex
(
32
)
. Empirically, mode-connectivity results show that independently trained solutions can often be connected by low-loss paths, sometimes making linear interpolation near-optimal
(
16
;
13
)
. This motivates weight averaging and related model-merging methods
(
2
;
65
)
, and is supported by recent theory under additional conditions
(
15
;
28
)
. Together, these observations help explain why full-parameter FedAvg is often stable: weighted averages of nearby solutions frequently remain within (or close to) a well-behaved region.

Federated LoRA differs from FFT.

For a linear module, LoRA parameterizes
\bm{W}=\bm{W}^{(0)}+\bm{B}\bm{A}
with rank
r\ll\min\{d_{\mathrm{out}},d_{\mathrm{in}}\}
, so each client update
\Delta\bm{W}=\bm{B}\bm{A}
lies in a nonconvex rank-
\leq r
set. Early federated LoRA methods aggregate
factors
: FedIT
(
70
)
uses

\Delta\bar{\bm{W}}^{k}=(\sum_{i}\tilde{p}_{i}\,\bm{B}^{i,k}_{T})(\sum_{i}\tilde{p}_{i}\,\bm{A}^{i,k}_{T})
,
and FFA-LoRA
(
52
)
fixes one factor so

\Delta\bar{\bm{W}}^{k}=(\sum_{i}\tilde{p}_{i}\,\bm{B}^{i,k}_{T})\bm{A}^{(0)}
.
These enforce a rank-
\leq r
aggregate (
low-rank-space aggregation
). In contrast,
full-space aggregation
lifts each client adapter to a weight delta, and averages in the ambient space
(
64
;
60
)
:

\Delta\bar{\bm{W}}^{k}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}_{k}}\tilde{p}_{i}\,(\bm{B}^{i,k}_{T}\bm{A}^{i,k}_{T}),

so
\mathrm{rank}(\Delta\bar{\bm{W}}^{k})
can grow up to
|\mathcal{P}_{k}|r
even when each client update is rank-
\leq r
, injecting out-of-subspace components that can destabilize aggregation.

To formalize the geometric tension, consider the rank-
r
manifold (away from rank-deficient points)

\mathcal{M}_{r}\coloneqq\{\Delta\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}:\ \mathrm{rank}(\Delta\bm{W})=r\},

with ambient dimension
D_{\mathrm{amb}}=d_{\mathrm{out}}d_{\mathrm{in}}
, manifold dimension
d_{\mathcal{M}}=r(d_{\mathrm{out}}+d_{\mathrm{in}}-r)
, and codimension
\mathrm{codim}(\mathcal{M}_{r})=(d_{\mathrm{out}}-r)(d_{\mathrm{in}}-r)
.
To make volumes finite, we restrict to a bounded Frobenius ball
\mathbb{B}_{F}(\rho)
and define the tube

\mathcal{M}_{r}^{(R)}\coloneqq\{\Delta\bm{W}\in\mathbb{B}_{F}(\rho):\ \mathrm{dist}_{F}(\Delta\bm{W},\mathcal{M}_{r})\leq R\}.

Theorem 4.2

(Weyl’s tube formula (informal)
(
18
)
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a compact
d_{\mathcal{M}}
-dimensional
C^{2}
submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{D_{\mathrm{amb}}}
. For sufficiently small
R
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{M}^{(R)})=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{M})\,\mathrm{Vol}\!\big(B^{D_{\mathrm{amb}}-d_{\mathcal{M}}}(R)\big)

(9)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle O\!\big(R^{D_{\mathrm{amb}}-d_{\mathcal{M}}+1}\big),

so
\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{M}^{(R)})
scales as
R^{D_{\mathrm{amb}}-d_{\mathcal{M}}}
to leading order.

Theorem
4.2
highlights an unforgiving geometry: when
\mathrm{codim}(\mathcal{M}_{r})
is large, an
R
-tube around rank-
r
updates occupies a vanishingly small fraction of the ambient space, so small off-manifold components can push
\Delta\bar{\bm{W}}^{k}
outside a “stable tube”
Q
.
A complementary view follows from Eckart–Young. Let
\mathcal{M}_{\leq r}\coloneqq\{\Delta\bm{W}:\mathrm{rank}(\Delta\bm{W})\leq r\}
:

\mathrm{dist}_{F}(\Delta\bar{\bm{W}}^{k},\mathcal{M}_{\leq r})=\Big(\sum_{j>r}\sigma_{j}(\Delta\bar{\bm{W}}^{k})^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

(10)

where
\{\sigma_{j}(\cdot)\}
are singular values in nonincreasing order; thus misaligned client updates induce nontrivial tail singular values beyond rank
r
.
We refer to this failure mode as
update-space mismatch
, which helps explain why more restrictive schemes (e.g., fixing one adaptation factor in FFA-LoRA
(
52
)
) can trade expressivity for stability as non-IID severity increases.

4.2
Optimizer-state Mismatch

While update-space mismatch governs aggregation stability (event
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{agg}}
), local containment (event
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{traj}}
) is governed by the stochastic dynamics of client-side optimization.
Adaptive optimizers (e.g., Adam/AdamW
(
25
;
22
)
) are the de facto standard for fine-tuning large models; LoRA typically uses AdamW. In FL, clients optimize different non-IID objectives, so their adaptive states (e.g., first/second moments) evolve inconsistently across clients and rounds. We term this phenomenon
optimizer-state mismatch
. Such mismatch can bias local update directions and amplify drift/noise, increasing the probability that a client trajectory exits the stable region (event
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{traj}}^{c}
). Since most federated LoRA methods do not explicitly synchronize adaptive states, characterizing state mismatch propagation through local dynamics is critical.

We compare each client to a centralized reference optimizer, which induces a canonical state
(m^{\star,k}_{0},v^{\star,k}_{0})
at the start of round
k
. Practical protocols approximate these states (e.g., pseudo-gradients from deltas
(
47
;
12
)
or state aggregation
(
51
;
33
)
); we model the resulting discrepancy as a bounded initialization error.

Definition 4.3

(Biased state initialization)
.

At the start of round
k
, client
i
initializes
(m^{i,k}_{0},v^{i,k}_{0})
while the reference uses
(m^{\star,k}_{0},v^{\star,k}_{0})
.
Assume
\|m^{i,k}_{0}-m^{\star,k}_{0}\|\leq B_{m}
and
\|v^{i,k}_{0}-v^{\star,k}_{0}\|\leq B_{v}
for all
i,k
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes
\ell_{2}
for vectors and Frobenius norm for matrices (set
B_{v}=0
for optimizers without
v
).

Let
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)
denote the W.H.P. bound from Lemma
B.2
such that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\max_{k\leq K-1,\;t\leq T,\;i\in[M]}\|\xi_{t}^{i,k}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta).

Theorem 4.4

(W.H.P. local-containment radius)
.

Assume (i) Assumptions
3.5
and
3.8
, (ii) the noise envelope above, and (iii) Definition
4.3
. Let
\{\theta_{t}^{\star,k}\}_{t=0}^{T}
be the within-round reference trajectory started from
\bar{\theta}^{k}
under the corresponding centralized update rule. Suppose
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2LT}
and standard hyperparameters are used (Appendix
A
).

Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over mini-batch sampling, simultaneously for all rounds
k\leq K-1
, all clients
i\in[M]
, and all local steps

,

\|\theta^{i,k}_{t}-\theta^{\star,k}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\delta),

where, up to absolute constants,

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\delta)\;\lesssim\;\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{drift}}(\delta)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{state}},

with

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{drift}}(\delta)=\begin{cases}\eta T\big(G+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)\big),&\mathrm{SGD/Momentum},\\[2.0pt]
\frac{\eta T}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\big(G+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)\big),&\mathrm{AdamW},\end{cases}

and

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{state}}=\begin{cases}0,&\mathrm{SGD},\\[2.0pt]
\eta\,\dfrac{B_{m}}{1-\beta_{1}},&\mathrm{Momentum},\\[6.0pt]
\eta\!\left(\dfrac{B_{m}}{(1-\beta_{1})\sqrt{\epsilon}}+\dfrac{G\,B_{v}}{(1-\beta_{2})\epsilon^{3/2}}\right),&\mathrm{AdamW}.\end{cases}

Here AdamW uses the elementwise preconditioner
(v+\epsilon)^{-1/2}
with
\epsilon>0
.

We defer the proof to Appendix
B.2
. Theorem
4.4
shows that optimizer-state mismatch directly
inflates
the local containment radius, increasing the chance of local escape and imposing a stricter geometric requirement on aggregation stability. Notably, the second-moment mismatch term is amplified by the preconditioner: small perturbations in
v
translate into larger changes in
(v+\epsilon)^{-1/2}
, which motivates prioritizing second-moment synchronization, consistent with
(
33
;
51
)
. Moreover, under bounded heterogeneity (Assumption
3.7
), worst-case drift control can be replaced by an average/RMS drift bound, yielding improved
in-expectation
convergence guarantees (Corollary
B.3
in Appendix).

Our analysis identifies two coupled failure modes in federated PEFT under heterogeneity:
(1)
Update-space mismatch:
FFT is often aggregation-robust but expensive, whereas LoRA constrains updates to a fixed low-rank manifold, making aggregation brittle under non-IID.
(2)
Optimizer-state mismatch:
adaptive optimizers speed up local training, but unsynchronized second-moment states amplify drift via preconditioning, degrading local containment and aggregation stability.

FedGaLore

Results and discussion.

We report IID and non-IID (
\alpha=0.5
) performance and use their gap as a simple sensitivity metric: smaller
\Delta
indicates lower sensitivity to non-IIDness.

NLU (GLUE) and vision (DomainNet).

Across GLUE (Table
3
) and DomainNet (Table
4
), federated LoRA baselines generally show much larger robustness gaps than FedAvg-Full, whose minimal degradation suggests that full-space optimization/aggregation can remain stable under these shifts. Several LoRA variants incur large drops (e.g., CoLA and multiple DomainNet domains), consistent with
update-space mismatch
: clients adapt in restricted low-rank directions that become misaligned across clients, and aggregation amplifies off-subspace components. Methods that constrain the update space more aggressively (e.g., FFA-LoRA, LoRA-Fair) reduce
\Delta
in some settings but at the cost of lower IID accuracy, indicating reduced expressivity.
FedGaLore
improves this trade-off, matching or improving IID performance while maintaining consistently small
\Delta
across tasks/domains. The ablation
FedGaLore
-
boosts IID accuracy but collapses under data heterogeneity (notably on DomainNet), showing that dynamic subspaces alone are insufficient—
drift-robust state synchronization
is essential.

NLG (LLM fine-tuning).

Robustness gaps are smaller on GSM8K and MATH (Table
5
), suggesting the pretrained Llama-2 backbone provides stronger invariances. Local-adaptive methods (FLoRA/FR-LoRA/
FedGaLore
-
) also achieve higher IID performance than static LoRA baselines, indicating that reasoning tasks benefit from greater optimization plasticity. However, local adaptivity increases sensitivity when optimizer states drift across clients.
FedGaLore
retains strong IID performance while reducing the non-IID drop, consistent with combining gradient-subspace exploration with drift-robust second-moment synchronization.

Practical takeaway.

FedAvg-Full is the most robust but costly. Federated LoRA is efficient, but can be brittle.
FedGaLore
bridges this gap, delivering near-FFT robustness with PEFT-level efficiency, especially when local adaptivity is needed.

7
Conclusions and Future Work

We present
FedGaLore
, a robust protocol for federated parameter-efficient fine-tuning under non-IID data. Our W.H.P. analysis attributes federated LoRA brittleness to two coupled mismatches—update-space and optimizer-state—and motivates combining GaLore-style gradient-subspace optimization with drift-robust synchronization of projected second-moment statistics, delivering consistent gains across NLU, vision, and LLM benchmarks. Beyond improved accuracy,
FedGaLore
yields a practical stability–efficiency trade-off: near full fine-tuning robustness without transmitting dense optimizer states, enabled by low-rank projected synchronization and lightweight server-side filtering. Future work includes extending the analysis to broader participation regimes and develop a tighter end-to-end W.H.P. guarantees.

A.1
Details of GaLore-AdamW

This section summarizes the GaLoreAdamW update used by
FedGaLore
and clarifies the tensor shapes under the
default
setting (
proj_type=std
, one-sided projection) for an adapted weight block
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
(square case
m{=}n
is a special case).

Default projection rule and shapes.

GaLore maintains a rank-
r
projector that is refreshed every
update_proj_gap
steps (denoted
\tau
in the paper). Under
proj_type=std
, the projector chooses the side based on the aspect ratio:

•

If
m\geq n
(including square blocks), use a
right
basis
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
and project

\tilde{\bm{g}}=\bm{g}\bm{P}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
.

•

If
m<n
, use a
left
basis
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
and project

\tilde{\bm{g}}=\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
.

For our exposition with square attention/MLP blocks
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the default is the
right
case, hence
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
and
\tilde{\bm{g}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
.

Projected AdamW state (no lifting).

GaLoreAdamW stores the AdamW buffers in the
same shape as the projected gradient
:

\tilde{\bm{m}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r},\quad\tilde{\bm{v}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}\qquad(\text{right projection, }m\geq n),

(and analogously
\tilde{\bm{m}}_{t},\tilde{\bm{v}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
for left projection).
Here
\tilde{\bm{v}}_{t}
denotes the
elementwise
second-moment buffer reshaped to the projected tensor shape. Importantly, the buffers are updated in this projected space without forming any dense
m\times n
second-moment matrix.

GaLoreAdamW update (right projection case).

Let
\bm{g}_{t}=\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell(\theta;\mathcal{B}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be the dense gradient for the block and
\tilde{\bm{g}}_{t}=\bm{g}_{t}\bm{P}_{t}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
its projected version. GaLoreAdamW performs standard AdamW updates in the projected shape:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{m}}_{t}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\beta_{1}\tilde{\bm{m}}_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{1})\tilde{\bm{g}}_{t},

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{v}}_{t}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\beta_{2}\tilde{\bm{v}}_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{2})(\tilde{\bm{g}}_{t}\odot\tilde{\bm{g}}_{t}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{d}}_{t}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\sqrt{\tilde{\bm{v}}_{t}}+\epsilon,\qquad\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}\leftarrow\tilde{\bm{m}}_{t}\oslash\tilde{\bm{d}}_{t},

optionally with bias correction (controlled by
correct_bias
). The projected, preconditioned update
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
is then
projected back
to the ambient shape:

\bm{u}_{t}\;=\;\tilde{\bm{u}}_{t}\,\bm{P}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},

and the parameter update is applied in the ambient space:

\bm{W}\leftarrow\bm{W}-\eta_{t}\,\bm{u}_{t}\;-\;\eta_{t}\,\lambda\,\bm{W},

where
\eta_{t}
includes the AdamW step size (with bias correction when enabled) and
\lambda
is the decoupled weight decay.

Projector refresh and buffer reprojection (stay low-rank).

When the projector is refreshed (every
\tau
steps), GaLoreAdamW reprojects the stored buffers to remain consistent with the new basis, without lifting to
m\times n
in the common right
\rightarrow
right case.
For right projection, let
\bm{P}_{\text{old}},\bm{P}_{\text{new}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
. Define their corresponding column-space bases
\bm{V}_{\text{old}}=\bm{P}_{\text{old}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and
\bm{V}_{\text{new}}=\bm{P}_{\text{new}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
. GaLoreAdamW applies the low-rank change-of-basis transform

\tilde{\bm{m}}\leftarrow\tilde{\bm{m}}\,(\bm{V}_{\text{old}}^{\top}\bm{V}_{\text{new}}),\qquad\tilde{\bm{v}}\leftarrow\tilde{\bm{v}}\,(\bm{V}_{\text{old}}^{\top}\bm{V}_{\text{new}}),

which maps the
m\times r
buffers to the new projected coordinates. For square blocks under
proj_type=std
, the side remains “right” across refreshes, so this low-rank reprojection avoids materializing dense
m\times n
tensors. (A rare fallback path lifts and reprojects only if the projection
side
changes.)

Seeded random-adaptive projectors.

Under
proj_type=random-adaptive
or
rsvd-adaptive
, the projector uses data-driven bases for the first
S
refreshes (
adaptive_proj_steps
), then switches to a seeded random orthonormal basis. In the random phase, the basis is fully determined by an integer seed; thus synchronizing the projector across clients requires broadcasting only the seed (not the basis matrix), while the projected buffers
\tilde{\bm{m}},\tilde{\bm{v}}
remain in the same
m\times r
(or
r\times n
) shape and are reprojected as above when the seed increments.

Summary of shapes (square block, default).

For
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and default
proj_type=std
(right projection):

\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n},\quad\tilde{\bm{g}},\tilde{\bm{m}},\tilde{\bm{v}},\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},\quad\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

Thus, GaLoreAdamW maintains adaptive states in
\mathcal{O}(nr)
memory per adapted block and applies dense parameter updates via
project_back
without storing dense second-moment matrices.

B.1
Conditional sub-Gaussian noise and a W.H.P. envelope

Definition B.1

(Conditional sub-Gaussian vector)
.

A random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
conditionally
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian given
\mathcal{F}
if

\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{F}]=0
and for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and all unit vectors
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\big(\lambda\langle u,X\rangle\big)\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}\right]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\right).

Lemma B.2

(W.H.P. noise envelope)
.

Assume the mini-batch noise
\xi_{t}^{i,k}
is conditionally mean-zero and
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian given
\mathcal{F}_{k,t-1}

in the sense of Definition
B.1
. Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\max_{0\leq k\leq K-1}\max_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\max_{i\in[M]}\|\xi_{t}^{i,k}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\varepsilon_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)\;\coloneqq\;\sigma\sqrt{2d\log\!\Big(\frac{2dMKT}{\delta}\Big)}.

(13)

Proof.

Fix indices
(i,k,t)
and a coordinate
j\in[d]
. By Definition
B.1
with
u=e_{j}
, the scalar

Z\coloneqq[\xi_{t}^{i,k}]_{j}
is conditionally
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian given
\mathcal{F}_{k,t-1}
, hence the standard tail bound
holds conditionally:

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|Z|\geq u\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{k,t-1}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\quad\forall u\geq 0.

Taking expectations over
\mathcal{F}_{k,t-1}
yields the same unconditional bound

\mathbb{P}(|Z|\geq u)\leq 2\exp(-u^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))
.

Union bound over all
(i,k,t,j)\in[M]\times\{0,\dots,K-1\}\times\{0,\dots,T-1\}\times[d]
gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{i,k,t}\|\xi_{t}^{i,k}\|_{\infty}\geq u\right)\leq 2dMKT\exp\!\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

Set
u=\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2dMKT/\delta)}
so the right-hand side is at most
\delta
.
On this event, for all
(i,k,t)
,

\|\xi_{t}^{i,k}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}\,\|\xi_{t}^{i,k}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{d}\,u=\sigma\sqrt{2d\log\!\Big(\frac{2dMKT}{\delta}\Big)}.

This proves equation
13
.
∎

B.2
Proof of Theorem
4.4

Throughout this proof, we work on the event

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)\;\coloneqq\;\left\{\max_{k,t,i}\|\xi_{t}^{i,k}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)\right\},

which holds with probability at least
1-\delta
by Lemma
B.2
.
For readability, write
\varepsilon\coloneqq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)
.

We fix a round
k
and suppress the superscript
k
; the bounds are uniform over
k
because

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)
already union-bounds over all rounds.

Setup.

Fix a client
i
. Let
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
be the client trajectory and
\{\theta_{t}^{\star}\}_{t=0}^{T}
the reference trajectory,
with common initialization
\theta_{0}=\theta_{0}^{\star}=\bar{\theta}^{k}
.
Define deviations

\Delta_{t}\coloneqq\theta_{t}-\theta_{t}^{\star},\qquad\Delta_{0}=0.

Assume
f=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}F_{i}
is
L
-smooth on
Q
and gradients are clipped/bounded on
Q
so

\|\nabla F_{i}(\theta)\|_{2}\leq G
for all
i
and
\theta\in Q
. Then
\|\nabla f(\theta)\|_{2}\leq G
and

\|\nabla F_{i}(\theta)-\nabla f(\theta)\|_{2}\leq 2G,\qquad\forall\theta\in Q.

(14)

We also assume the reference iterates
\theta_{t}^{\star}\in Q
and the client iterates remain in
Q
on the success event;
all inequalities below are conditioned on being in
Q
where smoothness/boundedness holds.

Case 1: SGD

The client update is

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta\big(\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t})+\xi_{t}\big),\qquad\theta^{\star}_{t+1}=\theta^{\star}_{t}-\eta\nabla f(\theta^{\star}_{t}).

Subtracting yields

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}-\eta\big(\nabla f(\theta_{t})-\nabla f(\theta^{\star}_{t})\big)-\eta\big(\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t})-\nabla f(\theta_{t})\big)-\eta\xi_{t}.

(15)

Taking norms and using
L
-smoothness of
f
and equation
14
, on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)
we obtain

\|\Delta_{t+1}\|_{2}\leq(1+\eta L)\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}+\eta(2G+\varepsilon).

Unrolling from
\Delta_{0}=0
gives

\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq\eta(2G+\varepsilon)\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}(1+\eta L)^{t-1-s}=\eta(2G+\varepsilon)\frac{(1+\eta L)^{t}-1}{\eta L}.

Using
(1+\eta L)^{t}\leq e^{\eta Lt}
and
e^{x}-1\leq xe^{x}
, we get

\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq(2G+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{e^{\eta Lt}-1}{L}\leq(2G+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{\eta Lt\,e^{\eta Lt}}{L}=\eta t(2G+\varepsilon)e^{\eta Lt}.

Under the step-size condition
\eta LT\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, we have
e^{\eta Lt}\leq e^{1/2}\leq 2
for all
t\leq T
, hence

\max_{t\leq T}\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq 2\eta T(2G+\varepsilon)=4\eta TG+2\eta T\varepsilon.

(16)

This matches the stated
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{drift}}
for SGD up to absolute constants.

Case 2: Momentum

Momentum recursions (one round) are

m_{t+1}=\beta_{1}m_{t}+(1-\beta_{1})\big(\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t})+\xi_{t}\big),\qquad\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta m_{t+1},

and for the reference

m_{t+1}^{\star}=\beta_{1}m_{t}^{\star}+(1-\beta_{1})\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star}),\qquad\theta_{t+1}^{\star}=\theta_{t}^{\star}-\eta m_{t+1}^{\star}.

Define
\Delta^{m}_{t}\coloneqq m_{t}-m_{t}^{\star}
and note
\|\Delta^{m}_{0}\|_{2}=\|m_{0}^{i,k}-m_{0}^{\star,k}\|_{2}\leq B_{m}

by Definition
4.3
. Subtracting the
m
-updates gives

\Delta^{m}_{t+1}=\beta_{1}\Delta^{m}_{t}+(1-\beta_{1})\Big(\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star})+\xi_{t}\Big).

(17)

Decompose
\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star})=\big(\nabla f(\theta_{t})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star})\big)+\big(\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t})-\nabla f(\theta_{t})\big)
and use

L
-smoothness plus equation
14
and
\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)
to obtain

\|\Delta^{m}_{t+1}\|_{2}\leq\beta_{1}\|\Delta^{m}_{t}\|_{2}+(1-\beta_{1})\big(L\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}+2G+\varepsilon\big).

(18)

Now express
\Delta_{t}
in terms of
\Delta^{m}
:

\Delta_{t+1}=\Delta_{t}-\eta\Delta^{m}_{t+1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Delta_{t}=-\eta\sum_{r=0}^{t-1}\Delta^{m}_{r+1}.

Using the closed-form solution of equation
17
,

\Delta^{m}_{r+1}=\beta_{1}^{r+1}\Delta^{m}_{0}+(1-\beta_{1})\sum_{s=0}^{r}\beta_{1}^{r-s}h_{s},\qquad h_{s}\coloneqq\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{s})-\nabla f(\theta_{s}^{\star})+\xi_{s},

we obtain (by summation and swapping sums) the identity

\Delta_{t}=-\eta\sum_{r=0}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{r+1}\Delta^{m}_{0}-\eta\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}w_{t,s}\,h_{s},\qquad w_{t,s}\coloneqq(1-\beta_{1})\sum_{r=s}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{r-s}=1-\beta_{1}^{t-s}\in[0,1].

Therefore,

\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq\underbrace{\eta\sum_{r=0}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{r+1}\|\Delta^{m}_{0}\|_{2}}_{\leq\eta B_{m}/(1-\beta_{1})}+\eta\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\|h_{s}\|_{2}.

(19)

Finally, bound
\|h_{s}\|_{2}
as

\|h_{s}\|_{2}\leq\|\nabla f(\theta_{s})-\nabla f(\theta_{s}^{\star})\|_{2}+\|\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{s})-\nabla f(\theta_{s})\|_{2}+\|\xi_{s}\|_{2}\leq L\|\Delta_{s}\|_{2}+2G+\varepsilon.

Plugging into equation
19
yields, for all
t\leq T
,

\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\eta}{1-\beta_{1}}B_{m}+\eta t(2G+\varepsilon)+\eta L\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\|\Delta_{s}\|_{2}.

(20)

We apply the standard discrete Grönwall inequality: if
a_{t}\geq 0
satisfies

a_{t}\leq A+Bt+C\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}a_{s}
, then
a_{t}\leq(A+Bt)e^{Ct}
.
Applying this to
a_{t}=\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}
with
A=\frac{\eta}{1-\beta_{1}}B_{m}
,
B=\eta(2G+\varepsilon)
,
C=\eta L
gives

\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq\left(\frac{\eta}{1-\beta_{1}}B_{m}+\eta t(2G+\varepsilon)\right)e^{\eta Lt}.

Under
\eta LT\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, we have
e^{\eta Lt}\leq 2
for
t\leq T
, hence

\max_{t\leq T}\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq 2\cdot\frac{\eta}{1-\beta_{1}}B_{m}+2\eta T(2G+\varepsilon)=\frac{2\eta}{1-\beta_{1}}B_{m}+4\eta TG+2\eta T\varepsilon.

(21)

This matches the theorem’s momentum bound up to absolute constants.

Case 3: AdamW with preconditioner
(v+\epsilon\mathbf{1})^{-1/2}

We analyze the AdamW-style update (bias corrections and weight decay omitted for clarity; they only affect constants).
Let
g_{t}=\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t})+\xi_{t}
(client) and
g_{t}^{\star}=\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star})
(reference), with recursions

m_{t+1}=\beta_{1}m_{t}+(1-\beta_{1})g_{t},\quad v_{t+1}=\beta_{2}v_{t}+(1-\beta_{2})g_{t}^{\odot 2},\quad\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta\,m_{t+1}\odot(v_{t+1}+\epsilon\mathbf{1})^{-1/2},

and similarly for
(m_{t}^{\star},v_{t}^{\star},\theta_{t}^{\star})
with
g_{t}^{\star}
.

Step 1: a sensitivity bound for the preconditioner.

For scalars
a,b\geq 0
,

\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{a+\epsilon}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{b+\epsilon}}\right|\leq\frac{|a-b|}{2\epsilon^{3/2}},\qquad\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{a+\epsilon}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{b+\epsilon}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}.

(22)

The first inequality follows from the mean value theorem and
|(d/dx)(x+\epsilon)^{-1/2}|\leq(2\epsilon^{3/2})^{-1}
, while
the second holds since
(a+\epsilon)^{-1/2}\in(0,\epsilon^{-1/2}]
.

Step 2: bounding the update-direction difference.

Define the update directions

u_{t+1}\coloneqq m_{t+1}\odot(v_{t+1}+\epsilon\mathbf{1})^{-1/2},\qquad u_{t+1}^{\star}\coloneqq m_{t+1}^{\star}\odot(v_{t+1}^{\star}+\epsilon\mathbf{1})^{-1/2}.

Then
\Delta_{t+1}=\Delta_{t}-\eta(u_{t+1}-u_{t+1}^{\star})
, so

\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq\eta\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\|u_{s+1}-u_{s+1}^{\star}\|_{2}.

Add and subtract
m_{t+1}^{\star}\odot(v_{t+1}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}
to get

u_{t+1}-u_{t+1}^{\star}=(m_{t+1}-m_{t+1}^{\star})\odot(v_{t+1}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}+m_{t+1}^{\star}\odot\left((v_{t+1}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}-(v_{t+1}^{\star}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}\right).

Using
\|a\odot b\|_{2}\leq\|a\|_{2}\|b\|_{\infty}
and
\|(v_{t+1}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon^{-1/2}
yields

\|u_{t+1}-u_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\|m_{t+1}-m_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}+\|m_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}\cdot\left\|(v_{t+1}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}-(v_{t+1}^{\star}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}\right\|_{\infty}.

(23)

On
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)
,
\|g_{t}\|_{2}\leq G+\varepsilon
and
\|g_{t}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq G
.
By induction on the EMA recursions (starting from bounded states at round start), we have
\|m_{t}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq G
for all
t
.
(If
m_{0}^{\star}=0
this is immediate; more generally it holds whenever
m_{0}^{\star}
is produced by the same EMA under bounded gradients.)

Step 3: isolating the effect of
initial
state mismatch.

Let
\Delta_{0}^{m}\coloneqq m_{0}-m_{0}^{\star}
and
\Delta_{0}^{v}\coloneqq v_{0}-v_{0}^{\star}
, with

\|\Delta_{0}^{m}\|_{2}\leq B_{m}
and
\|\Delta_{0}^{v}\|_{2}\leq B_{v}
.

(i) First moment.

Unrolling the
m
-recursion gives

m_{t+1}-m_{t+1}^{\star}=\beta_{1}^{t+1}\Delta_{0}^{m}+(1-\beta_{1})\sum_{s=0}^{t}\beta_{1}^{t-s}(g_{s}-g_{s}^{\star}).

On
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)
,
\|g_{s}-g_{s}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq\|g_{s}\|_{2}+\|g_{s}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq 2G+\varepsilon
,
and
(1-\beta_{1})\sum_{s=0}^{t}\beta_{1}^{t-s}\leq 1
, hence

\|m_{t+1}-m_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq\beta_{1}^{t+1}B_{m}+(2G+\varepsilon).

(24)

(ii) Second moment.

Similarly,

v_{t+1}-v_{t+1}^{\star}=\beta_{2}^{t+1}\Delta_{0}^{v}+(1-\beta_{2})\sum_{s=0}^{t}\beta_{2}^{t-s}\big(g_{s}^{\odot 2}-(g_{s}^{\star})^{\odot 2}\big).

We bound the preconditioner difference by separating the contribution of
\beta_{2}^{t+1}\Delta_{0}^{v}
from the remaining term.
Let
r_{t+1}\coloneqq(1-\beta_{2})\sum_{s=0}^{t}\beta_{2}^{t-s}\big(g_{s}^{\odot 2}-(g_{s}^{\star})^{\odot 2}\big)
so that

v_{t+1}=v_{t+1}^{\star}+\beta_{2}^{t+1}\Delta_{0}^{v}+r_{t+1}
.
Then by triangle inequality and equation
22
,

\displaystyle\left\|(v_{t+1}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}-(v_{t+1}^{\star}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(v_{t+1}^{\star}+r_{t+1}+\beta_{2}^{t+1}\Delta_{0}^{v}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}-(v_{t+1}^{\star}+r_{t+1}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|(v_{t+1}^{\star}+r_{t+1}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}-(v_{t+1}^{\star}+\epsilon)^{-1/2}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{2}^{t+1}\|\Delta_{0}^{v}\|_{\infty}}{2\epsilon^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\;\leq\;\frac{\beta_{2}^{t+1}B_{v}}{2\epsilon^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}.

(25)

(The last step uses
\|\Delta_{0}^{v}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\Delta_{0}^{v}\|_{2}\leq B_{v}
.)

Step 4: complete the bound.

Combine equation
23
,
\|m_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq G
, equation
24
, and equation
25
to obtain, for all
t
,

\|u_{t+1}-u_{t+1}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\big(\beta_{1}^{t+1}B_{m}+(2G+\varepsilon)\big)+G\left(\frac{\beta_{2}^{t+1}B_{v}}{2\epsilon^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right).

Summing over
t=0,\dots,T-1
yields, for all
t\leq T
,

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\eta\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\|u_{s+1}-u_{s+1}^{\star}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\beta_{1}^{s+1}\right)B_{m}+\frac{\eta G}{2\epsilon^{3/2}}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\beta_{2}^{s+1}\right)B_{v}+\frac{\eta t}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\Big((2G+\varepsilon)+G\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{(1-\beta_{1})\sqrt{\epsilon}}\,B_{m}+\frac{\eta G}{2(1-\beta_{2})\epsilon^{3/2}}\,B_{v}+\frac{\eta T}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\big(3G+\varepsilon\big).

Thus,

\max_{t\leq T}\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\eta}{(1-\beta_{1})\sqrt{\epsilon}}\,B_{m}+\frac{\eta G}{2(1-\beta_{2})\epsilon^{3/2}}\,B_{v}+\frac{\eta T}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\big(3G+\varepsilon\big).

(26)

This matches the theorem’s AdamW decomposition into a drift/noise term of order
\frac{\eta T}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}(G+\varepsilon)
and a
state term of order
\eta\left(\frac{B_{m}}{(1-\beta_{1})\sqrt{\epsilon}}+\frac{GB_{v}}{(1-\beta_{2})\epsilon^{3/2}}\right)
,
up to absolute constants.

Conclusion.

Combining equation
16
, equation
21
, and equation
26
on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{noise}}(\delta)
,
and noting that Lemma
B.2
already provides a simultaneous bound over all
(i,k,t)
, yields the claimed W.H.P. tube bound
simultaneously over all rounds, clients, and steps.

B.3
In-expectation refinement under bounded heterogeneity

The W.H.P. bound in Theorem
4.4
uses worst-case drift control induced by clipping (via
\|\nabla F_{i}-\nabla f\|\leq 2G
).
Under the assumption of bounded heterogeneity
3.7
, one can obtain tighter
average/RMS
drift bounds and corresponding in-expectation stability/convergence results.
We state one representative corollary for SGD (extensions to stateful optimizers follow the same template but are more notational).

Corollary B.3

(In-expectation stability and convergence under bounded heterogeneity)
.

Assume full participation with server averaging and local
SGD
for
T
steps per round:

\bar{\theta}^{k+1}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{T}^{i,k}
.
Assume on
Q
: (i)
f
is
L
-smooth and satisfies the PL inequality with parameter
\mu>0
; (ii) bounded heterogeneity holds:

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\nabla F_{i}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}\leq H^{2}+B^{2}\|\nabla f(\theta)\|_{2}^{2},\quad\forall\theta\in Q;

(iii) mini-batch noise is unbiased with bounded conditional second moment:

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{t}^{i,k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k,t-1}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\xi_{t}^{i,k}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k,t-1}]\leq\sigma^{2}
;
and (iv)
\|\nabla f(\theta)\|_{2}\leq G
on
Q
.
Let the within-round reference be
T
steps of (full-batch) gradient descent on
f
:

\theta_{t+1}^{\star,k}=\theta_{t}^{\star,k}-\eta\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star,k})
with
\theta_{0}^{\star,k}=\bar{\theta}^{k}
.
If
\eta LT\leq\frac{1}{6}
, then for each round
k
,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\bar{\theta}^{k+1}-\theta_{T}^{\star,k}\|_{2}\ \big|\ \bar{\theta}^{k}\Big]\;\leq\;2\eta T\left(\sqrt{H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)G^{2}}+\sigma\right).

Moreover, defining
r_{k}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[f(\bar{\theta}^{k})-f^{\star}]
, there exist absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

r_{k+1}\leq(1-\eta\mu)^{T}\,r_{k}+c_{1}\,\eta T\left(\sqrt{H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)G^{2}}+\sigma\right)+c_{2}\,\eta^{2}T^{2}\left(\sqrt{H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)G^{2}}+\sigma\right)^{2}.

In particular, if
H=\sigma=0
(and hence clients are effectively IID and gradients are deterministic), then
r_{k}
converges

linearly
to
0
(i.e.,
\bar{\theta}^{k}
converges in expectation to
f^{\star}
). More generally, with constant
\eta
the recursion
converges linearly to a neighborhood whose radius scales with
H
and
\sigma
, and with diminishing
\eta\to 0
the neighborhood vanishes.

Proof.

Fix a round
k
and omit the superscript
k
for readability.
Let
\Delta_{t}^{i}\coloneqq\theta_{t}^{i}-\theta_{t}^{\star}
and define the RMS deviation

D_{t}\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\Delta_{t}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\qquad D_{0}=0.

Step 1: an RMS recursion using bounded heterogeneity.

For SGD,
\theta_{t+1}^{i}=\theta_{t}^{i}-\eta(\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t}^{i})+\xi_{t}^{i})
and

\theta_{t+1}^{\star}=\theta_{t}^{\star}-\eta\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star})
, so

\Delta_{t+1}^{i}=\Delta_{t}^{i}-\eta\big(\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{i})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star})\big)-\eta\big(\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t}^{i})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{i})\big)-\eta\xi_{t}^{i}.

Stack the vectors across clients:
\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{Md}
collects
(\Delta_{t}^{1},\dots,\Delta_{t}^{M})
, and similarly define
stacked
\widetilde{A}_{t}
for
(\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{i})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star}))_{i=1}^{M}
,

\widetilde{B}_{t}
for
(\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t}^{i})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{i}))_{i=1}^{M}
, and
\widetilde{\xi}_{t}
for
(\xi_{t}^{i})_{i=1}^{M}
.
Then
\widetilde{\Delta}_{t+1}=\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{A}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{B}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{\xi}_{t}
and by the triangle inequality
in
\mathbb{R}^{Md}
,

\|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t+1}\|_{2}\leq\|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{A}_{t}\|_{2}+\eta\|\widetilde{B}_{t}\|_{2}+\eta\|\widetilde{\xi}_{t}\|_{2}.

(27)

Since
f
is
L
-smooth,
\|\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{i})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star})\|_{2}\leq L\|\Delta_{t}^{i}\|_{2}
for each
i
, hence

\|\widetilde{A}_{t}\|_{2}\leq L\|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}\|_{2}
, and therefore

\|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}-\eta\widetilde{A}_{t}\|_{2}\leq(1+\eta L)\|\widetilde{\Delta}_{t}\|_{2}
.
Dividing equation
27
by
\sqrt{M}
gives

D_{t+1}\leq(1+\eta L)D_{t}+\eta\,\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t}^{i})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}_{=:B_{t}}+\eta\,\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\xi_{t}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}}_{=:N_{t}}.

(28)

We bound
B_{t}
by relating drift at
\theta_{t}^{i}
to drift at the common reference point
\theta_{t}^{\star}
:

\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t}^{i})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{i})=\underbrace{\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t}^{\star})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star})}_{\delta_{i}(\theta_{t}^{\star})}+\big(\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t}^{i})-\nabla F_{i}(\theta_{t}^{\star})\big)+\big(\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{\star})-\nabla f(\theta_{t}^{i})\big).

Taking RMS over
i
and using the triangle inequality plus
L
-smoothness of
F_{i}
and
f
yields

B_{t}\leq\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\delta_{i}(\theta_{t}^{\star})\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}+2LD_{t}.

(29)

Now compute the RMS drift at a common point using bounded heterogeneity:

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\delta_{i}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\nabla F_{i}(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}-\|\nabla f(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}\leq H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)\|\nabla f(\theta)\|_{2}^{2}\leq H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)G^{2}.

Therefore
\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i}\|\delta_{i}(\theta_{t}^{\star})\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)G^{2}}
.
For the noise term, by Jensen and the conditional variance bound,

\mathbb{E}[N_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[N_{t}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[\|\xi_{t}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]}\leq\sigma.

Taking conditional expectations in equation
28
and using equation
29
gives

\mathbb{E}[D_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq(1+\eta L)D_{t}+\eta\big(\sqrt{H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)G^{2}}+2LD_{t}\big)+\eta\sigma=(1+3\eta L)D_{t}+\eta\sqrt{H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)G^{2}}+\eta\sigma.

Step 2: unrolling the RMS recursion.

Iterating from
D_{0}=0
yields

\mathbb{E}[D_{T}\mid\bar{\theta}^{k}]\leq\eta\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(1+3\eta L)^{T-1-t}\Big(\sqrt{H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)G^{2}}+\sigma\Big)\leq\eta T\,(1+3\eta L)^{T}\Big(\sqrt{H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)G^{2}}+\sigma\Big).

Under
\eta LT\leq\frac{1}{6}
, we have
(1+3\eta L)^{T}\leq e^{3\eta LT}\leq e^{1/2}\leq 2
, hence

\mathbb{E}[D_{T}\mid\bar{\theta}^{k}]\leq 2\eta T\Big(\sqrt{H^{2}+(B^{2}-1)G^{2}}+\sigma\Big).

Since
\bar{\theta}^{k+1}-\theta_{T}^{\star}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i}(\theta_{T}^{i}-\theta_{T}^{\star})
, we have

\|\bar{\theta}^{k+1}-\theta_{T}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i}\|\Delta_{T}^{i}\|_{2}\leq D_{T}
.
This proves the first inequality in the corollary.

Step 3: transferring reference contraction to the global iterate.

By Assumption
3.6
and Assumption
3.5
, gradient descent on
f
with step size
\eta\leq 1/L
satisfies the standard linear rate

f(\theta_{T}^{\star})-f^{\star}\leq(1-\eta\mu)^{T}\big(f(\bar{\theta}^{k})-f^{\star}\big).

Next, by
L
-smoothness of
f
,

f(\bar{\theta}^{k+1})\leq f(\theta_{T}^{\star})+\langle\nabla f(\theta_{T}^{\star}),\bar{\theta}^{k+1}-\theta_{T}^{\star}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\bar{\theta}^{k+1}-\theta_{T}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}.

Using
\|\nabla f(\theta)\|_{2}\leq G
on
Q
and
e_{k}\coloneqq\bar{\theta}^{k+1}-\theta_{T}^{\star}
,

f(\bar{\theta}^{k+1})-f^{\star}\leq f(\theta_{T}^{\star})-f^{\star}+G\|e_{k}\|_{2}+\frac{L}{2}\|e_{k}\|_{2}^{2}.

Taking expectations and using
\|e_{k}\|\leq D_{T}
and the bound on
\mathbb{E}[D_{T}\mid\bar{\theta}^{k}]
yields

r_{k+1}\leq(1-\eta\mu)^{T}r_{k}+G\,\mathbb{E}[D_{T}]+\frac{L}{2}\mathbb{E}[D_{T}^{2}].

Finally, use Jensen to bound
\mathbb{E}[D_{T}^{2}]\leq(\mathbb{E}[D_{T}])^{2}
and substitute the bound on
\mathbb{E}[D_{T}]
.
This gives the claimed recursion with explicit constants (absorbed into
c_{1},c_{2}
).
The special cases (
H=\sigma=0
and/or diminishing stepsizes) follow immediately.

Appendix C
Details of Synthetic Loss Landscape Experiments

To empirically validate our intuition that GaLore tends to find solutions with better loss landscape geometry, we designed a controlled synthetic experiment. The goal is to isolate how the
dynamics
of low-rank optimizers influence their ability to escape ”sharp, aligned” local minima in favor of ”flat, orthogonal” global basins.

C.1
Why Flatness implies Aggregation Robustness

In Federated Learning, the geometric property of
“flatness”
is a direct proxy for aggregability. We seek a global model
\bm{W}^{\star}
that remains valid even when averaged with perturbed local models. If client models drift by
\Delta\bm{W}_{i}
, the expected loss of the aggregate is approximately:

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\bm{W}_{\mathrm{agg}})]\approx\mathcal{L}(\bm{W}^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\bm{W}^{\star})\mathbb{E}[\Delta\bm{W}\Delta\bm{W}^{\top}]\right).

(30)

In a
sharp valley
, the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}
has large eigenvalues, so even small drift incurs a massive penalty. In a
flat basin
, eigenvalues are small, minimizing the impact of heterogeneity.

C.2
Landscape Construction: The ”Kinetic Trap”

We optimize a matrix
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. The landscape combines two quadratic basins via a smooth SoftMin function:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{W})=-\tau\log\left(\exp(-\mathcal{L}_{1}(\bm{W})/\tau)+\exp(-\mathcal{L}_{2}(\bm{W})/\tau)\right).

(31)

We define two key directions relative to the initialization
\bm{W}_{0}
:

•

\mathbf{e}_{1}
(
Aligned
): A direction lying within the initial LoRA subspace span(
\bm{A}_{0}^{\top}
).

•

\mathbf{e}_{2}
(
Orthogonal
): A direction orthogonal to the initialization.

The basins are constructed to create a specific geometric challenge:

•

Basin 1 (The Target):
A
flat basin
centered at
\bm{W}_{1}=c\cdot\mathbf{e}_{2}
. This minimum is robust (small eigenvalues in all directions) but requires significant rotation into the orthogonal direction
\mathbf{e}_{2}
to be discovered.

•

Basin 2 (The Trap):
A
sharp valley
centered at the origin but elongated along
\mathbf{e}_{1}
. We set the Hessian
\bm{H}_{2}
such that the valley is steep in orthogonal directions but shallow along
\mathbf{e}_{1}
. This creates a
“trap”
: LoRA’s dynamics, which prioritize movement in the current subspace, perceive a quick path of descent into this brittle minimum.

C.3
Optimization Setup

We compare three methods, initializing each trial from a randomized starting point
\bm{W}^{(0)}=\bm{W}_{\mathrm{ref}}+\bm{\Xi}
, where
\bm{W}_{\mathrm{ref}}
is a fixed reference point between the two basins and
\bm{\Xi}
is Gaussian noise drawn for each trial.

1.

Full-Space SGD:
Updates
\bm{W}_{t+1}\leftarrow\bm{W}_{t}-\alpha\nabla\mathcal{L}
.

2.

LoRA:
Updates
\bm{W}=\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{B}\bm{A}
. Although the subspace is not strictly fixed, the coupling between
\bm{B}
and
\bm{A}
creates an
“inertial alignment”
that resists rapid rotation into
\mathbf{e}_{2}
.

3.

GaLore:
Explicitly projects gradients onto a time-varying subspace
\bm{U}_{r}\bm{\Sigma}_{r}\bm{V}_{r}^{\top}
derived from the SVD of the gradient, allowing for active subspace rotation.

Motivation.

GaLore-style methods require periodically refreshing a rank-
r
projector. A purely data-driven refresh (SVD/RSVD every
\tau

(
42
)
, we adopt an
SVD-to-random
schedule:
In our implementation, we use SVD/RSVD for the first
S
projector refreshes (set by
adaptive_proj_steps
) and then switch to seeded random projectors thereafter. In the random phase, the projector is fully determined by an integer seed, so we broadcast only the seed (not the basis matrix), enabling deterministic reconstruction across clients.

Variants.

We compare three projector schedules:
(i)
FedGaLore
(ours): RSVD/SVD in early epochs
\rightarrow
seeded random thereafter;
(ii)
FedGaLore-SVD
: always RSVD/SVD refresh;
(iii)
FedGaLore-Pure Random
: always seeded random projector.

We provide a brief introduction to the Joint and Individual Variation Explained (JIVE) framework and the Angle-based JIVE (AJIVE) method. The decomposition of multi-view data into shared and specific components is a fundamental challenge that has sparked diverse applications, such as identifying communities within heterogeneous networks
(
39
)
, analyzing high-dimensional genomic data
(
37
)
, and discerning global and local features in federated learning
(
49
)
.

A significant challenge in these analyses lies in separating the joint and individual variations present in multi-view datasets. In their seminal work,
37

14
proposed AJIVE, a two-stage spectral method based on principal angle analysis. Recent theoretical work by
66

(
45
)

(
43
)

To justify our design choice of using AJIVE for synchronizing the second-moment estimator
v
, we conduct a controlled synthetic experiment. The core challenge in federated adaptive optimization is that the second moment is a non-linear transformation of the gradients (
v\approx g^{2}
). Even if client gradients share a low-rank subspace, the client drift term
c_{i}
introduces structured noise that does not vanish under simple averaging due to the quadratic nature of the update.

F.1
Experimental Setup

We simulate a scenario where the true global gradient signal lies in a low-rank subspace, but clients observe a drifted, noisy version. We aim to recover the
“true”
global second moment
\bm{V}^{\star}
.

Data Generation.

Let the global gradient signal be
\bm{G}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
with rank
r=5
. The ground truth second moment is
\bm{V}^{\star}=(\bm{G}^{\star})^{\odot 2}
(element-wise square). Note that while
\bm{G}^{\star}
is rank-5, its element-wise square
\bm{V}^{\star}
typically has a higher rank (approximated by
r(r+1)/2\approx 15
).
We simulate
K
clients. Client
k
observes a perturbed gradient:

\bm{G}_{k}=\bm{G}^{\star}+\underbrace{\bm{L}_{k}}_{\text{Drift}}+\underbrace{\bm{\Xi}_{k}}_{\text{Noise}},

(32)

where
\bm{L}_{k}
is a random rank-2 perturbation representing client-specific drift, and
\bm{\Xi}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
is unstructured noise. The client computes its local second moment
\bm{V}_{k}=(\bm{G}_{k})^{\odot 2}
.

Baselines.

We compare three aggregation strategies to estimate
\bm{V}^{\star}
given
\{V_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
:

1.

Naive Averaging:

\bar{\bm{V}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\bm{V}_{k}
. This is the standard FedOpt approach.

2.

Averaging + SVD:
We compute the naive average
\bar{\bm{V}}
and then project it to rank
r^{\prime}
via SVD. This attempts to denoise the result
after
aggregation.

3.

AJIVE (Ours):
We apply AJIVE to the collection
\{\bm{V}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
to extract the joint spectral component shared across clients, effectively filtering out the drift
\bm{L}_{k}
before it corrupts the signal.

F.2
Results and Analysis

We measure the reconstruction error
\|\bm{V}_{\text{est}}-\bm{V}^{\star}\|_{F}
as a function of the number of participating clients. The results are visualized in Figure
5
.

Our observations include:

•

Failure of Naive Averaging
: As shown by the red dashed line, simply averaging local states results in high error. This confirms our theoretical concern: non-linear drift does not cancel out linearly.

•

Rank Expansion
: The element-wise square of a rank-
r
matrix has an effective rank much larger than
r
. Consequently, estimators restricted to the original rank (Rank 5, blue lines) underfit the geometry. AJIVE Rank 15 (green circles) matches the theoretical rank of the squared signal, achieving near-perfect recovery as the number of clients increases.

•

Robustness
: AJIVE outperforms post-hoc SVD (solid green line) because it uses the
consensus
of subspaces across clients to identify the signal, whereas post-hoc SVD cannot distinguish between the signal and the dominant directions of the drift variance.

Dirichlet Distribution for Modeling Non-IID Data

(a)

(b)

(c)

Figure 6
:
Impact of concentration parameter
\alpha
on data distribution.
We illustrate the class distribution across clients for varying
\alpha
on MNIST datasets. Smaller values (e.g.,
\alpha=0.5
) result in severe class imbalance, where clients possess samples from very few classes.

Following common practice in federated learning
(
69
;
57
;
30
)
, we model client data heterogeneity using a Dirichlet distribution. Specifically, for a classification task with
K
classes, the label distribution for each client
i
is sampled from a Dirichlet distribution:

p_{i}\sim\text{Dir}(\alpha\cdot\mathbf{1}_{K}),

where
\alpha>0
is the concentration parameter and
\mathbf{1}_{K}
is a
K
-dimensional vector of ones. A smaller
\alpha
produces more skewed client label distributions (i.e., stronger non-IID conditions), while a larger
\alpha
yields more balanced distributions approaching the IID case.

After sampling
p_{i}
, we partition the dataset by allocating examples to client
i
according to
p_{i}
. Figure
6
illustrates how the Dirichlet partition leads to distinct label distributions across clients. For MetaMathQA
(
68
)
, we treat
“type”
as the label.

In our experiments, we set
\alpha=0.5
to study the impact of severe data heterogeneity.
