Novel percutaneous retrieval of a self-entangled decapolar catheter using a deflectable sheath and guidewire counter-traction
Authors: Kai-Hsiang Chang, Li-Chih Wu, Chi-Te Lee, Chang-Yi Lin, Shu-I Lin, Feng-Ching Liao, Ying-Hsiang Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCatheter entanglement is an uncommon but potentially serious complication during electrophysiological procedures. This is a rare case of self-entanglement of a diagnostic decapolar catheter during cryoablation for persistent atrial fibrillation. A 61-year-old man underwent catheter-based rhythm control therapy under conscious sedation. During coronary sinus cannulation, the catheter unexpectedly prolapsed and formed a self-loop within the right atrium. Repeated manipulation and traction resulted in progressive tightening of the loop around the Eustachian valve region and adjacent right atrial structures, making conventional withdrawal unsuccessful. Inferior vena cava venography demonstrated marked venous tortuosity, which likely impaired torque transmission and contributed to uncontrolled catheter rebound and looping. A bailout retrieval strategy using a right internal jugular approach, an Attain™ deflectable delivery system, guidewire-assisted loop stabilization, and EN Snare® counter-traction successfully released the entrapped catheter without surgical intervention or structural injury. This case highlights a reproducible percutaneous retrieval strategy for complex right atrial catheter entanglement.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old male with persistent atrial fibrillation (AF) was referred to our hospital for catheter-based rhythm control therapy. Preprocedural echocardiography and enhanced computed tomography revealed no significant structural heart disease apart from left atrial enlargement. The four pulmonary veins measured 28–30 mm in diameter, consistent with moderate atrial remodeling.

Cryoablation was planned under conscious sedation. An 8.5-F long sheath and two 6- F short sheaths were introduced via the right femoral vein. A decapolar diagnostic catheter (Abbott Livewire™ Electrophysiology Catheter Decapolar CSL™ Bi-directional) was advanced toward the coronary sinus under fluoroscopic guidance. During engagement of the coronary sinus ostium, the catheter unexpectedly prolapsed backward and formed a self-loop within the right atrium. (Figure A). The catheter subsequently exhibited abnormal oscillating motion synchronized with cardiac contraction. Multiple attempts at withdrawal and torque correction were unsuccessful and instead resulted in progressive tightening of the loop.Fluoroscopic assessment in RAO and LAO projections suggested that the loop had become fixed around the adjacent right atrial tissue near the coronary sinus. Inferior vena cava venography demonstrated marked venous tortuosity, which likely impaired torque transmission and contributed to excessive rebound of the catheter tip during manipulation. Further traction resulted in increasing catheter tension without loop reduction, raising concern for structural injury if forceful withdrawal was continued.

To facilitate controlled retrieval, a 10-F sheath was introduced via the right internal jugular vein. An Attain™ deflectable catheter delivery system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was advanced toward the entrapped loop to provide directional support and stabilization. Using the deflectable lumen, a 0.035-inch guidewire was carefully advanced through the center of the self-entangled loop under fluoroscopic guidance. (Figure B).

The guidewire functioned as an internal stabilizing rail that prevented further loop tightening and partially expanded the collapsed loop configuration. Subsequently, an EN Snare® Endovascular Snare System (Merit Medical, South Jordan, UT, USA) was advanced through the jugular sheath to capture the distal end of the guidewire. Controlled counter-traction was then simultaneously applied from both femoral and jugular access sites. With gradual tension redistribution and guidewire-assisted loop expansion, the entangled catheter was successfully disentangled and completely retrieved without surgical intervention. (Figure C).

Discussion

The present case demonstrates a rare form of right atrial catheter self-entanglement. Based on fluoroscopic findings and procedural characteristics, the entanglement was presumed to involve the Eustachian valve region or adjacent right atrial structures near the inferior vena cava-right atrial junction. Unlike Plasek et al.[1] demonstrated the direct knotting of the catheter in inferior vena cava, the mechanism in this case appeared to involve initial self-loop formation followed by secondary fixation around mobile right atrial structures. Marked inferior vena cava tortuosity may have impaired torque transmission and promoted excessive rebound of the catheter tip during coronary sinus engagement, resulting in uncontrolled looping. (Figure D). While Plasek et al. [1] demonstrated femoral snaring via an Agilis sheath, the present technique offers two distinct advantages: (1) the internal jugular approach provides a straighter trajectory to the right atrial appendage trabeculae, and (2) the Attain system’s deflectable lumen allows precise guidewire placement through the loop, converting a complex entanglement into a stable, retrievable configuration.

Importantly, this approach may reduce the risk of structural injury compared with forceful direct traction alone. Post-procedural echocardiography demonstrated no evidence of tricuspid valve injury, pericardial effusion, or right atrial damage.

This case also highlights several practical considerations for preventing catheter entanglement during electrophysiological procedures. Neutral catheter tension should be maintained before advancement or withdrawal to avoid sudden rebound and loop formation. Difficult venous anatomy, particularly inferior vena cava tortuosity, may impair torque transmission and increase the risk of uncontrolled catheter prolapse. In addition, persistent resistance during coronary sinus engagement should not be overcome by forceful manipulation; instead, the catheter should be withdrawn and reshaped. Immediate fluoroscopic reassessment is essential whenever unexpected catheter motion or resistance is encountered, particularly in contemporary low-fluoroscopy electrophysiology practice.
