Relation between Hitting Times and Probabilities for Imprecise Markov Chains
Marco Sangalli, Erik Quaeghebeur, Thomas Krak

Abstract
In the present paper, we investigate the relationship between hitting times and hitting probabilities in discrete-time imprecise Markov chains (IMCs). We define lower and upper hitting times and probabilities for IMCs whose set of transition matrices 𝒯 \mathcal{T} is compact, convex, and has separately specified rows. Building on reachability-based partitions of the state space, we prove two key implications: (i) finiteness of the upper expected hitting time entails the lower hitting probability equals one, and (ii) finiteness of the lower expected hitting time entails the upper hitting probability equals one. We further show an equivalence: the upper expected hitting time is finite if and only if the lower hitting probability is one. Finally, by presenting a counterexample, we show that the converse of the second implication can fail.

Introduction

Hitting times and hitting probabilities are two complementary lenses through which one studies reachability in stochastic systems. The former captures how long it takes a process to reach a designated target set, while the latter captures the chance that the process ever does so. In the classical, precise theory of homogeneous Markov chains on a finite state space these two notions are tightly linked: finiteness of the expected hitting time is equivalent to the hitting probability being one
5
;
6
.

When uncertainty is present and dynamics are described not by a single transition matrix but by a set of transition matrices
\mathcal{T}
, the relationship between hitting times and probabilities is substantially more subtle. Replacing (precise) expectations and probabilities with lower and upper expectations and probabilities naturally produces four distinct objects for each initial state: lower and upper expected hitting times, and lower and upper hitting probabilities
3
;
4
. Even though some one-way implications survive, the neat equivalences from the precise case need not hold in the imprecise framework. Understanding which implications persist and which fail is both theoretically interesting and practically important for robust reachability analysis under model uncertainty.

A fruitful approach to understanding these phenomena in discrete-time imprecise Markov chains (IMCs) is to partition the state space according to reachability properties relative to
\mathcal{T}
. Following the work of Sangalli et al.
8
;
7
, one distinguishes states that never lower reach the target,
\mathscr{A}_{\mathcal{T}}
, states that never upper reach it,
\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{T}}
, and further classes
\mathscr{U}_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\mathscr{V}_{\mathcal{T}}
that capture intermediate behaviours. These reachability-based classes allow a clean characterisation of when lower and upper hitting times are finite and when lower and upper hitting probabilities are strictly positive.

This paper completes and sharpens this line of investigation proving that the upper expected hitting time is finite if and only if the lower hitting probability equals one. We then show a counterexample demonstrating that the equivalence is lost if “lower” and “upper” are swapped.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section
2.1
and
2.2
recall basic notation and classical results on hitting times and hitting probabilities for homogeneous Markov chains. Section
2.3
introduces imprecise Markov chains and lower and upper hitting times and probabilities.
Section
3
discusses the relation between hitting times and hitting probabilities in this imprecise setting.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we present all the preliminary concepts needed to study expected hitting times and hitting probabilities for IMCs.

2.1
Stochastic Processes and Markov Chains

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the positive integers and set
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
A discrete-time stochastic process taking values in
\mathcal{X}
is a sequence of
\mathcal{X}
-valued random variables
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
; its probability law will be denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{X}
.
The process
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is called a
Markov chain
if it satisfies

\mathbb{P}_{X}(X_{n+1}=x_{n+1}\mid X_{0:n}=x_{0:n})=\mathbb{P}_{X}(X_{n+1}=x_{n+1}\mid X_{n}=x_{n})

(1)

for all
x_{0},\dots,x_{n},x_{n+1}\in\mathcal{X}
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
A Markov chain is said to be (time-)
homogeneous
if transition probabilities do not depend on
n
; that is, if

\mathbb{P}_{X}(X_{n+1}=y\mid X_{n}=x)=\mathbb{P}_{X}(X_{1}=y\mid X_{0}=x)

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The stochastic matrix
T\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
defined as
T(x,y)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{X}(X_{1}=y\mid X_{0}=x)
is the
transition matrix
of the homogeneous Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and determines the behaviour of the process up to its initial distribution.

2.2
Expected hitting times and hitting probabilities

Fix a nonempty target set
A\subset\mathcal{X}
. The
hitting time
of a homogeneous Markov chain is the random variable

\tau_{A}:=\inf\{n\geq 0:X_{n}\in A\}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{+\infty\}.

(2)

Conditioned on the chain starting at
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the
expected hitting time
is

h^{T}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{T}}\bigl[\tau_{A}\mid X_{0}=x\bigr],

(3)

where we write
\mathbb{P}_{T}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{X}
.
Intuitively,
h^{T}(x)
is the average number of steps needed to reach
A
when the process starts from
x
. Moreover, the vector of expected hitting times
h^{T}
is the minimal nonnegative solution of a system of equations
6
:

\begin{cases}h^{T}(x)=0&\text{if $x\in A$,}\\
h^{T}(x)=1+\textstyle\sum\limits_{y\in\mathcal{X}}T(x,y)h^{T}(y)&\text{if $x\notin A$.}\end{cases}

We define the
hitting probability
of the homogeneous Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
as

p^{T}(x)=\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}<+\infty\mid X_{0}=x).

(4)

Hitting probabilities are the minimal nonnegative solution of

\begin{cases}p^{T}(x)=1&\text{if $x\in A$,}\\
p^{T}(x)=\textstyle\sum\limits_{y\in\mathcal{X}}T(x,y)p^{T}(y)&\text{if $x\notin A$.}\end{cases}

There exists a well-known relation between expected hitting times and hitting probabilities
5
;
6
: the expected hitting time is finite if and only if the hitting probability is one. For completeness, we provide the whole argument below and we split it into its two implications for later reference.

Proposition 1

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite state space and
T
be a transition matrix. Then

h^{T}(x)<+\infty\ \Rightarrow\ p^{T}(x)=1.

(5)

Proof

Recall that
h^{T}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{T}}\bigl[\tau_{A}\mid X_{0}=x\bigr]<+\infty
. Since the positive random variable
\tau_{A}
has finite expected value, its probability of being infinite is zero and
p^{T}(x)=\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}<+\infty\mid X_{0}=x)=1
. ∎

Proposition 2

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite state space and
T
be a transition matrix. Then

h^{T}(x)<+\infty\ \Leftarrow\ p^{T}(x)=1.

(6)

Proof

Let
R(x)\coloneqq x\cup\{y\in\mathcal{X}:\exists n\in\mathbb{N},\ \mathbb{P}_{T}(X_{n}=y\mid X_{0}=x)>0\}.
Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=p^{T}(x)=\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}<+\infty\mid X_{0}=x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{T}}\left[\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}<+\infty\mid X_{n})\mid X_{0}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{P}_{T}(X_{n}=y\mid X_{0}=x)\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}<+\infty\mid X_{0}=y),

(7)

where we used the law of total expectation and Markov’s property. It follows that, for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
with
\mathbb{P}_{T}(X_{n}=y\mid X_{0}=x)>0
it must hold that
\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}<+\infty\mid X_{0}=y)=p^{T}(y)=1
. For the arbitrariness of
n
, we get
p^{T}(y)=1
for all
y\in R(x)
.

Fix
y\in R(x)
. Since
p^{T}(y)=\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}<+\infty\mid X_{0}=y)=1
, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
m_{y,\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq m_{y,\varepsilon}
we have
\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}\leq n\mid X_{0}=y)\geq 1-\varepsilon
or, equivalently,
\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}>n\mid X_{0}=y)\leq\varepsilon
.
Fix
0<\varepsilon<1
and set
M=\max_{y\in R(x)}m_{y,\varepsilon}
. Then, by picking
n=M
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}>M\mid X_{0}=y)\leq\varepsilon,

for all
y\in R(x)
.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then, using Markov’s property, we have

\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}>kM\mid X_{0}=x)\leq\varepsilon^{k}.

We can bound the expected value of
\tau_{A}
using these tail bounds:

\displaystyle h^{T}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{T}}[\tau_{A}\mid X_{0}=x]={\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}>n\mid X_{0}=x)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sum_{n=kM}^{(k+1)M-1}\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}>n\mid X_{0}=x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}M\,\mathbb{P}_{T}(\tau_{A}>kM\mid X_{0}=x)

\displaystyle\leq M\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\varepsilon^{k}=\dfrac{M}{1-\varepsilon}<+\infty.

∎

We seek to explore whether this, or a similar, relation holds true in the imprecise framework, when instead of a single (precise) process we are dealing with a set of homogeneous Markov chains.

2.3
Imprecise Markov chains

Rather than a single homogeneous Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
governed by a transition matrix
T
, we now consider a family of these processes. Given a set
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, we consider the set
\mathcal{P}
made of all homogeneous Markov chains with transition matrix contained in the set
\mathcal{T}
. The set
\mathcal{P}
is said to be an
imprecise Markov chain
(IMC).
This choice of
\mathcal{P}
is justified by Krak et al.
(
3
, Theorem 18)
showing that lower and upper hitting times and probabilities remain unchanged when the set
\mathcal{P}
is enlarged to include all (time-)inhomogeneous Markov processes that are compatible with
\mathcal{T}
.
In the following, we assume that the set
\mathcal{T}
is nonempty, compact, convex, and with separately specified rows (SSR)
2
;
3
.

For an imprecise Markov chain
\mathcal{P}
, we define lower and upper expectations:

\underline{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}}[\cdot\mid\cdot]\coloneqq\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot\mid\cdot]\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}}[\cdot\mid\cdot]\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot\mid\cdot].

These operators provide conservative, tight bounds for any quantity of interest.

For a fixed nonempty target set
A\subset\mathcal{X}
, we define the
lower and upper expected hitting times
for the imprecise Markov chain parametrised by
\mathcal{T}
as

\displaystyle\underline{h}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)\coloneqq\underline{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}}[\tau_{A}\mid X_{0}=x]=\textstyle\inf_{T\in\mathcal{T}}h^{T}(x);

\displaystyle{\overline{h}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)\coloneqq\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}}[\tau_{A}\mid X_{0}=x]=\textstyle\sup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}h^{T}(x).}

Analogously, we define
lower and upper hitting probabilities
as

\displaystyle\underline{p}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)\coloneqq\underline{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{A}<+\infty\}}\mid X_{0}=x]=\textstyle\inf_{T\in\mathcal{T}}p^{T}(x);

\displaystyle{\overline{p}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)\coloneqq\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{A}<+\infty\}}\mid X_{0}=x]=\textstyle\sup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}p^{T}(x).}

We can partition the state space
\mathcal{X}
into sets of states that have infinite lower or upper hitting time and states with lower or upper hitting probability equal to zero. To do so, we introduce the concept of reachability for IMCs

:

•

x

lower reaches

A
, denoted by
x\rightharpoondown A
, if for all
T\in\mathcal{T}

\smash{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

\smash{[T^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{A}](x)>0}

•

A

x\rightharpoonup A

\smash{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

\smash{[T^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{A}](x)>0}

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}_{\mathcal{T}}\coloneqq\{x\in A^{c}\setminus\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{T}}\mid x\rightharpoondown\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{T}}\}.

The following result strengthens these implications.

Theorem 3.1

Under the previous assumptions, it holds that

1)

\ \overline{h}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)<+\infty\Rightarrow\underline{p}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)=1
;

2)

\ \underline{h}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)<+\infty\Rightarrow\overline{p}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)=1
.

Proof

Fix any
x\in\mathcal{X}
. If the upper hitting time is finite, then, for all
T\in\mathcal{T}
, we have that
h^{T}(x)<+\infty
. By Proposition
1
, we have
p^{T}(x)=1
for all
T\in\mathcal{T}
, therefore
\underline{p}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)=\inf_{T\in\mathcal{T}}p^{T}(x)=1
.

Similarly, if the lower hitting time is finite, there exists
T\in\mathcal{T}
such that
h^{T}(x)<+\infty
. By Proposition
1
, we have
p^{T}(x)=1
therefore
\overline{p}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)=1
. ∎

We now invert implications 1) and 2) in Theorem
3.1
and either prove their correctness or find a counterexample in which they fail. The following result states that the converse of implication 1) holds true.

Theorem 3.2

The upper expected hitting time
\overline{h}^{\mathcal{T}}
is finite if and only if the lower hitting probability
\underline{p}^{\mathcal{T}}
is one, i.e.

\overline{h}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)<+\infty\ \Leftrightarrow\ \underline{p}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)=1

(8)

Proof

We need to prove that
\underline{p}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)=1\ \Rightarrow\ \overline{h}^{\mathcal{T}}(x)<+\infty
since we already know the converse.
By hypothesis, we have
p^{T}(x)=1
, for all
T\in T
. Therefore, by Proposition
2
,
h^{T}(x)<+\infty
for all
T\in\mathcal{T}
. By Krak et al.
(
3
, Theorem 12)
, there exists a transition matrix
T^{*}\in\mathcal{T}
such that
h^{T^{*}}=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}h^{T}=\overline{h}{{}^{\mathcal{T}}}
. Thus, we conclude that
\overline{h}{{}^{\mathcal{T}}}(x)=h^{T^{*}}(x)<+\infty
. ∎
