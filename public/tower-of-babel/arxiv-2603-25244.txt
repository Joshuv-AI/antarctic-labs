Efficient Preemptive Robustification with Image Sharpening
Jiaming Liang, Chi-Man Pun

Abstract
Despite their great success, deep neural networks rely on high-dimensional, non-robust representations, making them vulnerable to imperceptible perturbations, even in transfer scenarios. To address this, both training-time defenses (e.g., adversarial training and robust architecture design) and post-attack defenses (e.g., input purification and adversarial detection) have been extensively studied. Recently, a limited body of work has preliminarily explored a pre-attack defense paradigm, termed preemptive robustification, which introduces subtle modifications to benign samples prior to attack to proactively resist adversarial perturbations. Unfortunately, their practical applicability remains questionable due to several limitations, including (1) reliance on well-trained classifiers as surrogates to provide robustness priors, (2) substantial computational overhead arising from iterative optimization or trained generators for robustification, and (3) limited interpretability of the optimization- or generation-based robustification processes. Inspired by recent studies revealing a positive correlation between texture intensity and the robustness of benign samples, we show that image sharpening alone can efficiently robustify images. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first surrogate-free, optimization-free, generator-free, and human-interpretable robustification approach. Extensive experiments demonstrate that sharpening yields remarkable robustness gains with low computational cost, especially in transfer scenarios.

Owing to their strong pattern recognition capabilities, deep neural networks (DNNs) have been widely adopted across diverse applications
(
46
;
20
)
. Unfortunately, their reliance on high-dimensional and non-robust representations renders them vulnerable to imperceptible perturbations
(
47
;
14
)
, drawing growing attention to their robustness.

To improve the robustness of DNNs, two mainstream strategies, training-time defenses and post-attack defenses, have been extensively studied. Training-time defenses focus on learning more robust models, such as adversarial training
(
65
)
, robust architecture design
(
39
)
, and robustness-oriented regularization
(
66
)
. Post-attack defenses are designed to mitigate adversarial perturbations or reveal their presence after an attack has occurred, including input purification
(
25
)
, transformation-based preprocessing
(
30
)
, adversarial detection
(
23
)
, output correction
(
45
)
, and so on.

However, training-time defenses typically sacrifice considerable standard accuracy for robustness
(
50
)
, making this inherent trade-off a major limitation. In contrast, post-attack defenses introduce substantial inference-time overhead through transformations, purification or detection modules, or repeated inference with voting mechanisms, yet their effectiveness remains limited. Consequently, some recent studies have turned to pre-attack defenses that are orthogonal to these two defense stages.

Pre-attack defenses seek to safeguard benign samples before they are attacked, thereby reducing their susceptibility to adversarial perturbations. Grounded in benign samples, this strategy circumvents the standard-robustness trade-off while mitigating inference-time costs, especially for defending against man-in-the-middle attacks. Preemptive robustification defenses
(
36
)
protect benign samples via small robustness-inducing perturbations and have recently gained increasing attention as a promising branch of pre-attack defenses. Unfortunately, only a limited number of studies
(
36
;
11
)
have explored this direction, and existing methods typically suffer from the following limitations:

1.

To acquire prior knowledge for robustifying benign samples, these methods require a well-trained surrogate model. However, in specialized domains such as medical imaging, such models are often inaccessible.

2.

Existing methods are based on iterative optimization or DNN-based generation, resulting in prohibitive computational cost for real-time communication systems.

3.

Robustness-inducing perturbations generated by current optimization- or generation-based methods lack human interpretability, thus hindering their reliability.

Therefore, we seek to explore an effective preemptive robustification method that is surrogate-, optimization-, and generator-free, while remaining human-interpretable and computationally efficient. Intriguingly, a recent measure of image attackability
(
28
)
suggests that images with stronger semantic textures exhibit significantly superior resistance to adversarial perturbations. Moreover, a prior optimization-based preemptive robustification method
(
11
)
reports a similar phenomenon: the robustified samples it generates tend to possess enhanced textures and contrast. In addition, several studies
(
53
)
have shown that adversarial perturbations are predominantly concentrated in mid-to-high frequency components associated with texture. Together, these observations highlight the critical role of texture in preemptive robustness.

Motivated by these consistent insights, we put forward an intuitive hypothesis:
can image sharpening serve as an efficient preemptive robustification method?
In other words, can benign images be effectively preemptively robustified solely through texture enhancement via traditional sharpening operators? To this end, based on classical Laplacian sharpening, we conduct extensive experiments across a wide range of attacks, tasks, and datasets. Surprisingly, Laplacian sharpening introduces only imperceptible changes (i.e., mild sharpening enhancement) while significantly improving the resistance of benign images to adversarial perturbations, particularly those with high transferability. As a concrete example, building on the NIPS’17 Adversarial Competition benchmark, with ResNet-50 as the surrogate model, a perturbation budget of
\epsilon=10/255
, and iterations
T=10
for the MA attack, a modest Laplacian texture enhancement of only
0.15\times
improves the average classification accuracy of adversarial examples from
41.3\%
to
56.0\%
across 17 black-box models. Remarkably, this improvement is achieved with negligible computational cost, requiring only a single convolution. Visual examples are presented in Figure
1
.

Our findings suggest a promising direction for preemptive robustification, particularly in practical and industrial settings. The main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose image sharpening as a novel preemptive robustification method for the first time, introducing a distinct paradigm from the existing approaches.

•

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first preemptive robustification method that is surrogate-, optimization-, and generator-free, while being human-interpretable.

•

Extensive experiments across diverse attacks, tasks, and data domains demonstrate the high effectiveness and low time cost of our method. Owing to its favorable natures, this method exhibits strong applicability.

•

This work reveals new properties of traditional image sharpening in the deep learning era, providing valuable insights to the machine learning community.

Related Work

2.1
Adversarial Attacks

Adversarial attacks deceive DNNs by carefully crafted perturbations. In the white-box setting, adversaries have full access to the model architecture and parameters, which allows gradient-based iterative attacks such as I-FGSM
(
24
)
to achieve strong performance. In the more practical black-box setting, adversaries have no access to the target model and thus rely on transfer-based or query-based attacks. Transfer-based attacks exploit the transferability of adversarial examples to launch attacks. To improve transferability, various strategies have been proposed, including gradient-based
(
10
;
40
)
, model-related
(
35
;
51
)
, transformation-based
(
29
;
16
)
, objective-optimization
(
56
;
33
)
, generative
(
38
;
27
)
, and ensemble-based
(
4
;
49
)
approaches. Query-based attacks iteratively refine perturbations by leveraging the feedback from the target model. Depending on the type of accessible outputs, they can be categorized into decision-based
(
52
)
and score-based attacks
(
42
)
.

2.2
Adversarial Defenses

In response to the growing threat posed by adversarial attacks, a variety of defense strategies have been developed. Depending on the deployment stage, these strategies can be classified as training-time defenses, post-attack defenses, and the recently introduced pre-attack defenses.

Training-time defenses aim to enhance model robustness during the training phase. Adversarial training
(
57
;
65
)
does so by incorporating adversarial examples into the training set. Robust architecture design
(
41
;
39
)
leverages module-level design strategies to mitigate perturbation effects. Robustness-oriented regularization
(
8
;
66
)
improves model robustness through specially designed regularization terms.

Post-attack defenses reduce adversarial threats by detecting or mitigating adversarial examples after attacks occur. Input purification defenses
(
61
;
25
)
adjust adversarial examples toward the benign data distribution via methods such as GANs and diffusion models. Transformation-based preprocessing
(
6
;
30
)
improves robustness by transforming inputs to disrupt the structure of adversarial perturbations. Adversarial detection defenses
(
32
;
23
)
prevent malicious access by detecting adversarial threats in the input. Output correction defenses
(
45
)
aggregate multiple prediction copies to produce more reliable outputs.

Recently, pre-attack defenses have been proposed to protect benign samples before attacks take place. In particular, preemptive robustification defenses apply small robustness-inducing perturbations to benign samples, such that adversarial examples generated from them are rendered ineffective. Unfortunately, only a limited number of studies have explored this intriguing direction. Bi-Level
(
36
)
preemptively robustifies natural images by solving a bi-level optimization problem to find nearby points that are robust to adversarial perturbations.
A^{5}

(
11
)
leverages automatic perturbation analysis to craft certified defensive perturbations against bounded attacks.

Despite the progress achieved by Bi-Level and
A^{5}
, their practical applicability remains limited due to their reliance on surrogate models, optimization-based or generator-based designs, and the lack of human interpretability. To address these limitations, this paper explores a new efficient preemptive robustification method that overcomes these drawbacks.

2.3
Image Sharpening

Historically, image sharpening has been investigated as an image enhancement technique with the goal of improving human perceptual image quality. Such approaches range from classical spatial-domain and frequency-domain filtering
(
13
)
to adaptive filtering
(
44
;
63
)
methods, and more recently, DNN-based sharpening techniques
(
60
)
.

In recent years, pioneering works have explored new applications of image sharpening in the deep learning era, such as using image sharpening as an augmentation strategy to enhance model generalization and robustness
(
19
;
17
;
21
)
. Moreover, image sharpening has been employed as a post-attack defense by transforming adversarial examples, demonstrating promising robustness gains
(
37
;
17
)
. These observations indicate that image sharpening retains unique value in deep learning security. In this paper, we rethink image sharpening from a pre-attack defense perspective and reveal its effects of preemptive robustification on benign images.

3.1
Threat Model

Given a benign sample-label set
S=\{(x,y)\}
and a target model
f_{t}
, under a perceptual metric
q
, we aim to find an efficient and surrogate-free preemptive robustification mapping
R(x)
rather than
R(x,f_{r})
that produces the robustified sample-label set
S^{r}=\{(x^{r},y)|(x,y)\in S~\text{and}~x^{r}=R(x)\}
. The goal is to maximize the classification accuracy of the target model
f_{t}
on the adversarial examples generated from
S^{r}
by an attack
A\in\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathcal{A}
denotes the set of attack algorithms, which is conducted on a surrogate
f_{s}
with a
\ell_{p}
-bounded perturbation budget
\epsilon^{a}
:

\displaystyle\max_{R}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\in S}\mathbb{I}\big(f_{t}(A_{\epsilon^{a}}(x^{r}))=y\big),

(1)

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function, with the perceptual constraint of a perceptual budget
\epsilon^{r}
:

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad q(x,x^{r})\leq\epsilon^{r},

(2)

and the runtime constraint with a time budget
\tau
:

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad T\big(R(x)\big)\leq\tau.

(3)

Specifically, the perturbation budget
\epsilon^{a}
is defined with respect to the attacked sample, i.e., the input to attack
A_{\epsilon^{a}}(\cdot)
.

Previous methods
(
36
;
11
)
typically are surrogate-dependent and do not incorporate the time constraint, which allows Equation
1
to be addressed by optimization-based approaches or generator-based training. However, when the surrogate-free requirement and the strict runtime constraint are imposed on robustification, solving Equation
1
via time-consuming methods becomes less practical. Accordingly, we relax Equation
1
by enforcing:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S}\big[\mathbb{I}\big(f_{t}(A_{\epsilon^{a}}(x^{r}))=y\big)-\mathbb{I}\big(f_{t}(A_{\epsilon^{a}}(x))=y\big)\big]\geq\gamma,

(4)

where
\gamma
controls an expected robustness accuracy margin.

3.2
Motivation

Since optimization- and generator-based methods inevitably incur high computational costs and rely on the surrogate for robustification to provide prior knowledge, they fail to satisfy practical requirements. Consequently, building upon prior investigations into the characteristics of robust benign samples, we attempt to establish an intuitive paradigm.

Insights from Prior Preemptive Robustification Methods.
Visualizations reveal that samples robustified by the optimization-based method Bi-Level
(
36
)
and the generator-based certified method
A^{5}

(
11
)
exhibit higher contrast and stronger textures compared to their original counterparts. This observation has been explicitly discussed in
A^{5}
. Motivated by this, we hypothesize that enhancing the texture of benign samples constitutes an important pathway toward robustification.

Evidence from the Image Attackability Measure.
Recently, evidence from the image attackability measure OTI
(
28
)
provides a more direct indication of the role of texture enhancement in robustification. Specifically, OTI demonstrates a positive correlation between the texture strength of a benign image and its resistance to perturbations. Images with stronger textures tend to produce adversarial examples that are more difficult to attack. This correlation is particularly pronounced when texture statistics are restricted to semantic regions. From this perspective, proactively enhancing the texture strength of benign images may reduce their susceptibility to adversarial attacks.

Taken together, these insights suggest a common conjecture:
effective robustification can be achieved solely by sharpening.
To examine this conjecture, we formally describe and explain how image sharpening enhances robustness in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
. Extensive experiments in Section
4
will empirically demonstrate this intriguing proposal.

3.3
Preemptive Robustification with Image Sharpening

To avoid employing neural architectures for image sharpening (as being generator-free and time-efficient are key constraints of our work, and to avoid uncontrollable variables arising from specific network designs), we base our study on Laplacian sharpening
(
13
)
, a classical technique widely used for its visually pleasing results.

This process is formalized as follows. Given a benign sample
x
, we convolve it with an 8-neighbor Laplacian operator
K_{\text{Laplacian}}^{8}

to obtain a texture map. The sharpened counterpart
x^{r}
is then constructed by adding this texture map to
x
, scaled by a sharpening strength coefficient
\alpha
(
\alpha>0
):

\displaystyle x^{r}=x+\alpha\cdot\big(K_{\text{Laplacian}}^{8}\ast x\big).

(5)

Remarkably, by using Equation
5
alone as the robustification mapping
R
, even with a small sharpening strength of
\alpha=0.25
(as shown in Figure
2
, where the visual quality is slightly enhanced and the sharpening is nearly imperceptible to humans), the sharpened sample set
S^{r}
achieves an average accuracy improvement of
14.0\%
under non-targeted black-box attacks with the perturbation budget
\epsilon^{a}=10/255
, compared to the unsharpened set
S
.

3.4
Explanations of the Effectiveness

In fact, this result can be theoretically explained by the texture dependence of neural networks in conjunction with decision boundary theory
(
48
;
18
)
. A large body of prior work
(
12
;
22
;
64
;
43
)
consistently shows that both convolutional neural networks and attention-based models rely heavily on texture features. Therefore, samples with stronger semantic textures are easier for the model to classify and thus lie farther from the decision boundary. In contrast, samples with weaker textures exhibit higher uncertainty, are more difficult for the model to recognize, and lie closer to the decision boundary.

As illustrated in Figure

, when a sample is close to the decision boundary, an adversary can easily push it across the boundary with a small perturbation
\epsilon^{a}
, leading to misclassification. However, when a sample is far from the decision boundary, achieving the same effect requires a substantially larger perturbation. Image sharpening essentially strengthens the semantic textures of benign samples, pushing them farther away from the decision boundary, and thus serves as a preemptive robustification process. Moreover, since enhancing semantic textures drives samples away from the decision boundaries of different models, image sharpening also performs well in defending against black-box attacks.

Conclusions and Limitations

This work first reveals that traditional image sharpening can serve as an efficient preemptive robustification defense, and demonstrates its strong effectiveness across various attacks, tasks, and datasets.
The proposed method is resource-efficient, such that even one CPU is sufficient to support its deployment, enabling its practical applications.
While this work focuses on the image modality, extending analogous approaches to other modalities such as audio and text remains an interesting direction for future research. Moreover, how to further optimize the sharpening operator to achieve stronger robustification is still an open problem.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of machine learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.
