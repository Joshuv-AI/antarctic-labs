Principal Typing for Intersection Types, Forty-Five Years Later
Daniele Pautasso, Simona Ronchi Della Rocca

Abstract
A type assignment system for λ \lambda -calculus enjoys the principal typing property if every typable term M M has a special typing, called principal, from which all typings for M M can be obtained via suitable operations. The existence of principal typings in various intersection type disciplines has long been established using both semantical and syntactical approaches. Historically, on the syntactical side, proofs of this property and the description of type inference (semi-)algorithms computing principal typings have been complicated by many subtle technicalities; the present work aims at providing a more accessible formulation. To this end, we revisit some classical notions, and identify three elementary operations (substitution, expansion, erasure) that allow to build any type derivation in a system characterizing head normalization. We then use such operations in the design of an inference semi-algorithm that computes the principal typing of all and only the strongly normalizing terms, thus contributing to a modern perspective on results originally proven more than 40 years ago.

Introduction

The notion of
principal type
was introduced by Hindley in
[
18
]
,
and further developed in
[
19
]
, in the setting of simple type assignment
for
\lambda
-calculus

1

Actually, to be more precise, for combinatory logic.
.
The principal type of a
\lambda
-term
M

is the most general type derivable for it,
in the sense that all types one can assign to
M
are obtained
from the principal one by means of substitution.
Hindley proved that all simply typable terms have a principal type,
and moreover that the (principal) typability problem is decidable:
in fact, simple type inference can be regarded as an instance of
Robinson’s classical unification problem
[
30
]
.
Ben-Yelles used these ideas in the design of his
principal type inference algorithm
[
6
]
,
which later provided the basis for the type assignment
procedure in the ML programming language developed by Damas and Milner
[
16
]
.

The existence of principal typings has also been investigated in various
intersection type
disciplines.
Intersection types, pioneered by Coppo and Dezani

[
14
]
, increase the typability power of simple types
by assigning
multiple
(traditionally, a
set
of) types to terms.
Intersection type systems can be tailored to characterize
semantical classes of
\lambda
-terms such as the (head, strongly) normalizing ones,
and to describe models of
\lambda
-calculus in a finitary way
[
5
]
.
Due to the tight connections between intersection typability
and term normalization, the typability problem in such systems is usually undecidable;
nonetheless Coppo, Dezani and Venneri introduced an intersection type system
which admits a notion similar to that of principal type
[
15
]
.
In this system, for each typable term
M
it is possible to identify a
principal pair

(\Gamma,A)

such that, for every derivable typing judgement
\Gamma^{\prime}\vdash M:A^{\prime}
,
the pair
(\Gamma^{\prime},A^{\prime})
can be obtained from
(\Gamma,A)

by means of substitution and a sequence of operations called
expansions
.

The need for this additional (and, in its original formulation, rather technical)
operation can be intuitively motivated by observing that,
in simple type assignment systems, all type derivations for a given term
share the same tree structure, and differ from each other only by the types occurring at their
nodes; on the contrary, in intersection type systems, derivations for the same term can differ both
in the previous sense
and
in the structure of the derivation.
Thus, substitution is used to match the types in the nodes of a derivation,
while expansion adjusts its tree structure:
starting from the derivation corresponding to
(\Gamma,A)
,
which exhibits a minimal structure,
expansions introduce additional subderivations,
so to obtain a more complex structure corresponding to
(\Gamma^{\prime},A^{\prime})
.
Summing up, expansion over pairs can be understood as the projection (on the conclusion)
of structural changes applied to the whole derivation.

The already mentioned
[
15
]
considers a system where
intersection is seen as a non-idempotent connective,
and types are
strict
in the sense of
[
3
]
,

i.e.
intersection is not allowed on the right-hand side of arrows.
Ronchi Della Rocca and Venneri extended the notion of principal pair to a system
where intersection is idempotent, types are not strict,
and they come with a preorder relation
[
32
]
.
The system describes a filter model of
\lambda
-calculus,
and the preorder captures functional inclusion between elements of the model;
in this setting, generating all pairs from the principal one
requires three operations, namely substitution,
expansion, and
rise
, which deals with the preorder relation.
Despite their differences, the systems of
[
15
]
and
[
32
]

have the same typability power,
i.e.
they both characterize head normalization (see
[
3
]
); thus,
to keep the presentation as simple as possible, in this work we opt for a strict system.

Both
[
15
,
32
]
achieve their goals
exploiting the fact that the systems of interest enjoy an approximation theorem: a term can be
assigned all and only the types that can be assigned to its approximants, where approximants are
normal forms in a
\lambda
-calculus extended with a constant
\bot
and associated reduction rules.
Ronchi Della Rocca later supplied the first type inference
semi-algorithm

computing the principal pair of all and only the strongly normalizing terms
[
31
]
.
Since then, multiple authors investigated the analogies
between
intersection type inference
and
term reduction

[
26
,
12
,
9
,
8
]
.

Intersection types have been primarily used for semantical purposes;
still, in search of more practical applications,
several restrictions for which the typability problem is decidable have been proposed.
Many such restrictions are based on the notion of
rank

[
23
]
, which intuitively measures
the nesting of the intersection connective inside types.
Remarkably, Kfoury and Wells showed that typability in all the finite
rank restrictions is decidable
[
21
]
. In order to prove this result in a purely syntactical
way, they reformulate the original notion of expansion and connect it to the structure of the
derivation, by enriching the syntax of types with the so-called
expansion variables
,
i.e.
pointers
to the subtypes that can be modified by expansions
[
22
,
11
,
13
]
.
In our work a similar role is played by a system of constraints that,
despite not being as flexible, avoids some of the bureaucracy needed to handle expansion variables.

Recent contributions that discuss principal typings in non-idempotent intersection
type systems include
[
7
]
,
[
10
]
and
[
2
]
.
Compared to these works, which deal with the topic in a somewhat marginal way,
we delve deeper into algorithmic aspects of intersection type inference.

Contributions.

In this paper we supply a syntactic approach to principal typing in intersection type systems.
First, we introduce
pseudo-derivations
, together
with three operations acting on them (substitution, expansion, erasure),
and prove that such notions are
correct and complete
w.r.t. typability
in a system characterizing head normalization,

i.e.
they can be used to build any type derivation.
Second, we employ these ingredients in the design of an inference
semi-algorithm

computing the principal typing of the input term, in a subsystem characterizing strong normalization.
We place our work as an hopefully more accessible formulation
of long-established results, with the aim of
providing a gentle introduction to principal typings
and explaining the basic operations needed to construct them.
To this end, we streamline some classical notions,
in particular that of expansion, putting additional emphasis
on the parallelisms between the operations performed by our algorithm and term reduction.

2
Preliminaries

\lambda
-calculus.

Terms and term contexts of
\lambda
-calculus
are generated by the following grammars:

M,N\ \ ::=\ \ x\mid\lambda x.M\mid MN\quad\quad\quad\quad{\tt C}\ \ ::=\ \ \square\mid\lambda x.{\tt C}\mid{\tt C}M\mid M{\tt C}

where
x
ranges over a countable set of term variables.
The abstraction
\lambda x.M
binds
x
in
M
; the writing
\textsf{FV}(M)
denotes the set of free variables of
M
.
We assume an
hygiene condition on variables: free and bound variables have
different names, and so do variables bound by different binders.
Given a context
{\tt C}
and a term
M
, the writing
{\tt C}[M]
denotes
the term obtained by replacing the single occurrence of the hole
\square
in

{\tt C}
by
M
, potentially capturing free variables of
M
.

The
\beta
-reduction
, denoted by
\rightarrow_{\beta}
, is the contextual closure of the rewriting rule:

(\lambda x.M)N\mapsto_{\beta}M[N/x]

where
M[N/x]
denotes the
capture-free
substitution of
x
by
N
in
M
.
A term of shape
(\lambda x.M)N
is called a
\beta
-redex.
Such
\beta
-redexes are partitioned into
\mathsf{I}
-redexes and
\mathsf{K}
-redexes,
depending on whether the variable
x
occurs free in
M
or not.
Accordingly, we refer to the non-erasing part and the erasing part of
\beta
-reduction as

\mathsf{I}
-reduction and
\mathsf{K}
-reduction.

Given a binary reduction relation
\rightarrow_{r}
, we denote its
transitive closure with
\rightarrow_{r}^{+}
, and its reflexive,
transitive closure with
\rightarrow_{r}^{*}
.
A term is in
r
-normal form
when it contains no

r
-redex; it is
r
-normalizing
if it can be reduced to a term in

r
-normal form; it is
strongly
r
-normalizing
if every
r
-reduction
sequence starting from it eventually stops.

Terms in
\beta
-normal form
and
head
\beta
-normal form

are respectively generated by the following grammars (note that any normal form is an head normal form):

K\ \ ::=\ \ \lambda x.K\ \mid\ xK_{1}\dots K_{n}\qquad\qquad H\ \ ::=\ \ \lambda x.H\ \mid\ xM_{1}\dots M_{n}\qquad\qquad(n\geq 0)

The notion of head normal form is the syntactical counterpart
of the notion of
solvability
for (call-by-name)
\lambda
-calculus,
a term
M
being solvable iff there is an
head-context

{\tt C}
of shape
(\lambda x_{1}\dots x_{n}.\square)M_{1}\dots M_{m}

such that
{\tt C}[M]
reduces to a fully determined result,
traditionally the identity,
i.e.

{\tt C}[M]\rightarrow_{\beta}^{*}\lambda x.x

[
33
]
.
Solvable terms can be regarded as the “meaningful” terms that
produce some information, even if they may not be normalizing.

Intersection Types.

Let us briefly recollect a
non-idempotent
intersection type assignment system,
based on the notion of
multiset
.
A multiset (notation
\mu,\nu
) is an unordered list of elements;
we write
|\mu|
for the cardinality of the multiset
\mu
,
and
\uplus

Definition 1
.

The set
\mathbf{T}
of

is defined by the following grammar:

\begin{array}[]{lrcl}\textsc{Intersection Types}&A,B&\ ::=&a\ \mid\ \mu\rightarrow A\\
\textsc{Multisets}&\mu,\nu&\ ::=&[A_{1},\dots,A_{n}]\quad(n\geq 0)\end{array}

where
a
ranges over a countable set
\mathbf{V}
of type variables.

A
type environment
is a total function from term variables to
multisets, such that only a finite number of variables is not mapped to the empty multiset.
Environments are ranged over by
\Gamma
,
\Delta
.
The domain of an environment is
\mathsf{dom}(\Gamma)=\{x\mid\Gamma(x)\not=[\,]\}
;
the union of environments is defined as

(\Gamma\uplus\Delta)(x)=\Gamma(x)\uplus\Delta(x)
;
it can be abbreviated
\Gamma,\Delta
when
\Gamma
and
\Delta
have disjoint domains,
and
\Gamma,x:\mu
is a special case of such notation.

\Gamma\setminus x
denotes the environment such that
(\Gamma\setminus x)(x)=[\,]

and
(\Gamma\setminus x)(y)=\Gamma(y)
for every

.
For convenience, the writing

is considered to be the same as

.

Theorem 3

(Subject Reduction/Expansion)
.

Let

. Then
\Gamma\vdash M:A
if and only if
\Gamma\vdash N:A
.

Theorem 4

(Characterization)
.

•

M

•

is

Remark 1
.

Neither subject reduction nor subject expansion hold in the strong system
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathsf{s}}
.
Absence of subject reduction is due to the relevance of the system,
and can be recovered by adding a weakening rule. Note that a weaker property holds:
if
M
is
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathsf{s}}
-typable and
M\rightarrow_{\beta}N
,
then
N
is
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathsf{s}}
-typable, possibly with a different typing.
For example, consider the derivation

.
Although

is not derivable, one can derive

Concerning subject expansion,
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathsf{s}}
-typability is preserved (possibly with a different typing)
only if one considers expansion w.r.t.
specific
notions of reduction, namely those
that preserve strong normalization; unrestricted
\beta
-reduction does not fall in this category,
because of the erasing
\mathsf{K}
-reduction steps. For example, consider

, where
\delta=(\lambda z.zz)
. Clearly

is
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathsf{s}}
-typable, but

(\lambda y.x)(\delta\delta)
is not, since
\delta\delta
can only be typed using the empty multiset.

To better reason about (non-idempotent) intersection type systems, and significantly reduce

.

Intersection pre-types (pre-types for brevity) are analogous to intersection

\sigma,\tau

|\sigma|

\begin{array}[]{lrcl}\textsc{Intersection Pre-types}&{\tt A},{\tt B}&\ ::=&{\tt a}\;\mid\;\sigma\rightarrow{\tt A}\\

{\tt a}
ranges over a countable set

of pre-type variables.
Remark that the distinction between pre-type variables (notation

) and type variables
(notation

3.3
Correctness and Completeness

In this section we prove that the notion of (minimal) pseudo-derivation, together with
expansion, erasure, and substitution operations, is
correct and complete
w.r.t. typability in system
{\mathcal{N}}
.
This guarantees that such basic ingredients are suitable
for the search for (principal) typings in the systems of interest.

In order to transform a pre-type into an actual intersection type,
we define the following two mutually recursive functions
whose domains are, respectively, pre-types and lists
(for simplicity, we use the symbol
\mathsf{m}
for both of them):

\mathsf{m}({\tt a})=a\qquad\quad\mathsf{m}(\sigma\rightarrow{\tt A})=\mathsf{m}(\sigma)\rightarrow\mathsf{m}({\tt A})\qquad\quad\mathsf{m}(\langle{\tt A}_{1},\dots,{\tt A}_{n}\rangle)=[\mathsf{m}({\tt A}_{1}),\dots,\mathsf{m}({\tt A}_{n})]

\mathsf{m}
is injective on pre-type variables.
The function
\mathsf{m}
is extended to pre-type environments and derivations in the standard way.

Using
\mathsf{m}

pseudo-derivation, one obtains an infinite family of derivations in system
{\mathcal{N}}

(Correctness)

Building upon the notion of expansion,

To uniquely identify the

.

motivates this design choice by stating that applying unification rules to the system

Formally proving this statement is surprisingly difficult,
and interestingly none of the works dealing with intersection typability as
an extended unification problem seems to acknowledge the
relevance of such a result, even when they implicitly rely on it.
An intuitive justification can be provided by comparing

Indeed, when checking for simple typability of a given term, circular equations may be generated;
this is essentially due to the strict conditions imposed
on type environments, which are required to agree on common variables.
In contrast, intersection type systems
impose no constraint on the union of environments, thus preventing circularities.

because expansions are used only when strictly necessary (
i.e.
to unblock a blocked equation),
and in a minimal way (
i.e.
without enlarging lists more than required).

4.2
Termination

Since
{\tt InferStrong}
is non-deterministic, we say that
the algorithm terminates if
there exists
a terminating execution path.
We now show that if
M
is strongly
\beta
-normalizing, then
{\tt InferStrong}(M)
terminates.
The key idea for proving this result is to relate the behaviour of the algorithm to the
reduction
of
M
;
this intuition can be strengthened by stating that
{\tt InferStrong}
essentially behaves as a (non-deterministic)
interpreter of
\lambda
-calculus, incrementally constructing a type for
M
by reducing it.

From this perspective, taking minimal pseudo-derivations as the starting
point is motivated by the fact that the minimal pseudo-derivation for
M
matches the structure
of the term,
i.e.
each subterm of
M
is the subject of exactly one subderivation.
During the execution of
{\tt InferStrong}
, the
structure of the pseudo-derivation evolves according to the replication of subterms
happening at reduction time: indeed, a blocked equation always originates from a
non-linear

redex that is created along a reduction sequence starting from
M
. Accordingly, normal
forms generate no blocked equation.

Lemma 26
.

If
{\tt PD}_{\mathsf{s}}(M)=(\Pi,E)
and
M
is in normal form,
then
E
is solvable.

Proof.

By induction on the normal form
M
.
We show that applying unification
rules to

cannot generate equations between
lists, hence cannot generate blocked equations.
Case
M=x
is trivial, as
E=\emptyset
. For the case
M=\lambda x.N
,
let
{\tt PD}_{\mathsf{s}}(N)=(\Pi_{0},E_{0})
; then

E=E_{0}\cup\{{\tt c}\doteq\sigma\rightarrow{\tt b}\}
where
{\tt c}
is fresh.
Since by
i.h.

E_{0}
does not generate equations between lists,
neither does
E
, because
{\tt c}
does not occur in
E_{0}
.
Lastly, consider

for

.
Let
{\tt PD}_{\mathsf{s}}(x)=(\Pi_{0},\emptyset)
where
\Pi_{0}\,\triangleright\,x:\langle{\tt a}_{0}\rangle\vdash_{\tt p}x:{\tt a}_{0}
,
and
{\tt PD}_{\mathsf{s}}(M_{i})=(\Pi_{i},E_{i})
where

E_{i}*E_{j}
whenever

.
Since by
i.h.
none of the

generates equations between lists,
neither does

.

4.3
Confluence

We proceed to show that, although the algorithm is non-deterministic, its output (if any) is unique.
Before delving into details, some considerations about our definition of expansion are in order.

Expansion and erasure operations, as introduced by
Definition

11
,
allow for a compact presentation, but are clearly suboptimal from an efficiency standpoint.
Indeed, it is possible to provide a more refined definition of expansion (resp. erasure),
that modifies a given
\mathsf{many}
rule by adding minimal premises
(resp. by deleting premises) in
specific
positions, instead of “starting over” from minimal subderivations only.
To reason about confluence properties of the algorithm,
it is convenient to assume this refined notion of expansion:
we do so in stating the following
Lemma

32
and
Theorem

33
.
This way we can rely on the fact that,
during execution of
{\tt InferStrong}
, the length of all lists is
non-decreasing
.

Lemma 32
.

Let
(\Pi,E)\Rightarrow(\Pi_{1},E_{1})
and
(\Pi,E)\Rightarrow(\Pi_{2},E_{2})
.
Then there is
(\Pi_{3},E_{3})

such that
(\Pi_{1},E_{1})\Rightarrow^{*}(\Pi_{3},E_{3})

and either
(\Pi_{2},E_{2})\Rightarrow(\Pi_{3},E_{3})
or
(\Pi_{2},E_{2})=(\Pi_{3},E_{3})
.

Proof.

See
Appendix

C
.
∎

Lemma

32
implies that the relation
\Rightarrow
is confluent,
hence that the output of
{\tt InferStrong}
is uniquely determined.
Given the similarities between our type inference procedure and term reduction,
this result can be seen as a natural consequence of the confluence properties of
\lambda
-calculus.

.

If
{\tt InferStrong}(M)=(\Pi,\psi)
and
{\tt InferStrong}(M)=(\Pi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})
,
then
(\Pi,\psi)=(\Pi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})
modulo renaming of pre-type variables.

Proof.

By
Lemma

32
and
[
20
, Lemma 2.5]
,
plus
9
of unification.
∎

4.4
Principality

Let us recall that the principal typing for a term
M

is a typing from which all other typings for
M
can be derived
via suitable operations.
The procedure
{\tt InferStrong}(M)
, if it terminates,
constructs the principal typing of the (strongly normalizing) term
M
,
in the following sense.

Theorem 34
.

Let
\Sigma\,\triangleright\,\Gamma\vdash_{\mathsf{s}}M:A
and
{\tt InferStrong}(M)=(\Pi,\psi)
.
Then
\Sigma=\mathsf{m}\circ\psi^{\prime}\circ\bar{\mathsf{e}}(\Pi)
for some sequence of expansions
\bar{\mathsf{e}}

and substitution
\psi^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

18
, every derivation
\Sigma
in system
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathsf{s}}

can be obtained from a suitable strong pseudo-derivation;
moreover, the same Lemma guarantees that any strong pseudo-derivation can be
built starting from
{\tt PD^{min}_{\mathsf{s}}}(M)
via a sequence of expansions.

{\tt InferStrong}
starts from
{\tt PD^{min}_{\mathsf{s}}}(M)
and, if it terminates,
by
Theorem

33
its output is uniquely determined.
Observe that the algorithm only performs expansions that are strictly required
to solve blocked equations, and does so in a
minimal
way (
i.e.
lists are never expanded more than needed).
We conclude that every
\Sigma
can be obtained from
\Pi
via a suitable sequence
\bar{\mathsf{e}}
of additional
(
i.e.
not strictly necessary) expansions.
∎

Remark 3
.

Theorem

34
does not speak about the relationships
between
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
. A formal analysis would
be quite technical and goes beyond the scope of this paper.
Here we only point out that
\mathsf{dom}(\psi)\subseteq\mathsf{dom}(\psi^{\prime})
,
as it is safe to assume
\mathsf{Var}(\Pi)\subseteq\mathsf{Var}(\bar{\mathsf{e}}(\Pi))
.
Of course, even for
{\tt a}\in\mathsf{dom}(\psi)\cap\mathsf{dom}(\psi^{\prime})
one may have
\psi({\tt a})\not=\psi^{\prime}({\tt a})
,
since in general lists in
\bar{\mathsf{e}}(\Pi)
are larger than those in
\Pi
.
Observe that if
\Sigma
shares the same (minimal) tree structure as
\Pi
, then

\Sigma=\mathsf{m}\circ\psi^{\prime}(\Pi)
where
\psi^{\prime}=\psi^{\prime\prime}\circ\psi
for some
\psi^{\prime\prime}
;
this is because
\psi
is the most general unifier of the set of equations associated to
\Pi
.

5
Concluding Remarks

In this paper we
design a type inference semi-algorithm for system
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathsf{s}}
,
computing the principal typing
of all and only the strongly
\beta
-normalizing terms
(
Corollary

31
and
Theorem

34
).
The procedure is based on essential notions that we prove correct and complete
w.r.t.
{\mathcal{N}}
-typability (Theorems
14
and
16
).
Although our results were already known, the methodology is new, and we leverage advancements
in intersection type theory made over the past decades to streamline some technical details.

It is possible to see our semi-algorithm
as a “blueprint” from which inference procedures for other type systems can be derived.
For example, it is natural to consider a variation of
{\tt InferStrong}
that,
making use of both expansion and erasure operations, constructs a derivation in system
{\mathcal{N}}

for all and only the
\beta
-normalizing terms.
We point out that the interest of such a variation
is somewhat limited, as
{\mathcal{N}}
also admits an alternative
(and conceptually simpler) type inference semi-algorithm:
first, reduce
M
to normal form
N
(if any, otherwise the procedure does not terminate);
second, build a derivation for
N
(easy by
Lemma

26
); third, build a derivation
for
M
via subject expansion (
Theorem

3
).
Of course this approach cannot be easily adapted to
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathsf{s}}
,
because subject expansion does not hold “on the nose” in strong systems (
Remark

1
).
Yet another variation on the theme yields the
uniform

intersection type inference algorithm of
[
28
]
,
which is nothing more than an
always terminating
specialization of
{\tt InferStrong}
.
Additional details can be found in the first author’s Ph.D. thesis
[
27
]
.

Let us conclude by spending a few words about type inference
in
idempotent
intersection type systems. We highlight that the approach presented here can be
applied also in an idempotent setting, as is. Indeed, when performing type inference, the most
general assumption one can make is that each copy of a subterm exhibits a different behaviour,

i.e.
must be assigned a different intersection type. Observe that we already followed this principle by
imposing disjointness conditions on pseudo-derivations (see
Definition

5
): replacing multisets by sets would have
no real impact, because set union behaves exactly as multiset union when sets are disjoint.

|\sigma^{\prime}|=|\tau|
,
and
\bar{\mathsf{e}}_{1}
is still applicable.
Therefore, starting from

,
the algorithm can build the same pseudo-derivation as before,

\Pi_{1}\Rightarrow\Pi_{3}

\Pi_{2}\Rightarrow^{*}\Pi_{3}
.
If, on the other hand,
\mathsf{e}_{1}

\tau

\Pi_{1}\Rightarrow\Pi_{3}

.

Now observe that, during
the whole execution of
{\tt InferStrong}(M)
, the scenario
|\tau|<|\sigma|
cannot happen: indeed, the length of
the list occurring in the major premise of an
\mathsf{app}
rule is always greater or equal
than the length of the list occurring in its minor premise.
To see why, note that this property holds for
the starting point of the algorithm, namely the minimal strong pseudo-derivation
{\tt PD^{min}_{\mathsf{s}}}(M)

(since
n=1
in all rules
\mathsf{many}
), and is preserved by expansion operations (recall that we assume a definition
of expansion such that the length of lists is non-decreasing).

•

Second, consider the case where,
in addition to
\mathsf{e}_{2}
, the algorithm can also perform
an expansion
\mathsf{e}_{1}
inside
\Sigma_{2}
.
One can check that
\mathsf{e}_{1}
does not modify
\sigma

\tau
,
hence
\mathsf{e}_{2}
can be applied to
\Pi_{1}=\mathsf{e}_{1}(\Pi)
.
Similarly, since
\mathsf{e}_{2}

\Sigma_{2}

\Pi_{2}=\mathsf{e}_{2}(\Pi)

Therefore
\mathsf{e}_{2}\circ\mathsf{e}_{1}(\Pi)=\mathsf{e}_{1}\circ\mathsf{e}_{2}(\Pi)=\Pi_{3}
,

i.e.

\Pi_{1}\Rightarrow\Pi_{3}
and
\Pi_{2}\Rightarrow\Pi_{3}
.
