1.0 Executive Summary
 This project aims to develop a new wireless high-temperature sensor network for real-time continuous
boiler condition monitoring in harsh environments. Such a wireless high-temperature sensor network enables
network-based automatic temperature sensing and data collection, which combined with artificial intelligent
(AI) algorithms allow the construction of smart boiler systems with boiling condition management and
optimization for significant energy-saving and reliability improvement.

2.0 Background
 Boilers and furnaces are extensively used virtually everywhere, from thermal power plants to home heating
systems. These systems consume the most significant amount of energy. Due to the enormous energy
consumption, optimization of the operations of the boilers and furnaces to improve fuel efficiency and reduce
energy consumption can lead to tremendous energy-saving and thus bring significant benefits to the Earth and
its environment. On can achieve such optimization through analyzing the real-time data collected during
operation (e.g., temperature, fuel feed rate, etc.) and applying self-learning algorithms to allow boilers to self-
adjust their operating parameters to achieve the highest efficient fuel economy in responding to the energy
demands.
 To achieve smart boiler and furnace systems, real-time and continuous monitoring of the temperatures of
boilers and furnaces are essential. Existing boiler temperature monitoring technologies include:
(1) Thermocouples
 Thermocouples consist of an electrical junction formed at the interface of two metals with different
chemical potentials. The temperature-dependent chemical potential difference provides a simple and efficient
approach for temperature measurement. Thermocouples are widely used in the high-temperature
measurement. However, the significant limitations are: (a) limited precision in temperature measurement,
where the voltage generated by the thermocouples can change with the purity of the metals, the reference
temperature as well as the uncertainties introduced by the alloy manufacturing; (b) material aging, which causes
the chemical potential shift and change the temperature reading.
(2) Resistance Temperature Detector (RTD)
 RTD is based on the resistance variation with temperatures of platinum, copper or nickel resistors, i.e. the
 𝜕𝑅
temperature coefficient of resistance 𝛼 = ~0.0038/℃. RTD thermometers are simple and give better
 𝑅𝜕𝑇
stability and accuracy than thermocouples, but they have a limited temperature range of < 600 C due to the
resistance changes induced by the surface impurity doping in high-temperature boilers. Besides, thermometers
have the self-heating error issues and a slower response time of a few seconds.
(3) Thermistors
 Thermistors are made from polycrystalline ceramics such as barium titanate (BaTiO3), which measure the
temperatures based on the resistance change of the polycrystalline ceramics. Thermistors can provide a much
higher sensitivity than RTDs, but they can only measure a limited temperature range up to 250 C, which is not
useful in high-temperature measurement in the 1,800 C regime.
(4) Microstrip patch antenna temperature sensors[1-3]
 Microstrip patch antennas are narrow-band antennas. The operating frequency depends on the dimensions
of the patch antenna. The temperature-induced dimensional change can cause a shift of the operating
frequency through both the substrate’s coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) and the dielectric constant
change. Therefore, by measuring the resonant frequency shift, one can measure the temperature. This approach
has the advantages of capable of performing simultaneous strain and temperature sensing with a relatively
simple structrure[1-3]. However, the Q value of a microstrip patch antenna depends on the loss tangent 𝛿 and
antenna radiation, which is not high, usually in the range of 100 -500[4]. Such relatively low Q values lead to

 Page 1 of 45

broad resonant spectral profiles, which limits the temperature measurement resolution. Besides, due to the
temperature-dependent material loss tangent 𝛿 variation, the patch antenna temperature sensors have a
limited temperature range of < 1,000 C.
(5) Surface acoustic wave (SAW) temperature sensors
 SAW temperature sensors[5, 6] employ interdigital transducers (IDTs) on piezoelectric substrates such as
lithium niobite (LiNbO3), lithium tantalate (LiTaO3), and lead zirconate titanate (PZT)[7]. The transducer can
convert acoustic waves to electrical signals and vice versa through the piezoelectric effect of the substrate
materials[6]. SAW temperature sensors can be operated wirelessly and passively by taking RF signals from an RF
antenna, which enables wireless temperature sensing[8, 9].
 The SAW temperature sensor can have a high Q of more than 8000[10], which gives a sharp spectrum for
high-resolution measurement. However, since various acoustic modes can be involved in the sensing, the
transmission spectra show different side modes, which also change with temperatures and thus complicate the
temperature measurement. Also, to our best knowledge, there is no report for temperature sensors with an
ultra-high sensing temperature of over 1,800 °C. This is primarily due to the difficulties of getting suitable high-
temperature piezoelectric materials[5, 6] and the high-temperature electrodes.

Step 2: Pattern the SiO2 layer using the photolithography and the wet-etching process
 (a) The SiO2 layer will be first spin-coated with an S1812 photoresist (PR) at a spin speed of 4000 rpm
 followed by soft-baking at 90C for 5 mins.
(b) Photolithography UV exposure using a photomask and develop the pattern in a developer.
(c) Wet-etching using buffer-oxide etchant (BOE) to leave the central SiO2 rectangular pattern.
Step 3: Deposite the metal film outside the central SiO2 rectangular pattern
(a) Photolithography define the PR pattern on the centralO2 rectangular pattern
(b) E-beam deposition of a thin tantalum (Ta) layer on the device and perform lift-off process to leave the
metal surrounding the central SiO2 rectangular pattern. Due to the high melting point (3,017°C) of the Ta
metal, the E-beam process needs to be extremely careful and needs to be closely monitored. A special pre-
machined slug Ta metal instead of pellets are required as the E-beam target for the metal deposition.
After the E-beam deposition, the sample is put into the Acetone to finish the lift-off process. This finishes the
sample A fabrication.
(c) Repeat Step 1 to 2 to get another SiC wafer (sample B) and deposit a whole layer of Ta through the E-beam
deposition process.
Step 4: Flip-chip bonding Sample A and Sample B to form the device sample
The flip-bond process will be performed at Harvard University CNS nanofabrication center using the Fintech flip-
chip bonder

(b) flip Sample B and align it with the Sample A

force.

21. Simultaneous Authentication of Equals (SAE) authentication process
 To reduce the eavesdropping attack, authentication processes must be implemented. The WSN employes
the SAE key exchange processes followed by the AES-128-GCM encryption and decryption process. The SAE key
exchange mechanism is also used in WPA3 (Wi-Fi Protected Access 3) to provide secure authentication. In SAE,
the two parties in the communication verify the keys used in the encryption and decryption process for the
communication. The keys include a private key and public key. The private key of a party (noted as Private KeyA)
is generated from the party’s password using a Harsh 256 function. The public key (noted as Public KeyA) of the
party is derived from the private key (Private KeyA). The Public KeyA is shared with the other party (B). Party B
will do the same private key and the public key generation process and share Public KeyB with the other party A.
The two parties combine their own private keys with the other’s public keys and check whether they match. If
they match, the SAE authentication process is finished. The key exchange for the encryption and decryption
processes is then successful. Python has libraries for the SAE authentication process.
 The hash 256 function can be iterated multiple times to increase the difficulty level to break the keys. Figure
41 shows the time it takes to generate the session key for various iterations.

 Figure 41, Simulated SAE performance at various iterations.
22. AES-128-GCM Encryption and Decryption process
 After the key exchange, the information is then encrypted using the keys. The AES (Advanced Encryption
Standard) is a symmetric key encryption algorithm. It is standardized by NIST using a 128-bit key. Other length
keys such as 192, and 256 can also be used. Such encryption provides high security. The estimated time to brute-
force attack WPA3 is calculated to be 10,790,283,070,806,013,952.00 years.
 In the AES-128-GCM Encryption process, the plaintext is first encrypted in blocks using AES in counter mode
(CTR), where each block is combined with a unique counter value. The combined data is then encrypted with the
AES key to produce the ciphertext. A unique value (nonce) is used during each encryption operation to ensure
that the encryption process will produce different encrypted texts for the same plain text. Using Galois mode, an
authentication tag is also generated at the same time. This ensures the integrity and authenticity of the

5.0 Conclusions and Recommendations
 In conclusion, we have successfully completed the tasks for this project and developed a wireless high
temperature sensor capable of measuring more than 1000 ˚C without needing any cable or a transparent
window. We also built a wireless sensor network with real-time data sharing and multi-platform data access, i.e.,
on-demand data request, http dashboard, smart phone APP. We also demonstrated low package loss and low
network latency and Implemented SAE data authentication, encryption and decryption processes, calculated the
time needed for the processes (a few ms). Additionally, we have successfully demonstrated data routing and
fault-tolerance data connection.
 This research has set up a solid technical foundation for high-temperature sensor network. It also enables
automatic high accuracy boiler data collection for subsequently data analysis using artificial intelligence (AI)
algorithms to achieve smart boiler systems with extremely high efficiency to achieve tremendous energy saving.
In addition to the boiler systems in power plants and home heating systems, the wireless high-temperature
sensor network can also find important applications in wireless non-destructive monitoring engines for
hypersonic vehicles and space aircraft. The project advances technology and student training in the following
areas:
 • High-temperature materials
 • Advanced manufacturing of integrated RF sensors
 • Wireless sensor network communication and cloud-computing with high security
 • AI-enabled smart systems for high efficiency
 The research also trained graduate students in science and technology, especially in the strategic areas in
improving the efficiency of fossil energy and related applications such as power plants, home heaters, and AI-
enabled smart boiler systems with significantly enhanced efficiency and reliability.

6.0 Lessons Learned
 During the project, we encountered several technical and programmatic challenges, including student
qualification requirements, fabrication constraints, and COVID-induced schedule pressures that required careful
task plan and prioritization. One key obstacle was aligning fabrication processes with test measurement inputs
to provide feedback to the device fabrication while maintaining technical rigor and meeting milestone deadlines.
We addressed these challenges by conducting careful project task planning, periodic technical reviews,
improving documentation clarity, and establishing more frequent communication checkpoints to proactively
resolve issues. When technical uncertainties arose, we performed focused studies and validation tests to reduce
the uncertainties prior to final confirmation. We also develop theoretical models to model the system
measurement results. We are confident about the results when the theoretical model matches our
measurement data.
 Through this process, we reinforced the importance of early risk identification, prioritized task management,
and open communication across teams. These lessons have strengthened our approach to planning, task
alignment, and proactive risk mitigation in subsequent phases of the project.
