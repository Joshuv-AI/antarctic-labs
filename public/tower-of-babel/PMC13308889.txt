Giant Tubulovillous Adenoma of the Small Bowel as an Incidental Finding
Authors: Farah Abuazzam, Bilind Ismail, Micheal Chammany, Hussien Haidari, Hassan Khanani, Sathya Jaganmohan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTubulovillous adenoma (TVA) is a benign epithelial neoplasm typically found in the colon and rectum. While duodenal TVA is uncommon, it poses a significant risk of malignant transformation. When present, duodenal TVA is usually small and often associated with genetic syndromes. We report a rare case of a 58-year-old man with chronic hepatitis B infection who was incidentally found to have a giant TVA of the small bowel. The patient was admitted for healthcare-associated pneumonia and uncomplicated cystitis when he was incidentally found to have a small intestine mass on imaging. Further workup demonstrated a large, circumferential polypoid mass extending from the second and third portions of the duodenum into the proximal jejunum. Endoscopic biopsy confirmed a TVA without a report of malignant transformation. Given the size and location of the lesion, surgical resection was initially planned. This case represents the largest duodenal TVA reported in literature highlighting the importance of early detection.

INTRODUCTION

Tubulovillous adenoma (TVA) is a benign epithelial neoplasm typically of the large intestine. This adenoma is considered advanced adenoma per the US Multi-Society Task Force on Colorectal Cancer due to its potential for malignancy transformation, especially if equal or larger than 10 mm in diameter or contains significant proportion of villous histological features.1,2

TVA of the duodenum is relatively rare unless in patients with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP).3 Moreover, these adenomas are usually small in size, the largest reported in literature is 8 cm.4,5 Morphologically, it presents as polypoid lesions within the duodenum. Histologically, it exhibits a frond-like projection of the mucosa with branching papillary structures.6 It has the potential to harbor dysplasia and can progress to adenocarcinoma if left untreated. The American Gastroenterological Association recommends endoscopic resection as the preferred treatment of this lesion.3

Moreover, multiple risk factors were studied may contribute to the development of advanced colorectal adenoma including chronic hepatitis B infection, potentially through mechanisms involving chronic inflammation and immune modulation. Duodenal TVA has not been specifically studied in the context of hepatitis B virus infection.7,8

We describe a case report of the largest TVA involving the entire second and third portions of the duodenum and extending to jejunum in a patient who has chronic hepatitis B infection.

CASE REPORT

A 58-year-old African American man, with a medical history of chronic hepatitis B infection on treatment and ischemic stroke with residual right sided weakness who presented from a nursing home complaining of shortness of breath and dysuria and admitted to the hospital as a case of healthcare-associated pneumonia and uncomplicated cystitis.

On presentation, he was febrile but hemodynamically stable. Laboratory studies were unremarkable including liver aminotransferases.

While hospitalized for intravenous antibiotics, chest computed tomography (CT) partially captured a mass in the duodenum while performing an abdominal CT which showed a small intestinal mass lesion. He denied any nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain or distension, or constipation. And he reported regular daily bowel movement.

His abdominal CT showed a large mass in the duodenum which extends into the jejunum measuring up to at least 12.5 × 6.9 cm, most compatible with malignancy possibly lymphoma (Figure 1). There was no previous imaging available for comparison.

Computed tomography imaging of the abdomen, (A and B) coronal views showing mass in the duodenum 12.5 * 6.9 cm, (C) cross-sectional view showing the mass in the Jejunum, most compatible with malignancy.

Carcinoembryonic antigen and CA 19-9 were within normal limits for laboratory references.

An upper endoscopy with a tissue biopsy was performed, revealing a large frond-like, circumferential, and polypoid mass starting in the second part of duodenum just distal to the ampulla and extending to proximal jejunum, measuring approximately about 25 cm in length (Figure 2). The lesion appeared causing luminal narrowing without obstruction. Histology was typical for tubulo-villous adenoma without evidence of carcinoma (Figure 3).

Endoscopic images showing (A) the adenoma originating just beyond the ampulla of Vater, (B) the adenoma in the second part of the duodenum, and (C) the adenoma in the third part of the duodenum.

Hematoxylin and eosin stain showing surface epithelium of the duodenum with dysplastic cytologic surface epithelial changes composed of nuclear enlargement, hyperchromasia, and pseudostratification. 40× (left) and 200× (right).

Given the size and extent of the lesion, the patient initially agreed to surgical resection and was transferred to a hepatobiliary referral center for further surgical evaluation. However, due to frailty and comorbidities, he subsequently declined operative intervention and was ultimately lost to follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Tumors within the duodenum are rare, despite the great length and surface area the small intestine makes up in comparison with the rest of the gastrointestinal tract. Being the longest part of the digestive system, the small intestine has a remarkably low rate for being an origin of neoplastic growth, accounting for only 2% of gastrointestinal cancers occurring here and only 0.4% of these tumors originating in the duodenum.9,10

TVA are precancerous polyps commonly originating from the colonic or rectal mucosa, typically detected via routine colonoscopy.11 TVAs make up about 10%–15% of polyps, with about 20%–25% of TVAs exhibiting malignant potential.12 Histologically, adenomas are classified as tubular, villous, or a combination of both. Tubular adenomas are smaller, tube-shaped outgrowths that tend to be less likely to develop into cancer, whereas villous adenomas are larger, shaggy, cauliflower-like growths with a higher risk of malignancy.11,13 TVAs combine features of both types, with more than 25% of their structure consisting of villous components. TVAs are more frequently observed in individuals over the age of 50, as well as in those who are obese, have inflammatory bowel disease, or use tobacco or alcohol.12 While the vast majority of TVAs occur within the distal colon, less than 1% of tumors originating from the duodenum are adenomas. However, the incidence of duodenal TVAs is increasing due to advancements in upper endoscopy. Duodenal adenomas typically arise sporadically or in association with genetic syndromes such as the Gardner syndrome, Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, or FAP.10

Although often discovered incidentally during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, as with our patient, enlarging duodenal TVAs can lead to a range of complications, including diarrhea, cholangitis, jaundice, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, pancreatitis, gastrointestinal bleeding, or obstruction within the small intestine or at the ampulla of Vater. Tumors at the ampulla of Vater carry a 15%–25% risk of malignant transformation.10,14,15 The risk of malignancy can be further assessed based on histopathology and adenoma size. Tubulovillous adenomas larger than 2 cm have a 46% chance of malignancy, while villous adenomas exceeding 2 cm carry a 53% risk of malignant transformation.16

Several studies have reported large duodenal adenomas in the literature. Fanning et al described successful endoscopic resection of giant nonampullary duodenal laterally spreading adenomas measuring up to 80 mm, among the largest lesions reported, further demonstrating the feasibility of advanced endoscopic management for select large duodenal adenomas, although histologic subtypes were not specifically characterized.5 In a multicenter series of 299 patients, Kanzaki et al reported sporadic nonampullary duodenal adenomas ranging in size from 2 to 60 mm.17 More recently, Hou et al described a 45 mm giant sporadic nonampullary duodenal TVA that was successfully treated using hybrid endoscopic submucosal dissection, representing the first reported case managed with this technique.18

The management of duodenal adenomas required a multimodal approach with a vital assessment of ampullary involvement. If ampullary involvement is established, endoscopic ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging is used to evaluate for intraductal extension of the adenoma into the main pancreatic or biliary ducts, which may necessitate surgical resection. By contrast, the lesions limited to the ampullary wall may be managed with endoscopic snare ampullectomy. Nonampullary lesions are managed using endoscopic techniques such as snare polypectomy or endoscopic mucosal resection. In patients with FAP, numerous small polyps ranging is frequently seen in the duodenum and may be ablated with Argon plasma coagulation, cryoablation, or heater probe therapy.3,5,17–19

In addition, when evaluating our patient's risk factors, it is important to consider whether his hepatitis B diagnosis may have contributed to the formation of his duodenal TVA. Patients with chronic viral hepatitis have been shown to have increased propensity of developing adenomas within the distal colon.7,8,20 While hepatitis B and hepatitis C have strong correlations with hepatocellular carcinoma, fewer studies have explored the potential association between hepatitis B and gastrointestinal adenomas, particularly in the small bowel. In a large Korean cohort, Jung et al found a statically significant association between HBsAg and colorectal neoplasia including adenomas.7 Additionally, Kim et al reported an independent association between colorectal adenomas and hepatitis B virus in a small single-center study.8 Given the often-insidious nature of small bowel neoplasms and the advancements in upper endoscopy, further research is warranted to investigate a possible link between hepatitis B and the development of duodenal adenomas. Such studies could also examine whether early treatment of hepatitis B could reduce the progression of duodenal adenomas and assess whether more proactive upper endoscopy screening might be beneficial for the early detection and management of these adenomas in patients with hepatitis B.

This case report presents the largest reported duodenal TVA in a 58-year-old man with chronic hepatitis B. Early endoscopic detection and intervention are crucial due to the malignancy risk associated with TVAs. Further research is needed to explore the link between hepatitis B and duodenal adenomas.
