Executive Summary
Electric forecasts are showing increasing volumes of large load interconnection requests to the
U.S. grid, particularly from data centers [1-7]. This report examines the role of the U.S. electric
distribution system in serving large load customers. For our purposes, we consider the electric
distribution system as encompassing the utility- or customer-owned substation and feeder
facilities that are dedicated solely to serving these large loads or may serve these loads in a
“mixed-use” configuration, along with traditional utility customers.

Development of a wide range of data centers, from small edge facilities to larger colocation sites
to larger-still hyperscale and exascale operations, is being forecast to accelerate across all 50
states. These data centers demand rapid service, high reliability, and large power capacity (i.e.,
multi-megawatt service requests), which presents challenges for utility infrastructure planning
and grid capabilities. Considerable attention has been paid to related concerns about generation
and transmission bottlenecks that these load increases may cause. As part of the greater focus to
address grid interconnection of data centers, this report evaluates the role of the U.S. distribution
grid in serving large loads, particularly focused on substation and feeder expansion and
interconnection processes.

In this report, we examine both existing and proposed U.S. data centers, including a variety of
sizes and types, but with a focus on connected capacity. We also analyze expected service
configurations and infrastructure needs and suggest potential acceleration strategies for grid
upgrades. The role of the distribution system to accommodate these data centers either on
existing or new, dedicated, or mixed-use feeders and substations is examined. We examine key
considerations, including targeted investment in distribution capacity, updated permitting
frameworks, and the use of advanced planning tools such as hosting capacity maps.

Our analysis of data center forecasts, and those under construction and planned [1-7, 9], shows
that data centers are being projected to add more than 155 gigawatts (GW) of new demand in the
coming years, more than tripling current operational capacity. Whereas earlier developments
were often smaller and connected to a mixed-use distribution system, the future pipeline is
dominated by large projects requesting 50 to 200 megawatts (MW) of service that will require
dedicated substations. To date, we estimate nearly 15 GW of small to medium-sized data centers
remain connected to mixed-use distribution feeders and substations. By 2030, we estimate an
additional 9 GW could be added to these mixed-use distribution systems for service, and another
130 GW could drive the need for dedicated facilities during the same period. These increases
will challenge the ability of distribution systems to expand rapidly enough to meet demand,
particularly in light of constrained transformer supply chains and multiyear substation
construction timelines. This highlights the role of the existing distribution system to provide
temporary capacity, referred to as “bridging capacity,” while greenfield dedicated distribution
infrastructure is built for new large load service.

Interconnection timelines are another area of potential challenge for both data center developers
and utilities. Data center construction and load ramps typically unfold within 12 to 24 months,
while utility reinforcements often take 3 to 5 years to plan, permit, and deliver. This mismatch
raises the risk of reactive, high-cost upgrades. Bridging strategies such as phased energization,

 v

temporary feeders, and on-site generation can help, but long-term coordination between utilities
and developers is essential. Reliability requirements for data centers also exceed normal grid
planning standards. Data center tiers, defined by the Uptime Institute, classify facilities based on
their redundancy and uptime: Tier I provides basic capacity, Tier II adds redundant components,
Tier III and IV facilities often require redundant feeders and fault-tolerant substation
configurations [15, 17]. When the grid cannot meet reliability thresholds, developers are instead
increasingly relying on site-specific front-of-meter and behind-the-meter reliability investments
(e.g., batteries and backup generation).

Improved transparency tools can assist with streamlining the interconnection process for large
loads. Hosting capacity maps, though originally designed for distributed generation
interconnection feeders and substations, can help developers identify where capacity to serve
additional load exists and steer requests toward feasible sites. To be effective for large loads,
these maps could be refined to show area load supporting capability, updated more frequently,
and extended to higher voltage levels. Doing so could reveal locations where multiple large
customers could share substations and costs required to meet higher redundancy criteria. In
parallel, environmental and community concerns, including cooling water requirements, diesel
backup generator emissions, noise from cooling systems, and land use will increasingly shape
project approvals and utility planning. The following are the key lessons learned from this
analysis:

 • Future demand is shifting in scale; although a greater proportion of earlier load growth
 was connected directly to the existing distribution system, upcoming projects are
 dominated by requests of 50 to 200 MW, which will require dedicated substations and
 greenfield distribution infrastructure.
 • Existing distribution systems and expansion continue to supply a significant portion of
 existing and projected load growth, with nearly 15 GW of small to medium-sized
 facilities connected today and another ~10 GW expected by 2030, stressing transformer
 supply chains and substation expansion and construction timelines.
 • Meeting the projected demand could require on the order of 3,000–4,000 new feeders
 nationally, alongside several thousand new substation transformers across 13–34.5 kV
 distribution classes. While many of these additions can be staged at existing substations,
 the majority of growth above ~50–75 MW will necessitate hundreds of new substations
 nationwide.
 • Timelines are fundamentally misaligned, as data center buildouts occur in 12 to 24
 months whereas utility reinforcements often take 3 to 5 years, creating pressure for
 reactive and costly upgrades unless bridging strategies are deployed.
 • Reliability expectations exceed typical planning standards, with Tier III and IV facilities’
 downtime requirements resulting in the need for dual feeders and fault-tolerant
 substations, often driving significant investment in site-specific reliability solutions.
 • Planning tools such as hosting capacity maps can provide transparency and could be
 adapted for large load intake and extended to higher voltages to identify feasible sites and
 opportunities for shared infrastructure.
 • Local environmental and community concerns, including water use, diesel emissions, and
 land siting, are increasingly shaping approvals and utility planning decisions.

In sum, the challenge is not only how much new load could be accommodated to meet forecasts
but how quickly, how reliably, and at what cost. Strategies for meeting these needs include
proactive planning, coordinated permitting, and structured interconnection frameworks that align
customer demands with utility upgrade schedules. While this report focuses on utilization of the
distribution system, the U.S. Department of Energy has recently launched several key initiatives
to address the increasing need and demand for load across all stages of the electric grid. This
includes Speed to Power 1, which helps accelerate the speed of large-scale grid infrastructure
projects on the generation and transmission systems. This concerted effort by the Department of
Energy is to meet the challenge of interconnecting large loads and keeping pace with data center
growth while continuing to ensure affordable, reliable, and secure energy.

1 Introduction
The U.S. power grid is being forecast to experience an increase in the connection requests from
large loads. Among these, data center demand is forecast to increase, with forecasts showing an
increase in the number of connection requests, and in the volume of individual large capacity
requests. These facilities, critical to supporting cloud computing, artificial intelligence (AI), and
global digital infrastructure, now consume an estimated 2% to 3% of total U.S. electricity, a
share that is forecast to rise significantly in the coming decade [1].

Historically, large loads were relatively predictable and geographically concentrated in industrial
hubs [2]. Large load growth forecasts are being driven by multiple factors, including data
centers, electric vehicle charging stations, onshoring of manufacturing, hydrogen production, and
advanced manufacturing facilities. These are emerging across all 50 states, often with little
advance notice and short-timeline requests for service [3], [4]. These facilities frequently request
electrical service in the range of 10 to 100 megawatts (MW), with some larger data centers
requesting interconnection at levels of 200 MW or more [1], [5].

This evolution in service application activity poses fundamental challenges to utilities and their
grid planners. Modern large loads can require accelerated interconnection timelines along with
high reliability and redundancy, which requires significant substation and feeder expansion, as
well as flexible and scalable infrastructure planning. Data centers, in particular, have become
emblematic of this shift because of their unique combination of rapid deployment schedules,
scale, reliability needs, and spatial distribution. The majority of capacity of new data center
requests now fall into the range of 10 to 100 MW, with some exceeding 200 MW or more [1],
[6]. These loads are materializing in areas with little historic precedent for such demand, placing
pressure on utilities’ distribution infrastructure and planning frameworks.

Recent forecasts highlight the magnitude of this trend. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory
estimates that total U.S. data center electricity demand could reach between 325 and 580
terawatt-hours (TWh) by 2028, translating to 74 to 132 gigawatts (GW) of load, assuming 50%
utilization rates. This represents an increase of 34 to 92 GW in data center load alone over the
next few years, equivalent to the total peak load of California and Texas combined [1]. For
reference, total U.S. electricity generation production in 2023 was about 4,178 TWh,
corresponding to an average load of roughly 477 GW, indicating projected data center growth
could represent approximately 7% to 20% of current national electricity demand [7], [8].

The challenges faced by the data center industry are many: resource and transmission capacity to
meet demand, restoring these loads after outage events, their response to grid disturbances,
uncertainty in load forecasting generated by speculative applications, balancing challenges under
large load ramps, evolving regulatory requirements, and limited permitting capacity [6].
Although we acknowledge the importance of these and other related topics, this report focuses
on understanding the implications of large data center loads on the U.S. distribution grid. We
leverage Baxtel’s dataset of more than 3,600 data center sites, for operational, under
construction, and proposed data center sites [9], and combine these with industry reports and
utility filings. Overall, we assess:

 • The scale and characteristics of data center growth

 1

 • The ways utilities determine service levels and interconnection pathways
 • Infrastructure and permitting challenges at the distribution level
 • Best practices and emerging solutions to accelerate infrastructure readiness.
Although data centers are the central case in this report, the insights presented are broadly
applicable to other rapidly emerging high-density loads such as hydrogen production, advanced
manufacturing, and electric vehicle charging stations. Reliability and design considerations may
differ across these applications, but the effects on the distribution grid are likely to be similar.
Together, they present new challenges for electric infrastructure planning, challenges that require
a shift from reactive to proactive approaches across planning, permitting, and grid investment to
reduce costs, accelerate connection timelines, and ensure reliability.

2 Data Center Landscape
U.S. data centers are expanding rapidly in number, scale, and complexity. Understanding the
diversity of data center types, their physical and operational characteristics, and their
corresponding redundancy needs is important for utilities and planners aiming to assess grid
infrastructure needs. This section introduces a functional categorization of data centers based on
demand ranges, analyzes near-term and long-term load growth forecasts, and introduces a new
national dataset compiled by Baxtel from various sources of more than 3,600 sites, including
existing and new sites that currently have load, are in construction, and are proposed [9] to
support locational planning and capacity analysis.

2.1 Functional Categories and Tier Classification
Data centers can be categorized by attributes such as size, type, workload, and reliability [6],
[10]. Size can lend insights into the nature of the facility. Smaller sites, measured by square
footage, are commonly termed “edge” facilities and typically support business operations.
“Enterprise” data centers tend to be larger but are still owned and operated by a single entity and
for their dedicated internal uses. “Colocation” data center facilities are bigger still, with operator
leasing server space to multiple tenants. At the largest end of the size spectrum are “hyperscale”
and, larger again, “exascale” data centers, built by companies that deploy internet services and
platforms at large scale [1].

Different workloads, which can result in different load shapes, are interspersed among these
sizes and types of data centers. User-driven or autonomously run processes such as AI training
can create highly variable demand, sometimes with flexibility to shift operations in ways that
benefit grid management. Other varieties of workload such as streaming and other on-demand
services that require continuous data flow typically produce comparatively steadier demand,
punctuated by occasional spikes.

Data centers are also classified into four tiers based on redundancy (see Section 2.4 for more
details) and uptime expectations [9]:

 • Tier I: Basic infrastructure, susceptible to outages
 • Tier II: Redundant power
 • Tier III: Concurrently maintainable with redundant paths
 • Tier IV: Fault-tolerant, fully isolated redundant systems.

Higher-tier data centers impose stricter requirements for redundancy, often necessitating dual
feeds and backup generation, impacting both the scale and cost of utility service [6]. Section 2.4
delves deeper into the operational paradigm of these tiers.

For this report, we classify data centers by their ultimate demand capacity, as shown in Figure 1.
This provides a clear framework for anticipating both their load characteristics and the
infrastructure required for interconnection. It is this capacity, and its aggregation in areas where
clustered development materializes, that most directly drives grid use and expansion decisions.
In practice, utility screening for new large loads generally follows a progression of increasing
cost and construction complexity. The utility will first assess available feeder(s) and substation

 3

capacity, which is usually the simplest and least costly solution. If insufficient, the next step is
often expanding local capacity by adding feeders or upgrading substation transformers. When
these measures are still inadequate, new substations with subtransmission or transmission
connections to supply them are considered. Each utility applies its own criteria, and factors such
as knowledge of future developments or desired grid configuration changes can prompt planners
to bypass simpler steps in favor of more comprehensive and expensive solutions.

2.2 Energy Usage Forecasts and Load Growth Drivers
Recent forecasts highlight the scale of expected growth in data center energy consumption. A
2024 study projects U.S. data centers could consume between 325 and 580 terawatt-hours
(TWh) annually by 2028, up from about 176 TWh in 2023 (roughly 4% to 5% of U.S. electricity
use). Assuming a 50% capacity utilization factor, this equates to 74 to 132 GW of demand,
representing an incremental increase of 34 to 92 GW from 2022 levels [1].

Complementing this, scenario-based projections from another study indicate energy consumption
growth rates ranging from ~3.7% to 15% annually, depending on adoption of AI workloads and
efficiency improvements. Under these scenarios, data center consumption could reach 4.6% to
9.1% of U.S. electricity use by 2030 [10].

 4

Together, these estimates frame a range of roughly 150 to 200 TWh of annual consumption in
the near term, with long-run scenarios showing substantial upside risk if AI-driven computing
expands more quickly than expected. This growth reflects both the increasing number of new
facilities and the rising per-site load intensity, driven by:

 • Accelerated AI model training and inference, which require high-performance Graphics
 Processing Units clusters
 • Higher-density server architectures, increasing cooling and power delivery demands
 • Continuous cooling and redundancy requirements, particularly for Tier III and IV
 facilities.
In parallel, operators and utilities are exploring measures to relieve pressure on the grid,
including:

 • On-site generation (e.g., natural gas, fuel cells, renewables with storage)
 • Advanced cooling technologies [11]
 • AI-optimized load management, enabling smarter scheduling and improved power
 utilization effectiveness [12].

2.4 Redundancy Requirements in Facility Architectures
Redundancy expectations, i.e., the ability to provide alternative delivery should components fail,
fundamentally shape how utilities size and configure service to data centers. When developers
approach utilities, their requests typically center on four priorities: site location, target service
date, full load demand, and expected load ramp. Meeting these needs can require a combination
of both interim and permanent solutions, which often are negotiated to balance utility system
integrity with customer requirements.

At the data center site, redundancy is achieved through power distribution modules that combine
uninterruptible power supplies (UPS), batteries, and backup generation. These are deployed
incrementally as a site grows through its load ramp schedule, ensuring critical loads such as
servers and cooling systems remain protected. The degree of redundancy is described by
the Uptime Institute’s Tier classification system, which establishes reliability expectations [15],
as described in Section 2.1.

The following figures (Figure 5, Figure 6, and Figure 7) show how power delivery modules can
be installed to provide site infrastructure redundancy that meets the requirements of each tier.
Notice that a medium-voltage transfer switch is shown between each utility connection to a site
medium-voltage/low-voltage transformer. This equipment is used very frequently when these
connections are made via underground cable to allow for maintenance and repair of utility
feeders, and it may be utility or customer owned.

In the following descriptions and diagrams, it may be helpful to consider “N” as the number of
paths available to supply power to critical loads, and “+1” as an indicator of redundancy that
exists to the path(s).

Tier I {N}: Load is unserved during maintenance and repairs within power distribution module
or the distribution path connecting it to the data hall, i.e., the facilities which contain server
cabinets and IT infrastructure.

Tier II {N+1}: Load is served during maintenance and repairs within power distribution module
but unserved during maintenance and repair of the single distribution path connecting it to the
data hall.

These tight thresholds illustrate the scale of reliability required. For Tier III and IV facilities,
even short outages are unacceptable, leading to requirements for dual feeders, redundant transfer

IV standards [11], [12], as these provide the balance between cost and near-continuous
availability.

Because most interruptions in utility service originate at the medium-voltage feeder level rather
than at substations or transmission lines, feeder reliability is a critical consideration. This has
driven significant investment in both on-site redundancy (diesel backup generation, batteries)
and grid-side enhancements. An important research question is whether reallocating some
backup investment toward distribution system resilience could deliver greater long-term
reliability to a greater number of utility customers along with environmental benefits.

Although not the focus of this report, substation bus configurations and switching schemes can
affect redundancy within the substation and therefore have an impact on reliability for customers
served by that substation. Although not directly coupled with the redundancy tiers, utilities or
customer-owners of substations may choose to adopt a certain substation design to add to area
reliability, balancing cost, space, and redundancy to support large loads.

3 Grid Interconnection Pathways
As the scale of data center load requests has grown, so too has the complexity of determining the
appropriate configuration of interconnection. Building on the premise that every data center will
be served through some configuration of a distribution system as we have described it, mixed-
use or dedicated, utility- or customer-owned substations and feeders, is shaped by technical,
economic, regulatory, and logistical factors. This section describes the thresholds, criteria, and
decision-making frameworks utilities may apply when evaluating how to interconnect large
loads.

3.1 Data Center Grid Connection Options
Utilities have several pathways to serve large load customers, and the choice of pathway depends
on the requested megawatt capacity, required reliability tier, proximity to existing infrastructure,
and project timelines. Figure 8 illustrates the common interconnection options previously
discussed for data centers and other large loads, ranging from relatively simple feeder
connections to dedicated substations. Common approaches include:

 1. Mixed-use feeder connections:
 At the smallest scale, facilities may be served from existing mixed-use feeders (i.e., those
 already feeding existing residential, commercial, and/or industrial customers). This
 option is generally the fastest to implement and least capital-intensive but is constrained
 by limited available capacity and may provide minimal redundancy.
 2. Dedicated feeder extensions:
 As loads increase, many utilities find the need to extend dedicated feeders from existing
 substations. This approach isolates the data center from other commercial and residential
 loads while avoiding the cost of a new substation, though its capacity remains limited by
 the ratings of the host transformer banks and the presence of new feeder positions.
 3. Substation transformer bank additions:
 Larger facilities often require the addition of a transformer bank within a substation. This
 strategy can enable a staged approach to capacity expansion aligned with data center
 buildout. However, such upgrades typically involve significant costs, as they may require
 additional land, civil construction, and reinforcement of the upstream transmission or
 subtransmission system.
 4. New (greenfield) substation construction:
 For hyperscale and exascale campuses, or clusters of large loads concentrated within a
 small geographic area, utilities often face a choice between expanding an existing
 substation by adding transformer banks or constructing a new greenfield substation.
 Adding a transformer bank can be a faster, incremental approach if adequate land and
 upstream capacity are available. In contrast, building a new substation can provide
 additional levels of redundancy and scalability (e.g., N+1 or 2N redundancy at
 subtransmission or transmission voltage levels) but comes with a substantial trade-off:
 with costs for new substations running into millions of dollars and permitting and
 construction timelines running 3 to 5 years.

 5. Colocated generation interconnections:
 In some cases, developers pursue colocated generation interconnections, particularly
 where rapid deployment is critical, or local grid capacity is constrained. On-site natural
 gas plants, fuel cells, or renewable generation coupled with storage can provide
 immediate capacity and bridge service until permanent grid upgrades are complete.

3.2 Criteria and Thresholds for Interconnections
Determining whether a large load such as a data center will be connected to existing distribution
facilities, will create the need to expand an existing substation with transformer additions and
new feeders, or will require an entirely new substation and possibly subtransmission- or
transmission-level (e.g., 69–138 kV) reinforcement depends on both the magnitude of the
requested load and local system conditions. Public documentation of precise utility large load
interconnection criteria is limited, but some general thresholds can be inferred from utility
filings, distributed generation standards, and engineering practice.

 13

Load magnitude is the primary factor: facilities below about 20 MW may be served on existing
or lightly modified, often shared, distribution feeders, whereas requests exceeding 50 to 75 MW
often trigger the need for substation expansions or dedicated substations. Between these ranges, a
mixture of existing and expanded facilities is frequently used to balance cost and capacity.
Reliability requirements, particularly for Tier III and IV facilities, can accelerate the transition to
more complex and expensive service solutions mainly because of the need for redundant and
dual feeder paths.

An example comes from Texas, where Senate Bill 6 (2025) directed the Public Utility
Commission to establish interconnection standards for large loads on the Electric Reliability
Council of Texas’s grid. The adopted rules require customers with peak demands exceeding 75
MW to interconnect at transmission voltage, ensuring adequate capacity and reducing the risk of
stranded distribution investments. This strategy would likely require a new substation dedicated
to serving the particular applicant’s load, when it is greater than 75 MW.

Nationally, the current data center fleet reflects this progression. We estimate that nearly 75% of
operational data center facilities, accounting for about one-third of total load, are served by
mixed-use distribution feeders and substations, demonstrating that many legacy facilities remain
relatively small. The development pipeline, however, is weighted toward much larger facilities.
Roughly one-third of proposed projects, representing about 10 GW of demand, could feasibly be
served from mixed-use distribution feeders and substations, but most future growth will require
substation expansions or entirely new substations. Most of this growth is frontloaded, with nearly
90% projected by 2030. Further detail on the assumptions, substation capacity methodologies,
feeder limits, and buildout estimates that underpin this analysis is provided in Appendix A.

The implications for utilities are substantial. Existing substations will be stressed by the need for
transformer additions and new feeders, even in areas where such expansions are technically
feasible. The pace and scale of new substation construction driven by hyperscale and exascale
campuses will test the limits of utility planning, permitting timelines, and equipment supply
chains, particularly for distribution substation power transformers. These constraints make the
alignment of data center development timelines with utility buildout schedules an increasingly
critical challenge.

3.3 Bridging and Flexible Interconnection Strategies
Data center construction timelines are often shorter than utility infrastructure expansion cycles.
Developers may expect to achieve full operations within 1 to 2 years, whereas the planning,
permitting, and construction of a new substation can take 3 to 5 years. To reconcile these
mismatched timelines, utilities and developers increasingly employ “bridging” solutions that
allow initial load service while permanent grid reinforcements are underway.

Bridging practices include staged feeder additions, the deployment of mobile substations or
temporary transformer banks, and coordinated ramp-up of server halls to align with incremental
capacity increases. In some cases, on-site generation is used as a temporary supplement, ensuring
service continuity until dedicated utility infrastructure is commissioned. The Electric Power
Research Institute’s DCFlex initiative is currently cataloging these types of load-flexibility
strategies to better integrate them into planning frameworks [21].

 14

Another emerging strategy is colocation with power generation. Hyperscale and exascale
facilities, in particular, are exploring direct connections to nearby power plants or developing
behind-the-meter resources to bypass utility upgrade bottlenecks. For example, Meta’s 200-MW
data center in Licking County, Ohio, will be powered by a newly built natural gas plant that
operates independently of the local utility grid [22]. Similarly, American Electric Power has
announced plans to deploy up to 1 GW of solid oxide fuel cells to supply large customers,
including data centers, during the years it will take to expand local grid capacity [23]. Although
these approaches provide near-term flexibility, they raise long-term questions around emissions,
regulatory treatment, and the division of reliability responsibilities between utilities and
customers.

3.4 Grid Reliability and Redundancy Considerations
Reliability expectations from Tier III and IV data centers significantly exceed typical distribution
system design standards. Most utilities design feeders and substations around industry-average
performance metrics such as the System Average Interruption Duration Index (SAIDI) and
System Average Interruption Frequency Index (SAIFI). According to U.S. Energy Information
Administration Form 861 data, the typical U.S. distribution customer experiences roughly 300 to
400 minutes of outage annually when major event days (MEDs) are included and still about 100
to 200 minutes annually when MEDs are excluded [24]. By contrast, Tier III data centers are
designed for 99.982% availability, equivalent to only about 1.6 hours of downtime per year,
while Tier IV facilities aim for 99.995% availability, or less than 30 minutes annually. This gap
illustrates why conventional distribution service, even under “normal” operating conditions, falls
short of meeting hyperscale reliability expectations.

To bridge this difference, Tier III and IV data centers require N+1 or even 2N redundancy, in
which no single failure can interrupt service. Achieving this level of reliability generally requires
site infrastructure like the power delivery modules described in Section 2.4. This level is
necessary to overcome the fact that service delivered over standard distribution infrastructure
leaves operators exposed to outage durations well above their design tolerances.

Figure 9 compares average U.S. distribution system reliability, measured using SAIDI from U.S.
Energy Information Administration Form 861 [24], with the uptime requirements of Tier III and
Tier IV data centers. Typical U.S. customers experience approximately 300 to 400 minutes of
outage per year when MEDs are included, and around 100 to 200 minutes when they are
excluded. In contrast, Tier III facilities are designed to tolerate no more than ~96 minutes of
downtime annually, whereas Tier IV standards target fewer than 30 minutes. Figure 9 illustrates
the magnitude of the reliability gap and underscores why fault-tolerant site infrastructure designs
are necessary to hyperscale campuses. Without such redundancy measures, conventional
distribution service is insufficient to meet high-tier reliability standards.

Weather-related risks amplify these challenges. Storms, floods, and other major weather events
can trigger simultaneous equipment failures or cause voltage excursions that compromise
sensitive computing loads. Utilities are responding by piloting substation designs that can scale
in parallel with phased campus development, while also evaluating nonwire alternatives such as
battery energy storage systems to provide localized ride-through during short interruptions.
Although backup diesel generation remains the most common customer-side solution, concerns
around emissions, permitting, and reliability responsibilities could drive greater interest in
alternatives such as battery energy storage systems or fuel cells.

Finally, cost allocation remains an open issue. Building infrastructure to support speculative load
and that may fail to prove “used and useful” is a risk-prone practice for an electric utility.
Furthermore, installing oversized or redundant infrastructure solely to meet the uptime needs of a
single class of customer raises regulatory considerations. Commissions and utilities may need to
weigh the balance between private investment, cost recovery, and broader system planning to
ensure both service and redundancy investments meet both customer-specific requirements and
system-wide reliability goals.

4 Distribution System Planning Processes and
  Considerations of Large Loads
As data centers continue to grow in size and geographic spread, their impacts on the electric
distribution system are becoming increasingly pronounced. Distribution infrastructure was
historically designed to serve small, dispersed loads. New feeder additions and substation
expansions would occur relatively slowly as needs evolved over time. Substations dedicated to
serving one customer have always existed, but until now the call for them was a relative rarity
and usually occurred with ample time for a utility to respond. This section evaluates where and
how data center growth is stressing the distribution system, highlights infrastructure expansion
requirements, and explores system-level implications for utilities.

4.1 Forecast of Distribution-Connected Loads
Although the largest hyperscale campuses typically interconnect by way of dedicated feeders
from a dedicated substation that is served at transmission voltage, a substantial portion of near-
term growth is expected to receive service from a shared, mixed-use distribution system,
especially where transmission headroom is limited or existing substations can be expanded.
Using Baxtel’s most recent national dataset of more than 3,600 sites (Section 2.3) [9], we
estimate today’s 2,309 operational data centers account for roughly 44 GW of aggregate demand.
The forward pipeline is considerably larger: 454 facilities under construction total about 23 GW,
and 779 planned facilities total about 132 GW, for nearly 156 GW of additional prospective
capacity. In other words, the pipeline is more than three times the current operational capacity,
and average per-site scale in the pipeline is markedly higher than in the existing fleet.

Applying the interconnection criteria from Section 3.2 (i.e., mixed-use distribution suitability
declining sharply above ~40 to 100 MW) suggests only a minority of pipeline megawatts will
remain distribution connected, even though many individual sites by count still fall under
distribution-size thresholds. Using those thresholds across the construction and planned
categories indicates approximately 9 to 10 GW of the pipeline could feasibly interconnect at
distribution voltages if realized as currently sited, with roughly 4 GW of that already represented
in projects under construction. The rest will require substation expansions or new dedicated
substations (and, for the largest campuses, transmission-level, dedicated substation
interconnections). Methodology notes and category-by-category calculations that underlie the
estimates here are provided in Appendix A.

Geography amplifies these pressures. Baxtel’s data in Figure 2 show pronounced clustering
in Northern Virginia, Central Ohio, and the Phoenix metro area, among others. In such clusters,
cumulative demand can quickly exceed feeder thermal limits and substation capacities, even
where incremental upgrades are possible. As a result, utilities will need to identify which fraction
of the projected mixed-use distribution-eligible load can be absorbed by existing substations and
where new feeders, transformer banks, or greenfield substations should be prioritized.
Repurposing hosting capacity maps and substation headroom assessments traditionally used for
distributed energy resource integration and load hosting capacity can help triage locations with
available capacity, reveal where expansion is most urgent, and reduce the risk of stranded
investments.

 17

In short, the latest market data imply a step-change in both the scale and concentration of large
loads. Even under optimistic assumptions about distribution headroom, utilities can plan
for selective interconnections to existing and expanded distribution facilities (~9 to 10
GW) alongside a large wave of substation additions and new builds to accommodate the bulk of
growth.

4.2 Substation and Feeder Impacts
Substations serving large data center loads may need to increasingly consider and design
for modularity, scalability, and redundancy. Utilities are moving toward flexible designs that
allow capacity to be added in phases, aligning with the staged buildout of data center campuses.
A prominent, verified case is Dominion Energy Virginia’s Edsall Substation project, intended to
serve an approximately 176-MW data center campus in Fairfax County. The project includes
construction of a new 230 kV step down to 34.5 kV substation and extension of two 230-kV
transmission lines into the site [25]. The project is expected to enter service in October 2027.
This project illustrates the scale of infrastructure required when a data center load exceeds
distribution-only thresholds and requires transmission-level support as well.

Across the United States, key infrastructure needs associated with mixed-use distribution-
connected data centers include:

 • New greenfield substations in high-density zones where cumulative demand exceeds
 existing capacity.
 • Expansion of feeders and transformer banks at existing substations to provide
 incremental service.
 • Modernization of switching infrastructure and protective relaying to preserve power
 quality and reliability under high-load conditions.
Where permanent infrastructure cannot be deployed fast enough to match data center timelines,
utilities have adopted “bridge-to-wires” solutions such as mobile substations, temporary
transformer pads, and interim feeders. These solutions provide short-term service during initial
load ramp-up but are not substitutes for long-term grid reinforcements.

Planning studies and anecdotal evidence suggest many urban and suburban substations are
already nearing saturation. Loudoun County, Virginia, often referred to as “Data Center Alley,”
is the most prominent case, where substations such as Waxpool (230/34.5 kV, 168 MVA) and
Enterprise (240/34.5 kV, 252 MVA) are already heavily used [9], with multiple transformer
banks and feeders dedicated primarily to data center service. Similar conditions are reported in
Columbus, Ohio, and Phoenix, Arizona, where rapid load growth is testing system headroom.

High-level estimates presented in Appendix A indicate the scale of upcoming needs. Meeting
projected pipeline demand could require approximately 3,000 to 4,000 new feeders nationally,
alongside several thousand new substation transformers across 13- to 34.5-kV distribution
classes. Although many of these additions can be staged at existing substations, the majority of
growth above approximately 50 to 75 MW will necessitate hundreds of new
substations nationwide, particularly in clustered regions. These figures underscore the
importance of utilities engaging in proactive siting and interconnection strategies to ensure
adequate capacity.

 18

Hosting capacity maps (HCMs) and substation load reports provide one mechanism to quantify
available headroom and prioritize upgrades. Although originally developed for distributed
generation integration, these tools can be adapted for large load planning to identify where spare
capacity exists, where expansion is most urgent, and whether expansion or new construction
offers the most cost-effective path. Coupled with locational demand forecasts, such analyses can
inform “no regrets” investment strategies that balance near-term interconnection needs with
long-term grid resilience.

At the same time, utilities face practical constraints. Transformer supply chain
bottlenecks and multiyear construction timelines for new substations are already significant
challenges [26], [27]. These constraints highlight the importance of early coordination between
utilities, regulators, and data center developers to align infrastructure buildout with the
accelerated pace of demand growth.

4.3 Power Quality, and Operational Challenges
Beyond raw capacity needs, large data centers also introduce operational challenges that affect
protection coordination, voltage regulation, and overall distribution system performance.
Protection schemes have to consider high fault currents associated with redundant feeders and
transformer banks, as well as the large inrush currents from UPS and cooling equipment. These
factors can complicate the coordination of relays and circuit breakers, requiring utilities to
upgrade protective devices and refine settings to ensure faults are isolated without unnecessary
upstream outages. In areas with multiple clustered facilities, cumulative impacts on short-circuit
duty can further stress existing breaker and relay capabilities.

Concerns have also arisen at the transmission system level. A recent NERC incident review
provides a concrete example of how these challenges manifest at scale. On July 10, 2024, a
lightning arrestor failure on a 230-kV transmission line in the Eastern Interconnection triggered
multiple automatic reclosure attempts over ~82 seconds. During the resulting voltage
depressions, approximately 1,500 MW of voltage-sensitive data center–type load dropped offline
not because of utility breakers, but because customer protections and UPS systems disconnected
from the grid, as they were designed to do. The largest portion of this load loss (approximately
1,260 MW) occurred during the third reclosing attempt, and much of the affected load remained
disconnected for hours. The sudden disconnection produced localized overfrequency and
overvoltage conditions, requiring operators to switch capacitor banks and take other corrective
actions to stabilize the system [28]. This event underscores how sensitive computing loads,
combined with UPS ride-through and protection settings, can amplify grid disturbances rather
than mitigating them.

Recognizing these risks, NERC has begun gathering information on how data centers behave
during faults and restoration. Its Data Center Information Collection Questionnaire points to
concerns about how clustered sites may affect system inertia, restoration sequencing, and ride-
through performance in future events [29]. These efforts highlight the importance of developing
more standardized interconnection requirements and ensuring data center load behavior is
incorporated into planning and operating models.

Power quality is another pressing concern. Data centers depend on sensitive information
technology equipment that requires tight voltage tolerances, often more stringent than typical

 19

commercial service standards. Rapid load swings from server halls and cooling systems can
cause voltage flicker, whereas extensive use of power electronic interfaces (rectifiers, UPS
systems, and variable-frequency drives) introduces harmonics that propagate to distribution
feeders. Utilities may need to deploy capacitor banks, dynamic voltage regulators, or harmonic
filters at substations to maintain voltage stability and comply with Institute of Electrical and
Electronics Engineers 519 distortion limits [30].

Together, these operational challenges highlight that utilities can consider not only how much
capacity can be delivered but also how reliably that power can be supplied to meet the unique
operational characteristics of large data center loads.

5 Permitting and Interconnection Process
The rapid growth of large loads such as data centers, advanced manufacturing, and electrified
transportation has significantly increased the volume and complexity of interconnection requests
to U.S. utilities. These requests, ranging from more than 2-MW edge facilities to hyperscale
campuses exceeding 100 MW, challenge existing distribution planning processes and require
new approaches to permitting, transparency, and capacity expansion.

5.1 Hosting Capacity and Grid Transparency Tools
As interconnection requests from large loads accelerate, early visibility into available grid
capacity has become essential. HCMs are emerging as one of the most promising transparency
tools. Originally developed to assess feeder-level headroom for distributed energy resources,
several utilities including Xcel Energy and Con Edison are now piloting variants that identify
import capacity for large loads.

For developers, these maps provide critical siting intelligence by highlighting feeders and
substations with spare capacity, reducing the likelihood of speculative applications and failed
interconnection attempts. For utilities, integrating HCMs into interconnection portals helps direct
requests toward feasible locations, shortens study cycles, and improves coordination between
customer timelines and planned grid upgrades.

However, most current maps are still designed for generation export rather than large load intake.
They may not fully capture the thermal, protection, and voltage impacts of 10-MW to 100+-MW
service requests, and infrequent data updates limit their usefulness for fast-moving projects.
Approaches also vary widely across jurisdictions, complicating regional-scale development.

With greater standardization, HCMs could evolve into an important tool for large load planning,
although they will not replace the need for detailed interconnection studies. When paired with
locational demand forecasts for data centers, electric vehicle hubs, and industrial clusters, they
would enable utilities to identify “no regrets” upgrades and align investment decisions with areas
of most urgent growth [13].

5.2 Expansion Timeline Consideration
Even when the grid has technical capacity, timelines for equipment procurement and permitting
often clash with the rapid pace of large load development. Transformers are a primary
bottleneck: lead times for distribution units can extend from 18 to 24 months, and substation
power transformers may require 3 to 5 years from order to installation. By contrast, most data
centers and advanced manufacturing sites are designed and built within 12 to 24 months, creating
a persistent timing mismatch.

Permitting adds further complexity. Large load projects navigate local zoning, land use, and
environmental approvals, often in parallel with utility planning for water, cooling, and
communications infrastructure. These processes rarely align neatly with utility construction
schedules, and delays at any stage can create cascading impacts.

To bridge this gap, utilities are adopting modular and incremental approaches, such as phased
substation buildouts, mobile substations, temporary transformer pads, and interim feeders. These

 21

measures allow facilities to begin operating while permanent reinforcements are underway, but
they are temporary stopgaps rather than substitutes for full-scale upgrades.

Aligning utility infrastructure life cycles with the much faster deployment timelines of large
loads requires early coordination, transparent siting data, and in some cases, flexibility from both
parties. Utilities can sequence expansions so that existing headroom supports initial load ramps,
while customers may adapt workloads or deploy on-site generation and storage to ease the
transition until full capacity is available.

5.3 Illustrative Process for Large Load Interconnection
Given the challenges presented in the previous sections, utilities could move toward a
more structured, collaborative interconnection process for large loads, shown in Figure 10. The
structured high-level process overview provides a common language for utilities, developers, and
regulators. It ensures aggressive customer timelines, system reliability, and investment efficiency
are addressed together, rather than in conflict. As demand from data centers, electric vehicle
hubs, and industrial clusters accelerates, such a repeatable process will be essential to keep
projects on track while safeguarding grid stability.

Among many critical decisions that the developer of a large load will make, the following five
aspects of a project are of primary importance to the utility when the interconnection request
enters its queue:

 • Site location
 • Target service date
 • Full load and profile

 • Ramp schedule

From this information, utilities evaluate the state of nearby facilities, including feeder
configuration, loading, and any scheduled reinforcements to determine the capacity and voltage
levels that can be offered. The project’s load profile and Tier requirements then define the
service configuration. For example, a 40-MW Tier II facility may require twice as many feeders
as a Tier I facility of the same size, and a site that could be supported by two 34.5-kV feeders
may instead require six 13.2-kV feeders if only lower-voltage infrastructure is available.

The utility’s screening process follows a natural progression: first assessing existing feeder and
substation headroom, then considering transformer additions or feeder expansions, and finally
turning to new substations or transmission supply when needed. Figure 10 illustrates how these
elements come together in a structured, repeatable process. These outcomes correspond to
the Scenarios A, B, and C described earlier in Figure 8, although in practice the distinction
between mixed-use and dedicated facilities depends on local conditions and planning
preferences.

Because customer load ramps and utility construction timelines rarely align, ongoing dialogue is
essential. Figure 10 highlights this tension: customer demand accelerates quickly, while utility
reinforcements can take years to permit and build. Flexibility on both sides is therefore critical.
Utilities can sequence upgrades so that existing headroom supports initial operations until
expanded capacity or new substations are in place. Customers can adapt workloads, adjust ramp
schedules, or use on-site generation and storage to smooth demand until permanent service is
available.

This process also incorporates bridging measures such as phased energization, temporary
feeders, or mobile substations, which allow facilities to begin operating before full infrastructure
is complete. Subsequent steps involve finalizing upgrades, estimating lead times, allocating costs
and responsibilities, and producing an interconnection strategy package that aligns permits,
construction schedules, and customer milestones.

By making these decision points transparent, the process demonstrates how utilities can move
from an initial request to a selected configuration in a structured and repeatable way. This
approach balances customer timelines with grid reliability, reduces speculative requests, and
helps avoid stranded investments. Utilities that integrate capacity forecasting tools, transparent
siting data, and modular infrastructure design will be better positioned to manage the surge in
large load interconnection requests.

6 Conclusion
The rapid expansion of data centers and other large loads represents a structural shift in how U.S.
distribution and transmission systems are planned and operated. Although the current operational
fleet accounts for roughly 44 GW of demand, projects under construction and in the pipeline
represent more than 150 GW of additional capacity [9], an unprecedented increase that will test
the limits of distribution infrastructure.

The analysis presented in this report highlights several key takeaways. First, although the very
largest campuses will connect by way of dedicated substations to the transmission system, a
significant portion of near-term growth of approximately 9 to 10 GW will interconnect at the
mixed-use distribution level. This will accelerate saturation of feeder and substation headroom in
many regions, requiring rapid expansion of transformer banks, feeders, and new substations and
exacerbating already constrained supply chains for equipment such as transformers. Meeting the
projected demand could require on the order of 3,000–4,000 new feeders nationally, alongside
several thousand new substation transformers across 13–34.5 kV distribution classes. While
many of these additions can be staged at existing substations, the majority of growth above ~50–
75 MW will necessitate hundreds of new substations nationwide.

Second, reliability and operational expectations from Tier III and IV facilities exceed traditional
distribution planning standards. Meeting these requirements demands modularly expandable
substation designs, redundant feeders, and advanced switching, while also addressing power
quality challenges such as harmonics, flicker, and voltage excursions. Events such as the July
2024 NERC incident illustrate how clustered, voltage-sensitive loads can amplify grid
disturbances if interconnection standards are not strengthened.

Third, permitting and interconnection processes can evolve to keep pace with customer
timelines. HCMs and related transparency tools can reduce speculative requests and align siting
with available headroom, but they could be refined for large load intake. Equipment supply chain
bottlenecks and multiyear construction timelines highlight the importance of early coordination.
The high-level interconnection process presented here integrating customer inputs, utility
screenings, decision paths, and alignment of load ramps with upgrade schedules highlights areas
in the interconnection processes in which developers and utilities can come together to find
creative solutions that balance customer needs with system reliability and investment efficiency.

Finally, the path forward requires closer collaboration among utilities, developers, and
regulators. Proactive use of transparency tools, standardized interconnection criteria, and
modular infrastructure strategies can reduce bottlenecks and avoid stranded assets. Equally
important, regulatory treatment of cost allocation and redundancy can evolve to balance
customer-specific requirements with system-wide benefits. In parallel, consideration of
community and environmental impacts such as land use, emissions, and local infrastructure
strain will be critical to ensure large load growth aligns with broader public goals.

In short, the growth of large loads is not only a challenge but also an economic opportunity, and
by adapting planning processes, enhancing transparency, and aligning investments with demand,
utilities can ensure this wave of development strengthens the nation’s electric distribution grid.
The findings in this report complement other key initiatives the U.S. Department of Energy has

 24

recently launched to address load demand on the transmission and generations systems, such as
Speed to Power 2, which seeks to help accelerate the pace of large-scale grid infrastructure
projects needed for large load and data center growth. These efforts are focusing on meeting load
growth while ensuring affordable, reliable, and secure energy.
