Comparative accuracy of mean pulmonary artery pressure estimation using transthoracic echocardiography versus phase-contrast MRI in healthy adults
Authors: Shan Liu, Jun-Jie Chen, Ming-Xing Sun, Li-Ping Zhang, Jing Xu, Yi-Fan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-invasive assessment of mean pulmonary artery pressure (MPAP) is crucial for clinical screening and longitudinal follow-up of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) acts as the initial non-invasive modality, though it presents limited reliability in MPAP estimation. Phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (PC-MRI) allows direct and quantitative hemodynamic measurement, yet its comparative accuracy in healthy adults remains insufficiently explored. This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of MPAP estimation in healthy adults using TTE compared to PC-MRI, with relevance to non-invasive assessment in settings such as pulmonary hypertension.MethodsTwenty healthy volunteers underwent imaging with 3.0 Tesla PC-MRI to assess the main pulmonary artery (MPA) cross-sectional area (CSA) and forward peak velocity. MPAP was calculated using a previously validated formula. Within 48 hours, the same participants underwent TTE to obtain heart rate-adjusted pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAAT), from which MPAP was derived. The reference standard for MPAP was based on values obtained via right heart catheterization (RHC) in healthy adults. Agreement was defined as estimated MPAP falling within the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the RHC reference values. Between-modality differences were assessed using a paired t-test, with a two-tailed P value <0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsAll imaging studies were of diagnostic quality. MPAP estimated using PC-MRI (14.08±2.95 mmHg) fell within the 95% CI of the reference standard for all participant (100%). In contrast, TTE-derived MPAP (6.70±12.89 mmHg) demonstrated agreement in only 9 participants (45%), with 6 participants (30%) exhibiting non-physiological negative values. The difference in estimation agreement with established reference ranges between the two modalities was statistically significant (P<0.01). Mean deviation from the reference mean (14.0 mmHg) was +0.08 mmHg for PC-MRI and −7.30 mmHg for TTE. Bland-Altman analysis showed a mean bias of 7.38 mmHg (95% CI: 1.28 to 13.48 mmHg) with wide limits of agreement (−13.76 to 28.52 mmHg), indicating poor interchangeability.ConclusionsPC-MRI demonstrated superior agreement with established reference ranges and reproducibility compared to TTE for non-invasive MPAP measurement in healthy adults. The TTE-based PAAT method exhibited limited reliability in this population, frequently producing implausible results. PC-MRI may serve as a reliable non-invasive modality for quantitative evaluation of pulmonary artery pressure.

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive and life-threatening disorder characterized by structural and functional remodeling of the pulmonary vasculature. This remodeling leads to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance and subsequent right ventricular failure. According to the 2022 European Society of Cardiology and European Respiratory Society guidelines, PAH is hemodynamically defined as a resting mean pulmonary artery pressure (MPAP) >20 mmHg, a pulmonary artery wedge pressure ≤15 mmHg, and a pulmonary vascular resistance >2 Wood units (1). This revised definition reflects an emphasis on facilitating earlier detection and intervention.

Although right heart catheterization (RHC) remains the diagnostic gold standard—owing to its direct and precise hemodynamic measurements—its invasive nature limits its feasibility for routine screening and longitudinal follow-up (2). Consequently, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is widely employed as the initial non-invasive modality for assessing pulmonary hemodynamics. However, the reliability of TTE in estimating MPAP is inconsistent, often influenced by suboptimal acoustic windows and the requirement for adequate tricuspid regurgitant flow for Doppler-based calculations (3). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has increasingly been recognized as a valuable, non-invasive tool for the assessment of pulmonary circulation and right ventricular function (4). Specifically, phase-contrast MRI (PC-MRI) enables quantitative measurement of blood flow velocity and vessel cross-sectional area (CSA), thereby offering integrated anatomic and functional assessment (5). Although prior studies have demonstrated the feasibility of PC-MRI-based MPAP estimation, direct comparative data assessing its consistency with established reference ranges relative to TTE in a healthy population remain limited. The objective of the present study was to compare the agreement of MPAP estimation by TTE and PC-MRI in healthy adults, with previously established RHC-derived reference values. This comparison aims to assess the potential utility of each modality for early, non-invasive detection of pulmonary vascular pathology. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2630/rc).

Methods

Study population

A total of 22 healthy adult volunteers were prospectively recruited for this study. Data for this study were sourced from the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (Yinchuan, China). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (approval No. KYLL-2026-0079). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Comprehensive screening was conducted to exclude underlying cardiopulmonary pathology. This included a detailed medical history, blood tests, chest radiography, electrocardiography (ECG), and TTE. Participants were also evaluated for any contraindications to MRI.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) absence of known cardiovascular, pulmonary, or neurological disease; (II) normal findings on chest radiography and ECG; (III) normal cardiac structure and function as assessed by TTE; and (IV) normal pulmonary function and sinus rhythm.

Exclusion criteria were: (I) presence of hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg), dyslipidemia, or diabetes mellitus; (II) any acute illness within 15 days preceding the examination; and (III) contraindications to MRI.

Two individuals were excluded from the study due to previously undiagnosed pulmonary disease and hypertension, resulting in a final cohort of 20 participants (10 male, 10 female). The median age was 35 years (range, 23–54 years). The mean heart rate was 63.6±13.5 beats per minute, with a mean height of 1.69±0.06 meters, and a mean body weight of 73.00±11.35 kilograms.

As RHC is ethically inappropriate in healthy individuals, the MPAP reference values reported by Kovacs et al. (6), derived from RHC data in a healthy population, was used as the comparator standard.

Given the exploratory nature of this study and the absence of prior direct comparative data in healthy adults, a convenience sample of 20 participants was deemed sufficient to detect substantial differences in agreement rates and variability between modalities. This sample size is consistent with pilot method-comparison studies in imaging research.

Examination protocol

All participants first underwent PC-MRI, followed by TTE within 48 hours. Imaging was scheduled at comparable times of day to minimize the influence of diurnal physiological variation. A 48-hour interval was chosen to balance logistical feasibility with the need to minimize short-term hemodynamic changes.

PC-MRI

Image acquisition

MRI was performed using a 3.0 Tesla scanner (Signa HDxt, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) equipped with an 8-channel cardiac coil. Retrospective ECG and respiratory gating were employed to optimize image quality. Following standard localizer sequences, cine imaging of the main pulmonary artery (MPA) was conducted. Phase-contrast velocity-encoded sequences were obtained in a plane perpendicular to the MPA, approximately 15–20 mm proximal to the pulmonary valve annulus. The velocity encoding parameter was set to 150 cm/s. Each cardiac cycle was segmented into 30 phases. The acquisition time per slice ranged from 7 to 20 seconds, with the total scan duration kept under 5 minutes (Figure 1).

Image analysis and MPAP estimation

PC-MRI datasets were analyzed on a dedicated workstation (ADW 4.3, GE Healthcare) using ReportCARD 3.6 Flow software. Manual segmentation of the MPA CSA was performed for each phase of the cardiac cycle, with automated propagation across the sequence. The maximum CSA (Smax) and the peak systolic forward flow velocity (Umax).

MPAP was estimated using a previously validated computational model proposed by Laffon et al. (7). This model integrates Smax and Umax normalized to participant-specific variables (height, weight, and heart rate) to calculate a composite pressure metric(PpaComp). The detailed polynomial formulation is provided in the Appendix 1. All calculations were performed using a customized spreadsheet tool. All measurements were independently performed twice by a single radiologist with a one-week interval to assess intra-observer consistency. The average of the two measurements was used for statistical analysis.

TTE

Image acquisition

TTE was performed using a Philips iE33 ultrasound system (Philips Medical Systems, Andover, MA, USA) equipped with a 3.5–5.5 MHz transducer (Figure 2).

Measurement and MPAP estimation

From the left parasternal acoustic window, pulsed-wave Doppler imaging was employed with the sample volume placed within the MPA. Pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAAT) was defined as the interval from the onset of systolic flow to the point of peak flow velocity. The mean PAAT was calculated from three consecutive cardiac cycles. To account for heart rate variability, PAAT was corrected using Bazett’s formula (8): PAATc=PAAT/RR interval. MPAP was then estimated using the published regression model (9): MPAP=79−0.45×PAATc.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Stata version 12.0 (StataCorp LLC). Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the comparisons were examined by Shapiro-Wilk test. Agreement between each non-invasive method (PC-MRI and TTE) and the reference standard was defined as the estimated MPAP falling within the reported 95% confidence interval (CI) of the RHC-derived normative values reported by Kovacs et al. (6). While the use of the 95% CI of the reference mean provides a descriptive benchmark for central tendency, we acknowledge its limitations for individual-level classification. Therefore, we additionally calculated deviation from the reference mean and z-scores to provide a more granular assessment of agreement. A paired t-test was used to compare mean MPAP estimates derived from PC-MRI and TTE. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

All PC-MRI and TTE examinations were completed successfully without significant motion artifacts.

Quantitative parameters

The mean CSA of the MPA measured via PC-MRI was 5.14±0.99 cm2. The corresponding mean forward peak flow velocity was 72.59±11.58 cm/s. The heart rate-corrected PAATc, obtained from TTE, was 161±28 milliseconds.

Comparison of MPAP estimates

The mean MPAP estimated using PC-MRI was 14.08±2.95 mmHg, which fell entirely within the physiological range previously reported in healthy adults (6). In contrast, the mean MPAP derived from TTE was 6.70±12.89 mmHg, demonstrating markedly higher variability (Table 1).

While the paired t-test confirms a statistically significant difference between the two modalities (P<0.01), the primary evidence for superior reliability of PC-MRI lies in the consistently tighter clustering around the reference range and the absence of physiologically implausible estimates.

To further quantify agreement, we calculated the deviation of each participant’s MPAP estimate from the reference mean (14.0 mmHg) and the corresponding z-score using the reference SD (3.3 mmHg) reported by Kovacs et al. The mean deviation was +0.08 mmHg (range, −5.65 to +6.49) for PC-MRI and −7.30 mmHg (range, −30.27 to +10.11) for TTE. The mean z-score was +0.02 (range, −1.71 to +1.97) for PC-MRI and −2.21 (range, −9.17 to +3.06) for TTE.

A Bland-Altman analysis comparing PC-MRI and TTE MPAP estimates revealed a mean bias of 7.38 mmHg (95% CI: 1.28 to 13.48), with 95% limits of agreement ranging from -13.76 to 28.52 mmHg, indicating poor interchangeability between the two modalities.

Discussion

This study presents a direct comparative analysis of two non-invasive modalities, PC-MRI and TTE, for estimating MPAP in healthy adults. The principle finding is that PC-MRI demonstrated excellent agreement with established RHC-derived reference values, with all MPAP estimates (100%) falling within the expected physiological range. In contrast, TTE-derived estimates based on PAAT exhibited substantial variability and produced non-physiological negative values in 30% of the participants. These results underscore the superior consistency and reliability of the PC-MRI-based method for MPAP quantification in normotensive individuals.

The exceptional performance of PC-MRI may be attributed to its methodological foundation. Unlike TTE, which relies on indirect Doppler-based surrogates, PC-MRI enables direct quantification of central hemodynamic parameters, including peak systolic flow velocity, and anatomical measurements such as the MPA CSA (5). The computational model proposed by Laffon et al. (7) integrates these parameters along with anthropometric variables to derive a composite pressure estimate. This multidimensional, physics-based modeling approach has been further validated in subsequent investigations, including studies employing machine-learning techniques, which have demonstrated high concordance with invasive RHC measurements (10). Moreover, cardiac MRI offers intrinsic advantages over echocardiography, including reduced operator dependency and independence from acoustic window quality, factors that limit TTE reliability (3).

The growing clinical utility of cardiac MRI, particularly PC-MRI, lies in its ability to provide a comprehensive cardiovascular assessment in a single session, integrating ventricular volumetry, flow quantification, and tissue characterization (4). This capability aligns with current trends in pulmonary hypertension management. Recent advances in artificial intelligence have further enhanced the diagnostic potential of MRI-based techniques. For instance, deep learning models such as PHNet, derived from cine cardiac MRI data, have demonstrated high accuracy in the non-invasive identification of pulmonary hypertension (11). Such developments reinforce the potential of MRI, including PC-MRI, as a platform for future innovation in cardiovascular imaging.

Conversely, the suboptimal performance of the TTE-derived PAAT method in this healthy cohort highlights its well-recognized, yet frequently underestimated, limitations in low-pressure physiological states. The empirical formula used, MPAP=79−0.45×PAATc, represents a linear approximation of a complex nonlinear physiological relationship. In healthy individuals with prolonged PAAT, as is typical in normotensive individuals, this model becomes prone to extrapolation errors, occasionally resulting in non-physiological outputs, such as the negative MPAP values observed in this study (3,12). The negative MPAP values observed with TTE are likely attributable to the extrapolation of a linear regression model beyond its validated range. The Kitabatake formula was derived in patients with elevated MPAP, and its application to healthy individuals with longer PAATc values (mean 161 ms in our cohort) results in mathematical extrapolation that can yield negative values. This highlights a fundamental limitation of using linear models derived from diseased populations to estimate pressures in normal physiology.

Although heart rate correction was applied using Bazett’s formula, the inverse relationship between PAAT and heart rate remains a significant confounding factor. This sensitivity renders the measurement vulnerable to minor fluctuations in physiological parameters (8).

While TTE remains a valuable modality for initial structural screening and gross hemodynamic assessment, the present data suggest it lacks the reliability required for precise MPAP quantification in healthy individuals or in those with early stage pulmonary vascular disease. These findings are consistent with a recent Cochrane review, which reported considerable diagnostic variability in echocardiographic assessments of pulmonary hypertension, including significant rates of both false-negative and false-positive results across studies (13).

Additional methodological considerations should be acknowledged. The PC-MRI protocol used a fixed velocity encoding setting of 150 cm/s, which although commonly used, may not be optimal for all participants. Furthermore, this was a single-center study utilizing specific MRI hardware and software, which may limit external validity. Multicenter, investigations incorporating various scanner platforms are needed to evaluate reproducibility. The study population was relatively healthy, young, and free of comorbidities, which may restrict generalizability to older or clinically diverse populations. As RHC was not ethically justifiable in healthy volunteers, MPAP estimates were referenced against established RHC-derived normative values introducing an indirect element of comparison. Future studies should aim to correlate PC-MRI-derived MPAP with simultaneously acquired RHC measurements in clinical populations to strengthen validation.

These findings have important clinical and research implications. First, in settings where precise, serial, non-invasive monitoring of pulmonary pressures is required, such as in patients with systemic sclerosis or other connective tissue diseases, PC-MRI may serve as a more suitable surrogate endpoint than TTE-based estimates (4). Second, in routine clinical practice, PC-MRI should be considered when TTE yields inconclusive or discordant results in patients with a high pre-test probability of PAH, particularly in cases with suboptimal echocardiographic windows.

Finally, several limitations warrant mention. The modest sample size, while adequate to detect statistically significant differences, limits broader inference. However, this sample size of this study is consistent with pilot method-comparison studies in imaging research. Larger multi-center studies are warranted to confirm these findings. Second, the use of a literature-based RHC reference standard, though appropriate for healthy volunteer research (6), introduces an inherent limitation compared to direct hemodynamic validation. Additionally, although the PC-MRI acquisition protocol required brief breath-holding, which could present a challenge in individuals with advanced respiratory symptoms, the short scan duration (<5 minutes) renders it feasible for the majority of individuals. While our findings in healthy adults demonstrate superior agreement with established reference ranges and reliability of PC-MRI compared to TTE for MPAP estimation, these results serve as a necessary baseline. Future studies in patient populations with suspected or confirmed pulmonary hypertension are essential to validate the clinical utility of PC-MRI in settings where diagnostic agreement with established reference ranges is paramount.

Practical implementation of PC-MRI in routine clinical settings must consider factors such as cost, scanner availability, and longer acquisition times compared to TTE. A hybrid approach, using TTE for initial screening and reserving PC-MRI for cases requiring confirmatory assessment, may offer a pragmatic balance between accessibility and diagnostic consistency. Ongoing efforts to streamline acquisition protocols and develop automated post-processing tools are essential to facilitate broader clinical adoption. These findings do not support replacing echocardiography in routine practice but rather suggest that PC-MRI may serve as a complementary tool in specific scenarios where precise, non-invasive pressure estimation is required and TTE results are inconclusive or discordant. The narrow hemodynamic range inherent to healthy volunteers limits the generalizability of our findings to patients with pulmonary hypertension, who exhibit a broader range of MPAP values. The restricted range also reduces the precision of agreement estimates and increases sensitivity to outliers, though no outliers were observed in PC-MRI data.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that a comprehensive PC-MRI approach significantly outperforms TTE-based PAAT measurements in the non-invasive estimation of MPAP in healthy adults. The findings highlight the limitations of relying on TTE-derived pressure estimates for precise hemodynamic assessment in normotensive individuals and support the use of PC-MRI as a more accurate and reproducible alternative. These results position PC-MRI as a valuable research tool and a potential adjunct in the early detection and monitoring of pulmonary vascular disease. Future investigations should aim to validate this model in clinical populations with confirmed or suspected PAH, and to explore its integration with other CMR markers, such as right ventricular strain, volume, and myocardial tissue characterization, to support comprehensive, non-invasive risk stratification. Future studies incorporating simultaneous RHC in clinical populations are necessary to validate the diagnostic reliability of PC-MRI-based MPAP estimation.
